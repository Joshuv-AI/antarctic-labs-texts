High-fat diet-induced anorexia in rats: involving orosensory deficits, gastrointestinal dysfunction, intestinal taste receptor downregulation and dopamine signaling dysregulation
Authors: Qina Ye, Liying Zeng, Xiangna Yang, Jing Zhang, Zhuoming Lu, Jian Deng, Zequn Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore how a high-fat diet (HFD) led to anorexia in rats via peripheral–central axis integrated dysfunction.MethodsTwenty male Sprague‒Dawley rats were randomly assigned to the control group (G1, standard chow; n = 10) or the high-fat diet group (G2; n = 10) for 27 days. Body weight and food consumption were recorded at regular intervals. The gastric residual rate (GRR) and small intestinal advancement rate (SIAR) were used to evaluate gastrointestinal motility. The circumvallate papillae (CVP), proximal jejunum and hypothalamus were collected postintervention. Analyses included analyses of CVP histology (H&E staining), taste receptor type 1 member expression (T1R1/T1R2/T1R3) and transient receptor potential melastatin 5 (TRPM5) through immunohistochemistry and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and hypothalamic dopamine (DA) levels by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsTotal food intake (41.5% lower) and final body weight (21.8% lower) were significantly lower in the HFD group than in the control group. The morphology of CVP was significantly degenerated by HFD feeding, resulting in a 49.13% reduction in the taste bud count. Gastrointestinal dysfunction was evidenced by increased GRR and decreased SIAR. Although taste receptor expression in CVP remained unchanged, the proximaljejunal mRNA expression levels of T1R1, T1R3, and TRPM5 significantly decreased. Notably, the DA concentration in the hypothalamus was also significantly lower in HFD-fed rats than in control rats.ConclusionsHFD consumption effectively induced anorexia in young rats, which was accompanied by gastrointestinal dysfunction, a reduction in taste buds, impaired gut–brain axis taste transduction and a suppressed central DA response. These results indicate that peripheral–central axis impairment may be an important pathological phenomenon in HFD-induced anorexia.Keywords: Anorexia, High-fat diet, Taste receptors, Dopamine

Introduction

Taste perception is vital for the regulation of feeding behavior and nutritional balance through the integration of environmental signals with metabolic cues [1]. This chemosensory system largely determines what we like to eat and, therefore, consumes through hedonic evaluation, while reduced perception from either compromised detection or signal distortion is a common basis of hyporexia and anorexia [2]. The disease predominantly targets hedonic aspects of taste processing [3, 4], and the pleasure it conveys upon sweet and umami (perceived tastants are important modulators of food-reward networks) is diminished [5, 6].

Mammalian taste transduction is initiated in specialized taste buds containing dynamic taste receptor cells (TRCs) that undergo constant renewal [7]. These chemosensory units detect five canonical taste modalities (sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami) via molecular recognition events. Upon tastant binding, TRCs generate action potentials that propagate via primary afferent neurons to central gustatory pathways, ultimately generating conscious taste perception [8, 9]. Notably, taste receptor expression extends beyond the oral epithelium and includes gastrointestinal enteroendocrine cells, thus suggesting broader nutrient-sensing roles [10].

Sweet taste perception exhibits ligand-dependent pathway divergence. Natural sweeteners such as glucose use two routes of activation: the canonical route mediated through T1R2/T1R3 heterodimeric receptors and alternative transduction via nutrient sensors, such as sodium‒glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1). In contrast, artificial sweeteners, such as sucralose, require T1R2/T1R3 activation only. This split mirrors the conserved transduction logic in sweet and umami (where both use their own T1R heterodimers (T1R2/T1R3 for sweet, T1R1/T1R3 for umami) that converge onto a shared signallling axis [11]. Three sequential canals constitute the unified transduction cascade. The first step is receptor activation, with ligand binding leading to a conformational change in T1R complexes and stimulation of G protein-dependent phosphorylation of PLCβ2. The second step is the generation of a second messenger by phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) hydrolysis through PLCβ2 to generate inositol trisphosphate (IP3), which activates endoplasmic reticulum-localized IP3 receptor type 3 (IP3R3), resulting in Ca2+ release into the cytoplasm. A final stage is signal amplification, in which calcium entry into the cytosolic gate opening of transient receptor potential melastatin 5 (TRPM5) channels results in membrane depolarization and subsequent adenosine triphosphate (ATP) release to gustatory afferent neurons [12]. This multistep amplification system ensures precise discrimination between taste qualities while maintaining energy-state-dependent modulation of feeding behavior.

Disruptedhedonic processing is thought to be a central pathophysiology of anorexia-reduced eating induced by taste; however, the underlying molecular mechanism for this definition of a clear taste transduction pathway is still unclear. An investigation of this gap in our understanding will necessitate an animal model that correlates the behavioral effects of anorexia with particular molecular events occurring to activate the taste transduction pathway. Multiple methods exist for establishing anorexia models in rats. Neurogenic anorexia models primarily include those involving dietary restriction, stress-induced appetite suppression, stress-induced hyperactivity (SIH), and activity-based anorexia (ABA) [13]. In addition, pharmacological interventions such as GDF15 administration [14], cisplatin treatment [15], and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure [16] can effectively induce anorexia-like symptoms. Although current animal models can replicate certain physiological features of anorexia nervosa, they fail to capture its multidimensional pathological complexity. Thus, developing next-generation models that align more closely with the core pathophysiological mechanisms remains imperative. Notably, Zhao et al. [17] reported that a 25% high-fat diet (HFD) effectively induces juvenile anorexia phenotypes, characterized by decreased appetite and body weight loss. Given the substantial evidence linking an HFD to taste perception dysfunction and altered brain reward processing in experimental animals [18–20], our research used a rat model of anorexia induced by an HFD. Utilizing a combination of histological analysis, immunohistochemistry, qPCR and ELISA, we aimed to answer the following questions: first, do an HFD specifically injures orosensory structures and dysregulates taste-related molecules in both CVP and intestinal tissues while inhibiting gastrointestinal motility? Second, how are these underlying peripheral alterations connected with central DA reduction, and how does this integrated action between peripheral–central dysfunction result in feeding suppression?

Methods

Materials

An HFD was obtained from Shuyu Shengwu Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China; batch No. SYYSZ01). Hematoxylin staining solution (Servicebio, batch No. G1004), eosin staining solution (Servicebio, batch No. G1001), hematoxylin color separation solution (Servicebio, batch No. G1039), hematoxylin blue solution (Servicebio, batch No. G1040), polyformaldehyde (WestAsia reagent, batch No. 2024071201), isoflurane (RWD, batch No. 2024071201), and a DA Elisa kit (MEIMIAN, batch No. MM-0355R2) were purchased from Boyao Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). T1R1 (Bioss, batch No. Bs-8612R), TRPM5 (Proteintech, batch No. 18027-AP), T1R2 (Bioss, batch No. Bs-9599R), and T1R3 (Univ, batch No. Ls-c829369-100) were obtained from Chenxing Shengwu Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Animal preparation

All animal experiments were conducted with 4-week-old SPF-grade male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats weighing 150–200 g. The rats were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (license number: SCXK (Guangdong) 2022-0002). For 7 days before the experiments commenced, the animals were acclimated in a room with a controlled temperature (23–26 °C) and humidity (40–60%) and a 12 h day/night cycle. All animal experiments were approved by the animal ethical committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Approval No. KTDW-2024-00483).

Animal grouping and treatment

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (n = 20) were randomly allocated to a control group (G1; n = 10) or a high-fat diet group (G2; n = 10) and fed in 5 cages, with 2 animals per cage. The G1 group was fed standard chow, and the G2 group received a custom formulation comprising milk powder, fish meal, corn flour, soybean flour, egg yolk powder, lard, and sucrose at a weight-to-weight ratio of 1:1:2:1.8:2:1. The macronutrient values of the standard chaw and high-fat diet are listed in Table 1. Both groups were provided ad libitum access to their respective diets for 27 consecutive days, with precise measurements of food intake and body mass recorded at 48-h intervals throughout the intervention period. On day 28, after a 24-h fast, all the rats received 1 mL/100 g body weight of a charcoal-labeled nutrient semisolid paste (composed of 8% milk powder, 4% sucrose, 4% starch, and 3% activated charcoal in 3% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose solution). Thirty minute post-gavage, the animals were euthanized by an intraperitoneal overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg).

Following euthanasia, the hypothalamic tissue and circumvallate papillae (CVP) of the tongue were rapidly dissected. The hypothalamic samples and one portion of the CVP were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C. The other portion of the CVP was immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histological processing. All procedures were completed within 30 s of collection to preserve biomolecular integrity and tissue morphology. Subsequent gastrointestinal tract processing involved isolation of the stomach through pyloric junction transection for recording total organ weight (W1), followed by luminal content removal via three rinses with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (10 mL/wash) after an incision was made along the greater curvature. The blotted empty stomach was reweighed (W2) on precalibrated filter paper. For intestinal motility assessment, the intact small intestine was linearly arranged on a millimeter-graduated dissection tray, allowing precise measurement of total length (L1) and the activated charcoal migration distance (L2) from the pylorus to the ileocecal valve. The gastric residual rate (GRR) and small intestinal advancement rate (SIAR) were calculated as (W1–W2)/W0 ∗ 100% and (L2/L1) ∗ 100%, respectively, where W0 represents the administered NCSP weight. After the small intestine advancement rate was calculated, proximal jejunal specimens were sampled 9 cm distal to the pylorus. The processed proximal jejunal tissues were transferred to prelabeled cryovials and stored at − 80 °C. The specific experimental process is presented in Fig. 1.

Histological investigation of rat circumvallate papillae

The 4% PFA-fixed circumvallate papillae samples were sequentially dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70–100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sects. (5 μm thick) were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Bright-field imaging was performed using a white light microscope equipped with a Digital Sight MS60 camera (Mingmei, Guangzhou, China) at 200 × magnification. For quantitative analysis, ten consecutive tissue sections were systematically examined. The total number of taste buds was determined by counting bilateral regions along the contour groove in each section, with final values representing the summed counts from both anatomical sides.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Circumvallate papillae samples were processed through graded ethanol dehydration and xylene clearing, followed by paraffin embedding at 58–60 °C. Sections (3 μm thick) were mounted on slides, dried at 60 °C, and stored at 37 °C overnight. After sequential deparaffinization (TO-type clearing agents I/II, 15 min each) and rehydration through graded alcohols (100–70%, 2 min per step), antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) via microwave heating (high power for 3 min × 2, medium power for 3 min × 2). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with Reagent A (15 min), followed by nonspecific binding inhibition using Reagent B (30 min). The sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and then sequentially treated with a biotinylated secondary antibody (Reagent C, 30 min) and a streptavidin–HRP conjugate (Reagent D, 15 min). DAB chromogen (100 μL/slide) was applied under microscopic monitoring (30 s to 5 min) until brown‒yellow signal development. Counterstaining with hematoxylin (3 min) included differentiation (0.5% HCl–alcohol, 3 s) and bluing steps. Final dehydration through ethanol‒xylene gradients preceded neutral resin mounting for bright-field microscopy analysis [21]. Quantitative immunohistochemical analysis was performed using a standardized dual-parameter scoring system following whole-slide scanning. The proportion of positively stained cells was stratified as follows: < 10% (1 point), 10–50% (2 points), 50–75% (3 points), and > 75% (4 points). Concurrently, the staining intensity was graded on a 0–3 scale: 0 (no staining), 1 (light yellow/weak), 2 (golden brown/moderate), and 3 (dark brown/strong). The immunoreactivity score (IRS) was derived by multiplying these two independent scores (IRS = percent score × intensity score), generating a composite metric ranging from 0 to 12 for comparative analysis across samples.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from lingual tissue and proximal jejunum using RNAzol RT reagent (Bioteke, China; Cat# RP1202) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA purity/concentration was assessed by absorbance measurement using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the FastKing RT Kit (Vazyme, China; Cat# R223-01). Quantitative PCR was performed in triplicate on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA; Cat# 12016265) using TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, China; Cat# AQ141-02). Each 20-μL reaction contained 10 μL of 2 × SuperMix, 0.4 μL of forward primer (10 μmol/L), 0.4 μL of reverse primer (10 μmol/L), 1 μL of 1:10 diluted cDNA template, and 8.2 μL of nuclease-free water. The thermal profile consisted of initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min; 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s; and melt curve analysis (65–95 °C at 0.5 °C/s increments). The relative mRNA expression of T1R1, T1R2, T1R3, and TRPM5 was normalized to that of β-actin (ACTB) and calculated via the 2−∆∆Ct method [22], with the primer sequences detailed in Table 2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Hypothalamic tissues (target weight ≈50 mg) were homogenized in ice-cold PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4; 500 μL per 50 mg of tissue) using a polytron homogenizer (ice-bath cooled) or liquid nitrogen mortar grinding for RNA-sensitive applications. The homogenates were centrifuged at 5000 × g (4 °C, 15 min), and the supernatants were collected for ELISA analysis. For ELISA, 96-well plates were loaded with serially diluted standards (50 μL/well) and test samples (10 μL of supernatant + 40 μL of assay diluent). All the wells received 100 μL of HRP-conjugated detection antibody and were incubated at 37 °C (± 0.5 °C) for 60 min. After five wash cycles (350 μL/well, 1 min of static incubation), TMB substrate (50 μL of A + B mixture) was added for 15 min of light-protected development at 37 °C. The reactions were terminated with 2 N H2SO4 (50 μL/well), and the OD450 was measured within 15 min using a microplate reader [23].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as medians (interquartile ranges), and the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for group comparisons. A two-tailed α level of 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Changes in food intake and body weight

As expected, compared with those in G1, the average food intake and body weight were lower in G2. As shown in Fig. 2a, a statistically significant reduction in food intake was observed in G2 compared with that in G1 starting on day 13 (P < 0.05), with the difference increasing by day 15 (P < 0.01). During the 27-day intervention, the total food intake was significantly lower in G2 (30.82 ± 4.06 g) than in G1 (52.68 ± 6.96 g), corresponding to a 41.5% reduction. As shown in Fig. 2b, body weight gain was significantly slower in G2 than in G1 by day 7 (P < 0.05). This difference increased progressively until day 9 (P < 0.01). At the end of the experiment, the final body weight of G2 (243.50 ± 8.99 g) was 21.8% lower than that of G1 (311.30 ± 68.73 g). Notably, both groups also gained weight without interruption throughout the entire experimental period, which is characteristic of normal growth patterns in developing rodents.

Effects of an HFD on gastrointestinal motility in rats

As presented in Fig. 2c, compared with that in G1, the GRR in G2 clearly increased, with an absolute difference of 17.45% (P = 0.04). In addition, as shown in Fig. 2d, compared with G1, G2 showed a significant decrease in the SIAR, with an absolute reduction of 11.26% (P = 0.001).

Structural and taste bud alterations in circumvallate papillae

As illustrated in Fig. 3a, histological examination revealed well-organized circumvallate papillae with distinct contour grooves and abundant bilateral taste bud distribution in G1. Conversely, G2 exhibited marked structural degeneration of the CVP, characterized by groove obliteration and bilateral taste bud depletion. Quantitative morphometric analysis revealed a statistically significant 49.13% reduction in taste bud density among G2 CVPs compared with that among G1 CVPs (P < 0.01), with detailed comparative data presented in Fig. 3b.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of the circumvallate papillae revealed conserved mRNA expression profiles for taste-related signaling components across the experimental groups. As shown in Fig. 5a, the relative mRNA expression levels of T1R1, T1R2, T1R3, and TRPM5 did not significantly differ between the two groups (all P > 0.05); this transcriptional stability paralleled our immunohistochemical findings. In summary, our data indicate that exposure to an HFD results in dysfunction across multiple systems. These include decreased ingestion and body weight, dysmotility in the gastrointestinal tract, compromised orosensory organ morphology, impaired gut taste receptor expression (T1R1/T1R3/TRPM5), and concomitant depletion of hypothalamic dopamine. Taken together, these findings delineate a concerted pathophysiological pattern of HFD-induced anorexia in rats.

Discussion

Taste perception is an important chemosensory system in mammals that enables the selection of suitable foods and contributes to energy balance homeostasis. The function of taste buds, the predominant epithelial structures containing taste receptor cells (TRCs), is dependent on their anatomical and functional integrity. Indeed, changes in taste bud morphology, a common feature of metabolic disorders, dynamically regulate atypical dietary preferences and nutrient intake [24]. Molecularly, TRCs use membrane-bound receptors such as T1Rs and T2Rs in combination with downstream effectors, including TRPM5, to detect or transduce tastants. Mechanistically, this process initiates G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)-mediated transduction cascades to translate chemical signals into neural impulses [25]. Our data revealed a vast disarray of CVPs and significantly lower taste bud density in rats fed an HFD, indicating an evident loss of oral sensory information early in pathogenesis. Even though gross morphological atrophy of taste buds occurred, the expression of taste cell markers (T1R1, T1R2, T1R3 and TRPM5) within the lingua tissue was not altered at the protein or mRNA level. These findings imply the maintenance of molecular machinery in some taste buds, possibly representing functional compensation at the cellular level. Nevertheless, this restricted compensatory capability might be unable to compensate for the signal output loss due to massive degeneration of almost half of the sensory inventory.

In addition to orosensory impairment at an early stage, a concomitant deficit originating in the gut was revealed. The intestine is a secondary taste organ mediated in part by enteroendocrine cells [26–28], and the mRNA levels of T1R1, T1R3 and TRPM5 were found to be significantly lower in G2. Since l-glutamate is exclusively sensed by T1R1/T1R3 heterodimers [29], T1Rs serve as prototypical transducers of the umami taste stimulus, which is widely present in foodstuffs, such as tomatoes, seafood and dairy products [30, 31], and its deficiency might impair intestinal nutrient sensing. Moreover, the T1R1/T1R3 receptors identified on chromosome 1p36 [32] mediate amino acid tastesensation (l-glutamate, l-aspartate, and glycine) in multiple tissues [33]. This in turn may impair vagal afferent signaling in the gut–brain system [34], which is important for satiety mechanisms and energy homeostasis. This enteric signaling defect functions as a secondary hit to gut–brain crosstalk, impairing satiety signaling. Gastrointestinal dysmotility was also observed as delayed gastric emptying (increased GRR) and reduced intestinal propulsion (decreased SIAR). These physiological adaptations may perpetuate anorexia. They may act through stomach–brain axis signaling [35, 36] by producing antral distension and perhaps inducing dysphoria, creating a strong feedback mechanism that inhibits appetite.

Feeding behavior represents an evolutionarily conserved, reward-motivated action [37] that is hierarchically controlled by dopaminergic circuits. Information from oral taste receptors is transmitted via the tractus solitarii (NTS)–thalamocortical pathway, activating the projection of dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) [38]. Dopamine activation results in a transient dopamine peak that encodes incentive salience through NAc D1 receptor-expressing medium spiny neurons [39]. This neuroadaptation establishes a positive feedback loop that coordinates feeding behavior with the metabolic state to drive the consumption of palatable, high-calorie food. The midbrain dopaminergic system, including the VTA, SNpc and NAc [40–42], plays an integrative role in regulating feeding responses to reward. The most important central change in G2 in this study was a marked decrease in hypothalamic dopamine. This simultaneous peripheral loss leads to decreased orosensory pleasure and impaired enteroendocrine signaling. It seems to have a crucial effect on this central reward system. The release of dopamine in the reward circuit is regulated by palatable taste input and postingestive nutrient signaling [43]. The combined consequences of CVP disruption and intestinal T1R1/T1R3/TRPM5 downregulation reciprocally attenuate reward circuit activation. This reduction in simultaneously activated inputs could account for the reduced hedonic response and reduced motivation to eat. The anorexic behavior observed could thus be attributed to a dysfunctional behavioral pattern linked to the altered peripheral–central loop. In this negative feedback loop, poor sensory input and poor gut–brain signaling result in a failure to activate the mesolimbic dopamine system, and as such, eating behavior is decreased. Thus, the anorexic behavior in our model can be described as the behavioral endophenotype of central–peripheral circuit failure, whereby reduced orosensory input combined with defective gut–brain communication results in insufficient activation of the mesolimbic dopamine circuit and subsequently altered feeding behavior.

In contrast to the conventional paradigm in which an HFD is associated with obesity [44, 45], our data revealed significantly lower body weight and food intake in HFD-fed rats (G2) than in control rats (G1). This paradoxical phenomenon may be attributed to several critical experimental parameters: the shortened 27-day intervention period versus typical obesity studies (> 5 weeks) [46, 47] and the unique lipid composition of our HFD formulation containing 25% fat. Current international studies modeling diet-induced obesity predominantly utilize high-fat diets (HFDs) with 45–60% fat content [48]. More importantly, unlike these standard HFD formulations [49], our model with whole-food protein sources (skim milk powder, fish meal, and soybean flour) incorporates egg yolk powder and maintains matched sucrose levels. In addition, bioactive peptides from both plant and animal sources, including soy, pea and milk proteins, have been shown to induce satiety hormone release both in vitro and in vivo [50]. This effect may explain the appetite-suppressive effects demonstrated in our model. Notably, the strain of the SD rats employed in this study might have relatively low sensitivity to the induction of metabolic obesity. Wistar rats exhibit more profound metabolic alterations when fed an HFD [51]. Consistent with the findings of Zhao et al. [17], our 25% fat diet robustly induced anorexia in rodents, demonstrating technical accessibility and high reproducibility. These advantages may position our anorexia model for adoption and refinement into standardized methodological frameworks for appetite regulation research.

Limitations

While this work provides correlative insights into HFD-induced anorexia, several limitations demand attention. First and more importantly, although we observed coordinated alterations along the peripheral–central axis, we did not conduct functional intervention studies, such as restoring intestinal T1R1/T1R3/TRPM5 or supplementing dopamine in the brain, to establish a functional model. Second, the use of a single end-point after the 27-day treatment does not allow us to determine whether motor disorders in gastrointestinal motility disorders develop before or after taste bud degeneration or whether the decrease in hypothalamic dopamine precedes that of food intake. In the future, it would be valuable to use a more longitudinal approach, measuring at different timepoints, including follow-up participants after treatment termination, so that we could have a better view of anorexia-related changes over time. Third, while prioritizing multisystemic mechanisms necessitating omission of taste preference assays, the morphological disruption of circumvallate papillae strongly indicates gustatory impairment. Future research should incorporate functional taste validation. In addition, resource limitations precluded protein-level confirmation of intestinal targets and direct nausea assessment (e.g., pica). However, the concordance between transcriptional changes and functional impairments, coupled with the time-dependent progression of anorexic phenotypes, collectively substantiates our conclusions despite methodological constraints. Finally, reward circuitry (VTA/NAc) analysis was strategically omitted to maintain hypothalamic focus, although circuit-specific investigations are now prioritized in subsequent work.

Conclusions

The results of the present study revealed that an HFD induces anorexia associated with a series of physiological changes, such as gastrointestinal dysmotility, structural degeneration of the CVP with taste bud loss, and the downregulation of intestinal T1R1/T1R3/TRPM5 expression and a decrease in hypothalamic dopamine levels. Our findings indicate that these peripheral and central deficits collectively contribute to the anorexic phenotype. Integrated dysfunction across the orosensory, gastrointestinal and central dopaminergic systems represents a significant pathophysiological characteristic of HFD-induced anorexia, as demonstrated by our experimental model.
