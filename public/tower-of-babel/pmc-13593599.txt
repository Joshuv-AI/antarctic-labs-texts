Equine alveolar echinococcosis: a focus on epidemiology, clinical and pathological features, and diagnostics
Authors: Tatsuro HIFUMI, Tetsuya TANAKA, Kohei AKIOKA, Noriaki MIYOSHI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Here, we review equine alveolar echinococcosis (AE), focusing on its epidemiology, clinical and pathological features, and diagnostic challenges, through comparisons with AE in other host species. AE is a zoonotic disease caused by the larval form of the Echinococcus multilocularis tapeworm. Horses are considered dead-end hosts, in which AE infection can be detected at slaughter, highlighting their potential as sentinel animals in endemic areas. Equine AE is typically asymptomatic and is generally only found incidentally during meat inspection or necropsy. Standard diagnosis involves histopathology and conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing. Epidemiological surveys in Japan, Poland, and Canada have reported larval E. multilocularis detection rates ranging from 1.5–18.8%, where infections manifest as macroscopic grayish-white solid liver nodules in slaughtered horses. Molecular phylogenetic analysis targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome b(cob) gene has revealed that parasites isolated from horses in Japan and Canada belong to distinct haplogroups, indicating utility for elucidating the geographical genetic characteristics of larval E. multilocularis infection in horses. Isothermal amplification techniques such as loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) have demonstrated equivalent or superior sensitivity and specificity for detecting equine AE and may represent novel rapid genetic diagnostic tools as alternatives to PCR testing for rapid gene-based detection. These techniques will contribute to early detection of AE, especially in locations where specialized laboratory equipment is limited (e.g., abattoirs). Continued research on equine AE will thus greatly contribute to elucidating the environmental distribution of E. multilocularis and supporting the control of zoonotic disease.

Alveolar echinococcosis (AE), a zoonotic disease with the potential to pose a significant threat to public health, is caused by the larval form of Echinococcus multilocularis [8]. The life cycle of E. multilocularis involves wild canids and dogs as definitive hosts and small rodents as intermediate hosts [10]. Horses become infected through the oral ingestion of E. multilocularis eggs shed into the environment in definitive-host feces, and the larvae that develop from these eggs cause lesions, most commonly in the livers of their equine hosts [13] (Fig. 1). Affected horses are often clinically asymptomatic, making it difficult to detect equine AE while alive. However, the detection of larval E. multilocularis lesions in slaughtered horses provides important information from a public health perspective about the regional parasite prevalence in the region where the relevant horses were raised [21, 53]. Recently, there have been several reports in Japan on the identification of equine AE during meat inspections, and the insights gained from these detailed meat inspections are contributing to global epidemiological research on equine AE [13, 17,18,19,20,21, 54].

Here, we review equine AE, with a focus on its distribution, epidemiology, clinical and pathological features, and current diagnostic challenges. Through this discussion, we also compare the characteristics of infections in horses with those in humans and other host species.

Distribution and Epidemiology

Geographic distribution and epidemiology in human and definitive host animals

AE is endemic in large parts of the Northern Hemisphere, including central and eastern Europe, parts of North America, China, and Japan [8, 10]. Across these regions, human cases have emerged due to accidental ingestion of eggs excreted by infected canids [8, 10]. Red foxes and coyotes are important sylvatic hosts, and their increased presence in urban environments has extended the distribution of E. multilocularis, especially in Europe and Canada, with peri-domestic cycles involving dogs also further contribute to this spread [5, 35, 38, 48, 49].

E. multilocularis can reportedly be classified into four geographic clades based on haplogroup [39]. These are the European, Asian, North American, and Mongolian clades, and this geographical classification was established using the mitochondrial genes cytochrome b (cob), NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 (nad2), and cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1). Mitochondrial gene haplotyping for investigating the geographic classification of E. multilocularis has been reported for humans [1], rodents [57], and wild canids [12]. A mitogenome analysis in Japan revealed the presence of multi-origin E. multilocularis in Hokkaido. Specifically, this report identified a lineage originating from Alaska (St. Lawrence Island) distributed across all of Hokkaido and another lineage originating from Sichuan Province in China localized mainly in eastern Hokkaido [16].

Interestingly, E. multilocularis genes belonging to the European haplogroups have also been detected in North America in the past decade. These findings were based on isolates from humans and dogs with no history of travel to Europe, indicating some discrepancy between geographic clade membership and the location of detection [24, 36]. Two hypotheses have been proposed to explain the presence of European E. multilocularis tapeworms and larvae in North America [24]. First, they may have been introduced by dogs that were brought from Europe by owners migrating to North America. Second, E. multilocularis may have been carried into North America between the 1700s and 1900s by French and Scandinavian red foxes introduced for hunting with hounds.

Distribution and epidemiology in horses

Horses become infected with larval E. multilocularis through the ingestion of E. multilocularis eggs present in soil or vegetation contaminated by definitive-host feces [10, 13]. Generally, they will not develop evident clinical disease, and the infection will remain undetected until necropsy or meat inspection at slaughter [13, 17, 18, 21, 25, 53]. Hepatic AE lesions have been found in some of slaughtered horses originating from Japan, Canada, and Poland [13, 17, 18, 21, 53]. Larval E. multilocularis infection has been reported in these slaughtered horses at detection rates ranging from 1.5% to 18.8%, and the affected horses typically present with macroscopic grayish-white solid nodules in the liver [13, 17, 18, 21, 53]. Notably, reports of equine AE have covered major horse-producing regions, such as Hokkaido (Japan) and Alberta (Canada), where AE is endemic [21]. The prevalence in horses may reflect local environmental contamination and the prevalence in definitive host species [21]. The ages of the horses showing AE at slaughter ranged from 2 to 11 years, with no sex or seasonal predilection [13, 17, 21, 53]. Affected equine breeds include ponies, Hokkaido ponies, and heavy draft horses, as well as Thoroughbreds [13, 17, 21, 25, 53]. Although horses are the main equid species affected, AE has also been found in a kulan (Equus hemionus kulan) kept in a Slovakian zoo [6].

Genetic analyses of E. multilocularis isolates from horses also illustrate some interesting developments with regard to haplotype distribution across continents. In previous molecular phylogenetic and haplogroup analyses targeting the E. multilocularis cob gene in isolates obtained from Japanese slaughterhouses, we demonstrated that horses raised in Japan were carrying larvae belonging to the Asian clade, whereas horses imported from Alberta, Canada, were carrying larvae belonging to the European clade [21]. These findings corroborate earlier analyses targeting the E. multilocularis mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene in larvae isolated from Japanese horses, which also identified the Asian clade [13, 17]. Interestingly, recent studies have shown that the European strain has also invaded Alberta, a major horse-producing region in Canada [46]. Thus, it is highly likely that horses imported from Canada were infected with E. multilocularis before being exported to Japan. Therefore, molecular phylogenetic analysis targeting the cob gene of E. multilocularis is a highly useful tool for elucidating the geographical genetic characteristics of larval E. multilocularis isolated from horses.

Horses serving as potential sentinel animals for AE

Pigs are known to serve as sentinel animals for AE, as they may provide indications of the extent of environmental E. multilocularis egg contamination and the risk of human exposure to the parasite [30]. Similarly, horses are exposed primarily through the ingestion of eggs in contaminated pasture or water. However, due to their extensive grazing behavior and longer lifespans than those of other livestock species, horses have a markedly higher probability of encountering parasite eggs, especially when pastures are frequented by wild canids. Given these factors, there is a compelling argument that horses, alongside pigs, can serve as valuable sentinel animals for AE [21, 53].

Clinical Features and Pathology

Clinical features and pathology in humans, definitive hosts, intermediate hosts, and pigs

In humans, AE is often asymptomatic but may present with severe clinical signs such as hepatomegaly, jaundice, and liver failure decades after the initial infection with larval E. multilocularis in some cases [4, 8, 10, 28]. The disease progression can resemble that of a malignant tumor, with lesions found in both the liver and other organs and delays in diagnosis potentially leading to fatal outcomes [4]. Ultrasonographically, human AE typically appears as heterogeneous liver masses, punctate calcifications, and central necrosis [3]. Histopathological diagnosis is based on fibrous lesions with central necrosis and calcification as well as irregularly shaped laminated layers that are positive on PAS staining [2]. Protoscoleces are rarely seen in human AE cases [2].

While dogs generally act as definitive hosts of E. multilocularis, they can also act as aberrant intermediate hosts if they accidentally ingest E. multilocularis eggs, leading to the development of canine AE [8]. The clinical signs of canine AE include abdominal distension, vomiting, lethargy, weight loss, anorexia, diarrhea, and abdominal pain [32]. Ultrasonography typically shows multiple cavitary hepatic lesions with thickened walls and heterogeneous fluid accumulation [32]. Grossly, solitary or multiple hepatic cystic lesions ranging from 5 mm to 15 cm in size are present, often showing metastasis to other organs [32]. Histopathologically, the lesions are characterized by multilocular cysts, fibrous tissue proliferation, granulomatous inflammation, and numerous calcareous corpuscles; protoscoleces are rarely observed [32].

Wild voles, including Arvicola amphibius and Microtus agrestis, have been reported to be suitable intermediate hosts for E. multilocularis [37]. Grossly, livers of infected voles reportedly exhibit solitary or multiple multilocular cysts ranging from 3 mm to over 6 mm in diameter, which may contain serous fluid and protoscoleces within the cystic cavities [37]. Histopathologically, these cysts exhibit the characteristic laminated layers, germinal layers, calcareous corpuscles, and protoscoleces, with surrounding granulomatous inflammation [37].

Pigs are dead-end hosts of E. multilocularis, and porcine AE is usually asymptomatic, often found incidentally during meat inspections [8, 30]. Macroscopically, solitary or multiple white nodules ranging from 2 mm to 3 cm in diameter are observed in the liver [8, 26]. Histopathologically, lesions include laminated layers, fibrosis, necrosis, and occasional calcification [8, 26]. Generally, protoscoleces are not seen in porcine AE, but immature brood capsule formation has been observed histopathologically in one case [45].

Clinical features and pathology in horses

Clinically, equine AE is almost always asymptomatic, with diagnoses based on lesions discovered incidentally during necropsy or meat inspection [13, 17, 18, 21, 25, 53]. Therefore, there have been no reports of imaging findings in equine AE, marking a major difference compared with human and canine AE [3, 32]. Diagnostic Methods

Serological diagnosis

Human AE is often diagnosed through serological tests, for which enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) and Western blot assays are widely used [7, 14, 15, 23]. ELISAs employing antigens such as Em2 [14, 15], Em2-Em18 [7], Em18 [15, 23], and recombinant Em18 [15, 23] achieve particularly high sensitivity. In analyses of human serum samples, a Western blot assay using recombinant Em18 has demonstrated increased specificity by distinguishing E. multilocularis from related cestodes [23].

Given the above reports, attention has also turned to serological analyses for the diagnosis of equine AE. A commercial Western blot assay was applied to sera from 23 slaughtered horses naturally infected with E. multilocularis; although human AE patient-specific bands (7, 16, 18, and 26–28 kDa) were absent, the two horses with the largest hepatic nodules exhibited 22- or 30-kDa bands, suggesting these bands may be useful markers for diagnosing equine AE [54]. We have previously evaluated an ELISA using a commercially available kit for humans (E. multilocularis (Em2–Em18) IgG ELISA Kit, Bordier Affinity Products SA, Crissier, Switzerland) with serum collected from horses definitively diagnosed with AE based on histopathology and PCR testing. However, these human-optimized assays failed to demonstrate sufficient cross-reactivity, yielding inconclusive results for equine applications (data not shown).

In summary, ELISA and Western blot assays using commercial kits are reliable for the diagnosis of human AE. However, their application for the diagnosis of equine AE remains challenging. Therefore, further research may be needed to validate serological responses and tests in horses and to develop equine-specific commercial diagnostic kits. Given that horses are usually asymptomatic and serve as sentinel animals for AE, establishing a serological diagnostic method for equine AE will be useful for early diagnosis during life rather than for therapeutic purposes.

Conventional PCR testing

The standard molecular diagnosis for detecting E. multilocularis infection is based on conventional PCR testing [1, 5, 6, 12, 13, 16,17,18, 21, 27, 29, 30, 32, 36, 37, 39, 46, 53]. In canine AE, PCR testing can be performed on abdominal fluid or liver lesions [32]. In contrast, in horses and pigs as dead-end hosts, PCR tests are exclusively performed on DNA extracted from hepatic lesions [13, 16,17,18, 21, 27, 30, 53]. A major advantage of using PCR testing for the diagnosis of equine AE is that it enables detection of the parasite even in cases where histopathological diagnosis is not conclusive (e.g., when lesions lack the characteristic laminated layers) [13, 17, 18]. Target mitochondrial genes used for the detection of E. multilocularis in intermediate and definitive hosts include 12S rRNA, cox1, nad2, and cob mitochondrial genes [1, 12, 37, 57]. Apart from conventional PCR testing, a real-time PCR assay targeting the E. multilocularis cob gene has been reported for diagnosing human AE [31]. However, there have been no reports to date on the use of a real-time PCR assay for diagnosing equine AE.

Need for alternatives to conventional PCR testing for on-site diagnosis

Conventional PCR testing can be problematic, especially under field conditions, due to the requirement for complex experimental procedures and time-consuming gene amplification via three-step thermal cycling. Because horses can serve as sentinel animals for AE, the rapid and easy diagnosis of equine AE is important for veterinarians from a public health perspective. Establishing rapid diagnostic tests as an alternative to conventional PCR testing would be particularly useful for horse meat inspections in areas where AE is endemic. To address this need, we recently developed two novel, rapid molecular genetic diagnostic methods for equine AE: specifically, a loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) assay [19] and a recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) assay [20]. Currently, DNA extraction is a common prerequisite for both conventional PCR testing and these isothermal amplification methods. The fundamental advantages of isothermal amplification are significantly reduced amplification time and easier temperature control, making it suitable for rapid on-site diagnosis. However, there are a number of points that should be considered when comparing the relevant diagnostic methods. These include the complexity of the sample preparation process, the requirement for reagents and equipment (which encompasses the greater expense for LAMP- and RPA-assay reagents than those for conventional PCR testing, and the high cost of specialized isothermal amplification fluorometers when using fluorescence detection systems), and the skill level of the operator. LAMP assay

LAMP assays utilize a set of 4–6 primers and operate under isothermal conditions (60–65°C), enabling faster (in one hour or less) and simpler amplification than conventional PCR testing [44]. Consequently, LAMP assays have been widely employed for the rapid detection of various pathogens, including viruses [51], bacteria [58], protozoans [50], and parasites [56]. They have also been applied to the rapid detection of several equine infectious diseases [22, 40, 41]. LAMP assays targeting nad5 [42] and cox1 [11] genes have also been developed for the detection of E. multilocularis in samples from canine definitive hosts.

We recently developed a LAMP assay targeting the cob gene of E. multilocularis and evaluated it with liver nodule samples from 41 slaughtered horses diagnosed with AE based on histopathology and PCR testing [19]. Although the LAMP assay exhibited 10-fold lower sensitivity than conventional PCR testing, it only required 30 min for gene amplification, demonstrating its effectiveness for use with field samples.

RPA assay

The RPA assay exploits an isothermal amplification technique applied at 37–42°C to amplify as few as 10 DNA target copies within 20 to 40 min [34]. RPA assays resemble PCR testing in that they can be performed with two primers and are similar to LAMP assays in that they can be conducted under isothermal conditions [34]. Pathogens reported to be rapidly detectable by RPA assays include viruses [59], bacteria [9], protozoans [43], and helminths [52]. An RPA assay has also been applied to the rapid detection of some equine infectious diseases [33, 47].

We previously reported on the development of an RPA assay targeting the E. multilocularis nad5 gene as a diagnostic test for equine AE [20]. This assay involves amplification at 39°C, requires two primers and a fluorescence-labeled probe, and can yield results rapidly (in 30 min). Our previous report on this RPA assay was based on a comparison with histopathology and conventional PCR testing results for 36 solid liver nodules. The RPA assay we developed showed 94.4% concordance with the conventional PCR results, indicating high agreement statistically. However, in two cases, we found discrepancies between the RPA and conventional PCR results, and careful consideration may be needed to determine whether these cases were truly AE-positive or negative. Importantly, our established method showed no cross-reactivity with E. granulosus sensu stricto. Accordingly, we concluded that this RPA assay can specifically and rapidly detect equine AE within 30 min.

Discussion and Conclusion

The surveillance of equine AE plays an important role in monitoring E. multilocularis dynamics, even though the infection is currently regarded as clinically asymptomatic in horses. Due to their grazing habits, long lifespans, and broad geographic distribution, horses may serve as valuable epidemiological sentinel animals for AE. Investigating equine AE prevalence may provide an alternative means to monitor environmental contamination with E. multilocularis eggs, especially in areas where the direct surveillance of wildlife is challenging.

Definitive diagnoses of equine AE are routinely made via histopathological examination and conventional PCR testing using liver nodules collected from affected horses. However, novel molecular diagnostic tests, including the LAMP and RPA assays described herein, will enable more rapid on-site detection. Future research is required to develop non-invasive diagnostic tools for equine AE in live horses, such as ELISAs or Western blot assays using horse serum. The establishment of such serological diagnostic methods will enable longitudinal monitoring of E. multilocularis egg contamination in environments where horses graze.

In conclusion, continued research on equine AE is of paramount public health importance. Embracing a One Health approach to understanding and preventing this zoonotic disease will not only mitigate human risk but also profoundly enhance our overall comprehension of the eco-epidemiology of E. multilocularis and its implications for equine health.
