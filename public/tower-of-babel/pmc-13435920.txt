Role of repeat kidney biopsy after 6 months of treatment in proliferative lupus nephritis
Authors: Alexandra Vrabie, Bogdan Obrisca, Bogdan Sorohan, Roxana Jurubita, Camelia Achim, Diana S Zilișteanu, Andreea Andronesi, Gabriela Lupusoru, Elena E Rusu, Raluca Bobeică, Georgia Micu, Sonia Bălănică, Andreea Berechet, Valentin Mocanu, Stefan Lujinschi, Nicu Caceaune, Gener Ismail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterise histological response following induction therapy in a real-world cohort of patients with proliferative lupus nephritis (LN) undergoing per-protocol repeat kidney biopsy; to assess the concordance between clinical and histological response and to identify baseline factors associated with changes in activity and chronicity indices.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study including 20 adult patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and biopsy-proven proliferative LN who underwent a protocolised repeat kidney biopsy at 6 months (±3 months) after induction therapy. Clinical, laboratory and histopathological data were collected at both biopsy time points. Associations between baseline parameters and histological outcomes were explored.ResultsAfter a median of 7.5 months, proteinuria significantly decreased while estimated glomerular filtration rate and complement levels significantly improved. The histological activity index (AI) decreased significantly, with improvement observed in 90% of patients. Chronicity index (CI) increased in 50% of patients, without reaching statistical significance. At 6 months, 55% of patients achieved primary efficacy renal response, whereas 45% achieved histological response. Notably, discordance between clinical and histological responses was frequent: 45% of clinical responders exhibited persistent histological activity and 33% of histological responders failed to meet clinical response criteria. Lower baseline proteinuria and better baseline renal function were observed among patients with AI improvement. Likewise, patients with an increase in CI had a lower baseline CI and tended to have a better baseline renal function. Histological outcomes did not differ according to the type of immunosuppressive therapy used during induction.ConclusionsIn this real-world cohort of proliferative LN, substantial discordance between clinical and histological response was observed following induction therapy. While histological activity improved in most patients, chronic damage progressed in half of the cohort despite favourable clinical evolution. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive associations and highlight the need for prospective studies to define its role in personalised LN management.Keywords: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus; Lupus Nephritis; Outcome Assessment, Health Care

Introduction

Despite the important progress seen in the last decades in the field of proliferative lupus nephritis (LN) treatment, more than half of the patients did not achieve complete renal response (CRR) after 52–76 weeks of immunosuppressive treatment in the latest positive trials,1–4 leaving us with a high proportion of patients for whom we should refine our management strategies.

The concept of re-biopsy in LN has started to be recognised in the last years as an invaluable tool for a better characterisation of the patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with renal involvement and for a better understanding of the need for adjustments of the immunosuppressive and conservative therapy, intending to raise the probability of achieving a renal response on short-term and a favourable renal prognosis on long-term.5 6

Even if there is no consensus on whether a repeat biopsy should be performed routinely,7 8 KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of LN suggests that after the baseline indispensable kidney biopsy in patients with SLE with renal involvement, to consider a repeat biopsy in case of concern of chronicity or alternative diagnosis, if the diagnosis of flare is uncertain, and for confirming renal response in order to withdraw immunosuppression.9 The scientific community identified five clinical scenarios for repeat renal biopsies, included in two main categories: for-cause and per-protocol repeat renal biopsy.5 6 8 10 A for-cause repeat renal biopsy can bring important information in case of renal flare, persistent proteinuria or worsening of renal function, altering treatment decisions according to underlying histological findings.5 6 10 Per-protocol kidney biopsies are done at two timepoints: post-induction therapy between 6 and 12 months with the aim of evaluating the initial histological response, and during maintenance therapy after at least 36 months in order to identify residual intrarenal activity and guide the decision of stopping the immunosuppressive therapy.5 6 10

Post-induction per-protocol kidney biopsies revealed a striking discordance between clinical parameters and histopathological findings early during the treatment course, as around 30% of the patients that were considered complete clinical responders were still histologically active after 6 months of treatment.7 11 How to interpret the tissue-level activity by a certain time frame and how to adjust treatment accordingly are still open questions that will gain their answers as future repeat biopsy LN cohorts are being published.

The present study aimed to characterise a real-world cohort of patients with biopsy-proven proliferative LN who underwent a per-protocol repeat kidney biopsy at 6 months (±3 months) following induction therapy. The objectives were to identify baseline factors associated with activity index (AI) reduction and chronicity index (CI) progression, to determine factors associated with histological and clinical response and to assess the degree of concordance between histological response and established clinical response criteria after induction treatment.

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective, observational study on 20 consecutive patients with SLE and biopsy-proven proliferative LN who have undergone a per-protocol kidney biopsy at 6 months (±3 months) from baseline, followed in the Nephrology Department of Fundeni Clinical Institute from Bucharest, Romania, between 2020 and 2025. The diagnosis of SLE was made according to 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics or 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology criteria. The inclusion criteria were age over 18 years, SLE diagnosis, patients with proliferative LN (confirmed by a kidney biopsy) who have undergone a per-protocol kidney biopsy at month 6 (±3 months) from baseline as part of our clinical routine in our unit. We excluded patients under 18 years of age, non-adherence to treatment, medical conditions interfering with outcome evaluations, a short follow-up period and insufficient clinical data.

The clinical and laboratory parameters were obtained by reviewing the patient’s electronic medical records at the time of the kidney biopsies and at the follow-up and included: age, gender, renal function (serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) according to the 2009 CKD-EPI equation), proteinuria/day (g/day), haematuria (cells/µL), C3 and C4 levels (mg/dL). Kidney biopsies were evaluated by light microscopy, immunofluorescence and electron microscopy and were classified according to the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society LN criteria and scored according to the modified National Institutes of Health activity and chronicity indices. Patients with LN received immunosuppressive therapy in accordance with the EULAR or KDIGO treatment guidelines.

Biopsy consent was obtained from all subjects at both biopsy occasions. The need for informed consent for the study analysis was waived due to the exclusive use of deidentified information and the retrospective nature of the study.

Definitions of response

Primary efficacy renal response (PERR) was defined as a urinary protein-creatinine ratio of ≤0.7 g/g and an eGFR no worse than 20% below the pre-flare value or ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2.9 CRR was defined as a urinary protein-creatinine ratio <0.5 g/g and a stabilisation or improvement in kidney function (±10%–15% of baseline).9 Histological response was defined as an AI that has decreased by ≥50%, resulting in an AI ≤3 in the repeat kidney biopsy, based on the Lupus Nephritis Trial Network (LNTN) criteria.12

In our clinical practice and retrospective database, proteinuria values were primarily recorded as 24-hour proteinuria (g/24 hours). To harmonise the data, 24-hour proteinuria values were considered equivalent to urinary protein-creatinine ratio, based on the assumption that the average creatinine excretion rate is approximately 1.0 g/24 hours.13 No formal mathematical conversion formula was applied between the two measures.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as either mean (±SD) or median (IQR: 25th–75th percentiles), according to the distribution of the variables, and categorical variables as percentages. Groups were compared by the paired or independent sample Student’s t-test as appropriate. The non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test or the Mann-Whitney test was used for not normally distributed data as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation tests were used to assess the relationship between clinical and pathological variables. In all analyses, p values are two-tailed, and all p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS programme (SPSS V.26). Treatment comparisons and predictor analyses of this study are exploratory only. Data were visualised using paired dot plots (connected scatter plots) and colour-coded scatter plots generated in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software V.10, San Diego, California, USA).

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

Our cohort included 20 biopsy-proven patients with proliferative LN who had undergone repeat kidney biopsy after a median of 7.5 months (IQR: 6–9.75). The mean age of the patients was 34±10 years, and 85% of the patients were women. Cyclophosphamide was used for induction therapy in 75% of the patients, and mycophenolate mofetil was used in 25%; furthermore, 55% of the patients also received rituximab during the induction period (8/15 patients—53.3% of patients treated with cyclophosphamide and 3/5 patients—60% of patients treated with mycophenolate mofetil received also rituximab during the induction period). At the first biopsy, the distribution of LN classes was: 60% with class IV, 25% with class III and 15% with class III/IV+V. The majority of the patients (95%) received mycophenolate mofetil during maintenance treatment. No patient presented doubling of serum creatinine, end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) or death during the mean follow-up period of 48 months.

Clinical and histopathological response at 6 months

Nine patients (45%) met the criteria for histological response at repeat kidney biopsy. At 6 months’ evaluation, 11 patients (55%) achieved PERR and 10 patients (50%) achieved CRR.

Of the 11 patients who have achieved PERR at 6 months, 5 patients (45%) have not responded based on the histological response criteria. Furthermore, of the 9 patients who had met the criteria for histological response at the second biopsy, 3 patients (33%) have not achieved PERR at 6 months.

Of the 10 patients who have achieved CRR at 6 months, 5 patients (50%) have not achieved histological response criteria. Moreover, of the 9 patients who had met the criteria for histological response at the second biopsy, 4 patients (44%) have not achieved CRR at 6 months.

Exploratory comparison of patients with and without PERR at 6 months

Baseline proteinuria was lower among patients who achieved PERR at 6 months than among those who did not, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (1.7 g/24 hours vs 2.45 g/24 hours, p=0.09); also, PERR at 6 months was observed more frequently among patients treated with cyclophosphamide-based induction therapy (10/15 patients with cyclophosphamide vs 1/5 patients with mycophenolate mofetil, p=0.069), although interpretation is limited by treatment heterogeneity and concomitant rituximab use. No differences in baseline or at the second kidney biopsy clinical parameters were observed between patients with and without a clinical response at 6 months. Likewise, neither the baseline nor the second kidney biopsy AI or CI differed between patients with and without a clinical response at 6 months. Exploratory comparison of patients with and without histological response at repeat biopsy

Patients with histological response at repeat biopsy tended to have a lower baseline CI than those without histological response (2 vs 3, p=0.08). No other baseline or at second kidney biopsy clinical parameters differed according to histological response at repeat biopsy. Also, no differences were observed according to the type of immunosuppressive agent used in addition to corticosteroids during induction therapy, although interpretation is limited by treatment heterogeneity and concomitant rituximab use. Exploratory comparison of patients with and without a decrease in the AI at repeat biopsy

At the second kidney biopsy, AI decreased in 90% of the patients. Patients who showed a decrease in AI at the second biopsy had lower proteinuria (6.62 g/24 hours vs 1.75 g/24 hours, p=0.04), lower serum creatinine (2.2 mg/dL vs 0.87 mg/dL, p=0.04) and higher eGFR (32.5±12.02 mL/min/1.73 m2 vs 87.28±28.13 mL/min/1.73 m2, p=0.01) compared with patients who did not demonstrate a decrease in AI. No differences were observed according to the type of immunosuppressive agent used in addition to corticosteroids during induction therapy, although interpretation is limited by treatment heterogeneity and concomitant rituximab use.

Discussion

This study evaluated early clinical and histological responses following induction therapy in a real-world cohort of patients with proliferative LN who underwent a per-protocol repeat kidney biopsy after a median of 7.5 months. While clinical parameters improved significantly and more than half of the patients achieved PERR at 6 months, histological response was observed in fewer than half of the cohort, highlighting a substantial discordance between clinical and intrarenal disease activity.

We have observed a discrepancy between histological response and clinical response in our cohort, with a persistent histological activity in 45% of the patients who have responded clinically, and persistent clinical activity in 33% of the patients who have responded histologically after induction treatment. The poor correlation between clinical and histological features in LN has been demonstrated in recent years in studies that have evaluated the role of kidney biopsies in patients with low-level proteinuria below the threshold of 500 mg/day,14–17 and in studies with per-protocol kidney biopsies after induction therapy or during maintenance therapy.1 5 7 11 18–21 Zickert et al found in their cohort of 67 patients with LN with per-protocol kidney biopsy after a median of 7 months that 29% of patients with complete clinical response still had active lesions at the end of induction therapy, and 28% of patients who presented histopathological response were considered non-responders based on clinical criteria.7 Malvar et al evaluated 69 patients with proliferative LN with per-protocol post-induction kidney biopsy after a mean period of 6.6±0.7 months and found that 29% of the patients with complete clinical response were still having active lesions with an AI ≥5, and 62% of the patients that were having an AI of 0 (histological remission) at the second biopsy were interpreted clinically as still active.11 It is important to emphasise the different definitions of renal response used in our study (PERR and CRR criteria) and in the previously published studies (Malvar et al defined the CRR as a 24-hour proteinuria <500 mg/day and an improvement or maintenance of serum creatinine, whereas Zickert et al defined CRR as an inactive urine sediment, a 24-hour proteinuria ≤200 mg and a normal or stable renal function).7 11

55% of the patients from our cohort achieved PERR at 24 weeks. Baseline proteinuria was lower among patients who achieved PERR at 6 months than among those who did not. PERR at 6 months was observed more frequently among patients treated with cyclophosphamide-based induction therapy; no apparent differences were observed in other baseline or at second biopsy clinical characteristics or activity and chronicity histological parameters. In the study conducted by Malvar, the patients who had achieved a CRR at the second biopsy after induction therapy had significantly lower baseline proteinuria than those patients who were considered partial responders or non-responders, but the type of immunosuppressant used did not influence the clinical response.11 In Zickert’s cohort, lower AI on second biopsies predicted complete clinical response after induction therapy.7

Nine patients (45%) from our cohort achieved histological response after induction therapy, defined as in the LNTN repeat kidney biopsy-based definition of treatment response.12 In the study conducted by Zickert, the criteria used for histological response were the presence of Class I, II or III/IV-C LN on repeat biopsies, and they found it in a percentage of 42% in repeat biopsies after induction therapy.7 Other cohorts used as an endpoint the histopathological remission that was defined as the presence of class I or II LN,20 whereas in a more recent cohort, the definition of histopathological remission included an AI of 0 at repeat biopsy, which is in line with LNTN definitions,12 and was achieved after induction in 18.8% of the patients in the study conducted by Malvar.11 Patients with histological response at repeat biopsy tended to have a lower baseline CI than those without histological response (2 vs 3, p=0.08); no other baseline or at second kidney biopsy clinical parameters differed according to histological response at repeat biopsy. In other cohorts, histological response was predicted by a lower albuminuria at second biopsy7 20 but not by other clinical parameters at second biopsy7 20 or the type of immunosuppressant used during induction.20

At the second kidney biopsy, AI decreased in 90% of our patients. Patients with a decrease in AI at the second biopsy had lower baseline proteinuria and better baseline renal function than those without AI improvement. These findings probably suggest a less aggressive disease that responded faster to immunosuppression. No differences were observed according to the type of immunosuppressive agent used in addition to corticosteroids during induction therapy in our cohort. In the study conducted by Alvarado, the improvement of AI after a mean period of 45.2 months was significantly greater among the patients that have received cyclophosphamide during the induction period19; also, in their cohort, the improvement of serum creatinine and of C4 complement consumption over the first 6 months was significantly correlated with a lower AI at repeat biopsy.19

In our cohort, CI increased in 50% of the patients. Patients with an increase in CI had a lower baseline CI and tended to have a better baseline renal function. No differences were observed in the baseline or at the moment of repeat biopsy values of AI, haematuria, proteinuria or the values of complement fractions between patients with and without an increase in CI. Higher CI on repeat biopsies was associated with poor long-term renal outcome in other cohorts7 19 22; in our cohort, after a mean follow-up period of 48 months, no patient reached doubling of serum creatinine or ESKD. The increase of CI between the two biopsies in our patients did not reach statistical significance; other cohorts reported that CI increased significantly in the first 6 months of treatment,19 23–25 which suggests that we should refine our therapeutic strategy very early during the treatment course, to prevent the establishment of irreversible chronic lesions.19 The analysis conducted by Tannor of repeat biopsies in LN divided over three decades (1984–1994, 1995–2004, 2005–2015) showed that the CI and the development of ESKD did not vary significantly across decades, although the third decade was associated with the increased use of mycophenolate mofetil during induction.25 Another important observation is that in our cohort, in some of the patients, the CI decreased between biopsies. The only reasonable explanation is the sampling variability between the initial and repeat biopsies, an inherent limitation of renal biopsy assessment. These findings illustrate a limitation of the method itself, of using renal biopsy as a diagnostic and monitoring tool.

The practical, still open questions that remain are how to interpret the tissue-level activity after 6–12 months from initiation of immunosuppression therapy, how to adjust treatment accordingly and whether treatment decisions alter long-term outcomes in patients with LN. Residual activity in per-protocol kidney biopsies was associated in studies with an increased risk of renal flare occurrence during the follow-up.18 21 26 Repeated renal flares lead to additional irreversible nephron loss,27 which makes the rationale for early detection and intensifying immunosuppression therapy in order to correct this risk factor for chronic kidney disease progression, but this hypothesis has to be demonstrated in prospective clinical trials. ReBioLup study (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04449991) is an important ongoing multicentre, prospective, randomised trial that has the aim to form evidence for per-protocol kidney biopsies, by evaluating whether adjustments of immunosuppression therapy based on the tissue-level activity identified at the repeat biopsy lead to better renal outcomes compared with standard of care.5 6

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, small sample size and treatment heterogeneity, which limit statistical power and preclude multivariable analyses. Consequently, treatment comparisons and predictor analyses of this study are exploratory only. Moreover, the observed median time to repeat biopsy was 7.5 months, reflecting a distribution skewed toward later timepoints within the predefined window. This likely reflects real-world clinical practice, including scheduling constraints, clinical stabilisation delays, patient availability and COVID-19-era delays. Also, the relatively early timing of repeat biopsy in our study may have contributed, at least in part, to the observed discordance between clinical and histological outcomes, as residual inflammatory activity may persist despite early clinical improvement; our findings should be interpreted in the context of an early post-induction evaluation. Larger prospective and randomised studies are needed to determine whether incorporating post-induction repeat kidney biopsy into treatment algorithms can guide therapeutic decision-making and ultimately improve long-term renal outcomes in LN.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate substantial discordance between clinical and histological response following induction therapy in proliferative LN. Although histological activity improved in most patients, chronic damage progressed in half of the cohort, even among those patients with favourable clinical evolution. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive associations and underscore the need for prospective studies to define the role of repeat kidney biopsy in personalised LN management.
