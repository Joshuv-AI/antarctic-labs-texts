Nationwide Evaluation of the Validity of the Trauma and Injury Severity Score Method in Korean Regional Trauma Centers Using Multi-Institutional Large-Scale Data
Authors: Mahnjeong Ha, Seunghan Yu, Byung Chul Kim, Hyuk Jin Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) method is a widely used tool for assessing patient severity and predicting survival probability in trauma care. However, its accuracy and applicability in the Korean context, particularly for neurotrauma patients, have not been thoroughly validated yet. Previous studies at a single institution have identified significant discrepancies between TRISS predictions and actual outcomes, particularly in severe neurotrauma cases. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of the TRISS method on a national scale using data from multiple regional trauma centers (RTCs) in Korea.MethodsWe utilized data from the Korea Trauma Data Bank collected from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2021. A total of 70,785 patients were selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. The probability of survival was calculated using the TRISS method. Patients were categorized into neurotrauma and non-neurotrauma groups. Misclassification rate (MR) was measured by comparing the predicted survival or death using the TRISS method with the actual outcomes to assess the predictive validity of the TRISS method.ResultsThis study included 28,285 neurotrauma patients and 42,503 non-neurotrauma patients. The neurotrauma group had higher actual deaths (2,401) than the non-neurotrauma group (809). The neurotrauma group also had a significantly higher mortality rate per 100,000 population (8,489.50 vs. 1,903.40). MR was significantly higher in neurotrauma patients (8.07%) than in non-neurotrauma patients (1.92%). Patients with severe head injuries (Glasgow Coma Scale ≤ 8) had the highest MR (32.27%).ConclusionOur study confirms that the TRISS method's misclassification issues observed at a single institution are prevalent across multiple RTCs in Korea. The accuracy of the TRISS method decreases with increasing injury severity, particularly in neurotrauma patients. These findings highlight the need to revise evaluation criteria and develop more accurate prediction models tailored to the Korean trauma care system. Implementing these changes will enhance the reliability of trauma care assessments and ensure more equitable support for RTCs, ultimately improving the quality and equity of trauma care in Korea.Keywords: Trauma Severity Indices, Brain Injuries, Traumatic, Trauma Centers, Mortality, Survivor, Korea

INTRODUCTION

In the landscape of trauma care, the quality of care directly influences patient outcomes, particularly for those suffering from severe injuries such as neurotrauma. In South Korea, the assessment of regional trauma centers (RTCs) includes annual evaluations that rely on methodologies such as the Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) to gauge the quality of care through metrics including the W-score and the Observed-to-Expected ratio (O:E ratio) of mortality. These evaluations are critical as they can help guide improvements in trauma management across the nation. Additionally, RTCs can be evaluated with a variety of other metrics including the time to initiate massive transfusion, the duration of stay in the emergency room, the time taken to start a surgery, and the proportion of severe cases handled. These comprehensive assessments ensure that various aspects of trauma care are systematically reviewed, providing a holistic view of each center's performance.

Historically, the TRISS method, which calculates the probability of survival (Ps) based on factors such as age, physiological parameters, and anatomical injury severity has been a cornerstone in trauma assessment worldwide. However, its applicability and accuracy in the Korean setting, particularly for patients with neurotrauma, have not been thoroughly validated yet. At our institution, we conduct monthly mortality case review meetings. We have frequently observed discrepancies between the Ps calculated by the TRISS method and opinions of experts, especially for patients with severe neurotrauma, where the agreement tends to be even lower. We published a paper in 2023 analyzing these mismatches using data from our single institution.1

This study aimed to extend the investigation to a national level, analyzing data from 2017 through 2021, to determine if the mismatch observed at a single-institution level might be indicative of a widespread national phenomenon. By enrolling a comprehensive dataset of approximately 70,000 patients who meet our inclusion criteria, this research seeks to rigorously evaluate the validity of the TRISS method across multiple RTCs in Korea. This nationwide validation is crucial for potentially recalibrating TRISS parameters or even reevaluating the reliance on this method in the Korean trauma system.

METHODS

The Korea Trauma Data Bank (KTDB) was established to collect information related to the treatment of trauma patients, laying the foundation for the trauma care system and providing essential data for trauma-related research and policy formulation. The initial registration of information is conducted by emergency medical institutions or RTCs that operate the trauma registration system. Patients included in the database are those diagnosed with injuries coded under the 'S' and 'T' categories of the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases who have visited medical facilities with one or more injuries. Data collected from each institution are compiled, stored, and managed by the National Emergency Medical Center (NEMC). Data from 2017 to 2021 were made publicly available earlier this year. To determine whether inaccuracies observed in the TRISS method at a single institution were isolated or prevalent across all institutions, a nationwide analysis was conducted using this dataset. The dataset comprised a vast array of individual data elements for each patient, including demographic and epidemiological characteristics, physiological status, anatomical diagnoses, complications, and survival outcomes. Patients who visited RTCs due to trauma and injuries between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2021 were selected for the study. The following cases were excluded based on the patient selection criteria outlined by the Ministry of Health and Welfare's RTC evaluation guidelines: 1) cases of drowning, biting, poisoning, penetrating injuries, burns, or unknown causes; 2) patients under 15 years of age; 3) patients referred from outpatient departments; 4) patients transferred more than 48 hours after injury occurrence; 5) patients who returned home after emergency room treatment, were transferred, or died in the emergency room; 6) cases where the final Injury Severity Score (ISS) was missing; 7) patients whose systolic blood pressure (SBP) or respiratory rate (RR) was coded as '999'; 8) patients whose SBP was coded as '−1' and whose response at the time of visit was coded as 'alert,' 'verbal response,' or 'painful response'; 9) Patients whose RR was coded as '−1' and whose response at the time of visit was coded as 'alert,' 'verbal response,' or 'painful response'; and 10) patients with missing required variables.

We initially received the KTDB containing entries for 191,071 individuals from the NEMC. Applying the aforementioned criteria, we selected a final cohort of 70,785 patients for analysis. The Ps was calculated using the TRISS method, incorporating the revised trauma score (RTS), ISS, and age.2 Patient’s SBP, RR, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) were coded into RTS values according to Table 1.3 The ISS was calculated based on the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score assigned at the time of discharge. AIS scores categorize the body into six regions, assigning the highest score for diagnoses in each region.4 The ISS was determined by summing squares of the three highest AIS scores from the most severely injured body regions.5 If a patient sustains an AIS 6 injury, an automatic ISS value of 75 is assigned. Thus, a higher W-score and a lower mortality O:E ratio suggest a better quality of care. These metrics are included as evaluation criteria in the actual assessment of RTCs. Scores from these metrics influence administrative and financial support allocated to RTCs. Therefore, accurate assessments are crucial. However, if the reliability of Ps is low, these evaluation criteria may need to be improved.

Validity analysis of the TRISS method

Patients were categorized into neurotrauma patients and non-neurotrauma patients based on the presence or absence of head injury coding in the AIS. We compared SBP, RR, GCS, ISS, the number of patients aged 55 and over, Ps, W-score, and the mortality O:E ratio between these groups. In the TRISS method, a calculated Ps above 25% classifies a patient as likely to survive, while those with Ps of 25% or lower are predicted to die. A 2 × 2 table including actual survivors and deceased patients was constructed. To assess the predictive validity of survival or death by the TRISS method, the misclassification rate (MR) was calculated as described previously.1 MR was determined by dividing the sum of patients who actually died but were predicted to survive and those who actually survived but were predicted to die by the total number of patients.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by Pusan National University Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2401-011-135). The requirement of informed consent was exempted by the IRB due to its retrospective nature. This research involved analyzing data that did not include personal identifying information. Since the data analyzed pertained to patients who had already completed treatment, there was no direct risk or benefit to individuals. Consequently, a waiver of consent was submitted and approval for this study was obtained. All statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2016 for Windows version 2301 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Patient demographics and outcomes

The neurotrauma group experienced 2,401 actual deaths, whereas the non-neurotrauma group had 809 actual deaths. This, the total number of deaths was 3,210 for all patients. The number of survivors included 25,881 neurotrauma patients and 41,694 non-neurotrauma patients. Over the five-year period, the mortality rate per 100,000 population, defined as the ratio of the number of deaths to the total number of patients in each group, was significantly higher in the neurotrauma group at 8,489.50 than that (1,903.40) in the non-neurotrauma group. A χ2 test was performed to compare mortality rates between neurotrauma and non-neurotrauma patients. The P value obtained from the χ2 test was 0.000, indicating a statistically significant difference in mortality rate between the two groups. This result suggests that the TRISS method's ability to predict mortality may differ significantly between neurotrauma and non-neurotrauma patients. The P value of 0.000 implies that the observed difference in mortality rates between neurotrauma and non-neurotrauma patients is not due to random chance. Thus, the TRISS method's predictive accuracy for mortality of neurotrauma patients is significantly lower than that for mortality of non-neurotrauma patients, highlighting the method's limitations when it is applied to patients with severe neurotrauma.

Physiological and injury metrics

The average SBP was significantly (P < 0.001) higher in the neurotrauma group at 137.91 mmHg than that (133.59 mmHg) in the non-neurotrauma group. This is likely due to a reflexive increase in SBP caused by increased intracranial pressure in neurotrauma patients. Both groups had an identical average RR of 19.75 breaths per minute. The GCS average was lower in the neurotrauma group (12.87) than in the non-neurotrauma group (14.70), indicating more severe head injuries in the former group. The average ISS was 17.39 for neurotrauma and 11.01 for non-neurotrauma patients, further highlighting the greater severity of injuries in the neurotrauma group. These indicators suggest that the severity of injuries in neurotrauma patients is likely to be higher.

Ps and quality metrics

The calculated Ps was lower for neurotrauma patients (90.88%) than that for non-neurotrauma patients (96.84%). Although the number of patients in the neurotrauma group was smaller, the predicted number of deaths was higher at 2,578.41 for patients in the neurotrauma group compared to that (1,342.87) for patients in the non-neurotrauma group. The W-score indicating the quality of care was higher for non-neurotrauma patients than for neurotrauma patients (1.2561 vs. 0.6273). Similarly, the mortality O:E ratio was better for the non-neurotrauma group (0.6024) than for the neurotrauma group (0.9312). In other words, the actual survival rate in the neurotrauma group was lower than the expected survival rate. Furthermore, the predicted mortality rate was worse for the neurotrauma group than for the non-neurotrauma group.

DISCUSSION

This study extends our previous research1 by evaluating the accuracy of the TRISS method across multiple institutions using a comprehensive national dataset. Our earlier study identified discrepancies between predicted and actual survival outcomes using the TRISS method in mortality review meetings at our single institution. The TRISS method for assessing patient severity and survival probability has been repeatedly found to be problematic.7,8,9,10 However, due to the lack of suitable alternative evaluation tools, it has continued to be used. This is the first study in Korea that uses large-scale, multi-institutional data to demonstrate the inadequate accuracy of the TRISS method. These findings raise concerns about the validity of the TRISS method's predictions, particularly in patients with severe neurotrauma.

The sensitivity of the TRISS method was high for both neurotrauma patients (99.70%) and non-neurotrauma patients (99.91%), indicating that the method is effective in identifying patients who will survive. However, its specificity was low for both groups. This suggests that while the TRISS method is proficient in predicting survival, it struggles to accurately predict mortality. The low specificity indicates a higher rate of false positives, where the method incorrectly predicts survival for patients who ultimately die. These findings suggest that while the TRISS method may be suitable for general trauma populations, it is insufficient for accurately predicting mortality in trauma patients

The predictive validity of the TRISS method for survival has been reported in previous studies.11,12 These studies typically include a wide distribution of patient severities, ranging from mild to severe cases, without emphasizing the differentiation between varying degrees of severity. This comprehensive inclusion of patient severities may contribute to the seemingly appropriate predictive performance of the TRISS method in those studies. Similarly, in our study, when we assessed the overall patient population without distinguishing by severity, the MR was not excessively high. However, the primary finding of our research is that the inaccuracy of mortality predictions by the TRISS method is increased when the severity of trauma increases. Our findings indicate that while the TRISS method may perform adequately for a broad spectrum of trauma patients, it fails to provide accurate mortality predictions for patients with higher severity of injuries, particularly for neurotrauma patients. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of the TRISS method in contexts involving severe trauma and underscores the need for improved or alternative predictive models tailored to such high-risk patient groups. Furthermore, previous research by Moon et al.11 has applied the TRISS method to calculate preventable trauma death rates. It is important to note that the TRISS method is primarily designed to assess the Ps rather than directly measuring preventable deaths. Therefore, applying the TRISS method to determine preventable death rates represents a misapplication of its intended use. Although Moon et al.11 used TRISS in their study, they also acknowledged its limitations, particularly in predicting outcomes for severe cases. This aligned with our findings that the TRISS method's predictive accuracy decreased significantly as the severity of trauma increased. By including these points, we aim to clarify that while the TRISS method may show predictive validity in some contexts, it is inadequate for accurately predicting mortality in cases of severe trauma, particularly neurotrauma. The evidence from our study, combined with limitations acknowledged in the study of Moon et al.,11 underscores the necessity for developing improved or alternative predictive models that are better suited to the needs of high-risk trauma patients. This reaffirms our conclusion that the TRISS method is not sufficiently robust for use in severe trauma cases and that tailored predictive tools are essential for improving trauma care outcomes.

Our previous paper has demonstrated a statistically significant mismatch between the TRISS method's mortality predictions and expert opinions observed during single-institution mortality case reviews. To determine whether this issue was specific to our institution or a widespread problem, we initiated this study using a large-scale national dataset. The analysis of data from 70,785 patients across multiple RTCs corroborated our earlier findings, indicating that the issue is not confined to a single institution. Instead, this issue is inherent to the TRISS method itself. Consistent with our previous study, we observed higher MR in patients with severe neurotrauma, particularly those with low GCS. Additionally, we found even higher MR in patients meeting all three severe criteria: older age (55 years or more), GCS ≤ 8, and ISS ≥ 16. These findings reaffirm that the TRISS method inadequately accounts for the severity of head injuries. During mortality case panel discussions, patients deemed likely to die by expert opinions were not always predicted to die by the TRISS method. This discrepancy highlights the method's failure to accurately reflect the severity of neurotrauma, further undermining its reliability.

As detailed in our 2023 paper, primary reasons for the misclassification by the TRISS method include its reliance on outdated coefficients, its failure to adequately incorporate variables related to neurotrauma, and differences in the healthcare system between Korea and countries where the TRISS method was originally proposed. In brief, the TRISS method does not sufficiently account for complexities of severe head injuries, leading to significant predictive inaccuracies. Demetriades et al.10 have demonstrated that the TRISS methodology exhibits a high MR in severe trauma cases, particularly in patients with head injuries, which can exceed 30%.

Consequently, quality metrics such as the W-score and mortality O:E ratio derived from the TRISS method are inherently flawed. These inaccuracies can lead to misjudgments in assessing the quality of care provided by RTCs. Uniformly applying inaccurate W-scores and mortality O:E ratios to evaluate RTCs can result in unfair assessments. RTCs with a higher proportion of neurotrauma patients are disproportionately disadvantaged, while those with fewer neurotrauma cases possibly due to a lack of neurosurgical staffs might lead to overestimation. Other studies have also reported that using the TRISS method to compare trauma care facilities is inappropriate.10,13 This imbalance is particularly problematic in the current system where evaluation outcomes influence the allocation of support funds from the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Republic of Korea.

We propose several alternatives to address these issues. First, leveraging extensive data from the KTDB, we should consider developing a Korea-specific trauma survival or mortality prediction program. Although developing and validating a new model will require significant time and effort, it could include traditional statistical approaches and advanced methods such as deep learning. Meanwhile, adjusting and recalibrating TRISS coefficients to better reflect the Korean context is essential. Lane et al.14 have achieved better results by applying adjusted coefficients to data from 12 trauma centers in Ontario, Canada. Additionally, in the evaluation of RTCs, excluding neurotrauma patients from W-score and mortality O:E ratio calculations should be considered to ensure fairer assessments. Rather than uniformly applying the current year's W-score and O:E ratio for absolute evaluation in RTCs assessments, comparing a single institution's metrics from the previous year to the current year and performing evaluations based on the increase or decrease of these indicators might be an alternative approach.

Our study has several limitations. First, the exclusion of patients due to missing data and our strict exclusion criteria might have introduced bias, particularly as more severe patients are more likely to have incomplete data. This potential bias needs to be acknowledged as it might skew results towards less severe cases. Additionally, including patients with AIS 1 injuries such as scalp lacerations in the neurotrauma group might have diluted the severity of this group and potentially influenced overall results. This categorization could lead to an underestimation of the true impact and severity of neurotrauma, affecting the validity of our comparisons and conclusions. Furthermore, limitations of the provided data, including privacy concerns and the lack of identifiable codes for different RTCs, hindered our ability to perform comparative studies between individual institutions. This limitation prevented us from analyzing performance differences comprehensively across various centers to provide more nuanced insights into the TRISS method's applicability. Variations in the number of dedicated specialists and capabilities of different RTCs were not accounted for in this study. Centers with more specialists and resources might handle severe cases differently from those with fewer resources. This variability could influence patient outcomes and evaluation of the TRISS method's effectiveness, introducing another layer of complexity to our findings. Lastly, our study was based on data from Korea. Thus, our findings may not be directly applicable to other countries with different healthcare systems, trauma care protocols, or patient demographics. Further research is needed to validate our findings in other settings to ensure the generalizability of conclusions drawn from this study. By acknowledging these limitations, we aim to provide a balanced view of our study's findings and highlight areas for future research that could address these issues. Further studies, including multi-center and international comparisons, are needed to enhance the robustness and applicability of our conclusions.

In conclusion, our study confirms that the TRISS method's misclassification issues observed at a single institution are prevalent across multiple RTCs in Korea. Consistent with our previous study,1 the TRISS method demonstrated decreased accuracy with increasing injury severity. These findings underscore the need to revise the evaluation criteria and develop more accurate prediction models tailored to the Korean trauma care system. Our study highlights the importance of ongoing evaluation and optimization of trauma assessment tools to ensure they can accurately reflect patient outcomes and guide improvements in trauma care. Implementing these changes will enhance the reliability of trauma care assessments and ensure more equitable support for RTCs. By addressing these limitations of the TRISS method and developing more tailored prediction models, we can improve the quality and equity of trauma care in Korea.
