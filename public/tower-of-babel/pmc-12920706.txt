A comprehensive review on seroepidemiology and molecular epidemiology of hepatitis E Virus in Iran
Authors: Amir Hossein Aghapour, Seyed Masoud Hosseini, Shahrzad Shoraka, Amir Ghaemi, Afsaneh Sharifian, Seyed Reza Mohebbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis E virus (HEV) is a global threat to human health. The virus can cause acute liver disease in infected people, and although it is self-limiting in most people infected, it can cause serious liver problems and even death in some individuals, including pregnant women. HEV has eight genotypes, but usually four of its genotypes have been reported to cause infection in humans. Hepatitis E is expected to cause 20 million infections worldwide in a year, with 3.3 million symptomatic cases and about 44000 deaths. It's considered endemic in many developing countries, and poses a threat to global health. Mortality among pregnant women and newborns also highlights the importance of the control of the virus and the need for comprehensive techniques to monitor and reduce its spread. One way of transmitting HEV is through contaminated water and food, especially in the event of flooding. In addition, products of raw or undercooked meat from animals such as pigs and camels are likely to be a problem in developed countries. With close monitoring, the global prevalence of HEV can be reduced. It can also be significantly controlled with the availability of effective vaccines and improved hygiene standards. This review focuses on the molecular virology of HEV, pathogenesis, epidemiology, diagnostic methods, transmission routes and treatment options. Key Words: Hepatitis E virus, Chronic hepatitis, Water pollution, Food contamination, Outbreak, Infectious disease, Genotype

Introduction

Hepatitis E virus (HEV) is a global health risk worldwide. It is a serious worldwide health issue because it can cause acute liver infections in certain individuals and be self-limiting in others. HEV is a non-enveloped virus with single-stranded RNA genome that infects humans and several animals. This virus has been divided into eight genotypes, which affect specific geographic regions (1, 2). HEV belongs to the Hepeviridae family. The most recent classification places the virus in humans in the Orthohepevirus genus and in Orthohepevirus A species. Interestingly, this species infects not only humans but also some animals (3). Genotypes 1 and 2 are mainly responsible for hepatitis in developing countries, and for transmission via the passage of infection to other contaminated water, such as drinking water after heavy rainfall or flooding due to poor hygiene. Developed countries have a higher prevalence of genotypes 3 and 4, which are transmitted via food, particularly raw or undercooked products of animal origin, such as pork, camels and rarely goats and sheep (4-6).

The global HEV outbreaks cause 20 million infection cases globally within the year, of which 3.3 million are symptomatic, and approximately 44,000 die from the disease. While most people infected with HEV have mild symptoms and recover from the disease in a self-limited manner, in some cases the disease can lead to acute hepatitis and liver failure (6-9). The risks of this virus for pregnant women are particularly serious, as HEV can lead to increased maternal and neonatal mortality. Studies have documented that mortality from HEV infection can rise by 25–30% in pregnant women, especially in the third trimester (9).

Areas with poor sanitation, particularly in developing countries in Asia, Africa, and Central America, are known to be at high risk for the spread of this virus. In these areas, large epidemics occur following heavy rainfall or flooding, and the prevalence of the disease is high among young people and people with weakened immune systems, including pregnant women (1, 5). In contrast, in developed countries, the disease is mainly spread through the consumption of contaminated animal products, especially pork and camel meat (6, 9).

Although this disease was highly prevalent in various regions, most HEV cases in children typically showed asymptomatic or mild symptoms in endemic and developing nations; recent studies have also suggested that human-to-human and blood-borne transmission are possible. Therefore, preventing HEV by improving hygiene conditions, raising public awareness, and using available vaccines is one of the most effective ways to reduce the prevalence and mortality of this disease globally.

Virology

The single-stranded genome of HEV, a non-enveloped RNA virus, is roughly 7.2 kilobases long. This virus has an icosahedral structure and is typically 32–34 nm in diameter (10, 11). HEV is a member of the Hepeviridae family and the Orthohepevirus genus, four species are organized in this genus: Orthohepesvirus A, B, C, and D (12). HEV is stable under environmental conditions and can survive for a long time in polluted water, particularly in areas with poor hygiene, which is the primary route of transmission (13).

Genomic organization

The genome is divided into three open reading frames (ORFs), each of which plays a distinct role in the viral life cycle (Figure 1).

The genome structure of hepatitis E Virus: ORF1 encodes nonstructural polyproteins and includes approximately 5109 base pairs and has MET, methyltransferase; Y, Y domain; PCO, papain-like cysteine protease; HVR, hypervariable region; X, macro-domain; HEL, helicase; RdRp, RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. ORF2 encodes a capsid protein necessary for the entrance, assembly, and immunogenicity of viruses, overlapping with ORF3 partially and being produced by translation from a single sub-genomic RNA of approximately 2.2 kb. ORF3 interaction with endosomal sorting complexes for budding through microtubule attachment can also reduce immune responses and protect the cell.

ORF 1: this part is involved in coding a non-structural polyprotein of different lengths consisting of seven proteins, several of which perform essential functions for viral multiplication this includes an RNA helicase (Hel, it has 5'-3' helicase activity, which is required for unwinding viral RNA, and also has 5'-triphosphatase activity, which can be effective in regulating genomic replication), a section of hypervariable region (HVR), an X and Y domain, along with a papain-like cysteine protease (PCP), a methyl transferase (Met, it is located at the 5' end of the genome and is likely involved in RNA capping, a process essential for the stability and translation of viral RNA), and a polymerase enzyme that is RNA-dependent and RNA polymerizing (RdRp), It is responsible for the replication of the viral genome and the transcription of sub-genomic RNA. This enzyme uses negative-strand RNA as a template to produce positive-strand RNA (12). However, some functions of ORF1 remain unclear. For example, there is still no conclusive evidence that PCP truly has protease activity, and it is unclear whether ORF1 functions as a multifunctional polyprotein (like a utility knife) or requires processing and cleavage to become active. Overall, ORF1 plays a crucial role in HEV replication, and the full function of each of its domains remains to be elucidated. Only three proteins within this group of seven, Met, Hel, and RdRp, have well-defined functions (14, 15).

ORF2: The next part of the virus genome encodes the capsid protein. This part is transported to the extracellular layer by the N-terminal signal peptide (16, 17). It can be said that proteins encoded by ORF2 are the principal immunogenic sites recognized by neutralizing antibodies (16, 18). The ORF2 protein forms essential components of the virus that enable it to enter liver cells. Because this ORF is effective in generating immune responses, it has made it a good model for developing a vaccine against this virus. A notable example of this is the Hecolin p 239 vaccine, which is currently authorized exclusively in China (19, 20).

ORF3: This reading frame is mainly involved in the process of viruses' exit from infected cells. Tumor sensitivity genes tsg 101, a vital protein implicated in endosomal sorting complexes required for transport, interact with the ORF3 protein to activate the budding pathways through binding to microtubules for particle accumulation and exit (21, 22). In addition, it may be involved in the secretion of infectious particles through its palmitoylation and membrane association. This protein can reduce immune responses and protect virus-infected cells (23, 24). It could be said that ORF3 protein is an ion channel that acts by releasing infectious particles into the body (25).

Mechanism of HEV transmission and replication

Infection with this Virus generally occurs through the ingestion of contaminated water or food, particularly undercooked meat from infected host animals. The virus enters the host through the gastrointestinal tract and initially infects intestinal cells, then travels through the bloodstream to the liver. Within the liver, the virus infects the liver cells and replicates. It has specific binding to the hepatocytes via its binding with some specific surface proteins such as heat shock cognate 70 (HSC70) and heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSGPs), gaining entrance into the host cells. Furthermore, Integrin α3 has been identified as a potential receptor aiding viral internalization through clathrin-mediated endocytosis (25-27). After the virus enters the cell, it loses its coat with the help of the lysosome, and then the capsid breaks down. Following that, the positive-sense genomic RNA fragment is released (28). First, the virus transcribes ORF1 with its transcription machine and translates RdRp. Then RdRp is a complete negative genome from the positive-sense gene. Hel and RdRp then use the complete negative-sense segment that was created at this point to transcribe two RNAs, one variant consists of a 2.2 kb bicistronic sub-genomic RNA, and the other a full-length genomic RNA. The production of nonstructural ORF1 polyproteins and capsid proteins encoded by ORF2, as well as multifunctional ORF3 proteins, is facilitated by these two capped and polyadenylated RNAs, which function as templates (29).

Viral assembly and release

To exit the cell, it is well known that in the intermediate compartment between the ER and Golgi apparatus, a complex consisting of ORF2, ORF3, and HEV genomic positive-sense RNA forms and generates viral progeny particles (30, 31). Viral particles attach to the TSG101 protein and are secreted as quasi-enveloped particles. When exiting hepatocytes through the bile, they lose their envelope, becoming naked virions, which are more infectious (21, 32).

Host immune response and viral evasion

Among the virus proteins, ORF2 and ORF3 are key in interacting with the host immune system. The protein encoded by the ORF2 gene is generally considered a target of the host immune response since it induces antibody generation. This protein has received considerable attention, particularly in the development of vaccines against HEV, due to its role in eliciting immune responses. The ORF3 protein also indirectly contributes to the maintenance of infection and reduces the ability of the immune system to recognize the virus by modulating the immune system and helping to spread the virus (27).

Infectious pathways in the body

HEV infection in people is divided into self-limiting acute and chronic types depending on the condition of the disease and the strength of each person's immune system. Research has shown that the symptoms of HEV are more due to the body's immune response than direct damage to liver cells by the virus. In immunocompromised people (AIDS patients, blood cancers, and solid organ transplant recipients), this disease can become chronic. Research has shown that CD2, CD3 and CD4 lymphocyte levels fall in these people, indicating a poor T-cell response in the immune system, causing the infection become chronic (33-35).

In cases where this infection causes such conditions as self-limiting acute viral hepatitis (AVH) and acute liver failure (ALF), an increase in antibody levels and a decrease in viral load are observed simultaneously with the appearance of jaundice. In ALF patients, increased levels of IL-2, TNF-α, INF-ɣ, and IL-10 are seen. Interestingly, HEV RNA is still present in AVH patients, as opposed to ALF patients, suggesting that the virus has been cleared from the AVH patients, but this has been associated with severe liver damage. On the other hand, NK and T cells play a vital role in the response to HEV. In this disease, the decrease in NK and NKT in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) can be due to the accumulation of these cells in the liver or their death. Importantly, in pregnant women with this infection, the decrease in the number of NK cells may be the reason for their increased susceptibility to severe infection, as their ability to eliminate the virus from the liver is reduced (36, 37).

CD4+ T-cells that produce TNF-α, INF-ɣ play another important role in controlling this infection and regeneration of the liver. The decrease in these cells in acute infections can cause severe liver damage (38).

In most patients, over time, anti-HEV IgG antibodies become positive. In patients with liver failure, the number of these antibody-producing cells is higher, which in turn contributes to further damage to the liver by intensifying the activity of the immune system (39).

Diagnosis & detection of HEV

Although HEV disease in many people shows a self-limiting picture, people who indicate infection at first, have visible symptoms such as fever, malaise, fatigue, nausea, and vomiting. Then after a few days, the color of the urine becomes dark, and jaundice begins. In severe cases, this process leads to acute liver failure. With acute liver failure beginning, liver enzyme levels increase, and protein production decreases. However, the mentioned symptoms are not specific to this virus and precise detection techniques should be used.

There is various laboratory based techniques to identify HEV. Imaging techniques such as ultrasound, CT, and MRI are not specific and cannot rule out cases that have similar hepatitis symptoms with different etiology. To identify HEV, methods such as direct detection with electron microscopes, virus cultivation methods, and various molecular techniques (such as PCR), as well as indirect methods such as detection of specific anti-HEV antibodies and detection of specific cellular immune responses, can all help to accurately diagnose the virus (40).

The direct detection method for the hepatitis E virus, as mentioned earlier, is immune electron microscopy (IEM), which confirms the initial evidence of HEV in stool samples. However, this method requires relatively high viral load, sophisticated equipment and is also impractical for clinical use (40). Another method, which is based on nucleic acid (PCR, LAMP), is very sensitive and specialized. A type of real-time RT-PCR method, specific fluorescent probes are utilized and it reduces possible cross-contamination and also improves specificity. The development of multiplex PCR to detect HEV genome, along with other viruses (such as hepatitis A), can improve diagnostic efficiency. However, this method also requires expensive instruments and does not allow for downstream tasks such as sequencing (40, 41). The loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) method, does not require the equipment used in PCR and is faster than PCR, and its sensitivity is higher than conventional PCR. However, limited studies are available for HEV detection by LAMP and it cannot also be supported by the sequencing (42). Detection of viral antigens in clinical samples is the third HEV direct diagnostic method. In this technique, enzyme immunoassay (EIA) is used to identify HEV antigens in serum samples. The use of recombinant antigens from several HEV genotypes makes the results of this method more reliable and accurate (43, 44).

In addition to direct virus detection methods, indirect diagnostic methods (serological tests) are also useful for identifying the virus. Detection of HEV antibodies in samples provides researchers and doctors with important information for the diagnosis. The presence of IgM Anti-HEV in the blood indicates a recent infection (within 6 months). The presence of IgG Anti-HEV indicates a past infection that has persisted for a long time. IgA Anti-HEV is also observed during acute infection and has been suggested, along with IgM Anti-HEV, as a more specific marker for recent infection with HEV than the use of anti-HEV IgM antibody alone (45-49).

History and epidemiology

The first recorded exposure to hepatitis E was found in 1955-1956 in India with 29,300 cases and 266 deaths, in which years a classic type of hepatitis A was mistakenly diagnosed. This was later tested in 1978-1979 on the stored samples, and it was determined that this agent is a hepatitis virus other than A and B (50). This waterborne infectious illness, which is brought on by HEV and is pervasive in developing nations but uncommon in developed ones, was subsequently identified using electron microscopy and immunostaining (50-52). At a Soviet military base in Afghanistan in 1981, a Russian doctor willingly consumed a filtered mixture of fecal samples from diseased soldiers; as a result, he became critically ill. Uncoated virus-like particles, measuring 20–30 nm, were found in feces using immunoelectron microscopy investigations, confirming the identity of the novel non-HBV and non-HAV virus (53). In 1990, the viral genome was discovered, and the United States agencies detected zoonotic HEV in domestic pigs during the late 1990s (2, 54). In 1998, the routes of transmission between pigs and humans were demonstrated by confirming that the genome sequence of human HEV was identical to that of pig HEV (54). HEV infection was believed to be an exclusively human disease from the beginning (55).

As a result, HEV infection is characterized by self-limitation; only a few instances of severe HEV infection result in acute liver failure (55, 56). In addition, other strains of HEV were identified in several animal species, such as mice, deer, rabbits and camels, and the pathogenicity and host range of these viruses are being investigated (57). HEV genotypes 1 and 2 infections have appeared in parts of Asia, Africa, Mexico, and the Middle East. In the early stages of emergence, there have been no reports of animal-to-human transmission of these genotypes.

After the discovery of HAV and HBV, in 1975, Elter et al. first described a post-transfusion hepatitis agent that was not related to any of other viruses including EBV, CMV, HBV and HAV, and called it non-HAV, non-HBV hepatitis (50). In 1978, two teams of researchers again suggested an agent after injection that was not similar to any previous hepatitis. Later this agent was called hepatitis C virus (HCV). Then, Rizzetto and colleagues found a novel human RNA virus and referred it as the hepatitis D virus (HDV). Several researchers were simultaneously trying to discover the new hepatitis agent, one of whom was Dr. M. Balayan, who was working on Russian soldiers in one of the Afghan bases who had non-A and non-B symptoms. Finally, this enterically transmitted agent, which was endemic in developing countries, was called hepatitis E virus (HEV) as it was known after the discoveries of HAV, HBV, HCV and HDV (50, 58).

Global prevalence of hepatitis E

Hepatitis E is a viral disease that has spread to many parts of the world, especially developing countries (12). According to global information, HEV is thought to be one of the leading causes of hepatitis in many regions of the world. Areas at high risk for the outbreaks of the virus include Southeast Asia, parts of Africa and a few countries in the Middle East. In recent years, HEVs cases have been observed in developed countries. This applies particularly to the consumption of under-cooked meat and close contact with animals with an infection (2, 5). According to global data, the prevalence of the virus may be as high as 30 percent in developing countries, especially in tropical climates and in countries with poor sanitation (6, 10).

Prevalence in different countries:

Asia and Africa

In developing nations, particularly in South Asia (India, Bangladesh) and African countries (Kenya, Sudan, and Liberia), hepatitis E is recognized as an important disease that spreads during the rainy seasons and following floods. In these regions, the highest number of cases of sickness is pronounced due to a lack of access to clean water and inadequate sanitation structures. HEV occurrence rates in several of those nations were pronounced, with some nations reported to be as high as 40% (12, 14).

Middle East

Hepatitis E is also an endemic disease in the Middle East, with various occurrences among different populations. According to numerous studies, the prevalence in countries in this area, which include Iran, Iraq, and Saudi Arabia, ranges from 2.3% to 46.1% (13, 14). For example, in Iran, the prevalence of HEV within the general population has been reported in northern areas and southwestern regions to be between 2.3% and 46.1% (13).

In developed countries, the prevalence of HEV is much lower and generally transmitted from animal to human via the consumption of uncooked or under-cooked animal products such as pork meat. In some nations, including Germany, France, and the United Kingdom, the rate of HEV serology inside the general population levels varies from 1.5% to 16.8% (15, 16). These statistics indicate that HEV may also be transmitted via foodstuffs coming into contact with infected animals in the industrialized countries. Prevalence of hepatitis E virus among different studies

In Iran, various research was proven that the prevalence of hepatitis E varies among different population groups. Some of the most vital studies in Iran based on different regions are as follows: In a survey in Sari city (2009), which included 1,080 people of different ages between 2 to 25 years, the prevalence of HEV antibodies was reported to be approximately 2.3% (59). In another study in Tehran and Golestan provinces (2006) which was conducted on 1,423 people of different ages between 18 to 65 years, the prevalence of HEV antibodies was reported approximately 7.4% (60). A survey on 399 blood donors in Tabriz, revealed a 7.8 percent anti-HEV IgG seropositivity (61). In a study in Khuzestan province (2005), involving 400 people aged 18 to 60 years, the prevalence of antibodies against HEV was approximately 11.5 percent (62). In a further survey (2012) in Tehran, the prevalence of hepatitis E infection in the general population was assessed. Overall, IgG seroprevalence against HEV was 9.3 percent in the 551 subjects studied (63). A recent study on solid organ transplant recipients in Fars province, Amiri and colleagues found Anti-HEV IgG antibodies positivity in 35.3 percent of studied patients (64).

In Turkey, a neighbor country of Iran, a recent survey on 160 hemodialysis patients from three private dialysis centers in Ankara, showed 26.25 percent anti-HAV IgG seropositivity (65). Another study, Yasar and colleagues evaluated seroprevalence of anti-HEV total antibodies in 2011 Turkish blood donors using Wantai (Beijing, China) and the Dia.Pro (Milan, Italy) ELISA kits (66). They found a total of 10.6 percent seropositivity (11.5% and 12.2% positivity by Dia.Pro and Wantai kits respectively).

In Iraq, another Iranian neighbour, two studies revealed 18 and 20.3 percent HEV seroprevalence (68, 69). In Syria, (Damascus city), a hospital-based seroepidemiological survey was carried out on 193 patients with acute hepatitis between 1995 and 1998 (70). Among patients without A to E hepatitis, anti-HEV IgG antibodies were found positive for 31.9% cases. In Saudi Arabia, (Makkha city), anti-HEV IgG antibodies were found 23.8 percent in a community-based cross-sectional study on 1329 individuals (8). Another study conducted in Jaddah City, Saudi Arabia, on 593 blood donors of various nationalities found that 16.9% of them had anti-HEV IgG. For Saudi donors, the seroprevalence was 14.8%, while for non-Saudis of Middle Eastern descent, it was 33.3% (71).

In Pakistan, a study in 233 adults with chronic liver disease showed 17.5 percent anti-HEV seropositivity (72). However, another survey of 185 primary school children showed a 12.4 percent seroprevalence (73).

As shown in table 1, an example of studies in European countries have shown anti-HEV IgG seroprevalence of 13, 2.7, 16.8, 1.5, 7.3 and 39.1 percent in England, Italy, Germany, San Marino, Spain and France, respectively (74-79).

The HEV seroprevalence among blood donors in Northern (Babol) and Southern (Bushehr) Iran was 1.6 and 16.7 percent, respectively (80, 81).

In the southwestern region of Iran (Ahvaz City), the prevalence of anti-HEV IgM and IgG was 1.4 and 46.1 percent, respectively, among 510 subjects in the general population (82). In the south of Iran (Cities of Bushehr, Borazjan and Genaveh), of 226 patients on haemodialysis, 68.6 percent were positive for anti-HEV IgG and 14.6 percent for anti-HEV IgM (83).

These studies display that the prevalence of HEV is high in a few areas of Iran, especially in high risk groups such as hemodialysis patients. This highlights the need for preventive measures and increased awareness amongst special social groups.

Using data from a large multicenter prospective study on acute viral hepatitis, HEV infection was found in 33 out of 123 children (26.8%) in a cohort of children in Upper Egypt (84).

According to a study conducted on Egyptian patients, HEV is highly prevalent in chronic liver disease. 96.1% of individuals with advanced liver disease (204 patients) and 71.4% of people with chronic HCV (301 patients) had positive HEV IgG antibodies. For Egyptians with chronic liver disease, HCV-HEV co-infection may result in a worse prognosis (85). Surveys of HEV seroprevalence in Qatar revealed a percentage of anti-HEV IgG positivity of 20.8 (in blood donors), 34.2 (in patients with hepatitis A or C) and 27.3 (in the general population) (86-88). Various studies on HEV sero-epidemiology in Yemen (in individuals receiving primary healthcare in Aden), Lebanon (in patients receiving regular haemodialysis in Tripoli) and Jordan showed a positivity of 10.7, 21.63 and 30.9 percent, respectively (89-91).

These figures suggest that the prevalence of HEV in the general population, as well as among blood donors, may be relatively high, which could be a reason for increased attention to the screening of HEV in donated blood. Transmission and dissemination routes

HEV is one of the most important viral infections of the hepatitis virus, which is transmitted by a variety of different routes. The different modes of virus transmission include fecal-oral, zoonotic, blood, mother-to-child, and foodborne transmission. Spread through the fecal-oral route is the primary mode of HEV transmission, which predominantly occurs in areas with poor sanitation and limited access to safe water. Since HEV is excreted in human feces, contaminated water eventually enters surface water courses and water supply systems. The consumption of untreated municipal water in such water bodies can cause widespread epidemics. Most HEV outbreaks in developing countries have occurred due to drinking water contaminated with infectious persons' feces or using this contaminated water to irrigate fields (92). Outbreaks in areas without access to safe drinking water are often caused by people using river water for drinking, washing or bathing, or by people dumping their wastewater in rivers. These outbreaks also occur during the winter season when water levels drop, leading to an increase in water contamination due to the reduced inflow of water into the river (Figure 2) (93-95). Person-to-person transmission is a route that researchers are still debating. Research suggests that HEV is not generally transmitted person-to-person and that probability of intrafamilial transmission is low (56). However, a major outbreak of HEV associated with person-to-person transmission has been observed in northern Uganda. Attending funerals, washing hands in a basin before eating, and close contact with sick people have all been implicated in the outbreak (96). Hospital transmission is another way that the virus can spread. However, this mode of transmission is uncommon for the disease; nevertheless, there have been several reports of the disease spreading among hemodialysis units and among some hospital staff and workers (97, 98). Regarding sexual transmission, there has been no evidence of HEV transmission, but homosexuals have been found to have higher levels of anti-HEV IgG than non-homosexuals (99).

Zoonotic infections can occur in three ways: first, Consumption of contaminated food, second, direct contact with animals, and third, Environmental contamination from animal manure runoff (Figure 2). HEV has been identified in a wide variety of animals including pigs, wild boars, deer, rabbits, and rodents. Some genotypes of HEV (in particular genotypes 3 and 4) may be passed on to humans through these animals. Transmission of the virus can occur through direct contact with contaminated animal products, inhaling virus-laden droplets. Zoo workers, livestock keepers, and slaughterhouse workers are therefore at a higher risk of infection due to their constant contact with animals (100). A more common way of spreading HEV is through the consumption of raw liver from supermarkets or eating Figatelli sausages in Europe, which sometimes contain HEV (101, 102). Additionally, using pig slurry may contaminate agricultural products such as raspberries, strawberries, and scallions, which are often used in salads. Effluent wastewater from pig farms can contaminate coastal waters and seafood, including shellfish and fish, posing a risk to human health (103). There’s another route of transmission, also through blood, that is through blood products. Blood donation by HEV carriers without proper screening increases the possible risk of disease transmission. This is critical, especially for immunocompromised persons, such as organ transplant recipients or HIV infected patients, as these individuals have a high chance of chronic infection upon infection by HEV (104). Another route is from mother to fetus, especially during the third trimester of pregnancy. HEV has also been identified in mother's milk, but transmission through breastfeeding has not yet been definitively proven (104). However, the main route of HEV transmission in developed countries, is consumption of contaminated food. The virus may be transmitted through the meat of infected animals, shellfish, and contaminated fruits and vegetables (92, 104).

Over the years of investigation, a body of evidence has accumulated linking pigs and other animals to the transmission of HEV in humans. Currently, HEV is only thought to be endemic in low-income countries, including areas in Asia, Africa, and Central America, and cases of infection are spread by travel to non-endemic regions. However, research has shown that cases of HEV can occur in economically developed countries like the UK and Switzerland (105, 106).

HEV infection in animals is of great concern in India. Arankalle, et al. evaluated 284 domestic pigs, 42% of which had anti-HEV IgG and 4.6% had HEV-RNA, all of which were identified as genotype 4 hepatitis E virus. Since the main cause of infection in India is HEV genotype 1, it can be concluded that human and pig HEV infection are not related, and that zoonotic transmission is less important in India for HEV infection (102). In China, HEV genotype 1 was once endemic, but now HEV genotype 4 is commonly observed, probably due to the introduction of the disease via animal reservoirs, particularly domestic pigs (107). There have been cases in Spain where eating wild boars leaded to HEV infection. Another outbreak happened in France where the consumption of pork products was directly connected to HEV (108, 109). Pork products consumption is currently considered a significant risk factor for hepatitis E infection (110). According to reports, approximately 21% of pigs in the UK contain HEV RNA, and molecular tests were positive at the time of slaughter (111). Furthermore, 6.3% of slaughterhouse-derived swine in the USA were positive for HEV RNA (112). Therefore, eating raw or undercooked pig products is most likely contributing to the spread of HEV among people (113). Pigs are the primary source of infection in industrialized countries due to high levels of hepatitis E infection, according to several research on pigs and pork products. In 2005, 63.6% of Brazilians had HEV antibodies based on serological tests (114), and 1.7% of livers from slaughterhouses in 2012 were found to be HEV RNA positive (115). The rise in France from 31% to 60% during 2011-2018 is concerning and required more rigorous monitoring and legislations. The corresponding percentages were 49.8%, 45%, 20.4%, 62.3%, and 92.8% in Germany, Italy, Spain, Switzerland, and England. In the Netherlands, HEV antibodies were present in 89% of pigs from organic-certified farms, 72% of domestically farmed pigs, and 76% of wild pigs (57). Deer is another species that can spread hepatitis E, and since eating raw deer meat is a known cause of HEV outbreaks, it's critical to assess the risk of deer-to-human transmission. For example, HEV infections occurred in several individuals in Japan who had eaten raw sika deer meat six to seven weeks later; the HEV sequences in the meat and in the patients were identical (57, 116). Chinese bovines (Bos taurus) with HEV infections; seroprevalence of antibodies and HEV G4 RNA have been found in several investigations; the Xinjiang Autonomous Region has 8.8% of its cattle with G4 HEV RNA (117). In Yunnan Province, 37.1% of dairy cows identified as positive for HEV RNA (118). Milk has always been a source of controversy regarding the transmission of HEV infection. In China, Huang et al. studied 140 cows. Active HEV infection was found in 52 cows and was confirmed by positive viral RNA in fecal samples. All 52 cows excreted HEV-RNA in milk samples at high titers. It was also found that these raw and pasteurized milk samples were able to infect rhesus monkeys (118). Woo et al. reported the identification of genotype 7 in a study of camels in Dubai, which was also infected in a liver transplant patient who apparently regularly consumed camel meat and milk. Following further investigation of camel samples from African and Middle Eastern countries (Somalia, Sudan, Kenya, Pakistan, Egypt, and the United Arab Emirates), 45% of 210 camel serum samples were positive for anti-HEV IgG, however, viral RNA was isolated in less than 1% of 2171 serum samples and 2% of 267 fecal samples. However, due to the lack of sufficient information, there is still doubt whether this virus can cause human infection (118, 119). Three percent of yellow cattle in Shandong province were identified as positive for HEV-G4 RNA, and 47% of them had anti-HEV antibodies. According to another study in Turkey, 20.3% of raw milk specimens obtained from various domesticated animals (cow, sheep, goat, and donkey) had HEV G1, G3, and G4 (120). In Beijing, Chinese researchers found that 29.4% of cattle tested positive for HEV antibodies, nonetheless, HEV RNA could not be found (121).

Humans consume meat and dairy products from cattle frequently, so these results should be considered seriously because they could greatly affect our understanding of possible HEV transmission routes and mechanisms. Consequently, further studies are required globally to determine HEV genotypes that can infect cattle, as well as the rates of HEV detection in dairy products and cattle (57). Furthermore, it has been proposed that goats may serve as a reservoir for HEV infection. This is significant since goats are used to produce cheese, milk, and meat. 9.2% of feces samples obtained from goats across six farms in Italy in 2016 were identified as positive for HEV RNA; these samples belonged to G3 genetic strains, which are identical to strains identified in people and pigs (122). Another study conducted in the Tai'an region, China, discovered that 4% of livers from goats tested positive for HEV (G4), with high nucleotide similarity to cow HEV isolates from the same province (123). Dromedary camels in Algeria are found positive for anti-HEV antibodies (35.1%) in another recent study. However, HEV RNA could not be detected in pools of HEV antibody positive sera (124). Consumption of camel milk and meat could lead to chronic HEV-G7 infection in a liver transplant recipient. This chronicity was presumably opportunistic and affected by immunosuppressive medicines that avoided organ rejection (125). In another study, it was discovered that 23.1% of dromedary camels from Saudi Arabia and Sudan were HEV seropositive (126). Due to the possibility of HEV transmission to humans through meat consumption, rabbits and kindred species are also garnering more and more attention (127). Among the 919 cases that were reported HEV-positive in France during 2015 and 2016, five cases of rabbit HEV were found (128). Seropositive and HEV RNA-positive rabbits have been found in several countries (129, 130). It was suggested that this occurred because of human-to-animal HEV transmission. Nonetheless, rabbits are probably the source of a small percentage of cases, since the majority of human cases globally are associated with different genotypes and sub-genotypes. A study conducted in the Netherlands revealed that meat consumers had a higher blood prevalence of HEV antibodies (22.8%), however, vegetarians also had a notable high prevalence (13.8%) (57). HEV inactivation was reported by cooking in a manner that ensures an internal temperature of at least 72 °C for at least 20 minutes (57, 131). Determining which samples to test and which assays work best may therefore be an important step in developing global standardized testing protocols for detecting infected animals to prevent underestimating the frequency of HEV. Models for studying HEV transmission and pathogenesis

Genomic analysis may identify specific HEV mutations that may increase pathogenicity in high-risk groups, especially pregnant women. It requires targeted genomic surveillance to track and predict outbreaks in this vulnerable group (10, 132).

Cell culture models have been investigated in a variety of cell lines. Human hepatoma cell lines HepG2, Huh-7, and PLC/PRF/5 have been used to study HEV replication, but the efficiency was low. Some strains, such as JE03-1760F (genotype 3) and HE-JF5/15F (genotype 4), have been well replicated in the human lung cell line (A549). African green monkey kidney cells (Vero), human colon cancer cells (Caco-2), and human fetal lung fibroblast cells (MRC-5) are other cell lines that have been studied. However, the choice of suitable cell culture models faced several problems, including low viral replication efficiency, failure to produce high titers of infectious virus in culture, the need for genetic mutations for better growth, the inability to replicate the virus in wide range of cell lines, and the lack of complete compatibility with animal and human models (133). Animal models play a crucial role in studying HEV genomic evolution, transmission, and pregnancy-related mortality (134). It seems that the suitable model for pregnancy studies is the rabbit model. Rabbits infected with HEV show similar pregnancy-related mortality to humans. The rabbit model shows a high rate of maternal mortality and abortion, and vertical transmission of the virus to offspring. In this model, persistent shedding of HEV in the feces and elevated liver enzyme levels reflect the liver damage seen in pregnant women (133). Nonhuman primate (NHP) models are more suitable for vaccine and transmission studies as well as immune responses and chronic infections. However, pregnant rhesus macaques do not show increased mortality, which limits their utility for pregnancy studies. Pigs naturally harbor HEV (genotypes 3 and 4) and can serve as reservoirs for human-animal-to-human transmission of the disease. They have been used in vaccine trials and transmission studies. Mice (humanized mice with human liver cells) are well-suited for chronic infections and antiviral agents’ efficacy testing. The poultry model is useful for understanding viral evolution and possible transmission between species. Finally, the fish model, Cutthroat trout, can be used in comparative genomic studies, but has less relevance to human transmission than other models. Finally, the rabbit model has proven to be the most effective model for studying pregnancy-related mortality (133, 134).

Hepatitis E in pregnant women

Clinical course of Hepatitis E in pregnancy: mechanisms of disease and preventive strategies

Hepatitis E virus can cause a wide range of clinical symptoms in pregnant women and immunocompromised people. These symptoms range from asymptomatic to severe infections, including acute liver failure (ALF). HEV infection during pregnancy may quickly cause acute liver failure, which can bring about complications including disseminated intravascular coagulation, encephalopathy, cerebral edema, and, in rare situations, hepatic coma. The death rate can reach 100%, mainly within the third trimester, when there may be a significant increase in maternal and fetal mortality, as well as a high frequency of reported problems consisting of low birth weight, early delivery, and stillbirth. Genotype and viral load may be considered as to influential factors during pregnancy (135).

Research indicates that during pregnancy, innate immune responses become more potent, while adaptive immune responses become weaker. A lower T and natural killer (NK) cell activity and increased responses in monocyte and dendritic cells are the hallmarks of this immunological condition. Pregnancy-associated Th2-biased immune responses suppress cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses, which can impede cellular-mediated immunity, especially towards infections such as the hepatitis E virus (HEV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), and influenza. Immune changes that occur throughout pregnancy, along with an increase in CD8+ T cells and a decrease in CD4+ T cells, exacerbate this condition. Severe liver injury is a result of malfunctioning of NF-κB signaling and increased levels of cytokines, which include TNF-α and IL-6, in pregnant women with HEV (136, 137).

According to studies, extended ranges of progesterone, estrogen, and human chorionic gonadotropin during pregnancy may also influence cell-mediated immunity, making someone extra vulnerable to a severe HEV infection. Pregnant women with HEV may additionally have fulminant liver damage because of decreased immunological responses, due to inhibition of the NF-κB pathway. Oxidative pressure has also been linked to immunological suppression within the pathophysiology of HEV infection. Increased oxidative stress is seen in pregnant HEV women, which exacerbates liver damage. It is proposed that pregnant patients can benefit from antioxidant supplementation as low ranges of antioxidants, are associated with unfavorable consequences. Moreover, coagulopathy is an extreme side effect of HEV infection in pregnant women, which could result in gastrointestinal and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). Research shows that HEV infection may modify the synthesis of coagulation components, especially fibrinogen, which is crucial for maintaining hemostasis at the late phases of liver infection. The condition may become more complicated if HEV proteins interact with fibrinogen, enhancing viral survival and replication. Furthermore, immune evasion and viral persistence in hepatocytes are facilitated by specific mutations in HEV proteins, particularly those in ORF1 and ORF2. These viral proteins alter cellular pathways involved in survival and death, inhibiting interferon responses, which may increase the virulence of HEV (136, 137). Treatment-wise, pregnant women should avoid ribavirin and interferon-α because of their teratogenic consequences. Nevertheless, further study is needed to determine whether sofosbuvir is a safer antiviral treatment for HEV during pregnancy. Vaccination with the HEV 239 vaccine is one of the preventive measures. This vaccine has proven effective in reducing HEV infection and problems in pregnant women, and it may also lower the morbidity and mortality associated with HEV infection (135, 136).

Hepatitis E virus infection in pregnancy woman: global disparities, epidemiology, maternal-fetal outcomes

Acute or subacute liver failure accounted for 0.2–4% of HEV-related deaths during an outbreak. There are reports linking genotype 1 and 2 strains with increased incidence and severity in pregnant women, including acute liver failure (138). If pregnant women are in the second or third trimester, they are more likely to show worse consequences (139). Twenty-two percent of pregnant women, affected by an epidemic in Kashmir, had fulminant hepatic failure (31). Pregnant women often have death rates between 15% and 25%, with the third trimester being particularly high (140). Pregnant women with an infection with HEV also had higher rates of stillbirths, abortions, and neonatal mortality (141). The common assumption that HEV infects pregnant women at higher rates, originating from earlier reports, has been disproved by later studies showing that no distinction actually exists between pregnant and non-pregnant women in terms of the prevalence of anti-HEV seropositivity (139). Studies show that lack of education is one of the factors impacting HEV seroprevalence in pregnant women. When the seropositivity was compared to educational levels, at a statistically significant level, it was observed that decreased IgG-positive rates corresponded with increased educational attainment (139) (142). Outbreaks of hepatitis marked by severe hyperbilirubinemia and elevated CFR in pregnant women, reported from Tunisia between 1950 and 1953, Algeria between 1952 and 1956, the Congo between 1958 and 1960, and Libya between 1968 and 1970 as well as in 1975 (143). The death rate for CFR, which is based on research on outbreaks or sporadic cases among pregnant women in African nations, is noticeably higher, ranging from 42.1% for dispersed cases collected in Ethiopia between 1988 and 1991 (143, 144) reaching 12.5% in 2012, following an outbreak in Kenya's Dadaab Refugee Camp (145).

The understanding that co-infection with HIV and HEV may elevate the risk of fulminant or chronic hepatitis, particularly in the context of the high prevalence of HIV among pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa, underscores the importance of addressing this dual health challenge (143).

There is insufficient research on the outcome of pregnancy in women with acute viral hepatitis. In an Indian study, out of 186 deliveries, 84% were preterm, and women with HEV infection had a noticeably greater percentage of premature deliveries (146). Preterm deliveries among pregnant women infected with HEV relatively high, according to only one study (67%). The umbilical cord can also serve as a conduit for vertical transmission, meaning that there is always a chance that the mother's blood may contaminate the blood from the umbilical cord (147).

As the fecal-oral route is the primary route of transmission of highly pathogenic viruses, existing prevention strategies should focus on improving water, sanitation and hygiene, particularly in endemic areas. In summary, the interaction of viral virulence, immunological suppression, and hormonal changes makes HEV infection a distinct clinical issue during pregnancy. The intricacy of treating HEV in expectant mothers is highlighted by the variations in disease severity, HEV genotype and viral load, as well as outside variables like nutrition (135-137).

Hepatitis E in pregnant women in the Middle East

Hepatitis is a major problem in pregnant women, especially in the Middle East, which is one of the endemic areas of this infection, and every year this infection causes the death of many babies and pregnant women (135, 148). The mother's risk of developing liver failure and the severity of the infection both increase with fetal HEV infection. As previously mentioned, pregnancy is a physiological element that raises the risk of liver injury and does not appear to be induced by HEV alone (149, 150). As a result, pregnant hepatitis E patients have a higher chance of liver failure (151). Thus, genotypes 1 and 2, which are referred to as more potent genotypes, along with a high viral load are linked to the development of severe liver failure (FLF) (147). Moreover, due to adverse effects, using herbal remedies to treat HEV infection symptoms and inadequate nutrition during pregnancy are linked to a higher severity of hepatitis E (149, 152). When examining the infectivity of HEV virus during pregnancy, different geographical areas should also be considered. Pregnant women in endemic areas have a significantly higher rate and severity of infection than women who are not pregnant, and several studies have reported maternal mortality rates ranging from 30% to 100% in these areas. The amount of vertical transmission from mother to fetus can be between 30 and 79% and sometimes even 100% (153). Furthermore, nursing can transfer the virus from mother to child and usually does so in the acute stage of the illness. Every year, over 3000 stillbirths in developing nations are caused by HEV infection (154, 155). There aren't many reports on Iran's seroprevalence of HEV infection in expectant mothers. However, according to research that has already been done, the serum prevalence of HEV infection ranges from 3.6 to 7.6%. Among these reported pregnancies, the stage of pregnancy and the number of family members were all associated with an increased likelihood of seropositivity to HEV (153). Pregnant women have a greater seroprevalence of HEV infection based on their frequency of pregnancy (9, 156). The HEV seroprevalence of Iran varies significantly from other Asian countries like India (33.67%) (157). To lessen the occurrence and negative effects of infection caused by HEV in the population, preventative measures, appropriate precautions, educational programs about potential exposure routes, and regular testing for HEV infection before and during pregnancy are required. These measures take into account the detrimental effects of HEV infection on pregnancy outcomes (9). Several studies performed on HEV IgG prevalence in different African and Asian countries such as Burkina Faso (18.6%) (158), Cameron (3.3%) (159), Ethiopia (28.6%) (160), Sudan (61.2%) (161) and India (33.6%) (156). In one study, pregnant women between the ages of 26 and 34 are more likely to be infected with HEV than those younger than 25 (156).

46.1% of Iranian adults have HEV IgG antibodies, this is a significant prevalence of the antibody (82). Iraq, as another country in the Middle East, is endemic for HEV infection. People usually acquired early in childhood and whose seroprevalence rises progressively with age to 26.6% in the age group higher than 40 years (69). Exposure to HEV occurs early in life in northern India, an area with high endemicity for HEV infection. The occurrence of anti-HEV IgG seropositivity develops gradually from 7.2% to 14.2% in infancy to 33.3% to 38.0% at age 10 (162). Other studies from Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Turkey and other developing countries showed a similar epidemiological picture (162-170). These findings reveal a high incidence of HEV infection among various Middle Eastern populations, implying the need for treatment and vaccination.

Treatment

Sometimes HEV infection may lead to chronic or severe disease or liver failure, therefore it requires treatment. In chronic infection cases of immunocompromised patients such as transplant recipients, the dose of immunosuppressive drugs should be reduced. Occasionally, treatment with antiviral drugs is needed (171).

Ribavirin is used as a first-line drug for the treatment of hepatitis E infection. In recent years, ribavirin, with a duration of 3 to 6 months, has been able to control viral infection. This drug is used for cases of acute hepatitis E, acute liver failure (ALF), and chronic HEV in immunocompromised patients. A three-month course of this drug results in viral clearance in 78 percent of transplant patients. However, this drug is contraindicated for pregnant women due to teratogenicity. reports of viral resistance to this drug are being presented and have been observed in "in vitro” systems. This resistance is due to the G1634R mutation in the HEV polymerase. Ribavirin exhibits a synergistic effect with mycophenolate, an inhibitor of inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, in reducing the replication machinery of this virus.

Sofosbuvir, initially a drug originally developed to treat hepatitis C, also inhibits HEV replication in vitro. Studies show that this drug has a modest antiviral effect in immunocompromised patients with hepatitis E, but it fails to induce a sustained virologic response (172).

Interferon α (IFNα) is another treatment option that has shown the ability to achieve a sustained viral response in liver transplant and hemodialysis patients. However, IFNα is contraindicated in people with transplanted organs due to transplant rejection. There is also limited evidence of treatment with both ribavirin and IFNα in people with blood diseases or HIV, which requires further research on this topic (172).

Conclusion

HEV is an important infectious agent globally, posing a serious risk to public health, specifically in developing countries. It is transmitted through contaminated water and intake of uncooked or undercooked food and is customary in regions with poor sanitation. In these regions, hepatitis E can cause severe and even fatal complications, specifically in pregnant women, who are more susceptible to acute liver failure and fetal complications.

Despite its excessive incidence in developed nations through the consumption of contaminated animal products, HEV remains a major health problem in many countries. Studies display that genotypes 1 and 2 of this virus lead to larger outbreaks in developing nations, while genotypes 3 and 4, which are more commonplace in industrialized countries, are transmitted through food and contact with infected animals. However, newly identified genotypes such as genotype 7 require further investigation to determine whether they have the potential to cause future epidemics.

In Iran, hepatitis E is also endemic in some regions, specifically in certain populations which include pregnant women and hemodialysis patients. The prevalence rate in some of those groups and areas is significant, especially in southwestern Iran. This requires effective health applications, elevating public attention, and enhancing hygiene situations.

During pregnancy, hepatitis E can cause more severe complications, including acute liver failure and fetal complications such as miscarriage, premature birth, and fetal death. However, the usage of the HEV vaccine and preventive measures consisting of improved hygiene and access to clean water can significantly reduce the prevalence and complications of this disease in pregnant women. The existence of models to study the infectivity and transmission characteristics of hepatitis E has greatly contributed to understanding this illness, especially in pregnancy, and the development of vaccines and antiviral drugs to treat this disease.

Ultimately, fighting hepatitis E requires a multifaceted approach that includes enhancing hygiene practices, public education, using powerful vaccines, and early identification of patients. Attention to high-risk regions and vulnerable groups, specifically pregnant women, will play an important role in reducing the burden of this disease globally.
