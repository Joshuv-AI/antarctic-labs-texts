Changes in microbiota associated with the use of intragastric balloons
Authors: Natalia Komorniak, Danuta Cembrowska-Lech, Anna Wiechowska-Kozłowska, Katarzyna Gaweł, Jan Pawlus, Karolina Skonieczna-Żydecka, Tadeusz Sulikowski, Alexandra Martynova van Kley, Ewa Stachowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a chronic disease characterized by excessive accumulation of adipose tissue. Intragastric balloon (IGB) implantation is one of the methods used to treat obesity. The aim of the study was to investigate whether the composition of the balloon-associated microbiota changes based on the duration of implantation and whether there is an association between changes in microbiota and weight loss results. In patients who lost weight, the microbial community was more diverse. The individuals who had an IGB for 12 months and lost weight exhibited a distinct microbiome shift, with increased levels of beneficial or metabolically flexible taxa. Bacteroides uniformis (11%) and Blautia massiliensis (9%) were abundant, while Anaerostipes hadrus was present at 6.2%. Ruminococcaceae species and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii were also detected at moderate levels. Over time, the intragastric balloon might influence the distribution and availability of nutrients, favoring the growth of specific bacteria like Oscillibacter, Eubacterium, and Blautia. Certain bacteria may become more competitive and increase in abundance after prolonged adaptation. These findings indicate that in this small cohort extended IGB therapy may shift the microbial community toward higher levels of certain Bacillota, while also reducing specific Eubacterium populations that may be more prevalent at earlier time points.

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic inflammatory condition characterized by excessive accumulation of pro-inflammatory adipose tissue, which increases the risk of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obstructive sleep apnea. Growing evidence suggests that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in obesity pathophysiology by influencing nutrient digestion, absorption, metabolism, appetite regulation, bile acid metabolism, and endocrine and immune functions. Notably, specific bacterial taxa, such as Blautia wexlerae and Bacteroides dorei, have been identified as potential predictors of weight loss when present in high abundance at the onset of weight reduction1. Given the increasing prevalence of obesity, effective treatment strategies are essential. One such approach is intragastric balloon (IGB) implantation2, an endoscopic intervention designed to facilitate weight loss in individuals with a body mass index (BMI) of 30–40 kg/m2. IGBs serve as an alternative to bariatric surgery for patients who are ineligible or unwilling to undergo surgical intervention or as a preparatory step for those with severe obesity, where initial weight reduction can lower surgical risks. The balloon is introduced into the stomach either endoscopically or via capsule ingestion and subsequently filled with air or liquid (typically a saline solution containing methylene blue). The device remains in the stomach for approximately six months, with some variants extending up to 12 months, leading to an average weight reduction of 10–15% of initial body weight3–5. Although generally safe, IGB implantation is not without complications, the most common being nausea, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort6. A recent study comparing IGB efficacy to glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) found that weight loss occurs more rapidly in the initial six months following IGB placement compared to semaglutide treatment (− 12.7 vs. − 9.4 kg, p = 0.03). However, post-removal weight regain is more pronounced in IGB-treated individuals, with an average annual weight gain of 3 kg, a phenomenon not observed in patients undergoing pharmacological treatment7,8. Our previous research9 demonstrated that the IGB surface is colonized by diverse bacterial taxa, including Actinomycetota, Bacteroidia, Campylobacteria, Bacilli, Clostridia, and Negativicutes. Furthermore, variations in balloon-associated microbiota composition were observed between individuals experiencing gastrointestinal discomfort and those who did not.

Intragastric balloons (IGBs) introduce a long-term, abiotic surface into the stomach that remains in continuous contact with the gastric environment. Over the course of implantation, this surface provides a stable platform for biofilm formation under characteristic gastric conditions, including low pH, intermittent nutrient exposure, elevated oxygen tension relative to the distal gut, and rapid luminal turnover. Therefore, the microbial communities that establish on the balloon surface are expected to differ both compositionally and functionally from fecal microbiota and are not directly analogous to mucosa-adherent populations. Sampling this device-associated biofilm at the time of explantation offers a reproducible, minimally invasive means of characterizing a chronic gastric microbiome that develops alongside the IGB over time. Given that this niche is governed by gastric, not intestinal, physiology, interpretation of findings emphasizes features relevant to stomach ecology.

This study aims to determine whether the composition and ecological structure of the IGB-associated biofilm vary by implantation duration (6 vs. 12 months), and whether specific microbial features are associated with weight-loss outcomes. These analyses are exploratory in nature, intended to generate hypotheses regarding gastric host–microbiota interactions.

Results

Study population

The study included nine participants, comprising six women and three men. For five patients, the gastric balloon was implanted for six months, while for the remaining four patients, it was retained for twelve months.

The mean age of the participants was 40 years (± 10.2), with an average body weight of 98.3 kg (± 15.6) on the day of implantation. The mean Body Mass Index (BMI) was 33.7 kg/m2 (± 5.9). Microbiome composition

The study analyzed the gastric balloon microbiome of participants who underwent weight-loss intervention with an intragastric balloon (IGB). Two primary comparisons were conducted: Group 1 (6-month IGB) versus Group 2 (12-month IGB). Within each group, patients who successfully reduced their body weight were compared to those who did not, to identify associations between microbiota composition and weight-loss success. In a representative patient from the non-weight-loss cohort, the microbiome was predominantly composed of Bacteroides dorei/vulgatus (23%), with lower relative abundances of other taxa, including Blautia obeum/wexlerae (7%), Clostridium sp. In patients who lost weight, the microbial community was more diverse. While Bacteroides species (e.g., B. dorei/vulgatus, B. caccae, B. thetaiotaomicron, B. uniformis) remained present, their dominance was reduced compared to individuals without weight loss. The community included a wider range of taxa, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Ruminococcus bromii, Parabacteroides distasonis, Oscillibacter, Eubacterium, and Ruminococcaceae species. Based on these results, it can be hypothesized that a more diverse and balanced microbiome, enriched with taxa linked to beneficial metabolic functions (e.g., Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Ruminococcus), was associated with better weight-loss outcomes at six months.

Among individuals who did not lose weight after 12 months of IGB placement, the microbiome remained diverse, comprising Anaerostipes, multiple Bacteroides species, Barnesiella, Bifidobacterium, Blautia, Clostridiales, Collinsella, Dorea, Eubacterium, Faecalibacterium, Parabacteroides, Prevotella, Roseburia, and Ruminococcaceae species. Despite the presence of taxa linked to short-chain fatty acid production (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Eubacterium) and carbohydrate degradation (Prevotella, Ruminococcus), their abundance did not correspond with weight loss. In contrast, the individual who had an IGB for 12 months and lost weight exhibited a distinct microbiome shift, with increased levels of beneficial or metabolically flexible taxa. Bacteroides uniformis (11%) and Blautia massiliensis (9%) were abundant, while Anaerostipes hadrus was present at 6.2%. Ruminococcaceae species and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii were also detected at moderate levels. This composition aligns with beneficial patterns observed in the 6-month weight-loss group, which may suggest that the presence of butyrate producers (Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Anaerostipes) may enhance metabolic flexibility, contributing to weight reduction. Both 6- and 12-month responders exhibited a trend toward increased abundance of beneficial, SCFA-producing, and fiber-degrading taxa. The 12-month responder’s microbiome included Bacteroides, Blautia, Ruminococcaceae, and Faecalibacterium, mirroring patterns observed in the 6-month group.

Alpha and Beta Diversity Analysis of Microbiome Samples: When comparing the alpha diversity of microbiome samples collected after 6 months of IGB placement to those collected after 12 months, no statistically significant differences were observed across the four diversity indices (Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test: Observed, p = 0.32; Chao1, p = 0.41; Shannon, p = 1.00; Simpson, p = 0.90). These results indicate that the within-sample microbial richness and diversity did not differ significantly based on the duration of IGB placement. Similarly, when comparing individuals who achieved weight reduction to those who did not, the alpha diversity measures also did not reveal statistically significant differences (Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test: Observed, p = 0.54; Chao1, p = 0.063; Shannon, p = 0.73; Simpson, p = 0.28). Although the Chao1 estimator approached but did not reach the threshold for significance (p = 0.063), the lack of significant differences in alpha diversity suggests that neither the duration of intragastric balloon placement nor the achievement of weight reduction was strongly associated with changes in within-sample microbial richness or diversity. The Bray–Curtis PERMANOVA results for samples taken at 6 months versus 12 months indicated no statistically significant differences in microbial community composition between the time points (F-value = 1.17; R-squared = 0.14; p value = 0.24). Similarly, comparing the microbial communities of patients who experienced weight reduction with those who did not also revealed no statistically significant differences (F-value = 1.23; R-squared = 0.15; p value = 0.16) (Fig. 2). We found no significant shifts in microbiota composition when extending IGB placement from 6 to 12 months. The p value of 0.24 exceeds the conventional significance threshold (0.05), indicating that any changes in microbial structure were not statistically significant. The analysis of correlated taxa revealed several cohesive clusters, consisting of species that frequently co-occur. Some Alistipes species, such as Alistipes finegoldii/onderdonkii, showed positive correlations with various Bacteroides taxa, as well as with Eubacterium hallii, Flavonifractor plautii, and Firmicutes bacterium CAG:137. It should also be noted that strong positive correlations often extended beyond pairwise interactions, forming networks of microorganisms that regularly co-occur. Such clusters were particularly evident among bacteria involved in fiber degradation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium rectale, and Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, as well as Roseburia and Barnesiella species.

In contrast, some correlations were negative. Bacteroides uniformis exhibited negative correlations with Coprococcus comes and Fusicatenibacter saccharivorans, indicating that an increase in the abundance of Bacteroides uniformis may coincide with a decrease in these taxa. Similarly, some butyrate-producing taxa, such as Eubacterium hallii and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, showed mildly negative correlations with Ruminococcus torques, which could suggest potential competition for substrates or differing ecological niches that do not favor their simultaneous presence in the same environment.

Correlation network analysis based on weight-loss outcomes revealed patterns similar to those observed in participants grouped by IGB placement duration (Fig. 4b). This similarity largely arises because individuals who lost at least 10 kg were predominantly part of the 6-month treatment group. However, despite this overlap, distinct microbial co-occurrence and exclusion trends emerged between participants who achieved significant weight loss and those who did not.

In individuals who lost ≥ 10 kg, several microbial taxa exhibited strong positive correlations, which may suggest that they tended to fluctuate together. This pattern was particularly evident among fiber-degrading and SCFA-producing bacteria, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium rectale, and various Bacteroides species. In contrast, participants who failed to reach the 10 kg weight-loss threshold exhibited fewer strong positive correlations among SCFA-producing bacteria. Some butyrate-producing taxa, such as Eubacterium hallii, displayed weak to moderate negative correlations with other microbes, suggesting potential competition for substrates or ecological niches. Notably, Bacteroides uniformis negatively correlated with certain Coprococcus species, implying an inverse relationship in which an increase in one taxon’s abundance coincided with a decline in the other.

Discussion

Bariatric surgery is widely recognized as the most effective long-term treatment for obesity. A key component of certain bariatric protocols is the implantation of a gastric balloon, which serves as a preoperative intervention to facilitate the necessary weight reduction before surgery10–12. However, not all patients achieve the targeted weight loss of approximately 10% of their initial body weight13. One possible factor influencing weight loss outcomes is the microbiota, which plays a crucial role in energy metabolism and, consequently, in both the development and treatment of obesity14. While the impact of gut microbiota on colonic function is well-documented, its potential role in modifying the gastric environment following IGB implantation remains uncertain. It is plausible that the balloon acts as a novel ecological niche, altering gastrointestinal dynamics through changes in gastric emptying, motility, and hormone secretion. These shifts, in turn, may influence nutrient absorption and create conditions that selectively promote the growth of specific bacterial populations. Additionally, the mechanical reduction of stomach volume induced by the IGB enhances satiety and restricts food intake, further contributing to microbiota alterations. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these processes remain to be fully elucidated9,15.

Microbiota diversity is widely regarded as a key determinant of metabolic health. Higher microbial diversity in the colon is associated with a lower risk of long-term weight gain, whereas obesity is often linked to a reduced microbiota diversity index16. Several factors, including overall health status, medication use, and diet, influence microbiota diversity17–19. In particular, dietary components such as fiber play a regulatory role by shaping microbiota composition and stimulating the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which influence host metabolism20–24. Our findings suggest that neither the duration of balloon implantation (6 vs. 12 months) nor the extent of weight loss significantly affected overall microbiota richness or diversity (Fig. 1, 2 and 3). While alpha diversity serves as a key parameter of microbiota complexity25, our results indicate that changes in specific bacterial taxa, rather than overall diversity, may be more relevant to weight loss outcomes following IGB implantation. Notably, patients who achieved a ≥ 10% reduction in initial body weight exhibited distinct microbiota compositions. Their balloon-associated microbiota was characterized by a lower relative abundance of Bacteroides species and an increased presence of beneficial bacterial taxa, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Ruminococcus bromii, Parabacteroides distasonis, Oscillibacter, and species from the Eubacterium and Ruminococcaceae families. This microbial shift may suggest that certain bacteria with beneficial metabolic functions, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Ruminococcus bromii, may be associated with improved weight loss outcomes. Interestingly, Ruminococcus bromii has also been linked to reduced gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly in patients with irritable bowel syndrome, as evidenced by a genome-wide association study (GWAS) involving 799,429 individuals of European descent26.

In patients with successful weight loss, prolonged IGB implantation (12 months) was associated with an increased abundance of Bacteroides uniformis, Blautia massiliensis, and Anaerostipes hadrus. Previous studies have linked Anaerostipes hadrus and certain Blautia species to significant weight loss in obese individuals (BMI > 35 kg/m2). Notably, a recent study by Arbizu et al.27 reported a substantial reduction in these bacteria following dietary interventions in obese individuals. Conversely, other studies have suggested that Anaerostipes hadrus, a butyrate-producing species, may contribute to improvements in body weight and glycemic control in obese patients undergoing prebiotic interventions28. While differences in sample matrices must be considered, our findings suggest intriguing parallels between the microbial shifts observed in IGB-treated patients and those reported in fecal microbiota research (however, it should be noted that our study is preliminary and was conducted on a small sample size).

Among individuals who failed to achieve the desired weight loss, the balloon-associated microbiota displayed a markedly different profile. These patients exhibited a microbiota dominated by Bacteroides dorei/vulgatus, with reduced levels of Blautia obeum/wexlerae, Clostridium sp. CAG-7, Streptococcus thermophilus, Dorea longicatena, Parabacteroides merdae, and species from the Collinsella genus, including Collinsella aerofaciens. Prior studies suggest that these bacteria may contribute to obesity through several mechanisms: (1) enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, leading to increased caloric absorption; (2) disruption of SCFA production, triggering metabolic imbalances; and (3) modulation of appetite-regulating hormones, impairing satiety regulation29–32. Collectively, these factors may promote metabolic dysfunction and hinder weight loss. Our taxonomic co-occurrence analysis revealed distinct microbial clusters formed by frequently co-occurring taxa (Figs. 4 and 5). Notably, certain species from the Alistipes genus, such as Alistipes finegoldii/onderdonkii, exhibited strong positive correlations with various Bacteroides taxa, Eubacterium hallii, Flavonifractor plautii, and Firmicutes bacterium CAG:137. These bacterial networks extended beyond simple pairwise correlations, forming complex metabolic interactions. Similarly, we identified clusters of bacteria involved in fiber degradation and SCFA production, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium rectale, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, and species from the Roseburia and Barnesiella genera24. These taxa are often regarded as beneficial due to their ability to support each other’s growth and metabolic functions, particularly in environments altered by IGB implantation33.

It is important to note that this study is exploratory and pilot in nature. Therefore, its findings should be interpreted with caution and within the context of the need for more comprehensive research on the subject. The limited sample size, absence of a control group, and lack of consideration for potentially influential variables, such as participants’ lifestyle and dietary habits, represent key limitations that may affect the interpretation of changes in the microbiota.

Material and methods

Gastric balloon procedure

Nine patients from the Sonomed Medical Center in Szczecin, Poland, were selected for Orbera™ gastric balloon implantation (Apollo Endosurgery Inc., Austin, Texas, USA) under the guidance of their attending physicians. Patients in the study group denied having any chronic diseases or taking any medication. The implantation procedure began with a diagnostic endoscopy to assess the stomach’s condition and rule out contraindications such as a significant hiatal hernia or peptic ulcer disease. The balloon was introduced into the stomach via a flexible catheter and an endoscope, positioned precisely, and inflated with sterile saline containing a dye for leak detection. Correct placement was confirmed endoscopically. Balloon removal, performed under sedation, involved endoscopic guidance to locate the device. The balloon was punctured with an endoscopic needle to aspirate its contents before being extracted with forceps. Samples for microbiota analysis were collected during balloon removal using an endoscopic cytology brush (Cook Medical ECB 5-180-2-S, Jiangsu, China). To avoid contamination, the material was obtained directly from the balloon’s surface and protected during withdrawal. Two patients reported abdominal pain and nausea after the gastric balloon was inserted, while the others denied having any symptoms. Anthropometric measurements were recorded on the day of both implantation and removal. Considering that a 10% reduction in body weight contributes to numerous beneficial health implications34, we have adopted a threshold of 10 kg as the cut-off point in the analyses presented in this article (classification of participants into groups that have been successful in losing weight).

DNA extraction from balloon samples

DNA was extracted from cytology brush samples using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA kit (Qiagen). Samples were transferred to PowerFecal Bead tubes with buffer C1 and homogenized using an Omni Bead Ruptor 12 (three 30-s cycles with 30-s intervals). The remaining procedures followed the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified DNA was eluted in 70 µL of elution buffer and quantified using the Quantifluor ONE dsDNA system (Promega).

Shallow shotgun metagenomics sequencing

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the KAPA HyperPlus kit (ROCHE) following the manufacturer’s protocol, yielding DNA fragments of ~ 300 bp as described by Sanders et al.35. DNA was normalized to 10 ng, enzymatically fragmented for 10-min, indexed with KAPA Unique Dual Indexes, and amplified via nine PCR cycles. Libraries were purified, size-selected, and assessed for quality using fluorometry (Quantus, Promega) and chip electrophoresis (MultiNA, Shimadzu). Pooled libraries were normalized to 2 nM, denatured with NaOH, diluted in HT1 buffer (Illumina), and spiked with 1% PhiX control v3 (Illumina). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq System with a 2 × 150-cycles paired-end strategy, though only forward reads were used due to quality concerns. Data were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ files using Illumina bcl2fastq2 software (v2.20).

The forward-read yield per sample ranged from 389,095 to 737,105 reads (median 550,683; IQR 523,732–642,204; mean ± SD 574,336 ± 105,264). As expected for paired-end runs, forward and reverse read counts were equivalent at demultiplexing, yielding a combined paired-end total of 778,190–1,474,210 reads per library (median 1,101,365; IQR 1,047,463–1,284,407; mean ± SD 1,148,673 ± 210,527). Across the cohort this corresponds to 5,743,364 forward reads (11,486,728 paired-end reads) in aggregate.

Metagenomics data processing

Reads from sequencing were processed using NGless (v1.3.0). Quality filtering excluded reads with Phred scores < 25 or lengths < 45 bp. Contaminants were removed by mapping reads to GRCh38.p14. Taxonomic profiling was performed with the mOTUs module (v2.6), and functional profiling mapped reads to the Global Microbial Gene Catalog (GMGC, human-gut v1), aggregated at the KEGG KO level36. Genus level richness (Observed, Chao1), alpha diversity (Shannon, Simpson), and beta diversity (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) were computed using the vegan (v2.6-4) and stats (v4.2.2) packages. Beta diversity was visualized with principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Associations between bacterial genera and samples were analyzed with the metacoder package, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Statistical analysis

Taxa with less than 10% prevalence were excluded. Microbiome count data were rarefied to account for compositionality. Pairwise correlations between taxa were calculated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, with FDR correction applied to adjust p-values. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests assessed taxa abundance differences between baseline and post-treatment groups, with p-values corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Significant correlations were used to construct microbial networks with the igraph package, where nodes represent taxa and edges represent significant correlations between them. Positive correlations (r > 0) suggest potential mutualistic or commensal relationships, while negative correlations (r < 0) indicate possible competitive or antagonistic interactions.

Software and visualization

All analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.2) and Python (v3.10) in PyCharm Professional IDE v2024.3.3 (JetBrains Toolbox) on Fedora Linux (v40). Visualizations were generated with ggplot2 (v3.5.1), plotly (v5.24.1), seaborn (v0.13.2), and matplotlib (v3.10.0).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, with only nine patients undergoing IGB implantation, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Second, the comparison between 6-month and 12-month balloon implantation groups is constrained by the limited number of participants, potentially influencing the observed microbial trends. Thirdly, our study did not include an analysis of patients’ eating habits, which could also have a potential impact on changes in the studied microbiota. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as preliminary.

In summary, gastric microflora (examined on a balloon) may not be directly related to the metabolic functions characterizing the intestinal microbiota and the metabolism of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA). Therefore, the significance of gastric microflora sampling is cognitively interesting but limited compared to intestinal microflora testing.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide intriguing insights into the potential role of the gastric balloon as a modifier of microbiota composition. Further research involving larger cohorts is warranted to validate and expand upon these observations.
