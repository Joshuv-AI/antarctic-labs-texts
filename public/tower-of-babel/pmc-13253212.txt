Large-scale bathymetry in high-turbidity rivers enabled by remote sensing and artificial intelligence
Authors: Yizhe Pang, Yuan Xue, Yongxian Zhang, Zipu Ma, Xuanwei Xing, Mengzhen Xu, Xudong Fu, Chunhong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water depth, as a direct indicator of a river's continuous underwater topography, provides crucial information for studies such as investigating channel morphological evolution, modeling sediment transport, and quantifying material flux. Conventional approaches to acquiring localized water depth, involving in situ measurements or methods based on remote sensing imagery, are mostly employed in clear rivers at small scales. However, these techniques encounter substantial limitations when applied to extensive river reaches with high-sediment-concentration flow, where accurately determining water depth is generally only achievable through in situ field measurements. Here we present an intelligent model named RivDepth, designed to obtain water depth distributions in rivers with relatively high suspended sediment concentrations (SSC) exceeding 1 kg m−3. By integrating satellite-acquired spectral variables and an optically derived SSC proxy as inputs, together with an “AI expert” module capable of inference, decision-making, and prediction, the proposed model captures the coupled depth–reflectance–SSC relationship patterns of sample pixels. This enables pixel-wise retrieval of large-scale water depth distributions in high-SSC rivers. RivDepth was trained and tested on the lower Yellow River, one of the rivers with the highest SSC in the world. The proposed model delivered accurate depth estimates, achieving an R2 of 0.896, RMSE of 0.456 m, MAE of 0.228 m, and ME of −0.020 m. Shapley additive explanations-based feature-importance analysis indicates that shortwave infrared bands, the red band, a red-edge band, the water vapor band, the aerosol/blue band, and the SSC proxy are among the most influential predictors of water depth in this high-SSC river. Their contributions vary spatially in a complex, non-monotonic manner. This study presents a feasible and robust method for large-scale water-depth retrieval in rivers with high SSC. Furthermore, it provides a valuable reference for large-scale hydrological monitoring and basin-scale integrated management by integrating remote sensing imagery with in situ observations.

Introduction

Water depth distribution is a key morphological descriptor of rivers that directly reflects their cross-sectional form and bed structure. It provides essential information for investigating hydrological and hydrodynamic processes [1] and for simulating material fluxes [2]. Therefore, the accurate retrieval of large-scale river depth distributions is of paramount importance. According to data and reports from the Information Center of the Ministry of Water Resources of China, significant financial resources have been allocated cumulatively to surveys of key river features, including river bathymetry. The resulting data support nationwide applications, including water resource management and riverine ecosystem conservation [3,4].

Since 2000, research on river depth retrieval techniques has increased steadily. Searches were conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure using keywords such as “river depth retrieval,” “river bathymetry retrieval,” and “river cross-section measurement.” These searches identified over 4000 publications dating from 2000 onward. Over the past five years, the total annual citation count for these publications has exceeded 6500 (Fig. 1). Among these publications, more than half have attempted to apply remote-sensing techniques for water-depth retrieval, indicating that remote-sensing-based depth retrieval methods are becoming increasingly prevalent.Fig. 1Publication trends and literature characteristics in river-depth retrieval research. Currently, in situ measurements remain the primary means of acquiring river depths, owing to their high accuracy. While conventional surveys are commonly performed using vessel-based acoustic instruments, recent advances in autonomous and remote sensing technologies have enabled the growing application of intelligent platforms, such as unmanned surface vehicles equipped with acoustic sensors [5] and unmanned aerial vehicles equipped with sonar or light detection and ranging (LiDAR) systems [6,7]. However, these approaches usually have limited spatial coverage. Conducting a single survey often entails significant equipment investment and operational constraints. Extending coverage to larger areas, therefore, requires repeated surveys, which significantly increase demands on time, personnel, and resources, resulting in a considerable overall expense [5,7]. In contrast, remote sensing techniques, owing to their ability to acquire data over large spatial extents and at high temporal resolution, offer greater potential for retrieving river depths and delineating river boundaries [8,9]. Existing studies have proposed a variety of remote sensing-based approaches for mapping river bathymetry from various data sources. For example, photon-counting LiDAR can recover underwater topography by locating underwater signal photons, as demonstrated by Xu et al. [10]. Alternatively, when combined with hydraulic modeling and water surface slope estimates, it can also provide water depth distributions over broader areas, as demonstrated by Musaeus et al. [11]. Additionally, various approaches have been developed to indirectly obtain water depth distributions from optical imagery. Legleiter et al. [12,13] employed semi-empirical models to infer water depths, while Pan et al. [14] and Niroumand-Jadidi et al. [15] utilized machine-learning models to establish depth-reflectance relationships for river depth retrieval. Dietrich et al. [16] applied stereo photogrammetry techniques to acquire river bathymetry. Furthermore, Chan et al. [17] developed a convolutional neural network framework that combines reflectance transformations with diverse satellite-derived features required to map depth distributions. Kwon et al. [18] used airborne visible/infrared imaging spectrometer–next generation hyperspectral data and random forest to map depth in turbid deltaic channels; however, the method is limited to low-to-moderate turbidity (approximately 20–80 mg L−1). Legleiter et al. [19] introduced HIRBERT, an ensemble-of-regression-trees model trained on paired airborne hyperspectral reflectance and field-measured depth data obtained using acoustic Doppler current profiling and multibeam echo-sounding for river bathymetry retrieval and mapping. These optical approaches have been implemented using a variety of remote sensing data sources, including satellite imagery, airborne imagery, and imagery acquired by unmanned aerial vehicles, depending on data availability and study objectives. Moreover, some studies have also explored indirect methods for estimating water depth using non-optical remote sensing data. Almeida et al. [20] and Legleiter et al. [21,22] integrated flow velocities with hydrodynamic formulations, such as the shallow-water and flow-resistance equations, to infer water depths. Wu et al. [23] estimated cross-sectional depths under assumed channel geometries using relationships between water level and channel width derived from remote sensing observations. However, most of the aforementioned river depth retrieval methods have limited applicability, with some suitable only for reaches with clear, shallow water. In practice, such reaches are typically characterized by suspended sediment concentration (SSC) below 0.1 kg m−3 and water depths of no more than 3 m. Additionally, some methods are implemented only over relatively short river segments (e.g., less than 100 km), where variability in water-column properties can sometimes be neglected, while other methods are constrained by both conditions simultaneously. For longer river reaches with high SSC, the optical and acoustic properties of the water column become highly complex [24,25], making them difficult to represent using parameterized models. Moreover, water-column properties often exhibit substantial spatial heterogeneity across large areas. These characteristics severely constrain the applicability of many existing water-depth retrieval methods. Consequently, costly direct measurement techniques (e.g., LiDAR and sonar) and indirect approaches using mathematical models based on optical imagery (e.g., the optimal band ratio analysis, OBRA [12]) are not well suited for large-scale depth-retrieval tasks in high-SSC rivers. Obtaining large-scale bathymetry for such rivers, therefore, remains challenging. Relatively few studies have addressed this issue in the broader literature, particularly for conditions with high SSC above 1 kg m−3. As a result, research that relies on spatially distributed river-depth data is constrained in terms of both river type and spatial extent.

To address the challenges of retrieving large-scale river-depth distributions in high-SSC rivers, this study introduces RivDepth, an intelligent model that leverages broad-spatial-coverage optical satellite imagery. Under the RivDepth model, we used satellite-acquired spectral variables, along with a rarely incorporated variable representing optically derived suspended sediment concentration (ODSSC, hereafter referred to as the SSC proxy), as inputs to characterize reflectance and sediment concentration. Recent research indicates that, unlike traditional semi-empirical or band-ratio methods, machine-learning-based models often perform better in riverine and inland water environments [14,15,18,26]. These models can fully leverage spectral information and capture complex nonlinear dependencies between reflectance and water depth, which semi-empirical methods often oversimplify [18,19]. Therefore, we employed four classic machine-learning methods widely used in prior studies to train an advanced “AI expert” module, aiming to capitalize on their demonstrated flexibility and predictive power for depth retrieval under extremely challenging high-SSC conditions. The AI expert module integrates the strengths of different models at the pixel level and can automatically capture relationship patterns among water depth, reflectance, and SSC. Building on this module, RivDepth can generate accurate pixel-wise water-depth distributions. This approach overcomes the long-standing limitations of obtaining large-scale water-depth distributions in high-SSC rivers, significantly improving depth estimation accuracy. To our knowledge, it enables the first successful large-scale mapping of water-depth distributions using optical satellite imagery in rivers with SSC exceeding 1 kg m−3. The proposed model was then applied to an approximately 786-km-long segment of the lower Yellow River, from Xixiayuan to Lijin, generating a water-depth raster dataset at a 10-m spatial resolution. We further assessed the contribution of multispectral reflectance bands and the derived SSC proxy to water-depth prediction using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)-based feature-importance analysis. This study provides a practical and robust intelligent solution for large-scale depth retrieval in high-SSC rivers and offers technical guidance and data support for large-scale hydrological monitoring and remote-sensing-driven integrated management.

Material and methods

Study area

The study area is located in the lower Yellow River, spans Henan and Shandong provinces, and covers a drainage area of approximately 23000 km2 (Fig. 2). The region has a typical monsoon climate. The segment extends roughly 786 km, with river widths ranging from approximately 0.4 to 5.0 km. Observed data indicate that, since the operation of the Xiaolangdi Reservoir, the multiyear average discharge at the Huayuankou Station is 918.5 m3 s−1, and the long-term average suspended sediment concentration measured at the cross-section is 2.2 kg m−3. In this study, SSC is expressed in kg m−3 to compactly represent the high concentrations typical of the lower Yellow River. During the five years ending in 2020, measured cross-sectional water depths reached 11 m, and peak velocities at the Huayuankou cross-section ranged from 1.8 to 5.1 m s−1. Data sources

Satellite imagery

We obtained bottom-of-atmosphere (BOA) surface-reflectance satellite images at spatial resolutions of 10–60 m from the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem platform. The satellite images are Sentinel-2 Level-2A (L2A) products that have been radiometrically calibrated and terrain-corrected. The L2A products are generated from Level-1C (L1C) top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance using the operational Level-2A processor based on the Sen2Cor atmospheric correction algorithm, which converts TOA reflectance to BOA surface reflectance. This processing accounts for several atmospheric effects, including molecular (Rayleigh) scattering, aerosol scattering and absorption, and gaseous absorption. In addition, terrain effects are corrected using Digital Elevation Model (DEM)-based topographic correction. The L2A products also include auxiliary layers such as aerosol optical thickness (AOT), water vapor (WV) maps, and a scene classification map with masks for cloud, cloud shadow, water, snow/ice, vegetation, and other surface types (https://step.esa.int/main/snap-supported-plugins/sen2cor/). The Sentinel-2 MSI bands used in this study span a range of wavelengths and spatial resolutions (Supplementary Table S1). Clouds and cloud shadows in some Sentinel-2 images can obscure river surfaces and compromise large-scale water-depth mapping. We therefore reconstructed cloud-affected areas to provide complete spatial coverage for depth retrieval. For each Sentinel-2 image, cloud and cloud-shadow masks were generated using the Function of Mask (Fmask) algorithm (version 4.7), which automatically identifies clouds, cloud shadows, snow, water, and clear land in Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery [27,28]. Fmask was applied to the corresponding Sentinel-2 L1C images, and the pixels classified as either cloud or cloud shadow were combined into a single mask and transferred to the corresponding L2A surface-reflectance product. For target dates selected for retrieving river depth distributions, when the river's mainstream was obscured by clouds or cloud shadows, we applied a temporal gap-filling approach to the corresponding images: pixels flagged as cloud or cloud shadow in the target image were replaced with reflectance values from temporally adjacent, cloud-free Sentinel-2 L2A images covering the same locations. Only pixels flagged as cloud-free in the auxiliary images were used for replacement. We then applied histogram matching to reduce radiometric differences between images, producing a seamless cloud-free mosaic for subsequent depth retrieval. The cloud-removal procedure effectively restored obscured river pixels while maintaining radiometric consistency across the mosaic (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Water level, in situ SSC, and cross-sectional elevation data

A portion of the original water-level data used in this study was extracted from the October 2020 records of the Hydrological Yearbook of the Yellow River Basin, People's Republic of China, and the remaining data were obtained by processing remote sensing imagery. The in situ SSC dataset used in this study was obtained from the Hydrological Yearbook of the People's Republic of China. The field-measured cross-sectional elevation data were provided by the Yellow River Conservancy Commission of the Ministry of Water Resources.

Dataset construction

To accurately extract the mainstream water body, we applied common water indices, including the normalized difference water index (NDWI) [29], the modified normalized difference water index (MNDWI) [29], and the automated water extraction index without shadow influence (AWEInsh) and with shadow influence (AWEIsh) [30], in combination with Otsu's method for each satellite image. To reduce misclassification in complex terrain, we further employed the iron oxide (IO) index [31], defined as the red-band-to-blue-band ratio, as an auxiliary indicator to distinguish non-water features from water under ambiguous spectral conditions. In practice, for pixels with borderline water-index values, a relatively high IO value indicates a non-water class, thereby refining the final water mask. Extraction results were then manually verified to produce reliable water masks with well-defined boundaries (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Example of water-body extraction from a Sentinel-2 image. a, Original image of a representative reach in the lower Yellow River. b, Extracted mainstream mask overlaid in blue. Based on the extracted water masks, we applied an extended implementation of the automated river width extraction (ARWE) method [32], hereafter ARWE 2.0, to derive river morphological features, including the main-channel centerline, thalweg, and river width at target cross-sections (Supplementary Fig. S2). ARWE 2.0 is implemented in Python and operates on high-resolution binary water masks derived from Sentinel-2 imagery, along with the corresponding depth data used in this study. Relative to the original ARWE algorithm, it adds the capability to derive the thalweg from the provided cross-sectional depth data. Furthermore, for cross-sections lacking water-level observations, we used a pattern search method [33] to estimate water level and derive adjusted depth data by iteratively matching the cross-sectional width derived from elevation data to that observed from satellite imagery. The method repeatedly adjusted the water level, generated candidate depth distributions based on cross-sectional elevation data, and evaluated the resulting cross-sectional widths against satellite observations. The search terminated when the absolute difference between the two width estimates fell below a predefined tolerance. For cross-sections with water-level observations, the depth data were derived directly from cross-sectional elevation measurements and water level. Moreover, as the river segment is flanked by terrestrial non-water surfaces, the reflectance of the water pixels can be affected by radiative contributions and adjacency effects from nearby shore features [12]. To emphasize the spectral signal of water and reduce contamination from non-water features, we excluded one to two pixels nearest the riverbank at each cross-section.

Using the preprocessed data from the above sources, we compiled a dataset containing 4873 samples from 62 cross-sections along the lower Yellow River. This dataset was used for subsequent model training and accuracy validation.

Reflectance-based SSC retrieval

The propagation of light in water is affected by absorption and scattering by suspended particles, which substantially influence the water-surface reflectance ultimately recorded by satellites [34]. In high-SSC rivers, suspended sediment is generally the dominant optically active constituent governing these interactions. Therefore, explicitly accounting for SSC is essential for reliable depth retrieval. Although several publicly available SSC products cover parts of the lower Yellow River (e.g., the Global River Sediment Dataset [GSED]), these products provide reach-averaged monthly SSC estimates derived from a limited set of Landsat bands and are not suitable for pixel-level feature characterization required by depth-retrieval models. To address this limitation, we trained an SSC retrieval model using in situ SSC measurements and Sentinel-2 spectral predictors, thereby generating a pixel-level SSC proxy. This proxy was then incorporated into the proposed depth retrieval framework to represent SSC and to account for the coupled interactions among river depth, surface reflectance, and suspended sediment.

Data and preprocessing

Following Li et al. [35], the retrieval model for SSC was developed by combining in situ SSC measurements from seven hydrological stations in the lower Yellow River from 2016 to 2020, covering an SSC range of 0.3–27.3 kg m−3, with Sentinel-2 multispectral images. We assembled 78 spectral features as candidate predictors, comprising 12 spectral bands (B1, B2, B3, B4, B5, B6, B7, B8, B8A, B9, B11, B12) and 66 (C122) pairwise band ratios (e.g., B2/B1).

Clustering and model fitting

To allow for spectral heterogeneity among river surface pixels, we introduced an initial clustering step prior to model calibration. Pixels were first partitioned into two classes using an unsupervised k-means clustering approach based on their optical reflectance features. This allowed separate models to be trained for spectrally distinct pixel groups, thereby improving overall SSC prediction performance. The number of clusters (k) was determined based on multiple diagnostic metrics. The elbow criterion applied to the within-cluster sum of squares suggested a possible inflection at k = 2 or k = 3, indicating that larger k values explained limited additional variance. Although models with k ≥ 3 sometimes yielded slight improvements in overall predictive performance when considered in aggregate, this improvement was accompanied by a conspicuous decline in predictive accuracy within one or more clusters. In these cases, the submodels for specific clusters failed to capture stable relationships between predictors and ln(C), suggesting that those partitions were not suitable for robust cluster-specific modeling. Here, C (kg m−3) denotes the SSC value when used as a mathematical variable. For these reasons, a two-cluster partition was chosen to balance model complexity and predictive robustness (see Supplementary Fig. S3 and Table S2–S3 for detailed clustering diagnostics).

For each class, a five-fold cross-validated least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used to perform feature selection and model fitting [36]. In detail, LASSO estimates the coefficient vector β by minimizing the L1-penalized least-squares objective, which is defined as follows:(1)β=argmin{∑i=1N[ln(Ci)−βTxi]2+α‖β‖1}where i indexes the samples within a given cluster, and ln (Ci) is the response variable. xi is the predictor vector for sample i, comprising the spectral features within the corresponding k-means group, including individual bands such as B2 and B3, and band ratios such as B3/B2 and B4/B3. β is the vector of regression coefficients, and α is the regularization parameter, with α≥0. The objective function includes a data-fidelity term (the squared-error loss) and an L1 penalty applied to the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients. As the regularization parameter α increases, the penalty imposes stronger shrinkage, reducing some coefficients and potentially shrinking them to zero, thereby enabling automatic variable selection and dimensionality reduction.

Application

For application, each Sentinel-2 pixel was first assigned to one of the two clusters using the trained k-means centroids, after which the corresponding class-specific LASSO model was applied to estimate SSC. The derived SSC proxy was appended to each sample and subsequently used as an auxiliary predictor in the depth-retrieval models. The predictive performance of the SSC retrieval model is reported in Section 3.1.

Base model construction

We used the reflectance values of twelve Sentinel-2 spectral bands as optical predictors, excluding the cirrus band (B10) due to its typical omission from surface reflectance products and its known susceptibility to radiometric anomalies. Additionally, we incorporated the SSC proxy (derived in Section 2.4) as a predictor representing SSC. In total, thirteen predictors were used in the depth retrieval models.

We adopted four classic machine-learning algorithms—parallel random forest (PRF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector regression (SVR), and multilayer perceptron (MLP)—as base models for depth retrieval due to their ability to capture complex nonlinear reflectance-depth relationships and demonstrated efficacy in retrieving river and lake depths [14,15,18,19,26]. Specifically, the PRF method enhances computational efficiency and maintains the ability to model complex nonlinear relationships by parallelizing decision tree training and incorporating task parallelism, random sampling, and feature selection [37,38]. This approach enables rapid predictions on river segment datasets comprising millions of sample pixels. The XGBoost method reduces overfitting through regularization and models nonlinear relationships via iterative optimization [39]. The SVR method employs kernel functions to map input variables into a high-dimensional space, thereby modeling the nonlinearity influenced by multiple riverine factors [40]. The MLP method learns a nonlinear mapping from input spectral features to water depth via its hidden layers [41]. Collectively, these methods served as the basis for constructing the AI expert module.

During training, the hyperparameters for PRF, XGBoost, and SVR were optimized using Optuna by minimizing the three-fold cross-validated mean squared error (MSE). To control computational costs, only a subset of commonly used hyperparameters was tuned. For the MLP, we performed a grid search over a predefined, limited set of training options, and the final configuration was selected based on the three-fold cross-validated MSE criterion. The Adam optimizer was employed with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 32. Preliminary experiments showed that, with MSE as the loss function, the MLP models converged within 1000 epochs using the Adam optimizer. Therefore, the training was conducted for 1000 epochs to ensure adequate convergence while controlling computational cost. Tree-based models (PRF and XGBoost) were designated as local experts, as their partition-based structure enables them to model subset-specific patterns effectively in the feature space. Specifically, the PRF model was trained using a depth-specific sample-weighting scheme, where deep-water samples were assigned full weight and shallow-water samples were assigned zero weight. Although all samples were included during tree construction, only deep-water samples contributed to the split criterion and loss minimization, thereby effectively biasing the model toward the deep-water regime. XGBoost was trained using the complementary weighting scheme, where shallow-water samples were assigned full weight and deep-water samples were assigned zero weight, thereby emphasizing optimization in the shallow-water regime. In contrast, SVR and MLP were trained on all samples with equal weights, serving as experts that capture the overall depth-retrieval relationship across the full depth range.

AI expert module

Overview of AI expert module design

This section presents the intelligent model, RivDepth, developed to retrieve water depth distributions in high-SSC rivers. At its core, the workflow includes the AI expert module, which enables RivDepth to learn diverse patterns in the relationship among depth, reflectance, and SSC, as well as improve predictive accuracy. Training phase

During the training phase, we trained four base models (PRF, XGBoost, SVR, and MLP) identified in Section 2.5. A prediction-error classifier, implemented using a random forest, was constructed for each base model except XGBoost and for each error threshold (the sub-meter threshold s1 and the meter-level threshold s2). The XGBoost-associated classifiers were excluded because they showed limited classification performance under the considered error thresholds and were therefore not used for classifier-driven expert selection in the final system. Instead, alternative strategies were used to leverage the XGBoost shallow-water expert. Each sample pixel was assigned a label by each classifier, resulting in a total of six labels per pixel. Specifically, for a given model and threshold, Label 1 denotes samples whose absolute prediction error is less than or equal to the scale-specific threshold (s1 or s2), whereas Label 2 denotes samples whose absolute prediction error exceeds the threshold. At inference time, we adopted a conservative decision rule: a pixel was assigned to Label 1 only if the classifier's predicted probability for Label 1 was no less than 0.9; otherwise, it was assigned to Label 2. On the test set, all classifiers achieved precision values above 81% for Label 1, indicating stable classification performance, with detailed per-classifier metrics and evaluation protocols provided in Supplementary Table S4–S9 and Texts S1–S2. This suite of prediction-error classifiers (serving as inference tools within the inference module), together with the trained base models (serving as prediction tools that supply expert predictions), provided the AI expert module with predictive, inferential, and decision-making capabilities.

Application phase

During the application phase, the AI expert module first ran the preliminary prediction module for each input pixel. The module used two expert models—a shallow-water expert (the XGBoost base model) and an overall expert (the MLP base model), the latter of which exhibited the best overall predictive performance—to generate a preliminary depth estimate, referred to hereafter as the preliminary prediction (PP) (Fig. 5). In addition, the module obtained the four base models’ depth predictions and computed the simple summary statistics of these predictions (mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum), which were then used as inputs to the inference tools. If the predictions from both models were ≤ the empirical threshold of 1 m, PP was set to the XGBoost prediction; otherwise, PP was set to the MLP prediction. Pixels with PP ≤ 1 m were classified as shallow water, and PP was taken as the final prediction (FP). Pixels with PP > 1 m were classified as deeper water and were passed to the inference module, where an ensemble of prediction-error classifiers was applied.

The module then obtained two sets of class labels corresponding to the two error-threshold levels (s1 and s2) from six classifiers. For each threshold level, the IDs of base models assigned Label 1 were first collected. The intersection of the two resulting ID sets defined the Level-1 Optimal Model ID Set (L1 OMID Set), which contained models simultaneously classified as Label 1 by both the s1-level and s2-level classifiers, indicating that prediction errors below both thresholds were considered likely, thereby yielding a higher-confidence selection. This set corresponded to models capable of predicting water depth with an error margin of s1. If the L1 OMID Set was empty, the model IDs classified as Label 1 by the s2-level classifiers were selected instead to form the Level-2 Optimal Model ID Set (L2 OMID Set), which corresponded to models capable of predicting water depth with an error margin of s2. If no model satisfied this second criterion, the sample pixel was flagged, and the module fell back to the default deep-water expert; the identifier of this fallback model was recorded as the Level-3 Optimal Model ID Set (L3 OMID Set), which was a singleton by definition. Thus, without access to the measured water depth, the AI expert module used the input-pixel features to infer the most suitable prediction model, and recorded its inference result as the Optimal Model ID Set (OMID Set). The OMID Set was determined hierarchically: the L1 OMID Set was used if non-empty; otherwise, the L2 OMID Set was used; if both were empty, the singleton L3 OMID Set was used as the fallback. The OMID Set was then passed to the decision module, which selected the optimal prediction strategy based on the marked results. The detailed decision process is as follows:

If the decision module received an L1 or L2 OMID Set containing multiple model IDs, the FP was generally calculated as the average of the corresponding predicted depths. However, if the deep-water expert PRF was included together with any other model ID(s), PRF was prioritized because it was trained to emphasize the deep-water regime. Then all other IDs were removed, and the OMID Set was reduced to a singleton. If the resulting OMID Set contained a single model ID, FP was computed using that model's prediction alone. If the OMID Set was an L3 OMID Set, the deep-water expert was invoked by default to estimate water depth. Overall, the AI expert module employs two tools and three modules to identify which base model's learned depth–reflectance–SSC relationship best fits each input pixel. It then applies customized prediction schemes for sample pixels exhibiting different water characteristics, leveraging the complementary strengths of multiple prediction modes. Consequently, the AI expert module has the potential to improve the stability and accuracy of river depth retrieval.

Accuracy metrics

We assessed model accuracy using multiple metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean error (ME). R2 quantifies the proportion of variance accounted for by the model. RMSE represents the square root of the mean of squared errors, characterizing the magnitude of prediction-observation deviations. MAE represents the mean of absolute errors, an alternative way of analyzing prediction errors. ME represents the mean signed error and thus indicates the direction and magnitude of systematic bias, with positive values denoting overall overestimation and negative values indicating underestimation. Results and discussion

SSC retrieval and validation

Valid samples were split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) via stratified random sampling. The training set was used for model development, while the test set was used to evaluate the model's performance. The model demonstrated good performance on the test set, with an R2 of 0.615, RMSE of 3.266 kg m−3, MAE of 1.509 kg m−3, and ME of −0.718 kg m−3 (Table 2). We present a scatter plot comparing predicted and measured results, along with the spatial distribution of the derived SSC, for several river reaches without in situ data (Fig. 6). The scatter points in log10(SSC) space are generally aligned along the 1:1 line, indicating good overall agreement between predicted and measured SSC values. Taken together, these quantitative and visual results indicate that the derived SSC proxy can reliably represent the SSC of river pixels. In contrast to previous studies that estimated SSC for the lower Yellow River [42], our model was trained using samples with higher SSC values and a more comprehensive set of spectral predictors. Consequently, it yielded more accurate reach-averaged SSC estimates in the lower Yellow River, with predictions more closely matching observed SSC magnitudes. Assessment of RivDepth

We adopted a stratified random sampling scheme, in which pixel-scale samples were stratified by river reaches and by water-depth ranges, and then split into approximately 70% for training and 30% for testing within each stratum. This helped our model better capture spatial heterogeneity, learn depth–reflectance–SSC relationships, and obtain depth distributions over long river stretches. The training set was used for model development, while the test set was used to evaluate the models’ predictive performance along the extensive, high-SSC reaches of the lower Yellow River.

The accuracy metrics for all evaluated models are presented in Table 3. The PRF and XGBoost models were originally developed for task-specific objectives, which likely accounts for their relatively low R2 values when assessed across the full depth range. In contrast, SVR and MLP achieved comparatively stronger overall predictive performance. By incorporating the AI expert module, which adaptively leverages the complementary strengths of different base models under varying input conditions, RivDepth achieved the highest R2 of 0.896, the lowest RMSE of 0.456 m, the lowest MAE of 0.228 m, and an ME close to zero (−0.020 m). To examine the details of river depth retrieval, we partitioned the dataset by cross-section location. At the cross-sectional level, we compared the retrieved depth distributions with the in situ profiles. The agreement was generally favorable (Fig. 8). For 76% of the samples, the absolute deviation between the RivDepth prediction and the sample-wise optimal prediction (the smallest-error prediction among all compared models) was within 0.1 m. This indicates that RivDepth nearly achieved the optimal prediction among the compared models for most samples. However, the model exhibited relatively low predictive accuracy along steep transitions and in shallow areas. The former likely arises from the limited spatial resolution of Sentinel-2, which cannot resolve variations in the depth–reflectance–SSC relationship over distances shorter than 10 m along the cross-sectional direction. The latter likely reflects a breakdown of the primary water depth–reflectance–SSC relationship patterns learned by RivDepth under shallow-water conditions. Previous studies have shown that in very shallow waters, bottom reflectance often dominates the total remote sensing signal [43], making it difficult for regression-based or machine-learning models to distinguish incremental changes in depth. Others have shown that in very shallow waters, mixed-substrate effects and the difficulty of separating substrate contributions can lead to greater uncertainty in machine-learning-based depth retrieval, and spectral vectors arising from different bottom conditions may be difficult for models to distinguish [44]. These phenomena may be compounded by environmental noise and mixed-pixel effects, which are more pronounced in shallow nearshore environments, resulting in complex depth-reflectance relationships that deviate from the dominant patterns observed in deeper offshore regions. Moreover, these error patterns may be further amplified by spatial heterogeneity in hydrodynamic conditions between shallow nearshore and deeper mid-channel reaches [45], because the vertical structure of SSC is primarily governed by local hydrodynamics and water depth rather than sediment supply alone [46]. In addition to these factors, we also considered the potential impact of sun glint. Sun glint, the specular reflection of sunlight off the water surface, is a well-recognized source of noise in optical remote sensing that can obscure water-leaving radiance and degrade depth retrieval accuracy in some cases [47]. However, standard marine sun-glint removal algorithms are not directly applicable to fluvial environments [48], where complex interactions among flow hydraulics, water surface texture, and surface reflectance already pose significant challenges. These difficulties could be further exacerbated in large, heterogeneous river systems. Besides, in the Sentinel-2 images used in this study, we did not observe prominent sun-glint features near the cross-section locations used for training and testing. Therefore, comprehensive glint removal was not applied. While residual surface reflection may contribute some uncertainty, it is unlikely to be a dominant source of error compared to the effects described above.

Feature importance analysis using SHAP

SHAP is a unified additive feature attribution framework based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory. It quantifies the marginal contribution of each feature to the model's prediction, and thus can be used to characterize feature importance [49,50]. To assess the impact of input variables on RivDepth's predictions, we computed feature attributions using SHAP. We estimated SHAP values for each of the four base models using SamplingExplainer, a model-agnostic method that approximates Shapley values via sampling from the background distribution and is computationally efficient for complex black-box models with tabular inputs. Since all base models share the same predictor set, we aggregated the SHAP values of the base models according to the AI expert module's prediction logic, allowing either single-model selection or multi-model weighted combination. The resulting SHAP attributions summarize each feature's contribution to the final RivDepth predictions. The thirteen variables were categorized into three groups: blue spectral features (the reflectance of the aerosol band B1 and the blue band B2), green spectral features (the reflectance of the green band B3), and red-to-infrared spectral features, which included the reflectance of bands B4–B8A, B9 and B11–B12 (i.e., the red band, red-edge bands, the near-infrared band, the water vapor band, and shortwave infrared (SWIR) bands) as well as the SSC proxy. Subsequently, we derived the longitudinal distribution of feature-importance proportions for each feature category across the river reaches.

In most areas, red-to-infrared spectral features, which are closely related to SSC, play a more significant role and thus have a greater influence on the predictions (Supplementary Fig. S5). However, at specific locations, such as reaches approximately 196, 501, 565, and 756 km downstream of the Xiaolangdi Dam, blue and green spectral features exhibit relatively higher predictive importance and contribute more to depth predictions than elsewhere. While such locally enhanced sensitivity in the blue–green bands could, in principle, be influenced by sun-glint contamination, visual inspection of the Sentinel-2 imagery at these reaches did not reveal prominent sun-glint patterns (Section 3.2). Instead, the observed spatial heterogeneity in band importance is more plausibly attributed to genuine variations in the depth–reflectance–SSC relationship across the channel. Therefore, this might indicate that RivDepth captures spatially varying relationship patterns within these areas.

We further summarize the SHAP-based feature-importance ranking of the RivDepth model (Supplementary Fig. S6). The results show that B12 (2202 nm, SWIR) exhibits the largest overall mean contribution to the model output among the 13 predictors, implying the leading role of SWIR information in estimating water depth in high-SSC rivers such as the lower Yellow River. B11 (1614 nm, SWIR), B1 (443 nm, aerosol/blue), and B9 (945 nm, water vapor) are the next most influential features, highlighting the importance of SWIR, blue-band, and water-vapor information in the model predictions. Notably, the newly introduced SSC proxy ranks within the top seven, with mean absolute SHAP values (mean |SHAP values|) comparable to those of the traditional spectral bands, suggesting that this variable provides valuable information to help enhance the model's predictive capability. B5 (704 nm, vegetation red edge) and B4 (665 nm, red) also show relatively high importance. The remaining bands (e.g., B7, B8A, B6, B2, B8) exhibit lower mean |SHAP values|, indicating smaller marginal contributions under the current model configuration, while still providing complementary information.

Conclusions

This study proposes RivDepth, an intelligent water-depth retrieval model for high-SSC rivers, and demonstrates large-scale depth mapping in the lower Yellow River using Sentinel-2 imagery. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:(1)The input variables for RivDepth include the newly proposed suspended sediment concentration proxy and satellite-derived reflectance features. Based on these predictors, we implemented an AI expert module to infer candidate patterns of depth–reflectance–SSC relationship for each pixel and determine the most suitable prediction mode. The chosen mode is then used to derive the optimal river depth estimate for that pixel. The depth distributions generated by RivDepth, together with its success in extracting centerlines and thalwegs, demonstrate the effectiveness of the AI expert module over extended river reaches with high SSC. Statistical analysis shows that RivDepth outperforms four base models across multiple metrics. On the test set, it achieved an R2 of 0.896, RMSE of 0.456 m, MAE of 0.228 m, and ME of −0.020 m. Compared to base models, it delivered at least a 15.4% reduction in RMSE.(2)Larger errors are more likely to occur at steep transitions and in shallow water areas. In the case of steep transitions, these larger errors may result from the satellite's limited spatial resolution, which prevents RivDepth from capturing changes in the depth–reflectance–SSC relationship within a 10 m pixel. In shallow-water areas, larger errors may arise because the primary depth–reflectance–SSC relationship patterns learned by RivDepth become less applicable. Nevertheless, the proposed AI expert module still provides a suitable prediction mode for each pixel to a considerable degree.(3)The SHAP importance analysis of RivDepth, both along the river reaches and over the entire test set, reveals that, in depth retrieval tasks for high-SSC rivers, red-to-infrared (including SWIR) spectral features often associated with SSC variability tend to exhibit the greatest overall importance. B12 (SWIR), B11 (SWIR), B1 (aerosol/blue), B9 (water vapor), the SSC proxy, B5 (vegetation red edge), and B4 (red) rank among the most influential predictors, highlighting the contribution of spectral information and the SSC proxy to depth predictions. Furthermore, the relative contributions of predictors may vary substantially across different areas and demonstrate non-monotonic response patterns, possibly reflecting spatial heterogeneity and complexity in the key drivers of prediction performance.

This study presents a successful satellite-based retrieval of large-scale water depth distributions in high-SSC rivers (SSC exceeding 1 kg m−3). The proposed AI expert module enables intelligent, differentiated predictions for water pixels characterized by varying patterns of depth–reflectance–SSC relationship. At present, the introduction of an SSC proxy is treated as an exploratory, preliminary component following the recommendation of Kwon et al. [18]. As more accurate spatial indicators of SSC and its radiative effects become available, further improvements beyond those achieved here are expected. The workflow lays a robust methodological foundation for large-scale water-depth retrievals along the lower Yellow River and other high-SSC rivers, serving as a starting point for developing similar models in comparable environments.
