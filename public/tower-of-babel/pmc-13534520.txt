Technoeconomic competitiveness of small modular reactors across U.S. power and industrial sectors
Authors: Marisol Garrouste, Brendan Kochunas, Max Vanatta, Michael T. Craig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) are next-generation nuclear reactors proposed for United States deployment, but pathways for early scale-up remain uncertain. We present a large-scale, site-level quantitative assessment of SMR deployment potential across electricity, industrial heat, and hydrogen applications, evaluating economic performance, decarbonization potential, and sensitivity to capital-cost uncertainty. Deployment before the Inflation Reduction Act Hydrogen Production Tax Credit expires in 2033 is most attractive for industrial hydrogen production in ammonia, steel, and refining. At first-of-a-kind capital costs with the credit, 91 gigawatts electric of SMRs could be deployed at high internal rates of return, whereas dedicated power production is uneconomic. Profitable deployment is highly dependent on the credit which could expand future industrial process heat opportunities and maintain viability under doubled capital costs, consistent with recent large nuclear generation projects. These findings challenge the industry’s wholesale-electricity focus and underscore the urgency of evaluating these opportunities.

Introduction

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs), with their novel compact designs and largely centralized fabrication, are expected to play a central role in the deployment of new nuclear capacity in the United States (U.S.) energy mix1,2. While dozens of SMR designs exist, only a few are actively undergoing licensing, and none have yet been built3 for commercial applications in the United States. Ongoing licensing and deployment discussions related to SMRs focuses on their use for electricity generation4. However, given the recent economic difficulties faced by the current fleet of large Light Water Reactors (LWR)5, different applications for SMRs could prove critical to ensure their economic profitability.

To mitigate climate change, the U.S. must significantly reduce greenhouse gas emissions. The industrial and electrical sectors account for over 61% of national emissions6. Key sources of emissions within the industrial sector include refining, steel-making, and ammonia production, contributing to respectively 18%, 6%, and 3% of the total industrial emissions7,8. The adoption of hydrogen as an alternative energy carrier, when produced from clean energy such as nuclear energy, can substantially lower the emissions of these processes9,10. Emissions from fossil fuels combustion for process heat account for roughly half of total industrial emissions11. Solutions to reduce the carbon intensity of low- and medium-temperature process heat include energy efficiency, low-carbon fuels, renewable energy sources, and using low-carbon heat from small modular nuclear reactors12–14. Options for decarbonizing high-temperature process heat (above 800 °C) are limited, with hydrogen being a potential solution.

In 2022, the United States government enacted the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), marking a significant legislative milestone in support of clean energy initiatives15. This landmark act not only rejuvenated pre-existing tax incentives for clean energy development but also introduced new, substantial credits aimed specifically at fostering the rise of hydrogen16 and reinforcing the nuclear energy sector17. These financial incentives have the potential to disrupt U.S. energy markets. For SMRs in particular, optimal utilization of these newly available incentives is expected to significantly enhance their near-term economic potential, thereby catalyzing their widespread adoption and enhancing their prospective role in a low-carbon future16,18.

The current literature on SMR economic feasibility offers qualitative studies highlighting the economic and market barriers that could hinder their widespread adoption19,20. Morgan19 investigated several domestic markets for SMRs, namely industrial process heat, cogeneration of desalinated water and power, and leveraging the U.S. military for SMR deployment. Morgan19 concluded in 2018 that the nuclear industry faces commercialization challenges that may lead the U.S.to lose nuclear power, and find themselves unable to make a convincing case for developing a domestic market for SMRs. Shobeiri20 suggests SMR deployment target industrial process heat, electricity generation, and remote settings with limited infrastructure. They highlight technology choice and rapid scale-up to achieve economies of scale as essential for SMRs to succeed in these markets. Identified as a key solution for energy system decarbonization and energy security21, SMRs could serve an array of functions—industrial process heat generation, replacing aging nuclear power plants (NPPs), producing clean hydrogen22, and substituting natural gas and coal plants for clean electricity production20. Despite these potential applications, economic feasibility remains a challenge in the U.S., with studies published before the IRA enactment stressing the need for more advantageous energy market conditions, e.g., through a national commitment to decarbonize the energy system and generous subsidies directed towards the nuclear industry19.

Sector-specific quantitative studies have assessed the economic potential of SMRs. Vanatta14 assesses the economic potential of using SMRs to provide high-temperature industrial heat as a decarbonization strategy, analyzing five types of SMRs across various industrial processes in five U.S. states. It concludes that only with high electricity market years (2021), do SMRs for process heat generation become viable with cogeneration. Locatelli22 evaluates the techno-economic potential of SMRs for the cogeneration of hydrogen and electricity in the United Kingdom, concluding that, in the medium term, hydrogen from the coupling of SMRs and alkaline electrolysis with electricity cogeneration can be competitive.

To date, comprehensive quantitative assessments of the economic and environmental potential of multi-sectoral SMR deployment at the national scale in the United States remain limited. Such an analysis would provide valuable insights into the feasibility and strategic importance of SMRs within the national energy mix, helping policymakers in making informed decisions regarding infrastructure investments and legislation tailored to promote energy diversity and security. It would also allow for the evaluation of SMRs in meeting the energy demands of the power and industrial sectors while simultaneously addressing climate change concerns, as SMRs offer a lower-carbon alternative to traditional fossil fuels.

Our study introduces a United-States-wide, multi-sector deployment roadmap for SMRs by answering: What is the technoeconomic and emissions reduction potential for SMR deployment in industrial process hydrogen, industrial process heat, and electricity generation markets? To determine optimal deployment and operation of SMR systems in each market context, we build optimization models that determine the optimal number and types of SMRs to be deployed and their operation in two contexts. In industry, we optimize SMR electricity and heat output with or without electrolyzer modules to meet facility-level industrial heat and/or hydrogen demands. In wholesale power, we optimize SMRs hourly dispatch against state-wide energy prices. The industrial serving model minimizes annual costs while ensuring the industrial energy demand is served by the SMR. Our industrial facilities include all U.S. ammonia plants (34 plants with an average demand of 268 MMtH2—million metric tons of hydrogen—per year), blast furnace/basic oxygen furnace steel-making plants (9 plants with an average demand of 228,000 tH2 per year), and U.S. refineries (47 plants with an average demand of 24,000 tH2 per year), and 955 of the highest emitting industrial facilities requiring process heat. While previous studies have explored the mechanisms behind CAPEX (capital expenditure) savings in the nuclear industry23, our work instead identifies deployment pathways and economic thresholds necessary for economically viable SMR deployment in the U.S., clarifying the conditions under which SMRs can compete with both carbon-free and conventional energy sources. To address the critical barrier that investment risks pose to SMR deployment, we present our results in terms of return on investment (ROI) and conduct a sensitivity analysis to examine how variations in SMR capital costs impact economic viability. By quantifying profitability and breakeven capital costs for specific applications, we identify industrial sites where SMR deployment may yield high ROI, thereby highlighting investment opportunities that could compensate for the high investment risk with the potential for substantial returns. For hydrogen-related applications, we run our analysis with and without the Hydrogen Production Tax Credit (H2 PTC).

Results

We formulate a deployment roadmap for SMRs in the U.S. power and industrial sectors, identifying for which applications and at which deployment stage (manufacturer provided First Of A Kind (FOAK) or Nth Of A Kind (NOAK), with or without the Hydrogen Production Tax Credit) SMRs can be economically deployed. We then present additional results on the sensitivity of SMR deployment feasibility to capital cost uncertainty, their main economic driver; and present an economic comparison of decarbonization solutions for industrial applications.

Carbon emissions abatement costs

To evaluate the societal cost of decarbonizing these industrial processes via the deployment of SMRs, we compute the carbon emissions abatement cost at plants under the FOAK and NOAK scenarios as shown on Fig. 6. The abatement cost is calculated as the ratio of the costs from the new decarbonized nuclear-powered industrial process minus the avoided fossil fuel costs over the avoided emissions. The revenues from the hydrogen PTC are not included in the abatement cost since they do not constitute a net benefit to society. At the FOAK stage, most SMRs in process hydrogen for refining and steel-making achieve abatement costs less than $230 per ton of CO2 (carbon dioxide), the social cost of carbon calculated by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for the year 2030 at a 2% near term rate28. At the NOAK stage, 90% of the process hydrogen sites achieve abatement costs below this social cost limit. Sensitivity of deployment pathways to capital cost increases

All of our prior results use capital costs estimated by SMR manufacturers, which may underestimate actual capital costs realized during FOAK construction29. We quantify the sensitivity of our results to escalations in FOAK capital costs and to more limited learning in NOAK capital costs (see Supplementary Discussion). We specifically apply a 117% increase to all SMRs except the iPWR reactor, to which we apply a 156% increase. These cost increases reflect recent experiences with (large-scale) pressurized water reactors costs in the United States and general trends in cost escalations in the realized costs of electricity generators projects.

Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5 provide SMR deployment without and with the H2 PTC at escalated capital costs. Without H2 incentives, no industrial sites exist at which SMR deployment is profitable. Including the H2 PTC results in 41 profitable sites for H2 production (28 GWe), but does not yield any profitable sites for process heat provision. These results are in stark contrast to Figs. 1 and 3 above, where we find several process heat sites are profitable regardless of the H2 PTC. For profitable sites for H2 production, 79% achieve IRRs in excess of 7% (Supplementary Fig. 6), the cost of capital in the engineering and construction sector, indicating strong investment opportunities. All SMR deployments in the ammonia sector meet this 7% IRR threshold. Whether additional sites become profitable at NOAK costs that could be achieved through SMR deployment at the profitable sites at FOAK costs identified above, hinges on the H2 PTC. Without the H2 PTC, no sites become profitable at NOAK costs that are not already profitable at FOAK costs. With the H2 PTC, 10 more sites become profitable, mainly for industrial H2 (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Overall, applying FOAK capital cost escalation factors of 117% or 156% significantly reduces the potential for profitable SMR deployment across the U.S. (see Supplementary Discussion). To understand the relationship between escalating capital costs and deployment, we compute a capital cost profitability margin at each industrial facility as the ratio of the difference between the breakeven SMR capital cost and the FOAK capital costs over the FOAK capital costs. In this analysis, we use manufacturer capital costs, or those provided in Table 1. This metric represents a threshold increase in SMR capital cost beyond which the application will not be profitable anymore. A positive capital cost profitability margin indicates that the investment is profitable and will continue to be unless the SMR capital cost increases by more than the margin. Figure 7 presents the capital cost profitability margin distribution for process hydrogen applications—ammonia, refining, and steel-making—and process heat generation from SMRs. Without the H2 PTC, only process heat presents positive margins as seen with the FOAK deployment stage without PTC (Fig. 1). With the H2 PTC, each application features sites with positive margins. Process hydrogen for ammonia present the largest margins around 300%, most of the refining sites have a margin above 50%, steel presents smaller margins around 20%, and for process heat only a small proportion of sites have positive margins, mostly of less than 10%. Discussion

By evaluating and comparing SMR deployments in the potentially largest sectors for SMR growth—industry and electric power—we formulate deployment roadmaps for SMRs by highlighting key sectors for early adoption of SMRs. Under any set of assumptions where SMRs are economically viable, all SMR deployment pathways point to process heat as being economically viable, process hydrogen applications as being contingent on the H2 PTC support, and electricity production not being viable absent further cost reductions or incentives.

A roadmap for SMR deployment in the U.S. would target the most profitable industrial applications before the expiration of the H2 PTC in 2028: process hydrogen production for ammonia and refining. With the H2 PTC, a large portion of process heat demand is met by generating electrolytic hydrogen and burning it on-site, an artifact of the H2 PTC structure that prioritizes hydrogen production over thermodynamic and economic efficiency. This first deployment wave could bring SMR capital costs down by building up efficient supply chains, labor force, and construction. With reduced capital costs, steel-making and process heat would be the next deployment step, with a several hundreds of gigawatts potential. The scale of this later deployment will be highly dependent on the continued support of the H2 PTC. At FOAK capital costs levels, SMRs are not profitable when only producing electricity. After a 20% reduction in capital costs, some SMR designs are close to profitability, if SMR capital costs further decrease, SMRs could be integrated in the U.S. grid complementing intermittent renewable energy sources to decarbonize the power sector. Given their higher economic potential, shifting the focus of SMR licensing discussions from electricity to hydrogen and heat production applications will be key to enable large-scale profitable deployment of SMRs across the nation. Prototypes and demonstration projects are also critical to build up early operational experience with SMRs, demonstrate their operational and safety features, thereby building trust in the technology to increase interest from private investors. The H2 PTC has created the market conditions in which the nuclear industry will be able to overcome the economic and market challenges previously hindering deployment of new nuclear capacity19. The environmental and economic benefits of this H2 PTC-enabled deployment show the critical importance of this incentive for the nuclear industry and for the U.S. to meet its emissions targets. In light of those results, we recommend the continuation of this incentive beyond its current expiration date to support the continued deployment and use of nuclear power.

The robustness of the economic viability of SMR deployment against capital costs escalation hinges on the H2 PTC. Historical analysis of construction costs in the nuclear industry and in electricity generation projects point towards a doubling of capital costs or more for realized capital costs compared to vendor-announced costs. With the H2 PTC in place, applications revolving around hydrogen would remain profitable. With median CAPEX profitability margins at 100% or above for ammonia production and refining, most sites would still be viable if SMR CAPEX costs were to double. However, the reduced potential for profitable SMR deployment at the FOAK and at the NOAK deployment phase show the critical importance of the ability of SMR vendors to build on budget to realize the full economic potential of nuclear in the U.S.

Our analysis does not include large electrical load customers, such as data centers. Their quantitative modeling is difficult given the ubiquitousness of power purchase agreements and behind-the-meter agreements to supply their large electricity and reliability needs. The deployment of SMRs for those customers, given their long-term investment abilities and willingness to pay reliability premiums, could provide an alternate pathway to achieve NOAK capital costs for SMRs. While our overall findings regarding process heat and hydrogen remain unaffected, early SMR deployments for large load customers could accelerate cost declines and operational learning, potentially opening broader markets sooner. This segment represents a strategic opportunity for early SMR adoption and merits further qualitative exploration in future research.

Many SMR designs are developed but the nuclear industry’s history suggests that the first licensed designs will likely be deployed “en-masse”—mirroring the rapid adoption of the Pressurized Water Reactor design in the United States. Supply chains, workforce proficiency, and expertise would build up for the initial SMR designs, decreasing investment risks and streamlining supply chains. These designs would then benefit from further improvements in their techno-economics that would grant them an economic advantage for NOAK deployment stages. Our study suggests that one of those designs would, in hydrogen and heat provision and on the basis of techno-economics, be the one with the lowest starting capital costs, namely the integrated molten salt design in our analysis. However, due to cost reductions from early deployment and the further build-up of expertise around it, any SMR design with potentially less-than-optimal technological performance could become the default choice for industrial applications provided it is the first to be licensed and deployed.

In 2020, the U.S. industrial sector emitted an estimated 1360 million metric tons of CO230. Initial SMR deployment under FOAK capital costs and subsequent NOAK deployment could reduce industrial emissions by 8% or 14% whether the support from the H2 PTC is discontinued or not. However, the CO2 abatement cost estimates calculated for SMRs for industrial applications show that the H2 PTC makes it economically viable to deploy SMRs at costs substantially higher than those suggested by the EPA social cost of carbon. In the race to achieve Net-Zero by 2050, the H2 PTC could enable the decarbonization of high costs emissions in hard-to-abate sectors through the deployment of SMRs.

To understand the comparative advantage of SMRs, we conduct a preliminary assessment comparing using nuclear versus other clean energy sources for process hydrogen production (see Supplementary Discussion). We perform this analysis only for sites we identified as profitable for SMR deployment (Figs. 1, 2, 3, and 4). We specifically quantify a breakeven price of a clean energy solution at which point the price of the fossil fuel used in the incumbent process at which the cost of the fossil and clean pathways are equal. With the H2 PTC (Supplementary Fig. 16), breakeven prices for SMR deployment are generally negative while they are positive for alternative clean energy solutions like wind or the use of natural gas with Carbon Capture Utilization and Storage (CCUS). Thus, with the support of the H2 PTC, SMR deployment is more profitable than the use of alternative clean energy sources. Without the H2 PTC, the use of natural gas with CCUS becomes more competitive than SMR deployment at FOAK capital costs. At NOAK costs, SMRs could compete with CCUS for process hydrogen for ammonia production. For process heat generation we compare the cost of SMR deployment to the cost of capturing and storing carbon from natural gas-powered operations (Supplementary Fig. 17). In this case, SMR deployment is more profitable than natural gas with CCUS across all process heat scenarios. We note that, with the H2 PTC, SMRs are profitable at sites where the cost of natural gas with CCUS is low while without the H2 PTC, profitable sites present higher overall costs. Importantly, our analysis considers only cost-based comparisons and does not account for other critical non-cost constraints on natural gas technologies with CCUS, such as regional NG access, CO2 transportation costs, and storage availability. Future research should address these factors on a site-specific basis.

Future research should also consider the value of repurposing existing infrastructure (including reactor, turbine, and electric plant equipment) from conventional power plants for SMR deployment to reduce capital costs, potentially making more potential SMR sites profitable. Recent research found most sites with retired or operating coal plants have the basic characteristics required for hosting an SMR, and reusing infrastructure at those plants could reduce SMR capital costs by 15–35%, roughly in line with our NOAK cost scenario31. Reductions in capital costs could be achieved through mass production and deployment and through siting multiple units at a single location26. Capital cost reductions are only coarsely examined in our analysis. Future work should explore conditions for SMRs cost reduction mechanisms and consider the impact of declining financial risks and construction uncertainties on the cost of capital, which could further reinforce the argument for early SMR deployment support. Such reductions could position SMRs to eventually replace aging Generation III nuclear power plants as baseload providers. This study focused on site-specific demand and only accounted for high emitting EPA-reportable facilities. As hydrogen becomes a more prevalent fuel, the development of transportation infrastructure across the United States could lead to reduced transportation costs, making the model of centralized production more economically attractive for serving smaller-scale industrial operations. This centralized approach could be explored as an alternative viable pathway towards achieving deeper decarbonization of the industrial sector. Despite these future opportunities for research, our analysis indicates several deployment pathways for SMRs exist, with or without the hydrogen PTC, across the industrial sector via process heat and hydrogen provision.

Methods

Techno-economic framework for SMR deployment and operation, industrial process hydrogen and heat applications

To assess the techno-economic potential of SMRs and SMRs coupled with H2 production, we design a cost-minimizing mixed integer linear program that optimizes on a per-site basis the number and types of SMRs and hydrogen production modules to be deployed to satisfy the energy demands of the industrial process. Our model differentiates between five types of SMRs, which vary based on their costs, size, and efficiency. The revenues from avoided fossil fuel costs are not included in our objective function, which enables the calculation of breakeven fossil fuel prices and the comparison of SMR-H2 systems against renewable energy sources and incumbent fossil fuel. The revenues from the H2 PTC are calculated after the optimization to be able to evaluate the profitability of the system without this incentive and understand how close to profitability those deployments may be. For each facility, our model’s objective function is to minimize annualized costs. Our model constraints enforce physical and technological limits on SMR and H2 deployment and operation at industrial sites. Only one type of SMR and H2 technology can be deployed at each site. Physical limits are enforced by including maximum capacity and energy balance constraints. For process heat generation, the demand the SMR must serve corresponds to the complete displacement of the natural gas previously consumed for heat generation. At each facility, we combine heat loads in the following temperature ranges that correspond to the steam temperature of our studied SMR designs: 0–302 °C, 302–630 °C, 630–700 °C, 700–750 °C, 750–950 °C and beyond 950 °C. Each temperature band can only be served by an SMR design with a steam temperature superior or equal to the upper range of the band. For the highest temperature band, hydrogen must be produced and burned to meet the heat demand. Our model can also produce and use hydrogen to partly or totally serve lower temperature heat demand if more economical than direct process heat provision. For process hydrogen generation, the SMR is the only source of energy: it provides heat, and electricity for hydrogen production, as well as for the industrial process ultimately consuming the hydrogen if necessary. In the case where the nuclear capacity exceeds the demand, we consider cogeneration of electricity as an additional source of revenues. Revenues from sale of electricity on wholesale markets are calculated given surplus capacity and average state-level wholesale prices.

Techno-economic framework for SMR deployment and operation, wholesale electricity generation

To evaluate the economic potential of dedicated electricity production from SMRs, we build a cost-minimizing linear program to optimize SMR dispatch in wholesale electricity markets. We specifically optimize hourly electricity generation by each SMR design for a year using state-level wholesale prices for 2024 from Cambium25. The model’s objective function is the annualized net revenues computed as the electricity sales minus annualized capital and operational costs. The constraints on SMR operations include maximum power output, minimum stable load, and ramping rates (for SMR parameters, see Table 1). The details of the formulation for this price taker model are given in the Supplementary Methods section. The model is formulated in Pyomo32 and solved using the IBM CPLEX solver33.

Across wholesale electricity markets and industrial facilities, our analysis captures three important potential sources of revenues for SMRs: sales of process heat or hydrogen to industrial facilities, and sales of electricity into competitive wholesale markets (including by taking advantage of short-term price spikes). In the context of competitive wholesale markets, we do not capture all potential revenue sources, e.g., from ancillary service and capacity markets. But ancillary service and capacity payments are small relative to wholesale energy payments for most electricity generators, particularly those with high capacity factors like SMRs. Additionally, providing regulation reserves, the most lucrative ancillary service product, requires significant operational flexibility, which would impose tradeoffs with additional maintenance costs, the focus of other ongoing research34. Thus, we do not expect their omission to significantly affect our results. Additionally, for simplicity and model tractability across our scenarios and facilities, our cost equation for industrial heat and hydrogen provision does not account for operation-dependent VOM costs, i.e., it assumes a capacity factor of 1. In reality, SMRs will have lower capacity factors, biasing our cost estimates upwards. However, VOM costs are small relative to capital costs, and capacity factors will be high (close to 1), so this simplification has a minimal (less than 1%) effect on our costs.

Unless explicitly stated, all costs reported are in 2020 USD. All data used in this analysis is from14,25,35–42.

Process hydrogen energy demand

Decarbonizing industrial sectors via the deployment of SMRs will require different levels of plant and process retrofitting. For ammonia, refining, and steel-making, we describe in the SI the low-carbon process relying on nuclear energy from SMRs that could replace the conventional fossil-fuel based process for those applications. In these contexts, hydrogen is ’process hydrogen’, it used as an inflow for the industrial process. To estimate facility-level process hydrogen demand, we use published plant capacities (ammonia44, steel-making37, and refining38) to compute the hydrogen demand from the new low-carbon nuclear powered pathways. Hydrogen demand ranges from 72.1 to 477.2 MMtH2 per year for steel-making plants, from 12.6 to 1986 MMtH2 per year for ammonia plants and from 0.9 to 108 MMtH2 for refineries.

Process heat energy demand

We use industrial process heat demand data based on emission reporting to the EPA39, selecting the most recent data (2015) with natural gas reported as fuel type and fuel type blends that do not include recycled fuels. Our final dataset contains 955 facilities, representing 86.7 GW of thermal demand and 123.9 MMtCO2 of emissions per year. These facilities span plastics and resin, chemical (petro-, organic and inorganic), wet corn milling, lime, and paper and pulp milling industries. Assuming a constant heat load for each process, we convert the yearly demand into hourly demand. At each facility, we combine the units with temperatures in pre-defined batches corresponding to the ranges between the outlet temperatures of the SMR design (see Table 1) and calculate a batch temperature as a weighted average of the temperatures and the heat demand. We constrain the model such that an SMR design can only directly serve batch temperatures at or below its steam temperature. Any remaining heat must then be served by burning hydrogen. When the demand is directly served by the SMR, we use the SMR thermal efficiency to compute the surplus capacity. This simplification may underestimate surplus capacity for low-grade heat demand, for which the heat would not be extracted upstream in the secondary cycles. We use a 10% distribution factor to account for the distribution losses from a central heat production location. When hydrogen is burned for heat generation, the hydrogen demand is calculated using the higher heating value of hydrogen and the available heat fraction which correlates the required temperature required and the fuel efficiency45. Conversion costs for heat loads served via hydrogen are incurred since a dedicated hydrogen furnace is required (see Supplementary Methods). The heat load can be served by direct heat from SMRs or a mix of direct heat and burning hydrogen produced by the SMR.

Electricity prices

We use state-level projected electricity prices from Cambium for 202425. These hourly state-level prices projections are calculated from Energy Information Administration energy markets projections that are the same as used for the rest of our analysis42.

Scenarios and government incentives

Significant uncertainty surrounds future SMR capital costs, given that no SMRs have yet to be commercially deployed. Capital costs of large LWRs have increased with further deployment for a range of reasons including labor, regulation, construction, and other indirect costs46. In addition to the escalation of these nth-of-a-kind costs, SMRs are likely to experience FOAK cost escalations, which are common for all early stage (or “pioneer”) plant designs. Merrow et al.29 estimate pioneer plants in the research and development stage (where SMRs would be categorized) are likely to only estimate FOAK costs to be 49% of the actual costs (a 104% cost escalation). Given these values, we test the sensitivity of our results to a 117% FOAK capital cost increase relative to vendor expectations (Table 1) for HTGR, micro-reactor, iMSR, and PBR-HTGR designs, and a 156% cost increase for the iPWR design (see Supplementary Discussion).

To take into account potential SMR capital cost reductions that could result from increased building and supply chain efficiency after the first wave of SMR deployment, we analyze nth-of-a-kind capital cost scenarios. For NOAK costs, we use a 20% cost reduction compared to FOAK costs (Table 1) based on the cost reduction achieved after the deployment of at least four units with a learning rate of 13% calculated from26 (36% average cost reduction from FOAK to 10th-of-a-kind for small reactors). A 20% cost reduction also corresponds to a historical asymptotic trend for a French and Korean design27, as well as the total cost savings stemming from reduced construction time as calculated in a report on modular construction47. For the increased capital costs sensitivity study, we use a reduced learning rate of 7.5% with an asymptotic cost decrease reached after 5 units deployed (see Supplementary Discussion). We use this lumped decrease in capital costs since detailed analysis of the CAPEX savings from learning and multi-unit co-location are the topic of dedicated papers48. Despite these reasons for using a 20% NOAK cost reduction, significant uncertainty surrounds what future NOAK cost reductions will actually be realized. These uncertainties stem from cost reductions achieved per unit deployed (i.e., realized learning curves), the number of units that will ultimately be deployed, on-site learning, and manufacturing learning.
