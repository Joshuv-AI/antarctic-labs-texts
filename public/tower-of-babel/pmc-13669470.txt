Amelioration of chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and gut microbiota homeostasis by a combination of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Bacillus in obese type 2 diabetic mice
Authors: Sisi Fu, Fei Zhou, Liya Gong, Aili Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESTo examine the effects of a combination of live Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Bacillus (CBLEB) on glucose/lipid metabolism, inflammation, gut microbiota, and related mechanisms in obese type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) mice.MATERIALS/METHODSObese T2DM mice were established by feeding mice a high-fat diet for four weeks, followed by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (100 mg/kg). The mice were then divided randomly into 5 groups (n = 8/group): normal control (NC), T2DM model, CBLEB (585 mg/kg/d, by gavage), dimethylbiguanide (DMBG, 200 mg/kg/d, by gavage), and CBLEB+DMBG combination. All treatments were administered daily for four weeks. The body weight and fasting blood glucose were monitored weekly. An oral glucose tolerance test was performed to assess the glucose tolerance. The serum levels of lipids (triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol), insulin, and inflammatory factors (tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α, interleukin [IL]-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and lipopolysaccharide) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Pancreatic IL-6 and TNF-α expression was detected by immunohistochemistry. The histopathological changes in the pancreas, colon, and adipose tissues were evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin staining. The protein expression of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1)/p-IRS-1 (Ser307), Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/MyD88, tight junction proteins (zonula occludens protein 1 [ZO-1]/claudin 1 [CLDN1]/occludin), and B-cell lymphoma/leukemia-2 (Bcl-2)-associated X protein A (BAX)/Bcl-2 was analyzed by Western blot. The gut microbiota composition was profiled by 16S rRNA gene sequencing.RESULTSCBLEB significantly improved the weight gain, hyperglycemia, insulin resistance (IR), dyslipidemia, reduced adipocyte size, suppressed systemic inflammation, and alleviated pancreatic and colonic damage in T2DM mice. The combination of CBLEB and DMBG showed the most pronounced improvements in metabolic and inflammatory parameters. Western blot showed that CBLEB inhibited TLR4/MyD88 signaling, enhanced insulin signaling (IRS-1/p-IRS-1), repaired the intestinal barrier (ZO-1/CLDN1/occludin), and regulated the apoptotic balance (BAX/Bcl-2). Gut microbiota analysis suggested that CBLEB remodeled the gut microbiota, with a functional prediction suggesting shifts in the metabolic pathways and a decrease in the abundance of Escherichia spp.CONCLUSIONCBLEB ameliorated systemic inflammation and IR in obese T2DM mice through gut microbiota modulation, intestinal barrier restoration, and suppression of inflammatory pathways.

INTRODUCTION

The key pathological features of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are the progressive deterioration of insulin resistance (IR) and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction [1]. As a primary driver, IR manifests as diminished responsiveness of insulin target tissues to physiological insulin concentrations [2]. Modern epidemiological studies revealed a significant association between the surging prevalence of T2DM and the global obesity epidemic [3]. Clinical evidence further showed that weight-loss interventions during the early disease stages can effectively reverse hyperglycemia [4]. Previous studies have reported that high-fat and high-sugar diets can directly induce IR and damage/apoptosis in β-cells [5]. Furthermore, the differential susceptibility of mice with distinct genetic backgrounds to obesity-induced diabetes [6] underscores the complex interplay between obesity and metabolic dysregulation. Although obesity does not invariably lead to IR, large-scale population studies indicate a positive correlation between the fat-to-muscle mass ratio and T2DM risk [7], establishing obesity as a fundamental risk factor for the development of IR [8].

The precise mechanisms by which obesity promotes T2DM are incompletely understood, with chronic systemic inflammation recognized as a critical component [9]. High-fat-diet-fed mouse models exhibit progressive obesity, accompanied by the upregulated expression of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor [TNF]-α, interleukin [IL]-1β) in adipose tissue, which gradually extends to multiple organs such as the colon and liver [101112]. Studies have shown that the overexpression of inflammatory cytokines in obese individuals closely correlates with the severity of IR, while neutralizing TNF-α significantly improves insulin sensitivity in animal models [13]. This evidence suggests that adipose tissue inflammation plays a pivotal role in driving systemic metabolic dysregulation by activating innate immune pathways such as TLR4.

In recent years, the gut microbiota has attracted considerable attention for its function as a “metabolic–immunological bridge” [14]. Dysbiosis can compromise the intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating endotoxin translocation and triggering chronic inflammation, exacerbating obesity-associated IR [15]. Research suggests that high-fat diets and microbial dysbiosis synergistically promote metabolic imbalance. By contrast, probiotic interventions significantly improve glycemic control and reduce inflammation in diabetic models [16]. The bifidobacterium tetravaccine live combined preparation (CBLEB, containing Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus faecalis, and Bacillus) is used widely in China to modulate the gut microecology. CBLEB had multi-organ protective potential by increasing the mucus layer thickness and attenuating tissue inflammation [1718]. Nevertheless, further studies will be needed to determine if this preparation can ameliorate IR and organ damage in obese T2DM by remodeling the “microbiota-gut-metabolism axis” and its underlying mechanisms.

This study examined the effects of CBLEB intervention on glucose/lipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, and target organ damage in an obese T2DM model. In particular, this study focused on its regulatory effects on gut microbiota homeostasis, inflammation-associated pathways, and intestinal barrier function, providing a theoretical basis for probiotic therapy in metabolic diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (SPF grade, aged 5–6 weeks) were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (License No.: SCXK (Hu) 2022-0004; Shanghai, China) and housed in barrier facilities at Hangzhou Hanter Youjian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License No.: SYXK (Zhe) 2024-0003; Hangzhou, China). The animals were maintained under controlled conditions: temperature at 22 ± 2°C, relative humidity between 50% and 60%, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and an air exchange rate of 15–20 times per hour. Food and water were provided ad libitum, with feed replenished daily.

Type 2 diabetes mouse model establishment and grouping

After one week of acclimatization, 40 male C57BL/6J mice were allocated randomly into a normal control group (NC, n = 8) fed standard chow and a HFD group (n = 32) fed a HFD for 4 weeks (HFD composition: 7% sugar, 10% lard, 5% casein, 2% fish meal, 2% maltodextrin, 0.1% methionine, and 74% basal diet). At the end of this 4-week HFD feeding period, the body weight was recorded to confirm obesity (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subsequently, the mice in the HFD group received a single intraperitoneal injection of STZ (100 mg/kg, dissolved in 50 mM citrate buffer) to establish the T2DM model. This single high-dose STZ regimen combined with HFD is a well-established approach to induce an obese T2DM-like phenotype in rodents [19]. The NC group received an equivalent volume of citrate buffer. Five days post-injection, the 6-h fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured; mice with FBG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L were deemed successful T2DM models [2021].

Successful T2DM model mice were randomly assigned to 4 groups (n = 8/group): T2DM model group (T2DM), CBLEB intervention group (T2DM+CBLEB), dimethylbiguanide (DMBG and metformin) monotherapy group (T2DM+DMBG, serving as a positive control), and CBLEB and dimethylbiguanide combination group (T2DM+CBLEB+DMBG, to assess the potential synergistic effects). The NC group (n = 8) served as the healthy control. All groups received daily oral gavage interventions for four weeks. The NC and T2DM groups received equivalent volumes of sterile saline (0.2 mL/10 g body weight). The T2DM+CBLEB group received CBLEB suspension (prepared in saline, 585 mg/kg dose, equivalent to the human clinical dose based on body surface area conversion). The T2DM+DMBG group received DMBG (200 mg/kg dissolved in sterile saline, 0.1 mL/10 g). The T2DM+CBLEB+DMBG group received equivalent doses of the CBLEB and DMBG concurrently.

Body weight and blood glucose level monitoring

The mouse body weight was recorded weekly. After fasting, tail vein blood samples were collected, and the blood glucose levels were measured with a Roche glucometer (Model 6870279001; Roche Life Science, Mannheim, Germany).

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

After an overnight fast, mice received an oral glucose solution (1 g/kg). Tail vein blood was sampled at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-administration for blood glucose measurements using a glucometer. The area under the curve (AUC) for blood glucose was calculated using the trapezoidal rule: AUC = 0.25 × (BG0 + BG15)/2 + 0.25 × (BG15 + BG30)/2 + 0.5 × (BG30 + BG60)/2 + 0.5 × (BG60 + BG90)/2 + 0.5 × (BG90 + BG120)/2. BGx represents the blood glucose value (unit: mmol/L) at the corresponding time point.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The mouse serum samples were allowed to clot naturally at room temperature for 20 min, then centrifuged at 3,500 r/min for 15 min. The supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis. Detection was performed using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s protocols. All assays were replicated independently 8 times. The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated: HOMA-IR = Fasting Blood Gglucose × Fasting Insulin/22.5.

Serum2 lipid profile detection

Blood was collected by aortic puncture. The serum was separated through centrifugation (400 × g, 20 min, 4°C) and analyzed for lipid profile components: triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) using a Hitachi 3110 automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Fresh tissues were fixed in fixative for 24 h, trimmed, placed in dehydration cassettes, and sequentially processed through a graded series of alcohols, xylene, and paraffin impregnation. After paraffin embedding, sections (4 μm) were floated in warm water at 40°C, mounted on slides, and baked at 60°C. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded series of alcohol, stained with hematoxylin for nuclei (3–5 min), differentiated and blued, counterstained with eosin for cytoplasm (5 min), dehydrated through a graded series of alcohol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with neutral balsam. A microscopic examination revealed blue nuclei and red cytoplasm. The adipocyte morphology was quantified using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), and the area of adipocytes was measured in three fields per sample and averaged.

The colonic histopathology was assessed quantitatively. The H&E-stained sections were evaluated blindly by 2 independent pathologists using a scoring system [22]. Three parameters were assessed: (1) Extent of inflammation (0: None; 1: Mild inflammation; 2: Moderate inflammation; 3: Severe inflammation); (2) Inflammatory infiltration area (0: None; 1: Mucosal layer; 2: Submucous layer; 3: Muscular layer); (3) Crypt damage (0: None; 1: 0–1/3 crypt deformated or destroyed; 2: 1/3–2/3 crypt deformated or destroyed; 3: The crypt was almost lost and only the epithelium was retained; 4: The crypt and epithelium were destroyed). The total histology score was calculated as the sum of the 3 parameters. For each mouse (n = 3 per group), 5 random fields (200× magnification) were scored, and the average was used for statistical analysis.

Western blotting

The tissue samples were pulverized in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in pre-cooled lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged (12,000 g, 5 min, 4°C) after 30 min incubation on ice. The supernatant was collected for protein quantification (BCA method). The samples were then mixed with a loading buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol (4:1 ratio), denatured by boiling for 5 min, and stored at −20°C. The proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using 10% separating and 5% stacking gels: electrophoresis was initiated at 80 V, increased to 120 V upon entry into the separating gel, and terminated when the dye front reached a ~2/3 of the separating gel length. The proteins were then wet-transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk (2 h), washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween® 20 (TBST), and incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies (diluted as per manufacturer’s instructions). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature, 1.5 h), washed again with TBST, and visualized using ECL chemiluminescence. The target band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software; the results were averaged from three independent replicates per sample.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

After decalcification and embedding, 3–5 μm tissue sections were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides and baked (65°C, 2 h). The sections underwent deparaffinization in xylene, graded alcohol rehydration, and water rinsing. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2 (room temperature, 10 min), followed by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes. Antigen retrieval used Tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid buffer (pH 9.0, boiling 15 min + 15 min maintenance, natural cooling) with subsequent PBS rinses. After 5% bovine serum albumin blocking (20 min, room temperature), the primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C. The slides were then equilibrated to room temperature, rinsed with PBS, incubated with HRP-secondary antibodies (37°C, 30 min), and washed again. The 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate produced brown–yellow signals (microscopically monitored) that were stopped by water rinsing. Counterstaining with hematoxylin included acid alcohol differentiation and water bluing. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with neutral balsam. Cytoplasmic/membrane brown–yellow granules were deemed positive. The images were captured using a Nikon E100 microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and semi-quantitative analysis (Image-Pro Plus 6.0; Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA) was used to calculate the integrated optical density/area ratios from 200× fields.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Fecal samples from the NC, T2DM, and T2DM+CBLEB groups were collected for 16S rDNA sequencing to analyze the changes in the gut microbiota. Fresh feces (0.5 g) were collected aseptically using a sterile spoon and immediately transferred into sterile cryovials for storage at −80°C until shipment to Jiyin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) for sequencing.

Microbiota statistical analysis: Differentially abundant taxa between the groups were identified using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a threshold logarithmic linear discriminant analysis score > 2.0 and P < 0.05 [23]. Pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed for specific comparisons of the Escherichia spp. abundance. The Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test was applied to compare the relative abundances across multiple groups. The results are presented as boxplots showing the median, interquartile range, and individual data points. The statistical significance was set to P < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normally distributed data with homogeneous variance across groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s test for post-hoc comparisons. A Dunnett’s T3 test was applied for normally distributed data exhibiting unequal variances. Non-normally distributed data were assessed using a Kruskal–Wallis H test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted for all tests. The results are expressed as the mean ± SD, and the statistical significance is defined as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

CBLEB modulates glucose and lipid metabolic homeostasis in obese T2DM mice

Before diabetes induction after four weeks of HFD feeding, the HFD-fed mice exhibited significantly higher body weight than the NC mice (Supplementary Fig. 1), confirming successful diet-induced obesity. The impact of CBLEB on the metabolic phenotype of T2DM mice was evaluated by monitoring the dynamic changes in body weight, blood glucose levels, insulin sensitivity, serum lipid profiles, and adipose tissue morphology. The results showed that compared to the NC group, body weight in the T2DM group decreased significantly during weeks 7–9. Compared to the T2DM group, the body weight increased significantly in the T2DM+CBLEB and T2DM+DMBG groups at week 9, and increased significantly in the T2DM+CBLEB+DMBG group during weeks 7–9 (Fig. 1A). Blood glucose monitoring revealed significantly higher levels in the T2DM group from weeks 5–9 than in the NC group. The T2DM+CBLEB group showed significantly lower blood glucose levels at weeks 7 and 8 than the T2DM group. Furthermore, the T2DM+DMBG and T2DM+CBLEB+DMBG groups showed significant decreases in blood glucose levels during weeks 7–9 (Fig. 1B). The OGTT assay confirmed significantly elevated blood glucose levels at all time points and AUC values in the T2DM group than in the NC group. All treatment groups showed significantly lower blood glucose levels at all time points and AUC values than the T2DM group (Fig. 1C and D). The ELISA results indicated that compared to the NC group, the fasting insulin levels and HOMA-IR increased significantly in the T2DM group. The fasting insulin levels and HOMA-IR were significantly lower in all treatment groups than in the T2DM group (Fig. 1E). Concurrently, TG, TC, and LDL-C levels were significantly higher in the T2DM group than in the NC group. Compared to the T2DM group, all treatment groups showed significant decreases in TG, TC, and LDL-C levels. In addition, the HDL-C levels were significantly higher in the T2DM+CBLEB+DMBG group than in the NC group (Fig. 1F). H&E staining of adipose tissues revealed a significant increase in the adipocyte diameter in the T2DM group compared to the NC group. A significant decrease in adipocyte diameter was observed in all treatment groups compared with the T2DM group (Fig. 1G).

Collectively, these results show that CBLEB intervention significantly improved hyperglycemia, IR, dyslipidemia, and adipose tissue hypertrophy.

CBLEB alleviates systemic and tissue inflammation in obese T2DM mice

Given the critical role of chronic inflammation in T2DM pathogenesis, this study assessed the key inflammatory cytokine levels in serum and pancreatic tissue to evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of CBLEB. ELISA showed that the serum levels of LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly higher in the T2DM group than in the NC group, whereas the IL-10 levels were significantly lower. Compared to the T2DM group, all treatment groups showed significantly lower serum levels of LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, along with significantly higher IL-10 levels than the NC group (Fig. 2A). The IHC assay further confirmed that the average optical density of IL-6 and TNF-α in pancreatic tissue was significantly higher in the T2DM group than the NC group, while all treatment groups showed significantly lower expression (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, H&E staining was performed on pancreatic and colon tissues to investigate the protective effects of CBLEB against diabetes-related target organ damage (Fig. 2C and D). The results showed that the pancreatic tissue in the NC group was essentially normal, with well-defined islet boundaries and orderly cell arrangement. The pancreatic tissue in the T2DM group showed severe damage, characterized by indistinct islet boundaries and significant inflammatory cell infiltration. Compared with the T2DM group, pancreatic tissue damage was markedly alleviated in all treatment groups, with an improved islet morphology and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, the colon tissue in the NC group was essentially normal with abundant goblet cells. The T2DM group showed severe colon tissue damage, featuring the extensive loss of goblet cells and crypts, along with significant inflammatory cell infiltration in the mucosa and submucosa. Significant amelioration of colon tissue damage was observed in all treatment groups compared with the T2DM group (Fig. 2D). Quantitative histological scoring confirmed these observations. The total histology score was significantly lower in the T2DM+CBLEB, T2DM+DMBG, and T2DM+CBLEB+DMBG groups than in the T2DM group, with the combination group showing the greatest improvement (Supplementary Fig. 2).

CBLEB regulates key signaling pathways and barrier function in obese T2DM mice

The underlying molecular mechanisms by which CBLEB improves metabolism and inflammation were examined using Western blotting to assess protein expression related to the insulin signaling pathway (p-IRS-1 [Ser307]/IRS-1), inflammatory pathway (TNF-α/TLR4/MyD88), intestinal barrier function (ZO-1/CLDN1/occludin), and cell apoptosis (BAX/Bcl-2). Compared to the NC group, the protein expression levels of TNF-α, p-IRS-1 (Ser307)/IRS-1, TLR4, and MyD88 in adipose tissue were significantly upregulated in the T2DM group. Compared to the T2DM group, the expression levels of these proteins were significantly downregulated in the adipose tissue of all treatment groups, suggesting that CBLEB alleviates IR by reducing the inhibitory serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 (Fig. 3A). Regarding the intestinal barrier function, the protein expression levels of ZO-1, CLDN1, and occludin in the colon tissue were significantly lower in the T2DM group than in the NC group. The expression levels of these tight junction proteins were significantly upregulated in the colon tissue across all treatment groups relative to the T2DM group (Fig. 3B). Further analysis revealed significantly higher BAX expression and significantly lower Bcl-2 expression in the pancreatic tissue of the T2DM group than the NC group. All treatment groups showed significantly lower BAX expression and significantly higher Bcl-2 expression in pancreatic tissue than in the T2DM group (Fig. 3C).

These molecular-level findings suggest that CBLEB exerts its therapeutic benefits in obese T2DM potentially through multiple pathways: suppressing TLR4/MyD88-mediated inflammatory signaling, enhancing the phosphorylation and signal transduction of insulin receptor substrates, restoring the intestinal tight junction barrier integrity, and modulating the Bcl-2/BAX ratio to inhibit apoptosis.

CBLEB ameliorates gut microbiota homeostasis in obese T2DM mice

The impact of CBLEB on the gut microbiota was investigated further by conducting 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis on mouse fecal samples from the NC, T2DM, and T2DM+CBLEB groups (microbiome analysis was not performed for the DMBG monotherapy or combination groups). Venn diagram analysis showed that 472 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were shared among all three groups, indicating a stable core microbiome that persists despite disease and intervention. In particular, the NC group contained 58 unique OTUs, whereas the T2DM group had only nine unique OTUs, reflecting a marked loss of microbial diversity—a hallmark of dysbiosis—induced by the diabetic state. The T2DM+CBLEB group exhibited only four unique OTUs, suggesting that while the CBLEB treatment remodeled the microbiota, it did not fully restore the original diversity, consistent with a partial rather than complete recovery (Fig. 4A). Principal coordinates analysis based on Bray–Curtis distance matrices was used to assess the differences in overall microbial community structure. The clear separation between the NC and T2DM groups confirms that T2DM induces a profound shift in microbial community composition. Importantly, the T2DM+CBLEB group clustered separately from the T2DM group and shifted closer to the NC group. Therefore, CBLEB intervention partially restored the microbial community structure toward a healthy state. The relatively tight clustering within each group suggests a consistency of the treatment effects among individual mice (Fig. 4B). At the genus level, LEfSe analysis showed significant compositional shifts associated with T2DM and CBLEB treatment. Compared to the NC group, the T2DM group showed the enrichment of several taxa, including Escherichia spp. (Supplementary Fig. 3), a genus comprising potential pathobionts capable of producing LPS and triggering inflammation through TLR4 signaling. The T2DM+CBLEB group was compared with the NC group using LEfSe analysis. The abundance of Escherichia spp. was no longer significantly different (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting a partial normalization of the microbiota. A direct comparison between the T2DM and T2DM+CBLEB groups confirmed that CBLEB intervention significantly reduced the abundance of Escherichia spp. (Supplementary Fig. 5). Quantitative analysis of the relative abundance further corroborated these findings. The relative abundance of Escherichia coli was significantly higher in the T2DM group than in the NC group. By contrast, the relative abundance in the T2DM+CBLEB group was significantly lower than that in the T2DM group (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, the T2DM+CBLEB group exhibited remodeling of beneficial bacterial populations. The abundance of Roseburia, a well-known butyrate-producing genus, was elevated. Butyrate plays a critical role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating the tight junction proteins (ZO-1, CLDN1, and occludin), exerting anti-inflammatory effects, which align with the improved barrier function and reduced systemic inflammation observed in CBLEB-treated mice. Furthermore, the abundances of Prevotella and Lactobacillus, which were altered in the T2DM group, showed partial reversal toward the NC levels after the CBLEB treatment (Fig. 4C), suggesting a restoration of the microbial balance. Nevertheless, the precise functional roles of these genera in the context of T2DM warrant further investigation. Functional prediction based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis provided insights into the potential metabolic consequences of the observed taxonomic shifts. The T2DM group showed the enrichment of pathways associated with pathogenesis, such as “bacterial invasion of epithelial cells” and “Shigella infection,” consistent with the increased abundance of potential pathobionts like Escherichia spp. In contrast, the T2DM+CBLEB group showed greater enrichment of metabolic pathways beneficial to the host, including “insulin signaling pathway” and “carbohydrate metabolism” (Fig. 4D). These inferred functional changes are in line with the improved metabolic phenotype (enhanced glucose tolerance and reduced IR) and reduced inflammation observed in CBLEB-treated mice, suggesting that the probiotic-mediated microbial remodeling may contribute to the restoration of host metabolic homeostasis. Collectively, these results show that CBLEB intervention ameliorates T2DM-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis by reducing potential pathobionts (Escherichia spp.), enriching beneficial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria (Roseburia), and shifting the predicted metabolic functions of the microbiota from pathogenic toward host-beneficial pathways. These changes provide an ecological basis for the improved intestinal barrier function, reduced inflammation, and enhanced insulin sensitivity observed in CBLEB-treated obese T2DM mice.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed the synergistic therapeutic mechanism of CBLEB intervention in obese T2DM from 4 aspects: metabolic phenotype, inflammatory status, molecular pathways, and microbial ecology. At the levels of glucose and lipid metabolism, CBLEB effectively improved the early pathological weight loss in T2DM mice and significantly ameliorated hyperglycemia by improving insulin signaling. This effect was validated in the oral glucose tolerance test, where the significant reduction in the area under the blood glucose curve at various time points post-glucose administration suggested that CBLEB enhanced the capacity of the body for glucose disposal. Concurrently, CBLEB remodeled lipid metabolism. A decrease in LDL-C levels and an increase in HDL-C levels were observed, resulting in a favorable lipid profile. LDL-C primarily transports cholesterol to the peripheral tissues, and excess LDL-C is prone to promote atherosclerosis [2425]. In contrast, HDL-C is responsible for the reverse transport of peripheral cholesterol back to the liver for metabolism, exerting cardiovascular protective effects [2627]. Critically, the significant decrease in adipocyte diameter, as revealed by H&E staining, indicates more than just reduced lipid storage. It reflects the fundamental alleviation of pathological adipocyte hypertrophy and fat accumulation. Furthermore, this normalization of the adipocyte size strongly suggests a concurrent restoration of the adipose tissue endocrine function. These histological changes provide a direct structural and functional basis for the enhanced systemic insulin sensitivity observed in the treatment groups, linking tissue-level pathology to metabolic improvement [28].

Regarding inflammatory regulation, CBLEB systemically modulated chronic inflammation, a key pathological feature of T2DM [29]. The decrease in serum LPS levels indicated the suppression of gut-derived endotoxemia [3031]. Furthermore, key pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) showed a synchronous downward trend. This contrasted sharply with the significant elevation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, providing robust evidence for systemic inflammation resolution. TNF-α plays a crucial role in mediating IR [3233] and apoptosis [3435]. This systemic anti-inflammatory effect extended to the organ level. The repair of the islet structure and the reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration within pancreatic tissue were linked directly to improved insulin secretion. The regeneration of colonic mucosal goblet cells and restoration of crypt architecture served as important morphological markers for the reconstruction of intestinal physical barrier function.

Molecular mechanistic studies provided deeper explanations for the observed phenotypes. In adipose tissue, CBLEB suppressed the TLR4 and its downstream key adaptor protein, the MyD88-mediated signaling pathway. TLR4 recognizes the pathogen-associated molecular patterns, such as LPS, to initiate inflammation, while MyD88 mediates inflammatory signal transduction, effectively blocking the cascade amplification of metabolic inflammation [36]. Simultaneously, a reduction in the inhibitory phosphorylation of IRS-1 at p-IRS-1 (Ser307) was observed. The aberrant serine phosphorylation of IRS-1 is a critical event that impairs insulin signaling and contributes to IR [37]. The reduction in its phosphorylation level improved IR at the source of signal transduction [38]. In the intestinal barrier domain, the expression of key tight junction proteins (occludin, CLDN1, and ZO-1) was significantly upregulated. Crucial for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, these proteins serve as the core components of the tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells [39]. Their enhanced expression provided direct molecular evidence for the amelioration of colonic tissue pathology. In addition, within the pancreatic tissue, the concurrent downregulation of pro-apoptotic Bax and upregulation of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 synergistically supported β-cell survival, potentially constituting a major mechanism safeguarding islet β-cell function [40].

Finally, gut microbiota analysis revealed the ecological basis for the multi-organ protection of CBLEB. CBLEB significantly inhibited the overgrowth of Escherichia spp. in T2DM mice, a genus whose excessive proliferation has been associated with a disruption of the intestinal barrier and induction of inflammation through LPS production [41]. The decrease in Escherichia spp. abundance aligns well with the decreased serum LPS levels and suppressed TLR4/MyD88 signaling observed in CBLEB-treated mice, suggesting that modulation of this potential pathobiont helps reduce metabolic endotoxemia and subsequent inflammation. An increase in the abundance of Roseburia, a beneficial genus known for producing SCFAs, was also observed. This genus helps maintain the intestinal barrier integrity and exerts anti-inflammatory effects [4243]. Butyrate serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes, strengthens intestinal barrier function by upregulating tight junction proteins (ZO-1, CLDN1, occludin), and exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting nuclear factor (NF)-κB signaling and promoting regulatory T cell differentiation. Therefore, the observed increase in Roseburia abundance is functionally consistent with the improved intestinal barrier integrity and reduced systemic inflammation in the CBLEB-treated group. Furthermore, the partial reversal of Prevotella and Lactobacillus abundances toward the levels observed in the NC group suggests restoration of the microbial balance, which may contribute to the overall gut ecosystem stability. Nevertheless, the precise functional roles of these genera in the context of T2DM require further investigation. This specific optimization of the microbial structure was accompanied by a functional shift in the predicted metabolic capacity of the microbiota. The functional prediction based on KEGG analysis suggested that CBLEB intervention shifted the inferred microbial metabolic pathways from potentially pathogenic routes (e.g., bacterial invasion of epithelial cells) toward host-beneficial metabolic pathways (e.g., insulin signaling). Collectively, these findings establish a coherent link between CBLEB-induced microbiota remodeling and the observed improvements in intestinal barrier function, inflammation, and metabolic regulation, supporting the concept that CBLEB acts through the gut-microbiota-metabolism axis.

In particular, the combination of CBLEB and DMBG showed the most pronounced therapeutic effects across multiple parameters, including glycemic control, lipid profiles, and inflammatory markers. This synergistic effect may be attributed to their complementary mechanisms of action. CBLEB primarily targets the gut-barrier-inflammation axis by restoring the gut microbiota homeostasis and intestinal barrier integrity, reducing systemic endotoxemia and low-grade inflammation. By contrast, DMBG acts mainly on hepatic glucose production and peripheral insulin sensitivity through the AMPK-dependent pathways [44]. Their concurrent administration likely amplifies the overall metabolic improvement by simultaneously addressing multiple pathological nodes in T2DM. Although the gut microbiota was not analyzed in the combination group, the observed phenotypic improvements suggest potential additive or synergistic effects warranting further investigation, particularly regarding whether the CBLEB pretreatment or co-treatment enhances the therapeutic efficacy of metformin by modulating the gut ecosystem.

This study had several limitations. First, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed only on fecal samples from the NC, T2DM, and T2DM+CBLEB groups. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the specific effects of CBLEB on gut microbiota dysbiosis in obese T2DM. Therefore, the analysis was intentionally focused on these three core groups to clearly delineate the microbiota-modulating effects of CBLEB itself, independent of other glucose-lowering agents. The effects of DMBG on the gut microbiota have been reported, highlighting its ability to enrich beneficial taxa and improve the microbial diversity [4546]. Given this established evidence, a full comparative analysis of all intervention groups was beyond the immediate scope of this work. Consequently, all microbiome-related mechanistic interpretations in this study are strictly limited to the CBLEB-treated group, and the synergistic effects observed in the combination group were discussed only at the phenotypic level. Second, the precise causal relationships between gut microbiota alterations and host metabolic improvements remain to be established. Future studies incorporating fecal microbiota transplantation, metagenomic sequencing of all treatment groups, and germ-free animal models will be necessary to elucidate the microbiota-mediated mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of CBLEB, DMBG, and their combination. Despite these limitations, the present study provides robust evidence that CBLEB ameliorates obese T2DM through multi-pathway mechanisms that involve modulating the gut microbiota, repairing the intestinal barrier, and suppressing inflammation.

In summary, CBLEB effectively modulated the disordered gut microbiota to restore homeostasis, repaired the damaged intestinal mucosal barrier to enhance its integrity, and markedly suppressed excessive systemic inflammatory responses and activated key signaling pathways, such as TLR4/NF-κB. Through these synergistic actions, CBLEB significantly alleviated chronic systemic inflammation and reduced the IR levels. In particular, the combination of CBLEB with DMBG showed superior therapeutic efficacy, suggesting that probiotic supplementation may enhance the effects of conventional antidiabetic therapy. This finding elucidates the mechanism of action of CBLEB and provides a robust experimental foundation and highly promising novel therapeutic strategies for developing new probiotic-based therapies targeting obese-type diabetes.
