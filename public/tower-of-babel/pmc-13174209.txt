TargetGAN: A generative AI framework for the design of plant core promoters with targeted activity
Authors: Xianglei Xiang, Qi Yao, Kaixuan Deng, Yuanxin Ge, Qi Xiong, Yuming Lu, Xuehai Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant core promoters (PCPs) are key genetic elements that control gene expression and have significant value for crop breeding and plant synthetic biology. Natural promoters (NPs) are constrained by their limited diversity and narrow activity range, and it remains unclear whether synthetic promoters (SPs) can transcend these natural constraints. Here, we present TargetGAN, a deep-learning framework trained on 76,851 NPs that integrates a generative adversarial network (GAN) with a pre-trained activity predictor to enable the de novo design of PCPs with user-defined activity. We used TargetGAN to generate 55,296 SPs and selected 5,250 for high-throughput functional validation using STARR-seq. Of these, 2,909 were successfully characterized, with a moderate correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.6435) between predicted and experimental activity. Surprisingly, 29 SPs exhibited ultra-high activity, exceeding the maximum activity of the tested NPs. Further orthogonal validation using luciferase reporter assays showed a strong positive correlation with STARR-seq measurements across a broad dynamic range. Notably, the most active synthetic candidate, SP1482, significantly outperformed the strongest tested NP, the UBI core promoter, achieving a 128-fold increase in expression relative to the 35S minimal promoter. Interpretable motif analysis suggested that ultra-high-activity promoter design can be achieved through the precise arrangement of strong activating motifs. These results demonstrate that TargetGAN is a robust and generalizable framework for the targeted generation of PCPs tailored to user-defined activity levels and will be a powerful tool both for precise gene regulation in plant systems and for overexpression analysis in genetic engineering and synthetic biology.

Introduction

By 2050, the global population is projected to exceed 10 billion (FAO, 2017), which will necessitate a 60% increase in food production (Springmann et al., 2018). However, the intensification of climate change and the reduced availability of arable land have made it a critical global challenge to increase both agricultural productivity and sustainability (Gao, 2021). Synthetic biology, an emerging field focused on designing biological systems through engineering, offers a promising strategy to address this challenge.

In synthetic biology, the promoter is the central genetic element that controls transcription initiation (Huang et al., 2021). Structurally, it is a complex assembly that comprises distal and proximal regulatory elements and a core promoter located upstream of a gene (Mithra et al., 2017). These components function together to dictate transcriptional timing, spatial specificity, and intensity by recruiting RNA polymerase II and general transcription factors (Smale and Kadonaga, 2003). From an engineering perspective, promoters are not merely building blocks for genetic circuits; their standardization and modularity provide a critical foundation for the design and reconstruction of complex biological systems (Wurtzel et al., 2019; Khan et al., 2025). Their functional characteristics (activity, specificity, and inducibility) directly determine the efficiency of gene expression and the dynamic responsiveness of synthetic systems (Pierre-Jerome et al., 2016; Mohan et al., 2017; Jameel et al., 2020). In practical applications, promoters exhibit broad functionality in fields such as metabolic engineering and biosensing through interaction with other genetic elements. For example, using the strong constitutive promoter THI2 to overexpress mevalonate pathway genes significantly boosts artemisinin production (Guo et al., 2025). Recently, the precise insertion of a 10-bp heat-shock element into the promoters of cell wall invertase genes was shown to enable heat-inducible expression in elite rice and tomato cultivars (Lou et al., 2025). Together, these advances highlight the vast potential of promoters as tunable tools for genetic engineering.

In plant systems, the promoter region typically extends hundreds to thousands of base pairs upstream of the transcription start site (TSS). The functional boundaries of this region are defined by the genomic context (O’Malley et al., 2016; Brooks et al., 2023). Although distal elements modulate expression levels, the core promoter orchestrates transcription initiation by serving as the docking site for the pre-initiation complex (Haberle and Stark, 2018). This region often contains conserved motifs such as the TATA box, which is essential for recruiting the TATA-binding protein (Juven-Gershon and Kadonaga, 2010). Recently, a systematic characterization of plant core promoter (PCP) activity in Arabidopsis, maize, and sorghum was conducted by Jores et al. (2021) using the self-transcribing active regulatory region sequencing (STARR-seq) assay. They defined the PCP of a given plant gene as the genomic region spanning 165 bp upstream and 5 bp downstream of the TSS. The results showed that natural promoter (NP) activity in maize protoplasts ranged from −7.47 to 4.06 (log2-transformed ratio relative to the 35S minimal promoter) (Jores et al., 2021). This range is relatively narrow, with the highest activity being approximately 16-fold higher than that of the 35S minimal promoter (Jores et al., 2021). Notably, the observed activity range likely reflects evolutionary constraints rather than biochemical limitations. Although these promoters have been optimized for specific physiological contexts, they represent only a minuscule fraction of the possible sequence space (e.g., 4170 ≈ 2.23 × 10102 combinations for a 170-bp sequence), which indicates that their potential functions remain largely unexplored. Therefore, designing novel promoters that outperform natural ones and constructing synthetic promoter (SP) libraries with broader activity ranges have become urgent needs.

Traditional approaches for designing novel promoters, such as mutagenesis (Jores et al., 2021; de Almeida et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024) and combinatorial assembly of regulatory elements (Wu et al., 2018; Bai et al., 2020; Cai et al., 2020), primarily involve minor modifications or limited recombination of NPs. Because these methods primarily explore the sequence space near NPs, they typically yield only minor functional variations. Consequently, they are generally insufficient for the design of novel, highly active promoters that transcend natural activity boundaries. In addition, many of the resulting SPs lack the expected regulatory activity, necessitating large-scale experimental screening to identify functional promoters. This trial-and-error process greatly increases both development costs and time investment.

The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) has opened new avenues for synthetic biology through deep generative modeling techniques (DGMTs). By allowing models to autonomously extract regulatory patterns without reliance on prior knowledge, DGMTs facilitate the exploration of vast sequence spaces. The applicability of DGMTs has been validated across diverse biological systems. In prokaryotes, Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated the efficacy of generative adversarial networks (GANs) through the generation of Escherichia coli promoters with a 70.8% functional rate in vivo. In eukaryotic systems, Zrimec et al. (2022) used GANs to design full-length regulatory sequences capable of driving precisely targeted gene expression in yeast. Taskiran et al. (2024) extended these capabilities to complex multicellular organisms by combining deep learning with evolutionary strategies to design cell-type-specific enhancers in Drosophila. However, despite these successes, applying DGMTs to PCP design poses unique challenges. Unlike microbial or simple eukaryotic systems, plant promoters are characterized by longer sequences, more intricate cis-regulatory architectures, and complex chromatin environments (O’Malley et al., 2016). These complexities significantly hinder accurate modeling, making the de novo design of PCPs with precisely targeted activities a persistent challenge in the field.

In the present study, we demonstrate that DGMTs can be used to effectively design PCPs de novo (Figure 1). We introduce TargetGAN, a generative AI model trained on 76,851 NPs that uses an activation maximization strategy (Killoran et al., 2017) to steer latent variables toward user-defined activity targets (Figures 1A and 1B). Using this approach, we generated a library of 55,296 SPs covering a broad dynamic range (Figure 1C), and we then successfully validated 2,909 sequences using STARR-seq (Figure 1D). Crucially, this large-scale characterization revealed that our model effectively overcomes the evolutionary constraints of NPs: we identified 29 ultra-high-activity SPs that significantly surpass the maximum activity of the NPs tested in this study (Figure 1E). Luciferase (LUC) assays further confirmed these findings, with the top candidate, SP1482, exhibiting activity 128-fold higher than the 35S minimal promoter and outperforming the strongest tested NP, the UBI core promoter (a 170-bp fragment spanning 165 bp upstream to 5 bp downstream of the UBI gene [Zm00001d015327] TSS) (Figure 1F). Results

Designing novel PCPs using WGAN-GP

We first asked whether AI could be used to design novel PCPs. We used a Wasserstein generative adversarial network with gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) framework comprising a generator and a discriminator (Arjovsky et al., 2017). The training dataset, derived from Jores et al. (2021), included natural PCPs from Arabidopsis, maize, and sorghum. Promoter activities were measured by STARR-seq in maize protoplasts under dark conditions in the absence of enhancers. The dataset contained 76,851 sequences, which were randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio (Supplemental Dataset 1). The training set was used to train the WGAN-GP model, the validation set was used to tune model hyperparameters, and the test set served to assess model performance.

We generated a total of 76,851 promoters, matching the number of NPs, using WGAN-GP at 54,000 iterations (Supplemental Dataset 2). To confirm the novelty of the SPs, we used BLASTN to compare them with the NPs. Only 0.27% of the SPs (E value < 1 × 10−10) were found to be similar, which indicates that WGAN-GP generates novel PCPs rather than copying NP sequences. We next compared sequence properties between NPs and SPs, namely k-mer frequency distributions (Figure 2B), Jaccard and Levenshtein distances, GC content (Supplemental Figure 1), and DNA shape features (Figure 2C). These results demonstrate that WGAN-GP can effectively learn the essential features of NPs and use this acquired knowledge to generate SPs. To evaluate activity prediction, we used a published predictive model trained on NPs (Deng et al., 2023) that demonstrated high accuracy on independent held-out datasets (R2test = 0.42). However, the predicted activity distributions of the SPs closely matched those of the NPs (Figure 2D), which indicates that promoters generated by WGAN-GP are unlikely to exceed the activity range of NPs. This limitation necessitated the development of novel AI methods to design SPs with ultra-high activity.

TargetGAN effectively generates novel promoters with targeted activity levels

To overcome the activity constraints of natural sequences and generate promoters with a broader activity range, we designed a novel general framework named TargetGAN. We integrated a pre-trained activity predictor into a classical GAN architecture; the generator was trained to produce SPs using latent variables, and their activities were predicted by the trained predictor (Deng et al., 2023) (Figure 3A). The loss function was then calculated based on the difference between the predicted activity and the target value. Using backpropagation, we calculated the gradient of the loss with respect to the latent variables, which were then updated in the direction that adjusted promoter activity toward the target based on the gradient. We defined nine distinct target activity levels spanning the full range of NP activity. Trajectory analysis revealed that each population mean rapidly approached its target, whereas the variance converged more slowly. Therefore, to minimize variance and ensure precise alignment with the targets, the optimization process was extended to 10,000 iterations (Figure 3C). These results demonstrate the capability of TargetGAN to precisely guide promoter generation toward desired activity profiles.

To comprehensively evaluate how well promoter generation matched the desired activity ranges, we generated 2,048 promoters per target over 10,000 iterations, resulting in 18,432 SPs across nine targets. The activities of these promoters were predicted using the trained predictor (Deng et al., 2023), followed by statistical analysis to examine the distribution of these activities within each target interval. As shown in Figure 3D, success rates varied across targets. The highest accuracy was observed for the target of −3 (interval −3 ± 0.1), with 80.8% of promoters falling within the desired range. In contrast, the “min” target (interval ≤ −6) yielded a success rate of 24.9%. Although this percentage is lower than that of other targets, it represents approximately 600-fold enrichment relative to the natural background, in which such ultra-low-activity sequences constitute ∼0.04% of the population (n = 32 NPs). This result highlights the model’s capacity to capture and generate rare sequence features despite the scarcity of training data. Finally, the “max” target (interval ≥3) yielded a success rate of 63.6%, demonstrating strong potential for significantly enhancing gene expression.

We further validated TargetGAN’s robustness by repeating the experiments with different initializations of latent variables and observed consistently high-quality results (Figure 3E). Notably, promoters generated from these independent repetitions exhibited minimal sequence overlap, with only 4.45% similarity (E value < 1 × 10−10), highlighting the diversity of the generated sequences. Collectively, we compiled a total of 55,296 SPs from all three repetitions to construct a candidate PCP library (Supplemental Dataset 3). Subsequent evaluations of sequence properties—including k-mer frequency, Jaccard and Levenshtein distances, GC content, and DNA shape features—revealed that although SPs maintain biological plausibility, they exhibit distinct distributional shifts compared with NPs (Supplemental Figure 2). We attribute these differences to the distinct activity profiles observed in the two datasets (Supplemental Figure 2A) and the optimization process required to meet specific targets. Importantly, this reflects an inherent trade-off between maintaining a natural sequence composition and achieving extreme, out-of-distribution activity. Indeed, a target-specific analysis demonstrated a clear target-dependent divergence: SPs designed for moderate activity levels closely resembled the natural baseline, whereas those optimized for an extreme target (either ultra-high or ultra-low activity) exhibited progressively larger deviations in k-mer frequency correlations, GC content, and DNA shape features (Supplemental Figure 3). This confirms that TargetGAN does not merely mimic natural sequences but actively optimizes sequence features to satisfy specific activity constraints. Overall, these results establish TargetGAN as a robust framework for consistently generating diverse sets of novel promoters tailored to specific target activities.

Experimental validation confirms that TargetGAN-generated promoters extend beyond the activity range of NPs

To validate TargetGAN-generated promoter activity, we used STARR-seq, a high-throughput method for quantifying the activity of cis-regulatory elements (Jores et al., 2020). We synthesized a library of 6,000 sequences, consisting of 5,250 SPs from nine different targets and 750 NPs as positive controls (Supplemental Dataset 4). Selection criteria for the SPs were target specific. For the “max” target, we prioritized the 1,500 sequences with the highest predicted activities. For the “min” target, to ensure that the activities of the selected sequences remained detectable by STARR-seq and were not entirely lost to background noise, we similarly prioritized the 250 sequences with the highest predicted activities. For the remaining seven targets, we selected the 500 sequences closest to their designated target values. The NPs consisted of the top 749 sequences with the highest activities from a previous study (Jores et al., 2021) and a 170-bp Actin core promoter fragment (spanning 165 bp upstream to 5 bp downstream of the Actin [Zm00001d010159] TSS) (Shah et al., 1983). In addition, the 35S minimal promoter was included as an independent baseline control (Figure 4A). These sequences were cloned into reporter plasmids, placed upstream of the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of the maize histone H3.2 gene (Zm00001d041672), and used to drive the expression of a barcoded firefly luciferase (fLuc) reporter gene in transiently transformed maize protoplasts (Figure 4A). Three independently constructed libraries contained approximately 350,000 unique barcodes each. Barcode frequencies were quantified from cDNA and plasmid input reads, and promoter activity was calculated as the log2-transformed ratio of cDNA to input barcode frequencies (Figure 4A). To ensure high reproducibility and reliability, we used a rigorous two-step quality control strategy. First, we restricted our analysis to promoters for which activity was consistently detected across all three independent biological replicates. Accordingly, we filtered the original dataset to retain only sequences exhibiting a coefficient of variation (CV) < 30%. This strict filtering yielded a high-confidence dataset of 2,909 SPs and 671 NPs (Supplemental Dataset 4). Although this resulted in a reduction relative to the initial 6,000 sequences—primarily due to the exclusion of low-abundance and high-variability reads—the final dataset exhibited high consistency across replicates (Figure 4B), with pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) ranging from 0.7680 to 0.7797. These values indicate high replicability, comparable to high-quality benchmark studies (Jores et al., 2021), which confirms the reliability of our activity measurements. The mean promoter activity across the three biological replicates was used for all subsequent analyses, resulting in a final dataset of 2,909 SPs and 671 NPs.

To further assess data reliability, we compared the measured activities of the 671 NPs with their reference values (Jores et al., 2021) (Supplemental Figure 4); we observed a weak positive correlation (PCC = 0.3033) and a systematic upward shift in the activity range (with values clustering between 2 and 4 vs. 0 and 2). Importantly, despite these absolute differences, the relative ranking of promoter strengths remained informative. For the 2,909 SPs, the experimentally measured activities showed a moderate correlation with the predicted activities (PCC = 0.6435) (Figure 4C). We also examined the distribution of promoter activities across the nine targets. For targets ranging from −1 to “max,” the results exhibited a clear stepwise increasing gradient, reflecting the intended design trend. However, for targets below −1 (“min” to −2), the experimental activities plateaued at a basal level (approximately 2.5) and did not show distinct stratification (Supplemental Figure 5). This plateau likely reflects the sensitivity limit of the STARR-seq assay at extremely low transcriptional activities. When activity falls below a certain threshold, the signal becomes indistinguishable from background noise, causing measured values to converge to a baseline level.

We next investigated whether SPs could transcend the activity limits of natural sequences. A comparison of 1,337 SPs (predicted activity > 0) with 671 NPs revealed that the constitutive Actin core promoter exhibited an activity of 2.29 and that the UBI core promoter was the strongest tested NP at 5.19 (Figure 4D). Remarkably, 29 SPs surpassed the maximum activity among these tested NPs. To rigorously validate the reliability of our STARR-seq measurements and assess these candidates, we performed dual-LUC assays across a broad dynamic range. We evaluated top-performing SPs identified by STARR-seq and representative SPs from the lower target bins, alongside the UBI core promoter, the Actin core promoter, and the 35S minimal promoter as controls (Figure 4E). This comprehensive validation revealed a strong positive correlation (PCC = 0.7095) between the STARR-seq and LUC assays, thereby confirming that STARR-seq reliably captures the relative regulatory strengths of the generated promoters across different activity levels. Notably, the top-performing candidates, SP1482 and SP358, consistently outperformed the UBI core promoter in the LUC assay, with SP1482 achieving an extraordinary 128-fold higher activity than the 35S minimal promoter (Figure 4F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that TargetGAN-designed promoters can successfully transcend the evolutionary constraints of natural sequences, achieving both superior activity levels and a broadened dynamic range.

SPs achieve ultra-high activity via specific motif combinations

To provide a mechanistic explanation for the ultra-high-activity SPs, we investigated their sequence composition to understand the principles and mechanisms underlying their activity. We first used TF-MoDISco (Shrikumar et al., 2018) to identify enriched motifs present in the 55,296 SPs (Supplemental Dataset 3). A total of 13 motifs were identified, five of which were canonical motifs similar to those identified in NPs using the same method (Deng et al., 2023) (Figure 5A), whereas eight were newly identified (Figure 5B). Among the five canonical motifs, four are activators and one is a repressor (Figure 5C). Notably, this group includes well-characterized core promoter elements such as TATAAA and TATATA (Wasylyk et al., 1980; Smale and Kadonaga, 2003). Among the eight newly identified motifs, one is an activator (Seqlet3) and seven are repressors (Figure 5C). We then compared motif usage between natural and synthetic promoters. All 13 motifs were observed at least once in both sets, which suggests that both groups share the same syntactic vocabulary (Figure 5C, right). However, motif frequencies differed significantly between the two groups (Figure 5C). For example, canonical motifs such as TATAAA and TATATA occurred at much higher frequencies in SPs (TATAAA, 42.9%; TATATA, 42.7%) than in NPs (TATAAA, 8.4%; TATATA, 10.2%). In contrast, newly identified motifs such as Seqlet3 and AHL20 were more frequent in SPs (Seqlet3, 26.2%; AHL20, 14.4%) than in NPs (Seqlet3, 18.8%; AHL20, 6.1%). Importantly, motif co-occurrence patterns in natural vs. synthetic promoters revealed that certain motif pairs were more prevalent in SPs (Figure 5D). Notably, TATAAA and TATATA co-occurred significantly more often in SPs (35.2%) than in NPs (3.4%) (Figure 5D). This indicates that TargetGAN has learned to identify critical motifs that influence promoter activity and that it enriches and combines these elements during the generation process to synergistically enhance SP activity.

We then analyzed motif usage differences in SPs across various targets. Using FIMO (Grant et al., 2011), we scanned the 55,296 SPs and collected statistics on motif occurrences (Figure 5E). The TATAAA, TATATA, and Seqlet3 motifs were more common in high-activity promoters, whereas HDG11 and the seven newly identified repressive motifs were more frequent in low-activity promoters. This is consistent with their respective contributions. Although the frequencies of most motifs varied significantly across different target activities, their positional distributions generally remained consistent, with the notable exception of Seqlet4, which exhibited a distinct positional shift (Supplemental Figure 6). These findings suggest that SPs with different targets use distinct syntactic rules to produce the desired activity levels.

Finally, we focused on motif usage within ultra-high-activity promoters that surpassed natural constraints, namely those exhibiting STARR-seq activity greater than 5.19 (the maximum value observed for tested NPs). In terms of individual motifs, Seqlet3 was the most frequent, followed by TATAAA and TATATA, whereas motifs with negative contributions were rare (Supplemental Figure 7A). Motif combination analysis revealed that the ultra-high-activity promoters predominantly contained combinations of TATAAA, TATATA, Seqlet2, and Seqlet3, whereas negative regulatory motif combinations were largely absent (Supplemental Figure 7B). We selected two representative ultra-high-activity promoters, SP1482 and SP358, and used DeepLIFT (Shrikumar et al., 2017) to obtain contribution scores for each nucleotide position. FIMO (Grant et al., 2011) was then used to identify the locations of motifs within these sequences (Figure 5F). The results revealed the magnitude and direction of each motif’s effect on promoter activity. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that SPs predominantly rely on activator motif combinations to achieve ultra-high activity.

A motif-frequency-based model for PCPs

Guided by the motif analysis findings, we established a theoretical model for quantifying PCP activity. We determined the frequency of each motif in each SP and trained a linear regression model using these frequencies as input and the predicted activities as output (Figure 6A). Analysis of the regression coefficients revealed that the TATAAA motif had the strongest positive effect on promoter activity, whereas the Seqlet7 (AT3G46070) motif had the most significant negative effect (Figure 6B). Notably, these regression coefficients were strongly correlated with the average contribution scores derived from DeepLIFT (PCC = 0.8972, Figure 6C), validating the model’s reliability. To rigorously evaluate whether more complex architectures or additional sequence features could better capture the regulatory syntax, we benchmarked various machine-learning algorithms—including support vector machines, random forests, and feedforward neural networks (FNNs)—and integrated additional features such as GC content and DNA shape (Supplemental Figure 8). The results showed that although complex nonlinear models (e.g., FNN) and additional features yielded performance gains on the TargetGAN dataset, the improvement was marginal (increasing PCC from 0.8407 to 0.8868). Critically, we also assessed the transferability of these advanced models to NPs and found that they failed to significantly overcome the prediction barrier, yielding only a slight increase in PCC (from 0.2714 to 0.3037). Given that complex algorithms offered limited benefits and lacked intrinsic transparency, we retained the interpretable linear regression model as our final analytical tool. This decision prioritized direct biological insight into specific motif contributions over negligible gains in predictive precision.

We further tested the generalizability of this “syntactic grammar” by applying the linear regression model to promoters generated by WGAN-GP and to NPs. The model achieved a PCC of 0.5546 on WGAN-GP-generated promoters but showed reduced performance on NPs (PCC = 0.2714) (Figure 6E and 6F). This significant performance gap, which persisted even when using advanced neural networks, suggests that the limited transferability is not a methodological flaw but reflects a fundamental biological difference: TargetGAN-generated promoters rely on a “clean” and optimized syntax of motif accumulation, whereas NPs operate within a noisy evolutionary landscape containing complex distal interactions and chromatin-level constraints that simple motif-counting rules cannot fully capture.

Discussion

Our study establishes TargetGAN as a robust, interpretable framework for the de novo design of PCPs with user-defined activity targets. Using high-throughput STARR-seq validation of 2909 SPs, we demonstrated that TargetGAN-generated sequences span a significantly broader activity spectrum than NPs. Orthogonal LUC assays corroborated these findings and confirmed that top-performing candidates, such as SP1482 and SP358, surpassed the UBI core promoter, the strongest tested NP. Notably, SP1482 achieved an extraordinary 128-fold increase in expression relative to the 35S minimal promoter. Mechanistically, our interpretable motif analysis revealed that ultra-high activity is not a stochastic occurrence but is driven by the precise, synergistic arrangement of potent activating motifs. By providing quantitative insights into the contributions of individual motifs, our motif-based linear regression model transforms the “black box” of deep learning into a transparent, actionable tool for the rational design of synthetic promoters.

The primary innovation of this work lies in the ability of TargetGAN to generate a large-scale library of synthetic PCPs with customizable activity, free from natural evolutionary constraints. This library, which is publicly available to the research community, greatly expands the synthetic biology toolbox in plants. It enables fine-tuned gene regulation, facilitates the construction of complex genetic circuits, and supports studies of sequence–function relationships. Importantly, the experimentally validated ultra-high-activity promoters offer significant practical value, particularly in scenarios that require extremely high expression levels, including gene overexpression studies in functional genomics (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Xiong et al., 2025), efficient expression of rate-limiting enzymes in metabolic engineering (Alter et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b), and the biosynthesis of high-value compounds, including pharmaceuticals (Delayre et al., 2020), biofuel precursors (Liu et al., 2022), and specialty chemicals (Guo et al., 2025).

Despite these advances, our study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the absolute fold changes in our study must be interpreted in context. We observed a systematic numerical shift in our STARR-seq data relative to previous benchmarks (Jores et al., 2021). One possibility is that our setup underestimated the basal activity of the 35S minimal promoter, as the use of a single barcode for this control made it susceptible to barcode-specific bias and stochastic dropout. Normalization against this unusually low denominator could have mathematically inflated the apparent fold changes. Second, our evaluation primarily relied on transient expression in maize protoplasts. Although we validated the superior activity of our top candidates using LUC assays, transient systems cannot fully recapitulate the complex endogenous chromatin landscape or the heritability observed in stable transgenic plants (O’Malley et al., 2016). Third, it is important to note that the SPs generated by TargetGAN in this study are 170-bp core promoter fragments. Although these short sequences exhibit ultra-high basal transcriptional activity that significantly surpasses that of equivalent natural core promoters, they do not capture the broader regulatory landscape of full-length natural promoters, such as the approximately 2-kb-long UBI promoter. Because practical biotechnological applications often require complex spatiotemporal regulation, these short synthetic core promoters will likely need to be supplemented with specific distal enhancer elements to achieve optimal, finely tuned expression patterns in vivo. Finally, our primary objective was to overcome the evolutionary constraints on promoter strength. However, practical crop engineering often prioritizes precise spatiotemporal regulation over maximal expression (Mohan et al., 2017; Jameel et al., 2020). Constitutive ultra-high activity, if unregulated, may impose a metabolic burden on the host, induce cytotoxicity, or trigger transgene silencing (Khan et al., 2025). Consequently, the physiological impacts of these powerful synthetic elements remain to be fully understood in the context of whole-plant biology.

Looking forward, we envision TargetGAN not as a static tool but as an iteratively evolving framework. A central pillar of this ongoing optimization is the establishment of a closed “dry–wet” feedback loop. By periodically integrating newly acquired experimental data—such as the large-scale STARR-seq and LUC validation results generated herein—back into the training corpus, the model’s predictive accuracy and generative capability can be continuously refined. In parallel with these data-driven updates, upgrading the underlying generative architecture provides another avenue for systematic improvement. Recent advances in AI algorithms, such as diffusion models (Wang et al., 2024, 2025c), large language models (Lal et al., 2024), and reinforcement learning (Wang et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2025), have shown great promise in biomolecular design. Transitioning TargetGAN to these advanced architectures could further enhance the efficiency and structural precision of SPs. Ultimately, translating these computational advances into robust agricultural applications will require expanding the biological dimensions of the model. To move beyond constitutive expression, future iterations will integrate multi-omics constraints to develop tissue-specific or stress-responsive predictors (de Almeida et al., 2024; Gosai et al., 2024), thereby enabling the design of promoters tailored to specific cellular contexts. Complementing this spatial precision, the quantitative on-demand activity modulation offered by TargetGAN holds immense potential for metabolic engineering. Using these promoters to finely balance the ratio of upstream and downstream enzymes could optimize metabolic flux and mitigate feedback inhibition within complex pathways. For instance, precisely targeting rate-limiting steps such as DXS in terpene biosynthesis could maximize metabolite yields without compromising plant fitness (Wright et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2020). To achieve these goals, the establishment of systematic characterization platforms, such as reporter gene expression analyses in stable transgenic plants across different tissues or within engineered metabolic circuits, is urgently needed. These platforms will enable comprehensive assessment of spatiotemporal expression patterns and regulatory dynamics, ensuring that SPs are not only potent but also agronomically viable.

Methods

Datasets

All natural PCPs used in this study were derived from Arabidopsis, maize, and sorghum (Jores et al., 2021). The sequences of these promoters spanned from −165 to +5 relative to the annotated TSS. Promoter activity was normalized to that of the 35S minimal promoter. A total of 76,851 promoter sequences were randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 8:1:1. Subsequently, to ensure that the model adequately learned sequence features across varying activity levels—particularly for rare high- and low-activity promoters—we implemented a dynamic data-balancing strategy for the training set. The activity range was divided into 16 consecutive bins; during the construction of each training batch, an equal number of samples was drawn with replacement from each bin, thereby assembling batches that encompassed the full activity spectrum. This approach ensured that the model learned features uniformly across all activity intervals rather than passively adapting to the distribution bias present in the original data (Zrimec et al., 2022) (Supplemental Figure 9).

Evaluation of sequence properties and similarity

To assess the quality, novelty, and biological plausibility of the generated promoters, we comprehensively compared the sequence properties of synthetic promoters (from both WGAN-GP and TargetGAN) with those of natural promoters. The evaluation metrics included k-mer frequency distribution, Jaccard distance, Levenshtein distance, GC content, and DNA shape features.

For WGAN-GP optimization, these indicators were monitored in 640 SPs throughout the training process to track model performance and determine the optimal stopping point (54,000 iterations). To detect overfitting, sequence similarity between the test and training sets was used as a reference baseline. For TargetGAN validation, these metrics were used to verify that the generated sequences shared essential structural features with natural promoters, thereby distinguishing them from random artifacts. As a negative control, random sequences were generated by randomly assigning nucleotides (A, T, C, G) to match the length of the promoter sequences.

The specific calculation methods were as follows:(1)k-mer frequency analysis: Promoters were segmented into overlapping k-mers (k = 4, 5, or 6). The frequencies of all k-mers were compiled into a vector, and the PCC between the frequency vectors of synthetic and natural promoters was used to quantify their similarity.(2)Sequence distance metrics: The Jaccard distance between two DNA sequences was calculated as 1 minus the ratio of the intersection to the union of their unique 4-mers. The Levenshtein distance, defined as the minimum number of editing operations required to convert one sequence into another, was calculated using the Python-Levenshtein v.0.21.0 package. To assess the novelty of the generated sequences, we calculated the Jaccard and Levenshtein distances between each SP and its nearest neighbor in the training set. Specifically, for every SP, we calculated the distance for all natural promoters in the training set and recorded the minimum value. The same approach was used to compare the test set to the training set.(3)Physicochemical properties: GC content was calculated as the ratio of guanine and cytosine bases to the total sequence length. DNA shape features, including minor groove width (MGW), roll, propeller twist (ProT), and helix twist (HelT), were predicted using the DNAshape method (Zhou et al., 2013) to capture the 3D structural characteristics of the sequences.

BLAST search on the synthetic sequences

A nucleotide BLAST search was performed to compare the SPs with the NPs using the default settings. Sequences with an E value less than 1 × 10−10 were defined as similar.

TargetGAN

To generate promoters with desired target expression levels, we used a DNA-based activation maximization approach (Killoran et al., 2017) that integrates both the trained generator and predictor models (Figure 3A). Different loss functions L were defined based on the specific target. Let the target be denoted as T and let the mean and variance of the promoter activity of the current iteration be μ and σ, respectively.L={−μifT=“max”12×(μ−T)2+12×σ2ifT∈RμifT=“min”.

The backpropagation algorithm was used to calculate the gradient ∇ZL and then progressively update the original input Z along this gradient (using a small step size ∈) to decrease the loss function:Z←Z−ϵ∇ZL.

Based on activation maximization (Killoran et al., 2017) and the chain rule, the gradient ∇ZL can be defined as follows:∇ZL=∂L∂x∂x∂Z=∂f(P(x))∂x·∂G(Z)∂Z,where G is the generator that transforms latent variables Z into SPs x, and P is the predictor that maps x to the corresponding promoter activity t = P(x). Through this iterative process, SP activity continuously approaches the target expression level.

Library design and construction

Following screening, we obtained 6,000 candidate 170-bp promoter sequences (Supplemental Dataset 4). To facilitate cloning, we added a 15-bp universal sequence to the start and end of each sequence, extending them to 200 bp. These 200-bp candidate sequences were synthesized via array-based oligonucleotide synthesis (Twist Bioscience). The pSPO-01 vector was constructed using Gibson assembly; the ccdB-containing sequence in the pSTEM02 backbone was replaced with the candidate promoter followed by fLuc (Yao et al., 2024). To link barcodes with their corresponding promoters, we first isolated a 67-bp 5′ UTR from the maize histone H3.2 gene (Zm00001d041672). This UTR was positioned immediately upstream of a 3-bp ATG start codon and a 15-bp barcode sequence, which were introduced via primer design (Sangon Biotech, China). For the negative control, the 35S minimal promoter was flanked by the same 67-bp UTR and a distinct 12-bp barcode (CTACCGGCCCTA) using the same 5′ UTR–ATG–barcode design. To associate the barcodes with their respective promoters, PCR amplification was performed using a downstream primer containing the barcode and an upstream primer with the 15-bp universal sequence, typically for 20–25 cycles. Experimental libraries (containing candidate promoter sequences) and negative control libraries (containing the 35S minimal promoter) were then constructed via Gibson assembly. For each candidate promoter sequence, at least three barcodes were linked. All 170-bp oligonucleotides were synthesized on a chip (GenScript, China) for one-step library construction. PCR-amplified pSTEM02 and the oligonucleotide library were used as vector and insert, respectively. The vector (5 μg) and insert (300 ng) were ligated via Gibson assembly. The assembled DNA fragments were transformed directly into E. coli to generate plasmids, achieving ∼50× library coverage. Bacterial colonies were collected, and plasmid DNA was extracted to generate the final promoter–barcode libraries. All primers are listed in Supplemental Table 1. The full-length sequence and detailed plasmid map of the representative STARR-seq vector (using the 35S minimal promoter construct as an example) are provided in the supplemental text and Supplemental Figure 10, respectively.

Maize protoplast generation and transformation

Maize (Zea mays L. cultivar B73) seeds were germinated for 4 days in the light, and the seedlings were then grown in soil at 25°C in darkness for 9–10 days. Protoplast isolation and transformation followed a previously established protocol involving polyethylene glycol 4000 (PEG4000)–Ca2+-mediated transformation (Gao et al., 2019). We mixed the candidate promoter libraries with the negative control library at a ratio of 1000:1 to obtain 500 μg of mixed library plasmids. In each transfection, to ensure sufficient coverage of each library, five reactions were performed using a total of 50 μg of mixed plasmids. The transfected protoplasts were then incubated in darkness at 25°C for 12–16 h. After incubation, the protoplasts were collected by centrifugation at 100 g for 3 min.

STARR-seq assay and Illumina sequencing

Approximately 50 μg of plasmid was used to transform 5 × 106 protoplasts. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each experiment. Protoplasts were harvested after 12–16 h in darkness at 25°C and immediately resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol. Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RC411, Vazyme, China). Subsequently, a pSPO-01-specific primer (SPO-XSRT1) was used for reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA (AT341, TransGen, China). The barcode-containing sequences were amplified from the cDNA libraries or the corresponding plasmid libraries. The read lengths used to distinguish the input promoters and output libraries were 388 bp (promoter–UTR–barcode–intron–fLuc) and 182 bp (UTR–barcode–fLuc without intron), respectively. These amplicons were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis and were sequenced using 150-bp paired-end sequencing (NGS, Illumina HiSeq, PE150).

STARR-seq data analysis

Raw sequencing data from the cDNA library and the plasmid input library (derived from the initial, highly diverse plasmid DNA extracted from E. coli prior to protoplast transfection) were quality filtered using Trimmomatic v.0.39 (Bolger et al., 2014) with default parameters to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. The filtered plasmid input reads were aligned to our reference promoter library (containing 750 NPs and 5,250 SPs) using Bowtie2 v.2.5.2 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). This alignment enabled the construction of a barcode-to-promoter mapping reference table. For cDNA and input reads, we extracted and quantified the 15-bp barcodes by strictly matching the fixed sequences immediately flanking the barcode region using custom Python scripts (available at https://github.com/xlxianglei/TargetGAN). Barcode enrichment was determined by calculating the ratio of barcode frequency (i.e., the count of a specific barcode divided by the total barcode count in the respective library) in the cDNA sample to that in the input sample. Promoter activity was calculated using the median enrichment of all barcodes associated with each promoter. The promoter activity was then log2-transformed and normalized to the activity of the 35S minimal promoter. To ensure data reproducibility and reliability, we performed a quality control step based on the CV, which was calculated for each promoter across three biological replicates as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean. Only promoter sequences with a CV less than 30% were retained for downstream analysis. The average promoter activity across the three replicates was used for all subsequent analyses.

LUC activity assay

To validate the transcriptional activity of the SPs characterized by STARR-seq across a broad dynamic range, a dual-LUC transient expression assay was performed in maize protoplasts. Selected top-performing SPs and representative SPs from lower target bins, along with the UBI core promoter, Actin core promoter, and 35S minimal promoter controls, were synthesized (Sangon Biotech, China) and cloned into the pSTEM01 vector to drive fLuc expression (Yao et al., 2024). The complete sequence of the representative dual-LUC reporter plasmid is provided in the supplemental text. Mesophyll protoplasts were isolated from leaves of 10-day-old etiolated B73 seedlings, and the prepared plasmids were introduced into the protoplasts using PEG-mediated transformation. After 16 h of incubation in darkness, fLuc and nano-luciferase (nLuc) activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each assay comprised four independent biological replicates, each with two technical replicates. To ensure data reliability, technical replicates with nLuc internal control luminescence below 10 000 were excluded from downstream analyses on the grounds of low transformation efficiency or poor cell viability. For the remaining data, fLuc activity was first normalized to the nLuc internal control activity to correct for variation in transformation efficiency. The final relative LUC activity was determined by normalizing this ratio to that of the 35S minimal promoter, calculated using the following formula:LUCactivity=fLucactivity/nLucactivityfLucactivity35S/nLucactivity35S.

The relative LUC activities for all promoters across the four biological replicates are summarized in Supplemental Table 2. In addition, all raw luminescence data and corresponding plate maps for the assays are provided in Supplemental Dataset 5.

Nucleotide contribution scores and motif discovery

To quantify the contribution of individual nucleotides to predicted promoter activity, we used DeepLIFT (Shrikumar et al., 2017), a gradient-based interpretation method (Avsec et al., 2021; de Almeida et al., 2022). For each input promoter sequence, DeepLIFT generated 10 reference sequences by random shuffling while preserving the original dinucleotide frequency. The trained predictor iCREPCP (Deng et al., 2023) was then used to predict promoter activities for the original and reference sequences. The differences between these predictions were subsequently decomposed into nucleotide-level contribution scores through backpropagation, which attributes the contributions of all neurons in the network to the input features.

To identify sequence motifs, we used TF-MoDISco v.0.5.16.4 (Shrikumar et al., 2018) with the nucleotide-level contribution scores generated by DeepLIFT for each promoter. The analysis was performed with the following parameters: sliding_window_size = 8, flank_size = 3, target_seqlet_fdr = 0.15, and TfModiscoSeqletsToPatternsFactory (trim_to_window_size = 8, initial_flank_to_add = 3). Motifs supported by fewer than 30 seqlets were discarded.

DNA motif analysis

We used FIMO (Grant et al., 2011) to scan each sequence and identify the number of motif occurrences and their positions. Tomtom (Gupta et al., 2007) was used to compare motifs against known TF motifs in the JASPAR 2024 core plants database (Rauluseviciute et al., 2024). Both tools were implemented in the MEME Suite v.5.5.5 (Bailey et al., 2009) and used with default parameters.

Motif contributions

To calculate the average contribution of each motif, we summed the contribution scores and calculated the mean for each motif occurrence identified by FIMO (Grant et al., 2011). We used DeepLIFT (Shrikumar et al., 2017) to obtain contribution scores for each base, using a tanh transformation to normalize the scores to the range of −1 to 1. The regulatory role of a motif (activator or repressor) was determined by the sign of the average contribution.

Motif co-occurrence

A motif pair was considered to co-occur if both motifs were present at least once within the same sequence. The co-occurrence percentage of a motif pair was defined as the proportion of sequences within a specific dataset that contain both motifs.

Motif-based activity prediction models

To comprehensively evaluate the relationship between motif composition and promoter activity and explore potential non-linear regulatory rules, we benchmarked a diverse array of machine-learning algorithms using motif occurrence frequencies as primary inputs. We implemented linear regression (including ridge and lasso variants), k-nearest neighbors regression, support vector regression, decision trees, and random forests using the scikit-learn library. To ensure optimal performance, hyperparameters for these models were fine-tuned using RandomizedSearchCV. In addition, an FNN was constructed using TensorFlow 2.5, featuring a fully connected architecture with two hidden layers of 24 neurons each, between the input layer (13 motifs) and the single-neuron output layer. Beyond motif frequencies, we also assessed the effect of additional sequence features by training linear models that incorporated GC content and DNA shape (“linear regression + GC/DNA shape”). All models were trained on 75% of the TargetGAN dataset, and their performance was evaluated on the remaining 25% of the dataset, as well as on WGAN-GP-generated and natural promoters, using PCC and mean squared error as evaluation metrics.
