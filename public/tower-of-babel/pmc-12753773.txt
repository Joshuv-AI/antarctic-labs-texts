Targeting tumor metabolic flexibility enhances radiotherapeutic efficacy via mitochondrial complex I Inhibition in an intracranial S180 sarcoma mouse model
Authors: Zhipeng Liu, Sa-Hoe Lim, Shin Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radiotherapy (RT) eliminates cancer cells by inducing DNA damage. However, its efficacy is often reduced by mechanisms such as radiation resistance, effective DNA repair, and hypoxia within tumors. Tumor cells utilize metabolic flexibility, dynamically switching between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to survive and resist RT-induced stress. This study investigates how inhibiting mitochondrial complex I (CI) with IACS-010759 (IACS) enhances tumor cell sensitivity to RT, with a particular focus on metabolic disruption, DNA damage response, and overall cell survival. The potentiated effects of IACS combined with RT were evaluated in S-180 sarcoma cells using four experimental groups: control, IACS, RT, and RT + IACS. Apoptosis proteins (BAX, BCL-2, cleaved caspase-3) and radiosensitivity markers (SOD2, γH2AX) were analyzed via Western blot. ROS levels were measured by fluorescence, cell cycle by EdU-incorporated flow cytometry, p53 and p21 via Western blot, and stemness by sphere formation. In vivo, mouse intracranial tumor models underwent MRI-based tumor monitoring and survival assessment. The RT + IACS group showed heightened apoptosis via BAX/BCL-2 and caspase-3 activation, reduced SOD2, elevated ROS, and persistent γH2AX, indicating impaired DNA repair. Cell cycle analysis indicated G1/G2 phase arrest, which was associated with increased p53 and p21 expression levels, indicating p53/p21-mediated regulation, and stemness was reduced. MRI indicated the greatest tumor reduction and improved survival in the RT + IACS group. The combination of RT with metabolic inhibition effectively disrupts metabolic flexibility in S180 sarcoma, leading to ROS accumulation, impaired DNA repair, enhanced apoptosis, and reduced tumor stemness. Targeting tumor cell metabolism represents a promising strategy for enhancing RT efficacy by overcoming multiple resistance mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29326-2.

Introduction

Cancer cells undergo significant metabolic reprogramming to support their rapid proliferation, survival, and resistance to therapy1,2. While the Warburg effect traditionally highlights a preference for glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen3,4, many tumors are now known to retain functional mitochondria and rely on both glycolysis and OXPHOS to fulfil their substantial energy and biosynthetic requirements5–7. This dual reliance exemplifies metabolic flexibility, enabling cancer cells to shift between pathways in response to environmental pressures and nutrient fluctuations, ultimately fostering therapeutic resistance8,9.

In well-oxygenated, rapidly proliferating tumor cells, both glycolysis and OXPHOS are upregulated to provide efficient ATP production and rapid biomass accumulation8,10. OXPHOS is critical for supporting tumor growth by supplying the ATP and biosynthetic precursors necessary for cellular proliferation11. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that OXPHOS is particularly important in therapy-resistant cancers, where metabolic plasticity enables cancer cells to switch between glycolysis and OXPHOS depending on factors such as oxygen availability and nutrient supply12,13. This inherent metabolic adaptability allows cancer cells to withstand stress from conventional treatments, such as RT, by selectively engaging the most advantageous energy pathway12,14–17. Thus, targeting metabolic regulation can enhance the efficacy of conventional therapies by limiting cancer cells’ metabolic adaptability18,19.

Inhibiting OXPHOS offers several theoretical advantages in cancer therapy. Disrupting mitochondrial function with OXPHOS inhibitors can deprive cancer cells of a key energy source, impairing their ability to proliferate and survive20. Moreover, inhibiting OXPHOS can increase oxidative stress by elevating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which may exacerbate DNA damage, especially in cells already exposed to treatments such as RT21. By imposing a higher oxidative burden, these inhibitors can effectively reduce the capacity of tumor cells to repair RT-induced damage, enhancing cell kill. This strategy could also alleviate tumor hypoxia by reducing oxygen consumption, thereby further enhancing RT efficacy22,23.

Furthermore, OXPHOS inhibition has been proposed as a potential strategy to target cancer stem cells (CSCs), which rely heavily on mitochondrial respiration for energy and survival24. CSCs are implicated in tumor recurrence and therapy resistance due to their ability to regenerate tumors following treatment25. By diminishing OXPHOS-dependent energy production, it may be possible to selectively weaken CSC populations, curtailing the likelihood of relapse and improving long-term treatment effectiveness26,27.

An effective strategy for inhibiting OXPHOS involves targeting CI of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), which plays a crucial role in mitochondrial respiration28. Various compounds, such as metformin, rotenone, MS-L6, BAY 87-2243, phenformin, and IACS, have been identified as selective inhibitors of mitochondrial CI, effectively disrupting ETC, reducing oxygen consumption, and impairing ATP production29–32. Among these inhibitors, IACS has gained particular attention due to its high selectivity and potency in preclinical studies. Although clinical trials have shown limited efficacy when IACS is used as a standalone therapy33, its preclinical potency and selectivity highlight its utility in elucidating how mitochondrial metabolism inhibition affects cancer cell behavior. Notably, emerging preclinical evidence suggests that combining IACS with RT can significantly improve therapeutic outcomes by amplifying oxidative stress and blocking DNA repair pathways. This enhanced therapeutic effect results in greater tumor regression and improved survival outcomes in experimental models34.

Despite the recognized importance of metabolic flexibility in cancer therapy resistance, the mechanisms by which metabolic regulation can be exploited to enhance RT efficacy remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to investigate the role of metabolic regulation in enhancing the efficacy of RT by targeting mitochondrial metabolism in metabolically flexible cancer cells using IACS. We hypothesized that CI inhibition via IACS could disrupt mitochondrial function, increase oxidative stress, and amplify RT-induced tumor regression28,35,36. By delineating how OXPHOS inhibition restricts metabolic adaptability, intensifies oxidative damage, and impairs DNA repair, we sought to identify new opportunities for more effective, metabolism-based combination therapies. Given the critical role of OXPHOS in cancer cell survival and resistance, understanding how OXPHOS inhibition can improve treatment outcomes is highly important for the development of more effective cancer therapies37.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

The murine sarcoma CCRF S-180 II cell line was purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Korea). High-glucose and low-glucose DMEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and antibiotic-antimycotic mixture were purchased from Gibco (USA), while crystal violet reagent was obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich (USA). IACS (HY-112037 A) and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) (HY-B0215) were acquired from MedChemExpress (USA). A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and a Glycolysis/OXPHOS Assay Kit (G270) were purchased from Dojindo Laboratories (Japan). The ROS-ID® Total ROS Detection Kit was obtained from Enzo Life Sciences (USA). FxCycle™ Violet Stain and the Click-iT™ EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit were purchased from Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Measurement of metabolic responses to irradiation and inhibitors

S-180 cells (3 × 10³ cells/well) were seeded into white 96-well plates (SPL, Korea) and cultured overnight to allow for cell adhesion. On the following day, cells were treated with or without 6 Gy irradiation. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 1.25 µM of an OXPHOS inhibitor (oligomycin or IACS) and 22.5 mM of the glycolytic inhibitor 2-DG, or fresh medium without any inhibitors, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Dojindo Laboratories). Under these conditions, ATP levels were measured by luminescence, and lactate levels were quantified via absorbance using the SpectraMax® i3x Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Each condition was tested in three technical replicates.

Cell viability

S-180 cells were plated in 96-well plates at densities of either 1 × 10³ or 3 × 10³ cells/well (n = 4 per condition) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. On the second day, the cells were treated with IACS at concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 nM, with or without RT at doses of 2, 4, 6, 8, or 10 Gy, via a Gammacell 3000 Elan irradiator (Best Theratronics Ltd., Ottawa, Canada). The treatments were administered for either 24–48 h, depending on the experimental setup. Following treatment, the culture medium was removed and replaced with 100 µL of complete medium containing CCK-8 at a dilution of 1:10. The cells were then incubated for an additional 4 h. Absorbance was read at 450 nm. Experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Cell growth assay

S-180 cells (1 × 10³ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The following day, the culture medium was replaced with either high-glucose or low-glucose medium, with or without 5 nM IACS. After 48 h, cell proliferation was observed under a light microscope. Additionally, cell viability was quantitatively assessed via the CCK-8 assay.

Western blotting

S-180 cells (4 × 10⁴ cells/well) were plated in 6-well plates and cultured overnight. The following day, cells were treated with 5 nM IACS and 6 Gy, after which the medium was replaced with fresh medium. After 48 h, the cells were washed three times with fresh medium and then lased in RIPA buffer (BioSolutions, Korea). The total protein concentration was determined using BCA protein assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified with a SpectraMax® i3x multimode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Protein samples (25 µg per well) were separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

The membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: monoclonal rabbit anti-SOD2/MnSOD (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution), monoclonal rabbit anti-Bax (Abcam, USA; 1:2000 dilution), monoclonal rabbit anti-Bcl-2 (Abcam, USA; 1:2000 dilution), monoclonal rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution), and monoclonal rabbit anti-β-actin (Abcam, USA; 1:50,000 dilution). This was followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) secondary antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution).

To detect γH2A.X, S-180 cells (6 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight. The next day, cells were subjected to the specified treatments. At various time points post-treatment (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 96 h), cells were harvested for Western blot analysis following a standardized protocol. The membranes were incubated with a polyclonal rabbit anti-γH2A.X antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution) and a monoclonal rabbit anti-β-actin antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:50,000 dilution), followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) secondary antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution).

To detect p21, S-180 cells (6 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, cells were treated with 6 Gy, with or without 5 nM IACS and 5 mM NAC. After 24 h, cells were harvested for Western blot analysis following a standardized protocol. The membranes were incubated with a monoclonal rabbit anti-p21 antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution) and a monoclonal rabbit anti-β-actin antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:50,000 dilution), followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) secondary antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:1000 dilution).

Specific protein bands were visualized using an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, USA). Band intensities were analyzed with ImageJ v1.42q, with protein levels normalized to those of β-actin. The full-length, uncropped Western blot images corresponding to Figs. 1a–d and 2a–b, and 3g–h are provided in Supplementary Figures S1–S8.

Cellular ROS detection

S-180 cells (5 × 10³ cells/well) were seeded into 8-well chamber slides (Thermo Scientific, USA) and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with or without 5 nM IACS, 6 Gy, or NAC for 48 h. After treatment, the cells were washed three times with fresh medium. The intracellular ROS levels were quantified via the ROS-ID® Total ROS Detection Kit.

Colony formation assay

S-180 cells (100 cells per well) were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ to allow for cell adhesion. The next day, cells were exposed to doses of 0 Gy, 4 Gy, or 8 Gy. Following RT, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, either with or without 5 nM IACS at its IC50 concentration, to assess the potential combined effect of IACS and RT. Cells were then incubated for an additional 8 days under standard culture conditions to allow for colony formation. After this incubation period, the medium was removed, and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, followed by two washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The fixed cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min to facilitate visualization of the colonies, with any excess stain washed away using distilled water. Colonies containing ≥ 50 cells were counted by two independent observers in a blinded manner. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

EdU incorporation assay and flow cytometry

S-180 cells (6 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, the cells were treated with 5 nM IACS, 6 Gy, 5 mM NAC, or a combination of these treatments. After 24 h, EdU labelling was performed via the Click-iT™ EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit and FxCycle™ Violet Stain, according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To detect p53, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 90% methanol. Cells were then stained with a monoclonal rabbit anti-p53 antibody (Abcam, USA; ab302697) at a 1:500 dilution. An isotype control antibody (Abcam, USA; ab199093) was used at the same concentration and under identical conditions as the primary antibody. Flow cytometry overlay histograms were used to depict p53 expression. Fluorescence signals were detected and quantified using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and the data were analysed using FlowJo v10 software.

Sphere formation assay

S-180 cells (1 × 10³ cells/well) were plated in 96-well round-bottom cell floating plates (SPL, Korea) and incubated overnight. The following day, the medium was gently aspirated, and fresh complete medium or medium containing 5 nM IACS was added. The cells were subsequently treated with 6 Gy. The sphere formation efficiency was calculated when the control group spheroids reached a diameter of 1.0 mm.

Generation and treatment of tumor-bearing mice

Female C57BL/6 mice (H2b, I-Ab), aged six to eight weeks, were purchased from Orient Bio (Iksan, Republic of Korea) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. All experimental procedures, including animal handling, care, and euthanasia, were conducted in strict accordance with NIH guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chonnam National University. Euthanasia was performed using CO₂ inhalation at a displacement rate of 30% chamber volume per minute. Death was confirmed approximately 5–6 min after CO₂ exposure by the absence of respiration and corneal reflex, followed by cessation of CO₂ flow. Cervical dislocation was subsequently performed to ensure complete euthanasia. All protocols were reviewed and authorized by the Chonnam National University Animal Research Committee prior to study initiation. The study is in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

To construct the intracranial (i.c.) mouse model38,39, a total of 1 × 10⁵ S-180 cells, resuspended in 5 µL of PBS, were delivered into the right striatum via a stereotaxic injection approach. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine/xylazine/saline mixture (120 mg/kg ketamine, 16 mg/kg xylazine), administered at 0.1 mL per 10 g body weight. The injection coordinates were precisely calculated via stereotaxic mapping, targeting a site 1 mm posterior and 2 mm lateral from the bregma and 4 mm ventral from the cortical surface. The cell suspension was infused at a controlled rate of 1 µL/min. Upon completion of the injection, the needle was retained in situ for an additional 5 min to allow for cell stabilization, followed by gradual needle retraction to minimize backflow and tissue disruption.

For the treatment, the mice were randomly divided into four groups: (1) control, (2) IACS, (3) RT, and (4) IACS + RT. Starting on day 4, the mice in the IACS and IACS + RT groups received 10 mg/kg IACS orally once daily for 30 consecutive days. To administer radiation, on day 6, mice in the RT and IACS + RT groups received focused cranial irradiation using a Leksell Gamma Knife® Perfexion™ system (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden). Mice were anesthetized and immobilized in a custom-designed stereotactic head frame to ensure precise targeting. A single dose of 10 Gy was delivered to the right hemisphere at a dose rate of 4 Gy/min, corresponding to the site of tumor implantation. All irradiations were performed in a shielded small-animal irradiation suite40–42.

Animal MRI

A high-resolution animal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system (M7™ Compact MRI Scanner, Aspect Imaging, Shoham, Israel) was utilized to acquire detailed tumor images in the mouse model. Anaesthesia was induced via the use of 2.7% isoflurane in a mixture of 70% nitrous oxide (N₂O) and 30% oxygen (O₂) to ensure optimal immobilization during imaging. A total of 100 µL of the contrast agent Magnevist (Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany) was administered intravenously. Ten minutes postinjection, a preliminary scan was performed to localize the brain. Imaging parameters were then adjusted across the sagittal, coronal, and horizontal planes to precisely target the tumor region, ensuring high-resolution diagnostic accuracy. Finally, T1-weighted MR mapping was conducted to visualize the tumor distribution and contrast enhancement.

Statistical analysis

All results are shown as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data between two groups were compared using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Multi-group comparisons were performed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. IC₅₀ values were calculated using four-parameter nonlinear regression models. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed with log-rank tests. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). All experiments were repeated at least three times unless otherwise noted.

Results

IACS inhibits S180 cells

To investigate the effects of mitochondrial CI inhibition on cellular metabolism, S180 cells were treated with IACS, oligomycin, and 2-DG, and both ATP and lactate levels were measured (Fig. 4a and b), IACS treatment significantly decreased ATP levels compared with the control group (p < 0.0001), demonstrating its effectiveness in inhibiting OXPHOS. Lactate production also showed a significant increase in the IACS group compared to the control group (p < 0.05), suggesting a compensatory upregulation of glycolysis in response to OXPHOS inhibition.

In contrast, the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG significantly reduced both ATP and lactate levels (p < 0.0001), highlighting the importance of glycolysis for energy production in S180 cells. Oligomycin treatment, which also targets mitochondrial ATP production, led to an increase in lactate levels like that observed in the IACS group, suggesting that cells enhance glycolytic flux when mitochondrial ATP production is impaired. These findings indicate that S180 cells maintain metabolic flexibility by upregulating glycolysis to compensate for the inhibition of mitochondrial function.

Previous studies have underscored the metabolic flexibility of S180 cells. To further assess the interaction between IACS and mitochondrial CI, we treated S180 cells with increasing concentrations of IACS for 24 and 48 h. As shown in (Figure 4c), the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were 27.96 nM at 24 h and 24.64 nM at 48 h, indicating both time-dependent and concentration-dependent increases in drug efficacy.

To evaluate the impact of glucose availability on the efficacy of IACS treatment, S180 cells were cultured under high glucose (25 mM D-glucose) and low glucose (5.5 mM D-glucose) conditions, with or without IACS treatment (Fig. 4d). The results demonstrated that IACS treatment significantly reduced cell viability under both glucose conditions. Notably, the inhibitory effect of IACS was more pronounced under low glucose conditions, as indicated by a significant reduction in cell viability, which was significantly lower compared to the high glucose condition.

These findings suggest that S180 cells possess metabolic flexibility, allowing them to adapt to varying energy sources depending on environmental conditions. Under low glucose conditions, S180 cells exhibit increased reliance on OXPHOS for energy production. When OXPHOS is inhibited by IACS, glycolysis alone is insufficient to compensate for the energy demand, leading to a marked decrease in cell viability. This highlights the dual dependence of S180 cells on both glycolysis and OXPHOS for energy generation and demonstrates their metabolic adaptability under different environmental stressors. However, when both glucose availability and OXPHOS are compromised, their metabolic adaptability is overwhelmed, resulting in a substantial decline in cell viability.

We also evaluated the combined effect of IACS and RT on ATP levels in S180 cells, as shown in (Fig. 4e). Under typical conditions, ATP levels increased following RT treatment compared to the control group (p < 0.01), indicating the activation of stress responses, enhanced glycolysis, mitochondrial compensation, or increased energy demands for DNA repair43. When combined with IACS, ATP levels were significantly lower compared to the RT treatment alone (p < 0.0001), but there was no significant difference compared to the IACS-only treatment, indicating that IACS effectively counteracts the increase in ATP induced by RT by inhibiting OXPHOS. (Fig. 4f) shows the effect of different treatments on lactate production. Both IACS and RT treatments significantly increased lactate production compared to the control group (p < 0.05), indicating that these treatments might compensate for energy demands by enhancing glycolysis. There was no significant difference between the IACS and RT groups in terms of lactate production. The RT + IACS combination group showed significantly higher lactate production compared to RT alone (p < 0.05) and IACS alone (p < 0.01), suggesting that the combination treatment led to a more pronounced glycolytic response, possibly to compensate for the decreased ATP production.

These results suggest that RT treatment leads to increased ATP levels, while IACS can inhibit this increase. However, the combined treatment did not further significantly reduce ATP levels compared to IACS alone, but instead led to increased lactate production, indicating that cells may be compensating for energy inhibition by upregulating glycolysis. Although the RT + IACS combination treatment significantly elevated lactate production—indicative of enhanced glycolysis—ATP levels remained similar to the IACS only group. This likely reflects the lower energy yield of glycolysis, which cannot fully compensate for the energy deficit arising from inhibited OXPHOS.

The schematic in (Fig. 4g) illustrates the metabolic flexibility of S180 cells, which utilize both glycolysis and OXPHOS to meet energy demands during rapid proliferation and stress responses. The ability of S180 cells to shift between glycolytic and mitochondrial energy production pathways allows them to adapt effectively to changing environmental conditions and treatment-induced metabolic stress, contributing to their resistance to therapeutic interventions.

Inhibition of OXPHOS enhances the radiosensitivity of S180 cells

Western blot analysis of γH2AX, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), revealed two key effects of combined treatment with RT and IACS on DNA repair. First, compared with RT alone, the combined treatment led to an earlier and more pronounced increase in γH2AX levels, with significantly greater expression observed as early as 2 h post-treatment (p < 0.05), suggesting that IACS accelerates the DNA damage response through OXPHOS inhibition (Figure 1a). Second, the combined treatment maintained elevated γH2AX levels over a longer duration compared with RT alone, with delayed decline observed in the RT + IACS group. Significant differences persisted at 24 h (p < 0.01), 48 h (p < 0.05), 72 h (p < 0.01), and 96 h (p < 0.001), indicating sustained DNA damage compared to the more rapid decline in γH2AX expression in the RT group (Figure 1b). These findings suggest that the RT + IACS combination impairs DNA repair, prolonging DNA damage and the DNA damage response, and underscores the enhanced radiosensitizing effect of IACS when combined with RT.

The expression of SOD2 (superoxide dismutase 2), a mitochondrial enzyme responsible for detoxifying superoxide radicals, was analysed across different treatment conditions to assess its role in modulating oxidative stress (Fig. 1c). The SOD2 level in the control group served as the baseline for comparison. Treatment with IACS significantly decreased SOD2 protein levels compared to the control group (p < 0.05), suggesting that inhibiting OXPHOS downregulates SOD2 and potentially leads to increased oxidative stress. Conversely, RT treatment significantly increased SOD2 levels compared to the control group (p < 0.05), indicating a compensatory antioxidant response to radiation-induced oxidative stress. Interestingly, the combined treatment of RT and IACS significantly reduced SOD2 levels compared to RT alone (p < 0.01), yet there was no significant difference between the RT + IACS group and the IACS-only group. Compared to the control group, SOD2 levels in the RT + IACS group were still lower (p < 0.05), indicating that IACS attenuates the RT-induced upregulation of SOD2. These results suggest that the combined treatment results in insufficient compensatory antioxidant response, thereby enhancing oxidative stress and potentially increasing the radiosensitivity of the cells.

The effect of combined treatment on the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 expression was assessed to determine its influence on apoptosis regulation (Fig. 1d). Western blot analysis revealed that BCL-2 levels in the RT + IACS group were significantly lower than those in the RT group at all time points (24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h). At 24 h, BCL-2 expression was significantly reduced in the RT + IACS group compared to the RT-alone group (p < 0.01). Similarly, at 48 h, the combined treatment group showed a reduction in BCL-2 levels compared to RT alone, with statistical significance (p < 0.05). At 72 and 96 h, BCL-2 expression in the RT + IACS group remained significantly lower compared to the RT group (p < 0.001 for both time points). IACS mitigates the RT-induced increase in BCL-2 expression, promoting a pro-apoptotic environment by downregulating anti-apoptotic signals, which likely contributes to enhanced apoptosis in the combination treatment group.

Finally, we assessed changes in radiosensitivity by analysing the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), an important marker of radiosensitivity, under different treatment conditions (Figure 1e). Fluorescence imaging of ROS revealed that the combination of IACS and RT led to a significant increase in ROS levels compared with either treatment alone or the control. The RT + IACS group presented the greatest accumulation of ROS, indicating elevated oxidative stress in the cells. This enhanced ROS production suggests that the inhibition of OXPHOS by IACS amplifies the oxidative stress induced by RT, ultimately increasing the radiosensitivity of the treated cells.

Enhanced apoptosis induction by IACS and RT in S180 cells

To evaluate the effects of IACS and RT on apoptosis in S180 cells, Western blot analysis was used to measure the levels of the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2, the proapoptotic protein Bax, and both the proapoptotic and cleaved forms of caspase-3 (P19 and P17). Actin and β-actin served as loading controls to ensure consistent protein loading across samples. Quantitative analysis of the activation of caspase-3 was conducted, which revealed fold changes across treatment groups.

Compared with that in the control group, Bcl-2 expression significantly decreased in the group treated with IACS (p < 0.01). Conversely, RT treatment alone caused an increase in Bcl-2 levels compared with those of the control (p < 0.01), possibly representing a compensatory survival mechanism in which cells upregulate antiapoptotic proteins to counteract RT-induced stress. However, compared with RT alone, the RT + IACS combination significantly reduced Bcl-2 expression (p < 0.0001), indicating that IACS counteracts the RT-induced upregulation of antiapoptotic proteins (Figure 2a).

Bax expression was significantly greater in the IACS group and RT group than in the control group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01) and further increased in the RT + IACS group (p < 0.0001). This combination treatment resulted in the highest levels of Bax expression, reflecting an enhanced proapoptotic shift under co-treatment conditions. The Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, a crucial indicator of a cell’s propensity to undergo apoptosis, was significantly greater in the RT + IACS group than in the control, IACS, and RT groups (p < 0.0001), demonstrating a marked shift toward apoptosis in the combination treatment group (Figure 2a).

Cleaved caspase-3 levels were significantly greater in the RT + IACS group than in all the other groups (control p < 0.001, IACS p < 0.01 and RT alone p < 0.001), indicating the greatest increase in apoptosis (Figure 2b). Unlike P19, cleaved caspase-3 P17 levels did not increase significantly with IACS treatment alone compared with the control, but RT alone increased P17 levels significantly (p < 0.001), indicating its ability to induce apoptosis through this specific fragment. The combination of RT and IACS did not further increase P17 levels, suggesting that IACS preferentially promotes P19 activation, enhancing apoptosis through this specific caspase-3 fragment (Figure 2b).

To assess the short-term inhibitory effects of IACS and radiation (RT), a CCK-8 assay was performed on S-180 cells treated with increasing doses of RT (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 Gy), with or without 1 nM IACS. Inhibition rates, calculated from normalized cell viability values, are presented as a heatmap (Fig. 2c). RT alone induced minimal inhibition (< 10%) across all doses. In contrast, the addition of IACS led to a marked, dose-dependent increase in inhibition, reaching 47% at 8 Gy, indicating that IACS enhances RT-induced cytotoxicity.

To assess whether the combination of IACS and RT produces enhanced effects beyond monotherapy, Bliss independence analysis was performed using the same dataset (Fig. 2d). The bar graph depicts the observed inhibition from the combination treatment (red bars) and the expected additive effect based on the Bliss formula: EBliss = EA + EB − EA × EB, are the inhibition rates from IACS and RT alone, respectively. At all tested RT doses, the observed inhibition exceeded the predicted additive effect (dotted lines), indicating that co-treatment with IACS and RT more effectively suppresses S-180 cell viability than either treatment alone.

To validate these findings in a long-term proliferation context, clonogenic assays were conducted under the same treatment conditions (Fig. 2e). Representative images showed a substantial reduction in colony number and size in the combination group. Survival fraction analysis revealed significantly decreased clonogenic survival with IACS + RT compared to RT alone. Bliss synergy scores, derived from colony survival data, are displayed as a heatmap, showing synergy at 4 Gy (Bliss score = 0.58) and 8 Gy (Bliss score = 0.46), further confirming that IACS enhances the sensitivity of tumor cells to radiation beyond the effects of either treatment alone.

Together, these results demonstrate that IACS enhances both the acute cytotoxicity and long-term growth suppression of S-180 sarcoma cells in combination with RT, supporting its potential as a metabolic radiosensitizer.

IACS enhances radiosensitivity by modulating ROS and the cell cycle in S180 cells

ROS levels were assessed via fluorescence imaging (Figure 3a). NAC, a known ROS inhibitor44,45, was used to explore the role of ROS in the observed cellular responses. Without NAC, cells treated with RT + IACS showed noticeably higher ROS levels compared to those treated with RT alone, suggesting that IACS may enhance RT-induced oxidative stress and potentially contribute to increased cellular damage. In contrast, the addition of NAC effectively reduced ROS levels, demonstrating that NAC can significantly diminish the oxidative stress exacerbated by the combined treatment.

The ability of S180 cells to form spheroids, which reflects long-term cell viability and tumor-forming capacity, was evaluated under different treatments (Figure 3b). Compared with RT alone, RT + IACS without NAC significantly reduced spheroid formation, indicating that the combination treatment effectively suppressed the proliferative potential of S180 cells. The addition of NAC rescued spheroid formation, particularly in the RT + IACS group, suggesting that the suppression of spheroid formation by IACS and RT may be mediated through ROS generation.

Flow cytometric analysis and corresponding bar graph quantification (Fig. 3c and d) were performed to examine the effects of IACS and RT on the cell cycle distribution of S180 cells. In the untreated control group, the majority of cells were in the S phase (53.3%), with 42.4% in the G0/G1 phase and only 3.06% in the G2/M phase, reflecting normal cell cycle progression. A significant increase in the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase population (59.9%) and a corresponding decrease in the percentage of cells in the S phase (34.8%) were detected in the cells treated with IACS alone. The percentage of cells in the G2/M phase remained low (4.05%), indicating that IACS induces G0/G1 arrest, slowing the transition from the G0/G1 phase to the S phase. RT alone caused a redistribution of cells within the cell cycle, with 39.0% of cells in the G0/G1 phase, 48.5% in the S phase, and a significant increase in G2/M phase arrest (10.8%). These findings suggest that RT enhances G2/M phase arrest, where cells are more vulnerable to radiation-induced damage while maintaining a large portion of cells in the S phase. The combination of IACS and RT resulted in the most significant alterations in the cell cycle distribution. A large proportion of cells accumulated in the G0/G1 phase (65.6%), indicating enhanced G0/G1 arrest compared with RT or IACS alone. The S phase population was drastically reduced (17.2%), suggesting that the combination treatment effectively blocks cells from progressing through DNA synthesis. Furthermore, G2/M phase arrest was significantly increased (14.2%), indicating that the combined treatment enhances G2/M arrest more than RT alone does. The bar graph in (Figure 3d) quantitatively reflects these trends, showing a significant increase in G0/G1 arrest in the IACS and RT + IACS groups compared with the control group (p < 0.001). Similarly, the RT + IACS group presented the most pronounced reduction in the S phase population (p < 0.001), accompanied by an increase in G2/M phase arrest (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that IACS combined with RT promotes dual-phase cell cycle arrest, contributing to enhanced radiosensitivity.

However, in the presence of NAC, the effects of combined IACS and RT treatment on the cell cycle were significantly altered, particularly in the G1, S, and G2/M phases. In the RT + IACS group, the percentage of cells in the G1 phase decreased from 65.6% (without NAC) to 58.0% (with NAC), indicating that ROS contribute to the prolongation of G1 arrest. The reduction in the G1 phase with NAC suggests that ROS play a significant role in maintaining the G1 arrest observed in the combined treatment. The most noticeable change with NAC was the restoration of the S phase population. In the RT + IACS group, the percentage of S-phase cells increased dramatically from 17.2% (without NAC) to 31.3% (with NAC). These findings indicate that ROS are critical for suppressing S phase progression and that their scavenging allows cells to proceed with DNA synthesis. The percentage of the G2/M phase population was reduced from 14.2% (without NAC) to 9.33% (with NAC), demonstrating that ROS are essential for the strong G2/M arrest observed in the RT + IACS group. The decrease in G2/M arrest with NAC suggests that the ability of the combined treatment to block cell cycle progression at G2/M relies heavily on ROS (Figure 3e).

Flow cytometric overlay histograms were used to assess p53 expression in S180 cells treated with IACS, RT, or a combination of RT and IACS (Fig. 3f). The results showed that p53 levels were notably increased in the RT + IACS group compared to RT or IACS alone, indicating an enhanced effect of the combined treatment on p53 upregulation. Elevated p53 expression is consistent with increased DNA damage and cellular stress, suggesting that the combination of IACS and RT enhances tumor suppression through p53-mediated pathways.

Western blot analysis was conducted to evaluate p21 protein expression in S180 cells under various treatment conditions, including a control group, an RT-only group, and an IACS-only group (Fig. 3g). The results indicated that both RT and IACS treatments individually led to increased p21 expression compared to the control, highlighting their role in inducing cell cycle arrest.

Further analysis of p21 protein levels in S180 cells treated with RT alone, RT + IACS, RT with NAC, and RT + IACS with NAC was also performed (Fig. 3h). The results showed a significant increase in p21 expression in the RT + IACS group compared to RT alone (p < 0.001), indicating that RT combined with IACS leads to stronger p21 upregulation than either treatment alone, potentially enhancing cell cycle arrest and radiosensitivity via complementary mechanisms involving mitochondrial inhibition and DNA damage.

Notably, the addition of NAC, a known ROS inhibitor, significantly reduced p21 expression, the RT + NAC treatment group exhibited significantly lower p21 levels compared to RT alone (p < 0.01), underscoring the contribution of radiation-induced ROS to p21 expression. Similarly, in the RT + IACS + NAC group, p21 expression was reduced compared to the RT + IACS group without NAC, emphasizing that ROS is crucial for the heightened p21 levels observed with the combined treatment.

The bar graph in Fig. 3h quantifies these changes, revealing significant differences in p21 expression across treatment groups and demonstrating the impact of ROS inhibition by NAC. These findings indicate that increased p21 expression following RT and IACS treatment contributes to enhanced cell cycle arrest, especially in the G0/G1 and G2/M phases, thereby reinforcing radiosensitivity via ROS-dependent pathways. The reduction in p21 levels with NAC treatment supports the notion that ROS is a critical mediator in this process, and reducing ROS may mitigate cell cycle arrest and decrease radiosensitivity.

Collectively, these results illustrate the essential role of ROS in regulating p21 expression in response to combined RT and IACS treatment. Elevated ROS levels not only induce oxidative stress but also activate key regulators like p53 and p21, promoting cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. The addition of NAC effectively reversed these effects, further confirming that ROS is central to the enhanced radiosensitivity observed. Therefore, the combination of IACS and RT emerges as a potent strategy for tumor suppression, leveraging oxidative stress and cell cycle regulation to achieve enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Discussion

Recent research has shown that tumor cells often exhibit significant metabolic flexibility, utilizing multiple pathways to sustain their energy needs and adapt to various stress conditions46. Our findings are consistent with this notion, as S180 sarcoma cells remained sensitive to CI inhibition by IACS despite their adaptability. This underscores the integral role of mitochondria in supporting energy homeostasis and oxidative stress management, even under treatment-induced pressures. This highlights the metabolic plasticity of tumor cells, where mitochondrial activity plays a crucial role in maintaining energy homeostasis and managing oxidative stress, especially during exposure to external stressors like RT14. By disrupting mitochondrial function, IACS exerts profound effects on oxidative balance and DNA repair capacity, reinforcing the idea that targeting mitochondrial metabolism can effectively sensitize tumors to therapy47.

The mitochondrial disruption induced by IACS leads to an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), predominantly resulting from electron leakage within the ETC48. This excessive ROS generation triggers oxidative stress, damaging key cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA49. Under normal conditions, superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) detoxifies superoxide radicals by converting them into less harmful hydrogen peroxide50. IACS treatment paradoxically downregulates SOD2 expression by disrupting mitochondrial CI function, thereby altering normal electron transport and redox signaling pathways. This metabolic imbalance leads to reduced transcription of SOD2, ultimately weakening the cell’s antioxidant defense51–54. This reduction of SOD2 impairs the cell’s ability to effectively eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing ROS levels to rise unchecked. Elevated ROS establishes a self-amplifying cycle of mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress, diminishing the cell’s resilience, particularly under RT exposure55.

Elevated ROS levels not only compromise mitochondrial integrity but also significantly impair the capacity of cells for DNA repair. Specifically, ROS-induced DSBs are characterized by increased γH2AX expression, a well-established indicator of the DNA damage response56. Our data show that combining IACS with RT leads to sustained γH2AX expression, reflecting persistent DNA damage and diminished repair capacity due to the disrupted metabolic state57,58. The accumulation of γH2AX highlights the inability of cells to repair damage, driving apoptosis, as DNA lesions accumulate beyond the repair capacity59. This interconnected network—comprising reduced SOD2, elevated ROS, lowered BCL-2, and persistent γH2AX—fosters a pro-apoptotic environment, particularly under combined RT and IACS treatment, thereby enhancing tumor cell susceptibility to cell death.

In addition to oxidative stress and DNA damage, the regulation of apoptosis is critically influenced by the balance between proapoptotic BAX and antiapoptotic BCL-2 proteins60. BCL-2 stabilizes the mitochondrial outer membrane to inhibit cytochrome c release and subsequent apoptosis61. Conversely, BAX promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), facilitating cytochrome c release and triggering the caspase cascade62. Our study revealed that IACS downregulates BCL-2 while upregulating BAX, thereby shifting the balance toward a pro-apoptotic state. This shift magnifies mitochondrial permeability and kick-starts apoptotic signaling, demonstrating that mitochondrial metabolic disruption can be leveraged therapeutically63,64.

Following mitochondrial dysfunction and the activation of BAX, caspase-3 acts as the primary enzyme responsible for executing apoptosis65. Cleavage of caspase-3 into its active fragments, p19 and p17, promotes the dismantling of cellular components and leads to cell death66. We observed that the combination of IACS and RT significantly enhances caspase-3 cleavage, particularly increasing the p19 fragment, highlighting a potentiated apoptotic response that could contribute to increased therapeutic efficacy67.

Furthermore, the combination of IACS and RT had significant effects on cell cycle regulation, specifically leading to G1 and G2/M phase arrest. Mitochondrial dysfunction induced by CI inhibition disrupts the OXPHOS pathway, depleting ATP levels crucial for cell cycle progression68. This energy deprivation stalls cells in critical phases, impeding DNA replication and repair processes that are energy dependent69. Additionally, elevated ROS levels activate checkpoint pathways that halt the cell cycle, contributing to the observed cell cycle arrest70. Elevated ROS levels led to increased expression of p53, which is a key regulator of cell cycle checkpoints and the DNA damage response. Activation of p53 subsequently upregulated p21, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, which effectively induced G1 arrest by inhibiting cyclin-CDK complexes responsible for the G1 to S phase transition71,72. This blockade prevents cells from repairing RT-induced DNA damage, priming them for apoptosis and enhancing the therapeutic effects of RT.

Importantly, our data revealed that IACS significantly reduces tumor stemness, as indicated by a decrease in spheroid formation capacity. Cancer stem cells (CSCs), which are responsible for tumor regeneration and resistance to therapy, often rely heavily on OXPHOS for their metabolic needs73. IACS disrupt this critical pathway, leading to reduced ATP production and impaired ROS management, which is essential for maintaining stem cell function74. Elevated ROS levels impair the survival and self-renewal capacity of CSCs, inducing differentiation and apoptosis and thereby reducing the ability of tumors to regenerate following treatment75. By targeting CSCs, the IACS enhances the overall radiosensitivity of tumors, suggesting that disrupting metabolic plasticity can be an effective strategy for improving therapeutic outcomes76.

The in vivo tumor model data strongly confirmed the therapeutic potential of combining IACS with RT, suggesting avenues for future studies to explore combining this treatment with other therapeutic agents to increase efficacy. The dual treatment reduced tumor growth, significantly extending the survival of the treated mice. This outcome reflects the combined effects of mitochondrial inhibition and radiation, which target both bulk tumor cells and CSCs and affect cell cycle progression The ability of this combination to prolong survival emphasizes its potential to overcome radioresistance, offering a robust strategy for enhancing cancer treatment efficacy77. The combination of IACS, an inhibitor of mitochondrial CI in the ETC, and RT enhances antitumor efficacy. RT directly increases ROS levels, damaging tumor cells, while IACS further disrupts cellular metabolism and weakens antioxidant defenses. Within the mitochondria, IACS inhibits CI, resulting in increased electron leakage and subsequent ROS production. Downregulation of SOD2 reduces ROS clearance, exacerbating oxidative stress and causing significant damage to mitochondria and other cellular components. Elevated ROS decreases anti-apoptotic BCL-2 and increases pro-apoptotic BAX, thereby raising the BAX/BCL-2 ratio and promoting MOMP. This initiates the release of cytochrome c, leading to apoptosome formation and activation of the caspase cascade, ultimately inducing apoptosis. The treatment combination also leads to cell cycle arrest at both the G₀/G₁ and G₂/M phases, with a corresponding reduction in the S-phase population, thus preventing DNA replication and cell division. Elevated oxidative stress triggers increased expression of p53 and p21, two key regulators of the cell cycle. As a tumor suppressor, p53 upregulates p21 transcription, which subsequently inhibits CDKs, effectively inducing cell cycle arrest and halting cellular proliferation. Furthermore, increased ROS diminishes the efficiency of DNA repair, particularly in repairing DSBs. The combined treatments also diminish the stemness of cancer cells, limiting their self-renewal capacity and reducing the likelihood of tumor recurrence. Collectively, these mechanisms—including increased oxidative stress, impaired DNA repair, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and reduced cancer cell stemness—contribute to significant tumor suppression and enhanced antitumor effects.

Despite the promising results, this study was conducted using a single tumor model—S-180 sarcoma—for both in vitro and in vivo analyses. This cell line was specifically selected for its intrinsic radioresistance and distinctive metabolic profile, making it a suitable platform to evaluate the radiosensitizing potential of mitochondrial complex I inhibition. Although GL261 glioma cells were initially considered, their markedly reduced sensitivity to IACS-010759 (IC₅₀ ≈ 5977 nM at 24 h) made them unsuitable for further mechanistic investigation (Supplementary Figure S1).

Overall, our findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting mitochondrial metabolism to enhance radiotherapy. By disrupting oxidative phosphorylation and redox homeostasis, IACS sensitized tumor cells to radiation-induced damage and impaired their self-renewal capacity. These results shed light on the metabolic vulnerabilities of treatment-resistant tumors and provide a rationale for further investigation of metabolism-based radiosensitization strategies in more clinically relevant models.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the combined application RT and mitochondrial metabolic inhibition significantly disrupts the metabolic balance of S180 sarcoma cells, leading to the accumulation of ROS, impaired DNA repair, increased apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and reduced tumor stemness characteristics. By simultaneously targeting OXPHOS and the radiation stress response in tumor cells, we observed a marked increase in radiosensitivity, which may help overcome resistance mechanisms encountered in conventional treatments. However, it is noteworthy that despite its preclinical promise, IACS did not achieve the anticipated efficacy in clinical trials, underscoring the complexity and challenges inherent in translating metabolic inhibition strategies into successful clinical interventions. Therefore, further investigation into the combined mechanisms of metabolic inhibition and RT is crucial, particularly in understanding the interactions among metabolic response, oxidative stress, and cell cycle regulation. In summary, targeting tumor cell metabolism offers a promising new direction for enhancing the effects of RT, laying the foundation for more effective cancer treatment strategies.
