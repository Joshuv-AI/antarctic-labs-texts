ABRLog: a multi-device android bugreport log dataset for intelligent analysis
Authors: Ricardo Miranda Filho, João Alfredo Bessa, Rosiane de Freitas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAndroid devices generate a wealth of diagnostic data through their built-in logging subsystems, offering invaluable insights into application behavior, hardware performance, and operating system stability. However, the effective utilization of this data for intelligent analysis — particularly, for anomaly detection, failure diagnosis, and security monitoring — remains constrained by the scarcity of publicly available, well-structured, and multi-device log datasets. To address this gap, we introduce ABRLog (Android BugReport Log), a comprehensive multi-device log dataset derived from bug report captures collected across 15 distinct Android devices representing five major manufacturers (Samsung, Motorola, Google, Xiaomi, and Huawei) and spanning six Android versions, from 9 to 14. A total of 45 bugreport archives were systematically processed through two complementary extraction strategies: (i) line-by-line regular expression matching for granular event extraction; and (ii) section-based delimiter parsing for high-level block segmentation. This dual approach yielded 1012 structured log files, organized into 24 semantically coherent block types. The final dataset comprises approximately 10 million log lines (1.14 GiB), extracted from 6.34 GiB of raw bugreport data. By providing a systematically curated, multi-vendor, multi-version log corpus, ABRLog aims to serve as a foundational resource for the development and benchmarking of machine learning models for log-based anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, and intelligent system diagnostics on Android platforms.Keywords: Android logs, Anomaly detection, Bug report, Data science, Log analysis, Machine learning, Mobile computing

Value of the Data

These data are primarily intended for three research communities: (i) researchers who design and benchmark log parsers, who need heterogeneous real-world input beyond the server-oriented corpora currently available; (ii) mobile systems and digital forensics researchers, who require full-device diagnostic snapshots rather than a single log stream; and (iii) machine learning practitioners working on anomaly detection and failure prediction over operational telemetry.

•Multi-manufacturer variability. The dataset covers five Android manufacturers (Samsung, Motorola, Google, Xiaomi, and Huawei) across 15 devices, exposing the lack of standardization in log formatting and organization across the Android ecosystem. This variability is essential for evaluating the robustness of log analysis tools.

Background

Logs in mobile devices systematically capture events, system activities, and application behavior, offering critical insights into performance, diagnostics, and user interaction. These records include system-level events, application-specific messages, and error logs, typically enriched with timestamps, event metadata, and contextual information. Their analysis enables developers, administrators, and researchers to identify performance issues, usage patterns, and potential system failures [2,3].

The proliferation of Android devices has made system logs integral to mobile computing research, serving as a primary data source for fault detection, performance optimization, and security analysis [1,4]. However, effective analysis is hindered by diverse formats and the unstructured nature of these logs across different device manufacturers and Android versions. Each manufacturer implements its own logging conventions, resulting in significant variability in how log data is organized within bugreport captures.

Android’s bugreport tool, accessible through developer options, generates comprehensive diagnostic dumps that include kernel messages, system logs, battery statistics, process information, network state, and dozens of other system-level data sections. These bugreport files typically range from 50 MB to 500 MB per capture, containing hundreds of thousands of lines of mixed log data. While this richness makes bugreports valuable for diagnostics, the lack of standardization across devices creates challenges for automated analysis.

Existing log datasets in the literature, such as the Loghub collection [2], primarily focus on server-side systems (HDFS, Hadoop, BGL) and provide single-type log streams. Datasets specifically targeting Android mobile device logs, particularly those capturing the full diversity of bugreport sections across multiple manufacturers, remain scarce. ABRLog was compiled to address this gap, providing structured log blocks and parsed references extracted from raw bugreport captures across a diverse set of Android devices.

This dataset was originally motivated by the need to evaluate log parser performance on real-world mobile data, as reported in our related studies [5,6]. The current expanded version significantly broadens the scope by increasing the number of devices from 7 to 15, manufacturers from 3 to 5, and extracted block types from 8 to 24, while also adding Drain-based parsed log references. This data article directly extends the collection first described at SBESC 2025 [6], from which it inherits the extraction methodology; the description of the Android logging subsystem summarized above is developed at length in that companion paper and is reproduced here only to the extent needed to make the present data article self-contained.

The ABRLog dataset is one of the outcomes of the SWPERFI (Software Performance Intelligence) Research, Development, and Innovation (RD&I) project, a partnership between the Federal University of Amazonas (UFAM) and Motorola Mobility LLC [7]. This project explores advanced data mining, optimization, and artificial intelligence techniques for software performance analysis. Within the same collaboration, the MUVY (A multi-view user-generated sports video dataset) was also developed, focusing on user-generated sports videos from multiple perspectives, demonstrating the project's broad scope, ranging from system-level log analysis to computer vision applications [8,9].

Coverage analysis

Most block types achieve 100% coverage across all 45 bugreport captures. The interrupts block has the lowest coverage (22.2%), as it is present exclusively in Samsung devices. The battery_capacity block (68.9% coverage) is absent from some Samsung devices that use a different battery reporting format. The Pressure Stall Information (PSI) blocks achieve 91.1% coverage, as they are unavailable on Android versions prior to SDK 30.

Comparison with existing datasets

Table 3 compares ABRLog with related log datasets commonly used in the literature. While most existing datasets originate from server-side or supercomputer environments, ABRLog and ChiDroid [1] are among the few targeting Android mobile devices.

While ABRLog is smaller in total volume than Loghub, it is unique in several respects: (1) it targets mobile devices rather than server infrastructure; (2) it provides 24 distinct block types from the same source captures rather than separate systems; (3) it covers five manufacturers and six Android versions, enabling cross-device and cross-manufacturer analysis; and (4) it preserves the per-device, per-capture organization that enables temporal analysis. Compared to other Android-focused datasets such as ChiDroid [1], ABRLog provides significantly broader device coverage (15 vs. 2 devices), multiple log types (24 vs. 1), and includes parsed log references.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data collection

Bugreport captures were collected manually from each Android device using the built-in BugReport tool, accessible through the device’s developer options menu. Each capture produces a compressed archive containing the main bugreport text file along with supporting files (dumpstate logs, HAL debug information, protobuf data). The main bugreport file is a single large text file (typically 50–500 MB) containing all diagnostic information in a structured but heterogeneous format.

For Samsung devices, the main file is named dumpstate.txt; for other manufacturers, it follows the pattern bugreport-[device]-[build]-[timestamp].txt. The extraction scripts handle both naming conventions automatically.

No host-side adb tooling took part in the capture: the report is produced by the device itself and the resulting archive was transferred to the workstation over USB, so the output format depends only on the Android build of each device. Because bugreport section layout varies across API levels, Table 6 reports the exact firmware of every device, read from the ro.build.* properties of each capture (build identifier, security patch level and Android release), which allows any observed formatting difference to be traced back to a specific build. The captures span API levels 28 to 34.

Before publication, every capture was screened for personally identifiable information with a scripted procedure distributed with the dataset (scan_pii.py). The screening applies ten rules, of two kinds: four whose syntax is self-evident (e-mail addresses, Brazilian phone numbers, MAC addresses and Android Account records) and six anchored on the name of the field (IMEI/IMSI/MEID, ICCID/MSISDN, device serial numbers, Wi-Fi SSIDs, GPS coordinates and notification text), so that bare digit sequences such as timestamps and process identifiers are not misread as identifiers. Plain-text captures, the protobuf artifacts (incident reports and tombstones), and the two bugreport archives shipped as ZIP files were all covered.

Everything the screening reported as identifying a person, directly or indirectly, was then pseudonymized with sanitize_dataset.py, which imports its detection rules from the screening script so that the two cannot diverge. In total, 287,264 occurrences of 15,525 distinct identifiers were replaced: 9528 e-mail accounts (including WhatsApp identifiers, whose local part is a telephone number), 4336 MAC addresses, 1194 Wi-Fi network names, 307 notification texts, 74 account records, 62 telephone numbers, 11 IMEI/IMSI values, 8 GPS coordinates and 5 ICCID/MSISDN values.

Replacement is deterministic: each identifier is mapped through HMAC-SHA256 under a random key onto a pseudonym that preserves the shape of the original — digit counts, quoting, and the user@domain form. A given account is therefore represented by the same pseudonym in every file and on every device, so cross-capture correlation is retained, while the identity behind it cannot be recovered from the published data. Distinct originals yield distinct pseudonyms, which matters because log parsing measures precisely the variability between lines: collapsing different values onto a single placeholder would alter the statistic the dataset exists to support. Length-sensitive containers (protobuf) receive replacements of identical byte length, and their wire format was verified to remain parseable afterwards. Device serial numbers were deliberately preserved, as they identify the handset rather than a person. Re-running the screening over the published data reports pseudonyms only, and the table mapping originals to pseudonyms was not published.

All processing times reported in this article were measured on a workstation with a 12th-generation Intel Core i7–12700H processor (14 cores, 20 threads), 32 GB of RAM and a 1 TB NVMe solid-state drive, running Windows 11 (build 26,200) and Python 3.11. Both the extraction and the parsing stages run single-threaded, and the single-pass design of the extraction step makes its runtime scale linearly with the total size of the raw captures.

Block segmentation

Two complementary extraction approaches were implemented to maximize coverage of the diverse data present in bugreport files.

Method 1: Line-by-line regular expression matching. This method applies compiled regular expressions to each line of the bugreport file, collecting lines that match specific log formats. This approach is effective for log types with a consistent per-line format that may appear in multiple locations throughout the file. Eight block types are extracted using this method:

•dmesg: Kernel log messages matching [timestamp] message with optional priority prefix.

•logcat: System log entries in standard format MM-DD HH:MM:SS.mmm PID TID LEVEL TAG: message.

•batterystats: Battery statistics entries with duration-based timestamps.

•battery_capacity: Battery capacity and computed drain summaries.

•cpuinfo: Per-process CPU usage entries.

The regular expressions were designed based on Android source code documentation available at Android Code Search (https://cs.android.com).

Method 2: Section-based delimiter parsing. Android bugreport files organize data into clearly demarcated sections identified by start and end markers (Fig. 8). A state-machine parser reads the file once and extracts all content between matching start and end delimiters. Sixteen block types are extracted using this method, including event_log, radio_log, kernel_log, processes, binder_state, binder_stats, network_stats, vm_stats, iptables, filesystems, log_statistics, and the PSI blocks.

Table 7 lists the complete set of extraction rules, enabling the segmentation stage to be reproduced without consulting the source code. Section rules capture the first occurrence of each section only, which avoids duplicating content when a bugreport repeats a header.

Log parsing with drain

The Drain algorithm [12] was applied to seven major block types to generate structured parsed logs. Drain uses a fixed-depth tree structure to efficiently parse log messages into templates by identifying the constant and variable parts of each log line. The following Drain3 configuration parameters were used: similarity threshold sim_th = 0.4, tree depth = 4, and maximum children per node max_children = 100. For each block file, Drain produces:

1.A templates file listing all discovered log templates with their occurrence counts.

A templates file listing all discovered log templates with their occurrence counts.

2.A parsed log file mapping each original log line to its corresponding template cluster.

A parsed log file mapping each original log line to its corresponding template cluster.

These parsed references enable researchers to benchmark other log parsing algorithms (e.g., Logram [13], BRAIN [14], NuLog [15]) against the Drain-generated output on real Android log data.

Extensibility

To add a new regex-based block type, add an entry to PATTERNS in extract_blocks.py. For section-based blocks, add an entry to SECTION_PATTERNS. Re-running the extraction script generates the new block files for all bugreports. The parsing step can similarly be extended to additional parsers.

Ethics Statement

The authors have read and follow the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief and confirm that the current work does not involve human subjects, animal experiments, or any data collected from social media platforms. Bugreport captures were collected from devices owned by the research team members. Personal data present in the raw logs — including account addresses, telephone numbers, network identifiers, and notification content, some of it relating to third parties who appear in the logs of the researchers' own devices — were located by a scripted screening procedure and pseudonymized before publication, as described in Experimental Design, Materials and Methods. The published dataset therefore does not expose personally identifiable information; the screening and pseudonymization scripts are distributed with the data, and the mapping between original values and pseudonyms was not published.
