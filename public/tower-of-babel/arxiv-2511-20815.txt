Data-driven model order reduction for wave propagation in materials with nonlinearities or damage
Saddam Hijazi, Nikiema Fulgence, Hannah Burmester, Natalie Rauter, Carmen Gräßle

Abstract




Abstract

In this work, we consider wave propagation in materials characterized by nonlinear properties or damage. To accelerate the simulations of the resulting high-dimensional problems, we apply model order reduction methods. Depending on the knowledge of the underlying equations and the availability of their discrete operators, intrusive methods (here projection-based approaches based on proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)) or non-instrusive methods (here data-driven approaches including dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) and operator inference (OpInf)) can be used. We recall the theoretical foundations of the methods and apply them to the problem of wave propagation. In three different numerical examples, we evaluate the performance of the reduction techniques.

Novelty statement

We apply the methods proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), dynamic mode decomposition (DMD), multiresolution DMD (mrDMD) and operator inference (OpInf) to different problems of guided ultrasonic wave (GUW) propagation which are the classical wave equation, a mechanical system resulting from GUW propagation and a nonlinear hyperelastic model. The operator inference approach is modified by two aspects: we introduce a scaling approach for the snapshot data (see Section
3.5
) and propose an unconstrained operator inference formulation which preserves structure and allows the employment of first order optimizers, see (
36
). Further, we continue and extend our study on the nonlinear aluminium model started in
[
26
]
.

1
Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are fundamental tools in science and engineering, as they describe a wide range of physical phenomena derived from conservation laws such as mass and momentum
[
64
]
. Their numerical solution typically relies on discretization techniques, most prominently the finite element method (FEM), which converts the PDE into a high-dimensional system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
[
77
]
.

In structural mechanics, the governing equation of motion takes the form of a PDE derived from the balance of momentum
[
40
]
. This PDE depends on parameters that influence the structural response, such as material properties, geometry, or boundary and initial conditions. In this work, we focus on parameters associated with material degradation, often referred to as
damage parameters
. The motivation stems from structural health monitoring (SHM), where the identification and localization of damage is essential to prevent catastrophic failures and to reduce maintenance costs
[
22
]
.

Solving parameterized PDE problems for multiple parameter values is often computationally prohibitive. The FEM discretization typically yields systems of very large dimension, leading to high computational demands in terms of both time and memory. Repeated simulations, as required in real-time control, uncertainty quantification, inverse problems, or optimization, can therefore become infeasible. To address this issue, the scientific community has developed a wide range of
model order reduction
(MOR) techniques over the last decades
[
35
,
3
,
60
,
6
,
28
,
10
,
63
,
62
]
. These methods aim to construct low-dimensional surrogate models that retain the essential dynamics of the high-fidelity system while significantly reducing computational costs. MOR has proven particularly effective for problems in computational mechanics, encompassing both structural and fluid dynamics applications
[
67
,
29
,
45
,
15
,
5
]
.

In this article, we focus on the problem of wave propagation in damaged materials within the context of SHM
[
22
]
. Non-destructive evaluation (NDE) techniques are commonly employed to detect and localize defects in structures. The objective of SHM is to leverage NDE methods to continuously monitor structural integrity in an automated manner, using appropriately placed sensors. Among the various approaches, a widely used technique is guided ultrasonic waves (GUW)
[
2
]
. In this method, guided waves are propagated through the structure, and their interaction with damage alters the signals recorded by the sensors
[
48
,
71
,
31
,
55
,
54
]
. The objective is to process these measured signals using numerical methods to characterize the presence, location, and severity of damage.
We consider in this work two main models which are a linear wave equation (Section
2.1
) and a linear mechanical system resulting from GUW propagation in materials with damage (Section
2.2
). In addition, we consider wave propagation in nonlinear elastic material without defects (Section
2.4
) as a first step to later include damage in that setting. The type of damage considered in this work is always a material degradation damage. For spatial discretization, we use the finite element method for both the wave equation and the equation of motion. In the case of the GUW simulations, the discretization in space and time is fine due to the high frequency of the simulation. This results in a high number of degrees of freedom of the discretized model raising the computational cost needed to perform the simulation. This motivates the use of model order reduction techniques in order to mitigate the computational costs associated with running the GUW simulations such that the reduced model can later be used to estimate the damage parameters solving an inverse problem.

Model order reduction techniques can broadly be classified into intrusive and non-intrusive approaches. Intrusive methods require full knowledge of the governing equations and direct access to the discretized system matrices, while non-intrusive methods construct reduced models from solution data (and, in some cases, derivative data) without explicit access to the full order operators. In this work, we examine exemplary approaches from both categories in the context of the wave equation and subsequently focus on non-intrusive techniques for the mechanical system arising from the GUW simulations. Different non-intrusive methods are compared in terms of accuracy, computational efficiency, and additional performance metrics.

Intrusive approaches in reduced order modeling include the well-known greedy reduced basis method
[
35
]
, the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
73
,
11
,
4
,
13
,
5
,
27
]
, and the proper generalized decomposition (PGD)
[
21
,
18
]
, among others. These methods require a projection of the full order equations onto suitable reduced basis spaces, and are therefore also referred to as
projection-based reduced order models
.

In contrast, non-intrusive methods can be broadly classified into two categories. The first relies solely on solution data to construct the reduced order model, while the second may require additional knowledge about the underlying system, such as the governing equations, forcing terms, or initial and boundary conditions. A representative example of the first category is the dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) method
[
68
]
, which is a purely data-driven technique that seeks the best linear operator to advance the discrete solution in time. Other fully data-driven approaches employ regression techniques or artificial neural networks (ANNs) to approximate the mapping between time/parameter inputs and the reduced basis projection coefficients
[
49
,
29
,
34
,
30
]
. Non-intrusive approaches of the second category may require, in addition to data, some prior information about the structure of the system. A prominent example is the operator inference (OpInf) framework, which approximates the governing operators that generate the observed solution data. Originally introduced in
[
58
]
, the approach has since been extended in various directions
[
23
,
8
,
61
,
76
,
70
,
72
,
24
]
.

Finally, we also highlight
hybrid reduced order models
, which leverage both physical equations and solution data. Examples include calibration-based methods
[
56
,
25
,
19
]
, data-driven filtering and correction techniques
[
75
,
41
]
, POD with interpolation
[
20
,
53
,
36
,
39
]
, and the use of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) at the reduced level
[
17
,
38
]
.

2
Model description

In the following the models considered in this work are introduced. Apart from the classical wave equation, mechanical systems arising from GUW simulations are used.

2.1
Wave equation

The first model we consider is based on the classical wave equation, in which damage is incorporated via parameterization. Following
[
51
]
, damage is characterized by its spatial coordinates and by a local reduction of the wave propagation speed. Here, we study a modification of this model as proposed in
[
14
]
by additionally introducing the damage size and considering a smoothing modification of the initial condition and the damage parameterization.

Let
(t_{0},t_{end})
be a given time interval,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the spatial domain and
\partial\Omega
describe the boundary of
\Omega
. The scalar initial boundary value problem of the wave equation reads as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{tt}(t,\mathbf{x})-c^{2}(\mathbf{x})\Delta u(t,\mathbf{x})&=f(t,\mathbf{x}),&&\quad\text{in }(t_{0},t_{end})\times\Omega,\\
u(t,\mathbf{x})&=b(t,\mathbf{x}),&&\quad\text{on }(t_{0},t_{end})\times\partial\Omega,\\
u(t_{0},\mathbf{x})&=u_{0}(\mathbf{x}),&&\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{t}(t_{0},\mathbf{x})&=u_{1}(\mathbf{x}),&&\quad\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Here,
u
represents the displacement,
c
is the wave speed coefficient and
f
is the external forcing term. The initial conditions for the solution and the first time derivative at
t=t_{0}
, respectively, are given by
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
, which specify the initial displacement and its first partial temporal derivative, respectively. On the boundary
\partial\Omega
, the displacement is prescribed via Dirichlet boundary conditions given through the function
g
. Details on the choices for the data used in the numerical simulations are provided in Section
4.1
. For the numerical solution of (
1
), we use piecewise linear finite elements for discretization in space and the Crank-Nicolson scheme for discretization in time, see Section
2.4
for more details on this.

2.2
Mechanical system resulting from GUW propagation

The second model we investigate is a mechanical system resulting from guided ultrasonic wave (GUW) propagation in the presence of damage.
The governing equation which describes the virtual displacement resulted from the GUW reads as:

Div\,\mathbf{P}+\rho_{0}\mathbf{b}=\rho_{0}\mathbf{\ddot{u}}.

(2)

Here,
Div
is the material divergence operator,
\mathbf{P}
is the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor,
\rho_{0}
is the density of the material,
\mathbf{b}
is the density of the volumetric forces and
\mathbf{u}
is the displacement. The equation of motion (
2
) is solved together with the following boundary conditions for both the displacement
\mathbf{u}
and the stress tensor
\mathbf{P}
:

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{\bar{u}}\quad\textrm{at}\quad S_{u},\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbf{P}\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{\bar{t}^{I}}\quad\textrm{at}\quad S_{\sigma}.

(3)

Here, the boundary of the domain is given by
\partial\Omega=S_{u}\cup S_{\sigma}
, thus
\mathbf{\bar{u}}
is the value of the displacement field at the Dirichlet boundary
S_{u}
,
\mathbf{N}
is the normal vector and
\mathbf{\bar{t}^{I}}
is the strain vector at the Neumann boundary
S_{\sigma}
. In addition we have initial conditions which describe the displacement and first time derivative as follows:

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{0}\quad\textrm{at}\quad t=0,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbf{\dot{u}}=\mathbf{\dot{u}}_{0}\quad\textrm{at}\quad t=0.

(4)

The equation of motion is discretized using the finite element method, employing quadrilateral plane strain elements and quadratic Lagrange shape functions for spatial discretization. The time discretization is conducted within the commercial software of COMSOL Multiphysics
®
.
In the numerical experiments, we consider the linear two-dimensional model of fiber metal laminate (FML) with damage considered in
[
52
,
50
,
37
]
, see Section
4.2
for more details.

2.3
Nonlinear hyperelastic model

The third model considered in this work involves hyperelastic material with nonlinearity, see e.g.
[
7
]
. For this purpose we give a brief introduction of how the nonlineariy is entering the mathematical formulation of the problem.

As in the last section, we have the governing equation being the equation of motion in (
2
). We introduce the deformation gradient
\mathbf{F}
which relates to the displacement through the following relation

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{I}+\nabla\mathbf{u},

(5)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the unity tensor. The determinant of
\mathbf{F}
is called the Jacobian of the motion and is denoted by
J
. We further introduce rotation-independent deformation tensor denoted by
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}
which is called the right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor. In addition, we denote by
\mathbf{S}
the second Piola-Kirchoff tensor which relates to
\mathbf{P}
by the relation
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{F}^{T}
. The equation of motion could be re-written using the second Piola-Kirchoff tensor
\mathbf{S}
.

If we assume hyperelastic material behavior, these materials are characterized by the existence of a strain energy density function
\Psi
as the potential of the stresses
[
7
,
33
]
. Based on this, the second Piola-Kirchoff stress tensor is given as:

\mathbf{S}=2\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\mathbf{C}}.

(6)

We address one example of nonlinear material models which is the well known Neo-Hookean model. The Neo-Hookean material model serves as a simple extension of Hooke’s law to large deformations. For compressible isotropic material behavior, the strain energy function as a function of the right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor and the determinant of the material deformation tensor is given by:

\mathbf{\Psi}_{\textrm{Neo}}(\mathbf{C})=\frac{1}{2}\lambda{(\ln J)}^{2}-\mu\ln\left(J\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mu(tr(\mathbf{C})-3),

(7)

where
\lambda
is the first Lamé parameter and
\mu
is the shear modulus or the second Lamé parameter
[
59
]
. Then the resulting stress tensor is given by:

\mathbf{S}=\mu\ln(J)\mathbf{C}^{-1}+\mu(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{C}^{-1}).

(8)

The equation of motion is coupled with boundary and initial conditions as in (
3
) and (
4
).

3
Model order reduction techniques

In this work, we consider a common challenge that often appears in computational sciences: time series solution data of the state variable are generated by a numerical solver to which the user may not have full access. Such a situation motivates the search for an approximation of the mathematical system which produced the numerical data. Operator inference (OpInf, see Section
3.2
) utilizes knowledge of the structure of the algebraic form in order to infer reduced operators directly from solution snapshots.
In case we do not have any knowledge of the structure of the underlying dynamical system, dynamic mode decomposition (DMD, see Section
3.6
) can be considered which is entirely data-driven and extracts coherent spatio-temporal modes and associated dynamics. Both operator inference and DMD are non-intrusive approaches as they do not require the full order system matrices. In contrast, classical intrusive MOR methods like POD-Galerkin (see Section
3.3
) demand access to the full order matrices before reduction. In the following, we briefly recall these three approaches and then demonstrate their application to the second order models (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
).

3.1
Snapshot data

Let us introduce the notation for the snapshot data to which we refer in the subsequent chapters.
At given equidistant time instants
0=t_{1}<\dots<t_{M}=T
the data is gathered which consists of the input signals
\mathbf{z}(t)
and the nodal displacements of the mechanical system collected in the vector
\mathbf{u}(\bm{\theta},t)
at the given time points
t=t_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,M
for a fixed parameter
\bm{\theta}
. The snapshot matrices of the displacement and input signals are formed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}|&\cdots&|\\
\mathbf{u}(\bm{\theta},t_{1})&\cdots&\mathbf{u}(\bm{\theta},t_{M})\\
|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},\quad\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}|&\cdots&|\\
\mathbf{z}(t_{1})&\cdots&\mathbf{z}(t_{M})\\
|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{I}\times M}.

(15)

3.2
The operator inference approach: motivation and data preparation

The basic idea of operator inference is to recover a numerical model directly from snapshot data by approximating the system operators. Usually, the structure of the system that resulted in the numerical data is known to a certain extent. This partial knowledge is used to formulate a mathematical problem for the approximation of the system operators. Since the estimation of the full order operators would be computationally immense, one directly estimates reduced system operators which are of much smaller dimension. In
[
58
]
, this approach has been proposed and is termed as operator inference. The operator inference approach has also been extended to the non-linear case and to specific physical problems, see, e.g.
[
8
,
61
,
76
]
. An operator inference method tailored for second order mechanical systems has been proposed in
[
23
]
, which we closely follow here to apply it to our mechanical systems of interest.

In the operator inference approach, derivative information of the solution data is required. In many occasions, the numerical solver may provide this data. If that is not the case, then one may approximate the derivatives using finite difference schemes, e.g. using an eighth order finite difference scheme, see
[
69
]
. The second derivatives of the displacement are assembled in the following snapshot matrix:

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{U}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}|&\cdots&|\\
\ddot{\mathbf{u}}(\bm{\theta},t_{1})&\cdots&\ddot{\mathbf{u}}(\bm{\theta},t_{M})\\
|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}.

(16)

In order to obtain a reduced version of the snapshot data, POD can be used as follows:

\mathbf{\widehat{U}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{U},\quad\ddot{\mathbf{\widehat{U}}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\ddot{\mathbf{U}},

(17)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}
is the matrix of POD modes computed using e.g. a singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{U}
, see subsection
3.3
.

3.3
The intrusive POD-Galerkin ROM for second order mechanical systems

In this subsection, we describe the intrusive POD-Galerkin ROM for a mechanical system of second order without damping. For the derivation of a reduced order approximation of the full order model (FOM) (
12
) and (
13
), respectively, the FOM displacement field
\mathbf{u}(\bm{\theta},t)
is approximated using a Galerkin ansatz as follows:

\mathbf{u}(\bm{\theta},t)\approx\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t),

(18)

with coefficient vector
\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
of dimension
r\ll N
and reduced basis matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}=[\bm{\phi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\phi}_{r}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
. Here, the reduced basis vectors
\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
are generated using the POD method. For this a SVD of the snapshot matrix of the displacement can be used:

\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{T},

(19)

where
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a matrix containing the singular values in descending order,
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the matrix of left singular vectors and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
is the matrix of right singular vectors. Then, a reduced basis
\mathbf{\Phi}
is defined as the first
r
columns of the matrix
\mathbf{V}
.

By inserting the reduction assumption (
18
) in (
12
) and (
13
), respectively and performing a Galerkin projection onto the POD space, one may obtain the following linear reduced order dynamical system for a fixed parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{D}
:

\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{\Phi}\;\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t)+\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{K}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{\Phi}\;\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t)=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{f}(t),\quad\textrm{for}\quad t\in[0,T],

(20)

and nonlinear system, respectively:

\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{\Phi}\;\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t)+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{\Phi}\;\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t))=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{f}(t).

(21)

We define the reduced operators as follows:

\mathbf{M}_{r}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{\Phi},\quad\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{K}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{\Phi},\quad\mathbf{f}_{r}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{f}(t).

(22)

Thus, the linear reduced order system can be written as:

\mathbf{M}_{r}\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t)+\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t)=\mathbf{f}_{r},\quad\textrm{for}\quad t\in[0,T].

(23)

In case model (
11
) is considered, we obtain the right-hand side

\mathbf{B}_{r}\mathbf{z}(t),\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{z}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{I}},

(24)

where
\mathbf{B}_{r}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{I}}
is the reduced control operator.

Remark 3.1

(Hyper reduction)
.

We note that the reduced stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})
in (
23
) depends on the parameter
\bm{\theta}
and is in general not parameter separable, i.e. in general it cannot be written in the form
\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\alpha_{q}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{k}_{q}
with parameter dependent coefficients
\alpha_{q}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}
and parameter independent matrices
\mathbf{k}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
for
q=1,\dots,Q
. Therefore, for each new parameter
\bm{\theta}
the construction of the reduced stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})
requires the assembly of the full order stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\theta})
and its projection to the reduced space, so the reduced model remains coupled to the high dimension
N
. This bottleneck for multi-query scenarios is well-known such that hyper reduction techniques are developed like matrix gappy POD
[
16
]
or matrix DEIM
[
74
]
.

3.4
The operator inference approach for second order mechanical systems

In this subsection, we recall the main ideas of the operator inference approach applied to the linear second order mechanical system and closely follow
[
58
,
23
]
.
First, we write the reduced order system (
23
) (but for the case of input signal at the right hand side) for all time snapshots at once as follows:

\mathbf{M}_{r}\ddot{\mathbf{U}}_{r}+\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{U_{r}}=\mathbf{B}_{r}\mathbf{Z},

(25)

where
\mathbf{U_{r}}
and
\ddot{\mathbf{U}}_{r}
are the matrices of the ROM displacement and acceleration (second time derivative of the solution), respectively, and are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}|&\cdots&|\\
\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t_{1})&\cdots&\mathbf{u}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t_{M})\\
|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times M},\quad\ddot{\mathbf{U}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}|&\cdots&|\\
\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t_{1})&\cdots&\ddot{\mathbf{u}}_{r}(\bm{\theta},t_{M})\\
|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times M}.

(26)

Multiplying both sides of (
25
) by
\mathbf{M}_{r}^{-1}
(assuming that
\mathbf{M}_{r}
is invertible) from the left yields the following dynamical system:

\ddot{\mathbf{U}}_{r}+\mathbf{M}_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{r}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{U_{r}}=\mathbf{M}_{r}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{r}\mathbf{Z}.

(27)

The reduced dynamical system (
27
) is used as a model for inferring the reduced operators by putting forward a least squares problem formulation. Namely, we seek to identify the second order mechanical system which takes the following form:

\ddot{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}}(t)+\mathbf{\widehat{K}}_{M}\mathbf{\widehat{u}}(t)=\mathbf{\widehat{B}}_{M}\mathbf{z}(t),\quad\textrm{for}\quad t\in[0,T],

(28)

where
\mathbf{\widehat{K}}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\mathbf{\widehat{B}}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{I}}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{u}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. This is done using the reduced data in (
17
) and the input signals
\mathbf{z}(t)
. The system matrices are identified by solving the following minimization problem:

\min_{\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{M},\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{M}}\|\ddot{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}}+\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{M}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}-\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{M}\mathbf{Z}\|_{F}^{2},

(29)

where for a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
the Frobenius norm is defined by
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\sum^{m}_{i=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}{|a_{ij}|}^{2}}
with
a_{ij}
denoting the entries of
\mathbf{A}
.

The resulted operators
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{M}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{M}
can then be used to integrate the second order mechanical system in (
28
) and obtain the operator inference reduced solution.
Then by multiplying the reduced solution from the left by the matrix of POD modes
\mathbf{\Phi}
, one can approximate the FOM displacement. We note that this approach requires no access to the full order model code or algorithm. Therefore, it falls under the category of non-intrusive reduced order models or data-driven reduced order models. The approach so far assumes partial knowledge about the external forces represented in the input signals
\mathbf{z}
and is termed as
unconstrained operator inference

[
23
]
. In the remaining part of this section we will recall another version of operator inference which is based on full knowledge of the forces. This version is termed
forces-informed operator inference

[
23
]
.

Assume that the external forces or load vector
\mathbf{f}
is available at each time instant of the numerical simulation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}|&\cdots&|\\
\mathbf{f}(t_{1})&\cdots&\mathbf{f}(t_{M})\\
|&\cdots&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}.

(30)

In this case, the reduced solution of the intrusive ROM satisfies the following system:

\mathbf{M}_{r}\ddot{\mathbf{U}}_{r}+\mathbf{K}_{r}\mathbf{U_{r}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{F}.

(31)

The goal is to infer the reduced operators using the data for the solution and the second time derivative and the data of the forces. This implies that we are seeking a reduced order system which takes the following form:

\mathbf{\widehat{M}}\ddot{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}}(t)+\mathbf{\widehat{K}}\mathbf{\widehat{u}}(t)=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}(t),\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T],

(32)

with the condition that
\mathbf{\widehat{M}}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{K}}
are symmetric positive definite matrices:

\mathbf{\widehat{M}}\succ 0,\quad\mathbf{\widehat{K}}\succ 0.

(33)

The reduced forces data can be computed in a similar way to the reduced displacement as
\mathbf{\widehat{F}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{F}
.

The forces-informed operator inference problem is given by:

\min_{\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\succ 0,\widehat{\mathbf{K}}\succ 0}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\ddot{\mathbf{\widehat{U}}}+\widehat{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{\widehat{U}}-\mathbf{\widehat{F}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(34)

We remark that it is possible for certain optimization algorithms to transform the optimization problem in (
34
) into an unconstrained one. This is done by assuming that the mass and the stiffness matrices admit the following decompositions:

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{L}^{T}\mathbf{L},\quad\widehat{\mathbf{K}}=\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{W},

(35)

which implies that the unconstrained optimization problem for the forces-informed operator inference is:

\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{W}}\left\lVert\mathbf{L}^{T}\mathbf{L}\ddot{\mathbf{\widehat{U}}}+\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\widehat{U}}-\mathbf{\widehat{F}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(36)

Remark 3.2

(Operator inference for a nonlinear model)
.

We note that we consider operator inference here only for the linear mechanical model of Section
2.2
due to the complex structure of the nonlinear model. There exist approaches for operator inference for nonlinear models, see e.g.
[
57
,
44
]
, where the nonlinearity requires to be of polynomial structure or can be lifted to polynomial structure using a variable transformation, see
[
61
]
. In
[
9
]
an operator inference approach is introduced for systems with non-polynomial nonlinear terms which requires non-polynomial terms analytically.

3.5
Scaling the data for the operator inference approach

In several scientific applications, one may encounter the issue of having data which belong to different numerical scales. This issue could pose a difficulty for the operator inference approach and in particular for solving the optimization problem both in the unconstrained and in the forces-informed case. Therefore, we propose to deal with this problem by introducing scaling of the data snapshot matrices as follows:

\ddot{\tilde{\mathbf{U}}}=\frac{\ddot{\mathbf{U}}}{\lVert\ddot{\mathbf{U}}\rVert_{F}},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{U}}=\frac{\mathbf{U}}{\lVert\mathbf{U}\rVert_{F}},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}=\frac{\mathbf{Z}}{\lVert\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{F}}.

(37)

In case of employing the forces-informed approach the matrix
\mathbf{F}
will substitute the matrix
\mathbf{Z}
. The reduced order basis matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}
is obtained by doing a SVD on the newly scaled matrix of the displacement snapshots:

\tilde{\mathbf{U}}=\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\tilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{T},

(38)

and by retaining the first
r
columns of the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}
. The approach continues with computing the reduced data based on the scaled snapshots:

\mathbf{\widehat{U}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\mathbf{\tilde{U}},\quad\ddot{\mathbf{\widehat{U}}}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{T}\ddot{\tilde{\mathbf{U}}}.

(39)

Then, we solve the operator inference problem in (
29
) but with
\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}
instead of
\mathbf{Z}
.

The effects of the data scaling have to be considered when solving the reduced order system which takes the following form:

\frac{1}{\lVert\ddot{\mathbf{U}}\rVert_{F}}\ddot{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}}(t)+\frac{1}{\lVert\mathbf{U}\rVert_{F}}\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{M}\mathbf{\widehat{u}}(t)=\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{M}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(t),\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(t)=\frac{\mathbf{z}(t)}{\lVert\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{F}}.

(40)

After solving (
40
), an approximation of the full displacement can be computed by
\mathbf{u}^{\text{OpInf}}(t)=\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{\widehat{u}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

3.6
Dynamic mode decomposition

Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) is a purely data-driven approach proposed in
[
68
]
to extract spatio-temporal coherent structures from high-dimensional dynamical systems. For a comprehensive study of the method, we refer to
[
12
,
32
,
46
]
, for example. The basic idea is to treat a sequence of measurement snapshots as observations of an underlying linear operator (often interpreted as a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator, see
[
66
]
). In order to characterize the system’s dynamics, DMD computes eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this linear operator. The resulting modes evolve exponentially in time allowing to identify growth rates or oscillatory patterns without explicitly knowing the underlying governing equations.

Let us recall the main steps of the DMD algorithm. We denote the discrete approximation of the displacement for a fixed parameter
\bm{\theta}
at time
t_{k}
by
\mathbf{u}_{k}\approx\mathbf{u}(\bm{\theta},t_{k})
. The DMD technique seeks a best-fit linear operator
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
such that

\mathbf{u}_{k+1}\approx\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{k},\quad k=1,\dots,M-1.

(41)

Using the notations

\mathbf{U}=\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\
\mathbf{u}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{u}_{M}\\
|&&|\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}

(42)

and

\mathbf{U}_{j}^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\
\mathbf{u}_{j}&\cdots&\mathbf{u}_{k}\\
|&&|\end{bmatrix}

(43)

the DMD problem (
41
) can be written utilizing two time-shifted snapshot matrices as

\mathbf{U}_{2}^{M}\approx\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}_{1}^{M-1}.

The solution is given by:

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}_{2}^{M}{(\mathbf{U}_{1}^{M-1})}^{\dagger},

(44)

where
\dagger
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. However, explicitly forming and storing the full operator
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and consequently calculating its spectral decomposition quickly becomes infeasible when
N
is large. In order to circumvent this, a reduced operator
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
with
r\ll N
is constructed via

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{U}_{2}^{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},

where
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
denotes the POD modes,
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
contains the first
r
leading singular values on the diagonal and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1\times r}
has as its columns the associated right singular vectors from a truncated SVD
of
\mathbf{U}_{1}^{M-1}
given by

\mathbf{U}_{1}^{M-1}\approx\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}^{T}.

This reduced operator
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
allows to construct a model for the computation of the reduced coefficients
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
as

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k},

where the full order state
\mathbf{u}
can be reconstructed via
\mathbf{u}_{k}=\mathbf{V}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
.
We compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the reduced operator
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
as

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{\Psi}=\mathbf{\Psi}\mathbf{\Lambda}.

It can be shown that the reduced operator
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
has the same eigenvalues as the full order operator
\mathbf{A}
, see
[
32
]
. We use the notation
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})
and refer to
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
as the DMD eigenvalues. The eigenvectors of the reduced operator
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
can be utilized in order to construct the DMD modes
2
2

2

Note that we use the notation
\mathbf{\Phi}
to refer to the DMD modes in this Section and the POD modes in Section
3.3
; the intended interpretation is clear from the context.

\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times r}
as

\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{U}_{2}^{M}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}.

These DMD modes are the eigenvectors of the full order operator
\mathbf{A}
such that we can compute the spectral decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
without the need to carry out explicit computations on the matrix
\mathbf{A}
directly.
Utilizing the spectral decomposition
(\mathbf{\Phi},\mathbf{\Lambda})
it is possible to reconstruct the system state, here written in a formulation which is continuous in time (see e.g.
[
12
, (7.27)]
):

\mathbf{x}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}b_{j}\bm{\phi}_{j}e^{\omega_{j}t}=\mathbf{\Phi}\,\exp(\Omega t)\,\mathbf{b},\quad\omega_{i}=\log(\lambda_{i})/\Delta t.

(45)

For the computation of the initial mode amplitudes
\mathbf{b}
, we consider two choices in this work: the first option (see e.g.
[
12
, (7.29)]
) is given by

\mathbf{b}={\left(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)}^{-1}\mathbf{\tilde{x}}_{1},

(46)

where the second expression computes the amplitudes using POD projected data.
As second option, the optimal vector of amplitudes
\mathbf{b}
could be computed by solving an optimization problem as mentioned in
[
42
]
(section II.B). The final formula for the optimal
\mathbf{b}
is:

\mathbf{b}=\left(\left(\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}\right)\circ\left(\overline{\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{and}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{and}}^{*}}\right)\right)^{-1}\overline{\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{and}}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}\right)},

(47)

where
\circ
is the elementwise multiplication of two matrices, the overline denotes the complex-conjugate of a vector (matrix) and
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{and}}
is the Vandermonde matrix defined as:

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{and}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{1}^{M}\\
1&\lambda_{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{2}^{M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\lambda_{r}&\ddots&\lambda_{r}^{M}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times M}.

(48)

For the rest of the work, we refer to this approach as optimal DMD. The DMD approximation of the snapshot matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be also computed as follows:

\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{DMD}}=\mathbf{\Phi}\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{b})\,\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{and}}.

(49)

While classical DMD constructs spatio-temporal modes that are coherent over the entire time series, a well-known challenge arises in case phenomena appear only locally over short intervals. In order to overcome this limitation, multiresolution DMD (mrDMD) was introduced in
[
47
]
. The main idea of mrDMD is to combine DMD with a hierarchical, time-localized analysis and thereby separating the dynamics across multiple temporal scales. This not only retains the long-lasting global behaviors captured by classical DMD but also identifies localized patterns. In practice, the application of mrDMD involves recursively partitioning the time domain into different time levels with smaller sub-windows and applying DMD at each level of resolution. We denote the time domain split at level
j
and sub-window
w
as:

\mathbf{X}_{j,w}=[\mathbf{x}_{w_{1}},\mathbf{x}_{w_{2}},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{w_{k}}]
,
here
w_{1},w_{2},…,w_{k}
represent the time indices in the sub-window. At each level
j
, for sub-window
w
, standard DMD is applied to
\mathbf{X}_{j,w}
, yielding:

\mathbf{x}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{max}}b^{j,w}_{k}\bm{\phi}^{j,w}_{k}e^{\omega^{j,w}_{k}t}.

(50)

where in the last summation the terms
b^{j,w}_{k}
,
\bm{\phi}^{j,w}_{k}
and
\omega^{j,w}_{k}
have similar meaning to the ones in (
45
).

4
Numerical examples

This section presents the results of applying the model order reduction techniques of Section
3
to three different numerical examples, some of which involve localized damage. The first example considers the wave equation (
1
) in a two-dimensional domain, where the damage is modeled as a localized reduction in the wave speed coefficient. For this benchmark problem, we assume full access to the governing equations and simulation data, enabling a comprehensive comparison of both intrusive and non-intrusive MOR techniques. The second example involves a complex linear elasticity model of a composite material, specifically fiber metal laminate (FML), in which the damage is represented by a localized reduction in the Young’s modulus. Here, we apply the data-driven, non-intrusive MOR methods DMD and OpInf, reflecting a practical scenario where knowledge about the structure of the system is available as well as input-output data. The third example concerns GUW propagation in a nonlinear elastic model of intact aluminium, without damage. Here, we focus exclusively on data-driven DMD assuming only displacement data is available and the full order model is not accessible.

In order to assess the accuracy of the reduced order models, we consider the relative
L^{2}
-error at the sensor location defined as:

\epsilon_{s}=\frac{{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{s}-\mathbf{u}_{s}^{*}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(0,T)}}{{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}_{s}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(0,T)}}\times 100\%,

(51)

where
\mathbf{u}_{s}
and
\mathbf{u}_{s}^{*}
are time-dependent signals at the sensor for the full order model (FOM) and the reduced order model (ROM), respectively. We also compute the time-dependent relative
L^{2}
-error over the entire spatial domain:

\epsilon_{u}(t)=\frac{{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(t)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\times 100\%,

(52)

where
\mathbf{u}(t)
and
\mathbf{u}^{*}(t)
denote the spatial solutions of the FOM and ROM at time
t
, respectively. Moreover, we define the cumulative energy
c_{r}
(for a reduced dimension
r
) of the singular values as

c_{r}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sigma_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sigma_{k}},

(53)

where
\sigma_{k}
is
k
-th element in the diagonal of the matrix of the singular values
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, compare (
19
).

5
Outlook

In this work, we apply both intrusive and non-intrusive model reduction approaches to the wave equation posed on a domain containing damage. Subsequently, we apply non-intrusive reduction techniques to discretized second order mechanical systems obtained from guided ultrasonic wave (GUW) simulations. The test cases considered include models exhibiting either damage or nonlinear behavior.

Damage identification and characterization—determining both the location and severity of defects—are central challenges in the structural health monitoring (SHM) community. This motivation underlies our current research, which aims to develop a reduced order model (ROM) capable of generalizing across different damage configurations. To achieve this, we have to parameterize the damage and address the associated inverse problem, which involves estimating unknown damage parameters from observed system outputs.

As discussed in this article, the input damage parameters influence the full order stiffness matrix operator and, consequently, the displacement field. This dependency is carried over to the projected reduced operator of the stiffness matrix. Hence, a key challenge arises in approximating the reduced stiffness operator as a function of the parameters in a fully reduced fashion–i.e., without accessing the full order model or computing any full order quantities during the online stage of the ROM.

In the literature, this challenge is commonly addressed using hyper-reduction techniques such as the Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM) or the Discrete Empirical Interpolation Method (DEIM). For instance, DEIM evaluates a small subset of entries of the full order matrix during the online stage, where the corresponding indices are selected in a greedy manner. However, applying such methods requires that the full order solver or computational code allows direct computation of specific entries of the stiffness matrix without assembling the entire matrix. This is not feasible in our case, since we employ COMSOL Multiphysics as the simulator for the GUW problem, and direct access to the full order matrices or the time-discretization scheme is restricted.

Consequently, we currently adopt a non-intrusive yet projection-based reduction approach–namely, operator inference (OpInf). This choice is motivated by the need to construct a reduced representation of the second order mechanical system governing the dynamics without requiring intrusive modifications to the full order solver. To achieve the objectives of this work, we have to extend the existing OpInf-based ROM framework to handle parameterized systems. The proposed extension must be designed to accurately capture the dependence of the system outputs—in this case, the displacement field—on the damage-related input parameters.
