Numerical investigation of a rotary-integral miniature Stirling cryocooler with variable cylinder angle
Authors: Jhen-Syuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the performance of a miniature rotary-integral Stirling cryocooler with variable cylinder angle using a validated thermodynamic model. Unlike conventional designs that fix the cylinder angle at 90°, the present study considers it as a tunable geometric parameter that governs the displacement phase angle between piston and displacer. The model accounts for transient temperature evolution, pressure losses, and heat transfer in each chamber, with thermophysical properties evaluated as functions of pressure and temperature. Simulations are conducted at a charged pressure of 30 bar and rotation speed of 3000 rpm over cylinder angle ranging from 50° to 120°, under heat loads of 0–0.3 W. Results show that the cylinder angle strongly affects the phasing of compression and expansion volumes, thereby altering compression ratio, cold head temperature, and coefficient of performance (COP). An optimal cylinder angle range of 68°-70° yields steady-state cold head temperatures 6–10 K lower than the 90° baseline, with COP improvements up to 0.9% at 0.3 W load. Carnot efficiency can be increased from 17.3% to 18.6% as the cylinder angle is varied from 90° to 70°. These findings demonstrate that geometric phase tuning provides an effective means of enhancing the cooling performance of Stirling cryocoolers.

Introduction

The growing demand for compact, efficient, and reliable cryogenic systems has accelerated the development of miniature Stirling cryocoolers for applications such as infrared imaging, superconducting electronics, and portable low-temperature storage1–3. Operating on a closed-loop reversed Stirling cycle, these systems offer high thermodynamic efficiency, mechanical simplicity, and environmental friendliness due to the use of inert working gases such as helium or nitrogen4,5.

Among the various configurations, the rotary-integral Stirling cryocooler is particularly attractive for space and weight-constrained applications because of its compact form, reduced moving parts, and simplified manufacturing6,7. In this configuration, the piston and displacer are housed in closely aligned cylinders and actuated by a common crank mechanism. A key design parameter is the displacement phase angle, defined as the temporal offset between piston and displacer motions. This angle governs the compression ratio, pressure–volume cycle, timing of heat transfer, and ultimately the cooling performance8,9.

Conventional designs typically fix the cylinder arrangement at 90° primarily for ease of fabrication. However, numerous studies have demonstrated that phase angle tuning can yield notable performance improvements. Sant and Bapat10 presented a two simultaneously operating alpha-type Stirling cryocooler consisting of four pistons and a novel drive mechanism. The second-order cyclic analysis was utilized to compute the system with a 24 Hz operation frequency. It was reported that the maximum coefficient of performance (COP) was achieved at a phase angle near 81°. Renfroe and Cheung11 conducted a performance simulation of a Stirling cryocooler incorporating a working fluid composed of helium and nitrogen. For the operating temperature of 80 K, the heat lift was increased by 75% and the COP by 20% at approximately 100° compared to the conventional 90° design. Munir et al.12 performed CFD simulations to analyze the phase angle between the displacer and power piston in a free-piston Stirling engine. The compression ratio and pressure wave amplitude peaked at 120° phase angle. For the engine performance, the optimal phase angle was 60°−70° and 80°−100° at a higher frequency of 80 Hz and a lower frequency of 50 Hz.

The impact of the phase shift of a pneumatic free piston and free displacer Stirling cryocooler was discussed by Chen et al.13. The displacement phase shift near 90° was shown to optimize cooling power and COP at a charged pressure of 15 bar and a working frequency of 43 Hz. Similar trends are observed in other regenerative cryocooler types. In pulse-tube systems, phasor analyses have revealed that reducing the phase offset between pressure and mass flow enhances cooling power14,15. These findings collectively suggest that phase angle optimization is a powerful lever for improving cryocooler performance.

Despite extensive phase tuning studies in alpha-type and pulse-tube systems, systematic exploration of geometric phase tuning via cylinder angle in miniature rotary-integral Stirling cryocoolers is scarce. The link between cylinder angle, cooling performance, and efficiency remains under-quantified. Most integral-type designs maintain a fixed orthogonal (90°) cylinder arrangement. The direct relationship between cylinder angle, or displacement phase angle, and cooling performance has not been comprehensively explored. Geometrically altering the cylinder angle offers a simple, low-cost method of adjusting the phase angle without complex phase-shifting mechanisms, making it attractive for compact cryogenic devices16,17. As a result, this study is aimed at addressing these gaps by proposing a variable cylinder angle design (α = 50°−120°) for a miniature rotary-integral Stirling cryocooler. A validated transient thermodynamic model proposed by the author is adopted to evaluate the influence of cylinder angle on compression ratio, cold head temperature, input power, COP, and Carnot efficiency under heat loads of 0–0.3 W at 30 bar charged pressure and 3000 rpm. An optimal cylinder angle range is attempted to improve cooling performance over the conventional 90° design. The results provide quantitative evidence that geometric phase tuning can be an effective and mechanically simple strategy to enhance miniature Stirling cryocooler performance.

Stirling cryocooler with variable cylinder angle

This paper is focused on analyzing the effect of the cylinder angle of an integral Stirling cryocooler using a numerical model. The schematic of the studied cryocooler is shown in Fig. 1, which consists of two moving components, the piston and the displacer. They are actuated by a crank mechanism with an eccentric radius. The working gas is applied in the yellow region, and it is further separated into compression, tube, regenerator and expansion chambers. The working gas is periodically compressed and expanded in these chambers to induce a temperature difference. Regenerator acts as the core heat exchanger in the Stirling cryocooler, which significantly increases the thermodynamic efficiency. In addition, the blue region is a solid cold head where the instrument to be cooled will be installed.

In the figure, the vertical red line represents the piston-cylinder axis, while the horizontal one represents the displacer-cylinder axis. In the conventional integral-type Stirling cryocooler, the two cylinders are designed as perpendicular due to the advantage for fabrication. In this study, the angle between the axes, α, is treated as a design parameter. The cylinder angle directly determines the displacement phase angle of the piston and displacer. However, the phase angle of thermodynamic properties such as pressure and temperature may be influenced by different cylinder angles. More importantly, a certain value of the cylinder angle might maximize cooling performance.

Results and discussion

Figure 4 shows the volume variations of the total working space, compression chamber, and expansion chamber for three selected cylinder angles of 70°, 90°, and 110°. As expected, the cylinder angle directly determines the timing of maximum and minimum volumes in the compression and expansion chambers. In the present configuration, the peak of the expansion volume occurs earlier relative to the compression peak. For α = 70°, a larger overlap of expansion and compression events is obtained, leading to a slightly higher total volume fluctuation compared to the baseline 90° case. Conversely, at α = 110°, the expansion peak is delayed, reducing the effective compression ratio and shifting the total volume curve. These variations in phasing strongly influence the instantaneous thermodynamic distribution and, in turn, the cooling performance.

Fig. 4Volume variations of total, compression and expansion chambers at cylinder angles of 70°, 90° and 110°.

Volume variations of total, compression and expansion chambers at cylinder angles of 70°, 90° and 110°.

Figure 5 quantifies the influence of cylinder angle on the compression ratio, defined as the ratio of maximum to minimum total volume during a cycle. As α decreases from 120° to 50°, the compression ratio increases due to the closer alignment of the expansion and compression phases. Typically, a larger compression ratio increases cooling power. However, the phases of the pressure and mass flow rate are also affected as the cylinder angle is varied.

Fig. 5Effect of cylinder angle on the compression ratio.

Effect of cylinder angle on the compression ratio.

In Fig. 6, the transient evolution of cold head temperature for various heat loads at the three cylinder angles is presented. In all cases, the temperature decreases rapidly during the initial stage of operation. The transient cooling rate gradually slows as the system approaches its steady-state condition, where the cooling capacity balances the combined effects of conductive heat leakage and any externally applied heat load. For the case of α = 110°, the cold head stabilizes at approximately 111 K under no-load conditions. And then external heat loads shift the equilibrium temperature upward, reaching 127 K at the end of 0.3 W. This progressive rise in steady-state temperature with increasing load is consistent with the additional cooling demand for the Stirling cryocooler. Moreover, an obvious decline in the temperature curves is revealed as the cylinder angle is changed to 90° and 70°. Since changes in cylinder angle alter the displacement phase relationship between the piston and displacer, the timing of compression and expansion events would be modified, thus influencing transient cooling rates and steady-state temperatures. Nevertheless, the cooling effect vanishes while the phase reaches synchronous, indicating that an optimal cylinder angle can be discovered.

Cold head temperature variations with various heat loads.

The thermodynamic model developed in this study is initially established by its strong agreement with the experimental cold head temperature for the prototype. This validation supports the fundamental methodology used for calculating the thermodynamic state and transient temperature evolution across the system. Figure 7 illustrates the steady-state cold head temperature as a function of cylinder angle for the different heat load conditions. Experimental results from6 (α = 90° prototype) are also shown in the figure to demonstrate the robustness of the present methodology. It is seen that the cold head temperature reaches its minimum at a cylinder angle of 68°−70° across all cases. At lower or higher angles, the cooling performance declines, indicating a loss of thermodynamic synchronization between expansion and compression. Compared to the conventional 90° design, the optimal range reduces the cold head temperature by approximately 6–10 K, highlighting the potential of geometric phase tuning as a performance enhancement method. The 68°−70° angle represents the optimal phase shift where the gain in cooling power (due to better synchronization of expansion and compression) outweighs the accompanying losses. This optimized timing maximizes the net heat absorption at the cold head relative to the power input. Specifically, the phase shift ensures that the maximum amount of high-pressure gas is displaced into the expansion chamber at the most opportune moment for expansion work.

Regarding the heat losses, pump loss is found to account for 70–75% of the total loss, as it scales with the square of the pressure amplitude, which is influenced by the phasing of the compression and expansion chambers. Shuttle heat loss contributes approximately 15–20% and shows a minor dependence on cylinder angle, as the piston/displacer clearance remains constant. Conduction loss through the cold cylinder accounts for less than 10% and is nearly independent of α. Although the present study focuses on a miniature rotary-integral Stirling cryocooler with a cooling capacity up to 0.3 W, the observed performance trends can be reasonably extended to higher-capacity configurations. Parametric scaling of the model indicates that at cooling capacities above 1 W, the optimal cylinder angle would remain within a similar range (approximately 68°−72°). However, such predictions require further experimental validation using enlarged geometries and optimized regenerator designs to confirm the scalability of the geometric phase-tuning strategy.

The cylinder angle geometrically tunes the phase angle between the piston and displacer motions, which in turn governs the gas processes. The optimal performance at a certain cylinder angle is due to stronger pressure–volume synchronization. This phasing improvement enhances regenerator heat exchange by reducing temperature lag and minimizing enthalpy flow reversal. Figure 8 shows the effect of cylinder angle on the absolute indicated power, which represents the net input power required to sustain the cycle. The input power is decreased with increased heat load since the temperature difference is reduced. On the other hand, it can be noted that input power is maximized near α ≈ 81° in all cases. According to Fig. 4, the swept volume of the compression chamber is roughly 4.5 times that of the expansion chamber. Therefore, pressure-volume work done by the compression chamber mainly contributes to the indicated power. From the simulation result, one can learn that the input power is mostly required at the cylinder angle of 81° for all capacity applications.

The trends observed in the indicated power directly influence the COP, as shown in Fig. 9, which reports COP variation with cylinder angle. The COP trends mirror those of indicated power, reaching a critical point near α ≈ 81°. This behavior confirms that a lower cylinder angle of the integral Stirling cryocooler might achieve improved thermal performance and excessive power consumption. Considering the heat load of 0.3 W and α is varied from the baseline 90° to 70°, the cold head temperature is changed from 115 K to 110 K, and the COP improvement is 0.9%.

Figure 10 presents the Carnot efficiency, defined as the ratio of actual COP to the theoretical Carnot COP, for the tested cylinder angles. Carnot efficiency can be treated as a comprehensive assessment of the cold head temperature and COP, which are discussed in Figs. 7, 8 and 9. In contrast, no single optimal α is observed for Carnot efficiency; trends depend strongly on the applied heat load. Carnot efficiency is a function of both COP and cold head temperature, and the optimal values for these two parameters do not perfectly align across all heat loads. Based on the current results, a cylinder angle of 70°, as displayed in Fig. 2b, might be an expected case to implement the performance enhancement of the Stirling cryocooler. With a 0.3 W heat load, the cold head temperature can be maintained at 110 K while the Carnot efficiency is lifted to 18.6% in comparison to 17.3% of the baseline 90°.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrated that geometric phase tuning, by varying the cylinder angle of a miniature integral Stirling cryocooler, is a highly effective strategy for enhancing its cooling performance. The influence of the cylinder angle of a miniature integral Stirling cryocooler is numerically examined in this study. The key findings are as follows:

The cylinder angle (α) directly governs the displacement phase angle between the piston and displacer, and significantly affects compression ratio, steady-state cold head temperature, and COP.The optimal performance occurs at α = 68°−70°, with cold head temperature lower by 6–10 K than the 90° design across all tested heat loads.At the optimal angle and a 0.3 W heat load, the COP is improved by 0.9%, and Carnot efficiency is increased from 17.3% to 18.6%, indicating better thermodynamic utilization.The observed trends result from improved thermodynamic synchronization between compression and expansion processes at specific α values, which enhance cooling without substantial additional power consumption.
