Recent Advances in Diffusion Models for Hyperspectral Image Processing and Analysis: A Review
Xing Hu, Xiangcheng Liu, Danfeng Hong, Qianqian Duan, Linghua Jiang, Haima Yang, Dawei Zhang

Abstract
Hyperspectral image processing and analysis has important application value in remote sensing, agriculture and environmental monitoring, but its high dimensionality, data redundancy and noise interference etc. bring great challenges to the analysis. Traditional models have limitations in dealing with these complex data, and it is difficult to meet the increasing demand for analysis. In recent years, Diffusion models, as a class of emerging generative approaches, have demonstrated promising capabilities in hyperspectral image (HSI) processing tasks. By simulating the diffusion process of data in time, the Diffusion Model are capable of modeling high-dimensional spectral structures, generate high-quality samples, and achieve competitive performance in spectral-spatial denoising tasks and data enhancement. In this paper, we review the recent research advances in diffusion modeling for hyperspectral image processing and analysis, and discuss its applications in tasks such as high-dimensional data processing, noise removal, classification, and anomaly detection. The performance of diffusion-based models on image processing is compared and the challenges are summarized. It is shown that the diffusion model can significantly improve the accuracy and efficiency of hyperspectral image analysis, providing a new direction for future research.

Introduction

Hyperspectral image processing and analysis are crucial in remote sensing, agriculture, and environmental monitoring, offering significant application value. Continuous advancements in technology have led to improved resolution and broader spectral ranges in hyperspectral images, enabling more precise feature identification and classification. However, the inherent challenges of high dimensionality, data redundancy, and noise interference in these images often limit traditional processing methods. Consequently, the development of novel models and methods has become a key research focus. Recently, Diffusion Models (DMs), as an emerging generative model, have demonstrated unique advantages in addressing these complexities within hyperspectral image processing and analysis.

Hyperspectral imagery, with its ability to capture detailed spectral information across numerous narrow bands, offers a rich source of both spectral and spatial data. This high-dimensional and intricate data has found extensive applications in fields like agriculture, environmental monitoring, and mineral exploration. However, analyzing hyperspectral images presents several challenges. The sheer volume of high-dimensional data demands significant processing and storage capabilities, leading to high computational complexity. Moreover, the strong correlation among bands introduces data redundancy, complicating analysis. Issues such as noise interference and mixed pixel problems further impact the accuracy of the analysis.

2
Basics of hyperspectral imagery

2.1
Definition and Characteristics of Hyperspectral Imagery

A hyperspectral image is a spectral image characterized by a spectral resolution on the order of
{10}^{-2}\lambda
, encompassing tens to hundreds of continuously subdivided spectral bands across the visible to infrared light range. Captured by hyperspectral sensors (imaging spectrometers) mounted on various platforms, these images can cover the ultraviolet, visible, near-infrared, and mid-infrared regions of the electromagnetic spectrum within a target area. Crucially, each pixel in a hyperspectral image contains information from numerous spectral bands, forming a continuous spectral curve. This unique capability allows for the simultaneous acquisition of both spatial and spectral information, effectively integrating spectra with images.

Thanks to the maturation of imaging spectroscopy technology, hyperspectral images offer a unique fusion of image and spectral information. This integration provides a rich array of features, proving highly beneficial for both feature identification and processing tasks.

Image information captures external qualities like size, shape, and defects, while spectral information reveals internal physical structure and chemical composition. This dual capability allows for the acquisition of both multi-segment remote sensing imagery and continuous spectral data for each image element. This enables a multi-dimensional representation of feature characteristics, combining spatial and spectral information for more accurate identification and classification.

Hyperspectral images boast high spectral resolution, typically at the nanometer level. This allows for a detailed portrayal of feature reflectance spectra, enabling the differentiation of spectrally similar substances and significantly enhancing the accuracy of feature classification and identification. Simultaneously, these images contain an enormous amount of data, often comprising dozens to hundreds of bands, leading to information redundancy and multi-segmentation. This vast data volume necessitates high demands for storage and processing capacity. Despite these challenges, hyperspectral images provide exceptionally rich spectral information, capable of capturing subtle spectral differences and revealing correlations across both spatial and spectral dimensions.

Finally, hyperspectral images are characterized by their fine spectral feature expression, nonlinear characteristics, low signal-to-noise ratio, and high data dimensionality. These images are broadly applied across numerous fields, including agriculture, environmental monitoring, mineral exploration, and medicine, enabling the recognition and classification of diverse substances.

2.2
High-dimensional characterization of hyperspectral image data

Hyperspectral image data is inherently high-dimensional, primarily due to its multidimensional structure, significant data redundancy, high computational complexity, and exceptional information richness.

A hyperspectral image functions as a three-dimensional data cube, encompassing spatial, radiometric, and spectral dimensions. It features a large number of contiguous bands, forming high-dimensional vectors. The narrow bandwidths and nanometer-level resolution of these bands precisely reflect physicochemical properties. However, the high similarity between adjacent bands leads to information redundancy, increasing data complexity.

The ”map in one” nature of hyperspectral images, combining spatial and spectral information, inherently elevates the data’s dimensionality. This necessitates considering both spatial and spectral characteristics during analysis, thereby increasing model complexity. Processing such high-dimensional data, especially for tasks like feature extraction, classification, and segmentation, demands substantial computational resources, leading to significantly increased computational complexity. Storage requirements are also high due to the immense data volume, which in turn incurs high costs. Traditional machine learning methods struggle with slow training in high-dimensional spaces and are susceptible to noise interference, a problem exacerbated in high dimensions, making processing more difficult. While deep learning can automate feature extraction, it often requires a large number of costly labeled samples.

The high dimensionality of hyperspectral data can lead to excessive resource waste, often resulting in the ”curse of dimensionality” and diminished performance. To counter this, dimensionality reduction techniques like PCA and LDA are crucial for preserving essential information while simplifying the model. During this complex process, it is vital to mitigate issues such as overfitting during training.

2.3
Main tasks in hyperspectral image analysis

The primary tasks in hyperspectral image analysis, including classification, segmentation, and anomaly detection, hold significant value in processing these complex, high-dimensional datasets. They are essential for extracting meaningful and useful information. Let’s delve into each of these tasks:

Classification in hyperspectral imagery involves assigning each pixel or region to a predefined class, such as vegetation, water bodies, or buildings. This is widely applied in agriculture for crop classification and pest detection, in environmental monitoring for land use classification and forest cover monitoring, and in mineral exploration for identifying mineral types. However, several challenges persist: the high-dimensional nature of hyperspectral data increases classification complexity; data labeling is costly due to the extensive amount of labeled data required; and the mixed pixel problem, where a single pixel encompasses multiple features due to spatial resolution limitations, complicates accurate classification. Traditional approaches include statistical methods (e.g., maximum likelihood classification) and machine learning methods (e.g., Support Vector Machines, SVM). More recently, deep learning methods such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have also been employed.

Segmentation in hyperspectral imagery involves partitioning an image into distinct regions or objects that share similar spectral characteristics. This can be performed at either the pixel or object level. Common applications include segmenting tumor regions in medical image analysis, identifying buildings and roads in urban planning, and delineating crop areas in agriculture. However, several challenges arise: the high dimensionality of hyperspectral data complicates the segmentation process; noise interference can compromise accuracy; and the mixed pixel problem, where a single pixel represents multiple features, makes precise segmentation difficult. Traditional approaches often rely on clustering-based methods (e.g., K-means) or edge detection techniques. Deep learning methods, such as Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) and UNet, are increasingly employed for this task.

Target detection in hyperspectral imagery involves identifying and locating specific targets or objects, such as military assets, mineral resources, or vegetation types, within an image. Its applications are diverse, ranging from military target identification and tracking, to the detection of specific minerals in exploration, and the identification of particular vegetation or pollution sources in environmental monitoring. This task presents several challenges: the high dimensionality of hyperspectral data increases the complexity of detection; target diversity, where different targets may exhibit similar spectral characteristics, can hinder accurate detection; and background interference from complex environments can significantly impact detection accuracy. Traditional methods for target detection include spectral matching techniques and statistical methods. More recently, deep learning approaches like Region-based Convolutional Neural Networks (RCNN) and YOLO (You Only Look Once) have been widely adopted.

Anomaly detection in hyperspectral imagery identifies pixels or regions that significantly deviate from their surrounding environment. These anomalies can represent unknown targets or unique features. This technique finds applications in military contexts for detecting unidentified targets, in environmental monitoring for pinpointing pollution sources, and in agriculture for the early detection of pests and diseases. However, challenges arise due to the high dimensionality of hyperspectral images, which increases the complexity of anomaly detection. Furthermore, the diversity of anomalies, where different types may exhibit varying spectral characteristics, complicates their detection. Lastly, background interference from complex environments can negatively impact detection accuracy. Traditional approaches include statistical methods (e.g., the RX algorithm) and clustering-based methods. More recently, deep learning methods such as Autoencoders and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been employed.

Noise suppression in hyperspectral imagery aims to reduce or eliminate unwanted noise through specialized algorithms and techniques. This process is crucial for enhancing image quality and ensuring the accuracy of subsequent analyses. Its applications span diverse fields, including agriculture, military operations, the food industry, and mining exploration. Common methods for noise suppression primarily involve frequency or transform domain noise reduction, spatial noise reduction, and deep learning-based noise reduction.

Hyperspectral data recovery is a more advanced form of noise suppression, focused on removing noise and interference from hyperspectral images to restore their original spectral and spatial information. This process significantly enhances data quality and usability. Key application areas include agriculture, environmental monitoring, biomedicine, and geological exploration. Common methods for data recovery include spectral reconstruction, spectral dimension recovery, low-rank representation modeling, and various deep learning approaches.

Hyperspectral image data augmentation and synthesis involves generating new hyperspectral image samples to increase the diversity and quantity of training data, thereby enhancing a model’s generalization capability. This is frequently applied in model training to diversify data and improve model robustness, and in data completion to generate missing hyperspectral image data.Challenges in this area include: the high dimensionality of hyperspectral images, which complicates data augmentation and synthesis; and ensuring the quality and diversity of generated samples to maintain model validity. Traditional methods often rely on interpolation-based techniques and statistics-based methods. More recently, deep learning approaches like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Diffusion Models have shown promise.

Hyperspectral data generation and completion is a fundamental task in data science that addresses issues like missing data, noise interference, and data scarcity, while also expanding application possibilities through synthetic data. Data generation involves creating synthetic data that aligns with real data distributions, often to augment dataset scale or simulate specific scenarios. Data completion, on the other hand, focuses on filling in missing values or repairing incomplete data to ensure consistency and completeness. These techniques are commonly employed in fields such as remote sensing, medical imaging, and agricultural monitoring. Traditional methods include statistics-based approaches (e.g., interpolation) and machine learning techniques (e.g., K-nearest neighbor, decision trees). More recently, deep learning methods like GANs, VAEs, and Diffusion Models have become prominent for these tasks.

In summary, the core tasks of hyperspectral image analysis encompass classification, segmentation, target detection, anomaly detection, and data enhancement and synthesis. These tasks are vital in processing hyperspectral imagery, enabling the extraction of valuable information from complex, high-dimensional datasets. While these tasks contend with challenges such as high-dimensional data processing, noise interference, and mixed pixel issues, combining traditional and deep learning methodologies can significantly enhance the accuracy and efficiency of hyperspectral image analysis.

3
Diffusion Model

The Diffusion Model (DM) is an innovative generative model that has recently showcased remarkable capabilities in image generation, denoising, and data enhancement. Its core principle involves modeling a data diffusion process over time, progressively transforming noisy data into desired target data. Specifically, DMs operate by gradually converting original data into noise through a forward diffusion process, and then meticulously reconstructing the original data from this noise via a reverse diffusion process. This methodology proves highly effective for handling high-dimensional data, as well as for image generation and denoising tasks, demonstrating particular advantages in the realm of hyperspectral image processing.

3.1
Theoretical Foundations of the Diffusion Model

The Diffusion Model operates through two distinct processes: forward diffusion and backward diffusion. The training objective is to minimize the discrepancy between these two processes. Fundamentally, diffusion models are typically categorized into three main types: (1) Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), (2) Score-Based Generative Models (SGM), and (3) Stochastic Differential Equation-based Models (SDE/NCSN). The foundational principles of each of these three diffusion models are described below.

3.1.1
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM)

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)
[
19
,
22
]
employ two Markov chains. A forward chain systematically introduces noise to transform the data into a simple prior distribution, typically a standard Gaussian. Conversely, an inverse chain learns to reverse this process, converting the noise back into data through a transition kernel parameterized by a deep neural network.

New data points are generated by first randomly sampling a vector from the prior distribution, then using an inverse Markov chain to ancestrally sample it
[
23
]
.

]

Formally, given a data distribution
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, a forward Markov process generates a sequence of random variables,
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}
, through a transition kernel
q(x_{t}\mid{x_{t-1}})
. By applying the probability chain rule and the Markov property, the joint distribution of these variables, conditional on
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, is then decomposed and denoted as
q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\mid{x_{0}})
, which results in:

q(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1}).

(1)

In DDPMs, the transition kernel
q(x_{t}\mid{x_{t-1}})
is specifically designed to gradually transform the data distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
into a manageable prior distribution. A common and effective choice for this transition kernel is a Gaussian perturbation, typically defined as:

q(\mathrm{x}_{t}\mid\mathrm{x}_{t-1})=N(\mathrm{x}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\mathrm{x}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathrm{I}).

(2)

where
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
is a hyperparameter set before model training. While other kernel types exist, this Gaussian transition kernel is used to simplify the discussion. As noted by Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015)
[
22
]
, this specific kernel allows for the marginalization of the joint distribution in equation (1), yielding an analytical form for
q(x_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})
across all
t\in\{0,1,\cdots,T\}
. Specifically, with
\alpha_{t}:=1-\beta_{t}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}{:}=\prod_{s=0}^{t}\alpha_{s}
:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I}\right).

(3)

Intuitively, this forward process gradually introduces noise into the data until its original structure is completely lost. To generate new data samples, the DDPM first samples an unstructured noise vector from the prior distribution (which is typically straightforward to obtain). It then progressively removes this noise by running a learnable Markov chain in the reverse temporal direction. Specifically, this inverse Markov chain is parameterized by the prior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{T};0,\mathbf{I})
and the learnable transition kernel
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
. We choose
p(\mathbf{x}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{T};0,\mathbf{I})
for the prior because the forward process is designed such that
q(\mathbf{x}_{T})\approx\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{T};0,\mathbf{I})
. The learnable transition kernel
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
employs:

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\right).

(4)

where
\theta
represents the model’s parameters, while the mean
\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
and variance
\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
are parameterized by a deep neural network.

The key to successfully training the network lies in ensuring the reverse process
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T})
accurately matches the true time reversal of the forward process
q(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T})
. This is typically achieved by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the two:

\mathrm{KL}\left(q(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathrm{x}_{T})|\||p_{\theta}(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathrm{x}_{T})\right)

(5)

\overset{(i)}{\operatorname*{=}}-\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T})}[\log p_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\right)]+\mathrm{const}

(6)

\begin{split}\overset{(ii)}{\operatorname*{=}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T})}\left[-\mathrm{log}p(\mathbf{x}_{T})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}\right]\\
+\mathrm{const}:=-L_{NLB}(\mathbf{x}_{0}).\end{split}

(7)

\overset{(iii)}{\operatorname*{\operatorname*{\geq}}}\mathbb{E}[-\log p_{\theta}\left(\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)]+\mathrm{const}

(8)

where ”const” is a constant independent of the model parameters
\theta
. The objective of DDPM training is to maximize the variational lower bound, or equivalently, to minimize the negative variational lower bound. This can be efficiently estimated through Monte Carlo sampling
[
24
]
and optimized effectively via stochastic optimization
[
25
]
.

At the core of Score-Based Generative Models (SGM/NCSN)
[

20

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x})

\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})

\theta

\mathbf{x}
itself. Essentially, it’s a vector field that points in the direction of the steepest ascent of the probability density function.

[
20
]

[

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}):=\int q(\mathbf{x}_{t})q(\mathbf{x}_{0})d\mathbf{x}_{0}

+\mathrm{const}.\end{split}

(13)

where
\lambda(t)
represents a positive weighting function, while

is derived from
\varepsilon
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}

denotes a uniform distribution over the set

3.1.3
Diffusion model based on stochastic differential equations (Score-SDE)

Both DDPM and SGM can be further generalized to an infinite time step or noise level. In this advanced formulation, known as Score SDE
[
21
]
, the perturbation and denoising processes are governed by the solution of a stochastic differential equation (SDE). This approach is employed for both noise perturbation and sample generation, where the denoising process necessitates estimating a score function of the noisy data distribution.

The Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) was employed to score the data using a diffusion process, which is governed by the following SDE
[
21
]
:

\mathrm{dx}=\mathrm{f}(\mathrm{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{dw}.

(14)

where
f(\mathbf{x},t)
and
g(t)
represent the drift and diffusion functions of the SDE, respectively, while
w
denotes a standard Wiener process (also known as Brownian motion). The forward processes in both the DDPM and SGM are essentially discretizations of this SDE. As demonstrated by Song et al. in 2020

dx=-\frac{1}{2}\beta(t)xdt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}dw
. For the SGM, the corresponding SDE is
\mathrm{dx}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{d}[\sigma(t)^{2}]}{\mathrm{d}t}}\mathrm{dw}
. This holds true as
T
approaches infinity, where
e\beta(\frac{t}{T})=\beta_{t}T
and
\sigma(\frac{t}{T})=\sigma_{t}
.

Here,
q_{t}({x})
represents the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
in the forward process. For any diffusion process structured like equation (14), Anderson
[
42
]
demonstrated that it can be inverted by solving the following reverse-time SDE:

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=[\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{log}q_{t}(\mathbf{x})]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{w}}.

(15)

where
\bar{\mathbf{W}}
represents the standard Wiener process as time flows backward, and
dt
signifies an infinitesimal negative time step. The solution trajectories of this reverse SDE share the same marginal density as those of the forward SDE, but evolve in the opposite temporal direction
[
21
]
. Conceptually, the solution of a reverse-time SDE describes a diffusion process that progressively transforms noise into data. Furthermore, Song et al. (2020)
[
21
]
demonstrated the existence of an ordinary differential equation (ODE), known as the probability flow ODE, whose trajectory exhibits the same marginals as the reverse-time SDE. The probability flow ODE is defined by the following equation:

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\left[\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{x},t)-\frac{1}{2}g(t)^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{log}q_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]\mathrm{d}t.

(16)

Both the inverse time SDE and the probabilistic flow ODE allow for sampling from the same data distribution because their trajectories share identical marginals. If the fractional function
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{t}(\mathbf{x})
is known for each time step
t
, its inverse-time equations can be derived. These equations can then be used to generate samples via numerical techniques such as annealed Ronzwan dynamics
[
20
]
, numerical SDE solvers
[
32
,
21
]
, numerical ODE solvers
[
33
,
34
,
21
,
36
,
43
]
, and predictor-corrector methods (a combination of MCMC and numerical ODE/SDE solvers)
[
21
]
. Similar to the SGM, the transient fractional model
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is parameterized, and the fractional function is estimated by generalizing the fractional matching objective in Equation (13) to continuous time, resulting in the following objective:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,T],\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{0}),\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim q(\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0})}\\
\left[\lambda(t)\|\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log q_{0t}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)\|^{2}\right].\end{split}

(17)

where
\mathcal{U}[0,T]
denotes the uniform distribution over the interval
[0,T]
.

The preceding discussion has introduced the fundamental principles and formulas of the basic diffusion model. This theoretical framework underpins the success of diffusion models in modern image processing, enabling them to overcome certain limitations of traditional models, albeit with their own inherent drawbacks. In the context of hyperspectral imaging, research primarily focuses on Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), which are the most frequently applied diffusion models in this domain. While the other two types have seen comparatively less application, they will also be explored in subsequent sections.

Evolution of the Diffusion model with different variants

The genesis of Diffusion Models dates back to 2015, when Sohl-Dickstein et al. first introduced their theoretical framework, uniquely merging nonequilibrium thermodynamics with generative models
[
22

[

[

[

Addressing DDPM’s limitations in generation quality, training efficiency, and log-likelihood optimization, Nichol et al. introduced Improve-DDPM (IDDPM)
[
44
]
in 2021. This variant incorporated learnable variance, cosine noise scheduling, and a hybrid loss function, while also accelerating sampling through time-step-importance sampling. Despite these improvements, IDDPM still faced complexities in computation, dataset limitations, insufficient generalization, and trade-offs between generation quality and speed. Concurrently in 2021, Song et al. proposed DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models)
[
35

[

3.3
Traditional Deep Learning Models

Generative Models (GMs) are a class of machine learning models capable of learning data distributions and generating novel samples. Their applications are widespread in fields like computer vision and natural language processing, encompassing tasks such as data generation, data enhancement, image restoration, and anomaly detection. In this paper, we will primarily focus on traditional generative models, specifically Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and autoregressive models.

The Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), proposed by Ian Goodfellow et al. in 2014
[
8
]
, is another significant framework for unsupervised anomaly detection, rooted in the concept of a zero-sum game. At its core, a GAN generates data through the adversarial training of two neural networks: a generator and a discriminator. The generator’s role is to produce data, while the discriminator’s task is to differentiate between this generated data and real data. This adversarial process drives the generator to progressively learn how to create data that closely resembles the distribution of real data. While GANs offer fast generation, they often suffer from unstable training and are prone to issues like mode collapse
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
.

The Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE)
[
9
]
is a generative model that blends the strengths of Autoencoders with probabilistic graphical models. Its core idea is to learn latent representations from data and subsequently generate new samples. VAEs achieve this by using Variational Inference (VI) to approximate the data’s latent distributions. While they offer stable training, the quality of the generated data can sometimes be lower
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
.

Autoregressive Models (ARMs)
[
64
]
are a class of generative models that operate by assuming the current data point in a sequence is dependent on preceding ones. Their core idea is to leverage this temporal dependence to generate new data points sequentially. Examples include Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), which employ a recursive structure for data processing, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) Neural Networks, a specialized type of RNN. While ARMs are known for generating high-quality data, their step-by-step generation process can result in slower speeds
[
65
,
66
]
.

Simultaneously, the diffusion model is poised for continued innovation and development within the hyperspectral domain. Future advancements in this area will build upon existing diffusion models, with a focus on resolving challenges in hyperspectral image classification, anomaly detection, and recovery.

4.1.1
Conditional Diffusion Model (CDM)

Similar to the evolution of GANs, diffusion models initially focused on unconditional generation, swiftly transitioning to conditional generation
[
67
]
. While unconditional generation is often used to push the boundaries of a model’s capabilities, conditional generation holds more practical value for applications, as it enables the output to be controlled based on human intent.

The introduction of conditioning into diffusion models first appeared in
[
11
]
. This pioneering work guided the generation of diffusion models by incorporating classifiers into pre-trained models, leading to the term ”guided diffusion models.” While this method offered low training costs, it increased inference costs by using classification results to steer the diffusion model’s sampling process, and it struggled to precisely control details for optimal image output. Consequently, the Google team
[
68
]
opted for a simpler approach: retraining the DDPM conditionally to control generated results, a technique they named Classifier-Free Guidance.

Formally, when given conditional information
\mathbf{c}
, the combined DDPM distribution changes from equations (2) and (3). Consequently, both the optimization objective and the sampling process are modified to take the following form:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{con}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},\epsilon,c,t}\left[\left\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}x_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\epsilon,c,t\right)\right\|^{2}\right].

(18)

y_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(y_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}}\epsilon_{\theta}(y_{t},c,t)\right)+\hat{\beta}_{t}z.

(19)

This approach has seen broader adoption than guided diffusion models and forms the foundation for many significant diffusion models, including DALL-E2
[
45
]
, Imagen
[
49
]
, and Stable Diffusion (SD)
[
18
]
.

4.1.2
Latent Diffusion Model (LDM)

Directly manipulating diffusion models in the original image pixel space incurs high computational costs. To address this, Rombach et al.
[
60
]
proposed training and performing inference with diffusion models within a low-dimensional latent space, terming this approach the Latent Diffusion Model (LDM).

Architecture of LDM, where E and D denote the encoder and decoder, respectively

As depicted in Fig. 7, the Latent Diffusion Model (LDM) comprises three distinct parts. Firstly, the leftmost gray box illustrates the LDM’s specific diffusion process: it shifts its operational domain from pixel space to a low-dimensional latent space by utilizing a pre-trained autoencoder. Within this space, noise is incrementally added to feature
\mathbf{z}
, yielding
\mathbf{z_{T}}
as part of the forward diffusion. Secondly, the central orange box, also in latent space, houses a U-Net with cross-attention that supports multimodal inputs. This U-Net facilitates the reverse diffusion, restoring

\mathbf{z}

The LDM’s ability to effectively reduce computational cost without sacrificing image synthesis quality, coupled with its accelerated inference speed, has led to its widespread adoption in natural scene generation and offers significant inspiration for applying diffusion models to hyperspectral applications.

4.2.1
Data Enhancement and Synthesis

In data enhancement and synthesis, super-resolution techniques play a crucial role in improving image quality. Super-resolution aims to reconstruct high-resolution images from their low-resolution counterparts
[
69
]
. However, these images can be degraded by factors like imaging equipment, weather conditions, or downsampling. This loss of detail is often more pronounced in hyperspectral images compared to natural images, making the inverse problem of reconstructing high-resolution versions significantly more challenging.

Before the advent of diffusion models, approaches based on deep neural networks, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Transformers, sought to integrate feature information in a single step by finding suitable mapping functions. Methods like self-supervised learning
[

[

This iterative integration of different image information has proven highly effective and has garnered significant attention in the hyperspectral field.

Although Hyperspectral Images (HSIs) boast high spectral resolution, their spatial resolution is often comparatively low

Panchromatic sharpening enhances HSIs by injecting detailed spatial information from a high-resolution panchromatic (PAN) image. Conversely, multispectral and HSI fusion leverages multispectral images to help hyperspectral maps acquire spatial details. For instance, Shi et al.
[

utilized both multispectral and hyperspectral images as conditional inputs to a diffusion model, subsequently generating high-resolution hyperspectral maps by integrating information from these two image modalities. Similarly, Qu et al.
[

developed a conditional recurrent diffusion framework. This framework enables the spatial super-resolution process to be guided by the multispectral image and the spectral super-resolution process by the hyperspectral map, leading to the generation of high-resolution hyperspectral maps that boast complementary spatial and spectral information.

More contemporary research is increasingly focused on utilizing Panchromatic (PAN) images for HSI super-resolution

[

Target Detection

In contrast to pixel-level ground classification, target detection for hyperspectral images focuses on localizing specific instances, such as an airplane, vehicle, or ship, within the image using bounding boxes
[

]
. A significant challenge in this area is the ambiguity and lack of realism and diversity in training data, often exacerbated by external disturbances
[
123
,
118

. This issue stems from the inherent nature of hyperspectral images, where objects needing detection are frequently complex, difficult to distinguish, and distributed across various image regions. Consequently, augmenting the objects to be detected using diffusion models has emerged as an effective solution.

To address the aforementioned challenges, Yu et al. proposed Diff-Mosaic (Enhanced Target Detection via Diffusion Prior)
[
118
]
. This diffusion model-based approach introduces an enhancement network called Pixel-Prior, which coordinates pixels to generate highly realistic and diverse mosaic images. It leverages a powerful diffusion model prior that resamples pixels with samples, thereby introducing real-world information and ensuring the generated samples are both realistic and varied.

Similarly, Qi et al. presented a novel joint framework for underwater hyperspectral image restoration and target detection, based on a conditional diffusion framework
[
123
]
. This framework employs a variable spectral group extraction module and a joint underwater hyperspectral image restoration and target detection (JURTD) module to simulate different ranges of underwater target spectra. This achieves high-quality restored images and superior detection performance, effectively overcoming the inherent challenges of underwater hyperspectral imaging. Zhu et al. introduced ODGEN (Domain-specific Object Detection Data Generation with Diffusion Models)
[
124
]
, a novel approach to generate high-quality, bounding-box-conditioned images. This facilitates data synthesis specifically for object detection. By utilizing synthetic visual cues with spatial constraints and textual descriptions per object to control the diffusion model, ODGEN demonstrates robust performance when handling complex scenes and specific domains.

Meanwhile, to enhance precision and accuracy, Wang et al. proposed OrientedDiffDet (pairwise object-oriented diffusion model)
[
125
]
. This approach combines oriented frame representation, feature alignment, and accelerated diffusion, allowing for direct object detection from a set of random frames. During the training phase, horizontal detection boxes are initially converted to oriented detection boxes. The model then learns to reverse this conversion by diffusing from ground truth oriented boxes to a random distribution. In the inference phase, the model progressively refines a set of randomly generated boxes to produce the final output.

Image anomaly detection, a critical area in computer vision, focuses on highlighting unusual regions within an image

This section will explore how diffusion models, as a prominent generative model, can be applied to hyperspectral image anomaly detection. While studies specifically on diffusion model-based hyperspectral image anomaly detection are relatively few, there are numerous applications in other fields, such as medical imaging

In the field of anomaly detection, a persistent challenge is the complexity of background clutter in hyperspectral images, which often obscures anomalies. Additionally, the scarcity of labeled hyperspectral samples leads to poor generalization in existing anomaly detection methods.

To tackle these issues, Ma et al. proposedBSDM (Background Suppression Diffusion Model)
[
133
]
, a novel anomaly detection solution. BSDM simultaneously learns the latent background distribution and generalizes it across different datasets to suppress complex backgrounds. This approach is characterized by three key aspects: 1. Pseudo background noise is designed for the complex backgrounds of HSIs, and a Diffusion Model (DM) is employed to learn the underlying latent background distribution. 2. To address generalizability, a statistical bias module adapts BSDM to datasets from various domains without requiring labeled samples. 3. For effective background suppression, the DM’s inference process was innovatively improved by feeding the original hyperspectral image into a denoising network, which removes the background as if it were noise. This work also marked the first application of diffusion modeling to hyperspectral image anomaly detection.

Wu et al. introduced the diffusion background dictionary method (DBD)
[
134
]
, combining the strengths of model-driven and data-driven approaches based on HSI and HAD (Hyperspectral Anomaly Detection) task characteristics. DBD intrinsically integrates the DM with the low-rank representation (LRR) model, utilizing the DM to obtain the crucial background dictionary tensor within the tensor LRR, thus enabling accurate anomaly detection. Following the principles of the RX algorithm in anomaly detection, the multivariate normal distribution approximating the HSI background is diffused to better suit background suppression. Furthermore, hyperspectral image anomaly detection often grapples with a lack of prior information, primarily the challenge of accurately reconstructing background regions while inferring the latent background of anomalous areas. To this end, Chen et al. proposed Double Window Spectral Diffusion (DWSDiff)
[
56
]
. This novel approach models accurate background estimation through an iterative spectral diffusion and reverse diffusion process. A dual-window strategy is also introduced to mitigate the impact of extended anomalous regions in the neighborhood on background estimation. Finally, an anomaly generation strategy based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and the Linear Spectral Mixture Model (LSMM) is devised to create realistic training data, overcoming the scarcity of labeled hyperspectral data and leading to improved performance.

Noise Suppression

A primary challenge in hyperspectral imaging is acquiring images without losing crucial information. During acquisition or subsequent processing, images can be corrupted by noise or artifacts, significantly degrading quality, especially with small objects or low contrast. Due to their generative nature, diffusion models are well-suited to addressing various noise reduction problems

In image processing, hyperspectral images (HSIs) are invariably affected by various types of noise due to the inherent limitations of imaging techniques and environmental factors. Fundamentally, the learning process of diffusion models is akin to a denoising process
[
19

, making them exceptionally effective for noise reduction in hyperspectral contexts. Different image modalities face unique noise challenges. For instance, HSI recovery can be particularly susceptible to distributional biases and often suffers from data scarcity

Ceng and Cao et al. proposed Diff-Unmix
[

This method employs a learnable block-based spectral unmixing strategy, supported by a purely transformer-based backbone. They then introduce a self-supervised generative diffusion network to augment the abundance maps from the spectrally unmixed blocks, reconstructing noise-free decomposition probability distributions and effectively mitigating noise-induced degradation within these components. Finally, the HSI is reconstructed through de-mixing by combining the diffusion-adjusted abundance maps with the spectral end-members. He et al.
[

[

They also introduced a fusion operator to combine HSI-DM and RGB-DM, further enhancing the performance of DM-based HSI denoising. Kai et al. proposed a multi-scale spatial spectral joint denoising network for hyperspectral images (MSFDN)
[
137

They also designed a noise separation network and a subspace inverse projection network, leveraging a conditional diffusion model for enhanced robustness.

5
Challenges and Limitations of the Diffusion Model

5.1
Computational complexity and Time cost of the model

Diffusion Models achieve data generation and denoising through multi-step iterative Markov chains, whose computational complexity increases exponentially with the data dimensions. Taking the denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)
[
19
]
as an example, hundreds of forward and reverse diffusion computations are required to generate a single hyperspectral image, especially when processing hyperspectral data above 200 bands, and the GPU memory occupancy can be more than 20 GB (e.g., 1024 × 1024 resolution scenes). Although the improved DDIM
[
35
]
reduces the number of sampling steps to less than 50 through non-Markov chains, the spatial-spectral three-dimensional nature of hyperspectral data (see Section 2.2) still leads to a single inference time as long as several minutes, which makes it difficult to satisfy real-time demanding tasks, such as agricultural monitoring and disaster warning. Although the Latent Diffusion Model (LDM)
[
18
]
reduces the computational volume by compressing the latent space, the information compression in the spectral dimension may lose key features (e.g., mineral absorption peaks), which affects the classification and anomaly detection accuracy (see Table 2).

5.2
Impact of high dimensionality of hyperspectral data on model training

The performance of diffusion models is highly dependent on large-scale labeled datasets, and the cost of labeling hyperspectral data far exceeds that of traditional RGB images. For example, field sampling is required to validate crop classes in agricultural applications, and the cost of labeling a single image can be hundreds of dollars. Although DDPM
[
19
]
generates synthetic samples through data augmentation, its fidelity is limited by the distribution of training data, and it is prone to Mode Collapse in scenarios with fewer samples (e.g., rare mineral detection). Experiments show that when the training samples are less than 1000, the classification accuracy of the diffusion model decreases by about 12% (F1-score from 0.85 to 0.75) compared to Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE)
[
9
]
. In addition, conditional diffusion models based on classifier guidance (e.g., ADM-G
[
11
]
) need to pre-train high-precision classifiers, but the class imbalance problem of hyperspectral data (e.g., less than 1% of anomaly samples in anomaly detection) leads to difficulties in classifier training, which restricts the model generalization ability.

5.3
Adaptability issues to large-scale data

Real hyperspectral images often contain non-Gaussian noise (e.g., sensor thermal noise, atmospheric scattering noise), and most of the existing diffusion models are based on additive Gaussian noise assumptions (see Section 3.1.1) The forward process design of the DDPM
[
19
]
is only applicable to isotropic Gaussian noise, and the denoising effect is significantly degraded for streak or PoissonGaussian hybrid noise (PSNR reduction of 8 15 dB). Although Score-SDE
[
21
]
extends the scope of noise modeling through stochastic differential equations, its inverse process relies on the accurate estimation of the score function, which is susceptible to error accumulation in bands where the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) fluctuates drastically (e.g., near-infrared bands). For example, the Diffusion Models’ denoising performance degrades by about 20% compared to the conventional wavelet approach under extreme conditions where the noise standard deviation exceeds 30 dB. In addition, the mixed image element problem (see Section 2.3) leads to a high degree of spatial coupling between the noise and the target signal, further exacerbating the modeling difficulty.

5.4
Limitations of current models in practical applications

The black-box nature of diffusion models exposes them to credibility challenges in hyperspectral critical applications. For example, in anomaly detection tasks, the model may misclassify rare features as noise by overfitting the training data (literature
[
16
]
). Experiments have shown that LDM
[
18
]
based anomaly detectors have a 18% higher false alarm rate than the traditional RX algorithm (see Section 2.3) in the detection of unknown mineral types. In addition, although the Latent Diffusion Model improves the computational efficiency, the physical meaning of the latent vectors is unclear, which makes it difficult for users to trace the generation logic of the detection results. In scenarios that require strict validation, such as military target identification, the interpretability disadvantage of diffusion models significantly constrains their practical application. Comparative studies have shown that traditional methods (e.g., SVM)
[
154
]
can provide a decision basis through feature importance analysis, whereas diffusion models can only output probability estimates and lack a transparent inference chain.

In summary, the practical deployment of diffusion models in hyperspectral image processing is still limited by core bottlenecks such as computational efficiency, data dependency, noise robustness and interpretability. Future research needs to combine hyperspectral data characteristics (e.g., spectral continuity, spatial-spectral correlation), develop lightweight architectures and adaptive noise modeling methods, and explore semi-supervised learning frameworks to reduce annotation costs, so as to promote its large-scale application in remote sensing, agriculture and other fields.

6.1.1
Introduction to evaluation metrics and Datasets

The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) is a qualitative analysis of the model. The horizontal coordinate of the curve is the false positive rate (FPR) and the vertical coordinate is the true positive rate (TPR).

AUC (area under ROC curve) is the area under ROC curve, which is a quantitative analysis of the model. In practice, AUC is often used as a metrics for evaluating the accuracy of a method. The larger the AUC value, the better the detection performance of the algorithm, with mAUC representing the average AUC. where larger
{\rm AUC}_{(D,F)}
and
{\rm AUC}_{(D,\tau)}
indicate better detection performance, and are combined together defined as
{\rm AUC}_{TD}
;
{\rm AUC}_{BS}
represents the ability of the detector to minimize false alarms due to background interference;
{\rm AUC}_{ODP}
denotes the overall probability of detection;
{\rm AUC}_{TD-BS}
specifically evaluates the performance of the detector in recognizing a targets in background noise, taking into account both detectability and background suppression;
{\rm AUC}_{SNPR}
reflects the detector’s ability to distinguish between signal and noise.

OA denotes the ratio of the number of correctly classified samples to the total number of samples in all test samples; AA denotes the average of the classification accuracy for each category; k × 100 is the Kappa coefficient is a measure of the consistency of the classifier, which takes into account stochastic consistency and provides a measure of the consistency between the classifier and the ground truth.

IoU is a measure of the degree of overlap between the predicted frame and the real frame;
\mathbf{P}_{d}
is defined as the ratio of the number of correctly detected targets to the total number of targets; and
\mathbf{F}_{a}
is defined as the ratio of the number of false alarms to the total number of pixels in the image.

PSNR is the Peak Signal to Noise Ratio, a measure of the difference in quality between a super-resolution processed image and the original high-resolution image.FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) is a metrics used to assess the quality of the generated images, measuring their similarity to each other. Patch Similarity evaluates the perceptual difference between the generated image and the original high-resolution image. parmas is the number of parameters for model training. entropy is usually used to measure the uncertainty and complexity of image information. niqe is the Natural Image Quality Evaluator, which evaluates the image quality metrics. SSIM (Structural Similarity) measures the similarity between two images, comparing images in terms of brightness, contrast and structure; FSIM (Feature Similarity) evaluates the image quality by calculating the phase coherence and gradient similarity in localized regions of the image, and is an improved version of SSIM; SAM (Sum of Absolute Errors) is another measure of the effectiveness of image denoising, which calculates the sum of the absolute value of the pixel differences between a denoised image and the original image counterparts.

6.2
Key discoveries and Summaries from the Experiments

As evidenced by the experimental comparisons in Section 5.2.2, diffusion models exhibit significant advantages in hyperspectral image processing tasks, while also revealing certain limitations.

In classification tasks, the SpectralDiff method, based on diffusion models, demonstrates superior accuracy metrics (OA, AA, and Kappa coefficients) across the Indian Pines, Pavia University, and Salinas datasets. For instance, its OA values were 93.15%, 94.78%, and 98.97% respectively, outperforming traditional deep learning methods like GANs and VAEs. This indicates its effectiveness in capturing complex spectral-spatial features. However, a significant drawback is its inference time, which is considerably higher (e.g., 48.53 s on Indian Pines) compared to lightweight models such as miniGCN (0.95 s), exposing a computational efficiency bottleneck for high-dimensional data processing.

For target detection, the diffusion-based Diff-Mosaic method achieves optimal performance on the SIRST and NUDT-SIRST datasets, with excellent IoU (79.44% on SIRST), high detection probability (99.99% on SIRST), and a low false alarm rate (3.19% on SIRST). It particularly excels at complex background suppression and precise target localization. Despite this, its parameter count (4.7M) and inference time (43.42 s) remain higher than traditional methods like ACM (0.29M parameters, 18.53 s inference time). This suggests that further lightweighting of diffusion models is needed to meet real-time demands in target detection.

In anomaly detection, DWSDiff comprehensively leads in mAUC metrics (e.g.,
{mAUC}_{TD}
of 0.97427), validating its ability to suppress complex backgrounds through its dual-window strategy and iterative diffusion. While BSDM’s performance is also respectable, DWSDiff stands out. Nevertheless, DWSDiff’s inference time (0.8094 s) is still substantially higher than highly efficient methods like Auto-AD (0.0022 s). Additionally, the model’s sensitivity to hyperparameters (e.g., noise scheduling) and its need for further optimization in generalization ability indicate that practical deployment of diffusion models in anomaly detection requires a careful balance between accuracy and efficiency.

For image recovery, diffusion model methods such as FastDiffSR, EDiffSR, and DDPM significantly outperform conventional methods in key metrics like PSNR and FID. For example, FastDiffSR achieved a PSNR of 33.13 dB and an FID of 22.83 on the Potsdam dataset, demonstrating superior detail fidelity and distributional consistency. Similarly, on the Toronto dataset, FastDiffSR maintained its advantage with a PSNR of 25.53 dB and an FID of 55.76, confirming its strength in semantic information retention. However, the high number of parameters in these diffusion models (e.g., FastDiffSR with 23.23M parameters) leads to a substantial increase in training time. This highlights that while diffusion models possess strong generative capabilities, their computational efficiency and model complexity require further optimization for practical deployment.

In noise suppression tasks, diffusion model-based methods, including Diff-Unmix, DDRM, and DDS2M, show significant advantages across metrics like PSNR, SSIM, and FSIM. On the KAIST dataset, Diff-Unmix achieved a PSNR of 31.41 dB, an SSIM of 0.902, and an FSIM of 0.958, underscoring its benefits in noise suppression, structure preservation, and spectral fidelity. Its superiority was further validated on the CAVE dataset with a PSNR of 32.71 dB and an SSIM of 0.940. Despite their effectiveness in removing noise and retaining details, the long inference time of diffusion models (e.g., 42 s for Diff-Unmix) restricts their application in scenarios demanding high real-time performance, suggesting a need for improved computational efficiency.

Some key points are also found.1. Accuracy advantage: diffusion models are significantly mainstream deep learning methods (e.g., GAN, VAE) in tasks such as classification, target detection, and anomaly detection, especially in complex background suppression, high-dimensional feature modeling, and noise robustness.2. Computational cost: the computational complexity and inference time of diffusion models are generally high, e.g., SpectralDiff The inference time on Salinas dataset reaches 242 seconds, which is far more than miniGCN (2.13 seconds), limiting its application in real-time scenarios (e.g., disaster monitoring).3. Model adaptation: diffusion models are sensitive to hyper-parameters (e.g., noise scheduling, number of diffusion steps), and need to be customized with optimization strategies for hyperspectral data characteristics. For example, DWSDiff mitigates the background estimation bias by a double-window strategy, but its generalization still needs to be verified.4. Complementarity with traditional methods: traditional methods (e.g., SVM) are still advantageous in terms of interpretability and lightness, while diffusion models are more suitable for high-precision and complex scene tasks. Future research can explore hybrid architectures to balance efficiency and performance.

7
Outlook

In recent years, diffusion models have made significant progress in tasks such as image generation, denoising and reconstruction by virtue of their powerful generative capabilities and modeling advantages over complex data distributions. The diffusion model shows unique potential in the multidimensional and high-information-density field of hyperspectral image processing. Hyperspectral images contain spectral information in hundreds of consecutive bands, but their high-dimensional characteristics and noise interference pose challenges for traditional methods. As shown in the previous chapter, diffusion models can accurately model spectral-spatial features through a step-by-step denoising process, thus achieving higher accuracy in the task. However, the fact is that diffusion models still have a long way to go in the hyperspectral domain, and there are many tasks that still need to be explored and further improved, and the current research is still limited by the consumption of computational resources, insufficient cross-modal fusion and lack of interpretability. In the future, technical innovations can be promoted in the following three aspects: innovation in fusion with other traditional deep learning models, optimization and deployment in real scenarios, and automated model design and hyperparameter tuning.

1, Fusion innovation with other deep learning models, so that the diffusion model and the mainstream architecture to form a complementary, such as CNN + diffusion model, the use of CNN to extract spatial-spectral features as the a priori conditions of the diffusion process, to improve the efficiency of the generation; Transformer + diffusion model, through the attention mechanism to model the long-range dependence, to optimize the super-resolution reconstruction of hyperspectral images; GAN / VAE + diffusion model, which first generates low-resolution samples with GAN or VAE, and then performs detail enhancement and denoising by diffusion model to balance the generation speed and quality; Physics-driven fusion: combining physical a priori such as radiative transfer model, to enhance the generalization ability of diffusion model in complex environments (e.g., atmospheric interference).

2. Optimization and deployment in real-world scenarios need to be achieved through model lightweighting (e.g., knowledge distillation, dynamic diffusion step size), hardware adaptation (parallelized FPGA/GPU-based design) and embedded deployment (low-precision inference on edge devices). For vertical domains such as agricultural disease monitoring and mineral exploration, task-oriented diffusion frameworks need to be developed to balance accuracy and real-time requirements.

3. Automated model design and hyperparameter tuning. The performance of diffusion models is highly dependent on noise scheduling, network architecture and training strategies. Automated techniques (e.g., neural architecture search, meta-learning) can adaptively optimize the model architecture; reinforcement learning-driven hyperparameter search can dynamically adjust the denoising process for different hyperspectral tasks. Meanwhile, the development of unified evaluation benchmarks (e.g., cross-dataset generalization metrics) will accelerate the application and iteration of automated techniques.

In conclusion, diffusion model provides a new paradigm for hyperspectral image processing, but its development still needs to break through the bottlenecks of computational efficiency, cross-modal fusion and domain adaptation. In the future, through the combination of algorithmic innovation, hardware synergy and automation technology, the diffusion model is expected to realize smarter and more efficient analysis systems in the fields of environmental monitoring, precision agriculture, etc., and to promote hyperspectral technology from the laboratory to large-scale applications.

8
Conclusion

In summary, diffusion models offer significant advantages over other deep generative models for hyperspectral image analysis. They demonstrate robustness to the inherent noise in hyperspectral images, adapt better to their variability and complexity, and provide a more stable training process. With their progressive denoising mechanism and high-dimensional data modeling capabilities, diffusion models have injected a revolutionary impetus into the field of hyperspectral image analysis. They effectively overcome the limitations of traditional methods in handling high-dimensional data, noise interference, and small sample scenarios through their Markov chain noise injection and inverse generation process. Therefore, the application of diffusion models in hyperspectral imaging is an inevitable trend. This paper first introduced relevant knowledge about hyperspectral images and the basic theory of diffusion models. Subsequently, it reviewed research on diffusion models within the hyperspectral domain, covering tasks such as data enhancement, generation and complementation, classification segmentation, anomaly detection, target detection, and denoising. Moreover, by comparing various visualization results and quantitative indices between diffusion models and traditional models in hyperspectral image processing, this paper fully demonstrated the superiority of diffusion models in this field. Finally, the limitations of existing diffusion models in the hyperspectral domain were discussed, emphasizing the need for further exploratory studies concerning extended applications and model deployment.
