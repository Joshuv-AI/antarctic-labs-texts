X-CART: a multimodal real-world data pipeline for explainable AI-driven patient navigation in radiation oncology
Authors: JA Zink, R Rashid, L Chinthala, S Kheirinejad, BM White, P Kheirkhah Rahimabad, S Hashtarkhani, FA Kumsa, CL Brett, RL Davis, DL Schwartz, A Shaban-Nejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimodal explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) is transforming oncology, yet its clinical adoption is constrained by limited interpretability and fragmented use of real-world data (RWD), including electronic medical records, geospatial data, and social determinants of health. We present the XAI CAncer Radiation Therapy (X-CART) platform, a multimodal RWD pipeline designed to enable explainable, clinically actionable precision oncology.Materials and methodsX-CART integrates heterogeneous RWD into a unified, AI-ready architecture for explainable radiotherapy interruption (RTI) risk prediction and patient navigation. A retrospective proof-of-implementation analysis demonstrated the pipeline’s predictive modeling and explainability. This framework supports targeted, patient-level intervention strategies.ResultsWe developed a scalable, interoperable pipeline that synthesizes clinical and community-level data into a secure multimodal framework for RTI risk prediction and patient navigation. The system employs iterative machine learning with feedback-driven retraining and delivers real-time, interpretable outputs through an electronic medical record-embedded dashboard. The architecture is registry-extensible and aligned with interoperability standards to support multi-institutional deployment. As proof of implementation of the Data Science module, a retrospective cohort analysis including 2525 patients identified 622 RTI events (24.6%) and demonstrated modest discrimination with eXtreme Gradient Boosting on an independent hold-out test set (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve = 0.689) with cohort-level SHapley Additive exPlanations interpretability.ConclusionThe framework addresses critical RWD challenges, including data quality, completeness, and traceability. X-CART establishes a generalizable, privacy-preserving foundation for XAI in oncology and provides a practical blueprint for enriching cancer registries with multimodal RWD to support scalable, real-world evidence generation.Key words: cancer registries, data interoperability, explainable AI (XAI), HL7 FHIR, real-world data (RWD)

Introduction

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) are revolutionizing health care by incorporating predictive models that provide pertinent clinical decision support (CDS) and improve patient outcomes. In particular, deep learning approaches have shown promise in diagnostic radiomics, offering better-quality image analysis and feature extraction capabilities.1 However, the complexity of these models can result in a ‘black box’ problem, in which a lack of explanatory transparency driving recommendations can limit provider confidence in results and clinical adoption.2 To address this, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) frameworks such as Local Interpretable Model Agnostic Explanations and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) have emerged, providing interpretable visualizations of model predictions.3 These tools have been applied across various oncology domains, including prognostication, diagnosis, and treatment selection, facilitating feature importance determination and relationship visualization.3 A recent review of XAI applications in oncology emphasizes the need for interpretability and trustworthiness in AI-mediated tools as deployed in health care.4

Multimodal AI platforms incorporate diverse data types to enhance the performance of these diagnostic and prognostic models.5 The integration of varied data sources, including imaging, dosimetry, and electronic medical record (EMR), has enabled the creation of comprehensive predictive models in Radiation Oncology.6 Although promising, challenges remain in data harmonization and the generalizability of AI models in this area, such as small dataset size and varying feature extraction sensitivity.1 These challenges are compounded when data originates from heterogeneous real-world sources, including routine clinical care records, community health databases, and population-level geospatial repositories that were not designed with AI readiness or interoperability in mind.7 Ongoing research aims to develop robust, XAI models that can be effectively integrated into clinical workflows, particularly in oncology treatment settings.3 Cancer registries represent one important source of such real-world data (RWD), yet they often lack the granularity of social determinants of health (SDoH) variables, unstructured clinical narratives, and imaging data needed to power next-generation predictive models.6 Architectures that can bridge registry-level population data with institution-level multimodal clinical data are therefore increasingly needed.

Furthermore, in prior work, our group demonstrated an emerging AI predictive model platform that has the potential to accurately triage radiotherapy interruption (RTI) risk and direct personalized patient support to reduce barriers to receiving radiotherapy (RT).8 Uninterrupted RT is crucial to ensure local control and cure. RTIs, especially total interruptions of >5 days, lead to accelerated tumor cell repopulation and radioresistance9 and poorer clinical outcomes,10, 11, 12, 13, 14 particularly for fast-growing carcinomas such as head and neck cancers.15 Preventing interruptions can be challenging given that RT is time-consuming (up to 6-7 weeks), toxic, and generates high out-of-pocket patient expense16; prior studies suggest that upward of 50% of socially vulnerable RT patients may experience treatment interruptions, particularly uninsured patients and Medicaid beneficiaries.17, 18, 19 RTI risk is shaped not only by clinical factors but also by social and environmental determinants that are rarely captured in traditional registry data, including transportation barriers, housing instability, and neighborhood-level deprivation.20 In a related retrospective analysis of 2130 RT patients treated in Eastern Tennessee, nearly 1 in 4 missed two or more scheduled treatments; Medicaid coverage, marital instability, and high neighborhood social vulnerability index scores predicted interruption risk and geospatial mapping tied elevated risk to specific rural ZIP codes.21 These findings echo our group’s prior work in West Tennessee, which found similar treatment-access barriers by neighborhood, race, and insurance status in Memphis22 and pandemic-era declines in RT referrals that disproportionately affected high-poverty areas.23 Therefore, identifying high-risk patient candidates for individualized support interventions is key to maintaining cancer care quality.7 Doing so effectively requires data pipelines capable of integrating and harmonizing these diverse RWD streams in a privacy-preserving, AI-ready format.

Objectives

This manuscript presents the XAI CAncer Radiation Therapy (X-CART) platform, a multimodal RWD architecture designed to support XAI-driven patient navigation in radiation oncology. To demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed framework, we report a retrospective proof of implementation of its predictive modeling component using a real-world clinical cohort. The X-CART platform integrates and transforms diverse, heterogeneous health care data into an AI-ready format8 for predicting individual patient risk for RTIs. In doing so, it addresses the need for scalable, interoperable pipelines that transform fragmented real-world clinical and community data into structured, registry-compatible formats suitable for XAI.

Ultimately, the X-CART architecture provides a scalable and privacy-preserving foundation for implementing explainable RTI risk prediction and future causal inference workflows in Radiation Oncology while offering a blueprint for multimodal AI integration in other high-acuity clinical domains. More broadly, X-CART illustrates how institution-level RWD infrastructure can be designed with registry readiness in mind, supporting the harmonization, enrichment, and mobilization of cancer data for real-world evidence generation.

Materials and methods

Many existing XAI tools in Radiation Oncology rely on single-modality data24 or exclude unstructured data sources such as clinical narratives and imaging.25,26 In contrast, X-CART is designed to leverage a contextually diverse, AI-ready dataset that prioritizes interpretability and explainability for clinical usability. Our planned pipeline integrates structured clinical data (e.g. demographics, prescriptions), unstructured clinical data (e.g. clinical narratives, imaging), and nonclinical data sources (e.g. geospatial and SDoH variables) into a unified data model. The retrospective proof-of-implementation analysis reported in later sections uses structured clinical, treatment, demographic, behavioral, geospatial, and SDoH variables; clinical text, imaging, radiomics, and dosimetry streams are retained as future pipeline extensions.

The X-CART pipeline comprises three operational modules: the Data Architecture Team, which acquires, harmonizes, and deidentifies multimodal data; the Data Science Team, which performs preprocessing, feature engineering, predictive modeling, and explainability analyses; and the User Experience Team, which integrates model outputs into clinician-facing workflows (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100771). Although the architecture is designed to support future causal inference extensions, the retrospective proof of implementation presented here focuses on predictive modeling.27 The architecture incorporates explainability as a core design principle, ensuring that AI-generated outputs are understandable, interpretable, and actionable for oncologists, care coordinators, and community health support specialists (CHSSs).

The architecture is designed to return model outputs to the EMR through Health Level Seven International (HL7) Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR)28 interfaces, where they can be displayed through role-specific dashboards to support point-of-care decision making and patient navigation.

The architecture is designed around standard HL7 FHIR resources, including Patient, Condition, Observation, Procedure, and CarePlan, to represent patient demographics, cancer diagnoses, treatment information, RTI risk scores, and navigation recommendations. AI-generated predictions and explainability outputs can be encoded as FHIR Observation resources linked to relevant clinical records, facilitating interoperability, registry-compatible data exchange, and integration into clinical workflows.

The use of HL7 FHIR-based registry standards also ensures that pipeline outputs are structured in formats compatible with interoperability requirements increasingly adopted by cancer registry systems.

X-CART study population and ethical oversight

The X-CART framework is being implemented within University of Tennessee Health Science Center (UTHSC)-affiliated oncology clinics, including collaborating clinical sites at the University of Tennessee Medical Center (UTMC). The ongoing implementation study evaluates integration of multimodal clinical and SDoH data to support XAI-assisted patient navigation. All study procedures were approved by the UTHSC Institutional Review Board and are conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

Eligible participants include adults (≥18 years) undergoing consultation with a UTHSC-affiliated oncology provider for a malignant condition. Prospective participants provide written informed consent before enrollment and eligible retrospective clinical data may also be incorporated under institutional review board approval to support development and evaluation of the platform.

The X-CART architecture

X-CART is designed to help clinic providers identify patients at elevated risk for RTIs and understand and mitigate the underlying factors contributing to that risk.7 To that end, the platform integrates an individualized patient navigation intervention centered on CHSSs, who provide longitudinal support to patients throughout the cancer treatment journey.

After automated risk stratification, patients identified to be at elevated risk are referred to a CHSS, who assesses patient-identified social and logistical barriers to treatment, develops individualized navigation plans, and coordinates referrals to community resources. Within the X-CART implementation study, CHSSs work alongside clinicians, research personnel, and community partners to deliver personalized navigation strategies. The CHSS–patient relationship is maintained through the early survivorship period for ≥3 months (Figure 1).

Proposed cancer patient support journey during treatment, informed by real-world clinical and community data. CHSS, community health support specialist; X-CART, XAI CAncer Radiation Therapy.

The X-CART framework is an automated, interoperable health data platform including three major modules developed and implemented by three corresponding teams, albeit with some overlap as data move iteratively through the multimodal pipeline: Data Architecture, Data Science, and User Experience. The Data Architecture Team oversees secure data acquisition, harmonization, interoperability, and governance. The Data Science Team performs data preprocessing, feature engineering, predictive model development, validation, explainability analyses, and future causal inference extension deployment. The User Experience Team designs clinician-facing decision support interfaces and evaluates workflow integration.

The three operational modules are supported by existing multidisciplinary expertise and institutional infrastructure within the participating organizations rather than newly established organizational units. Collectively, these teams include biomedical informaticians, clinicians, data scientists, software engineers, and implementation researchers collaborating across the UTHSC, UTMC, and affiliated clinical partners, providing the institutional infrastructure necessary for scalable implementation.

Data processing and modeling

Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4 depict a high-level overview of the framework. Input data include individual-level EMR data such as clinical text, SDoH variables, and population-level geospatial data, which are RWD sources often siloed and underutilized in registry-based oncology research. Once a clean, deidentified dataset is produced by the Data Architecture Team, the Data Science Team performs preprocessing and feature extraction. The resulting unified dataset is then fed into the machine learning (ML) modeling module for training, validation, and refinement, with outputs routed to an EMR-integrated dashboard for pilot user testing and production version deployment by the User Experience Team.

The Data Architecture Team uses Extract, Transform, Load (ETL) methods29 to load diverse data sources, including hospital EMR-sourced demographic, behavioral, and clinical data, geospatial data linked to patients’ geographic identifiers, and census-tract level SDoH data. Before preprocessing, all patient data undergoes deidentification and encryption to support HIPAA compliance. Figure 2 outlines the preparation of a standardized, deidentified, AI-ready multimodal dataset.

During preprocessing, the Data Science Team performs feature extraction as a core component of the workflow. Structured and tabular data are normalized and imputed, while unstructured clinical text is processed using the clinical natural language processing toolkit medSpaCy.30 A data compliance checkpoint identifies noncompliant or low-quality records for manual review or automated reprocessing before model development.

Clinical text is processed for named-entity recognition, negation detection, assertion classification, and section segmentation, with extracted concepts aggregated into patient-level features. The retrospective proof-of-implementation analysis used structured clinical, treatment, demographic, behavioral, geospatial, and SDoH variables, while imaging, radiomics, and dosimetry remain as future pipeline extensions. Records missing the RTI outcome were excluded, patients with >50% missing data were removed, minor categorical missingness was labeled ‘unknown,’ outliers were capped at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and highly skewed variables were log-transformed. Addressing data quality, completeness, and traceability at this stage mirrors the data governance priorities increasingly adopted by cancer registries worldwide.31 The resulting harmonized dataset was then used for predictive modeling (Figure 3).

After preprocessing, the Data Science Team feeds the unified dataset into the predictive ML model hosted on a designated platform. We evaluated a majority-class reference, a class-weighted logistic regression baseline, and a gradient boosted decision tree model implemented using eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). The finalized analytic cohort was divided into training, validation, and independent hold-out test sets. The development set, composed of the training and validation partitions, was also evaluated using stratified 10-fold cross-validation, while the independent hold-out test set was reserved for final model evaluation. Causal inference techniques are layered on top of this AI-ready data architecture using rigorous methods such as structural causal models, directed acyclic graphs, and knowledge graphs integrated with advanced ML approaches. Knowledge graphs explicitly represent domain-specific entities and their semantic relationships, facilitating structured integration of multimodal clinical and SDoH data. When combined with causal inference frameworks, these semantic representations allow for systematic exploration of the rich dataset produced by the pipeline, supporting future causal analysis of the drivers of RTIs. In this report, the empirical analysis is predictive rather than causal, with predictions explained by SHAP as associational feature attributions rather than causal effects.

Both model and system-level validation metrics are employed to confirm reliability. If the model’s predictions are not valid, an iterative feedback loop triggers a refinement process with data scientist-mediated hyperparameter tuning and bias mitigation before retraining. Once validated, an HL7 FHIR interface transmits the model’s output back into the hospital EMR system. The use of HL7 FHIR as the interoperability standard ensures that model outputs can, in principle, be federated to external systems, including cancer registry platforms, thereby supporting broader RWD integration efforts. The User Experience Team is responsible for the design of a CDS dashboard within the EMR that filters and presents real-time data and ML model-generated recommendation alerts.

Deployment and user experience

As demonstrated in Figure 4, pipeline outputs are displayed on AI dashboard views tailored for different user roles. Clinicians receive information on treatment schedules, missed therapy sessions, and predicted RTI risk, while CHSSs receive referral suggestions and relevant SDoH data. Neighborhood-level data can also be included through publicly available environmental factors datasets and population datasets such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention PLACES.32

Dashboard elements displayed for each role are designed to support explainability by presenting model outputs in clinically interpretable formats via alerts. Risk tiers are based on the model’s predicted probability of RTI and are displayed in the clinician view using color-coded indicators: green (low risk) indicates standard monitoring, yellow (moderate risk) triggers a proactive CHSS referral, and red (high risk) prompts an urgent clinical alert. These color-coded indicators visually convey the urgency of each AI-generated recommendation and enhance interpretability and explainability of the model outputs, while supporting timely interventions and enabling evaluation of their effectiveness.33 Probability thresholds that define these tiers will be determined from model performance in the development cohort and refined during prospective pilot testing through clinician feedback.

Before full EMR deployment, the dashboard will undergo pilot testing in a simulated clinical environment to assess usability, clinical relevance, and workflow integration. A continuous user feedback loop and adjustment loop will allow clinicians and CHSSs to review, modify, override, or provide input on model outputs, supporting iterative refinement, improving explainability and long-term system adaptability.

Privacy and security

Data at every step of the pipeline are managed in accordance with HIPAA requirements and institutional privacy and security standards. Study centers employ network firewalls and secure virtual private network protocols to transfer data between institutional servers. During preprocessing, a dedicated deidentification and encryption module anonymizes patient data before model development.

The pipeline follows the HIPAA minimum necessary standard34 so that only essential data elements are accessed and retained at each stage of processing. Role-based access control and audit logging mechanisms restrict and monitor access to sensitive health data. Deidentified data used for model training is stored in a secure, access-controlled research enclave, isolated from EMR environments. Moreover, all access to model outputs and clinical dashboards is tracked for compliance and auditing purposes.

In anticipation of scaling to multi-institutional deployments, the architecture is designed to support modular security configurations and federated learning extensions, enabling collaborative model training across firewalled institutions without centralizing raw patient data. This approach addresses a key challenge in cancer registry research: enabling multisite data collaboration while preserving local data storage, patient privacy, and compliance with varied institutional privacy policies across different geographic and regulatory jurisdictions.

Validation strategy

The X-CART framework includes a validation strategy spanning model performance, data quality review, interoperability checks, and future dashboard usability testing. These validation layers are designed to be reproducible across institutions.

Model-level validation

The ML component’s ability to discriminate between interrupted and uninterrupted treatments is quantified via precision recall matrices, F1 scores, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) on held-out folds. Class imbalance was addressed using class weighting. The threshold used for hold-out reporting was selected from development set out-of-fold predictions to prioritize detection of the interruption class. This study is single-institution and retrospective; the independent hold-out test set approximates out-of-sample performance.

Calibration was assessed using the Brier scores on the validation cohort to evaluate agreement between predicted probabilities and observed RTI frequencies.

To deliver actionable, interpretable insights, we carry out post hoc SHAP analyses on the final, calibrated model:

•Global explanations: SHAP summary plots rank feature importance across clinical, geospatial, and SDoH RWD areas.

The SHAP analyses are particularly relevant in the context of RWD-driven oncology research, as they provide transparent attribution of risk to specific real-world variables, including SDoH and geospatial factors that are typically absent from registry datasets, thereby demonstrating the added explanatory value of multimodal RWD integration.

Data quality assurance and system-level validation

Although model validation is essential, X-CART’s reliability also depends on robust upstream data processing and dependable downstream deployment. System-level validation is planned as part of prospective deployment and is not reported as an empirical outcome in the present retrospective proof-of-implementation analysis. The framework includes planned system-level validation of data extraction and transformation, end-to-end pipeline testing, and human–computer interaction evaluations.

Data extraction and transformation quality assurance will be evaluated using synthetic test harnesses in which ‘ground-truth’ records are injected into each RWD source (EMR, geospatial, and clinical notes) to verify accurate ETL extraction, deidentification, normalization, and integration without schema drift or data loss. Automated integrity checks will monitor data completeness, consistency, and distribution shifts across development and production environments. End-to-end pipeline testing will verify workflow integrity, HL7 FHIR interoperability, and alert generation from data ingestion through dashboard rendering.

The prospective deployment will include usability evaluation of the EMR-embedded dashboards using established human–computer interaction methods, including usability testing, user feedback, interviews, and heuristic evaluation, to refine dashboard design, workflow integration, and explainability.

Results

In the retrospective proof-of-implementation analysis, the Data Science module was applied to a finalized analytic cohort of 2525 radiation oncology patients between August 2020 and May 2024. Overall, 622 (24.6%) experienced the binary RTI outcome, defined as two or more missed radiation treatment sessions.

Proof-of-implementation model performance

In the independent hold-out test set (n = 631), the XGBoost model achieved an AUROC of 0.689 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.644-0.734], an area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.413 (95% CI 0.353-0.501), and a Brier score of 0.216. At a development-set threshold selected to prioritize detection of patients with two or more missed sessions (0.455), sensitivity was 0.780 (95% CI 0.713-0.839), specificity 0.502 (95% CI 0.456-0.550), and weighted F1 0.597 (95% CI 0.561-0.631).

For comparison, the logistic regression baseline evaluated at the default 0.50 threshold achieved an AUROC of 0.678, AUPRC of 0.429, Brier score of 0.228, sensitivity of 0.660, specificity of 0.646, and weighted F1 of 0.672. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of the predictive modeling component of the pipeline, including benchmarking conventional and nonlinear models and supporting explainability through the XGBoost-SHAP component, rather than establishing a deployment-ready clinical prediction model.

Explainability output

Cohort-level SHAP analysis identified prescribed RT dose (21.1% of total attribution), dose per fraction (18.8%), and marital status (7.9%) as leading contributors in the implementation analysis (Figure 5). These values represent model attributions, not causal effects, illustrating the explanatory value of multimodal RWD integration.

Discussion

X-CART represents a novel multimodal RWD pipeline linking explainable RTI risk with patient support. Our proof-of-implementation analysis demonstrates that the Data Science module can generate interpretable RTI risk outputs from real-world clinical, treatment, behavioral, geospatial, and SDoH data. Our pilot work confirms that XGBoost achieved modest discrimination on an independent hold-out test set, with performance comparable to baseline, while enabling cohort-level SHAP attributions spanning treatment intensity, demographic, clinical, and SDoH domains. These findings support the feasibility of the pipeline while underscoring need for prospective workflow testing and multi-institutional validation before deployment.

The model was developed using a single-institution cohort with a specific regional case mix and SDoH profile. Feature distributions, calibration, and risk thresholds may therefore shift across settings. Although geospatial and SDoH features are conceptually transportable, implementation at other sites will require local geocoding/SDoH linkage, data element mapping, recalibration, and multi-institutional external validation.

Key challenges and limitations

Primary challenges include multimodal data integration, interoperability, harmonization of heterogeneous data, and mitigation of biases inherent in real-world datasets. These challenges are common across registry-based oncology research and continue to constrain real-world evidence generation. To address these issues, the framework is designed to support feedback-driven model monitoring and refinement that iteratively identifies and corrects bias, model drift, and performance limitations over time. As the predictive modeling components of X-CART continue to mature, future model development, validation, and reporting will align with emerging reporting recommendations for AI-based clinical prediction models, including the TRIPOD+AI statement35 (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100771), to promote transparency and reproducibility and to facilitate clinical translation.

The present proof of implementation did not incorporate automated treatment delivery, dosimetry, or fraction-level treatment data, as these factors are currently outside the scope of the initial pilot build. However, future versions of X-CART will explicitly incorporate automated treatment delivery and dosimetry data ingestion, harmonization, and integrated analytics as a key technical enhancement to broaden the framework’s modeling capabilities.

Future deployment will require continued optimization of model maintenance,36 workflow integration, and human-in-the-loop oversight while balancing interpretability, predictive performance, and usability.

Another limitation in the context of registry-based research is that X-CART currently operates within a single-institution EMR environment rather than a population-based cancer registry. Expanding the framework through multi-institutional deployment and alignment with common registry standards will strengthen its generalizability and real-world evidence capabilities.

Multisite collaborations across Tennessee are underway to improve geographic and demographic representativeness, although external validation remains necessary before clinical deployment. Beyond RTI prediction, the modular X-CART architecture may be adapted to related oncology applications while providing a generalizable framework for integrating EMR, geospatial, and SDoH data to support equitable patient navigation.

Conclusion

This study presents the X-CART platform, a multimodal RWD architecture developed for XAI-driven RTI risk prediction and patient navigation. The retrospective proof of implementation demonstrates the feasibility of the Data Science module for generating interpretable RTI risk predictions from multimodal RWD. Rather than replacing cancer registries, X-CART complements them by enriching registry data with higher-resolution clinical, geospatial, and SDoH information. Future work will focus on prospective workflow evaluation, multi-institutional validation, and broader deployment across diverse oncology settings.
