Effect of Pre-breakfast Whey Protein Supplement on Post-breakfast Glycaemic Excursion and Insulinotropic Response in Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Sagar R. Barasara, Yash V. Chauhan, Jugal V. Gada, Prudwiraj Sanamandra, Sachin S. Rahate, Jayashree J. Paranjape, Nikhil M. Bhagwat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Postprandial glucose excursions can contribute to increased cardiovascular risk, microvascular complications, and glycaemic variability in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Nutritional interventions play a major role. Whey protein, known for its insulinotropic and incretin-stimulating effects, could be an effective supplement. The objective of the study was to evaluate the acute effects of pre-breakfast whey protein supplementation on postprandial glucose, insulin, GLP-1, and C-peptide responses in patients with T2DM.Methods:This single-centre, prospective, within-subject comparative study included two test visits per participant: a water pre-load (control) and a whey protein pre-load (30 g in 250 mL water) given 30 min before breakfast. Uniform breakfast and lunch meals were provided, and blood samples were collected up to 2 h after lunch. Plasma glucose, insulin, GLP-1, and C-peptide were recorded, and the incremental area under the curve (iAUC) was calculated.Results:Ten patients with T2DM were studied (mean age 47.8 years, body mass index 29.2 kg/m², glycated haemoglobin 7.4%, disease duration 3.2 years). Compared with water, whey protein significantly reduced post-breakfast and post-lunch glucose excursions (glucose iAUC 48,329 vs. 54,743; P < 0.01) and increased insulin (13,377 vs. 9,492; P < 0.01), GLP-1 (18,803 vs. 12,814; P < 0.01), and C-peptide (1860 vs. 1535; P < 0.01) iAUCs. The effect persisted through the second meal.Conclusion:Pre-breakfast whey protein supplementation reduced postprandial glucose excursions and enhanced insulin and incretin responses in T2DM. It may serve as a practical and low-cost dietary approach to improve postprandial glycaemic control.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus is one of the significant global health burdens, and its burden is very high in India, with its prevalence estimated to be about 8.9%.[1] One of the early pathophysiological defects in T2DM is loss of first-phase insulin secretion, reflecting beta-cell dysfunction. Second, the incretin effect, mainly mediated by GLP-1, is attenuated in patients with T2DM.[2]

Impaired first-phase insulin response, in combination with a reduced incretin effect, leads to exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia, which may drive overall glycaemic burden and cardiovascular risk.[23]

Nutritional modulation is considered a potential physiological approach to enhancing insulin and incretin responses beyond pharmacological interventions. In healthy subjects, a dose of 30–50 g of protein has been found to cause a two- to three-fold elevation in plasma insulin levels from the baseline value, which may last as long as 3 h.[4] Of dietary proteins, whey protein is highly recognised for its rapid absorption combined with strong insulinotropic potency due in part to its stimulation of incretin secretion.

Given this background, pre-meal whey protein supplementation may represent a simple, cost-effective strategy to augment early insulin secretion, enhance incretin effect, and reduce postprandial glycaemic excursions in patients with T2DM. The objective of this study is to evaluate the effect of pre-breakfast whey protein supplementation on insulin secretion and incretin response, and to determine its impact on post-breakfast and post-lunch glycaemic excursions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A pilot, single-centre, open-label, within-subject crossover interventional study was conducted at a tertiary care centre.

Study participants

Participants were recruited from the diabetes clinic of the tertiary care hospital. The major inclusion criteria were as follows: age >18 years, duration of T2DM <10 years, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) between 6.5% and 8%, body mass index (BMI) >23 kg/m², normal renal and liver function, and normal thyroid function. Subjects included those who had followed a stable dietary pattern for at least 3 months and were prescribed only the oral hypoglycaemic drug metformin, with dose and frequency stable for the last 3 months.

Excluded subjects were those prescribed insulin or on oral hypoglycaemic agents other than metformin, those receiving alternative system medications or steroids, and those with impaired renal function (glomerular filtration rate <60 ml/min by CKD-EPI formula), coronary artery disease, impaired liver function (SGOT and SGPT >3 × ULN), pregnancy or lactation, acute or chronic systemic infective illness, psychiatric disorders, history of illicit drug use, chronic smoking, alcoholism, or allergy to milk or milk products. Patients receiving sulfonylureas were excluded, as these agents stimulate insulin secretion independent of nutrient intake and could confound interpretation of insulin and C-peptide responses.

Study procedure

Patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were recruited after Ethics Committee approval. Demographic details, disease history, and treatment history were obtained. A detailed physical and anthropometric examination was performed, and findings were recorded on a Case Record Form.

Subjects were called to the clinic on a separate day between 8:00 and 8:30 am after an overnight fast of at least 8 h [Figure 1]. On the first visit, an intravenous cannula was inserted for repeated venous blood sampling. The fasting sample was drawn for investigations (fasting plasma glucose, serum insulin, C-peptide, and plasma GLP-1) between 8:30 and 9:00 am (t = –30 min), followed by 250 ml of water. After 30 min, another sample was drawn (t = 0 min), after which the test breakfast meal (white wheat bread 38 g, paneer 15 g, butter 5 g, toned milk 200 ml; total calories 300 kcal, carbohydrates 26 g, protein 13.9 g, fat 15 g) was served. Blood samples were subsequently collected at t = 15, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 180 min.

Study design and sample collection schedule for control (water) and intervention (whey protein) visits. Legend: The crossover study consisted of two test visits separated by a washout period. On Day 1 (green section), participants received 250 mL of plain water 30 min before breakfast (control condition). On Day 2 (blue section), the same participants received whey protein mixed in 250 mL of water 30 min before breakfast (intervention condition). The timing of meals and blood sample collection remained identical to Day 1

Three hours after breakfast (at 180 min), lunch (calories 513 kcal; carbohydrates 88 g, protein 19.7 g, fat 7.1 g) was served, and blood was drawn again 2 h post-lunch. The subject was then discharged and called for a second visit after 1 week.

On the second visit, each participant repeated the same procedure, except that at –30 min, 30 g whey protein (MuscleBlaze®, providing approximately 24 g protein, 1.5 g fat, and <3 g carbohydrate per serving) dissolved in 250 mL of water, administered 30 min before breakfast. Breakfast and lunch were served at the same timings as the previous visit, and samples were collected accordingly.

Biochemical and hormonal blood analyses

Plasma glucose concentrations were measured using the glucose oxidase method. Serum insulin and C-peptide were measured by chemiluminescence immunoassay. Plasma glucose, insulin, and C-peptide were analysed on the same day of the collection.

Special tubes were made to collect samples for collecting GLP-1. One tablet of cocktail mini-protease inhibitor (Sigma Aldrich) mixed with 750 microliters of distilled water was added to EDTA tubes; 75 μL of this solution was used to collect GLP-1. It was kept at −20° for maximum use of 2 weeks. Blood samples for total GLP-1 were collected in chilled EDTA vacutainers, centrifuged at 4°C at 2000 g for 10 min, and plasma was separated and immediately stored at –80°C. Plasma total GLP-1 was measured using the Merck Millipore ELISA kit (Cat: EZGLP1T-36K) (GLP-1 ELISA Intra-assay CV <5% and inter-assay CV <12%). The limit of sensitivity of the assay was 1.5 pm.

Sample size and power analysis

Based on previously published data by Jakubowicz et al. demonstrating approximately 15–20% reductions in postprandial glucose area under the curve following whey protein pre-load in patients with type 2 diabetes, a 15% difference was assumed for sample size estimation.[5] With this assumption, a sample size of ten participants provided 90% power to detect differences in postprandial glucose, insulin, C-peptide, and GLP-1 responses.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and SPSS version 26.0 IBM, USA. Incremental area under the curve (iAUC₀–₁₈₀) was calculated using the trapezoidal method for 180 min above the immediate pre-breakfast/30 min post-intervention value. These were calculated separately for the water and whey visits for glucose, insulin, C-peptide, and GLP-1.

Continuous data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and a paired t-test was used to compare differences between conditions. For non-normally distributed data, results are expressed as median ± interquartile range, and the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test was applied.

Graphs are presented as means ± 95% confidence intervals. Significant differences in graphs were calculated separately for whey and water at each time point. Time-series variables were assessed using two-way ANOVA for normally distributed data (after checking and correcting for sphericity) and Friedman’s test for non-normally distributed data. Post-hoc Bonferroni correction was applied when appropriate. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

This study was approved by “Ethics Committee for Academic Research Projects (ECARP)” at Topiwala National Medical College and BYL Nair Charitable Hospital. The approval letter number is ECARP/2020/138 dated 21/01/2021 and 10/06/2021. The written informed consent was obtained for participation in the study and use of the patient data for research and educational purposes. The study procedures adhered to guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, 1964 and its subsequent revisions.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The study included ten participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus [Table 1]. The mean age was 47.8 ± 7.9 years, with a mean disease duration of 3.2 ± 3.2 years. The mean BMI was 29.2 ± 2.4 kg/m². Glycaemic parameters were as follows: fasting plasma glucose 149.6 ± 38.3 mg/dl, postprandial plasma glucose (PP-S) 212.8 ± 55.8 mg/dl, and HbA1c 7.42% ± 0.42%. Among comorbidities, one participant each had hypothyroidism and coronary artery disease.

Plasma glucose response

At baseline (–30 min and 0 min), fasting plasma glucose levels were similar between visits. After breakfast, glucose rose in both groups but with a higher excursion in the water arm. Peak glucose occurred at 90 min (178.1 ± 27.8 mg/dl with water vs. 150.7 ± 13.4 mg/dl with whey). Glucose concentrations remained constantly lower with whey supplement across 90–180 min. At 2 h post-lunch, the whey group again confirmed a blunted rise (194.9 ± 56.3 mg/dl) compared to the water group (221.4 ± 48.9 mg/dl) (P = 0.017) [Figure 2a].

Serum insulin response

Baseline fasting insulin values were comparable between visits. After whey supplementation, insulin rose earlier and more strongly (P = 0.002), peak insulin at 120 mins, 47.2 ± 43.2 with whey vs. 33.0 ± 24.7 μIU/ml after water. Higher concentrations persisted up to 120 min, however, post-lunch insulin levels not significantly different between the two visits (P = 0.412) [Figure 2b].

Plasma GLP-1 response

Whey protein activated a vigorous increase in GLP-1, peaking at ~75 pmol/L at 30 min versus ~45 pmol/L with water (P < 0.0001). GLP-1 levels remained elevated through 120 min and were still higher at 180 min and 2 h post-lunch (P = 0.030), indicating continued incretin [Figure 2c] stimulus.

C-peptide response

C-peptide profiles mirrored insulin, with consistently greater values after whey supplementation (4.5 vs. 3.4; P = 0.001). This confirmed enhanced endogenous insulin secretion [Figure 2d].

Area under the curve analysis

Incremental AUC analyses confirmed the graphical findings [Table 2]. Whey protein significantly reduced glucose exposure (P < 0.01), while increasing insulin, GLP-1, and C-peptide exposure (all P < 0.01). These findings highlight whey protein’s ability to blunt glycaemic excursions and augment both insulinotropic and incretin responses, including a beneficial second-meal effect.

DISCUSSION

This study showed that pre-breakfast whey protein supplementation significantly reduced post-breakfast and post-lunch glycaemic excursions while increasing insulin, GLP-1, and C-peptide responses in patients with T2DM. The study interpretations focused on within-subject iAUC comparisons, which provide a robust measure of integrated postprandial exposure in crossover physiological studies, rather than isolated time-point differences. These changes are clinically relevant because postprandial hyperglycaemia contributes to the overall glycaemic burden and has been related to cardiovascular risk and complications.[12] This is of particular consequence in India, where the prevalence of T2DM is already 8.9% and is likely to continue rising,[1] and where approaches aimed at postprandial control are very appropriate. By lessening glycaemic excursions, whey protein could represent a simple adjunctive tool that may contribute to decreasing vascular complications while improving overall glycaemic stability.

Routine and nutritional measures remain the foundation for the management of T2DM, even though GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors effectively address postprandial dysregulation pharmacologically. Wide dietary variability and confounding cultural factors commonly make it difficult for population trials to demonstrate the impact of single nutrients at a population level; hence, targeted mechanistic studies are of value. We studied ten patients with relatively early T2DM (mean HbA1c 7.4%), overweight phenotype (mean BMI 29.2 kg/m²), and preserved renal and hepatic function. This narrow selection allowed us to minimize variability and focus on the acute metabolic effects of whey protein.

In this study, participants followed standardised, dietitian-advised mixed meals. This helped minimize dietary variability and isolate the effect of the whey protein pre-load. Patients adhering to other dietary patterns, such as very low-carbohydrate or fat-rich diets, were not specifically evaluated. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect responses under standardised mixed-meal conditions, and extrapolation to other dietary patterns should be made with caution.

Evidence from regional studies, randomised trials, and meta-analyses supports the metabolic effects of whey protein supplementation. In an Indian crossover study among healthy adults, Sambashivaiah et al. reported a significantly greater insulinogenic response with higher-dose whey protein compared with soy protein (insulin iAUC, P < 0.01), despite no significant differences in postprandial glucose.[6] A systematic review and meta-analysis by Chiang et al. demonstrated that pre-meal whey protein supplementation reduced postprandial glucose by 2.67 mmol/L at 60 min and 1.59 mmol/L at 120 min, alongside a significant increase in insulin exposure (insulin iAUC + 24.66 nmol/L × min) and favourable trends toward higher total GLP-1 responses in T2DM patients.[7] Complementing these findings, a broader mechanistic systematic review by Nouri et al., encompassing 58 human trials, showed reductions in postprandial glucose iAUC accompanied by increases in insulin and incretin hormone secretion across both healthy and dysglycaemic populations.[8] Extending beyond acute postprandial effects, a meta-analysis by Amirani et al. in metabolic syndrome and related conditions reported modest yet significant longer-term improvements, including reductions in HbA1c (−0.15%), fasting insulin (−0.94 μIU/mL), and HOMA-IR (−0.20).[9] Collectively, these data align with our findings that a pre-breakfast whey protein pre-load attenuates postprandial glucose excursions predominantly through enhancement of insulin and incretin responses, supporting its physiological relevance in patients with early T2DM studied under standardised mixed-meal conditions.

Our results are in accordance with previous studies that demonstrated long-term benefits. Jakubowicz et al. demonstrated that adding whey protein to a high-energy breakfast for 12 weeks resulted in a 0.5% reduction in HbA1c and a ~4 kg reduction in body weight in patients with T2DM.[5] The study observation is in line with previous studies demonstrating ~20-30% reductions in postprandial glucose and ~100%–140% increases in insulin and incretin responses after whey supplementation.[4510111213] These findings indicate that whey protein elicits not only acute postprandial effects but may also play a role in sustained glycaemic and metabolic improvements with regular use.

Limitations

The calculated sample size was small (n = 10); the duration of the study was quite short and comprised only patients with early diabetes on metformin monotherapy. Given the pilot nature of this study and the small sample size, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in larger, long-term studies. As the study was restricted by inclusion criteria to patients with early type 2 diabetes mellitus on metformin monotherapy, the findings should not be extrapolated to individuals with advanced insulin resistance or special populations such as elderly patients, those with chronic kidney disease, or post-menopausal women, in whom metabolic responses may differ. Assessment of gastric emptying and glucagon levels would direct understanding to mechanistic insights.

CONCLUSION

Pre-breakfast whey protein supplementation significantly reduces postprandial glucose excursions and augmented insulin, C-peptide, and GLP-1 responses in patients with T2DM. Whey protein may serve as a practical nutritional adjunct to improve postprandial glycaemic control, including a beneficial second-meal effect.
