Culture and Leadership Shape the Impact of Private Equity in Emergency Medicine
Authors: Sara M Gotheridge, Shannon Werb
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with interest the article by Dilip et al1 on the implications of private equity (PE) in emergency medicine. The authors are right to raise concerns about profit motives, clinical autonomy, and skewed incentives. However, when the conversation stops at policy and legal reform and advocacy, the solutions feel necessary but not sufficient.

The real question is not whether there are guardrails. It is whether organizations can honor them under pressure. For example, Corporate Practice of Medicine (CPOM) was designed to prevent lay control over clinical decision making, yet enforcement has historically been inconsistent.2 Even when formal protections are in place, they do not automatically translate into practice. Policies define who should have authority, but they do not guarantee that clinicians can exercise it when operational or financial pressures mount.

What enables the ethical conduct of physicians is culture. A large body of evidence shows that organizational culture is directly tied to patient outcomes.3 More importantly, culture is what determines whether clinicians feel empowered to escalate concerns, deviate from volume or throughput pressures, override operational directives, and engage in daily decisions that align with the best interests of patients.

Well-designed systems make these decisions easier, not harder. High-performing organizations are physician-led, and authority is not just assigned; it is operationalized. Decision rights are aligned with clinical expertise, financial incentives align with clinical quality and outcomes, and clinicians are supported, both structurally and culturally, to act on their judgment.4In these environments, CPOM is not just a policy, but a lived reality, while culture supplies the “control environment” that makes guardrails real in day-to-day practice. Together, they shift the central PE risk from “profit versus care” toward a more practical question: whether incentives, oversight, and accountability are designed so that performance improvement and clinical integrity advance in parallel.

To leverage potential advantages of PE backing and minimize potential disadvantages, leadership is critical. Culture reflects what leaders model, reinforce, and protect.5 When leaders consistently prioritize clinical judgment over operational expediency, they create an environment where clinician autonomy is credible, not symbolic.

This distinction matters in the context of PE, but it extends far beyond it. Ethical risk is not unique to ownership structure. In fact, it is inherent across modern health care systems. The question is not whether risk exists, but whether organizations are designed to manage it.

A narrow focus on policy risks missing the larger point. The real safeguard is alignment: enforceable policy, clinician-led governance, and a leadership and larger culture that expects and supports independent clinical judgment. Without that alignment, policies erode. With it, even complex operational models can maintain trust, safety, and defensible decision making.
