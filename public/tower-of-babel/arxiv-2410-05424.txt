Whole-disk sampling of molecular clouds in M83 Facilities: ALMA
Akihiko Hirota, Jin Koda, Fumi Egusa, Tsuyoshi Sawada, Kazushi Sakamoto, Mark Heyer, Amanda M Lee, Fumiya Maeda, Samuel Boissier, Daniela Calzetti, Bruce G. Elmegreen, Nanase Harada, Luis C. Ho, Masato I. N. Kobayashi, Nario Kuno, Barry F. Madore, Sergio Martín, Jennifer Donovan Meyer, Kazuyuki Muraoka, Yoshimasa Watanabe

Abstract
We present a catalog of clouds identified from the 12 CO (1–0) data of M83, which was observed using Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) with a spatial resolution of ∼ \sim 46 pc and a mass sensitivity of ∼ \sim 10 4 M ⊙ {M}_{\odot} (3 σ \sigma ). The almost full-disk coverage and high sensitivity of the data allowed us to sample 5724 molecular clouds with a median mass of ∼ 1.9 \sim 1.9 × \times 10 5 10^{5} M ⊙ {M}_{\odot} , which is comparable to the most frequently sampled mass of Giant Molecular Clouds (GMCs) by surveys in the Milky Way. About 60 percent of the total CO luminosity in M83’s disk arises from clouds more massive than 10 6 M ⊙ {M}_{\odot} . Such massive clouds comprise 16 percent of the total clouds in number and tend to concentrate toward the arm, bar, and center, while smaller clouds are more prevalent in inter-arm regions. Most > 10 6 >10^{6} M ⊙ {M}_{\odot} clouds have peak brightness temperatures T peak T_{\mathrm{peak}} above 2 K with the current resolution. Comparing the observed cloud properties with the scaling relations determined by Solomon et al. 1987 (S87), T peak T_{\mathrm{peak}} > 2 >2 K clouds follow the relations, but T peak T_{\mathrm{peak}} < 2 <2 K clouds, which are dominant in number, deviate significantly. Without considering the effect of beam dilution, the deviations would suggest modestly high virial parameters (median α vir {\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}} ∼ 2.7 \sim 2.7 ) and low surface mass densities (median Σ \Sigma ∼ \sim 22 M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 {M}_{\odot}\ \mathrm{pc}^{-2} ) for the entire cloud samples, which are similar to values found for the Milky Way clouds by Rice et al. (2016) and Miville-Deschênes et al. (2017). However, once beam dilution is taken into account, the observed α vir {\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}} and Σ \Sigma for a majority of the clouds (mostly T peak T_{\mathrm{peak}} < 2 <2 K) can be potentially explained with intrinsic Σ \Sigma of ∼ \sim 100 M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 {M}_{\odot}\ \mathrm{pc}^{-2} and α vir {\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}} of ∼ \sim 1, which are similar to the clouds of S87.

II
Molecular Cloud Identification

II.1
Data

We used the
12
CO (J=1–0) mapping data of M83 observed with ALMA
[
43
, project code 2017.1.00079.S; ]
.
The observations used the main 12-m array and Atacama Compact Array (ACA), i.e., the 7-m short-baseline interferometric array and the Total-Power (TP) single-dish array, to recover the flux.
The interferometric and single-dish data were calibrated separately.
The calibration procedure is described in
Koda et al. [43]
.
The calibrated TP array data was converted into interferometric visibilities using the TP2VIS software
[
42
]
. Then, the 12-m, 7-m, and TP array visibilities were inverted onto the image plane and jointly deconvolved using the MIRIAD software package
[
68
]
.
We refer the readers to
[
43
]
for further details on the data processing.

Three data cubes were prepared in
[
43
]
with velocity resolutions of 1, 2, and 5
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
, respectively.
We here adopt the one with 1
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
resolution.
The restoring beam of the original data is a Gaussian with major and minor axes of 2
\arcsec
.09
\times
1
\arcsec
.68 in full-width half maximum (FWHM), which corresponds to 45.6 pc
\times
36.7 pc at the assumed distance of M83
[
76
, 4.5 Mpc;]
.
To avoid complexities associated with the non-circular beam, we smoothed the original data cube in spatial directions to achieve a circular beam with an FWHM size of 2
\arcsec
.1, corresponding to
\sim
46 pc.

Figure
1
(a) shows the two-dimensional map of the root-mean-square (rms) noise level.
We adopted the following procedure to obtain a noise-free estimate of the noise distribution. First, we used MIRIAD’s
mossen
command to generate a two-dimensional noise distribution expected from the integration time and system noise temperature assigned to visibilities. Next, the ’expected’ noise map produced by
mossen
was compared with the ’measured’ noise map, which we made by measuring rms noise along the velocity axis at each position in the data cube.
The comparison confirmed that both maps are consistent regarding the relative spatial variation within each map but are discrepant in the absolute scaling by a few tens of percent. Therefore, as the final step, we scaled the ’expected’ map so that the median noise level matches that of the ’measured’ noise map. The median noise level across the observed field is 6.5 mJy per beam.

II.2
Cloud identification

We here describe our procedures to identify molecular clouds in the
12
CO (1–0) data cube.

As a preparatory step, we constructed a signal mask to isolate voxels with significant detection.
The mask was created by first identifying a set of regions that consist of voxels with SNR
\geq
4,
then discarding small regions whose spatial extents are less than 0.6 times the beam area or whose emissions are confined in a single
1\,\rm km\,s^{-1}
channel along the velocity axis, and finally expanding the remaining regions to include the morphologically connected surrounding voxels with SNR
\geq
2.

The masked data cube was decomposed into a set of independent closed surfaces using the
astrodendro
2
2

2

http://www.dendrograms.org
software package.
Although
astrodendro
identifies hierarchically nested sets of structures, we adopted only the structures at the finest spatial scales, commonly referred to as
leaves
.
This is because the utilized spatial resolution,
\sim
46 pc at FWHM, is only a factor of two smaller than the typical height of the thin molecular gas disk
[
31
, e.g., about 100 pc in FWHM for the MW disk, see ]
.
As long as the disk height is similar between M83 and Milky Way and the size of the largest cloud is limited by the disk height, examining structures larger than individual leaves is unnecessary.
Parameters for the cloud decomposition algorithm are configured such that only voxels with SNR
>2
are included in the analysis, and
leaf
candidates with SNR contrasts between their peak and edge-level
<2
are discarded.
In addition, to avoid picking up noise fluctuations as clouds,
we discarded small
leaves
with the number of voxels less than
A_{\mathrm{beam}}/A_{\mathrm{voxel}}
, where
A_{\mathrm{beam}}
and
A_{\mathrm{voxel}}
are the area of the beam and a voxel.

The identified
leaves
generally trace the small regions around the peaks of molecular clouds, especially in crowded areas, because by nature of the algorithm, the leaves are defined such that they do not overlap each other.
The
astrodendro
decomposition left about 80% of the CO emission unassigned to any
leaves
.
The unassigned voxels to any seed structures are further segmented with the watershed algorithm, using the
scikit-image
software package
[
78
]
.

5724 leaves, which are expanded by the watershed segmentation, are accepted as molecular clouds
About 91 percent of the total CO luminosity within the mapped area was sampled as molecular clouds, as we will see later in §
II.6
.

II.3
Derivation of cloud properties

The physical properties of the identified clouds are calculated in a way similar to the one adopted in
[
34
]
.
A brief description is provided here.

II.3.1
Measured properties

Each cloud’s centroid position and velocity are measured as the intensity-weighted first moments.
We record the maximum brightness temperature within the cloud as peak temperature
T_{\mathrm{peak}}
.
The size parameters in two dimensions and velocity dispersion of each cloud are calculated as second moments and then extrapolated to the 0 K level with the procedure of
[
65
]
.
Hereafter, we denote the extrapolated moment values as
\sigma_{\mathrm{x,r}}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{v,r}}
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{x,r}}

>

\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}
.
The CO luminosity for each cloud (
{L}_{\mathrm{CO}}
) is derived as a summation in each cloud boundary and extrapolated to 0 K level.
We estimated the uncertainty in
\sigma_{\mathrm{x,r}}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}
, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{v,r}}
by performing a bootstrap error estimation with 1000 trials.

II.3.2
Derived properties

We derived the physical parameters of the clouds from the directly measured parameters presented above.

Effective radius:

Effective radius of the cloud (
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
) is derived as

R_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{3.4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}}

(1)

where
3.4/\sqrt{\pi}
(
\sim 1.91
) is an empirical factor that is determined by S87, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}
are the deconvolved cloud sizes defined below.
We note that although this definition of
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
is long used as common custom, some of the recent studies also define cloud radius as half width half maximum,
R_{\mathrm{HWHM}}
= 1.18
\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}}

[
66
]
, and thus care is needed when comparing values.

The deconvolved sizes are obtained by subtracting the beam size in quadrature from the extrapolated cloud sizes of
\sigma_{\mathrm{x,r}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{x,r}}^{2}-\theta_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}/\left(8\log{2}\right)}

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}^{2}-\theta_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}/\left(8\log{2}\right)}

(3)

where
\theta_{\mathrm{b}}
is the FWHM resolution of the data (2
\arcsec
.1 ,
\sim
46 pc).
Out of the 5724 clouds identified, 225 clouds have
\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}
smaller than
\theta_{\mathrm{b}}/\sqrt{8\log{2}}
.
We marked them as “deconvolution-failed clouds” and assigned
\sigma_{\mathrm{x,r}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{y,r}}
as the upper limits on
\sigma_{\mathrm{x}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}
, respectively.

Velocity dispersion:

The velocity dispersion of the cloud (
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
) is calculated by subtracting the instrumental velocity resolution from the extrapolated
\sigma_{\mathrm{v,r}}
as

\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}=\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{v,r}}^{2}-\left(\Delta{V}_{\mathrm{ch}}/\sqrt{12}\right)^{2}},

(4)

where
\Delta{V}_{\mathrm{ch}}
is the channel width of the data (1
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
).
The factor of
1/\sqrt{12}
is the ratio of the 2nd moment to the width for a boxcar profile.

Molecular gas mass:

Molecular gas mass for each GMC (
{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}
) is calculated from
{L}_{\mathrm{CO}}
by applying a CO-to-H
2
conversion factor of 2.0
\times
10
20
cm
-2

(K km s
-1
)
-1
in combination with a correction factor of 1.36 that accounts for the contribution of Helium and other elements.
[
7
]
:

\left(\frac{{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}}{{M}_{\odot}}\right)=\left(\frac{{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}}{4.4{M}_{\odot}/\left(\mathrm{K}\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}\mathrm{pc}^{2}\right)}\right)\left(\frac{{L}_{\mathrm{CO}}}{\mathrm{K}\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}\ \mathrm{pc}^{2}}\right),

(5)

where
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
is the mass-to-luminosity ratio.
The
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
of 4.4
{M}_{\odot}

\left(\mathrm{K}\ \mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}\mathrm{pc}^{2}\right)
is often regarded as the ‘standard’ value for GMCs in metal-rich environments, such as in M83’s inner disk
[
8
]
, and thus the use of the ‘standard’ conversion factor is justified as the first order approximation
[
7
]
.
We note that, however,
Lee et al. [49]
suggested a kpc-scale radial variation in
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
, which is a factor of a few increase from the galactocentric radius of
\sim 2
kpc to
\sim
6 kpc.

Lee et al. [49]
noted that the radial gradient of metallicity in the disk of M83 is insufficient to explain the observed variation in
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
as it can only produce a factor of
\sim 1.2
to
\sim 1.3
variations in
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
over the corresponding radial range by adopting the metallicity measurement of
Bresolin et al. [8]
and the metallicity-dependent descriptions of
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
of
Wilson [81]
and
Arimoto et al. [2]
.
They suggested that the weak dependence of
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
on
{L}_{\mathrm{CO}}
, expected for gravitationally bound clouds with constant surface densities
[
71
,
7
]
, combined with the radial variation in the cloud mass function can provide a relative variation in
{\alpha_{\mathrm{CO}}}
comparable to the observed variation.

Surface density:
Average gas surface density of a cloud (
\Sigma
) is calculated as,

\Sigma={M}_{\mathrm{cl}}/(\pi R_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}).

(6)

Virial mass:
Following
[
5
]
virial mass is calculated as

{M}_{\mathrm{vir}}=\frac{1}{a_{1}}\frac{5R\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}}{G},

(7)

where
G
is the gravitational constant and
a_{1}
is a geometrical factor that is (1 -
k
/3) / (1 - 2
k
/5) for a power law density distribution of
\rho(r)

\propto

r^{-k}
. We assumed
k
to be 1, following S87.
Examinations of the nearby Galactic clouds suggest slightly larger values of
k

[
39
,
50
, e.g., 1.3–1.4,]
.
As the dependence of the geometrical factor
a_{1}
on k is small, we adopt
k
=1 to keep consistency with previous studies.

Virial parameter:
The virial parameter
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
is a ratio between the virial mass and gas mass and is a measure of the ratio of the kinetic to gravitational energy
[
5
]
:

{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}\equiv{M}_{\mathrm{vir}}/{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}.

(8)

Ignoring the support from external pressure and magnetic fields, a state of simple virial equilibrium is achieved when
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
is 1 or a factor of few around 1, considering the uncertainty in the assumed density profile and CO-to-H
2
conversion factor.
Clouds are loosely bound when the equipartition between the kinetic and gravitational energy is achieved with
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}

\sim
2
[
47
,
3
]
.
We note a general caveat on interpreting
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
. As isolated clouds with simple geometry and density distribution are assumed, the observationally derived
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
has a limitation in characterizing the dynamical status of molecular clouds. Numerical simulations suggest that clouds could be either more bound
[
4
,
58
]
or more unbound
[
52
]
than what the face value of
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
would suggest.

II.4
Extrapolation factors and measurement uncertainties

The extrapolation to 0 K emission level made to basic cloud parameters §
II.3.1
is needed to correct for the underestimation of the parameters that would arise if the parameters are determined at a higher edge level
[
65
]
.
However, the extrapolation result becomes less reliable for a cloud that requires a higher correction factor. Thus, we expect clouds with higher correction factors to have higher measurement uncertainties assigned by the bootstrap estimate.
We show the amount of correction and measurement uncertainties to the clouds here.

Figure

(a-c) shows the correction factor of the extrapolation, which is the ratio between the extrapolated to un-extrapolated values for CO luminosity, radius, and the velocity dispersion of the clouds, plotted as functions of the cloud mass.
The correction factors tend to increase toward lower cloud masses.
At a mass of 2
\times
10
5

{M}_{\odot}
, the median of the correction factors are about 1.25, 1.16, and 1.12 for luminosity, radius, and velocity dispersion, respectively, and are modest.
At the lowest mass bin of 2
\times
10
4

{M}_{\odot}
, the median factors are 1.96, 1.43, and 1.41, respectively.

Figure

(d-f) shows the measurement errors in CO luminosity, radius, and velocity dispersion expressed in the fractional form.
Uncertainties in these basic three quantities are transferred to other advanced quantities following uncertainty propagation.

The extrapolation process recovers part of the emission removed by applying the signal mask.

A radially declining trend of

It decreases radially down to
\sim

We estimate the detectable minimum cloud mass that is based on the observation and segmentation parameters.
The cloud identification process in (§

\sigma

The mass distribution of GMCs is characterized by a mass function of a power-law form, i.e.,
{dN}/{dM}\propto M^{\gamma}
.
The early GMC surveys in the inner Galactic disk consistently found the index
\gamma
of around
-1.5

[
67
,
71
,
80
,
63
]
.
Subsequent studies in other environments also found
\gamma
mainly in the range of -1.5 to -2.5
[
29
,
12
]
, but
even steeper relations
(more negative) values of
\gamma
are also found in some environments
[
22
,
27
, e.g.,]
.
For a power-law distribution,
\gamma>-2
means that a small number of massive clouds carry most of the total cloud mass, like the GMC mass distribution in the Galactic disk.
In contrast,
\gamma<-2
means that most cloud mass resides in the smaller mass end.

At the higher end of the mass distribution, the deficit of massive clouds is also often seen: in the Galactic disk, clouds more massive than a few 10
6

{M}_{\odot}
are frequently under-populated with respect to the power-law relation
[
80
]
.
Some previous studies reported
\gamma
steeper (smaller) than -2, but this might be a consequence of fitting cloud mass distributions above such an upper limit mass
[
63
]
.

IV.1
Functional forms

We fit the cloud mass distribution in two forms.
The first form is a power-law, restricted by the upper mass limit
{M_{\mathrm{lim}}}
as,

\frac{dN}{dM}\propto\left(\frac{{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}}{{M_{\mathrm{lim}}}}\right)^{{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}},\ \ {M}_{\mathrm{cl}}<{M_{\mathrm{lim}}}.

(13)

The form is conventionally applied to the cloud mass distributions

[
80
,
63
,
12
, e.g.,]
.
It can be written in the integral form as follows

N(>M)={N_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}\left[\left(\frac{{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}}{{M_{\mathrm{lim}}}}\right)^{{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}+1}-1\right],

(14)

where
{N_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
is the number of clouds more massive than
2^{1/({\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}+1)}{M_{\mathrm{lim}}}
, which we denote as
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
.
We call
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
the cutoff mass for the restricted power-law form because the deviation from a power-law becomes obvious above
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
.

The second form combines the power-law distribution with an exponential cutoff, which is often called a Schechter-like function:

\frac{dN}{dM}\propto\left(\frac{M}{{M_{\mathrm{u,exp}}}}\right)^{{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}}\exp{\left(-\frac{M}{{M_{\mathrm{u,exp}}}}\right)},

(15)

where
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
is the index of a power-law distribution, and
{M_{\mathrm{u,exp}}}
is the cutoff mass limit above which the distribution is suppressed with respect to the power-law.
Fitting the cloud mass distribution with this Schechter-like form would help test the connection between the mass spectra of GMCs and stellar clusters as the cluster mass distributions are often characterized with this form
[
44
,
1
, e.g.,]
.
However, the application of this form to molecular cloud distributions is limited in recent studies
[
26
,
66
]
.

IV.3
Comparison of the fitted results between the two forms

Because of the discrepancies between
{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}
and
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
, one would wonder which of the two forms, namely the restricted power-law form or Schechter-like form, provides a better characterization of the cloud mass distributions.
The
p
-values of the K-S test in table
4
are inconclusive.
By taking the significance level of
p
-value as 0.05, the K-S test accepts almost all the cases except the restricted power-law form applied to all samples.
The values of
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
are shallow in most radial ranges with a slight variation (-1.2 to -1.4).
The shallow and limited variation in
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
might be due to the limited dynamic range in fitting – there is only about an order of magnitude variation between
{M_{\mathrm{u,exp}}}
and the adopted completeness limit of 2
\times
10
5

{M}_{\odot}
.
The exponential term induces a decline even below
{M_{\mathrm{u,exp}}}
to some extent, further limiting the range available to fit as a power-law.
The determination of
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
might become reliable only if higher resolution data becomes available to overcome the crowding effect to lower the mass completeness limit
[
66
, see also]
.
As the K-S test gives no clear preference and as we suspect the fitted values
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
might be biased, we consider that
{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}
to be preferable to
{\gamma_{\mathrm{exp}}}
, at least with the available data.

IV.4
Possible origins of the radial variation of mass spectra

The power-law fitting shows a radially steepening of
{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}
from -1.3 to -2.0 and a decrease of
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
, indicating a relative deficit of massive clouds toward the outer disk.
The trends agree with other studies in M83 with shallower sensitivities
[
26
,
34
]
and also in different environments
[
12
, e.g., ]
.
Several mechanisms might explain the radial trends in
{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}
and
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
.
First, given that M83 has many bubble structures in infrared, HI, and CO, which likely influence and drive the evolution of molecular clouds.

Inutsuka et al. [37]
and
Kobayashi et al. [40]
provide semi-analytical calculations to describe the time evolution of molecular cloud mass functions regulated by multiple episodes of bubble expansions, which drive molecular cloud formation via shock compression of atomic gas. The idea would explain the steeper
{\gamma_{\mathrm{PL}}}
in the outer disk as the slower cloud formation because the supernovae, which drive bubble expansions, are less frequent there.
Kobayashi et al. [40]
also suggested the formation of massive clouds comparable to
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
requires about 100 Myr by solving the time evolution, which agrees with the time scale predicted from the CO morphology of M83
[
43
]
.
Second, the under-population of massive clouds above
{M_{\mathrm{u,PL}}}
might also be due to a lower molecular fraction in massive clouds –

Elmegreen [21]
provides that self-shielding decreases approximately as
\propto

M^{-0.5}
for virialized clouds, and therefore, CO line observations might trace only the core regions in most massive clouds.
As there is a radial decrease of per-cloud molecular fraction in the MW
[
20
]
, it would be intriguing to compare molecular clouds with massive HI clouds in M83.

V
Cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion

Vertical cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion of a gaseous disk is a parameter that reflects the stability of the disk. Despite its importance, the measurements of the cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion of molecular gas are still limited.
In the MW,
Stark [72]
found that the cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion within the Galactic plane weakly depends on cloud mass, with 6.6
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
and 9.0
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
for cloud masses of 10
4-5.5

{M}_{\odot}
and 10
2-4

{M}_{\odot}
, respectively. However, the estimation from the MW data involves an assumption of the isotropy of velocity dispersion.

Extragalactic observations of nearly face-on galaxies permit estimation of the vertical cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion.
Previous measurements were performed at sub-kpc scale resolutions, which blend several GMCs within resolution elements; they found dispersions of 6 to 9
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
when an individual spectrum of each pointing is analyzed
[
14
,
82
,
54
]
and about 12 to 14
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
when the spectra at multiple pointings are stacked
[
9
,
10
]
.
With the new cloud catalog and low inclination angle of M83, we can make a more direct estimation of the vertical cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion.

V.1
Method to estimate vertical velocity dispersion of clouds

We adopt a method similar to the one used by
[
32
]
to estimate the vertical velocity dispersion of stellar clusters.
The line-of-sight velocity
{v_{\mathrm{LOS}}}
of a cloud rotating in the disk
is written as

{v_{\mathrm{LOS}}}={v_{\mathrm{sys}}}+({v_{\mathrm{rot}}}+{v_{\mathrm{phi}}})\cos{\phi}\sin{i}+{v_{\mathrm{R}}}\sin{\phi}\sin{i}+{v_{\mathrm{z}}}\cos{i},

(16)

where
{v_{\mathrm{sys}}}
,
{v_{\mathrm{rot}}}
,
{v_{\mathrm{phi}}}
,
{v_{\mathrm{R}}}
,
{v_{\mathrm{z}}}
,
\phi
, and
i
are the systemic recession velocity, the circular rotational velocity expected from the galactic rotation curve, in-plane azimuthal and radial non-circular velocities that are mostly governed by streaming motions, vertical motion velocity, the angle from the major axis, and the inclination angle.
We subtract the contributions of assumed
{v_{\mathrm{sys}}}
and
{v_{\mathrm{rot}}}
from
{v_{\mathrm{LOS}}}
for each cloud.
Then, the residual velocity
{v_{\mathrm{res}}}
is left with the contributions from
{v_{\mathrm{z}}}
and streaming motions as

{v_{\mathrm{res}}}={v_{\mathrm{z}}}\cos{i}+{v_{\mathrm{phi}}}\cos{\phi}\sin{i}+{v_{\mathrm{R}}}\sin{\phi}\sin{i}.

(17)

As long as the contributions from the in-plane non-circular motions, i.e., the last two terms in this equation, are negligible, we can estimate the cloud-to-cloud velocity dispersion,
\mathrm{<}{v_{\mathrm{z}}}\mathrm{>}
, as the dispersion of
{v_{\mathrm{res}}}/\cos{i}
among a sample of clouds.

We adopt
{v_{\mathrm{sys}}}
of 511
\mathrm{km\ s}^{-1}
and
i
of 24
\arcdeg

[
43
]
.
For
{v_{\mathrm{rot}}}
, we use the rotation curve of
[
33
]
, which is obtained by subtracting the rotational velocities estimated from the stellar mass distribution using the 2MASS Large Galaxy Atlas image
[
38
]
with an assumption of the maximum disk contribution.

VI.1
Impact of sampling clouds with a relatively large beam

We consider the relations between intrinsic and observed parameters of the clouds affected by the effects of a limited resolution.
When clouds are sampled with marginal resolution and SNR, measured cloud sizes could be overestimated even when the beam deconvolution (§
II.3.1
) is made
[
65
]
.
This over-estimation would arise as a consequence of the SNR thresholding imposed during the cloud identification process, as only positive value pixels are included in cloud masks, and thus, the presence of noise tends to bias the measured cloud sizes toward larger values.
For a cloud located in a crowded region, blending of emissions from the surrounding material also leads to the overestimation of cloud sizes.
The observed distribution of
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
appears to imply the sign of the over-estimation of radii, as over 80 percent of the clouds have
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
larger than 40 pc §
III.1.3
, which is approximately the resolution limit

3
3

3

As
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
relates to root-mean-square cloud size
\sigma_{r}
as
R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1.91\sigma_{r}
, the beam size
\theta_{\mathrm{b}}
of 46 pc corresponds to
\sim
37 pc (=1.91
\times

\theta_{\mathrm{b}}
/
\sqrt{8\log{2}}
)
.
The velocity dispersion
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
could be similarly affected by the resolution bias, but the observed values of
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
are well above the resolution limit §
III.1.3
, suggesting
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
is less affected.
Therefore, we consider that the resolution bias mostly affects
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
for the M83 catalog.
This is also supported by the bootstrap error estimation made in §
II.3.1
, which indicates significantly larger fractional uncertainties on
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
than
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
.

From the nature of the data considered here, we assume that the beam dilution affects the observed cloud radius
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
such that it overestimates the intrinsic radius
{R_{{0}}}
, but does not affect the velocity dispersion
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
.
We also assume that cloud mass
{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is unaffected.
When observing a cloud with a larger beam, the cloud emission is diluted, and thus, the observed brightness temperature is lowered, but the luminosity, and hence cloud mass
{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}

Under the assumed simplified configuration, the relation between the observed and intrinsic radii is

R_{\mathrm{eff}}={{f}}^{-1/2}{R_{{0}}},

(18)

where
{{f}}
is the area-filling factor, the intrinsic-to-observed area ratio.
By definition,
{{f}}
is
\leq
1.

The cloud surface mass density
\Sigma

R_{\mathrm{eff}}

The relation between the intrinsic
{\Sigma_{{0}}}
and the observed

\Sigma={{f}}{\Sigma_{{0}}}.

(19)

\propto

R_{\mathrm{eff}}\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}

7

As

\leq

is elevated as
{{f}}^{-1/2}

For example, suppose a bound cloud with

\sim 46

\sim

The observed

The observed virial parameter

\propto

(figure

In §

We assumed there that (1) the resolution effect overestimates

but does not affect the velocity dispersion

We test these assumptions with a simulation test, which injects model sources with known properties into a portion of the data cube and samples the injected sources via the same identification procedure utilized in §

The test is made with two setups.
A

Filling factor dependence seen in size-normalized velocity dispersion to surface density relation

The relation between the size-normalized velocity dispersion
C
(
\equiv

\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R}
) and surface mass density
\Sigma
is often used to gauge the dynamical state of clouds
[
30
,
3
]
.
As
{M}_{\mathrm{vir}}

\left(\propto R\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}\right)
=
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}

{M}_{\mathrm{cl}}
is mathematically equivalent to
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}/R

\propto

{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}\Sigma
, clouds with a constant
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
should follow a line on the
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R}-\Sigma
plane following equation (
10
).

We saw in §
III.2
that a majority of the M83 clouds are located upward of the line expected for
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
= 1 on the
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R}
–
\Sigma
plot.
The clouds with such upward deviations are often considered to be confined by external pressure
[
30
,
24
,
75
]
, but we also saw that the deviations are rather systematic; the fitted relation indicated that
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R}
varies as
\propto

\Sigma^{\sim 0.3}
(eq.
12

\Sigma^{1/2}
is expected for a constant value of
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir}}}
(eq.
10

The observed index of
\sim 0.3
for the

–
\Sigma
relation is explainable with the beam-dilution effect if a significant fraction of the clouds share a similar set of combinations of the intrinsic virial parameter and surface mass density.
Consider a cloud with intrinsic radius
{R_{{0}}}
, surface mass density
{\Sigma_{{0}}}
, virial parameter
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir,0}}}
, and velocity dispersion
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}
. As we assume the beam dilution does not affect the velocity dispersion,

does not have a subscription.
If the cloud follows Larson’s relations, the following applies to the cloud,

\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{{R_{{0}}}}=\left(\frac{2}{9}{{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir,0}}}}\pi G{\Sigma_{{0}}}\right)^{1/2}.

(B1)

The observed cloud radius
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
and surface mass density
\Sigma
are related to
{R_{{0}}}
and
{\Sigma_{{0}}}
with equation (
18
) and equation (
19

Substituting them into equation (
B1

\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}}=\left(\frac{2}{9}{{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir,0}}}}\pi G\right)^{1/2}\left({\Sigma_{{0}}}\Sigma\right)^{1/4}.

(B2)

The equation postulates that if
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir,0}}}
and
{\Sigma_{{0}}}
are constant, the scaling between the observed coefficient,
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}}
to be proportional to
\Sigma^{1/4}
, which is similar to the observed relation of
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{eff}}}

\propto

\Sigma^{\sim 0.3}
.

Figure

(a) shows the

–
\Sigma
relation for the M83 clouds. The red line on the plot is the expected behavior of equation (
B1
) in the case of
{\alpha_{\mathrm{vir,0}}}
of 1 and
{\Sigma_{{0}}}
of 160
{M}_{\odot}\ \mathrm{pc}^{-2}
for a range of filling factor between 0.1 and 1, showing an alignment with the running median of the observed
\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}/R_{\mathrm{eff}}
