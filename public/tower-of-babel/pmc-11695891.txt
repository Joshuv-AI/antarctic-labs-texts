Ultrasound in Shock: Is It the Elusive Magic Bullet?
Authors: Balaji Kannamani, Shrikanth Srinivasan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

We read with interest the systematic review by Dr Gupta et al. appraising the role of ultrasonography in undifferentiated shock in ICU patients.1 We congratulate the authors for their commendable work, especially for detailed data search and addressing the clinically relevant question in modern practice. There are a few keynotes that we want to highlight and comment on.

Ultrasonography (USG) has changed the practice of critical care and emergency medicine not only for early diagnosis of shock but for serial monitoring and guidance of resuscitation, especially in mixed shock.

Generally, USG has high sensitivity and specificity to diagnose the obstructive and cardiogenic shock, as the findings remain static until pathology is reversed. However, in distributive and mixed shock, the dynamicity in pathophysiology and resuscitation challenges the diagnosis.

In their meta-analysis, the authors included 4 studies (375 patients) for the systematic review, and have concluded that ultrasound has high diagnostic accuracy for detecting obstructive and cardiogenic shock in critically ill patients (AUC 0.99) but for other types of shock, the utility was limited as the accuracy was low summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves showed an AUC of 0.5 for hypovolemic and mixed shock, and 0.76 for distributive shock.

We would, however, like to point out that, out of 4 studies, only 2 studies assessed the role of USG in undifferentiated shock, out of those, the study by Geng P et al., was conducted predominantly in the Emergency Department (ED), which was one of the exclusion criteria for the review.2,3

In the other study conducted by Joseph M et al., we observed significant application bias as the assessment was conducted by trained echo cardiologists to rule out cardiac causes of shock and included primarily patients with evidence of low cardiac output and pulmonary congestion in their study.4 Hence, general application of the author's results would be limited by a low study population and a population bias.

Based on these observations, we can agree that ultrasound has a definite role in accurately diagnosing obstructive and cardiogenic shock, however, we cannot undermine the role of USG in diagnosing mixed and distributive shock. In ICU, assessment of shock by USG is limited by certain factors, which are as follows: initial management in the ED, under or over resuscitation, changes in pathophysiology, duration of the ED stay and delay in ICU admission of the patient.

Ultrasonography assessment of mixed shock needs a systemic approach and serial measurements. Rapid ultrasound in shock and hypotension assessment protocol (RUSH) excludes the other causes of shock to diagnose distributive shock. The majority of protocols to diagnose shock, such as RUSH, focused assessment with sonography in trauma (FAST), and focused assessment for free fluid (FAFF) protocols, are based on single point of care USG measurement in emergency situations.5

Comprehensive examination along with serial quantitative measurements such as cardiac index, volume assessment are required to diagnose shock in complex situations.6 For example, in a patient with septic shock the initial assessment of vasoplegia and hypovolemia, changes according to the resuscitation received in ED. Also, an association of septic cardiomyopathy can underestimate or hinder the finding of hypovolemia. There is no doubt that USG significantly decreases the delay in diagnosis of undifferentiated shock, and it needs further data to validate its role. From our perspective, serial USG examination and better understanding of the pathophysiology of the illness are mandatory for the diagnosis of mixed/undifferentiated shock.
