Real-World Clinical Outcomes of Polydioxanone Filament Insertion into the Vastus Medialis Obliquus for Knee Osteoarthritis: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Seungpyo Nam, Kaehong Lee, Seungcheol Yu, Jihwan Park, Jeongsoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMEST (Muscle Enhancement and Support Therapy) is a device for intramuscular placement of bidirectional barbed polydioxanone filaments. Placement of these filaments into the vastus medialis obliquus has been reported to reduce knee pain and improve quadriceps function in patients with knee osteoarthritis. However, clinical evidence remains limited and is based primarily on a small number of controlled trials in selected populations. This study evaluated the real-world clinical outcomes and safety of the MEST procedure across Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grades I–IV.Patients and MethodsIn this retrospective study, 62 patients with knee osteoarthritis who underwent the MEST procedure between March 2025 and February 2026 were analyzed. Pain intensity, functional outcomes, patient-reported overall improvement, effusion grade, and adverse events were assessed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks. The primary outcome was the change in numeric rating scale (NRS) pain score from baseline to 8 weeks. Factors associated with treatment response, defined as ≥2-point NRS reduction at 8 weeks, were also evaluated by logistic regression.ResultsNRS scores decreased from 5.8 ± 1.7 at baseline to 2.5 ± 2.4 at 8 weeks (Padj < 0.001), and 50 patients (80.6%) achieved a ≥2-point reduction. At 8 weeks, walking and stair climbing improved in 54 (87.1%) and 38 patients (61.3%), respectively, and 55 patients (88.7%) rated the procedure as effective or very effective. In multivariable analysis, moderate-to-severe baseline effusion was associated with a lower response likelihood (odds ratio, 0.22; 95% confidence interval, 0.05–0.94; P = 0.041). No serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionThe MEST procedure was associated with clinically meaningful pain reduction and favorable patient-reported outcomes across KL grades I–IV, with no serious adverse events. In an exploratory analysis, moderate-to-severe baseline effusion grade was associated with a lower likelihood of response, suggesting that baseline effusion warrants further investigation as a potential marker of treatment response.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most prevalent degenerative joint disorders worldwide and a major cause of pain, joint stiffness, functional impairment, and disability.1,2 Its global burden continues to rise with population aging and obesity, imposing substantial socioeconomic and healthcare costs. OA has a multifactorial pathogenesis involving mechanical stress, chronic inflammation, metabolic and immune dysregulation, and genetic susceptibility.2,3 Structural changes affect not only the articular cartilage and subchondral bone but also the synovium, ligaments, joint capsule, and surrounding musculature, consistent with OA as a whole-joint disease.4–6 Because no cure or disease-modifying treatment has been established, current management focuses on relieving pain and preserving mobility and quality of life. Evidence-based guidelines recommend non-pharmacologic measures as core treatment, particularly therapeutic exercise, weight management, and self-management support, with pharmacologic treatments and intra-articular injections added when required.7 Among available treatment options, exercise carries the strongest and most consistent recommendation across major international guidelines and is endorsed for most patients regardless of age, pain severity, or radiographic grade.8–12

Quadriceps weakness is frequently observed in knee OA, and quadriceps-strengthening programs have been associated with reductions in pain and improvements in physical function and quality of life.13–15 The quadriceps femoris plays an important role in shock absorption during walking and in dynamic stabilization of the knee joint, which partly explains why quadriceps-strengthening exercise has been emphasized in the management of knee OA. Recently, MEST (Muscle Enhancement and Support Therapy), a medical device designed for the intramuscular placement of biodegradable, bidirectional barbed polydioxanone (PDO) filaments, has been introduced for patients with knee OA.16,17 The procedure using the MEST device involves insertion of these filaments into the vastus medialis obliquus (VMO) with the aim of providing mechanical support to the weakened muscle. The VMO is the distal oblique component of the vastus medialis and contributes to medial patellar stabilization by exerting a medially directed force on the patella during knee extension.18 Weakness or atrophy of the vastus medialis has been reported in patients with knee OA and may contribute to quadriceps imbalance, altered patellofemoral mechanics, and uneven joint loading.16,19 To date, only two clinical studies have evaluated this procedure in patients with knee OA. In a single-center exploratory clinical study, Kim et al reported improvements in quadriceps contractile strength, weight-bearing pain, and Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) compared with sham treatment.16 Similarly, Kwon et al reported significant reductions in weight-bearing and walking pain at 4 and 8 weeks after the MEST procedure in a multicenter randomized controlled study.17

The potential mechanisms underlying the MEST procedure have been investigated mainly in preclinical studies. Experimental findings suggest that insertion of PDO filaments into weakened muscle may induce a controlled microinjury response, followed by muscle regeneration. This process has been associated with increases in muscle volume and contractile function, as well as changes in biomarkers related to muscle structure, regeneration, and energy metabolism.20–22 PDO filament insertion has also been shown to reduce peripheral sensitization in animal models, suggesting that its pain-relieving effect may involve modulation of peripheral pain signaling as well as mechanical and regenerative effects.23 However, these mechanisms have not been confirmed in humans. Moreover, the clinical effectiveness of the MEST procedure remains insufficiently established, as previous studies have been limited and have only included selected patients with Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade II–III knee OA. Therefore, further studies are needed to evaluate its effectiveness across a broader range of disease severity.

This study aimed to evaluate real-world clinical outcomes over 12 weeks following the MEST procedure in patients with knee OA across KL grades I–IV. We hypothesized that numeric rating scale (NRS) pain scores would decrease from baseline to 8 weeks and that patient-reported functional outcomes and overall treatment effectiveness would improve during follow-up. In addition, we explored clinical and demographic factors associated with treatment response, defined as a reduction of at least 2 points in the NRS pain score at 8 weeks.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted to evaluate the clinical outcomes and safety of the MEST procedure in patients with knee OA. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2604–093-1735) and performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.24 The requirement for informed consent from patients was waived because of the retrospective study design. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, and all data used for analysis were de-identified with no personally identifiable information reported. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.25

We reviewed all patients who underwent the MEST procedure at Seoul National University Hospital Pain Center between March 2025 and February 2026. Since all patients were scheduled to visit the center at 4-week intervals for 3 months after the MEST procedure, clinical data were extracted from medical records on the day of the procedure and at scheduled follow-up visits 4, 8, and 12 weeks after the procedure. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥19 years; (2) radiographically confirmed knee OA based on the KL grading system; (3) persistent knee pain with an NRS score ≥3 for at least 3 months despite conventional treatments, including oral analgesics, physical therapy, or previous injection therapies. Among patients with KL grade IV knee OA, only those who declined total knee arthroplasty and opted for conservative management were included.

Patients were excluded from the analysis if they met any of the following criteria: (1) previous ipsilateral total knee arthroplasty, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, or other ipsilateral knee surgery resulting in substantial structural alteration; (2) a history of genicular nerve radiofrequency ablation, including conventional or cooled radiofrequency ablation, in the ipsilateral knee;26 (3) receipt of intra-articular corticosteroid or hyaluronic acid injection or other ipsilateral knee-directed interventions, including genicular nerve block, during the follow-up period; (4) receipt of lumbar epidural steroid injection during the follow-up period, which could confound the assessment of knee pain; (5) any change in concurrent analgesic medications, including initiation, discontinuation, or dose adjustment, during the follow-up period; or (6) failure to complete scheduled follow-up assessments at 4, 8, and 12 weeks, including NRS or functional outcome assessments.

MEST Procedure

MEST (MEST-B2375, OV MEDI, Seongnam, Republic of Korea) is a device designed for the intramuscular insertion of biodegradable PDO filaments. It consists of a 23-gauge, 75-mm insertion needle with a handle; an 18-gauge, 35-mm protective outer catheter with a handle; and a bidirectional barbed PDO filament with an outer diameter of 0.3 mm and cogs spaced at 1-mm intervals.20 Each filament is preloaded onto the needle in a folded configuration and enclosed within the protective outer catheter. The device is supplied sterile and intended for single use.

In the MEST procedure, six filaments were inserted into the VMO, with approximately 6.5 cm of filament deployed at each insertion site. The procedure was performed according to a previously described standardized protocol,27 and all procedures were performed in a single session by a single pain medicine specialist (J Kim) with more than five years of clinical experience in pain medicine. The procedure was performed with the patient in the supine position and the ipsilateral knee flexed approximately 20–30° using a small pad placed beneath the popliteal fossa. After skin preparation with an antiseptic solution, standard aseptic draping was applied. Following palpation of the patella, two reference points were marked: one 5 cm proximal to the superior border of the patella and the other 5 cm from the superomedial corner of the patella in a 40–50° proximomedial direction. Along the line connecting these two points, potential entry sites were marked at 0.5–1-cm intervals. A high-frequency linear-array transducer (LA2-14A, 2–14 MHz; Samsung RS80 EVO, Samsung Medison, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was then used to identify the center of the VMO along this line, and six marked points centered on this location were selected as the entry sites (Figure 1A).

Under ultrasound guidance, each planned entry site was infiltrated with 0.5% lidocaine (approximately 20 mL in total) via a 25-gauge spinal needle (Figure 1B). The transducer was aligned parallel to the VMO muscle fibers to obtain a long-axis view, and the MEST needle was advanced in-plane under real-time ultrasonographic guidance, allowing continuous visualization of the needle throughout its course.28 Color Doppler ultrasonography was used to identify and avoid intramuscular vessels along the planned needle trajectory. The needle was advanced to a depth of 3–5 cm, adjusted to the thickness of the VMO in each patient, to accurately engage the VMO muscle belly (Figure 1C). Once the needle tip reached the intended depth, sequential withdrawal of the needle and outer catheter allowed the bidirectional PDO barbs to anchor within the adjacent muscle tissue, securing the filament within the VMO. Any portion of the filament protruding beyond the skin surface was trimmed flush with the entry site (Figure 1D).

Post-procedural ultrasonography immediately confirmed intramuscular filament position, checked the integrity of nearby vessels, and ruled out hematoma at the entry points or within the muscle belly. Antibiotic ointment, a sterile dressing, and an elastic compression bandage were then applied to each entry point to limit oozing. During a subsequent 20-minute observation period, the entry points were checked for oozing or swelling and the treated limb for motor weakness before the patient was discharged. Acetaminophen was prescribed as rescue medication, up to a maximum of 2 g per day for 5 days after the procedure, to manage procedure-related discomfort after the local anesthetic effect had worn off. After discharge, patients were instructed to keep the entry sites dry and to visit a primary care physician every other day for one week to assess the procedure sites and monitor for signs of infection. Walking and light household activities were permitted, whereas exercise was restricted for one to two weeks and then gradually resumed. No structured rehabilitation or exercise program was prescribed.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical variables were collected by retrospective review of electronic medical records. Baseline demographic data included age, sex, height, body weight, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and psychiatric disorders), alcohol consumption, smoking status, laterality of knee OA (right, left, or bilateral), and history of knee surgery (total knee arthroplasty, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, and high tibial osteotomy). Pain duration was recorded in months. The types of prescribed analgesic medications were also reviewed, including strong opioids (oral morphine, oxycodone, tapentadol, hydromorphone, and transdermal fentanyl), weak opioids (oral tramadol and codeine, and transdermal buprenorphine), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acetaminophen, adjuvant analgesics including anticonvulsants (pregabalin and gabapentin) and antidepressants (duloxetine, milnacipran, and tricyclic antidepressants), and symptomatic slow-acting drugs for osteoarthritis.

Radiographic severity was graded according to the KL classification, as determined by the formal radiology report. Clinical outcomes included pain intensity, knee joint effusion, patient-reported functional outcomes, and overall treatment effectiveness, assessed at baseline and at scheduled follow-up visits 4, 8, and 12 weeks after the procedure. At baseline, pain intensity and joint effusion grade were recorded. At each follow-up visit, pain intensity, joint effusion grade, functional outcomes during walking and stair climbing, and the patient-reported overall assessment of treatment effectiveness were documented.

Pain intensity was assessed using an 11-point NRS ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable). At baseline, patients were asked to rate the recent average pain intensity of the knee scheduled for the MEST procedure. At each follow-up visit, they were asked to rate the average pain intensity of the treated knee since the previous visit. Suprapatellar recess effusion was graded by ultrasonography using a 4-point ordinal scale (0 = absent, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe).29 At each visit, two pain medicine specialists independently performed real-time ultrasonographic examinations of the knee and assigned an effusion grade. When the two grades differed, the assessors discussed the findings, re-examined the knee together, and recorded a consensus grade, which was used in the analysis. Self-reported changes from baseline in walking on level ground and stair climbing were each assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = much worse, 2 = slightly worse, 3 = unchanged, 4 = slightly improved, and 5 = much improved). Overall treatment effectiveness was assessed using a 5-point PGIC scale, on which patients rated the change in their condition relative to baseline rather than to the preceding visit (1 = much aggravated, 2 = aggravated, 3 = no change, 4 = effective, and 5 = very effective).

Safety was assessed by reviewing medical records from each follow-up visit for documented procedure-related complications, including ecchymosis, hematoma, local inflammation, and any other adverse events.

Statistical Analysis

Given that this study was a single-arm retrospective observational analysis, a formal sample size calculation was not conducted. All eligible patients within the study period were included. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages (%).

The primary outcome was the change in NRS pain score from baseline to 8 weeks. Normality of NRS pain scores at each time point was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Changes in NRS pain scores across the four time points (baseline and 4, 8, and 12 weeks) were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated. When the overall time effect was significant, post hoc comparisons between baseline and each follow-up time point, including the prespecified primary comparison between baseline and 8 weeks, were performed using paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction.

Treatment response was evaluated using two thresholds of absolute reduction in NRS pain score from baseline: a reduction of ≥2 points, corresponding to a previously established clinically important change in chronic pain and chronic musculoskeletal pain populations, and a reduction of ≥3 points, representing a greater degree of pain relief.30,31 The proportions of responders were calculated at each follow-up visit. Functional outcomes for walking and stair climbing, as well as overall treatment effectiveness by PGIC, were assessed relative to baseline, with both mean scores and the proportions of patients reporting favorable outcomes presented at each follow-up visit.

Factors associated with treatment response at 8 weeks, defined as a ≥2-point reduction in NRS pain score from baseline, were examined using logistic regression analysis. Univariable logistic regression was first performed for prespecified candidate variables, including age, sex, BMI, baseline NRS pain score, pain duration, KL grade, and baseline effusion grade. For regression analysis, KL grade was dichotomized as grade IV versus grade I–III to examine whether treatment response differed in patients with advanced radiographic OA. Baseline effusion grade was dichotomized as grade 2–3 versus grade 0–1 to distinguish moderate-to-severe effusion from absent or mild effusion, which was considered more likely to reflect clinically meaningful synovial involvement. This cutoff was also consistent with a previous study that used grade 2 to dichotomize effusion severity.32 Variables showing a significant association in univariable analyses (P < 0.05) were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Sensitivity analyses were performed using broader adjusted models that additionally included demographic and baseline clinical covariates, including sex, BMI, and baseline NRS pain score. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Between March 2025 and February 2026, 78 patients who underwent the MEST procedure for knee OA were screened for eligibility. Sixteen were excluded: six for previous total knee arthroplasty, five for incomplete follow-up data, and five for receipt of an additional intervention during the follow-up period, comprising lumbar epidural steroid injection in three patients and intra-articular knee injection and/or genicular nerve block in two (Figure 2). The remaining 62 patients were included in the final analysis. Of these, 55 patients (88.7%) had bilateral knee OA; however, no patient underwent the MEST procedure on both knees during the study period.

Demographic and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 71.4 ± 10.6 years, and 40 patients (64.5%) were female. Regarding radiographic severity, 20 patients (32.3%) had KL grade III disease, and 23 patients (37.1%) had KL grade IV disease. The baseline NRS pain score was 5.8 ± 1.7, the mean pain duration was 65.6 ± 63.1 months, and moderate-to-severe baseline effusion, defined as grade 2–3, was present in 26 patients (41.9%).

Clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 2. NRS pain scores decreased significantly over time after the MEST procedure (RM-ANOVA with Greenhouse–Geisser correction, F(1.95, 118.82) = 101.11, P < 0.001). The mean NRS pain score decreased from 5.8 ± 1.7 at baseline to 2.5 ± 2.4 at 8 weeks. Compared with baseline, NRS pain scores were significantly reduced at 4, 8, and 12 weeks following the procedure, with all comparisons remaining significant after Bonferroni correction (all Padj < 0.001; Figure 3A). A ≥2-point reduction in NRS pain score was achieved by 50 patients (80.6%), 50 patients (80.6%), and 48 patients (77.4%) at 4, 8, and 12 weeks, respectively. A ≥3-point reduction was achieved by 37 patients (59.7%), 41 patients (66.1%), and 39 patients (62.9%) at the same time points, respectively (Figure 3B). Responder rates at 8 weeks were also examined according to radiographic severity and baseline effusion grade. The responder rates for KL grades I, II, III, and IV were 77.8%, 80.0%, 90.0%, and 73.9%, respectively. The corresponding responder rates for baseline effusion grades 0, 1, 2, and 3 were 88.2%, 94.7%, 63.2%, and 71.4%, respectively (Supplementary Table S1).

Patient-reported functional outcomes also improved during follow-up. Improvement in walking on level ground was reported by 52 patients (83.9%), 54 patients (87.1%), and 54 patients (87.1%) at 4, 8, and 12 weeks, respectively (Figure 3C). Improvement in stair climbing was reported by 35 patients (56.5%), 38 patients (61.3%), and 40 patients (64.5%) at the same time points (Figure 3D). On the PGIC, 55 patients (88.7%) rated the treatment as effective or very effective at 4 weeks, and this proportion remained unchanged through 12 weeks. Effusion grade distributions remained generally stable across follow-up visits, with no clear trend toward progressive worsening.

Factors associated with treatment response at 8 weeks, defined as a ≥2-point reduction in NRS pain score from baseline, were evaluated using logistic regression analysis (Table 3). In univariable analysis, age (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.86–0.998; P = 0.044) and moderate-to-severe baseline knee joint effusion (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.72; P = 0.016) were significantly associated with treatment response. In the multivariable model, moderate-to-severe baseline knee joint effusion remained associated with a lower likelihood of treatment response (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–0.94; P = 0.041), whereas age was no longer statistically significant (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.86–1.02; P = 0.113). The findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses using broader adjusted models that additionally included sex, BMI, or baseline NRS pain score (Supplementary Table S2). Multicollinearity was not observed among covariates, with all variance inflation factors close to 1.

No serious adverse events, including infection, neurovascular injury, or procedure-related hospitalization, were recorded during the follow-up period. Mild ecchymosis was observed in 5 patients (8.1%) and filament extrusion in 3 patients (4.8%) at the 4-week follow-up. In one patient, filament extrusion persisted at 8 weeks and was managed by trimming the extruded filament at an outpatient visit, which resolved without further complication.

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated the MEST procedure in a real-world cohort spanning the full spectrum of radiographic knee OA severity, from KL grade I to IV. Improvements in pain and patient-reported function were observed in the majority of patients over 12 weeks. In addition to describing overall clinical outcomes, exploratory analyses examined factors associated with treatment response and identified baseline effusion as a potentially relevant clinical characteristic. These findings extend the evidence for the MEST procedure to a broader clinical population than that examined in previous studies and suggest that patient characteristics may influence treatment response.

The clinical outcomes observed in the present study were broadly consistent with those of the two previous clinical studies. In an exploratory sham-controlled study, Kim et al reported that PDO filament insertion into the vastus medialis reduced weight-bearing pain, with improvement sustained through 30 weeks, and increased isometric quadriceps contractile strength, reaching statistical significance at 4 weeks.16 In a subsequent multicenter randomized sham-controlled trial, Kwon et al reported that the MEST procedure reduced weight-bearing pain from 54.4 mm at baseline to 16.9 mm at 8 weeks, compared with 35.8 mm in the sham group, with concomitant improvement in quadriceps strength and global improvement reported by 95.5% of treated patients.17 In the present study, NRS pain scores decreased from 5.8 ± 1.7 at baseline to 2.5 ± 2.4 at 8 weeks, 80.6% of patients achieved a ≥2-point reduction, and 88.7% rated the treatment as effective or very effective. Although direct numerical comparisons are limited by differences in outcome measures, study design, and patient selection, the direction of improvement in pain and function was consistent across studies.

An important distinction, however, is that previous studies were limited to patients with KL grade II–III disease, whereas the present cohort included the full spectrum of radiographic severity. At 8 weeks, responder rates exceeded 70% across all KL grades, and KL grade IV was not significantly associated with a lower likelihood of treatment response in regression analysis. These findings suggest that favorable symptomatic outcomes may also be observed in patients with advanced radiographic OA, although this interpretation should be viewed cautiously given the small KL-specific subgroups and limited statistical power. This observation may be clinically relevant because patients with KL grade IV OA who remain symptomatic despite conservative management are often considered for arthroplasty.11 In this context, the MEST procedure may warrant further investigation as a nonsurgical adjunct or interim option for patients who wish to defer surgery or are not yet ready to undergo arthroplasty. However, given the uncontrolled study design, these findings should be considered preliminary and require prospective evaluation of MEST in patients with advanced radiographic OA.

Several mechanisms may plausibly contribute to the symptomatic improvement observed after the MEST procedure. One proposed mechanism is mechanical reinforcement of the weakened quadriceps. Because the quadriceps contributes to shock absorption and dynamic stabilization of the knee, quadriceps weakness may impair joint stability and increase mechanical loading during weight-bearing activities.33–35 By anchoring bidirectional barbed PDO filaments within the VMO along the orientation of the muscle fibers, the procedure may partially compensate for this weakness, improve dynamic stabilization, and reduce mechanical stress on the knee joint.16,17 This hypothesis is indirectly supported by previous clinical studies showing improvements in isometric quadriceps strength after VMO-targeted filament insertion.16,17 However, these studies could not determine whether the observed strength gains resulted from direct mechanical support, improved voluntary muscle activation following pain relief, or other effects of the procedure. Beyond this proposed mechanical effect, preclinical studies suggest that PDO filaments may induce biological changes within the quadriceps and modulate peripheral nociceptive signaling. In monoiodoacetate-induced OA rats, intramuscular PDO filament insertion produced a controlled local muscle injury followed by macrophage and satellite-cell activation, with changes in regenerative signaling involving PAX7, MyoD, interleukin-6, and insulin-like growth factor-1, and subsequent recovery of quadriceps muscle mass and contractile function.20,21 A subsequent study using the same rat model demonstrated increases in quadriceps volume, hindlimb torque, and markers of muscle structure and energy metabolism, including α-actin, myosin, and COX4; importantly, these measures of muscle recovery were associated with improvements in OA-related pain behaviors.22 In a separate preclinical study, PDO filament insertion attenuated L3/L4 dorsal root ganglion hyperresponsiveness and reduced overexpression of TRPA1 and NaV1.7 in parallel with reduced mechanically evoked pain, suggesting that attenuation of peripheral sensitization may represent an additional analgesic pathway.23 However, these biological and neurophysiological mechanisms have not been confirmed in humans, and their relative contributions to clinical improvement remain uncertain.

An important observation in this study was that moderate-to-severe baseline effusion was associated with a lower likelihood of treatment response. This association was also reflected in treatment response rates of 91.7% in patients with grade 0–1 effusion and 65.4% in those with grade 2–3 effusion. One possible explanation is that, in patients with greater effusion, a larger proportion of knee pain may arise from intra-articular pathology rather than periarticular muscle dysfunction. Effusion-synovitis and synovitis have been associated with knee pain in knee OA.36,37 Thus, even if a muscle-targeted intervention improves the periarticular component of symptoms, pain arising from intra-articular structures may persist and limit the overall clinical response. This interpretation is consistent with the minimal change in ultrasonographic effusion grades during follow-up despite substantial pain reduction, suggesting that symptomatic improvement after the MEST procedure may occur without a corresponding reduction in intra-articular effusion. A second possible explanation is that greater joint effusion may attenuate the effects of the MEST procedure through arthrogenic muscle inhibition (AMI). AMI is characterized by impaired voluntary quadriceps activation resulting from altered articular afferent input and has been described in patients with knee OA.38 Joint effusion is a recognized trigger of this inhibitory response. In an experimental study of otherwise healthy knees, increasing intra-articular effusion progressively inhibited the quadriceps motoneuron pool, with the vastus medialis exhibiting reflex inhibition at the lowest effusion volume among the quadriceps muscles examined.39 Impaired VMO activation has also been documented in patients with AMI and has been associated with functional deficits.40 Greater effusion may therefore create a neuromuscular environment in which VMO activation is more strongly inhibited, potentially attenuating the functional or symptomatic benefits of the MEST procedure. However, these interpretations remain hypothetical because synovitis, inflammatory biomarkers, and quadriceps activation were not directly assessed. Moreover, the association with effusion was based on only 12 non-response events, resulting in considerable uncertainty in the regression estimates. Prospective studies are therefore needed to confirm the relationship between baseline effusion and treatment response.

Functional improvement differed according to the activity assessed. At 8 weeks, improvement was more frequently reported for walking than for stair climbing (87.1% vs 61.3%). Stair climbing requires greater quadriceps force and imposes higher loads on the knee joint than level walking,41 which may partly explain the lower proportion of patients reporting improvement in stair-related function. The effects of the MEST procedure may therefore be more readily expressed in lower-demand activities such as walking than in more demanding tasks such as stair climbing, particularly during the earlier phase after treatment. Notably, the proportion of patients reporting improvement in stair climbing appeared to increase over time. This pattern may be consistent with a delayed contribution from muscle remodeling or regenerative responses after PDO filament insertion, beyond any immediate mechanical support. However, this interpretation remains speculative because muscle strength, muscle morphology, and regenerative changes were not directly assessed. In addition, both functional outcomes were self-reported on ordinal scales, and stair-climbing performance may be influenced by factors beyond knee extensor function, including balance and hip and ankle function, none of which were evaluated in this study.

The observed reductions in pain and improvements in function raise the question of how the MEST procedure might be positioned within contemporary knee OA management. Therapeutic exercise remains a cornerstone of treatment and is consistently recommended across major international guidelines.8–12 However, pain and quadriceps weakness may limit patients’ ability to initiate or sustain strengthening exercise, particularly in older patients with persistent symptoms.42 In this context, the MEST procedure could potentially serve as an adjunct to rehabilitation by reducing symptom burden and supporting muscle function, thereby facilitating participation in exercise-based therapy. A potential interim role may also warrant consideration in patients with advanced radiographic OA who wish to continue nonsurgical management or defer arthroplasty. Future prospective studies should define the clinical positioning of this procedure by comparing it with established nonsurgical treatments, including prolotherapy, platelet-rich plasma, and hyaluronic acid, and by evaluating whether its combination with structured exercise or other conservative therapies provides additional benefit.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-arm retrospective design precludes causal inference and does not allow the observed improvements to be distinguished from natural symptom fluctuation, regression to the mean, placebo responses, or unmeasured confounding. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as observational rather than as evidence of efficacy. Second, exclusion of patients who received additional interventions during follow-up may have introduced selection bias if these interventions reflected inadequate response to the MEST procedure. However, classifying all five such patients as non-responders reduced the 8-week response rate only from 80.6% to 74.6%, suggesting that the impact of this bias may have been limited. Third, the 12-week follow-up period was insufficient to assess long-term durability, particularly because PDO filaments are reported to be absorbed over approximately four to six months.17 Fourth, reliance on routinely collected clinical data limited the standardization of outcome assessment. The NRS recall period was not predefined, transient adverse events may have been undercaptured, and objective quadriceps strength testing and validated knee-specific instruments, such as the Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index, were unavailable. Finally, the single-center, single-operator design and limited sample size constrain generalizability and the robustness of the predictor analysis. The regression findings should therefore be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study, the MEST procedure was associated with clinically meaningful improvement in pain and patient-reported function across KL grades I–IV, with no serious adverse events. Given the single-arm retrospective design, the extent to which these improvements were attributable to the MEST procedure remains uncertain. Nevertheless, the procedure may have potential as an adjunctive modality within multimodal knee OA management and warrants prospective evaluation. Although exploratory, the observed association between baseline effusion and treatment response also merits further investigation to clarify its clinical relevance and potential role in treatment selection. Ultrasound-guided MEST procedure for the VMO. (A) Surface marking and entry-point selection. Two reference points are marked, one 5 cm proximal to the superior border of the patella and another 5 cm from the superomedial corner of the patella in a 40–50° proximomedial direction; along the connecting line, candidate entry sites are marked at 0.5–1 cm intervals, and six points centered on the sonographically identified center of the VMO are selected as entry points (indicated by red arrows). (B) Administration of local anesthesia through a 25-gauge spinal needle under ultrasound guidance at each planned insertion site. (C) Advancement of the MEST needle (indicated by white arrows) using an in-plane technique under real-time ultrasonographic guidance to confirm needle position, avoid vascular structures, and maximize engagement of the muscle belly.
