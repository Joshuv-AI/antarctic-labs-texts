Practical Improvements of A/B Testing with Off-Policy Estimation
Otmane Sakhi, Alexandre Gilotte, David Rohde

Abstract
We address the problem of A/B testing, a widely used protocol for evaluating the potential improvement achieved by a new decision system compared to a baseline. This protocol segments the population into two subgroups, each exposed to a version of the system and estimates the improvement as the difference between the measured effects. In this work, we demonstrate that the commonly used difference-in-means estimator, while unbiased, can be improved. We introduce a family of unbiased off-policy estimators that achieves lower variance than the standard approach. Among this family, we identify the estimator with the lowest variance. The resulting estimator is simple, and offers substantial variance reduction when the two tested systems exhibit similarities. Our theoretical analysis and experimental results validate the effectiveness and practicality of the proposed method.

Introduction

Online interactive systems are pervasive in society, with key use cases including computational advertising
(
bottou2013counterfactual
)
, search
(
search
)
and recommendation
(
sakhi2020blob
)
to name just a few. Their remarkable success relies on our ability to rapidly iterate, testing new system updates and ensuring that each new iteration delivers measurable improvements
(
m1
;
m2
)
. Central to this process are estimation protocols designed to efficiently evaluate the expected benefits of proposed updates, enabling informed decision-making and driving continuous system improvement.

A/B testing
kohavi2012trustworthy
is considered the
gold standard
estimation protocol for quantifying the improvement brought by proposed change due to its ease of use, weak assumptions, and safety when best practices are followed
kohavi2013online
. The protocol segments the user population into two subgroups: one exposed to the current system and the other to a new, potentially improved version. The improvement is estimated as the difference between the estimated values of the two systems in each sub-population. Under reasonable assumptions, the resulting difference-in-means estimator is unbiased, and its variance primarily depends on the variance of the targeted signal. This variance plays a crucial role in decision-making. In particular, for a fixed sample size, increasing the sensitivity of these experiments directly depends on reducing estimation variance. Various approaches leveraging regression adjustments with auxiliary data have been explored to achieve this reduction
cuped
;
in_experiment
.

Independent of variance reduction, a large body of research has emerged in recent years to facilitate the evaluation of system updates by framing the problem within the policy evaluation and comparison framework
bottou2013counterfactual
. In particular, Off-Policy Estimation (OPE)
sutton98reinforcement
has garnered significant interest, as it aims to evaluate new decision systems using only historical interactions, thereby reducing the need for A/B testing. These approaches center around the introduction and study of new off-policy estimators, often relying on importance weighting techniques
horvitz1952generalization
;
bottou2013counterfactual
;
dudik14doubly
.

When evaluating system updates that are similar to the current system, these methods can provide unbiased and accurate estimates of the improvement brought by the new system
horvitz1952generalization
;
bottou2013counterfactual
. However, their accuracy deteriorates as the difference between the compared systems increases
sakhi2024logarithmicsmoothingpessimisticoffpolicy
. This presents a significant challenge for off-policy estimation, as the resulting estimators become highly biased
sachdeva2020off
and suffer from high variance
swaminathan2015batch
. Recent research has attempted to mitigate these issues
saito2022off
;
pol_conv
by introducing additional, often strong assumptions, which in turn limit the applicability of these methods in real-world scenarios.

Recognizing the limitations of importance weighting, we leverage its strengths to improve the A/B testing protocol. In this work, we first demonstrate that, surprisingly, importance weighting can yield more accurate estimates than the standard A/B test estimator when the tested policies are similar. This advantage arises because the classical difference-in-means A/B test estimator does not incorporate information about the tested policies. Motivated by this insight, we investigate improved A/B test estimators, that exploit similarities between tested policies, leading to the following contributions.

Contributions.
We propose an intuitive construction of A/B testing estimators, leveraging importance weighting. Our construction leads to a family of
unbiased
estimators, that always improves on the difference-in-means variance once the compared policies share some similarities. These estimators have the property to default to the difference-in-means estimator when the policies are completely different. Our family of estimators does not need any additional assumption, applies to realistic scenarios and can replace the standard approach as long as we have access to the two systems propensities. We proceed at studying this family of estimators and look for the estimator that minimizes the variance. The obtained estimator is simple to use and demonstrates substantial variance reduction when there is an overlap between the tested policies. We validate our results on practical settings and demonstrate its viability and ease of use in real world scenarios.

Related Work.

The earliest work on off-policy estimation applied Horvitz-Thompson Estimator
horvitz1952generalization
in order to allow estimates of the average reward of new decision rules without actually deploying the decision rule on new traffic
(
beygelzimer2011contextual
;
bottou2013counterfactual
;
swaminathan2015counterfactual
;
saito2021counterfactual
)
. Although only applicable in highly simplified settings, these methods were compelling because they represented a first attempt to measure actual business value without an explicit deployment of the new system.

Typically, these methods work on contextual bandit problems of small action spaces and large treatment effects
joachims2018deep
. Even in these highly idealized settings, these estimators experience potentially severe variance problems, that might be mitigated by controlling the variance of the weights
swaminathan2015self
;
aouali23a
;
sakhi2024logarithmicsmoothingpessimisticoffpolicy
. Despite substantial efforts, these methods remain difficult to apply in more realistic settings
gilotte2018offline
and cannot replace the gold standard A/B Testing protocol. Our work acknowledges the fundamental limitations of off-policy estimation
sakhi2024logarithmicsmoothingpessimisticoffpolicy
and instead leverage its strength to improve the A/B test protocol itself, enabling it to exploit similarities between the tested policies.

With a similar motivation of exploiting off-policy estimation for A/B Testing,
agarwal2016multiworld
use IPS to leverage data from past policies, and
pmlr-v162-wan22b
develop safe and efficient data collection strategies that alter the A/B testing protocol. These methods improve the approach, but rely on a strong contextual bandit assumption and change the experimental design. In contrast, we propose novel, improved off-policy estimators for
standard
A/B testing that work beyond the contextual bandit assumption.

Recent work in online decision systems has tackled
Markovian interference

(
farias_neurips
)
, where the
Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption
(SUTVA), crucial for unbiased difference-in-means estimation, fails due to dependencies between treated users. These approaches model interference via Markov Decision Processes
mdp
and design new experiments and estimators to handle it
(
farias_neurips
;
farias_dq
;
shi2021time
;
chen2024experimenting
)
. In contrast, our setting assumes independent users (SUTVA holds) but captures user-level, long term effects induced by the policy. We improve A/B testing estimators in this regime by exploiting similarities between policies, without modifying the experimental design.

Our work aligns more with the literature on variance reduction in classical A/B testing
cuped
;
in_experiment
;
in_out
. Known approaches regress the target signal on auxiliary data, achieving variance reduction when the resulting model correlates with the target. However, these methods treat the tested systems as black boxes and do not exploit their similarity. Our work can be combined with these methods, yielding estimators akin to doubly robust estimation
dudik14doubly
, with improved variance reduction.

Setting

We model the online decision problem, and specifically
the dynamics of each user
with an extension of the Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework
mdp
. Let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
state
space which is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be our
action set
. This action set can be either finite or continuous. The agent, representing our decision system is guided by a
stochastic
and
stationary
policy
\pi\in\Pi
within a policy space
\Pi
. Given a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
\pi(\cdot|s)
is a probability distribution over the action set
\mathcal{A}
;
\pi(a|s)
is the probability that the agent selects action
a
in state
s
. The decision system interacts with users
independently
. For a user, the interaction begins with an initial state
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
coming from an unknown distribution
d_{0}(\cdot)
. At each time step
t
, the agent observes the user’s current state
s_{t}
, selects an action
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
, and receives a stochastic reward
r_{t}\in[0,1]
, sampled from an unknown distribution
p(\cdot|s^{u}_{t},a^{u}_{t})
of expectation
r(a_{t},s_{t})
. Then, the interaction at time
t
transitions the user to the next state
s_{t+1}
following an unknown transition kernel
\mathcal{K}(\cdot|[s_{0},...,s_{t}],a_{t})

1

1

This framework accounts for non-Markovian, long term dependencies between states and outcomes.
. These rounds of interactions continue till we reach a terminal state
\bar{s}\in\mathcal{S}
at time
T
, defining a complete user trajectory
\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t})\}_{t\in[T]}
, guided by the agent’s policy
\pi
. We assume that actions taken by the decision system
\pi
influence only the next state of the same user,
s_{t+1}
, not affecting other users. For notational convenience, we denote by
\nu(\pi)
the trajectory distribution induced by
\pi
in the extended MDP.

The performance of a policy
\pi
is determined by its
value
defined as the expectation of the cumulative reward computed over trajectories coming from the distribution
\nu(\pi)
induced by
\pi
. The
value
writes:

\displaystyle V(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\nu(\pi)}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}r(a_{t},s_{t})\right].

(1)

A/B Testing.
We are interested in comparing two policies
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
, and measure wether the target policy
\pi_{A}
is
significantly
better than our base policy
\pi_{B}
. We define the true improvement brought by
\pi_{A}
, over
\pi_{B}
as the difference between their two values, such as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})=V(\pi_{A})-V(\pi_{B}).

(2)

The objective of an A/B test is to build an estimate of this improvement and leverage it to
statistically
test wether or not, the target policy
\pi_{A}
is
significantly
better than
\pi_{B}
. We build our improvement estimate using collected,
independent
trajectories of interactions
\mathcal{D}_{A}
(respectively
\mathcal{D}_{B}
) of the policy
\pi_{A}
(respectively
\pi_{B}
) of the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{A}=\{\{s^{i}_{t},a^{i}_{t},r^{i}_{t}\}_{t\in[T_{i}]}\}_{i\in u_{A}}\,,\quad\mathcal{D}_{B}=\{\{s^{j}_{t},a^{j}_{t},r^{j}_{t}\}_{t\in[T_{j}]}\}_{j\in u_{B}}\,.

(3)

Where
u_{A}
and
u_{B}
are disjoint subsets of users allocated to
\pi_{A}
or
\pi_{B}
respectively.
n_{A}
is the number of users in
u_{A}
and
n_{B}
is the number of users in
u_{B}
. The classical improvement estimator is defined as the difference of the empirical estimates of the values. Particularly, we first leverage the collected data
\mathcal{D}_{A}
by policy
\pi_{A}
(respectively
\mathcal{D}_{B}
by policy
\pi_{B}
), to compute their estimated values:

\displaystyle\hat{V}(\pi_{A})=\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}r^{i}_{t}\,,\quad\hat{V}(\pi_{B})=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}r^{j}_{t}\,.

These estimates are naturally used to define the improvement estimator as their respective difference:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}=\hat{V}(\pi_{A})-\hat{V}(\pi_{B})\,.

(4)

The difference-in-means estimator is
unbiased
and its variance (or its empirical counterpart) will determine the statistical significance of the improvement. Specifically, we consider the following test:

\displaystyle H_{0}:\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})\leq 0\quad\text{ v.s. }\quad H_{1}:\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})>0.

(5)

We denote by
\sigma^{2}(\hat{\mathcal{I}})
the variance of the improvement estimator and suppose that
n_{A}=n_{B}=n
. Using the Central Limit Theorem and Slutsky Lemma, we can be confident of the improvement with an (asymptotic)
\alpha
level test
(
alpha_test
)
when:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}(\hat{\mathcal{I}})^{-1}\sqrt{n}\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}\geq z_{1-\alpha}\,,

where
z_{1-\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
-th quintile of the standard normal distribution. For any
\Delta>0
, the (asymptotic) power of our test under the local alternative
H_{1,n}:\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})=\Delta/\sqrt{n}
is consequently
1-\Phi(z_{1-\alpha}-\sigma(\hat{\mathcal{I}})\Delta)
, where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of a normal Gaussian. This means that under the same budget, and since all terms are constant, the power of our test is fully determined by the variance of the improvement estimator. This also means that our A/B test procedure will be more efficient if we can find
unbiased
estimators of the improvement with smaller variance. This will be explored next.

3
Motivating Example

We study the variance of the difference-in-means estimator defined in Equation (
4
). To simplify our argument, let us suppose that
n_{A}=n_{B}=n
for the rest of the analysis. By exploiting the independence assumption, one can write down the estimator’s variance as:

\displaystyle n\sigma^{2}(\mathcal{\hat{I}})=\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\right]+\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{B})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\right],

with
\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}
(resp.
\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{B})}
) the variance of the cumulative reward under trajectories generated by
\pi_{A}
(resp.
\pi_{B}
). The variance of this estimator will be equal to
0
if and only if the reward signal is
constant

and
the transition kernel is
deterministic
. This case never happens in practice as it means that all policies are equivalent for the problem in hand. Therefore, the classical difference estimator will always suffer non negligible variance, primarily driven by the variance of the cumulative reward.

A/A Test and Overlapping Policies.
Imagine that we compare identical policies (
\pi_{A}=\pi_{B}
). The true improvement
\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})
is equal to
0
in this case, the classical difference-in-means estimator
\hat{\mathcal{I}}
is centered around
0
and suffers a variance of:

\displaystyle n\sigma^{2}(\mathcal{\hat{I}})=2\text{ var}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}\right].

The variance of the estimator is still of the same order of magnitude as before even if
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
are identical. This is a sub-optimal behavior that can be improved. Importance weighting approaches
horvitz1952generalization
;
bottou2013counterfactual
were demonstrated to produce low variance estimators when the tested policies overlap and can be leveraged in these scenarios. We begin by defining the following quantities as they will be of interest in the rest of this work.
Let
t\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and let
\tau_{t}
be a trajectory composed of states and performed actions up to time step
t
:
\tau_{t}=\{s_{0},a_{0},\dots,s_{t},a_{t}\}
. We define the propensity of a trajectory
\tau_{t}
under a policy
\pi
by the following:

\displaystyle\pi(\tau_{t})=\prod_{l=1}^{t}\pi(a_{l}|s_{l}).

(6)

We define
w_{A}(\tau_{t})
, the importance weight of a trajectory
\tau_{t}
collected under
\pi_{A}
(
\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})>0
), and evaluated with
\pi_{B}
and respectively, we define
w_{B}(\tau_{t})
, the importance weight of a trajectory
\tau^{t}
collected under
\pi_{B}
(
\pi_{B}(\tau^{t})>0
), and evaluated with
\pi_{A}
:

\displaystyle w_{B}(\tau_{t})=\frac{\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})}{\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,,\quad w_{B}(\tau_{t})=\frac{\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})}{\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\,.

(7)

Finally, when
\tau_{t}
has a non null propensities under both policies, we have:
w_{B}(\tau_{t})w_{A}(\tau_{t})=1
. With these definitions, let us write down an Inverse Propensity Scoring estimator (IPS)
horvitz1952generalization
of the improvement using the collected interactions
\mathcal{D}_{B}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\textsc{IPS},\mathcal{D}_{B}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t})-1)r^{j}_{t}\,.

(8)

If
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
have a common support, it is straightforward to prove that the IPS estimator is unbiased
(
mcbook
)
.

We observe that we can achieve substantial variance reduction compared to the difference estimator of Equation (
4
), when
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
are similar, translating to
\forall\tau,\,w_{A}(\tau)\approx 1
. Precisely, in the special case of comparing identical policies, the obtained estimator as well as its variance reduce exactly to
0
. This proves that the sub-optimality previously identified of the difference estimator can be corrected, and highlights the advantage of using importance weighting in an A/B testing scenario. However, this estimator, as advantageous as it can be, will suffer from substantial bias and variance in practical settings.

Bias.
The common support assumption is restrictive and does not hold in practice
sachdeva2020off
. The two systems may deliver similar actions on some states, and in other states differ. Our goal is to develop
unbiased
estimators that leverage this inherent structure without relying on additional assumptions.

Variance.
Intuitively, the variance of this estimator is small in trajectories where the two policies are similar, but the global variance is dominated by trajectories where the policies differ
sakhi2024logarithmicsmoothingpessimisticoffpolicy
. This makes the proposed IPS estimator less appealing than the difference-in-means in practical scenarios. Our goal is to develop an estimator that exploits the similarity structure to reduce variance, while matching the variance of the difference estimator at worst.

4
Off-Policy Augmented A/B Testing

4.1
Construction of our family of estimators

Direct application of importance weighting produces estimators with substantial bias and variance in practice
sachdeva2020off
. To address the variance problem, numerous importance weight transforms were developed that enable effective bias-variance trade-offs, defining the large family of regularized IPS estimators
bottou2013counterfactual
;
su2020doubly
;
metelli2021subgaussian
;
aouali23a
;
sakhi2024logarithmicsmoothingpessimisticoffpolicy
. While these transforms successfully reduce variance, they introduce additional bias that must be accounted for
gilotte2018offline
;
sakhi2023pac
. For instance, when using data
\mathcal{D}_{B}
collected under policy
\pi_{B}
to construct the regularized IPS estimator of the improvement, the bias suffered can be expressed as an expectation under trajectories from policy
\pi_{A}
. Fortunately, in A/B testing, we have access to
\mathcal{D}_{A}
, trajectories collected under
\pi_{A}
, which allows us to estimate and correct this bias.

Formally, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the importance weights transform characterizing our novel off-policy estimator. We choose our function to be bounded, respecting
\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}f(x)\in\mathbb{R}.
We also adopt the convention
f(1/0^{+})=f(+\infty)
. With this function
f
, we introduce our estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}f(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t}))r^{j}_{t}}_{\text{$f$-Regularized IPS}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau^{i}_{t}))\right]\right)r^{i}_{t}}_{\text{Bias Correction}}\,,

Our estimator
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}
follows the previously outlined construction. Its first term corresponds to the
f
-regularized IPS estimator of the improvement using the interactions
\mathcal{D}_{B}
, while its second term corresponds to the bias correction computed using
\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

Remark.
One might consider applying the same approach in reverse: using
\mathcal{D}_{A}
to compute an
f^{\prime}
-regularized IPS estimator and correcting its bias using
\mathcal{D}_{B}
. Furthermore, we could even think of defining a convex combination of the two estimators with
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Appendix
C.1
proves that this approach is equivalent to using a function
g
with our estimator, demonstrating that our current parametrization is already efficient in leveraging both sets of interactions.

Unbiased estimator.
Our estimator properties are determined by the choice of

Proposition 4.1

(Unbiased family of estimators)

Let

\displaystyle f(0)=-1\,.

(

This means that better choices of

Lower variance.
Let us first write down the variance of our general estimator. For any

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{B}}\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{B})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\right]+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)r_{t}\right].

The first desired behavior it for the estimator to suffer no variance when the tested policies are identical. In this case, all importance weights are equal to

and a null variance is obtained when:

\displaystyle f(1)=0\,.

(
C_{2}

Condition (

Definition 4.2

(Variance Surrogate)
.

Let

be a bounded function, We define

\displaystyle S_{f}=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\pi_{B})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)^{2}r_{t}^{2}\right].

4.3
Surrogate-Optimal Improvement Estimator

After demonstrating that a large panel of choices can lead to improved estimators, we focus on identifying the function

4.4
Cases of marginal improvement

After studying our family of estimators, we want to understand the edge cases where it cannot improve on the classical difference estimator. We develop two specific scenarios representing worst case settings for our family of estimators.

Perfectly distinct policies.
Recall that the motivation of our family of estimators is to exploit the similarities between the two tested policies, achieving a variance reduction on states for which the two policies will have the same behavior. In the special case where the two policies never play the same actions, our newly introduced family of estimators will default to the difference estimator. Precisely, if policies
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
have a completely distinct support, meaning that for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
\pi_{A}(a|s)\pi_{B}(a|s)=0
, then the importance weight
w_{B}(\tau)
of
\pi_{B}
computed for any trajectory
\tau
played with
\pi_{A}
will be
w_{B}(\tau)=0
and similarly, the importance weight
w_{A}(\tau)
of
\pi_{A}
computed for any trajectory
\tau
played with
\pi_{B}
will be
w_{A}(\tau)=0
. This means that for all terms will be evaluated at
0
, and as
f(0)=-1
, our estimator will default to the difference estimator giving
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}=\hat{\mathcal{I}}\,.
This behavior is expected no matter the function
f
used.

Long horizon rewards.
In some applications, the reward signal can only be measured after multiple interactions. One can think of a sales signal that happens after multiple recommendations. Formally, let us suppose that the observed reward can only be positive after
T_{0}\gg 1
interactions. In this scenario, even if the two tested policies are only slightly different, their propensities of playing the same trajectory will drift apart, becoming less and less similar as the trajectory grows. The following example details this phenomenon. Let
0<\epsilon\leq 1
, suppose that our action space is binary
\mathcal{A}=\{a^{-},a^{+}\}
, and take the simple example of testing the two following policies: for all states
s
,
\pi_{A}(a^{+}|s)=0
and
\pi_{B}(a^{+}|s)=\epsilon.
These policies can be made very close by varying
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.

To simplify the example even further, let us suppose that the trajectory stops when we get a positive reward, and let that time always be
T_{0}
. The first term of our estimator is an average over
\pi_{B}
. The probability of the event
\{w_{A}(\tau_{T_{0}})>0\}
is equal to
(1-\epsilon)^{T_{0}}
, the probability of
\pi_{B}
playing
T_{0}
consecutive
a^{+}
. This probability decays exponentially to
0
, meaning that for
T_{0}\gg 1
, the first term of our estimator will consist of importance weights of
w_{A}(\tau_{T_{0}})=0
. Similarly, the second term of the estimator is an average over trajectories generated by
\pi_{A}
, which is deterministic in our case and results in importance weights of
w_{B}(\tau_{T_{0}})=(1-\epsilon)^{T_{0}}
. For large

, this converges to
0
, leading to an estimator evaluated on null importance weights. As
f(0)=-1
, our family of estimators defaults back to the difference-in-means estimator when dealing with long-horizon rewards, and leads to no variance reduction. However, this limitation is linked to the importance weights used, and can be addressed through advanced importance weighting techniques that exploit the sequential structure of the problem

6
Conclusion

Under SUTVA, classical A/B testing remains the gold standard for comparing decision systems in most applications. We showed that its core estimation strategy can be improved by introducing a family of novel importance weighting estimators that significantly reduce variance when the tested policies are similar. While these estimators represent a clear improvement, there is still room to further reduce variance. This work lays the groundwork for incorporating advanced off-policy techniques into A/B testing, with extensions including doubly robust methods
(
dudik14doubly
)
to reduce reward variance, and leveraging the dynamics structure to address challenges posed by long horizons
(
curse_long
;
cond_is
;
long_hor_cond
)
.

Appendix A
Limitations

We propose an intuitive yet theoretically grounded family of off-policy A/B testing estimators that provably improve upon the classical difference-in-means estimator. While designed to reduce variance by exploiting policy similarity, the estimator, as discussed in the marginal improvement section
4.4
, reverts to the difference-in-means baseline in certain edge cases:
(i)
when policies are fully disjoint, resulting in zero importance weights, and
(ii)
in long-horizon settings, where small policy differences cause trajectory overlap to vanish exponentially. These limitations, inherent to importance weighting, could be addressed by methods that better exploit the sequential structure, an avenue we leave for future work. Another limitation of the approach is its reliance on the
Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption
(SUTVA), which may be violated in certain applications. While our estimators are developed under SUTVA, extending this framework to more general settings remains an important direction for future work.

Appendix B
Broader Impact Statement

Our work advances the development of improved A/B testing estimators that increase the statistical power of experiments and strengthen the robustness of decision-making. These estimators enable practitioners to make more informed decisions, especially in settings with limited data. While our framework has broad and potentially beneficial applications, its specific social impact will ultimately depend on the domain in which it is applied.

C.1
Our family of estimators is efficient

Recall the definition of our family of estimators. Let
f
be a bounded function. Our estimator writes:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}f(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t}))r^{j}_{t}+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau^{i}_{t}))\right]\right)r^{i}_{t}\,.

This estimator is constructed using an

\mathcal{D}_{A}
. If we reverse the roles of
\mathcal{D}_{B}

\mathcal{D}_{A}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{f^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}f^{\prime}(w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t}))r^{i}_{t}+\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}\left(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t})\left[1-f^{\prime}(w_{B}(\tau^{j}_{t}))\right]-1\right)r^{j}_{t}\,.

This construction is also valid. It uses the

\mathcal{D}_{A}
and corrects its bias with interactions of
\mathcal{D}_{B}
. It seems equivalent to using
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}
with a specific function
f
. Let

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{+*},z(x)=x(1-f^{\prime}(1/x))-1,

we have for any

\displaystyle z(x)=x(1-f^{\prime}(1/x))-1\iff f^{\prime}(x)=1-x(1+z(1/x)).

This means that:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}z(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t}))r^{j}_{t}+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t})\left[1+z(w_{A}(\tau^{i}_{t}))\right]\right)r^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}\left(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t})\left[1-f^{\prime}(w_{B}(\tau^{j}_{t}))\right]-1\right)r^{j}_{t}+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}f^{\prime}(w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t}))r^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{f^{\prime}}.

This means that the reversed estimator

as we have for any

\displaystyle\lambda\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}+(1-\lambda)\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{f^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}+(1-\lambda)\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{z}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda f+(1-\lambda)z}.

This proves the efficiency of the parametrization of our estimator

Variance and its surrogate

For any function

\displaystyle\text{var}\left[\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}\right]=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{B})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\right]+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\text{var}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)r_{t}\right].

C.3
Beyond Minimizing Variance

In the main paper, we were interested in finding the function
f
that minimizes the variance of our improvement estimator
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}

[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}-\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})|\right].

We can build a simple to optimize, tractable upper bound on the MAE with. We start with the triangle inequality to obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}-\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}\rvert\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\mathcal{I}(\pi_{A},\pi_{B})\rvert\right].

We expect this first upper bound to be tight when the improvement is small, which is the case in real life scenarios. By linearity of the expectation and by another application of the triangle inequality, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{f}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}f(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t}))r^{j}_{t}+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\sum_{i\in u_{A}}^{n_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau^{i}_{t}))\right]\right)r^{i}_{t}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{B}}\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\sum_{j\in u_{B}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}f(w_{A}(\tau^{j}_{t}))r^{j}_{t}\rvert\right]+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[|\sum_{i\in u_{A}}^{n_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau^{i}_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau^{i}_{t}))\right]\right)r^{i}_{t}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\sum_{t=1}^{T}f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\rvert\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)r_{t}\rvert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lvert f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\rvert+\lvert\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)r_{t}\rvert\right]=\mathcal{M}_{f}\,.

As for the variance surrogate reduction, the function
\mathcal{M}_{f}
we want to minimize decomposes over the expectation, and every trajectory
\tau_{t}
can be linked to a one dimensional variable
f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))
. This means that we can solve this minimization problem by looking for one variable

at a time, that minimizes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{A},\pi_{B}}\left[\lvert f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\rvert+\lvert\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)r_{t}\rvert\right]\,,

equivalent to minimizing:

\displaystyle\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\lvert f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\rvert+\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})\lvert\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)\rvert\,,

finally giving:

\displaystyle\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\lvert f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\rvert+\lvert\left(\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)\rvert\,.

Now, let us study the following function and look for its minimizer in
[-1,1]
:

F(f_{0})=\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\lvert f_{0}\rvert+\lvert\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f_{0}\right]\rvert\,.

Proof.

\displaystyle\text{Define }c=w_{A}(\tau_{t})-1\text{ for simplicity.}

Case 1:

f_{0}\geq 0

•

If
f_{0}\leq c
:

\displaystyle F(f_{0})

\displaystyle=\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0}+(\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0})

\displaystyle=\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\quad\text{(constant)}

\displaystyle F(f_{0})

\displaystyle=\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0}-(\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0})

Case 2:

f_{0}<0

•

If
f_{0}\leq c

\displaystyle F(f_{0})

\displaystyle=-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0}+(\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0})

If

\displaystyle=-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0}-(\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})-\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})f_{0})

\displaystyle=-\pi_{A}(\tau_{t})+\pi_{B}(\tau_{t})\quad\text{(constant)}

•

f_{0}=c

Recall the definition of our family of estimators. Let

D.3
Optimal Off-Policy estimator

For any function
f
, let us first recall the expression of the variance surrogate:

\displaystyle S_{f}=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\pi_{B})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))r_{t}\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{n_{A}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(\pi_{A})}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-w_{B}(\tau_{t})\left[1+f(w_{A}(\tau_{t}))\right]\right)^{2}r_{t}^{2}\right].

We proceed to prove Proposition
4.4
.

Proof.

Similarly to the variance study in Appendix
