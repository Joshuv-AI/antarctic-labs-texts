Spectral and dynamical invariants of Hecke triangle groups via transfer operators
Ksenia Fedosova

Abstract
In this paper, we consider Hecke triangle groups Γ w \Gamma_{w} for w > 2 w>2 and associated infinite-volume orbifolds Γ w \ ℍ \Gamma_{w}\backslash\mathbb{H} . We show that the Selberg zeta function Z Γ w ​ ( s ) Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s) can be approximated for s ∈ ℂ ∖ 1 2 ​ ( 1 − 2 ​ ℕ 0 ) s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0}) by determinants of finite-dimensional matrices with an explicitly computed error term that decays exponentially as the matrix size increases. As an application, we evaluate the Hausdorff dimensions of Hecke triangle groups with high precision, explicitly compute the values of the corresponding Ruelle zeta functions at zero, and obtain estimates on orders of trivial zeroes of the Selberg zeta function.



1
Introduction

The Hecke triangle group
\Gamma_{w}
for
w>0
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
generated by the elements

T_{w}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&w\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad S:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right].

(1)

For admissible values of
w
, the group
\Gamma_{w}
acts on the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}
with hyperbolic orbifold quotients. In particular,
\Gamma_{1}
coincides with the modular group
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. However, for
w>2
, the case of interest of this article, the quotient
\Gamma_{w}\backslash\mathbb{H}
is an infinite-volume orbifold with one cusp and one conical singularity. We define the
limit set

\Lambda_{w}
of
\Gamma_{w}
to be the set of limit points of any fixed orbit of
\Gamma_{w}
. For
w>2
,
\Lambda_{w}
is a fractal subset of
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, and we define
\delta_{w}
as its
Hausdorff dimension
.

One of the goals of this article is to obtain
\delta_{w}
with rigorously controlled high precision, achieved through approximation of the Selberg zeta function with an explicit exponentially decaying error bound.
Such an approximation of the Selberg zeta function (extended to its meromorphic continuation domain) enables the
explicit evaluation
of the Ruelle zeta function at
s=0
using the transfer operator approach.
To the best of the author’s knowledge, these computations represent the first instance where transfer operators are used to study Ruelle zeta values in a geometric setting. In addition, the approach allows us to derive estimates on the orders of the trivial zeros of the Selberg zeta function.

For a hyperbolic orbifold
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
, the
Selberg zeta function
is originally defined for
\text{Re}(s)
sufficiently large by

Z_{\Gamma}(s)=\prod_{[\gamma]}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-(s+k)\ell(\gamma)}),

(2)

where
[\gamma]
runs over conjugacy classes of primitive hyperbolic elements of
\Gamma
and
\ell(\gamma)
denotes the length of a closed geodesic in
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
associated to
\gamma\in\Gamma
. In many settings, the Selberg zeta function admits a meromorphic continuation to the complex plane, and its zeros and poles encode spectral information of the orbifold – specifically, the spectrum of the Laplace operator on
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
. For the group
\Gamma_{w}
with
w>2
, the Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)
has exactly one zero in the half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
, located at
s=\delta_{w}
. This zero corresponds to the
base eigenvalue

\delta_{w}(1-\delta_{w})
of the Laplacian on
\Gamma_{w}\backslash\mathbb{H}
.

In his seminal paper
[
10
]
, Mayer introduced a thermodynamical approach to the Selberg zeta function for the modular surface
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
. He defined a so-called
transfer operator

\mathcal{L}_{s}
such that, for
\text{Re}(s)
sufficiently large, the Selberg zeta function associated to the modular surface was realized as the Fredholm determinant of the operator
1-\mathcal{L}_{s}
,

Z_{\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}}(s)=\operatorname{det}(1-\mathcal{L}_{s}).

(3)

Mayer then showed that
s\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{s}
admits a meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
, that allowed to obtain the meromorphic continuation of the Selberg zeta function. This result was extended to other groups and different settings, see e.g.
[
4
,
11
,
18
,
19
,
8
,
21
,
20
]
and references therein. In this paper, we analyze the extension of these results to Hecke triangle groups with
w>2
and the transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
introduced in
[
14
]
.

However, we introduce a slightly different approach from Mayer. Instead of studying the meromorphic continuation of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
, we directly prove the meromorphic continuation of the determinant
\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{s})
. To do this, we use a sequence of meromorphic functions
F_{N}(s)
, first introduced in
[
24
]
, which (as we will show in Theorem
1.1
) approximate
\det(1-\mathcal{L}_{s})
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
as
N\to\infty
. In
[
24
]
, only the asymptotic convergence of
F_{N}(s)
to the Selberg zeta function was established, and only in the restricted half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\tfrac{1}{2}
. In this paper, we extend the method to the full holomorphic domain
\mathbb{C}\setminus\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
, where the Selberg zeta function exhibits its most interesting behavior. Second, instead of merely asymptotic convergence, we provide an explicit and exponentially decaying error bound (Theorem
1.1
). This allows for high-precision computation of Hausdorff dimensions of the boundaries of Hecke triangle groups with the help of the bisection method, improving the three digits previously reported in
[
22
]
and
[
24
]
to at least fifty.

In order to formulate the main theorem, define

\begin{split}C(s)=\sup_{n\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}}|\zeta(2s+n)|,\quad s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0}),\end{split}

(4)

where
\zeta
denotes the Riemann zeta function.
For

and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

to be a determinant of the
N\times N
matrix
(1-L(s))

F_{N}(s)=\det(1-L(s)),

(5)

where the
(i,j)
-th entry
\ell_{ij}(s)
of
L(s)
is equal to

\ell_{ij}(s)=2\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{i+1}}\cdot\frac{\zeta(2s+i+j)}{w^{2s+i+j}}\binom{2s+i+j-1}{i}\quad\text{for }i+j\text{ even},

(6)

and

\ell_{ij}(s)=0\quad\text{for }i+j\text{ odd}.

(7)

We additionally introduce the functions

and

(8)

and

Q(s)=2C(s)\frac{w^{2|s|-2\operatorname{Re}(s)}}{(w-1)^{2|s|-1}}\cdot\operatorname{Li}_{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{w-1}\right),

(9)

where
\operatorname{Li}
denotes the polylogarithm
[
15
, 25.12.10]
. We note that for a fixed value of
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
and
w>2
, we have

P_{N}(s)=O(N^{1/2}(w/2)^{-N}),\quad N\to\infty.

(10)

One of the main results of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For

for

and

as in (

) and (

Additionally, we apply Theorem

to study the Ruelle zeta function defined for

.

.

For
w>2
, the Ruelle zeta

.

Let

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Functional analysis

In this section, we collect the necessary information about trace class operators and provide a crude estimate of their nuclear norms. Let
\mathscr{H}
be a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Suppose that
A:\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is a compact operator defined via its action on an orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of
\mathscr{H}
:

\langle\psi_{i},A\psi_{j}\rangle=\ell_{ij}.

(14)

Denote by
\mu_{m}(A)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the singular values of
A
, arranged in decreasing order
[
7
, p. 49, (2.1)]
.
They satisfy the min-max principle
[
7
, p. 49, (2.2)]
: for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\mu_{m}(A)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\subset\mathscr{H}\\
\operatorname{dim}(V)=m-1\end{subarray}}\max_{\psi\in V^{\perp}}\frac{\|A\psi\|}{\|\psi\|},

(15)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm.
To estimate
\mu_{m}(A)
from above, consider an
(m-1)
-dimensional subspace
V
of
\mathscr{H}
spanned by
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m-2}
, then (fixing a minor misprint in
[
24
, (16)]
)

\mu_{m}(A)\leq\max_{\psi\in V^{\perp}}\frac{\|A\psi\|}{\|\psi\|}\leq\sum_{j=m-1}^{\infty}\|A\psi_{j}\|.

(16)

We recall that the trace norm of the operator
A
is by definition equal to the sum of singular values of
A
:

\|A\|_{1}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}(A).

(17)

If
\|A\|_{1}<\infty
, then
A
is called a nuclear operator.
We can evaluate the right hand side of (
17
) as follows:

\|A\|_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:sing_value_est}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=m-1}^{\infty}\|A\psi_{j}\|=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)\|A\psi_{j}\|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)|\ell_{ij}|.

(18)

If
A
is of trace class, then so is
A^{*}

[
7
, p. 51, Corollary 2.2]
, and given that
\|A^{*}\|_{1}=\|A\|_{1}
, we obtain

\|A\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)|\ell_{ji}|=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(i+1)|\ell_{ij}|.

(19)

The estimates are in no way sharp; e.g., for an operator
A
defined by (
14
) with

\ell_{ij}=\begin{cases}(j+1)^{-2},\quad&i=j,\\
0,\quad&\text{else},\end{cases}

(20)

we have
A=|A|
and hence
\|A\|_{1}=\zeta(2)
. On the other hand, we cannot even deduce from either (
18
) or (
19
) that the operator
A
is of trace class, because the bounds read
\|A\|_{1}\leq\infty
.

2.2
Transfer operator

In this section, we recall the construction of the transfer operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
from
[
24
]
.
Let
\mathbb{D}=\{|z|<1\}
, and define the Bergman space as

H^{2}(\mathbb{D}):=\{f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\text{ holomorphic }\mid\|f\|<\infty\}

(21)

with
L^{2}
-norm given by
\|f\|^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|^{2}\operatorname{dvol}(z)
. For
\text{Re}(s)>1/2
, following
[
24
, (20)-(22)]
, we introduce the
transfer operator

\mathcal{L}_{s}
defined by its action on functions
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
as

\mathcal{L}_{s}f(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}(|n|w)^{-2s}e^{-2s\log(1+\frac{z}{nw})}f\left(-\frac{1}{z+nw}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(22)

Then,
[
24
, Proposition 2.1]
implies that
\mathcal{L}_{s}
, defined in this way, is a self-map and is a nuclear operator. Moreover, for
\text{Re}(s)>1/2
, the Selberg zeta function can be realized as the Fredholm determinant of the operator
1-\mathcal{L}_{s}

[
24
, (36)]

Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)=\operatorname{det}(1-\mathcal{L}_{s}).

(23)

Remark 2.1
.

The next step in
[
14
]
as well as in
[
24
]
, which is standard in the literature, is to introduce a certain finite rank operator
\mathcal{F}_{s}
that is holomorphic as a function of
s
, and a bounded operator
\Psi
such that (see
[
14
, Section 2.3]
or
[
24
, (43)]
)

\mathcal{L}_{s}=\mathcal{F}_{s}+\mathcal{L}_{s+1/2}\circ\Psi.

(24)

From the expression above, if
\mathcal{L}_{s}
defines a meromorphic function in a half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(s)>s_{0}
for
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, then it also defines a meromorphic function in the larger domain
\operatorname{Re}(s)>s_{0}-1/2
. Iterating this argument yields a meromorphic continuation of
\mathcal{L}_{s}
to the entire complex plane (see, e.g.,
[
24
, proof of Corollary 1.3]
). However, we will not rely on this result here.

In
[
24
, (26)]
, the author wrote an explicit representation of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
in an orthonormal basis
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
for the Bergman space
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,

\psi_{j}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{\pi}}z^{j},\quad j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(25)

and wrote an explicit action of
\mathcal{L}_{s}
in this basis
[
24
, Proposition 4.1]
:

\left\langle\psi_{i},\mathcal{L}_{s}\psi_{j}\right\rangle=\ell_{ij}(s),\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2,

(26)

where
\ell_{ij}(s)
is as in (
6
) and (
7
).

Further,
[
24
]
constructed a family of finite rank operators

\operatorname{Pr}_{N}\circ\mathcal{L}_{s}\circ\operatorname{Pr}_{N},

(27)

where
\operatorname{Pr}_{N}
denotes the projection onto
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N-1}
. As
[
24
]
showed, such operators approximate
\mathcal{L}_{s}
exponentially fast in the sense of trace norm for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

For
i,j\in N_{0}
, each of
\ell_{ij}(s)
, defined by (
6
) and (
7
), is either a meromorphic function in
s\in\mathbb{C}
with the only pole at
\frac{1-i-j}{2}
for
i+j
even, or a constant function for
i+j
odd. Let
\psi_{j}
,

be as in (
25

\mathcal{W}(s)
and
\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)
the operators defined by their actions on

\left\langle\psi_{i},\mathcal{W}(s)\psi_{j}\right\rangle=\ell_{ij}(s),\quad s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})

\left\langle\psi_{i},\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\psi_{j}\right\rangle=\delta_{0\leq i,j\leq N-1}\cdot\ell_{ij}(s),\quad s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0}),

and zero otherwise.

Note that meromorphy of

is not enough to conclude that
\mathcal{W}(s)

\mathcal{W}(s)
coincides with meromorphic continuation of
\mathcal{L}_{s}
from Remark

1.1
, we will need an additional lemma. We aim to prove that for
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})

\mathcal{W}(s)
is of trace class, and that operators
\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)
approximate

exponentially fast in

in the sense of trace norm. We follow the general route of

, but provide explicit estimates instead of asymptotic ones, perform evaluations for all

instead of the region
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
and show that the convergence is, in fact, uniform in
s
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
and let
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let

and

be as in (

) and (

), respectively. The operator

\|\mathcal{W}(s)\|_{1}\leq Q(s).

(30)

Moreover, the operators
\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)

\mathcal{W}(s)
exponentially fast in the trace norm as a function of

:

\begin{split}\left\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)-\mathcal{W}(s)\right\|_{1}&\leq P_{N}(s).\end{split}

(31)

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

We begin the proof by analyzing the definition (
6
) and (
7
) of
\ell_{ij}(s)
. Let
s\in K
for a compact subset
K

s\in\mathbb{C}
and

\left|\binom{2s+i+j-1}{i}\right|\leq\binom{|2s|+i+j-1}{i}.

The inequality can be shown by comparing individual factors.
To examine the contribution of the Riemann zeta function to
\ell_{ij}(s)
, let
n_{0}=n_{0}(K)
be the smallest natural number such that

for

|\zeta(2s+n)|\leq\max\{\max_{0\leq n\leq n_{0}}|\zeta(2s+n)|,\sup_{n>n_{0}}|\zeta(2s+n)|\}.

Moreover, for

\sup_{n>n_{0}}|\zeta(2s+n)|\leq\zeta(2\operatorname{Re}(s)+n_{0}),

that implies that

Thus, we have

|\ell_{ij}(s)|\leq 2C(s)\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{i+1}}\binom{2|s|+i+j-1}{i}\frac{1}{w^{2\operatorname{Re}(s)+i+j}}.

\begin{split}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}w^{-i}\binom{2|s|+i+j-1}{i}&=\left(\frac{w}{w-1}\right)^{2|s|+j},\end{split}

and by the definition of the polylogarithm,

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(j+1)^{3/2}}{(w-1)^{j}}=(w-1)\operatorname{Li}_{-\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{w-1}\right).

Thus,

\begin{split}\left\|\mathcal{W}(s)\right\|_{1}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:crude_trace_estimate2}}}{{\leq}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)|\ell_{ij}(s)|\\

\end{split}

(38)

that finishes the proof of (
30

and

instead of asymptotically sharper
N^{1/2}(2/w)^{N}
.

We use a cruder estimate for the binomial coefficients
[
24
, p. 1264]

\binom{2|s|+i+j-1}{i}\leq 2^{\lceil 2|s|\rceil+i+j-1},

(40)

from which we obtain

|\ell_{ij}(s)|\leq C(s)\frac{2^{\lceil 2|s|\rceil+i+j}}{w^{2\operatorname{Re}(s)+i+j}}\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{i+1}},

(41)

and thus

\begin{split}\left\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)-\mathcal{W}(s)\right\|_{1}&\leq C(s)\frac{2^{\lceil 2|s|\rceil}}{w^{2\operatorname{Re}(s)}}\left(\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}+\sum_{i=N}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\right)(\frac{2}{w})^{i+j}\sqrt{(i+1)(j+1)}\\
&\leq C(s)\frac{2^{\lceil 2|s|\rceil}}{w^{2\operatorname{Re}(s)}}\left(\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}+\sum_{i=N}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\right)(\frac{2}{w})^{i+j}\sqrt{(i+1)(j+1)}\\
&=2C(s)\frac{2^{\lceil 2|s|\rceil}}{w^{2\operatorname{Re}(s)}}\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\frac{2}{w})^{i+j}\sqrt{(i+1)(j+1)}.\\
\end{split}

(42)

By the definition of the polylogarithm, we have

\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{(i+1)(j+1)}(\frac{2}{w})^{i+j}=\frac{w}{2}\operatorname{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{2}{w})\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}(\frac{2}{w})^{j}\sqrt{j+1}.

(43)

We evaluate the sum in the right hand side of the previous expression as follows:

\begin{split}\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}(\frac{2}{w})^{j}(j+1)^{1/2}&=\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(\frac{2}{w})^{j^{\prime}+N}(j^{\prime}+N+1)^{1/2}\\
&=(N+1)^{1/2}(\frac{2}{w})^{N}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(\frac{2}{w})^{j^{\prime}}(\frac{j^{\prime}}{N+1}+1)^{1/2}\\
&\leq(N+1)^{1/2}(\frac{2}{w})^{N}\left(1+\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}(\frac{2}{w})^{j^{\prime}}(2j^{\prime})^{1/2}\right)\\
&=(\frac{2}{w})^{N}(N+1)^{1/2}\left[1+\sqrt{2}\text{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{2}{w})\right].\\
\end{split}

(44)

Combining (
42
), (
43
) and (
44
), we obtain

\begin{split}\left\|\mathcal{W}(s)-\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\right\|_{1}\leq(\frac{2}{w})^{N}(N+1)^{1/2}\cdot wC(s)\frac{2^{\lceil 2|s|\rceil}}{w^{2\operatorname{Re}(s)}}\operatorname{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{2}{w})\left[1+\sqrt{2}\text{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}(\frac{2}{w})\right]\end{split}

(45)

that finishes the proof.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
:

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By the lemma above,
\mathcal{W}(s)
is a trace class operator, and we can define, for every
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
, the Fredholm determinant of
1-\mathcal{W}(s)
,

F(s)=\operatorname{det}(1-\mathcal{W}(s)).

(46)

Note that

F_{N}(s)=\det(1-\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)).

(47)

Note that for each fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the function
F_{N}(s)

is holomorphic in
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
as a finite sums of products of finitely many holomorphic functions.
We have

\begin{split}|F_{N}(s)-F(s)|&=|\det(1-\mathcal{W}(s))-\det(1-\mathcal{W}_{N}(s))|\\
&\leq\|\mathcal{W}(s)-\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\|_{1}\cdot\exp(\max\{\|\mathcal{W}(s)\|_{1},\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\|_{1}\}+1).\end{split}

(48)

The first factor is evaluated in (
31
). To evaluate the second factor, note

\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\|_{1}\leq\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\|_{1}+\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)-\mathcal{W}(s)\|_{1}

(49)

and hence

|F_{N}(s)-F(s)|\leq\|\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)-\mathcal{W}(s)\|_{1}\cdot\exp(\|\mathcal{W}(s)\|_{1}+\|\mathcal{W}(s)-\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)\|_{1}+1).

(50)

For
s\in K
, (
31
) yields that the right hand side (
50
) is bounded by a constant depending on
K
. Hence, the function
F(s)
is a uniform limit of holomorphic functions, that implies by Morera’s theorem its own holomorphy. By
[
24
, (36)]
, for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
the function
F(s)
coincides with the Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)
, that together with uniqueness of analytic continuation finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The estimate (
11
) is not good for large values of
\operatorname{Im}(s)
– specifically, it grows like
\exp(\exp(\operatorname{Im}(s))))
. Consequently, to maintain accuracy,
N
must be chosen of order
\exp(\operatorname{Im}(s))
.

The bottleneck of the proof is in the fact that in (
50
), we need to find the exponent of
\|\mathcal{W}(s)\|_{1}
, and the latter already exhibits exponential growth in
|\operatorname{Im}(s)|
. However, other examples in the literature of similar estimates (that the author is aware of) use an estimate of singular values that also depends on
\operatorname{Im}(s)
exponentially; cf.
[
14
, Proposition 2.3]
or
[
8
, discussion above Lemma 3.3]
.

4
Numerical estimates of Hausdorff dimensions

To find roots of
Z(s)
that belong to the real line, we can use the bisection method.

More precisely, note that
\zeta(s)
is a real-valued function for
s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}
, that implies the function
F(s)
is real-valued as well. To prove that there is a root that belongs to an interval
[s_{1},s_{2}]\subset\mathbb{R}
(not containing poles of
F(s)
), it suffices to show
F(s_{1})\cdot F(s_{2})<0
. To prove, in turn, that
F(s_{1})<0
, we write
F(s_{1})=F_{N}(s_{1})+(F(s_{1})-F_{N}(s_{1}))
and note it will be sufficient to find
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

1.

F_{N}(s_{1})<0
and

2.

|F(s_{1})-F_{N}(s_{1})|<|F_{N}(s_{1})|
, that can be evaluated as the right hand side of (
11
) where instead of
s
we substitute
s_{1}
.

For example, take
s_{1}=0.75
,
w=3
. For
|F(s_{1})-F_{N}(s_{1})|
to be of order
10^{-50}
, it suffices to take
N=350
. For larger
w
, smaller
N
will suffice: for example, let
s_{1}=0.55
and
w=8
, then for
|F(s_{1})-F_{N}(s_{1})|
to be of order
10^{-50}
, it will suffice to take
N=100
.

Now that we have the method of proving that in a given interval, there exists a zero of
Z(s)
, we can start with an interval where such a zero exists a priori; e.g., from
[
24
, Theorem 1.4]
and
[
17
, Theorem 6.1]
, the Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)
always has a zero between
1/2
and
1
. Then, one improves the precision with the help of a bisection method.

The result of the calculations, performed with the help of
[
16
]
, are present in Table
1
. Additionally, for
w=2\pi
, the Hausdorff dimension can be approximated by the the reciprocal of the golden ratio with an approximate error of
3\cdot 10^{-4}
(though, of course, this is merely a coincidence).

5
Proof of Corollary
1.2
(Ruelle zeta function at zero)

The idea of the proof is to approximate
R_{\Gamma_{w}}(0)
by a ratio of determinants of finite matrices
1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(0)
and
1-\mathcal{V}_{N-1}(1)^{T}
(to be defined later). We will see that
1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(0)
admits an upper block-diagonal structure, where one of the blocks coincides with the diagonal transpose
1-\mathcal{V}_{N-1}(1)^{T}
of the other matrix.

For
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\frac{1}{2}(1-2\mathbb{N}_{0})
and
i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

a_{ij}(s)=\sqrt{\frac{i+1}{j+1}}\ell_{ij}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:elsijeven},\eqref{eq:elsijodd}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}2\frac{\zeta(2s+i+j)}{w^{2s+i+j}}\binom{2s+i+j-1}{i},&\quad i+j\text{ even},\\
0,&\quad i+j\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(51)

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{V}_{N}(s)
be defined similarly to (
29
) with the only exception that we replace all occurances of
\ell_{ij}(s)
by
a_{ij}(s)
. That is, the operator
\mathcal{V}_{N}(s)
can be represented by an
(N\times N)
-matrix with the
(i,j)
-th entry equal to
a_{ij}(s)
instead of
\ell_{ij}(s)
. The respective determinants do not change,

\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(s))=\det(1-\mathcal{W}_{N}(s)).

(52)

Note for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
a_{i0}(0)=0
from the properties of binomial coefficients, thus every
(i,0)
-th entry of
\mathcal{V}_{N}(0)
vanishes. Hence, the matrix
\mathcal{V}_{N}(0)
is an upper-block diagonal matrix, where the first block is equal to an
(1\times 1)
-matrix with the single entry

a_{00}(0)=2\zeta(0)=-1.

(53)

Additionally, from (
51
) and

\binom{(i+1)+(j+1)-1}{i+1}=\binom{2+i+j-1}{j},

(54)

one has

a_{i+1,j+1}(0)=a_{ji}(1),

(55)

thus the
(i+1,j+1)
-st entry of
\mathcal{V}_{N}(0)
coincides with the
(j,i)
-th entry of
\mathcal{V}_{N-1}(1)
, that together with (
53
) implies

\mathcal{V}_{N}(0)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-1&\ldots\\
0&\mathcal{V}_{N-1}(1)^{T}\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

From this, we obtain an equality for the determinants,

\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(0))=2\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N-1}(1)),

(57)

and, from (
52
) and (
47
), we get

F_{N}(0)=2F_{N-1}(1).

(58)

By Theorem
1.1
, the Selberg zeta function
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)
is approximated by
F_{N}(s)
, that together with (
12
) finishes the proof.

Remark 5.1
.

It may at first appear that our result is not consistent with the works of
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
, or
[
5
]
. In those references, the behavior of the Ruelle zeta function at the origin is described in terms of the Euler characteristic
\chi(X)
of the surface
X
, namely

R_{X}(s)\sim s^{-\chi(X)},\quad s\to 0,

(59)

that is not the case of the article. However, in our case, the Euler characteristic of the surface
\Gamma_{w}\backslash\mathbb{H}
is not an integer, and a literal application of (
59
) would suggest a non-integer exponent, hence a non-analytic behavior of the Ruelle zeta function at the origin.

6
Trivial zeroes of
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)

An argument similar to the one in the previous section allows to evaluate the orders of trivial zeros of
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)
.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\mathcal{V}_{N}(s)
be as in the proof of Corollary
1.2
. From the definition of binomial coefficients, we get

a_{ij}(-m)=0\quad\text{ for }0\leq j\leq 2m\text{ and }\,i\geq 2m+1,

(60)

hence
\mathcal{V}_{N+2m+1}(-m)
is a block matrix.
Moreover,

a_{i+m+1,j+m+1}(-m)=a_{ji}(1+m),\quad i,j\geq 0.

(61)

Note that the right hand side of (
61
) is an
(i,j)
-th entry of the transpose of
\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m)
, and the left hand side of (
61
) is the
(i+m+1,j+m+1)
-th entry of
\mathcal{V}_{N+2m+1}(-m)
. Thus, the matrix
\mathcal{V}_{N+2m+1}(-m)
splits in blocks as follows:

\mathcal{V}_{N+2m+1}(-m)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}U(0)&\ldots\\
0&\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m)^{T}\end{pmatrix}

(62)

where

U(s)=(a_{ij}(-m+s))_{i,j=0}^{2m}.

(63)

The analyticity of
a_{ij}(s)
together with (
62
) implies that as
s\to 0
,

\mathcal{V}_{N+2m+1}(-m+s)\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}U(s)&\ldots\\
V(s)&\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m)^{T}+O(s)\end{pmatrix},

(64)

where
V(s)
is an
N\times(2m+1)
-dimensional matrix with entries of order
O(s)
as
s\to 0
,
thus

\begin{split}&\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N+2m+1}(-m+s))\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ =\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m))\cdot\det(1-U(s))+O(s^{N}).\end{split}

(65)

From the properties of
a_{ij}(-m+s)
, the matrix
1-U(0)
has nonzero
entries only on its diagonal and anti-diagonal. If
m
is even, all positions on these two diagonals are nonzero; if
m
is odd, the central entry at their intersection vanishes.
Moreover, for
j=0,1,\ldots,m
, the
j
-th and
(2m+1-j)
-th columns in
1-U(0)
are linearly dependent. That implies
1-U(0)
has rank
m+1
for even
m
and rank
m
for odd
m
. For example, for
m=1
, we get

1-U(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}

(66)

is of rank
1
, and
in the case
m=2
, we have

1-U(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&1\\
0&1&0&-1&0\\
0&0&2&0&0\\
0&-1&0&1&0\\
1&0&0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}

(67)

is of rank
3
.

Thus,
\det(1-U(s))
has a zero at
s=0
of degree between

\dim(1-U(0))-\operatorname{rank}(1-U(0))\geq(2m+1)-(m+1)=m

(68)

and
2m+1
. On the other hand, as
N\to\infty
, the determinant
\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m))
approximates
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(m+1)
, and the latter is not equal to zero as a convergent product (
2
). Thus for some sufficiently large
N
,
\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m))\neq 0
.

Hence, for large values of
N
,
\det(1-\mathcal{V}_{N}(1+m))\cdot\det(1-U(s))
has a zero at
s=0
of a degree between
m
and
2m+1
. Together with (
65
), it finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.1
.

The proof of the lemma above illustrates the limitation of approximating
Z_{\Gamma_{w}}(s)
by
F_{N}(s)
for
\operatorname{Re}(s)\ll 0
. Namely, that it shows that for
s=-m
to be a zero of
F_{N}(s)
, we need to take
N
to be at least equal to
m+1
, else
F_{N}(-m)
will have the full rank, and
\det F_{N}(-m)\neq 0
.
