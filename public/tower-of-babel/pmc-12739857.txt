Demyelination-related pain: role of lysophosphatidic acid in satellite glial cell–neuron crosstalk
Authors: Yuchen Wang, Qiong Sun, Menachem Hanani, Jiaze Qu, Xu Guo, Wei Yang, Tuo Shao, Siyang Zheng, Shupei Qiao, Ying Jia, Yujia Huang, Yaorui Yang, Hui Zhu, Xinzhong Dong, Feng-Quan Zhou, Yun Guan, Xiaodan Song, Zhiyong Chen

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDemyelination diseases are characterized by injury to large (A-type) myelinated nerve fibers, and by secondary damage to small (C-type) sensory fibers, which leads to chronic pain symptoms, such as allodynia. The mechanisms underlying the interactions between the two fiber types are not clear. This study aims to investigate the role of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) signaling in satellite glial cells (SGCs) within the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) in demyelination-induced chronic pain.MethodsA demyelination model was established by injecting cobra venom into the tibial nerve of 8–10-week-old Sprague-Dawley rats to selectively damage A-fiber myelin. Myelin morphology was observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days post-injection. Pain behaviors (mechanical hypersensitivity, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain) were assessed to evaluate progression. In vivo electrophysiology was performed to analyze sensory conduction and excitability changes in A- and C-type neurons. Immunofluorescence staining assessed SGC activation, LPA1 receptor (LPA1R) expression, and connexin 43 (Cx43) dynamics in the L4 DRG over time. Pharmacological interventions targeting LPA1R and SGC activation were applied to evaluate their effects on pain behaviors, cytokine release, and neuronal excitability using RT-PCR, ELISA, and spinal electrophysiology.ResultsCobra venom induced a selective A-fiber demyelination and persistent pain in rats. It also upregulated the expression of LPA1R on SGCs that surround large DRG neurons, which normally mediate non-noxious input, and increased gap junction-mediated coupling via Cx43, leading to the activation of SGCs surrounding small nociceptive neurons. The activated SGCs released inflammatory mediators that increased nociceptive neuron excitability, driving chronic pain. In support of these results, pharmacological inhibition of LPA1R-mediated SGCs activation reversed this process.ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that LPA–LPA1R signaling in SGCs drives A-fiber demyelination-induced neuropathic pain by promoting Cx43-mediated SGC–neuron crosstalk and cytokine release. Introduction

Demyelinating diseases (DDs) are a group of disorders characterized by damage to the myelin sheaths of peripheral myelinated nerve fibers, resulting in significant motor dysfunctions and sensory abnormalities, such as tingling and numbness [1]. Paradoxically, these diseases are frequently associated with dysfunctions of small fiber types, including those that mediate sharp and burning pain, which are typically linked to C-fiber damage. This suggests that the pathological processes in DDs extend beyond large fiber injury, involving complex interactions between different types of nerve fibers. One of the most widely recognized DDs is Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), a condition marked by both severe muscle weakness and debilitating pain, often following an infection [2, 3].

Another prominent example of a DD is chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), which is characterized by an autoimmune attack on the peripheral nerve myelin and typically presents with chronic symptoms that include sensory loss and muscle weakness [4, 5]. In addition to CIDP and GBS, other severe pain syndromes, such as trigeminal neuralgia, also involve demyelination of nerve axons, often leading to excruciating pain in the regions innervated by the trigeminal nerve [6]. The spectrum of DDs is extensive and includes various paraneoplastic syndromes [7, 8].

However, it should be noted that CIDP does not commonly present with pain symptoms, a phenomenon that is thought to occur because nerve fibers with little or no myelin, such as autonomic and pain fibers, remain relatively unaffected [9]. This observation raises questions about the mechanisms underlying small fiber injury in DDs. Although the initial event in DDs is often the injury to large myelinated fibers, this injury can subsequently affect small fibers, potentially through secondary mechanisms, though the precise nature of these interactions remains unclear.

Despite the growing understanding of the mechanisms of pain in DDs, these diseases remain difficult to treat, and the precise etiology of neuropathic pain remains an area of active investigation. Although animal models have been instrumental in advancing our knowledge of the pathophysiology of pain in DDs, most existing models involve mixed injury to both nociceptive and non-nociceptive neurons, as well as concurrent damage to myelin [10–12]. This makes it difficult to isolate and study the specific contribution of A-fiber demyelination to the development of neuropathic allodynia.

Dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons, which are critical for sensory processing, are surrounded by satellite glial cells (SGCs). Recent studies suggest that DRG neurons and SGCs engage in bidirectional communication, with SGCs playing an essential role in modulating neuronal excitability and contributing to the development of neuropathic pain [13–15]. After nerve injury, the increased numbers of gap junctions between SGCs may enhance their coupling, facilitating the release of inflammatory mediators that augment neuronal excitability and contribute to pain hypersensitivity [15–17]. Despite these findings, the role of SGCs in the context of demyelination-induced neuropathic pain, especially in the A-fiber demyelination model, remains poorly understood.

Recent evidence has also pointed to the involvement of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) signaling, particularly via LPA1 receptor (LPA1R), in modulating the activity of SGCs and influencing the excitability of DRG neurons [18–20]. Using a model of selective A-fiber demyelination induced by cobra venom injection (CVI), we aimed to further investigate the role of LPA signaling in SGC–neuron crosstalk and its contribution to chronic pain in rats.

Methods

Animals and surgical procedures

Adult Sprague-Dawley rats (8–10 weeks old, 250–300 g, both sexes) were used for all experiments. Animals were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and kept under aseptic conditions during surgery. A longitudinal incision was made in the lateral skin of the right thigh to bluntly separate the biceps femoris muscle and expose the sciatic nerve and its branches: sural, tibial, and common peroneal nerves. Cobra venom (0.15 mg/5 µL saline) was injected into the right tibial nerve to induce targeted demyelination. Sham-operated control rats underwent identical procedures but received saline injections instead of venom. Rats were housed in pairs under standard conditions, including a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. The environmental temperature was maintained at 22 °C with 60% humidity. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Harbin Medical University, and efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.

Drug administration

The LPA1/3 inhibitor Ki16425, the glial cell inhibitor minocycline, and the gap junction decoupler carbenoxolone (CBX) were purchased from MCE. Stock solutions were freshly prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and diluted to the final concentrations with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) before use. Drugs were delivered to the L4-DRG via a cannula implanted above the ganglion, defined as intraganglionic injection (i.drg.).

Behavioral tests

Mechanical allodynia: Mechanical allodynia was evaluated using von Frey filaments following the up-down method. Rats were placed individually in acrylic glass chambers with a wire mesh floor, allowing access to the plantar surface of their hind paws. Von Frey filaments were applied to the plantar surface of the hind paw for approximately 3 s, and a positive response was recorded if there was an abrupt paw withdrawal, licking, or shaking of the paw. Measurements were repeated three times for each paw, and the average was taken as the paw withdrawal threshold (PWT).

The maximum cutoff time was set at 20 s to prevent tissue damage. A positive response was indicated by paw withdrawal, licking, or shaking. Immunofluorescence

Rats were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium and perfused intracardially with 1× PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. L4-DRG was dissected out and then post-fixed in 30% sucrose solution for 24 h for cryoprotection. Ten-micrometer sections were cut for DRG with a Leica CM1950 cryostat. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in 1× PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against GFAP (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:200), LPA1R (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:50), and Cx43 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:50) overnight at room temperature. After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) for 1 h and visualized using a Nikon fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed on one section per DRG from five animals per group.

Electron microscopy

Rats were deeply anesthetized on days 1, 3, 7, and 14 post-injection, and tibial nerve segments were collected after perfusion with 1× PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Samples were fixed overnight in glutaraldehyde, sectioned into 65 nm slices, and analyzed under a transmission electron microscope. All images were evaluated by an experienced examiner to assess changes in myelin structure. Morphological and molecular analyses were conducted at representative time points (days 1, 3, 7, and 14), corresponding to early demyelination, peak pathology, and partial recovery, as supported by prior studies [22] and our pilot data. This combined design ensured both detailed behavioral resolution and efficient use of animals for invasive assays.

In vivo extracellular recording of C-Neurons in L4-DRG

Referring to the research method of Ma et al., electrophysiological recordings were conducted on Sprague-Dawley rats aged 8-10 weeks on day 7 post-venom injection. Under deep anesthesia, vertebral segments from L1 to L6 were removed to expose the L4-DRG, which was bathed in oxygenated ACSF at 35 °C [23, 24]. The nerve bundle membranes and epineurium were carefully removed, and the rats were transferred to a recording platform. Recordings of C-neuron cell bodies were obtained using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1550B, and peripheral receptive fields were mapped using various mechanical stimuli, including von Frey filaments and cotton swabs.

In vivo extracellular recording of spinal wide-dynamic-range (WDR) neurons

A laminectomy was performed on lumbar vertebrae T13–L1, and segments L4–L5 were exposed and bathed in ACSF at 35–37 °C. The L4-DRG was also exposed and bathed in ACSF. Electrical stimulation was applied to the tibial nerve ipsilateral to the recording site using bipolar electrodes to isolate nociceptive neurons. Recordings were obtained using parylene-coated tungsten microelectrodes inserted 400–800 μm below the dorsal surface of the L4 spinal cord, and signals were analyzed using Spike2 software (CED Ltd.).

Acute focal application of drugs to DRGs in vivo

A stainless-steel cannula was implanted above the L4-DRG for acute focal drug administration. A small hole (~ 1 mm) was drilled through the transverse process over the L4-DRG, and a hooked cannula was inserted and secured with dental cement. Substances were administered to the DRG through this cannula for localized treatment [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). Data are presented as mean ± S. E. M. Behavioral and DRG electrophysiology data were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test was used for analyzing WDR electrophysiology, immunofluorescence, qPCR, and ELISA data. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) data were analyzed by unpaired Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Sample sizes were determined based on pilot data and previous studies to provide at least 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect an effect size ≥ 0.8. No animals or data points were excluded from analysis.

Results

Morphological changes in myelin sheaths following CVI

Cobra venom contains abundant phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which degrades phosphatidylcholine-rich myelin sheaths, leading to nerve injury [21, 27, 28]. The overall experimental design, including behavioral, morphological, and molecular assessments at defined time points, is summarized in Fig. S1. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to observe morphological changes in the myelin sheaths of large-diameter fibers in the tibial nerve of sham and demyelination model rats at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days post-CVI (Fig. 1A–E). Compared with the saline-injected group, the myelin structure of treated rats exhibited degradation as early as day 1 after CVI (Fig. 1A–B). By day 3, significant myelin degeneration was observed, with most sheaths showing degradation (Fig. 1C). On day 7, nearly all myelin sheaths in large-diameter fibers had degraded and disappeared (Fig. 1D). By day 14, extensive remyelination was observed, characterized by thin and structurally immature myelin sheaths surrounded by Schwann cells (Fig. 1E). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the number of intact myelin sheaths in treated rats was significantly reduced at days 3 and 7 compared with sham controls, with full recovery by day 14 (Fig. 1F).

Notably, substantial infiltration of neutrophils and eosinophils was observed at days 3 and 7 after CVI, which markedly decreased by day 14 (Fig. S2).

Demyelination triggers persistent pain behavior and local inflammation

To investigate whether myelin morphological changes correlated with pain behaviors, we assessed mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain in the ipsilateral hindpaw of rats at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 14 days after CVI. Behavioral tests were conducted after CVI into the tibial nerve, as shown in the Fig. 2A, compared with sham controls, CVI rats exhibited persistent spontaneous and evoked pain behaviors (mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia) lasting at least 14 days (Fig. 2B–D). Sham rats showed no significant changes in mechanical or thermal thresholds relative to baseline. Mechanical paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) progressively decreased from day 1, reaching the lowest value at day 7, followed by gradual recovery, though remaining significantly lower than sham levels at day 14 (Fig. 2B). Similarly, thermal paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was significantly reduced at all timepoints, with the lowest values at day 7 and partial recovery by day 14 (Fig. 2C). Spontaneous foot lifting (SFL) duration was also elevated in CVI rats from day 1, peaking at day 7 and remaining significantly higher than sham levels at day 14 (Fig. 2D).

Interestingly, the temporal progression of spontaneous and evoked pain behaviors mirrored myelin changes. Comparative analysis at days 1, 7, and 14 confirmed that pain behaviors (PWT, PWL, SFL) significantly improved by day 14 relative to day 7, indicating that myelin damage and repair critically influence neuropathic pain development (Fig. S3). No sex differences were observed in pain behaviors (Fig. S4).

To learn about the mechanisms underlying pain in this model, we examined changes in inflammatory factors in L4-DRG using both ELISA and qPCR. The results demonstrated that demyelination significantly elevated TNF-α and IL-6 levels, whereas IL-1β showed only non-significant increases (Fig. 2E–G). A similar pattern was also observed in qPCR results (Fig. 2H–J).

Electrophysiological changes in DRG neurons following demyelination

Large (A-type) and small (C-type) neurons in DRG exhibit different physiological properties, mediating non-nociceptive and nociceptive signaling respectively [29, 30]. We carried out in vivo DRG electrophysiological recordings in control rats and CVI ones (at day 7) to evaluate the differential contributions of large and small neurons to sensory processing. Neurons were classified as non-nociceptive large neurons or nociceptive small neurons based on somal area (Fig. 3A) and conduction velocity (Fig. 3B).

The recordings revealed that, when different mechanical stimuli (cotton, brush, and 5mN) were applied to the peripheral receptive fields, large neurons in the CVI rats exhibited complete conduction blockade, whereas the controls displayed normal activity (Fig. 3C). Quantitative analysis of this phenomenon is presented in Fig. 3D.

We also found CVI rats exhibited reduced mechanical stimulation thresholds. Application of low-intensity mechanical stimuli to the peripheral receptive fields of these rats readily evoked action potentials in small neurons (Fig. 3E–F). Moreover, following myelin injury, small neurons displayed spontaneous firing, which was not observed in controls (Fig. 3G). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a statistically significant difference in the incidence of spontaneous activity between small neurons of sham and CVI groups (Fig. 3H).

Activation of SGCs and upregulation of LPA1R in the ipsilateral L4-DRG following demyelination

Peripheral nerve injury and inflammation are well-known activators of SGCs within the DRG [17, 31, 32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that activated SGCs can release proinflammatory cytokines that directly act on sensory neurons, increasing neuronal excitability and firing. We observed a significant activation of SGCs in the ipsilateral L4-DRG following demyelination of A-fibers. Compared with sham-operated controls, CVI rats showed a slight increase in SGCs activation in L4-DRG at day 1 (Fig. 4A–B). Notably, these activated SGCs were predominantly distributed around large neurons (somal area >700 μm²). SGC activation became more pronounced at days 3, peaking at day 7. During this period, activated SGCs no longer localized exclusively around large neurons but showed focal activation patterns. By day 14, SGC activation had decreased, but remained significantly higher than in sham (Fig. 4A–B).

Immunofluorescence analysis of LPA1R, a key receptor in LPA signaling pathway associated with neuropathic pain, revealed significant upregulation in CVI rats as early as day 1 (Fig. 4A, C). Intriguingly, similar to the pattern of SGC activation, LPA1R expression was primarily observed around large neurons. LPA1R expression continued to increase through day 7 before gradually declining by day 14, though remaining elevated compared to sham group (Fig. 4A, C). Colocalization studies confirmed that LPA1R was predominantly expressed in GFAP-positive SGCs surrounding large-diameter neurons, with significant colocalization between LPA1R and large neuronal markers (Fig. 4A, Fig. S5).

We also measured LPA levels in the L4-DRG of sham and CVI rats (at day 7). As shown in Fig. 4D, LPA content was significantly elevated in the DRG of CVI rats compared with controls. Furthermore, behavioral pain tests following intraganglionic injection of exogenous LPA (1 nmol/L, 0.5 µg) revealed that LPA could induce both spontaneous and evoked pain behaviors in normal rats (Fig. S6). These results suggest that LPA signaling may be a critical factor in SGC activation during myelin injury-induced neuropathic pain.

Functional role of activated SGCs and LPA1R in demyelination-induced neuropathic pain

To investigate the contribution of SGCs to neuropathic pain in our model, we inhibited SGC activation pharmacologically using minocycline. Immunofluorescence confirmed that intraganglionic minocycline administration (50 µmol/L, 0.1 µg) significantly reduced SGC activation in CVI rats (Fig. S7). Behavioral testing at day 7 demonstrated that minocycline effectively alleviated demyelination-induced pain behaviors, with analgesic effects lasting approximately 4 h (Fig. 5A–C). However, when administered at day 14 —when SGC activation had naturally subsided —minocycline showed markedly reduced efficacy and a shorter duration of action, suggesting that glial inhibitors primarily exert potent analgesic effects during the early phase of demyelination (Fig. 5D–F).

Similarly, local administration of the LPA1R antagonist Ki16425 (0.1 µg, 50 µmol/L) into L4-DRG significantly ameliorated pain behaviors at days 1, 3, and 7 (Fig. 5G–I, Fig. S8). By day 11, during the remyelination phase, the analgesic effects were substantially diminished (Fig. 5J–L). These results indicate that LPA1R antagonism is effective during the active demyelination phase, but shows limited efficacy during later regenerative stages.

Inhibition of SGC activation and Cx43 upregulation via the LPA1R antagonist Ki16425

To elucidate the role of the LPA–LPA1R signaling pathway in SGC activation, immunofluorescence staining was performed on sham, 7-day CVI rats, and Ki16425-treated rats. The results demonstrated that Ki16425 significantly suppressed SGC activation in DRG compared with the CVI group (Fig. 6A–B). These findings indicate that the LPA/LPA1R signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating SGC activation in DRG during demyelination-induced neuropathic pain.

Previous studies have shown that nerve injury can lead to upregulated expression of gap junction proteins, enhancing functional coupling between adjacent SGCs and thereby contributing to chronic pain maintenance [33, 34]. Therefore, we focused on investigating the role of coupling mediated by the gap junction protein connexin 43 (Cx43) between SGCs in demyelination-induced neuropathic pain. Immunostaining revealed significantly increased Cx43 expression in rats at 7 days after CVI, indicating enhanced coupling between SGCs. We hypothesized that this enhanced coupling might establish a functional pathway between activated SGCs surrounding both large and small neurons, thereby promoting the sensitization of nociceptive small neurons following A-fiber injury and subsequent pain development. To test this hypothesis, Ki16425 was injected into the DRG of rats at day 7. The results showed that Ki16425 significantly inhibited the upregulation of Cx43 expression (Fig. 6A, C).

These findings suggest that Cx43-mediated coupling between SGCs represents an important molecular mechanism underlying C-fiber sensitization following A-fiber demyelination. Furthermore, the LPA–LPA1R signaling pathway may influence activation among SGCs by regulating Cx43 expression, thereby enabling initially activated SGCs surrounding large neurons to activate SGCs enveloping adjacent small neurons through Cx43-mediated coupling.

To further investigate the role of Cx43 in demyelination-induced neuropathic pain, we tested the effect of the gap junction blocker carbenoxolone (CBX) on pain behavior. The results demonstrated that local DRG injection of CBX significantly alleviated pain behaviors in rats at day 7 (Fig. 6D–F).

LPA1R-mediated release of inflammatory factors by activated SGCs

Previous studies have demonstrated that activated SGCs can enhance neuronal excitability through the release of inflammatory factors, which play a significant role in the development of chronic pain [17, 31]. Based on these findings, this study focused on investigating the effects of LPA1 receptor-mediated SGC activation on inflammatory responses in the DRG of CVI rats. We examined changes in the transcriptional levels and protein expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the DRG of sham, CVI and CVI rats treated with local DRG injections of either minocycline or Ki16425, all at day 7.

ELISA results revealed that both minocycline and Ki16425 significantly reversed the increased protein levels of TNF-α and IL-6 induced by demyelination in the DRG at day 7, with no effect on IL-1β protein levels (Fig. 7A–C). Similarly, qPCR experiments demonstrated that, compared with CVI rats at day 7, both minocycline and Ki16425 treatment groups downregulated the transcriptional levels of Tnf-α and Il-6 in the DRG, without affecting Il-1β expression (Fig. 7D–F). Notably, the administration of glial cell inhibitors led to a significant reduction in TNF-α and IL-6 levels in the DRG, suggesting that these inflammatory cytokines may primarily originate from activated SGCs.

Inhibition of SGC activation reduces demyelination-induced hyperexcitability of C-type neurons

To elucidate the relationship between LPA1R-mediated SGC activation and increased excitability of nociceptive C-type neurons, we employed electrophysiological techniques to compare changes in C-neuron excitability in CVI rats before and after minocycline treatment. The results demonstrated that minocycline significantly reduced the number of action potentials evoked by mechanical stimuli of varying intensities in C-type neurons compared with pre-treatment levels (Fig. 8A–C). These findings establish a direct link between LPA1R-mediated SGC activation and C-neuron sensitization following A-fiber demyelination.

Discussion

Our findings reveal that A-fiber demyelination triggers the activation of LPA signaling in SGCs surrounding demyelinated large DRG neurons. This activation leads to increased cell–cell coupling via Cx43-mediated gap junctions, which in turn activates SGCs surrounding small nociceptive neurons. The resultant sensitization of nociceptive neurons contributes to the development of chronic pain. In support of this, pharmacological inhibition of LPA1R and suppression of SGC activation significantly reduced nociceptive neuron excitability and alleviated pain behaviors. These results underscore the critical role of SGC–neuron interactions in a demyelination model and identify LPA signaling as a promising therapeutic target for treating demyelination-associated neuropathic pain.

We used cobra venom to selectively damage the myelin sheath of A-fibers. The morphological changes in myelin sheaths correlated with the onset of increased pain sensitivity. Complete degradation of the myelin sheath occurred by the seventh day, coinciding with peak mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain. Moreover, demyelination led to upregulated LPA levels in the DRG, concomitant with SGC activation, and increased expression of LPA1R and Cx43. Treatment with Ki16425, a selective antagonist of LPA1/3R, inhibited both SGC activation and Cx43 upregulation. Functionally, both Ki16425 and minocycline reduced the increased excitability of nociceptive neurons following demyelination, with Ki16425 also inhibiting nociceptive inputs to the spinal cord. Additionally, LPA1R inhibition reduced the levels of inflammatory mediators in the DRG and alleviated mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain induced by demyelination.

Cobra venom is rich in PLA2, which catalyzes the breakdown of phosphatidylcholine, a major myelin component, into lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), a precursor to LPA [37, 38]. Seven days after CVI, we detected a robust increase in LPA levels in the DRG, suggesting that LPC generated by myelin breakdown is converted into LPA, likely by autotaxin. LPA may then be released from demyelinated neuronal somata to act on LPA1R in adjacent SGCs, initiating downstream signaling that amplifies neuronal excitability. Consistent with earlier studies, LPA1R expression was predominantly detected in SGCs surrounding large DRG neurons, supporting a neuron-to-SGC signaling mechanism [18, 20]. Although our findings suggest that demyelinated neurons are the primary source of LPA, contributions from other cell types cannot be completely excluded, and further studies are warranted to clarify this issue.

Following demyelination, SGCs associated with large neurons exhibited marked upregulation of LPA1R. Direct administration of Ki16425 to the DRG suppressed SGCs activation, reduced inflammatory mediator levels, dampened nociceptive neuron excitability, and alleviated pain. Although Ki16425 is a dual antagonist of both LPA1 and LPA3 receptors [20, 39], several lines of evidence indicate that LPA1 is the predominant receptor in this context. First, 18:1 LPA—the main isoform in DRGs—shows markedly higher affinity for LPA1R (EC50 ~ 10 nM) than LPA3R (EC50 ~ 100 nM) [40–42], and only 18:1 LPA elicited responses in DRG cultures. Second, single-cell RNA-sequencing demonstrates strong Lpar1 expression in SGCs, while Lpar3 expression is minimal [43]. Third, genetic deletion of Lpar1 significantly attenuates demyelination following sciatic nerve injury [44, 45]. Although Ki16425 exhibits similar Ki values for LPA1R and LPA3R (250 and 350 nM, respectively), recent studies have reported sub-nanomolar KD values for LPA1R [46, 47], emphasizing its high sensitivity to local LPA concentrations within the DRG microenvironment. Together, pharmacological, molecular, and genetic evidence strongly implicates LPA1R as the principal receptor mediating our observed effects. Although the minor potential role of LPA3R cannot be completely excluded, subsequent studies could be designed, for instance Lpar3 knockout animal experiments to improve the mechanism research.

Our study also emphasizes the importance of gap junction-mediated communication among SGCs in sustaining increased pain responses. After CVI, SGCs surrounding demyelinated large neurons may be activated by LPA, enhancing gap junction coupling between SGCs and subsequently triggering the activation of SGCs around small nociceptive neurons. Cx43, the major gap junction protein in SGCs, was significantly upregulated following myelin injury, increasing coupling between SGCs [15, 48]. Prior work by Hanani and colleagues demonstrated that signals can spread from SGCs surrounding injured neurons to those around uninjured neurons via gap junctions, with ATP and other mediators also contributing [15, 49, 50]. Our findings are consistent with these previous findings. Notably, direct Ki16425 administration downregulated Cx43 expression, suggesting that LPA1R activation may regulate connexin levels. LPA1R is known to engage ERK/MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and Rho/ROCK pathways [20, 51–53], all of which can modulate connexin transcription and stability. Therefore, Cx43 upregulation may result either from direct transcriptional regulation via these signaling cascades or indirectly through SGC-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines [50]. Both mechanisms remain plausible and warrant further investigation. Supporting the functional relevance of Cx43, administration of CBX, a gap junction blocker, significantly alleviated pain behaviors, further implicating gap junction-mediated SGC–neuron crosstalk in nociceptor sensitization.

In addition to gap junction signaling, SGCs are activated during inflammation and nerve injury, where they release bioactive substances that increase neuronal excitability and pain sensitivity [54, 55]. ELISA and qPCR results demonstrated a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) in the DRG following myelin injury, whereas IL-1β showed a clear upward trend but did not reach statistical significance, which may simply reflect the relatively small sample size. Overall, TNF-α and IL-6 exhibited more robust inflammatory responses under the current experimental conditions, while the potential contribution of IL-1β warrants further investigation in larger cohorts or different conditions. Importantly, minocycline treatment significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-6 levels, supporting SGCs as a major source of pro-inflammatory mediators in the DRG following demyelination [15, 18, 32]. Correspondingly, electrophysiological recordings and behavioral assays demonstrated that minocycline reduced C-neuron excitability and alleviated pain behaviors. These findings indicate that LPA-induced activation of SGCs promotes cytokine release, which in turn enhances neuronal excitability and contributes to pain sensitization.

Finally, while our study emphasized LPA–LPA1R and Cx43-mediated mechanisms, other signaling pathways may also contribute to demyelination-induced neuropathic pain. Purinergic receptor signaling, particularly P2X7 receptor-mediated neuron–glia communication, has been shown to enhance SGC activation and neuronal excitability, thereby promoting pain hypersensitivity [14, 56]. Compared with purinergic pathways, LPA1R signaling exhibits distinct temporal regulation during demyelination and uniquely modulates Cx43 expression, promoting gap junction–mediated SGC–SGC and SGC–neuron communication. These characteristics distinguish LPA1R from more general purinergic mechanisms and highlight its potential as a selective therapeutic target.

In conclusion, our study indicates that communication between SGCs and neurons in the DRG is a key factor in the development of A-fiber demyelination-induced chronic pain. Specifically, following myelin injury, the increased secretion of LPA from large DRG neurons acts on LPA1R on surrounding SGCs, leading to their activation. This activation enhances coupling between SGCs via gap junctions, which subsequently activates SGCs surrounding small nociceptive neurons. These activated SGCs release inflammatory mediators that increase neuronal excitability, leading to pain sensitization (Fig. 10). These findings offer new insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms in the sensory ganglion that contribute to allodynia, suggesting that targeting LPA1R and disrupting SGC–neuron crosstalk may provide effective strategies to mitigate demyelination-induced chronic pain.

Conclusions

Our study identifies SGC–neuron communication in the DRG as a key driver of A-fiber demyelination-induced neuropathic pain. LPA released after myelin injury activates LPA1R on SGCs, leading to gap junction-mediated coupling and secondary activation of SGCs surrounding nociceptive neurons. These activated SGCs release pro-inflammatory cytokines that enhance neuronal excitability and pain hypersensitivity. Targeting LPA1R and disrupting SGC–neuron crosstalk therefore represent promising strategies for mitigating demyelination-associated chronic pain.
