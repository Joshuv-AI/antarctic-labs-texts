Focus on cell walls
Authors: Anja Geitmann, Antony (Tony) Bacic
Published: 2023

Plant cell walls considered largely inert structural supports were mostly ignored by biologists for centuries and are now recognized as sophisticated biosensors that can modulate plant growth and development and in response to (a)biotic stresses (Geitmann 2024). The cell wall is an integral yet external component of every plant cell. Its components are synthesized in the endomembrane system (noncellulosic polysaccharides and pectins), the plasma membrane (cellulose, callose, and mixed linkage glucans [MLGs]), and within the wall (lignins) and then largely assembled into a complex biphasic biocomposite outside of the plasma membrane in the extracellular milieu (Doblin et al. 2024). The primary wall (deposited during cellular expansion) constitutes mostly a polysaccharide-based extracellular matrix with minor, but important, glycoproteins, whereas most secondary walls (laid down when expansion ceases) also contain significant levels (∼30%) of a heterocyclic hydrophobic polymer, lignin. Depending on cell type, plant species, developmental stage, and environmental conditions, the wall can be as thin as 100 nm and pliable, or it is many micrometers thick and stiffened through a variety of chemical processes linking polymers and macromolecules conveying visco-elastic material properties. In developing tissues, cell walls must be strong enough to resist turgor pressure, yet flexible enough to allow plant growth via cell expansion (Höfte and Vissenberg 2024). In vascular tissues such as xylem, the walls of dead cells must resist negative pressures created by evaporation and compressive stress induced by gravity. The tunability of the cell wall properties as well as the combination of strength and flexibility makes plant cell walls versatile biomaterials and design platforms for complex synthetic composites. Plant cell walls are also an important sink for atmospheric carbon as their synthesis utilizes CO2 captured during photosynthesis. The role of cell walls in the context of human and livestock nutrition; their use for construction and consumables in the form of wood, paper, and fiber; and the possibility of the cell wall's macromolecules to be converted to bioplastics and biofuels highlights the importance of understanding how they are formed and assembled. This focus issue and the accompanying online collection bring together a series of update articles summarizing recent exciting developments in plant sciences and a number of original research papers that illustrate the leading edge of ongoing investigations.

The diversity of wall polysaccharides present in the cell wall is particularly striking between the Poaceae and eudicots. Pancaldi et al. (2023) conclude this is correlated with the profound genomic differentiation in the 150 cell wall gene families by comparing genes/gene families across 169 angiosperm genomes, and use this to infer evolutionary and biological relevance of wall diversification between these lineages. The extracellular matrix of charophytes, the extant group of green algae ancestral and most closely related to land plants, is reviewed by Domozych and LoRicco (2023), providing further insights into their structure and function from genomic/transcriptomic, biochemical, immunochemical, and ecophysiological studies.

The polysaccharides constituting the plant cell wall are assembled from single sugars via their activated nucleotide sugar intermediates. This synthesis requires an array of enzymes that act in concert to synthesize the macromolecule and remodel it during assembly in the endomembrane system or following deposition into the wall. An update article by Hrmova et al. (2023) summarizes how investigations including X-ray crystallography and 3-dimensional (3D) cryo-electron microscopy have generated information on how the CslF6 enzyme can incorporate both (1,3)- and (1,4)-β-linkages into the nascent polysaccharide chain of (1,3; 1,4)-β-glucans. The role of these polysaccharides is particularly prominent in cell walls of commercially relevant cereals and grasses. The synthesis of cellulose has been the focus of numerous studies, and the process is sensitive to an array of environmental conditions. A study testing the effect of phosphate depletion revealed that at reduced levels of the macronutrient, cellulose synthesis is affected, and this is reflected in the thickness and structure of the cell wall (Khan et al. 2023). A synthetic herbicide widely used to interfere with cellulose synthesis is isoxaben. Ogden et al. (2023) revealed that the phenotypes caused by the inhibitor vary with the nutrient status of the plants and the effects are tissue specific. This study highlights the importance of understanding the mechanism of action of cellulose biosynthesis inhibitors, in particular in the fields of plant mechanobiology and cell wall integrity where isoxaben is employed as a tool to induce cell wall stress.

Many of the proteins involved in the biosynthesis and remodeling of the cell wall are posttranslationally modified with lipids. This promotes the interaction of proteins with membranes, but little is known about the contribution of lipid-driven membrane domain localization to protein function in cell wall metabolism. An update article by Quinn et al. (2023) highlights this topic delving into glycosylphosphatidyl-inositol anchors, N-myristylation, prenylation, and S-acylation. Cell wall proteins, which in terms of weight fraction are minor components of walls, play key roles in regulating cell expansion. Papain-type cysteine endopeptidases, CEPs, located in the endoplasmic reticulum process the cell wall scaffolding EXTENSIN (EXTs) proteins, thereby acting as cell wall–weakening agents which are highly expressed in root hairs (Rodríguez-García et al. 2023). Cell wall–modifying proteins are not necessarily enzymes. Expansins are known to modulate cell properties through pH-dependent cell wall loosening which facilitates expansion (Anderson 2024). The situation may be more complex since Samalova et al. (2023) found that EXPANSIN1 (EXPA1) overexpression in the root upregulates cell wall stiffness in the root transition zone. This effect was mediated by changes in the transcription of numerous cell wall-associated genes including several EXPAs and XYLOGLUCAN:XYLOGLUCOSYL TRANSFERASEs (XTHs), and it was associated with rapid pectin methylesterification. The modulation of pectin is an important mechanism for the cell to regulate growth processes in time and space. It is well-known that demethyl esterification is a crucial modification employed by the pollen tube to focus the cellular growth process to the growing tip of the cell (Fayant et al. 2010; Chebli et al. 2012). Chebli and Geitmann (2023) found that pectate lyases may play a dual role in pollen tube growth. On the one hand, they are produced during germination and can be hypothesized to loosen the pollen grain wall to facilitate the process. On the other hand, it was shown that the pollen tube secretes pectate lyases to the external substrate where they might act to digest the apoplast of the invaded stylar tissues in the flower as the tube advances toward its target. Similarly, a sophisticated regulation of pectin appears to be involved in the interaction between parasitic plants and their hosts. The formation of haustoria and the establishment of xylem connections between the parasitic plant Phtheirospermum japonicum and different hosts were found to be associated with tissue-specific changes in the status of pectin methylesterification at the interface between host and parasite, mediated by the upregulation of parasite pectin methylesterases and their inhibitors (Leso et al. 2023). The parasitic plant Cuscuta campestris was shown to finetune the expression of enzymes depending on the response of the host plants to the infection. The study focused on the expression endo-(1,4)-β-mannanase as one of the key players in the process (Bawin et al. 2023). This illustrates the intriguing complexity of the processes happening at the cell wall upon biotic interactions. The complexity and diversity of molecular players is particularly staggering upon infection by phytopathogens. We have an increasingly clearer understanding of how plant cells are alerted to the presence of potentially harmful microorganisms through externally produced microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) and endogenously produced damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)—oligosaccharides that are released through the hydrolysis of plant cell wall polysaccharides by microbial enzymes (Pontiggia et al. 2024; Schneitz and Wolf 2024). Costantini et al. (2024) characterize a member of the Berberine Bridge Enzyme-Like Protein Family which has oxidative activity on DAMPS leading to their inactivation, thereby proposing their involvement in the homeostasis of DAMPs and MAMPs and modulating plant immunity responses. The cell wall is also employed as a physical defense against microorganisms. Callose deposition in response to pathogen invasion is a well-characterized response, but it was unknown how this affects cellulose deposition given that the biosynthesis of both shares the same substrate, UDP-glucose. Liu et al. (2023) found that the balance of callose and cellulose is mediated through the activity of β-1,3-glucanases. Cell wall–located β-1,3-glucanases have also been found to promote cotton fiber cell elongation and secondary cell wall deposition during later stages of trichome development through breakdown of early deposits of callose and recycling of the resulting glucose for the synthesis of cellulose (Fang et al. 2023).

The biochemical composition of the cell wall influences its permeability to water, ions, heavy metals, and a host of other substances both essential to cell functioning and potentially damaging. The speed with which carbon dioxide traverses the cell walls in the leaf mesophyll determines tissue conductance and has a significant impact on photosynthetic efficiency. Similarly, water transport through these walls limits hydraulic conductance. Pathare et al. (2023) found that changes in cell wall composition, specifically hydroxycinnamic acid content, can increase mesophyll conductance and photosynthesis in C3 cereal crops like rice.

Heteroxylans are major components of primary walls of the Poaceae but are found in the secondary walls of all angiosperms where they are a significant contributor to the recalcitrance of the lignocellulosic biomass due to their acetylation. Zhang et al. (2023) show that overexpression of a Populus PtRWA-C (REDUCED WALL ACETYLATION) gene increases the level of xylan acetylation, lignin content, and S/G ratio, resulting in poplar biomass with reduced saccharification potential. They show that PtRWA-C is regulated by both the secondary cell wall hierarchical regulatory network and also by an AP2 family transcription factor HRD, thereby unlocking a mechanism to control the quality of poplar woody biomass for biotechnological applications. Heteroxylans play an entirely different role in the cell wall of the male gametophyte, pollen. The highly sculptured and resistant outer coat of pollen, the exine, is composed primarily of sporopollenin. It transpires that xylan clustering on the mature pollen surface in different plant species is indispensable for the formation of sculpted exine patterns in dicot and monocot plants. Xua et al. (2023) demonstrate that a deficiency in xylan biosynthesis abolished exine patterning on pollen and compromised male fertility.

Walls play crucial roles in seed development. In the aquatic plant Trapa natans, unique woody fruits develop with unusually large seeds that are protected by the inner fruit tissue, the endocarp. Huss et al. (2023) show that hydrolyzable (ester linked) tannins, specifically gallotannins, are deposited into the endocarp tissue during secondary wall formation and are integrated into cell walls along with lignin during maturation. These are proposed to act as defense molecules upon hydrolysis. In the economically important cereal grains, the endosperm is the source of starch and the cell walls are packed with soluble dietary fiber polysaccharides, including the (1,3;1,4)-β-glucans (MLGs) and arabinoxylans. Oat (Avena sativa) grains are a particularly rich source of MLGs, so understanding endosperm cellularization and wall formation will enable manipulation of grain MLG content and grain morphology, the later a key determinant of milling quality. Nadiminti et al. (2023) compared endosperm cellularization and wall polysaccharide deposition in two oat varieties with differing levels of MLG and surprisingly found that MLG was the last polysaccharide synthesized and was deposited in different patterns into the wall between the two varieties.

Plants are subjected to mechanical forces. These forces resolve into tensile forces in primary walls and both tensile and compressive forces in secondary walls which are then transduced through cellulose microfibrils and the matrix phase polysaccharides. Jarvis (2023) reviews the cell wall polymer associations and concludes that cellulose–cellulose interactions appear to have a more important role, and some of the noncellulosic polysaccharides may have a role in keeping cellulose microfibrils apart rather than cross-linking them as previously proposed.

Walls have now demanded the full attention of the plant sciences research community and industry. This focus issue is but a “snippet” of the smörgåsbord of research being conducted by researchers—both fundamental and applied. We forecast the plant wall biology field to continue to flourish, ushering in a new era of intensified research with development with the application of new tools and technologies to meet this challenge.
