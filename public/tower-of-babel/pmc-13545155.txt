Impact of Baseline Polyneuropathy Severity on Eplontersen Efficacy in the NEURO‐TTRansform Clinical Trial
Authors: Thomas H. Brannagan, Markus Weiler, Márcia Waddington Cruz, Sami Khella, John L. Berk, Morie Gertz, Ahmad Masri, Mathew S. Maurer, Jonas Wixner, Jonatan Nåtman, T. Jesse Kwoh, Maksym Pola, Laura Obici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsIn the NEURO‐TTRansform clinical trial (NCT04136184), eplontersen improved neuropathy impairment and quality of life (QoL) through Week 66 versus the NEURO‐TTR historical placebo in patients with hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis with polyneuropathy (ATTRv‐PN). This analysis assessed the impact of baseline ATTRv‐PN severity on eplontersen response in patients from NEURO‐TTRansform.MethodsThis post hoc analysis grouped patients into tertiles by baseline Neuropathy Impairment Score (NIS): T1 (least baseline impairment: 3.5 to < 27.5; n = 67), T2 (27.5 to < 55.0; n = 67), and T3 (55.0 to < 127.8; n = 66). Outcomes assessed were neuropathy impairment (modified NIS+7 [mNIS+7] and NIS, Neuropathy Symptom and Change, Polyneuropathy Disability), QoL (Norfolk QoL‐Diabetic Neuropathy), physical functioning (36‐Item Short‐Form Health Survey Physical Component Summary), nutritional status (modified body mass index), and serum transthyretin levels; these were compared with NEURO‐TTR historical placebo. Autonomic dysfunction (Composite Autonomic Symptom Score‐31), disability (Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale), and walking speed (10‐Meter Walk Test) were also assessed. Final assessments were carried out following 65/66 or 81/85 weeks of treatment.ResultsMean mNIS+7 composite scores were maintained over 85 weeks with eplontersen (changes from baseline of −4.5 [T1], −1.3 [T2], and −2.6 [T3] points). Other disease parameter scores were similarly maintained or improved with eplontersen. Patients receiving placebo experienced disease worsening across outcomes. T1 mean disease scores were typically better than in T2 and T3.InterpretationConsistent, sustained benefits of eplontersen were observed regardless of baseline ATTRv‐PN severity. These findings strengthen the importance of early treatment initiation for patients with ATTRv‐PN across the disease spectrum.

Introduction

Hereditary transthyretin (ATTRv) amyloidosis is a rare, inherited autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic transthyretin (TTR) gene variants [1, 2, 3]. TTR gene variants cause TTR protein misfolding and deposition as insoluble amyloid fibrils in the nerves, heart, eyes, gastrointestinal tract, and kidneys, as well as in other organs and tissues [1, 2, 3, 4]. ATTRv amyloidosis leads to sensorimotor and autonomic neuropathy, as well as other manifestations including cardiomyopathy [1, 2]. If left untreated, ATTRv amyloidosis causes significant disability, impacts quality of life (QoL), and is a progressive and often fatal disorder [1, 2, 4].

Available pharmacotherapies for ATTRv amyloidosis can be categorized into two groups based on their mechanism of action: TTR stabilizers and TTR gene silencers. TTR stabilizers function by binding specifically to TTR tetramers, thereby reducing the propensity of TTR to dissociate into monomers and reducing the accumulation of misfolded TTR protein. TTR gene silencers suppress TTR protein expression at the transcriptional or post‐transcriptional level, thereby attenuating amyloid deposition [5].

Eplontersen is an antisense oligonucleotide (TTR gene silencer) approved in multiple regions, including the United States, Canada, Europe, and the United Kingdom, for use in adults with hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis with polyneuropathy (ATTRv‐PN) [6, 7, 8, 9]. The efficacy and tolerability of eplontersen in patients with ATTRv‐PN were demonstrated to Week 65/66 in the NEURO‐TTRansform trial (NCT04136184) compared with a historical placebo group from the NEURO‐TTR trial (NCT01737398) [10]. At Week 65 in NEURO‐TTRansform, eplontersen led to a reduction in serum TTR of 82% versus 11% with historical placebo [10], as well as stabilization or improvements in other efficacy endpoints, including the modified Neuropathy Impairment Score + 7 (mNIS+7) composite score, Norfolk QoL‐Diabetic Neuropathy (Norfolk QoL‐DN) total score, Neuropathy Symptom and Change (NSC) total score, and modified body mass index (mBMI) [10]. These benefits were sustained through Week 85 in NEURO‐TTRansform [11].

Previous analyses of the APOLLO trial of patisiran and the HELIOS‐A trial of vutrisiran have shown that baseline polyneuropathy severity can impact the trajectory of treatment outcomes, in terms of neurologic function, QoL, and other functional assessments, in patients with ATTRv‐PN [12, 13]. The objective of the post hoc analysis reported here was to assess the impact of baseline polyneuropathy severity on eplontersen treatment response in patients with ATTRv‐PN from the NEURO‐TTRansform clinical trial.

Materials and Methods

Trial Design

The full methodological details and results of NEURO‐TTRansform and the switch analyses have been published elsewhere [10, 11, 14, 15, 16]. Briefly, the global, multicenter, open‐label, single‐group, Phase 3 NEURO‐TTRansform trial assessed the efficacy and safety of eplontersen 45 mg, administered subcutaneously every 4 weeks through Week 81, with an end‐of‐treatment evaluation at Week 85 (4 weeks after the last dose) [10, 11, 14, 15, 16]. The study design is illustrated in Figure S1. The NEURO‐TTRansform trial protocol was approved by local institutional review boards or ethics committees. NEURO‐TTRansform adhered to the International Council for Harmonisation guidelines, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before enrolment [14].

A historical placebo group was derived from the Week‐66 endpoint of the randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, Phase 3 inotersen trial in ATTRv‐PN (NEURO‐TTR). The inclusion criteria and endpoints of NEURO‐TTRansform were aligned with those of the NEURO‐TTR clinical trial to allow for comparisons with its prespecified historical placebo arm [14, 17]. In NEURO‐TTRansform, a small reference group (randomized 1:6) initially received inotersen 300 mg weekly during Weeks 1–34, then switched to eplontersen from Weeks 37–81 [10, 14, 16]. This design allowed comparisons to be made with outcomes from NEURO‐TTR, and any differences in patient characteristics or response to be identified [10, 14].

Analyses of the NEURO‐TTRansform key efficacy endpoints were conducted at three time points: an interim analysis at Week 35/37; a final analysis at Week 65/66 (distributed over two visits, marking the end of the historical placebo‐controlled comparison from NEURO‐TTR); and an end‐of‐treatment analysis at Week 81/85 [10, 14].

Patients

Key inclusion criteria for enrolment in NEURO‐TTRansform were: adults aged 18–82 years; a documented genetic mutation in the TTR gene; diagnosis of Coutinho Stage 1 (ambulatory without assistance) or 2 (ambulatory with assistance) disease; and signs and symptoms consistent with ATTRv‐PN including a baseline Neuropathy Impairment Score of ≥ 10 to ≤ 130 points. Key exclusion criteria were: other causes of sensorimotor or autonomic neuropathy; Karnofsky performance status ≤ 50%; New York Heart Association functional classification ≥ 3; current or prior treatment with inotersen, patisiran, or other antisense oligonucleotide or RNA therapeutics; and current treatment with tafamidis, diflunisal, or doxycycline [14].

Outcome Measures

This post hoc subgroup analysis of NEURO‐TTRansform evaluated changes from baseline or percentage changes from baseline in the study's primary efficacy endpoints and several secondary efficacy endpoints. Patients were divided into tertiles (T) 1–3 based on their NEURO‐TTRansform baseline NIS score: T1: NIS score of 3.5 to < 27.5 (least baseline impairment); T2: NIS score of 27.5 to < 55.0; T3: NIS score of 55.0 to < 127.8 (most baseline impairment). Based on the total numbers of patients included in the primary analysis, each NIS tertile group consisted of 66–67 patients (eplontersen and placebo recipients). Patients from the inotersen switch group were excluded from this analysis.

NEURO‐TTRansform Primary Endpoints

Changes from baseline to Week 85 were measured for serum TTR concentration (g/L) and reported as mean percentage change. Serum TTR concentrations from NEURO‐TTR (measured using a prealbumin assay via an immunoturbidometric method) were adjusted to allow comparison with data generated using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay in NEURO‐TTRansform [10]. Changes from baseline to Week 85 were measured for the mNIS+7 composite score (range, −22.3 to 346.3) and the Norfolk QoL‐DN questionnaire total score (range, −4 to 136) and were reported as mean changes. Higher mNIS+7 and Norfolk QoL‐DN scores indicate worsening neuropathy and poorer QoL, respectively [10, 18, 19]. These endpoints were compared with the historical placebo group from the NEURO‐TTR trial as a control; historical placebo was measured to Week 65/66 only [14, 17].

NEURO‐TTRansform Secondary Endpoints

Changes from baseline or percentage change from baseline to Week 85 were measured for: NSC total score; mBMI (measured as BMI [kg/m2] × serum albumin [g/L]); the 36‐Item Short‐Form Health Survey (SF‐36) Physical Component Summary (PCS) score; and the NIS composite score. These endpoints used historical placebo from NEURO‐TTR as a control; historical placebo was measured to Week 65/66 only [14, 17]. Change from baseline in the Polyneuropathy Disability (PND) score was measured to Week 65 and compared with historical placebo from the NEURO‐TTR trial. Change in PND score was presented as the proportion of patients with improved, unchanged, or worsened scores from baseline. Three endpoints were measured as changes from baseline to Week 81: the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score‐31 (COMPASS‐31); the 24‐item Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (R‐ODS); and the 10‐Meter Walk Test (10MWT). Changes from baseline for these endpoints for this analysis by tertiles were not placebo‐controlled. In addition, changes from baseline to Week 81 were analyzed for R‐ODS and 10MWT for the overall NEURO‐TTRansform population and compared with the historical placebo group from the APOLLO clinical trial of patisiran [20, 21]. Scoring and interpretation of secondary endpoints can be found in the footnotes to Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics and assessment scores were summarized descriptively by treatment group and baseline NIS tertile. Post hoc analyses were performed in the full analysis set (excluding patients who switched from inotersen), comprising all patients who received at least one dose of trial medication (eplontersen or historical placebo) and who had a baseline and at least one post‐baseline mNIS+7 or Norfolk QoL‐DN assessment [14]. No formal statistical calculations were performed.

Results

Patient Demographics

NIS T1 included 67 patients (eplontersen n = 49, placebo n = 18), T2 included 67 patients (eplontersen n = 45, placebo n = 22), and T3 included 66 patients (eplontersen n = 47, placebo n = 19). Patient demographics for each NIS tertile are presented in Table 1. Across the NIS tertiles, mean age was 5–8 years lower among patients receiving eplontersen compared with those in the historical placebo group. Patients were predominantly male (67%–72%) and White (68%–91%). The most common TTR gene variant was Val30Met (p.Val50Met). Across the NIS tertiles, patients receiving eplontersen had longer polyneuropathy symptom duration than those in the historical placebo group (3.5–10 months longer). Furthermore, there was a trend of a longer polyneuropathy symptom duration from NIS T1 (least baseline NIS impairment) to T3 (most baseline NIS impairment) for both eplontersen and the historical placebo group. Patients in NIS T3 had the shortest time from polyneuropathy diagnosis to treatment initiation (21.0–22.0 months) compared with patients in NIS T1 and NIS T2 (30.0–38.5 months). With the exception of the historical placebo group in NIS T1, the majority of patients in each subgroup had previous treatment with either tafamidis or diflunisal. At baseline, the majority of patients had a PND score of I–II; no patients in NIS T1 had a PND score of IIIa or higher, and no patients had a PND score of IV (Table S1).

mNIS+7, Norfolk QoL‐DN, and NCS Scores, and mBMI, by Baseline NIS Tertile

Within each NIS tertile, mean baseline values for mNIS+7, Norfolk QoL‐DN, NSC, and mBMI were generally comparable between the eplontersen and historical placebo groups. Mean scores at baseline for mNIS+7, Norfolk QoL‐DN, and NSC increased with increasing baseline NIS (T1 to T3) with both eplontersen and historical placebo, while mean scores decreased for mBMI (Table S1). Mean mNIS+7 composite scores were maintained over 85 weeks with eplontersen in all NIS tertiles (changes from baseline of −4.5 [T1], −1.3 [T2], and −2.6 [T3] points); scores increased over 66 weeks with historical placebo (Figure 1A). Mean Norfolk QoL‐DN total scores improved over 85 weeks with eplontersen in NIS T1 and T2 and were maintained in T3 (changes from baseline of −5.3 [T1], −11.8 [T2], and −1.3 [T3] points); scores increased over 66 weeks with historical placebo (Figure 1B). Mean NSC total scores were maintained over 85 weeks with eplontersen in all NIS tertiles (changes from baseline of −1.9 [T1], −0.8 [T2], and 0.2 [T3] points); scores increased over 66 weeks with historical placebo (Figure 1C). Mean mBMI scores were maintained over 85 weeks with eplontersen in all NIS tertiles (changes from baseline of 20.2 [T1], −11.1 [T2], and −3.3 [T3] points); scores decreased over 65 weeks with historical placebo (Figure 1D).

COMPASS‐31, SF‐36 PCS, and NIS by Baseline NIS Tertile

Mean baseline values for COMPASS‐31 were within similar ranges across NIS tertiles. Mean SF‐36 scores at baseline decreased with increasing baseline NIS (T1 to T3), and mean NIS composite scores increased with increasing baseline NIS with both eplontersen and historical placebo (Table S1).

Mean COMPASS‐31 scores were maintained over 81 weeks with eplontersen in T1 and decreased in T2 and T3 (changes from baseline of −0.9 [T1], −3.6 [T2], and −3.5 [T3] points) (Figure 2A). Mean SF‐36 PCS scores were maintained over 85 weeks with eplontersen in T1 and T3 and tended to increase in T2 (changes from baseline of 0.9 [T1], 2.7 [T2] and 1.6 [T3] points); scores were maintained (T1 and T3) or decreased (T2) over 65 weeks with historical placebo (Figure 2B). Mean NIS composite scores were maintained over 85 weeks with eplontersen in all NIS tertiles (changes from baseline of −1.0 [T1], 2.9 [T2], and 0.8 [T3] points); scores increased over 66 weeks with historical placebo (Figure 2C).

PND by Baseline NIS Tertile

At baseline and at Week 65, the proportions of patients in the lower PND stages decreased with increasing baseline NIS (T1 to T3) with both eplontersen and historical placebo (Table S1). In all NIS tertiles, the proportion of patients whose PND score worsened from baseline to Week 65 was lower with eplontersen than with historical placebo, and PND score worsening correlated with NIS baseline severity. The proportion of patients with no change/improvement was higher with eplontersen than with historical placebo (Figure 3).

Serum TTR by Baseline NIS Tertile

Mean baseline serum TTR levels did not vary with increasing baseline NIS (T1 to T3) for either eplontersen or historical placebo (Table S1). Mean serum TTR levels decreased by ≥ 80% by Week 35 with eplontersen; these levels were maintained to Week 85. Levels decreased between 5% and 16% to Week 35 with historical placebo; at Week 65, these lower levels were maintained in tertiles T1 and T2 but had returned to approximate baseline levels in T3 (Figure S2).

R‐ODS and 10MWT in Overall NEURO‐TTRansform Population and by Baseline NIS Tertile

In the overall population of patients treated with eplontersen in NEURO‐TTRansform, R‐ODS remained stable compared with baseline at both Weeks 37 and 81. In patients randomized to historical placebo in the APOLLO clinical trial, R‐ODS deteriorated to Weeks 39 and 78 (Figure S3A). 10MWT results in the overall population improved from baseline to Weeks 37 and 81 for both fast and comfortable walking speeds in patients treated with eplontersen, while in the APOLLO historical placebo group, walking speed (test type unspecified) declined to Week 78 (Figure S3B).

Mean R‐ODS and 10MWT results at baseline and Week 81 decreased with increasing baseline NIS (T1 to T3) (Table S1). Mean R‐ODS scores were maintained over 81 weeks with eplontersen in T1 and T2 but tended to decrease in T3 (changes from baseline of 0.3 [T1], 0.1 [T2], and −1.8 [T3] points) (Figure S4A). Mean 10MWT results were maintained (comfortable and fast speeds) over 81 weeks with eplontersen across tertiles (changes from baseline of 0.05 [T1], 0.03 [T2], and −0.06 [T3] points [comfortable] and 0.08 [T1], 0.11 [T2], and −0.08 [T3] points [fast]) (Figure S4B).

Discussion

This was a post hoc analysis of the impact of baseline polyneuropathy severity on eplontersen treatment response in patients with ATTRv‐PN from the NEURO‐TTRansform clinical trial. Across NIS tertiles, benefits were seen with eplontersen treatment from baseline to Week 65/66 compared with placebo for all outcomes for which placebo data were available. Patients in T1 (least NIS impairment at baseline) typically had better baseline scores across outcome measures than patients in T2 and T3.

Consistent and sustained benefits of eplontersen treatment in the degree of neuropathy impairment (mNIS+7, NIS, NSC, and PND), QoL (Norfolk QoL‐DN), functioning (SF‐36 PCS), and nutritional status (mBMI) were observed from baseline to Week 85, regardless of baseline peripheral neuropathy severity. Improvements with eplontersen were also typically seen from baseline to Week 81 in autonomic dysfunction (COMPASS‐31). R‐ODS and 10MWT score maintenance to Week 81 contrasted with deterioration with historical placebo in the APOLLO trial [20]. Serum TTR reduction with eplontersen was similar across NIS tertiles. For all measures in this analysis, with the exception of COMPASS‐31 and serum TTR levels, patients in T3 (most NIS impairment at baseline) continued to have more severe disease scores than patients in T1 and T2 at the end of the analysis period (Week 81/85). However, the similar disease trajectories from baseline to Week 81/85 between tertile groups suggest that eplontersen is efficacious in controlling the progression of ATTRv‐PN, regardless of baseline polyneuropathy severity.

Similar findings to those of this analysis have been observed in the APOLLO trial of patisiran and the HELIOS‐A trial of vutrisiran (both 18 months of follow‐up) [12, 13]. In APOLLO, patisiran demonstrated benefit versus placebo across baseline polyneuropathy (NIS) severity quartiles, with improvements or stabilization observed in measures of neuropathy impairment, functioning, and QoL at 18 months; patients receiving placebo showed worsening across these outcomes, regardless of baseline NIS severity [12]. Similarly, in HELIOS‐A, vutrisiran demonstrated benefit versus placebo across all baseline polyneuropathy severity quartiles for neurologic impairment, disability, gait speed, QoL, and nutritional status at 18 months; patients receiving placebo showed progressive worsening across all assessed endpoints at 18 months, again irrespective of baseline neuropathy severity [13]. In both studies, patients with more advanced neuropathy at the start of treatment (Quartiles 3 and 4) generally did not regain the same level of function as those who began therapy earlier in their disease course [12, 13]. A retrospective cohort study of tafamidis in patients with ATTRv‐PN has shown that those with mild neurological impairment were significantly more likely to become treatment responders in a univariate analysis of baseline outcome predictors [23]. In addition, an analysis of NIS‐lower limbs (NIS‐LL) data from a clinical trial of tafamidis found that baseline NIS‐LL severity was a significant predictor of disease progression in patients with ATTR‐PN [24]. Taken together, these studies demonstrate the importance of early intervention for patients with ATTRv‐PN.

Estimates of meaningful improvements and deterioration have been proposed for the mNIS+7 composite, Norfolk QoL‐DN total, and NSC total scores, for mBMI, and for COMPASS‐31 and R‐ODS [18, 25]. In our analysis, patients receiving eplontersen in T1 met or exceeded the threshold for clinically meaningful improvement from baseline to study end on all six instruments; patients in T2 met or exceeded thresholds for meaningful improvement on the Norfolk QoL‐DN total, COMPASS‐31, and R‐ODS; and patients in T3 met or exceeded thresholds for meaningful improvement on COMPASS‐31 (Table S2). Patients receiving placebo experienced clinically meaningful deterioration on the mNIS+7 composite, Norfolk QoL‐DN total, NSC total, and for mBMI across tertiles; the only exception was on the Norfolk QoL‐DN for patients in T1, where the change in score from baseline to study end, although increased, did not meet the threshold for meaningful difference (Table S2). Overall, these data suggest that patients with the least impairment at baseline who started eplontersen earlier in their disease course are more likely than patients with more severe disease to experience clinically meaningful treatment benefits. However, benefits were still observed in patients receiving eplontersen in T2 and T3.

Limitations of this analysis include the lack of historical placebo at Week 85 for all outcome measures, and throughout for the COMPASS‐31, R‐ODS, and 10MWT measures, the relatively small sample sizes compared with the parent study, and the post hoc nature of the analysis which did not allow for statistical analysis of the results, and which means that score changes (improvement vs. maintenance vs. deterioration) were interpreted by clinical judgment. As this study was not randomized, some differences in baseline factors between subgroups could have impacted the results (e.g., patients receiving eplontersen were on average younger than those in the historical placebo group but had longer peripheral neuropathy symptom duration before treatment initiation). As there were no patients with a baseline PND score of IV in this analysis, further evidence is required to confirm findings in this group.

The clinical implications of this analysis, especially taken together with other studies, highlight the importance of early diagnosis and treatment initiation for patients with ATTRv‐PN, regardless of baseline peripheral neuropathy severity. Future directions include a need for prospective studies stratified by baseline severity, as well as real‐world data and long‐term follow‐up to confirm findings.
