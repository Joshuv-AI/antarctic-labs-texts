A Computed Tomography Radiomics Approach for Assessing the T Cell‐Inflamed Gene Expression Profile in Hepatocellular Carcinoma and Its Association With Immunotherapy Response
Authors: Mimi Tang, Kaiyu Sun, Dasheng Wu, Yujian Zou, Jian Zhou, Bin Li, Huanjing Hu, Zhenpeng Peng, Jifei Wang, Bingsheng Huang, Shi‐Ting Feng, Shuling Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe T cell‐inflamed gene expression profile (GEP) provides a biomarker for immunotherapy response across various cancers. However, specific biomarkers for immunotherapy response in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are lacking. In this study, we established a computed tomography (CT)‐based radiomics model to predict the T cell‐inflamed GEP and immunotherapy response in HCC.MethodsThis retrospective, multicenter study included 270 patients with HCC, who were divided into training (n = 227) and test sets (n = 43). All included patients had available contrast‐enhanced CT and RNA sequencing data. The T cell‐inflamed GEP consisted of 18 genes derived from RNA sequencing data, and patients were classified into GEP‐high and GEP‐low groups. A support vector machine was used to establish the radiomics model. The immunotherapy dataset comprised patients who underwent contrast‐enhanced CT and received anti‐programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death ligand 1 immunotherapy at three hospitals (209 lesions). This immunotherapy dataset was used to evaluate the association between the predicted GEP status and immunotherapy response. Additionally, overall survival was compared between patients with and without predicted GEP‐high lesions in the immunotherapy dataset.ResultsThe areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of the radiomics model for predicting GEP in the training and test sets were 0.824 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.757–0.892) and 0.827 (95% CI: 0.674–0.980), respectively. In the immunotherapy dataset, the response rate of liver lesions at 6 months was significantly higher in the GEP‐high group than in the GEP‐low group (48.1% vs. 9.3%, p < 0.001). The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve of the radiomics model for predicting immunotherapy response at 6 months was 0.750 (95% CI: 0.614–0.886). Median overall survival among patients with GEP‐high lesions was 17.5 months, which notably exceeded the value of 8.3 months for patients without GEP‐high lesions.ConclusionsThis study provides the first CT‐based radiomics model for predicting the T cell‐inflamed GEP in HCC. This model highlights the potential of the T cell‐inflamed GEP as a noninvasive biomarker to help guide immunotherapy selection.Keywords: biomarker, computed tomography, hepatocellular carcinoma, immunotherapy, radiomics, T cell‐inflamed gene expression profile

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for approximately 90% of liver cancers [1]. Globally, liver cancers represent the fourth leading cause of cancer‐related death [2]. It is estimated that 50%–60% of patients with HCC will require systemic therapies during their lifespan [1]. Immunotherapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic option for HCC. Treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has achieved overall survival (OS) of 16.4 months in the first‐line setting, as evidenced by the CheckMate 040 and KEYNOTE‐224 trials [3, 4, 5]. However, fewer than 30% of patients with HCC respond to ICIs. Therefore, there is an urgent need for effective methods to identify patients most likely to benefit from immunotherapy.

Programmed cell death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression, CD8+ T cell infiltration, microsatellite instability (MSI), and the tumor mutational burden (TMB) are reportedly associated with the response to ICIs [6, 7, 8, 9]. Nevertheless, these biomarkers only provide limited insight into the biology of the tumor–immune interface. For example, PD‐L1 expression might reflect only a component of T cell‐related biology that is correlated with a positive immune microenvironment. The T cell‐inflamed gene expression profile (GEP), which is related to antigen presentation, cytotoxic activity, chemokine expression, and adaptive immune response, can be used to evaluate the complexities of tumor and host immune cell interactions within the tumor–immune microenvironment [10]. It has been widely accepted as an effective biomarker for predicting clinical response to ICIs across various cancers. Higher response rates and longer progression‐free survival have been demonstrated in tumors with a higher T cell‐inflamed GEP [9, 10, 11]. However, the T cell‐inflamed GEP is based on the mRNA expression of 18 immune‐related genes isolated from tumor tissues obtained via surgery or biopsy. Given the invasive nature of these procedures and the infeasibility of multiple biopsies over the course of the disease, it is particularly important to develop a noninvasive method to assess the T cell‐inflamed GEP in patients with HCC. This would facilitate the pretreatment prediction of patients who could benefit from ICI therapy and permit the longitudinal evaluation of changes in the tumor–immune microenvironment associated with immunotherapy response.

Several studies have reported that radiomics features can be used to predict tumor gene expression, pathological classification, molecular subtyping, or treatment response [12, 13, 14, 15]. A radiomics signature for CD8+ T cells has emerged as a promising imaging biomarker for predicting the immune phenotype of tumors and inferring clinical outcomes for ICI‐treated patients [12]. However, this study was a pan‐cancer analysis featuring only nine patients with HCC. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically focused on the efficacy of radiomics computed tomography (CT) for assessing the T cell‐inflamed GEP in HCC.

In this study, we developed and validated a radiomics model for predicting the T cell‐inflamed GEP score based on pretreatment CT images, and the model's ability to predict immunotherapy response was evaluated in patients with HCC treated with anti‐programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) immunotherapy.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Cohorts

This study was approved by the Human Research Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat‐sen University (approval number: [2021]244), Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center (approval number: B2025‐782‐01), and Dongguan People's Hospital (approval number: KYKT2022‐069‐C1) and was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of this study.

This retrospective multicenter study consisted of two steps (Figure 1, and Supporting Figure S1).

First, patients with HCC who underwent contrast‐enhanced CT (CECT) before hepatectomy were included in the training dataset. We performed RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) on tumor tissues to analyze the T cell‐inflamed GEP, which consisted of the following genes: TIGIT, CD27, CD8A, PDCD1LG2(PD‐L2), LAG3, CD274(PD‐L1), CXCL6, CMKLR1, NKG7, CCL5, PSMB10, IDO1, CXCL9, HLA‐DQA1, CD276, STAT1, HLA‐DRB1, and HLA‐E. Based on the GEP score cutoff from previous research [10], patients were categorized into the GEP‐high (GEPhi) and GEP‐low (GEPlo) groups. A radiomics model based on CECT was developed to predict the T cell‐inflamed GEP in the training dataset and validated using a dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Second, this model was confirmed via application to patients with HCC who received anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 immunotherapy.

To develop the radiomics model in the first phase, we included patients with histologically diagnosed HCC who underwent CECT within 1 month prior to hepatectomy, which was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat‐sen University (FAHSYSU) between May 2018 and May 2020. The exclusion criteria were as follows (Supporting Figure S2): prior anticancer treatment before hepatectomy; presence of other malignancies; extrahepatic metastasis; multiple tumor lesions; lack of preserved snap‐frozen tumor tissue; and unqualified snap‐frozen specimens for RNA‐seq. To validate the radiomics model, patients with histologically confirmed HCC who underwent CECT between November 1992 and November 2006 were included from the TCGA database via the Genomic Data Commons (https://gdc.cancer.gov/), and corresponding CT images were retrieved from The Cancer Imaging Archive (https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/; Supporting Figure S2). Patients with multiple tumor lesions and those who received other locoregional therapies were excluded. Finally, the training dataset included 227 patients (227 lesions), and the test dataset included 43 patients (43 lesions), with all patients having available CECT and RNA‐seq data. The immunotherapy dataset included patients with histologically or radiologically diagnosed HCC who received anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 and underwent CECT within 1 month prior to immunotherapy at FAHSYSU, Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center, and Dongguan People's Hospital between January 2019 and January 2021 (Supporting Figure S2). Patients without measurable liver lesions and those who received other anticancer treatments within 6 months after immunotherapy were excluded. The immunotherapy dataset consisted of 40 patients with 209 evaluable tumor lesions.

RNA‐seq and Gene Expression Profiling

Details about RNA sequencing and gene expression profiling are provided in the Supporting Materials.

CECT Protocol

All patients in the training, test, and immunotherapy datasets underwent pretreatment CECT using one of the CT scanners listed in Supporting Table S1.

Radiomics Preprocessing, Feature Extraction, and Model Building

Whole‐tumor segmentation was manually performed on portal venous phase CECT images by two independent radiologists. In total, 1130 quantitative radiomics features, including shape, first‐order, and texture features, were extracted from each segmented lesion using the pyradiomics package (version 3.0.1). Feature robustness was assessed by calculating the interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). For feature selection, we first identified features that were significantly different between the GEPhi and GEPlo groups and then applied a decision tree algorithm to select the most informative features. Finally, a support vector machine (SVM) classifier was employed to build the radiomics model for predicting the T cell‐inflamed GEP status. Detailed methodological descriptions are provided in the Supporting Materials.

Immunotherapy Dataset

The radiomics model for GEP prediction developed from the training dataset and validated in the test datasets was directly applied to pretreatment CECT images in the immunotherapy dataset. Lesions were categorized into the predicted GEPhi or predicted GEPlo group based on the radiomics signature. The difference in treatment response rates between these two groups was evaluated. As the previous model was built based on a single lesion and in consideration of the possibility of mixed responses from different lesions to immunotherapy, we compared each lesion's relative change in diameter between baseline and follow‐up images according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 criteria at 3 and 6 months [16]. An objective response was defined as the achievement of a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), whereas no response was defined as stable disease or progressive disease. Tumor response assessments were independently performed on target lesions by two experienced radiologists (Shi‐Ting Feng and Zhenpeng Peng, both with 20 years of experience in liver imaging), who were blinded to the patients' clinical information. Any discrepancies in assessment results were resolved by consensus. For patient‐level classification, a patient was assigned to the GEPhi group if at least one lesion was predicted to be GEPhi; otherwise, the patient was classified as GEPlo.

The immunotherapy regimens consisted of either monotherapy with an anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 agent or combination therapy with an anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 agent plus lenvatinib/bevacizumab. Specifically, 10 patients received ICI monotherapy, whereas the remaining 30 patients received combination therapy. The agents used included anti‐PD‐1 monoclonal antibodies (pembrolizumab, sintilimab, or tislelizumab) and an anti‐PD‐L1 monoclonal antibody (atezolizumab). The regimen (monotherapy vs. combination) and the specific agent were selected by the treating physician based on standard clinical guidelines and the patient's individual condition. Patients were followed up after every two cycles of immunotherapy during the treatment period and then every 3 months after the end of treatment, or earlier if clinically indicated. OS was defined as the interval between the date of initiation of immunotherapy and that of death or the last follow‐up. Follow‐up was censored on February 25, 2024.

Radiomics‐Based Clinical Model Development

The differences in clinical data between the GEPhi and GEPlo groups in the training dataset were compared. Clinical features exhibiting significant differences were integrated with the radiomics score (Rad score) to construct a combined radiomics‐based clinical model. Clinical variables significant at p < 0.1 were included in the multivariable logistic regression model.

Statistical Analysis

The independent‐samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to compare clinical characteristics and the radiomics signature between the GEPhi and GEPlo groups for continuous variables. The chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used for between‐group comparisons of categorical variables. Variables exhibiting a potential association with the GEP status (p < 0.1 in univariable between‐group comparisons) were selected as candidates. These variables, along with the Rad score, were entered simultaneously into a multivariable logistic regression model using the forced‐entry method to build the combined radiomics‐based clinical model. The consistency of the volume of interest selected by the two radiologists was assessed using the ICC [17], with ICC ≥ 0.8 denoting high consistency, ICC = 0.5–0.79 denoting moderate consistency, and ICC < 0.5 denoting low consistency. The Delong test was used to test whether the predictive performance of these two models was statistically different. OS curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. The median follow‐up was calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Because the censoring proportion was low, the restricted mean follow‐up duration was also calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM, Armonk, United States) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, United States). A two‐sided significance level of 0.05 was used.

Radiomics Feature Selection

Of the 1130 radiomics features extracted from the training dataset, 576 features that significantly differed between the GEPhi and GEPlo groups were selected for further analysis. Subsequently, seven features were chosen using a decision tree with a maximum depth of 3. The Gini index was used to measure the contribution degree of each feature, and features with contribution degree > 0 were selected. All selected features were texture features. Details of these features are listed in Supporting Table S4, and their heatmaps are presented in Supporting Figure S3.

Interobserver Reproducibility of Radiomics Feature Extraction

The median interobserver ICC for the 1130 radiomics features was 0.998. The interobserver ICC was ≥ 0.8 for 95.04% of the features, between 0.5 and 0.79 for 4.43% of the features, and < 0.5 for 0.53% of the features. The seven selected features also demonstrated robust reproducibility, with interobserver ICCs ranging from 0.849 to 1.000 (all p < 0.001).

Radiomics Model Development and Validation

The radiomics model for GEP was developed using an SVM classifier on the training dataset and subsequently applied to the test dataset. Each patient's Rad score in the training and test cohorts is presented in Figure 2a,b, respectively. The cutoff determined by the maximum Youden index was 0.255. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the training and testing datasets are shown in Figure 2c,d, respectively. In the training cohort, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.824 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.757–0.892), with accuracy of 80.2%, sensitivity of 74.6%, and specificity of 82.5%. In the test cohort, the AUC was 0.827 (95% CI: 0.674–0.980), with accuracy of 69.8%, sensitivity of 88.9%, and specificity of 64.7%. The calibration curves for the model in both the training and test cohorts were aligned with the standard lines (Figure 2e,f).

Radiomics‐Based Clinical Model Development and Comparison with the Radiomics Model

Among all clinical variables and the Rad score, only the Rad score emerged as an independent predictor of the GEP status (p < 0.001; Table 2). The ROC curve for the radiomics‐based clinical model in predicting GEP is presented in Supporting Figure S4a. The AUC for this model was 0.831 (95% CI: 0.766–0.896). There was no significant difference in the AUC between the radiomics‐based clinical model and the radiomics model (p = 0.772). The calibration curve for the radiomics‐based clinical model in the training cohort was aligned with the standard lines (Supporting Figure S4b). Decision curve analysis indicated that the radiomics‐based clinical model did not achieve a higher overall net benefit than the radiomics model, suggesting the high performance of the radiomics model in predicting GEP (Supporting Figure S4c).

In the analysis of liver lesions within the immunotherapy dataset, 34.2% of the lesions (n = 39) were classified into the GEPhi group. After 3 months of immunotherapy, 28.2% of these lesions responded, versus 16.0% of lesions in the predicted GEPlo group (p = 0.123). Given the significantly different response rates of liver lesions at 6 months after immunotherapy between the predicted GEPhi and GEPlo groups, we further evaluated the performance of the predictive GEPhi group in predicting liver lesion response rates. Discussion

With the increasingly recognized role of immunotherapy in the treatment of HCC, effective pretreatment biomarkers for identifying potential responders are essential. In our study, we integrated RNA transcriptomic data with CECT images to develop a radiomics model to predict the T cell‐inflamed GEP status in patients with HCC. This model was validated using an external test dataset (TCGA cohort), and it displayed potential in predicting the immunotherapy response among patients treated with anti‐PD‐1 therapy in a clinical cohort.

The use of immunotherapy has led to a paradigm shift in HCC management, but the response rate remains relatively low [18]. Many biomarkers, such as PD‐L1, TMB, and MSI, have not proven predictive of treatment response. The T cell‐inflamed GEP, which includes genes closely related to antigen presentation, cytotoxic activity, chemokine expression, and adaptive immune response, provides a comprehensive evaluation of tumor microenvironment features [10]. It has been utilized as a biomarker for predicting ICI responsiveness in multiple large‐scale clinical trials [9, 10, 11]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to establish a CT radiomics model for predicting T cell‐inflamed GEP in HCC. In our study, the Rad score consisted of several textural features that quantified the complexity of the texture (original_glcm_Imc1), the homogeneity or heterogeneity of the tumor image (log‐sigma‐1‐0‐mm‐3D_glcm_Idn, original_gldm_DependenceEntropy, log‐sigma‐1‐0‐mm‐3D_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized), and the distribution of the different gray levels (wavelet‐LHH_glszm_SmallAreaLowGrayLevelEmphasis, wavelet‐HLH_glrlm_HighGrayLevelRunEmphasis, log‐sigma‐1‐0‐mm‐3D_glrlm_LongRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis), which are increasingly recognized as imaging features of the underlying histological diversity, including variations in cellular density, necrosis, stromal content, and vascularity. Based on these features, we established an effective model for predicting the T cell‐inflamed GEP in HCC. Previous studies explored the relationship between CT texture features and the tumor microenvironment. In Sun's study [12], homogeneous tumors, lower CT density, and peripheral rings reflected a high CD8+ T cell score in various advanced solid tumors. In Trebeschi et al.'s study [19], advanced melanoma and non‐small‐cell lung cancer with more nonuniform morphological profiles, heterogeneous density patterns, and compact borders were more likely to respond to immunotherapy. As an SVM classifier was used in the modeling in our study, it was challenging to clarify the monotonicity between features and the T cell‐inflamed GEP, making it difficult to intuitively understand their relationship [20]. However, the AUC for the external test set was similar to that in the training dataset, supporting the performance of the model.

Our study sought to connect CT‐based radiomics features with RNA transcriptomic profiles in patients with HCC and successfully demonstrated that radiomics can effectively predict the response to immunotherapy in routine clinical practice. Although six studies have investigated the use of radiologic or histologic imaging features as biomarkers to predict immune infiltration or immune expression signatures in HCC [14, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], none evaluated the potential of these imaging biomarkers to directly predict immunotherapy response and patient survival. Zeng et al. developed and validated artificial intelligence‐based pathology models to predict the activation of several immune and inflammatory gene signatures in patients with HCC and achieved high performance (AUC = 0.81–0.92) [22]. This is the only published HCC study to assess the association between imaging data and transcriptomic data, but it focused on histologic data that cannot be acquired before immunotherapy. Moreover, it remains unclear whether their models can be used to identify immunotherapy responders. In our study, the AUC of the radiomics model for predicting the response of liver lesions to immunotherapy was 0.750 in multicenter data, supporting the model's potential utility in predicting tumor microenvironment features and clinical outcomes of immunotherapy for patients with HCC. The 6‐month endpoint was selected to capture the delayed clinical benefit typical of anti‐PD‐1 therapy, which likely accounts for the weaker association observed at 3 months. In Sun et al.'s study [21], the ability of gadoxetic acid‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features to predict tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells and PD‐L1 expression in HCC was examined. Although different combinations of MRI features were associated with the objective response to immunotherapy in 34 patients, only six patients responded, and the median follow‐up was only 8 months. Moreover, these models were based on imaging features assessed by radiologists, which could be influenced by investigator‐related factors to some extent.

Our radiomics model was developed and validated exclusively using intrahepatic HCC lesions from surgical cohorts. This is because patients undergoing resection typically present with intrahepatic lesions, whereas extrahepatic (e.g., pulmonary) lesions were rarely available for initial model development. Patients receiving systemic immunotherapy often have advanced disease with distant metastases. To address a common clinical scenario and explore the model's broader utility, we applied it to lesions in various anatomical sites, including the liver and lungs, within the immunotherapy cohort. This was based on the biological hypothesis that the T cell‐inflamed GEP, as a signature of the tumor–immune microenvironment, might be reflected by inherent tumor radiomics phenotypes that are potentially transferable across different anatomical sites for the same cancer type. The results provide preliminary support for this approach. Lesions predicted as GEPhi exhibited a higher response rate across all sites, with the strongest predictive performance observed for liver lesions. However, we fully acknowledge the potential limitations of this extrapolation. Differences in organ parenchyma and background tissue density between the liver and other sites could influence radiomics feature interpretation. Furthermore, biological heterogeneity between primary and metastatic lesions might not be fully captured by a model trained solely on intrahepatic tumors. The observed site‐dependent performance underscores the need for caution and suggests that the model's predictive power is likely optimal for intrahepatic HCC. Future studies using larger, multicenter cohorts that include explicitly annotated lesions from various anatomical sites are essential to rigorously validate the model's generalizability.

Our study had several limitations. First, the sample size of the test dataset was relatively small because of the requirements for both CT images and RNA‐seq data for each patient. In the multicenter immunotherapy cohort, although the real‐world setting strengthened clinical relevance, only a limited number of patients achieved an objective response (n = 9). Therefore, although the survival trend was biologically reasonable, it should be considered exploratory given the small number of objective responses and limited statistical power. Second, this was a retrospective study, and the CT images were acquired from different scanners in different centers, which might have influenced the extraction of imaging features to some extent. Nevertheless, the performance of our model, developed from the training cohort, was validated in the external test dataset, implying that it is generalizable to different datasets, scanners, and populations. Notably, the model was developed using Python 3.6 and Scikit‐learn 0.22, which are currently legacy versions. This dependency could potentially limit the direct reproducibility of the model, and future validation is recommended in updated computational environments.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that the CT‐based radiomics model can effectively predict the T cell‐inflamed GEP in HCC. The predicted GEP was correlated with immunotherapy response and patient survival. These results suggest that this CT‐based radiomics model can potentially support treatment decision‐making for patients with HCC.
