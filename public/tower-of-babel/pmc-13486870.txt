The social dilemma of load shedding in Community Grids with vulnerable populations
Authors: Zhe Gong, Bedoor Alshebli, Jimmy Chih-Hsien Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn situations of emergency failures and power supply shortages, utility companies face the critical decision of load-shedding (disconnecting customers involuntarily). Determining the principles by which load-shedding can best align with both economic imperatives and social norms presents a complex challenge. Existing load-shedding protocols, largely governed by economic considerations, risk undermining principles of energy justice by disproportionately affecting individuals dependent on durable medical equipment (DME). Here, we investigate the equity implications of load-shedding in cities. Taking northern New York City as an example, we identified the systemic disadvantages faced by residential DME users during energy outages. Through modeling and public surveys of 856 U.S. residents, we show that incorporating DME-related settlement costs of loss into load-shedding optimization can better safeguard medically vulnerable households and align outage management with public preferences for prioritizing vulnerable populations. Our findings also reveal that current compensation structures inadequately capture the social harm of outages, supporting policy reforms that embed social justice into grid resilience strategies and more systematically protect at-risk populations.Keywords: energy justice, load shedding, vulnerable populations, durable medical equipment

Introduction

In October 2019, Pacific Gas and Electric Company (PG&E), California’s largest utility, initiated extensive power shut-offs to reduce the risks of wildfires, leaving millions of customers without electricity.1 This action sparked significant criticism, with accusations that the utility was transferring operational risks to the public.2,3 Similarly, during the 2021 Texas winter storm, power was cut to over two million homes at the peak of the crisis, while those who retained electricity faced astronomical bills, sometimes hundreds of times higher than usual.4,5 As extreme weather events become more frequent and the aging power infrastructure struggles to cope, distribution networks are increasingly vulnerable to failure. As a precaution, utilities often implement preemptive power load-shedding to avoid catastrophic consequences.

A central challenge for utilities lies in deciding which loads to curtail and which to maintain. In many liberalized energy markets, electricity dispatch is driven by an order of economic merit and cleared using marginal pricing.6,7 Although this approach optimizes economic efficiency and ensures network reliability, it often overlooks considerations of energy justice, which emphasizes the fair distribution of energy resources, inclusive decision-making, and recognition of vulnerable populations. This raises concerns about the equity of power distribution during shortages, motivating us to address this gap.

Electricity is a necessity to societal function for all, yet the consequences of outages vary significantly among consumers, and energy-vulnerable populations deserve greater attention. One particularly at-risk group comprises individuals reliant on electrically powered durable medical equipment (DME), such as ventilators and oxygen concentrators, to sustain life.8 For these patients, any interruption in electricity poses an immediate, severe threat.9

DME users are often not prepared for outages, and fewer than half have a backup power solution.10,11,12,13,14 Without such provisions, device-integrated batteries typically last only 2–4 h.8 Evacuation is a possible recourse, but it also presents major challenges. A surge in emergency evacuations can overwhelm hospital capacities, hindering access to timely care.15,16,17,18,19 Additionally, physical limitations and reduced mobility may deter patients with DME from evacuating, leaving them highly vulnerable at home.11,12,20

Reliable electricity supply is critical for energy-vulnerable populations, but substantial gaps persist. In the United States, patients with DME are more concentrated in rural and low-income areas compared to wealthier communities.8 Longer outages are associated with increased rates of sensory, physical, and mental disabilities.21 While counties with a higher prevalence of DME users report shorter average outages, individual DME users are twice as likely as non-DME users to experience outages exceeding 24 h, suggesting county-level data analysis may underestimate their vulnerability.22

The deepening aging of society has led to a growing number of individuals with special needs and disabilities who rely on home electrical medical devices. Home-based care not only reduces medical expenses and eases the burden on healthcare systems but also allows patients to recover in a familiar and comfortable environment.23 This reliance underscores the critical need for an uninterrupted power supply for their well-being. However, from an economic perspective, patients with DME, as residential electricity users, often occupy a disadvantaged position in load-shedding decisions, resulting in lower power reliability. This disparity contributes to inequitable load-shedding practices during emergency outages.

Motivated by the above observations, we aim to address the following questions. First, under current cost-minimizing load-shedding practice, are residential DME-dependent households systematically over-exposed to curtailment relative to commercial and industrial loads? Second, if the legal liability that utilities face when patients with DME are harmed during outages, quantified through observed wrongful-death settlement costs, is internalized as an explicit term in the utility’s own optimization, does it materially change which loads are shed, by how much, and at what cost to the utility? Third, is the resulting prioritization of medically vulnerable households consistent with the moral preferences of the U.S. public, including when respondents themselves bear the trade-off?

Existing studies in energy equity often skip this fundamental question and directly propose decision-making frameworks to promote fairness, even though energy equity has become a prominent research frontier. For example, one study formulates a two-stage stochastic planning model for both pre-event resource allocation and post-event microgrid load restoration, imposing an equity constraint such that the interruption ratio across all nodes must not exceed a threshold.24 While this definition ensures equality, it falls short of equity, given the heterogeneity of communities and the absence of sociological grounding in the threshold formulation. Another line of work identifies disadvantaged communities (DACs) versus non-DACs to perform equity-aware outage analysis. By reordering interruption events according to DAC-customer impacts, this approach reveals previously overlooked risks of unfairness.25 However, it treats DAC customer counts as the exclusive equity metric, neglecting the utility’s economic incentives and loss landscapes. A further category of research assigns higher weights to socially vulnerable communities via indices such as the social vulnerability index (SVI) in decision models.26 Yet SVI was not designed for outage contexts, and the community of practice lacks consensus on how to calibrate such weights.

In contrast to existing studies, our work explicitly centers on the vulnerable population of residential patients dependent on DME, a group rarely incorporated into outage or load-shedding models. Leveraging real-world data, we reconstruct the load-shedding decisions made by utility companies and incorporate realistic economic factors to reflect the prioritization of DME users within economic models. We further conduct moral-dilemma experiments to elicit societal preferences for prioritizing medically dependent households, providing sociological evidence for integrating moral equity into outage management. Our analysis yields three concrete findings. First, both conventional cost-minimizing load-shedding (S1) and load-volume-minimizing shedding (S3) expose substantial numbers of DME-dependent households to outage in our northern-New York City (NYC) case, with 4,368 and 2,616 affected patients with DME under S1 and S3, respectively. Second, internalizing legal liability through the proposed settlement-cost-from-lawsuits (SCLs) term (S2) reduces this number to 979 at the empirically observed USD 25M settlement benchmark, a 77.6% reduction, and simultaneously lowers the utility’s total economic loss by 20.1% relative to S1. Equity and utility-side efficiency are therefore aligned, not in tension, under realistic settlement magnitudes. Third, a national stated-preference (SP) survey of 856 U.S. residents shows that this prioritization is broadly endorsed: Over 80% of respondents support cutting commercial load to protect a single amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) patient even at a 10% mortality risk, and respondents assign, on average, 66.9% of the resulting financial burden to the utility itself. Together, these results reveal a tractable, legally grounded, and publicly supported mechanism for institutionalizing energy justice during load-shedding events, without requiring infrastructure-scale interventions.

Results

Modeling of the Northern New York City distribution network

The northern NYC is selected as the test area for this study, comprising Manhattan and the Bronx (Figure S1). Ranked fourth among the 25 U.S. cities most vulnerable to climate change,27 NYC’s power grid faces threats from rising sea levels, severe storms, and extreme temperatures, as evidenced by events such as the 2003 Northeast Blackout and Hurricane Sandy in 2012. The city’s electrical grid, one of the oldest in the nation, dates back to the early 20th century.28 A significant portion of NYC’s electricity is generated by aging power plants along the waterfront in Queens, some of which were built as early as the 1960s.29 Deferred maintenance of this aging infrastructure has further intensified the risks posed by climate change and extreme weather events.

To account for the varying economic characteristics of different power load types, the loads were categorized into commercial, industrial, and residential consumers. To simulate the actual load distribution in NYC, the model utilized publicly available data from NYC’s Zoning and Land Use Map (Figure S2)30 to estimate the loads. Each sub-region in Figure S2 is modeled as a single load. The total estimated load for NYC was validated against annual reports31 provided by the city’s primary utility company, Con Edison, ensuring the model’s accuracy. We utilized data from32 to develop a synthetic medium-voltage distribution network for northern NYC (Figure S3). The model assumes two high-to-medium voltage substations supplying 1,803 load nodes. The total load is primarily composed of commercial (44.8%), residential (45.8%), and industrial (9.4%) consumers, with park loads being negligible. Model of the emergency load-shedding decisions

Utility companies typically make load-shedding decisions based on economic considerations, opting for strategies that minimize overall costs. In energy economics, the economic cost of unserved electricity is commonly discussed through the concept of the value of lost load (VoLL), a monetary indicator of outage impacts that varies across customer classes, outage durations, locations, timing, and available mitigation options.33,34 Consistent with this literature, the financial impact of power interruptions varies substantially across different types of users. According to a study by the Ernest Orlando Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, the economic costs of power interruptions are significantly higher for commercial and industrial users compared to residential customers.35 For instance, the cost per average kW for medium and large commercial and industrial users during a 16-h outage is $203.0, whereas for residential users, it is only $21.2. Additionally, information from Con Edison’s official website indicates that customers can file claims for economic losses caused by outages lasting 24 h. Residential customers have a maximum claim limit of $580,36 while commercial users can claim up to $11,460.37 These VoLL-related and compensation-based cost differences explain why conventional economic load-shedding models tend to assign higher interruption costs to commercial and industrial loads than to residential loads.

Building on these insights, we developed a model to simulate utility companies’ emergency load-shedding decisions. This study focuses on outage scenarios caused by insufficient power supply. Specifically, power interruptions result in economic losses for grid operators, including revenue losses (RLs) from the inability to supply power and compensation payments (CPs) to customers under insurance agreements.38 The economic loss of service disruptions is determined by the duration of the power interruption, as well as the number and type of customers affected. As illustrated in Figure 1, the objective of the utility company’s emergency load-shedding model is to minimize the total economic loss, expressed as economic loss = f(RL, CP), which is adopted in Scenario 1 (S1).

Modeling load-shedding decisions and ethical trade-offs

An overview of the emergency load-shedding model and the associated social dilemma used to elicit public ethical judgments. The framework contrasts two scenarios: cutting electricity to commercial areas, resulting in financial losses, versus cutting electricity to residential areas with medically vulnerable patients, posing risks to human life.

Power outages caused by utility companies can pose significant risks to vulnerable customers, potentially leading to legal liability. For example, in 2021, customers in Hidalgo County who relied on oxygen machines filed a lawsuit against American Electric Power (AEP) and Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) over outages during a winter storm.39 Similarly, Arizona Public Service Co. faced substantial repercussions after reaching a costly settlement with the estates of two individuals who tragically lost their lives during summer power outages in 2011 and 2018.40 This liability channel is conceptually related to, but distinct from, conventional VoLL-based outage-cost valuation. VoLL and compensation-based costs typically capture customer-class-level economic losses from interrupted electricity service, and therefore do not distinguish medically vulnerable DME-dependent households from other residential customers. By contrast, settlement costs from lawsuits capture a utility-facing liability cost that arises when outage-induced harm to vulnerable customers leads to legal claims. Since patients with DME are among the most vulnerable populations to power outages and face a high risk of life-threatening health consequences due to power loss, this study introduces, for the first time, the settlement costs from lawsuits filed by customers for losses incurred during power outages (SCL). The proposed objective of the utility company’s emergency load-shedding model, considering patients with DME, is expressed as economic loss = f(RL, CP, SCL), and is implemented in Scenario 2 (S2).

To date, studies on load-shedding have predominantly focused on achieving efficient and rapid load curtailment, overlooking the economic heterogeneity across different load types. Therefore, as a baseline, we established Scenario 3 (S3), in which the objective function is designed to minimize the total load-shedding amount.

Modeling the distribution of patients with DME and the impact of power outages on their well-being

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services’ emPOWER program provides monthly updates on Medicare beneficiaries who rely on life-sustaining DME devices.41 Utilizing this dataset, we used a point mapping strategy to map the spatial distribution of patients with DME in northern NYC to 667 residential load points (see “DME patients mapping” in the STAR Methods Section), identifying a total of 12,689 individuals. The detailed spatial distribution of DME patient density is depicted in Figure 2B. The distribution of DME patient density is uneven, with higher concentrations observed in areas with lower residential load but a greater number of patients with DME, particularly in Upper Manhattan, the South Bronx, and the East Village.

The total non-accidental mortality risk in NYC increased by 28%, resulting in approximately 90 excess deaths due to the Northeast blackout of August 2003.42 Among individuals aged 65–74, the mortality rate rose by 44%, with most excess deaths attributed to disease-related causes. Although the data did not specifically categorize deaths among DME users, the strong correlation between DME reliance and age8 suggests that power outages likely had significant negative health impacts on DME-dependent individuals. Consequently, in the survey described in Section the social dilemma of load-shedding: Survey research purpose and overview, we assume a 50% increase in non-accidental mortality among patients with DME during a 24-h power outage. Furthermore, we utilized the data from a previous study43 to estimate the average mortality rate per hour of power outage for patients with DME (see “emergency load-shedding modeling” in the STAR Methods Section) in the NYC case.

Emergency load-shedding scenarios analysis

In a hypothetical 24-h fault scenario, we assume that a partial failure of the southwest substation due to flooding causes the load in central Manhattan to be redistributed to two feeders in the northeast. This redistribution leads to an overload in one of the feeders, resulting in voltage magnitudes that were originally within the normal operating range (0.95 p.u.–1.05 p.u.) exhibiting instability and dropping to low voltages. This triggers load-shedding controls. Following emergency load-shedding, the voltage magnitude is restored to the operational range of 0.95 p.u. (Figures S4–S6). This study compares the load-shedding strategies implemented in the three scenarios described in Section model of the emergency load-shedding decisions.

Figures 3A–3C illustrate the load-shedding decisions, with disconnected loads encircled in black. To further quantify the scale and composition of load-shedding, Figure 4A reports the total shed load and the curtailed share of each load type under the three scenarios. The total amount of shed load is similar across scenarios, corresponding to 28.3% of the total load in the studied feeders, because all scenarios are subject to the same transformer capacity constraint and therefore require comparable load curtailment to avoid transformer overloading. However, the burden is distributed differently across load types. In S1, shedding is concentrated in residential loads, with 64.6% curtailed, while commercial and industrial loads remain unaffected. In S2, settlement costs reduce the curtailed share of residential load to 41.7%, but shift part of the burden to industrial loads, while commercial shedding remains negligible at 0.4%. In S3, where load-shedding is minimized without distinguishing load types, curtailment is more broadly distributed across residential, commercial, and industrial loads, at 35.6%, 25.6%, and 8.6%, respectively.

To illustrate how residential loads are prioritized for disconnection across different scenarios, we use DME patient density as the primary indicator and present the results in Figures 3D–3F. It can be observed that under S2, where settlement costs are considered—costs largely determined by the DME patient density—there is a tendency to disconnect residential customers with the lowest DME patient density first. In contrast, the load-shedding decisions in S1 and S3 do not exhibit this pattern. Consequently, the number of affected patients with DME is significantly reduced in S2.

From another perspective, even with the inclusion of settlement costs, a substantial number of patients with DME are still disconnected in S2, indicating that not all DME patient loads can be guaranteed uninterrupted power supply using settlement costs. This highlights the complex trade-offs involved in balancing economic efficiency, settlement costs, and the protection of vulnerable populations. Settlement costs in wrongful death lawsuits can vary substantially. For example, payouts in Texas typically range from $500,000 to $1 million, though some cases have resulted in settlements of $10 million or more.44 In another case, the family of a South Carolina woman who was struck and killed by a decaying utility pole received a $30 million settlement from the electric company.45 In S2, we set the settlement cost at $25 million to examine its impact on the number of patients with DME affected by power outages. To explore this relationship further, a sensitivity analysis was conducted, and the results are presented in Figure 4B. In the absence of any financial compensation mechanism (S1 with no settlement cost), the total number of affected patients with DME would reach 4,368. Within the typical settlement cost range, approximately 2,163 patients with DME would experience a one-day power outage. Even under the relatively optimistic setting of S2, where the settlement cost is set at $25 million, this number would still be 979. Increasing the settlement cost to $50 million would only reduce it to 191. Ensuring an uninterrupted power supply for all patients with DME would require raising settlement costs to over $100.3 million, an amount far exceeding the typical payout ranges observed in existing wrongful death settlements.

A comprehensive comparison of economic losses across the three scenarios is presented in Figure 4C. S3, which excludes economic impact considerations, demonstrates the highest total economic loss. The inclusion of settlement costs in S2 provides financial incentives for utility providers to minimize power interruptions for patients with DME by prioritizing electricity supply, thereby reducing settlement-related financial liabilities. As a result, S2 reduces total economic loss by 20.1% compared to S1, highlighting that this refined load-shedding strategy better reconciles economic efficiency with social responsibility.

The social dilemma of load-shedding: Survey research purpose and overview

While the optimization model demonstrates how internalizing the social and legal consequences of DME interruption changes utility-side load-shedding decisions, it does not determine whether the resulting prioritization is socially acceptable. The model and survey therefore address different parts of the same load-shedding dilemma. The model evaluates the operational and economic consequences of the SCL-based formulation, whereas the survey examines whether members of the public support the resulting prioritization of medically vulnerable households when doing so imposes explicit moral and economic trade-offs. The survey is not used to calibrate the optimization model or provide direct inputs to the utility objective; rather, it extends the technical analysis into a broader social context by testing the public acceptability of the SCL-based prioritization.

Based on the optimization results presented in Section emergency load-shedding scenarios analysis, under conditions of power scarcity, prioritizing electricity for patients with DME necessarily reduces the supply available for other loads. In practice, it is difficult to guarantee uninterrupted supply to all patients with DME. This tension raises fundamental questions about energy justice: To what extent should society prioritize vulnerable populations? Are individuals willing to sacrifice their own electricity consumption or income to protect patients with DME? What individual characteristics shape these decisions? These questions motivate the survey analysis later in discussion.

In this study, we focus specifically on patients with ALS who depend on home ventilators, representing a typical case of patients with DME. The primary objective is to examine whether individuals are willing to prioritize electricity for patients with DME, even when such prioritization might leave them without power or reduce their income. This willingness to save (WTS) is likely influenced by various factors. Beyond demographic variables, we hypothesize that the more severe the health consequences of power outages for patients with DME and the smaller the impact of such prioritization on individuals’ own electricity access and income, the greater their willingness to prioritize patients with DME.

We designed two surveys to test this hypothesis. In Survey 1, we conceptualized relationships with patients with DME across distinct roles, ranging from strangers (distant) to family members (close). The health impact of power outages on patients with ALS was quantified using a hypothetical mortality rate ranging from 0% to 100%. To validate the trends of WTS responses obtained in Survey 1, Survey 2 revisited participants’ moral attitudes toward load prioritization by progressively providing additional information, including shopping-mall income losses, utility revenue losses, and corresponding increases in ALS patient mortality risk, thereby situating the questions in more realistic outage scenarios. In addition to the binary load-disconnection choices, respondents in Survey 1 were asked to report the highest percentage of daily income that could be sacrificed under the stated circumstances. This additional question was included to make the economic trade-off more explicit and to encourage respondents to consider the affordability of prioritizing medically vulnerable patients, rather than making only a binary moral choice. Our design follows a moral-dilemma framework but moves beyond abstract thought experiments by introducing clear trade-offs and realistic context. This design enables us to observe how people balance efficiency and fairness in practical power-shortage decisions.

For utility companies, decisions regarding load-shedding are likely influenced not only by social ethics but also by techno-economic considerations. Therefore, after determining the WTS, we further explored questions regarding whether load priority can be purchased and how financial responsibility for the electricity bill should be allocated. These aspects were explored in the latter part of Survey 2. The aim is to provide guidance and insight for designing a load-shedding strategy framework that is both economically viable and aligned with social norms and ethics.

The survey instrument is a structured moral-dilemma task in the tradition of trolley-style sacrificial-dilemma research in moral psychology,46,47,48,49 adapted to a power-system load-shedding context. From this tradition we adopt three design choices: systematic dose-response variation of a single causal parameter (ALS-patient mortality risk, 0–100%), role assignment to test the effect of social proximity (worker, operator, bystander, neighbor, family member), and explicit hypothetical framing as the field-standard ethical protocol for morally sensitive elicitation. The role-assignment-plus-parameter-variation structure most directly parallels the Moral Machine paradigm of Bonnefon et al.46 and Awad et al.49 adapted from autonomous-vehicle ethics to utility load-shedding ethics. The WTS component further draws on the SP and discrete-choice tradition in health economics, in which respondents evaluate quantified trade-offs between health outcomes and money under structured scenarios. Recent applications include trade-offs between health impacts and other social consequences of lockdown policies,50,51 the allocation of health and social care funding,52 and a general review of moral choice analysis in health economics.53 Our Survey 2 adopts the staged-information protocol from this literature, sequentially introducing shopping-mall income losses, utility revenue losses, and increased mortality information, analogous to “informed preferences” elicitation.52 Within this combined methodological lineage, the present study extends moral-choice and SP methods, which have previously been applied to autonomous-vehicle ethics, lockdown stringency, pandemic resource allocation, and health-care funding, to a power-system context involving DME-dependent households, a setting in which, to our knowledge, these methods have not previously been used.

A total of 856 valid responses were obtained from U.S. residents via the Prolific online survey platform between June and August 2024. Of these, 448 responses were collected for Survey 1 and 408 for Survey 2. The average completion time was approximately 8 min for Survey 1 and 7 min for Survey 2. Participants were compensated at rates of £1.00 and £0.90, respectively, in accordance with fair-pay standards on the platform. Moreover, the recruitment strategy on Prolific ensured diversity across age, gender, income, and education, as detailed in Note S3.

Individual’s choices on willingness to save

Under the principle of prioritizing electricity supply for vulnerable customers, load-shedding decisions may result in increased load-shedding volumes, greater economic losses, and disruptions to the lives and finances of other customers. Consequently, utility companies face a complex dilemma. Here, we examine individual WTS choices, providing valuable insights for policymakers and utility companies to develop decision strategies that are more likely to gain societal acceptance and support. To this end, we construct hypothetical scenarios to illustrate the social dilemma of load-shedding. These scenarios are intentionally straightforward, allowing respondents to fully grasp the consequences of each decision and make informed choices. Participants are specifically asked to evaluate whether prioritizing electricity supply for residential communities with patients with ALS, thereby ensuring absolute power security for these individuals, is the correct course of action. The proportion of respondents who favor this prioritization serves as a measure of their WTS.

The WTS results from Survey 1 and Survey 2 are presented in Figures 6A and 6B, respectively. In Survey 1, shopping mall workers reported the lowest WTS among all roles, while family members showed the highest, aligning with the hypothesis that proximity to the patient is associated with stronger willingness to support electricity prioritization (logistic regression slope = −0.142, p = 0.002). Notably, even among mall workers, who would face potential income loss and job disruption due to power cuts, 81.7% still supported cutting electricity to the mall to safeguard supply for an ALS patient. On average, 84.3% of respondents in Survey 1 endorsed this action. Similarly, in Survey 2, 88.9% of respondents in Cohort 1 (a mix of shopping mall and residential community with one ALS patient) made the same choice. These findings demonstrate a high consistency between the two surveys and indicate broad public support for prioritizing electricity for patients reliant on DME.

Overall, the variation in WTS across different roles is relatively small compared to the changes observed with different mortality rates (Figure 6A). Notably, over 80% of participants still express a high WTS even when the mortality risk is as low as 10%. As the mortality rate increases from 10% to 100%, WTS increases only modestly (logistic regression slope = 0.399, p ≈ 0). When the full range from 0% to 100% is considered, the increase becomes more pronounced (slope = 0.791, p ≈ 0). Surprisingly, nearly half of the participants believe that the correct action is to cut power to the mall despite the fact that the power outage poses no threat to patient health or life, i.e., the mortality rate is 0%. This decision is made despite the potential negative consequences, including reduced revenues for the utility company and the shopping mall, as well as income losses for workers at the shopping mall.

Without considering a specific role, participants’ decisions in Survey 2 are found to be influenced by the nature and the amount of information they received, i.e., when informed about the expected financial loss (Additional Information 1) and the mortality rate of patients with ALS from the power outage (Additional Information 2), as shown in Figure 6B. As participants are presented with additional economic and health-related information in an objective manner, their responses become more rational, gradually shifting from an initial emphasis on protecting more vulnerable groups to favoring economically advantageous options. Nevertheless, participants consistently demonstrate a strong preference for avoiding power cuts to options that save a greater number of patients with ALS.

To further validate WTS and quantify the economic burden respondents are willing to bear, we asked participants to assess the maximum percentage of daily income a worker at the shopping mall would be willing to sacrifice to help save the ALS patient. The density plot results, summarized by different roles and varying survival rates of the ALS patient, are presented in Figure 7. It can be observed that the affordable daily income loss predominantly falls within three ranges: 0–20%, 50%, and 100%. Additionally, as the ALS patient’s mortality rate increases, there is a noticeable tendency for respondents to enhance the economic loss they are willing to endure. Interestingly, respondents in the role of workers show the highest proportion choosing to sacrifice 100% of their daily income to save the ALS patient. Although workers at a shopping mall exhibit the lowest WTS overall, their average financial sacrifice stands at 47.1%, surpassing even that of the patient’s family members (45.0%), making it the highest average among all roles. This further highlights people’s compassion and willingness to help those in need during emergencies.

Energy justice and financial responsibility in power outage

Next, we explored whether load priority should be a purchasable service. Initially, participants were asked if access to uninterrupted electricity should be a paid service. We then introduced the context of residential communities with patients with ALS and inquired if uninterrupted electricity for these disadvantaged communities should also be paid. The findings revealed that while 44.5% of participants initially agreed that priority access should be purchasable, this number dropped to 35.9% when focusing on communities with patients with ALS. Such decline highlights concerns about energy inequality for vulnerable groups if priority access is commodified, suggesting that most people believe load priority should be based on necessity rather than financial capacity.

However, when electric utilities implement suboptimal load-shedding strategies to accommodate the needs of patients with DME, they may incur significant economic losses. If these losses cannot be recouped through commodifying electricity priority, the question of who should bear this burden arises. To address this, participants were asked to allocate a $1,000 loss incurred by the utility company among four parties: the utility company, the family with an ALS patient, other households in the prioritized community, and other customers in the community power grids. Here, two variables are examined: whether the ALS patient resided in the participant’s community (implying increased load priority) and whether the ALS patient’s household was impoverished. These variables created four scenarios: Scene 1, ALS household in the participant’s community; Scene 2, impoverished ALS household in the participant’s community; Scene 3, ALS household in a neighboring community; and Scene 4, impoverished ALS household in a neighboring community. The average allocation results are presented in Figure 8.

Participants’ allocation patterns revealed a strong preference for assigning the greatest share of the utility’s economic loss to the utility company itself (with average allocations exceeding $600 across all scenarios). Notably, the average amount allocated to the household with an ALS patient declined significantly when the patient’s family was impoverished, falling from $131 in Scene 1 to $81 in Scene 2 and from $110 in Scene 3 to $71 in Scene 4, indicating consistent sensitivity to economic vulnerability. Additionally, participants demonstrate altruism by allocating more responsibility to themselves, consistently choosing higher allocations for the same role across different scenarios. These results imply that utilities might reasonably expect to recover approximately one-third of the losses associated with prioritizing vulnerable populations. While all electricity consumers were perceived as sharing responsibility, those whose load priority was increased (such as the ALS household and their surrounding community) were consistently assigned a greater share of the cost burden.

To summarize, our findings reveal strong public support for prioritizing electricity access for patients with DME, even when doing so entails personal financial loss or when patients face only mild mortality risks. A clear majority of participants (64.1%) believe that load priority should not be monetized, particularly within distribution networks serving patients with DME. Moreover, when asked how the financial burden of such prioritization should be shared, respondents indicated that the majority of these losses (an average of 66.9%) should be absorbed by the utility companies, with the remainder distributed among electricity users.

These survey findings, combined with technical modeling, provide robust evidence that both economic and social frameworks favor prioritizing patients with DME during load-shedding events. Building upon these insights, we now discuss the implications for energy policy and social justice in outage management.

Discussion

Power outages disproportionately impact vulnerable populations, highlighting pressing concerns over energy equity during electricity shortages. Ensuring access to power in such crises is not just a technical issue but a fundamental matter of social justice. Recent disasters have exposed the tragic inadequacies of current approaches. For example, a 77-year-old DME patient in Portugal died during the recent 2025 Iberian Peninsula blackout when her ventilator lost power. During California’s 2019 public safety power shutoffs, more than 76,000 “Medical Baseline” customers were relegated to a mere notification list, leaving about 10,000 disabled residents unable to power essential medical devices and unsupported during the blackout.54 Similarly, in the aftermath of Hurricane Sandy, thousands of elderly and disabled residents were stranded in high-rise buildings for weeks, deprived of power, elevator service, and heating. A class-action lawsuit filed against NYC highlighted the failure of emergency planning to address the unique needs of these vulnerable individuals.55

Existing governmental and utility-driven energy justice policies primarily focus on pre-disaster preventive measures and post-disaster recovery efforts. For example, PG&E provides the “Medical Baseline” program to reduce electricity costs for medically vulnerable customers56 and launched a Portable Battery Program (PBP) in 2020 to offer backup power to those dependent on medical devices.57 Similarly, San Diego Gas & Electric (SDG&E) provides a Generator Grant Program (GGP) to supply backup batteries for highly vulnerable customers.58 Post-disaster measures often involve allocating additional funds to assist low- and medium-income (LMI) communities in rebuilding and repairing housing.59 Although these groups of patients with DME and low-income populations may overlap, they are not interchangeable. The specific and immediate risks faced by patients with DME during an emergency, such as loss of life-sustaining medical support, are distinct from the broader socioeconomic vulnerabilities. Neglecting this distinction underestimates the physical harm inflicted on medically vulnerable individuals during a crisis.

Despite widespread recognition of the need to prevent life-threatening outages for the elderly and physically vulnerable,59 effective emergency interventions remain insufficient. A key lesson from past disasters is the persistence of silo effects that hinder effective response.60 These silos exist both horizontally among government agencies, medical systems, and utilities, and vertically across different disaster phases, including preparation, response, and recovery. Governments and utilities cannot assume that their preparatory measures (e.g., outage notifications) alone will safeguard vulnerable populations, nor can they afford a “wait-and-rebuild” approach that passively responds after the damage is done. Instead, a paradigm shift is needed: even during power shortages, utilities should leverage their control mechanisms to prioritize electricity supply for the most at-risk individuals, a dimension that is currently lacking in policy frameworks.

Our work addresses a crucial gap in the application of an energy justice framework during the outage itself. The central, and non-obvious, finding is that under realistic legal-exposure magnitudes the equity objective and the utility’s own economic objective are aligned rather than competing. Specifically, in the northern-NYC case, the SCL-based formulation (S2) reduces the number of patients with DME exposed to a one-day outage from 4,368 to 979 (a 77.6% reduction) while simultaneously lowering the utility’s total economic loss by 20.1% relative to the conventional cost-minimization scenario (S1). The mechanism is straightforward: wrongful-death settlement exposure is large enough, and concentrated enough in DME-dense distribution segments, that internalizing it as an operational cost term redirects curtailment toward commercial and industrial loads whose interruption carries lower expected legal liability. Settlement costs thus function not as a moral surcharge imposed from outside, but as an economic proxy that makes the utility’s own optimization account for the social and legal consequences of DME interruption. The national survey complements this technical result: Public moral preferences track the same prioritization, with broad support for protecting patients with DME even when respondents themselves bear the financial trade-off.

Backup-power programs and evacuation planning are valuable resilience measures, but they require advance deployment and coordination, and historically face slow household-level uptake and limited surge capacity during wide-area outages. By contrast, the proposed SCL-based formulation can be embedded directly into utility optimization and applied in real time when supply is constrained. In the studied feeders, one-day backup for 6,475 DME households would require about 236.2 MWh of storage, costing roughly $97.1 million at $15,000 per household system.61 This estimate is intended as an order-of-magnitude comparison rather than a full cost-benefit analysis, as actual feasibility would also depend on battery degradation, maintenance, installation constraints, reserve margins, inverter losses, household-level co-benefits, and existing backup resources. Since S2 raises utility costs by $0.86 million per 24-h outage compared to S1, the battery deployment cost would be comparable to the cumulative additional S2 cost over roughly 112 such outage events. Whether this favors one approach depends on outage frequency, battery lifetime, and the co-benefits of distributed storage outside outage events, which are beyond the scope of this comparison. These measures therefore address different stages of outage management: backup storage and evacuation as preparatory measures requiring advance deployment, and SCL-based optimization as an operational lever available during the outage itself. The most effective protection of patients with DME requires a combination of these mechanisms rather than any single one.

To further explore whether it is necessary to prioritize all patients with DME, we designed a series of surveys focusing on the social dilemma of load-shedding. The survey results demonstrate broad public support for prioritizing electricity access for medically vulnerable individuals, despite the potential trade-offs in personal power consumption and income. Respondents were even willing to forgo more than 44.5% of their daily income to save an ALS patient.

The U.S. economy has faced mounting challenges this year. The International Monetary Fund recently raised its inflation forecast for the U.S. from 2% to 3% for 2025.62 According to the Yale Budget Lab, tariffs imposed under the Trump administration are projected to increase the annual costs for the average American family by $3,800, a figure that has since been revised upward to $4,700.63 These rising financial pressures have had a significant impact on daily life and may influence attitudes captured in our previous survey. To assess this potential shift, we relaunched our survey in April 2025, inviting previous participants to complete the updated questionnaire. At the end of the survey, we asked respondents to rate the extent to which they had recently been affected by inflation (on a scale from 1 to 7). We collected 152 responses for Survey 1 and 111 responses for Survey 2. On average, participants reported an inflation impact score of 4.4, corresponding to a level between “moderately” and “considerably” affected.

Analysis of Survey 1 reveals that respondents’ attitudes toward WTS show no significant association with their reported experiences of inflation. Similarly, there is no significant change in these attitudes over the two-year period. However, responses regarding the maximum percentage of daily income individuals are willing to sacrifice to help save an ALS patient differ significantly. Specifically, individuals who report greater impacts from inflation are willing to sacrifice a smaller proportion of their daily income (Figure S7, linear regression slope = −4.474, p ≈ 0). Among them, those experiencing the most severe inflation impacts are willing to forgo only 23.7% of their daily income (Figure S8). Moreover, respondents in 2025 are generally willing to sacrifice less than those in 2024 (slope = −4.898, p = 0.006). This trend is further corroborated by findings from Survey 2. In the $1,000 loss allocation task, participants assigned a higher share of the utility company’s economic loss to the company itself, while reducing the proportion of loss they were personally willing to bear compared to previous responses (Figure S9).

Our analysis demonstrates that, even amid mounting inflationary pressures, the public continues to recognize the moral imperative to prioritize electricity access for patients with DME, reflecting a sustained sense of social responsibility. Nevertheless, when personal sacrifice is required, the extent of economic self-sacrifice individuals are willing to undertake diminishes. Despite this decline, the overall willingness to bear financial costs remains substantial to support meaningful upgrades to the power grid, ensuring the protection of most, if not all, patients who rely on life-sustaining electrical devices. These findings highlight the delicate balance between social responsibility and self-sacrifice: Although economic hardship moderates the degree of individual willingness to give, the collective commitment to safeguarding vulnerable populations remains strong. This resilience underscores both the social feasibility and ethical necessity of such prioritization in future community grid load-shedding decisions.

Importantly, our results also offer a realistic and actionable policy pathway in the U.S. context, where large-scale infrastructure transformations remain difficult to achieve. While physically exempting individual households from community-scale outages is operationally infeasible, our modeling and survey findings indicate that restructuring the economic logic of utility decision-making can be both effective and socially supported. First, we call for breaking down existing silo effects by strengthening data collection on patients with DME and promoting data-sharing among government agencies, medical systems, and utilities. Standardizing and continuously updating such data across city, state, and federal levels would provide the informational foundation necessary to better identify and support medically vulnerable populations. Second, regulators can incentivize utilities to deprioritize load-shedding in vulnerable residential areas by embedding higher proxy costs for DME disconnections, which represent potential legal liabilities and the public’s demonstrated willingness to bear trade-offs, into outage optimization models. Unlike infrastructure overhauls, this strategy can be implemented within existing technical and regulatory constraints. By redefining how utilities account for risk and loss, this approach leverages economic logic to align operational behavior with public values and medical vulnerability. In doing so, it provides a feasible and ethically grounded pathway to mitigate harm during outages and to institutionalize energy justice where it is most urgently needed.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. The current model for DME patient mortality risk assumes a constant hourly mortality rate accumulated linearly with outage duration. To examine the robustness of this assumption, we evaluated four nonlinear logistic-type mortality functions that allow mortality risk to vary with outage duration (Figure S10). These sensitivity analyses show that, although the exact numerical threshold depends on the assumed temporal profile of mortality risk, the central directional finding holds across all tested mortality functions: Incorporating the social consequences of DME interruption materially shifts the optimization toward stronger protection of patients with DME. However, these nonlinear functions remain stylized representations of health deterioration during outages. Future research should develop clinically grounded dynamic models that more directly capture the physiological impacts of prolonged power loss on medically vulnerable individuals.

As in all SP research on morally salient topics, the survey-based WTS values should be interpreted as preference indicators rather than as direct measures of real-world financial sacrifice. SP tasks may lack consequentiality and may not fully reflect real-world decisions,53 and WTS values may also be affected by social desirability bias.64 The survey design incorporates standard mitigations for this bias: anonymous online self-administration through the Prolific platform, forced numerical quantification of the income-sacrifice position rather than abstract moral endorsement, role assignments that place respondents in self-interested positions, and a 0%-mortality scenario that allowed respondents to defect from the prosocial choice without social cost. The variation across mortality-rate scenarios in Survey 1, the substantial drop in WTS at 0% mortality, and the shifts in Survey 2 responses as economic information was made more salient indicate that respondents were tracking the substantive content of the scenarios rather than producing purely framing-driven or socially desirable responses. Nevertheless, these features should be interpreted as bias-mitigating design elements rather than as formal controls that eliminate social desirability or hypothetical bias.

Additionally, our work is based on the synthetic medium-voltage distribution networks developed in,32 so the numerical results should be interpreted as case-specific rather than universally generalizable. Different cities, network topologies, load compositions, DME-user distributions, and operational settings may lead to different levels of load shedding and affected patients with DME. The formulation is intended to evaluate how the proposed SCL term changes emergency load-shedding allocations in the studied network, rather than to provide a universally generalizable estimate of outage impacts.

The utility-side objective used in this study should also not be interpreted as a universal representation of all grid operators. Public utilities, private utilities, cooperatives, and vertically integrated system operators may face different regulatory duties, financial incentives, reliability obligations, and accountability mechanisms. Because the directional effect of the SCL term is mechanistic—raising the marginal cost of shedding DME-dependent loads under any cost-minimizing formulation—the SCL term is modular with respect to operator type and transports across different operator embeddings. In investor-owned or regulated utility settings, it may enter as an internalized liability cost; in public or welfare-oriented settings, DME protection can be represented as a hard priority constraint; and in cooperative or vertically integrated settings, it may enter as a weighted equity term within the operator’s broader objective.

Finally, the revenue-loss term used in the present formulation is simplified relative to real-world utility operation. Actual utility-side financial impacts during outage events depend on electricity procurement costs, wholesale-market conditions, tariff structures, bilateral contracts, compensation mechanisms, regulatory rules, and operator-specific market arrangements. The present study therefore does not attempt to reproduce full utility financial settlement or electricity-market operation. Instead, the revenue-loss term functions as a stylized utility-side economic proxy that allows comparison of load-shedding allocations across scenarios with different economic and social cost components.

Future work should test the SCL-based formulation across multiple real and synthetic networks and embed it into more operationally realistic formulations, including multi-period emergency load-shedding models with time-varying load, DME exposure, and restoration variables; stochastic or chance-constrained formulations for uncertain outage duration, demand, renewable generation, and feeder availability; CVaR-based risk measures to limit worst-case DME exposure; dynamic-security-constrained models that couple load-shedding decisions with frequency, voltage, and protection-system behavior65,66,67; and market-aware formulations that account for operator-specific objectives and detailed electricity-market and procurement conditions. These extensions would help calibrate the SCL term across different operational, institutional, and market settings, while preserving the central purpose of the framework: making the social and legal consequences of interrupting medically vulnerable electricity users visible within power-system decision-making.

Method details

Study overview

This study integrates geospatial analysis, power system optimization, and social survey methods to investigate ethical and economic trade-offs in emergency load-shedding decisions involving electricity-dependent vulnerable populations. First, geospatial datasets, distribution network information, and HHS emPOWER data were combined to estimate the spatial distribution of DME patients and load demand across the study area. A mixed-integer second-order cone programming (MISOCP) model based on a three-phase linear DistFlow approximation was then developed to optimize load-shedding strategies under different objective functions representing economic costs, legal liabilities, and technical objectives. To complement the optimization analysis, two online surveys were conducted to examine public preferences regarding electricity prioritization under different ethical scenarios and economic conditions. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the optimization results under alternative mortality assumptions.

Geospatial data integration

DME patients mapping

The New York City Zoning and Land Use Map,30 ZIP code area data,68 and distribution network data32 were downloaded and imported into QGIS 3.36 for preprocessing. Only the load data for Manhattan and the Bronx were retained for analysis. Using the QGIS Python console, we computed the geometric centroid of each zone and mapped each load point to its corresponding ZIP code and the nearest distribution network bus based on geographic proximity. DME patient data obtained from the HHS emPOWER dataset41 provides the number of DME-dependent individuals for each ZIP code. To distribute these patients spatially, we allocated them proportionally to the residential load within each ZIP code. As a result, ZIP codes with lower total residential load but a higher number of DME patients exhibited a greater DME patient density. The processed geospatial dataset was exported in GeoPackage format for further analysis.

Social preference surveys

Survey design

The trolley problem has long been used to explore ethical decision-making in life-or-death scenarios, providing insights into how individuals prioritize moral values.46 Similarly, in the context of electricity allocation during crises, it is essential to understand how individuals weigh the life and well-being of health-vulnerable populations, such as patients dependent on DME, against the electricity consumption rights and economic considerations of other users.

Survey 1 establishes a moral dilemma requiring participants to decide whether to prioritize electricity supply to residential buildings housing an ALS patient or to a shopping mall. Participants could assume one of the following roles: a worker at a shopping mall, a community power grid operator, a bystander, a neighbor of an ALS patient, or a family member of an ALS patient. The mortality risk for the ALS patient due to power outages was systematically varied across six levels: 0%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, resulting in 30 distinct scenarios. Each respondent was randomly assigned to evaluate five of these scenarios and was asked questions assessing their WTS to benefit the ALS patient.

Survey 2 expands on the framework established in Survey 1 by presenting participants with three distinct load-shedding dilemmas:

•Cohort 1: whether to prioritize electricity supply to a shopping mall or a residential community housing an ALS patient;

Cohort 1: whether to prioritize electricity supply to a shopping mall or a residential community housing an ALS patient;

•Cohort 2: whether to prioritize a large residential community or a small residential community with an ALS patient;

Cohort 2: whether to prioritize a large residential community or a small residential community with an ALS patient;

•Cohort 3: whether to prioritize a large residential community with one ALS patient or a small residential community with two ALS patients.

Cohort 3: whether to prioritize a large residential community with one ALS patient or a small residential community with two ALS patients.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of these scenarios and initially reported their WTS for the benefit of the ALS patient(s). To enhance participants’ understanding of the potential consequences of these decisions, additional contextual information was progressively introduced in two stages. After reviewing the information at each stage, participants reassessed their WTS. The survey concluded by exploring participants’ attitudes toward making load-shedding priority a purchasable service and their perspectives on how financial costs associated with electricity prioritization should be distributed among stakeholders.

Participant recruitment and ethics

Participation was restricted to individuals who (a) were at least 18 years of age and (b) had resided in the continental United States (excluding Hawaii) for the previous five years. This study received an exemption from review by the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (IRB). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation through an online consent form. The study employed convenience sampling to recruit participants. A small-scale pilot survey was conducted on Amazon Mechanical Turk in March and April 2024 to refine the questionnaire. The final surveys were subsequently administered through the Prolific platform between June and August 2024.

Survey screening process

A total of 987 responses were collected across all versions of the Prolific online survey platform (484 for Survey 1 and 503 for Survey 2). Responses were initially screened to retain only fully completed and non-duplicated entries. Subsequently, additional filtering was applied to exclude participants who failed attention check questions. Following these quality control procedures, 856 responses were retained for the final analysis. The effective response rate was 92.6% for Survey 1 and 81.1% for Survey 2, with the lower rate in Survey 2 attributable to the inclusion of two attention check questions, compared to one in Survey 1. The full screening process is detailed in Figure S11.

Participant characteristics

Demographic information, including age, sex, education, annual personal income, self-rated health, previous power outage experience, and whether participants had friends or relatives using DME, was collected through the surveys. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table S1.

Overall, the demographic distributions were highly similar between the two surveys and broadly consistent with those of the U.S. population. Most participants were 25–34 years of age (35.5%–38.2%), held a 4-year bachelor’s degree (40.4%–41.3%), and reported an annual personal income below USD 20,000 (18.4%–20.5%). The most commonly reported self-rated health status was ”very good” (39.5%–41.1%). Most participants did not have friends or relatives using DME (76.1%–81.1%), although the majority had experienced at least one power outage since 2020 (80.6%–84.8%), and more than half (55.8%–56.6%) had experienced an outage lasting 12 h or longer.

Mortality functions robustness analyses

Alternative mortality functions

To examine whether the optimization results are robust to the assumed linear accumulation of DME-related mortality risk, we performed an additional robustness analysis using alternative outage-duration-dependent mortality functions. In the main analysis, the DME mortality term was represented by a constant hourly mortality rate accumulated linearly with outage duration. Here, we replaced this baseline with four nonlinear alternatives (Figure S10).

The alternative four functions follow a logistic-type form motivated by study43 from which the average mortality estimate used in the baseline analysis was derived. They represent cases in which mortality risk is initially low immediately after outage onset, then increases with outage duration, and eventually approaches a plateau. We considered three representative profiles, denoted high-risk, medium-risk, and low-risk, which differ in the timing of risk escalation. These three functions were calibrated such that their mortality rate at T = 24-h matches that of the baseline linear case, allowing the effect of temporal shape to be assessed while holding the 24-h endpoint constant. We further tested a fourth nonlinear case, denoted med-double, which shares the same midpoint timing as the medium-risk function but reaches a mortality rate at T = 24 h equal to twice the baseline value, to examine the effect of potential underestimation of mortality risk.

Robustness evaluation

For each mortality function, mortality coefficients corresponding to outage durations from 1 to 24 h were calculated and incorporated into the objective function of Scenario 2 while all other model parameters remained unchanged.

Model robustness was evaluated using two complementary analyses. First, the settlement cost was fixed at USD 25 million to compare the resulting number of affected DME patients under each mortality assumption. Second, for each outage duration and mortality function, the minimum settlement cost required to prevent interruptions to all DME patients was determined. The optimization outcomes obtained under the alternative mortality functions were then compared with those of the baseline model to evaluate the sensitivity of the proposed framework to different assumptions regarding outage-duration-dependent mortality risk.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Optimization analyses were performed using MATLAB R2022b. Survey data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, medians, standard deviations (SD), and standard errors of the mean (SEM), were used to summarize participant characteristics and survey responses.

For Survey 1, willingness to save (WTS) was analyzed using logistic regression, whereas the maximum percentage of daily income participants were willing to sacrifice was analyzed using linear regression. For the comparison between the 2024 and 2025 surveys, participants who completed both survey rounds were matched and a binary indicator variable representing survey year was incorporated into the regression models. Regression results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) or regression coefficients with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), where appropriate. Unless otherwise stated, statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05.
