Adaptive Power Iteration Method for Differentially Private PCA
Ta Duy Nguyen, Alina Ene, Huy L. Nguyen

Abstract
We study ( ε , δ ) \left(\varepsilon,\delta\right) -differentially private algorithms for the problem of approximately computing the top singular vector of a matrix A ∈ ℝ n × d A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d} where each row of A A is a datapoint in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . In our privacy model, neighboring inputs differ by one single row/datapoint. We study the private variant of the power iteration method, which is widely adopted in practice. Our algorithm is based on a filtering technique which adapts to the coherence parameter of the input matrix. This technique provides a utility that goes beyond the worst-case guarantees for matrices with low coherence parameter. Our work departs from and complements the work by Hardt-Roth (STOC 2013) which designed a private power iteration method for the privacy model where neighboring inputs differ in one single entry by at most 1.

Introduction

In this paper, we study
\left(\varepsilon,\delta\right)
-differentially
private algorithms for the problem of approximately computing the
top singular vector of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where each
row of
A
is a datapoint in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that constitutes the private
data of an individual. The problem of computing the top singular vector
is a fundamental primitive with many applications in theoretical computer
science, machine learning, statistics, data mining, and many other
areas. In particular, it is the main component of algorithms for widely
used algorithmic primitives such as principal component analysis (PCA)
and computing low-rank factorizations and approximations of matrices

[
BHK20
]
. Importantly, in many applications, the
input matrix records sensitive data and providing strong privacy guarantees
is a non-negotiable constraint
[
MM09
,
NS08
]
.
This important consideration has motivated the development of algorithms
that preserve the privacy of the individuals while simultaneously
constructing accurate solutions with the best possible approximation
guarantees.

The design of private algorithms for top singular vector computation
and related matrix computations has received significant attention,
leading to the development of algorithms that provide several different
levels of privacy protections
[
DTTZ14
,
HR13
,
CSS13
,
HR12
,
BDMN05
,
MM09
,
DMNS16
,
KT13
,
HP14
,
BDWY16
,
MV22
,
LKJO22
]
.
In particular, this line of work led to a complete answer to the question
of what is the best additive approximation guarantee that is achievable
in the worst case:
[
DTTZ14
]
give an algorithm
that achieves an additive approximation of
\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{d}\right)

and they showed that this guarantee is best possible up to logarithmic
factors. Notably, these results show that there is a significant cost
to ensuring privacy for worst-case data, particularly when the datapoints
are high-dimensional, which is often the case in applications. Furthermore,
this error may make the algorithms completely unusable in practice,
such as in high-dimensional data analysis applications with structured
matrices
[
HR13
]
. Fortunately, matrices arising
in applications are far from worst-case instances, which motivates
the development of algorithms that leverage the structure to achieve
improved approximation guarantees while still ensuring the same privacy
guarantees. In a highly influential work,
[
HR13
]

made significant progress in this direction
in a different
model of privacy
. Motivated by the work in matrix completion starting
with the celebrated work of
[
CT10
]
, Hardt
and Roth ask whether it is possible to achieve improved privacy guarantees
for matrices with low coherence, where the coherence value is proportional
to the maximum entry of the singular vectors of the matrix. The coherence
is a very natural parameter and it is relatively small in practically
relevant settings, such as random data as well as non-random data
arising in applications. Hardt and Roth design an algorithm that provides
privacy protection
for each individual entry of the matrix

and it achieves an error guarantee that is proportional to the coherence
instead of the dimension. Notably, this work achieves a significant
improvement over the algorithms for worst-case data in many practical
settings. However, it can only protect each
individual entry

in the matrix, and it cannot be used in our setting where there is
a much stronger privacy requirement of protecting each
entire
row
of the matrix. The stronger privacy requirement that we consider
is very natural in data analysis applications where each row is the
private data of a user
[
DTTZ14
]
. This natural
setting has attracted significant attention from the community over
the last decade
[
BDMN05
,
DMNS16
,
KT13
,
CSS13
,
DTTZ14
,
MV22
,
LKJO22
]
.
However, obtaining beyond-worst-case guarantees for it has remained
out of reach for the techniques developed in
[
HR13
]

and subsequent work
[
HP14
]
. Motivated by
these considerations, our work addresses the following question:

In the privacy model where each row of the matrix is the private
data of a user, is it possible to design an algorithm that is private
on all input matrices and achieves beyond-worst-case guarantees for
input matrices with low coherence?

In this work, we give an affirmative answer to this question. Our
algorithm does not require any knowledge of the coherence parameter
and it automatically adapts to it. In contrast to prior works, our
algorithm ensures privacy for each row via a novel adaptive filtering
technique and it achieves fine-grained utility guarantees via a new
analysis. In addition, our algorithm is a private variant of the
classical power iteration method that is widely used in practice.

While the main focus of our work is on deterministic data with beyond-worst-case
guarantees, our algorithm can be applicable to random data that come
i.i.d from a distribution. In particular, when we have i.i.d data
from a Gaussian distribution, which naturally has a low coherence
parameter, our algorithm provides guarantees that compare favorably
with the state-of-the-art results. We give an overview of our contributions
and techniques in the following subsections.

Our algorithm contributes to the broader line of work that developed
private algorithms with fine-grained and beyond-worst-case guarantees
for other fundamental optimization problems, such as private mean
estimation
[
BGS+21
,
BHS23
,
KDH23
,
BZ25
]

and regression
[
DGJ23
,
BHH+24
]
.

1.2
Our algorithm and techniques

We consider the problem with the input matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
Our algorithm follows the power iteration template with Gaussian noise
added to each update, ie

\displaystyle x^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=A^{(t)\top}A^{(t)}x^{(t)}+\text{ noise}.

In prior works,

is the same as the input matrix

in our algorithm

differ from

. Our algorithm departs
from the algorithm by
[

]
due to the change
in the differential privacy model. In the work of
[
HR13
]
,
neighboring input matrices (in this case,
A^{\top}A
and
A^{\prime}{}^{\top}A^{\prime}
)
differ in one entry by at most
1
. This model allows to bound the
sensitivity of the update by
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{\infty}
.
From here, imposing a threshold on
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{\infty}

is sufficient to guarantee the privacy of the update. On the other
hand, in our model, neighboring inputs (
A
and
A^{\prime}
) differ by
a datapoint (a row in the matrix). The
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity of
the power iteration is now
\max_{a}\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|
,
which grows with
\left\|a\right\|_{2}
. Hence, simply thresholding

x^{(t)}
does not ensure the boundedness of the sensitivity. Instead,
the main idea of our algorithm is to filter out in each iteration
datapoints with large value of
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|
.
This step brings out two challenges:

\quad\bullet
Determining the filtering threshold. A naive way is
to bound
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|\leq\left\|a\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}

and set the filtering threshold based on
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}=1

(if the algorithm normalizes
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}
).
This guarantees the privacy and still allows us to bound the utility
of the algorithm when
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\leq 1
by adding
Gaussian noise proportion to
1
. However,
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}

can be much larger than
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|
.
Indeed, consider the following thought exercise: suppose that
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}=1

and
a
comes from a Gaussian
{\cal N}(0,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}^{2}))

independent from
x^{(t)}
such that
\sigma_{1}^{2}+\dots+\sigma_{d}^{2}=1

and
\sigma_{1}>\sigma_{i}
for all
i\neq 1
. With high probability

\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|

is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\sum\sigma_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{(t)2}})\leq\widetilde{O}(\sigma_{1})

which can be significantly smaller than
\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}=1
,
for example if
\sigma_{1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}}.
This means the amount
of noise added proportional to
1
can be too large, which leads
to bad utility guarantee.

\quad\bullet
Filtering breaks the analysis by
[
HR13
]
.
One key technique from
[
HR13
]
is showing
sign symmetry of
\left\langle v_{i},x^{(t)}\right\rangle
for
\{v_{i}\}

being an eigen-basis of the input. That is the
(\mathrm{sign}(\left\langle v_{i},x^{(t)}\right\rangle))_{i=1}^{d}

is uniformly distributed in
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. This allows to bound

\left\|x^{(t)}\right\|_{\infty}
by concentration inequalities.
However, updating the iterates by the filtered matrix introduces a
noise term coming from the filtered-out rows. This noise term breaks
the independence and symmetry of the signs and the analysis from
[
HR13
]

no longer works in our case.

To overcome these challenges, we introduce adaptive filtering and
a new approach to analyze the algorithm. To filter the rows with large
value
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|
,
the algorithm uses the sparse vector technique to find the suitable
threshold in each iteration. The update becomes
x^{(t+1)}=A^{(t)\top}A^{(t)}x^{(t)}+\frac{\theta^{(t)}\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}{\varepsilon}g^{(t)}

where
A^{(t)}
is the filtered matrix,
g^{(t)}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
and

\theta^{(t)}
is the threshold (see more detail in Algorithm
1
).
This method ensures that the threshold is adaptive to the magnitude
of
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|

and the number of filtered datapoints is small,
\widetilde{O}\left(1/\varepsilon\right)
.
For practical purpose to avoid numerical issues,
x^{(t)}
can be
normalized to have length
1
. However, for simplicity of the analysis,
we do not normalize the iterates
x^{(t)}
in our algorithm.

The fact that the algorithm runs in many iterations brings additional
challenges. When the solution
x
is initialized randomly, the eigen-basis
of the input and
x
are uncorrelated. Furthermore, as we explained
above,
x
is also uncorrelated with the rows of
A
. However, after
the first power iteration update, they start to become correlated.
A serious issue arises: the maximum value of
\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|

can grow dramatically and our filtering could become ineffective.
We show that this is not the case, and the correlation between
x

and the rows of
A
can only grow slowly, and much slower than the
correlation between
x
and the top eigenvector. Therefore our filtering
is effective in all iterations and the analysis can proceed similarly
to the analysis of the power method.

We now give some intuition for the analysis. We can expect the component
of
x
along the top eigenvector grows exponentially with the top
eigenvalue of the input, while the remain components grows significantly
slower. This motivates us to study two sequences
M^{(t)}
and
m^{(t)}

defined as follows. We let
A=U\Sigma V^{\top}
be the compact SVD
decomposition of the input matrix
A
,
B=AV
be the input matrix
after the change of basis to the column space of
V
, and
y^{(t)}=V^{\top}x^{(t)}

be the projection of the iterate
x^{(t)}
onto the same space. We
define
m^{(t)}=\left|y_{1}^{(t)}\right|
be the component of
y^{(t)}

along the top eigenvector, which for simplicity, we assume to be the
first coordinate, and
M^{(t)}=\max_{b\in B}\left\langle y_{-1}^{(t)},b_{-1}\right\rangle

be the maximum dot product between
y^{(t)}
and the rows of
B

in the remaining coordinates
1
1

1

Here we simplify
M^{(t)}
, assuming that all eigenvalues of the
remaining components are equal. In the analysis, we need to take into
account the difference between them.
. Here,
m^{(t)}
and
M^{(t)}
signify the evolution of the iterates
along the top and the remaining components, and we want to show that

m^{(t)}
grows with
\sigma_{1}^{2t}
and
M^{(t)}
grows significantly
slower. We will track the relation between the two quantities by using
an upper bound for
M^{(t)}
and a lower bound for
m^{(t)}
. A
simplified recurrence between them is as follows, where we omit the
term
\sigma_{1}\Upsilon

\displaystyle M^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\leq(\sigma_{2}^{2}+\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}))M^{(t)}+\widetilde{O}(\frac{\sigma_{1}\Upsilon}{\varepsilon})m^{(t)}+\text{ noise}.

\displaystyle m^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\geq(\sigma_{1}^{2}-\widetilde{O}(\frac{\sigma_{1}\Upsilon}{\varepsilon}))m^{(t)}-\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})M^{(t)}+\text{ noise.}

These bounds are established using the concentration of Gaussian distributions.
We will show that if the gap
\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}
is sufficiently
large,
m^{(t+1)}
grows as fast as
\sigma_{1}^{2t}
and
M^{(t+1)}

grows as
\sigma_{2}^{2t}
instead. After establishing the growth
rate of
M^{(t)}
, we bound the growth of each individual component

\left|y_{i}^{(t)}\right|
using the same recurrence style and finally
show the convergence and the error of the approximation.

Differential Privacy
. We consider the following privacy model.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that two matrices

are neighboring

and
A^{\prime}
differ by one row. A randomized algorithm

is said to be

A,A^{\prime}
and every subset of possible outcomes

\displaystyle\Pr\left[{\cal M}(A)\in{\cal O}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr\left[{\cal M}(A^{\prime})\in{\cal O}\right]+\delta.

When
\delta=0
, we say the algorithm is
\varepsilon
-DP.

The Gaussian mechanism
[
DKM+06
]
is a commonly used approach
to achieve differential privacy.

Theorem 2.1

(Gaussian mechanism,
[
DKM+06
]
)
.

Let

be a function of
\ell_{2}
-sensitivity

and

\left\|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\right\|_{2}\leq k
.
The algorithm
{\cal M}
that on an input

where

to

is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.

Our algorithm also makes use of the Sparse Vector Technique, in particular,
the AboveThreshold algorithm
[
DR14
]
.
This algorithm takes in a set of queries with sensitivity 1 and a
threshold
\theta
and answers with differential privacy if any query
value exceeds the threshold, by adding Laplace noise to both the threshold
and the query values. We provide the utility guarantee of this algorithm
in the analysis of our algorithm in Section

.

For the analysis of our algorithm, we use the following well-known

]
.

Lemma 2.1

(

3.1

In this section, we show the privacy guarantee of Algorithm

.
This is a straightforward application of the privacy guarantee of
the AboveThreshold algorithm (see for example
[
DR14
]
)
and the Gaussian mechanism.

Lemma 3.1
.

Algorithm
1
is
(\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
-DP
for
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\left(T\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}T}\varepsilon\right)

and
\delta^{\prime}=(T+1)\delta

Proof.

In each iteration, the algorithm executes two steps. The first step
is to determine the threshold
\theta
. Since all queries
Q_{k}

has sensitivity
1
and the algorithm adds Laplace noise to both

and the query

\left|Q_{k}\right|

AboveThreshold algorithm, which is
\varepsilon
-DP. The second step
is the update of the solution

. We show that this step is

-DP.

First, consider the sensitivity of

. For two input

with

having an extra row

on the output

of all previous iterations being identical
when the input is

or

sensitivity of

is given by

\displaystyle\left\|aa^{\top}x^{(t)}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|a\right\|_{2}\left|\left\langle a,x^{(t)}\right\rangle\right|\leq\theta^{(t)}.

Therefore, adding noise
\frac{2\theta^{(t)}\sqrt{\log\frac{2}{\delta}}}{\varepsilon}g^{(t)}

where

ensures that the update is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.

By composition over

.

\displaystyle\text{and }\left|y_{1}^{(t+1)}\right|

Proof overview
. The privacy guarantee of Algorithm

is shown in Lemma

of the analysis for the utility guarantee, i.e., show the upper bound
on the error

in Theorem

and the previous case with deterministic data is that the error is
measured with respect to the underlying distribution, instead of the
sample dataset. Another challenge is that to obtain an explicit guarantee,
we need to obtain a bound for the coherence parameter of the data,
which appear in the guarantee of Theorem

To upper bound

upper bound the error

with the top eigenvector

(Lemma

of

\overline{v}_{1}
of the
covariance
\overline{\Sigma}^{2}

We can bound this error using concentration inequalities, specifically
the matrix Bernstein inequality, and Wedin’s

The second error

of the solution with respect to the empirical data, which we have
bounded in the previous section. To justify that we can use the bound
from Theorem

, we show that the data when sampled from
a gaussian distribution with

has length

The complete proof is provided in Appendix

Theorems

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\langle g,x\right\rangle^{2}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle g,x\right\rangle\left\langle g,\overline{\Sigma}^{2}x\right\rangle\right]+x^{\top}\overline{\Sigma}^{4}x

We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\langle g,x\right\rangle^{2}\right]=\sum_{i,j,k}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}g_{j}\right]x_{i}x_{j}

Note that, for any two indices
i\neq j
, using that different coordinates
of
g
are independent, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}g_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=i}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}g_{j}\right]+\sum_{k=j}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}g_{j}\right]+\sum_{k\neq i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}g_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=i}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{3}\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{j}\right]}_{=0}+\sum_{k=j}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\right]}_{=0}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{j}^{3}\right]+\sum_{k\neq i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}g_{j}\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\right]}_{=0}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{j}\right]}_{=0}

\displaystyle=0

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\langle g,x\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}g_{i}^{2}\right]x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{3}\right]}_{=0}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{k\neq i}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{k}^{2}\right]}_{=\overline{\sigma}_{k}^{2}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}\right]}_{=\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}x_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)

where we have used that
\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{3}\right]=0
by the symmetry
of the Gaussian PDF and that
\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Var}\left[g_{i}\right]=\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
.

Since for
i\neq j
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}g_{j}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[g_{j}\right]=0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle g,x\right\rangle\left\langle g,\overline{\Sigma}^{2}x\right\rangle\right]=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}g_{j}\right]x_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{j}^{2}x_{j}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{2}\right]\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{4}x_{i}^{2}

Therefore

\displaystyle x^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(gg^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]x

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\langle g,x\right\rangle^{2}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle g,x\right\rangle\left\langle g,\overline{\Sigma}^{2}x\right\rangle\right]+x^{\top}\overline{\Sigma}^{4}x

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)-2\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{4}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{4}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\sigma_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\right)

Thus

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\left(gg^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\right\|_{2}=\max_{x\colon\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq 1}x^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(gg^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]x\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)

as needed.
∎

We now recall the matrix Bernstein inequality
[
Tro12
]
.

Theorem B.1

(Matrix Bernstein inequality, Theorem 1.4)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

be independent, random, self-adjoint matrices. Assume that each random
matrix satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\right]=0\text{ and }\left\|X_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq R\text{ almost surely}.

Then, for all
t\geq 0
,

\Pr\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\right\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{M+Rt/3}\right)\text{ where }M=\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}^{2}\right]\right\|_{2}.

Using the matrix Bernstein inequality together with the guarantee
in Lemma
B.1
, we obtain the following
lemma.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\beta>0
. With probability
at least
1-\beta
, we have

\left\|A^{\top}A-n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}\leq G

where
G=\max\left\{O\left(\sqrt{n\cdot\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\log\frac{d}{\beta}}\right),O\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\log\frac{n}{\beta}\log\frac{d}{\beta}\right)\right\}
.

Proof.

Recall that the rows of
A
are iid samples
g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}

from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\overline{\Sigma}^{2})
.
Thus we have
A^{\top}A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}
.

Let
C
be an absolute constant as in Lemma
B.1
.
Let
R=C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\log\frac{2n}{\beta}
.
Consider the following matrices
\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
:

X_{i}=\begin{cases}g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}&\text{if }\left\|g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}\leq R\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Let us now show that Lemma
B.1
implies
the following:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=A^{\top}A-n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\text{ with probability }1-\frac{\beta}{2}

(25)

and

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{2}\right]\right\|_{2}\leq M=n\cdot\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)

(26)

To show (
25
), we apply Lemma
B.1

with probability parameter
\frac{\beta}{2n}
, and obtain that
\Pr\left[\left\|g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}\geq R\right]\leq\frac{\beta}{2n}
.
By taking an union bound over these
n
evens, we obtain that
\Pr\left[\exists i\colon\left\|g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}\geq R\right]\leq\frac{\beta}{2}
.
Thus, with probability at least
1-\frac{\beta}{2}
, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right)=A^{\top}A-n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}
.

To show (
26
), we note that, since the
g_{i}
are iid samples
from
\mathcal{N}(0,\overline{\Sigma}^{2})
, we have

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g_{i}g_{i}^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\right\|_{2}=n\cdot\left\|\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\overline{\Sigma}^{2})}\left[\left(gg^{\top}-\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]\right\|_{2}\leq n\cdot\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right),

where the inequality follows from Lemma
B.1
.

Note that the matrices
X_{i}
are symmetric. Additionally, we have

\left\|X_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq R
by definition and
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq M

by (
26
). Thus, by the matrix Bernstein inequality (Theorem

B.1
), for all
t\geq 0
,

\Pr\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right\|_{2}\geq t\right]\leq d\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{M+Rt/3}\right).

For
t=G:=\max\left\{\sqrt{2M\log\frac{2d}{\beta}},6R\log\frac{2d}{\beta}\right\}
,
the right-hand side of the inequality above is at most
\frac{\beta}{2}
.
By combining with (
25
), we obtain that, with probability
at least
1-\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|A^{\top}A-n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G=\max\left\{\sqrt{2M\log\frac{2d}{\beta}},6R\log\frac{2d}{\beta}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{O\left(\sqrt{n\cdot\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\log\frac{d}{\beta}}\right),O\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right)\log\frac{n}{\beta}\log\frac{d}{\beta}\right)\right\}

∎

\displaystyle\left\|A^{\top}A-n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\beta>0

C\sqrt{\log\frac{n}{\beta}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\beta>0
. With probability
at least
1-\beta

\left\|U\right\|_{\infty}\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{\log\frac{n}{\beta}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

Since the rows of
A

for an orthogonal matrix

Let

u
of

and

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}\leq n-2\sqrt{n\log\frac{n^{2}}{\beta}}\right]

We recall Weyl’s inequality.

Theorem B.4

(Weyl’s inequality)

Let
\widehat{\Sigma}=\Sigma+E

\Sigma

Lemma B.5
.

Suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}=1

\left\|A^{\top}A-n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}\right\|_{2}\leq K_{3}\cdot\max\left\{\sqrt{n}\overline{\sigma}_{1},1\right\}

We apply Weyl’s theorem (Theorem
B.4

\Sigma=n\overline{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle\left|\sigma_{i}^{2}-n\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{2}\right|

T=3K_{2}\frac{1}{\overline{\kappa}}
. Since
\kappa\geq\frac{1}{3}\overline{\kappa}
,
the second condition is satisfied. Finally, consider the third condition.
Since
\sigma_{1}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}n\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and

\Upsilon\leq K_{4}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, we have

\displaystyle 4\left(\frac{K}{\varepsilon\sigma_{1}}\Upsilon+\frac{K^{2}}{\varepsilon\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}KK_{4}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}n}+\frac{2K_{4}^{2}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}n}\right)\leq 8\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{2}KK_{4}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}n},\frac{2K_{4}^{2}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}n}\right\}

Thus, to satisfy the third condition, it suffices to ensure that
n\geq d

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\overline{\kappa}

\displaystyle\geq 8\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{2}KK_{4}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}n},\frac{2K_{4}^{2}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}n}\right\}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle n\geq 24\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{2}KK_{4}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}\overline{\kappa}},\frac{2K_{4}^{2}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\overline{\kappa}}\right\}

Thus, all conditions are satisfied provided that

n\geq\max\left\{d,\frac{24\sqrt{2}KK_{4}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}\overline{\kappa}},\frac{48K_{4}^{2}}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\overline{\kappa}}\right\}=\widetilde{O}\left(d+\frac{1}{\varepsilon\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\overline{\kappa}}\right).

By applying Corollary
1.1
with probability parameter
\frac{\beta}{4}
,
we obtain that, with probability at least
1-\frac{\beta}{4}
, we
have (for
K_{5}=\widetilde{O}(1)
):

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\left(v_{1},x\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{5}\cdot\left(\frac{\min\left\{4n/\sigma_{1}^{2},d\right\}}{\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\kappa^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\kappa^{3}}+\frac{d}{\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\kappa}\right)\Upsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{5}\cdot\left(\frac{2d}{\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\kappa^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\kappa^{3}}\right)\Upsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K_{4}K_{5}\cdot\left(\frac{4\cdot 3^{2}d}{\varepsilon^{2}n\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\overline{\kappa}^{2}}+\frac{2\cdot 3^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}n\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{2}\overline{\kappa}^{3}}\right)\frac{1}{n}

where we used that

,
\kappa\geq\frac{1}{3}\overline{\kappa}
,
and

.

∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Using standard arguments, we can show the following lemma.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
u,v,w
be unit length vectors. We have

\sin^{2}\left(u,w\right)\leq 2\left(\sin^{2}\left(u,v\right)+\sin^{2}\left(v,w\right)\right)
.

Theorem
1.2
now follows by combining Theorem
B.3
,
Theorem
B.5
, and Lemma
B.6
.

Proof of Lemma
B.6
.

Using the identity
\sin^{2}\left(a,b\right)=1-\cos^{2}\left(a,b\right)=1-\left(\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\right)^{2}
,
we can see that the inequality is equivalent to

2\left(\left\langle u,v\right\rangle\right)^{2}+2\left(\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\right)^{2}\leq 3+\left(\left\langle u,w\right\rangle\right)^{2}

Let
a=\left\langle u,w\right\rangle
and
u=aw+u_{\perp}
where

u_{\perp}
is a vector orthogonal to
w
, i.e.
\left\langle u_{\perp},w\right\rangle=0
.
Similarly, let
b=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
and
v=bw+v_{\perp}
,

\left\langle v_{\perp},w\right\rangle=0
. We have

\displaystyle\left\langle u,v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle aw+u_{\perp},bw+v_{\perp}\right\rangle=ab\underbrace{\left\|w\right\|^{2}}_{=1}+a\underbrace{\left\langle w,v_{\perp}\right\rangle}_{=0}+b\underbrace{\left\langle w,u_{\perp}\right\rangle}_{=0}+\left\langle u_{\perp},v_{\perp}\right\rangle=ab+\left\langle u_{\perp},v_{\perp}\right\rangle

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\left\langle u_{\perp},v_{\perp}\right\rangle\leq\left\|u_{\perp}\right\|\left\|v_{\perp}\right\|

Since the vectors are unit length, we have

\underbrace{\left\|u\right\|^{2}}_{=1}=a^{2}\underbrace{\left\|w\right\|^{2}}_{=1}+\left\|u_{\perp}\right\|^{2}+2a\underbrace{\left\langle w,u_{\perp}\right\rangle}_{=0}\Rightarrow\left\|u_{\perp}\right\|=\sqrt{1-a^{2}}

Similarly,
\left\|v_{\perp}\right\|=\sqrt{1-b^{2}}
. Thus

\left\langle u_{\perp},v_{\perp}\right\rangle\leq\sqrt{\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\right)}

Thus it suffices to prove that the following inequality holds for
all
a,b\in\left[-1,1\right]
:

\displaystyle 2\left(ab+\sqrt{\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\right)}\right)^{2}+2b^{2}\leq 3+a^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 4a^{2}b^{2}+4ab\sqrt{\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\right)}\leq 1+3a^{2}

If
ab\leq 0
, since
a^{2}\leq 1
and
b^{2}\leq 1
, we have

4a^{2}b^{2}+4ab\sqrt{\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\right)}\leq 4a^{2}b^{2}=a^{2}b^{2}+3a^{2}b^{2}\leq 1+3a^{2}

Therefore we may assume that
ab\geq 0
. Moreover, we can assume that

a\geq 0
and
b\geq 0
, since substituting
a=-\left|a\right|
and

b=-\left|b\right|
leaves the inequality unchanged. Thus we can
write
a=\cos\alpha
and
b=\cos\beta
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\left[0,\pi/2\right]
.
Using that
\sin\alpha,\sin\beta\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle ab+\sqrt{\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(1-b^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sqrt{\sin^{2}\alpha\sin^{2}\beta}

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\left|\sin\alpha\right|\left|\sin\beta\right|

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\alpha-\beta\right)

Thus the inequality becomes

\displaystyle 2\cos^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)+2\cos^{2}\beta\leq 3+\cos^{2}\alpha

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1-\cos^{2}\alpha\leq 2\left(1-\cos^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)+1-\cos^{2}\beta\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\alpha\leq 2\left(\sin^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)+\sin^{2}\beta\right)

Using the identity
\sin\left(x+y\right)=\sin x\cos y+\cos x\sin y

and the inequalities
\left(x+y\right)^{2}\leq 2\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)

and
\cos^{2}x\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\alpha

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta+\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sin\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\cos\beta+\cos\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\sin\beta\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\sin^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\cos^{2}\beta+\cos^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)\sin^{2}\beta\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\sin^{2}\left(\alpha-\beta\right)+\sin^{2}\beta\right)

as needed.
∎
