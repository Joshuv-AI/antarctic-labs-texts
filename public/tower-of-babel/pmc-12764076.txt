Comparative analysis of breast cancer care models and patient journeys across six European countries: implications for the Austrian healthcare system
Authors: Nicole Grössmann-Waniek, Michaela Riegelnegg, Claudia Wild, Christoph Strohmaier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe increasing prevalence of breast cancer is straining hospital-centred healthcare systems worldwide, including Austria’s. This study examined breast cancer care models across six European countries to identify alternative approaches for improving resource use and care delivery in Austria and other hospital-centred countries.MethodsA mixed-methods approach combined a scoping review with expert consultations (n=17) across Austria, Germany, Denmark, Sweden, the Netherlands, and Belgium. The analysis mapped breast cancer patient journeys through five phases from diagnosis to follow-up care and compared healthcare infrastructures, service provision, and professional roles across different care settings. Expert consultations were conducted through online meetings (n=11) and written responses (n=6), with at least two experts from each country representing diverse professional backgrounds.ResultsThree care models were identified: (1) Centralised Specialist Model with accredited centres and strict volume requirements (Austria, Denmark, Belgium); (2) Decentralised Model utilising certified hospitals with national quality standards (Germany); and (3) Network-based Model combining specialist centres with regional facilities (Netherlands, Sweden). The models showed significant differences in surgical settings, with Sweden performing procedures exclusively in outpatient settings, while Austria and Germany relied on inpatient care. Care coordination mechanisms varied substantially, from Sweden’s mandatory contact nurses to Germany’s strong outpatient oncologist networks. All models maintained quality through regulation and accreditation but differed significantly in their approach to accessibility, cross-sectoral coordination, and resource allocation.ConclusionFindings indicate opportunities for hospital-centred healthcare systems to optimise cancer care through strategic integration of centralised and network-based elements. Success factors include addressing workforce shortages, particularly among specialised nurses, enhancing data integration, and strengthening cross-sectoral coordination. Implementing hybrid care models that combine centralised expertise with distributed service delivery could significantly improve both the efficiency and accessibility of breast cancer care across different healthcare systems, while accounting for specific national contexts and existing infrastructures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13737-y.Keywords: Breast cancer care, Healthcare models, Cross-sectoral care integration, European healthcare systems, Centralised specialist model, Network-based care, Hospital-centric healthcare, Cancer care pathways, Resource allocation

Introduction

Cancer presents a growing global healthcare challenge, with increasing incidence and prevalence observed across developed nations. This trend is driven by demographic ageing, extended life expectancy, environmental exposures, and expanded screening programmes [1, 2]. Among women, breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer and represents a major driver of this burden. In recent decades, outcomes for patients with breast cancer have improved substantially, with five-year relative survival rates in high-income countries now exceeding 90% [3]. Beyond survival, quality of life (QoL) and patient-reported outcomes have become essential indicators of effective breast cancer care. Multidisciplinary care, rehabilitation, and psychosocial support can help preserve QoL during and after treatment [4, 5], while the routine use of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) has been linked to better symptom management and, in some cases, improved survival [4, 6].

In Europe, several countries—including Austria—are experiencing sustained increases in breast cancer incidence. In Austria, for example, 81,174 new cases were reported in 2018, and by 2020, breast cancer accounted for 28% of all female cancer diagnoses [1, 7]. More broadly, cancer incidence is projected to continue rising globally, with some countries forecasting increases of up to 39% by 2030 compared to 2014 levels [8]. This growing burden is expected to place additional pressure on healthcare systems that are already managing high per capita cancer care costs. In Austria, for instance, cancer care expenditures exceeded the European Union average, amounting to €440 per capita in 2018 compared to the EU average of €326 [1]. One of the key challenges lies in the continued reliance on hospital-based treatment models, which hinder the shift toward more accessible, community-based care. This issue is not unique to Austria but is observed across other countries with similarly structured healthcare systems, where inpatient settings still dominate despite efforts to expand outpatient services [9–11].

Such structural inefficiencies, combined with rising incidence and increasingly complex treatments, have contributed to the sharp growth of cancer-related healthcare expenditures across Europe. Between 1995 and 2023, direct spending on cancer care more than doubled—from €62 billion to €146 billion—while total economic burden rose by 43% to €228 billion, despite a decline in indirect costs as survival improved. Per-capita spending in 2023 varied nearly threefold across Europe, averaging €260 per person, or 6.6% of total health expenditure [12]. These trends underline the need not only for sufficient investment but also for more efficient use of resources. In Austria, Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) points for oncology more than tripled between 2010 and 2019, outpacing other sectors [8]. Although Austria’s National Cancer Framework recognises the need for reform, implementation is hampered by fragmented funding—a challenge also common in other European countries, where binding mechanisms for equitable resource allocation remain limited [1].

These systemic and financial pressures—coupled with persistent fragmentation in service delivery—underscore the need for more integrated and sustainable models of cancer care delivery. Given the growing strain on inpatient services and the imperative to improve both clinical and patient-reported outcomes, it is essential to explore alternative, cross-sectoral models of cancer care that optimise resource use and enhance care quality. This study aimed to investigate models of cancer care in different European countries, with a particular focus on multidisciplinary coordination across sectors in breast cancer care. We sought to provide an overview of innovative care models from selected European countries—including Austria, Germany, the Scandinavian countries, the Netherlands, and Belgium—by examining care infrastructures, service settings, and the roles of relevant professional groups. We also analysed how breast cancer care in Austria differs from that in the selected countries in terms of healthcare structures, involved professions, and the organisation of services across inpatient, outpatient, and hospital outpatient settings, with special attention to cross-sectoral coordination.

Methods

Approach

A mixed-methods approach was employed, combining a scoping review with expert consultations to address the research questions. The study focused on challenges in hospital-centred cancer care and examined cross-sectoral care models, with particular emphasis on healthcare infrastructures and the roles of health professionals across the breast cancer care continuum. The scoping review included targeted searches of scientific publications, grey literature, and national policy documents to identify organisational features, structural models, and care delivery strategies relevant to breast cancer services in selected European countries.

For the purposes of this study, a care model is defined as the overarching structural framework through which breast cancer care is organised and delivered within a given country. This includes factors such as the degree of centralisation, the presence of accreditation or certification systems, and the extent of integration between care settings (e.g. centralised specialist models, decentralised models with certification, or network-based models).

In contrast, the breast cancer patient journey refers to the sequence of care phases experienced by individuals diagnosed with breast cancer, from diagnosis through treatment and follow-up care. This concept was used as an analytical framework to assess how and where specific services are delivered across different healthcare infrastructures.

Country selection process

An iterative search process was conducted to identify countries with established or emerging cross-sectoral breast cancer care infrastructures. In addition, we focused on countries with healthcare systems broadly comparable to Austria, particularly those operating under a Social Health Insurance (SHI) model. This process initially prioritised eight countries: Austria, Germany, Denmark, Finland, Sweden, Netherlands, Norway, and Belgium, based on healthcare system comparability and potential for alternative care models. Due to a lack of responses to our requests in the expert recruitment phase, six countries were ultimately included in the final analysis: Austria, Germany, Denmark, Sweden, Netherlands, and Belgium.

Patient journey development

A patient journey framework was developed based on evidence-based clinical guidelines and relevant literature, identifying key phases from diagnosis to follow-up care. This framework was initially developed in consultation with an oncologist and subsequently reviewed and refined by a multidisciplinary group of clinical experts to ensure completeness and applicability. It was then adapted to reflect the care structures of each country included in the study. The final patient journey framework was visually represented using Canva, a web-based graphic design platform. Canva was used to develop a clear, visual illustration of the sequential phases of breast cancer care and to map the corresponding medical services across care settings for each country.

Data collection

Expert consultation process

A structured questionnaire addressing general cancer care organisation and decision-making processes guided the expert consultations (see Supplementary Material, Chapter 4). Of the 86 clinical experts and professional societies contacted, 17 experts from six European countries participated either through online consultations (n = 11) or written responses (n = 6). Between two and three experts contributed per country. The consulted experts included oncologists, breast cancer surgeons, cancer care nurses and, in the case of the Netherlands, nurse practitioners, as well as representatives from national cancer societies and, where applicable, breast cancer organisations. The composition of experts varied slightly between countries depending on the organisation of local care systems and professional availability. Experts provided policy documents and relevant literature, with additional publications identified through a snowball approach. All experts were informed about the aims of the study and provided consent to participate; online consultations were recorded with permission, and each expert reviewed the compiled information for accuracy.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As the research involved only expert consultations and did not include patients, interventions, or the collection of personal or sensitive data, it did not fall within the scope of ethics committee review under Austrian regulations. Accordingly, no ethics approval was required.

Data analysis and synthesis

Data from expert consultations were synthesised into narrative country profiles, supported by relevant literature. These included descriptions of healthcare structures, professional roles, and service delivery across the cancer care continuum. The previously developed patient journey framework served as the basis for mapping care pathways in each country, allowing for visual comparison. A cross-country analysis was then conducted to identify structural differences and commonalities in cross-sectoral coordination and breast cancer care delivery.

Austria

Care model:Centralised specialist model

Breast cancer care in Austria is structured around a centralised specialist model, organised through a three-tier system comprising Oncology Reference Centres (ONKZ, n = 14), Specialist Centres (ONKS, n = 26), and Associated Oncology Centres (ONKA, n = 33) [8, 66]. While these centres follow specific structural requirements, certification is overseen by the Austrian Certification Commission (ÖZK). In 2022, over 5,300 breast cancer cases were treated in ÖZK-accredited centres, which account for the majority of the 5,700 annual breast cancer diagnoses in Austria. Certification standards require minimum case volumes: 100 primary cases annually for independent centres and 50 for affiliated partner sites within certified breast health centres, as defined by the ÖZK [13–15].

Austria’s cancer care is guided by the National Cancer Framework Programme (2014), which outlines six strategic goals, including prevention, treatment quality, access, and research [42]. However, implementation faces challenges due to fragmented financing between federal and state levels [1]. Austria’s electronic health records system, ELGA, is set for expansion under the 2024 e-health strategy, though the country lacks a centralised cross-sectoral care coordination network [51, 52]. Regarding the role of cancer nurses, their tasks in Austria are generally limited to general medical duties, with less emphasis on care coordination [34–36].

Belgium

Care model: centralised specialist model

Belgium follows a centralised specialist model for breast cancer care, formalised through the 2008 Royal Decree, which established standards for breast cancer centre recognition based on EUSOMA guidelines. This decree defines criteria for both coordinating and satellite clinics, including minimum volume thresholds of 125 surgeries per year for coordinating clinics and 60 for satellite clinics. By 2022, Belgium had 51 coordinating and 20 satellite clinics, though 20% of patients still received treatment outside these accredited centres [27, 28].

The Belgian National Cancer Plan (2009) introduced a wide range of measures to enhance care through multidisciplinary approaches, psychosocial support, and improved data collection [49]. The Belgian Cancer Registry (mandated since 2003) monitors patient outcomes and collaborates with international databases [67, 68]. In Belgium, the electronic health records system is based on individual hospital systems linked through a national eHealth Platform, though outpatient providers still face limited access [28, 40, 60]. Recent trends in Belgium include legally regulated home-based treatment initiatives, where cancer nurses play a key role in delivering therapies and ensuring continuity of care beyond the hospital setting [40, 41].

Denmark

Care model: centralised specialist model

Denmark has adopted a centralised model for breast cancer care, where oncology departments within hospitals provide certified care rather than standalone centres [31–33]. After research in the 1990s revealed lower survival rates and higher cancer incidence compared to neighbouring countries, Denmark introduced Cancer Care Pathways (Pakkeforløb) in 2005, which included treatment algorithms and clear timeframes for diagnostics and treatment [50]. To support ongoing quality monitoring and system evaluation, Denmark maintains a national Cancer Registry (Danish Cancer Registry, DCR), which has collected data on all incident cancers since 1943. In addition, the Danish Quality Database for Breast Cancer collects detailed clinical data with over 95% coverage and tracks key quality indicators to inform care improvement [69–73].

The country has implemented four National Cancer Plans since 2000, progressively centralising breast cancer surgery. Hospitals are required to meet minimum volume standards (150 surgeries per department, 50 per surgeon annually) [18–22]. However, care coordination remains a challenge, which has led to the introduction of rehabilitation coordinators to improve cross-sectoral coherence. Denmark’s eHealth Portal (sundhed.dk) provides access to medical records, though cross-sectoral data exchange is still limited [57]. Current trends include increasing utilisation of outpatient chemotherapy and home treatment initiatives [31–33, 37, 61]. Cancer nurses in Denmark have significantly expanded their roles, particularly in the area of care coordination, helping bridge gaps between different care settings and providing long-term patient support [31–33, 37].

Germany

Care model: decentralised model with certification

Germany follows a decentralised model with certification in breast cancer care, guided by a three-tier certification system developed by the German Cancer Society (DKG), comprising Organ Cancer Centres (C), Oncology Centres (CC), and Comprehensive Cancer Centres (CCC) [16, 29]. According to the Krankenhausreport 2024, 13.8% of breast cancer surgeries in 2021 were performed in non-certified hospitals, which accounted for 42.6% of all facilities conducting such surgeries [74]. Unlike many other countries, outpatient haematologists and oncologists play a pivotal role in care delivery, operating under agreements with statutory health insurance via the regional Associations of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (Kassenärztliche Vereinigungen) [75, 76].

Germany’s approach to breast cancer care is guided by a three-tier certification system developed by the German Cancer Society (DKG), comprising Organ Cancer Centres (C), Oncology Centres (CC), and Comprehensive Cancer Centres (CCC). According to the Krankenhausreport 2024, 13.8% of breast cancer surgeries in 2021 were performed in non-certified hospitals. These hospitals accounted for 42.6% of all facilities performing breast cancer surgeries. Unlike many other countries, outpatient haematologists and oncologists play a pivotal role in care delivery, forming agreements with statutory health insurance associations.

Germany’s cancer care is shaped by the National Cancer Plan (2008) and the Disease Management Programme (DMP) for breast cancer, which has been periodically revised since its introduction in 2002 [43–45]. With the Hospital Care Improvement Act (KHVVG), the country is moving toward centralisation, aiming to reduce the number of hospitals performing breast cancer surgeries from approximately 700 to 300 within two years [29, 30]. Germany’s electronic health records system is institutional, but the Digital Act (2025) will establish universal electronic patient files, consolidating clinical findings and communications [53–56]. In terms of nursing roles, cancer nurses in Germany primarily perform general medical tasks, with limited involvement in care coordination, though this may evolve with ongoing reforms in care delivery [16, 17].

Netherlands

Care model: Network-based model

Unlike many countries, the Netherlands does not have a comprehensive national cancer plan. Instead, care is coordinated through the Dutch Cancer Agenda and regional oncology networks (Network-Based Model) [48]. Cancer care standards are defined by SONCOS (Dutch Foundation for Oncological Collaboration), which requires hospitals to perform at least 50 breast cancer surgeries annually with multidisciplinary teams [24–26]. These surgeries are mainly conducted in peripheral hospitals, with academic centres providing support through dual affiliations and online consultations [25]. Although these collaborative structures are well established, the total number of hospitals performing breast cancer surgery is not officially specified. A major challenge remains the lack of a unified electronic health records system, resulting in fragmented clinical information across institutions [24, 25]. Nonetheless, the Netherlands Cancer Registry, established in 1989, ensures comprehensive cancer data collection through pseudonymised linkage of medical records [48, 77].

Nursing roles have evolved substantially. Since 2012, nurse practitioners have been legally authorised to prescribe treatments and perform procedures independently [64, 65]. These expanded roles enhance continuity and patient-centred care, particularly in breast cancer pathways [24, 25, 78]. Digital decision aids are increasingly used to support shared decision-making between patients and clinicians [24, 63].

Sweden

Care model: Network-based model

Sweden’s approach to breast cancer care reflects a network-based model integrated into its decentralised healthcare system. The country is divided into six healthcare regions, each anchored by at least one university hospital [23, 46]. Breast cancer care is delivered through hospital units, and the number of hospitals performing breast cancer surgery has reduced from 64 to 33 since 2009, reflecting a shift toward centralisation in high-volume centres. There are no formal minimum volume requirements for breast cancer surgeries in Sweden [23].

Sweden’s National Cancer Strategy (2009) introduced national guidelines and standardised Cancer Care Pathways, supported by six Regional Cancer Centers (RCCs), to ensure quality assurance [46, 47]. A particularly distinctive feature of the Swedish system is the mandatory assignment of a contact nurse at diagnosis. These nurses are key in coordinating the patient’s care pathway, offering clinical and emotional support throughout diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up [23, 38, 39].

Sweden is also expanding outpatient services and investing in nurse-led clinics and digital tools to enhance accessibility and continuity of care [23, 46, 62]. Although Sweden maintains national cancer data through the Swedish Cancer Registry, quality assurance and care planning are primarily coordinated by the RCCs. Despite a national Health Information Exchange platform, implementation varies locally, which limits comprehensive cross-sectoral data access [58, 59].

Diagnosis

Physical examinations are commonly conducted in hospital outpatient clinics and outpatient practices in most countries, with only Belgium relying exclusively on hospital outpatient clinics [23–25, 31–37, 39–41, 78]. Germany demonstrates a broader approach by incorporating external radiologic centres and outpatient oncologists [16, 17]. Blood sampling and analysis practices vary, with some countries using a combination of external and internal laboratories, while Sweden also integrates home treatment options. Diagnostic imaging is generally performed in hospital outpatient clinics and external radiologic centres, except in the Netherlands, where it is exclusively conducted in hospital outpatient clinics. Biopsies, histology, and genetic analyses follow similar patterns, with Germany relying on outpatient oncologist practices and Austria utilising external laboratories as additional infrastructures [16, 17, 23–25, 31–37, 39–41, 78].

These diagnostic patterns reflect the underlying care models. Centralised specialist countries (Austria, Belgium, Denmark) concentrate diagnostic services within hospital settings, while in Germany’s decentralised model, these services are conducted by multiple certified providers. The network-based approaches in Sweden and the Netherlands maintain core diagnostic capabilities in hospitals while strategically extending certain services to community settings, facilitating faster and close-to-home entry points into the care pathway while maintaining quality control.

Therapy options and management

The transition from diagnosis to treatment planning marks a critical phase in a breast cancer patient’s journey. Multidisciplinary expertise is required to support evidence-based decisions. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings—also referred to as tumour boards—are typically held in hospital outpatient clinics across all countries. In Germany, outpatient oncologists also participate in MDT meetings through their involvement in outpatient specialist practices.

Surgical interventions vary across countries. In Sweden, breast cancer surgeries are carried out exclusively in hospital outpatient clinics without overnight stays [23, 39]. In Germany and Austria, all surgeries are performed in inpatient hospital settings and generally require at least one overnight stay [16, 17, 34–36]. In Belgium, Denmark, and the Netherlands, both hospital outpatient and inpatient settings are used. The choice of setting depends on clinical factors such as the complexity of the surgery and individual patient needs [24, 25, 31, 32, 37, 40, 41, 78].

Radiotherapy and medicinal therapies—such as chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and targeted treatments—are primarily administered in hospital outpatient clinics. In addition, some countries, including Germany and Sweden, also provide these therapies in outpatient specialist practices and through home-based care [16, 17, 23–25, 31–37, 39–41, 78].

The organisation of therapy management across countries illustrates how different care models balance specialisation with accessibility. In Sweden’s network-based model, breast cancer surgery is fully outpatient-based, aligned with national efforts to reduce hospital stays and improve resource efficiency through same-day procedures. By contrast, in the centralised specialist models of Austria and Belgium, complex interventions remain largely inpatient-based, with care concentrated in specialised centres. Germany’s decentralised model, involving certified hospitals and outpatient oncologists, offers multiple access points for treatment planning while maintaining defined quality standards through certification.

Patient support and follow-up care

As patients progress through treatment to recovery, support services and follow-up care become increasingly important for long-term outcomes. Patient support services, including psycho-oncological care, fertility counselling, and genetic counselling, are predominantly provided in hospital outpatient clinics [16, 17, 23–25, 31–37, 39–41, 78]. However, Germany and Austria utilise additional settings, with Germany relying on outpatient practices and Austria on external laboratories [16, 17, 34–36]. Clinical social work and discharge planning show varying levels of integration, with Denmark solely providing social work in outpatient settings, while other countries use a combination of healthcare infrastructures [16, 17, 23–25, 31–37, 39–41, 78]. Follow-up care, including survivorship and palliative care, highlights differences in care delivery, with Germany and Sweden focusing on outpatient settings [16, 17, 23, 39], while other countries use a mix of home treatment, hospital outpatient clinics, and inpatient facilities [24, 25, 31–37, 40, 41, 78]. Survivorship care refers to the period following a cancer diagnosis and includes ongoing surveillance, management of therapy-related side effects, and support for returning to everyday life. Breast reconstruction surgeries are typically conducted in inpatient settings, except in Sweden, where they are performed exclusively in hospital outpatient clinics [16, 17, 23–25, 31–37, 39–41, 78].

The follow-up care approaches particularly illustrate each model’s strengths and limitations. The network-based models in Sweden and the Netherlands demonstrate greater flexibility in offering follow-up services across settings, including innovative home-based palliative care and survivorship support approaches. This reflects these systems’ emphasis on care coordination and seamless transitions between providers. In contrast, countries with centralised specialist models tend to maintain follow-up services within hospital settings, potentially creating access barriers but ensuring continuity with the specialised teams that provided initial treatment. Germany’s decentralised model leverages its strong outpatient sector to provide accessible follow-up care but faces challenges in coordination between multiple providers.

Discussion

Our analysis of breast cancer care across six European countries reveals three distinct care delivery models varying in centralisation and cross-sectoral integration. Each model represents a different approach to balancing quality, accessibility, and resource efficiency in breast cancer care.

Strengths and challenges of each care model

The centralised specialist model, predominantly present in Austria, Belgium and Denmark, offers several distinct advantages. Concentrating expertise, specialised equipment, and high patient volumes in designated centres ensures consistently high-quality care and standardised treatment protocols [15, 79, 80]. This approach enables the efficient allocation of specialised resources and advanced technologies while creating environments conducive to clinical research integration and innovative treatment approaches [36, 80]. Patients benefit from comprehensive multidisciplinary expertise in a single setting, reducing care fragmentation and potentially improving clinical decision-making [16]. However, this model creates significant geographical access barriers for patients in rural or remote regions [81, 82], increasing travel burden, costs, and time investments for patients and families [36]. The concentration of services can place capacity strain on specialised centres, potentially leading to longer waiting times while reducing continuity with local healthcare providers who may have established long-term patient relationships [16, 17, 83]. Additionally, centralised models often contribute to higher system costs through greater reliance on inpatient stays, for example, for breast cancer surgeries [16, 17, 36].

Germany’s decentralised model with certification presents a different set of trade-offs. By distributing care delivery across a broader network of certified facilities, this approach significantly improves geographical access while maintaining quality through rigorous certification requirements [16, 84]. The model allows for flexible adaptation to local population needs and characteristics, enabling continuity with local care providers throughout the treatment journey. The strong involvement of outpatient oncologists in care delivery creates additional patient access points [85].

While Germany’s certification framework establishes quality requirements, a considerable proportion of breast cancer surgeries continue to be performed in non-certified hospitals. This coexistence of certified and non-certified providers illustrates the structural complexity of decentralised care models and can pose challenges for ensuring consistent adherence to clinical standards [74]. As a result, quality and guideline implementation may vary across facilities, despite the presence of formal certification processes [17, 84, 85].

To address these limitations, the Hospital Care Improvement Act (KHVVG), in force since January 2025, aims to reorganise hospital service provision by introducing 65 “Leistungsgruppen” (service groups) linked to specific quality and structural criteria. The reform also establishes a tiered hospital system (Levels I–III) and is intended to improve care quality by concentrating complex services in fewer, more specialised hospitals [86]. Nevertheless, the distributed nature of the current system continues to duplicate resources across multiple facilities and complicates coordination between numerous care providers, particularly across sectors. These challenges underline the need for robust quality monitoring to ensure consistent standards across all participating institutions [17, 56].

The network-based model implemented in the Netherlands and Sweden attempts to balance the benefits of both approaches. By strategically combining specialised expertise with broader geographical access, this model promotes innovation through collaborative approaches between academic and community settings [23, 26, 39, 78]. It enables appropriate resource allocation based on treatment complexity and facilitates smoother transitions between care settings [23, 46]. The approach effectively supports outpatient and home-based care for appropriate treatments, potentially improving patient convenience and system efficiency [62, 87]. However, successful implementation requires sophisticated coordination mechanisms and clearly defined responsibilities. The model depends heavily on effective information systems for communication between providers and may create confusion about accountability within the network [50, 88]. Adoption necessitates a cultural shift toward collaborative practice among professionals from different settings and specialities. Furthermore, network-based approaches are particularly vulnerable to specialist nurse shortages that impede effective implementation [62, 87, 89].

Evolving care models and cross-sectoral integration

While centralisation ensures high standards through concentrated expertise, it challenges equitable access, particularly in underserved regions. Cross-sectoral networks help bridge these gaps as care shifts toward outpatient and home-based models [81, 82]. Countries with centralised models often rely on inpatient stays for breast cancer surgeries, increasing costs and capacity strain [17, 36], while network-based models increasingly utilise outpatient clinics for surgeries without overnight stays [23, 39, 78]. Evidence supports this shift, showing that same-day discharge is safe, with high patient satisfaction and cost savings, though success depends on well-coordinated follow-up care [90, 91].

We have observed a trend toward integrating centralised and network-based approaches across all countries studied. Sweden and Denmark have implemented regionalised cancer networks, while Belgium and the Netherlands created multi-tier oncology networks for smooth transitions between care settings [23, 26, 27, 37, 78]. Germany’s recent healthcare reforms aim to enhance cross-sectoral care by promoting outpatient models [17, 85]. Home-based treatment and nurse-led care are expanding, particularly in Sweden and Belgium, though specialist nurse shortages present implementation challenges [62, 87, 89].

Patient pathways and electronic health records

Standardised Cancer Care Pathways provide foundations for coherent cross-sectoral care in several countries, addressing coordination concerns and providing clear guidelines for investigation and referral [50, 83, 92–94]. However, implementation faces challenges, including differing views on responsibilities among healthcare professionals [50]. These pathways significantly impact patient experience, with evidence suggesting that clear protocols and designated contact persons improve patient satisfaction and reduce anxiety throughout the treatment journey [93, 95].

None of the countries examined in our study has achieved fully integrated electronic health records across all healthcare settings [88, 96]. Fragmented systems cause information loss and inefficiencies between inpatient and outpatient services. Each country faces unique challenges, from Austria’s exclusion of private-sector patients to Germany’s decentralised data management [17, 36]. Greater interoperability is needed to support cross-sectoral care and ensure data continuity [97, 98], particularly as patient journeys increasingly span multiple care settings and providers.

Implications for hospital-centred healthcare systems

The comparative analysis suggests hybrid approaches combining elements from different models may offer the most promising path forward for hospital-centred countries like Austria. Centralised expertise for complex diagnostics and specialised treatments, coupled with distributed delivery networks for routine care and follow-up, could optimise both quality and accessibility [81, 82]. Several key lessons emerge from this analysis for countries with traditional hospital-centric models.

First, strategically implementing cross-sectoral pathways can reduce hospital burden without compromising quality. Countries like Denmark demonstrate that even systems with strong centralisation can successfully incorporate outpatient elements through standardised care pathways and coordination mechanisms [50, 93]. However, it is essential to recognise that the necessary healthcare infrastructures must be established before any significant shift can occur. This requires substantial upfront investment in community-based facilities and services to ensure continuity of care during the transition period.

Second, the gradual transition toward outpatient surgeries seen in network-based models offers a blueprint for hospital-centred systems to reduce inpatient capacity strain while maintaining quality [23, 39, 90]. This transition requires time as well as strategic implementation and depends on developing appropriate facilities, equipment, and protocols to ensure patient safety in outpatient settings.

Third, the role of specialised nursing personnel appears critical across all successful models, suggesting that hospital-centred countries should prioritise developing this workforce as a foundation for any care model transition [62, 87, 89].

Fourth, all countries struggle with health information continuity, indicating that early investment in interoperable electronic health records should precede structural reorganisation [88, 96].

From a resource allocation perspective, shifting appropriate treatments to outpatient and home-based settings offers potential cost savings [90, 91] but requires upfront investment in training and coordination systems. Hospital-centred countries must recognise that successful transition requires initial parallel investment before resource redistribution can occur—maintaining existing hospital infrastructure while simultaneously building new outpatient and community capacities. The experiences of the studied countries suggest that successful reform balances the efficiency gains of centralisation with the accessibility benefits of decentralised care networks while accounting for specific geographical, demographic, and health system characteristics.

These findings illustrate that most countries do not adhere strictly to one care model. Rather, they incorporate elements from different approaches, adapting them to their specific historical, geographical, and socioeconomic contexts. These hybrid systems continue to evolve in response to changing healthcare needs, technological advancements, and economic pressures.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. First, breast cancer is a high-incidence malignancy with well-established regulations and certification frameworks, which may not be representative of the broader oncology landscape. The findings may, therefore, not be generalisable to cancers with lower incidence rates or less structured care pathways. Second, differences in healthcare system organisation and reimbursement structures across countries may influence the comparability of specific care delivery elements, potentially affecting the generalisability of findings beyond the analysed contexts. Third, disparities in the availability and granularity of published literature across countries may have influenced the comprehensiveness of the comparison.

Fourth, the study focused on structural and organisational aspects of care delivery and resource allocation but did not include specific metrics of efficiency or productivity. Future research should incorporate performance indicators such as treatment throughput, cost-effectiveness, and system-level productivity to better assess the impact of different care models. Fifth, clinical outcome data—such as survival rates or quality-of-life indicators—were not included in the comparative analysis and represent an important area for future investigation. In particular, five-year survival rates could serve as a meaningful benchmark for comparing care effectiveness across systems. Future studies should incorporate such indicators to more comprehensively evaluate the relationship between structural models and patient outcomes. Finally, the scope of expert consultations was limited, potentially underrepresenting perspectives from smaller hospitals and non-specialist settings.

Despite these limitations, this comparative analysis provides valuable insights into the diversity of cancer care organisations across Europe and highlights opportunities for integrating effective elements from different models to enhance patient outcomes and healthcare system efficiency.

Conclusion

Our comparative analysis of breast cancer care across six European countries highlights that no single model provides a universally optimal solution. Instead, elements from each approach can inform improvements in hospital-centred systems such as Austria’s. The centralised specialist model ensures high-quality, standardised care but struggles with accessibility, while the decentralised certification model improves access but presents coordination challenges. The network-based model, which integrates centralised expertise with distributed service delivery, offers promising directions for reform but requires sophisticated coordination mechanisms, sufficient personnel, and supporting infrastructure.

The critical challenge for Austria and similar hospital-centred healthcare systems lies in strategically balancing these approaches to leverage existing strengths while addressing accessibility limitations, particularly in rural regions. Specialised cancer centres deliver high-quality treatment but must be complemented by accessible outpatient and home-based alternatives. Any successful transition from a hospital-based model requires addressing workforce shortages—especially among specialised nurses—who play a key role in coordinating care within distributed models.

Cross-sector coordination is essential for delivering efficient and patient-centred cancer care. This requires seamless collaboration between specialised centres, hospitals, community providers, and home care services. A viable strategy involves developing integrated care networks that uphold high treatment standards through centralised expertise while enhancing accessibility through distributed delivery structures.

In addition to structural and organisational considerations, several features analysed in this study—such as surgical volume requirements, accreditation mechanisms, and the shift toward outpatient surgery—may also serve as proxies for care quality and efficiency. Emerging practices, including same-day discharge for breast cancer surgery and nurse-led or home-based treatment pathways (as seen in Sweden, Denmark, and Belgium), are supported by evidence demonstrating safety, patient satisfaction, and cost-effectiveness [90, 91, 99]. Although this study did not assess performance indicators such as survival rates, patient-reported outcomes, or cost data, these structural trends highlight evolving approaches to performance-oriented care. Future research should examine the relationship between care models and clinical or economic outcomes by linking organisational features with data from national cancer registries and health systems.

Based on this comparative analysis, several structural elements emerge as transferable best practices for improving breast cancer care across diverse EU healthcare systems. These include cross-sectoral care pathways with defined timelines, structural and volume requirements for surgical procedures, and strong roles for specialised nursing personnel. Countries transitioning from hospital-centred systems should prioritise the development of regional outpatient infrastructures and invest early in coordination and data integration systems to ensure care continuity. While specific implementation must reflect national contexts, these elements provide a broadly applicable framework for aligning access, quality, and efficiency in cancer care reform.

Further investigation into the barriers and facilitators of implementing such models would be valuable for countries considering structural reform. This approach will support evidence-based policy development by offering insights into the effectiveness, efficiency, and equity of different cancer care models.
