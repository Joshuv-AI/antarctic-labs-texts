Risk of Bias Associated with Varying Sampling Strategies for External Comparator Cohorts for Single‐Arm Trials: Insights from Aggressive Lymphoma
Authors: Mikkel Runason Simonsen, Shiva Leisner, Ahmed Ludvigsen Al‐Mashhadi, Laurids Østergaard Poulsen, Matthew Maurer, Lasse Hjort Jakobsen, Tarec Christoffer El‐Galaly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarketing authorizations for oncology drugs are often based on single‐arm trials (SATs), and real‐world data‐based external comparator cohorts (ECCs) are increasingly explored to contextualize results. In retrospective studies, comparators may be identified by time of diagnosis but included based on time of relapse; such sampling strategies can introduce bias, potentially leading to erroneous efficacy conclusions. Simulated diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) cohorts were generated using published time‐to‐relapse and post‐relapse survival distributions. Three ECC sampling strategies were evaluated: naïve (screening and inclusion periods coincide), delayed (inclusion period extends beyond screening), and displacement (screening and inclusion periods shifted relative to each other). Different sampling strategies caused significant changes in the time‐to‐relapse distribution in the ECC and, by proxy, the estimated 2‐year post‐relapse overall survival (22.5–52.2%). Naïve sampling consistently resulted in oversampling of early relapses, though bias was reduced with extended inclusion periods; in hypothetical trials, it produced inflated Type I errors (10.5–84.9%) despite α = 0.025. Delayed sampling reduced bias when the inclusion period was sufficiently extended to capture late relapses, yielding unbiased estimates under long delays (Type I error of 2.5% at a 6‐year delay). Displacement sampling depended strongly on the degree of shift: moderate shifts minimized bias, whereas long displacements introduced substantial distortion (Type I error of 2.7% and 0.0% at displacements of 2 and 5, respectively). Real‐world data‐based ECCs in relapsed/refractory oncology are susceptible to selection bias from sampling strategies, particularly through distortions in time‐to‐relapse, leading to erroneous efficacy conclusions; similar bias may occur in non‐oncology settings where time‐to‐treatment initiation is prognostic.

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

External comparator cohorts based on real‐world data are increasingly used to contextualize single‐arm trials in oncology. However, methodological challenges remain, and certain sources of bias related to sampling strategies in the relapse/post‐diagnosis setting have not been systematically described.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study investigated a previously underrecognized sampling bias arising when patients are identified based on time of diagnosis but included based on time of relapse. It evaluated the magnitude of this bias, its impact on estimated outcomes and Type I error in hypothetical trials, and whether alternative sampling schemes or adjustments can mitigate its effects.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

This study demonstrates that commonly used sampling strategies can substantially distort time‐to‐relapse distributions and downstream survival estimates, leading to biased efficacy conclusions and inflated Type I error. It further shows that the direction and magnitude of bias depend on the chosen sampling approach, and that certain strategies (e.g., sufficiently delayed inclusion) can reduce or eliminate bias under specific conditions.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

These findings highlight the need for careful design and critical evaluation of external comparator cohort construction in single‐arm trials. Awareness of this bias can improve the validity of real‐world evidence, inform regulatory and methodological standards, and guide more robust comparative analyses in both oncology and other disease areas where timing of treatment initiation is prognostic.

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for assessing the efficacy of new treatments. However, marketing authorizations in oncology are increasingly granted based on single‐arm trials (SATs) when immediate access to therapy outweighs the risks associated with uncertainties or in rare diseases where RCTs are difficult to perform. Most approvals are conditional (European Medicines Agency, EMA) or accelerated (Food and Drug Administration, FDA), which require documentation of a major therapeutic advantage in addition to overall favorable risk/benefit for the considered indication. 1 , 2 In a review of 65 conditional marketing approvals granted by the EMA from 2006 to 2023, the major therapeutic advantages were mainly demonstrated in SATs (81.5%) with contextualization of results based on literature or historical patient cohorts. 3

Regulatory perspectives on SATs are evolving, particularly in oncology with time‐to‐event endpoints. Recent guidance from EMA and FDA highlights challenges in interpreting treatment effects, especially when strong prognostic factors are present and endpoints such as progression‐free and overall survival (OS) are considered. 4 , 5 SATs with ECCs may still contribute with important evidence and, in some settings, represent an alternative when randomized trials are not feasible or not possible. This can be true in rare diseases or when randomization is not ethical. While regulatory caution remains, the EMA's 2030 vision emphasizes more use of RWD for regulatory decisions. 6 Among applications for EMA marketing authorization and for extension of indication submitted in 2018–2019, 46 included RWD to support efficacy claims, but the RWD was regarded by EMA as supportive in only 10. 7 Common limitations included missing data, nonrepresentative populations, and small sample sizes. This underscores the need for improved use of RWD in comparative settings, particularly through better understanding of bias arising from different cohort designs.

With the availability of RWD from electronic health records and disease registers, there is a growing interest in clinical trials where external comparator cohorts (ECC) can be used to emulate an active comparator arm. 8 In such studies, SAT outcomes are compared against a similar cohort, the ECC, treated with any given contemporary standard of care (SoC). Methods from causal inference intended to mimic the randomization of RCTs are often applied to provide comparative effectiveness data. This facilitates several potential advantages. First, fewer patients are needed for enrollment, lowering costs and shortening development timelines. Second, all trial participants receive an investigational therapy, which is attractive if SoC is ineffective. However, reliable results require high‐quality RWD with complete and accurate capture of patient characteristics, treatments, outcomes, and key prognostic factors. This is essential for adequate confounder adjustment in ECC studies, where systematic differences between cohorts are expected due to the lack of randomization. In oncology, evolving disease classifications and treatment landscapes may further introduce time‐varying confounding, while nonrandom missingness can bias estimates when data availability is associated with patient outcomes. FDA has issued draft guidance for ECCs, which emphasize the necessity of full overview of the validity and relevance of the data sources used for ECC generation as well as a predefined, detailed statistical analysis plan. 9 Despite the significant interest for SAT‐ECC designs, they have not been widely used for marketing authorizations so far. 10

The sampling schemes used for creating ECCs for cancer studies are potentially a critical, but previously, to the best of our knowledge, unattended source of bias. Researchers often retrieve patient lists based on diagnostic codes. When cases of relapsed/refractory (R/R) cancer are needed, it is common to screen patients with a relevant first cancer diagnosis in a defined time period, that is, the screening period, to identify the subgroup of relevant patients that later experience R/R disease during a defined time period, the inclusion period. This is particularly common when registries record information on initial cancer diagnosis but lack sufficient detail on relapsed disease, requiring relapse timing to be obtained from other data sources. However, even when all relevant information is available within a single dataset, bias may still arise if the dataset is relatively immature and contains predominantly recently diagnosed patients, limiting the ability to accurately characterize the time‐to‐relapse distribution under full follow‐up. This methodology leads to a different sampling strategy than when patients are enrolled in a clinical trial. Here, patients are consecutively enrolled as they present to the clinic, irrespective of the timing of relapse in relation to the primary diagnosis. Thus, the sampling in SATs will typically approximate the true time‐to‐relapse distribution, whereas the sampling scheme for ECC may deviate substantially from this true distribution. As time‐to‐relapse is one of the strongest prognostic factors for cancer survival in the R/R setting, the time‐to‐relapse distribution in a cohort influences observed OS. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Generally, as for most cancers, OS of R/R diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is better for late relapses, where a second durable remission after salvage therapy is more achieved. In contrast, early DLBCL relapses have distinct molecular genetic phenotypes, are more often chemo‐refractory, and carry a dismal prognosis. 16 , 17 , 18 In this simulation study, selection bias introduced by different sampling schemes for ECCs was explored using DLBCL as an example.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

Data were simulated based on published time‐to‐relapse distributions and post‐relapse survival distributions, which were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer. 11 , 19 , 20 The true 2‐year OS is independent of sampling scheme and was calculated from the time‐to‐relapse distributions and post‐relapse OS distribution. According to the time‐to‐relapse distribution reported by Maurer et al, 37% of relapses occur within 6 months, 25% between 6 and 12 months, 8% between 12 and 18 months, 7% between 18 and 24 months, 9% between 24 and 36 months, and 14% after 36 months. 19 The 2‐year OS of each respective time‐to‐relapse interval were 19%, 25%, 38%, 45%, 58%, and 69%. 11 Multiplying the probability of relapse occurring in each follow‐up interval with the conditional 2‐year OS for that interval and then summing across all intervals yields a true marginal 2‐year OS for R/R DLBCL of 34.3% in this cohort.

Sampling schemes for ECC

If R/R DLBCL patients are selected for an ECC based on a registry of patients with first diagnoses, there are two important time periods to consider: the screening period, where patients with a primary diagnosis are identified, and the inclusion period, during which identified patients who experience relapse are included in the study. This reflects a typical workflow when patient records are screened for eligibility by criteria that are not captured in the hospital databases or national registries. The chronological interplay between these two time periods may result in a skewed time‐to‐relapse distribution. To illustrate this, three different sampling schemes were considered. The first was the naïve sampling scheme (Figure 1 ) in which the screening period and inclusion periods are the same.

Exemplification of the sampling schemes for three patients with time of diagnosis indicated with a circle and time of relapse indicated by a square. Green indicates that the patient will be included in the sample according to the specified sampling scheme as opposed to red, which indicates the exclusion for the patient.

For example, patients with a primary diagnosis of DLBCL in 2020–2023 are screened for R/R DLBCL in the same period 2020–2023. This would result in a skewed time‐to‐relapse distribution with oversampling of early DLBCL relapses.

This can be alleviated by a delayed sampling scheme (Figure 1 ). Using this strategy, the inclusion period is the same as the screening period but with additional extension time, referred to as the delay. For example, if the screening period was 2020–2023 (time for primary DLBCL diagnosis), the inclusion period could be 2020–2025 (time where R/R DLBCL had to occur). With longer delays, more relapses among the screened patients are included and if the length of delay is long enough, all cases of R/R DLBCL in the screened population are captured. This sampling strategy can be equivalent to having full follow‐up.

The third tested sampling scheme was the displacement sampling scheme (Figure 1 ). Here, the two periods are of similar length but displaced from each other such that the inclusion period begins after the beginning of the screening period. For example, the screening period could be 2020–2023 and the inclusion period could be 2022–2025. This strategy can lead to the exclusion of some (very) early relapses but also of late relapses similar to the other sampling schemes, and the actual impact on the observed time‐to‐relapse distribution would depend on the balance between the exclusion of early and late relapses.

Simulation strategy

A simulation strategy is presented for generating patient cohorts based on time‐to‐relapse and post‐relapse survival distributions. The simulated population is characterized by an unbiased 2‐year OS of 34.3% with an undistorted time‐to‐relapse distribution. Different sampling schemes of the same population are then analyzed with respect to survival estimates. This simulation strategy allows for direct measurement of bias arising from the sampling scheme itself, without incorporating interactions with other sources of bias that may arise in a real‐world SAT–ECC setting.

For a given sampling scheme (choice of screening and inclusion period), data for 10,000 relapse patients were simulated as follows:

Simulate date of diagnosis according to a uniform distribution in a screening period and the date of relapse according to the time‐to‐relapse distribution.

Simulate date of diagnosis according to a uniform distribution in a screening period and the date of relapse according to the time‐to‐relapse distribution.

For each relapse patient, simulate the 2‐year OS indicator according to the conditional survival distribution given the time from diagnosis to relapse.

For each relapse patient, simulate the 2‐year OS indicator according to the conditional survival distribution given the time from diagnosis to relapse.

Exclude patients for whom the date of relapse is not within the inclusion period of the given sampling scheme and calculate the proportion of patients included out of all R/R DLBCL occurring in the screening population.

Exclude patients for whom the date of relapse is not within the inclusion period of the given sampling scheme and calculate the proportion of patients included out of all R/R DLBCL occurring in the screening population.

Estimate the post‐relapse 2‐year OS as the proportion of patients alive according to their 2‐year OS indicator.

Estimate the post‐relapse 2‐year OS as the proportion of patients alive according to their 2‐year OS indicator.

Simulations were run 1,000 times, and the average estimates of the simulations were calculated.

The average 2‐year OS estimates were compared with the true 2‐year OS to assess the magnitude of the selection bias of each respective sampling scheme.

For the simulations, screening period lengths of 2–10 years were used. For the screening periods, delays of 1–6 years and displacement of 1–5 years were explored.

Trial success rates

The impact of the three sampling schemes was assessed in hypothetical trial scenarios for varying efficacy of the investigational therapy (IT) compared with SoC. In the absence of selection bias, the 2‐year OS of R/R DLBCL was set to 34.3% in the ECC and no selection bias was anticipated in the SAT with perfect consecutive enrollment of patients as they present.

Results of simulated SAT‐ECC (ratio 1:2, 200 patients in SAT vs. 400 in ECC) scenarios were assessed using a one‐sided z‐test for SAT superiority in 2‐year OS (alpha 0.025). The calculated probability of rejecting the null hypothesis and concluding superiority of the SAT is referred to as the trial success rate.

This is provided in different scenarios of equivalence, futility, and superiority of the IT against SoC, and hence the trial success rate corresponds to either a Type I error or power, depending on the scenario. In the hypothetical trials where the IT has a positive effect, the trial success rate corresponds to statistical power. In contrast, when the IT has a negative or no effect, concluding superiority constitutes a Type I error.

Naïve sampling scheme

The naïve sampling scheme skewed the time‐to‐relapse distribution in the ECC toward early relapses and therefore poorer outcomes, creating bias by underestimating the 2‐year OS (Figure 2 ).

Out of all relapses that would occur in the screened population, 51.4%, 73.4%, and 86.5% were included using naïve sampling schemes of 2, 5, and 10 years, respectively (Table 1 ). With a screening and inclusion period of 2 years, the estimated 2‐year OS was 22.4% corresponding to 11.8 percentage points (%p) below the true 2‐year OS. With 5‐ and 10‐year periods, the 2‐year OS estimates were 6.6%p and 2.9%p lower than the true OS (Figure 2 ).

In the hypothetical trial of 600 subjects (SAT‐ECC 1:2 matched), when the IT was assigned a similar 2‐year OS as the SoC, the trial success rate (Type I error rate) was 10.5%–84.9% depending on period length (Table 1 ). When the IT was assigned a 5% lower 2‐year OS than SoC, the trial success rate (Type I error rate) was 42.8% for the shortest period and 0.6% for the longest period. In contrast, the power to detect true improvement in 2‐year OS from the investigational therapy was high, with the trial success rate (power) reaching 98.6% for the shortest period length for a 5% improvement in OS of the IT compared with the SoC.

Delayed sampling scheme

By extending the inclusion period beyond the screening period, biases were substantially reduced even at small delays due to an early/late relapse balance approaching the true distribution (Figure 3 ).

For a delay of 2 years and screening periods of 2, 5, and 10 years, the bias in 2‐year OS estimates was 5.7%p, 2.8%p, and 1.3%p, respectively, with the proportion of captured relapses being 85.2%, 92.4%, and 96.2%, respectively (Table 2 ). Increasing delay reduced bias further and led to a greater proportion of captured relapses. With a delay of 6 years, all relapses were captured and no bias occurred regardless of screening period length, as no relapses would occur later than 6 years from diagnosis according to the used time‐to‐relapse distribution (Table 2 ).

Trial success rate reduced as the delay increased (both power and Type I error), and was much lower with a fixed screening period length of 5 years than for the naïve sampling scheme. When the 2‐year OS was similar between experimental therapy (SAT) and SoC, the probability of concluding superiority of SAT ranged from 2.5%–9.8% depending on the delay. When the hypothetical IT was assigned a true effect of 5% lower 2‐year OS compared with SoC, the trial success rate (Type I error rate) ranged between 0.1% and 0.6% depending on the delay. Similarly, for a true improvement in 2‐year OS of 5% for the investigational therapy, trial success rate ranged from 21.9% to 45.9%, depending on the delay.

Displaced sampling scheme

By keeping screening and inclusion periods at the same fixed lengths but displacing the inclusion period relative to the screening period, the scheme included relatively more late relapses compared with the naïve sampling scheme. This was due to reduced capture of early relapses and increased capture of late relapses, which shifted the bias accordingly (Figure 4 ).

For a fixed period length of 5 years and displacements of 1, 2, and 5 years, respectively, the bias was 3.9%p (overrepresentation of early relapses), 0.14%p, and −17.9%p (overrepresentation of late relapses) with proportion of captured relapses of 80%, 71.9%, and 26.2%, respectively (Table 3 ).

With the displaced sampling scheme and a displacement of 2 years, the trial success rate was similar to the unbiased trial success rate with the delayed sampling scheme. However, for a displacement of 5 years, the time‐to‐relapse distribution in the ECC was skewed toward late relapses to a degree that the trial success rate (power) was reduced to 0% even when the IT was associated with a 10% increase in 2‐year OS compared with SoC.

DISCUSSION

The present study explored different sampling methods for patients with R/R DLBCL and the impact on OS. While we used DLBCL as a case example, results apply to cancer studies more broadly and beyond cancer as well. Naïve sampling underestimated OS, but the bias decreased as the screening period increased. Delayed sampling reduced bias and produced entirely unbiased estimates when the delay matched the maximum relapse time. Although displacement sampling often reduced bias more effectively than comparable delays, excessive displacement led to OS overestimation, making it a less reliable strategy. In a real‐world SAT‐ECC setting, additional structural differences may exist between the SAT and ECC populations, including differences in age, calendar time of diagnosis, geographic region, and other patient characteristics. These factors may themselves affect outcomes and could potentially interact with the bias arising from distortions in the time‐to‐relapse distribution, thereby either amplifying or attenuating its impact.

This simulation study highlighted an important and previously unaddressed selection bias that can arise in studies of R/R malignancies, here illustrated with DLBCL. This specific bias occurs when the sampling strategies condition on both the time of diagnosis and the time of relapse, leading to a skewed time‐to‐relapse distribution. However, the issue is also relevant outside oncology, where time‐to‐inclusion from diagnosis is prognostic for future outcomes.

Our findings also highlight the importance of addressing potential sources of bias at the design stage of observational studies and external control construction. This is in line with FDA guidance, 21 which emphasizes careful prespecification of study design and analysis to reduce bias. The selection bias can also be viewed as an estimand‐alignment problem. By distorting the time‐to‐relapse distribution, the sampling scheme effectively shifts the target population represented by the ECC, such that the SAT and ECC may no longer estimate outcomes in comparable populations. It should also be noted that even though the date of relapse was chosen as the index date for simulations, similar situations would arise if other events were used as index dates, for example, the date of commencing salvage treatment.

For context, consider the common inclusion strategy in clinical trials. Participants may be recruited when the trial is open for inclusion based on a presentation of R/R cancer during that window, regardless of when their original first cancer diagnoses were made. Such trials enroll a balanced number of early and late relapses mimicking the true time‐to‐relapse distribution for that cancer and with no impact on OS estimates for the trial population. In real‐world studies however, patients may be identified based on their first cancer diagnosis date and the subgroup with a relapse will be included in an ECC with start of follow‐up on the date of relapse. Provided a sufficiently long inclusion period to capture all relapses occurring in the surveyed patient population identified in the screening period, this strategy also leads to unbiased results. The required length of follow‐up would depend on the natural history of the studied disease and the typical time‐to‐relapse pattern. For diseases with predominantly refractory/early relapse patterns, the time needed for inclusion of all relapses will be short. For diseases with many late relapses, the time will be longer.

The explored selection bias occurs only when inclusion in an ECC is dependent on both the date of primary cancer diagnosis and date of R/R cancer. This situation is common and may occur unintentionally in RWD studies. For example, in an RWD setting using electronic health records, suppose all patients diagnosed since 2010 are captured, with relapse information available through 2025. Including all relapses observed during 2010–2025 implicitly conditions on both diagnosis and relapse dates. Our simulations show that this seemingly natural sampling scheme can lead to substantial bias, particularly when the inclusion period is short. A potential remedy is to introduce a delay (e.g., 5 years), but this would require either excluding patients diagnosed after 2020 or waiting an additional 5 years to observe relapses, reducing sample size or delaying study completion. Such practical limitations may necessitate alternative approaches to address the resulting selection bias. If delayed sampling is too problematic due to sample size loss, and displacement sampling carries too much risk, a potential alternative could be to maintain the naïve sampling scheme and attempt to use statistical adjustment to handle the bias. For instance, inverse probability weighting could be applied to make the weighted time‐to‐relapse distributions in the SAT and ECC identical. Alternatively, if the ECC is constructed based on matching, the time‐to‐relapse should be included among the matching variables.

Although this selection bias and the considerations about sampling schemes have not been directly described previously, different sampling schemes have been used in recent RWD studies. A Danish study examined real‐world outcomes of 189 patients with R/R DLBCL following third or subsequent lines of therapy. 22 In this study, a type of displacement sampling was applied: patients diagnosed with DLBCL between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2019 were screened for eligibility, and those initiating third or later line therapy between January 1, 2015 and August 31, 2021 were included. This would mitigate the bias associated with the naïve sampling scheme, and as the sampling strategy is very close to a 2‐year displacement which greatly reduced bias in the simulations of the current paper, this strategy would work well to produce unbiased estimates of first relapses. However, as this Danish study considered third or later lines, the time‐to‐second‐relapse distribution would be later than the time‐to‐first‐relapse distribution considered in our simulations, and an even greater displacement might have been needed to mitigate the bias completely.

Similarly, a recent US study based on the Lymphoma Epidemiology of Outcomes Consortium for Real World Evidence (LEO CReWE) cohort investigated patients with R/R follicular lymphoma (FL) in third or later lines. 23 One of the goals was to establish survival expectations among these patients for comparison to SATs, effectively developing an ECC. Patients diagnosed between 2002 and 2018 were included, although the available follow‐up extended further, imposing a sampling delay of 4 years and the simulations in the current paper indicate that even relatively short delays can substantially reduce bias. However, late relapses are more common in FL than in DLBCL, 24 and the study considered third or later lines rather than first relapse. Consequently, the time‐to‐second‐relapse distribution of FL is much later than the time‐to‐relapse distribution considered in the present study, and residual bias due to an overrepresentation of early relapses likely remains in the FL study.

Another US study considered outcomes and genetic dynamics in patients with relapsed acute myeloid leukemia (AML). 25 Among 875 patients diagnosed between April 2017 and October 2022, 197 who relapsed in the same period were included. This study applied a naïve sampling strategy over a relatively short 5‐year period, which indicates a substantial risk of bias. Given AML's tendency for early relapses, with a median time‐to‐relapse of 6.5 months among those included in this US study, and with studies indicating that over 80% of relapses occur within 2 years of consolidation chemotherapy, 26 even a modest delay could have reduced this bias considerably.

ECC‐SAT designs have been considered as an alternative to RCTs in specific situations of high unmet need or in rare diseases where recruitment is difficult for logistical and competitive reasons. Conventionally, the accepted false‐positive rate is set to 0.05 but taking into consideration the context and totality of data. In RCTs, the false‐positive rate is more easily controlled through the analysis plan assuming the randomization eliminates imbalances in known and unknown confounders. In the hypothetical simulated trials of the present study, there were substantially inflated Type I error rates. For some sampling schemes, Type I error rates rose above 50% when the true effect was a 5% reduction in 2‐year OS.

This issue is also relevant in health technology assessments. Suppose that a new treatment for R/R disease is introduced into a broader patient population after a successful trial, and a retrospective study is conducted within a few years to verify the effect on the broader real‐world patient population. Due to the natural time constraint in this setting, naïve sampling would be the most feasible approach but could introduce substantial bias due to an overrepresentation of early relapse thereby underestimating OS of the RWD patients. If this selection bias is not considered, investigators might erroneously, at least partly, attribute the gap in efficacy between trial patients and the broader patient population to other factors.

Importantly, while the present article has focused primarily on time‐to‐relapse in DLBCL, the same principles apply to time‐to‐treatment or the more general time‐to‐inclusion, and the concepts are relevant to any disease setting in which time‐to‐inclusion is prognostic for the outcome of interest.

Furthermore, while this article presents three specific sampling strategies, these should by no means be considered an exhaustive list of possible approaches. Rather, they were chosen to help illustrate the underlying design principles, the associated trade‐offs, and the potential for bias if these issues are not handled appropriately.

In conclusion, sampling strategies can introduce substantial selection bias in situations where time from diagnosis to a given clinical event has prognostic implications and when sampling is conditioned on both first diagnosis and date of the given therapy/situation of interest. This is problematic when comparing RWD cohort studies to clinical trials. Modest delays or long observation periods only partially mitigate the bias, but the actual impact on outcomes depends on the disease of interest, the time‐to‐inclusion distribution, and the correlations between time‐to‐inclusion and survival. Careful design of screening and inclusion periods or statistical adjustment after sampling is essential in producing trustworthy and reliable SAT‐ECC studies.
