Evaluation of dentin bridge formation and postoperative pain reduction after direct pulp capping using photobiomodulation therapy, platelet-rich fibrin, and mineral trioxide aggregate – A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Rishika Mohanty, Sumit Dash, Shashirekha Govind
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Direct pulp capping (DPC) preserves pulp vitality and stimulates reparative dentin formation. Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) may enhance healing especially with platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) and mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA). This study aims to assess the effects of PBMT, alone or with PRF or MTA, on postoperative pain after DPC and on dentin bridge thickness using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).Materials and Methods:Sixty patients (17–45 years) with reversible pulpitis in permanent molars were randomly divided into three groups (n = 20 each): Group A: MTA with PBMT (A1) and without PBMT (A2), Group B: PRF with PBMT (B1) and without PBMT (B2), and Group C: only PBMT. Clinical and radiographic follow-up was done at 3, 6, and 12 months. The thickness of the dentin bridge was measured at all the intervals using CBCT.Results:At 24 h, there was no significant reduction in pain compared to baseline across all the groups. However, in the first and second weeks, groups treated with PBMT showed a significant reduction in pain (P = 0.00). CBCT revealed that PBMT-treated groups had greater dentin bridge development, particularly in Group B2.Conclusion:PBMT with PRF was associated with better pain reduction and dentin bridge formation than other interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) is a treatment approach aimed at preserving the vitality and functionality of the dental pulp, which may be compromised by factors such as carious lesions, restorative procedures, or trauma. The primary goal of VPT is to stop or prevent pulpitis by promoting the formation of reparative dentin or a calcified bridge. This helps ensure the long-term survival and function of the tooth.[12]

In young permanent teeth, vital pulp therapies include indirect pulp capping (IPC), direct pulp capping (DPC), partial pulpotomy, and full pulpotomy. DPC is recommended for minimal pulp exposure with healthy, controllable bleeding. The factors such as exposure size, bleeding amount and duration, patient age, tooth position, and type influence the success of DPC. Choosing the right pulp capping material is also crucial.[34]

Studies have reported high success rates of vital pulp therapy with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), where the survival analysis in a multicenter clinical trial with a 3-year follow-up showed an 85% cumulative survival rate of pulp.[5] According to a systematic review and meta-analysis, the success rate of DPC using MTA varies from 80.3% to 100% (average of 91.1%), whereas dentin bridge development ranges from 33.3% to 100% (average of 74%).[6]

Laser therapy has the capacity to improve tissue repair, lower the microbial load, and achieve hemostasis and recently been introduced as an adjunct in DPC. Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) particularly with diode lasers, can stimulate cellular activity, accelerate healing, and promote mineralized tissue formation.[7] A 12-month randomized clinical trial comparing platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) with MTA reported a clinical and radiographic success rate of 92.59% for PRF in DPC.[8]

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis comparing PRF with MTA found no statistically significant difference in clinical success rates at 6- and 12-months follow-up suggesting PRF can be a promising biological alternative for vital pulp therapy.[9] However, clinical evidence regarding the combined use of PBMT with PRF and MTA remains limited.[10] Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of PBMT alone and in combination with PRF and MTA, on postoperative pain and dentin bridge formation using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT). The null hypothesis for this study was that there would be no effect of PBMT alone or in combination with PRF and MTA on postoperative pain and dentin bridge formation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This randomized clinical trial received ethical approval from Siksha ‘O’ Anusandhan University (Ref: IEC-IDS/IDS/SOA/2024/I-06) and was registered on Clinical Trials.gov (CTRI/2025/06/088275). The study followed CONSORT 2010 guidelines and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2008).

Criteria for patient selection

Patients attending the Institute of Dental Sciences were screened for eligibility. Written informed consent was obtained after the study details were provided. Based on Gunaydin et al.,[11] the sample size was calculated at a 5% significance level with 80% power and accounting for a 10% dropout rate, n = 60 participants were recruited (n = 20 per group). Inclusion criteria: Reversible pulpitis; Mandibular and Maxillary permanent molars with a pulpal exposure of 0.5–1.0 mm; periodontally healthy teeth with a probing depth of 3 mm or less; healthy patients aged between 17 and 45 years; no use of analgesics or antibiotics within the past two weeks;Absence of signs or symptoms, such as pain, swelling, or tenderness to percussion, along with periapical pathology.

Exclusion Criteria: Presence of irreversible pulpitis; History of previous restoration on the affected tooth; Inability to localize tooth pain; Presence of any systemic disease; Use of pain medication within one week prior to the procedure; Presence of signs or symptoms such as pain, swelling, or tenderness to percussion, along with the presence of periapical pathology.

Randomization and blinding

The sequentially, numbered, opaque, sealed, envelopes method was used to ensure an unbiased distribution of patients. A nonclinical assistant handed the envelopes to selected patients and grouped them. To eliminate bias in evaluating pain scores and dentin bridge formation, a single evaluator who was blinded to the study conducted the assessments.

Clinical procedure

To minimize protocol variability, a single trained operator conducted the clinical procedure for all groups. A mouthwash containing 0.2% chlorhexidine was given to the patients. Preoperative vitality using (cold test and electric pulp test) and verbal rating scale (Score 0: no pain; score 1: mild pain; score 2: moderate pain and score 3: severe pain) were recorded. After local anesthesia, a rubber dam (Hygenic, Coltene, USA) was placed and under 3.5x magnification using loupes (Orikam, Eighteeth, China) caries detecting dye (Kuraray, Japan) was used to identify infected dentin, which was then removed with a low-speed handpiece (20,000 rpm) and sterile round diamond bur (1.0 mm), starting from the periphery to the center. Any remaining soft caries was eliminated using a sharp spoon excavator (Etch endo, India). For pinpoint pulpal exposures of 0.5–1.0 mm, hemostasis was achieved by dabbing the area with a cotton pellet soaked in 3% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, followed by rinsing with saline to neutralize the sodium hypochlorite.[12]

The DPC procedure was randomly assigned to one of three groups (n = 20) mentioned below: Group A1: MTA with PBMT: A single cycle of a 980 nm diode laser (Novo Lase, India) was administered in continuous mode using a 4-mm multi-tip, positioned 1 mm away from the pulp. The laser was set to a low-level biostimulation program with a power of 1W, an energy of 100 mJ, and a frequency of 10 Hz, and it was applied for 60 s. Both the operator and the patient wore safety goggles during the procedure. A 2-mm thick layer of MTA (Prevest MTA Plus, Prevest Denpro, India) was placed over the exposed pulp. A 1-mm layer of GIC (Voco Ionofil Plus, Germany) was applied over the MTA, followed by composite restoration (Charisma, Kulzer, Germany) [Figure 1]. Group A2: MTA without PBMT: The MTA placement was completed as described above.

For the preparation of PRF, 10 mL of venous blood was collected and centrifuged (Remi R8C, Remi Elektrotechnik Ltd, India) following Choukroun’s protocol. Once extracted, the PRF clot, measuring 3 mm × 3 mm, was carefully placed over the pulpal exposure and compressed for proper adaptation. A 1-mm layer of glass ionomer cement (GIC) was then applied, followed by a composite restoration.[13]

Group B2: PRF without PBMT: The PRF placement was completed as described above.

Group C: PBMT was administered using the same laser protocol as that used in Group A1/B1. Approximately 1 mm of TheraCal LC (BISCO, USA) was applied to the exposed pulp and light-cured (20 s) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, a 1-mm layer of GIC was added, followed by a composite restoration. Pre and postclinical radiographs were recorded for all the groups.

Outcome assessment

Postoperative pain was assessed using the Verbal Rating Scale (VRS) through telephone interviews conducted at 24 h, 1 week, and 2 weeks.

To assess pulp vitality, cold test and electric pulp test were done at 3, 6, and 12 months. In addition, the thickness of the dentin bridge was measured using CBCT at the same follow-up intervals as a secondary outcome. The scoring system used in the present study classified dentin bridge formation into five categories based on CBCT appearance and estimated thickness.[14] Score 0: No visible dentin bridge (0 mm), Score 1: Thin, incomplete radiopaque bridge (<0.2 mm), Score 2: Partial but continuous thin bridge (0.2–0.5 mm), Score 3: Well-defined, continuous moderately thick bridge (0.5–1.0 mm), and Score 4: Thick, uniform, fully formed dentin bridge (>1 mm).

Signs of clinical failure included a loss of vitality, spontaneous discomfort, sensitivity to heat stimuli, tenderness to percussion, as well as the presence of swelling, sinus tracts, fistulas, or abnormal mobility. In addition, if follow-up radiographs showed periapical or furcal radiolucency, a widened periodontal ligament space, or root resorption that indicated a radiographic failure. Based on both clinical and radiographic characteristics, a blinded assessor evaluated the overall success or failure of the treatment.

Statistical analysis

The data were coded and imported into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using Armonk, NY, USA: IBM Corp. The mean and standard deviation (SD), two descriptive statistics, were computed. The comparisons within groups at different times were carried out using t-tests for paired samples. A 95% confidence interval and a 5% significance level were used.

RESULTS

The study involved 60 participants with a mean age of 33.2 years and a SD of 7.46 years, reflecting a relatively young adult population. The gender distribution was balanced, with 48.3% females and 51.7% males. All treated teeth were molars and 40% of cases involved occlusal cavities, while 60% involved proximal cavities, indicating a higher prevalence of proximal caries [Table 1].

In all groups, the preoperative pain score had a mean of 1.65 (SD = 0.48), indicating mild discomfort before the intervention. After treatment, pain levels significantly decreased over time. At 24 h, the mean pain score was statistically insignificant. By 1 week, pain scores decreased in Group B2, C (0.000), A2 (0.003), B1 (0.015), and A1 (0.168). At 2 weeks, all groups showed significant pain reduction (0.00), indicating continued relief throughout the healing period.

The vitality test indicated that Group B2 had a 100% positive response at all intervals, while other groups had at least one negative response. A total of 15 cases showed treatment failure, who underwent root canal treatment, and predominantly were female patients aged 41–45.

Five patients reported pain at 24 h and were advised to take anti-inflammatory medication for 3–5 days based on pain severity. According to the VRS, three had severe pain and two had moderate pain. After 5 days, all were asymptomatic, but over 3–6 months, one patient from each group developed swelling. CBCT revealed no changes in all groups at 3 months. However, by 6 months, 90% of cases in the B2 group (score 2) formed a dentin bridge, followed by groups C, A2, B1, and A. By 12 months, 50% of the B2 group achieved a score of 3, while others remained at score 2. Overall, the combination of PRF and PBMT yielded the best healing outcomes [Table 2 and Figure 3].

DISCUSSION

Dental pulp exposure can result from deep caries, trauma, or iatrogenic causes, and the pulpal response depends on the nature and severity of the injury.[715] In this study, only permanent molars with carious pulp exposure were selected because molars are more prone to caries due to their deep pits and fissures, larger pulp chambers, and ease of clinical access.[16] They are also suitable for research, as dentin bridge formation can be reliably evaluated using radiographic methods such as CBCT.[17]

The success of DPC relies on proper case selection, effective bleeding control, prevention of bacterial contamination, and an adequate coronal seal. The factors such as patient age, caries experience, and exposure site also influence the outcomes.[6] Younger patients (18–40 years old) in this study had higher success rates (~70.9%) than older patients (40–45 years old; ~61.2%). This is probably because younger pulps had superior vascularity, more stem cell availability, and higher growth factor expression.

Postoperative pain within 24 h is due to the initial inflammatory response triggered by pulp exposure, operative trauma, and mechanical manipulation of dentinal tissues. The early inflammatory cascade involving prostaglandins, bradykinin, and other mediators may dominate the pain perception during this period, regardless of the pulp capping materials used.[18] The biological advantages of PRF (growth factor release), MTA (biocompatibility and sealing ability), or PBMT (anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects) may not be evident within 24 h.[1920]

MTA promotes odontoblast-like cell development and the creation of more homogeneous dentin bridges by providing better sealing, increased bioactivity, and greater biocompatibility.[21] MTA has been shown in clinical trials to have greater success rates than calcium hydroxide.[22]

A promising substitute is PRF, an autologous biomaterial rich in growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor-beta, and vascular endothelial growth factor. PRF serves as a scaffold for the creation of dentin bridges and promotes angiogenesis, tissue regeneration, and odontoblastic differentiation. Research has shown positive results with PRF, suggesting it may be useful for controlling exposure to carious pulp.[23]

Recently, laser-assisted DPC has attracted interest for its antibacterial properties, rapid hemostasis, and tissue-healing effects.[2425] Diode lasers efficiently penetrate dentin, lower the bacterial burden, and foster a healing environment. Their biostimulatory properties encourage the creation of mineralized tissue, decrease inflammation, and increase cell proliferation.[262728] After a year, 45 out of 60 treated teeth in this study were still viable. Because of improved healing and early mineralized tissue development, postoperative pain and sensitivity were lower in laser-assisted groups. Fifteen cases demonstrated failure characterized by persistent pain and swelling, necessitating root canal therapy (RCT). On access opening, the involved teeth were found to be necrotic due to microleakage after restoration, operative trauma, severe inflammatory response, occlusal stress, and host factors. Failures in MTA groups could be due to bacterial microlekage and preexisting irreversible pulpal inflammation. A recent randomized clinical trial reported approximately 92.6% success for MTA in DPC.[8] However, some contradictory studies have reported high success rate (100%) for MTA IPC.[29]

Failures in PRF group might have occurred due to rapid degradation of the PRF matrix, insufficient sealing ability compared with bioceramic materials and persistent bacterial contaminatiom. A clinical trial comparing PRF with MTA reported similar success rate (92.6%), but failures were still observed. Some investigations have shown that PRF can produce greater dentin bridge formation and favorable pulp healing.[30]

Six cases from the PBMT group failed. This could be due to polymerization shrinkage caused by Theracal LC. In addition, Theracal LC also contains resin components which may have caused initial cytotoxicity.[31]

The CBCT findings of the present study demonstrate progressive dentin bridge formation with increasing follow-up duration, indicating ongoing pulpal healing and reparative dentin deposition.[14] Among the groups evaluated, the PRF + PBMT group demonstrated the most advanced dentin bridge formation, as evidenced by higher CBCT scores and greater dentin bridge thickness [Table 2]. These findings suggest that the combination of biological scaffolds and photobiomodulation may enhance pulp regeneration following DPC.[32] Diode laser-assisted pulp capping is a useful method for preserving pulp vitality, lowering postoperative pain and improving dentin bridge formation when paired with PRF. Limitations of the study include the need to increase the sample size and the follow-up period.

CONCLUSION

As to conclude, DPC proved to be an effective treatment modality for maintaining pulp vitality when proper case selection and aseptic protocol were followed. The combination of PRF and PBMT demonstrated superior clinical outcomes, showing greater reductions in postoperative pain and enhanced dentin bridge formation at 3, 6, and 12 months of follow-up compared to other groups.
