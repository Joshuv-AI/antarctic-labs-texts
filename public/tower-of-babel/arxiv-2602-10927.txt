Two-Scale Analysis of the Electrostatics of Dielectric Crystals: Emergence of Polarization Density and Boundary Charges Preprint: SIAM Multiscale Modeling and Simulation 24(1):162-186, 2026 (DOI: 10.1137/25M1747907)
Shoham Sen, Yang Wang, Timothy Breitzman, Kaushik Dayal

Abstract
Ionic crystals, such as solid electrolytes and complex oxides, are central to modern technologies for energy storage, sensing, actuation, and other functional applications. An important fundamental issue in the atomic and quantum-scale modeling of these materials is defining the macroscopic polarization. In a periodic crystal, the usual definition of the polarization as the first moment of the charge density in a unit cell is found to depend qualitatively – allowing even a change in the sign! – and quantitatively on the choice of unit cell. We examine this issue using a rigorous approach based on the framework of 2-scale convergence. By examining the continuum limit of when the lattice spacing is much smaller than the characteristic dimensions of the body, we show that the 2-scale limit provides both a bulk polarization as well as a surface charge density supported on the boundary of the body. Further, different choices of the periodic unit cell of the body lead to correspondingly different partial unit cells at the boundary; these choices give to different bulk polarization and surface charges but compensate such that the electric field and energy are independent of the choice of unit cell.

\displaystyle l\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}

The definition of a unit-cell and partial unit cell can be obtained from their scaled counterparts by re-scaling each by

We define the family of charge densities as
\displaystyle\{\rho_{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{I}}
for each lattice
\displaystyle l\mathcal{L}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
represents an indexing set for the different values of the scaling parameter. To establish the polarization theorem (

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}\oplus l\Box\subset\Omega

51

All 92
. It is worth noting that while the polarization theorem (
58

As noted earlier, we require charge neutrality to obtain a convergent energy density. We impose charge neutrality by requiring that

{\int_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}\oplus l\Box}\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=0\qquad\qquad\forall\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}\oplus l\Box\subset\Omega,~\forall l\in\mathcal{I}.}

(13)

We employ the dipole scaling that requires that as
\displaystyle l\to 0
, we proportionately increase the magnitude of
\displaystyle\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})
. We introduce the scaled charge density
\displaystyle\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{l}\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=\rho_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}+\boldsymbol{z})=:\rho_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l},\boldsymbol{z})
where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}\in l\Box
. We now introduce the dipole scaling in the second argument as

{\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=\rho_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{z})=\dfrac{1}{l}\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\dfrac{\boldsymbol{z}}{l}),}

(14)

where in the above expression, we are locally scaling the magnitude of the charge density inversely with the size of the unit cell.

Example 2.1

Consider

(15)

\displaystyle[0,L]

\displaystyle[0,1]
.
The corner map is

Using the two-scale identification, we have:

\tilde{\rho}_{l}(x,y)=\sin(2\pi n+2\pi y)=\sin(2\pi y)

(17)

To check the charge neutrality condition (

\int_{nl+[0,l]}\rho_{l}\ \mathrm{d}x=\int_{nl+[0,l]}\dfrac{\sin(2\pi x/l)}{l}\ \mathrm{d}x=\int_{[0,l]}\dfrac{1}{l}\sin(2\pi z/l)\ \mathrm{d}z=0,

3
Problem Formulation

We examine two problems; the first is posed on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{3}
arising from a compactly supported charge distribution, while the second is an interior problem formulated to determine the electrostatic potential within a bounded domain
\displaystyle\Omega
. We determine the homogenized and corrector equations corresponding to the Poisson problem for each.

The boundary is decomposed as
\displaystyle\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{d}\cup\Gamma_{n}
with
\displaystyle\Gamma_{d}\cap\Gamma_{n}=\{\}
, where
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
is the boundary, and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{d}
and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}
are parts of the boundary where Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions are specified respectively;
\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}
is assumed
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{1}
.

In the first problem, we consider a sequence of charge distributions
\displaystyle\{\tilde{\rho}_{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{I}}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\displaystyle\text{Sppt}(\tilde{\rho}_{l})=\Omega~\forall l\in\mathcal{I}
. The charge distribution is locally periodic with respect to the lattice
\displaystyle l\mathcal{L}
with zero mean and
\displaystyle L^{2}
bound in each cell. The potential is specified on
\displaystyle\Gamma_{d}
with decay boundary conditions at infinity. The family of PDEs are stated as follows:

\begin{split}-\Delta&\Phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=\dfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})}{l}~~\text{in}~\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\Gamma_{d},\\
&\Phi_{l}=f~~~\text{on}~\Gamma_{d},\\
&\Phi_{l}=0~~~\text{as}~~|\boldsymbol{x}|\to\infty.\\
&\Phi_{l}\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3})~~\text{and}~~\nabla\Phi_{l}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),~\forall l\in\mathcal{I}\end{split},

(19)

where the last condition ensures that the energy is finite.

In the second problem, we analyze a sequence of charge distributions
\displaystyle\{\tilde{\rho}_{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{I}}
defined on a domain
\displaystyle\Omega
, which is locally periodic with respect to the lattice
\displaystyle l\mathcal{L}
and is
\displaystyle L^{2}
bounded with zero mean on each unit cell. A family of partial differential equations associated with these charge distributions
\displaystyle\{\tilde{\rho}_{l}\}
is studied, complemented with appropriate boundary conditions. The family of PDEs are stated as follows

\begin{split}-\Delta&\Phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=\dfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})}{l}~~\text{in}~\Omega,\\
&\Phi_{l}=f~~~\text{on}~\Gamma_{d},\\
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial n}&\Phi_{l}=g~~~\text{on}~\Gamma_{n},\\
\nabla&\Phi_{l}~\text{is bounded uniformly in $\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega),~\forall l\in\mathcal{I}$},\end{split}

(20)

where we are imposing the same boundary conditions for each of the problems. The last condition corresponds to the energy being finite.

Since our objective is to compute the polarization in each unit cell, it is reasonable to require that the charge density be
\displaystyle L^{2}
bounded within each unit cell. Furthermore, as the charge density is defined over the entire domain, this local
\displaystyle L^{2}
boundedness is expected to imply a global
\displaystyle L^{2}
bound on the entire domain. To establish this relationship, we impose the condition that the charge density is
\displaystyle L^{2}
bounded within each unit cell.

{||\tilde{\rho}_{l}||_{L^{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}\oplus l\Box)}:=\bigg[\int_{\Box}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+l\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C,~~\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,~\forall l\in\mathcal{I}}.

(21)

Note that
\displaystyle C
is independent of
\displaystyle l
.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\displaystyle||\tilde{\rho}_{l}||_{L^{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}\oplus l\Box)}<C
for all
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
and for all
\displaystyle l\in\mathcal{I}
, then
\displaystyle||\tilde{\rho}_{l}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<C
for all
\displaystyle l\in\mathcal{I}
and
\displaystyle C
independent of
\displaystyle l
.

Proof.

We demonstrate that the above bounds also implies that
\displaystyle\exists~C>0
such that
\displaystyle||\tilde{\rho}_{l}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<C

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&=\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\Box)}l^{3}\int_{\Box}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}+\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
})}l^{3}\underbrace{\int_{\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
}}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}}_{\leq\int_{\Box}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}}\\
&\leq\underbrace{\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\Box)\cup\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
})}}_{\#(\Omega/l^{3})}l^{3}\underbrace{\int_{\Box}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}}_{\leq C}\leq C|\Omega|+o(1)\end{split}

(22)

where
\displaystyle\#(\Omega/l^{3})
represents the number of boxes of size
\displaystyle l
covering
\displaystyle\Omega
. The fact that
\displaystyle\sum_{\#(\Omega/l^{3})}l^{3}
is an approximation to
\displaystyle|\Omega|
form ‘above’ is captured by the
\displaystyle o(1)
term, i.e., we have

\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\Box)}l^{3}\leq|\Omega|\leq\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\Box)\cup\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
})}l^{3}~~\forall l\in\mathcal{I};

(23)

we justify that we will get
\displaystyle|\Omega|
in the limit by adding an
\displaystyle o(1)
term which goes to 0 as
\displaystyle l\to 0
.
∎

Recall the two-scale identification process outlined in (
14
). Since
\displaystyle\{\rho_{l}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
is defined on
\displaystyle\Omega
, it depends on a single variable. In contrast, a two-scale limit requires two variables. The mapping of a single-variable function to a two-variable function is achieved through the two-scale identification process. To extract a well-defined two-scale limit, it is necessary to demonstrate that for a given sequence in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a corresponding sequence that is bounded in the space
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega\times\Box)
. This allows extraction of a weak limit in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega\times\Box)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\{\tilde{\rho}_{l}\}_{l\in\mathcal{I}}
be a bounded sequence in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
be a given sequence of functions. For functions defined through the two-scale identification process (
14
), it follows that
\displaystyle\{\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\}_{l\in\mathcal{I}}
is a bounded sequence in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega\times\Box)
.

Proof.

Noting that
\displaystyle||\tilde{\rho}_{l}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C
,

\begin{split}C\geq\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}&=\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\Box)}l^{3}\int_{\Box}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}+\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
})}l^{3}\int_{\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
}}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\\
&=\sum_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(\Box)\cup\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
})}l^{3}\int_{\Box}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{x})\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y},\end{split}

(24)

where the characteristic function
\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega}
is employed to substitute the integral over the partial unit cells with the integrals over the corresponding full unit cells.

Observe that the identified sequence
\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})
remains constant with respect to the first argument. Consequently, the summation can be replaced by an integral, yielding:

C\geq\int_{\Omega\times\Box}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y},

(25)

where the characteristic function on
\displaystyle\Omega
is omitted, as the integration is already performed over
\displaystyle\Omega
. Consequently, we have demonstrated that the identified sequence is bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega\times\Box)
.
∎

Our formulation is presented with respect to the scaled charge density
\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})\left(=l\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)
. This scaling is chosen to ensure the extraction of a dipole moment in the limit. If the objective were to extract a quadrupole moment in the homogenized limit, the appropriate scaling would involve scaling the charge density with
\displaystyle l^{-2}
while imposing the conditions that the zeroth- and first-order moments vanish. A similar approach can be applied to derive all higher-order moments.

The homogenization process is organized as follows: for each
\displaystyle l\in\mathcal{I}
, the lattice
\displaystyle l\mathcal{L}
is introduced, and the domain
\displaystyle\Omega
is partitioned into two distinct subsets: a collection of unit cells fully contained within
\displaystyle\Omega
, denoted as
\displaystyle\Omega(\Box)
, and a collection of partial unit cells that are not entirely contained within
\displaystyle\Omega
, denoted as
\displaystyle\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}
})
. Leveraging charge neutrality within each unit cell, the charge distribution in each unit cell is replaced by the net polarization. In contrast, the partial unit cells, which are not charge neutral, contribute a surface charge denoted by
\displaystyle\sigma
. This surface charge is assigned to the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
, resulting in a homogenized problem defined on
\displaystyle\Omega(\Box)\cup\partial\Omega
. In the limit as
\displaystyle l\to 0
, the homogenized problem is posed on the entire domain
\displaystyle\Omega
. For a visual explanation of this process, along with illustrative examples, the interested reader is directed to Section 2 of
SWBD 24
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let

denote a family of charge distributions satisfying the following constraints on the charge density:

(56)

along with the following scaling constraints on the domain
\displaystyle\Omega

\begin{split}\#(\Omega(\Box))&=C\dfrac{|\Omega|}{l^{3}}+o(1),\\
\#(\Omega(\mbox{\begin{picture}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{5.0}}\put(1.0,0.0){\line{1}{2}{5.0}}\put(6.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{10.0}}\end{picture}

(57)

where

Under the above assumptions, it can be established that, for all

Remark 5.1
.

We make the following observation regarding the charge neutrality condition employed above. Imposing that the function be locally periodic and charge-neutral on each unit cell may prove excessively restrictive. Specifically, such a constraint could lead to either the set of admissible functions being periodic or the function itself dictating the choice of the lattice, thereby precluding arbitrary lattice shifts from yielding charge-neutral unit cells. A more flexible approach involves relaxing the charge neutrality condition to allow for charge neutrality with an error of
\displaystyle O(l^{\alpha})
, where the parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
must be sufficiently large to ensure that the total charge in the domain vanishes in the homogenized limit. This relaxation offers the advantage that, even when the function is locally periodic, charge neutrality with an error of
\displaystyle O(l^{\alpha})
can be enforced for any given choice of the lattice.

6
Non-uniqueness of the dipole moment

The polarization, defined as the dipole density per unit volume, is an inherently ill-defined quantity. To illustrate this, we consider the following example:

Example 6.1
.

Consider a charge distribution
\displaystyle\rho_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
\displaystyle\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{l}\sin\left(2\pi\frac{x_{1}}{l}\right)
, which is
\displaystyle l
-periodic. We now evaluate the dipole moment with respect to an arbitrary unit cell located at
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\oplus l[0,1]^{3}
.

\boldsymbol{p}(\boldsymbol{a})=\int_{\boldsymbol{a}\oplus l[0,1]^{3}}\dfrac{(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a})}{l}\dfrac{\rho_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})}{l^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}V_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\int_{[0,1]}\boldsymbol{y}\sin(2\pi\dfrac{a_{1}}{l}+2\pi y_{1})\ \mathrm{d}y_{1}=-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\cos{(2\pi\dfrac{a_{1}}{l})}(1,0,0).

(77)

Thus, depending on the choice of the unit cell (determined by the position of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}
), different values for the polarization vector
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}
are obtained. It is important to emphasize that while the total charge remains invariant, the polarization varies based on the selection of the unit cell.∎

It is essential to address the issue of non-uniqueness in the polarization distribution arising from the choice of unit cell. The problem has been formulated such that the selection of the origin
\displaystyle O
, the lattice
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
, and the shape of the unit cell
\displaystyle\Box
are arbitrary. We denote this tuple by
\displaystyle(O,\mathcal{L},\Box)
.

Consider a sequence of charge distributions. The polarization and total surface charge calculated for a given tuple
\displaystyle(O,\mathcal{L},\Box)
are denoted by
\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{p}_{l}\}:\Omega(\Box)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\displaystyle\{\sigma_{l}+\boldsymbol{p}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, respectively. If, instead, a different tuple
\displaystyle(\underaccent{\tilde}{O},\underaccent{\tilde}{\scr{L}},\underaccent{\tilde}{\Box})
were chosen, the corresponding sequence of polarization and total surface charge would be denoted by
\displaystyle\underaccent{\tilde}{\ve{p}}_{l}:\Omega(\underaccent{\tilde}{\Box})\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\displaystyle\{\underaccent{\tilde}{\sigma}_{l}+\underaccent{\tilde}{\ve{p}}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

From Example
6.1
, it can be demonstrated that for any
\displaystyle l
,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{l}\neq\underaccent{\tilde}{\ve{p}}_{l}
and
\displaystyle\sigma_{l}\neq\underaccent{\tilde}{\sigma}_{l}
. The discrepancy in the surface charge arises due to variations in the shape of the partial unit cells for different choices of unit cells. Consequently, since the polarization and surface charge distribution differ for arbitrary
\displaystyle l\in\mathcal{I}
, they will also differ in the limit. This represents a significant issue, as it implies that the homogenized equations being solved in (

We assert that, although

\displaystyle\sigma

To begin, we note that the sequence of potentials

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle\Phi_{l}

\displaystyle\Omega

Now, consider a different choice of lattice and unit cell. The sequence of potentials
\displaystyle\{\Phi_{l}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
would still satisfy Poisson’s equation for the same charge distribution
\displaystyle\{\rho_{l}\}_{\mathcal{I}}
