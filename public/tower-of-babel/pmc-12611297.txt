Virological Success With Long-Acting Cabotegravir and Rilpivirine Despite Historical Rilpivirine Resistance: A Real-World Case Series
Authors: Félix Gutiérrez, Marta Fernández-González, Christian Ledesma, Javier García-Abellán, Leandro López, Melissa Bello-Perez, Sergio Padilla, Federico García, Mar Masiá
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn a case series of 13 individuals with historical rilpivirine (RPV) resistance-associated mutations, predominantly E138A, long-acting cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB/RPV) maintained suppression over a median 24-month follow-up. Twelve were virologically suppressed at switch. No virological failures occurred, supporting cautious CAB/RPV use in selected patients with remote or polymorphic mutations.Keywords: E138A, long-acting cabotegravir and rilpivirine, NNRTI resistance, rilpivirine mutations, virological failure

Long-acting injectable cabotegravir/rilpivirine (CAB/RPV LA) offers a maintenance option that avoids daily oral dosing. However, regulatory authorities and clinical guidelines advise against its use in individuals with current or past RPV resistance, due to concerns about virological failure (VF) [1]. These recommendations are based on data from registrational trials, which identified RPV resistance-associated mutations (RAMs), HIV-1 subtype A6/A1, and a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m² as independent risk factors for VF [2]. Notably, individuals with known RPV resistance were excluded from these trials, and RAMs were retrospectively detected through proviral DNA testing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells.

While proviral DNA testing may identify archived resistance in virologically suppressed individuals, its availability in routine care is limited, and its clinical utility remains controversial. Detection may both under- and overestimate clinically relevant resistance due to defective proviral genomes [3–5].

In real-world settings, longitudinal viral load trajectories, treatment history, and historical plasma RNA-based resistance genotypes often provide more actionable data. Yet, the exclusion of patients with known RPV RAMs from clinical trials has left a gap in evidence regarding the safety of CAB/RPV LA in this population.

To address this, we conducted a retrospective analysis of individuals who transitioned to CAB/RPV LA at our center, focusing on those with documented historical RPV RAMs in plasma genotypes. No archived resistance testing was performed before switching.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective case series was drawn from a prospective cohort of adults with HIV who transitioned from daily oral ART to CAB/RPV LA between January 2023 and April 2024 at Hospital General Universitario de Elche, Spain. For this analysis, we included individuals who met all of the following criteria: (1) CAB/RPV LA initiation during the study period, (2) documented RPV RAMs in historical plasma genotypes, and (3) ≥12 months of available follow-up data at the time of analysis. We excluded individuals with documented VF to non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) or integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), defined as ≥2 consecutive HIV-1 RNA values ≥200 copies/mL or any ≥1000 copies/mL with proper drug adherence.

Patient Consent Statement

The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hospital General Universitario de Elche. All participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures and Laboratory Assessments

Participants were followed using a standardized protocol that included regular close virological monitoring. HIV RNA was measured at baseline, one month after the first injection, and every 2 months thereafter [6]. Baseline data collected included demographic information, BMI, HIV-1 subtype, and prior ART history. All historical resistance genotypes were thoroughly reviewed retrospectively for the presence of RAMs. Mutations were interpreted using the Stanford HIV Drug Resistance Database (HIVDB, version 9.8) and categorized as conferring low-, intermediate-, or high-level resistance.

Clinical switching decisions relied on treatment history and resistance data available in the primary electronic medical record. Additional historical RPV RAMs, documented in paper archives or secondary electronic databases not routinely accessible to clinicians, were reviewed retrospectively for this study.

Virological Definitions and Outcomes

Virological responses were classified as follows: (1) fully suppressed viremia: all HIV RNA <20 copies/mL; (2) intermittent viremia: a single HIV RNA 20–999 copies/mL with adjacent values <20 copies/mL; (3) persistent low-level viremia: ≥2 consecutive values between 20 and 199 copies/mL; and (4) confirmed VF: ≥2 consecutive HIV RNA values ≥200 copies/mL or any single value ≥1000 copies/mL.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized cohort characteristics. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).

Role of the Funding Source

The funder had no role in the study design, conduct, or reporting. All authors had full data access and approved the final version.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Of the 216 individuals who initiated CAB/RPV LA during the study period, 124 (57.4%) had historical HIV resistance genotyping available. Among them, 13 (10.5%) had at least one documented RPV RAM and were included. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1, with full case-level details provided in Supplementary Table 1. The median age was 49 years (IQR: 33–51), and 84.6% were male. The most common mutation was E138A. According to the Stanford HIVDB, three individuals had high-level resistance to RPV, three had intermediate-level, and seven had low-level resistance. The median time since detection of RPV RAMs was 6.5 years (IQR: 5–14). Only one individual had an INSTI RAM (L74I/M) potentially associated with low-level resistance to cabotegravir. HIV subtype A6/A1 was not detected in any participant (subtype data available for 12 of 13), and four individuals (30.8%) had a BMI ≥30 kg/m².

All but one participant were virologically suppressed at switch (median 6.8 years, IQR: 4.4–13.5). The exception was a 64-year-old woman with long-standing adherence difficulties and a historic L100I mutation—conferring high-level RPV resistance—identified 22.5 years earlier. Despite support, she had experienced intermittent viremia due to inconsistent oral ART adherence. At CAB/RPV LA selection, her viral load was 64 copies/mL, but returned 83 400 copies/mL on the injection day. She admitted stopping ART 3 weeks prior. Genotyping from that sample showed susceptibility to both CAB and RPV. This finding most likely reflects replication of wild-type virus in the absence of drug-selective pressure.

Virological Outcomes

The median follow-up duration on CAB/RPV LA was 24 months (IQR: 18–25). Seven individuals maintained full suppression (<20 copies/mL throughout follow-up). Five experienced intermittent viremia with spontaneous resuppression, and one exhibited persistent low-level viremia.

DISCUSSION

In this real-world case series, long-acting CAB/RPV maintained virological suppression in all individuals with historical RPV RAMs. No VF occurred, even among those with high-level resistance mutations or elevated BMI.

These findings align with post hoc analyses from the ATLAS and FLAIR trials, in which a single VF occurred among 31 participants with isolated archived RPV RAMs, and primarily in the absence of A6/A1 subtype or obesity as co-factors for failure [3]. The African CARES study, similarly found low failure rates despite prior NNRTI exposure and archived RPV RAMs [7].

Although proviral DNA testing can aid in the detection of archived resistance, its clinical utility remains controversial. Several studies suggest that historical plasma RNA-based genotypes often more accurately reflect clinically relevant resistance than proviral DNA, particularly in individuals with long-term virological suppression [8, 9]. Defective proviruses, decay of RAMs over time, and reductions in total and intact HIV DNA during prolonged ART further diminish the predictive value of proviral sequencing [5, 10, 11]. Indeed, multiple studies have demonstrated that mutations such as M184V and NNRTI RAMs—including E138A—may become undetectable with time [12–14].

Notably, 12 of 13 participants in our cohort, were long-term virologically suppressed before switching to CAB/RPV LA, likely reducing reservoir size and the clinical impact of archived or low-level RPV RAMs. In contrast, initiating CAB/RPV LA in viremic patients with such mutations could increase the risk of failure, underscoring the importance of timing and patient selection.

Most cases in our cohort involved E138A, a polymorphic mutation found in 2%–5% of ART-naive individuals and associated with an approximately twofold reduction in RPV susceptibility [15, 16]. Our findings should therefore be interpreted primarily in the context of this mutation. In contrast, mutations such as L100I and Y181C are unequivocally selected by RPV and confer significant reduction in susceptibility. The favorable outcomes in the few patients with these major RAMs should not imply that such mutations are benign. In these cases, clinicians were unaware of archived RAMs at the time of switch, and proviral DNA burden was not assessed, limiting confounding by indication. The absence of VF may instead reflect other mitigating factors, such as long-term virological suppression, decay of archived resistant variants, or extended wild-type replication after resistance first emerged. These considerations highlight the complexity of interpreting archived resistance and caution against generalizing our findings to all RPV RAMs.

Another consideration is the potential role of lenacapavir (LEN). In our cohort, CAB/RPV LA was used without LEN, as historical RPV RAMs were unknown at the time of switch. In setting where archived NNRTI resistance is documented, adding LEN could help ensure two fully active agents [17]. Further clinical experience will clarify optimal patient selection and the role of adjunctive LEN with CAB.

Overall, our observations emphasize real-world challenges in integrating archived resistance into treatment decisions: switches relied on treatment history and available resistance records, with additional RPV RAMs identified only retrospectively.

This study has limitations. The small sample size, and retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability. Although participants with known NNRTI or INSTI failure were excluded, undiagnosed prior VF cannot be rule out, especially with rare mutations like L100I. Nonetheless, the absence of VF, despite the presence of RPV RAMs, reinforces the potential virological durability of CAB/RPV LA in well-selected patients.

In conclusion, these findings support the cautious expansion of CAB/RPV LA use beyond current guideline exclusions, particularly in patients with remote or polymorphic RAMs such as E138A. However, they should not be interpreted as evidence that all past RPV RAMs are clinically irrelevant. Larger, prospective studies are needed to define the impact of major RAMs such as L100I and Y181C and to refine patient selection criteria.
