Distribution of blood eosinophil counts in the Chinese general population
Authors: Peiji Yao, Lan Yang, Dan Liu, Dongyu He, Lei Li, Yifei Lin, Jin Huang, Weimin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Eosinophils (EOS) are a subset of multifunctional granulocytes, produced in the bone marrow and subsequently released into the peripheral blood [1]. Under homeostatic conditions, EOS primarily migrate to various tissues and organs, including the gastrointestinal tract, uterus, respiratory tract, spleen, adipose tissue and thymus [2], where they play crucial roles in tissue homeostasis, host defence and immune regulation [3]. Elevated EOS counts are critically involved in the pathogenesis of a broad spectrum of diseases, encompassing infectious, allergic, autoimmune and malignancies [4].

Despite clinical relevance, defining thresholds to differentiate “high EOS counts” from “normal EOS counts” remains contentious. Conventionally, eosinophilia is defined as an absolute peripheral blood EOS count >500 cells·μL−1 [5, 6]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that a uniform EOS threshold inadequately reflects the heterogeneous prognostic and therapeutic relevance of EOS across disease states and demographic groups. EOS counts below the conventional cut-off of 500 cells·μL−1 are still clinically associated with disease severity and adverse outcomes in some diseases. For instance, in COPD and asthma, EOS counts >300 cells·μL−1 are associated with a significantly increased risk of exacerbations, poor disease control and treatment escalation [7–9]. Furthermore, multiple studies have confirmed EOS counts >150 cells·μL−1 predict more favourable clinical responses to anti-interleukin (IL)-5/IL-5 receptor α treatments in severe asthma patients [10, 11]. This evidence suggests that the current upper limit of the EOS reference range may be set too high. Consequently, a detailed characterisation of blood EOS distributions and their associations with disease is essential for establishing reference ranges and actionable thresholds. In the general population, blood EOS counts typically exhibit a right-skewed distribution, with a median of 157.0 cells·μL−1 reported in European cohorts [12]. However, population heterogeneity due to ethnic differences must be considered, and data from Asian populations remain scarce, with only a single cross-sectional study with a limited sample size [13].

Therefore, we constructed cross-sectional analyses by leveraging data from a prospective, population-based health examination dataset comprising over 680 000 participants. We aimed to comprehensively characterise the overall and demographic-specific distributions of blood EOS counts in the general population and to identify key determinants. First, we described the overall distribution of EOS counts and their variation across demographic characteristics. Then, we conducted the age- and sex-specific analysis, generating percentile curves to visualise distributional changes across the life course. Finally, we explored the associations of EOS counts with demographic factors and diseases.

Methods

Study design

This study conducted a cross-sectional analysis utilising data from the West-China Hospital Alliance Longitudinal Epidemiology Wellness (WHALE) study, a large-scale, multicentre, prospective longitudinal investigation of individuals undergoing health check-ups at West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Chengdu, China) from 2010 to 2023 (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2200066950). Data were obtained from an expanding hospital consortium, which is being scaled to include 11 centres, with all participating sites adhering to standardised protocols for staff qualifications, equipment and data management, as detailed in the published cohort profile [14]. We included all participants aged ≥10 years who voluntarily joined the study and completed at least one general health examination. Exclusion criteria included missing blood EOS counts or missing height or weight data across all available records. Baseline EOS counts were used to analyse distribution characteristics, while EOS counts at disease diagnosis were used to investigate associations with specific diseases. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval number 2024-1762) and was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guideline (www.strobe-statement.org).

Data collection

Blood EOS counts

Absolute EOS counts were measured as part of routine complete blood count testing during health examinations. Following a 10–12 h overnight fast, peripheral blood samples were collected in the early morning by skilled nursing staff at the Health Management Center of West China Hospital and subsequently analysed in the Department of Laboratory Medicine using the XE-2100 system (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) according to standardised protocols.

Covariates

Body weight and height were obtained using the ultrasonic auto anthropometer SG-1001SC (Chioy, Beijing, China), with participants wearing light indoor clothing and standing barefoot. Body mass index (BMI) was categorised according to the World Health Organization classification as underweight (<18.5 kg·m−2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg·m−2) and overweight or obese (≥25.0 kg·m−2) [15]. Smoking status and alcohol consumption in the questionnaire were categorised as current, former, never and unknown. Corticosteroid use was obtained from self-reported and clinical prescription records (outpatient and inpatient) extracted from the hospital's electronic health record system [16], and defined as any record of inhaled or systemic corticosteroid use within the past year [17]. Individuals with records indicating both routes of administration were preferentially classified as systemic.

Disease definitions

Spirometry was performed using the MasterScreen PFT System (Jaeger, Hoechberg, Germany) in accordance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines [18]. COPD was defined as a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <70% or self-reported diagnosis. Small airway dysfunction (SAD) was defined as the condition where at least two of the following three parameters, forced expiratory flow at 50% of FVC (FEF50%), forced expiratory flow at 75% of FVC (FEF75%) and forced expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of FVC (FEF25–75%), were <65% of their predicted values [19]. Pulmonary nodules were identified as focal, round, dense opacities with clear or indistinct margins and a diameter of ≤3 cm, detected via chest computed tomography [20]. Impaired liver function was defined as a total bilirubin level >1.5× upper limit of normal (ULN), or serum alanine transaminase or aspartate transaminase levels >3× ULN [21], and impaired renal function was defined as serum creatinine >1.5× ULN or glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <90 mL·min−1·1.73 m−2 [22]. Asthma, hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, rhinitis/rhinosinusitis and lung cancer were defined based on physician diagnoses recorded in electronic health records or self-reported history during the check-ups [23].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarised as number (percentage), while continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range (IQR)). Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests, histograms and Q–Q plots. For continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U-test or Kruskal–Wallis H-test was used for non-normal data distribution, and the t-test was used for normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test.

To explore the non-linearity between EOS counts and age, generalised additive models (GAMs) [24] were used to generate smoothed curves for the overall population and sex-stratified subgroups. Age- and sex-specific EOS percentile curves (5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 95th) were then constructed using the Box–Cox t (BCT) distribution within a GAM for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS) [25]. We assessed the distributional consistency by performing an internal temporal split validation, fitting percentile curves separately for two periods (2010–2018 and 2019–2023).

For multivariable linear regression with EOS counts as the outcome, analyses were performed on a log10-transformed scale to account for distributional skewness, and also on the original scale for interpretability. An initial model (Model 0) included all demographic variables. For each disease, sequential models were fitted: unadjusted (Model 1), adjusted for sex and age (Model 2), further adjusted for BMI and corticosteroid use (Model 3), and additionally adjusted for smoking and alcohol consumption (Model 4). Participants without any of the 10 diseases listed in the Disease definition section constituted the control group. Results are presented as coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals. In sensitivity analysis, logistic regression models with identical adjustment strategies were applied to identify factors associated with “high EOS” defined by three thresholds: the distributional 75th percentile [13, 26] (170 cells·μL−1) and two clinical cut-offs (300 and 500 cells·μL−1). Results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Two-sided p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method and pBonferroni<0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (www.r-project.org).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

The flow diagram of the study is shown in figure 1. A total of 643 922 participants were included in this study. Baseline data from all included participants were used to characterise the EOS distribution. Of these participants, 53.5% (n=344 799) were male and 46.5% (n=299 123) were female (table 1). The mean±sd age of participants was 44.1±13.4 years, with 96.2% aged between 20 and 70 years. The mean±sd BMI was 23.5±3.4 kg·m−2, with 6.0% being underweight, 63.5% having normal weight and 30.5% being overweight or obese. The majority of participants were never-smokers (70.9%) and without alcohol consumption (57.1%). The use of systemic (0.3%) or inhaled (0.2%) corticosteroids within the past year was reported in a small proportion.

Distribution features of blood EOS counts

Blood EOS counts exhibited a right-skewed distribution across the entire population and across sex, as illustrated in figure 2 and detailed in supplementary table S2. The median (range) count was 100 (0–6650) cells·μL−1, with the 5th, 25th, 75th and 95th percentiles at 30, 60, 170 and 360 cells·μL−1, respectively (table 2). Only a small proportion of participants exceeded 500 cells·μL−1 (2.41% in males and 1.25% in females) (supplementary table S2).

Distribution of blood eosinophil counts across demographic and disease subgroups

BMI: body mass index.

Blood EOS counts showed significant variation across strata of demographics and disease, presented in table 2 and supplementary figure S1. Males had higher EOS counts than females (median (IQR) 120 (80–200) versus 90 (50–140) cells·μL−1). Participants aged 20–70 years had lower EOS counts (100 (60–170) cells·μL−1) than those aged <20 years (120 (70–190) cells·μL−1) or ≥70 years (120 (80–200) cells·μL−1). Overweight or obese, current smokers and current alcohol drinkers had significantly higher EOS counts (all p<0.001), with consistent trends in sex-stratified analyses (supplementary table S3). EOS were significantly elevated in patients with COPD, asthma, SAD, pulmonary nodules, hypertension, allergic rhinitis/rhinosinusitis or impaired renal function (table 2), whereas lung cancer, rheumatoid arthritis or impaired liver function were significant only on sex stratification (supplementary table S3).

Sex- and age-specific distributions of blood EOS counts

EOS counts exhibited an approximately U-shaped trajectory across the overall population (supplementary figure S2) and in sex-stratified analyses, with consistently higher counts in males than females across all ages (figure 3a). In males, EOS counts decreased to a minimum between the ages of 20 and 30 years, whereas in females, the lowest point occurred between 35 and 45 years. Thereafter, EOS counts progressively increased with age, with a more pronounced upward trend observed beyond 60 years.

The derived sex- and age-specific 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 95th percentile curves for EOS counts are presented in figure 3b and c, providing corresponding population-based reference values. Temporal split validation of the percentile curves by refitting GAMLSS models to data from 2010–2018 and 2019–2023 confirmed their stability across periods (supplementary figure S3). The 5th, 25th, 50th and 75th percentile curves remained nearly consistent between the two periods, with minor differences observed for the 95th percentile curve in a limited number of age ranges (supplementary figure S3).

Factors associated with elevated blood EOS counts

In multivariate linear regression analyses, male gender, age <20 or >70 years, 1 kg·m−2 increase in BMI, former as well as current smoking were significantly associated with elevated EOS (all pBonferroni<0.05), with regression coefficients (β) ranging from 0.01 to 0.07 in log10-transformed analyses (table 3) and from 2 to 35 in untransformed analyses (supplementary table S4). Regarding diseases, after sequential covariate adjustment, COPD, asthma, rhinitis/rhinosinusitis, rheumatoid arthritis and impaired renal function were significantly associated with higher EOS (all pBonferroni<0.05), with β ranging from 0.03 to 0.18 in log10-transformed analyses (table 4) and from 10 to 75 in untransformed analyses (supplementary table S5). Pulmonary nodules and hypertension also showed a positive but weaker effect (both β=0.01, pBonferroni<0.05), whereas impaired liver function was associated with a slight decrease in EOS counts.

In sensitivity analyses, male gender, higher BMI and current smoking were identified as risk factors for EOS >500 cells·μL−1 (supplementary table S6). In terms of age, <20 or >70 years only reached statistical significance at the lower threshold of 170 cells·μL−1. Furthermore, diseases such as COPD, asthma, hypertension, rhinitis/rhinosinusitis, rheumatoid arthritis and impaired renal function were identified significant for EOS >500 cells·μL−1. However, pulmonary nodules only reached statistical significance at the threshold of 300 cells·μL−1 (supplementary table S7).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest multicentre population-based study to characterise blood EOS distributions in a general Chinese health-check population. We show that EOS counts are markedly right-skewed and exhibit substantial demographic heterogeneity, and we provide age- and sex-specific percentile curves that define robust population-based reference ranges, revealing a U-shaped pattern across age. Clinically, higher EOS counts were consistently associated with key respiratory conditions, particularly asthma and COPD, and were also linked to selected systemic comorbidities, supporting a more context-aware interpretation of eosinophilia in practice.

Blood EOS count is increasingly recognised as a biomarker of type 2 inflammation across multiple organ systems [27]. The expanding use of biologic therapies targeting type 2 inflammatory pathways further underscores the need for reliable population-based reference ranges for EOS counts [28]. Consistent with our findings, several studies have reported a right-skewed distribution of EOS counts. For instance, the Copenhagen General Population Study of 81 668 individuals aged 20–100 years reported a median EOS count of 170 cells·μL−1 [29]. An Austrian population-based study of 11 042 individuals aged 6–82 years reported a median EOS count of 130 cells·μL−1, with 75th and 95th percentiles of 210 and 430 cells·μL−1, respectively [26]. In contrast, a higher median of 200 cells·μL−1 was observed in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cohorts [30], suggesting potential ethnic and geographic heterogeneity. To date, only one Chinese study of 3310 participants has reported a median EOS count of 110 cells·μL−1, with 75th and 95th percentiles of 192.9 and 449 cells·μL−1, respectively [13]. Although broadly consistent with our results, that study was limited by a smaller sample size and lacked detailed age- and sex-specific distribution.

In this study, males exhibited higher EOS counts than females, consistent with several previous reports [31]. This sex difference may be related to oestrogen status. Animal models indicate that oestrogen signalling suppresses EOS proliferation and survival, thereby reducing EOS counts in both tumour tissues and peripheral blood in mice [32]. In addition, age was also significantly associated with EOS, with higher counts observed at younger and older ages, which has been reported in the Austrian general population study [26]; however, that study categorised age rather than modelling it as a continuous variable. By contrast, our approach allowed a more precise characterisation of the lifelong trajectory of EOS counts and enabled the estimation of age- and sex-specific percentile values.

Moreover, smoking was significantly associated with higher EOS counts. This association may be mediated through cigarette smoke-induced activation of IL-33-related inflammatory pathways [33], which in turn regulate EOS activation [34]. An analysis of 12 059 individuals in the USA found that excessive alcohol consumption was associated with higher EOS counts in unadjusted analyses [35], which aligns with our observational results. However, after adjustment for demographic factors, it was associated with lower EOS counts. Regarding BMI, the community-based Nagahama cohort study in Japan reported a non-linear positive association between BMI and EOS counts [36], possibly mediated by type 2 innate lymphoid cell-related mechanisms [37].

Furthermore, consistent with previous research, we observed significantly higher EOS counts in individuals with asthma, COPD and rhinitis compared with the general population [12]. Pulmonary nodules and hypertension also showed positive but weaker associations, warranting further investigation [38]. Notably, this study is the first to identify impaired renal function as an independent factor associated with elevated EOS counts. This finding is supported by evidence from a US veteran cohort showing mildly increased EOS counts in patients with chronic kidney disease [39], as well as recent reports demonstrating an inverse association between EOS counts and eGFR in adult chronic kidney disease [40]. Further studies are needed to clarify the causal relationship between EOS counts and renal dysfunction.

Therefore, our findings demonstrate that the distribution of EOS counts in the Chinese general population is substantially lower than the internationally applied threshold of 500 cells·μL−1. These results highlight the need for updated, demographic-specific reference ranges for Chinese populations. Moreover, given the pivotal role of EOS counts in guiding stratified treatment strategies for diseases such as COPD and asthma, future guidelines should consider demographic-specific distribution data when defining clinical EOS thresholds.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, self-reported diagnoses may be prone to information bias, but they were complemented by linked inpatient and outpatient hospital records. Second, while the WHALE study did not follow a conventional multicentre design, it included one headquarters and many subcentres, providing a geographically and demographically diverse representation of the Chinese population. Furthermore, this study focused on cross-sectional analyses and did not fully utilise repeated EOS measurements or other variables [14]. Future studies will leverage the extensive longitudinal follow-up data of the WHALE study to investigate temporal trajectories and causal relationships.

Conclusions

Based on the largest prospective check-up study in China, we characterised the overall and demographic-specific distributions of blood EOS counts in the general Chinese population and identified independent factors associated with elevated EOS. These findings provide population-specific, demographic-stratified reference values for Asian populations and offer a robust basis for defining eosinophilia in clinical practice.
