Coupling of magnetic and lattice collective excitations in the 2D van der Waals antiferromagnet FePS 3
Kartik Panda, Itzik Kapon, Dumitru Dumcenco, Dirk van der Marel, Alexey Kuzmenko, Nimrod Bachar

Abstract
We report a comprehensive magneto-optical study of FePS 3 single crystals using temperature-dependent polarized transmission spectroscopy and Faraday rotation measurements. Our data reveal strong in-plane anisotropy of both phonon and magnon excitations. These anisotropies emerge distinctly below the Néel temperature ( T N ≈ 118 ​ K T_{N}\approx 118\penalty\ K ). A prominent magnon mode at 122 c ​ m − 1 cm^{-1} exhibits characteristic redshifting with temperature and field-induced splitting, confirming its magnetic origin. The optical conductivity, extracted via Drude–Lorentz modeling, displays polarization-sensitive spectral changes across the magnetic transition. The observation of Raman-active modes in the infrared spectra and the detection of multiple phonons exhibiting Faraday rotation provide compelling evidence of spin–phonon coupling and inversion symmetry breaking in the magnetic state. These findings underscore the intricate interplay between magnetic and lattice degrees of freedom in FePS 3 and establish its relevance for exploring spin–lattice interactions in two-dimensional antiferromagnets.



I
Introduction

In recent years, atomically thin crystals, from graphene to transition-metal dichalcogenides, have transformed condensed-matter research and inspired a wide range of device concepts
Geim 2009
;
Chhowalla et al. 2013
. Beyond their celebrated electronic properties, an expanding family of two-dimensional (2D) compounds displays intrinsic magnetism, unlocking fresh opportunities for spintronic and information-processing technologies
Ahn 2020
;
Němec et al. 2018
. Consequently, major efforts have focused on reliable exfoliation, scalable synthesis, and device integration of 2D magnets. Despite these advances, fundamental questions remain about how magnetic order couples with lattice dynamics in 2D antiferromagnets, and whether unique optical signatures of spin–lattice interactions can be harnessed for probing or controlling magnetic phases.

Two-dimensional magnets are also an unparalleled platform for testing the fundamental physics of spin models. They permit direct access to Ising
Onsager 1944
, XY
Berezinskii 1971
;
Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973
, Kitaev, and Heisenberg-type physics within a single atomic layer. This allows one to observe, manipulate, and exploit fluctuations of the magnetic order, and to probe how external stimuli modify the ground state
Burch et al. 2018
;
Gibertini et al. 2019
;
Huang et al. 2017
;
Gong et al. 2017
. Robust (anti)ferromagnetic order at the 2D limit can emerge in the presence of magnetic anisotropy, interlayer coupling, or engineered perturbations. In practice, the resulting order and its excitations are exquisitely sensitive to the spin dimensionality, crystal-field anisotropy, and external tuning parameters such as strain, doping, and defects.

Magnetic van der Waals materials, including metal phosphorus trichalcogenides (MPX
3
) [M=Fe, Co, Ni, Mn; X=S, Se], represent a broad class of compounds with diverse spin dimensionality models, exhibiting both ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic states. For example, FePS
3
(
T_{\mathrm{N}}\approx 120
K), MnPS
3
(
T_{\mathrm{N}}\approx 78
K), and NiPS
3
(
T_{\mathrm{N}}\approx 155
K), all with monoclinic crystal structures (space group C2/m), display distinct magnetic behaviors
Brec 1986
;
Joy and Vasudevan 1992
. FePS
3
adheres to an Ising model with a zigzag antiferromagnetic configuration, featuring in-plane ferromagnetic chains that are antiparallel along one of the in-plane crystal axes, while NiPS
3
and MnPS
3
are best described by a XY and Heisenberg model, respectively
Brec 1986
;
Joy and Vasudevan 1992
. MnPS
3
shows a Néel-type antiferromagnetic order, where neighboring atoms have antiparallel spins, in contrast to the zig-zag antiferromagnetic structure in FePS
3
. Notably, FePS
3
maintains its magnetic order at the monolayer limit
Lee et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2016
and undergoes an insulator-to-metal transition under quasi-hydrostatic pressure
Mallick et al. 2024
. Inelastic neutron scattering measurements of FePS
3
have identified two magnon excitations with finite gaps of 15 meV and 40 meV at
q=0

Lançon et al. 2016
. Moreover, the comparable magnitudes of spin-orbit coupling and crystal field energies in FePS
3
favor the presence of low-energy magnetic excitations
Wei et al. 2024
. Yet, a comprehensive understanding of how these low-energy magnetic excitations interact with phonons, what their circular conductivity spectral weight is, and how they affect crystal symmetries in FePS
3
is still lacking.

The magnetic properties of these vdW materials are strongly coupled to the charge and lattice degrees of freedom and can be studied effectively using far-infrared spectroscopy. The additional advantage of optical spectroscopy is that it is also susceptible to the monolayer limit. Therefore, the magnetic state can be easily studied and detected using optical probes, such as Kerr/Faraday angle rotation, compared to other magnetometry techniques
Mak et al. 2019
;
Shree et al. 2020
. The advantage of infrared spectroscopy is the direct access to the spectral weight of the different collective modes in the magnetic phase and, as a result, to the investigation of their coupling and relations to the underlying physics of the magnetic state.

In this article, we present a comprehensive infrared spectroscopy investigation of the 2D antiferromagnetic semiconductor FePS
3
. Using polarized infrared transmission spectroscopy and Faraday rotation measurements, we aim to elucidate the temperature and magnetic field dependence of both phonon and magnon excitations. Our primary objectives are to probe anisotropic optical responses, detect potential inversion symmetry breaking, and clarify the interplay between spin and lattice degrees of freedom in FePS
3
. To address these objectives, we synthesized high-quality FePS
3
crystals and performed detailed magneto-optical measurements as described below.

II
Methods

II.1
Sample preparation

FePS
3
crystals were grown by the Chemical Vapor Transport (CVT) method, like other magnetic van der Waals materials in the MPX3 group
Dumcenco and Giannini 2020
. A stoichiometric amount in the ratio 1:1:3 of elements Fe (99.9%, Chempure), P (99.99%, Alfa Aesar), and S (99.999%, Fluka), corresponding to approximately 0.5 gram total mass, were inserted in a quartz tube inside a glove box, together with approximately 2 mg/cm
3
of iodine as transport agent. The tube was subsequently evacuated down to 10
-4
mbar, with intermediate Ar flushing, and sealed to a length of 16 cm. The sealed tube was inserted in a horizontal furnace, in a controlled temperature gradient with 680
∘
C and 630
∘
C for the hot and cold ends, respectively. At the end of 20 days the furnace was switched off and the crystals were extracted from the tube.

II.2
Optical spectroscopy

To measure the magneto-optical coefficients, we have used a Bruker Vertex 70v Fourier Transform Infra-Red (FTIR) spectrometer coupled to a Cryogenic magneto-optical cryostat, allowing transmission and reflection measurements as a function of temperature, magnetic field, and incoming/outgoing polarization. We have used Hg arc lamp and Globar (SiC) sources for the far-infrared (FIR) and mid-infrared (MIR) frequency ranges, respectively. Residual magnetic fields affect the arc lamp, limiting reliable measurements below 40
cm^{-1}
. Therefore, we primarily present results obtained with the Globar source. To detect the transmitted signal intensity in our measurements, we have used several liquid-helium-cooled Si bolometers from Infra-Red Labs, covering the range from about 10 cm
-1
to about 4000 cm
-1
. The magnetic field of the cryostat can be tuned from -7 T to +7 T and was aligned parallel (perpendicular) to the light propagation vector (sample surface normal) in the Faraday configuration. The temperature range of the cryostat sample holder is from 4 K to about 300 K, while the sample is mounted with silver paste to the sample holder and situated in the vacuum chamber as the superconducting magnet during the measurement. The cryostat is positioned on an external linear translation stage, allowing the measurement of a sample in transmission (reflection) configuration and a bare hole (mirror) reference at any desired temperature, magnetic field, and incoming/outgoing polarization.

For the polarization-dependent measurements, we have installed gold or KRS polarizers for the FIR or MIR frequency ranges, respectively, along the incoming and outgoing light beam paths. The incoming polarizer was used for the temperature and magnetic field-dependent measurement for each principal polarization axis without an outgoing analyzer. In the Faraday rotation angle measurements, we fix the analyzer and rotate the incoming polarizer. Since FePS
3
has a monoclinic crystal structure and shows an anisotropic optical response in the antiferromagnetic state, the typical fast protocol procedure
Levallois et al. 2015
, taking into account only four measurements, i.e. combination of external magnetic field
\pm B
and
A=P=\pm 45^{\circ}
, where
P
and
A
are the incoming polarizer and outgoing analyzer angles, respectively, is subject to issues of numerical error. Therefore, we have applied the full protocol to measure the Faraday rotation angle for each of the two principal polarizations used in this work. In this protocol, the analyzer angle is fixed to one of the principal angles while measurement of the transmission intensity is being taken at intervals of
15^{\circ}
for a full
360^{\circ}
polarizer rotation. We have conducted the full protocol at a temperature of 5 K, i.e., deep in the antiferromagnetic state, and at fields of
B=\pm 7\penalty\ T
. In addition, we have applied the fast protocol to investigate the temperature and magnetic field evolution of the Faraday angle rotation in FePS
3
.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Crystal Symmetries in FePS
3

Several vibrational modes of FePS
3
exhibit a clear Faraday angle rotation, providing strong evidence for spin–phonon coupling. This observation is consistent with earlier reports
Ghosh et al. 2021
. Interestingly, the Raman-active phonon mode at 108
cm^{-1}
appears as a weak absorption feature in the infrared magneto-transmission data, where it develops a measurable Faraday rotation of approximately 25 mrad. The same mode is also showing an avoided crossing behavior with the 122
cm^{-1}
magnon at high magnetic fields
Ghosh et al. 2021
.

The appearance of the Raman modes in the IR data suggests a breaking of crystal symmetry. Application of magnetic field naturally breaks time-reversal symmetry. It hence can lead to the mixing of odd and even collective modes, such as those seen in our magneto-transmission measurements but also at the high field magneto transmission data of Ref.
Wyzula et al. 2022
, particularly the avoided crossing of the magnon low energy excitation and the Raman mode in the IR data. However, there is no conventional interpretation why this specific Raman mode will also develop a Faraday angle rotation.

Two additional modes show a different behavior, which also cannot be attributed to magnetic field-induced time-reversal symmetry (TRS) breaking. The first mode at 133
cm^{-1}
is an infrared active mode but develops a Faraday angle rotation of about 25 mRad. Therefore, this mode already shows a clear spin-phonon coupling through the Faraday angle rotation at low magnetic fields. The second mode at 166
cm^{-1}
is also intriguing, as it is a Raman-active mode that appears in the infrared transmission data even at zero magnetic field, but only below the antiferromagnetic transition temperature of FePS
3

McCreary et al. 2020
. This mode also develops a Faraday rotation angle of approximately 50 mrad at 7 T, as shown in Fig.
6
.

Figure
8
shows a color map of the polarized mid-infrared transmission data in our FePS
3
single crystals and another intriguing spectral feature which is centered around 900
cm^{-1}
. A wide absorption, and also a wide peak in the optical conductivity, can be seen mainly in the Pol
2
data and below the AFM phase transition. Similarly, a peak in the Raman spectroscopy data of our samples is observed below the AFM phase transition and around a Raman shift of about 900
cm^{-1}
.

Therefore, the appearance of the 122
cm^{-1}
magnon, the 166
cm^{-1}
Raman-active phonon, and the mid-infrared excitation at 900
cm^{-1}

. This claim is in line with recent experimental indications
Geraffy et al. 2022
and in contrast to second harmonic generation measurements
Chu et al. 2020
, which argue that FePS
3
preserves inversion symmetry in the magnetic state.

IV.2
Dichroism of the Optical Circular Conductivity

Since we can quantitatively obtain the absolute transmission, the complex conductivity, and the Faraday angle of FePS
3
in our measurements, it is, therefore, possible to extract from our data the circular optical conductivity of the split magnon mode. The two field-separated collective modes can be associated with the right- and left-handed circular polarizations of the two opposite spin sublattices in the antiferromagnetic state
Lan et al. 2017
. At zero field, there is no difference between one spin population and the other, and the magnon has a single eigen energy. Hence, the two circular polarizations combine into one linearly polarized mode at 122
cm^{-1}
. It is important to note here that our measurements show no difference between the magnon mode at zero field and the two principal polarizations used in this work. We attribute it to the fact that FePS
3
is considered to have an Ising-like (1D) spin Hamiltonian and that the spins are directed along the
c
-axis. Hence, there is no considerable effect on different polarizations along the crystal’s
ab
plane. However, with the application of a magnetic field, the modes split into low and high energy modes with a corresponding circular optical conductivity
\sigma_{+}
and
\sigma_{-}
.

We follow the procedure described in Ref.
(
Levallois et al. 2015
)
however, due to the anisotropy in the magnetic state, the in-plane optical conductivity tensor should be described as follows:

\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx}&\sigma_{xy}\\
-\sigma_{xy}&\sigma_{yy}\end{pmatrix}

(2)

where the in-plane anisotropy of the FePS
3
optical conductivity is given by different dynamic conductivity functions
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{yy}
. The latter terms capture the ordinary absorption and the birefringence of the sample, while the diagonal term,
\sigma_{xy}
, is associated with the magneto-optical effects and the optical Hall conductivity. Plugging in the eigenvectors of the Right- and Left-handed circular polarized light
e_{\pm}=1/\sqrt{2}\left(1,\pm i\right)
results in the circular optical conductivity:

\sigma_{\pm}(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{xx}(\omega)+\sigma_{yy}(\omega)}{2}\pm i\sigma_{xy}(\omega)

(3)

for the Right-handed (
\sigma_{+}
) and Left-handed (
\sigma_{-}
) optical circular conductivity. It is important to note here that we work under the assumption that the monoclinic tilt is non-negligible and therefore take the orthorhombic approximation because the optic axes in our measurement stay nearly in-plane. We also note that
\sigma_{xy}
should be independent of the actual axis in which we have resolved the Faraday angle rotation. Moreover, while linear birefringence mixes the two circular eigenmodes, so they are not perfectly circular, it does not affect the Faraday angle rotation since it is not related to the off-diagonal terms and cancels out in the cross-polarization measurement. However, the values of
\sigma_{xy}
can be different for the measurement and analysis of the Faraday angle rotation in Pol
1
vs Pol
2
due to systematic errors, e.g. residual cross polarization signal, and/or crystal misalignment. Our results show that throughout most of the spectral range, including the lower energy magnon mode and phonons, there is a perfect agreement between the two sets of data with respect to
\sigma_{xy}
obtained from the two in-plane polarizations. Some discrepancy between the values can be detected around the upper energy magnon mode (see App.
VII
), which shows that it is local in frequency and probably not related to systematic errors or any sample tilt. Therefore, we have averaged the two
\sigma_{xy}
outputs in order to obtain the optical circular conductivity
\sigma_{\pm}
.

V
Conclusions

We have conducted a comprehensive infrared spectroscopic investigation of FePS
3
. By combining absolute transmission, optical conductivity, and Faraday rotation measurements, we explored its collective excitations. Our measurements reveal that the lattice vibrational modes exhibit marked anisotropy and field sensitivity in the antiferromagnetic state, providing direct evidence of strong spin–lattice coupling. A key feature is the 122
cm^{-1}
magnon mode, which appears in both infrared and Raman spectra. It exhibits isotropic temperature evolution and undergoes magnetic-field-induced splitting, consistent with antiferromagnetic order.

Significantly, several phonon modes display pronounced Faraday rotation, and certain Raman-active modes appear in the infrared spectra at zero field—features suggesting inversion symmetry breaking, likely driven by spin–phonon interactions. These observations challenge earlier reports suggesting that inversion symmetry is preserved in FePS
3
, highlighting the need for further studies to reconcile these findings. Moreover, our analysis of the circular optical conductivity reveals dichroism associated with magnon splitting. Anomalous behavior near certain phonon energies points to avoided crossings and magneto-elastic hybridization.

Our results reveal a rich magneto-optical landscape in FePS
3
and demonstrate the critical role of spin–lattice interactions in shaping its collective excitation spectrum. Future experiments combining ultrafast optical probes or monolayer measurements could provide deeper insight into the dynamics of spin–lattice coupling and its potential for controlling magnetic phases in two-dimensional materials, and for their potential applications in spintronic and magneto-optical technologies.
