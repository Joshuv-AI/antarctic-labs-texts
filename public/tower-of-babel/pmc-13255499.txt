Sustainable strategies for enhancing maize yields, nutrition, and economic security through legume intercropping in semi-arid regions
Authors: Manjanagouda S Sannagoudar, K. N. Kalyana Murthy, Dinesh Jinger, Vijay Singh Meena, G. A. Rajanna, Hanamant M Halli, Gaurendra Gupta, M. N. Harish, C. S. Shantharaja, C. Gireesh, K. Bhojaraja Naik, K. V. Sripathy, A. Anandan, Mahantesh B. Nagangoudar, Lalu Prasad Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Over the years, maize productivity in the semi-arid regions is increasingly constrained by climate variability, soil nutrient depletion and rising input costs, questioning the sustained productivity posing risks to food and livelihood security. Numerous earlier studies involving plant geometry options (PGOs), legume intercropping (ICs) and nutrient management options (NMOs) in maize showed enhanced yield and economic benefits. However, region-specific strategies integrating productivity, nutritional grains and economic resilience remain underexplored. To address this, a field experiment was carried for two consecutive years employing two plant geometry options (normal planting vs. paired row planting), four intercrops (French bean, cowpea, field bean, and pole bean) and two nutrient management options [(N1: recommended dose of fertilizer to base crop (RBC) and N2: RBC + intercrop (RBI)]. Results showed that, paired row planting of maize enhanced seed yield, stover yield, maize equivalent yield, and net returns by 8.14%, 8.50%, 8.04%, and 11.7%, respectively over normal planting. Between the NMOs, recommended dose of fertilizers supplied to both base and intercrops (RBC + RBI) significantly improved the seed yield, stover yield, maize equivalent yield, and net returns by12.5%, 11.3%, 12.5%, and 14.4%, respectively compared to supplying recommended dose of nutrients to maize only (RBC). Further, soil beneficial microbial activity, nutrient status after crop harvest, land utilization indices and economic returns were also higher under intercropping supplied with the recommended dose of nutrients to both maize and intercrops. Specifically, maize intercropped with French bean in a paired row planting method supplied with recommended dose of nutrients to both the crops is recommended for achieving higher yield and economic returns besides yield sustainability in the semi-arid regions.

Introduction

Soil fertility reduction is a major constraint to yield enhancement in maize mono-cropping systems, making them unsustainable [1] over the years. Maize’s high nutrient demand lead farmers to overuse inorganic fertilizers, harming soil productivity and the agro-ecosystem [2, 3]. Maize dominating the farming community over years, yielding them larger economic benefits rendering them to follow monocropping. Further, this monocropping has been adopted majorly for the ease in crop management, specifically mechanizing the farm activities from sowing to harvest. However, studies proved that continuous monocropping encourages pest and disease incidences causing drastic reduction in yield levels. Further, monocropping practice leads to rapid depletion of the essential nutrients from the same crop root zone, making it inevitable for supply of external inorganic nutrient sources. Thus, the aggravated effect of monocropping specifically in a high nutrient demanding crops like maize, yielding poor grains quantity despite supplying other inputs optimally over the years [4, 5]. The solution to these issues was, growing a group of diverse crops in combination viz., row intercropping, mixed cropping and relay cropping rather than sole cropping [6, 7]. Specifically, maize is blessed with tremendous potential for offering intercropping opportunities, particularly with pulse crops. Demanding wider row spacing, maize owes ample of space between the rows for introducing small statured pulses of short duration paving way for successful intercropping. This is further aided by the wider crop elasticity of maize to yield optimally even under higher inter-specific competition. Numerous studies have shown that, legume-based cereal production systems found more sustainable in terms of productivity, profitability and sustainability. The various benefits of intercropping depend on the quality of the crops and their adaptability to local conditions [8–13]. Studies showed that, including economically high yielding pulse crops would further aid in attaining self-sufficiency and economic stability to the farming community specifically in the semi-arid regions [14–20]. Though, the studies pertaining to intercropping of maize with short durated pulse crops has been undertaken, meagre data is available on the comparative study on maize-based intercropping of various pulses with differential yielding capacity, improving soil productivity, increasing economic returns, and improving overall system sustainability specific to semi-arid region.

Agronomic approaches can be employed to manipulate the micro-environment, especially within the soil, in order to maximize the actual yield potential of any crop [21, 22]. For any successful harvest, planting geometry play a vital role as it ensures an optimum plant density that is capable of yielding maximum economic output under minimal competition from the surrounding plants. Since ages, sole maize is cultivated adopting ridge and furrow method of planting varying in row width for its ease in agronomic crop management. Later, intercrops were introduced between the crop rows by reducing the maize population at varied row proportions. However, paired row planting method emerged as an alternate to normal planting wherein, intercrops are easy to introduce additionally without reducing the base crop population [23–25]. In addition to helping to make better use of natural resources, paired row planting of maize often accommodates intercrops (vegetables and pulses) between the paired rows without any major negative impact on main crop growth and yield, providing the intercrop yield as a bonus [20, 26]. Further, maize being a nutri-positive crop, demands nutrients in large quantities specifically, nitrogen. Severe soil nutrient depletion has been recorded across the world where monocropping of maize is prevalent [27, 28]. Under such monoculture, effective nutrient management has become very essential in order to achieve higher yield and yield sustainability over the years [29, 30]. Similarly, the major yield gap in maize cropping system stems from inadequate and imbalanced fertilization and unsuitable fertilizer recommendations [31–34]. Sustainable crop production requires improving soil resources through balanced fertilization. Controlled fertilization maximizes crop yield potential by ensuring optimal nutrient supply [35–39]. Recent studies revealed that, effective cropping geometry and fertilization protocols tailored to specific needs are crucial for achieving higher crop yields [33, 40, 41]. Therefore, standardization of cropping geometry and fertilization in maize-based intercropping systems is imperative to improve soil quality and achieve sustainability. Several studies have shown that, increased supply of nutrient will yield significantly higher maize grains, yet parallelly incur higher production cost besides harming soil quality, questioning the yield stability in the long run. Introducing pulse crops additionally between the maize rows would reduce the dependency on external fertilizers (through atmospheric N-fixation) besides conserving soil and water resources, ultimately improving the soil quality and yield sustainability.

However, large knowledge gaps exist under region specific maize based intercropping systems under semi-arid agro-ecological systems. For a successful adoption of maize-based intercropping system, integrated knowledge on planting geometry of the base crop and nutrient management strategies is the key requirement. Because, under intercropping system higher inter- and intra-specific competition is observed for production resources. Though several researchers has already addressed these issues, they aimed mainly either of the agronomic practices, rather than focusing on a comprehensive package of practice suitable for maize-based intercropping system specifically in the semi-arid regions of Southern Plateau and Hills region. Additionally, scientific evidence regarding the impact of maize-based legume intercropping on the system nutrient dynamics, soil microbial activity, yield sustainability and economic profitability is very meagre. Keeping these research gaps in mind, the present study was formulated to assess the impact of different plant geometry options (PGO), intercrops (ICs) and nutrient management options (NMOs) on system yield, nutrient dynamics, soil quality, sustainability, and economic viability of the maize-based pulse intercropping systems. Further, the outcomes of the present study aid in increasing maize productivity and improved nutritional quality, enhanced soil fertility and resources-use efficiency, increased farmer income and livelihood security, greater resilience in semi-arid farming systems and reduced dependency on chemical inputs, and improved environmental sustainability through soil health, biodiversity conservation and lower ecological footprint specifically in the Southern Plateau and Hills Region of India.

Material and methods

Experimental site and treatment details

The experiment was carried out at the Zonal Agricultural Research Station, University of Agricultural Sciences, Bengaluru, Karnataka, India [12° 58 ‘N latitude, 77° 35’ E longitude, and 930 MSL altitude] during kharif seasons of 2016 and 2017. The data pertaining to monthly weather conditions prevailed during the experimentation period recorded at the experimental site is depicted in Fig. 1. The average normal rainfall of the locality was 915.0 mm, while during first year (2016) of experimentation it was lesser (695.10 mm) than the normal rainfall and during second year of experimentation (2017) it was higher (1110.9 mm) than the normal rainfall. The mean maximum and minimum temperature recorded were 29.58 °C and 29.4 °C, and 18.26 °C and 18.30 °C during 2016 and 2017 cropping seasons, respectively. The mean relative humidity recorded between 87.04 and 88.10% during both the years. The experimental site soil was reddish brown laterite resulting from genesis under a semi-arid sub-tropical climate. To examine the physico-chemical properties of the soil, the initial soil sampling was collected from 0 to 30 cm depth. Two-year field experiment was conducted using a Randomized Block Design with Factorial concept, comprising three replications. The study consisted of sixteen treatment combinations, which included two planting geometries; P1: Normal planting at 60 × 30 cm and P2: Paired row planting at 30/90 × 30 cm; Four different intercrops; I1: French bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), I2: Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata), I3: Field bean (Dolichos lablab), and I4: Pole bean (Phaseolus vulgaris); along with two nutrient management options; N1: Recommended Dose of Fertilizers (RDF) for the base crop alone, and N2: RDF for the base crop + proportionate RDF for intercrops. The experimental details pertaining to the scientific names of the crop, varieties used, spacing and nutrient recommendations supplied to each crop are given in Fig. 2. The land was prepared by ploughing twice and leveling to a consistent tilth. The experimental area was laid out according to the layout plan, with distinct plots delineated. Furrows were opened 60 cm apart in standard planting geometry, and two seeds were sown per hill at a 30 cm spacing within the row, depending on the treatment. For paired row planting furrows were opened at 30/90 × 30 cm spacing, with intercrops were sown between paired maize rows. Fig. 3a and b depicts the plant geometry of the cropping system, while Figs. 4 and 5 represents the general view of experimental plots at 45 DAS. The recommended intra-row spacing was based on conventional cultivation techniques for each intercrop when produced as sole crop. Fertilizer application followed treatment specifications. Maize received 150:75:40 kg N, P₂O₅, and K₂O per hectare. Intercrops received recommended doses of 63:100:75 kg N, P₂O₅, K₂O ha⁻¹ for French and Pole beans, 10:30:24 kg N, P₂O₅, K₂O ha⁻¹ for Cowpea, and 10:20:10 kg N, P₂O₅, and K₂O ha⁻¹ for Field bean (*Note- The package of practice recommended by University of Agricultural Sciences, Bangalore for each crop was adopted for the experimentation in the present study). Growth and yield attributes were measured and recorded 30, 60, and 90 days after seeding, as well as at harvest. The acquired data were statistically analyzed using standard methodologies. Separate RCBD trials were also undertaken for intercrops and base crops to calculate the Land Equivalent Ratio (LER) and Area Time Equivalent Ratio (ATER) for comparison purposes. The treatment details and agronomic practices followed including the varieties of main and intercrop used during the experimentation have been mentioned in Fig. 1. Schematic diagram of normal planting and paired row planting have been illustrated in Fig. 2a-b. The treatment photos of the field experiments have also been presented in Figs. 3 and 4. The details of the harvesting dates of the main and intercrops has been provided in the Supplementary Table 1.

Microbial biomass carbon (MBC), urease and dehydrogenase activity

Soil samples were collected from top 15 cm of the rhizosphere immediately after the harvest of the crop. For soil microbial analysis, samples were stored in the refrigerators and for each microbial analysis required quantity of soil was taken treatment wise and subjected for the analysis following standard procedures. Soil microbial biomass carbon was assessed using the fumigation-extraction method. Moist soil sub-samples were fumigated with CHCl₃ and subsequently extracted with K₂SO₄. The MBC content was determined by calculating the difference in carbon levels between fumigated and non-fumigated samples. Urease activity was assessed using a colorimetric technique outlined by Kandeler et al., 1996 [45]. DHA was evaluated using a colorimetric procedure given by Ohlinger et al., 1996 [46].

Plant nutrient

Plant samples collected at harvest were dried for 24 h in a hot-air oven maintained at 70 °C. The oven-dried material was then ground in a Macro-Wiley mill and passed through a 40-mesh sieve. For nutrient estimation, 0.5 g of the processed plant material from each treatment was used. Nitrogen content was analyzed using the colorimetric method with Nessler’s reagent [47], while phosphorus was measured following the vanadomolybdophosphoric yellow-color method [47]. Potassium content was determined directly with a flame photometer. Nutrient uptake was computed by multiplying the nutrient concentration by the corresponding plant yield (Eq. 5).

Economics

Gross returns (GR) were estimated using the current market prices of major inputs. Net returns (NR) were obtained by deducting the total cost of cultivation (TCC) from the gross returns (NR = GR − TCC). The benefit–cost (BC) ratio was computed as the ratio of gross returns to the total cost of cultivation (BC ratio = GR/TCC).

Data analysis

Treatment wise data was calculated from the mean of replications presented in the tables or figures as results. The data was analyzed statistically by following the procedures of Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and the significance of different sources of variation was tested by the error mean square of the ‘F’ test (Fisher’s Test) at a probability level of 0.05 as per the techniques suggested by Gomez and Gomez (1984) [48]. The mean values of yield underwent individual analysis via Duncan’s Multiple Range Test to identify specific differences among treatment means. Statistical analyses, including ANOVA, were conducted using SAS® software (version 9.3).

Results

Yield and yield attributes of maize

Adoption of paired row method of planting (30/90 × 30 cm) significantly enhanced grain yield (7.15 t ha− 1), and stover yield (8.94 t ha− 1) of maize as compared to the normal method of planting (Table 1). The increase in maize seed yield and stover yield was to the tune of 8.50% and 8.04% respectively, over normal method of planting. The superiority of paired row method of planting was depicted in terms of better yield attributing parameters such as, significantly higher cob length (18.0 cm), cob girth (7.19 cm), seed weight cob− 1 (131.3 g), number of seeds row− 1 (30), number of seed rows cob− 1 (16.9), number of seeds cob− 1 (511) and 100 seed weight (27.4 g) over normal planting (16.6 cm, 6.65 cm, 121.4 g, 27.7, 15.6, 440 and 25.3 g, respectively) (Tables 2 and 3). Likewise, paired row planting yielded 8.1–8.3% higher values of yield attributes of maize except number of seeds cob−1where it was 16.1% higher compared to normal planting. Among the different intercropping systems, maize + french bean intercropping has recorded significantly higher grain yield (7.16 t ha− 1) and stover yield (8.81 t ha− 1) compared to other ICs.

Among nutrient management strategies, significantly higher grain and stover yield (7.29 and 9.05 t ha− 1, respectively) was recorded wherein nutrients was supplied to both maize and intercrops (RBI) as compared to supplying nutrients to maize alone (RBC-6.48 and 8.13 t ha− 1, respectively) (Table 1). Thus, adoption of RBI enhanced the seed and stover yield by 12.5% and 11.3%, respectively over RBC. This higher yield was a cumulative result of superior yield attributing parameters viz., cob length (18.3 cm), cob girth (7.33 cm), kernel weight cob− 1 (133.8 g), number of kernels row− 1 (30.6), number of kernel rows cob− 1 (17.2), number of kernels cob− 1 (529) and 100 kernel weight (27.9 g) as compared to RBI (Tables 3 and 4). Among the interactions, significantly higher seed yield (7.71 t ha− 1) and stover yield (9.45 t ha− 1) were recorded under the combination of paired row planting of maize intercropped with french bean supplied with nutrients to both maize and intercrop over other combinations. Moreover, lowest seed yield (5.92 t ha− 1) and stover yield (7.50 t ha− 1) was recorded under normal planting of maize intercropped with cowpea and nutrients supplied to only maize crop. The paired row planting of maize intercropped with french bean and supplying proportionate nutrients to both the crops increased the seed and stover yield by 30.2% and 26% compared to normal planting of maize intercropped with cowpea and wherein nutrients supplied only to maize.

Maize equivalent yield (MEY)

Data presented in Table 4 revealed that paired row planting of maize exhibited significantly higher MEY (10.7 t ha− 1) than normal planting (9.91 t ha− 1). This system enhanced the MEY by 7.9% over normal planting. Among the different intercropping systems, maize + french bean combination recorded the highest MEY, surpassing maize + cowpea, maize + field bean, and maize + pole bean intercropping systems by 53.3%, 48.7%, and 9.4%, respectively. Between NMOs, significantly higher MEY (10.9 t ha− 1) was noticed in RBI than RBC (9.69t ha− 1) accounting for 12.4% higher yield. The paired row planting of maize intercropped with French bean and supplying nutrients to both the crops significantly increased the MEY by 85.1% compared to normal planting of maize intercropped with cowpea and nutrients supplied to maize crop alone.

LER and ATER

Between planting geometry, paired row planting of maize was found superior in terms of LER and ATER than normal planting (Table 4). There was 7.90% and 7.61% increase in LER and ATER, respectively under paired row planting over normal planting. Among the intercropping systems, LER and ATER were significantly higher in the maize + french bean (1.33 and 1.16) system followed by maize + pole bean (1.25 and 1.11), maize + field bean (1.15 and 1.04) and maize + cowpea (1.14 & 1.03). Moreover, the RBI expressed its supremacy in terms of higher LER (1.29) and ATER (1.15) as compared to RBC (1.15 and 1.02, respectively). The RBI increased the LER and ATER by 12.1% and 12.7% respectively over RBC. Among the interactions, paired row planting of maize intercropped with French bean and nutrients supplied to both the crops found to be more advantageous in resource use efficiency over other agrotechnique combinations.

Nutrient uptake

The nutrient uptake of maize influenced significantly by different nutrient management practices in various intercrops and planting geometry (Table 5). Paired row planting of maize increased the N, P, and K uptake by 9.36%, 6.38%, and 9.41%, respectively compared to normal planting. However, the impact of intercrops on maize nutrient uptake was statistically insignificant. Nevertheless, maize intercropped with French beans showed the highest numerical uptake of N, P, and K. This was followed by intercropping with pole beans, field beans, and cowpea. Moreover, application of nutrients to both the intercrops in proportion significantly improved the nutrient uptake and enhanced the uptake of N, P, and K by 13.8%, 16.6%, and 13.8, respectively over supplying nutrients to maize crop alone.

Profitability

The profitability indicators of maize differed significantly in response to nutrient application techniques under varied intercrops and planting methods (Table 6). Paired row has recorded higher gross profit ($1880 ha− 1), net profit ($ 1346 ha− 1) and B: C ratio (3.51) than normal planting ($1739, $ 1204 ha− 1 and 3.24, respectively). This treatment increased the gross and net profit by 8.10% and 11.7% over normal planting. Maize + French Bean has recorded considerably higher gross profit ($ 2172ha− 1), net profit ($ 1562ha− 1) and B: C ratio (3.56). Thus, this system enhanced the net profit by 49.8%, 44.9%, and 10.0% compared to maize + cowpea, maize + field bean, and maize + pole bean, respectively. Similarly, proportionate supply of nutrients to both maize and intercrops recorded higher gross profit, net profit, and B: C ratio compared to supplying full dose of nutrients to maize alone. There was 12.5% and 14.4% increment in gross and net returns under RBI compared to RBC. The paired row planting of maize intercropped with French bean supplied with proportionate nutrients to both the crops recorded higher gross returns ($ 2320ha− 1), net returns ($ 1675ha− 1) and B: C ratio (3.60) compared to other treatment combinations, while normal planting of maize intercropped with cowpea and supplied with nutrients to maize alone recorded the least economic benefits.

Discussion

Productivity and intercropping indices

The PGOs and ICs are the most important factors deciding the resource utilization efficiency of the crop [49]. The available resources like accumulation of solar radiations, and utilization of applied water and nutrients influence better crop growth and development [22]. In the present study, the better yield attributing characters were ascribed to higher percent light absorption resulted from efficient management practices [50, 51]. In general, crop growth and yields in the intercropping system are stated to be lower than sole cropping, while combined yields may be higher than single crops, leading to higher inter-row competition under legume intercropping [52, 53]. This was witnessed in terms of decreased yield and yield parameters of maize in ICs where nutrients were applied only to maize crop. The improved yield attributing characters with higher fertilizer levels could be due to the luxuriant growth of maize as reflected in terms of higher dry matter production [49]. Despite, the maize yield reduction in intercropping, higher MEY was recorded in the intercropping systems. The higher MEY under paired row systems was attributed to the yield advantage achieved in the intercropping system [54–56]. This may also be correlated with the synergistic effect of maize and French bean in combination, which leads to the successful use of solar energy [57–60]. While, maize yield was reduced due to intercropping, the MEY in the intercropping systems reported higher yields. The higher MEY of 13.58 t ha− 1 was recorded by paired row planted maize + french bean with application of RBI attributed to the positive interaction between the chosen agrotechniques in enhancing system yield [54, 60–62].

The substantial yield increase observed with the paired row planting method can be attributed to the different ways in which the component crops utilize natural resources [52]. This more efficient use of resources by the intercropped species leads to higher yields per unit area compared to those achieved by the crops grown individually [54, 61, 63]. The LER increased with intercropping system [64, 65]. For other intercropping combinations, lower LER and ATER values will possibly be attributable to the decreased performance [66, 67]. Among the NMOs, significantly higher LER (1.29) and ATER (1.15) were obtained when both maize and intercrop received proportionate nutrient supply as compared to nutrient supply to maize alone. The application of the recommended dose of fertilizers to both main and component crops has resulted in higher LER and ATER. Higher LER and higher yield benefits suggest greater biological viability and yield benefits of maize intercropping than maize production alone [68].

Nutrient uptake and available soil nutrient status after harvest

For better crop growth and development, the optimum availability of the major nutrients in supplementation to proper plant geometry is essential. Any hindrance in getting sufficient nutrients would further affect crop growth and yield. The improvement in yield was associated with nutrient uptake by the crop, which reflects both biomass production and the soil’s nutrient availability. According to [69, 70], nutrient uptake is an indicator of biomass production and the soil’s usable nutrient status. This increased uptake can be attributed to enhanced biomass production and greater availability of these nutrients in the soil solution [71]. This enhanced nutrient uptake likely contributed to improved vegetative growth, resulting in increased nutrient concentration in the total biomass of the maize plants.

The comparison with the sole maize system serves as a reference point for evaluating the effectiveness of intercropping and nutrient management practices in enhancing soil fertility [72]. The generally higher levels of available nutrients in the intercropping systems underscore the potential benefits of diversifying cropping systems particularly with leguminous crops [73, 74]. ICs involving maize with different leguminous crops exhibit varying effects on soil nutrient levels [51, 75]. Pulses, besides possessing the capacity to fix atmospheric nitrogen through their root nodules, through their taproot system enhances soil aeration and increases soil water holding capacity. This would in turn improves soil beneficial microbial activity specifically those working as nutrient mineralizers. This could explain the generally higher levels of available nitrogen in ICs compared to sole maize cultivation. Additionally, leguminous crops contribute organic matter to the soil upon decomposition, which enhances soil structure and nutrient retention [76, 77]. This organic matter decomposition process may have contributed to higher levels of available P and K in the intercropping systems compared to the sole maize system. Between the employed nutrient management strategies, supplying proportionate nutrients to both maize and intercrops likely benefitted from additional nutrient inputs, resulting in higher residual levels of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the soil compared to practice of supplying nutrients to only maize crop. Further, this could also be attributed to inter and intraspecific competition for minimal available soil nutrients under application of nutrients to intercropping systems based on only base crop recommendations [76].

MBC and enzymatic activity

Intercropping systems typically involve the cultivation of multiple crop species within the same field. These crops often have different root architectures and exudation patterns, leading to a greater diversity and quantity of root exudates released into the soil [6, 76]. Additionally, the presence of multiple crops in intercropping systems can increase the input of organic matter through plant residues and root turnover, further enriching the soil microbial community [77]. As a result, the higher availability of root exudates and organic matter in intercropping systems provides favorable conditions for microbial proliferation, leading to elevated levels of MBC, DHA, and urease activity. These organic substances released in paramount quantities under intercropping systems serve both as nutritional sources to beneficial soil microbes and also as catalyzing solvents to soil nutrient mineralizing enzymes.

Intercropping systems often create a more diverse and heterogeneous soil microenvironment compared to monoculture systems [76, 78]. Different crop species may exhibit varying rhizosphere effects, altering soil physicochemical properties such as pH, moisture, and nutrient availability [72, 79]. The spatial and temporal heterogeneity introduced by intercropping can promote niche differentiation among soil microorganisms, leading to increased microbial diversity and metabolic activity [80, 81]. Additionally, the presence of leguminous intercrops in maize-based intercropping systems can enhance nitrogen fixation and nutrient cycling, providing additional resources for soil microorganisms and stimulating microbial activity [9, 82].

Nutrient management strategies varying in supply quantities, play a crucial role in shaping soil microbial communities and activities [83]. In intercropping systems where nutrient management is optimized, the provision of balanced nutrients supports the growth and metabolic activities of soil microorganisms [77, 78]. For instance, nitrogen is a key component of microbial biomass and is involved in various enzymatic processes, including those related to nitrogen mineralization. Adequate nitrogen availability in intercropping systems can fuel microbial growth and enhance nitrogen cycling processes, leading to increased urease activity [6, 76, 83]. This can be enhanced by introducing leguminous crops as intercrops possessing the atmospheric nitrogen fixing capacity and build up soil nitrogen reserve. Higher levels of MBC, DHA, and urease activity in intercropping systems compared to sole maize systems can be attributed to a combination of factors, including increased root exudates and organic matter inputs, enhanced soil microenvironment, balanced nutrient availability, positive plant-microbe interactions, and potential effects of intercropping practices [84].

Profitability

Intercropping maize with French beans has resulted in significantly higher gross returns, net returns, and benefit-to-cost (B: C) ratio compared to other intercrop combinations like maize + pole beans, maize + field beans, and maize + cowpeas. This improvement is attributed to both the higher grain and stover yields of maize and the elevated yield and market price of french beans [85]. The increased gross and net returns from this intercropping system contribute to a higher B: C ratio, as the maximum economic yield (MEY) of the component crops in the intercropping system exceeds that of their individual stands due to higher yields and prices [64, 65]. Growing multiple crops simultaneously on the same land area offers complementary benefits and results in greater returns compared to cultivating a single crop on that land [86].

Implications for research and policy

Promoting integrated farming system can significantly enhance maize production and overall agricultural sustainability (Fig. 8). Policies should encourage the adoption of maize-legume intercropping with optimized nutrient management and planting geometries. This approach not only improves soil health but also increases crop yield and resilience. Implementation involves robust research and development to identify the best combinations of practices suited to various regions. Extension services are crucial to educate farmers on these methods, supported by subsidies and incentives to encourage adoption. Regular soil testing and tailored nutrient management recommendations will ensure balanced fertilization, enhancing soil health. Supporting smallholder farmers through access to resources, capacity-building initiatives, market facilitation, and financial inclusion is essential for equitable growth and sustainability in the agricultural sector.

Conclusion

The study concludes that paired-row maize planting (30/90 × 30 cm) intercropped with french bean, along with the recommended fertilizer dose to both base and intercrop, significantly improved crop productivity, profitability, and soil health. This system recorded 8–12% higher economic yield, 11–14% greater net returns, and 33–41% enhanced soil enzymatic activity over sole maize. Beyond agronomic gains, the system offers important socio-economic benefits by improving farmers’ income stability, generating additional employment opportunities, and strengthening nutritional security through legume inclusion. Environmentally, it promotes better soil fertility, enhanced biological activity, efficient resource use, and long-term sustainability, making it a suitable climate-resilient production strategy for semi-arid agro-ecological regions of India. However, the future studies should be aimed at understanding complete nutrient dynamics of the intercropping systems, their sustainability in the changing climatic conditions and should explore economically profitable location specific crop combinations (Fig. 9).
