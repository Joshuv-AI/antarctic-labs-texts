Minimal dissipation with active baths in weakly driven processes
Pierre Nazé, Fabrício Q. Potiguar

Abstract
Although vastly studied, white noise is just an ideal case of heat baths, which limits us from real-world scenarios. Understanding the underlying thermodynamics of active heat baths is therefore a demanding task, mainly in optimal control. This work investigates such a scenario for finite-time weakly driven processes, observing how the relaxation function changes from the passive to the active case. In particular, we show that the relaxation time modifies according to the active heat bath, bringing the system far from the passive heat bath case from the non-equilibrium point of view. Optimal protocols to minimize the average work and its fluctuation are immediately derived from such observation. Conditions on the kernel are determined to guarantee that the active system also obeys the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Brownian motions subject to harmonic traps with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck baths exemplify our results.

Due to its characteristics of describing fluids under self-propelled motion, they are coined as active heat baths

We observe that the relaxation function is modified, increasing its relaxation time associated with the passive heat bath

II
Weakly driven processes

There are numerous ways to model active baths: one can add for instance an ad-hoc stochastic velocity in the Langevin equation to simulate the self-propelled activity of the particle of the active heat bath
Potiguar et al. 2014
Another way is including colored noise at the random force intending to simulate memory effects from a non-Markovian model
Zwanzig 2001
. In this work, this last description will be used to simulate the active heat bath.

Consider a system ruled by the generalized time-dependent overdamped Langevin equation
Zwanzig 2001

\gamma\int_{0}^{t}K(t-t^{\prime})\dot{x}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}+\partial_{x}V(x(t),\lambda(t))=\eta(t),

(1)

where
\gamma
is the damping coefficient,
V
is the potential,
\lambda(t)=\lambda_{0}+g(t)\delta\lambda
the time-dependent external parameter, and
\eta(t)
the active noise, whose properties are

\overline{\eta(t)}=0,\quad\overline{\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})}=\frac{2\gamma}{\beta}K(t-t^{\prime}),

(2)

where
\beta
is the noise intensity of the active heat bath,
K(t)
is the kernel associated, assuming a positive and even function. The persistence time associated with the active heat bath is given by

\tau_{P}=\left.\mathcal{L}_{s}\left[\frac{K(t)}{K(0)}\right]\right|_{s=0},

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is the Laplace transform. Such quantity must be positive, which is guaranteed by the nature of the kernel. The passive heat bath case is given by a kernel proportional to Dirac’s delta,
K(t)=2\delta(t)
, with persistence time
\tau_{P}=0
. Using linear response theory
Kubo et al. 2012
, the relaxation function associated to the system is

\Psi_{0}(t)=\beta\langle\overline{\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x(t),\lambda_{0})}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}+\mathcal{C},

(4)

where
\overline{\cdot}
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
are the averages over the noise and initial canonical distribution, given by

\rho(x_{0})\propto\exp(-\beta V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})).

(5)

Here,
x_{0}=x(0)
and
\lambda_{0}=\lambda(0)
. Remark that the system can achieve steady state if it waits for long times, with a probability distribution that maintains detailed balance under the activity of the bath
Denisov et al. 2009
. However, the initial canonical distribution is not this steady state probability distribution, except for instance for harmonic trap potentials
Łuczka 2005
;
Goswami 2019
, systems under weak activity
Caprini et al. 2021
and active bath subject to inertia
Khali et al. 2024
. In other words, applying linear response theory in this context generally occurs in a non-equilibrium initial configuration
Davis et al. 2024
. Even though, in this work, we focus on harmonic trap potentials. Also,
\mathcal{C}=\langle\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}^{2}
, which refers to the decorrelation of the relaxation function for long times
Nazé and Bonança 2020
. Here,
x(t)
evolves according to the solution of the non-perturbed system. In particular, the relaxation time associated to the relaxation function is
Nazé and Bonança 2020

\tau_{R}=\left.\mathcal{L}_{s}\left[\frac{\Psi_{0}(t)}{\Psi_{0}(0)}\right]\right|_{s=0}.

(6)

In linear response theory, the thermodynamic average work produced by a weakly driven process with a switching time
\tau
is given by
Nazé and Bonança 2020

\langle W\rangle=\Delta F^{(2)}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\Psi_{0}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\dot{\lambda}(t^{\prime})\dot{\lambda}(t^{\prime\prime})dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime},

(7)

where
\Delta F^{(2)}
is the difference in Hemlholtz’s free energy expanded up to second order. Because of such a functional, the relaxation function must have its relaxation time and Fourier transform both positive to satisfy the Second Law of Thermodynamics
Nazé and Bonança 2020
. Intense work has been done recently to find optimal protocol
g^{*}(t)
to minimize the thermodynamic average work and its fluctuation
Schmiedl and Seifert 2007
;
Gomez-Marin et al. 2008
;
Nazé et al. 2022
;
Nazé 2023
;
Nazé 2024
;
Loos et al. 2024
;
Davis et al. 2024
;
Bonança and Deffner 2018
;
Deffner and Bonança 2020
;
Blaber and Sivak 2023
;
Kamizaki et al. 2022
;
Solon and Horowitz 2018
;
Engel et al. 2023
;
Gingrich et al. 2016
.

This work aims to characterize the relaxation function of systems ruled by Eq. (
1
), where the active heat bath is present. In particular, we want to know what will be the effect on optimal protocols in weakly driven processes, where general solutions have been found
Nazé 2024
. First, we study a particular example of a Brownian motion subject to an arbitrary heat bath and to a moving laser trap and expand to a general potential to characterize completely these desired features. Conditions on the kernel are found so the system satisfy the Second Law of Thermodynamics as well.

III
Brownian motion in moving trap

Consider a Brownian motion in a moving trap, where the time-dependent potential is given by

V(x(t),\lambda(t))=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{2}{(x(t)-\lambda(t))}^{2},

(8)

whose the passive relaxation time for a passive heat bath is
\tau_{R}=\gamma/\omega_{0}^{2}
. Considering the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck kernel
Uhlenbeck and Ornstein 1930
;
Zwanzig 2001
, given by

K(t)=\frac{\exp{\left(-\frac{|t|}{\tau_{P}}\right)}}{\tau_{P}},

(9)

and, using Eq. (
4
), the relaxation function of such a system will be

\Psi_{0}(t)=\Psi_{0}(0)\exp{\left(-\frac{|t|}{\tau_{R}+\tau_{P}}\right)},

(10)

whose change from the passive bath case was the addition of persistence time in the new relaxation time of the same relaxation function obtained without the activity. Observe that the system starts from its equilibrium state
Grigolini et al. 1988
.

These results yield natural questions: Does the addition of an active heat bath change the structure of the original relaxation function? If it changes, what will be the characteristics in the kernel to guarantee a model that satisfies the Second Law of Thermodynamics? How does the relaxation time of the new relaxation function change? Is it only the addition of the original relaxation time plus the persistence time of the active heat bath?

III.1
General kernel

To start, let us consider a general kernel, subject to the same moving trap potential. Using Eq. (
4
), its active relaxation function
\Psi_{0}^{B}(t)
will be

\Psi_{0}^{B}(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}_{t}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}[K(t)]}{\omega_{0}^{2}/\gamma+s\mathcal{L}_{s}[K(t)]}\right],

(11)

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}^{-1}
is the inverse Laplace transform. Calculating its relaxation time
\tau_{R}^{B}
via Eq. (
6
), we will have

\tau_{R}^{B}=\frac{\gamma}{\omega_{0}^{2}}+\tau_{P},

(12)

which indicates that the active relaxation function changes in respect to the passive one with the addition of a new term
\Psi_{B}(t)
, whose relaxation time is equal to the persistence time of the active heat bath. In the next sections, we will treat the case for an arbitrary potential and observe that it is not always true.

IV
Relaxation function

under active heat baths

To answer the questions from the last section, consider the generalized Langevin equation (
1
) for the passive kernel
K(t)=2\delta(t)
and generic potential. By integrating the generalized Langevin equation, the generic potential can be expressed as

\begin{split}V(x(t),\lambda(t))&=V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\eta(t^{\prime})\dot{x}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\\
&-\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\dot{x}^{2}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\end{split}

(13)

Considering the approximation of linear response theory, Eq. (
13
) is evolved from the equilibrium solution, which requires some particularities on the active heat bath
Goswami 2019
;
Caprini et al. 2021
;
Khali et al. 2024
. Considering the definition (
4
), the relaxation function will be

\begin{split}\Psi_{0}(t)&=\Psi_{0}(0)+\beta\int_{0}^{t}\langle\overline{\eta(t^{\prime})\partial_{\lambda_{0}}\dot{x}(t^{\prime})}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}dt^{\prime}\\
&-\beta\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\langle\overline{\partial_{\lambda_{0}}\dot{x}^{2}(t^{\prime})}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}dt^{\prime}\end{split}

(14)

Using definition (
6
), the relaxation time is

\begin{split}\tau_{R}&=\mathcal{L}_{s}\left[1+\frac{\beta}{\Psi_{0}(0)}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\overline{\eta(t)\partial_{\lambda_{0}}\dot{x}(t)}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}dt\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{\beta\gamma}{\Psi_{0}(0)}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\overline{\partial_{\lambda_{0}}\dot{x}^{2}(t)}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}dt\right](0).\end{split}

(15)

Consider now a generic active heat bath, whose relaxation function of the system we will denote by
\Psi_{0}^{B}(t)
. Using the same idea used in the passive heat bath case above, where the potential is expressed in terms of the solution of the generalized Langevin equation, one has

\Psi_{0}^{B}(t)=\Psi_{0}(t)+\Upsilon_{B}(t),

(16)

where

\Upsilon_{B}(t)=\beta\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\langle\overline{\partial_{\lambda_{0}}\dot{x}^{2}(t)}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\rangle_{0}dt-\frac{\beta\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}K(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\left\langle\overline{\partial_{\lambda_{0}}(\dot{x}(t^{\prime})\dot{x}(t^{\prime\prime}))}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\right\rangle_{0}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime},

(17)

which vanishes when the active heat bath is equal to the passive one. In this case, this new term measures how far the active heat bath is from the inertial one. In the end, the form of the new relaxation function will change with the addition of an active heat bath, becoming the example illustrated in Sec.
III
just a fortunate case.

IV.1
Compatibility with the 2nd Law

To guarantee that the kernel will produce a model that satisfies the Second Law of Thermodynamics, we rewrite Eq. (
17
) as a quadratic form

\Upsilon_{B}(t)=\beta\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\left[\delta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})-\frac{K(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}{2}\right]\left\langle\overline{\partial_{\lambda_{0}}(\dot{x}(t^{\prime})\dot{x}(t^{\prime\prime}))}\partial_{\lambda_{0}}V(x_{0},\lambda_{0})\right\rangle_{0}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime},

(18)

and observe that the following condition
Nazé and Bonança 2020

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}\left[\Upsilon_{B}(t)\right]\geq 0,

(19)

is the necessary one to achieve the agreement, where
\mathcal{F}_{\omega}
is the Fourier transform. Using Bochner’s theorem
Nazé and Bonança 2020
twice, and considering that the kernel is an even function, the condition over the kernel will be

K(t)\geq 2\delta(t),

(20)

meaning in practice that
K(t)\geq 0
and that
K(t)=2\delta(t)
for
\tau_{P}\rightarrow 0
. This is guaranteed according to the definition of the kernel. Therefore, the definition of the kernel guarantees its compatibility with the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

IV.2
Timescale of the active heat bath

The new relaxation time will be

\tau_{R}^{B}=\tau_{R}+\tau_{B},

(21)

where

\tau_{B}=\left.\mathcal{L}_{s}\left[\frac{\Upsilon_{B}(t)}{\Psi_{0}(0)}\right]\right|_{s=0}

(22)

is the new timescale of the active heat bath. To guarantee that
\tau_{B}
will be finite and a positive number, it is necessary that
\Upsilon_{B}(t)
may be positive. For this, the kernel should obey

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}[K(t)]\leq 2\mathcal{F}_{\omega}[\delta(t)]|_{\omega=0}.

(23)

If the kernel is an even function, its maximum is achieved for
\omega=0
. Therefore, the final condition is

\mathcal{F}_{\omega}[K(t)]|_{\omega=0}=2\mathcal{F}_{\omega}[\delta(t)]|_{\omega=0},

(24)

which is always true according to its definition. Therefore, the definition of the kernel guarantees that
\tau_{B}
will be finite and a positive number. Observe that
\tau_{B}
is not necessarily equal to the persistence time. Again, the example presented in Sec.
III
is just a fortunate case. Also, such addition on the passive relaxation time, for the same switching time
\tau
, leads to

\frac{\tau}{\tau_{R}}>\frac{\tau}{\tau_{R}^{B}},

(25)

indicating that the system is far from the passive heat bath case from the non-equilibrium point of view
Nazé et al. 2022
. This fact is expected since the dynamics of the system is affected by the active heat bath, which is intuitively a source of non-equilibrium features
Vrugt and Wittkowski 2024
;
Volpe et al. 2024
;
Schüttler et al. 2025
.

V
Optimal protocols

under active heat baths

In Ref.
Nazé 2024
, the optimal protocol to achieve the minimal thermodynamic average work and its fluctuation is given by the following symmetric protocol

g^{*}(t)=\frac{t+\tau_{R}}{\tau+2\tau_{R}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\left[\frac{\delta^{(n)}(t)-\delta^{(n)}(\tau-t)}{\tau+2\tau_{R}}\right],

(26)

where
a_{n}
is a coefficient related to the relaxation function. Adding the active heat bath, as illustrated in this article, the modification happens in the relaxation time. Therefore

g^{*}(t)=\frac{t+\tau_{R}^{B}}{\tau+2\tau_{R}^{B}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}^{B}\left[\frac{\delta^{(n)}(t)-\delta^{(n)}(\tau-t)}{\tau+2\tau_{R}^{B}}\right],

(27)

where
a_{n}^{B}
is a new coefficient related to the new relaxation function. However, although this result is optimal, its singular part could be a problem in effectively reproducing it in the laboratory.

Observe that the only important feature in this optimal protocol is the timescale related to the active heat bath. Therefore, one is free to choose any active heat bath that reproduces such a quantity. In this case, a simpler and more controlled one is the best choice to achieve minimal dissipation. Also, the symmetric protocol is related to the general result of linear response theory
Nazé 2023
;
Nazé 2024
and to the characteristics of systems ruled by generalized overdamped Langevin equations, whose symmetry is valid for arbitrary driving strength
Loos et al. 2024
.

VI
Stiffening trap in active bath

To illustrate the results presented above, let us consider the following system: a Brownian particle, ruled by the generalized overdamped Langevin equation, with a stiffening trap potential

V(x(t),\lambda(t))=\lambda(t)\frac{x(t)^{2}}{2}

(28)

and an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck kernel (
9
). Observe that the relaxation time of the system in a passive bath is
\tau_{R}=\gamma/(2\lambda_{0})

Nazé et al. 2022
, while the persistence time of the active heat bath is
\tau_{P}
. Also, the system achieves thermal equilibrium in the initial canonical ensemble
Grigolini et al. 1988
. Using Eq. (
4
) with Eq. (
28
), the active relaxation function is

\Psi_{0}^{B}(t)=\frac{\exp{\left(-\frac{2\lambda_{0}|t|}{\gamma+\lambda_{0}\tau_{P}}\right)}\gamma^{2}}{4\beta\lambda_{0}^{2}(\gamma+\lambda_{0}\tau_{P})^{2}},

(29)

which has
\mathcal{F}_{\omega}[\Psi_{0}^{B}(t)](\omega)\geq 0
, satisfying therefore the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Observe that, for
\tau_{P}=0
, we recover the passive relaxation function
Nazé et al. 2022
. In this manner, the relaxation function changes from that one that comes from the passive heat bath. Also, the relaxation time of the new relaxation function is

\tau_{R}^{B}=\frac{\gamma}{2\lambda_{0}}+\underbrace{\frac{\tau_{P}}{2}}_{\tau_{B}}>0,

(30)

which shows that
\tau_{B}\neq\tau_{P}
. Also, as expected,
\tau_{B}>0
. In this manner, the active heat bath increases the relaxation time, bringing the system far from the passive heat bath case from the non-equilibrium point of view.

The optimal protocol will be

\begin{split}g^{*}(t)&=\frac{t+\gamma/(2\omega_{0}^{2})+\tau_{P}/2}{\tau+\gamma/\omega_{0}^{2}+\tau_{P}}\\
&+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}^{B}\left[\frac{\delta^{(n)}(t)-\delta^{(n)}(\tau-t)}{\tau+\gamma/\omega_{0}^{2}+\tau_{P}}\right],\end{split}

(31)

which brings it far from the near-optimal protocol of the passive heat bath case, where
\tau_{P}=0
, from the non-equilibrium point of view. Therefore, if the system is more out of equilibrium, its optimal protocol must change to supply such modification.

VII
Final remarks

In this work, focusing on harmonic trap potentials, we showed the modifications in the characteristics of weakly driven processes under active heat baths when compared to the passive ones. Here, the active heat bath is model with a colored noise, indicating its non-Markovian feature. The relaxation function and its relaxation times change, bringing the system far from the passive heat bath situation from the non-equilibrium point of view, as expected due to the features of the active heat bath. The optimal protocols for minimizing the average work and its fluctuation were immediately derived. Also, conditions on the kernel are determined to guarantee the compatibility of the new system with the Second Law of Thermodynamics: it must be positive, even, and achieves Markovian feature for
\tau_{B}\rightarrow 0
. Future research will explore measures of how far systems under active heat baths can be brought outside the passive heat bath case using entropy production rates
Nazé and Bonança 2020
.
