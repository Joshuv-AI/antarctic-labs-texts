Trajectories of sleep disturbance among school teachers in Shenzhen, China: patterns, predictors, and mental health correlates
Authors: Dongfang Wang, Yunge Fan, Yuanyuan Li, Shuyi Zhai, Xiangting Zhang, Huolian Li, Zijuan Ma, Fang Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate the trajectories of sleep disturbance among school teachers in China, the factors that influencing these trajectories, and their association with mental health outcomes.MethodsThe participants in this study were 3,634 teachers from Shenzhen city, China, with 21.2% of them being males and a mean age of 34.69 years. The survey was conducted in three waves between April 2021 and June 2022, and assessed sleep disturbance, neuroticism, resilience, negative life events, depression, anxiety, and demographic variables. Latent Growth Mixture Modeling (LGMM) was used to identify different latent trajectory classes of sleep disturbance. Multivariable logistic regression was then employed within the best-fitting LGMM to examine the associations between trajectory membership with both predictors and mental health outcomes.ResultsThe prevalence of sleep disturbance among school teachers in the three surveys was 19.0%, 16.6%, and 18.8%, respectively. Four trajectories of sleep disturbance were identified within one year: no/low (73.3%), persistent (16.2%), new-onset (5.3%), and remission (5.2%). Higher neuroticism and lower resilience were associated with increased risks of persistent or new-onset sleep disturbance. A greater number of negative life events was a potential risk factor for new-onset sleep disturbance, whereas younger age protected against persistent sleep disturbance. Additionally, teachers in the persistent and new-onset trajectories were more likely to report depression and anxiety.ConclusionsAlthough most teachers’ sleep disturbance remains mild or resolved over time, a subset of teachers, particularly those with the aforementioned risk factors, experience persistent or new-onset sleep disturbance. Therefore, targeted interventions for these teachers are warranted.Keywords: Sleep disturbance, Prevalence, Longitudinal trajectory, Teacher, Follow-up study

Introduction

Teaching is a demanding and challenging profession, both physically and mentally. Teachers invest considerable effort into their daily responsibilities and often support students with personal and familial commitments, which can be a constant source of stress [1, 2]. Increasing evidence suggests that teaching ranks among the most stressful occupations [3, 4], with the situation being particularly pronounced in China. Findings from the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS) indicate that middle school teachers in China work on average 47.5 h per week [5], significantly surpassing the 38-hour weekly average recorded in the other 34 surveyed countries [6]. The cultural and societal context in China also places greater emphasis on general education, resulting in heightened parental expectations and concerns regarding their children’s grades. Additionally, teachers’ performance evaluations can be influenced by their students’ test scores, adding to the stressors they face [7]. Despite these challenges, previous research has paid limited attention to the well-being and health status of the teaching population.

Sleep disturbance, including insomnia and poor sleep quality, is a significant indicator of teacher health and a common complaint within the teaching profession. Sleep disturbance not only impairs work performance, such as cognitive decline [8] and low job satisfaction [9], but also increases the risk of various health conditions, such as mental disorders [10], diabetes, obesity, hypertension [11], and even suicidal behavior [12]. Theoretically, detrimental job stressors, such as high work demands and low job control, negatively affect individual sleep quality [13]. Empirical studies have shown that higher levels of work-related stress or fatigue and longer working hours are associated with an increased risk of sleep disturbance [14, 15]. Hori et al. sampled 11,390 teachers in Japan and found the prevalence of insomnia symptoms to be 41.7% of male teachers and 44.0% of female teachers [16]. Recently, a comprehensive review of 33 articles from 15 countries indicates that 36–61% of the surveyed teachers experience symptoms of insomnia [17]. Despite the importance of teachers and their work, high-quality research on sleep disturbance in teachers is limited [17].

In fact, the sleep quality of teachers may vary due to different workloads across different semesters. Previous studies have found that individuals experience diverse trajectories in longitudinal changes in sleep disturbance, underscoring the importance of assessing these longitudinal changes [18, 19]. Following the initial acute symptoms of insomnia, which may be triggered by stressful life events, some individuals may see an improvement over time, while others may continue to experience sleep problems. The latter group of individuals is also more susceptible to developing mental disorders [20]. Therefore, it is worthwhile to delve deeper into the development trajectory of sleep disturbance among teachers, especially within the Chinese cultural context. An effective approach to identifying heterogeneity in symptom trajectories and the significant factors influencing them is to use individual-centered methods (e.g., latent growth mixture modeling) to identify groups with different patterns of change over time. These methods have been employed to examine the longitudinal change patterns and influence factors of sleep disturbance in adolescent and general adult populations [21, 22]. By identifying meaningful subtypes within the course of sleep disturbance that exhibit distinct health characteristics, valuable information can be obtained for screening high-risk individuals and implementing targeted interventions from a clinical perspective. However, research on specific professions at higher risk of sleep disturbance, such as teachers, remains limited to the best of our knowledge.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was to investigate the developmental trajectories of sleep disturbance among Chinese teachers using a longitudinal web-based survey. The first objective was to identify the longitudinal differences in sleep disturbance by employing Latent Growth Mixture Modeling (LGMM) [23]. Previous research has indicated that the risks of experiencing sleep disturbance are associated with various psychosocial factors, including female gender [24], divorced, separated, or single [25], and experiencing stressful life events [26]. Moreover, it has been found that better resilience [27] and lower neuroticism [28] play a protective role in development of sleep disturbance. Therefore, this study also aimed to determine which factors can differentiate the developmental trajectory profiles of sleep disturbance among teachers. Furthermore, there is ample evidence demonstrating that sleep disturbance is linked to several of negative mental health outcomes, such as depression [29] and anxiety [30]. Therefore, this study also examined the influencing factors associated with different trajectory patterns of sleep disturbance and their impact on psychological outcomes.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The data for this study were drawn from the routine mental-health survey of primary- and secondary-school teachers and students in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, China. The survey is usually conducted once each semester, typically in the second month after the new term begins. This study was a three-point repeated cross-sectional design with a nested longitudinal subsample [31]. The first data (referred to as Time 1, T1) was collected from April 21 to May 12, 2021. The second set (Time 2, T2) was collected from December 17 to 26, 2021. The third set (Time 3, T3) was collected from May 17 to June 5, 2022. Initially, the study involved 14,532 teachers at T1, 11,466 at T2, and 13,065 at T3. However, in order to enhance the quality of the survey responses, certain exclusion criteria were applied to the participants. These included: (1) incorrect identity information (e.g., implausible age), (2) unusually short response time, (3) inconsistent survey responses (e.g., repetitive or patterned answers), and/or (4) self-reported mental health diagnoses. A small number of participants met these exclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample of 14,057 teachers at T1, 10,702 at T2, and 12,546 at T3. Among these participants, a total of 3,634 teachers provided complete responses to the measurement questions in all three surveys (refer to Fig. 1). To compare the prevalence of sleep disturbance at T1 between participants who provided complete data across three waves and those with missing data in the subsequent surveys (T2 and T3), a chi-square test was used. The results indicated no significant difference in prevalence of sleep disturbance between these two groups (20.1% vs. 19.7%, χ2 = 0.18, p = 0.685).

Participation flow chart

The three surveys were conducted in collaboration with the local education bureau, using the web-based “Survey Star” questionnaire platform. Prior to each survey, all schools were requested to send invitation letters to eligible teachers. The teachers then completed the online survey by scanning the Quick Response (QR) code of the questionnaire using their mobile phones. Confidentiality was guaranteed, and participants could withdraw at any time during the survey period. After each survey, we operated a toll-free psychological support hotline (the “Xinqing” hotline) for all participants. Participants were encouraged to call the hotline for psychological support whenever needed.

Measures

Due to time constraints, it was not feasible to assess every construct at every wave. Accordingly, variables were drawn from the time points at which they were measured: sleep disturbance was assessed in all three waves; demographic variables were taken from the baseline survey; depression and anxiety were measured at T1 and T3; negative life events at T2 and T3; neuroticism at T2; and resilience at T3.

Sleep disturbance

In this study, sleep disturbance primarily encompassed insomnia symptoms and poor sleep quality. Four items from the Youth Self-rating Insomnia Scale (YSIS) [32] were used to assess sleep disturbance over a one-month period. Insomnia symptoms were evaluated using three questions: (1) Difficulty initiating sleep (DIS): “Do you experience difficulty falling asleep?” (2) Difficulty maintaining sleep (DMS): “Do you wake up frequently during your sleep?” (3) Early morning awakening (EMA): “Do you wake up very early and struggle to fall back to sleep?” Participants could respond to each question on a scale of 1–5, with 1 representing “never” and 5 representing “5–7 times per week”. Participants were classified as having insomnia if any symptom was rated ≥ 3 times per week [24, 33]. Poor sleep quality (PSQ) was measured by asking participants to rate their overall sleep quality on a scale of 1–5, with 1 being “very good’ and 5 being “very poor”. Participants with either insomnia or poor sleep quality were classified as having sleep disturbance. This definition has been employed in previous studies that used the same items [34, 35]. Additionally, for the trajectory analysis, a continuous sleep disturbance score was calculated by summing the scores of the four items, with higher scores indicating more severe sleep disturbance symptoms [36]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the four sleep items were 0.87, 0.88 and 0.88 at the three time points, respectively.

Neuroticism

Neuroticism was assessed by the NEO Five-Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI) at T2 [37]. It consists of 12 items rated on a 5-point scale, with each item scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating a higher level of neuroticism. The Chinese version of NEO-FFI has been widely applied in the adult population with satisfactory psychometric properties [38, 39]. The Cronbach’s α was 0.96 in this sample.

Resilience

Resilience was measured by the 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) at T3 [40]. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with each item scored from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (true nearly all). Higher total scores indicate greater resilience. The CD-RISC-10 shows satisfactory internal consistency in the Chinese population [41, 42], and its Cronbach’s α was 0.98 in the present sample.

Negative life events

Negative life events experienced during the past six months were assessed with a self-administered scale. The scale comprises 12 items that were derived from the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) [43]. Items pertinent to adolescents (e.g., failing important exams) were removed, and adult-relevant negative events (e.g., serious financial crisis) were added. Each item was rated on a 2-point Likert scale: 0 - not happened, 1 – happened. The total number of endorsed events indicates the extent of negative life-event exposure. The Cronbach’s αs were 0.83 and 0.62 at T2 and T3, respectively.

Depression and anxiety

The 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-2) were used to assess depressive and anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks at T1 and T3, respectively [44, 45]. Each item is rated from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with a higher score indicating more severe symptoms. A cut-off score of 3 was used to identify probable clinical levels of depression and anxiety in this study [46, 47]. The Cronbach’s αs for PHQ-2 were 0.84 and 0.87 at T1 and T3, respectively; that of GAD-2 were 0.87 and 0.90 at T1 and T3, respectively.

Demographic variables

Demographic variables were collected in a self-report format, including sex (male; female), age, ethnicity (ethnicity Han [the major ethnic group in China]; others), school type (public; private), teaching qualifications (primary school; junior high school; senior high school), marital status (married; not married), immigrant status (yes [have a local household registration]; no), education level (college and less; undergraduate; master and above), monthly family income (12000 RMB and below; 12000 ~ 30000 RMB; 30000 RMB and above), chronic physical illness (yes [having at least one of arthritis, angina, asthma, diabetes, visual impairment or hearing problem [48]; no), family history of psychiatric illness (yes; no), and lifestyle variables (e.g., exercise, smoking, and alcohol intake).

Statistical analysis

First, descriptive analyses were conducted with SPSS 24.0 to characterize the sample. The participants’ age is described as mean and standard deviation [M(SD)], and the other demographic variables are reported as frequency [N(%)]. Second, McNemar tests were used to compare the prevalence of sleep disturbance across the three time points. Third, LGMM [23] was employed using Mplus 7.30, with a robust maximum likelihood estimator, to identify sleep disturbance trajectories. The analysis initially assumed a single class in the model, and the number of categories was gradually increased until the model that best fitted the data was determined. The optimal class was determined according to several indicators: lower Akaike Information Criteria (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample size adjusted BIC (a-BIC), entropy approaching 1, and a significant Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (VLMR-LRT) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT). Moreover, the percentage of participants in the smallest category was required to be greater than 5% [49]. Furthermore, the demographic variables were compared using the Chi-square test and ANOVA test. Finally, to explore the predictors of changes in sleep disturbance, multivariate logistic regressions were conducted to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Because we focused on factors associated with new-onset or persistent sleep disturbance, the no/low group was used as the reference in comparisons with the new-onset group. Similarly, the remission group served as the reference when contrasted with the persistent group. All demographic variables, neuroticism, resilience, negative life events, and baseline depression/anxiety were included as covariates. Additionally, logistic regression was performed to further examine the relationship between different trajectories of sleep disturbance and depression and anxiety. This analysis controlled for demographic variables, negative life events, and baseline depression/ anxiety. Two-tailed p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Ethics

The study procedure was carried out in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration as revised in 1989 and was approved by the Ethics Committees of School of Psychology, South China Normal University (approval No. SCNU-PSY-2021-094). All participants provided electronic informed consent before starting each web-based survey.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The final sample consisted of 711 males (21.2%) and 2863 females (78.8%), with ages ranging from 20 to 66 years old (mean age = 34.69 ± 8.64 years) at T1. The majority of teachers (96.6%) belonged to the Han ethnicity, and 40.5% of the teachers (N = 1473) worked in public schools. Other demographic variables are presented in Table 1. Regarding sleep disturbance, the prevalence of positive sleep disturbance was 19.0% at T1, 16.6% at T2, and 18.8% at T3. The prevalence of sleep disturbance was significantly lower in T2 compared with the other two time points, while there was no significant difference between T1 and T3 (T1 vs. T2, p < 0.001; T1 vs. T3, p = 0.728; T2 vs. T3, p = 0.002). Identifying latent trajectory classes

The fit indices obtained from LGMM were used to examine the relative fit of models with 1 to 5 trajectory classes. As presented in Table 2, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC consistently decreased for models ranging from 1 to 5 classes. All models with different classes showed significant p-values in VLMR-LRT and BLRT statistics. Among these trajectory classes, the 4- and 5- class models demonstrated relatively good fit to the data, as indicated by the lower AIC and BIC values, as well as the higher Entropy. Upon further evaluation of the distributions of the last two latent classes, the 5- class model was excluded because its class size was less than 5% of the total sample. Therefore, the 4-class model was deemed the final optimal model.

Description of latent trajectory classes

Figure 3 presents the four latent trajectory classes of sleep disturbance. Class 1, labeled as “Persistent” (16.2%) consisted of participants who experienced severe sleep disturbance at all three time periods (intercept = 12.31, p < 0.001; slope = 0.07, p = 0.573). Class 4, referred to as “No/low”, encompassed the majority of participants (73.3%) and was characterized by consistently low sleep disturbance scores across the three time points (intercept = 7.00, p < 0.001; slope = 0.09, p = 0.038). Class 2, labeled as “Remission”, and included 5.2% of participants. These individuals initially exhibited a higher level of sleep disturbance that gradually decreased over time (intercept = 12.99, p < 0.001; slope = − 2.65, p < 0.001). In contrast, Class 3, referred to as “New-onset”, comprised 5.3% of the participants. These individuals initially had lower sleep disturbance but experienced worsening symptoms over time (intercept = 6.78, p < 0.001; slope = 3.14, p < 0.001). Predictors of sleep disturbance trajectory membership

According to Table 3, we initially designated the “Remission” class as the reference group for our logistic regressions. The probability of experiencing symptoms in the “Persistent” class increased when the teachers were older (OR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.02–1.07), exhibited higher levels of neuroticism (OR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.02–1.06), had lower levels of resilience (OR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.94–0.98), and reported baseline anxiety (OR = 2.14, 95% CI = 1.29–3.56). Subsequently, we designated the “No/low” class as the reference group for further logistic regressions. Our findings revealed that neuroticism (OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.02–1.05) and negative life events (OR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.04–1.21) were potential risk factors for the new-onset sleep disturbance. On the other hand, resilience acted as a protective factor against sleep disturbance (OR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.94–0.97).

Trajectories of sleep disturbance in relation to psychological outcomes

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the ability of sleep disturbance trajectories to predict levels of depression and anxiety. The “No/low” class was designated as the reference group and was compared to other trajectory groups. As depicted in Table 4, after adjusting for sample characteristics variables, negative life events, and baseline depression, teachers belonging to the “New-onset” class (OR = 6.54, 95% CI = 4.57–9.36) and “Persistent” class (OR = 2.91, 95% CI = 2.22–3.80) exhibited a higher likelihood of experiencing depression at T3 when compared to individuals in the “No/low” class. Similar results were observed for anxiety at T3, with both the “New-onset” class (OR = 7.47, 95% CI = 5.12–11.91) and “Persistent” class (OR = 3.28, 95% CI = 2.44–4.41) showing significantly higher odds of anxiety compared to the “No/low” class. These findings remained consistent even after accounting for sample characteristics variables, negative life events, and baseline anxiety.

Discussion

This longitudinal study was, to our knowledge, the first to examine developmental trajectories of sleep disturbance in a large sample of teachers. The prevalence estimates of sleep disturbance in the past-month were found to be in the range of 16.6% to 19.0% across three time points. These rates were significantly higher than those reported for students (14.1%) using consistent screening criteria [35]. Another survey conducted during the COVID-19 lockdown among a large sample of college students using the same measure and criteria reported a prevalence of sleep disturbance ranging from approximately 8.5 to 9.7% [50]. These rates were significantly lower than the findings from our study. Consequently, it is essential to identify and address sleep disturbance and its developmental patterns among teachers. Additionally, our data revealed that teachers reported a higher prevalence of sleep disturbance during the spring academic year (March - July: T1 and T3) compared to the winter academic year (September – January: T2). One explanation is that Chinese students advance to the next grade in spring, increasing teachers’ workload and stress, which in turn may exacerbate sleep disturbance.

Using a person-centered approach, we identified four classes of sleep disturbance trajectories. The most prevalent class was “No/low”, which accounted for 73.3% of the sample. This was followed by the “Persistent” class at 16.2%, the “New-onset” class at 5.3%, and the “Remission” class at 5.2%. Previous longitudinal studies on sleep disturbance have consistently found that the “No/low” class, characterized by stable low symptoms, is the most common trajectory [18, 24, 51]. This suggests that most individuals are able to cope well with life stressors and maintain good overall healthful functioning. In addition, we examined the associations between teachers’ membership in different sleep disturbance trajectory classes and their influential predictors, as well as their mental health outcomes.

In this study, significant differences were observed among different trajectory classes in terms of certain predictors. Consistent with previous studies [28, 52], neuroticism was found to contribute to the emergence or persistence of sleep disturbance. Neuroticism has been linked to difficulty in regulating emotions (such as depression and anxiety) and behavior (such as sleep hygiene) [53]. It is also associated with sensitivity to signs of threat and dysfunction, all of which may increase the risk of dysfunction in response to perceived sleep disturbance [54]. Our findings also indicated that resilience could potentially predict distinct trajectories of sleep disturbance. This is consistent with a previous study [27] which found that higher levels of resilience were associated with a lower likelihood of experiencing sleep problems. Resilience is defined as an individual’s capacity to cope with negative stress and challenges [55], and it is considered as a positive psychological resource that contributes to mental health [56]. Additionally, our results demonstrated that teachers who reported anxiety at baseline were approximately twice as likely to experience persistent sleep disturbance in the following year compared with those without anxiety. However, baseline depression did not predict changes in sleep disturbance. These findings align with a study by Jansson et al., suggesting that anxiety may have a stronger association than depression with the development of sleep disturbance one year later [57]. Furthermore, an increased number of negative life events was found to significantly increase the likelihood of a new-onset sleep disturbance trajectory compare to a no/low trajectory, which is in line with previous studies [58]. Additionally, consistent with previous findings [59] older age was associated with poor sleep quality when considering the influence of demographic variables. In light of these findings, future psychosocial interventions targeting sleep disturbance among teachers may need to take these factors into consideration.

Previous studies have demonstrated the associations between sleep disturbance and depression in both cross-sectional [60] and longitudinal [34] studies. Building upon these findings, our results indicated that teachers who experienced persistent or new-onset sleep disturbance were more likely to have depression, even after controlling for baseline levels of depression. Furthermore, we found that teachers with severe sleep disturbance were also more prone to emotional dysregulation, which could contribute to the development of depressive symptoms in the future [61]. Similarly, our study revealed that both persistent and new-onset sleep disturbance patterns were significant predictors of anxiety among teachers one year later. This finding aligns with previous research [62], which has shown that individuals with poor sleep quality are more likely to engage in prolonged negative repetitive thoughts before falling asleep, such as worrying, leading to increased anxiety [63]. To identify teachers at high-risk for mental health problems, it is important to consider different trajectories of sleep disturbance, particularly those displaying persistent or new-onset patterns as opposed to a no/low trajectory. In conclusion, it is crucial to identify specific patterns of sleep disturbance and implement appropriate interventions, such as increasing physical activity [64] and providing sleep hygiene education [65], to effectively reduce mental health issues.

The current study has several strengths. These strengths include a substantial sample size, adjustment for a wide range of covariates, and data collection over multiple time points. These strengths allowed us to identify heterogeneous trajectories within the teacher population. However, despite the valuable findings, some limitations need to be acknowledged. First, it is important to note that our measures of sleep disturbance, depression, and anxiety were obtained through self-report questionnaires, and lacked clinical diagnostic interviews and objective sleep measures (e.g., actigraphy). This may raise concerns regarding the validity of our findings. Second, as our survey was conducted online, some teachers may have overlooked the recruitment invitation, resulting in a high attrition rate. Although there were no significant differences in the baseline prevalence of sleep disturbance between teachers who followed up and those who were lost to follow-up, caution must be exercised when interpreting the results. Meanwhile, it is crucial to highlight that this study was conducted during a stage of normalized COVID-19 prevention and control in China. The concerns related to the pandemic may have had a negative impact on sleep outcomes [66]. Therefore, when controlling for negative life events, this study considered stressors that may have been caused by the pandemic, such as illness of oneself or a close relative, or facing a serious economic crisis. However, internal consistency of the negative life events scale declined at T3, probably because teachers encountered new pandemic-related stressors that the scale did not cover. Finally, it should be noted that, although the sample size was large, all teachers were initially recruited from Shenzhen, China. The sample also showed a marked sex imbalance (male vs. female: 21.2% vs. 78.8%), which, while consistent with the current sex distribution among primary and secondary school teachers in China [67], may somewhat limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study contributes to the literature by examining the prevalence and the heterogeneity of sleep disturbance among teachers. Over the course of one year, we identified four distinct trajectories: no/low, persistent, new-onset, and remission. Age, neuroticism, resilience, and negative life events significantly predicted these divergent patterns. The findings have important implications for educational administrators and mental-health professionals, underscoring the need for personalised, targeted interventions that support teachers in their daily work.
