Risk factors associated with mortality in patients with catheter-related and catheter-associated bloodstream infections: a retrospective observational study focused on CVADs and midlines
Authors: Rebecca Fattore, Claudia Conflitti, Giovanni Scaglione, Giovanni De Capitani, Fabio Borgonovo, Daniele Zizzo, Monica Schiavini, Federico Fassio, Antonio Gidaro, Maria Calloni, Francesco Casella, Alba Taino, Arianna Bartoli, Chiara Cogliati, Andrea Gori, Spinello Antinori, Nicola Busatto, Antonella Foschi, Marta Colaneri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVascular access devices (VADs) are essential in healthcare but carry risks, including bloodstream infections and increased mortality. This study aimed to identify risk factors for in-hospital all-cause mortality in non-ICU patients with CVADs and midlines and for infection-related mortality in patients with catheter-related/associated bloodstream infections (CRBSI/CABSI).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center study at an Italian tertiary hospital, enrolling hospitalized non-ICU patients with VADs (CVADs and midlines in particular) between July 2021 and March 2024. We analyzed demographic, clinical, VAD, and microbiological data. Risk factors for mortality were assessed using competing risk models.ResultsAmong 2062 patients with CVADs or midlines, in-hospital all-cause mortality was 13.9% (4.41 deaths/1000 patient-days). Independent risk factors included advanced age (HR: 1.05 per year), > 4 comorbidities (HR: 1.55), COVID-19-related pneumonia (HR: 2.58), and cachexia (HR: 2.59). Female sex (HR: 0.71) and multiple VAD placements (HR: 0.54) were protective. Among 159 CRBSI/CABSI patients, infection-related mortality was 12.6% (5.24 deaths/1000 patient-days). Prompt catheter removal (HR: 0.09) and appropriate antibiotic therapy (HR: 0.37) were independently protective. Coagulase-negative Staphylococci were less frequent in CRBSI/CABSI deaths (25% vs. 53.7%).ConclusionMortality in non-ICU patients with CVAD or midlines is significantly influenced by age, comorbidity burden, and COVID-19 related pneumonia. For CRBSI/CABSI, timely catheter removal and appropriate antibiotics are crucial for improving survival. These findings underscore the importance of targeted risk assessment and aggressive management strategies in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01671-x.

Background

Vascular access devices (VADs) are essential tools in clinical practice and are broadly categorized into arterial catheters, central venous catheters (CVADs), and peripheral venous catheters (PVADs), which are also abbreviated as peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC). CVADs include centrally inserted central catheters (CICCs), peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), femoral inserted central catheters (FICCs), and totally implantable devices such as ports and PICC-ports. In contrast, PVADs comprise short peripheral cannulas (< 6 cm), long peripheral catheters (6–15 cm), and midline catheters (>15 cm) [1].

Both CVADs and PVADs are widely used in inpatient care and are associated with infectious complications, notably catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) and catheter-associated bloodstream infections (CABSIs) [2]. The incidence of CRBSI/CABSI has been extensively studied in intensive care units (ICUs), with rates ranging from 0.18 to 3.84 per 1,000 catheter-days [3, 4]. However, evidence from general hospital wards remains limited. A European study reported a CRBSI incidence of 0.97 per 1,000 patient-days in non-ICU settings [5], while an Italian study documented CRBSI incidence of 2.35 per 1,000 catheter-days [6].

Notoriously, CRBSI/CABSI are significant causes of in-hospital mortality, increased healthcare costs, and prolonged lengths of stay (LOS) [7]. Reported mortality rates vary widely depending on the setting and population, ranging from 13.9% [8], to 18% in non-ICU patients [9]. Recent European studies suggest a declining trend in CRBSI-related mortality, possibly due to the adoption of infection prevention bundles and growing awareness among healthcare providers [10, 11]. Yet, understanding the risk factors for unfavorable clinical outcomes of CRBSI/CABSI patients remains scarcely explored. The PROBAC study (PROspective BACteremia study, a multicenter Spanish cohort) identified older age, high comorbidity burden, and heart failure as risk factors for in-hospital mortality in non-ICU patients with CRBSI, and the use of PICCs, timely catheter removal, and appropriate empirical antibiotic therapy as protective factors [12]. Moreover, mortality may also be influenced by VAD management, which is not consistently standardized across institutions [2]. For this reason, vascular access teams (VAT) and a multidisciplinary team should always manage VADs to reduce costs and patients’ morbidity and mortality [13]. For instance, retention of the infected catheter has been associated with worse outcomes in some studies [14, 15], but its effect was less clear in others [16].

Finally, a large Japanese multicenter study found that bloodstream infections due to Candida spp., co-infections, and hypoalbuminemia were associated with increased in-hospital mortality, whereas the use of PICC was a protective factor [13].

In Italy, data on CRBSI/CABSI are scarce outside the ICU setting and even less data is available regarding risk factors of in-hospital mortality in CRBSI/CABSI patients. With this in mind, we conducted this study with the aim of providing all-cause mortality estimates and related risk factors in non-ICU patients with VADs as well as identifying and characterizing independent infection-related mortality risk factors in CRBSI/CABSI non-ICU patients.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the incidence of in-hospital all-cause mortality of patients with CVADs or midlines. The secondary objectives were (i) to identify the risk factors for in-hospital all-cause mortality of patients with CVAD or midline, (ii) to assess the incidence of infection-related mortality of patients with CRBSI/CABSI, and (iii) to identify risk factors for infection-related mortality of patients diagnosed with CRBSI/CABSI.

Methods

Study design and clinical setting

This retrospective, monocentric, observational study was conducted at Luigi Sacco University Hospital, Milan, Northern Italy from July 2021 until March 2024.

In this hospital, a VAT has been established since 2018. The VAT supplies standardized procedures and medical reports and inserts all the VADs according to the institutional procedure named “Insertion of PICC or Midline Catheter in adult patients” applicable to all procedures performed by VAT and based on “Safe insertion of PICCs” protocol (SIP) [17].

Study population

The Department of Infectious Diseases retrieved all the demographic, clinical, laboratory and microbiological data of the hospitalized patients who had a VAD placement during their LOS. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients aged over 18 years; (ii) placement of a CVAD (PICC, CICC, FICC) or a midline by the Luigi Sacco University Hospital VAD team between 2021 and 2024; (iii) hospitalization in a non-ICU setting. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) outpatients undergoing CVAD or midline placement; (ii) patients with short peripheral venous access devices (< 15 cm), namely long peripheral catheters and/or short peripheral cannulas. The patients included could have multiple VAD placements during their LOS and could also have multiple CABSI and CRBSI events.

Definitions

According to the CONSIDER study: assessing the risk of catheter-associated bloodstream infections beyond the intensive care setting [18], CRBSI was defined by matching both of the following criteria: (i) the differential time to positivity, which means that the same microorganism must be isolated from blood cultures (BCs) obtained simultaneously from a peripheral vein and the CVAD or midline, with catheter cultures positive at least 2 h before peripheral vein BCs, and (ii) the microorganism isolation, which means that the same microorganism must be isolated from the catheter tip culture and peripheral vein BCs. This definition was used to diagnose CRBSI originating from CVADs or midline catheters.

The Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines define the presence of Staphylococcus aureus or Candida spp. in peripheral BCs as a CRBSI, even in the absence of complete microbiological confirmation, due to their low likelihood of contamination and high associated infectious risk. In the case of Candida spp., a single positive peripheral blood culture should prompt consideration of the catheter as the source of infection [19].

CABSI was defined as a bloodstream infection originating from a midline or CVAD, after excluding other causes of infection from a different site [20].

Immunosuppression was determined if at least one of the following conditions occurred: human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, solid organ transplantation (SOT) history of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), autoimmune diseases, and treatment with steroids and other immunosuppressive therapies.

VAD dwell time was defined as the number of days a catheter remains in situ divided by the total LOS, expressed as a proportion, and then classified into 4 categories: low (0–25%); medium to low (26–50%); medium to high (51–75%); high (76–100%).

COVID-19 related pneumonia was defined as a pulmonary infection caused by SARS-CoV-2 - determined by positive antigen nasopharyngeal swab, characterized by clinical, radiologic, and laboratory evidence of lower respiratory tract involvement [21].

Empiric therapy was considered appropriate if it was active against the isolated microorganisms [22]. Ineffective therapy with a higher risk of death for CRBSIs was considered if empirical antibiotic therapy was not initiated, if incorrect empirical therapy was administered, or if incorrect targeted therapy was given for more than 48 h.

Data availability

Demographical data such as age and biological sex were collected. Clinical data included: immunosuppression, SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19-related pneumonia, and cachexia. Clinical decisions were also retrieved (appropriate antibiotic therapy, removal or retention of the catheter after the diagnosis of CRBSI and CABSI). Comorbidities comprised diabetes, any cardiovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, arterial hypertension, chronic neurological disease, immunological disease, hepatic cirrhosis, psychiatric disease, obesity, chronic kidney disease, and active intravenous drug use. Antibiotics’ data for empiric and targeted therapies along with the microbiological identification of the CRBSI pathogen were recorded. VAD data included the type of catheter (CVAD or midline), the number of lumens, whether tunneled or not, the characteristics of the exit site and intraprocedural complications. Moreover, the number of attempts to insert the VAD and any complications that occurred during and after the procedure were retrieved. Outcome data were considered, such as LOS, ICU admission, in-hospital all-cause mortality, and in-hospital CRBSI/CABSI-associated mortality. Microbiology data of the pathogens identified in BCs were collected.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median and 1st, 3rd quartiles (Q1, Q3), and categoric variables as percentages. Differences between patients who were discharged and patients who died during the hospitalization were assessed through statistical tests: the chi-square (or Fisher, where appropriate) test for differences in proportions and the Mann–Whitney test for continuous variables. Statistical significance was reached if p-value < 0.05.

The incidence rate of both in-hospital all-cause and infection-related mortality was calculated as the number of events over the follow-up time per thousand days of hospitalization.

To assess in-hospital all-cause mortality, patients were observed from the hospital admission until the date of death or discharge. For patients admitted to ICU the observation period was censored at the date of transfer.

For infection-related mortality, the observation period of patients with CRBSI/CABSI started from the date of diagnosis of the infection.

Regarding the risk factors of in-hospital all-cause mortality, since patients could experience multiple hospitalizations and CRBSI/CABSI events, a frailty model was performed to assess the risk of in-hospital all-cause mortality to account for unmeasured heterogeneity, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated. The covariates were chosen based on clinical experience, according to the available literature [23–25], and they were: sex, age, comorbidity count, state of immunodeficiency, previous COVID-19 pneumonia, cachexia, and time of catheter positioning. They were all considered potential risk factors associated with mortality. Patients were considered the frailty component.

Regarding the risk factors of in-hospital CRBSI/CABSI-associated mortality, a competing risk model was fitted: in-hospital mortality associated with infection was considered as the event of interest, and in-hospital mortality for other causes as a competing event. For this model, according to clinical relevance and available literature [12, 14, 26], age, sex, infections caused by Candida spp. and/or S. aureus, removal of catheter after the diagnosis, presence of a CVAD, and appropriateness of empirical antibiotic therapy have been evaluated and used as covariates. When no pathogen was identified (in patients with CABSI), this variable could not be assessed, and these patients were therefore excluded from the analysis. It is important to specify that some patients had multiple insertions during the hospitalization and might have also had multiple infections; in this study, they were considered independent events.

Results

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the total sample of 2062 patients undergoing CVAD or midline placement during hospitalization are described in Table 1. Among them, 287 died during hospitalization, with an incidence rate of in-hospital all-cause mortality of 4.41 deaths/1000 patient-days (95% CI: 4.40, 4.43) and a prevalence of 13.9% (95% CI: 12.4%, 15.4%). Of the total cohort, 1062 (51.5%) were female and the median age was 78 years [Q1, Q3: 65, 85]. The most prevalent comorbidities were cardiovascular diseases (49.2%), followed by neurological diseases (36.4%). Non-survivors were significantly older (83 vs. 76 years, p < 0.0001), less frequently female (44.6% vs. 52.6%, p = 0.0139), and had more comorbidities (4 vs. 3, p < 0.0001) than survivors (Table 1).

The patients experienced a total of 2256 hospitalizations, with a median time of VAD insertion (CVAD or midline) of 7.8 days (IQR: 2, 9) from the hospital admission. Hospitalization characteristics and VAD-related data on the total of hospitalizations (n = 2256) of patients who underwent VAD (CVAD or midline) placement are detailed in Table 2.

The median LOS was shorter among non survivors compared to survivors (19 vs. 22 days, p < 0.0001) as was the median duration of catheter dwell time (10 days vs. 14 days, p < 0.0001). Regarding the type of VAD, PICCs were more frequently used in non-survivors (74.3% vs. 65.2%, p < 0.0001), VAD removal after diagnosed CRBSI/CABSI occurred less frequently in non-survivors (58.4% vs. 81.6%, p < 0.0001), and ICU admission rates were similar in both groups (3.0% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.7092). In total, 163 (7.2%) hospitalizations were burdened by CRBSI/CABSI (Table 2).

Risk factors of in-hospital all-cause mortality are shown in Fig. 1. The placement of more than 1 VAD (CVADs or midline) was independently associated with a lower risk of death (HR: 0.54, 95%, CI 0.35, 0.83), as was female sex (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.55, 0.92). Conversely, a comorbidity count greater than 4 (HR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.05, 2.30), COVID-19-related pneumonia (HR: 2.58, 95% CI: 1.95, 3.43), and cachexia (HR: 2.59, 95% CI: 1.71, 3.92) were significantly associated with increased risk of death.

Patients who experienced either CRBSI/CABSI were 159 and are described in Table S1 of the Supplementary materials. Among the total 159 patients, 82 (51.6%) were female, and the median age was 76 years [Q1, Q3: 63.5, 84.0]. Arterial hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity (63.5%), and 9 patients had previously experienced CRBSI/CABSI. Twenty patients (12.6%, 95% CI: 7.4%, 17.7%) died because of CRBSI/CABSI, and 21 for other causes. The incidence rate of infection-related mortality was 5.24 (95% CI: 5.17, 5.31) deaths/1000 patient-days. The detailed data of the total of hospitalizations where CRBSI/CABSI occurred (n = 163), with their microbiological characteristics, are shown in Table 3.

The median LOS was longer in patients who did not die during hospitalization (45 days [Q1, Q3: 29, 64]), compared to deaths, both CRBSI/CABSI-related (28 days [Q1, Q3: 19, 70.2]) and other causes-related deaths (30.5 days [Q1, Q3: 18.2, 48.2]), with statistical significance (p = 0.0494). ICU admission during hospitalization significantly differed between groups: 10.0% among CRBSI/CABSI-related deaths, 4.5% among deaths from other causes and 4.1% among CRBSI/CABSI survivors (p < 0.0001).

Regarding CVAD and midline management, deferred catheter removal with systemic antibiotics alone was more frequent in hospitalizations with CRBSI/CABSI-related deaths (44.4% vs. 33.3% and 26.7%), even if the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.2865) whereas prompt removal without replacement was more frequently performed in all CRBSI/CABSI population compared to CRBSI/CABSI related deaths (22.6% vs. 11.1%, p = 0.3881).

Appropriate antibiotic therapy was administered to more than half of patients and did not significantly differ among groups (p = 0.0988).

Regarding the microbiological analysis, the most frequently isolated pathogens were coagulase-negative Staphylococci (CoNS) (79, 48.5%), 25% among CRBSI/CABSI related deaths, 40.9% among deaths for other causes and 53.7% among the hospitalizations where patients did not die, with statistical significance (p = 0.044). Enterococcus spp. accounted for 22.1% of isolates and was the most frequent pathogen in CRBSI/CABSI-related deaths (40% vs. 22.7% vs. 19%, p = 0.1107) while Candida spp. was more frequent in deaths from other causes (22.7% vs. 20% vs. 12.4%, p = 0.3521), though both not statistically significant.

In 10 patients experiencing 11 CABSI events, no microbiological pathogen was identified, preventing assessment of the appropriateness of antimicrobial therapy.

Discussion

This study investigated the incidence and risk factors associated with in-hospital all-cause mortality in non-ICU patients with VADs (CVADs and midlines) and incidence and risk factors of infection-related mortality in patients with CRBSI/CABSI. In line with a systematic review on the impact of CRBSIs on mortality and increased LOS [27], our findings highlight the significant burden of mortality in these patient populations and underscore the importance of underlying comorbidities, timely CVAD or midline management, and appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

The overall in-hospital mortality rate among patients with VADs (CVADs or midlines) in our non-ICU cohort was 13.9%, with an incidence rate of 4.41 deaths per 1000 patient-days, while among patients diagnosed with CRBSI/CABSI, 12.6% died due to the infection, with an incidence rate of 5.24 deaths per 1000 patient-days. This latter mortality rate is consistent with those previously reported for CRBSI, which can be as high as 18% in non-ICU patients [8, 9]. However, it is essential to note that data on all-cause mortality in patients with CVADs or midlines are limited, which emphasizes the need for comprehensive risk assessment and management in patients receiving VADs, and not only in those with CRBSI/CABSI.

Our analysis identified several independent risk factors for increased in-hospital all-cause mortality. Elderly and higher comorbidity burden (more than 4 comorbidities) were significant predictors of death, which aligns with well-established epidemiological data showing increased mortality risk in older patients with multiple comorbidities [23, 28].

Similarly, cachexia showed a significant association with increased mortality, reinforcing its role as a marker of severe underlying disease [25]. Not surprisingly, COVID-19 pneumonia emerged as a particularly strong independent risk factor for in-hospital all-cause mortality. This highlights the impact of both acute SARS-CoV-2 infection itself [29, 30], and that of concurrent viral infections on poor prognosis of hospitalized patients [31]. Conversely, the finding that multiple VADs placements were protective is intriguing and counter-intuitive at first glance and requires deeper exploration in future studies. This might suggest that patients requiring multiple VADs are under closer clinical surveillance, receive more intensive care management, or that the re-insertion of a new VAD might be a proactive measure to prevent complications from a failing or potentially infected existing line. Alternatively, it could be related to the specific reasons for multiple placements, which might select for a subgroup of patients with better overall health status or more acute, manageable conditions rather than chronic deterioration. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have yet supported these hypotheses. On the contrary, in contrast to our findings, some studies suggest minimizing the number of VAD placements whenever possible, as well as carefully selecting the insertion site with the lowest risk of infection [32].

Regarding the factors associated to infection-related mortality in patients with CRBSI/CABSI, our study showed that prompt catheter removal and appropriate antibiotic therapy were independently associated with significantly lower infection-related mortality. This strongly supports current guidelines and clinical practices emphasizing aggressive management of CRBSI/CABSI [19, 33]. In particular, the importance of catheter removal is remarked by several studies, showing retention of infected catheters being associated with worse outcomes [16]. In different settings, including oncology outpatients [34], patients receiving parenteral nutrition [35], and those in hemodialysis units, catheter retention with lock-therapy combined with a short period of “watchful waiting” to assess clinical and microbiologic response is a supported strategy for selected, clinically stable patients and does not necessarily require immediate catheter removal in all CRBSI/CABSI episodes. These strategies help to avoid unnecessary catheter removal and could be considered in clinical practice when catheter retention is necessary due to poor venous access or the need to continue intravenous therapy [36].

Regarding microbiological characteristics, CoNS were the most frequently isolated pathogens overall but were significantly less frequent among CRBSI/CABSI-related deaths compared to survivors. This suggests that while CoNS are common causes of CRBSI/CABSI [37, 38], infections with other pathogens may carry a higher mortality risk. Enterococcus spp. was more frequently isolated in CRBSI/CABSI-related deaths, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. This trend, however, is concerning, as Enterococcus faecium, in particular, can be challenging to treat and is associated with severe outcomes [39]. Infections with Candida spp. were also noted more frequently in deaths from other causes, aligning with the understanding that candidemia is a serious complication often associated with underlying severe illness and mortality [6]. However, in contrast with previous findings [9], neither Candida spp. nor Staphylococcus aureus CRBSI/CABSI were independently associated with mortality in our cohort. This discrepancy might be explained by the limited sample size of this specific population.

A key strength of our study is its focus on a substantial cohort of non-ICU hospitalized patients, an area where data on CVAD or midline-related mortality and its risk factors remain limited. The monocentric design, conducted at a hospital with a dedicated VAT and standardized VAD insertion protocols, potentially reduces variability in catheters management and data collection. The use of frailty and competing risk models strengthened our statistical analysis by accounting for complex factors like multiple hospitalizations and competing causes of death. However, this study also has limitations. Its retrospective and monocentric nature means the findings might not be fully generalizable to other healthcare settings or populations with different VAD practices or patient demographics. While we accounted for multiple VAD placements and infection events by considering them independent, this approach might not fully capture the cumulative effect of recurrent VAD use on patient outcomes. The data regarding VAD removal practices, while suggestive, were not statistically significant in some comparisons, possibly due to sample size or the complexity of clinical decision-making surrounding catheter retention.

Conclusions

Our study provides valuable insights into the risk factors associated with mortality in non-ICU hospitalized patients with CVADs or midlines, particularly those complicated by CRBSI/CABSI. Advanced age, high comorbidity burden, COVID-19-related pneumonia and cachexia are significant predictors of all-cause mortality. For CRBSI/CABSI, prompt catheter removal and appropriate antibiotic therapy are critical interventions independently associated with reduced infection-related mortality. These findings underscore the importance of robust VAD management protocols and tailored patient care strategies to improve outcomes in this vulnerable patient population.

Future multicenter studies with prospective design are warranted to expand the available evidence and to further investigate risk factors, etiology, and mortality among patients with VADs in non-ICU settings.
