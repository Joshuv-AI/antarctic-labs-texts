Higher-Order Corrections to Scrambling Dynamics in Brownian Spin SYK Models
Tingfei Li, Miao Wang, Jianghui Yu

Abstract
We investigate operator growth in a Brownian spin Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model with random all-to-all interactions, focusing on the full operator-size distribution. For Hamiltonians containing interactions of order two up to L L , we derive a closed master equation for the Pauli-string expansion coefficients and recast their dynamics into a generating-function formulation suitable for the large- N N limit. This approach allows us to diagonalize the leading-order evolution operator explicitly and obtain exact solutions for arbitrary initial operator distributions, including the effects of decoherence. Going beyond leading order, we develop a systematic 1 / N 1/N expansion that captures higher-order corrections to the operator-size dynamics and the late-time behavior. Our results demonstrate that higher-order effects play a crucial role in operator scrambling and that the full operator-size distribution provides a more refined probe of quantum chaos in Brownian and open quantum systems.

Main results

At leading order, we find the eigenfunctions obey a power-law structure

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\left(G_{1}^{(0)}(x)\right)^{k}.

\displaystyle G^{(n)}_{k}(x)=\widehat{O}^{(n)}_{x}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\,.

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}^{(n)}(x,t)=\widehat{D}_{x}^{(n)}\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x_{t})+\widehat{K}_{x}^{(n)}\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)\big|_{x\to x_{t}}

\displaystyle G_{1}^{(0)}(x_{t})=e^{\lambda_{1}^{(0)}t}G_{1}^{(0)}(x)\,.

Excellent agreement with numerical simulations is found, especially in the late-time regime, confirming that higher-order corrections are essential for capturing the complete temporal evolution of operator growth within the dilute limit. More broadly, our results provide a systematic and physically transparent framework for understanding scrambling dynamics in Brownian circuit models in the presence of experimental imperfections. Model and Setup

2.1
Brownian spin SYK model and operator dynamics

In this work, we study operator growth in a generalized Brownian spin model, which extends the Brownian spin SYK–type constructions introduced in
Nadie2025
;
Lashkari_2013
;
Xu_2025
. The system consists of
N
qubits evolving under a time-dependent, stochastic Hamiltonian with all-to-all interactions. We allow for interactions of arbitrary order, including mixed interaction types, so that the Hamiltonian takes the schematic form

\displaystyle H=\sum_{n=2}^{L}\sum_{A_{n}}J_{A_{n}}(t)O_{A_{n}}\,,

(10)

where
O_{A_{n}}
denotes an
n
-body operator acting on the subset of sites
A_{n}
. Here
A_{n}=\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{n}\}
and

\displaystyle O_{A_{n}}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{j_{k}},\sigma_{j_{k}}\in\{X,Y,Z\}\,.

(11)

The couplings
J_{A_{n}}(t)
are independent Gaussian random variables with

\mathbb{E}(J_{A_{n}}(t)J_{A_{m}^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}))=\delta_{A_{n}A_{m}^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\mu_{n}^{2}\,.

(12)

Given a realization of the Hamiltonian, an operator
O(t)
evolves in the Heisenberg picture as

\displaystyle O(t)=e^{iHt}O(0)e^{-iHt}\,.

(13)

Because the dynamics are Brownian, the physical observables of interest must be obtained by averaging over an ensemble of Hamiltonian realizations. In this work, we concentrate on ensemble-averaged quantities that characterize operator growth and information scrambling.

Here, we temporarily disregard decoherence and experimental imperfections, so a sample realization of the model is equivalent to a conventional quantum mechanical system. Under time evolution, a simple operator can grow increasingly complex. This growth can be quantified using measures such as
Krylov complexity
and
operator size
, which provide effective diagnostics for quantum chaos. While Krylov complexity offers a universal framework for general quantum systems, the operator size and its distribution are particularly well-suited for systems of qubits. In this paper, we focus on the dynamics of the operator size distribution. A convenient basis for describing operator dynamics is provided by Pauli strings, since any operator can be expanded in this basis. A
Pauli string
of length
N
is a tensor product of
N
operators, where each operator is chosen from the four
2\times 2
Hermitian basis matrices: the identity matrix
I
and the three Pauli matrices
X
,
Y
, and
Z
:

\displaystyle P=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i},\quad\text{where }\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}\,.

(14)

The
weight

\mathsf{w}(P)
of a Pauli string is defined as the number of non-identity operators (
X,Y,
or
Z
) appearing in its tensor product. Then we can expand any operator in the system into Pauli string basis

\displaystyle O(t)=\sum_{P}c_{P}(t)P,~c_{P}(t)=(P|O(t))\,,

(15)

where we have defined the inner product of two operators

\displaystyle(O_{1}|O_{2})\equiv{\text{Tr}(O_{1}^{\dagger}O_{2})\over\text{Tr}{1}}\,.

(16)

Here, we define the
average weight
of the operator as a measure of complexity, which is given by:

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(O(t))\equiv\sum_{P}w_{P}\left|c_{P}(t)\right|^{2}=\sum_{w=1}^{\infty}wb^{\text{clean}}_{w}

(17)

where we collect the contribution of Pauli strings with the same weight:

\displaystyle b^{\text{clean}}_{w}=\sum_{\mathsf{w}(P)=w}|c_{P}(t)|^{2}\,.

(18)

Under unitary evolution, this distribution is normalized
\sum_{w}b^{\text{clean}}_{w}(t)=1
, reflecting the preservation of the operator’s trace.

2.2
Construct ROTOC with operator size distribution
b_{w}

A major motivation for studying Brownian circuit models comes from their relevance to experimental probes of quantum scrambling. In realistic experiments,

Let’s consider the case where the forward and backward time evolution are governed by distinct Hamiltonians, denoted as

(same as Eq. (

\displaystyle H=\sum_{n=2}^{L}\sum_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})=n}J_{A}(t)O_{A}\;,\ \ \mathbb{E}(J_{A}(t)J_{B}(t^{\prime}))=\delta_{AB}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\mu_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})}^{2}\,;

(19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}=\sum_{n=2}^{L}\sum_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})=n}\widetilde{J}_{A}(t)O_{A}\;,\ \ \mathbb{E}(\widetilde{J}_{A}(t)\widetilde{J}_{B}(t^{\prime}))=\delta_{AB}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\mu_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})}^{2}\,;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(J_{A}(t)\widetilde{J}_{B}(t^{\prime}))=r\delta_{AB}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\mu_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})}^{2}\,.

The correlation between

is characterized by the parameter

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}(t)=e^{i\widetilde{H}t}O(0)e^{-i\widetilde{H}t}\,.

(20)

In this paper, since we focus on ensemble-averaged dynamics and incorporate experimental imperfections, we define
b_{w}
as

\displaystyle b_{w}\equiv\sum_{\mathsf{w}(P)=w}\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{c}_{P}(t)c_{P}(t))

(21)

where

and

denote the Pauli-string coefficients of the forward- and backward-evolved operators, respectively, and
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the ensemble average. The quantities

encode the full information content of operator growth in the presence of imperfections.

We now incorporate

\kappa

\displaystyle c_{w}={b_{w}\over\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}}\;,\;\langle f\rangle_{c}\equiv\sum_{w}f(w)c_{w}\,.

The dressed echo and OTOC is defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{W}(t)=\left\langle\widetilde{W}(t)W(t)\right\rangle,~\widetilde{C}_{W,V}(t)=\left\langle\left[\widetilde{W}(t),V\right]^{\dagger}\Big[W(t),V\Big]\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\text{Echo}_{W}=\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{C}_{W}(t)\right]=4\sum_{w}b_{w},~\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{C}_{W,V}(t)\right]=\sum_{P|\{P,V\}=0}4\mathbb{E}\left[c_{P}(t)\widetilde{c}_{P}(t)\right]\,.

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{C}_{W,V}(t)\right]=\sum_{w}\frac{4C_{w}^{\mathsf{w}(V)}}{N_{w}}b_{w}(t)\,.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{C}_{W,V}(t)\right]\Big|_{\mathsf{w}(V)=1}=\sum_{w}\frac{8w}{3N}b_{w}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{C}_{W,V}(t)\right]\Big|_{\mathsf{w}(V)=2}=\sum_{w}\frac{16w(3N-2w-1)}{9(N-1)N}b_{w}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{C}_{W,V}(t)\right]\Big|_{\mathsf{w}(V)=3}=\sum_{w}\frac{8w\left(9N^{2}-3N(4w+5)+4w^{2}+12w+2\right)}{9(N-2)(N-1)N}b_{w}\,.

3
Equation for operator size distribution

We now turn to the dynamical description of operator growth. As emphasized in the previous section, the central quantity controlling both operator size and dressed OTOC is the distribution
b_{w}(t)
of Pauli-string weights.

In this section, we derive the time evolution equation for
b_{w}
in the Brownian spin model. Our approach is based on a generating-function formulation, which allows us to organize the dynamics systematically and to incorporate higher-order corrections within the dilute limit. This framework avoids the need to diagonalize large transition matrices and provides direct access to perturbative corrections beyond leading order. Hence it will make transparent both the leading order structure and the mechanisms by which subleading corrections influence late-time behavior.

3.1
Time evolution of
b_{w}

We now derive the time evolution equation for the operator weight distribution

. To this end, we analyze the infinitesimal change of Pauli-string coefficients

over a short time interval

under Brownian Hamiltonian evolution, and then perform an ensemble average to obtain a closed dynamical equation for

.

Expanding the Heisenberg evolution operators to second order in

\displaystyle\delta O(t)

\displaystyle=e^{iH_{t}\delta t}O(t)e^{-iH_{t}\delta t}-O(t)-\kappa\sum_{P}\mathsf{w}_{P}c_{P}(t)P\delta t

\displaystyle=i[H,O(t)]\delta t+\left(HO(t)H-\frac{1}{2}\{H^{2},O(t)\}\right)\delta t^{2}-\kappa\sum_{P}\mathsf{w}_{P}c_{P}(t)P\delta t+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{3})\,,

(32)

so that

\displaystyle\delta c_{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(P\bigg|i[H,O(t)]\delta t+\left(HO(t)H-\frac{1}{2}\{H^{2},O(t)\}\right)\delta t^{2}\right)-\kappa\sum_{P}\mathsf{w}_{P}c_{P}(t)\delta t\;.

(33)

We use

, and
(A|B)
to represent the commutator, anticommutator, and the operator inner product of operators
A
and
B

19

are Gaussian and uncorrelated in time, only terms quadratic in the Hamiltonian survive in the ensemble average. As a result, contributions linear in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(c_{P}(t+\delta t)\widetilde{c}_{P}(t+\delta t)-c_{P}(t)\widetilde{c}_{P}(t)\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(c_{P}\delta\widetilde{c}_{P}+\widetilde{c}_{P}\delta c_{P}+\delta c_{P}\delta\widetilde{c}_{P}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{A,Q}\bigg[\mu_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(c_{P}\widetilde{c}_{Q}+\widetilde{c}_{P}c_{Q}\right)\left(P\bigg|\left(O_{A}QO_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\{O_{A}^{2},O(t)\}\right)\right)\right]\delta t

\displaystyle-r\mu_{\mathsf{w}(O_{A})}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{Q}\widetilde{c}_{Q}(P|[O_{A},Q])^{2}\right]\delta t\bigg]-2\kappa\sum_{P}\mathsf{w}_{P}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{P}\widetilde{c}_{P}\right]\delta t\,.

Since any two Pauli strings either commute or anticommute, only operators

To ensure the anticommutation relation

\displaystyle\frac{db_{w}}{dt}=\sum_{n=2}^{L}\,4\mu_{n}^{2}\left[\sum_{p\ \text{odd},p+m\leq n}rC_{n,p,m}^{w+2m+p-n}\,b_{w+2m+p-n}-C_{n}^{w}b_{w}\right]-2\kappa wb_{w}\,.

The evolution can be written in matrix form as
\frac{db_{w}}{dt}=\sum_{w^{\prime}=1}^{N}M_{ww^{\prime}}b_{w^{\prime}}
. Direct diagonalization of the

is generally intractable. Following the approach outlined in the introduction, we circumvent this difficulty by formally extending

to an infinite-dimensional matrix

, while retaining explicit

. We then define an approximate generating function

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}(x,t)=X_{\infty}^{T}.e^{M_{\infty}t}\boldsymbol{b}_{\infty}(0),~X_{\infty}\equiv[x,x^{2},\ldots]\,.

(41)

Replacing each power

in Eq. (

\mathsf{G}(x,t)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathsf{G}(x,t)=\mathcal{M}_{x}\mathsf{G}(x,t)\,.

(42)

We then expand all quantities in powers of
1/N
; for example, the generating function is written as
\mathsf{G}(x,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{G}^{(k)}(x,t)N^{-k}
. The eigenvalue problem is subsequently solved order by order using perturbation theory:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{x}G_{k}(x,t)=\lambda_{k}G_{k}(x,t)\,.

(43)

At leading order in
1/N
within the dilute limit, the evolution matrix becomes strictly lower triangular, enabling an explicit spectral solution. We now analyze this limit and solve the dynamics using the generating-function method.

3.2
Leading order solution

We now specialize to the dilute limit, where the operator-weight distribution is concentrated at weights
w\ll N
. In this regime, the factor
C^{w}_{n,p,m}
scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{n-m-p})
, so the dominant contributions come from terms with the smallest possible
p
and
m
. Because anticommutation requires
p
to be odd, the leading contribution corresponds to
p=1
and
m=0
. Retaining only this
C^{w}_{n,1,0}
term gives

\displaystyle\frac{db_{w}}{dt}=4\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu_{n}^{2}[rC_{n,1,0}^{w-(n-1)}b_{w-(n-1)}-C^{w}_{n,1,0}b_{w}]-2\kappa wb_{w}+\mathcal{O}(1/N)\;.

(44)

In the large-
N
limit, we scale the couplings as
\mu_{n}^{2}=\frac{a_{n}(n-1)!}{4\times 3^{\,n-1}N^{\,n-1}}
so that the leading-order equation in the dilute limit becomes

\displaystyle\frac{db_{w}}{dt}=-2w\bigl(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa\bigr)b_{w}+2r\sum_{n=2}^{L}\bigl[w-(n-1)\bigr]a_{n}b_{w-(n-1)}+\mathcal{O}(1/N)

(45)

where
a_{\Sigma}=\sum_{n=2}^{L}a_{n}
. Writing the evolution in matrix form

\displaystyle\frac{db_{w}}{dt}=\sum_{w^{\prime}=1}^{N}M^{(0)}_{ww^{\prime}}b_{w^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(1/N)\,,

(46)

we find that the resulting generator
M^{(0)}
is strictly lower triangular in the weight basis. Consequently, its spectrum is given directly by the diagonal entries, yielding eigenvalues

\lambda_{l}^{(0)}=-2l\,(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa),\qquad l=1,2,3,\dots.

(47)

To facilitate the generating-function analysis, we extend the evolution matrix
M^{(0)}
to infinite dimensions by formally letting the weight index
w\to\infty
, obtaining
M_{\infty}^{(0)}
. Denoting the
k
-th eigenvector of this extended matrix by
{v}_{k}
, we define its generating function as

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}v_{k;j}\,x^{j}

(48)

where
v_{k;j}
denotes the
j
-th component of
{v}_{k}
. The equation for
G^{(0)}_{k}(x)
follows from the eigenvector equation for
{v}_{k}
,

-(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)x\partial_{x}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)+r\sum_{n=2}^{L}a_{n}x^{n}\partial_{x}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=-k(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\,.

(49)

Its solution is

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=A_{k}\exp\!\left(\int_{1}^{x}ds\,\frac{(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)k}{(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)s-r\sum_{n=2}^{L}a_{n}s^{n}}\right)=\bigl(G_{1}^{(0)}(x)\bigr)^{k}\,.

(50)

Analogous to an eigenvector decomposition, the generating function of the initial distribution can be expanded in the eigenbasis
\{G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}_{\mathrm{init}}(x)\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}(0)x^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}G_{j}^{(0)}(x)\,.

(51)

Consequently, the time-evolved generating function at leading order is

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}^{(0)}(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}(t)x^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}e^{-2j(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)t}c_{j}G_{j}^{(0)}(x)\,.

(52)

The expansion coefficients
c_{l}
are determined via the generating function of the left eigenvectors. Because
M^{(0)}_{\infty}
is non-Hermitian, its left and right eigenvectors are distinct. Denoting the
k
-th left eigenvector by
{w}_{k}
, it satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{i}w_{k;i}M_{ij}^{(0)}=\lambda_{k}w_{k;j}\,.

(53)

Since
M_{\infty}^{(0)}
is lower triangular,
{w}_{k}
has non-zero entries only for indices
i\leq k
. Proceeding as before, we define the left-eigenvector generating function

\displaystyle W_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}w_{k;j}x^{j}\,.

(54)

Its governing equation reads

\displaystyle-x(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)\frac{d}{dx}W^{(0)}_{k}+r\sum_{n=2}^{L}a_{n}x\frac{d}{dx}\!\left(\frac{W_{k}^{(0)}}{x^{\,n-1}}-\frac{W_{k}^{{(0)}\leq n-2}}{x^{\,n-1}}\right)=k(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)W_{k}^{(0)}

(55)

where
W_{k}^{{(0)}\leq m}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{k;j}x^{j}
. The function
W_{k}^{(0)}(x)
is typically a polynomial of finite degree.
As an alternative to solving the differential equation directly,
W_{k}^{(0)}(x)
can be constructed recursively from the coefficients
w_{k;j}
.
The bi-orthogonality condition can then be imposed as

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{j}^{(0)}|G_{k}^{(0)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{z=0}\frac{\text{d}z}{z}\,W^{(0)*}_{j}(1/z)\,G_{k}^{(0)}(z)=\delta_{jk}

(56)

where
W^{(0)*}_{j}(x)
denotes complex conjugation of every parameter in the function except the formal variable
x
. Here the bracket on the left-hand side denotes a bi-orthogonal pairing between left and right generating functions, defined via a contour integral. This biorthogonal normalization ensures a complete spectral decomposition despite the non-Hermitian nature of
M^{(0)}_{\infty}
.
In principle, the expansion coefficients
c_{l}
can be obtained from

\displaystyle c_{j}=\left\langle W_{j}^{(0)}|G_{\text{init}}\right\rangle\,.

(57)

However, a more direct approach is to introduce a time-dependent variable
x_{t}(x,t)
defined implicitly by

\displaystyle G_{1}^{(0)}(x_{t})=G_{1}^{(0)}(x)\,e^{-2(a_{\Sigma}+\kappa)t}\,.

(58)

With this definition, the time evolution of the generating function takes the simple form

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}^{(0)}(x,t)=\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x_{t})\,.

(59)

This framework holds for any initial size distribution, showing that the generating function approach provides a powerful method for deriving analytical results for arbitrary initial conditions and interaction types. The main challenge lies in obtaining an explicit expression for
x_{t}
by solving Eq. (
58
). For many scenarios, such as systems with mixed two body and three body interactions, an exact analytical expression for
x_{t}
may not be available. Nevertheless, Eq. (
58
) can always be treated as a definition of
x_{t}
, which remains useful for numerical computations.

Once the generating function
\mathsf{G}^{(0)}(x,t)
is determined, physical observables follow directly. For example,
\langle w\rangle_{c}
used in the calculation of OTOC can be easily obtained as

\displaystyle\langle w\rangle_{c}=\frac{\sum_{j}wb_{w}(t)x^{w}|_{x=1}}{\sum_{w}b_{w}(t)}=\frac{\partial_{x}\mathsf{G}^{(0)}(x,t)}{\mathsf{G}^{(0)}(x,t)}\bigg|_{x=1}+\mathcal{O}(1/N)\;.

(60)

3.3
Two-body and three-body interactions

In this subsection, we analyze the dilute-limit solution for systems with two-body and three-body interactions.

Two-body interactions

We begin with the two-body case, which has been studied previously in
Nadie2025
, and use it as a benchmark to validate our formalism and clarify the structure of the generating-function approach. For
L=2
, the eigenvalue equation for the generating function reduces to

\displaystyle-2(a_{2}+\kappa)x\partial_{x}G^{(0)}_{k}(x)+2ra_{2}x^{2}\partial_{x}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\lambda_{k}^{(0)}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\;.

(61)

Requiring the solution to admit a power-series expansion in positive integer powers of
x
enforces the quantization condition

\lambda_{k}^{(0)}=-2k(a_{2}+\kappa)\;,\;k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\;,

(62)

in agreement with the general triangular structure identified in Section
3.2
. We fix the normalization by setting the coefficient off
x^{k}
in the expansion of the
k
-th eigenfunction to unity. The resulting eigenfunctions take the simple form

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\frac{x^{k}}{(1-r_{\text{eff}}x)^{k}}\;,\;\ r_{\text{eff}}\equiv\frac{a_{2}r}{a_{2}+\kappa}\;.

(63)

Notice that the generating functions factorize as

G^{(0)}_{k}(x)=(G_{1}^{(0)}(x))^{k}\;,

(64)

a property that greatly simplifies the time evolution and, as we show below, extends to more general interaction structures.
The time-dependent generating function becomes

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}(x,t)=\sum_{l}c_{l}G_{1}(x_{t})^{l}=G_{\text{init}}(x_{t})\;,x_{t}=\frac{xe^{-2(a_{2}+\kappa)t}}{1-r_{\mathsf{eff}}x(1-e^{-2(a_{2}+\kappa)t})}\;.

(65)

which is valid for arbitrary initial distributions.
For an operator with an initial distribution
b_{w}(0)=\delta_{w,w_{0}}
, we have
\mathsf{G}(x,t)=x^{w_{0}}_{t}
. Hence

\displaystyle\left\langle w\right\rangle_{c}=\left.\frac{\partial_{x}\mathsf{G}(x,t)}{\mathsf{G}(x,t)}\right|_{x=1}=\frac{w_{0}}{1-r_{\text{eff}}+r_{\text{eff}}e^{-2(a_{2}+\kappa)t}}\;,

(66)

At late times,

\displaystyle\left\langle w\right\rangle_{c}|_{t\to\infty}=w_{0}/(1-r_{\text{eff}})\;.

(67)

This solution reproduces the known leading-order behavior in
Nadie2025
.

Three-body interactions

We now turn to systems with purely three-body interactions. Unlike the two-body case, three-body interactions induce operator weight changes in steps of
\pm 2
, leading to qualitatively new structural features in the dynamics even at leading order.
The generating function for the
k
-th eigenvector is found to be

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-r_{\text{eff}}x^{2}}}\right)^{k}\,,

(68)

where we have introduced the effective imperfection parameter
r_{\text{eff}}=\frac{a_{3}r}{a_{3}+\kappa}
.
Correspondingly, the flow of the generating-function argument is given by

\displaystyle x_{t}(x,t)=\frac{xe^{-2t(a_{3}+\kappa)}}{\sqrt{1+r_{\text{eff}}\,x^{2}(e^{-4t(a_{3}+\kappa)}-1)}}\,.

(69)

We note that the expansion of
x_{t}
in
x
contains only odd powers of
x
, which reflects the fact that under three-body interactions, the change in operator weight
b_{w}
occurs in steps of
\pm 2
. More specifically, considering an initial operator with weight
w_{0}
, its generating function is simply given by
x_{t}^{w_{0}}
. Expanding this reveals that the powers of
x
share the same parity as
w_{0}
. The leading order calculation gives the late-time behavior

\displaystyle\langle w\rangle_{c}|_{t\to\infty}=w_{0}/(1-r_{\text{eff}})\,.

(70)

This result is consistent with that obtained in two-body interactions.

4
Higher-order effects: Two-body interactions

As noted in the previous section, the evolution matrix governing the operator weight distribution is lower triangular at leading order in
N
. As a result, the
k
-th eigenvector has support only on components
v_{k,j}
with
j\geq k
, and its eigenvalue is given by
\lambda_{k}^{(0)}=-2k(\kappa+a)
. Consequently, the late-time dynamics predicted at leading order is controlled solely by the smallest nonzero eigenvalue present in the initial state.

However, this prediction is generically violated in numerical simulations at finite
N
. Although
1/N
corrections are parametrically small, they qualitatively alter the late-time dynamics by enabling population transfer between eigenmodes that are decoupled at leading order. At sufficiently long times, these higher-order effects dominate and determine the true asymptotic behavior. Accurately capturing the late-time operator growth therefore requires a systematic inclusion of
1/N
corrections.

4.1
First order perturbation

We now treat the
1/N
terms as a perturbation to the leading order generator and compute the resulting corrections to both the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The full evolution equation of
b_{w}
turns to be

\displaystyle\frac{db_{w}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2w\left((w-1)+3(N-w)\right)}{3N}b_{w}-2w\kappa b_{w}

(71)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+r\left[\frac{2(N-w+1)(w-1)}{N}b_{w-1}+\frac{2w(w+1)}{3N}b_{w+1}\right].

For notational simplicity, we set
a_{2}=1
in the following. The general case can be recovered by replacement
t\to a_{2}t
and
\kappa\to\kappa/a_{2}
. We now recast this equation in terms of the generating function
\mathsf{G}(x,t)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathsf{G}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\left(2rx^{2}\partial_{x}-2(\kappa+1)x\partial_{x}\right)\mathsf{G}(x,t)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{3N}\left(-3r\left(x^{3}\partial_{x}^{2}+x^{2}\partial_{x}\right)+rx\partial_{x}^{2}+2x^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+3x\partial_{x}\right)\mathsf{G}(x,t)\;.

(72)

We denote it as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathsf{G}(x,t)=\left[\widehat{A}_{0}+{\widehat{A}_{1}\over N}\right]\mathsf{G}(x,t)\;.

(73)

We now employ perturbation theory to compute the corrections to the eigenvector generating function and the eigenvalues. Let
G_{k}^{(0)}(x)
and
\lambda_{k}^{(0)}
denote the results obtained in the previous chapter. Let
G_{k}^{(n)}(x)
and
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
denote the

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{0}G_{k}^{(1)}(x)+\widehat{A}_{1}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\lambda_{k}^{(1)}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)+\lambda_{k}^{(0)}G_{k}^{(1)}(x)\,.

(74)

Solving the equation and imposing the condition
G_{k}^{(1)}(0)=0
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k\left(\kappa+k\left(2\kappa+3r^{2}+2\right)-r^{2}+1\right)}{3(\kappa+1)}\equiv B^{(1)}_{2}k^{2}+B^{(1)}_{1}k\;,

(75)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(1)}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k(1+\kappa)^{k}x^{k-1}}{6(\kappa+1)(\kappa-rx+1)^{k+2}}\bigg[2(3k-1)r^{3}x^{2}-3(\kappa+1)(3k-1)r^{2}x

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+2(\kappa+1)r\left(-\kappa+k\left(\kappa+2x^{2}+1\right)+(3\kappa+4)x^{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+(\kappa+1)^{2}x(-3\kappa+3(\kappa-1)k-7)\bigg]+C_{k}^{(1)}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)

where the constant

reflects a freedom in the normalization of eigenvectors and does not affect physical observables. Similar to the zeroth-order calculation, self-consistency yields the result for
\lambda_{k}^{(1)}
. We can impose
\left\langle W_{k}^{(0)}|G_{k}^{(1)}\right\rangle=0
to determine

. It is easy to find

\displaystyle W_{k}^{(0)}(x)=x^{k}(1-r_{\text{eff}}/x)^{k-1}\,.

(76)

Direct calculation gives

\displaystyle C_{k}^{(1)}=-\frac{k\left(3\kappa^{2}+10\kappa+3k\left(-\kappa^{2}+r^{2}+1\right)+3r^{2}+7\right)}{6(\kappa+1)^{2}}\equiv R_{1}^{(1)}k+R_{2}^{(1)}k^{2}\;,

(77)

where

\displaystyle R_{1}^{(1)}=-\frac{3\kappa^{2}+10\kappa+3r^{2}+7}{6(\kappa+1)^{2}}\;,\;R_{2}^{(1)}=-\frac{-\kappa^{2}+r^{2}+1}{2(\kappa+1)^{2}}\;.

(78)

Now we want to find an operator
\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}
, such that
\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)=G_{k}^{(1)}(x)

, one can finally find

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(1)}(x)=\left(f(x)\partial_{x}+g(x)\partial_{x}^{2}+R_{2}^{(1)}\frac{\widehat{A}^{2}_{0}}{\left(\lambda_{1}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}+R_{1}^{(1)}\frac{\widehat{A}_{0}}{\lambda_{1}^{(0)}}\right)G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\equiv\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\;,

(79)

with

\displaystyle f(x)=h_{1}x+h_{2}x^{2}+h_{3}x^{3},g(x)=s_{1}x+s_{2}x^{2}+s_{3}x^{3}+s_{4}x^{4}\;,

(80)

\displaystyle h_{1}=\frac{2r^{2}}{3(\kappa+1)^{2}},h_{2}=-\frac{(3\kappa-4)r}{3(\kappa+1)^{2}},h_{3}=-\frac{r\left(3r^{3}-3(\kappa-1)(\kappa+1)r\right)}{3(\kappa+1)^{4}}\;,

\displaystyle s_{1}=-\frac{r}{3\kappa+3},s_{2}=\frac{r^{2}}{(\kappa+1)^{2}},s_{3}=-\frac{(3\kappa-1)r}{3(\kappa+1)^{2}},s_{4}=-\frac{r^{2}\left(-\kappa^{2}+r^{2}+1\right)}{2(\kappa+1)^{4}}\;.

(81)

By applying the same approach, we can derive an operator
\widehat{\Lambda}_{x}^{(1)}
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{x}^{(1)}G_{k}^{0}(x)=\lambda_{k}^{(1)}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\;.

(82)

The expansion coefficients is calculated by
c_{l}=\left\langle W_{l}|\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}\right\rangle
.
We now expand
\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)
to first order in
1/N
and we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l}\left(c_{l}^{(0)}G^{(0)}_{l}(x)+\frac{1}{N}\left(c_{l}^{(1)}G_{l}^{(0)}(x)+c_{l}^{(0)}G_{l}^{(1)}(x)\right)\right)=\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)\;.

(83)

Since
\{W_{i}^{(0)},G_{i}^{(0)}(x)\}
forms a complete basis, we have
\sum_{l}c_{l}^{(0)}G^{(0)}_{l}(x)=\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)
. Then using

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l}c_{l}^{(1)}G_{l}^{(0)}(x)+\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)=0\;.

(84)

Then, replace
x\to x_{t}
leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{l}e^{\lambda_{l}^{(0)}t}c_{l}^{(1)}G_{l}^{(0)}(x)+\left(\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)\big|_{x\to x_{t}}\right)=0\;.

(85)

We now expand
\mathsf{G}(x,t)
to first order in

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}e^{\left(\lambda_{k}^{(0)}+\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(1)}}{N}\right)t}\left(G_{k}^{(0)}(x)+\frac{G_{k}^{(1)}(x)}{N}\right)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-2})

(86)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x_{t})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(c_{k}^{(0)}\lambda_{k}^{(1)}te^{\lambda_{k}^{(0)}t}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}c_{k}^{(0)}e^{\lambda_{k}^{(0)}t}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)+c_{k}^{(1)}e^{\lambda_{k}^{(0)}t}G_{k}^{(0)}(x)\right)+\mathcal{O}(N^{-2})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x_{t})+\frac{1}{N}\left(t\widehat{\Lambda}^{(1)}+\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}\right)\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x_{t})-\frac{1}{N}\widehat{O}_{x}^{(1)}\mathsf{G}_{\text{init}}(x)|_{x\to x_{t}}+\mathcal{O}(N^{-2})\;.

where we have used Eq. (

This systematic pattern indicates that while the dilute-limit approximation correctly captures the dynamics of initially small operators, it fails in a controlled and hierarchical manner as the initial operator weight increases. Higher-order corrections are required not because the perturbative expansion breaks down, but because the leading order dynamics artificially decouples sectors that are weakly coupled at finite

The leading-order (LO) solution predicts a steady-state plateau (Eq. (

1/N

The phenomenon shown in the figure can be understood within the interaction picture: the

6
Discussion

In this work, we developed a systematic framework for analyzing operator growth beyond leading order in Brownian spin models, with particular emphasis on the dilute regime where operator weights remain parametrically smaller than the system size. By extending the standard Brownian circuit approach to incorporate higher-order corrections and decoherence effects, we obtained a closed and analytically tractable description of the time evolution of the full operator size distribution
b_{w}(t)
. Our generating-function approach provides a unifying and flexible language for treating these dynamics. It allows arbitrary initial operator size distributions to be evolved analytically, and it cleanly separates model-dependent ingredients—such as the interaction order and decoherence rate—from universal structural features of the evolution.

A central conceptual outcome of our analysis is that the leading order dynamics, while simple and often analytically solvable, can obscure important physical mechanisms governing late-time operator size behavior. At leading order in the dilute limit, the evolution matrix governing
b_{w}
becomes lower triangular, leading to a hierarchy of decay rates that are straightforward to diagonalize. However, this structure relies sensitively on truncating the dynamics to the leading order of
N
. Once higher-order corrections are included, the triangular structure is generically lifted, and the late-time dynamics become both richer and more physically transparent. In this sense, higher-order terms are not merely quantitative refinements, but qualitatively essential for understanding how operator growth ultimately relaxes toward its universal attractor.

The generating function method employed in this work relies on an approximation: the transfer matrix is extended to infinite dimensions while retaining
1/N
as the perturbative parameter. This extension is natural within the formalism but introduces an uncontrolled error. Qualitatively, this approximation is justified in the
dilute limit
where the average operator size is much smaller than
N
. However, a quantitative error estimate is not provided here. Numerically, we expect that, at least in the dilute limit, the error from this infinite-dimensional extension is exponentially suppressed in large
N
, whereas the error from the perturbative
1/N
expansion is only algebraically suppressed. Therefore, the perturbative generating function expansion is expected to be valid, and we aim to furnish a rigorous mathematical proof in future work. The validity and error estimation of this method beyond the dilute limit remain open questions for future exploration.

A related technical point is that the underlying calculation concerns time evolution governed by a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (or Liouvillian) matrix. The perturbative expansion in
1/N
can, in principle, be formulated within an interaction picture framework. Conceptually, such an interaction picture approach does not require the infinite-dimensional extension inherent to the generating function method but would involve repeated direct matrix multiplications, which are computationally cumbersome. The generating function approximation effectively translates these matrix operations into manipulations of differential operators, offering significant calculational simplicity. We speculate that for scenarios far from the dilute limit, the direct interaction picture method might be more robust.

The operator-size evolution equation derived here bears structural similarity to the equation governing
\varphi_{n}
(
{d\over dt}\varphi_{n}=\sum_{m}M_{nm}\varphi_{m}
) in the calculation of Krylov complexity. The key difference lies in their respective transfer matrices
M
. For the
\varphi_{n}
-equation, the non-zero elements of the transfer matrix are distributed along two sub-diagonals, which generally precludes a simple lower-triangular structure and thus hinders exact solvability. Consequently, orthogonal polynomial methods are typically employed for its analysis. Perturbative approaches to Krylov complexity have been considered previously but appear to face significant challenges. Nevertheless, exploring the applicability of the present generating function-based perturbation theory to Krylov complexity remains an intriguing possibility for future work.

Several directions for future work are suggested. Extending the present analysis beyond the dilute limit would allow one to address saturation and finite-
N
effects in a controlled manner, potentially connecting to Haar-random or fully chaotic regimes. It would also be interesting to explore whether similar parity-protected or sectorized metastable dynamics arise for higher-body interactions or in spatially structured systems. Finally, connecting the operator growth picture developed here more directly to experimentally accessible observables—such as OTOCs or randomized measurement protocols—could help bridge the gap between abstract scrambling diagnostics and realistic quantum platforms.

Finally, we clarify the distinction between the generating function method and the continuum approximation. In the latter, the discrete weight
w
is treated as a continuous spatial coordinate. By truncating a Taylor expansion, weight-shift terms in the evolution equation are replaced by spatial derivatives, leading to a partial differential equation. This approach is suitable when the size distribution is relatively smooth and broad. In contrast, in our generating function formalism, the variable
x
is an auxiliary parameter with no direct physical meaning as a spatial coordinate.
