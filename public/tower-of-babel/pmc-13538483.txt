Airway administered artificial mitochondria-enriched nanovesicles for efficient and selective mitochondria transfer to intervene in pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Jinsong Zhang, Siying Fu, Jiawen Wang, Honghui Wu, Zhuoting Li, Ruyi Lin, Hao Sun, Seungho Choe, Yuhuan Kong, Donghang Xu, Jinqiang Wang, Zhen Gu, Jianqing Gao, Tianyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial transplantation is a promising therapeutic approach involving the transfer of exogenous mitochondria into diseased cells to restore impaired mitochondrial homeostasis. However, its clinical translation is severely limited by the lack of efficient methods for precise and potent mitochondria transfer. Inspired by natural mitochondria-containing vesicles, we develop mesenchymal stem cell-derived biomimetic nanovesicles with high mitochondrial loading capacity and augmented extracellular mitochondrial stability. These nanovesicles exhibit an ability to efficiently and selectively deliver mitochondrial cargo to injured cells, which is potentially ascribed to the specific interaction between very late antigen-5 on the nanovesicle surface and pathologically upregulated fibronectin on injured cells. In a mouse pulmonary fibrosis model, these nanovesicles successfully deliver healthy mitochondria to injured lung epithelial cells through airway administration, resulting in a significant reduction in fibrotic progression. This study introduces a design of mitochondria-enriched biomimetic nanovesicles for effective and targeted mitochondria transfer, offering a nanotechnology-based strategy to advance mitochondrial transplantation therapy.

Introduction

Mitochondria are the central hubs of energy metabolism in eukaryotic cells and play a key role in maintaining cellular homeostasis1. Recent studies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by impaired energy production, oxidative stress, and dysregulated dynamics, can trigger pathological cascades by disrupting cellular homeostasis, with growing evidence directly linking these processes to the mechanistic onset of various diseases2. Mitochondrial transplantation therapy, also known as mitochondrial replenishment therapy, refers to the transfer of exogenous functional mitochondria into injured cells to restore mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism. This approach has shown significant promise for treating multiple conditions, including pulmonary injury, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiac diseases3,4. In this therapeutic strategy, the precise and efficient delivery of functional mitochondria into injured cells is critical and largely determines therapeutic outcomes5. Currently, the most widely used strategy involves directly transplanting mitochondria isolated from healthy donor cells into target tissues6. However, this approach has major limitations: mitochondrial activity and integrity inevitably decline during the isolation process, and the isolated mitochondria typically exhibit a short lifespan in the extracellular environment7. Furthermore, isolated mitochondria lack the ability to selectively target dysfunctional cells and generally show limited transfer efficiency3. Even more concerning, exposed mitochondrial components may exacerbate inflammatory responses8 and trigger host immune reactions9, resulting in severe tissue damage. These limitations in both mitochondria transfer efficiency and safety risks have compelled researchers to develop new methods for efficient, precise, and safe mitochondrial transplantation.

Recently, intercellular mitochondria transfer has attracted considerable attention and has been proposed as a potential solution for mitochondrial transplantation10. The use of cellular carriers can effectively bypass the mitochondrial isolation step and largely avoid the immune hazards associated with extracellular exposure of mitochondria11,12. Notably, employing cells for mitochondria transfer offers an additional advantage of targeted delivery to injured cells, offering a promising route for precise mitochondrial transplantation. For example, we previously used iron oxide nanoparticle-engineered mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) to achieve specialized mitochondria transfer to injured cells through connexin 43 (Cx43)-mediated intercellular communication13,14. In addition to direct cell-to-cell mitochondria transfer, several recent studies have shown that mitochondria can also be transferred between cells via extracellular vesicles (EV)15, suggesting a potential strategy for intercellular mitochondria delivery. Compared with the cellular approach, vesicular transport does not require close cell-to-cell contact and can be administered more flexibly. However, mitochondria-containing EV (MitoEV) face the same challenges as other types of EV in terms of production yield and uniformity control16. Their limited mitochondrial loading capacity17 and uncontrolled mitochondrial quality further hinder their therapeutic application. Particularly, MitoEV has been reported as a major pathway for the elimination of damaged mitochondria from cells18,19, raising potential safety concerns for therapeutic use.

Inspired by naturally occurring EV-mediated mitochondria transfer, we developed the MSC-derived artificial mitochondria-enriched nanovesicles (AMV), which were designed to overcome the inherent limitations of MitoEV and offer a potential solution for in vivo mitochondria delivery. AMV were synthesized from MSC using a nano-encapsulation method with a specific microporous membrane, enabling efficient incorporation of active mitochondria into AMV. Comparisons of vesicle yield, loading capacity, and mitochondrial activity confirmed that AMV possessed a marked superiority over natural MitoEV. In particular, AMV showed better protection of mitochondria cargo than other types of artificial nanovesicle prepared by cell membrane coating or liposomal materials encapsulating in the extracellular environment, a critical feature for therapeutic application. Moreover, it was demonstrated that AMV could selectively deliver their mitochondrial cargo into lung cells via a nanovesicle-mediated approach, allowing precise mitochondria transfer in the cellular levels. The mechanism study revealed that this ability was potentially through a vesicular transport pathway mediated by the specific interaction between very late antigen-5 (VLA-5) on the AMV surface and pathologically upregulated fibronectin on injured cells. Thus, this finding provided a nano-sized strategy for precise mitochondria delivery. Furthermore, compared with other mitochondrial transplantation approaches, our artificial nanovesicles could be administered through the airway for more direct mitochondria delivery. Impaired mitochondrial homeostasis in lung epithelial cells (LEC) has been identified as a key factor in pulmonary fibrosis (PF) progression20. Therapeutic studies in a murine model examined the potential of AMV for early intervention in PF and showed that AMV-mediated mitochondria delivery led to far more effective mitigation of PF compared with direct administration of isolated mitochondria, highlighting the promising potential of AMV for advancing mitochondrial transplantation therapy (Fig. 1). Collectively, the AMV described in this study provide a strategy to address the challenges of mitochondria transfer and thereby possess promising potential as mitochondrial nano-agents for targeted disease intervention.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of efficient and selective mitochondria transfer mediated by artificial mitochondria-enriched nanovesicles (AMV) resulting in the potent mitigation of pulmonary fibrosis (PF).AMV were prepared by nano-encapsulation using mesenchymal stem cells (MSC). Results

AMV exhibited typical vesicle characteristics with high mitochondrial loading efficiency

AMV were prepared using the designed nano-encapsulation strategy and observations via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that AMV displayed the typical cup-shaped morphology of natural EV (Supplementary Fig. 1). The average particle size of AMV was 291.5 ± 10.66 nm, and their membranes carried a negative charge similar to most natural EV (−12.4 ± 1.2 mV) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Signature proteins of MSC-derived EV, including CD105, TSG101, and CD9, could also be detected in AMV (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, unlike classical EV, multiple lines of ultrastructural and optical imaging evidence collectively verified the successful loading of structurally intact mitochondria inside AMV. The high-resolution TEM image confirmed the successful loading of mitochondria cargo within AMV (Fig. 2a), and wide-field TEM observations showed canonical mitochondrial structures within the multiple single-layer vesicles (Supplementary Fig. 4). To rigorously confirm that the intraluminal cargos correspond to authentic mitochondrial components, immunogold TEM characterization was then performed. Colloidal gold nanoparticle probes were used to selectively bind mitochondrial signature proteins for specific identification of mitochondrial ultrastructure. Complementing the aforementioned TEM-derived ultrastructural findings, lattice structured illumination super-resolution microscopy (SIM) was adopted as an orthogonal optical imaging method to cross-validate the mitochondrial loading capacity of nanovesicles. The SIM images showed that the mitochondria specifically labelled by MitoTracker Green (as described in a previous report11) were enclosed within vesicle membranes stained with a red fluorescent probe (Fig. 2b). To improve mitochondrial labelling specificity, we additionally implemented fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies against intrinsic marker proteins of mitochondria and vesicle membranes for precise component profiling of AMV. Briefly, the outer mitochondrial membrane biomarker TOM20 was immunolabelled with Fluor 488 to demarcate mitochondrial compartments, whereas the vesicle membrane signature protein CD63 was probed with APC fluorophore to delineate the intact boundary of individual AMV particles. Resultant SIM images exhibited prominent colocalization between green TOM20 mitochondrial fluorescence and circumferential red CD63 vesicle membrane signals (Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistent with the MitoTracker Green staining results, this dual protein labelling approach delivers independent fluorescence validation that mitochondria are efficiently encapsulated within CD63-positive artificial nanovesicles.

Additionally, nanoflow cytometry quantification revealed a relatively high mitochondrial loading efficiency of 55.3 ± 3.3% in AMV (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, to directly visualize how many mitochondria were present within individual AMV, we further quantified the mitochondrial content within AMV from the TEM images. The calculating results showed that the majority of artificial vesicles harbored at least one intact mitochondrial structure (Supplementary Fig. 7b), and the encapsulation efficiency of mitochondria within AMV was quantified at 52.5 ± 6.0% (Supplementary Fig. 7c), consistent with nanoflow cytometry results. This efficiency was further confirmed by comparing the fluorescence signals of the stem cell vesicle marker (CD63) and mitochondria, which showed a similar loading efficiency of 58.8% (Supplementary Fig. 8). To further validate mitochondrial encapsulation, proteins in AMV were functionally annotated using proteomic techniques. Results showed the presence of 771 mitochondria-associated proteins in AMV, and gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated significant enrichment of mitochondria-associated proteins (Fig. 2d). Importantly, this proteomic profiling confirmed the marked enrichment of mitochondria‑related proteins, especially those essential for sustaining mitochondrial function. These findings establish an important molecular basis supporting the preservation of biological function in mitochondria encapsulated within AMV, although further functional validation is warranted in our subsequent experiments.

Because AMV were inspired by natural MitoEV, we next compared vesicle yield, mitochondrial content, and proteomic profiles between artificial AMV and natural MitoEV (Fig. 2e). The number of vesicles generated from one million donor MSC showed a 17.4-fold increase for AMV compared with MitoEV (Fig. 2f). Similarly, AMV contained 3.5-fold more protein per one million donor MSC than MitoEV (Fig. 2g). The fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker Green-labeled mitochondria encapsulated in vesicles was also measured, revealing a 6.2-fold higher intensity in AMV compared with MitoEV (Fig. 2h). These results indicated that AMV had a higher vesicle yield and greater mitochondrial loading capacity than natural MitoEV. To further confirm the superior mitochondrial loading capacity of AMV, GO analysis was performed on the top 15 most significantly enriched categories in AMV and MitoEV. AMV were significantly enriched in multiple mitochondrial compartments, including the inner mitochondrial membrane protein complex, mitochondrial protein-containing complex, mitochondrial inner membrane, and mitochondrial membrane, compared with MitoEV (Fig. 2i), supporting their higher mitochondrial loading capacity. Proteomic analysis of biological processes further revealed that AMV were prominently enriched in ATP biosynthetic processes, ATP synthesis coupled proton transport, and mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled proton transport (Fig. 2j). These findings further supported the high loading capacity of functional mitochondria in AMV compared with MitoEV. Notably, the well-preserved mitochondrial activity observed in AMV suggested that they may not only excel in mitochondrial loading but also maintain mitochondrial function.

In addition, the mitochondrial loading capacity of AMV was compared with that of conventional nanovesicle (NV), a widely reported type of cell-derived artificial vesicle preparation21. Unlike AMV preparation, NV is prepared by sequential extruding MSC suspensions through graded pore-size membranes (5, 1, and 0.4 μm) under gradient shear forces, followed by direct collection without additional purification21. In contrast, AMV is prepared using a tailored extrusion-based nano-encapsulation strategy optimized for high-efficiency mitochondrial loading. We herein have evaluated mitochondrial loading efficiency using extrusion membranes of varying pore sizes and identified that a single 10 μm pore-size membrane yielded the highest mitochondrial enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 9). This is likely because this specific pore size achieves an optimal mechanical balance, which not only enables efficient encapsulation of intact mitochondria, but also is sufficiently restrictive to limit the incorporation of larger cellular structures and debris. Critically, this single-membrane design generates moderate shear forces favorable for mitochondrial packaging, and subsequent purification steps are applied to further enrich mitochondria-loaded vesicles, resulting in a highly homogeneous, mitochondria-enriched vesicle product. Moreover, proteomic analysis showed a significantly higher enrichment of mitochondrial components in AMV than in NV (Supplementary Fig. 10). This finding suggests that the current reported artificial vesicle preparation strategy offers superior mitochondrial enrichment efficiency compared with conventional methods.

AMV showed a protective effect on the loaded mitochondria

Our proteomic analysis indicated that mitochondrial biosynthetic activity was well preserved, prompting us to investigate whether AMV could protect the biological activity of the loaded mitochondria. It is well established that mitochondria rapidly lose their functionality in the extracellular environment7, presenting a major barrier to the therapeutic use of isolated mitochondria. Moreover, recent studies have shown that direct exposure of isolated mitochondria to the body carries a high risk of triggering host immune responses22. Therefore, an appropriate carrier that can encapsulate mitochondria, maintain their activity, and reduce immune risks is critically needed, and we sought to determine whether AMV could fulfill this role.

Because the expression of mitochondrial functional proteins significantly influences mitochondrial biological processes, we first performed Western blot (WB) analysis to assess the expression of key mitochondrial proteins, including ATP5A1, NDUFB8, COXIV, HSP60, TOM20, and CYT C. Results confirmed the presence of these functional proteins in AMV, suggesting that mitochondria within AMV retained biological function (Fig. 3a). Next, mitochondria in AMV were dual-labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red, which labeled bioactive mitochondria, and MitoTracker Green, which labeled all mitochondria. This allowed calculation of the proportion of bioactive mitochondria in AMV. We found that 87.0% of total mitochondria were bioactive, confirming that AMV effectively preserved mitochondrial activity (Fig. 3b). The loading efficiency of bioactive mitochondria in AMV was further measured using MitoTracker Deep Red for mitochondria and 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorates (DIO) for vesicles, revealing a high loading capacity of 51.1% (Fig. 3c). These results demonstrated that artificially prepared vesicles can efficiently encapsulate bioactive mitochondria. Furthermore, mitochondrial function within AMV was assessed by dynamically monitoring oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using a Seahorse energy metabolism analyzer. Time-resolved OCR profiles showed that AMV-encapsulated mitochondria maintained consistently higher oxygen consumption rates than free mitochondria throughout the assay, suggesting that mitochondria encapsulated within AMV remained respirational competent and displayed more stable oxidative metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Consistently, quantitative analysis revealed that AMV‑encapsulated mitochondria exhibited significantly elevated basal respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP production capacity relative to free mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 11b–e). We then assessed whether AMV could protect encapsulated mitochondria under challenging conditions, which is crucial for their potential clinical translation. Proteinase K was used to simulate a proteolytic extracellular environment, representing a major threat to mitochondrial integrity and function during transplantation19. WB analysis showed that AMV exhibited good resistance to Proteinase K digestion, particularly at concentrations below 0.2 μg/mL, with TOM20 expression largely preserved (Fig. 3d). In contrast, unprotected free mitochondria (Free-mito) showed a dramatic decrease in TOM20 expression even at the Proteinase K concentration of 0.2 μg/mL (Fig. 3d). To further confirm mitochondrial protection, the ATP level was quantitatively measured in both AMV-encapsulated mitochondria and Free-mito. The ATP in Free-mito were significantly reduced after Proteinase K treatment, whereas AMV-encapsulated mitochondria maintained their ATP levels (Fig. 3e). These results confirmed that AMV not only efficiently loaded bioactive mitochondria but also protected them from proteolytic degradation in the extracellular environment.

We also observed that the expression of vesicular membrane proteins in AMV decreased after treatment with Proteinase K at 0.2 μg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 12), whereas mitochondrial marker proteins remained well protected at the same concentration (Fig. 3d). This implied that the membrane structure of AMV obtained from the parental cells serve as a biological barrier, and contributes to shielding against extracellular damage and preserving the integrity of encapsulated mitochondria. To verify this finding, mitochondria were coated with either lipid-based liposomes (Lipo-mito) or stem cell membrane-derived vesicles (CM-mito), and their mitochondrial activity was compared with AMV-encapsulated mitochondria (Fig. 3f). TEM observations verified successful encapsulation of mitochondria within Lipo-mito and CM-mito (Supplementary Fig. 13). Measurements of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and ATP levels showed that membrane coating significantly delayed mitochondrial inactivation during extracellular storage at both 4 ℃ and 25 °C, as compared with Free-mito (Fig. 3g–j). Notably, AMV afforded superior protection for encapsulated mitochondria relative to Lipo-mito and CM-mito, which relied solely on membrane structures. These findings indicated that while membrane coating offered some protection, the retained intracellular components in AMV were also critical for maintaining mitochondrial activity. Particularly, the above comparisons also revealed that mitochondrial encapsulation using cell membranes alone exhibited no significant difference relative to liposome encapsulation, despite their distinct permeability properties. This observation suggests that vesicle membrane permeability plays only a modest role in mitochondrial protection. Furthermore, we also prepared AMV derived from the B16F10 cell line using the same nano‑encapsulation procedure (Supplementary Fig. 13), which likewise conferred robust mitochondrial protection as reflected by both MMP and ATP levels (Supplementary Fig. 14). This supported the conclusion that our nano-encapsulation strategy provided significant advantages in protecting mitochondria compared with direct membrane coating and can be applied to various donor cell types, demonstrating potential as a platform technology for producing mitochondria-containing agents.

To further verify the contributions of intracellular components within AMV to mitochondrial protection, a proteomic analysis of AMV was performed. The results showed that, in addition to enriched mitochondrial components, this artificial vesicle harbored abundant cytosolic constituents, encompassing a total of 4173 distinct proteins. These cytosolic proteins, in conjunction with the vesicular membrane structure, are hypothesized that contribute substantially to the promoted mitochondrial preservation. Subsequent co-incubation assays of isolated mitochondria with different cytosolic fractions demonstrated that intact cytoplasmic extracts effectively maintained mitochondrial activity, whereas depletion of cytosolic proteins markedly diminished mitochondrial functional markers (Supplementary Fig. 15), although metabolic substrates and ionic components in the cytosol may be partially retained after protein depletion. Therefore, these findings indicated that cytosolic proteins play a more critical role in sustaining mitochondrial activity than either metabolic substrates or ionic environment alone. Furthermore, proteomic analyses identified several mitochondria‑associated chaperone proteins in the cytosolic fraction of AMV, including HSPA6, HSPD1, HSPE1, and HSP90AB1 (Supplementary Fig. 16). These chaperones are thought to assist maintain proper mitochondrial protein conformation, facilitate the repair of damaged proteins, and preserve mitochondrial structural and functional stability23. In addition to mitochondrial chaperone proteins, multiple endogenous protease inhibitors, including SERPIN and TIMP families, were also detected in the cytosolic fraction of AMV (Supplementary Fig. 17). These inhibitors have been reported to prevent abnormal intracellular protease activation and attenuate the degradation of mitochondrial structural and respiratory chain proteins24,25. Accordingly, we propose that the combined action of chaperone proteins and protease inhibitors within the cytosolic components of AMV contributes substantially to the good protection of encapsulated mitochondria. Collectively, the above analyses indicated that the proteins in the cytosolic fraction of AMV contribute substantially to the mitochondrial protection. In particular, the retention of cytoplasmic chaperones and anti-proteolytic factors within AMV may constitute a key mechanism underlying the preservation of mitochondrial function.

Having verified the protective effect of AMV in vitro, we next evaluated their performance in vivo using a bleomycin (BLM)-induced mouse model of PF14. AMV-encapsulated mitochondria and Free-mito were labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red and administered via intratracheal instillation at equivalent doses based on the number of bioactive mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 18). Fluorescence imaging showed that administered mitochondria primarily localized to the lung and liver (Supplementary Fig. 19), and AMV-encapsulated mitochondria persisted longer in injured lungs compared with Free-mito (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 19). Semi-quantitative analysis confirmed that a single administration of AMV preserved active mitochondrial signals in fibrotic lungs for at least 8 h without significant decline, and signals were detectable even at 12 h post-administration. In contrast, Free-mito signals diminished within 8 h and were barely detectable at 12 h (Fig. 3k, l). Thus, these findings demonstrated that AMV can effectively protect encapsulated mitochondria from rapid clearance in the lung, validating their ability to prolong mitochondrial retention within the injured lung. Moreover, fluorescence signals corresponding AMV‑encapsulated mitochondria were also detected in the liver following intratracheal administration. This hepatic redistribution of AMV is likely attributable to compromised alveolar–capillary barrier integrity, as persistent inflammation disrupts pulmonary epithelial and endothelial layers following BLM treatment26. Such pathological alterations may enable a subset of nanovesicles to enter the pulmonary circulation and subsequently translocate to peripheral organs including the liver27,28.

AMV enabled selective delivery of mitochondria into injured LEC via the VLA-5/fibronectin interaction pathway

Having confirmed that AMV can efficiently load and protect bioactive mitochondria, we next investigated whether these artificial vesicles could serve as vehicles for targeted mitochondria delivery. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed successful delivery of mitochondria (labeled with MitoTracker red fluorescence) into BLM-induced injured TC-1 cells (BLM-TC-1) (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, AMV-encapsulated mitochondria were immunolabeled with fluorophore-tagged antibodies targeting the outer mitochondrial membrane protein TOM20. Compared with direct mitochondrial dye staining, this strategy substantially reduced false-positive signals arising from dye leakage. CLSM observations showed prominent FITC-TOM20 fluorescent signals in recipient BLM-TC-1 cells, verifying the efficient mitochondria delivery capacity of AMV (Supplementary Fig. 20). To further verify mitochondria delivery, human mtDNA levels were measured in BLM-TC-1 cells, a murine-derived cell line, by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). While almost no human mtDNA was detected in untreated BLM-TC-1 cells, a significant presence was observed following co-incubation with AMV prepared from human MSC, confirming successful transfer of human mitochondria into murine cells (Fig. 4b). Quantitative determination of human mtDNA in mouse TC-1 cells showed that each injured cell can receive 0.3732 ± 0.0104 relative units of human mtDNA. Furthermore, the minimal effective dose of AMV for therapeutic effects on injured cells was investigated. A positive correlation was observed between AMV dosage and therapeutic efficacy in injured cells. Furthermore, the persistence duration of mitochondria delivered by AMV in recipient cells was also determined by dynamically monitoring human mtDNA levels in mouse TC-1 cells. It was observed that the level of delivered mtDNA dropped to approximately 25% on day 7 relative to day 1 following a single therapeutic dose of AMV. Moreover, exogenous human mtDNA was barely detectable by day 9, indicating that the detectable persistence duration was less than 9 days (Supplementary Fig. 22). These findings suggested that AMV‑delivered exogenous mitochondria can persist in recipient cells for up to 9 days, which is slightly longer than the previously reported 7 days delivered by tunnelling nanotubes29. Notably, we further observed that the MMP levels in recipient cells remained high on day 9, even though exogenous mitochondria were almost undetectable (Supplementary Fig. 23). This finding implied that although the exogenously delivered mitochondria do not persist long‑term in recipient cells, their associated therapeutic effect may be sustained for a substantially longer period.

Notably, AMV exhibited selective delivery of mitochondria to injured cells: the proportion of mitochondria delivered into BLM-TC-1 cells was over four times higher than that in healthy TC-1 cells (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 24). Consistently, the fluorescence intensity of MitoTracker-labeled mitochondria was significantly higher in BLM-TC-1 cells compared with healthy TC-1 cells (Fig. 4d). In addition, given the potential risks of false-positive signals derived from MitoTracker dye leakage, we further adopted fluorophore-conjugated TOM20 immunolabeling to more specifically trace the mitochondrial transfer from AMV into healthy and BLM-injured TC-1 cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that only 11.9% of healthy TC-1 cells exhibited mitochondrial fluorescence signals, while the positive rate reached up to 33.3% in BLM-injured TC-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 25a). Consistent with this observation, both the mitochondria transfer efficiency and the mean fluorescence intensity of internalized mitochondria were markedly higher in the BLM-TC-1 group than those in the control TC-1 group, as quantitatively validated by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 25b, c). Collectively, these results suggested that AMV can efficiently and selectively deliver encapsulated mitochondria into injured cells. Real-time imaging of living cells further confirmed that AMV preferentially delivered mitochondria to injured TC-1 cells over healthy counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 26). These results indicated that AMV can serve as carriers for selective mitochondria delivery into injured cells, a property likely inherited from their parent MSC30. This capability is critical for improving therapeutic efficacy and safety.

To evaluate mitochondria delivery via airway administration, we established an in vitro transwell system with artificial mucus in the upper chamber to mimic the airway mucus barrier and assessed AMV penetration (Supplementary Fig. 27a). The results showed that over 40% of AMV successfully traversed the mucus barrier (Supplementary Fig. 27b). Morphological analysis confirmed that AMV maintained their integrity after passing through the mucus (Supplementary Fig. 27c), with no change in particle size (Supplementary Fig. 27d). Importantly, AMV retained their selective mitochondria delivery capability after passing through the mucus barrier (Supplementary Fig. 27e, f).

To further confirm the in vivo mitochondria delivery, BLM-injured mice were used to assess AMV distribution after airway administration. Fluorescence imaging of lung sections showed that AMV-delivered exogenous mitochondria were predominantly localized in CK7-labeled LEC, with almost no signal in bronchial epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 28). While Free-mito also primarily localized to alveolar epithelial cells, the fluorescence intensity was much weaker than that of AMV (Supplementary Fig. 29), highlighting the superiority of AMV in mitochondria delivery. Airway administration of AMV in both BLM-injured and healthy mice showed significantly stronger mitochondrial signals in injured LEC than that in healthy LEC (Supplementary Fig. 30), indicating that AMV preserved selective delivery in vivo. In contrast, Free-mito administration resulted in weaker fluorescence with no difference between injured and healthy lungs. Biodistribution analysis further revealed prolonged retention of mitochondria in BLM-injured lungs compared with healthy lungs when delivered via AMV (Supplementary Fig. 31), which is advantageous for mitochondrial therapy. Collectively, these observations demonstrated that AMV enabled highly efficient and selective delivery of exogenous mitochondria into injured LEC, making them an ideal carrier for mitochondrial transplantation.

This important finding prompted us to investigate the potential mechanism underlying the selective mitochondria delivery by AMV. We first evaluated mitochondria delivery efficiency into TC-1 cells subjected to varying degrees of BLM-induced injury. Results showed a positive correlation between the extent of cellular damage and mitochondria delivery efficiency, with more severely injured cells exhibiting progressively enhanced mitochondrial uptake from AMV (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 32). These findings indicated that the degree of cellular damage influences the efficiency of AMV-mediated mitochondria delivery. In contrast, mitochondria delivered without encapsulation lost the ability to preferentially target injured cells (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 33), suggesting that the interplay between AMV and injured recipient cells is critical for the delivery efficiency. To further elucidate these findings, we evaluated fibronectin abundance in BLM-injured TC-1 cells. Fibronectin is a kind of secreted glycoprotein and a core component of the extracellular matrix, which has been reported to be extensively deposited and enriched in LEC under fibrotic injury, and is thus widely regarded as a key biomarker of fibrosis3. WB analysis verified that fibronectin levels were significantly elevated in TC-1 cells following BLM treatment (Fig. 4g, h). We then evaluated the expression of VLA-5 (also known as integrin α5β1) on AMV. VLA-5 is an integrin expressed on MSC membranes that specifically recognizes the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid sequence in fibronectin, enabling high-affinity interaction with fibrotic cells31. WB analysis confirmed that AMV retained VLA-5 expression inherited from MSC (Fig. 4i). Together, these results suggested that the VLA-5/fibronectin interaction might mediate the selective affinity of AMV for BLM-injured cells. To further validate this mechanism, we examined AMV binding to fibronectin on TC-1 cells before and after BLM injury. BLM treatment markedly increased fibronectin enrichment (green fluorescence) on TC-1 cells. Co-localization of VLA-5 on AMV with fibronectin on TC-1 cells was also observed, indicating that this binding likely played a critical role in selective mitochondria delivery to injured cells (Fig. 4j). Consistently, in vivo fluorescence imaging of mouse lung sections confirmed increased fibronectin deposition in response to BLM-induced injury, with markedly stronger fluorescence signals observed in fibrotic lungs relative to healthy controls (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35). Furthermore, AMV co-localized with fibronectin-enriched LEC, further supporting the functional importance of this interaction for targeted mitochondria delivery (Supplementary Fig. 34).

In order to directly test the role of the VLA-5/fibronectin interaction, either VLA-5 on AMV or fibronectin on TC-1 cells was blocked. Flow cytometry analysis showed that suppressing either component significantly reduced mitochondria delivery efficiency into injured TC-1 cells (Fig. 4k, l). Similarly, treatment of AMV with GLPG0187, a broad-spectrum integrin inhibitor, markedly suppressed their mitochondria delivery to injured cells (Supplementary Fig. 36). These results demonstrated that integrins on AMV, such as VLA-5, are essential for anchoring AMV to deposited fibronectin, thereby facilitating subsequent cellular uptake. Collectively, these findings indicated that the interaction between AMV-expressed integrins (e.g., VLA-5) and deposited fibronectin on BLM-injured cells represented the likely mechanism underlying selective mitochondria delivery by AMV (Fig. 4m).

Furthermore, to confirm the cellular uptake of AMV by injured TC-1 cells, the delivery process was observed using CLSM. Previous studies have suggested that larger bio-vesicles can facilitate cargo delivery via membrane fusion with recipient cells32. However, no typical membrane fusion between AMV and recipient cells was observed under microscopic examination (Supplementary Fig. 37). We therefore investigated the potential endocytic pathways involved in AMV uptake. Four different endocytic inhibitors were tested, and flow cytometry analysis revealed that AMV uptake was an energy-dependent process primarily mediated by macropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 38). Notably, we further analyzed the intracellular fate of AMV following endocytosis and found almost no co-localization with lysosomes. In contrast, Free-mito showed significant co-localization with lysosomes (Supplementary Fig. 39a–c), as confirmed by Pearson’s coefficient analysis (Supplementary Fig. 39d). These results indicated that AMV not only utilized an endocytic pathway for cellular entry but also protected exogenous mitochondria from lysosomal degradation, thereby enhancing the likelihood that delivered mitochondria can maintain their bioactivity within recipient cells.

AMV potently rescued injured lung cells via efficient mitochondria delivery

Mitochondrial dysfunction in LEC has recently been recognized as a key contributor to PF pathogenesis20. The dysfunction of mitochondria can disrupt intracellular ATP metabolism and elevate ROS levels, leading to LEC apoptosis and activation of fibrogenic signaling pathways33. However, these pathological changes can be reversed by replenishing exogenous mitochondria13,14. Therefore, we investigated whether AMV could rescue injured TC-1 cells and suppress their fibrogenic activation via mitochondria delivery. To further ensure that the observed therapeutic effects were specifically due to mitochondria delivery rather than other factors, several control groups were included: AMV-stored and Free-mito-stored, in which AMV or free mitochondria were stored at 4 °C for 6 h to inactivate mitochondrial biofunction (Fig. 3g, i); AMV-Ld, in which AMV were filtered through a 220 nm polycarbonate (PC) membrane to remove the majority of mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 40); and AMV-CCCP, in which AMV contained dysfunctional mitochondria obtained from donor MSC pretreated with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP), a classical electron transport chain inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. 41). Morphological observations using TEM showed that mitochondria in healthy TC-1 cells had intact structures with clearly defined cristae, whereas injured BLM-TC-1 cells exhibited marked mitochondrial shrinkage and severe disruption of cristae architecture (Fig. 5a). Incubation of BLM-TC-1 cells with Free-mito resulted in only limited restoration of damaged mitochondrial morphology. In contrast, AMV treatment led to a significant recovery of mitochondrial structure, likely due to the effective protection and facilitated delivery of mitochondria by AMV. Treatment of BLM-TC-1 cells with Free-mito-stored had no effect on restoring mitochondrial morphology, indicating that the observed recovery was largely attributable to the import of exogenous mitochondria. Notably, AMV stored at 4 °C still partially restored mitochondrial morphology, supporting its role in preserving mitochondrial bioactivity. Conversely, AMV-Ld and AMV-CCCP failed to restore damaged mitochondria, confirming that the recovery induced by AMV was primarily due to its loaded mitochondria. In addition to morphological assessments, the mitochondrial functions of BLM-TC-1 cells were evaluated after co-incubation with Free-mito and AMV, including their respective pre-treatments. Measurements of MMP and intracellular ATP levels showed that AMV provided the most effective recovery of mitochondrial quality and energy production (Fig. 5d, e). Free-mito and AMV-stored also promoted some functional recovery, but to a much lesser extent than AMV. Conversely, removal of mitochondrial components or disruption of mitochondrial biofunction impaired the restoration of mitochondrial function, supporting the conclusion that functional recovery in injured cells is primarily due to the delivery of exogenous mitochondria. Examination of ROS generation in injured BLM-TC-1 cells following the indicated treatments further confirmed that AMV most effectively restored mitochondrial function, highlighting the importance of bioactive mitochondria in functional recovery (Fig. 5f). Moreover, assessments of cell viability and apoptosis demonstrated that AMV provided the strongest rescue of injured BLM-TC-1 cells (Fig. 5g, h). Notably, Free-mito showed comparable therapeutic effects to AMV in terms of ATP generation (Fig. 5e) and cell viability (Fig. 5g), likely because equal amounts of mitochondria were applied in both treatments, making the delivery advantage of AMV less apparent in these cellular assays. Additionally, the expression of TGF-β, a classical fibrotic marker associated with mitochondrial dysfunction13, was evaluated. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that AMV treatment most efficiently suppressed TGF-β expression in injured BLM-TC-1 cells (Fig. 5i), indicating that preservation of mitochondrial activity is critical for preventing fibrotic progression through restoration of mitochondrial function. Collectively, the effective mitochondrial protection and delivery capabilities of AMV enabled restoration of both mitochondrial morphology and function, demonstrating a potent rescue of injured lung cells and the potential to inhibit the initiation of fibrosis.

AMV achieved efficient intervention against PF progression in a murine model

As described above, although the cellular experiments demonstrated the promising potential of AMV in rescuing injured lung cells and suppressing TGF-β expression, the superiority of AMV in targeted mitochondria delivery in vivo was not fully apparent compared to the treatment with Free-mito. Therefore, both AMV and Free-mito were administered via intratracheal instillation, and their therapeutic efficacy against PF progression in a murine model was assessed. Mice were pre-treated with BLM to induce PF13,14. AMV and Free-mito were then administered intratracheally starting the next day, and the therapeutic outcomes were compared at 28 days post-initial BLM challenge (Fig. 6a). A continuous loss of body weight was observed after the BLM challenge; however, treatment with either AMV or Free-mito mitigated this weight loss (Supplementary Fig. 42). Survival monitoring showed that 90% of AMV-treated mice remained alive at the end of the study, with significantly delayed mortality compared to controls. In contrast, Free-mito-treated mice had a survival rate of 60%, indicating a limited mitigation of PF-caused death (Fig. 6b). This difference was likely due to the more efficient, injured lung cell-targeted mitochondria delivery by AMV, as demonstrated above. Micro-CT imaging provided direct observation of lung tissue, showing that BLM-challenged mice exhibited blurred margins and extensive, uneven patchy shadows. These fibrotic features were partially alleviated by Free-mito and substantially reduced by AMV treatment (Fig. 6c). Histopathological analysis of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained lung sections revealed severe alveolar structure damage following BLM treatment. Administration of Free-mito slightly alleviated this damage, whereas AMV markedly preserved alveolar architecture (Fig. 6d). Masson’s trichrome staining further confirmed the superior antifibrotic effect of AMV, with significantly reduced collagen deposition (blue) (Fig. 6e). Ashcroft scoring (Fig. 6f), along with quantitative analysis of airspace area fraction (Supplementary Fig. 43) and fibrotic ratio (Fig. 6g), corroborated the remarkable therapeutic efficacy of AMV in PF intervention. Lung weight measurements also indicated that AMV significantly suppressed the abnormal increase in lung weight caused by BLM treatment (Fig. 6h). Furthermore, analysis of typical fibrotic indicators, including matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9), TGF-β, and hydroxyproline in homogenized lung samples, demonstrated that only mitochondria delivered via AMV significantly reduced all these markers (Fig. 6i–k). To further verify that the observed therapeutic benefits arise from mitochondria delivery mediated by AMV, a transcriptomic profiling on lung epithelial cells isolated from the treated lung samples was performed (Supplementary Fig. 44). The transcriptomic analysis identified 146 differentially expressed mitochondrial‑related genes following AMV administration (Supplementary Fig. 45). GO enrichment analysis further revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways associated with ATP production, energy homeostasis, and mitochondrial respiratory chain function (Supplementary Fig. 46a). Concurrently, KEGG pathway analysis confirmed the pronounced upregulation of key metabolic genes, including Atp6v1b1, Cox6a2, and Me3 (Supplementary Fig. 46b). Collectively, these results demonstrated at the transcriptional level that AMV-mediated mitochondria delivery enhances cellular energy metabolism. Thus, the present findings suggest that the effective PF intervention of AMV may be associated with its successful mitochondria delivery, thereby improving metabolic homeostasis in injured LEC.

Furthermore, preliminary safety assessments of AMV treatment were conducted. H&E staining of major organs (brain, heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidneys) showed no apparent pathological changes in healthy mice after AMV treatment (Supplementary Fig. 47). Also, no significant alterations in body weight were observed (Supplementary Fig. 48a). Hematological analyses revealed consistent levels of white blood cells (WBC), neutrophils (NEU), and lymphocytes (LYM) across groups (Supplementary Fig. 48b–d). Similarly, no significant changes were observed in serum markers of liver function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), or renal function, including creatinine (CREA), urea (UREA), uric acid (UA), after AMV treatment (Supplementary Fig. 48e–i). These preliminary evaluations indicated that AMV administration at the therapeutic dose was unlikely to pose severe safety concerns. In addition, the potential immune toxicity associated with airway delivery of AMV was examined (Supplementary Fig. 49). It was noted that levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, in both lung tissue and serum samples from AMV-treated mice remained comparable to those in healthy controls, indicating no overt activation of innate immune responses. In contrast, intratracheal administration of Free-mito significantly increased IL-6 and TNF-α levels. These results indicated that AMV can shield encapsulated mitochondria from recognition by the innate immune system. This effect is likely attributed to the immune‑privileged properties of MSC, which confer low immunogenicity to the artificial vesicles34,35. Collectively, these findings suggest that intratracheal instillation of AMV at the therapeutic dose does not induce significant systemic toxicity, and facilitates immune evasion of administered mitochondria.

Discussion

Mitochondrial transplantation has recently shown great promise in the treatment of various diseases36. However, its successful clinical application remains the challenge with several major obstacles to overcome, such as maintaining mitochondrial activity during transplantation and achieving precise delivery of exogenous mitochondria into target cells. Currently, direct transplantation of isolated mitochondria from healthy donor cells is the most commonly reported approach37, offering the advantages of high mitochondrial concentration and flexible administration. However, this strategy has inherent limitations, including poor survival of isolated mitochondria, lack of selectivity for injured cells, and potential immune responses, which restrict its broader therapeutic applications. To address these limitations, we previously proposed a cell-based mitochondria delivery strategy using Cx43-overexpressing MSC for targeted transfer of mitochondria via intercellular mitochondria transfer13. This approach allowed mitochondria to be directly transferred from donor to recipient cells, avoiding isolation and preserving mitochondrial bioactivity. Nonetheless, the efficiency of intercellular mitochondria transfer is affected by multiple factors and is inherently limited by mitochondrial homeostasis in donor cells. Recently, MitoEV have been proposed as a third delivery strategy15, combining the administration flexibility of isolated mitochondria with improved protection during delivery. However, MitoEV-based therapies face challenges, such as low production yield and difficulty in controlling mitochondrial quality20. To overcome these limitations, some innovative approaches have been recently explored. For instance, Jiang et al. demonstrated that genetically activating the CD38/IP3R/Ca2+ pathway in MSC could markedly promote mitochondrial vesicle biogenesis and improve mitochondrial quality. These super mitochondria‑enriched extracellular vesicles exerted significant therapeutic effects in animal models of Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy38. Furthermore, another study reported that pretreatment of donor cells with cytochalasin B can increase the abundance of mitochondria‑related components in vesicles. These studies have provided promising solutions to address the inherent limitations of natural MitoEV, yet their production still depends on endogenous biological processes involved in extracellular vesicle biogenesis. Therefore, in the present study, we further developed a biomimetic strategy to fabricate artificial vesicles via a modified extrusion approach. These artificial vesicles can closely mimic natural MitoEV, while bypassing the inherent biogenesis and secretion process of EV, thus yielding significantly enhanced mitochondrial loading efficiency and vesicle production yield compared with natural MitoEV. Furthermore, this method requires no additional cell engineering or chemical stimulation of donor cells. Such a direct fabrication route confers promising potential for scalable manufacturing. Moreover, the present study demonstrates the feasibility of applying extrusion strategy for the efficient loading of active mitochondria into optimally formulated nanovesicles, thereby expanding the application of this technology to the encapsulation of large subcellular organelles. Comparing to the well-established mitochondrial wrapping technologies, including liposome-based encapsulation and microfluid-applied fabrication39,40, our extrusion strategy holds an advantage in eliminating the need for extra mitochondrial isolation procedures, thereby minimizing potential structural and functional damage to mitochondria during the isolation process. In addition, certain cytoplasmic components are spontaneously co-encapsulated into AMV during extrusion, which contributes to the extended longevity of the encapsulated mitochondria (Fig. 3g–j). However, further investigations to figure out all the components in AMV are necessary, and in‑depth mechanistic studies are also warranted to elucidate how these cytosolic components synergistically maintain mitochondrial structural integrity and bioenergetic function.

Furthermore, our results demonstrated that AMV could be efficiently loaded with bioactive mitochondria, showing a 17.4-fold increase in vesicle yield and a 6.2-fold increase in mitochondrial content per million donor cells compared with natural MitoEV. Proteomic analysis further confirmed the significant enrichment of mitochondrial components in AMV relative to MitoEV, and nanoflow cytometry detection demonstrated that 87.0% of mitochondria in AMV remained bioactive. Thus, AMV exhibited higher vesicle yield, more efficient mitochondrial loading, and superior mitochondrial quality compared to natural MitoEV. Mechanistically, the efficient mitochondrial packaging achieved by extrusion-based nano-encapsulation may be attributed to the high cytoplasmic abundance and favorable structural flexibility of mitochondria, which enable their efficient enclosure during membrane rupture and reassembly under controlled shear force, whereas larger or more rigid organelles are less readily incorporated. Moreover, we found the pore size of the extrusion membrane plays another critical role in determining the enrichment efficiency of mitochondria in the vesicles. The usage of a 10 μm extrusion membrane contributes to a favorable balance between efficient mitochondrial encapsulation and limited incorporation of larger cellular structures, which could help achieve relatively high mitochondrial enrichment and reduce non-mitochondrial cargo.

Moreover, AMV demonstrated a remarkable ability to protect encapsulated mitochondria from rapid inactivation in the extracellular environment, a major challenge for mitochondrial transplantation therapeutics. Encapsulated mitochondria retained their activity for over 12 h, far longer than isolated mitochondria. Our study further revealed that this protection was not solely due to the physical barrier of the cell membrane coating, as vesicles coated only with cell membranes or liposomal membranes provided limited protection. Instead, it may also result from synergistic interactions between donor cell components and the loaded mitochondria. Our further studies indicated that proteins in the cytosolic fraction of AMV made a substantial contribution to mitochondrial functional protection. Notably, the cytoplasmic chaperones and anti-proteolytic factors within AMV exert pivotal roles in this protection. Nonetheless, future studies are warranted to dissect the individual contribution of each cytosolic component toward mitochondrial protection in detail.

Additionally, a precise and efficient transfer of therapeutic mitochondria into the injured cells is leveraged to initiate subsequent mitochondrial transplantation therapy41. Pulmonary delivery via the airways represents a direct and efficient strategy for targeted drug administration to lung cells. However, the complex physiological environment of the airway and lung tissues poses significant challenges for the direct administration of free mitochondria. While cellular vehicles have shown promising capabilities for targeted mitochondria transfer13,14, their application through airway administration remains problematic. Our artificial nanovesicles offer a promising solution for pulmonary delivery of mitochondria, demonstrating the ability to efficiently deliver mitochondria to lung cells and maintain mitochondrial bioactivity during the delivery process. In the present study, we only evaluated the effects of the mucus layer on the lung delivery efficiency of intratracheally administered AMV. This is because a thickened mucus layer under pathological conditions represents a major barrier to effective airway delivery42. Additionally, mucociliary clearance may constitute an additional obstacle for AMV to reach pulmonary cells via airway administration. Nevertheless, given that mucociliary clearance predominantly acts on relatively large particles (>5 μm)41,43, this mechanism may confer a degree of resistance on our nanoscale AMV. Crucially, our findings revealed that AMV retained the injured cell-selective mitochondria delivery capability from parental MSC and, using this artificial nanovesicle-mediated strategy, enabled injured cells selected mitochondria transfer. This property offered a potential solution to current challenges of precise mitochondria delivery for disease treatment. Our study further indicated that the selective mitochondria delivery mediated by AMV is potentially associated with the interaction between VLA-5 and pathologically upregulated fibronectin on injured LEC. In addition, the stress microenvironment induced by cellular injury may significantly activate endocytosis pathways in injured cells44, thereby enhancing the cellular uptake of AMV-encapsulated mitochondria by providing additional internalization routes. Meanwhile, injury-induced impairment of membrane integrity, characterized by increased membrane fluidity and disrupted tight junctions in injured lung epithelial cells45, may lower the physical barrier for mitochondrial internalization, allowing AMV and their cargo mitochondria to more easily traverse the cell membrane without relying on specific receptor binding. Furthermore, dysfunctional mitophagy in injured cells may reduce the recognition and degradation of exogenous mitochondria by the cellular autophagic machinery, thereby prolonging their intracellular retention and functional persistence. Moreover, extracellular matrix remodeling in injured lung tissue can form a favorable adhesive microenvironment that may strengthen the interaction between AMV and injured cells, further supporting the efficient delivery of mitochondria and complementing the integrin-dependent targeting process. Taken together, the selective mitochondria delivery mediated by AMV is a multifactorial process, in which the VLA-5/fibronectin interaction acts as one potential contributing mechanism.

In summary, this study provided a insight into strategies for efficient and targeted mitochondria transfer. The development of mitochondrial-enriched biomimetic nanovesicles, along with the elucidation of their underlying mechanisms and therapeutic potential, may inspire new approaches for applying mitochondria-encapsulated nanovesicles as therapeutic agents for disease intervention.

Methods

Ethics declarations

The cell handling protocols were approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Ningbo First Hospital (ethics approval number 2021-R108). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (ethics approval number ZJU20250393) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Cell culture

Human umbilical cord-derived MSC was obtained from Ningbo SinoCell Technology Ltd. The handling protocols were approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Ningbo First Hospital. MSCs were cultured in serum-free medium (BC-T4, Baso) supplemented with 5% serum substitute (UltraGRO™-PURE, Helios). TC-1 cells (BFN60805941, Bluefcell Biotechnology) were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (350000CL, Wisent) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 086150, Wisent) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (CR15140, Cienry Biotechnology). B16F10 cells (iCell-m006, iCell Bioscience) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 319010CL, Wisent) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Preparation of diverse vesicles encapsulating mitochondria

After cell digestion and collection, MSC were resuspended in Ca2+/Mg2+-free PBS (C35800500, Vivacell) to prepare the cell suspension. The suspension was repeatedly extruded through a mini-extruder (LF-1, Avestin) equipped with PC membranes (Whatman) of specific pore sizes to efficiently encapsulate bioactive mitochondria from donor cells. The resulting crude product was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C for twice to remove cell debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the AMV pellet. The pellet was then resuspended in Ca2+/Mg2+-free PBS for immediate use.

Natural MitoEV were collected following a previously reported method46. Conditioned medium from MSC was subjected to differential centrifugation for purification. The medium was first centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris and apoptotic bodies, and the supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was collected as the MitoEV fraction.

NV were generated by extruding MSC through PC membranes of different pore sizes as described in reported studies21,47. After collection, MSC were resuspended in PBS and repeatedly extruded through an extruder loaded with PC membranes of varying pore sizes (5, 1, and 0.4 μm) to acquire the NV derived from MSC.

Lipo-mito was prepared according to a documented method32. Briefly, 100 mg lecithin and 20 mg cholesterol were dissolved in 10 mL ethanol. The solution was transferred to a flask, and the solvent was completely evaporated using a rotary evaporator to form a lipid film on the inner wall. Subsequently, 5 mL of PBS containing Free-mito was added in the flask to fully hydrate the lipid film. The suspension was then extruded through a mini-extruder to efficiently load Free-mito. The product was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the Lipo-mito pellet.

Cell membrane was isolated and purified from MSC according to previously published protocols32. Equal volumes of Free-mito suspension and cell membrane suspension were thoroughly mixed for 10 min and then extruded using a mini-extruder to enable cell membrane encapsulation of Free-mito. The product was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the CM-mito pellet.

Isolation of Free-mito

Intact and purified mitochondria were isolated from donor cells using a commercial mitochondrial extraction kit (SM0020, Solarbio). Briefly, MSC were resuspended in 1 mL of pre-cooled lysis buffer and ground 40 times on ice at 0 °C. The cell homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged again under the same conditions. The resulting supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain a crude mitochondrial pellet. The pellet was resuspended in 0.5 mL of washing buffer and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then centrifuged again at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain a high-purity mitochondrial pellet, which was resuspended in Ca2+/Mg2+-free PBS for use.

Characterization of AMV

For the immunogold TEM characterization of AMV, vesicle samples loaded with mitochondria were firstly immersed in mixed fixative solution containing 2% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime) and 0.1% glutaraldehyde (G5882, Sigma), followed by fixation at room temperature for 8 h. After fixation, samples were permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 (648466, Millipore) for 15 min to improve the permeability of lipid membranes. Subsequently, the above samples were incubated with 0.1% BSA solution at room temperature for 30 min to block non-specific antigen-binding sites. The blocked vesicle samples were then incubated with primary TOM20 antibody (ET1609-25, Huabio) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS buffer, FluoroNanogold-conjugated secondary antibody (2003-1ML, Nanoprobes) was added, and samples were incubated at room temperature for 2 h to complete immunolabelling. Subsequent to immunostaining, samples underwent post-fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (G5882, Sigma), followed by silver enhancement treatment to amplify signals of colloidal gold particles. The processed samples were subjected to serial dehydration, infiltration and resin embedding, then polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome, and ultrastructural imaging and observation were finally conducted by TEM (Tecnai G2 Spirit 120 kV, Thermo FEI).

Mitochondria and vesicle membrane structures of AMV were labeled through conventional fluorescent dye staining and specific immunofluorescence labeling strategies. For chemical dye labeling, mitochondria in AMV were labeled with 200 nM MitoTracker Green (C1048, Beyotime), 1× TMRE (C2001S, Beyotime), and 200 nM MitoTracker Deep Red (C1032, Beyotime), while membrane structures were labeled with 10 μM DID (C1039, Beyotime) and 10 μM DIO (C1038, Beyotime). For specific immunolabeling, mitochondrial cargo was probed with a primary antibody targeting the outer mitochondrial membrane biomarker of TOM20 (ET1609-25, Huabio). Samples were subsequently incubated with Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (HA1124, Huabio) to enable specific fluorescent identification of encapsulated mitochondria. Meanwhile, vesicle membrane integrity was specifically marked using APC-conjugated CD63 antibody (353008, Biolegend). After staining and immunolabeling, the microstructural characteristics of biological vesicles was examined using a lattice super-resolution light-sheet microscope (SIM3, Lattice), and the mitochondrial loading efficiency in AMV was evaluated by nanoflow cytometry (N30, Nanofcm).

Proteomic analysis

Proteins were extracted from vesicle samples and digested into peptides using trypsin. After desalting, the peptides were analyzed by data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry (Orbitrap, Thermo Scientific). The resulting DIA raw data were imported into DIA-NN software for analysis. Biological information analysis was performed based on the protein quantification results generated by DIA-NN. Subcellular structure annotation of proteins was carried out using WoLF PSORT. Differentially expressed proteins in vesicles from different groups were classified by GO to assess enrichment trends across three dimensions: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function.

Seahorse analysis of mitochondria

The OCR of AMV and Free-mito was measured using the Seahorse energy metabolism analyzer (XFe96, Agilent) according to the previous reported method38,48. To guarantee experimental rigor and valid intergroup comparison, the dosages of AMV and free mitochondria were normalized based on mitochondrial content quantified via MitoTracker Deep Red staining. Briefly, 100 μg AMV and 25 μg free mitochondria were separately resuspended in mitochondrial assay solution (220 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 10 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, and 0.2% (w/v) BSA), and 20 μL of each sample was dispensed per well into the XFe96 microplate. Subsequently, the microplate was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to ensure gentle attachment of AMV and free mitochondria to the well bottom. Post centrifugation, 180 μL prewarmed mitochondrial assay solution was dispensed into each well, followed by sequential supplementation with oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (1.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM). The microplate was subsequently measured by a Seahorse analyzer to assess mitochondrial respiratory function.

WB analysis

WB was conducted to determine the expression levels of target proteins in samples. Briefly, prepared samples were mixed with a specific loading buffer (RIPA lysis buffer + 1× SDS buffer + 1× protease inhibitor) and boiled at 95 °C for 15 min in a heating block. The prepared samples were then loaded onto separation and stacking gels for electrophoresis. Proteins were transferred from the gel to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membrane was blocked at room temperature for 1 h, followed with three times of washing, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies against the target proteins. The following primary antibodies were used: CD105 (10862-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), TSG101 (ET1701-59, 1:2000, Huabio), CD9 (ET1601-9, 1:1000, Huabio), ATP5A1 (ET1703-53, 1:1000, Huabio), NDUFB8 (ET7108-25, 1:2000, Huabio), COX IV (ET1701-63, 1:1000, Huabio), HSP60 (ET1609-45, 1:20,000, Huabio), TOM20 (ET1609-25, 1:5000, Huabio), CYT C (ET1610-60, 1:2000, Huabio), integrin α5 (ET1701-58, 1:1000, Huabio), integrin β1 (ET1601-17, 1:2000, Huabio), Fibronectin (ab268020, 1:1000, Abcam), and GAPDH (ab181602, 1:10,000, Abcam). After washing, the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 1 h with either goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) antibodies (A0216, 1:1000, Beyotime) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) antibodies (A0208, 1:1000, Beyotime). Protein bands were then visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (P0018S, Beyotime) and a multifunctional molecular imager (FUSION FX, Vilber). The optical density (OD) values were quantified using ImageJ software. The uncropped scans have been supplied in the Source Data file.

Proteinase K stimulation assay

Based on previous reports19, the protective effect of vesicles on mitochondria was evaluated using Proteinase K. To ensure a fair comparison between the AMV and Free-mito groups, mitochondrial content was pre-normalized to equivalent fluorescence intensity using a mitochondrial fluorescent probe. Equal protein amounts of AMV were then co-incubated with varying concentrations of Proteinase K (0.2, 2, and 20 μg/mL) on ice for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by incubating the samples with 5 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride for 5 min. WB analysis was then performed to detect changes in the expression levels of membrane-associated proteins (CD9, CD105, and TSG101) and the mitochondrial protein TOM20. Furthermore, AMV and Free-mito were exposed to 0.2 μg/mL Proteinase K for 10 min, followed by measurements of the alterations in ATP levels in the resulting samples.

Detection of mitochondrial activity in AMV under different storage conditions

Mitochondria-loaded vesicles, prepared according to the above protocols, were resuspended in Ca2+/Mg2+-free PBS and stored at either 4 °C or 25 °C. ATP and MMP levels were measured at different storage time points (6, 12, and 24 h). The values detected at each time point were normalized to the initial values at 0 h. Changes in ATP and MMP levels were measured to mirror the variations in mitochondrial activity among different sample groups, allowing the evaluation of how different mitochondrial encapsulation approaches safeguard intact mitochondria.

Protective effect of cytoplasmic components on mitochondria

Cells resuspended in lysis buffer were homogenized 30 times using a glass homogenizer (FGH025, Beyotime). The cell homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g and 4 °C for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and further centrifuged at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to retain residual organelles, and the filtrate was used as the cytoplasmic extract. To deplete proteins, cytoplasmic extracts were treated with Proteinase K (ST533, Beyotime) at 37 °C for 10 min. Free mitochondria isolated from MSC were co-incubated with the differently treated cytoplasmic extracts and stored at room temperature for 6 h. Changes in ATP and MMP levels were then determined to evaluate the protective effects of cytoplasmic proteins on mitochondrial activity.

Mitochondria delivery capacity of AMV in vitro

The in vitro mitochondria delivery capability of AMV was estimated in TC-1 cells that were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in plate wells. As a 24-well plate example, 4 × 104 TC-1 cells per well were cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for full adhesion. To induce cell injury, TC-1 cells were treated with 10 μg/mL BLM in complete medium for 24 h, thereby yielding BLM-TC-1 cells. Mitochondria encapsulated within AMV were fluorescently labeled with MitoTracker Red (200 nM), MitoTracker Green (200 nM), or immunolabeled with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against mitochondrial membrane markers. For immunofluorescence labeling, the primary antibody against the outer mitochondrial membrane protein TOM20 (ET1609-25, Huabio) was used to specifically label AMV-encapsulated mitochondria. Following primary antibody incubation, the Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (HA1124, Huabio) was applied to visualize TOM20-positive mitochondrial signals. Subsequently, AMV were co-incubated with TC-1 or BLM-TC-1 cells at a ratio of 6 μg protein per well. After 6 h of culture, cells were washed twice with PBS, and mitochondria transfer efficiency was observed using CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica) or flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman). A live-cell real-time imaging system (Incucyte, Sartorius) was used to monitor the process of mitochondria transfer at different incubation time points.

The mitochondria delivery efficiency was further characterized by analyzing the human mitochondrial copy number in mouse TC-1 cells using PCR. After co-incubation with AMV for 6 h, the relative mtDNA content in TC-1 or BLM-TC-1 cells was quantitatively calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences were then designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software. The primer sequences for the reference gene were 5′-CCA ATC TGC TCA CAC AGG ATA GAG AGG GCA GG-3′ (forward) and 5′-CCT TGA GGC TGT CCA AGT GAT TCA GGC CAT CG-3′ (reverse). The primer sequences for the human-specific mtDNA were 5′-CCC CAC AAA CCC CAT TAC TAA ACC CA-3′ (forward) and 5′-TTT CAT CAT GCG GAG ATG TTG GAT GG-3′ (reverse).

Impact of mucus layer on the mitochondria delivery capacity of AMV

The effect of mucus layer on the delivery capacity of AMV was evaluated using a transwell system. In brief, transwell plates with a pore size of 3 μm and a membrane diameter of 6.5 mm (FTW073, Beyotime) were used. TC-1 cells were seeded into the lower chamber at a density of 4 × 104 cells/well, and cell injury was induced by BLM treatment as aforementioned. Subsequently, 10 μL of artificial mucus (BZ275, Biochemazone) was added to the upper chamber, followed by the addition of MitoTracker-labeled AMV. The penetration efficiency of AMV was assessed at different incubation time points. After 24 h of co-incubation, TC-1 or BLM-TC-1 cells in the lower chamber were collected, and the proportion of recipient cells that had received exogenous mitochondria was determined using flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman).

Mechanism of selective mitochondria delivery of AMV to injured cells

TC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 104 cells/well in glass-bottom 24-well plates, and BLM-TC-1 cells were established by treatment with BLM as aforementioned. AMV were pre-labeled with integrin α5 antibody (ET1701-58, Huabio) followed by Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (HA1123, Huabio). The labeled AMV were then co-incubated with TC-1 or BLM-TC-1 cells at 6 μg protein/well for 2 h. After removing the supernatant, cells were gently washed with PBS and fixed with paraformaldehyde solution (P0099, Beyotime). TC-1 or BLM-TC-1 cells were subsequently labeled with fibronectin antibody (ET1702-25, Huabio) by co-incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (HA1124, Huabio) was then used as the secondary antibody to fluorescently label fibronectin. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime). Co-localization of AMV with fibronectin on recipient cells was observed and analyzed using CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica).

To further investigate AMV binding to fibronectin in lung tissue, AMV fluorescently labeled with integrin α5 was administered via intratracheal instillation to mice 24 h after establishing the PF model. Healthy mice received an identical dosage of AMV via the same administration route. Two hours post-administration, mice were euthanized, and lung tissues were harvested for frozen sectioning. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime). LEC were labeled with FITC-conjugated CK7 antibody (CL488-15539, Proteintech), and fibronectin on LEC was labeled with primary antibody (ET1702-25, Huabio) followed by Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (HA1123, Huabio). The sections were examined under CLSM, and images were acquired.

For in vitro blocking experiments, BLM-TC-1 cells were pre-incubated with fibronectin antibody (ET1702-25, Huabio) for 1 h to block fibronectin binding. Simultaneously, AMV were treated with integrin α5 antibody (ET1701, Huabio) or integrin β1 antibody (ET1601, Huabio) to block corresponding integrins on the AMV surface. In addition, a broad-spectrum integrin inhibitor, 10 μM GLPG0187 (HY-100506, MCE), was used to fully block integrins on the AMV surface. After co-incubating the treated AMV with BLM-TC-1 cells for 6 h, flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman) was performed to determine changes in the proportion of BLM-TC-1 cells that had received exogenous mitochondria.

Processes of AMV uptake by injured cells

Membrane fusion assay: DID-labeled AMV (10 μM) was co-incubated with BLM-TC-1 cells at 37 °C for 6 h. After washing the cells twice with PBS, cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. CLSM was then used to observe the localization of AMV relative to BLM-TC-1 cells, allowing determination of whether membrane fusion occurred between AMV and the injured cells.

Endocytosis inhibition assay: to investigate the cellular uptake pathways of AMV by BLM-TC-1 cells, specific endocytosis inhibitors were employed. TC-1 cells were seeded at 4 × 104 cells/well in 24-well plates and cultured overnight to allow complete adhesion. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh media supplemented with one of four endocytosis inhibitors (30 μM chlorpromazine, 10 mM methyl-β-cyclodextrin, 500 nM cytochalasin D, or 20 μM ethylisopropylamiloride), followed by1 h of pre-incubation. MitoTracker-labeled AMV were subsequently added, followed by 6 h of co-incubation with the inhibitor-treated cells. After washing twice with PBS, flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman) was performed to assess mitochondria transfer efficiency, thereby elucidating the endocytic pathways involved in AMV uptake.

Intracellular fate of AMV in injured cells

TC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 104 cells/well in glass-bottom 24-well plates. MitoTracker-labeled AMV were co-incubated with TC-1 or BLM-TC-1 cells at a ratio of 6 μg protein/well for 6 h. After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Lysosomes in the recipient cells were labeled with 200 nM Lyso-Tracker Green (C1047S, Beyotime) for 30 min, and cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime) for 10 min. CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica) was used to observe the co-localization of AMV-delivered mitochondria with lysosomes, allowing evaluation of whether AMV could protect mitochondria from lysosomal degradation.

Mitochondrial morphologic observations in injured cells

Mitochondrial morphological changes in injured cells were examined using TEM. Briefly, cells were collected and fixed overnight at 4 °C with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (BL911A, Biosharp). Samples were then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, stained with 2% uranyl acetate, and dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol and acetone. After infiltration and embedding, ultra-thin sections were prepared and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. TEM images were captured, and the area and perimeter of mitochondria in each group were quantified using ImageJ.

Therapeutic effect of AMV on the mitochondrial function of injured cells

In brief, TC-1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and, after achieving complete attachment, cell injury was induced using BLM treatment as aforementioned. AMV were added to BLM-TC-1 cells at a concentration of 6 μg protein per well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Following treatment, MMP levels, ATP levels, ROS, cell viability, apoptosis rate, and intracellular TGF-β levels were measured.

Determination of intracellular ATP levels

ATP levels in cells were measured using a commercial bioluminescence kit (C0056, Beyotime). In brief, the detection substrate was fully dissolved in the detection buffer to prepare the ATP working solution. Samples were mixed with an equal volume of the working solution and incubated for 10 min. Relative light units were then measured using a luminometer (E6080, Promega) to determine ATP levels.

Determination of MMP levels

MMP levels were assessed using an MMP detection kit (C2001S, Beyotime). Briefly, the 1× TMRE staining solution was prepared in detection buffer. Cells were collected, resuspended in the staining solution, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min. After centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min at room temperature, cell pellets were washed twice with pre-warmed culture medium. MMP levels were analyzed using a fluorescence microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek) or flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman).

Examination of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a dihydroethidium (DHE)-based assay kit (S0063, Beyotime). In brief, target cells were collected and resuspended in 1× DHE staining solution, incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were gently washed three times with PBS, and ROS levels were analyzed by flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman).

Assessment of cell viability

Cell viability of injured TC-1 cells after treatment was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, AR1160-500, Boster). In short, a CCK-8 working solution was prepared at a 10:1 ratio of blank medium to CCK-8 solution. The original culture medium was removed, and the working solution was added to the cells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was measured to determine differences in cell viability between groups.

Determination of cell apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was determined following the manufacturer’s instructions (C1062S, Beyotime). In brief, cells were collected and gently resuspended in 195 μL of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer. Afterwards, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL of propidium iodide staining solution were added and followed with a gentle mixture. After incubation for 15 min in the dark at room temperature, the proportion of apoptotic cells was immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (DxFLEX, Beckman).

Establishment of mice PF model

A BLM-induced PF model was established following our previous studies13,14. Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd) were housed in individually ventilated cages (ZJ-4, Suzhou Fengshi Laboratory Equipment) under standard conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 50% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) for 1 week. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol solution at 20 μL/g body weight. After anesthesia, a neck incision was made to expose the trachea, and 50 μL of BLM solution was administered intratracheally using a sterile insulin needle. The incision was then carefully sutured. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University and conducted in accordance with the committee’s guidelines.

Distribution of active mitochondria in fibrotic lung tissue of model mice

Active mitochondria in AMV and Free-mito were labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red. Twenty-four hours after establishing the PF mouse model, mice were intratracheally administered with AMV (100 μg protein) or Free-mito (25 μg protein), ensuring equivalent levels of active mitochondria. Healthy control mice received AMV at the same protein dose via the same administration route. At the designated time points post-administration, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Following cardiac perfusion, major organs, including heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys, were collected, and the distribution of active mitochondria was evaluated using a small-animal in vivo imaging system (IVIS, PerkinElmer). For lung tissue analysis, mice were euthanized 8 h after administration, and the lung samples were collected for frozen sectioning. Cell nuclei and LEC were labeled with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime) and FITC-conjugated CK7 antibody (CL488-15539, Proteintech), respectively. Sections were then imaged using a panoramic scanner (Pannoramic, 3DHISTECH) for observation and data acquisition.

Intervention efficacy of AMV against fibrosis in mice PF model

To evaluate the antifibrotic efficacy of AMV, the intervention was initiated on day 1 after BLM challenge. Each mouse received AMV (100 μg protein) or Free-mito (25 μg protein) via intratracheal instillation. Healthy and PF control groups were administered an equivalent volume of PBS. The treatment period concluded on day 28 post-BLM challenge. During this time, changes in body weight and survival were monitored. At the end of the study, lung tissues were harvested to assess fibrosis-related therapeutic indices.

Histologic evaluation of lung sections

At the end of the treatment period, lung tissues from each group were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime). The lung tissues were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 6 μm-thick slices. H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed to assess pathological changes and collagen deposition in the lung tissues. Images of the stained sections were acquired using a panoramic scanner (Pannoramic, 3DHISTECH), and changes in airspace and collagen content were quantified using ImageJ.

Micro-CT scanning

The imaging of mouse lung was performed using a micro-CT system (U-CT-XUHR, Milabs). In short, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane gas and positioned in the scanning chamber. The lung region was defined, and micro-precision scanning mode was selected. Scanning parameters were set as follows: voltage 50 kV, current 0.21 mA, exposure time 75 ms, and total scan duration 5–10 min. Acquired images were reconstructed to analyze structural changes in the lung tissue across different groups.

Determinations of MMP-9 and TGF-β

The levels of MMP-9 and TGF-β in mice lung tissues were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Briefly, lung tissues from each group were thoroughly homogenized using a fully automatic rapid grinder (JXFSTPR, Jingxin). The homogenates were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. In detail, 100 μL of each sample or standard was added to a 96-well microplate and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After removing the liquid, 100 μL of the biotin-antibody working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. The wells were washed three times with 1× washing buffer, followed by the addition of 100 μL of ABC working solution and incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After five washes with 1× washing buffer, 90 μL of tetramethylbenzidine chromogenic solution was added to each well and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 20 min. Finally, 100 μL of stop solution was added, and the OD at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (ELX800, BioTek). Sample concentrations were calculated by fitting the OD values to the standard curve via regression analysis.

Measurement of hydroxyproline concentrations

Hydroxyproline levels in mouse lung tissues were measured using a hydroxyproline detection kit (BC0255, Solarbio). Upon completing the treatments, lung tissues from each group were collected, weighed, and minced. The tissues were digested in 6 M hydrochloric acid by boiling until no large clumps remained, followed by centrifugation at 24,000 × g for 20 min at 25 °C to remove debris. The supernatant was neutralized with 10 M NaOH, brought to a constant volume with distilled water, and mixed with an equal volume of Reagent 1. After incubation at room temperature for 20 min, 200 μL of Reagent 2 and 400 μL of distilled water were added. The mixture was heated at 60 °C for 20 min, cooled at room temperature for 15 min, and then the absorbance was measured at 560 nm. Hydroxyproline concentrations in the samples were determined by interpolation from a standard curve prepared with known hydroxyproline concentrations.

RNA sequencing analysis

At the end of the treatment, mice in AMV-treated group and non-treatment group were euthanized. Lung tissues were digested to generate single-cell suspensions. Afterwards, LEC were pre-labeled with CD326 antibody and further isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Total RNA was extracted and purified, followed by quality assessment using an automated bioanalyzer (2100, Agilent). After mRNA enrichment, cDNA synthesis, and library quality control, RNA sequencing was performed on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech). Raw sequencing data were filtered using Trimmomatic to improve data quality and reliability. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted based on quantified gene expression levels, and functional enrichment analysis was performed using GO and KEGG databases to identify significantly enriched biological pathways.

Preliminary safety evaluation of AMV in mice model

Healthy mice were randomly assigned to three groups: PBS, Free-mito, and AMV. Each group received intratracheal instillation of Free-mito (25 μg protein/mouse) or AMV (100 μg protein/mouse), ensuring equivalent levels of active mitochondria. The safety evaluation was conducted over a 28-day period, during which body weight was regularly recorded. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized, and major organs including brain, heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney were collected for histological examination using H&E staining. To further investigate immune toxicity associated with intratracheal administration of AMV, lung tissues and serum samples were collected to quantify inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) using ELISA. In addition, blood samples were collected to measure WBC counts, as well as NEU and LYM counts. Serum biochemical analyses were also performed to determine liver function markers (ALT, AST) and kidney function markers (CREA, UREA, UA).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1. Data were quantified and presented as mean ± standard deviation. For statistical analysis of differences between two groups, a two-sided Student’s t test was performed. For comparisons across multiple groups, a one-way ANOVA was used. For determining differences between two groups with two independent variables, a two-way ANOVA was employed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
