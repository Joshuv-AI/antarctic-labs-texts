TopoFair: Linking Topological Bias to Fairness in Link Prediction Benchmarks
Lilian Marey, Mathilde Perez, Tiphaine Viard, Charlotte Laclau

Abstract
Graph link prediction (LP) plays a critical role in socially impactful applications, such as job recommendation and friendship formation. Ensuring fairness in this task is thus essential. While many fairness-aware methods manipulate graph structures to mitigate prediction disparities, the topological biases inherent to social graph structures remain poorly understood and are often reduced to homophily alone. This undermines the generalization potential of fairness interventions and limits their applicability across diverse network topologies. In this work, we propose a novel benchmarking framework for fair LP, centered on the structural biases of the underlying graphs. We begin by reviewing and formalizing a broad taxonomy of topological bias measures relevant to fairness in graphs. In parallel, we introduce a flexible graph generation method that simultaneously ensures fidelity to real-world graph patterns and enables controlled variation across a wide spectrum of structural biases. We apply this framework to evaluate both classical and fairness-aware LP models across multiple use cases. Our results provide a fine-grained empirical analysis of the interactions between predictive fairness and structural biases. This new perspective reveals the sensitivity of fairness interventions to beyond-homophily biases and underscores the need for structurally grounded fairness evaluations in graph learning.



1
Introduction

Graphs are increasingly used in machine learning due to their ability to model complex interactions between individuals.
Over the past fifteen years, research has highlighted ethical concerns in the use of learning algorithms, leading to the emergence of algorithmic fairness as a necessary perspective in all areas of machine learning.
In this context, researchers and practitioners aim to develop algorithms that do not disproportionately favor or disadvantage specific groups.
Such groups are characterized by sensitive attributes (features like age, gender, or race), that hold legal or ethical importance and are particularly susceptible to algorithmic bias.

In graph link prediction (LP), fairness studies have uncovered several issues related to homophily
[
26
]
(the tendency of individuals to connect with similar others), ubiquitous in real-world graphs and leading to undesirable effects such as echo chambers or filter bubbles
[
28
]
.
The evolution from accuracy-focused approaches to fairness-aware methods then became crucial as LP found applications in consequential domains, from job recommendation systems that influence economic opportunity, to friendship suggestion algorithms that shape social circles.
To address the reproduction or amplification of identified biases, the
dyadic fairness
paradigm
[
19
]
has gradually become canonical.
By specifically targeting unfairness at the level of edges, dyadic fairness suggests equitable treatment of both intra-link (connecting entities within the same sensitive group) and inter-link (connecting entities across different sensitive groups), ensuring that the likelihood of predicting a link does not disproportionately favor any group.
By categorizing predicted links according to the sensitive attributes of the nodes they connect, major works aim to balance fairness and LP performance metrics, acting across each step of the predictive pipeline.

That said, measuring unfairness through the lens of model outputs has gradually tended to sideline the study of structural biases inherent in the graphs themselves, biases upon which all fairness techniques aim to operate, whether explicitly or implicitly.
Moreover, the limited research connecting graph structural bias and fairness remains overly focused on homophily. While many studies have rightly examined its impact on both performance
[
29
]
and fairness
[
16
]
, other forms of bias embedded in network topology might also play a critical role.
This problem is further exacerbated by the scarcity of publicly available real-world graphs, which obscures the diversity of structural biases that often constitute primary sources of unfairness.
Beyond enabling more fine-grained model comparisons, quantifying structural bias is essential for the design of fair predictors, as it reveals inherent disparities that influence model behavior.
For example, GNNs often exhibit higher accuracy on high-degree nodes while underperforming on low-degree ones, as shown in
[
34
]
.
Such bias is arguably a fairness concern, particularly when disparities in node degree are unevenly distributed across groups defined by sensitive attributes.
Without careful attention to these structural properties, fairness interventions risk overlooking core sources of unfairness embedded in graph topology, thereby limiting their generalizability.

To address these shortcomings, we propose a graph-specific evaluation framework for fair LP that incorporates a comprehensive taxonomy of structural bias measures. By using carefully designed synthetic graphs, our approach enables a detailed assessment of biases underlying group disparities, which is essential to effectively address fairness in graph-based tasks.

The main contributions of this work are:

1.

A formalization and categorization of structural biases in graphs, providing a more complete understanding of existing disparities within network structures.

2.

A parametrizable graph generation process, enabling the synthesis of graphs that span diverse structural biases across several typical structures used in LP.

3.

A benchmark framework for fair LP that goes beyond standard evaluation practices by explicitly quantifying dependencies between fairness and structural graph properties.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
reviews related work on fairness in link prediction and graph generation. Section
3
introduces our taxonomy of structural biases, followed by the proposed graph generation process. Section
4
presents our benchmarking framework, and Section
5
reports experiments linking structural biases to fairness outcomes.

Related Works

2.1
Linking Graph Topology and Fair Link Prediction

Fairness in graph learning has been pursued through structural modifications, either by altering the adjacency matrix
[
21
,
32
]
, adjusting random-walk transition probabilities
[
30
,
15
]
, or modifying GNN aggregation mechanisms
[
7
]
, all effectively reweighting edges.

The link between topological bias and predictor fairness has received limited formal attention. Notable exceptions address homophily
[
16
,
20
]
, frame structural bias as information access
[
14
,
9
,
2
]
or neighborhood heterogeneity
[
6
]
. In this regard, broader structural biases have long been discussed outside learning frameworks
[
4
]
, and recent work quantitatively relates structural features to predictive performance, albeit without a fairness perspective
[
24
]
.

In this work, we aim to bridge these strands of literature by providing a comprehensive overview of structural biases in graph topology and quantitatively assessing their impact on the fairness of LP models.

2.2
Graph Generation

Classical graph generation models have long provided foundational tools for replicating key network properties, such as community structure, degree distribution, and scale-free topology
[
13
,
27
,
3
]
. However, these models were not designed with fairness considerations in mind and generally lack mechanisms to explicitly control relevant parameters like homophily or sensitive attribute imbalance.

Fairness-aware approaches have therefore emerged, often relying on graph cloning techniques
[
37
,
36
]
, which replicate an existing graph. While such methods have proven effective for addressing privacy concerns, they depend on a source graph, limiting their flexibility in generating networks of arbitrary size or with customizable properties.

Additionally, the Biases Preferential Attachment model
[
33
]
enables homophily tuning during graph generation, providing a tool to study bias in growing networks. However, its reliance on a single growth mechanism limits structural diversity, making it less suitable for exploring the broader range of biases relevant to fairness evaluation.

Finally, learning-based generators such as GenCAT
[
23
]
have been proposed to synthesize graphs with controllable community structures and attribute distributions. While effective for realistic graph synthesis, such approaches are less suited for controlled fairness evaluation, where explicit parameter tuning and interpretable structural mechanisms are preferable.

Here, we aim to introduce a simple, non-learning-based graph generation method tailored to the study of structural biases, while preserving typical topological patterns observed in LP tasks.

2.3
Existing Benchmarks

Benchmarking efforts in graph learning have primarily focused on establishing standardized evaluation protocols for classical models, mostly relying on a few real-world datasets
[
31
,
18
,
8
]
. These studies have been valuable for ensuring consistent comparisons in LP, but their dependence on a narrow set of graphs limits their ability to capture the structural biases that are critical for fairness assessment. Such biases are often complex and heterogeneous, and fairness-aware methods are specifically designed to address these subtle dependencies.

Benchmarking graph models on synthetic data was first introduced for classical node classification algorithms
[
22
]
, with the goal of enabling more robust performance assessments.
We consider this direction particularly valuable, as it not only allows for more reliable model ranking in controlled settings, but also helps reveal the dependencies of model behavior on specific graph characteristics. In the context of fairness, however, this approach could be further enriched, as the considered dimensions of variation in the paper (class imbalance, homophily, attribute distribution, and graph size) are relatively high-level and do not sufficiently capture the finer-grained structural biases that fairness-oriented methods aim to mitigate.

To systematically evaluate fairness in LP, we propose a unified taxonomy of structural bias measures (Table
1
) that integrates classical graph metrics
[
4

with recent fairness-oriented ones. Covering node-level properties like neighborhood diversity and centrality, as well as group-level patterns such as homophily and information imbalance, our taxonomy synthesizes these measures into a coherent framework distinguishing local versus global, and topological versus flow-based biases. This broader view highlights less-studied biases like heterogeneity and information unfairness. The taxonomy also serves as the foundation for our graph generation method, which creates graphs with controlled bias variation to enable rigorous fairness evaluation in LP.

Node-level Measures

To capture local structural disparities between sensitive groups, we analyze node-level metrics reflecting different dimensions of prominence and neighborhood composition. Centrality measures such as closeness, betweenness, and prestige quantify a node’s influence within the network, while neighborhood-based metrics like degree, constraint, density, and heterogeneity describe local connectivity and diversity.
Each metric captures distinct aspects of bias that can affect fairness in LP. For example, differences in degree centrality can cause unequal chances of forming new links across groups, while heterogeneity influences whether nodes connect primarily within their own group or across groups, impacting exposure and representation. Focusing solely on common metrics like assortativity or degree risks overlooking these nuances, which may underlie unfair disparities in predicted links.
To quantify bias from these measures, we define the normalized difference in expected values across sensitive groups as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Node-level Disparity)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a graph,
S\in\{0,1\}
a sensitive attribute, and
M:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
a node-level measure (e.g., node degree).
The bias associated with
M
is defined as:

\omega_{M}(\mathcal{G})=\frac{\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{V})\,|\,S=0]-\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{V})\,|\,S=1]}{\mathbb{E}[M(\mathcal{V})]}.

Positive values indicate an advantage for the non-sensitive group, while
\omega_{M}(\mathcal{G})=0
corresponds to parity.

This unified framework allows us to evaluate disparate structural advantages consistently across metrics.

Beyond static topology, we include flow-based node measures such as effective resistance and information control
[
2
]
, which model the dynamics of information dissemination via random walks. These dynamic measures capture biases in how information spreads locally, identifying disparities not evident through classical centrality or neighborhood statistics. For instance, two groups might have similar average degree but differ in effective resistance, indicating differences in accessibility or resilience in information flow.

Graph-level Bias

Graph-level measures complement node-level metrics by capturing global structural disparities between sensitive groups. While assortativity quantifies homophily, i.e., the tendency of nodes to connect within their own group, other metrics reveal additional dimensions of group separation and influence. For instance, the average distance (
avg mixed dist
) between groups reflects how closely nodes from different groups are connected, which can limit cross-group link formation. The power law exponent ratio compares degree distributions across groups, indicating whether one group disproportionately holds hub positions critical for network connectivity
[
3
]
. Information unfairness
[
14
]
measures differences in how information propagates across groups, highlighting dynamic biases beyond static structure.

This taxonomy provides a comprehensive framework to characterize structural biases relevant to fairness in graph learning. It highlights key structural parameters that affect these biases and sets the stage for graph generation methods designed to create synthetic graphs with varied bias profiles. In the next section, we present such a generation method, enabling controlled evaluation of fairness in link prediction.

Benchmarking Framework

We design a comprehensive benchmarking framework to evaluate LP algorithms under a wide range of structural biases. This framework consists of three main components: 1) generation of realistic synthetic graphs exhibiting diverse structural biases; 2) training and evaluation of LP models on these graphs; and 3) systematic analysis of the relationships between structural biases and fairness outcomes.

4.2
Training and Evaluation Pipeline

We frame LP as a recommendation task where each node is suggested
k=10
new connections. Edges are split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. LP models are trained on the training graph and evaluated by ranking candidate edges for each node based on predicted existence probabilities.

We evaluate classical embedding-based models such as Node2Vec
[
10
]
(N2V), Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
[

]

[

, Debayes
[
5

, CrossWalk
[
15

, FairAdj
[

, and FLIP
[
25
]
. Performance is measured via HitRank and AUC; fairness is assessed through Statistical Parity and Equal Opportunity
[
30

While these methods are not recent in terms of publication date, they represent the principal body of work addressing fairness in LP, an area that has received comparatively limited attention relative to the more extensively studied problem of fair node classification, as noted in
[
35
]
. Consequently, our study offers a comprehensive overview of existing approaches in this domain.

4.3
Estimating LP Algorithms Dependencies to Structural Bias

To explore the relationship between structural bias and fairness, we generate a corpus of over 1,000 graphs for each use case using our fairness-aware BA extensions, varying sensitive attribute imbalance
\alpha
and homophily levels
\beta
. Each graph is characterized by bias measures from our taxonomy.

We then train selected LP algorithms on these graphs, recording predictive performance and fairness metrics on test edges. This process is repeated across
5
train/test splits to ensure robustness. This systematic framework provides a controlled environment to investigate the impact of structural biases on LP fairness and accuracy. Using the generated graph corpus and recorded metrics, we pose the following research questions:

RQ1
: To what extent do topological biases affect the fairness of classical LP methods?

RQ2
: How sensitive are fairness-aware LP methods to structural biases?

RQ3
: Are fairness-aware methods robust when homophily is fixed but other structural biases vary?

The results reveal clear differences across the three use cases, each exhibiting distinct patterns of variability. In the

These findings confirm that fairness-aware methods respond differently to various types of topological bias, with each use case presenting unique challenges in balancing accuracy and fairness. Methods like Crosswalk and Fairwalk, which rely on biased random walks over node embeddings, may be more robust to certain structural variations. In contrast, models such as DeBayes and FairAdj, which incorporate structural information more explicitly, can be more sensitive to graph density or connectivity patterns. Importantly, our graph generation framework enables systematic variation of these structural properties within each scenario, providing detailed insights into the strengths and limitations of current fairness-aware approaches.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we formalized structural bias concepts relevant to fairness in graph learning and introduced a graph generation framework that models real-world structures while enabling controlled exploration of diverse biases. This framework not only facilitates systematic study but also provides a practical tool for benchmarking and developing fairness-aware algorithms under varying structural conditions. By evaluating classical and fairness-aware link prediction methods on these graphs, we uncovered complex, use-case-specific interactions between fairness and structural bias. Our findings reveal that fairness interventions do not uniformly address all biases, highlighting the need for tailored strategies.

Overall, our study emphasizes that fairness analysis must extend beyond homophily to fully capture bias in graphs. Future work could generalize bias definitions to multivalued and multivariate sensitive attributes and adapt the graph generation process accordingly.

Appendix A
Formal Definitions of Structural Bias Measures

We detail here the mathematical formalization of structural bias measures defined in Section 3.1.

In the following, we denote an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
where
\mathcal{V}
is the set of nodes and
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{E}
is the set of edges.
We assume
S:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
assigns to each node a sensitive attribute value (
1
for sensitive,
0
for non-sensitive), and denote
\mathcal{V}_{0}
and
\mathcal{V}_{1}
the sets of non-sensitive and sensitive nodes, respectively.

More, for
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
, and for
V\subset\mathcal{V}
:

•

\sigma(v,v^{\prime})
is the set of shortest paths between
v
and
v^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{v,v^{\prime}}
is their length.

•

\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}
and
x_{max}
the corresponding eigenvector.

•

\mathcal{G}_{V}
is the sub-graph of
\mathcal{G}
induced by
V
nodes.

•

N(v)
is the set of
v
neighbors.

•

d(\mathcal{G})
is the classical graph density of
\mathcal{G}
.

A.1
Node-based Measures

In this section, we provide the formulas for node-based measures.
Hence, all measures of bias take a node (denoted by
u
) as input.

\text{{closeness}}(u)=\frac{|\mathcal{V}|-1}{\sum\limits_{v\in\mathcal{V},v\neq u}\sigma_{u,v}}

\text{{betweenness}}(u)=\sum\limits_{v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{|\{p\in\sigma(v,v^{\prime})|u\in p\}|}{|\sigma(v,v^{\prime})|}

\text{{prestige}}(u)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum\limits_{v\in N(u)}x_{max,v}

\textsc{degree}(u)=|N(u)|

\textsc{constraint}(u)=\sum_{v\in N(u)}\left|N(v)\right|

\textsc{density}(u)=1-d(\mathcal{G}_{N(u)\cup\{u\}})

\textsc{heterogeneity}(u)=1-2\left|\left(\frac{1}{\left|N(u)\right|}\sum_{v\in N(u)}S(v)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\right|

Effective Resistance Measures
[
2
]

The effective resistance is defined
\forall u,v\in\mathcal{V}
as
\mathcal{R}_{uv}=(e_{u}-e_{v})L^{\dagger}(e_{u}-e_{v})^{T}
, where
e_{u}
is the unit vector with a 1 value at
u
-th index and zero elsewhere, and
L^{\dagger}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of the graph’s Laplacian.
Then, the
strength
of a node
u
can be computed with three different ways:

R_{tot}(u)=\sum\limits_{v\in V}\mathcal{R}_{uv},

R_{diam}(u)=\max\limits_{v\in V}\mathcal{R}_{uv},

B_{R}(u)=\sum\limits_{v\in N(u)}\mathcal{R}_{uv}.

For aggregating these node-based measures, the authors use the following function:

\Delta_{R}(\mathcal{G})=\Big|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{0}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{0}}R(u)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{1}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}R(u)\Big|

with
R\in\{R_{tot},R_{diam},B_{R}\}
, defining
isolation
,
diameter
and
control
, respectively.

A.2
Graph/Group measures

Here, we give the formula for group measures, which apply directly on
\mathcal{G}
.

\text{{assortativity}}(\mathcal{G})=\frac{Tr(\textbf{e})-\sum_{i,j}\textbf{e}_{i,j}}{1-\sum_{i,j}\textbf{e}_{i,j}},

where
e
is a matrix of size 2, with
\textbf{e}_{0,0}
(resp.
\textbf{e}_{1,1}
) representing the proportion of edges connecting two sensitive (respectively non-sensitive) nodes, and
\textbf{e}_{1,0}=\textbf{e}_{0,1}
representing each half of the proportion of edges connecting a sensitive and a non-sensitive node.

\text{{avg mixed dist}}(\mathcal{G})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{0}||\mathcal{V}_{1}|}\sum\limits_{v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{0}\times\mathcal{V}_{1}}\sigma_{v,v^{\prime}}

\text{{power exp}}=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{0}}.

where
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}
are the exponent of the degree distribution of non-sensitive and sensitive power law of (non-)sensitive nodes.

Then Information Unfairness
[
14
]
can be written as

\displaystyle\text{{info unfairness}}=\text{max}

\displaystyle(\text{dist}(D_{00},D_{01}),

\displaystyle\text{dist}(D_{00},D_{11}),

\displaystyle\text{dist}(D_{11},D_{01}))

where
D_{s_{1},s_{2}}=\{A_{uv}|S(u)=s_{1},S(v)=s_{2},u\neq v\}
, with
A_{uv}
being the probability of random walk information flowing from node
u
to node
v
.

B.1
Real-world datasets

We provide hereafter a more detailed description of the three scenarios to which we compare the generated graphs.

Polblogs (
Opinion
)

It consists of networks of opinion blogs, where the sensitive attribute is defined as the political affiliation of bloggers (left wing and right wing).
LP here refers to blog recommendations to bloggers and raises challenges with fairness, such as filter bubbles constraining users within narrow opinion spectrum.

Facebook (
Fiendship
)

It is an ego network of friendship among Facebook members, with the sensitive attribute being the users’ gender.
Common LP tasks in these types of networks include recommending friends or professional connections.
The natural tendency for homophily in social relationships can exacerbate existing societal biases, leading to the amplification of discriminatory outcomes, such as the perpetuation of gender or racial biases in professional opportunities, or the segregation of communities within the network.

Collab (
Collab
)

The network dataset represents scientific collaborations among researchers on the HAL platform, from 2017 to 2022, where edges are co-authorship in research papers and the sensitive attribute is the researcher’s gender (name-based association, according to INSEE).
Note that sensitive attributes in these networks could also include factors such as nationality, or institutional affiliation.
The LP task involves suggesting potential collaborators or recommending relevant content, which is susceptible to fairness concerns, as biases in collaboration suggestions can result in the marginalization of underrepresented groups, reinforcing existing disparities in access to resources and opportunities.
