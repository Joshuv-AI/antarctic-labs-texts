Overspecified Mixture Discriminant Analysis: Exponential Convergence, Statistical Guarantees, and Remote Sensing Applications
Arman Bolatov, Alan Legg, Igor Melnykov, Amantay Nurlanuly, Maxat Tezekbayev, Zhenisbek Assylbekov

Abstract
This study explores the classification error of Mixture Discriminant Analysis (MDA) in scenarios where the number of mixture components exceeds those present in the actual data distribution, a condition known as overspecification. We use a two-component Gaussian mixture model within each class to fit data generated from a single Gaussian, analyzing both the algorithmic convergence of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm and the statistical classification error. We demonstrate that, with suitable initialization, the EM algorithm converges exponentially fast to the Bayes risk at the population level. Further, we extend our results to finite samples, showing that the classification error converges to Bayes risk with a rate n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} under mild conditions on the initial parameter estimates and sample size. This work provides a rigorous theoretical framework for understanding the performance of overspecified MDA, which is often used empirically in complex data settings, such as image and text classification. To validate our theory, we conduct experiments on remote sensing datasets.

Introduction

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA), a fundamental classification approach, dates back to the works of
Fisher 1936
and
Rao 1948
. It assumes that in each class the features are generated from a multivariate Gaussian distribution with its own mean, and all classes share the same covariance matrix. For specifics, let us consider the simplest LDA setup, in which the classes are balanced and the class-conditional distributions are spherical
d
-variate Gaussians
\mathcal{N}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I})
. The celebrated Neyman-Pearson lemma
(
Neyman et al. 1933
)
implies that the Bayes classifier in this case is

\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathrm{sgn}\left[\log\frac{\phi(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}{\phi(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\mu})}\right]
and its misclassification error (Bayes risk) is

\Phi(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|)
(see Section 1.2.1 in
Wainwright 2019
), where
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is the probability density function of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and
\Phi(x)
is the cumulative distribution function of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

In practice, the true value of the parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is unknown, and must be estimated from a finite sample (of size
n
). Using standard concentration inequalities, it is easy to verify that replacing
\boldsymbol{\mu}
with its maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
we can get a finite-sample error bound for the LDA classifier in the form
\Phi(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|)+{O}(\sqrt{d/n})

with high probability over the choice of a training sample.

However, the main problem with LDA when applied to natural data—such as images or texts—is that such data typically consists of subpopulations. For example, consider the well-known MNIST dataset
(
Deng 2012
)
, which consists of handwritten digit examples, where the task is to recognize the digit from the image. If we take one class, say ‘three’, there are examples of a typically written ‘3’, as shown in the top row of the right part of Figure
1
. These correspond to the major blue cluster in the center of the left part of Figure
1
, which shows projections of images from the class ‘three’ on a 2D plane with UMAP
(
McInnes et al. 2018
)
. However, there are also examples of less typically written ‘3’s that correspond to the other clusters.

Figure 1:
Projections of MNIST
(
Deng 2012
)
images of ‘3’ on a 2D plane with UMAP
(
McInnes et al. 2018
)
(left) and examples of images from each cluster (right). Clustering was performed with HDBSCAN
(
Campello et al. 2013
)
.

Thus, we see that the distribution even within one class might consist of several clusters, which a unimodal Gaussian distribution cannot model.

Fortunately, in this case we can replace the Gaussians in each class with mixtures of Gaussians
(
Hastie and Tibshirani 1996
)
. This modification of LDA was dubbed Mixture Discriminant Analysis (MDA) and is widely used due to its greater flexibility.

As of this writing, the work of
Hastie and Tibshirani 1996
has been cited more than 1000 times according to Google Scholar.
Despite this, to the best of our knowledge, all earlier studies on MDA were purely empirical offering no rigorous statistical or algorithmic guarantees for MDA.

An especially important case is when the true number of mixture components is
unknown
, and in practice, this is usually the case. One way to alleviate this problem is to estimate the required number of components before fitting a mixture
(
Gkalelis et al. 2013
)
. An alternative is
over-specification

(
Chen 1995
)
, that is, when during training, obviously more components are used than actually in the data distribution, in the hope that the learning algorithm—usually expectation-maximization
(
Dempster et al. 1977
, EM)
—will fit a mixture in such a way that it will not differ noticeably from a true mixture that has fewer components.

In this work, we consider the scenario of over-specification, when for each class the data is generated from one Gaussian, but we fit an
unbalanced
Gaussian mixture of two components by the EM algorithm. We are interested in both the algorithmic convergence of the EM and the statistical classification error. To distinguish between these, we initially consider the problem at the population level by assuming access to an infinitely large sample. On this front, we demonstrate that the classification error converges to the Bayes risk exponentially fast. Namely, under suitable initialization the EM algorithm takes
O(\log(1/\epsilon))
steps to reduce the classification error to
\Phi(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|)+\epsilon
. Encouraged by this result, we move on to study statistical convergence when learning an MDA classifier from a finite sample. Here we are able to show that the EM algorithm produces an MDA classifier whose classification error is
\Phi(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|)+O(\sqrt{d/n})
with high probability over the choice of a training sample of size
n
, and for this it suffices to take
O(\log(n/d))
steps.

Recently,
Legg et al. 2025
provided MDA error bounds for the
balanced
overspecified two-component Gaussian mixture: they showed that the EM algorithm requires
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n/d})
iterations to learn an MDA classifier with error rate
\Phi(-\lVert\mu\rVert)+O\!\left(\sqrt{d/n}\right)
. Our contribution addresses the
unbalanced
two-component case. We establish exponential population contraction of the misclassification error to the Bayes risk and achieve the same error rate,
\Phi(-\lVert\mu\rVert)+O\!\left(\sqrt{d/n}\right)
, but in only
O(\log(n/d))
EM iterations. In what follows, we state our results formally.

Related Work

The convergence analysis of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm for Gaussian mixture models has undergone significant development in recent years.
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
proposed a framework for characterizing the algorithm’s convergence region with respect to distribution parameters, distinguishing between population-level analysis and the sample-based implementation used in practice. Their results, focused on the correctly specified setting with
k=2
components, covered both balanced and unbalanced mixtures.

A central issue within this framework is the initialization strategy required for convergence to the global optimum.
Klusowski and Brinda 2016
extended the local convergence region identified by
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
for the two-component case, while
Zhao et al. 2020
investigated initialization effects for an arbitrary number of well-separated components.
Daskalakis et al. 2017
proved global convergence in the two-component case with symmetric means about the origin. For
k
well-separated components,
Segol and Nadler 2021
established convergence even when initialization occurs near the midpoint between two clusters, improved estimation error bounds, and obtained similar results for Gradient EM. The convergence rate and local contraction properties of Gradient EM for arbitrary
k
were further analyzed by
Yan et al. 2017
.

Model misspecification has also attracted considerable attention.
Dwivedi et al. 2018
analyzed an underspecified setting where a two-component Gaussian mixture is fitted to data from a three-component mixture, quantifying the resulting bias and examining initialization effects. The benefits of overspecified models have been documented in
Dwivedi et al. 2020b
,
Dwivedi et al. 2020a
,
Chen et al. 2024
, and related work. In particular,
Dwivedi et al. 2020b
and
Dwivedi et al. 2020a
studied the case of fitting two Gaussians to data from a single Gaussian, comparing balanced and unbalanced mixtures. In the sample-based EM, they showed that the unbalanced case attains an
O(1/\sqrt{n})
statistical rate—substantially faster than the
O(n^{-1/4})
rate observed in the balanced case—under both known and estimated isotropic covariances, and also enjoys exponentially faster algorithmic convergence.

In the Bayesian context,
Rousseau and Mengersen 2011
showed that overspecified mixtures tend to yield highly imbalanced component weights, with some components effectively vanishing; removing these can improve the model. Regarding spurious components,
Chen et al. 2024
demonstrated that every local minimum of the negative log-likelihood, including spurious ones, retains structural information about component means. They emphasized the advantages of overspecification over underspecification, contrasting “many-fit-one” with “one-fit-many.”
Dasgupta and Schulman 2000
further advocated deliberate overspecification, suggesting
\frac{\log(k)}{w_{\min}}
initial clusters—where
w_{\min}
is the smallest mixture weight—to accelerate convergence.

While the literature has largely focused on convergence in parameter space, fewer works assess fit quality using the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence.
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2001
derived a statistical rate of
(\log n)^{\kappa}/\sqrt{n}
in Hellinger distance, implying a KL lower bound of
(\log n)^{2\kappa}/n
in the well-specified setting, without addressing algorithmic convergence.
Dwivedi et al. 2018
employed KL divergence for underspecified mixtures, but, to our knowledge, the first investigation of KL bounds for overspecified mixtures appeared in
Xu et al. 2024
, who analyzed the population Gradient EM for
k
-component mixtures with known variances. Our work extends these results to both population and sample-based EM with learned variances in the two-component case, and establishes a sharper algorithmic convergence rate in KL distance than that obtained by
Xu et al. 2024
.

3
Main results

Let
Y
be a Rademacher random variable, i.e.
\Pr[Y=-1]=\Pr[Y=+1]=1/2
, and let
\mathbf{X}
given
Y=y
have a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(y\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I})
. These conditions define the distribution for the pair
(\mathbf{X},Y)
, which we denote by
\mathcal{D}
. Suppose that we approximate each of the Gaussians
\mathcal{N}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I})
by an unbalanced two-component location-scale Gaussian mixture

\mathcal{G}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})=(1-p)\cdot\mathcal{N}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})+p\cdot\mathcal{N}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}),\quad p\neq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}.

(1)

The choice of an unbalanced mixture is motivated by the fact that in this case the EM algorithm converges much faster than in the case of a balanced mixture
(
Dwivedi et al. 2020b
)
.

The parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}
in (
1
) are estimated from a finite sample
\{(\mathbf{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{D}
. Due to symmetry,
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I})
, and thus for parameters estimation it is sufficient to fit a single mixture
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
to the sample
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Specifically, the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is given by

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}\cdot Y_{i},

(2)

while the MLE of
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
can be obtained by the EM algorithm which produces a sequence of parameter estimates
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
. Hence we are dealing with a sequence of the corresponding MDA classifiers

\hat{h}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}+1\quad&\text{if }f(\mathbf{x};\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})>f(\mathbf{x};-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})\\
-1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3)

where
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
is the density of
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
.

By the law of large numbers,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{\mu}
as
n\to\infty
. Similarly, let
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
be the population versions of
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
, i.e.
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
,
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}\sigma^{2}_{t}
as
n\to\infty
, and consider the sequence of the corresponding population-level MDA classifiers

{h}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}+1\quad&\text{if }f(\mathbf{x};{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})>f(\mathbf{x};-{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})\\
-1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4)

Our first result shows that if we set aside sample complexity for a moment and give the EM algorithm access to a sample of infinite size, we get an
exponential
(in
t
) convergence of the MDA classification error rate to the Bayes risk.

Theorem 1
.

For any starting point
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|<\min\left[\sqrt{d\cdot\frac{2+q-\sqrt{8q+q^{2}}}{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}d}}\right]

where
q:=1-\frac{(2p-1)^{2}}{2}\in(0,1)
, the population EM algorithm produces a sequence
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
such that the MDA classifier (
4
) satisfies

\Pr_{\mathbf{X},Y\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[h_{T}(\mathbf{X})\neq Y\right]\leq\Phi(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|)+\epsilon,

for
T\geq c\log(1/\epsilon)
where
c>0
is a constant.

Such fast algorithmic convergence of the EM gives reason to hope that when moving to a sample of finite size, the statistical error will not accumulate quickly. Indeed, our second result shows that the statistical convergence of misclassification error rate to Bayes risk is of order
O(\sqrt{d/n})
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, and let
n\succsim\log^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\left(1/\delta\right)
. Then with any starting point
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|<\min\left[\sqrt{d\cdot\frac{2+q-\sqrt{8q+q^{2}}}{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}d}}\right]
where
q:=1-\frac{(2p-1)^{2}}{2}
, the EM algorithm produces a sequence of parameter estimates
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
such that the MDA classifier (
3
) satisfies

\Pr_{\mathbf{X},Y\sim\mathcal{D}}[\hat{h}_{T}(\mathbf{X})\neq Y]\leq\Phi\left(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|\right)+c_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

(5)

for
T\geq c_{2}\log\frac{n}{\log(1/\delta)}
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

The proof of both results is based on the fact that if we can well approximate the underlying class-conditional distributions (in the sense of Total Variation Distance, TVD), then the classification error will be low (Lemma
6
). Moreover, due to the well-known Pinsker inequality, for convergence in the TVD metric, convergence in the KL distance is sufficient. That is, everything reduces to studying the convergence of the sequence of mixtures
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
(or
\mathcal{G}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
) to the true underlying distribution
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I})
in the KL metric using the EM algorithm, and such a study is the main technical contribution of this work (Section
4
).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
4.1
, we study the convergence in KL distance in the infinite sample size regime. It turns out that for a sequence of EM iterates
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
, the current value of the variance
\sigma^{2}_{t}
is a function of the norm
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|^{2}
, and thus the sequence itself lies on a
d
-dimensional hypersurface. It is noteworthy that on this hypersurface, the expected negative log-likelihood is a
convex radial
function in the vicinity of the optimal point, and it satisfies a so-called
Polyak-Lojasiewicz
inequality. To the best of our knowledge, in the context of the considered setup, we are the first to make these observations and they greatly simplify the study of the convergence of the KL distance between the fitted and true distributions. Next, in Section
4.2
, we extend our result to the finite sample case. Here we rely on the convergence of the parameters themselves to optimal ones. Our result for the finite sample case is novel as well, since in the work of
Dwivedi et al. 2020b
, a similar result was obtained for the case when the variance parameter is known and is not learned from the data, while the work by
Dwivedi et al. 2020a
considers the case where variance is learned (along with location parameter), but a mixture is balanced. In Section
5
we provide proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
. Proofs not included in the main body of the paper are given in Appendix
A
. Finally, in Section
6
, we present our experimental results on remote sensing data.

4
Convergence in KL distance.

Formally, let
\mathbf{Z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{Z}_{n}
be a random sample from a multivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
with mean vector
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and identity covariance matrix
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Suppose that we want to fit an unbalanced two-component location-scale Gaussian mixture

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})=(1-p)\cdot\mathcal{N}(-\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})+p\cdot\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})

(6)

to this sample, assuming
p
is fixed. In this section, with a slight abuse of notation, we will write
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
instead of
\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
.

Let
f(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
be the probability density function of the mixture (
6
). Then the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
is given by

(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{\sigma}^{2})\in\arg\max_{(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(\mathbf{Z}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}).

(7)

There is no closed-form expression for
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\hat{\sigma}^{2})
. In practice, the optimization problem (
7
) is solved by an iterative method such as the EM algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
. Note that the objective function (log-likelihood) in (
7
) is not concave, and accordingly, there is no guarantee that the iterative method will converge to the global optimum.

4.1
Population-Level Analysis

First, we will look at the behavior of the so-called
Population EM
, which is a theoretical construct that allows us to focus on algorithmic complexity without being distracted by sample complexity. Population EM assumes access to the data-generating distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, and instead of the sample-based log-likelihood in (
7
) it optimizes the population log-likelihood

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}):=\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}[\log f(\mathbf{Z};\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})].

(8)

This is done by iteratively applying the following two steps:

•

Expectation step
uses the current estimate
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
to compute the function

Q(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t}):=\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[w(\mathbf{Z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})\log\left(p\phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{Z}-\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\sigma}\right)\right)\right.\\
\left.+(1-w(\mathbf{Z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t}))\log\left((1-p)\phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{Z}+\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\sigma}\right)\right)\right],

where
w(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})=\frac{p}{p+(1-p)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{2\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{z}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\right)}
.

•

Maximization step
solves the optimization problem

(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1},\sigma^{2}_{t+1})\in\arg\max_{(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t}).

One can show that in this special case of fitting the mixture (
6
) to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
by the Population EM, the recurrence relations governing the evolution of the parameters can be written down explicitly (Section
A.1
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[t_{p}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}}{1-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|^{2}}{d}}\right)\mathbf{Z}\right],

(9)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|^{2}}{d},

(10)

where
t_{p}(x):=\frac{p\cdot e^{x}-(1-p)\cdot e^{-x}}{p\cdot e^{x}+(1-p)\cdot e^{-x}}
. As we see, the iterates
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
lie on the hypersurface

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\,\,\sigma^{2}=1-{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}}/{d}\right\},

(11)

and the value of
\sigma^{2}_{t}
is completely determined by the norm
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|
. In this regard, we are interested in the form of the population log-likelihood (
8
) when
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})\in\mathcal{S}
. First of all, we notice that in this case, the population log-likelihood depends on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
only through its norm.

Radial form of the risk function.

Consider the function
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

L(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},1-\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}/d).

(12)

Then
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a
radial
function of
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see Appendix
A.2
). Specifically, let
\theta:=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|
, and define

\ell(\theta):=\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi(1-\theta^{2}/d))+\frac{d+\theta^{2}}{2(1-\theta^{2}/d)}-\E_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[\log\left(c_{p}\left(\frac{\theta Z}{1-\theta^{2}/d}\right)\right)\right].

(13)

Then we have

\ell(\theta)=L(\boldsymbol{\theta})\qquad\text{i.e. the risk depends on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ only via $\theta$}.

(14)

We put a minus sign in front of the population log-likelihood to make
L(\cdot)
have the meaning of a risk function. In this way, maximizing
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
on
\mathcal{S}
is equivalent to minimizing
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which in turn reduces to minimizing
\ell(\theta)
on
\mathbb{R}
.

Population EM operator in one dimension.

According to the EM updates (
9
)–(
10
), it is sufficient to focus on analyzing the evolution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
which is governed by the
population EM operator

M(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[t_{p}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}}{1-\nicefrac{{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}}}{{d}}}\right)\mathbf{Z}\right].

(15)

Thus, for the EM sequence
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
, we have
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=M(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
. However, as we see from (
12
), when maximizing
\mathcal{L}
—or, equivalently, minimizing
L
—by the EM algorithm, the value of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
depends on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
only through
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|
. This means that we are interested in the dynamics of evolution not of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
itself, but of its norm
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|
. In Appendix
A.3
, we show that for the population EM operator
M(\boldsymbol{\theta})
defined by (
15
), we have

\|M(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|=\E_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[t_{p}\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|Z}{1-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}}{d}}\right)Z\right].

(16)

Using the shorthand notation
\theta_{t}:=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|
, and in light of (
16
), the sequence of norms
\theta_{t}
satisfies
\theta_{t+1}=m(\theta_{t})
, where

m(\theta):=\E_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\left[t_{p}\left(\frac{\theta Z}{1-\theta^{2}/d}\right)Z\right].

(17)

Properties of
\ell(\theta)
and
m(\theta)
.

The radiality of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\|M(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|
allows us, instead of studying the population risk (
12
) and population EM operator (
15
), to study the univaritate functions
\ell(\theta)
and
m(\theta)
. Some of their properties are given in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\ell(\theta)
and
m(\theta)
be the functions defined in (
13
) and (
17
), respectively. Then for
\theta\in[0,\theta_{0}]
, where
\theta_{0}<\sqrt{d\cdot\frac{2+q-\sqrt{8q+q^{2}}}{2}}
with
q:=1-\frac{(2p-1)^{2}}{2}
,

1.

m^{\prime}(\theta)<1
,

2.

m(\theta)\leq\rho\cdot\theta
for
\rho:=\frac{1+\theta_{0}^{2}/d}{(1-\theta_{0}^{2}/d)^{2}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{(2p-1)^{2}}{2}\right)\in(0,1)
,

3.

\ell(\theta)
is convex,

4.

[\ell^{\prime}(\theta)]^{2}\geq(1-\rho)\cdot[\ell(\theta)-\ell(0)]
, where
\rho\in(0,1)
is defined in Part
2
.

Part
4
of Lemma
3
is key to establishing the exponential convergence of the mixture (
6
) to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
in the KL distance. This is the so-called Polyak-Lojasiewicz inequality
(
Polyak 1963
;
Lojasiewicz 1963
)
, which is a sufficient condition for
\ell(\theta_{t})-\ell(0)
to converge exponentially to zero if we were to minimize
\ell(\theta)
using the gradient descent method with a suitable choice of step size. Note that the EM algorithm is not identical to gradient descent, but is related to it, as discussed in
Wang et al. 2015
,
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
.

Convergence in KL distance.

We are ready to prove the exponential decay of the KL divergence between the true distribution and the sequence of fitted mixtures.

Theorem 4
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|<\sqrt{d\cdot\frac{2+q-\sqrt{8q+q^{2}}}{2}}
with
q:=1-\frac{(2p-1)^{2}}{2}
, we have

\kl\left[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\parallel\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T},\sigma^{2}_{T})\right]\leq\frac{1}{(1+c)^{T}}\cdot\kl\left[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\parallel\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})\right],

where
c>0
.

Proof.

First of all notice that

\kl\left[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\parallel\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})\right]=L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})-L(\mathbf{0}).

Recall that
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\ell(\theta)
, where
\theta:=\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|
(see (
14
)). Thus, from Lemma
3
part
3
, we have

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})-L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1})=\ell(\theta_{t})-\ell(\theta_{t+1})

\displaystyle\geq\ell^{\prime}(\theta_{t+1})(\theta_{t}-\theta_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(\theta_{t+1})(\theta_{t}-m(\theta_{t})).

(18)

From (
50
), taking into account that
\theta_{t+1}=m(\theta_{t})\leq\rho\theta_{t}<\theta_{t}
, and by convexity of
\ell
, we have

\theta_{t}-m(\theta_{t})=\frac{(1-\theta_{t}^{2}/d)^{2}}{1+\theta_{t}^{2}/d}\cdot\ell^{\prime}(\theta_{t})\geq\underbrace{\frac{(1-\theta_{0}^{2}/d)^{2}}{1+\theta_{0}^{2}/d}}_{c_{1}(\theta_{0},d)}\cdot\ell^{\prime}(\theta_{t+1}).

(19)

Substituting (
19
) into (
18
) and taking into account Lemma
3
part
4
, we have

\ell(\theta_{t})-\ell(\theta_{t+1})\geq c_{1}(\theta_{0},d)\left[\ell^{\prime}(\theta_{t+1})\right]^{2}\geq c_{2}(\theta_{0},d,p)\cdot[\ell(\theta_{t+1})-\ell(0)],

where
c_{2}(\theta_{0},d,p):=c_{1}(\theta_{0},d)-\left(1-\frac{(2p-1)^{2}}{2}\right)
. Rearranging terms and subtracting
\ell(0)
from both sides of the latter inequality we have

\displaystyle\ell(\theta_{t})-\ell(0)\geq\ell(\theta_{t+1})-\ell(0)+c_{2}(\theta_{0},d,p)\cdot[\ell(\theta_{t+1})-\ell(0)]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\ell(\theta_{t+1})-\ell(0)\leq\frac{1}{1+c_{2}(\theta_{0},d,p)}[\ell(\theta_{t})-\ell(0)]

Applying this inequality recursively, we get

\ell(\theta_{T})-\ell(0)\leq\frac{\ell(\theta_{0})-\ell(0)}{(1+c_{2}(\theta_{0},d,p))^{T}}

which completes the proof.
∎

Note that to prove Theorem
4
we did not use the convergence of the parameters themselves
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})
to the optimal
(\mathbf{0},1)
. This happened because the PL inequality (Lemma
3
part
4
) provides a sufficient condition for the rapid convergence of the values of the function
\ell(\theta_{t})
to the optimal value
\ell(0)
.

Finite-Sample Analysis

In this section we show that the EM algorithm takes
O\left(\log(n)\right)
steps to produce an estimate
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
such that

with high probability. More precisely, our key result is the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, and let
n\succsim\log^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\left(1/\delta\right)
. Then with any starting point
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|<\min\left[\sqrt{d\cdot\frac{2+q-\sqrt{8q+q^{2}}}{2}},\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+1/d}}\right]
where

(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
such that

D_{\text{KL}}\left[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\parallel\mathcal{G}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{T})\right]\leq c_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}

(20)

for
T\geq c_{2}\log\frac{n}{\log(1/\delta)}
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Figure
3
reports on empirical verification of the bound (
20
). As we can see, the KL divergence between
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
and
\mathcal{G}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{T})
indeed drops at a rate of
O(1/n)
.

. We consider the case

, and use the starting value

Denote the sample-based averaged log-likelihood in (
7
) as

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(\mathbf{Z}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}).

(21)

When it is maximized by the EM algorithm, the parameter updates can also be written explicitly (as in the case of the population EM, see Appendix
A.5
):

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{p}\left(\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}}{\nicefrac{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}\|^{2}}}{{nd}}-\nicefrac{{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|^{2}}}{{d}}}\right)\mathbf{Z}_{i},

(22)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}\|^{2}}{nd}-\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}\|^{2}}{d}.

(23)

As we can see, the evolution of the sample-based estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
is governed by the operator
M_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as

M_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{p}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}}{\nicefrac{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{2}\|}}{{nd}}-\nicefrac{{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}}}{{d}}}\right)\mathbf{Z}_{i}

Let
Z_{i}:=\nicefrac{{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}}}{{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|}}
, then
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\,\,{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}}\,\,\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. By analogy with the population case, we can move from the operator
M_{n}
to the function of one variable
m_{n}(\theta)
given by

m_{n}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{p}\left(\frac{\theta Z_{i}}{\nicefrac{{U_{nd}}}{{nd}}-\nicefrac{{\theta^{2}}}{{d}}}\right)Z_{i},

where
U_{nd}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}\|^{2}
is a chi-square random variable with
nd
degrees of freedom.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5
, the following perturbation bound relates the sample-based EM operator to its population-level counterpart:

\Pr\left[\sup_{\theta\in[0,r]}|{m}_{n}(\theta)-{m}(\theta)|\leq cr\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(24)

The proof idea of the bound (
24
) is due to
Dwivedi et al. 2020b
and
Dwivedi et al. 2020a
, and utilizes conventional methods for establishing Rademacher complexity bounds. Initially, we employ symmetrization using Rademacher variables and apply the Ledoux-Talagrand contraction inequality. Subsequently, we use findings related to the chi-square distribution and carry out computations related to Chernoff bounds to achieve the intended outcome. Full proof is given in Appendix
A.6
.

Thanks to the strict contractivity of the population EM operator (Lemma
3
part
2
) and the perturbation bound (
24
), we can show that the sequence of EM iterates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}=M_{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
satisfies

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}\|\leq\frac{c\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|}{1-\rho}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}

(25)

for
T\geq\log_{1/\rho}\left((1-\rho)\sqrt{\frac{n}{\log(1/\delta)}}\right)
with probability at least
1-\delta
. The proof is given in Appendix
A.7
. This is a new result. In the work of
Dwivedi et al. 2020b
, a similar result was obtained for the case when the variance
\sigma^{2}
is known and is not learned from the data. The work by
Dwivedi et al. 2020a
considers the case where
\sigma^{2}
is learned (along with
\boldsymbol{\theta}
), but a mixture is balanced (i.e. when
p=1/2
in (
6
)).

We need the following lower bound for
m(\theta)
to upperbound the derivative
\ell^{\prime}(\theta)
later: for
\theta\in\left[0,\left(\sqrt{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}d}\right)^{-1}\right]
, we have (see Appendix
A.8
)

m(\theta)\geq 4p(1-p)\left(1-\frac{4\theta^{2}}{(1-\theta^{2}/d)^{2}}\right)\theta.

(26)

Finally, we are ready to prove our key result on convergence in KL distance for the finite-sample case.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

First of all, notice that

\kl\left[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\parallel\mathcal{G}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})\right]=\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\log\frac{\phi(\mathbf{Z})}{f(\mathbf{Z};\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})}\right]=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})-\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}),

(27)

where
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
is the population-level expected log-likelihood (
8
). Note that the first term in the update rule for
\sigma^{2}
in (
23
) is not exactly one (but concentrates around one since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{nd}
), and thus the point
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
does not lie on the hypersurface
\mathcal{S}
defined by (
11
). This does not allow us to move from
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
directly to
\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|^{2}/d\right)
. However, let us consider their difference

\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|^{2}/d\right)-\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}\right)=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\sigma^{2}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\xi\right)\left(\frac{U}{nd}-1\right),

(28)

where
U:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{Z}_{i}\|^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{nd}
, and
\xi
is a point between
\frac{U}{nd}-\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{2}}{d}
and

\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}:=\left\{\left|\frac{U}{nd}-1\right|\leq\epsilon\right\}

\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}]\geq 1-2e^{-\epsilon^{2}nd/8}
by standard chi-square tail bounds. Conditional on
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
, the point
\xi
is in the interval
1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|^{2}/d\pm\epsilon
. Thus, by direct calculation and elementary inequalities,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\sigma^{2}}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\xi\right)\right|=\left|\frac{d}{2\xi}-\frac{d+\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}_{t}}{2\xi^{2}}+\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}}{\xi}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{d}{2\left(1-\frac{\hat{\theta}_{t}^{2}}{d}\right)}-\frac{d+\hat{\theta}^{2}_{t}}{2\left(1-\frac{\hat{\theta}_{t}^{2}}{d}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{\hat{\theta}_{t}^{2}}{d}\right)^{2}}\E\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{t}{Z}}{1-\frac{\hat{\theta}^{2}}{d}}\right)\hat{\theta}_{t}{Z}\right]\right|+c\epsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{t}\left[\hat{\theta}_{t}-m(\hat{\theta}_{t})\right]}{(1-\hat{\theta}^{2}_{t}/d)^{2}}+c\epsilon\leq\frac{\left(1-4p(1-p)\left\{1-\frac{4\theta^{2}_{0}}{(1-\theta^{2}_{0}/d)^{2}}\right\}\right)\cdot\hat{\theta}_{t}^{2}}{(1-\theta_{0}^{2}/d)^{2}}\precsim\hat{\theta}^{2}_{t}+\epsilon,

(29)

where
\hat{\theta}_{t}=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|
, and we used the lower bound for
m(\theta)
from (
26
).
Solving
2e^{-\epsilon^{2}(nd)/8}=\delta
for
\epsilon
, and then combining (
28
) and (
29
), we have

\left|\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|^{2}/d\right)-\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t}\right)\right|\precsim\hat{\theta}^{2}_{t}\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{nd}}+\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{nd}

(30)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Thus, instead of
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{t})
we can focus on
\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},1-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\|^{2}/{d})=:-L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
.

By radiality of
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(see (
14

\ell(\theta)
(Lemma
3
part

), we have

L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})-L(\mathbf{0})=\ell(\hat{\theta}_{t})-\ell(0)\leq\ell^{\prime}(\hat{\theta}_{t})\cdot\hat{\theta}_{t},

(31)

By direct calculation (see (
50
)) and using (
26
), the derivative
\ell^{\prime}(\hat{\theta}_{T})
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}(\hat{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\hat{\theta}_{t}^{2}/d}{(1-\hat{\theta}^{2}_{t}/d)^{2}}\cdot(\hat{\theta}_{t}-m(\hat{\theta}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\theta_{0}^{2}/d}{(1-\theta_{0}^{2}/d)^{2}}\cdot\left(1-4p(1-p)\left\{1-\frac{4\theta^{2}_{0}}{(1-\theta^{2}_{0}/d)^{2}}\right\}\right)\cdot\hat{\theta}_{t}.

(32)

From (
31
), (
32
), we obtain

L(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})-L(\mathbf{0})\precsim\hat{\theta}_{t}^{2}

Let
n\succsim\log^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\left(1/\delta\right)
. Then by (
25
),
\theta_{T}\precsim\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}
for

with probability at least

\kl\left[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\parallel\mathcal{G}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{T})\right]\precsim\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\|^{2}\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}

5
Proof of Main Results

We start with an auxiliary result on binary classification. Let
(\mathbf{X},Y)
be a pair of random variables taking their respective values from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{-1,+1\}
. Define a function

\eta(\mathbf{x}):=\Pr[Y=+1\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}],

which is sometimes called the
a posteriori probability
. Any function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{-1,+1\}
defines a
classifier
. The misclassification error rate of
h
is

\Err[h]:=\Pr[h(\mathbf{X})\neq Y],

which is minimized by the Bayes classifier
(
Devroye et al. 1996
, Theorem 2.1)

h^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}+1\quad&\text{if }\eta(\mathbf{x})>1-\eta(\mathbf{x})\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The misclassification error rate of the Bayes classifier
\Err[h^{\ast}]
is referred to as Bayes risk. In the case of balanced classes, i.e.
\Pr[Y=-1]=\Pr[Y=+1]=1/2
, if the class-conditional densities
f_{-}(\mathbf{x})
and
f_{+}(\mathbf{x})
are not known and are approximated by
\tilde{f}_{-}(\mathbf{x})
and
\tilde{f}_{+}(\mathbf{x})
, respectively, it is natural to use the plug-in classifier

h(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}+1\quad&\text{if }\tilde{f}_{+}(\mathbf{x})>\tilde{f}_{-}(\mathbf{x})\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

to approximate the Bayes classifier. The next lemma states that if
\tilde{f}_{+}(\mathbf{x})
is close to
f_{+}(\mathbf{x})
and
\tilde{f}_{-}(\mathbf{x})
is close to
f_{-}(\mathbf{x})
in
L_{1}
-sense, then misclassification error of
h(\mathbf{x})
is near the Bayes risk.

Lemma 6
.

Assume that the true class-conditional densities
f_{-}(\mathbf{x})
,
f_{+}(\mathbf{x})
are approximated by the densities
\tilde{f}_{-}(\mathbf{x})
,
\tilde{f}_{+}(\mathbf{x})
, respectively. Let
\Pr[Y=+1]=\Pr[Y=-1]=\frac{1}{2}
. Then for the plug-in classifier
h
defined above

\Err[h]-\Err[h^{\ast}]\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{-}(\mathbf{x})-\tilde{f}_{-}(\mathbf{x})\right|d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{+}(\mathbf{x})-\tilde{f}_{+}(\mathbf{x})\right|d\mathbf{x}.

In the context of over-specified MDA that we consider in this work, the true class-conditional distributions are
\mathcal{N}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I})
and they are approximated by unbalanced Gaussian mixtures

\mathcal{G}(\pm\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2})
defined in (
1
). The sequences of population-level and sample-based MDA classifiers fit by the EM algorithm are specified in (
4
) and (
3
), respectively.
Notice that the Bayes classifier in this case is the LDA classifier

h^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if }\phi(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})>\phi(\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\mu})\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and its error rate (i.e., Bayes risk) is

\Err[h^{\ast}]=\Phi(-\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|),

(33)

see Section 1.2.1 in
Wainwright 2019
.

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Since
\sigma^{2}_{t}
is fully determined by
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
(
23
), we will write
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{t})

Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

\displaystyle\tv\left[\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{I}),\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{T})\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\E_{\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})}\left[\log\frac{\phi\left(\mathbf{Z}\right)}{f(\mathbf{Z};\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{T})}\right]}

7
Conclusion

In this work, we provided a rigorous theoretical analysis of Mixture Discriminant Analysis (MDA) in the overspecified regime, where the number of Gaussian components used in model fitting exceeds that in the true data-generating process. Focusing on the practically relevant case of fitting an unbalanced two-component Gaussian mixture to data from a single Gaussian per class, we established sharp bounds on the excess classification error over the Bayes risk.

At the population level, we proved that, under suitable initialization, the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm achieves exponential convergence of the classification error to the Bayes risk, requiring only
{O}(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations to reach accuracy
\epsilon
. In the finite-sample setting, we derived non-asymptotic high-probability bounds showing that the misclassification error converges to the Bayes risk at the optimal
{O}(n^{-1/2})
rate, with only
{O}(\log(n/d))
EM iterations needed. The analysis hinges on controlling the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the fitted and true class-conditional densities, leveraging a Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality on a hypersurface where the EM iterates reside.

Our results extend and refine existing theory for overspecified mixtures by incorporating both learned variance parameters and unbalanced component weights, leading to faster algorithmic convergence and optimal statistical rates. This provides a principled justification for the empirical success of overspecified MDA in complex classification problems, such as those arising in image or text analysis.
