Economic cost analysis of severe acute malnutrition treatment in two districts of Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Getu Gizaw, Idris Siraj, Mhiret Teshome Adimassie, Teshome Girma Feyisa, Muluneh Girma Mekonnen, Tesfatsion Woldetsadik, Negalign Berhanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere acute malnutrition (SAM) affects millions of children globally, and treatment coverage remains below 30% in many settings, including Ethiopia. Although the Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) program has expanded nationwide, persistent service gaps remains, partly due to insufficient evidence for accurate cost estimation and budgeting. To address this gap, this study estimated the total economic cost, including provider-side financial and caregiver costs, of treating SAM through the CMAM program in two operational areas of Action Against Hunger, Ethiopia, and identified major cost drivers.MethodsA cross-sectional cost analysis was conducted in Girawa district (Oromia Region) and Adadle district (Somali Region) in 2024 from a societal perspective, including both provider-side financial and caregiver costs. Provider-side financial costs include personnel, medical supplies, therapeutic foods, equipment, transport and storage, and training and supervision. Caregiver costs include both direct costs (transport, food, and hospitalization-related expenses) and indirect costs (lost income and coping strategies). Provider-side financial costs were extracted and estimated using the FANTA CMAM costing tool. The tool automatically generated total cost per district, and the provider-side financial cost per SAM child was calculated by dividing total annual provider-side financial expenditure by the number of SAM cases treated. Caregiver costs were collected through structured exit interviews, analyzed using Excel, and summarized as mean cost per treatment episode. The mean caregiver cost was added to the provider-side financial cost to estimate the total economic cost per SAM child.ResultsThe total annual provider-side financial cost for SAM treatment was USD 386,598 in Girawa district and USD 289,433 in Adadle district. Supplies, particularly RUTF and therapeutic milk, constituted the largest cost category in Girawa (57.7%), whereas repeated SAM-specific training and supervision represented the major share in Adadle (40.9%). The total economic cost per SAM child, including caregiver expenses, was USD 224.65 in Girawa and USD 378.75 in Adadle.ConclusionThere is substantial variation in the cost of delivering SAM treatment across districts, highlighting the importance of context-specific district-level cost analyses. SAM-specific supplies and training intensity were the primary cost drivers. Background

Acute malnutrition remains a major global public health challenge, affecting an estimated 42.8 million children under five years of age worldwide, equivalent to a 6.6% prevalence [1]. Despite decades of programmatic investment, the burden of acute malnutrition remains high and threaten progress toward Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2.2, which aims to reduce the prevalence of acute malnutrition to less than 3% by 2030 [2]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), limited access to treatment, resource constraints, and weak health systems continue to hinder the effective delivery and coverage of malnutrition interventions [3].

The Community-Based Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) model has substantially improved access to treatment by decentralizing care and enabling outpatient management of uncomplicated SAM [4–6]. Under the CMAM model, children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) rely on specialized nutritious foods (SNFs), such as ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) for uncomplicated SAM and F-75 and F-100 for the management of complicated SAM [3]. However, despite its effectiveness, fewer than 30% of children globally receive the SAM treatment they need [7]. The low coverage of CMAM services is multifactorial, with poor cost estimation being a significant contributing factor [7].

In Ethiopia, acute malnutrition remains a significant public health concern. According to the most recent national data, 5% of children under five years of age are wasted, and 1% are severely wasted [8]. Despite this burden, persistent underfunding of CMAM programs and inconsistent cost estimation contribute to treatment gaps [9]. The 2024 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) targeted 3.5 million acutely malnourished children and women but received only 80% of the required US$276 million [9]. Moreover, existing national cost estimates lack rigorous, evidence-based unit costs, limiting accurate budgeting and resource allocation. Therefore, partners are strongly encouraged to conduct more cost analyses in their operation area for nutrition in emergency interventions in Ethiopia [10].

This study addresses critical gaps by generating a comprehensive economic cost analysis of SAM treatment in two districts of Action Against Hunger operations. The findings aim to improve program planning, optimize resource allocation, and support evidence-based advocacy for scaling SAM treatment in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional economic costing design from a societal perspective to estimate the total economic cost of treating SAM cases.

Study setting and scope

This study conducted in two Action Against Hunger (ACF) operational districts in Ethiopia: Girawa in the East Hararghe Zone of the Oromia Region and Adadle in the Shabelle Zone of the Somali Region. ACF is an international humanitarian organization that has been working in Ethiopia since 1985 to prevent and treat acute malnutrition and address its underlying causes. The organization operates in six regions, reaching over 3.5 million people through integrated programs in health and nutrition, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH), food security and livelihoods, and protection. In Ethiopia, ACF supports the implementation of CMAM programs in collaboration with the government health system, particularly the Ministry of Health, through capacity building, technical and logistical support, and supportive supervision.

The health system in both districts follows the national primary health care structure. Both districts implement the national CMAM program, which provides outpatient care for uncomplicated SAM through outpatient therapeutic program (OTP) sites and inpatient care for complicated SAM through stabilization centers (SCs). The geographic scope of the study encompassed predominantly rural contexts, with pastoralist communities in Adadle and agrarian communities in Girawa, an important distinction for understanding contextual operational costs and service delivery dynamics. Both districts are among high-caseload areas for acute malnutrition in Ethiopia, with substantial demand for CMAM services. Data collection was conducted in March 2025. Retrospective financial and program data from January to December 2024 were reviewed to capture provider-side financial costs, while prospective caregiver interviews were carried out to document caregiver expenses during treatment episodes.

Study population

The study population included children aged 6–59 months who received treatment for SAM, either at OTP or SC sites, within the two study districts during the 2024 program year. Caregivers of these children and health personnel involved in SAM service delivery were also included. To document household costs, exit interviews were conducted with caregivers whose children were receiving SAM treatment at the time of data collection.

Sampling design

Health facilities were first categorized by type—hospital, health center, and health post—and a complete listing was prepared for each category within both districts. Sampling was conducted separately for each category to ensure adequate representation of all levels of SAM service delivery. In the study area, there were 73 health posts (47 in Girawa and 26 in Adadle), 13 health centers (9 in Girawa and 4 in Adadle), and one primary hospital located in Girawa district. The single hospital in Girawa was included by default. At the health center level, three health centers were randomly selected from each district via the recommendation of the World Health Organization (WHO) [11]. From each selected health center, two health posts were randomly selected from its catchment area to ensure representation of community-level service delivery. Based on this sampling approach, the expected total number of health facilities to be included in the assessment was 19 (1 hospital, 6 health centers, and 12 health posts). However, only 18 health facilities were ultimately included in the study because one health post in Adadle district was closed during the data collection period despite repeated visits. To capture caregiver-incurring costs, a convenience sample of caregivers visiting the selected facilities during the data collection period was interviewed at exits.

Data collection methods and tools

Provider-side financial cost data

Provider-side financial cost data were collected through document review and structured interviews with heads and key staff from the government and Action Against Hunger involved in the implementation of the CMAM program. Data collection tools were adapted from validated Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) CMAM costing instruments [12] and was pretested in Gode district, a setting comparable to the study areas, to assess clarity, relevance, and completeness, and was refined prior to the main data collection. This Microsoft Excel–based tool was designed to estimate the costs of CMAM programs at the national, subnational, and district levels. Data collectors trained with supervision from the principal investigator conducted the fieldwork.

The tool was structured into six categories on the basis of the FANTA CMAM costing framework: medical personnel, medical supplies, therapeutic food, equipment, transport and storage, and training and supervision. Medical personnel cost includes staff salaries, categorized as senior, mid-level, and junior service providers at both district health office and health facility levels, working in outpatient or inpatient sites on either full-time or part-time basis. Health workers and program staff were interviewed to estimate the proportion of their working time dedicated to SAM treatment. Medical personnel costs were estimated using staff salary records and time allocation data collected through interviews with health workers and program staff. Medical supplies costs included expenses related to treatment of infections, such as deworming medications (Albendazole), antibiotics (Amoxicillin), antimalarial drugs, ReSoMal, IV kits, nasogastric tubes, and others consumables. Therapeutic food costs included RUTF, F-75, and F-100. Equipment costs covered items such as child weighing scales, height board and other relevant materials. Facility-level stock records and financial documents were reviewed to determine the quantities and unit prices of therapeutic foods, medicines, equipment, and consumables. Transport and storage costs were estimated using program logistics and distribution records, including costs associated with transporting food items to the last mile based on delivery frequency and related resource use. Training and supervision costs included hall rental, per diems for trainees and supervisors, and refreshments, calculated as a function of the number of training and supervision conducted.

All available documents related to SAM service delivery for the 2024 calendar year were reviewed, and these document reviews were supplemented by interviews with program management, administrative staff, and clinical personnel to clarify resource use when needed. To obtain the total number of SAM cases diagnosed and treated in 2024 in both districts, caseload data were gathered from health and nutrition managers. These caseload figures covered all health facilities providing SAM treatment in two districts, not only the sampled facilities, ensuring accurate calculation of provider-side financial cost per child. In this study, total cost refers to the aggregated cost at the district level, encompassing all health facilities providing CMAM services, whereas facility-level data were collected from sampled facilities to inform resource use and cost structure.

Caretaker cost data

Caregiver costs were assessed via a structured questionnaire adapted from the relevant literature. Caregivers’ cost data were collected via exit interviews with caregivers to obtain sociodemographic information and to recall all expenses incurred during their child’s SAM treatment. The data collection tools, including the caregiver interview questionnaire and facility-level checklist, are provided as Supplementary Material (See Additional file 1).

Costing perspective and cost components

A societal perspective was adopted, capturing both provider-side financial costs and caregiver (household) costs. Provider-side financial costs reflected expenses incurred by Action Against Hunger and government health facilities to deliver SAM services at the district level. We categorized cost centers into six major categories: personnel time; medical supplies; therapeutic foods such as RUTF and F-75/F-100; equipment; storage and transportation; and expenditures for training and supervision.

Caregiver costs included both direct and indirect costs during the SAM treatment episode. Direct costs included expenditures such as transport, food for caregivers and children during visits, expenses incurred during hospitalization, and nutrition supplements purchased for the child to aid their child’s recovery from SAM. Indirect costs included lost income due to missed work, time spent seeking care, and coping mechanisms such as borrowing money or selling household assets. All costs were converted to US dollars via the 2024 average exchange rate (1 USD = 57.6 Ethiopian Birr).

Data management and analysis

We used CMAM costing tools developed by the FANTA project to analyze the programmatic (provider-side financial) cost data [12], which organizes expenditures into standard CMAM cost categories and calculates total and per-child costs. We collected district-specific data, including estimates of SAM caseloads, unit prices of commodities, training intensity, service delivery inputs, and logistical parameters such as transport distances, and entered into the Microsoft Excel–based tool. The tool systematically guides users through the identification of CMAM activities and required inputs, including personnel, therapeutic foods, training, supervision, and transport. We generated cost estimates by aggregating provider-side financial costs across districts, with outputs disaggregated by cost category to allow detailed analysis of cost drivers. The tool automatically calculates total and unit costs based on entered assumptions and caseload projections, enabling comparisons across districts. We calculated the cost per SAM child by dividing total provider-side financial expenditure for 2024 by the annual number of SAM cases treated in the two districts. Caregiver data were entered into EpiData and analyzed using Excel. We added the mean caregiver cost per treatment episode to the provider-side financial cost to estimate the total economic cost per SAM child. We presented the results using tables and narrative texts and compared the findings descriptively between the two districts. Based on the cost center categories, we identified cost drivers and estimated the unit cost of the program as the cost per SAM child.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the respective Zonal Health Offices institutional review board in the East Hararghe (reference number: EF21/687/2017) and Shabelle (reference number: 06/sh/386/2017) zones. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers and health workers prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Results

SAM caseload and service delivery structure

In 2024, a total of 2,260 SAM cases were reported in Girawa district, and 890 cases were reported in Adadle district. The majority of children were managed through outpatient care, with 47 OTP sites in Girawa and 26 OTP sites in Adadle. Inpatient services for complicated SAM were provided through 10 SCs in Girawa and 4 SCs in Adadle. This distribution reflects a predominantly outpatient service delivery model across both districts (Table 1).

Total provider-side financial costs

The total annual provider-side financial cost for delivering SAM treatment services in 2024 was USD 386,598 in Girawa and USD 289,433 in Adadle. In Girawa, therapeutic foods constituted the largest cost category, accounting for 57.7% of the total expenditure. This included RUTF for OTP sites and F-75/F-100 for inpatient care. Training and supervision constituted 13.1% of the total costs, followed by medical personnel (8.5%) and transport and storage (6.3%). In Adadle, training and supervision represented the largest share at 40.9%. Therapeutic food and equipment accounted for 38.1%, followed by transport and storage (10.4%) and medical personnel costs (7.5%). Medical supplies—including antibiotics, malaria treatment, diarrheal treatments, vitamins, deworming tablets, and vaccinations—represented smaller proportions in both districts (Girawa: 5.6%; Adadle: 3.1%) (Table 2).

Unit cost for SAM treatment

The average cost per SAM case was calculated by dividing the total annual cost by the number of SAM cases treated in each district. The average cost of treating a SAM case in Girawa was USD 171.5, whereas in Adadle, the cost was significantly higher at USD 325.3 per child (Table 3).

Caretaker-side costs

A total of 62 caregivers were interviewed through exit interviews to document caregiver-incurred costs associated with SAM treatment. The majority of the respondents were mothers, accounting for 57 (91.9%) of all caregivers. More than half of the children, 37 (59.7%), were female, and most SAM cases (54 children; 87.1%) received treatment at OTP. Health posts were the primary point of service delivery, with 38 children (61.3%) treated at this level and only 2 children (3.2%) managed at the primary hospital (Table 4).

On average, caregivers spent USD 6.48 on transportation-related expenses to access treatment services. For children requiring inpatient care, hospitalization poses an additional financial burden, with caregivers incurring an average cost of USD 18.32 per child. Caregivers also purchased or prepared supplemental foods to support the child’s recovery, spending an average of USD 8.70. Coping strategies were common, with 30.6% of caregivers borrowing money or selling livestock, resulting in an average coping cost of USD 10.83 per affected child. Lost productivity contributed to household economic strain, with caregivers reporting an average income loss of USD 9.22 due to the child’s illness. Productivity loss was estimated based on caregivers’ self-reported time spent on treatment-related activities, valued using a daily income derived from reported annual income converted to monthly and then daily equivalents. In summary, the unit cost of SAM treatment is estimated at an average of 53.5 USD from the caretaker’s perspective (Fig. 1).

Overall, the total economic cost for treating SAM children at the study sites was estimated at USD 224.6 per child in Girawa district and USD 378.7 per child in Adadle district. These estimates reflect the combined unit costs from both the provider-side financial and caregiver perspectives.

Discussion

This study provides detailed, district-level cost estimates for the treatment of SAM in two Ethiopian settings via the CMAM platform. The findings highlight significant variation in provider-side financial costs between Girawa and Adadle, driven by differences in caseload, program implementation intensity, and operational context. Such evidence is critically needed to inform realistic budgeting and planning for SAM services at scale in Ethiopia.

The total annual provider-side financial cost of SAM treatment exceeded USD 380,000 in Girawa and USD 280,000 in Adadle. Therapeutic foods, primarily RUTF and F-75/F-100, were the dominant cost drivers in Girawa, accounting for more than two-fourths of total expenditures. This aligns with global evidence showing that RUTF procurement consistently represents the majority of CMAM provider-side financial costs across low-resource settings [10, 13]. The high proportion of costs attributed to therapeutic foods also underscores the importance of improving supply chain efficiency and exploring the feasibility of low-cost local production strategies to increase affordability and sustainability. The larger caseload in Girawa (2,260 vs. 890 cases) and its greater reliance on inpatient services likely contributed to its substantial therapeutic food expenditure. In contrast, Adadle incurred unusually high training and supervision costs, driven by three rounds of training conducted in 2024, which resulted in significant spending on trainer time, hall rental, per diems, and refreshments. This pattern reflects the operational realities of pastoralist settings, where high staff turnover—driven by factors such as livelihood hardships, geographic remoteness, and limited access to social services—as well as population mobility, necessitate more frequent capacity-building and supervision efforts, thereby increasing overall provider-side financial costs [14].

The unit cost of treating a SAM case was substantially higher in the Adadle district (325.3 USD) than in the Girawa district (171.5 USD). This difference is largely driven by the more frequent training conducted in Adadle, which significantly increased the per-child cost. The elevated training and supervision costs in pastoralist settings suggest a need for more tailored and flexible training approaches. Additionally, the smaller number of SAM cases treated annually in Adadle reduced the denominator in the unit cost calculation, resulting in a higher cost per case. Furthermore, transportation and storage costs were proportionally higher in Adadle, reflecting greater logistical challenges common in remote and sparsely populated areas. The unit costs in both districts were notably higher than the 2023 Ethiopian Nutrition Cluster’s estimated unit cost for SAM treatment (118 USD per SAM case) [15]. A likely explanation is that the 2023 estimate did not include several key cost drivers—such as human resources, equipment, logistics, caregiver expenses, and other operational costs—that were fully captured in the present analysis.

Compared with a previous study conducted in Ethiopia in 2012 [15], where the cost per child admitted for treatment was $135, our findings indicate a higher cost in both districts. This difference may be explained by the fact that our study was conducted in 2024, a period marked by significantly higher inflation than that in 2012. However, our study reported lower costs than studies conducted in Kenya and South Sudan [16], which estimated costs of $451 per child. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in treatment center types, cost inclusions, and contextual factors. Additionally, this study demonstrates that the cost per child varies by district, emphasizing that unit costs can fluctuate within a single country and even more significantly across different nations.

This study also demonstrates that the average caregiver cost associated with SAM treatment ($53.55) represents a substantial financial burden for families and contributes meaningfully to the overall economic cost of the program. These expenses—comprising transportation, supplemental foods, hospitalization costs, coping strategies, and income loss—reflect not only direct out-of-pocket payments but also broader livelihood impacts. In rural and low-income settings, such costs can reduce treatment adherence, discourage follow-up visits, and increase default rates, ultimately undermining the effectiveness of CMAM services. Our finding is comparable to household cost estimates reported in Ghana [17], where caregivers similarly faced significant financial challenges in accessing SAM treatment. This consistency across contexts reinforces the need for policy measures that minimize household expenditures—such as decentralizing services, improving community outreach, and integrating social protection mechanisms—to reduce financial barriers and enhance treatment continuity. However, compared to the World Bank Ethiopia Socioeconomic Panel Survey 2021/22 report, average annual household health expenditure for children was approximately 824 Birr (≈ USD 16 in 2022 exchange rates), with Oromia Region reporting among the highest expenditure levels nationally. In our study, caregivers incurred an average cost of USD 53.5 per SAM treatment episode, which is substantially higher. This difference may be explained by the nature of SAM treatment, which requires weekly health facility visits for approximately 8–10 weeks, unlike many other childhood illnesses that typically involve a single visit. This results in repeated transportation costs, food expenses during travel, and income loss. In addition, inflation between 2022 and 2024 and the life-threatening nature of SAM, which often leads caregivers to spend additional resources on food for the child, may further contribute to the higher observed costs [18].

One major strength of our study is the adoption of a societal perspective for economic costing, capturing both provider-side financial and caregiver-side costs. This approach produces a more comprehensive estimate of the true economic burden of treating SAM rather than limiting the analysis to programmatic expenses alone. In this study, the societal perspective included provider-side financial costs (direct medical and provider costs such as personnel, supplies, therapeutic foods, training, supervision, transport, and logistics) as well as caregiver costs (direct non-medical costs such as transport and food, and indirect costs including lost income and coping-related costs incurred during treatment episodes). Although some broader societal costs, such as long-term productivity losses beyond the treatment period, were not captured, the inclusion of both provider-side financial and caregiver costs still reflects the major economic consequences of SAM treatment. This dual-perspective costing is consistent with best practices in health economics and allows for more realistic planning and resource allocation, including considerations of household financial burden. Another strength is the contextual diversity of the study settings: by including one predominantly rural agrarian area (Girawa) and one pastoralist/rural district (Adadle), the analysis captures cost variation across different socioeconomic and livelihood contexts. This enhances the generalizability of findings to different types of rural settings common in low-income countries. Because operational costs — such as transport, training, supervision, and supply chain logistics — may differ substantially by setting, comparing agrarian and pastoralist districts provides valuable insight for future planning and budgeting. This contextual depth enhances the relevance of the findings for Ethiopia’s ongoing efforts to integrate simplified approaches within the health extension program. As the country moves toward broader CMAM scale-up, understanding district-level cost variability becomes essential for effective budgeting, and these findings support global recommendations emphasizing the need for locally grounded, fully costed implementation plans to ensure sustainable service expansion.

Despite these strengths, the study also has important limitations. First, although we calculated the unit cost per SAM case, we did not differentiate between outpatient and inpatient service costs in our analysis. As a result, we cannot disaggregate the cost burden for children receiving only outpatient therapeutic feeding versus those requiring stabilization, which may differ considerably owing to the variable intensity of treatment and resource use. Second, our analysis focused exclusively on SAM treatment and did not include costs associated with moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) or supplementary feeding. As MAMs constitute a substantial share of the acute malnutrition burden and program workload in many settings, excluding MAMs limits the capacity of our cost estimates to fully represent the financial requirements of a comprehensive CMAM program. This is particularly important in the Ethiopian context, where the burden of MAM is significantly higher than that of SAM. According to the most recent DHS (2024-25) report, MAM prevalence is 13.7% compared to 3.4% SAM in the Somali region, and 4.4% compared to 0.8% in Oromia region [8]. Third, caregiver costs were collected using convenience sampling during OTP visits, which may limit generalizability beyond the study settings.

Additionally, certain contextual and methodological limitations merit acknowledgment. Caregiver cost data—particularly caregiver-reported expenses such as transport, food, and coping strategies—are subject to recall bias and variability in reporting accuracy. Provider-side financial cost data were drawn from routine financial and program records complemented by staff interviews; in settings with incomplete records or informal resource use, some costs may be under or overestimated. Capital costs were estimated using replacement prices without annualization, which may have led to an overestimation of provider-side financial costs. Finally, because the analysis reflects costs from only two rural districts, caution is advised when generalizing the findings to urban settings; cost drivers may differ with caseload, infrastructure, market access, and program maturity.

Despite these limitations, the strengths of our study — particularly the societal-perspective cost approach and the comparative analysis across diverse rural livelihood contexts — provide valuable and robust evidence for policymakers and program planners. The findings highlight how cost drivers vary by context, underscoring the need for context-specific budgeting and resource allocation rather than one-size-fits-all estimates. However, for comprehensive national planning or for evaluating full CMAM provider-side financial costs (including MAMs), additional studies addressing the above limitations — especially disaggregation of cost by service type and inclusion of MAM costs — are warranted.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive, district-level economic cost estimates for SAM treatment delivered through the CMAM program in two contrasting Ethiopian settings. Total provider-side financial costs were substantial and varied significantly between districts, driven primarily by differences in therapeutic food needs, training intensity, and logistical requirements. Girawa’s costs were dominated by therapeutic foods, reflecting its higher caseload, whereas Adadle incurred disproportionately high training and supervision expenses owing to repeated capacity-building efforts. These findings underscore the need for context-specific budgeting and highlight the financial implications of delivering SAM services in rural agrarian versus pastoralist settings. The cost estimates generated here offer valuable evidence for policymakers, planners, and implementers seeking to scale up SAM treatment within Ethiopia. They further contribute to the global evidence base on CMAM costing, demonstrating the importance of incorporating both provider-side financial and caregiver perspectives when evaluating program affordability and sustainability.
