Nutrition literacy, dietary diversity, and frailty among older adults in China
Authors: Xi Tian, Ying Duan, Yuhui Sun, Xiaoting Hu, Huaqing Liu, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty has become a public health concern among older adults. Nutrition is closely associated with health. This study aimed to evaluate the relationships between nutrition literacy (NL) and dietary diversity and frailty.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis included 1,187 participants. NL was evaluated using the short-form nutrition literacy self-assessment questionnaire. Frailty was assessed via a modified Fried phenotype. Generalized estimating equations were used to examine the association between NL and frailty by considering odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI); and generalized linear models were performed to calculate regression coefficients (ꞵ) and 95% CI for the association between NL and dietary diversity.ResultsThe prevalence of frailty was approximately 13.7% in the study sample. Higher NL was associated with lower prevalence of frailty (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99), and was correlated with higher dietary diversity (ꞵ= 0.06, 95% CI: 0.05–0.07). Further exploratory statistical analysis indicated that the relationship between NL and frailty was related to dietary diversity, with an associative coefficient of − 0.0028 (95% CI: −0.0044 to − 0.0013) observed in the results.ConclusionsThese findings showed that NL and dietary diversity were significantly associated with frailty in older adults, and dietary diversity may form an exploratory statistical association between NL with frailty. Our results underscore the potential value of strategies aimed at greater NL and dietary diversity for the management of frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07726-x.

Background

Populations in various regions are ageing rapidly, and consequently, frailty, an age-related clinical syndrome, has become a public health concern for older adults. A recent study reported the prevalence of prefrailty and frailty among older individuals in Chinese communities was 43.9% and 10.1%, respectively [1]. Frailty has been linked to negative health outcomes, including disability, hospitalization, reduced quality of life, and mortality [2–5]. Frailty poses major challenges with respect to health care systems and the financial burden of society [6]. Notably, frailty onset and development are reversible. However, the factors related to frailty must be identified to strengthen early frailty management and control measures and promote healthy ageing [3, 7].

Frailty is a complex progress that affected by a range of physical, sociodemographic, cognitive, lifestyle and nutrition-related factors [8, 9]. Particularly, nutrition and diet are key modifiable factors influencing frailty [10, 11]. One study showed that there was a dose–effect relationship between nutritional risk and frailty [12]. Improved nutrition status was crucial to frailty criteria, which focused on increasing protein and energy intake were beneficial for slowing down the decline of muscle mass, walking speed, and weight loss [13]. Conversely, poor nutritional intake adversely affects daily life and worsens frailty progression. In one study involving patients with malnutrition, the combined prevalence of frailty and prefrailty was 90.7% [14]. Furthermore, healthy diet is the cornerstone for a healthy life, exerting observable effects on the ageing process among older adults [15]. For example, frequent consumption of fruit, vegetable, and whole grains may decelerate frailty onset [16]. A diet with the high protein and dietary total antioxidant capacity was inversely associated with the prevalence of frailty in older population [17]. Maintaining a moderate consumption of dairy products, especially fermented ones, as part of a well-balanced diet to promote healthy aging [18]. Moreover, in a study of 1,701 individuals, a proinflammatory diet was associated with an increased odds of frailty [19]. A prospective study also revealed that vegetarian diet was associated with a higher risk of frailty, compared to an omnivorous diet [20]. Dietary diversity is an important component of greater diet patterns and endorsed by dietary guidelines in many countries [21, 22], serves as an indicator of whether an individual has greater nutritional intake and reflects the consumption of multiple food groups within a specific time frame [23]. Individuals with greater dietary diversity could improve their nutrition status [2], and increased dietary variety has been associated with a decreased likelihood of frailty among older adults [22, 24]. Hence, higher food group variety is important for frailty management in older adults.

Additionally, nutrition-related education can improve individuals’ nutritional knowledge and understanding of food and nutrition [25]. Previous studies have reported that participants with greater exposure to nutritional education tend to have better nutritional status and health outcomes [26]. However, evidence remains inconsistent, as increased nutritional knowledge alone often fails to translate into healthier dietary practices [27]. Therefore, it is critical to identify key explanatory factors in the nutrition–frailty relationship to explore how nutritional factors ultimately influence frailty development.

The term nutrition literacy (NL) refers to an individual’s ability to obtain, interpret, and comprehend fundamental nutritional information, and it can lead to informed dietary decision-making. NL is a subset of health literacy that specifically pertains to nutrition [28]. NL considerably affects food choices, eating behaviour formation, and overall lifestyle development, which indicates it plays a crucial role in both health promotion and the management of nutrition-related ailments [29]. A cross-sectional study revealed that individuals with higher NL tended to consume less take-out food and to favour vegetable and fruit salads over spicy hot pot [30]. Another investigation directly linked NL to the use of nutrition labels, suggesting that NL can be increased to encourage individuals to inspect such labels and select healthier food options [31]. In one study, individuals with high NL adhered more closely to the Mediterranean dietary principles [32]. Furthermore, among elementary school students, a positive association was observed between NL and dietary variety [33]. Notably, NL is a skill that can be developed, integrated into daily routines, and sustained to achieve enduring benefits [34]. Accordingly, the correlation between NL and frailty in older adults warrants further examination, with such an examination having potential to offer valuable theoretical perspectives on this relationship.

In this study, we built on current evidence to establish three hypotheses: (1) NL is associated with frailty prevalence in older adults; (2) NL is correlated with an individual’s dietary diversity; and (3) dietary diversity is related to frailty status, and may form an exploratory association between NL and frailty among older adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

This investigation was conducted from May to July 2023 in Bengbu City, China. Individuals from urban and rural areas were selected for participation through multistage random sampling. Firstly, two urban districts and two rural counties/townships were randomly selected as urban and rural sampling sites. Then, two streets were randomly selected from each urban district, and two towns/villages were chosen from each rural county/township. Ultimately, 110 households were randomly sampled from each selected street and town/village. The inclusion criteria specified participants being 18 years of age or older, keeping clear consciousness, exhibiting sufficient verbal communication capacity, and being able to complete questionnaires either independently or with the support of researchers. Further details regarding the methodology employed are provided in previous study [35]. Information regarding demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviours, dietary intake, and health status were obtained through a self-reported questionnaire. All subjects were interviewed in person by trained medical professionals and provided written informed consent after receiving an explanation of the voluntary nature of the study. This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and all procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Bengbu Medical University (2021-099).

A total of 2,400 individuals were invited to participate in the survey by community workers. Of these participants, 2,279 original participants who successfully finished the interview, resulting in a completion rate of 95.0%. Given that the prevalence of frailty is common among older adults, this study analyzed the participants who aged 60 years and older. In total, 1355 older adults completed the survey. In this study, 168 participants were excluded because of missing data on nutrition literacy (4) and missing data on frailty assessment (164), ultimately, the data of 1,187 individuals were included, and the mean age of the participants (SD) was 71.39 (6.90) years in this study. NL evaluation

The 12-item short-form nutrition literacy self-assessment questionnaire (NL-SF12) was, which was developed by Liu and colleagues [36] based on the original 43-item NL scale, used to assess individual’s NL and has demonstrated acceptable validity [31, 37, 38], and reliability among Chinese older adults [35]. The NL-SF12 has six dimensions: nutritional knowledge and understanding, nutrition-related information acquisition and practical application skills, and capacity for interactive and critical analysis. Each item was scored ranging from 1 to 5 on a five-point Likert type scale, which was rated as strongly disagree (coded as 1), disagree (coded as 2), neutral (coded as 3), agree (coded as 4), or strongly agree (coded as 5), respectively. The total NL score was derived by summing the scores of each item, and the scores of six dimensions were calculated through its respectively corresponding two items. The maximum score on the NL-SF12 was 60, with higher scores reflecting superior NL. Detailed questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Table S2. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the NL-SF12 was 0.857.

Frailty assessment

Frailty was evaluated according to a modified Fried phenotype across five components: (1) Weight loss: unintentional weight loss of ≥ 4.5 kg (by direct measurement of weight) or ≥ 5% of previous year’s body weight, [weight loss was calculated as: (Weight in previous year – current measured weight) / (weight in previous year)]; (2) Weakness: grip strength of dominant hand was stratified by sex and body mass index (BMI): cut-off for handgrip strength for male was 29 kg (BMI ≤ 24 kg/m2), ≤ 30 kg (BMI 24.1–26 kg/m2), ≤ 30 kg (BMI 26.1–28 kg/m2), or ≤ 32 kg (BMI > 28 kg/m2). Cut-off for handgrip strength for female was ≤ 17 kg (BMI ≤ 23 kg/m2), ≤ 17.3 kg (BMI 23.1–26 kg/m2), ≤ 18 kg (BMI 26.1–29 kg/m2), or ≤ 21 kg (BMI > 29 kg/m2) [39]. Individuals meeting the above criteria were classified as having a low grip strength, while those who did not were classified as having a normal grip strength; (3) Low physical activity: the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) is a reliable tool used to measure physical activity in many countries, which has shown a good reliability and validity. Given the length and content of the questionnaire, the Chinese short version of IPAQ (IPAQ-C) containing seven items was employed to calculate metabolic equivalent of task (METs) at four different intensity levels [40]. Weekly physical activity was quantified as kilocalories based on the same original adjusted cutoff points described, and low activity was defined as less than 383 kcal per week for men and less than 270 kcal per week for women [41]; (4) Self-reported exhaustion: according to two items in the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression scale (CES-D), in the past week, participants stating that they felt that everything they did was an effort or that they could not get going a moderate amount of the time (3–4 days) or most of the time (5–7 days) [42]; (5) Slowness: Gait speed over 4.5 m, adjusted for gender and height: ≥ 7 s (men height ≤ 173 cm, women height ≤ 159 cm) or ≥ 6 s (men height > 173 cm, women height > 159 cm) [43]. Participants received 1 point for each met indicator, with total scores ranging from 0 to 5. Scores of 0, 1 to 2, and 3 to 5 indicated robustness, prefrailty, and frailty, respectively [10].

Dietary diversity evaluation

The food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was commonly used to estimate dietary intake, with the participants reporting their consumption frequency of various food groups [44], according to the food group categories outlined in the Chinese Food Pagoda and Plate [45]. Cereal products, cooking oil and salt were excluded from the construction of dietary diversity score because that they are consumed daily by nearly all Chinese people.

In this study, dietary diversity was defined as a food-group variety score calculated based on eight food groups derived from the FFQ: vegetables, fruits, meat, eggs, dairy products, beans, nuts, and aquatic products. The intake frequency for each food group was assessed on a 5-point scale, on which the participants selected “almost every day”, “at least once a week”, “at least once a month”, “occasionally”, and “never” (Supplementary Table S3). Individuals were assigned 1 point for the responses “almost every day” or “at least once a week,” with total scores ranging from 0 to 8 [46]. A higher dietary diversity score shows the variety of food groups intake.

Covariates

Consistent with existing literature, frailty is known to be shaped by a broad range of factors, including sociodemographic, behavioral, nutritional, and health-related conditions [47–49]. These factors were adjusted for as covariates in the present study. Age (60–69 years, 70–79 years, and ≥ 80 years), gender (male and female), region (rural area and urban area), marital status (married and other), living arrangement (living alone and living with others), educational attainment (primary school or below and junior high school or above), annual household income (< 20,000 RMB and > 20,000 RMB), smoking status (non-smoker, former smoker, and current smoker), alcohol consumption status (non-drinker, former drinker, and current drinker), BMI (kg/m²): underweight, < 18.5; normal, 18.5–23.9; overweight, 24-27.9; and obese, ≥ 28), and presence of chronic diseases (yes and no). Considering that residence, education and income level are predetermined socioeconomic factors and potential confounders, we performed three sequential models to examine robustness of the association across different covariate specifications.

Statistical analysis

The participants’ demographic characteristics are presented as numbers and proportions. Chi-square tests were performed to evaluate the differences between the variables in each group. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD), t-tests and analyses of variance were performed to evaluate differences between groups, and Spearman’s correlation analyses was performed to test the linear association between continuous variables. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were used to compare baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants, with SMD < 0.1 indicating an acceptable balance among the groups, 0.1 < SMD < 0.2 a slight difference, and when SMD > 0.2, a significant imbalance and suggesting potential presence of selection bias. Subsequently, generalized estimating equation (GEE) was performed to assess the correlation between NL and frailty, with the lowest level group used as a reference. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Generalized linear models were performed to assess the association between NL and dietary diversity, with the results reported as regression coefficients (β) and 95% CI. Another sensitivity analysis was conducted after excluding behaviorally overlapping items or application domain of the NL-SF12 scale that were conceptually similar to dietary variety (Supplementary Table S4). The PROCESS macro was used for exploratory statistical decomposition of dietary diversity on the association between NL and frailty. Results of the exploratory statistical analysis were considered significant when the 95% CI excluded zero [50]. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 23.0. Significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Baseline characteristics

Participants’ characteristics were presented in Table 1. Among the 1,187 questionnaire respondents, 512 (43.1%), 505 (42.5%), and 170 (14.3%) were aged 60 to 69, 70 to 79, and 80 or more years, respectively. Association of NL with frailty and dietary diversity

As displayed in Table 3, a negative relationship was found between the total NL and frailty in the crude model (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92–0.95). After adjusting for socioeconomic status confounders in Model 2, the OR of the participants with NL for frailty was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.93–0.96). Following adjustment for all the covariates in Model 3, the inverse association between NL and frailty remained statistically significant (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99). Furthermore, this significant association with frailty were observed across understanding of nutrition (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.99), information acquisition skills (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.87–0.98), interactive skills (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.83–0.95), and critical skills (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96); but not with nutritional knowledge and applying skills.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the associations between NL, dietary diversity, and frailty in older adults. The findings revealed a negative association between NL and frailty within this population. Dietary diversity was used to perform an exploratory statistical analysis of this relationship. The current study provides exploratory findings into the relationship between NL and prevalence of frailty in older adults.

The prevalence of prefrailty and frailty in our sample was 50.0% and 13.7%, respectively, which are higher percentages than those reported in previous studies [1]. A cross-sectional study conducted in Guayaquil revealed a prefrailty and frailty prevalence of 66.7% and 28.9%, respectively [51], while an Indonesian study reported that 59.8% and 27.8% of older adults were classified as prefrail and frail, respectively [52]. Another cohort study conducted in England reported that 33.8% of participants were categorised as prefrail or frail [53]. Frailty is highly prevalent among older adults, although the exact prevalence differs between countries and regions, indicating that frailty emerges as a novel health concern at later life stages. Notably, frailty is dynamic, modifiable and even reversible [54]. Therefore, strengthening frailty management is necessary to promote healthy aging and enhance healthy condition among older adults.

This study demonstrates an observational association between NL and frailty among older adults. The results showed that higher NL is related to healthier eating habits, whereas deficient NL tends to be linked to poorer dietary patterns [35, 55]. In one study, NL was reported to be positively correlated with consuming a Mediterranean diet (typically rich in whole grains, fruits, vegetables, legumes, and olive oil) [32], which was linked to lower frailty prevalence among older populations [56]. Hence, higher NL may be associated with a lower observed prevalence of frailty, which could be linked to healthier dietary behaviors. Notably, there was no significant associations of nutritional knowledge and application skills with frailty, whereas the understanding, information acquisition, interactive, and critical skills were significantly associated with a lower frailty prevalence. This phenomenon may reflect that individuals’ nutritional knowledge does not always translate into dietary practices or alter nutritional status in daily life [57]. The capacity to understand nutrition-related information may help individuals to better engage in health self-management [58]. Nutritional literacy skills included information acquisition, interactive, and critical skills may be beneficial to healthier dietary behaviors, and the ability to comprehend nutrition labels is important to adopt suitable eating habits formation [59]. Overall, these findings indicate a potential association between NL and the prevalence of frailty. However, it is necessary to emphasize that the observed differences in specific domains of NL should not be overstated in terms of their importance owing to the lack of prespecified hypotheses and adjustments for multiple testing. The subscale results should be considered exploratory and not be interpreted as mechanistic insights. Further research with larger samples is needed to validate these results.

This study observed a positive association between NL and dietary diversity. Adequate NL has been linked to increased fruit consumption and a propensity for selecting nutritional food [60]. Individuals with higher NL are more likely to consume more low-fat dairy products, vegetables, nuts and seeds, olive oil, and soya products [61]. Conversely, low NL may lead to limited dietary variety and inadequate nutrition, as indicated by its reported correlation with lower protein, calcium, and vitamin intake [33]. Our findings showed that increased NL may help older adults to promote greater dietary diversity. Similarly, another study reported that certain diets were associated with frailty [22], and greater dietary variety may predict better quality of life and health metrics among older adults [62]. Diverse diets have also been linked to a lower likelihood of toxic ingredient consumption, optimised telomere length, and sustained muscle strength [63]. Consequently, dietary variety must be prioritized in strategies focused on managing frailty in older populations.

This study further explored the statistical interrelationships among NL, dietary diversity and frailty. Higher NL was found to be correlated with a more varied diet, which was related to a lower prevalence of frailty. NL, which encompasses both conceptual knowledge and procedural facts, is crucial in resolving an individual’s confusion regarding food choices, which can shape their subsequent dietary behaviour [64]. As a modifiable competency, NL can be improved through nutritional education and training. Improved NL is related to dietary changes, including increasing consumption of higher-quality and more diverse diets [65, 66]. Diverse diets provide a rich supply of micronutrients and macronutrients, and nutrient interactions are more critical in older adults than isolated nutrients are [24], indicating a need for managing frailty that influence diverse dietary habits, especially among individuals with lower NL.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the relationships among NL, dietary diversity and frailty in older adults. However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional study design prevents us from evaluating causality in relationships between nutrition literacy and dietary diversity and frailty; the exclusion of participants with missing frailty data may introduce selection bias, particularly in participants with poor physical function or health conditions, or differences in age, socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle factors. Thus, the results should be interpreted with caution, further longitudinal studies should aim to improve data collection procedures to minimize missing information, and employ more rigorous methods to validate their associations. Secondly, the study reliance on self-reported nutrition literacy, coarse diet survey, and partly self-reported indicators on frailty, and these may lead to recall and shared-method bias; moreover, dietary diversity is a simple food-group variety score based only on weekly consumption frequency with equal weighting for all food groups, it does not consider food portion size or energy adjustment, thus, results cannot represent overall diet quality or nutrient adequacy. In the future, researches should consider conducting more scientific methods and evaluation indicator. Thirdly, clustering effects in the analysis are not adjusted, and results should be interpreted with caution because statistical inference may be overstated; in addition, although the study adjusted for confounding factors of sociodemographic, lifestyle and health conditions, residual confounding may still exist. Studies should be further developed and refined in the future. Finally, the present study was limited to older adults living in a singular city in Anhui Province, influenced by differences in social, geographic environments and dietary habits, it would be inappropriate to generalize the results to other regions. Future studies need to explore the generalizability of these findings across different regions and populations.

Conclusions

The higher prevalence of frailty among older adults in China underscores the importance of exploring modifiable correlated factors for frailty prevalence among older adults. This study observed a correlation between higher NL and lower frailty prevalence, suggesting that approaches targeting greater NL may be related to progression of frailty. Dietary diversity was associated with lower frailty prevalence, showing the potential value of varied dietary intake. Furthermore, dietary diversity may form the statistical association between NL and frailty, indicating that strategies for NL and dietary diversity could be relevant to frailty prevalence. Our findings provide a theoretical basis for frailty management, suggesting that community practitioners may consider implementing nutritional education and skills training programme. Such efforts may support healthier eating habits and diverse food choices, highlighting potential targets for frailty management among older adults in the future.
