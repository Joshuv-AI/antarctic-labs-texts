The prolonged impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on routine childhood vaccinations in Armenia: a time-series analysis
Authors: Anya Agopian, Tamara Sedrakyan, Diana Muradyan, Gayane Sahakyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic caused disruptions to health services, including routine vaccination programs, affecting the uptake and timeliness of childhood vaccinations. This study explored the continued impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on routine vaccination coverage among children living in Armenia by examining full vaccination coverage a few years before and after the initial COVID-19 wave.MethodsA quasi-experimental research design that included a single cohort interrupted time series analysis was used to assess full vaccination coverage among children ages one, two, and six years old based on monthly data from January 2019 through December 2023. ARIMA models were utilized to understand changes in routine vaccination coverage after the initial lockdown as well as the recovery levels among the different the age groups.ResultsAn initial decrease in full vaccination coverage among one-year-olds (from 91.44% in January 2020 to 88.53% in January 2021) was observed. Similarly, coverage for two- and six-year-olds decreased from 91.55% to 89.04% and from 96.07% to 94.26%, respectively. Coverage rose to 92.04% for one-year-olds, 90.99% for two-year-olds, and 93.05% for six-year-olds by December 2023. The ARIMA models showed significant changes in vaccination coverage after the initial lockdown period, particularly among children two and six years old, with recovery levels differing among age groups.ConclusionsDespite signs of recovery, routine vaccination coverage remained lower than before the pandemic for children two and six years old. The interventions by the Ministry of Health and National Centers for Disease Control and Prevention may have helped recover some lost vaccination levels. Yet, there remain ongoing challenges to routine vaccinations. This study highlights the importance of assessing coverage and timeliness of routine vaccinations and the need for targeted strategies to address lapses in vaccinations, particularly among older children, in Armenia.Keywords: Vaccinations, Vaccination coverage, Routine vaccinations, Childhood vaccinations, COVID-19.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has impacted the world in numerous ways, affecting not only individuals’ daily lives, but disrupting economic and social systems as well. Studies have shown that essential health services declined during the pandemic as health systems were stretched thin and individuals, unsure of what the emergence of this new virus meant, remained home [1, 2]. A key essential health service that was impacted is routine childhood vaccinations. According to UNICEF, between 2019 and 2021, 67 million children were under-vaccinated or unvaccinated [3]. Multiple studies have shown that, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, childhood vaccinations declined globally, especially during the first half of 2020 [4–6]. As the pandemic persisted, some countries that had initially experienced a decline in vaccination coverage have been able to return closer to original levels, although potential lingering effects of the pandemic may have prevented full restoration of previous vaccination coverage levels [6].

Furthermore, newly developing vaccine hesitancy and general concerns about childhood vaccinations, stimulated by COVID-19, have been reported to contribute to the decrease in coverage [7].

Armenia is a middle income country located in the Caucusus with a population of roughly 2.9 million. The country follows the WHO’s recommendations on routine vaccinations, which are free of charge and received at either polyclinics, hospitals or ambulatories. Due to the accessibility of vaccinations and widespread efforts, Armenia was declared polio-free in 2002, malaria-free in 2011, and measles-free in 2021 [8]. Childhood vaccinations may not historically be one of the biggest concerns in Armenia, yet, although coverage has thus far been reported to be high, the timing of those vaccinations has not been optimal [9, 10]. The importance of examining childhood vaccination coverage levels and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic is especially critical given the increase in vaccine hesitancy and reemergence of VPDs globally, as well as in Armenia. Of note, the Ministry of Health (MOH) declared an outbreak of measles on March 2, 2023, with a total of 1046 confirmed cases—688 of which were children (including those less than one year) as of July 2024 [11]. COVID-19 lockdowns and healthcare disruptions combined with concerns and hesitancy towards vaccines emerging in the “post-pandemic” phase have led to disruptions in routine vaccinations for many children [12]. Research indicates that inappropriate receipt of vaccines can lead to the recurrence of infectious disease outbreaks even in countries with high vaccination coverage [9, 13]. Therefore, it is crucial for communities or countries, such as Armenia, to maintain high coverage of timely routine childhood vaccinations to protect the population from preventable diseases. The main objective of this study was to examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on routine vaccination coverage among children in Armenia, specifically comparing metrics before 2020 to after, in order to provide insight into the status of childhood vaccinations to guide services and interventions.

Methods

Utilizing a quasi-experimental design, we conducted a single cohort interrupted time series analysis to assess the impact of COVID-19 lockdown measures on childhood vaccination coverage. Interrupted time series analysis is a useful method to examine the impact of COVID-19 lockdown measures on various healthcare utilization indicators as it provides a robust method that takes into account both pre- and post-event trends [14].

Vaccination coverage was defined as the proportion of children who received all scheduled vaccinations by one, two and six years of age as defined by the National Center for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) Immunization Program. The National Immunization Calendar that provides the schedule of recommended routine vaccinations is presented in Table 1. Medical care for children in Armenia is subsidized by the government, thus children are registered at one local primary health care facility where they receive their vaccinations. Vaccination data is recorded at the point of delivery (primary health care facilities) and is collected by the NCDC on a monthly basis. The data is currently recorded in both the electronic immunization registry and the paper registry, as well as in individual outpatient medical cards, as the system has not yet fully transitioned to an electronic method. The data is completed in the electronic database of the NCDC and is also extracted from the electronic immunization registry․ For summary reports and analyses, paper-based data is mostly used, since the electronic registry is not yet fully operational. All primary healthcare facilities (including private medical centers) send monthly data in a special Excel format to the NCDC, where they are summarized monthly and cumulatively. The Excel format corresponds to the format approved by the National Statistical Service, which contains the following data on vaccinations: the number of children registered in medical institutions by age (denominator), the number of children who received a complete vaccination course in accordance with the National Immunization Calendar (numerator), the total number of vaccinations by individual antigens, the number of those vaccinated on time, the number of contraindications by individual antigens, as well as aggregated data on adverse events following immunization.

The completness and accuracy of the data is checked on a quarterly basis by NCDC specialists (including NCDC central office and branches countrywide), during selective monitoring, quarterly supportive supervisions in medical facilities, as well as by combining and comparing different sources (mainly children’s age specific data with reports from the National Statistical Service, newborn’s data with civil registries, and maternity hospitals). For these analyses, only aggregate monthly coverage at the national level via an Excel spreadsheet for each age group was provided and utilized.

Each age group was examined as a separate outcome. We assessed the coverage by recommended routine vaccinations of children ages one, two and six years separately to gain a more holistic understanding of the impact of COVID-19 and associated lockdown measures on routine childhood vaccinations. Monthly data on each of the coverage levels from January 2019 to December 2023 were included in the respective analyses. As the data was collected and verified on the national level, and aggregate proportions were utilized, all months during the study period had a corresponding monthly coverage level.

To estimate the impact of the initial lockdown on each of the outcomes (coverage at one, two and six years), we used Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models. Monthly coverage levels by each age were first plotted and trends and patterns were examined. The models also estimate the counterfactual, or what would have happened if the event had not occurred (i.e., what was expected is forecasted and compared to what was observed). Autocorrelation functions (ACFs) were used to check for stationarity and autocorrelation. The Dicky-Fuller test was also used to assess the series stationarity. We used “auto.arima” in the forecast package in R which identifies the best models based on minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The Ljung-Box test was used to check residuals. All analyses were performed using R (v4.3.2). The study protocol was reviewed and deemed “exempt” by the American University of Armenia Institutional Review Board (PROTOCOL #: AUA-2023-013).

Full coverage of routine vaccinations for one-year-old children dropped from 91.44% in January 2020 to 88.53% in January 2021. A similar trend was observed in the coverage of two-year-old children, which decreased slightly from 91.55% in January 2020 to 91.33% in January 2021, as well as of six-year-old children, which decreased from 96.07% to 92.87% during the same time period. By 2022, vaccination levels showed differing trends for each age group with coverage among one-year-old and six-year-old children continuing to decrease, while coverage for two-year-olds showed some improvement. In 2023, vaccination coverage started to rise, especially among one-year-olds, increasing to 92.04% in December, surpassing “pre-pandemic” levels. Among two-year-olds, coverage rose from 90.99% by the end of the year, close to but still slightly below pre-pandemic levels. Among six-year-olds, coverage increased to 93.05%, but stayed below pre-pandemic levels.

These results indicate that the initial lockdown in April 2020 resulted in a decrease of 0.3454% (95%CI: −1.0254, 0.3346) in coverage levels among one-year-olds, after which there was a slow monthly increase of 0.0455% (95%CI: 0.0237,0.0674) in coverage.

Discussion

As it has become apparent, beyond the short-term consequences on health and the economy, the COVID-19 pandemic has had far-reaching effects. Our study examined the impact of COVID-19 on routine vaccination coverage among children in Armenia, comparing “pre-pandemic” and “post-pandemic” periods. The data showed changes in coverage of routine vaccinations between 2019 and 2023, although it should be noted that coverage levels were slightly lower in 2019 than the start of 2020 before the pandemic. We found that the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted routine vaccination coverage, with the impact on coverage varying among children one, two, and six years old. In 2019, the mean routine vaccination coverage for one-year-olds was 88.95%, which is closely aligned with global vaccination coverage levels, but falls below the required 95% for developing herd immunity [15]. Although the first half of 2020 saw the most severe decline, the second half showed promise with the increase in vaccination coverage among one-year-old children. During the following “post-pandemic” years, routine vaccination coverage increased among one-year-old children, contradicting predicted routine vaccination coverage for Armenia and the global rates, which declined significantly [16].

The observed recovery in vaccination coverage can be credited to the efforts of the MOH and NCDC. Their initiatives were crucial in ensuring childhood vaccinations were consistently administered. In order to ensure continuity in the administration of routine vaccinations, the MOH published guidelines in April 2020 about the deployment of vaccination measures during the pandemic. The recommendations called for routine vaccinations during the pandemic in addition to upholding stringent anti-pandemic measures and increasing public awareness to boost vaccination coverage. The MOH carried out continuous awareness programs and improved the nation’s cold chain capabilities in partnership with UNICEF and WHO. They also conducted workshops, such as “Using Primary Health Care Platforms to Enhance Immunization Service Delivery”, to promote increased uptake of routine vaccinations during the “post-pandemic” period [17, 18]. In addition to these initiatives, during the initial months of the pandemic, the pentavalent whole-cell pertussis (DTwP) containing vaccine was replaced with the hexavalent acellular pertussis (DTaP) containing vaccine, which has been reported to have a decrease in post-vaccination side effects and may have resulted in higher acceptance among parents in Armenia [19], although these vaccines have been shown to be less protective against pertussis. These efforts can be seen as having a positive impact on vaccination delivery during the height of the pandemic and the basis for the relatively quick return to near “pre-pandemic” vaccination coverage levels, thus serving as a model for measures to take during periods of disruption in essential services.

Coverage among one-year-olds at the start of 2020 was higher than 2019 (91.44% compared to 89.52% in January of both years), even before the commencement of the pandemic. This may be reflective of efforts by the MOH to increase vaccinations as they had been, as seen, falling below desired levels. The rapid increase in vaccinations among one-year-olds during the “post-pandemic” period may be the result of the receipt of delayed vaccinations. This finding is similar to global findings where the vaccination rates for young children bounced back to pre-pandemic levels [20, 21]. Children may have received all of the recommended vaccinations later than the recommended age and thus were included in the aggregate counts of vaccinated for the older age group, resulting in higher coverage levels for the six-year-olds. Studies have shown that incorrectly timed vaccinations have been responsible for VPD-related hospitalizations and outbreaks, as well as the reemergence of certain VPDs in communities [22–26]. Countries also often express concerns that implementing catch-up vaccinations could lead to increased demand and potential shortages or stock-outs of vaccines among cohorts who missed vaccination at the recommended age [27]. Consequently, the timing of vaccinations should be examined more closely as part of the evaluation of routine vaccinations to ensure proper coverage among children.

Vaccination coverage experienced a substantial immediate drop among two-year-old children from approximately 91% before the start of the COVID-19 pandemic to 88% during the peak pandemic period in Armenia, followed by a slower monthly recovery rate. The modest “post-pandemic” recovery among two-year-old children may be a result of the increase of vaccination coverage among one-year-old children after the pandemic. However, the 2023 coverage for two-year-olds remained lower compared to the “pre-pandemic” period for two-year-olds and “post-pandemic” period for one-year-olds, highlighting the greater vulnerability of older children. This finding aligns with the literature, which indicates that older children experienced a more significant drop in vaccination recovery than younger children, a trend that has been observed globally [5, 6, 21, 28].

A decrease in routine vaccination coverage among six-year-olds in Armenia was observed with a continued decline since the start of the pandemic. This is in line with the literature, with countries reporting decreased vaccination coverage among children as they age [21, 29]. Overall, our study found higher levels of coverage among six-year-old children, which may suggest that vaccinations are not received at the age at which they are recommended, although the most consistent decline in vaccination coverage was seen in this age group. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the decline in vaccination catchups during the “post-pandemic” period, such as caregivers’ fears of bringing their children to healthcare facilities due to concerns about COVID-19 transmission and inadequate safety precautions [30, 31]. However, in the Armenian population, the fear of contracting COVID-19 was mainly during the initial pandemic period, and thus may not be the reason for continued missed vaccinations [32]. It is also possible that parents returned to full-time work which reduced their availability to take their children for their vaccinations [28]. This illustrates the differential impact and recovery patterns after the pandemic, emphasizing the need to better understand the reasons and develop targeted interventions for older children to address these gaps and ensure timely vaccination coverage. For example, trained staff at primary health clinics should review vaccination records monthly, identify those under-vaccinated, and call the parents and encourage them to bring their children to receive the necessary vaccinations.

The overall decline in vaccination coverage may also be a result of growing vaccine hesitancy, which the WHO identifies as a significant reason for the delay and low uptake of vaccinations. The phenomenon of vaccine hesitancy, which was present, albeit less apparent prior to the pandemic, has been rising over time globally as well as in Armenia and may have been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Questions about the rapid development of COVID-19 vaccines, increased use of social media during periods of physical distancing and social isolation, and reduced access to health services have all contributed to the refusal or delay of vaccinations. Increased vaccine misunderstanding, skepticism, and hesitancy among caregivers that emerged at the start of the pandemic has led to further disruptions in childhood vaccinations. Several studies show that concerns regarding vaccination safety, effectiveness, and possible side effects are the main causes of caregivers’ hesitancy. Many parents worry about potential health issues that vaccines might cause, believe that vaccines compromise their children’s immunity, or think their children receive more vaccinations than needed. This misunderstanding of the benefits of vaccinations leads them to question the overall effectiveness of vaccines [33–36]. These concerns also might explain why some caregivers delayed or did not take their children in for their additional vaccinations as they age, leading to a decline in routine vaccination coverage for children ages two and six years during the post-pandemic period.

This study is not without limitations. The data provided was on an aggregate level, making it difficult to parse out if there were any issues with specific vaccines and/or doses. By not being able to assess vaccines and vaccination series independently, we are not able to understand whether a single vaccine poses a barrier to full vaccination or whether multiple doses may typically be incomplete. This also makes it difficult to understand if there were missed opportunities for vaccination (i.e., parents brought the children to get vaccinated but only received some and not all of the recommended vaccinations). Aggregate data also poses issues with assessing missingness, and thus we were unable to assess the completeness of the data. While the overall number of children was not provided, making it difficult to provide exact estimates of how many children the data represents, the data collected by the NCDC covers all children across Armenia as all are registered at their local primary health care facilities from birth. The data used in this analysis is also as reliable as is the vaccination data and data collection process, and thus there may be some inconsistencies that were not made apparent during the analysis process. Additionally, the lack of data on hesistancy and other potentially impactful occurances makes it difficult to fully contextualize some of the trends that were made apparent by the analysis.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that in Armenia there were issues with timely vaccinations and a reliance on catch-up vaccinations during the “post-pandemic” period. These appear to be predominantly influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic and potentially by the subsequent vaccine hesitancy that emerged during this time. Although the MOH and NCDC were proactive in their campaigns to maintain childhood vaccination coverage levels, the impact of the pandemic is evident. Given that high levels of population immunity are essential for the prevention of VPDs, lack of vaccinations may increase the risk of outbreaks of infectious diseases. Therefore, it is imperative to emphasize the importance of adhering to the recommended vaccination schedules to ensure timely protection against VPDs. Efforts should also focus on enhancing parents’ and caregivers’ awareness and understanding of vaccinations, especially those scheduled for later in a child’s life, in order to ensure that older children receive the recommended vaccinations. Additionally, future studies should be conducted to examine coverage by each vaccine separately, as well as timeliness, and vaccine hesitancy and its potential impact on coverage. These studies may help reveal areas for improvement and inform targeted interventions to improve overall routine vaccination coverage and protect children and the population at large in Armenia.
