Non-small cell lung carcinoma with co-expression of thyroid transcription factor-1 and p40: a case report and literature review
Authors: Chusheng Wei, Yishan Wang, Weijia Lu, Bi-yun Lin, Danhui Gan, Yuanzhi Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) with co-expression of thyroid transcription factor-1 and p40 is an extremely rare and diagnostically challenging subtype. These tumors exhibit both glandular and squamous features, which reflect a dual-lineage differentiation pattern.Case presentationA 78-year-old male with a history of smoking and alcohol consumption was found to have a solid nodule in the right peripheral lung on CT. Background

Non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) is the most common type of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all cases, and includes lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and large cell carcinoma. In pathological diagnosis, immunohistochemical markers such as thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) and p40 are essential for distinguishing LUAD from LUSC, and they typically show mutually exclusive expression patterns [1].

However, recent studies have identified rare cases of NSCLC in which TTF-1 and p40 are diffusely co-expressed within the same tumor cell population [2]. The specific phenotype challenges the traditional paradigm of NSCLC classification based on morphological and immunophenotypic features. The underlying molecular mechanisms and clinical significance of these tumors remain poorly understood, and no consensus exists regarding the pathological classification and clinical stratification of patients with this disease. In particular, it is not yet established whether this subtype harbors unique driver alterations. Here we report a case of NSCLC with co-expression of TTF-1 and p40 and provide a comprehensive analysis of its clinicopathological and molecular features. Additionally, we review 48 similarly published cases, including 26 with next-generation sequencing (NGS) data, to summarize the clinicopathological and molecular features and to propose diagnostic and pathogenic implications for this rare NSCLC subtype.

Case presentation

The patient was a 78-year-old male with a long-standing history of smoking, alcohol consumption, past tuberculosis, and chronic bronchitis. Three years prior, elevated serum tumor markers were noted: carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA): 10.08 ng/mL, neuron specific enolase (NSE): 20.0 µg/L, and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC): 4.1 ng/mL. Chest CT showed a 0.7 cm×1.3 cm patchy high-density nodule in the lateral segment of the right middle lobe with internal cavitation and ill-defined margins (Fig. 1A). Two years later, chest CT showed nodule growth to 1.2 cm×1.4 cm, with persistent cavitation, spiculated margins, and pleural adhesion, strongly suggestive of malignancy (Fig. 1B). PET-CT showed a 1.4 cm×1.3 cm×1.2 cm solid nodule with lobulated contours, pleural traction, and increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake (standard uptake value max 6.9), consistent with peripheral lung carcinoma (Fig. 1C). Contrast-enhanced CT showed moderate heterogeneous enhancement and persistent spiculation, further supporting malignancy (Fig. 1D). The patient subsequently underwent thoracoscopic radical resection of the right lung lesion, and the specimen was submitted for pathological evaluation. At seven months postoperatively, the patient had recovered well without radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and there was no radiological or clinical evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis.

The resected lung tissue measured approximately 13.0 cm×8.0 cm×2.5 cm, appearing red and soft in texture. A grey and solid nodule measuring approximately 1.2 cm in diameter was identified adjacent to the visceral pleura with an ill-defined border (Fig. 2A). Histologically, the tumor exhibited solid and nested architecture (Fig. 2B), with occasional pseudoglandular or slit-like spaces and areas of palisading or basal-like structures (Fig. 2C). No definitive features of LUAD (acinar, papillary, micropapillary) or LUSC (keratinization, intercellular bridges) were identified. Periodic acid-Schiff staining and Alcian blue staining revealed focal deposition of neutral and acidic mucin within luminal spaces and the cytoplasm. The tumor was composed of moderately sized cells characterized by eosinophilic cytoplasm, enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and frequent mitotic figure (Fig. 2D), with scattered giant tumor cells also observed (Fig. 2E). Multifocal spread through air spaces was observed at the tumor periphery (Fig. 2F). No invasion of the visceral pleura (confirmed by elastic fiber staining), vessels, nerves, or 26 resected lymph nodes was detected.

Immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridization results were as follows: TTF-1 (diffuse strong positivity, Fig. 2G), CK7 (diffuse strong positivity), Napsin A (-), p40 (diffuse strong positivity, Fig. 2H), p63 (diffuse strong positivity), CK5/6 (diffuse moderate positivity), EGFR (+), HER2 (1+), Ki-67 (35% +, Fig. 2I), p53 (70% +), RB (+), SMARCA4 (+), CD56 (-), Chromogranin A (-), Synaptophysin (-), ALK (-), Cyclin D1 (-), p16 (-), Epstein-Barr virus-encoded small RNAs (-, by in situ hybridization).

NGS was performed using a targeted panel covered 638 common oncogenic genes, including full-exon sequencing of 553 genes, 38 gene rearrangements, 60 genes related to chemotherapeutic response, and approximately 9,200 single nucleotide polymorphisms (1.2 Mb). The assay detected single nucleotide variants, small insertions/deletion, gene fusions, and copy number variations. In addition, tumor mutation burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), and human leucocyte antigen typing were also evaluated. NGS revealed multiple gene alterations associated with LUSC, including somatic mutations in LRP1B and TP53, as well as copy number gains in TP63, SOX2, and CDKN2A/B (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusions

This case represents a poorly differentiated NSCLC lacking definitive features of either LUAD or LUSC. Immunohistochemistry revealed diffuse strong expression of TTF-1, CK7, p40, and p63, indicating dual immunophenotypic differentiation. Unlike classical adenosquamous carcinoma, this tumor exhibited clear co-expression of both TTF-1 and p40 within individual tumor cells. Negative staining for neuroendocrine markers and Epstein-Barr virus-encoded small RNAs ruled out neuroendocrine carcinoma and Epstein-Barr virus related tumors. Histologically, basal-like features, scattered giant tumor cell, Napsin A negativity, and partial positivity mucin staining were detected in some cells or regions and consistent with previously reported cases of NSCLC with co-expression of TTF-1 and p40, suggesting a distinct and reproducible entity with shared biological features and mechanisms.

From a clinical and radiological perspective, review of 49 reported cases, including the present case, indicates that patients’ ages ranged from 23 to 94 years (mean 66, median 70), with 35 males and 14 females. Smoking history was present in 29 cases and absent in 10 cases. Tumors were located mostly in the peripheral lung (35 cases) and less frequently in central regions (9 cases). Radiologically, most tumors were solid nodules (11 cases), with a few mixed (1 case) or ground-glass nodules (2 cases); margins were clear in 6 cases and blurred or spiculated in 9 cases; internal vacuoles were observed in 4 cases, absent in 7 cases. PD-L1 expression was reported in 11 cases, ranging from 0 to 95%, with a mean of 35% and a median of 10%. Considering these tumors frequently occur in the peripheral lung, future studies exploring the molecular programs of basal cells in both central and peripheral regions may help delineate the ontogeny of this unique biphenotypic tumor, providing insight into the mechanisms underlying dual-lineage differentiation.

In the present case, NGS-based genomic profiling revealed recurrent alterations, including somatic mutations in TP53 and LRP1B, copy number gains of TP63 and SOX2, and copy number loss of CDKN2A/B. These alterations, typically enriched in LUSC, support a squamous-dominant molecular profile. Savari et al. reported a similarly squamous-skewed mutation spectrum in eight such cases [3]. Review of 26 published cases with NGS data (Table 2), revealed recurrent alterations in TP53, FGFR1, CDKN2A, EGFR, KRAS, MYC, NF1 and AKT1 (Table 3). EGFR and KRAS are typical of LUAD, whereas the remaining gene are enriched in LUSC, collectively reflecting a molecular landscape of “squamous predominance with glandular coexistence”.

Mutations in TP53, copy number loss of CDKN2A, and amplifications of SOX2, TP63, FGFR1, and MYC are frequently observed in LUSC. Loss of TP53 contributes to genomic instability and may provide a “genetic background” for biphenotypic differentiation [16]. CDKN2A alterations promote dysregulated cell proliferation and differentiation [17]. Amplification of SOX2, TP63, FGFR1, and MYC plays a critical role in maintaining squamous lineage commitment. In particular, SOX2 protein may initiate epithelial differentiation by activating TP63 expression, and together they cooperatively regulate squamous differentiation [18]. Amplification of FGFR1 can promote a stem cell-like phenotype through activation of the GLI2 signalling pathway, which may underlie the biphenotypic (adenosquamous) features observed in this NSCLC subtype [19]. Meanwhile, a subset of LUAD-associated driver genes, such as EGFR and KRAS, are also present in some cases, suggesting that glandular differentiation pathways might be activated under specific microenvironmental conditions, leading to biphenotypic differentiation through interference with complex signaling networks [20].

Two main hypotheses have been proposed regarding the histogenesis of these tumors. The first suggests stem/progenitor plasticity, in which basal-like cells in distal airways co-expressing TTF-1 and p40 undergo dual-lineage differentiation under microenvironmental pressure [2, 6]. The second point to FGFR1-driven transdifferentiation from basal progenitors modulated by concomitant adenocarcinoma-related drivers [3]. Nevertheless, a consensus on nomenclature and classification has yet to be reached. Pelosi and Savari have advocated recognizing this entity as a distinct NSCLC subtype with dual differentiation [3, 6]. In view of the concurrent squamous and glandular features observed across morphology, immunophenotype, and genomic alterations, it seems reasonable to consider these tumors as a subtype of NSCLC, termed “bi-phenotypic NSCLC with squamous and glandular differentiation”.

Interestingly, despite lacking typical differentiation morphology, this tumor demonstrated slow progression over a two-year period, evolving from a 0.7 cm×1.3 cm patchy nodule into a 1.2 cm×1.4 cm solid, lobulated nodule without evidence of locoregional spread. This is in contrast to the aggressive nature of these tumors reported in the literature, where most patients presented with stage III/IV disease and exhibited metastatic disease at diagnosis or during progression [3]. This unusually indolent course in our case may reflect patient-specific influences, underscoring inter-individual variability in tumor biology. From a therapeutic perspective, complete surgical resection served as the mainstay of treatment, providing satisfactory disease control. Due to advanced age and other factors, the patient did not receive chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Genomic profiling did not reveal actionable drivers such as EGFR, ALK or FGFR1, limiting the feasibility of targeted therapy. Notably, in previously reported cases, the presence of alterations such as EGFR mutations or FGFR1 amplification provided opportunities for targeted therapy, highlighting the importance of comprehensive NGS in these rare tumors. Currently, however, there remains insufficient information to accurately assess the prognosis and overall survival for patients with this rare tumor type.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of NSCLC with co-expression of TTF-1 and p40, displaying both squamous and glandular immunophenotypes. Integrated histological, immunophenotypic, and molecular analyses suggest that these tumors may originate from stem-like basal cells with dual-lineage potential. Genomic profiling reveals a molecular landscape of “squamous predominance with glandular features”, driven by activation of oncogenes (EGFR, KRAS, SOX2, TP63, FGFR1) and inactivation of tumor suppressors (TP53, CDKN2A). Given the diagnostic complexity and lineage ambiguity of this subtype of NSCLC, molecular testing such as NGS-based genomic profiling will be helpful for diagnosis and stratification of these patients for personalized therapy.
