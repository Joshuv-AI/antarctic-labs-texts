Single-Cell Transcriptomics Reveals the Immune Pathogenesis of Isolated Coronary Arteritis Presenting as Refractory In-Stent Restenosis
Authors: Miao Yu, Yu Jiang, Ji Huang, Zhi-Yao Wei, Zhi-Feng Song, Fang-Shun Tan, Si-Zhuang Huang, Jian-Song Yuan, Shao-Dong Ye, Jin-Gang Cui, Han-Jun Zhao, Li-Jian Gao, Hong-Zhao You, Yan Zeng, Wei-Xian Yang, Hai-Yan Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Refractory recurrent in-stent restenosis (RISR), characterized by frequent and persistent recurrence of luminal narrowing at previously treated lesions, has emerged as a major challenge in contemporary interventional cardiology. With the rapidly expanding population undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), RISR is no longer a rare clinical phenomenon. Patients typically require repeated target lesion revascularization within short intervals, with reported 2-year target lesion revascularization rates exceeding 50%.1, 2, 3 Moreover, target lesions are often anatomically complex, frequently involving severe calcification or chronic total occlusion, rendering clinical management difficult. One in 4 patients present with acute coronary syndrome at admission, and ultimately experience unfavorable long-term outcomes.4 Traditional strategies, including repeated interventions, intravascular brachytherapy, rotational atherectomy, and intensified lipid- and glucose-lowering therapies, have failed to interrupt the vicious cycle of recurrent restenosis, suggesting that RISR is unlikely to represent a purely mechanical failure or atherosclerosis-driven process. In this context, coronary arteritis warrants consideration as a potential underlying mechanism.

Coronary arteritis generally refers to coronary artery involvement secondary to autoimmune diseases or systemic vasculitis, most commonly observed in systemic lupus erythematosus, Takayasu arteritis, giant cell arteritis, polyarteritis nodosa, and IgG4-related disease.5 It has been recognized as an independent risk factor for in-stent restenosis (ISR) and a cause of nonatherosclerotic myocardial ischemia, myocardial infarction, and even sudden cardiac death.6 However, in recent years, multiple case reports have described a distinct clinical phenotype in which vasculitis was confined exclusively to the coronary arteries, defined as isolated coronary arteritis (ICA).7, 8, 9 These patients have shown no detectable systemic involvement, normal serum autoantibodies and inflammatory marker values, such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate. Frequent episodes of RISR represented the first typical clinical manifestation prompting evaluation.7 Imaging findings supported the inflammatory hypothesis: positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) demonstrated increased tracer uptake along stented segments,9 and coronary computed tomography angiography revealed elevated pericoronary fat attenuation index,10 sometimes even accompanied by perivascular soft tissue thickening.11,12 These lesions responded poorly to conventional revascularization or intensive atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease–directed therapy, whereas some exploratory cases suggested benefit from corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy, further implying an immune-inflammatory mechanism distinct from conventional ISR.7,9

Conventional determinants of ISR have predominantly involved mechanical and biological factors,13 with stent underexpansion recognized as the leading cause.14,15 However, advances in contemporary practice has substantially improved this issue. Pathologically, ISRs are generally attributed to 2 major processes: early, associated with neointimal hyperplasia after mechanical vascular injury; and late, primarily attributed to neoatherosclerosis secondary to delayed endothelial repair, typically occurring more than 1 year after stent implantation. These 2 pathological processes are readily distinguishable by intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or optical coherence tomography (OCT).16 Based on published literature and our prior intravascular observations, ICA-RISR lesions are predominantly composed of dense fibrotic neointima, indicating sustained inflammatory activation and abnormal vascular smooth muscle cell migration, proliferation, and phenotypic switching, consistent with the clinical course of RISR occurring within 1 year.

To sum up, within the increasing PCI population, ICA-RISR has emerged as a critical issue but lack of precise mechanistic investigations and a systematic immunological framework make clinical management very challenging. Even coronary artery bypass grafting does not eliminate risk, as persistent vascular inflammation predisposes patients to early graft failure and adverse cardiovascular events.

Against this background, the present study innovatively collected coronary blood samples directly from culprit lesions in patients with isolated coronary arteritis presenting as RISR (ICA-RISR) and performed single-cell RNA sequencing to delineate the local immune landscape. We further integrated in vitro functional experiments and in vivo vascular wire injury models to validate key molecular pathways, aiming to elucidate the immunopathogenic mechanisms underlying RISR driven by ICA and to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

Data availability and ethics statements

The datasets that underpin the results of this study are accessible from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Gene expression datasets are available in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE283461). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CAMS) and Peking Union Medical College (PUMC), and all human blood samples were collected with written informed consent from participants.

Enrollment of study participants

Diagnosed by coronary angiography, 18 RISR patients and 18 non-ISR patients were enrolled, of whom 20 patients (10 RISR and 10 non-ISR) were used for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and the remaining 16 patients (8 RISR and 8 non-ISR) were for experimental validation.

In this study, isolated coronary arteritis presenting as refractory recurrent in-stent restenosis (ICA-RISR) was defined as a clinical entity fulfilling all of the following criteria: 1) coronary angiography confirming that at least 1 target lesion met the definition of refractory recurrent ISR, defined as ≥2 episodes of angiographically confirmed ISR (diameter stenosis ≥50% or its edges) at the same target lesion after technically successful revascularization; 2) intravascular imaging with IVUS or OCT was mandatory to exclude restenosis attributable to mechanical causes, including stent underexpansion, malapposition, or fracture, as well as neoatherosclerosis characterized by lipid-rich or calcified plaque, with only lesions predominantly composed of homogeneous fibrotic neointima without features of neoatherosclerosis being eligible; 3) PET/CT using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) or fibroblast activation protein inhibitor demonstrating increased tracer uptake localized to the RISR target lesion or adjacent coronary segment, consistent with active coronary inflammation; 4) comprehensive clinical, serological, and imaging evaluations excluding systemic inflammatory, autoimmune, genetic, or thrombotic disorders known to involve the coronary arteries, including but not limited to systemic vasculitides, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, IgG4-related disease, Erdheim-Chester disease, antiphospholipid syndrome, and congenital or inherited thrombotic disorders; 5) no clinical or imaging evidence of inflammatory or structural involvement of extra-coronary arterial beds was identified, supporting isolated coronary artery involvement; and 6) all patients achieving guideline-recommended targets for optimal control of conventional atherosclerotic risk factors, including blood pressure <130/80 mm Hg, glycated hemoglobin <7% in diabetic patients, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol <1.8 mmol/L, and confirmed smoking cessation.

Conversely, non-ISR was defined as stent patency at least 1 year after implantation. All target lesions in this study involved initially implanted drug-eluting stents.

All RISR patients were also enrolled in our anti-inflammatory clinical trial for RISR (prospective, randomized, open-label and multicenter, NCT06090890). Supplemental Table 1 provides a detailed presentation of the inclusion and exclusion criteria for RISR patients. Supplemental Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3 detail the clinical information of the discovery and validation groups, respectively, along with OCT image examples shown in Supplemental Figure 1.

Sample collection

Coronary blood was collected during coronary angiography. With coronary angiography guidance, we used a catheter to collect coronary artery blood from the side of the target lesion, instantly stored in EDTA tubes and placed it on ice at 4 °C for processing. To maintain consistency, all samples were collected before the PCI procedure to minimize the influence of intravascular manipulations.

Sample processing and coronary blood mononuclear cell isolation

Coronary blood mononuclear cells (CBMCs) were isolated using density gradient centrifugation with Histopaque 1119 (Sigma, 11191) and 1077 (Sigma, 10771). Fresh blood was layered over this gradient and centrifuged to separate the blood components by density. The CBMC layer was then washed, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Solarbio, D1040), and purified by removing residual red blood cells with a lysis solution (Solarbio, R1010), preparing them for further analysis.

10× library preparation and sequencing

Beads with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and cell barcodes were loaded nearly to saturation, ensuring each cell was paired with a bead within a Gel Beads–in-emulsion. Upon exposure to a cell lysis buffer, polyadenylated RNA molecules hybridized to the beads. The beads were then collected into a single tube for reverse transcription. During cDNA synthesis, each cDNA molecule was tagged at the 3′ end (corresponding to the 5′ end of an mRNA transcript) with a UMI and a cell label indicating its cell of origin. Subsequently, the 10× beads underwent second-strand cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and universal amplification. Sequencing libraries were prepared using randomly interrupted whole-transcriptome amplification products to enrich the 5′ ends of transcripts linked with the cell barcode and UMI. Single-cell RNA libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 5′ v2 Reagent kit (10× Genomics) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each sequencing library was assigned a unique sample index. The libraries were quantified using a High-Sensitivity DNA Chip on a Bioanalyzer 2100 and the Qubit High-Sensitivity DNA Assay from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using PE150 chemistry.

V(D)J libraries were also generated for immune repertoire sequencing. However, these data were not included in the present analysis.

scRNA-seq data processing

Reads were processed using the Cell Ranger (v7.1.0) pipeline with default settings and recommended parameters. FASTQ files from Illumina sequencing were aligned to the homo sapiens genome (GRCh38.p13) using the STAR algorithm. Gene-barcode matrices were created for each sample by counting UMIs and filtering out non–cell associated barcodes. A comprehensive gene-barcode matrix displaying cell-specific barcodes and gene expression counts was then generated. This data were imported into the Seurat (v4.1.1) R toolkit for quality control and subsequent scRNA-seq analysis. Quality control filtering was performed in Seurat based on nFeature_RNA (number of detected genes), nCount_RNA (UMI counts), mitochondrial gene percentage, ribosomal gene percentage, and doublet identification using DoubletFinder. To account for interindividual biological and technical heterogeneity, a per-sample distribution-based approach was applied. High-end outliers of percent.mt were removed using mean + 2SD, while nFeature_RNA and nCount_RNA were filtered using mean±2SD to exclude low-complexity and abnormally high-count cells. The expression of mitochondrial genes was assessed using the PercentageFeatureSet function in Seurat. Data normalization (NormalizeData function) was carried out to extract a subset of variable genes, which were identified while accounting for the correlation between variability and average expression. We integrated data from various samples by identifying “anchors” between datasets using FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions in Seurat. Subsequent principal component analysis (PCA) reduced the data to the top 30 PCA components after scaling. The clusters were visualized on a 2-dimensional map using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Autophagy pathway activity was quantified using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat, based on a curated autophagy-related gene set obtained from the Human Autophagy Database (HADb; https://autophagy.lu/genes/), which is provided in Supplemental data 4.

SCENIC analysis

Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) transcription factor activity analysis was carried out using the “SCENIC” R package, which is designed to deduce coexpression regulatory networks between transcription factors and potential target genes from scRNA-seq data.17 The initial input was a normalized feature-barcode expression matrix. The process involved 3 main steps: 1) the “GENIE3” R package was used to identify potential transcription factor targets through coexpression analysis; (2) the “RcisTarget” R package conducted transcription factor motif enrichment analysis to pinpoint direct targets; and 3) the “AUCell” R package quantified the activity levels of these direct targets in individual cells.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

To investigate differences in biological states and pathways between the monocytes of RISR and non-ISR groups, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, a computational method that determines whether an a priori defined set of genes shows statistically significant, concordant differences between 2 biological states, was performed. The gene sets for the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp), with a sort algorithm of Signal2Noise. Our study specifically used the curated gene sets (C2) and ontology gene sets (C5).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

The pseudotime series analysis was conducted using the R package monocle2 (v2.22.0). Initially, the “differentialGeneTest” function within Monocle2 was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from each cluster. Subsequent dimensionality reduction was conducted using the DDRTree algorithm, based on these significant DEGs. The cell lineage trajectory, which includes cell cluster and pseudotime, was deduced using the default settings of Monocle2 after reducing dimensionality and ordering cells and was visualized using the “plot cell trajectory” function. Subsequently, DEGs along the pseudotime, referred to as “pseudotime-dependent genes,” were identified using the “differentialGeneTest” function. The “plot genes in pseudotime” and “plot pseudotime heatmap” functions were then used to display the dynamic changes in gene expression of pseudotime-dependent genes along the pseudotime, with significant ones illustrated via a heatmap. Additionally, the BEAM test in Monocle2 was used to detect dynamically expressed branch-dependent gene expression along each branch, and significant branch-dependent genes were also displayed using a heatmap.

RNA velocity

RNA velocity analysis was conducted utilizing the scVelo package,18 which employs a likelihood-based dynamical model to decipher the comprehensive transcriptional dynamics of both spliced and unsliced mRNA for each gene. This analysis enabled the identification of transient cellular states within our dataset and facilitated predictions of directional shifts in transcriptomic signatures across the trajectories delineated. These predictions hinge on gene-specific transcription, splicing, and mRNA degradation rates, allowing for an estimation of each cell's stage in its differentiation process. The overall RNA velocity, represented by a stream of arrows, is then visually integrated onto the UMAP embedding.

Intercellular communication

Analysis of cell-cell communication was performed using the scRNA-seq data through CellPhoneDB software (v4.1.0), available at www.cellphonedb.org. We focused on receptors and ligands that were expressed >10% of cells in any cell type from either the RISR or non-ISR group. If a ligand or receptor was not detectable, the corresponding cell-cell communication was deemed absent. The mean expression levels of each ligand-receptor pair across different cell types were examined, and only those pairs displaying a P value of <0.01 were considered significant and used to predict cell-cell communication across any 2 cell types.

CellChat receptor-ligand analysis

To investigate potential interactions between monocytes and other cell types, we employed the CellChat platform.19 Our analysis involved integrating our scRNA-seq Seurat object with the standalone CellChat Shiny App, specifically designed for exploring the Cell-Cell Communication Atlas. We employed default settings for all analyses, focusing on Secreted Signaling, ECM-Receptor interactions, and Cell-Cell Contact dynamics.

Cell culture

Human CBMCs, purchased from Procell Life Science and Technology (CP-H182), were cultured in CBMC complete culture medium (Procell Life Science & Technology, CM-H182). Human coronary artery smooth muscle cells (HCASMCs) (Procell Life Science & Technology, CP-H167) were cultured in HCASMC complete culture medium (Procell Life Science and Technology, CM-H167). All cells were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Flow cytometry cell sorting and flow cytometric analysis

Flow cytometry was performed to validate both the redistribution of monocyte subsets identified by scRNA-seq, particularly the FCGR3A+ monocytes, and the differential activation of the AP-1/MAPK signaling axis, including c-JUN, c-FOS expression, and MAPK phosphorylation levels, using monocytes derived from the RISR and non-ISR patient cohorts (including both discovery and validation cohorts where applicable).

A complete gating scheme is included in Supplemental Figure 6.

FITC 488

Cryopreserved CBMCs from RISR and non-ISR patients were quick-thawed, centrifuged, and incubated the CBMCs in blocking buffer for 5 minutes. For flow cytometry cell sorting, the CBMCs were stained using the FITC antihuman CD14 antibody (Biolegend, 366808). After 30 minutes of staining at 4 °C, CBMCs were washed and immediately kept on ice to sort monocytes (FACSAria II cell sorter, BD Biosciences). Only CD14+ monocytes were sorted into test tubes with 100 μL 2% BSA solution. For flow cytometric analysis, CBMCs were stained using the following fluorochrome-labeled antibodies: PE/Cyanine7 antihuman CD16 antibody (Biolegend, 302016), PE antihuman c-JUN antibody (CST, 15683), Alexa Fluor 647 antihuman c-FOS antibody (CST, 14188), APC antihuman phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) antibody (CST, 33370), PE antihuman phospho-p38 MAPK antibody (CST, 6908), and PE antihuman phospho-SAPK/JNK antibody (CST, 5755). After 30 minutes of staining at 4 °C, the stained CBMCs were washed with FACSAria II and immediately analyzed with FlowJo Software.

Western blot

Cells were collected and then lysed in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89901) with a mixture of protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1048). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay before electrophoresed on 4% to 12% Bis-Tris Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NP0322BOX). The protein was then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Beyotime, FFP24). Then, the membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 hours at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies: p38 MAPK (CST, 8690), phospho-p38 MAPK (CST, 4511), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (CST, 4695), phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (CST, 4370), SAPK/JNK (CST, 9252), phospho-SAPK/JNK (CST, 4668), JUN (CST, 9165), c-FOS (CST, 2250), CCR1 (Abcam, 233,832), and GAPDH (CST, 2118) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. Detection was performed using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5800 Multi). The densitometry of target bands was normalized to internal control GAPDH.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA from cultured cells were extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596-018), according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using Reverse Transcription Reagent kits (Takara, RR047B). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A25743) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The expression levels of mRNA were normalized to GAPDH to account for cDNA loading differences, and the data were calculated using comparative 2−ΔΔCt methods. Each experiment included 3 technical replicates 6 independent repeats. The primer sequences for PCR are summarized in Supplemental Table 9.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay

Human IL-1β enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (eBioscience, E-HSEL-H0001), human IL-8 ELISA kit (eBioscience, E-HSEL-H0004), and human MIP-1α (macrophage inflammatory protein 1 alpha) ELISA kit (eBioscience, E-EL-H6213) were used to quantify the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-8, and CCL3 in the coronary plasma or supernatant. The following procedures were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Coculture experiment

Monocytes from RISR or non-ISR patients coculturing with HCASMCs was conducted using a Transwell coculture system (Corning, 3412). HCASMCs were seeded into the lower chamber, and once they reached 80% to 90% confluence, the inserts with monocytes from RISR or non-ISR patients were immediately placed onto the HCASMC wells. Cell proliferation and migration assays were subsequently performed after 12 hours of incubation.

EdU staining assay

EdU staining (Beyotime Biotechnology, C0078) was used to analyze HCASMC proliferation. Briefly, HCASMCs were seeded into a 6-well plate and cocultured with monocytes from RISR or non-ISR patients for 12 hours. The cells were fixed with PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Cell proliferation was observed using laser confocal microscopy.

Transwell assay and scratch wound healing assay

For the Transwell assay, HCASMCs were placed into the upper chamber of 24-well Transwells (Corning, 3422). Then, the monocytes from RISR or non-ISR patients were added in the lower chambers with complete medium. After 12 hours, the HCASMCs attached to the upper surface of the filter membranes were removed, and migrated cells on the lower surface were stained with crystal violet (0.2%). For the scratch wound healing assay, the HCASMCs were plated into 6-well plates and grown to 90% confluence. The monolayer was scratched with the tip of the pipette and washed with serum-free medium to remove detached cells. Then, the inserts with monocytes from RISR or non-ISR patients were placed onto the HCASMC wells. The scratches were photographed at 0 hours and 12 hours after wounding. The HCASMC migration distance was calculated as follows: migration area (%) = (M0 – Mn)/M0 × 100, where M0 = the initial scratch wound area and Mn = the scratch wound area at 12 hours of measurement.

Adeno-associated virus Vector design and production

To achieve targeted knockdown of the Fos gene, an adeno-associated virus (AAV) expression system carrying a miR30-based shRNA sequence was constructed. The target sequence (GCAGATGTCCGTCTCTA) was derived from the murine Fos gene (Gene ID: 14,281). The viral vector used was adeno-associated virus serotype 2 (AAV2), with a CMV promoter driving transcription.

To enable conditional knockdown of Fos specifically in monocytes, a double-floxed inverted orientation (DIO) cassette was incorporated into the construct, rendering shRNA expression dependent on Cre recombinase–mediated recombination at loxP sites. This strategy restricted gene silencing to Lyz2-Cre–positive monocytes. The final viral construct was designated as AAV2-CMV-DIO-shRNA-miR30, with a viral titer of 1 × 1013 vg/mL.

The control virus (Con-AAV) contained a nontargeting scramble shRNA sequence, matched in viral titer and vector backbone.

Validation of Fos gene knockdown efficiency

Two 4-week-old mice were intramedullarily injected with either Fos-AAV or Con-AAV into the femoral bone marrow cavity. After 3 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and monocytes were isolated from orbital blood using flow cytometry. Quantitative PCR was then performed to assess c-Fos mRNA levels, validating the knockdown efficiency of the AAV-delivered shRNA in monocytes.

Wire-induced carotid artery injury model in mice

Seven-week-old male mice (24-26 g) were used for the carotid artery injury model. Anesthesia was administered via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol. The surgical area was sterilized, and a 1-cm midline cervical incision was made. The left common carotid artery (LCCA), internal carotid artery, and external carotid artery (ECA) were bluntly dissected.

The distal end of the ECA and the superior thyroid artery were ligated using 8-0 sutures. Another 8-0 suture was placed loosely at the proximal ECA. Blood flow in the internal carotid artery and occipital artery was temporarily occluded using a 45° arterial clamp, and the proximal LCCA was clamped with a 90° vascular clip.

A small transverse arteriotomy was created between the ligature and carotid bifurcation on the ECA wall using microsurgical scissors. A guidewire was inserted through this opening into the LCCA, advanced gently until resistance was met at the proximal vascular clip. The wire was rotated alternately clockwise and counterclockwise during insertion and withdrawal, with each phase lasting approximately 5 seconds. After insertion, the wire was kept in place for 30 seconds to ensure sufficient endothelial contact. This procedure was repeated 5 times to simulate mechanical vascular injury.

In the sham-operated group, the same surgical exposure and vessel incision were performed without wire insertion or rotation.

Mice were sacrificed at 28 days after wire injury, and the injured LCCA was harvested for subsequent analyses.

Replicates and statistical considerations

Biological replicates (n) represent independent biological samples, including patient-derived monocytes, experimental animals, or independently prepared cell samples. Technical replicates were performed in parallel for each biological sample, and quantitative values represent the mean of technical replicates. All statistical analyses were based on biological replicates, which served as the unit of inference.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between 2 groups, if data followed a normal distribution, a 2-tailed Student's t-test was used. For non-normally distributed data, the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. For comparisons involving more than 2 groups, if data followed a normal distribution and satisfied the assumption of homogeneity of variances, analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test was used. For non-normally distributed data, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparison test was applied.

All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.3.3; R Core Team, 2024) or GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Coronary blood samples were obtained from culprit lesions in these RISR patients, while 10 non-ISR patients (defined as angiographically confirmed stent patency for at least 1 year after implantation) served as controls (Figure 1A). The scRNA-seq was performed on CBMCs using a droplet-based platform. After quality control, 151,424 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis: 68,350 (45.1%) from the RISR cohort and 83,074 (54.9%) from the non-ISR cohort, with a median of 7,310 (range 3,301-8,672) and 8,295 (range 3,244-13,162) cells per sample, respectively (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5). We applied the Seurat package for unsupervised clustering and visualized the data using UMAP (Figure 1C). Guided by canonical cell markers (Figure 1D, Supplemental Table 6), 9 major cell types were generated as follows: T cells (33.21%), natural killer (NK) cells (15.72%), B cells (13.55%), neutrophils (2.42%), monocytes (28.22%), macrophages (4.23%), conventional dendritic cells (0.63%), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (0.3%), and megakaryocytes (1.74%) (Supplemental data 1). Bar charts showed no significant differences in cell type distribution across individuals, suggesting no strong genetic backgrounds effects (Supplemental Figure 2B). Additionally, no significant differences were observed in each cell proportion between the RISR and non-ISR cohorts (Figure 1F).

Next, differential expression analysis performed on the total monocyte population identified 76 upregulated and 40 downregulated DEGs in the RISR group. Enrichment analysis indicated these RISR-upregulated DEGs primarily enriched in inflammation-related pathways (Figure 2K). Among these, the core subunits of the AP-1 transcription factor complex (JUN, JUNB, JUND, FOS, FOSB) exhibited the most pronounced upregulation (Figure 2H). Ingenuity pathway analysis supported its dominant position within the network of RISR-upregulated DEGs (Figure 2I). In parallel, SCENIC transcriptional regulatory network analysis confirmed that both the expression levels and transcriptional activity of AP-1 were significantly enhanced in RISR monocytes (Supplemental Figure 4A, Figure 2J left). Further motif enrichment analysis predicted that AP-1 may directly regulate several proinflammatory cytokine genes such as CXCL8, IL1B, and CCL3 (Figure 2I), whose expression levels were also significantly elevated in the RISR group (Figure 2H).

To further characterize the activation pattern of AP-1, we examined the expression of its core subunits across all 7 monocyte subsets. Consistently, all 5 key AP-1 components (FOS, FOSB, JUN, JUNB, and JUND) were significantly upregulated in each of the monocyte subsets in RISR patients (Supplemental data 3), and transcriptional activity scores based on AP-1 target gene sets were similarly significantly increased in all subsets (Figure 2J-right).

Collectively, these results indicated that the aberrant activation of AP-1 transcription factor constituted a core transcriptional feature of coronary monocytes in RISR. This dysregulation was pervasive across all monocyte subsets and drove the upregulation of key proinflammatory factors (eg, CXCL8, IL1B, CCL3), indicating that AP-1 may function as a central regulatory hub in the pathogenesis of RISR.

To pinpoint the specific MAPK subpathway involved, we performed flow cytometry (obtained from RISR and non-ISR, n = 5 per group) and Western blotting (n = 6 per group), which demonstrated selective upregulation of phosphorylated p38 MAPK (p-p38 Thr180/Tyr182) in RISR monocytes, whereas extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) activation showed no consistent changes (Figures 3B and 3C). To directly validate p38-mediated regulation of AP-1, we treated human monocytes with the MAPK activator (anisomycin), which successfully induced significant increases in both gene and protein expression of AP-1 subunits (FOS/JUN family members) (Figures 3D and 3E), confirming p38 MAPK as an upstream regulator of AP-1. These experiments established the p38 MAPK/AP-1 signaling relationship at the mechanistic level, providing a basis for subsequent investigation of physiologically relevant upstream triggers.

Next, cell communication analysis was performed to delineate the immune regulatory signals received by monocytes, which identified the CCL signaling pathway as a prominent incoming signal received by monocytes in the RISR group (Figure 3F, Supplemental Figure 5A), whereas monocytes from non-ISR group were largely unresponsive to this signal (Figure 3F, Supplemental Figure 5B). Thus, CCL pathway activation was considered to potentially underlie the exaggerated inflammatory response observed in RISR monocytes. Specifically, although CCL5 expression itself did not differ between groups, its receptor CCR1 was highly and specifically expressed on the surface of RISR monocytes (Figure 3G). Functional assays further confirmed that CCL5 stimulation of human monocytes activated p38 phosphorylation, upregulated AP-1 expression (Figures 3H to 3J), and promoted secretion of downstream inflammatory factors (CXCL8, IL1B, CCL3; n = 10 per group) (Figure 3K). These effects were completely abolished by pretreatment with the CCR1 antagonist BX471, which blocked both p38 MAPK/AP-1 activation and cytokine release (Figures 3I to 3K). These results established a core mechanism whereby CCL5 triggered the p38 MAPK-AP-1 inflammatory cascade via the CCR1 receptor.

Collectively, these results delineated the genetic alterations in RISR: elevated CCR1 expression on monocytes sensitized them to microenvironmental CCL5 stimulation, triggering p38 MAPK activation and subsequent upregulation of AP-1 transcriptional complex, ultimately leading to the excessive secretion of proinflammatory cytokines (CXCL8, IL1B, CCL3). This mechanistic insight provided a solid theoretical foundation for targeted anti-inflammatory therapies in RISR.

To further validate the role of AP-1 activation in modulating VSMC functional alterations, we experimentally manipulated the inflammatory state of monocytes. Pretreatment of monocytes with CCL5 (mimicking AP-1 activation) before coculture with HCASMCs significantly promoted VSMC proliferation and migration. Conversely, blocking monocyte AP-1 by its inhibitor T-5224 completely reversed the CCL5-induced enhancement of VSMC proliferative and migratory capacity (Figures 5H to 5M).

In summary, monocytes from RISR patients promoted functional alterations of VSMCs via an AP-1 dependent inflammatory signaling mechanism, ultimately contributing to pathological vascular remodeling and restenosis.

Discussion

The present study systematically elucidated the immunological mechanisms underlying ICA-RISR. By integrating single-cell transcriptomic profiling of coronary blood from culprit lesions with validation in independent clinical samples and complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments, we identified the monocyte CCR1/p38 MAPK/AP-1/inflammatory cytokine axis as a central driver of persistent vascular inflammation and abnormal VSMC functional alterations contributing to NIH. These findings provided a novel etiological framework for RISR, supported the recognition of ICA-RISR as an inflammatory vascular disease distinct from conventional mechanical or atherosclerotic ISR, and revealed a precise therapeutic target.

Traditionally, ISR has been attributed primarily to mechanical factors, and at later stages, to progressive neoatherosclerosis. However, these canonical mechanisms failed to adequately explain the distinctive features observed in ICA-RISR. Despite technically successful revascularization, target lesions progressed rapidly at the same target segment. Intravascular imaging consistently demonstrated dense fibrotic neointima without typical neoatherosclerosis, and PET/CT revealed increased tracer uptake at restenotic sites. Importantly, although RISR had been reported in patients with autoimmune disease or systemic vasculitis, none of the patients in our study fulfilled diagnostic criteria for these conditions based on available clinical, serologic, and imaging evidence.20 In addition, patch testing excluded hypersensitivity to stent materials.21 Taken together, these strict clinical criteria supported ICA-RISR as a localized immune-mediated vascular process. Increasing case reports have drawn clinical attention to this entity, but the lack of systematic mechanistic studies has left treatment largely empirical, relying on exploratory immunosuppressive therapy. In this setting, our study provided a clear operational definition and interrogated ICA-RISR at single-cell resolution.

Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that overall immune cell composition did not differ significantly between RISR and non-ISR patients; however, monocytes exhibited a highly specific inflammatory activation profile. Notably, we observed a redistribution of monocyte subsets in ICA-RISR, characterized by a reduction in MHC-II+ monocytes and a relative expansion of FCGR3A+ monocytes. This shift may reflect chronic activation-induced functional reprogramming toward an innate inflammatory effector phenotype, consistent with prior reports linking expansion of nonclassic monocytes to adverse clinical outcomes. Under physiological conditions, monocytes participated in vascular repair by responding to injury and differentiating into macrophages. In ICA-RISR, genes encoding AP-1 components, particularly FOS and JUN family members, remained broadly upregulated across all monocyte subsets long after stent implantation, accompanied by markedly enhanced transcriptional activity. Multiple downstream inflammatory mediators and pathways were concurrently upregulated, positioning AP-1 as a central regulatory hub. AP-1 is a leucine zipper transcription factor that regulates gene transcription, through dimeric binding to specific DNA motifs (5′-TGAG/CTCA-3′). Aberrant AP-1 activation has been implicated in multiple systemic immunoinflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and autoimmune uveoretinitis.22,23 Our study is to our knowledge the first to report its pivotal role as an inflammatory hub in RISR ICA-RISR pathogenesis.

We further delineated the upstream mechanisms responsible for AP-1 dysregulation. Monocytes from ICA-RISR patients displayed selective upregulation of CCR1, rendering them abnormally sensitive to CCL5 in the local microenvironment. Activation of the CCL5/CCR1 axis selectively triggered phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, rather than ERK or JNK, thereby driving AP-1 expression and activation. This mechanism provided a plausible explanation for why intense local coronary inflammation occurred in ICA patients despite normal systemic inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein. Altered CCR1 expression led to exaggerated responses to homeostatic chemokine signals and amplified an inflammatory cascade, resulting in the production of cytokines such as CXCL8 and IL-1β. Coculture experiments demonstrated that monocytes from ICA-RISR patients, or monocytes prestimulated with CCL5, significantly promoted VSMC proliferation and migration, an effect completely abolished by the AP-1 inhibitor T-5224. These findings outlined a mechanistic sequence linking monocyte chemokine sensing, p38/AP-1 activation, cytokine release, VSMC functional alterations, and neointimal hyperplasia.

The translational relevance of these findings was reinforced by in vivo experiments. Given that AP-1 required dimerization for DNA binding and transcriptional function, and that loss of any subunit can effectively disrupt its regulator capacity, we designed an shRNA specifically targeting the Fos gene to inhibit AP-1 activity.24 In a guidewire-induced carotid artery injury model, monocyte-specific Fos knockdown resulted in approximately a 45% reduction in neointimal area, accompanied by significant decreases in luminal stenosis and the intima-to-media thickness ratio, accompanied by modulation of VSMC marker expression, including reduced α-SMA and increased OPN, the latter being a recognized marker associated with the synthetic VSMC phenotype. These results provided causal evidence that monocyte-derived AP-1 signaling was a key driver of pathological vascular remodeling after injury. These observations suggested that, in ICA-RISR, an immune-driven restenosis disease, therapeutic strategies might need to shift from repeated mechanical reintervention toward immune-targeted modulation.

Encouragingly, AP-1 has shown therapeutic promise across a range of inflammatory models. Inhibitors such as T-5224, doxycycline, and curcumin have demonstrated efficacy in inflammation-related models such as arthritis, aneurysm formation, and dermatitis.25,26 Notably, T-5224 has been shown to suppress VSMC phenotypic switching in experimental models of aortic dissection.27 Our findings offered a rational foundation for lesion-directed delivery of AP-1 inhibitors, potentially via drug-coated balloons or stent platforms, to interrupt the inflammation restenosis cycle while limiting systemic immunosuppression.

Our group is currently establishing a prospective RISR cohort and conducting a randomized anti-inflammatory trial (NCT06090890). Nonetheless, important gaps remain. The determinants of individual susceptibility to ICA-RISR are unknown, and effective screening strategies are lacking. Whether chronic mechanical stimulation from stent implantation serves as a trigger for ICA onset, whether removal of mechanical stress could ameliorate disease activity, whether strategies that avoid permanent stent implantation (such as drug-coated balloons or bioresorbable scaffolds) might address the condition at its root, or whether high-risk individuals could be identified for stratified intervention before stent implantation remain important questions for future investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provided the first systematic immunological characterization of RISR and identified aberrant activation of the monocyte CCR1/p38 MAPK/AP-1 signaling axis as the central driver of localized immune inflammation. These insights reframed RISR from a purely mechanical complication to a mechanistically defined inflammatory disorder, opening avenues for precision, immune-based therapy.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Refractory in-stent restenosis may represent an immune-mediated vascular inflammatory disorder rather than a purely mechanical complication. Recognition of this mechanism may refine diagnostic evaluation and support consideration of targeted anti-inflammatory strategies in selected patients.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future studies are needed to validate the CCR1/p38 MAPK/AP-1 axis in larger clinical cohorts and to explore targeted anti-inflammatory interventions. These may include development of AP-1–directed therapies, such as lesion-targeted drug delivery platforms (eg, drug-coated balloons), to modulate local vascular inflammation while minimizing systemic immunosuppression.
