Individualized Network Topography in Pre-adolescent Children and Adults Using Naturalistic Precision fMRI
Authors: Shefali Rai, Kate J. Godfrey, Kirk Graff, Ryann Tansey, Daria Merrikh, Shelly Yin, Matthew Feigelis, Damion V. Demeter, Tamara Vanderwal, Deanna J. Greene, Signe Bray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Group-averaged network definitions, commonly used in developmental functional connectivity research, limit our understanding of how network topography may change with age and can lead to inaccurate estimates of age effects on intra- and inter-network functional connectivity. Here, we collected a precision fMRI dataset from 24 parent-child pairs (children 6–8 years, 13 females; adults 33–47 years, 12 females) during passive video viewing and derived individual template-matched functional network maps from 60 min of motion-censored data per participant. Overall, large-scale network architecture was broadly shared between children and adults, with no age effects observed for network-level surface area, few age differences in network boundaries, and generally lower assignment confidence in children. Considering interindividual similarity in network topography, association networks showed stronger within-family similarity and no overall effect of age on similarity of networks between pairs of individuals. We further asked how individualized network definitions might impact estimates of age effects on dense functional connectivity. While all approaches pointed to greater within-network functional connectivity in adults, we found that individualized approaches had larger age effects and lower sensitivity to head motion. Together, our results suggest that, relative to adults, preadolescent children show reduced network assignment confidence and weaker within-network connectivity but limited differences in network borders and size and underscore the value of individualized mapping for improving topographic reliability and increasing sensitivity to age effects.

Significance Statement

Brain network organization varies between individuals, yet most research on age effects on functional connectivity relies on adult-derived group templates, potentially misrepresenting pediatric brain organization and hindering interpretations of age differences in network topography and functional connectivity. Here, we used naturalistic precision fMRI to define functional networks in individuals and compared topography and connectivity between adults and preadolescent children. We found limited age effects on network topography and that children had lower confidence in their network assignments. Importantly, we found that defining networks in individuals improved sensitivity to age effects on functional connectivity and reduced dependence on head motion. These findings highlight the potential for person-specific network definitions for improving sensitivity and specificity of findings in pediatric functional connectivity research.

Introduction

Describing how functional brain networks change across childhood can provide insights into cognitive and behavioral maturation and establish a foundation for clinical investigations (Fair et al., 2009; Grayson and Fair, 2017; Satterthwaite et al., 2018). Studying developmental effects on functional connectivity has been challenging; however, as pediatric scans tend to have high head motion (Satterthwaite et al., 2012; Engelhardt et al., 2017; Frew et al., 2022), obtaining longer scans needed to optimize test–retest reliability presents challenges to compliance (Raschle et al., 2012; Barkovich et al., 2019), and the network templates used to define FC and interpret within- and between-network differences have largely been derived from groups of adults (Power et al., 2011; Thomas Yeo et al., 2011). Precision fMRI (Gordon et al., 2017b; Gratton and Braga, 2026), a technique in which relatively long fMRI timeseries are collected per individual in order to achieve reliable mapping of networks and connectivity estimates, can help to circumvent these issues and ultimately improve our understanding of how functional connectivity strength and topography associates with age. Here, we use precision fMRI in a sample of preadolescent children and adults to investigate age effects on individually defined network topography and dense functional connectivity.

Functional network topography refers to the spatial location of functional networks (Gordon et al., 2017b; Lynch et al., 2020; Demeter and Greene, 2025). While canonical network topography is broadly similar across individuals (Damoiseaux et al., 2006), precision functional mapping in adults has shown profound and reliable interindividual variations (Gordon et al., 2017b; Seitzman et al., 2019; Gratton et al., 2020b; Dworetsky et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2025), with greater intervariability in association relative to sensory networks (Braga and Buckner, 2017; Kong et al., 2019). Emerging literature is using precision approaches to describe topography in children (Keller et al., 2023), including in infants (Labonte et al., 2024; Moore et al., 2024). However, while some work has combined across datasets to consider age effects on network boundaries across adolescence (Tooley et al., 2022a,b; Sun et al., 2025), data are limited on whether and how networks defined precisely in individual adults and children may differ (Demeter et al., 2025), a gap we aim to fill here.

To address the challenge of compliance in acquiring low-motion fMRI across multiple sessions in children, we chose passive viewing over task-free rest during fMRI acquisition. Passive viewing improves compliance and reduces head motion in pediatric populations (Vanderwal et al., 2015; Greene et al., 2018) and yields reliable individualized connectomes (O'Connor et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017; Jiahui et al., 2023; Rai et al., 2025; Shearer et al., 2025). Furthermore, naturalistic paradigms maintain intrinsic FC patterns and network architecture while offering greater ecological validity (Bray et al., 2015; Finn et al., 2017; Vanderwal et al., 2017) and can increase sensitivity in FC–behavior associations (Finn and Bandettini, 2021). Here, we asked whether network topography defined during passive viewing differs between adults and preadolescent children.

Specifically, we compared individualized network organization between preadolescent children (6.56–8.92 years) and parents recruited as adult controls, with familial relationships considered in analyses. We applied template-matching (TM) to derive individualized network maps and assessed age-related differences in topography, network assignment confidence, network size, and spatial location. We tested whether topography of parent–child pairs were more similar than unrelated pairs, given prior evidence of heritability in FC and network topography (Sinclair et al., 2015; Anderson et al., 2021), and whether adults or children had more individually unique network topography. Finally, we examined whether individualized network definitions improved sensitivity to age-related differences in FC.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The PreciseKIDS study recruited 25 parent–child pairs, comprising five female–female, five male–male, seven female–male, and eight male–female dyads. At the first session, children were aged between 6.56 and 8.92 years (mean, 7.88 years; SD, 0.69 years; 13 F) and one of their parents aged between 33.75 and 47.13 years (mean, 41.39 years; SD, 3.63 years; 12 F). One parent–child pair was excluded from this study due to excessive motion (<40 min of usable data for the child), resulting in a final sample of 24 pairs (Table 1). Two families contributed data from two parents and two children. Participants were recruited through community advertisements and a database of participants from previous studies who provided consent to contact for future research. Exclusion criteria were self-reported diagnosis of neurological, mental health condition, brain injury, or chronic health condition. Children gave assent, and parents provided written informed consent for their own and their child's participation. All procedures were approved by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB19-1973). As done previously (Rai et al., 2025), we classified motion groups based on a threshold of 3,863 volumes, resulting in a low-motion adult (LMA) group (n = 22, 11 M, 11 F; aged 33.75–47.13 years; mean FD, 0.048–0.163), a low-motion child (LMC) group (n = 10, 4 M, 6 F; aged 6.56–8.92 years; mean FD, 0.050–0.270), and a high-motion child (HMC) group (n = 14, 8 M, 6 F; aged 7.03–8.90 years; mean FD: 0.122–0.939) for analyses, where appropriate.

MRI data collection

Parent–child pairs each completed four MRI sessions at the Alberta Children's Hospital in Calgary, Canada, ∼3–4 weeks apart (mean = 25.9 d between visits). Scans were conducted on a 3 T GE MR750w system with a 32-channel head coil. EEG and cognitive assessments were also collected as part of the study but not reported here. Session order was pseudorandomized, i.e., for half of the families, parents underwent EEG followed by MRI while their children completed MRI first, then EEG; the order was reversed for the other half of the families. Each MRI session included a T1-weighted 3D BRAVO sequence (0.8 mm isotropic voxels; TR, 6.764 ms; TE, 2.908 ms; FA, 10°). Functional imaging data were acquired in six runs per session using multiecho, multiband T2*-weighted gradient-echo echoplanar sequence (ME-EPI) that covered the whole brain (in-plane resolution, 3.4 mm; 45 slices; slice thickness, 3 mm; slice spacing, 0.4 mm; TR, 2 s; TEs, 13, 32.3, 51.6 ms; flip angle, 70°; field of view, 220 mm; phase encoding, anterior–posterior; phase acceleration, 2; multiband factor, 3). Each run included 205 volumes (after excluding five dummy volumes), resulting in ∼41 min of functional data per session and a total of ∼2.8 h per participant across all sessions.

During each session, participants completed two runs of each of three passive viewing conditions. Parents and children within a family viewed the videos in the same fixed order within a session, but this order was randomized across families. Behavioral data collection

Post-MRI, each participant completed surveys that assessed recall of the videos viewed during the six functional runs, along with self-reported measures of drowsiness and attention. Although these data were not analyzed as part of this study, participants reported remaining awake throughout scans, with a full analysis of these measures reported previously (Rai et al., 2025).

fMRI preprocessing

Preprocessing was implemented using a custom Nipype pipeline (v1.5.0; Gorgolewski et al., 2011) with tools from FSL (v6.0.0; Smith et al., 2004), ANTs (v2.3.4; Avants et al., 2011), and AFNI (v21.1.16; Cox, 1996).

For anatomical processing, T1-weighted images were processed using ANTs to perform bias field correction and skull stripping. Images were nonlinearly registered to either the adult MNI 152 atlas or the pediatric NIHPD 7–11-year-old atlas (Fonov et al., 2009, 2011) based on each participant's age. Tissue segmentations of gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid were generated and warped back to native space. Following this, FSL was used to create tissue masks, and AFNI was used to erode these masks by seven levels.

For functional processing, each echo was processed separately. The first five volumes were discarded using FSL's ExtractROI and motion correction was performed with FSL MCFLIRT, using the first volume of each run as the reference. Framewise displacement (FD; Power et al., 2014) was computed from the second echo and used as the motion reference for subsequent steps. Slice timing correction was applied using FSL slicetimer, and coregistration across echoes was performed using FSL FLIRT, aligning Echoes 2 and 3 to Echo 1 for each run. Echoes were then combined using FSL Merge. All video runs within each session were then coregistered to the reference image (Session 1, Echo 1) using FSL FLIRT, and the six runs across conditions were concatenated using FSL MERGE.

MEPI denoising and optimal combination

Using the tedana workflow (v0.0.12; DuPre et al., 2021), preprocessed echoes were denoised and optimally combined (OC) via the T2* combination method (Posse et al., 1999). Next, PCA dimensional reduction was performed using the Akaike information criterion option to improve independent component analysis (ICA) convergence. ICA was used to classify based on the Kundu decision tree (v2.5; Kundu et al., 2013) into BOLD (TE-dependent), non-BOLD (TE-independent), and uncertain (low-variance) components. Those automatically labeled as uncertain were then manually reviewed and reclassified as necessary to improve denoising accuracy, as suggested in prior work (Dipasquale et al., 2017; Griffanti et al., 2017; Mejia et al., 2020).

OC MEPI processing

Subsequent processing was applied on the individual OC data (4 sessions × 3 conditions × 2 runs). Each run was skull stripped using FSL BET and then FSL FLIRT for boundary-based registration to align to each participant's T1-weighted image. We removed linear, quadratic, and mean trends, along with bandpass temporal filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz). We filtered high-frequency motion (>0.1 Hz) in the phase-encoding direction (Gratton et al., 2020a), prior to nuisance regression (24 head motion parameters, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and the global signal). Volumes were then censored above a FD threshold of 0.15 mm (Power et al., 2012) as visual inspection suggested that there was an appropriate motion floor in these data. Lastly, runs were warped to native T1 space and concatenated together by viewing condition.

Cortical surface projection

Cortical surfaces were generated from T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer's recon-all pipeline (v6.0; Dale et al., 1999) and converted to Connectivity Informatics Technology Initiative (CIFTI) format (Glasser et al., 2013) via Ciftify's ciftify_recon_all pipeline (Dickie et al., 2019).

CIFTI fMRI data

Surface-based fMRI time series were generated via Ciftify's ciftify_subject_fmri pipeline, using the ribbon-constrained sampling approach as in Glasser et al. (2013) and smoothed with σ = 4 mm geodesic kernel.

Network identification in individuals via TM

A TM approach (Gordon et al., 2017a; Dworetsky et al., 2021) was used to generate individualized functional networks for each participant shown in Figure S1. We opted for this approach over data-driven techniques (i.e., clustering algorithms such as Infomap or ICA) given the focus here was on comparing networks across age groups, and TM ensures this comparison is more reliable and direct (Dworetsky et al., 2021) while still reducing susceptibility to motion artifacts (Sylvester et al., 2023).

This approach requires a set of predefined network templates for individual-level matching. To avoid age-related bias in template creation, we created a combined child and adult network template using independent data. Specifically, group-level templates for adults (n = 384; mean age, 28.4 years; data, 60 min of resting-state fMRI; Dworetsky-HCP) and children (n = 38; mean age, 9.16 years; data, 26 min of resting-state fMRI over four runs; HCP-D) were obtained from the public MIDB Precision Brain Atlas repository (Somerville et al., 2018; Dworetsky et al., 2021). Specifically, these datasets were included as a reference for comparison to show that the TM approach yields identifiable large-scale networks in our independent naturalistic and densely sampled dataset. Adult and child network templates were derived from unthresholded probabilistic maps (v1.0) based on the WashU 14-network atlas (Power et al., 2011). We binarized each child and adult network template by thresholding each network's probabilistic map at 0.2. The final group-level maps for each network were then computed with a logical AND between child and adult templates. The resulting 14 network group-level consensus maps were labeled following the nomenclature from Power et al. (2011) as follows: default mode (DMN), frontoparietal (FP), visual (VIS), dorsal attention (DAN), ventral attention (VAN), salience (SAL), auditory (AUD), cingulo-opercular (CON), somatomotor dorsal (SMd), somatomotor lateral (SMl), temporal pole (Tpole), medial temporal lobe (MTL), parietal memory (PMN), and parietal occipital (PON). The 14 network group-level templates were visualized using Connectome Workbench's wb_view function (Glasser et al., 2013) and are shown in Figure 2.

To match each PreciseKIDS child and adult participant to this network template, vertex-wise dense FC matrices for the cortical surface were computed for each participant from 60 min of postcensored data (20 min of each viewing condition) using the Connectome Workbench function –cifti-correlation. Then, for each vertex, the FC profile was thresholded at the 95th percentile, binarized, and compared against each network template by using the Dice similarity (Sørensen–Dice coefficient), defined as follows (Dice, 1945): Dice(A,B)=2|A∩B||A|+|B|, where A represents a vertex's FC profile and B represents the group template.

Each vertex was assigned to the network with the highest Dice similarity, and continuous dice values were retained for analysis as described below.

Final individual-level networks were determined with a winner-take-all approach, where each vertex was assigned to the network with the highest Dice similarity; however continuous dice values were retained for further analysis as described below. Individual PreciseKIDS child and adult network maps were also combined to produce group-level network maps, by determining the most common network label within the child and adult groups (Fig. 2). To facilitate comparison with other studies, we provide alternate network labels based on template-matched Dice similarity computed using the Network Correspondence Toolbox (Kong et al., 2025) across four reference atlases (Thomas Yeo et al., 2011; Shirer et al., 2012; Glasser et al., 2016; Gordon et al., 2016) as shown in Table S1.

Model covariates

All analyses below modeled age group as a categorical effect, given that age varies differently and nonlinearly across child and adult groups, making a single continuous age predictor across groups inappropriate. Sex and head motion (coded as number of censored volumes at FD > 0.15 mm) were included as covariates in all participant-level group comparisons, specifically network assignment, entropy, network surface area, and within- and between-network functional connectivity. In network topography similarity analyses using pairwise Dice within groups, motion was instead addressed by creating three groups (LMA, LMC, HMC) rather than included as a covariate.

Age group differences in network topography

Next, for each network we determined the overlap between individual-level adult and child network maps and thresholded by the number of participants at 0.2 for direct comparison to the HCP templates. We computed heat maps to indicate the number of participants in each age group who were assigned a network label at a given vertex. Differences between age groups were then visualized on the surface. Analyses in this study focused on 11 cortical networks: DMN, FP, DAN, VAN, SAL, CON, AUD, SMd, SMl, VIS, and PON. The following three networks were excluded from analyses: Tpole, MTL, and PMN, where appropriate. Tpole and MTL were excluded due to known signal dropout in anterior and medial temporal regions (Rua et al., 2018, p. 201; Rai et al., 2024, 2025), and PMN was excluded as it is more consistently detected with high-resolution imaging (Kwon et al., 2025). Group differences in network assignment at each vertex were assessed using mixed-effects logistic regression across networks, with age group as the main effect, controlling for fixed effects of sex, head motion as number of censored volumes, and random family ID intercepts. The resulting age group-effect beta coefficients were visualized with uncorrected p values <0.05 on the surface.

Network assignment confidence differences

To assess whether there were age group differences in the network assignment confidence, we computed the Shannon entropy (Shannon, 1948) of the normalized Dice similarity distribution. Specifically, for each vertex, the binarized connectivity profile (top 5% of connectivity values; Dworetsky et al., 2021) was compared against each of the 14 group network templates using Dice similarity, resulting in a distribution of 14 Dice values per vertex that were then normalized to sum to 1. Shannon entropy was defined for each vertex as follows: H=−∑i=1Npilog2(pi+ε), where pi is the normalized Dice similarity for network i at a given vertex and N is the total number of networks.

Entropy reflects within-individual uncertainty about network assignment and is computed independently for each subject and therefore does not directly assess between-subject variability. Higher entropy values indicate lower confidence, and lower values indicate greater confidence for a particular network assignment. Compared with the silhouette measure (Rousseeuw, 1987) which compares a vertex's similarity to other vertices across communities (Yeo et al., 2011; Tooley et al., 2022a), entropy provides a computationally efficient estimate of how confidently a vertex belongs to its assigned network based solely on its own Dice similarity distribution.

We visualized entropy (as an index of confidence for network assignment) across the cortical surface. First, we tested whether children differed significantly from adults in subject-wise overall entropy (averaged across all vertices) using mixed-effects models with fixed effects of sex, number of censored volumes, and random family ID intercepts. We next applied this approach to vertex-wise entropy, where at each vertex, a mixed-effects model was used to examine the main effect of age group while controlling for fixed effects of sex, number of censored volumes, and random family ID intercepts. Standardized vertex-wise beta coefficients and uncorrected p values <0.05 were visualized on the cortical surface. We visualized vertex-wise entropy across motion groups (LMA, LMC, HMC), with vertex-wise beta coefficients and uncorrected p values <0.05 on the surface.

Network surface area differences

We compared the cortical surface area occupied by each network in individualized child and adult network maps. The surface area of each vertex was computed separately for the left and right hemisphere using Connectome Workbench's –surface-vertex-areas function across individual midthickness surfaces. Then, for each network, we summed the surface area across network-assigned vertices and calculated proportions by dividing by total cortical surface area, thus taking into account absolute brain size. We visualized the distribution of these percentages across networks and, for each network, tested age group differences using a linear mixed-effects model with the main effect of age group (adults, children) while controlling for fixed effects of sex, number of censored volumes, and a random family intercept: SurfaceArea∼AgeGroup+Sex+HeadMotion+(1|Family)withFDRcorrectionacrossnetworks. Age group differences were examined across all vertices and, in a sensitivity analysis, only included thresholded vertices (Dice ≥ 0.3). This cutoff was chosen to exclude ambiguous network assignments while retaining broad cortical coverage and has been used in previous work (Boukhdhir et al., 2021). Across-network multiple-comparisons correction was applied using the false discovery rate (FDR).

Individualization of network topography

It has been suggested that development is associated with increasing individualization of the functional connectome (Kaufmann et al., 2017; Demeter et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Here, we asked whether network topography is more unique in LMC relative to LMA (i.e., whether pairs of children are less similar than pairs of adults). Individualized network maps were used to compute Dice similarity between all pairs of children (excluding siblings) and all pairs of adults, for each network. The ciftiTools package was used to assess similarity on the CIFTI-format data (Pham et al., 2022), and R's lmer function was used for linear modeling. To test for differences in within-group similarity, we used a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects for motion group (low-motion child–child vs low-motion adult–adult), network, their interaction, and sex dyads as male–male, female–female, and male–female pairs (ordered as parent–child sex). Random intercepts were added for each participant to account for the repeated measures across pairs as follows: DiceSimilarityij∼MotionGroupij×Network+SexDyad+(1|Subjecti)+(1|Subjectj)whereiandjareparticipantpairs. Post hoc comparisons were performed to determine differences in within-group similarity per network with FDR correction applied. Given prior evidence that adults achieved higher test–retest reliability than children when using high-probability regions of interest (Rai et al., 2025), we repeated analyses and used a linear mixed-effects model as described above with 30 min of adult data to approximate the reliability obtained by LMC with 60 min of data.

Familial similarity in topography

Given prior evidence on the heritability of the connectome (Miranda-Dominguez et al., 2018), we examined whether relatedness resulted in greater similarity in network topography. We compared related child–adult pairs (i.e., child–parent) against unrelated child–adult pairs. First, we computed vertex-wise Dice similarity between all possible child and adult individualized network map pairs (n = 576 pairs across 24 children × 24 adults) for each of the 11 cortical networks. Differences in Dice similarity were tested with linear mixed-effects models, including main effects of group relation (two-level variable of related vs unrelated) and network; fixed effects of sex dyads as male–male, female–female, and male–female pairs; and random intercepts for each participant to account for repeated pairings as follows: DiceSimilarityij∼GroupRelationij*Network+SexDyad+(1|Subjecti)+(1|Subjectj)whereiandj,areparticipantpairs. We applied the same linear mixed-effects model to compare Dice similarity for each network separately between related and unrelated pairs, defined as follows: DiceSimilarityij∼GroupRelationij*SexDyad+(1|Subjecti)+(1|Subjectj).

Age effects on dense within- and between-network FC: impact of individualized networks

To complement the above network topography analyses, we examined age group differences in within- and between-network FC, using both individualized networks and the PreciseKIDS group templates. Dense FC matrices were computed using the same 60 min of postcensored data used for template-matched network creation. In a separate validation analysis, an additional 15 min of postcensored data (5 min of each condition) were analyzed to assess results when data used to calculate functional connectivity are independent of the data used to define networks. We applied vertex-wise distance censoring to each vertex's FC value using the Conte-69 surface mesh (Van Essen et al., 2012). All FC edges within 30 mm were excluded as in Demeter et al. (2023) to reduce spatial autocorrelation (Jeganathan et al., 2024). FC values were then Fisher z-transformed and averaged across vertices using three network approaches: (1) group-level winner-take-all templates applied to individuals, (2) individualized network maps derived from TM, and (3) high-confidence individualized network maps that included only those vertices with a Dice similarity ≥0.3.

We compared age groups on each within- and between-network FC measure using linear mixed-effects models with the main effect of age group, controlling for fixed effects of sex, number of censored volumes, and random family ID intercepts. Multiple-comparisons correction was applied using the FDR, and effect sizes are shown as the standardized beta coefficients from the model. We noted differences in significant findings across network approaches.

Impact of data quantity

We have previously shown in this dataset that functional connectivity reliability continues to improve up to 60 min of data (Rai et al., 2025). Here, we extend this to consider how reliability of network topography is impacted by data quantity. To evaluate the benefit of scan time on individual topographic reliability, we computed split-half normalized mutual information (NMI) between-network topography maps (Hermosillo et al., 2024) derived from two independent halves of 9, 15, and 30 min of data, with the number of volumes matched equally across viewing conditions. To test for effects of data quantity and motion group (LMA, LMC, HMC) on split-half NMI, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model as follows: NMI∼DataLength*MotionGroup+(1|FamilyID)+(1|SubjectID). We further examined how scan duration influences network assignment confidence and whether this interacts with age/motion group (LMA, LMC, HMC). We computed mean vertex-wise entropy across durations, 9, 15, 30, and 60 min, and entered these into a linear mixed-effects model as follows: MeanEntropy∼Datalength*MotionGroup+(1|FamilyID)+(1|SubjectID). Next, we assessed the impact of topographic reliability on confidence findings. We calculated mean entropy using 30 min of data and assessed whether including topographic NMI also calculated at 30 min impacted age group effects: 30-minMeanEntropy∼AgeGroup+Sex+HeadMotion+(1|FamilyID), 30-minMeanEntropy∼AgeGroup+Sex+HeadMotion+30minNMI+(1|FamilyID). Lastly, we assessed fragmentation of individualized networks between 9 and 60 min. Two networks can occupy the same total surface area while differing in how contiguous that territory is, so we quantified the number of separate pieces or fragments for each network. For each participant and network, vertices assigned to that network in the winner-take-all map were grouped into spatially contiguous clusters on the surface, only retaining clusters ≥15 mm2 (areas were computed using the Connectome Workbench function –surface-vertex-areas). A network made up of many small clusters is therefore more fragmented than one composed of a few large clusters. Fragmentation was visualized for the visual network in one exemplar child participant. To test whether fragmentation changed with data length, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model for each of the 11 cortical networks as follows: NumberofFragments∼DataLength+Sex+HeadMotion+(1|FamilyID)+(1|SubjectID)withFDRcorrectionappliedacrossnetworks.

Results

Age group differences in network topography

Templates derived from adult HCP and child HCP-D data provide a point of reference for potential expected age differences in topography. Comparing the thresholded (i.e., network was present in 20% of the sample) adult HCP and child HCP-D network maps (Fig. 1A), we observed that all large-scale cortical networks had regions of convergence (overlap) and regions of divergence (no overlap) between age groups. Specifically, DMN, SMd, SMl, and VIS networks showed greater cortical coverage in adults compared with children, while CON, DAN, VAN, and PON networks showed greater coverage in children.

Adults exhibit expanded coverage in DMN, SMd, SMl, and VIS networks, while children show broader coverage in CON, DAN, VAN, and PON. B, Overlap maps derived from the PreciseKIDS dataset thresholded at ≥0.2 are broadly similar network architecture to HCP/HCP-D-derived templates and show high convergence between children and adults.

Next, we considered networks identified in our data and applied the same threshold (0.2) to the individualized networks (Fig. 1B). In our data, relative to the HCP/HCP-D data, we found greater convergence (overlap) of network maps between children and adults, with subtle differences in network boundaries. The remaining networks (Tpole, MTL, and PMN) are shown in Figure S2. When derived from the winner-take-all maps, the PreciseKIDS networks encompassed more of the cortical surface and showed a reduced PMN and expanded FP, DAN, and VAN, relative to the HCP winner-take-all maps in Figure 2. Of note, relative to the HCP/HCP-D atlases, which are derived from resting-state data, PreciseKIDS networks show expanded overlap in DAN, VAN, and AUD regions, which may reflect the naturalistic stimuli. We also note that network labeling can vary across commonly used atlases (Thomas Yeo et al., 2011; Shirer et al., 2012; Glasser et al., 2016; Gordon et al., 2016) and provide alternative network labels to facilitate comparison with prior studies in Table S1. Taken together these observations suggest that inferring topography age effects using data collected with different parameters should be done with caution and that for the age range considered here, age effects on topography are subtle.

Vertex-wise network assignment density for children and adults is shown in Figure 3. For most networks there are “core” regions where all 24 individuals in each age group had overlapping assignment (Fig. 3, red), whereas network boundaries displayed more heterogeneous assignment patterns across individuals, with some vertices assigned to a network in only a small number of individuals (Fig. 3, blue regions). Regions that showed idiosyncratic assignment patterns (i.e. vertices where a small proportion of the sample was assigned to a given network) occurred at similar rates across both children and adults reflecting individual differences in network organization rather than age-related effects (Fig. 3). For example, a similar proportion of the child and adult samples showed a temporoparietal region that associated with the PON and regions of the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus that associated with the FP network. Replicating these surface maps in LMC and family-matched LMA, we found individual differences in similar regions as the full sample (Fig. S3). Calculating the surface area of each network density map—a measure sensitive to the spread of a network across individuals—showed that child network assignments covered a larger surface area for nearly all networks compared with adults, with the most pronounced differences for the DAN, VIS, FP, VAN, SMd, SML, and CON networks (Table S2). The SAL network was the single network that exhibited a larger spread in adults compared with children. Comparing between all LMC and a family-matched subsample of LMA, LMC continued to show larger network spread than LMA across most networks, except between SAL, CON, and AUD regions (Table S2). This suggests that children's network territories are generally more variable across individuals and/or more spatially diffuse, even when specifically considering lower motion children.

Network assignment confidence differences

To evaluate age effects on network assignment confidence, we computed individual vertex-wise Dice coefficient entropy, which were then averaged within groups for each vertex (Fig. 5). Network assignment confidence was highest in regions of the visual and motor cortices (Fig. 5A) and lowest in high dropout regions (temporal pole and orbitofrontal cortex). In association regions, there was relatively higher confidence in core regions of the DMN and relatively lower confidence in boundaries of association networks, such as the right temporoparietal junction (Fig. 5A). Averaged entropy values, using 60 min, across all vertices for each individual showed that children (mean, 2.200; SD = 0.131) had significantly higher overall entropy relative to adults (mean, 2.012; SD = 0.093; β = 0.114, z = 2.94, p = 0.003) and that entropy significantly associated with motion (β = 0.0001, z = 2.78, p = 0.005), but not sex (p = 0.472).

Lower entropy values (dark purple regions) indicate higher confidence in network assignment, whereas higher entropy values (yellow regions) reflect less certainty. B, The left panel shows the standardized regression coefficients from the mixed-effects model contrasting between children and adults. Warm colors reflect greater entropy in children relative to adults (less confidence in children), and cooler colors indicate greater entropy in adults (less confidence in adults). The right panel shows vertices with uncorrected p < 0.05 group differences (pFDR > 0.05) in red for children and blue for adults. DMN, SMd, AUD, and VIS networks are outlined in white.

Vertex-wise entropy comparisons showed many regions where entropy was higher in children (lower confidence) relative to adults (positive β; Fig. 5B). When vertex-wise entropy maps were thresholded at p < 0.05 uncorrected (Fig. 5B), children showed lower confidence in larger clusters within the boundaries of DMN (largest cluster size, 329.06 mm2), SMd (largest cluster size, 576.54 mm2), and AUD (largest cluster size, 468.47 mm2). Adults showed lower confidence only in one region, along the border of the VIS network (largest cluster size, 695.09 mm2). To further assess robustness of findings to head motion, comparing vertex-wise entropy for LMC (n = 10) and a family-matched subsample of LMA (n = 10) showed consistent patterns (Fig. S5) with LMC displaying overall higher entropy than LMA (lower assignment confidence; positive β; Fig. S5B, left). Both LMA and LMC groups showed reduced confidence at network boundaries (e.g., particularly SMd and DMN), and VIS network borders again had lower confidence in adults, though these clusters were visually smaller compared with clusters shown in Figure 5.

Network surface area differences

For each network, we compared surface area between children and adults and found that PON and SMl were relatively smaller in adults (p-uncorrected < 0.05); however, this did not survive FDR correction (pFDR > 0.05; Fig. 6; Table S3). Head motion was associated with surface area for the DAN and SAL networks (p-uncorrected < 0.05; pFDR > 0.05), with only the SAL network significantly impacted by motion after correction (pFDR = 0.005). No significant effects of sex were observed across any networks after correction (pFDR > 0.05). Findings were comparable when analyses were restricted to high-confidence regions (Dice ≥ 0.3; Table S3).

Individualization of network topography

We next asked whether there is increased individualization of network topography in LMA (n = 22) relative to LMC (n = 10). To this end, we asked whether network topography is more individualized (i.e., less similar, on average) between pairs of adults relative to pairs of children (Fig. 7). Within-group similarity in network topography showed no significant overall effect of age group (p = 0.157), a significant effect of network (all p < 0.002), and no effect of sex dyad (p = 0.656). There were significant interactions between networks and age group, using DMN as the dummy-coded reference network. We followed up with post hoc comparisons which showed that LMA pairs had greater within-group similarity (i.e., less within-group individualization) compared with LMC pairs for DAN (β = 0.047, z = 3.30, pFDR = 0.001), CON (β = 0.069, z = 4.86, pFDR < 0.001), and SMd (β = 0.081, z = 5.67, pFDR < 0.001). LMC pairs had significantly less individualization (greater similarity) than LMA pairs only for the SAL network (β = −0.107, z = −7.46, pFDR < 0.001), and differences were not significant for remaining networks (Table S4). To ask whether these results are driven by greater reliability in adults (Rai et al., 2025), we repeated this analysis using 30 min of postcensored data in adults and 60 min in children. We found comparable results (Fig. S6), suggesting that greater reliability in adults is unlikely to be driving this effect.

There was a significant effect of network (p < 0.001) and interaction between network and group (p < 0.05). Asterisks denote post hoc comparisons with FDR correction: ***p < 0.001.

Familial similarity in topography

As this dataset included parent–child pairs, we asked to what extent network topography is similar within families. In an omnibus test across all networks, we found that the overall effect of relatedness was not significant (p = 0.33; Fig. 8) across all networks. There was a significant effect of network (all p < 0.001) and no significant effect of sex dyad (p = 0.872) or interaction between network and relatedness (all p > 0.05). On a network-wise level, however, we found significantly greater network topography similarity for related pairs over unrelated pairs across multiple networks: DMN (β = −0.013, z = −2.88, pFDR = 0.015), FP (β = −0.021, z = −3.30, pFDR = 0.006), DAN (β = −0.022, z = −3.62, pFDR = 0.004), and VAN (β = −0.017, z = −2.42, pFDR = 0.043).

Network topography similarity in related versus unrelated pairs. Pairwise Dice similarity in network assignment is shown for related pairs (green) and unrelated pairs (magenta) across networks. Overall relatedness effects were nonsignificant, though network-wise comparisons showed greater similarity for related pairs across DMN, FP, DAN, and VAN networks. Significance asterisks denote network-wise comparisons with FDR correction: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

Age effects on dense within- and between-network FC: impact of individualized networks

Next, we considered age effects on within- and between-network FC and the impact of individually defined networks on group differences. We compared FC measures derived with the PreciseKIDS group network maps, individualized network maps, and individualized network maps thresholded to include only high-confidence vertices (Dice ≥ 0.3). Across all three approaches, adults showed stronger within-network FC across most networks compared with children (Fig. 9A), and effects were most pronounced within and between SMd and SMl networks (β range = 0.051–0.143). Other strong adult > child within-network effects included DMN–DMN (β range = 0.029–0.067), DAN–DAN (β = 0.056–0.057), and VAN–VAN (β = 0.041–0.070). In a separate analysis using 15 min of independent data for calculating FC (i.e., distinct from the data used for template-matched networks), we observed similar results, with adults showing greater within-network FC relative to children (Fig. S7).

Differences in FC across networks and mapping approaches between children and adults. Standardized beta coefficients from linear mixed-effects models are shown for analyses using group-level templates (left), individualized networks (middle), and high-confidence individualized (Dice ≥ 0.3; right) approaches. A, Beta coefficients from the group effect indicate stronger FC in adults relative to children in red and stronger FC in children relative to adults in blue. Across all approaches, adults exhibited stronger within-network FC than children. B, Shades of red indicate motion effects where higher motion decreased FC estimates (negative betas), whereas shades of blue indicate motion increased FC estimates (positive betas). Significance asterisks denote comparisons with FDR correction (p < 0.05). Red stars reflect significance for a specific approach only. Beyond commonalities, individual and high-confidence mapping showed effects that are suggestive of both false positives and false negatives in the group template approach. Specifically, certain age group effects on between-network FC (e.g., FP–DAN, FP–PON, SAL–PON) were significant only when using the group network map, but these patterns were not significant when using the individual or high-confidence network maps. Additionally, specific age group effects on within-network FC detected with individualized network maps (e.g., SAL–SAL for both individualized maps and AUD–AUD for the high-confidence maps) were not significant with the group network map. The individual mapping approach showed no uniquely significant effects, while the high-confidence approach showed unique differences between DAN–VAN and AUD–AUD networks. There were significant effects of head motion in a majority of edges using the group template approach, with the largest effects for SAL–SAL, SMd–SMd, CON–CON, and PON–PON (Fig. 9B). Effects were reduced using the individualized approach and only found within-networks for DAN–DAN, VAN–VAN, and CON–CON edges. The high-confidence approach further diminished motion effects, where significant motion effects were only detected for DMN–DMN (inflated FC due to motion) and CON–AUD (reduced FC due to motion) edges. These differences demonstrate the utility of individual mapping for ascertaining robust group differences in FC.

Impact of data quantity

To assess the benefits of longer scan time for analyses presented here, we first considered benefits to topographic reliability. We examined split-half NMI across data lengths (Fig. 10A). Split-half NMI significantly increased with data length, as shown by a main effect of data length (p < 0.001), and there was a significant main effect of motion group (p < 0.001). Both low-motion groups (LMA and LMC) showed significantly higher NMI than HMC (LMA vs HMC: p = 0.0005; LMC vs HMC: p = 0.0007), whereas LMA and LMC did not differ significantly (p = 0.699). From 9 to 30 min, all motion groups showed gains in topographic reliability (p < 0.001). HMC showed the largest increase in NMI (+0.051 NMI), followed by LMC (+0.050 NMI) and then LMA (+0.041 NMI), the data length by motion group interaction was trend-level (p = 0.056).

Next, we assessed network assignment confidence differences across motion groups with varying scan time, to provide context on whether entropy differences reflect lower reliability rather than developmental differences. As shown in Figure 10B, we found a significant difference in mean entropy across motion groups (p < 0.001) and across data lengths (p < 0.001), as well as a significant interaction effect of motion group by data length (p < 0.001). Across all data lengths, low-motion adults showed consistently lower entropy (i.e., higher confidence) than both LMC (difference = 0.092, p = 0.007) and HMC (difference = 0.180, p < 0.001). LMC also had lower entropy than HMC (difference = 0.088, p = 0.018). Mean entropy decreased with increasing data lengths (all pairwise contrasts p < 0.001 for LMA and LMC; all contrasts p < 0.001 for HMC, except 30 vs 60 min, p = 0.216), suggesting that longer data acquisitions increase network assignment confidence for all motion groups. We note that while all participants showed higher reliability and lower confidence with increasing data, the relative position of individuals was stable, as were gaps between motion groups. Using 30 min instead of 60 min of data, we similarly found averaged entropy at 30 min was significantly higher in children (mean, 2.221; SD = 0.119) than adults (mean, 2.034; SD = 0.073), with a significant effect of age group (β = 0.121, z = 3.59, p < 0.001) and motion (β = 0.0001, z = 2.82, p = 0.005), but not sex (p = 0.775). Additionally controlling for NMI (i.e., topographic reliability), the age group difference in confidence remained robust (β = 0.168, z = 7.01, p < 0.001), suggesting that the group difference in confidence this is not simply an effect of reliability.

To contextualize the value of additional data within individual participants, in Figure 10C we show that additional data from 9 to 60 min significantly reduced the number of spatially separate fragments in all networks (all pFDR < 0.05). The largest reductions were in the visual (29.8–21.2 fragments; t = 11.76, pFDR < 0.001), dorsal somatomotor (29.2–22.2; t = 7.83, pFDR < 0.001), and default mode networks (37.3–31.4; t = 8.46, pFDR < 0.001), and the smallest was in the salience network (24.0–22.4; t = 2.61, pFDR = 0.012). The exemplar child participant shown in Figure 10D showed one of the largest reductions in fragmentation between 9 and 60 min. Although fragmentation decreasing in all networks supports the use of longer acquisitions for reliable individual network mapping, the relative position of participants was again preserved (mean Spearman ρ = 0.67, range 0.41–0.86), such that participants with more fragmented maps at 9 min tended to remain more fragmented at 60 min.

Discussion

Using deeply sampled fMRI from parent–child pairs during passive viewing and individual network mapping, we found that while large-scale network architecture is broadly shared across age groups, children exhibit lower overall confidence in network assignments and weaker within-network FC relative to adults. We further found age effects on FC differed when using individualized network mapping relative to a group network map, highlighting the potential for individual-specific network definitions to improve specificity in developmental studies.

Individualized network topography was broadly similar between preadolescent children and adults with only small age group differences in assignment frequency, despite interindividual variation in both. While these findings are consistent with other work showing general similarity in network topography from relatively early in childhood through adolescence (Tooley et al., 2022b; Tu et al., 2025), we also found fewer age group differences than suggested by other work (Tooley et al., 2022a; Sun et al., 2025). Specifically, Tooley et al. (2022a) found expanded limbic and visual territory and contracted default and frontoparietal network territory in children compared with adults. A mega-analysis pooling data across many samples also showed network maps suggestive of differences in network assignment of ventral attention and somatomotor regions across age groups (Sun et al., 2025). We note that both these studies used different datasets in child and adult samples, which underscores the need for wide age range studies with harmonized protocols.

We did not observe significant differences between children and adults in surface area for any networks, and age effects at network boundaries were small. With those caveats, we note that age effects were most prominent along dorsal somatomotor and parieto-occipital network boundaries, and within core regions of the cingulo-opercular and salience networks. Differences observed in sensory and motor regions suggest that though these networks mature earlier, they may nonetheless undergo continued refinement (Fair et al., 2009; Supekar et al., 2009; Satterthwaite et al., 2013; Koolwijk et al., 2024). We also note that alternative parcellations, such as the three-network sensorimotor model (Gordon et al., 2017a; Demeter et al., 2025) or an interdigitated action network (Gordon et al., 2023), could impact findings. Effects within regions of the cingulo-opercular and salience networks align with prior work suggesting that these networks exhibit continued strengthening and refinement with age (Marek et al., 2015). Although cross-sectional age effects can be suggestive of changes, longitudinal data are needed to assess developmental stability, or change, in individual topography (Lynch et al., 2024).

Network assignment entropy analyses showed that children exhibit lower confidence (i.e., higher entropy) than adults, particularly at the boundaries of the default mode, dorsal somatomotor, auditory, and visual networks. This extends prior work by Tooley et al. (2022a), who found lower overall confidence in group-level children's functional organization compared with a separate sample of adults. Though somatomotor regions generally develop ahead of association regions, with regard to structural changes (Gogtay et al., 2004; Lebel and Beaulieu, 2011), our results of lower confidence in somatomotor regions in children compared with adults could reflect ongoing functional integration of sensorimotor cortices through childhood (Luo et al., 2024). These somatomotor differences persisted in motion stratified analyses. The exception was reduced confidence at visual network borders in adults compared with children, an unexpected finding that may reflect a unique developmental pattern in primary visual cortices during childhood (Luo et al., 2024), potentially confounded by age-related differences in response to passive viewing videos. Importantly, regions with lower confidence in adults have previously been interpreted as integration zones or “hub” regions that support cross-network communication (Dworetsky et al., 2021). Hence, lower network assignment confidence in children could reflect increased functional flexibility rather than immature network organization or reduced functional specialization.

Comparing group- versus individual-network definitions in terms of impact on functional connectivity analyses demonstrated that the specific patterns of significant differences varied substantially across approaches. Age effects echo previous work showing increased network segregation and functional specialization with age (Fair et al., 2009; Supekar et al., 2009; Tooley et al., 2022b). The group template approach also showed several between-network connectivity differences that were absent when using individualized approaches, and these models had the highest effects of head motion. In contrast, the high-confidence approach isolated only three motion-sensitive functional connections (e.g., DMN–DMN, SMl–AUD, CON–AUD), underscoring the relative robustness of individualized approaches to motion. Collectively, these results indicate that individualized methods offer improved specificity and sensitivity to variation in FC (Dworetsky et al., 2021; Hermosillo et al., 2024).

Heritability of FC and network topography have been demonstrated by prior twin studies (Glahn et al., 2010; Sinclair et al., 2015; Colclough et al., 2017; Miranda-Dominguez et al., 2018; Demeter et al., 2020; Anderson et al., 2021; Busch et al., 2024). Extending on these, we found greater similarity in DMN, FP, DAN, and VAN topography in parent–child pairs than in unrelated adult–child pairs. We note that our design may capture both shared genetics and environmental effects (Boomsma et al., 2002), which are more separable using heritability designs. Parent–child pairs also span different developmental stages, introducing age-related variance that may mask overall genetic similarity. Our findings suggest familial relatedness is a potential source of individual variation in network topography (Anderson et al., 2021) and underscore the need for larger, genetically informed precision samples to robustly characterize the heritability of the functional network architecture (Colclough et al., 2017; Ge et al., 2017).

Network assignment density maps showed greater spread for nearly all networks in children (except for the salience network). Network size was not larger in children, when assessed by surface area. Together this suggests children's topography is more individually unique, a finding supported by pairwise similarity analyses showing a predominant pattern of higher similarity in adults. These findings are somewhat contradictory with prior literature suggesting that functional connectivity becomes more individualized with increasing age (Kaufmann et al., 2017; Demeter et al., 2025), although no prior studies to our knowledge have looked at individualization of topography, focusing instead on dense (Demeter et al., 2025) or parcellated (Kaufmann et al., 2017) connectome individualization. The measure used here, dice similarity of network topography, captures one aspect of individualization—dissimilarity from others—and not the other contributor to fingerprinting techniques which is self-similarity. Prior work has suggested that increasing individualization with age is primarily driven by increased self-stability (Kaufmann et al., 2017; Graff et al., 2022), with a smaller impact of self-other distance.

Additional methodological considerations may contribute to different findings across studies. First, we used naturalistic viewing instead of resting-state scans, which may have reduced individualization effects due to shared stimulus-driven activation patterns (Finn et al., 2017; Vanderwal et al., 2017, 2021). Additionally, although video content was similar, we used different clips across runs, which could have attenuated stable individualization effects. Here, we used 60 min of fMRI per person, while other work has used upward of 90 min of low-motion data (Lynch et al., 2024; Demeter et al., 2025). Although precision sampling improves the reliability of network topography, effects of motion and associated data censoring (Mejia et al., 2026) persist for higher-motion children and could impact group differences.

Strengths of this study included 60 min of multiecho fMRI data to define individual functional networks; however, we acknowledge several limitations. Precision and large cohort datasets offer complementary strengths (Ali and Lynn, 2026). With 24 adult–child pairs, our sample size is relatively large for precision functional mapping but small for detecting subtle age group differences, and thus replication in larger, independent datasets is needed to confirm our findings. The narrow age range of children (6–8 years) can be a strength as it focuses on a specific developmental period but precludes inferences about other periods or longitudinal change. Given that over 90% of this sample reported European ancestry, our findings may not generalize to broader populations. Longitudinal designs and more representative samples are needed for a complete picture of developmental network organization. We also note that network nomenclature remains inconsistent across the field (Uddin et al., 2019, 2023), and although we provide alternative taxonomy for our dataset, this may further impact interpretability of individualized network findings. Though attention and engagement were sustained similarly across age groups in our sample (Rai et al., 2025), differences noted in this study may be driven by age-related variability in stimulus processing (Cantlon and Li, 2013; Campbell et al., 2015; Hutchison and Morton, 2015). Similarly, although sex effects on functional network topography warrant attention (Shanmugan et al., 2022; Keller et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026), the sample size of this study limits our ability to resolve sex and age interactions. Lastly, though new packages such as VertexWiseR (Billaud and Yu, 2024) and FEMA (Parekh et al., 2024) allow for vertex-wise linear mixed-effects modeling, they were not sufficiently flexible for analyses here, and therefore vertex-wise multiple-comparisons corrections were not performed.

Overall, this work adds to our understanding of age and familial effects on functional topography and connectivity, and our findings highlight the potential for individualized mapping approaches to improve topographic reliability and sensitivity of functional connectivity analyses.
