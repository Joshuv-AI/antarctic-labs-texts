Bioactive Properties of Achillea biserrata M.Bieb. Extracts: Phytochemical Analysis, Antioxidant Activity, Target Enzyme Inhibition, and Cytotoxic Effects
Authors: Zühal Bayrakçeken Güven, Mustafa Abdullah Yilmaz, Huseyin Inceer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, the enzyme inhibition and antioxidant capacity of Achillea biserrata were determined, and their relationship with cytotoxic effects was investigated. Antiproliferative activity was evaluated by the MTT assay in L929, A549, B16F10, Hep‐3B, PC‐3, MCF‐7, and CaCo‐2 cell lines, and enzyme inhibition was assessed against tyrosinase, urease, PTP‐1B, and DPP‐4. Ethanol extracts showed the greatest bioactivity, whereas water extracts were the least active. The capitulum ethanol extract (CEE) exhibited the strongest inhibition of tyrosinase, urease, DPP‐4, and PTP‐1B (IC50: 72.83, 40.84, 70.12, and 302.42 µg/mL, respectively) and the highest cytotoxicity (IC50: 168.42–325.42 µg/mL). LC‐MS/MS analysis identified quinic acid as the major constituent in the stem (44.614 mg/g extract) and capitulum (2.063 mg/g extract), and chlorogenic acid in the leaves (45.502 mg/g extract). Principal component analysis (PCA) integrating LC‐MS/MS and bioactivity data supported the bioactive potential of A. biserrata, highlighting its promise as a pharmaceutical resource.

Introduction

Cancer is the second most common cause of disease‐related deaths worldwide. It is expected that the number of patients diagnosed with cancer will continue to rise in the coming years. Data on rapidly increasing cancer cases predict that the number of diagnoses for all types of cancer will double by 2070 compared to 2020 [1]. Enzyme inhibitors, used to regulate biochemical pathways by targeting enzyme activity, offer promising treatment options for many diseases [2]. Enzyme inhibitors are also an attractive group in anticancer drug discovery. By blocking enzymes that signal cancer cell growth, cancer growth and spread can be prevented [3, 4]. Melanin, which is responsible for the pigmentation of living organisms, protects the skin from harmful chemicals and UV‐induced DNA damage and thus prevents the formation of melanomas. Tyrosinase is one of the key enzymes involved in melanogenesis, and its role in melanoma pathogenesis is remarkable. Studies therefore suggest that regulation of tyrosinase can achieve effective results in the fight against melanoma cancer [5, 6].

The enzyme urease catalyses the hydrolysis of urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide. The resulting ammonia neutralises stomach acid and facilitates colonisation with Helicobacter pylori [7]. H. pylori infection is one of the most common chronic bacterial infections worldwide, especially in developing countries [8]. This bacterium is a risk factor for many diseases, including lymphoma, gastritis, stomach ulcers and stomach cancer [9]. Recent reports have shown that H. pylori infection can increase the risk of colorectal cancer [10, 11]. Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B), which plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus, dephosphorylates insulin and leptin receptors, decreasing their sensitivity. Insulin receptor desensitisation is considered a key factor in the progression of type 2 diabetes mellitus. Furthermore, overexpression of PTP1B reduces the level of GLUT4 on the cell surface, which is responsible for transporting circulating glucose into cells, leading to elevated blood glucose levels. Desensitisation of leptin receptors limits the blood glucose‐lowering function of leptin, which is involved in blood sugar regulation [12]. However, it has been reported that the PTP1B enzyme is linked not only to diabetes but also to cancer, and that overexpression of PTP1B increases breast cancer metastasis [13, 14]. Dipeptidyl peptidase IV (DPP4), an enzyme targeted in diabetes treatment, inactivates glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) and glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). Therefore, DPP4 inhibitors stimulate insulin secretion and suppress glucagon levels, resulting in lower blood sugar [15]. DPP4 inhibitors have been reported to have positive effects on the prognosis of patients with colorectal cancer [16, 17]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins and are kept under control by antioxidant mechanisms. However, if ROS production is not balanced by antioxidant defence mechanisms, oxidative stress occurs, which poses a risk for many diseases. Numerous studies indicate that oxidative stress plays an important role in cancer pathophysiology and cytotoxicity [18, 19]. Studies investigating the link between enzyme inhibition, antioxidant defense mechanisms, and cancer are ongoing, and efforts to identify new anticancer agents are accelerating. Traditionally used medicinal plants are natural resources that attract researchers' attention because of their rich phytochemical content and scientifically proven biological effects.

Achillea L. include important species used worldwide in traditional folk medicine, dating back to ancient times. Its rich phytochemical content has highlighted diverse biological effects, with different species reported to have antidiabetic, antiulcer, antispasmodic, immunosuppressive, and anti‐inflammatory activities [20]. Studies in the literature indicate that the cytotoxic effects of the species and their secondary metabolites, along with their potential for use in skin diseases, are noteworthy, and a patent has been filed in this area [21, 22]. Recent studies have revealed that the phytochemical composition of Achillea species varies significantly depending on the plant part. Significant differences have been observed in the phenolic compound content and antioxidant activities of the leaf, capitulum, and stem. In particular, the flower and leaf have been reported to possess higher phenolic content and antioxidant capacity. These findings suggest that different parts of Achillea species may exhibit distinct biological potentials, not only in terms of phytochemical composition but also regarding antioxidant, cytotoxic, and enzyme inhibitor activities. However, the relationship between phytochemical profile and biological activities in many Achillea species remains unclear. Therefore, comprehensive phytochemical characterisation and evaluation of the biological activities of extracts from different plant parts are important for the development of new natural therapeutic agents [23, 24, 25].

Achillea biserrata M.Bieb. is a perennial herb with spreading rhizomatous. It is mainly distributed in Caucasia. Like other species of Achillea, A. biserrata has pharmaceutical potential with essential oils, phenolics, and antioxidant capacity of total phenolic compounds and flavonoids as well as and antimicrobial properties [26, 27, 28]. Besides, the areal parts of this species are used to asthma treatment in Turkish the traditional folk medicine [29].

In view of this information, the antioxidant capacity and the inhibition of the enzymes tyrosinase, urease, PTP‐1B and DPP‐IV were investigated in relation to the cytotoxicity of A. biserrata and its cytotoxic potential was determined in healthy and cancer cell lines. The cancer cell lines studied were lung, breast and colorectal cancer cell lines, the three most commonly diagnosed cancers worldwide according to the World Health Organisation report, as well as melanoma, liver and prostate cancer cell lines. Comparisons were made with healthy cell lines to determine cell selectivity. Exploratory chemometric analysis integrating LC–MS/MS and bioactivity data indicated distinct phytochemical–bioactivity signatures among capitulum, leaf, and stem ethanol extracts, with phenolic acids and flavonoid glycosides contributing most strongly to the multivariate bioactivity profile. Due to the limited number of extracts analysed, PLS regression was used as an exploratory tool for variable prioritisation rather than for predictive model development. This study is the first to investigate the inhibition of extracts obtained from different parts of A. biserrata against four different enzymes and to analyse the detailed phytochemical content of the extracts.

Results and Discussion

Antiproliferative Activity Evaluation of the Extracts

The antiproliferative activities of the extracts were evaluated after incubating samples prepared at various concentrations with cells for 48 h. The cytotoxic effects of A. biserrata capitulum, leaf, and stem extracts on the healthy L929 cell line were evaluated at concentrations ranging from 20 to 800 µg/mL, and the concentration range that did not harm the cells was identified. Since cell viability decreased by 8.6% at 800 µg/mL in the ethanol extract, the maximum concentration used for the cancer cell lines was set at 400 µg/mL. Antiproliferative effects were analysed against six different cancer cell lines, revealing that the lowest effect was observed with water extracts and the highest with ethanol extracts. The extracts showed greater cytotoxic effects, particularly on B16F10, CaCo‐2, and Hep3B cells, with capitulum extracts demonstrating stronger effects than those from other plant parts. Among all extracts, the capitulum ethanol extract exhibited the highest activity against B16F10, with an IC50 value of 168.42 µg/mL (Table 1).

Antioxidant Capacity of the Extracts

The antioxidant capacity of the plant parts of A. biserrata was determined using three different methods. In contrast to the cytotoxic effect, ethanol extracts from the capitulum, leaves and stem were found to have the lowest antioxidant activity, while ethanol‐water and water extracts showed the highest antioxidant capacity. As indicated in Table 2, the water extract of capitulum had the highest antioxidant capacity with 686.24 mg Trolox/g extract in the DPPH method, 818.20 mg Trolox/g extract in CUPRAC method. The ethanol‐water extract of capitulum had the highest antioxidant capacity 816.24 mg Trolox/g extract in TEAC method.

Assessment of Enzyme Inhibition

The inhibition of tyrosinase, urease, PTP‐1B, and DPP‐4 enzymes by extracts of varying polarities obtained from the capitulum, leaf, and stem of A. biserrata was investigated. The relationships between tyrosinase inhibition and cytotoxicity in B16F10 melanoma cells, urease inhibition and cytotoxicity in CaCo‐2 colon adenocarcinoma cells, PTP‐1B inhibition and cytotoxicity in MCF‐7 breast adenocarcinoma cells, and DPP‐IV inhibition and cytotoxicity in CaCo‐2 colon adenocarcinoma cells were examined. For all enzymes, the highest inhibition was observed in ethanol extracts and the lowest in water extracts. The results were consistent with the antiproliferative effect. Compared to the standard compounds, the ethanol extract of capitulum showed the highest inhibitory effect on tyrosinase, urease, DPP4 and PTP‐1B (Table 3).

Correlation Between Biological Activities

Pearson correlation analysis revealed correlations between DPPH, TEAC, and CUPRAC antioxidant capacities, antiproliferative effects, and enzyme inhibitions. A correlation coefficient between 0.6 and 0.8 indicates a high correlation, while a value greater than 0.8 indicates a very strong correlation [30]. According to Figure 1, there is a very strong correlation between PTP1B and DPP4 enzyme inhibitions and the cytotoxicity of MCF‐7, B16F10, and A549 cells. A correlation greater than 0.8 was found between urease enzyme inhibition and all tested cancer cells. The correlation between tyrosinase enzyme inhibition and the cytotoxicity of B16F10 and MCF‐7 cells is 0.73 and 0.74, respectively.

LC‐MS/MS Based Qualitative and Quantitative Identification of Phytochemicals

Using a validated LC‐MS/MS method, 53 phenolic compounds in ethanol extracts from the capitulum, leaves, and stems of the plant were comprehensively analysed. Of all the plant part extracts, the ethanol extracts exhibited the highest cytotoxic and enzyme inhibitory activity. Therefore, the phytochemical constituents of the ethanol extracts were analysed and compared with each other (Figure S1). The highest amount of compounds in the capitulum was quinic acid with 2.063 mg analyte/g extract, followed by chlorogenic acid with 2.004 mg analyte/g extract. Chlorogenic acid was the most abundant compound in the leaf with 45.502 mg analyte/g extract, followed by chlorogenic acid and protocatechuic acid with 40.295 and 3.216 mg analyte/g extract, respectively. The highest amounts of quinic acid and chlorogenic acid were also found in the stem extract. (Table 4).

Integrated Phytochemical–Bioactivity Analysis

Although pairwise correlations provide useful information regarding relationships among biological activities, they do not account for the contribution of individual phytochemicals. Therefore, an integrated multivariate approach combining LC–MS/MS phytochemical data with antioxidant, enzyme inhibition, and antiproliferative activity results was employed.

To obtain an integrated view of the relationship between chemical composition and bioactivity, the LC–MS/MS profile of the ethanol extracts was combined with antioxidant, cytotoxic, and enzyme inhibition data and evaluated using PCA, clustered correlation analysis, and PLS regression. The PCA biplot clearly separated the capitulum, leaf, and stem extracts, indicating that the three plant parts have distinct phytochemical–bioactivity signatures (Figure 2). The first two principal components explained 100.0% of the total variance (PC1 = 74.1%, PC2 = 25.9%), indicating that the two‐dimensional PCA representation adequately described the variation among the extracts. The capitulum extract was positioned on the positive side of PC1, whereas leaf and stem extracts were located on the negative side, suggesting that PC1 mainly captured the contrast between the more cytotoxic/enzyme‐inhibitory capitulum profile and the phenolic‐acid‐rich aerial tissue profiles. The location of the capitulum extract near reciprocal cytotoxicity and enzyme‐inhibitory potency vectors supports the experimental observation that this extract generally produced stronger antiproliferative and enzyme inhibitory effects than the corresponding leaf and stem ethanol extracts. The clear separation of the three ethanol extracts demonstrates that phytochemical differences among plant parts are sufficient to generate distinct biological response patterns. The PCA loading distribution further suggested that enzyme inhibition and antiproliferative activity contributed strongly to the discrimination of the capitulum extract. The corresponding loading plot indicated that several phytochemicals contributed substantially to sample discrimination. Detailed loading values are provided in Table S2.

Principal component analysis (PCA) biplot integrating LC–MS/MS phytochemical variables and bioactivity parameters of ethanol extracts from capitulum, leaf, and stem of A. biserrata.

In contrast, the leaf and stem extracts were associated with higher concentrations of several polar phenolics, especially quinic acid and chlorogenic acid derivatives. The LC–MS/MS data showed that quinic acid and chlorogenic acid were the dominant analytes in leaf and stem extracts, whereas the capitulum extract contained markedly lower total quantified phenolics. This pattern suggests that the antioxidant profile is influenced primarily by abundant hydrophilic phenolic acids, while cytotoxicity and enzyme inhibition may be affected by a more specific phytochemical balance rather than total detected phenolic content alone. The clustered correlation heatmap supported this interpretation by grouping abundant phenolic acids separately from several potency‐oriented endpoints (Figure 3). However, these correlations must be interpreted cautiously because the integrated correlation coefficients were calculated from three plant‐part extracts. The heatmap revealed two major clusters. The first cluster was dominated by abundant phenolic acids, including quinic acid and chlorogenic acid derivatives, which showed stronger associations with antioxidant parameters. The second cluster contained several flavonoid glycosides and potency‐related biological variables, suggesting a closer relationship with enzyme inhibition and cytotoxic effects. Such clustering indicates that different classes of phytochemicals may contribute to different biological mechanisms. The complete high‐resolution clustered heatmap is provided in the Supplementary Information (Table S1).

Hierarchical clustered heatmap showing correlations between major phytochemicals and measured biological activities in ethanol extracts of A. biserrata.

VIP analysis identified both phenolic acids and flavonoid glycosides as important contributors to the integrated biological profile. The simultaneous appearance of quinic acid, dicaffeoylquinic acid derivatives, cynaroside, cosmosiin, and isoquercitrin among the highest‐ranked variables suggests that the observed biological effects likely result from synergistic interactions rather than a single dominant constituent. Exploratory PLS regression suggested that several compounds may contribute to the observed bioactivity profile. Compounds with VIP values greater than 1.0 included quinic acid, cosmosiin, cynaroside, p‐coumaric acid, gallic acid, gentisic acid, protocatechuic acid, fumaric acid, protocatechuic aldehyde, isoquercitrin, and 1,5‐dicaffeoylquinic acid (Figure 4, Table 5). Among these, quinic acid had the highest VIP score (1.22), reflecting its strong contribution to the chemical discrimination of the extracts and to the multivariate relationship with biological responses. Flavonoid glycosides such as cynaroside, cosmosiin, isoquercitrin, and miquelianin also appeared among the influential variables, suggesting that both phenolic acids and flavonoids may participate in the observed antioxidant, antiproliferative, and enzyme inhibitory profiles. Nevertheless, because the PLS model was built from only three LC–MS/MS‐profiled samples, the VIP results should be considered hypothesis‐generating. These candidate compounds should be discussed as possible contributors to activity and, ideally, verified in future work using purified compounds, additional Achillea samples, or targeted mixture experiments. The results should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than predictive because of the limited number of samples.

Chemotherapy, a common treatment for malignant tumours in which various chemical substances are used to destroy cancer cells, often leads to negative side effects that reduce patients' quality of life and, in some cases, even lead to complications that can result in death. In view of these factors, there is a growing need for new, naturally derived sources that are safe to use, have a high cytotoxic effect against cancer cells and do not damage healthy cells. Multifunctional enzyme inhibitors offer promising, selective, and effective treatment options for cancer. Plants have the potential to be cytotoxic agents due to their rich components, strong antioxidants, and enzyme inhibitory effects [31, 32, 33]. Numerous research studies have investigated the biological effects of Achillea species with regard to their traditional use. These studies have focused not only on crude extracts but also on secondary metabolites purified from the extracts. In particular, sesquiterpene lactones and flavanols have been reported to have strong cytotoxic effects [34, 35].

In a study, the water extract of A. maritima was reported to have selective cytotoxicity against the cancer cell line HepG2, reducing cell viability to 38.4%. In the same study, the highest inhibition of the enzyme tyrosinase was found in the ethanol extract with a value of 55.59 mg KAE/g [36]. The dichloromethane extract of A. millefolium herba showed a cytotoxic effect against the cancer cell lines A549 and HCT 116 with an IC50 value of 700 µg/mL, whereas it showed no cytotoxic effect against normal MRC‐5 cells [37]. In another study comparing the cytotoxic effects of essential oil and apolar extracts of A. millefolium against Panc‐1 and MCF‐7‐Adr, the essential oils showed low activity, while n‐hexane and diethyl ether extracts showed significant activity with IC50 values between 35 and 57.1 µg/mL. It was concluded that the high concentrations of germacrene D, viridiflorol and caryophyllene oxide compounds were responsible for the effect [38]. The cytotoxic effect of methanol extract of A. fragrantissima leaves against HepG2, A549, HCT116 and MCF‐7 cancer cells was investigated, and it was reported to have the highest selectivity against A549 cells with an IC50 value of 1.21 µg/mL. The extracts were observed to exhibit cytotoxic effects by affecting the S‐phase and inducing apoptosis [39]. A. multifida showed cytotoxic effect against MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cancer cells by inducing apoptosis [40]. Triterpene, sesquiterpene, and sterol compounds isolated from A. alpina have been reported to inhibit the PTP1B enzyme, with IC50 values ranging from 14.87 to 23.09 µM [41]. In addition, previous studies have reported that acrylic acid derivative compounds isolated from A. mellifolium and A. lycaonica extracts, as well as essential oils obtained from 16 Achillea species, showed urease enzyme inhibition [42, 43, 44]. A study showed that A. maritima extract had strong antioxidant activity, attributed to its high phenolic (234.44 ± 6.05 mg GAE/g) and flavonoid (74.14 ± 4.59 mg QE/g) content. DPPH (IC50 = 0.32 ± 0.01 mg/mL) and iron chelation (IC50 = 5.64 ± 0.33 mg/mL) assays confirmed this activity, while GC‐MS analysis indicated that polyphenols and flavonoids were the main components [45]. In another study, carbon quantum dots (AM‐CQDs) were synthesised from A. maritima extract using a sustainable method, and their AI‐assisted characterisation was conducted. The resulting AM‐CQDs demonstrated high antioxidant activity in CUPRAC, ABTS/HRP, DPPH, and Folin assays, and their antioxidant behaviour was evaluated in detail through kinetic modelling. Additionally, they achieved an adsorption efficiency of 64.82% in the removal of methylene blue. These findings indicate that AM‐CQDs are promising nanomaterials for biomedical applications due to their strong antioxidant properties, and for environmental remediation studies owing to their effective adsorption capacities [46].

This study investigated the enzyme inhibition, cytotoxicity, and antioxidant effects of ethanol, ethanol–water, and water extracts prepared from the capitulum, leaf, and stem of A. biserrata using non‐toxic solvents suitable for herbal medicine. Detailed phytochemical analyses of all plant parts were performed separately and compared using LC‐MS/MS. Ethanol extracts showed stronger antiproliferative and enzyme inhibitory effects in all parts, while antioxidant capacity was higher in aqueous extracts. When the extracts were compared for tyrosinase enzyme inhibition and B16F10 cytotoxicity, urease and DPP‐IV enzyme inhibition and CaCo‐2 cytotoxicity, PTP‐1B inhibition and MCF‐7 cytotoxicity, the results were found to be correlated. Ethanol extracts with high enzyme inhibition also showed high antiproliferative effects on the corresponding cell lines. Comprehensive analyses of 53 compounds in the extracts were conducted using a validated LC‐MS/MS method. The major compounds identified were quinic acid and chlorogenic acid. This study, for the first time, investigated the correlation between the enzyme inhibitory activity of A. biserrata and its antiproliferative activity against specific cancer cell lines.

Conclusions

This study provides the comprehensive evaluation of the phytochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, enzyme inhibitory activity, antiproliferative potential, and phytochemical–bioactivity relationships of different aerial parts of Achillea biserrata. Ethanol extracts, particularly those obtained from the capitulum, exhibited the strongest biological activities, including pronounced antioxidant effects, significant inhibition of tyrosinase, urease, PTP1B, and DPP‐4 enzymes, and selective antiproliferative activity against B16F10 and CaCo‐2 cancer cell lines. LC–MS/MS analysis revealed substantial qualitative and quantitative differences among capitulum, leaf, and stem extracts, with phenolic acids and flavonoid glycosides representing the dominant phytochemical classes. Multivariate analyses further demonstrated that variations in biological activity were closely associated with phytochemical composition. PCA clearly discriminated the extracts according to their chemical and biological profiles, while hierarchical clustering highlighted distinct phytochemical–bioactivity relationships. PLS regression identified quinic acid, cosmosiin, cynaroside, p‐coumaric acid, gallic acid, and related phenolic constituents as major contributors to the observed activities. The integration of phytochemical profiling with biological and chemometric analyses suggests that the biological properties of A. biserrata arise from synergistic interactions among multiple phytochemicals rather than from a single dominant constituent. These findings support the potential of A. biserrata as a promising source of multifunctional bioactive compounds for the development of antioxidant, enzyme‐targeted, and anticancer applications. The main limitation of this study is that antiproliferative activity was evaluated only through in vitro screening on a single normal cell line. Therefore, further studies using different cancer and normal cell lines, as well as in vivo research, are needed to confirm the anticancer potential and mechanisms of action of the extracts. Furthermore, it is necessary to isolate and purify bioactive compounds and elucidate their mechanisms of action in detail through enzyme kinetic studies.

Experimental Section

Plant Material

The samples of A. biserrata studied in this work were collected from a natural population in Gumushane provience of Türkiye (A7 Gumushane: Zigana Mountain, between Zigana pass and Torul, 1300‐1350 m a.s.l., 17.06.2021). Taxonomic identification and verification of the specimens were performed by Prof. Dr. Huseyin Inceer (Department of Biology, Faculty of Science, Karadeniz Technical University, Trabzon, Türkiye) according to the key of Achillea in the Flora of Turkey and the East Aegean Islands [47]. A voucher spicemen (Inceer 1227) was deposited in the herbarium at the Karadeniz Technical University, Department of Biology (KTUB).

Preparation of Extracts

The aerial parts of A. biserrata were dried in a cool, dry place without direct sunlight, and then the capitulum, leaves and stems were manually separated. Each part of the plant was powdered (30 g) and divided into three equal portions. The first portion (10 g) was macerated in ethanol (EtOH) for 6 h at room temperature, the second portion (10 g) in ethanol–water 50:50 (EtOH‐Water) and the third portion (10 g) in water. The extracts were then filtered through filter paper, and the solvents were removed by evaporation. Each extract was freeze‐dried and used for biological effect studies.

Antiproliferative Effect

According to the MTT method, proliferating cells convert MTT, a yellow formazan salt, into purple, water‐insoluble formazan crystals through increased mitochondrial dehydrogenase enzyme activity. These crystals are dissolved in dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO). The viability of the cells is then determined spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. The viability of the untreated cells is assumed to be 100% and the viability of the treated cells is compared with these cells and expressed as a percentage (%) [48].

Cytotoxicity studies were performed with the healthy cell line L929 (mouse subcutaneous connective tissue, ATCC, CCL‐1, RRID: CVCL_0462) and with the cancer cell lines A549 (human lung adenocarcinoma, ATCC, CCL‐185, RRID: CVCL_0023), B16F10 (murine melanoma, ATCC, CRL‐6475, RRID: CVCL_0159), Hep‐3B (human hepatoma, ATCC, HB‐8064, RRID: CVCL_0326), PC‐3 (human prostate adenocarcinoma, ATCC, CRL‐1435, RRID: CVCL_0035), MCF‐7 (human breast adenocarcinoma, ATCC, HTB‐22, RRID: CVCL_0031) and CaCo‐2 (human colorectal adenocarcinoma, ATCC, HTB‐37, RRID: CVCL_0025). All cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and contamination‐free cell lines were used.

All cell lines except PC‐3 were cultured in DMEM (Ecotech‐Hg NutriCulture) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), while PC‐3 was cultured in RPMI (NutriCulture RPMI 1640). Cells were prepared at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and seeded in 96‐well plates with 1 × 104 cells per well. The plates were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h to allow the cells to attach and proliferate. The extracts were prepared at different concentrations in the medium and treated with the cells. After incubating the cells with the extracts for 48 h, the culture medium containing the extracts was completely removed and replaced with fresh culture medium containing 10 µL of MTT reagent. This procedure was performed to prevent direct interaction between the plant extracts and the MTT reagent, ensuring that cell viability was assessed solely based on metabolic activity. After a further 4 h of incubation, the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and measured spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. The results were compared with the control group, and the percentage viability was calculated.

Antioxidant Capacity

2,2‐Diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) Radical Scavenging Effect

DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl) is a stable free radical. It appears violet in methanol solution and has a strong absorption around 517 nm. Lower absorbance values with the DPPH method indicated higher free radical scavenging activity. The DPPH free radical scavenging activity of the extracts was determined according to the previously described method [49]. Trolox was used as the standard compound and the results were expressed as Trolox equivalents.

Copper Reducing Antioxidant Capacity (CUPRAC)

To determine the antioxidant capacity of the extracts using the CUPRAC method, 1000 µL of 7.5 mM neocuproine solution and 1000 µL of 1000 M ammonium acetate buffer were added to 1000 µL of a 10 mM CuCl2 solution to form a mixture. Samples prepared in the concentration range of 0–200 µg/mL were added to this mixture and the total volume was made up to 4.1 mL with ethanol. After ensuring that all solutions were thoroughly mixed, they were incubated in the dark for 30 min and at the end of this time the absorbance values were measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm [50]. Trolox was used as the standard compound and the standard curves were generated by plotting the absorbance values against the concentration. The results were expressed as Trolox equivalent

Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (TEAC)

The ABTS radical scavenging test is a rapid and reliable spectrophotometric method for determining the ability of a compound to measurably neutralise the free ABTS+ radical. The ABTS radical scavenging activity of the extracts was tested as described in previous studies using Trolox as a comparative standard [51]. 2.45 mM potassium persulfate solution was added in equal parts to 7 mM ABTS and incubated for 12–16 h in the dark at room temperature. The solution was then diluted with ethanol to determine the concentration at which the absorbance was measured as 0.7 ± 0.02. 140 µL of this solution was added to 60 µL of the extract and after 6 min incubation at room temperature, the absorbance was measured again at 734 nm. The TEAC value indicates the amount of the compound in milligrams with antioxidant capacity equivalent to that of Trolox in one gram of the extract.

Assessment of Tyrosinase Inhibition

Tyrosinase inhibitory activity was evaluated using a modified protocol based on the method described by Kim et al. In this assay, L‐tyrosine was employed as the substrate, and tyrosinase‐catalysed oxidation of L‐tyrosine resulted in the formation of dopachrome. The amount of dopachrome produced was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 475 nm, providing an indirect assessment of tyrosinase inhibitory activity [52, 53].

Initially, 130 µL of mushroom tyrosinase solution (approximately 100 U/mL) prepared in 0.05 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) was dispensed into each well of a 96‐well microplate. Subsequently, 20 µL of sample solutions at varying concentrations (20, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL), prepared in the same phosphate buffer, were added. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding 50 µL of 1.5 mM L‐tyrosine to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 20 min. Upon completion of the incubation period, the absorbance of each well was measured at 475 Nm.

Assessment of Urease Inhibition

The inhibition of the urease enzyme was determined by some modifications of the previously described methods. 0.01 mL of the samples prepared in the concentration range of 20–400 µg/mL were taken and mixed with 0.025 mL of a urease enzyme solution prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 8.2). After a 15 min incubation period, 60 µL of urea phosphate buffer (pH 8.2) containing phenol red was added. After 30 min at room temperature, the absorbance values were measured spectrophotometrically at 630 nm and the percentage inhibition values were calculated. Thiourea, prepared under the same conditions as the extracts, was used as the standard compound. To minimise experimental errors, all analyses were repeated at least three times and the mean values were calculated [54].

Assessment of PTP1B Inhibition

PTP1B enzyme inhibition by the extracts was assessed using human recombinant PTP1B enzyme according to the procedure described below [55]. The assay buffer was prepared with 50 mM citrate, 0.1 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), adjusted to pH 6.0. p‐Nitrophenyl phosphate (p‐NPP), used as the substrate, was prepared at a concentration of 2 mM in the assay buffer. Enzyme, substrate, and samples were added to 96‐well plates for a final volume of 200 µL. After incubation for 30 min at 37°C, the reaction was stopped with 10 M NaOH. The released p‐nitrophenol was measured by ELISA at 405 nm. PTP1B enzyme inhibition was determined by comparing the absorbance of the sample with that of the control group containing buffer and p‐NPP. Suramin was used as the standard substance (positive control) under the same conditions and at the same concentrations as the samples.

Assessment of DPP4 Inhibition

Gly‐Pro‐p‐nitroanilide was used as a substrate in the DPP‐4 enzyme inhibition assay. The enzyme (0.01 unit/mL), substrate (1.59 mM), and extracts were prepared in Tris‐HCl buffer (pH 8.6). In a 96‐well plate, 25 µL of assay buffer, 15 µL of enzyme, and 10 µL of sample were added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 5 min. Then, 50 µL of substrate was added, and incubation continued for another 30 min at 37°C. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 µL of acetate buffer (1 M, pH 4.0). Measurements were made by ELISA at 405 nm. For DPP‐4 enzyme inhibition determination, the absorbance of the control solution containing DPP‐4 enzyme, buffer, and Gly‐Pro‐p‐nitroanilide was compared with the absorbance of the samples [56].

LC‐MS/MS Based Qualitative and Quantitative Identification of Phytochemicals

The comprehensive phytochemical contents of ethanol extracts of A. biserrata capitulum, stem and leaves were determined by LC‐MS/MS technique and compared with each other. The qualitative and quantitative analysis of 53 different phytochemical compounds was performed according to the validated method [57].

Statistical Analysis

The experiments were repeated six times. Data analysis was performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 25, IBM Corp, Amonk, NY, USA) and Graphpad (version 10.2) software packages. The normality of the data distribution was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the data showed a normal distribution, the differences between extracts, cell cultures, antioxidant activity, and enzyme inhibitions were evaluated using one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and the Tukey test was selected as the post hoc analysis. The linear relationship between two variables was examined using Pearson correlation analysis. The correlation coefficient was evaluated according to Table 5 and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The letters (a, b, c, …) in the tables indicate the results of the post hoc Tukey analysis comparing the means within the same column. Means sharing the same letter (or common letter) are not significantly different (p > 0.05); if there is no common letter, the difference is significant (p < 0.05). Similarly, the symbols (□, ■, °, ●, ▲, ▼) indicate the comparison results of the means within the same row. There is no difference between means sharing the same symbol (or common symbol) (p > 0.05); if there is no common symbol, the difference is significant (p < 0.05). The statistical analysis was conducted using Graph Pad Prism (version 9.2).

Multivariate Statistical Analysis

Multivariate analyses were performed to explore the relationships between LC–MS/MS phytochemical composition and biological activities of the ethanol extracts of A. biserrata. The phytochemical matrix (X) consisted of quantified LC–MS/MS analytes detected in at least one sample, whereas the biological response matrix (Y) included antioxidant capacity, antiproliferative activity, and enzyme inhibition endpoints. Antioxidant values were retained in their original direction, while IC50 values obtained from cytotoxicity and enzyme inhibition assays were transformed to reciprocal potency values (1000/IC50) so that higher values consistently indicated stronger biological activity. Prior to chemometric analysis, all variables were autoscaled to zero mean and unit variance to prevent variables with larger numerical ranges from dominating the models. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used as an unsupervised pattern‐recognition method to visualise similarity among plant parts and to identify variables contributing to sample separation. PCA biplots were interpreted using sample scores and variable loadings according to the classical biplot approach of Gabriel [58]. Pearson correlation analysis and hierarchical clustering were then used to evaluate pairwise associations between dominant phytochemical constituents and bioactivity endpoints; clustered heatmaps were used to visualise correlation structure and variable grouping. Partial least squares regression (PLS‐R) was applied as an exploratory supervised chemometric method to relate phytochemical predictors to the combined bioactivity response matrix, following its established use in chemometrics [59]. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores was calculated to rank phytochemicals according to their contribution to the PLS model, and VIP > 1.0 was considered indicative of above‐average contribution to the model [60]. Since LC–MS/MS profiling was available for three ethanol extracts only, the multivariate analyses were considered exploratory and descriptive. Therefore, no inferential p‐values, external validation statistics, or permutation‐based significance claims were reported for the PCA, correlation heatmap, or PLS‐VIP model.
