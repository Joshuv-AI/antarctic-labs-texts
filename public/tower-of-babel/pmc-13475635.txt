Access to essential medicines in Albania: a cross-sectional survey of availability, prices and affordability using the WHO/Health Action International (HAI) Global Core List
Authors: Eriona Petro, Aukje K Mantel-Teeuwisse, Iris Rebecca Joosse, Arthur C M Siebers, Fatima Suleman, Hendrika Annie van den Ham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess the availability, prices and affordability of essential medicines in Albania, focusing on a standardised set of medicines from the Global Core List (GCL), using an adapted WHO/Health Action International (WHO/HAI) methodology.DesignCross-sectional survey.Setting30 private community pharmacies across six urban areas in Albania (Tirana, Durrës, Fier, Vlorë, Kavajë and Lushnjë).ParticipantsLicensed private community pharmacies, including both contracted and non-contracted pharmacies under the national health insurance scheme.OutcomesAvailability, patient prices and affordability of 14 medicines from the GCL. Affordability was assessed using the daily wage of the lowest-paid government worker (LPGW), and prices were evaluated using the median price ratio (MPR) compared with international reference prices.ResultsThe mean availability of WHO/HAI GCL medicines was 79.8%, slightly below the WHO target of 80%. In a secondary analysis that included therapeutically equivalent alternative formulations availability increased to 83.3%. Ceftriaxone, amoxicillin and salbutamol were among the least available medicines and were frequently out of stock. All medicines, except ceftriaxone, met affordability criteria based on LPGW. MPR analysis showed that while most medicines were priced competitively, some, including diclofenac and ceftriaxone, were substantially higher than international reference prices, with five medicines exceeding an MPR of 4.0. Overall, 64% of medicines met both availability and affordability thresholds.ConclusionsAccess to essential medicines in Albania appears to be primarily constrained by availability rather than affordability. Gaps in the availability of key antibiotics and asthma medicines highlight the need for targeted policy interventions, including improved procurement, supply chain management and rational use strategies to strengthen equitable access to essential medicines.Keywords: Medicine, PUBLIC HEALTH, Health Services Accessibility, Health policy

Introduction

Access to medicines is a multifaceted concept encompassing availability, affordability, accessibility and acceptability. Availability pertains to the presence of required medicines in healthcare facilities, while affordability refers to the patient’s financial feasibility of obtaining these medicines. Accessibility involves the geographical proximity of healthcare outlets, and acceptability reflects the extent to which medicines meet user expectations in terms of effectiveness, quality and suitability.1 2 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), barriers to access to essential medicines remain pervasive, undermining efforts to improve public health and reduce disease burden.3

Albania, an upper-middle-income country (UMIC) is located in Southeast Europe. With a population of approximately 2.36 million and an average per capita healthcare expenditure of US$464 per year.4 5 Albania’s health system faces significant challenges in delivering equitable access to essential medicines. This is particularly evident in the pharmaceutical sector, where the majority of services are delivered by the private sector,6 7 through the Albanian Mandatory Health Insurance Fund (AMHIF), which was established in 1995. This agency is funded by the government and covers individuals, including minors under 18 years of age, students up to 25 years old, pensioners and individuals receiving unemployment, social assistance or disability benefits.6 In Albania, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the predominant cause of mortality, accounting for approximately 96% of all deaths in 2018, with cardiovascular diseases alone responsible for 54% of these deaths.8 Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is also a significant concern; in 2019, there were 339 deaths directly attributable to AMR and an additional 1300 associated deaths.9

The services provided by AMHIF encompass public healthcare centres, privately contracted primary care centres, tertiary hospitals and contracted pharmacies. Under this arrangement, contracted pharmacies reimburse AMHIF listed medicines, subject to a referral process through primary and secondary healthcare centres, where specialist physicians assess whether patients meet the necessary criteria and for more complex cases final decisions are made at the tertiary healthcare centre, the central university hospital centre in Tirana ‘Mother Teresa’ (QSUT). If these criteria are met, medicines listed in the AMHIF reimbursement list may qualify for government reimbursement.10 Although the reimbursement scheme is available to the entire population, it does not cover all medical conditions and the reimbursement rates vary depending on the class of medicine.11

The WHO/Health Action International (WHO/HAI) methodology, developed in 2000, provides a standardised approach for evaluating the availability, affordability and pricing of essential medicines in healthcare systems worldwide. It includes a Global Core List (GCL) of 14 essential medicines addressing both communicable and NCDs, several of which are central to managing chronic conditions and serves as a benchmark for cross-country comparisons.12 Although the methodology has been widely applied in other LMICs, its application in middle-income countries in Europe, including Albania, has been limited representing a significant gap in the literature.

As a European Union (EU) candidate country, Albania is required to align its healthcare system with EU standards, including the provision of access to essential medicines as outlined in Chapter 28 of the EU acquis.7 Health-related indicators show that Albania underperforms compared with EU averages across all key health and socioeconomic metrics.13 14 Life expectancy is lower for both males (70.7 vs 77.9 years) and females (77.1 vs 83.3 years),15 while neonatal mortality is more than three times higher (7 vs 2 per 1000 live births).16 17 Health spending is also considerably lower (6.2% vs 10.4% of gross domestic product).18 Although gaps in unemployment and poverty are narrower, the overall pattern reflects systemic weaknesses.19–22 These disparities may indicate suboptimal access to essential medicines, yet no comprehensive study has assessed the situation. Therefore, it was important to assess the availability and affordability of essential medicines, particularly those included in the WHO GCL, within Albania’s healthcare system using the WHO/HAI methodology. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine whether WHO GCL medicines in Albania exceeded the WHO target of 80% accessibility for essential medicines which is a combined measure of availability and affordability.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess the availability, prices and affordability of medicines in Albania, using an adapted version of the WHO/HAI methodology.12 Six survey areas were selected based on population density and proximity to Tirana, the main urban centre. These included Tirana, Durrës, Fier, Vlorë, Kavajë and Lushnjë. In Albania, medicines, including those reimbursed under the national health insurance scheme, are dispensed through private community pharmacies, which function as the primary point of access for outpatient medicines. Public healthcare facilities do not routinely dispense outpatient medicines and are limited to providing a restricted list of emergency medicines as part of the primary healthcare package.23 Therefore, surveying private pharmacies provides an appropriate and representative assessment of medicine availability within the national health system.

The sample of 30 private pharmacies was selected based on proportional representation by population size across the six urban areas. A spatial map was developed to ensure coverage of all parts of each city, rather than using a fixed distance criterion. The list of licensed private pharmacies was obtained from the registry of the Albanian Order of Pharmacists, which provides a publicly accessible and regularly updated database. Pharmacies were first contacted by phone to request participation, and visits were conducted following verbal consent. The total number of registered pharmacies per city is provided to indicate sample representativeness. All surveyed facilities were privately owned and categorised as either contracted or uncontracted by the AMHIF. Contracted pharmacies are authorised to dispense reimbursed medicines to patients with valid reimbursement prescriptions, reducing out-of-pocket costs.

Medicine selection

The survey followed an adapted WHO/HAI methodology to ensure international comparability.12 From the WHO GCL, 12 out of 14 medicines are included in the AMHIF list, while all the medicines are registered in Albania.11 24 The selected medicines were validated for exact alignment with the reimbursement list of the AMHIF (see online supplemental table S1). In cases where the standard formulations (diclofenac 50 mg tablets/capsules and co-trimoxazole 8+40 mg/mL oral suspension) were not available at surveyed facilities, alternative formulations such as diclofenac (Voltfast 50 mg powder for oral suspension) and co-trimoxazole (400/80 mg tablets) were included as potential therapeutic alternatives.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, outcome selection or dissemination of this facility-based study.

Questionnaire development and validation

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed specifically for this study and implemented in the KoboToolbox platform (see online supplemental file 1). The questionnaire was based on the WHO/HAI methodology for measuring medicine availability and affordability and adapted to the WHO GCL of medicines and the Albanian community pharmacy context. The instrument collected information on pharmacy characteristics, medicine availability, originator brands (OB) and lowest-priced generics (LPG), package characteristics, manufacturer information, expiry dates, prices and stock-outs.

Prior to data collection, the electronic questionnaire was pilot tested to evaluate clarity, feasibility and consistency of data collection procedures. Minor revisions were made to improve wording, response options and standardisation of data entry before implementation in the main survey.

Data collection

Data collection occurred between 7 and 16 March 2024 using a structured questionnaire administered through the KoboCollect mobile application (see online supplemental file 1).25 Four trained data collectors, two pharmacy students, one medical student and one pharmacist, used the KoboCollect mobile application for standardised data entry.25 For each medicine, data on formulation, strength, price, expiry date, packaging and availability were recorded. Both OB and LPG were included. Photos of medicine packages were taken for verification processes.

Data collection procedures, including interviewer training, duration and logistics, followed the approach detailed in our methodological paper ‘Time and cost involved in measuring access to medicines: the case of Albania’.26 In brief, four trained data collectors conducted the surveys inside participating pharmacies, requiring approximately 3.5 hours per survey area (36 hours in total across six areas).

Data management and analysis

Collected data were exported to Excel for cleaning and validation. Discrepancies were resolved using photo verification. Random cross-checks were conducted by the researchers to verify the accuracy of the data.

Determination of availability

Availability was calculated as the percentage of pharmacies with each medicine available on the day of data collection, and was analysed overall, by medicine type (OB/LPG) and by survey area. Availability was categorised as: not available (0%), very low (<30%), low (30–49%), fairly high (50–80%) and high (>80%).12 A subanalysis was conducted to include ‘similar formulations’, defined as alternative dosage forms with equivalent therapeutic use (eg, diclofenac oral suspension in place of tablets, or co-trimoxazole 400/80 mg tablets in place of paediatric-specific formulations), to provide additional insight into potential therapeutic options in the absence of age-appropriate products (see online supplemental table S2).

Determination of affordability

Affordability was assessed by considering both the day’s wages of the lowest paid governmental worker (LPGW),12 and the median price ratio (MPR). In the WHO/HAI methodology affordability is calculated as the number of days’ wages required to purchase a full treatment course. The course of treatment refers to the total quantity of a medicine required to complete a full therapeutic regimen for a specific condition, based on the standard dosage, frequency and duration of treatment as recommended in standard treatment guidelines (eg, 30 days for chronic treatment). The price per treatment is found by multiplying the median unit price of the medicine by the quantity needed. The LPGW’s monthly salary was 40 000 Albanian lek (ALL) (≈US$421.83), equating to ~1430 ALL/day.27 Affordability was determined by dividing the price per treatment by the LPGW. A medicine was deemed unaffordable if it exceeded 1 day’s wage, according to the WHO/HAI methodology.12

Median price ratio

The Management Sciences for Health (MSH) international reference prices (IRPs), last published in 2015, were used to calculate the MPR. These IRPs reflect procurement prices paid by governments and non-profit organisations in LMICs and are recommended by WHO/HAI as reliable external benchmarks for assessing affordability.28 To approximate current values, the 2015 IRPs were adjusted using Consumer Price Index (CPI) data.29 The ‘average CPI of 48 European countries’ was calculated as a simple mean of national CPI values for 2015–2024 and applied to update the 2015 MSH reference prices for inflation. All collected prices were expressed as retail unit prices.

In addition to using IRPs, unit prices from selected comparator countries were included to contextualise local pricing. These countries comprised Croatia, Cyprus and Slovenia, as well as early EU member states such as Belgium, France, the Netherlands, Italy and Denmark. These prices, updated using national CPIs and converted to ALL, were drawn from sources that included retail, wholesale and unspecified price types.30 31 The resulting inflation-adjusted unit prices and corresponding MPRs are presented in online supplemental table S3.

MPRs were calculated by dividing the median local unit price by the corresponding IRP, in accordance with WHO/HAI methodology. Although WHO/HAI does not prescribe a fixed threshold, an MPR greater than 4.0 for generics is often considered indicative of affordability issues. Median values were used to reduce the influence of outliers and better represent typical price levels.12

While inflation adjustment was applied to approximate current values, purchasing power parity (PPP) and recent currency fluctuations were not considered, which may introduce minor cross-country variation in affordability comparisons.

Stock-outs

Stock-outs were recorded when a medicine was unavailable at the time of visit. Durations were categorised as: unavailable within the last week, last month, over a month or never available. Medicines not stocked due to low demand were excluded. If either the OB or LPG was available, the medicine was not considered out of stock. While medicine availability was verified through on-site checking, information on stock-outs was based solely on pharmacists’ self-reports.

Overall accessibility

A scatter plot illustrated the relationship between availability and affordability (LPGW method) for each medicine. Medicines available and affordable in >80% of surveyed facilities were considered accessible.

Availability of essential medicines

The overall availability of essential medicines from the WHO/HAI GCL in surveyed pharmacies averaged 79.8%, narrowly missing the WHO’s 80% benchmark. Ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin (both Watch antibiotics according to the WHO Access, Watch, Reserve classification), metformin and paracetamol were consistently available across all outlets. In contrast, co-trimoxazole (Access antibiotic) showed the lowest availability at 40.0%, while amoxicillin (Access antibiotic) and salbutamol were also limited, at 43.3% and 46.7%, respectively (see figure 1). However, the inclusion of similar or therapeutically equivalent formulations for co-trimoxazole and diclofenac significantly improved availability for these medicines. Co-trimoxazole increased to 63.3%, and diclofenac reached the WHO-recommended target of 80%. As a result, the overall mean availability of essential medicines rose from 79.8% to 83.3%, highlighting the importance of considering alternative formulations in assessments of medicine access. Among the 14 WHO /HAI GCL medicines assessed, 6 were ≥80% available across all survey areas. In contrast, co-trimoxazole and salbutamol were not sufficiently available in any location. Notably, amoxicillin 500 mg was completely absent in Kavajë, while diclofenac and salbutamol were entirely unavailable in Tirana. The 80% national availability target was not reached in Durrës (75.7%), Kavajë (74.3%) and Tirana (72.9%), representing half of the surveyed locations. These figures underscore regional disparities in the availability of essential medicines (see figure 2).

The availability of essential medicines showed clear disparities between OB and LPG formulations, as well as between contracted and uncontracted pharmacies (see online supplementary file S4). LPG formulations of core medicines were widely available, with ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin and metformin each reaching 100% availability. In comparison, OB formulations were found for only four medicines, and only metformin achieved an acceptable availability rate of 86.7%. Contracted pharmacies demonstrated higher mean availability (87.5%) compared with uncontracted ones (73.0%). Notably, medicines such as salbutamol (75.0% vs 7.1%), diclofenac (75.0% vs 28.6%) and simvastatin (100.0% vs 64.3%) were significantly more available in contracted pharmacies, highlighting pronounced supply differences. In most cases where LPG formulations were lacking, OB alternatives were also not stocked. Diclofenac was the only exception, with OB versions more frequently available than LPG.

Stock-outs were reported in 25 of the 30 surveyed health facilities (83%), with a total of 58 individual stock-out incidents. These varied in duration, with 31 medicines experiencing stock-outs exceeding 1 month, 21 lasting 1 month and 4 lasting 1 week; in two cases, the duration was unspecified. The most frequently affected medicines were salbutamol (19 stock-outs), co-trimoxazole (n=13), amoxicillin (n=12) and diclofenac (n=8), which were also among those most commonly reported as unavailable.

Affordability

As per the WHO/HAI methodology, median unit prices were calculated for all surveyed medicines, along with MPRs relative to selected Balkan countries, EU member states and IRPs (see online supplemental table S3). Median prices varied considerably, for example, 1.14 ALL per dose of salbutamol and 813.50 ALL per vial of ceftriaxone possibly reflecting differences in dosage form, therapeutic category and procurement context. Several medicines, including diclofenac (MPR=11.35) and ceftriaxone (MPR=15.08), showed particularly high MPRs compared with IRPs, raising concerns about affordability and pricing efficiency.

Affordability as per lowest paid government worker approach

According to the LPGW approach, all core medicines were considered affordable except for ceftriaxone 1 g/vial (see table 2). Diazepam and simvastatin consistently had very low day’s wage values of 0.01 and 0.10, respectively. Co-trimoxazole had daily’s wage values of 0.08 in contracted pharmacies, compared with 0.14 in uncontracted pharmacies.

Accessibility

An assessment of overall accessibility of core medicines in Albania was undertaken by combining data on availability and affordability, the latter calculated using the LPGW method (see figure 4). The analysis revealed that limited accessibility was primarily driven by low availability, which affected 4 out of the 14 core medicines surveyed. In contrast, affordability was not a widespread issue and was identified only for ceftriaxone 1 g/vial treatments, where the cost exceeded the accepted affordability threshold.

Based on the combined criteria, 9 out of 14 core medicines were classified as accessible, resulting in an overall accessibility level of 64%. This falls short of the widely accepted benchmark of 80% accessibility for essential medicines.

Discussion

Access to essential medicines, particularly those listed in the WHO/HAI GCL, is central to the right to health and critical for reducing the burden of NCDs. In 2013, the WHO set a target of 80% availability of affordable essential NCD medicines and technologies by 2025 to support a 25% reduction in premature NCD mortality.32 This goal was reinforced by Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 3.4, which aims to reduce premature NCD mortality by one-third by 2030 through prevention and treatment.33 To support progress, the WHO launched the Implementation Roadmap 2023–2030, which outlines strategies to expand access to essential medicines and strengthen health systems.34

In this context, the present Albanian study assessed the availability, affordability and pricing of core essential NCD medicines. The organisation of the Albanian healthcare system is important for interpreting these findings. As outpatient medicines, including reimbursed medicines, are dispensed through private community pharmacies, these facilities represent the primary point of access to medicines. The focus on private pharmacies therefore reflects the real-world structure of medicine access in Albania and supports the interpretation of availability and affordability findings.

Of the 14 core medicines surveyed, 4 were unavailable at the time of assessment. Affordability analysis based on the LPGW method showed that all medicines except ceftriaxone were considered affordable. However, pricing analysis using the MPR method revealed that only 3 out of 14 medicines were priced below the IRP, while five had MPRs exceeding the affordability benchmark of 4.0. With an overall accessibility rate of 64%, the study indicates that Albania did not meet the WHO’s 2025 target.

These findings should also be interpreted in light of the study scope in terms of surveyed medicines. The analysis is based on the WHO/HAI GCL, which includes a limited number of tracer medicines. While the inclusion of 14 medicines does not fully capture the complexity of access to medicines in Albania, this standardised basket is designed for global benchmarking and enables cross-country comparisons. As such, it provides a meaningful indication of access to essential medicines at the national level in comparison to other countries.

The average availability of medicines from the GCL in Albania was 79.8%, with regional disparities observed. LPGs were 2.5 times more available than OBs. The availability of medicines from the GCL in the WHO Europe region has been assessed in Kazakhstan, Moldova and Russia (UMICs) and Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Ukraine (LMICs) (see online supplemental table S5). Moldova, comparable to Albania in population size and healthcare challenges, found a mean overall availability of 67.9%, compared with 70.1% and 70.9% in the three UMICs and LMICs, respectively.12 35

Beyond these comparisons, the use of the GCL also highlights a broader limitation in the evidence base. Access to medicines across different populations and conditions remains unevenly captured within global monitoring frameworks. The restricted set of tracer medicines and formulations reflects wider challenges in comprehensively assessing access and informing policy.

Notably, several antibiotics from the Access group, which the WHO defined as first-line or second-line treatments that should be widely available, affordable and have a lower potential for resistance, were among the medicines found to be unavailable.36 This included commonly used antibiotics such as amoxicillin and co-trimoxazole, both of which were available in fewer than half of surveyed pharmacies. In contrast, ceftriaxone, a Watch antibiotic, was available in all surveyed pharmacies. This pattern raises concerns from an antimicrobial stewardship perspective, as limited availability of Access antibiotics may lead to greater reliance on broader-spectrum alternatives that are recommended only for specific indications due to their higher potential to contribute to antimicrobial resistance.36 These findings are consistent with those of our recent study assessing access to child-appropriate medicines in Albania using SDG indicator 3.b.3, which also identified medicine availability as the principal barrier to access.37 In that study, benzylpenicillin, an Access antibiotic, was unavailable across surveyed facilities, whereas ceftriaxone was consistently available. Taken together, the findings of the two studies suggest challenges in ensuring access to recommended first-line antibiotics and reinforce the importance of strengthening procurement systems and antimicrobial stewardship efforts in Albania. One potential solution is the development and implementation of a comprehensive National Action Plan (NAP) on AMR. While Albania has a draft version of this NAP, delays in its approval contribute to the continued use of Watch antibiotics. The NAP aims to promote rational prescribing, strengthen surveillance and support the use and availability of Access antibiotics through better stewardship and procurement alignment.9 Although the present study did not assess prescribing patterns or antibiotic consumption and therefore cannot determine which alternatives are used when Access antibiotics are unavailable, the observed availability pattern hints at a potential mismatch between access and stewardship objectives. Furthermore, the relationship between the availability of essential antibiotics and the burden of infectious diseases in Albania warrants further investigation. Future studies should explore whether patterns of antibiotic availability are associated with disease burden, prescribing practices and antibiotic utilisation.

Salbutamol, an essential medicine for asthma treatment, was also unavailable in several uncontracted pharmacies in Albania, reflecting a broader issue observed across Europe.38–41 Shortages have been reported in multiple EU countries, largely due to increased demand and global supply disruptions.40 A key vulnerability lies in the concentration of active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) production in a small number of Asian manufacturers.41 No data on prescribing practices were collected, limiting our ability to determine which medicines were ultimately used in clinical practice when salbutamol was unavailable. Reduced availability of this essential medicine may negatively affect timely access to treatment for children with asthma. While alternative treatment options may exist, the implications of salbutamol shortages on prescribing patterns and patient outcomes warrant further investigation. To address these challenges, the European Commission has proposed the Critical Medicines Act, which aims to diversify API sources and strengthen EU pharmaceutical resilience.42 The observed medicine shortages may have important clinical consequences. Limited availability of essential medicines can delay treatment initiation, increase the economic burden on patients and caregivers seeking medicines and reduce timely access to appropriate care, particularly for children with acute or chronic conditions.

Only ceftriaxone, both its OB and LPG, was unaffordable by LPGW standards. On average, LPG medicines were 1.9 times more affordable and 2.5 times more available than OB medicines. Retail medicine prices in Albania were relatively high, with an average MPR of 4.3 based on the WHO/HAI methodology using 2015 MSH IRPs adjusted for inflation. Only 3 of 14 core medicines had MPRs below 1.0, while five exceeded 4.0, suggesting pricing inefficiencies.12 30 35 43 It should be noted that IRPs reflect global procurement prices and are not corrected for national PPP, which is methodologically complex and rarely done.44

Generic medicines were generally more affordable and available than OBs. While most core medicines were priced lower in Albania than in selected EU and Balkan countries, cross-country comparisons were complicated by differences in regulatory frameworks, price levels (wholesale vs retail) and reimbursement structures.28 45 Notably, the only medicine deemed unaffordable (ceftriaxone) using the LPGW approach was not included in the AMHIF, highlighting a gap in financial protection. These findings underscore the need for further investigation into procurement pricing transparency, supply chain mark-ups and the role of reimbursement mechanisms in ensuring medicine affordability and access in Albania.

While this study did not assess supply chain performance, patient satisfaction or prescriber behaviour, these aspects represent important complementary dimensions of access that may further explain observed availability and affordability patterns. Future research should therefore consider incorporating supply chain mapping, expanding sampling to rural settings and using more current procurement or price data to strengthen national monitoring efforts.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It used an adapted version of the WHO/HAI methodology, a well-established and reliable tool for measuring the availability and affordability of medicines. This allows the results to be compared with studies from other countries. By focusing on medicines from the GCL, the study supports international efforts to monitor access to essential medicines. An extra analysis on how long medicines were out of stock and similar formulations was included, helping to better understand medicine availability in Albania. The results are also important for health policy, especially since Albania does not have its own Essential Medicines List. Finally, the study is one of the few of its kind in Southeast Europe, and it could help guide future research and the creation of a regional medicine list that better reflects the health needs of this area.

However, there are also some limitations, with several stemming from inherent constraints of the WHO/HAI methodology. First, medicine availability was measured only on the day of the survey. This means the study did not capture how often or how long medicines were out of stock. Although an additional analysis was conducted, data may still be incomplete due to inconsistent reporting or pharmacists’ reluctance to repeat information. In addition, the duration of medicine shortages was based on pharmacists’ self-reported estimates, which may be subject to recall bias or incomplete reporting. Second, data were collected exclusively from urban pharmacies, which may underestimate access inequalities and variations in medicine availability across rural or remote areas. Future surveys should expand coverage to include rural and semi-urban settings to provide a more representative national picture. Only one strength and form per medicine was assessed; however, similar therapeutic alternatives were included to provide a broader view of availability. Furthermore, the study focused only on the 14 medicines included in the WHO/HAI GCL and did not consider medicines from regional or supplementary lists that may better reflect national disease burdens. As the study was designed to apply the existing WHO/HAI methodology rather than evaluate it, our findings are insufficient to determine whether the current GCL should be revised or expanded. However, it should be noted that this list of 14 medicines was selected two decades ago, and its continued relevance to current public health priorities and antimicrobial resistance patterns warrants evaluation moving forward, including whether assessment of therapeutic equivalents and/or therapeutic groups may complement the current medicine-specific approach. Future research incorporating disease burden, antimicrobial resistance, prescribing practices and evolving public health priorities across diverse settings is needed to inform any potential revisions to the WHO/HAI methodology and the GCL.

Finally, affordability was assessed using a general income estimate based on the LPGW, which does not consider other living costs or patients using multiple medicines. As a result, treatments may appear more affordable than they actually are in practice. Additionally, the analysis relied on outdated IRPs from the 2015 MSH price guide, which may limit the accuracy of present-day price comparisons. As highlighted by Wirtz et al,46 transparent and current price data are essential for equitable access and sound pharmaceutical policy. Outdated benchmarks may therefore compromise the validity of cross-country comparisons.

The affordability indicator, based on the daily wage of the LPGW, provides an internationally comparable benchmark but may underestimate the real financial burden for patients with multiple conditions or lower incomes, especially in settings with high out-of-pocket spending. Lastly, the study did not assess medicine acceptability, including patient preferences, adherence and perceptions of quality, which are critical dimensions of access and may significantly influence medicine use and treatment outcomes. Future research should address these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of access to essential medicines in Albania.

Conclusion

This study provides insights into the accessibility of the WHO/HAI GCL of essential medicines in Albania. Availability issues, particularly with antibiotics and asthma medications, highlight the need for better policy on rational use and other interventions along the pharmaceutical value chain. While medicines were generally more affordable in Albania compared with Europe, affordability concerns arose with ceftriaxone. These insights should inform Albania’s health policy and align with EU health protection goals, while underscoring the importance of regular accessibility studies to support the achievement of SDG 3.b.3 by 2030.
