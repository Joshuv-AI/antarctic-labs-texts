An inter-regional input-output table series of China from 1987–2017 with integrated carbon emission data
Authors: Qiong Zhao, Chen Pan, Zengkai Zhang, Xuefan Guo, Kunfu Zhu, Jianwu He, Shantong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Inter-regional input-output (IRIO) tables are essential for socioeconomic and environmental analysis. This paper compiled a continuous time series of Chinese IRIO tables with a detailed regional and sectoral classification, covering a longer period from 1987 to 2017 than existing Chinese IRIO tables. Additionally, we integrated the China provincial CO2 emission inventory data (1987–2017) to analyze trends and identify the main driving factors behind regional economic linkages and carbon emissions changes. Specifically, we adjusted the initial values of the international and interprovincial imports and exports using customs data. Subsequently, we employed the minimum cross-entropy model to balance the adjusted input-output tables (IOTs) from both row and column perspectives. Following this, we estimated the interprovincial trade flow for each sector using the gravity model. Then, we balanced these estimates with the minimum cross-entropy model to form the multi-regional input-output (MRIO) table. Finally, we constructed the IRIO table based on the proportion assumption. The compiled IRIO tables, integrated with carbon emission data, have significant applications in regional economic analysis, policy evaluation, and environmental studies.

Background & Summary

With the acceleration of global economic integration and the increasingly prominent problem of climate change, understanding interregional economic linkages and their environmental impact, particularly the interregional embodied carbon emissions, has become an essential topic in global sustainable development research. China is the largest contributor to global CO2 emissions, responsible for almost all emissions growth between 2019 and 20211,2, as reported by the International Energy Agency (IEA). In 2021, China’s CO2 emissions reached 12.8 billion tons, accounting for 31.5% of the total global emissions1. While China has committed to ambitious carbon reduction targets, namely peaking emissions by 2030 and becoming carbon neutral by 2060, socioeconomic disparities among regions3–5 lead to varying carbon emissions6,7. Economic links between provinces have also resulted in carbon emissions transfer8, which will affect the scale and distribution of carbon emissions in provinces5,9 and hinder the attainment of national carbon reduction goals10,11. Therefore, understanding China’s interregional economic linkages and carbon emissions is crucial for grasping regional disparities and achieving ecological and economic balance.

Inter-regional input-output (IRIO) tables are a powerful tool for analyzing economic flows and capturing transactions and embodied carbon emissions between regions12,13. Many studies have explored carbon transfer within China based on the IRIO table14–20. They found that the developed provinces often shift carbon-intensive industries to less developed provinces and import carbon-intensive products from these regions to meet emission targets21–23. For instance, Mi (2017)24 investigated carbon transfers and their main drivers in 2012, concluding that less-developed regions experienced the highest net carbon inflows. Similarly, Meng et al.16 discovered that developed coastal regions’ international exports resulted in emissions from less-developed inland regions between 2002–2007. Several driving factors contribute to carbon emission transfers among Chinese provinces25–28, such as population differences15, production structures24,29, energy efficiency25,30, and trade activities29,31, as well as economic growth deceleration15,25 and changes in consumption patterns25,32. Additionally, some researchers have used multi-regional input-output (MRIO) to assess emission responsibilities33,34, outline carbon transfer in international trade35,36, and analyze the equity of interprovincial trade in China5,37. However, there is still the problem of short time series, hindering the comprehensive understanding of long-term trends and cyclical changes. Compiling various IRIOs depends on diverse data sources, statistical criteria, and time frames, posing challenges for cross-reference tables and impacting data consistency and accuracy12,38. To conduct an in-depth analysis of China’s regional economic structure, industrial linkages, and their impact on carbon emissions, compiling a comprehensive, accurate, and time-spanning set of China IRIO with integrated carbon emission data is essential.

Several organizations in China have linked provincial input-output tables (IOTs) based on socioeconomic datasets to compile the China Multi-Regional Input-Output Table (MRIO). Existing studies primarily use two trade data estimation strategies: direct methods based on surveys and indirect methods based on models. For example, the State Information Center (SIC) utilized a combination of survey-based direct methods and non-survey methods to compile MRIO for the years 1997, 2002, and 2007, covering 17 sectors (30 sectors in 1997) across 8 regions (Northeast, Beijing Tianjin, northern coastal, eastern coastal, southern coastal, central, northwest and southwest)39. Most studies primarily rely on indirect methods, particularly gravity models. For instance, the Development Research Center (DRC) of the State Council released the 1987 MRIO for 7 regions and 9 sectors in 200340, followed by a series of MRIO publications from 1987 to 201741–45. The Key Laboratory of Regional Sustainable Development Modeling at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS-RSDM) has sequentially published China MRIOs for 200746, 201047, and 201248. Additionally, the Research Centre on Fictitious Economy and Data Science of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS-FEDS) has compiled China IRIO for 2002, covering 60 sectors in 30 provinces, making it the largest number of sectors in the existing interregional input-output database of China49. Carbon Emission Accounts and Datasets (CEADs) compiled IRIO for 42 sectors in 31 provinces in 2012, 2015, and 201750,51. To visualize these differences, we have created Table S1 to summarize the comparisons. Compared to the aforementioned datasets, the IRIO table compiled in this study has a long time coverage (1987–2017), achieving time-series continuity and offering a more detailed categorization of regions and industries. Importantly, the dataset integrates carbon emission accounts, enabling a holistic perspective encompassing economic and environmental dimensions. This comprehensive and meticulously categorized long-term dataset is crucial for conducting in-depth analyses of the evolving trends within China’s economic structure, assessing the impacts of policy measures, and elucidating the dynamic interconnections among regional economies and carbon emissions changes.

Furthermore, our methodological innovation in the compilation the IRIO tables underscores three key aspects: Firstly, we ensure methodological consistency when compiling IRIO across various years. This ensures the comparability and reliability of time series data. Although the compilation method remains consistent annually, adjustments are occasionally necessary based on the specific data situation of each year. Secondly, we aim to retain as much information as possible by maintaining the total output constant and avoiding modifications to the intermediate use matrix and the value-added matrix. Finally, we adopt a ‘bottom-up’ approach, which prioritizes high-quality provincial data over national data tables and aligns with other provincial datasets, such as energy data, to improve the accuracy of regional research analysis.

Methods

Data source

1987–2017 provincial single region input-output table

The provincial single region input-output table (IOT) is the foundational data for constructing the China IRIO model. This data is sourced from the National Bureau of Statistics of China (https://data.stats.gov.cn/) and its publications related to input-output tables52–55. However, discrepancies exist across different years regarding the number of regions and sectors and the breakdowns of value-added and final-use data. For instance, some provinces lack detailed value-added data for 1987, and the value-added entries based on the income approach for 1987 and 1992 are inconsistent with subsequent years. Additionally, the final-use breakdowns in the regional IOT for 1987 differ from those in later years. This also explains the annual variation in the number of sectors and the breakdown of value added. Trade data also exhibit inconsistencies. Some provinces provide comprehensive trade data, including exports, imports, interprovincial exports, and interprovincial imports. Others offer only total exports and total imports or merely net exports. Even among provinces with complete trade data, significant imbalances exist between interprovincial exports and interprovincial imports. This imbalance arises from independent provincial tabulations and the lack of a statistical or survey basis for quantifying interprovincial trade. Theoretically, the sum of interprovincial exports of a product within a country should equal the sum of its interprovincial imports. Therefore, we subsequently adjusted inter-provincial trade flows using customs import and export data and railway transportation data.

1987–2017 customs import and export data

The customs import and export (IMEX) data used in this study is the value of customs IMEX goods, calculated based on domestic source/destination statistics, classified according to the Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS). Subscription access to detailed customs data is available through guidance on the official website (http://www.customs.gov.cn/customs/302249/zfxxgk/2799825/302274/tjfwzn/2319672/index.html). Some years are based on the Standard International Trade Classification (SITC) standard. The statistics include data from mainland provinces, cities, and districts, consistent with the provincial administrative divisions of the respective years. The concordance of customs IMEX data to IRIO is shown in Table 1. In practical applications, it is essential to preprocess the customs IMEX data annually. The first step involves merging the customs IMEX data at the IO sector level based on the correspondence between the IO sector and the customs commodity code. Although the correspondence between customs commodity codes and IO sectors is generally consistent each year, slight variations necessitate annual adjustments based on customs data. Therefore, we provide examples of each type for reference: 1987 SITC codes matched to 33 sectors of the 1987 IOT refer to SITCto1987Iomap.xlsx file. For 2017, we utilized the correspondence provided by Chen et al.56. The next step involves converting prices into producer prices. Precisely, the value of exported goods by customs is calculated based on offshore prices, from which circulation costs are deducted to convert them into domestic producer prices. 2017 Inter-provincial trade survey data of beijing and tianjin-hebei

Some interregional trade survey data, sourced from the Beijing Municipal Bureau of Statistics (https://tjj.beijing.gov.cn/), were also available in 2017 as a supplement. Beijing conducted a trade statistics survey with Tianjin and Hebei in 2017 to better understand the interregional trade dynamics. This survey provided detailed data on the sources and destinations of products from industrial enterprises. Specifically, it included information on the quantities of agricultural and industrial products purchased from local sources, Tianjin, Hebei, other domestic provinces, and foreign countries. It also detailed the quantities of these products sold to local markets, Tianjin, Hebei, other domestic provinces, and international destinations. This study will utilize this data to refine Beijing’s inter-provincial trade statistics.

The compilation method of IRIO tables

This paper complied the Chinese IRIO tables spanning from 1987 to 2017. These IRIO tables illustrate the interprovince economic structure of China over a period of three decades, including the intermediate use matrix, final demand matrix, export and import information for each province. The layout follows the standard IRIO table (Fig. S2).

The compilation method remains consistent annually, this paper takes 2017 as an example to illustrate the six steps involved in compiling IRIO, offering readers a representative reference (Fig. 1). Firstly, based on the official provincial IOTs (Original IOTs) of 2017 published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBS), we use the customs data from the General Administration of Customs of the People’s Republic of China to adjust the initial values of the international and interprovincial imports and exports. Secondly, we used the minimum cross-entropy model to balance the adjusted IOTs from both row and column perspectives. Third, we estimate the interprovincial trade flow for each sector using the railway transport data provided by the Compliation of National Railway Statistics and then using the minimum cross-entropy model to balance the interprovincial trade flows at the sectoral level to form the MRIO table of China. Finally, we constructed the IRIO table of 2017 using the proportionality assumption. Based on data availability, we compared our IRIO tables (DRC) of 2012 and 2017 with that compiled by the Research Centre on Fictitious Economy and Data Science of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS-FEDS), Key Laboratory of Regional Sustainable Development Modeling at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS-RSDM), and Carbon Emission Accounts and Datasets (CEADs). Adjust international and interprovincial trade data

In the original IOTs, the sum of export and interprovincial export is greater than total exports due to entrepot trade. To solve this problem, we need to make a preliminary adjustment to international and interprovincial trade data. First, we collect the import and export (IMEX) data at the sectoral level based on the customs data and then use it to replace the corresponding IMEX data in the original IOTs of each province. Next, we need to adjust interprovincial trade data based on the relationships between two data relationships: the relationship between IMEX data and interprovincial trade under entrepot trade, the relationship between IMEX data in customs and IMEX data in the original IOTs). We will replace the negative value cross interprovincial trade with zero.

Balance the adjusted IOTs

In the adjusted IOTs, a discrepancy arises between the total interprovincial exports and imports at the sectoral level. To solve this problem, we need to balance the adjusted IOTs with less change to the original data. This involves two main perspectives: 1) balancing by row, which means that the total output should equal the row sum of intermediate use and final demand, and 2) balancing by column, which requires that the column sum of intermediate use plus value-added is equal to the total output.

The adjustment terms in our model included t the row sum of intermediate use, consumption (including rural household consumption, urban household consumption, and government consumption), gross capital formation (gross fixed capital formation, change in inventories), exports, imports, interprovincial exports and interprovincial imports. This tendency mostly arises from the inherent resource advantages in different places. The underdeveloped Northwest region has been pivotal in supporting the economic growth of the southern provinces by offering high-carbon-intensive and low-value-added products. For instance, Inner Mongolia, a significant energy supplier24, contributed 68% of its local coal production (23.711 million tons) to other provinces in 2012. In addition, environmental restrictions require the elimination of obsolete industrial capacity, which necessitates the importation of energy-intensive items from other regions. Guangdong recorded the highest net carbon inflow among provinces, amounting to 177.2 million tons in 2012.

Structural decomposition analysis

Structural decomposition analysis (SDA) is widely employed to estimate the driving factors of changes in carbon emissions and energy consumption by combining with IO tables24,57. This method distinguishes the direct and indirect effects of changes in final demand and intermediate inputs on the economy58. SDA decomposes the changes in dependent variables within the economic system into the sum of corresponding driving factors, thereby enabling the measurement of the contribution of each driving factor.

To conduct this study effectively, the first step involved adjusting the IRIO tables to constant prices, aligning with the requirements of SDA to ensure the comparability of economic data. The methodology outlined by Pan et al.59 was employed to obtain sector-level price indices from the China Statistical Yearbook. Then, we adopted SDA to analyze the driving factors of the current carbon transfer pattern. Figure 3(b) illustrates the changes in driving factors for carbon transfers from NN to CS in China. The increase in carbon transfer from NN to CS is mainly attributed to the consumption growth in CS, including changes in gross capital formation (GCF) and total consumption (TC). For the initial subperiod (1987–1992), GCF and TC resulted in respective increases of 47.4% and 44.5% in carbon transfer from NN to CS, without accounting for other influencing factors. The decline in carbon intensity (CI) resulted in a 55.5% drop in carbon transfers from NN to CS. Furthermore, the production structure (PS) alteration accounted for 44.5%. The deceleration in carbon transfers from NN to CS in China during 1992–2002 was primarily driven by shifts in production structure (PS) and carbon intensity (CI). The trend of carbon transfers from NN to CS between 2002 and 2007 aligns with the findings of Pan et al.62. Although the carbon transfers continued to rise, the growth rates slowed in the fifth (2007–2012) and sixth (2012–2017) subperiods, recording increments of 7.9% and 20.9% respectively. Notably, during 2007–2012, carbon intensity was dominant role in reducing carbon transfers from NN to CS in China, potentially leading to an 18.9% decrease. Between 2012 and 2017, changes in production structure were more important than carbon intensity in counteracting China’s carbon transfers from NN to CS. These data indicate that adjusting the production structure was vital in decreasing carbon transfer from NN to CS in China between 2012 and 2017. Therefore, production structure changes will remain crucial in minimizing carbon transfers in the future.

Data Records

All IRIO tables data files are available for public access at figshare63, providing comprehensive insights into China’s regional economic structure across provinces and sectors from 1987 to 2017 (named (1987–2017)-IRIO.xlsx file). These tables include the intermediate use matrix, final demand matrix, and export and import information for each province, following the standard layout of IRIO tables (Fig. S2). The number of provinces included fluctuates year, with a range of 28 to 31 provinces (28 provinces in 1987, 29 provinces in 1992, 30 provinces in 1997, 2002, and 2007, and 31 provinces in 2012 and 2017). Furthermore, the sector classification may slightly differ due to using different national sector classifications (Table S2). The final demand comprises five categories: rural household consumption, urban household consumption, government consumption, fixed capital formation, and inventory change. However, in 1987, rural and urban household consumption was merged into household consumption. The value-added data includes employee compensation, net production tax, fixed capital depreciation, and operating surplus for each year, except for 1987, when only total value-added data is provided. In addition, the integrated carbon emission data are China provincial CO2 emission inventory from 1987 to 2017.

Technical Validation

Compare with other IRIO datasets

Based on data availability, we compared our IRIO tables (DRC) of 2012 and 2017 with those compiled by the Research Centre on Fictitious Economy and Data Science of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS-FEDS), Key Laboratory of Regional Sustainable Development Modeling at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS-RSDM), and carbon emission accounts and datasets (CEADs). The IRIO tables obtained from these sources comprise 42 sectors in 31 provinces and are compatible with our IRIO tables. We used the mean absolute deviation (MAD), Isard-Romanoff similarity index (DSIM), and absolute entropy distance (AED) indicators, as recommended by Zheng et al.3, to assess the similarity between the intermediate demand matrices. The results of these comparisons are shown in Table S3.

In 2012, the IRIO constructed in the present study showed higher similarity to that of CEADs when compared with CAS-FEDS and CAS-RSDM, with a marginally smaller mean absolute distance (MAD). The average MAD between our IRIO and CEADs is 0.24, while 0.40 for CAS-FEDS and 0.26 for CAS-RSDM. The MAD measure, weighted according to the distance, can be more sensitive to larger and relatively richer provinces. Developed provinces such as Shanghai, Jiangsu, Beijing, Zhejiang, Hebei, and Shandong, indicated lower similarity with our IRIO, as shown by larger MAD indicators. Conversely, less developed provinces like Qinghai and Xinjiang displayed significantly smaller MAD indicators, indicating higher similarity. However, the results shown in Table 5 indicate that the DSIM, employed to evaluate relative distance, is more similar to CAS-FEDS, with an average DSIM of 0.56 (compared to 0.62 for CEADs and 0.61 for CAS-RSDM). As for AED, it displays higher similarity to CEADs.
