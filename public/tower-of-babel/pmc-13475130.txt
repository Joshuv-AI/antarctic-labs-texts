Prevalence and factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Charity Lebogang Manganyi, Magdeline Rantho, Mpsanyana Makgahlela, Mahlatse Rapau, Tholene Sodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSuicide is a major public health concern and one of the leading causes of death among young people globally. Unemployment has been associated with an increased risk of suicide and suicidal behaviour through its effects on mental health, financial security and social well-being. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), high levels of youth unemployment, poverty and limited access to mental healthcare may further increase vulnerability to suicidal behaviour. This systematic review aims to synthesise evidence on the prevalence, associated factors and methods of suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA.Methods and analysisThe review will be conducted and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. Eligible studies will focus on unemployed youth aged 15–35 years residing in SSA countries and report on suicide, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts or completed suicide. Electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, EBSCOhost, Scopus, ProQuest and Sabinet African Journals, will be searched using a comprehensive search strategy supplemented by backward and forward citation searching. Three reviewers will independently screen studies, extract data and assess methodological quality using appropriate critical appraisal tools. Prevalence estimates will be pooled using random-effects meta-analysis where appropriate. Associated factors will be synthesised narratively and organised according to the socioecological framework.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as the review will use data from published studies and publicly available sources. Findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal, conference presentations and engagement with relevant stakeholders. The review is expected to provide evidence to inform suicide prevention strategies, mental health interventions and employment-related policies targeting unemployed youth in SSA.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42025642679.

Introduction

The issue of suicide among unemployed youth is often overlooked as a public health concern, despite its multifaceted nature. Globally, suicide is considered the fourth leading cause of death among youth, with over 700 000 deaths,1 and unemployment has been found to increase individual suicide risk by 20%–30%.2 Earlier, a longitudinal analysis conducted across 63 countries reported that unemployment was associated with a 20%–30% increase in suicide rates, with an estimated 45 000 suicide deaths attributable to unemployment worldwide.3 The burden of suicide is particularly concerning among youth, as this developmental period is often characterised by significant social, educational and economic transitions that may increase vulnerability to mental health challenges and suicidal behaviours. Although suicide is a global phenomenon, its determinants and burden vary across regions, highlighting the need for context-specific evidence. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), youth unemployment remains a significant socioeconomic challenge,4 exposing many young people to financial hardship, uncertainty and reduced opportunities for social and economic advancement.5 The financial strain associated with unemployment may contribute to feelings of loneliness, social isolation and psychological distress, thereby increasing the risk of suicidal behaviour.6 7 A multicountry analysis of WHO data examining prevalence and determinants of suicidal behaviour across 12 African countries determined that young, unemployed individuals, particularly females, exhibited significantly higher adjusted odds for suicidal thoughts and attempts8; on the other hand, a qualitative study focused directly on the mental health effects of unemployment among youth in South Africa identified a direct link between prolonged joblessness, feelings of worthlessness, substance abuse and suicidal ideation.9 Another study that looked at poverty and suicide among young people in South Africa found a 6.13% past-month prevalence of non-fatal suicidal behaviour among youth. While prolonged unemployment was a background factor, severe immediate economic stressors (such as food insecurity and previously being fired) were significantly associated with increased risk.10

Many countries in SSA continue to experience persistently high levels of youth unemployment, underemployment and informal employment. As a result, many young people remain unemployed for prolonged periods, even after completing secondary or tertiary education.11 This situation may differ from that of many high-income countries, where stronger labour markets, unemployment benefits and employment support programmes provide greater economic and social protection for unemployed youth. Furthermore, many unemployed youth in SSA live in low-income households and resource-constrained communities, where access to basic necessities such as food, transportation, housing and healthcare may be limited.10 12 The financial strain caused by unemployment may worsen stress and emotional distress, making individuals more vulnerable to common mental health disorders.13 14 Consequently, the socioeconomic conditions experienced by many unemployed individuals in SSA can have serious implications for their mental health and well-being.

Despite growing evidence linking unemployment and suicidal behaviours among young people, there remains a limited synthesis of evidence focusing specifically on unemployed youth in SSA. While extensive reviews have explored suicide among youth,15–17 adolescents, children18 19 and adults,20 21 some studies have targeted specific countries within SSA without addressing the region as a whole.16 19 22 23 As a result, the prevalence and associated factors of suicide and suicidal behaviour among unemployed youth in SSA countries remain poorly understood. Those that have been done have looked at insecure employment and were conducted in high-income countries.24 More comprehensive reviews are still needed to systematically analyse the prevalence and the nature of these factors. Given the high levels of youth unemployment, poverty and limited mental health resources in the region, unemployed youth may face a unique set of risk factors and protective factors that differ from those reported in other settings. Therefore, this review aims to synthesise available evidence on the prevalence of suicide and suicidal behaviours, the associated factors and the methods of suicide reported among unemployed youth in SSA. The findings may help inform the development of evidence-based interventions, policies and suicide prevention strategies tailored to this vulnerable population.

Theoretical framework

To capture the multilayered factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviour, this systematic review will be guided by the Socio-Ecological Framework originally proposed by Bronfenbrenner in the 1970s25 to explain how human development is shaped by interactions between individuals and their surrounding social environments.26 The framework has been widely applied in public health research to understand health behaviours, identify determinants of health outcomes and inform prevention and intervention strategies.27–29 It posits that behaviours and health outcomes are influenced by multiple interacting factors operating at different levels, including the individual, interpersonal, community and societal levels.30 The socioecological framework is particularly relevant to the study of suicide and suicidal behaviours because these outcomes are influenced by a complex interplay of psychological, social, environmental and structural factors.28 31 Consistent with recommendations from the WHO and previous suicide research, the framework provides a comprehensive lens through which the multifaceted nature of suicidal behaviour can be examined.28 Previous studies have successfully applied the framework to understand risk and protective factors associated with suicide among adolescents and young people across diverse settings.28 31–33 In the context of unemployment, the framework highlights how suicidal behaviour may arise from interacting influences across multiple levels.7 28 33 34

At the individual level, unemployment may contribute to psychological distress, depression, anxiety, hopelessness and substance use.35 The financial strain associated with unemployment often serves as a major stressor that can prompt individuals to self-isolate and experience heightened feelings of loneliness, potentially leading to suicide or suicidal behaviour.36 Research has shown that unemployment can exacerbate mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety.37 These conditions, particularly depression, are significant risk factors for suicidal behaviour.13 The unemployed youth may experience a range of psychological and behavioural challenges that increase their vulnerability to suicidal behaviour. Prolonged unemployment can have significant psychological effects, leading to feelings of hopelessness, loss of purpose, low self-worth and poor mental well-being, all of which have been associated with an increased risk of suicidal behaviour.2 38 In some instances, educational disruption and school dropout resulting from poverty or other socioeconomic challenges39 40 may further limit employment opportunities and increase vulnerability to substance use.41 42 Studies have shown that substance misuse is associated with an elevated risk of suicide and suicidal behaviours among young people.43 When youth drop out of school and have limited chances of securing stable employment, they often find themselves with excessive free time, which can lead to substance abuse and, in some cases, addiction.43 44 This situation may drive them to engage in criminal activities to support their addiction,45 exposing them to dangerous lifestyles that may make suicide seem like the only escape.46

In addition to individual factors, family and social circumstances may further influence suicidal behaviour among unemployed youth at the interpersonal level. In many SSA settings, many youths are often expected to contribute financially to their households and support family members.5 However, unemployment may limit their ability to fulfil these expectations, resulting in feelings of inadequacy, guilt, perceived failure and shame.9 38 As a result, these financial hardships may strain family relationships, increase social isolation and reduce social support.38 Some youth may also reside in economically disadvantaged households, including child-headed households, where they assume significant caregiving and financial responsibilities at an early age.47 48 Such pressures, coupled with limited social support, may increase vulnerability to suicide and suicidal behaviours.35

Community and societal factors may also contribute to the risk of suicide among unemployed youth in SSA. Many young people reside in communities characterised by poverty, limited access to mental health services, and inadequate opportunities for social and economic participation.31 49 These challenges may be particularly pronounced in rural and underserved settings, where access to mental healthcare and psychosocial support services is often limited.15 Moreover, in some settings in SSA, mental illness remains highly stigmatised, making help-seeking behaviour discouraged.50 51 Some individuals may fear being discriminated against or social rejection. Many youths may end up suffering in silence rather than seeking help, which leads them to engage in suicide and suicidal behaviour.52 To make matters worse, many countries in this region have a shortage of psychologists, psychiatrists and mental health facilities, which may make it challenging for unemployed youth to seek immediate psychological help.53 54 Many of these services are often concentrated in urban areas. This can cause a lot of psychological distress to go unrecognised, untreated and unsupported,55 leading to suicide or suicidal behaviour among the unemployed youth. Furthermore, some communities provide easier access to potentially lethal means of self-harm, including agricultural pesticides, which have been identified as an important risk factor for suicide in low- and middle-income countries.56 57 Persistent poverty, social exclusion and structural unemployment may therefore compound existing vulnerabilities and increase the likelihood of suicidal behaviour among unemployed youth.

Given the high burden of youth unemployment and socioeconomic disadvantage in many SSA countries,58 the socioecological framework provides an appropriate theoretical foundation for this review. It enables a comprehensive examination of factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth by recognising that these behaviours are shaped by influences operating across multiple levels of the social environment. The framework will guide the identification, organisation, synthesis and interpretation of associated factors reported in the included studies, thereby facilitating a more holistic understanding of suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA.

Methods

This review protocol is prepared according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement59 (see online supplemental material 1). We will include studies that have presented the prevalence and associated factors of suicide and suicidal behaviour among unemployed youth in SSA. We have registered the protocol on the PROSPERO database with the registration number CRD42025642679. The completed review will adhere to the PRISMA-2020 guidelines.59 The review is planned to be conducted between July 2026 and June 2027.

Participants

The participants in this review will be unemployed youth living in SSA. Consistent with the African Youth Charter, youth will be defined as individuals aged 15–35 years.60 Studies will be eligible if the majority of participants fall within this age range or if results for participants aged 15–35 years are reported separately. Studies including broader age ranges will only be included where data specific to the target age group can be extracted.

Setting

We will include studies conducted in countries located within SSA among unemployed youth. Those conducted outside of SSA will be excluded.

Study design

This review will include quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies to ensure a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence. Quantitative studies will provide data on the prevalence and factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours, while qualitative studies will contribute contextual and experiential insights. Mixed-methods studies will help integrate both types of findings. Including all three designs supports a holistic understanding of suicidal behaviour, associated factors and methods used to commit suicide among unemployed youth. Studies without full-text availability will be excluded due to the inability to extract essential methodological and outcome data or to perform a risk of bias assessment. Peer-reviewed journal articles, dissertations and theses with full-text availability will be eligible for inclusion. Dissertations and theses will be considered because they often contain detailed methodological information and may provide valuable evidence that is not published in peer-reviewed journals, particularly within the SSA context. Conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, opinion pieces, correspondence articles, blogs and other forms of grey literature will be excluded because they typically provide insufficient methodological detail to permit comprehensive data extraction and quality appraisal. Review articles and study protocols will also be excluded.

The review will include peer-reviewed quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods studies. To effectively include qualitative and mixed-methods studies, we have decided to use the SPIDER tool (see table 2), as it is particularly well-suited for these types of research.61 While we acknowledge that our study has a quantitative component, the inclusion of qualitative data and mixed methods validates the appropriateness of using the SPIDER tool.62

Evaluation/outcome of interest

Type of suicidal behaviour

Suicidal behaviour encompasses a spectrum of thoughts and actions related to intentionally ending one’s own life. This behaviour can manifest in different stages, from thinking about suicide to attempting or completing the act. It is often associated with common mental health disorders such as depression,63 post-traumatic stress disorder, substance abuse or trauma,64 65 among others. Suicidal behaviour can affect individuals from any background and age group, though it is more prevalent among youth.15 There are different types of suicidal behaviour, such as parasuicide, suicide planning, ideation, attempted suicide, non-suicidal self-injury and completed suicide. For this review, we will include suicidal ideation, which involves having thoughts about killing oneself,66 suicide attempt, which involves a non-fatal act where an individual tries to end their life but survives,15 and completed suicide, which is a self-inflicted act that results in death.67

Search strategy

The search strategy will include Boolean operators, keywords, truncation and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms appropriate and relevant to each selected database to ensure a comprehensive collection of published articles. We will adjust our search strategy as needed for each specific database, including Web of Science, Scopus, ProQuest, PubMed, EBSCO and regional databases such as Sabinet African Journals. For example, our search in the PubMed database will be guided and refined with input from an information scientist who is an institutional librarian at the University of Limpopo, South Africa (see online supplemental material 2). The search will include the following combinations of terms: (Prevalence OR incidence OR Epidemiology OR Common) AND (associated factors OR correlates OR risk factors OR determinants OR contributing factors OR protective factors) AND (Suicid* OR “Suicidal behaviour” OR “Suicide plan” OR “Suicidal ideation” OR “Suicide attempt” OR completed suicide OR “fatal-suicide” OR “non-fatal suicide” OR “Suicide risk”) AND (unemploy* OR unemployment OR unemployed OR jobless) AND (youth OR “young adults” OR young OR “young people” OR “emerging adult”). The geographic location filter will include all 49 countries within SSA. We undertook a preliminary search of peer-reviewed literature on 22 June 2026 for the PubMed database (see online supplemental appendix B) and other databases on 24 June 2026 (see online supplemental appendix C) to assess the scope of the review and to support the proposed review and planned synthesis. The search yielded 3936 potentially relevant records.

The preliminary search will further be refined in consultation with a librarian, and the final search strategy will be evaluated using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategy checklist, and the results will be presented in the final review. In addition, we will perform a backward search on Google Scholar by reviewing the reference list of the included studies to identify more studies that meet our inclusion criteria. We will also conduct a forward search by clicking on the “cited by” icon on Google Scholar. The authors of the studies considered eligible may be contacted to make data clarifications/requests or access any full-text articles that are not accessible. The review will include studies published between 2000 and 2026. This time frame was selected to capture contemporary evidence on youth unemployment and suicidal behaviour within SSA, reflecting major socioeconomic, labour market and public health changes that have occurred over the past two decades. Restricting the review to studies published from 2000 onwards is expected to improve the relevance of the findings to current policy and intervention efforts. Only studies published in English will be included. This decision was made due to resource and translation constraints within the review team. We acknowledge that excluding studies published in other languages may introduce language bias and may result in the omission of relevant evidence from some sub-Saharan African countries. This limitation will be considered when interpreting the findings of the review.

Study selection

The search results will be imported into ZOTERO (V.6.0.36), a reference management software. The software will be used to remove duplicates of the records and to access the full-text records for screening. Then, one reviewer (CLM) will create individual groups for each database used and a main group to store all references. This reviewer will invite two reviewers (MR and MM) with editing rights. Three reviewers (CLM, MM and MR) will independently screen all titles and abstracts identified by the search for eligibility using the include, exclude and maybe tags. After the title and abstract screening, the three reviewers (CLM, MM and MR) will have a meeting to compare their studies and identify disputes that need to be resolved. All the studies that were identified will be manually transferred to the resolve conflict tag for a fourth reviewer (TS) to mediate and make the final decision to include or exclude the studies. The screening will be based on the inclusion criteria, and studies that include both employed and unemployed youth will be included only if disaggregated data on the unemployed population are available. For full-text screening, the reviewers will follow the same tagging process, wherein three reviewers (MM, MR and CLM) will perform full-text screening of studies that passed the initial phase. In this case, one reviewer (TS) will act as the mediator to resolve conflicts. The PRISMA flow chart showing the number of studies remaining at each stage will be presented.61

Data extraction

Data extraction will be performed independently by three reviewers (CLM, MR and MM) using a standardised Microsoft Excel data extraction form to be developed and pilot-tested by the review team. The primary investigator will contact the primary authors of articles with missing information to provide the missing or additional data.18 Data to be extracted from reviewed articles will include author, year of publication, study title, country, study design, setting, sample size, participants’ characteristics (age and sex), prevalence of suicide and suicidal behaviour, method used for suicide, type of suicidal behaviour (suicidal ideation, suicide attempt or completed suicide), sampling technique, method of data analysis and associated factors. Associated factors reported in the included studies will be extracted and classified according to the individual, interpersonal, community and societal domains of the Socio-Ecological Framework. A log of excluded studies and reasons for exclusion will be maintained and reported in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Quality assessment

All included studies will be critically appraised using the Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool to appraise the methodological quality of quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies.68 RoB 2 tool will be used to assess the risk of bias in randomised trials.69 The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, consisting of eight items,70 will be used by two reviewers (CLM and MM) to assess the risk of bias for cohort studies with the assistance of an expert when needed (TS). Lastly, we will use AMSTAR 271 guidelines, comprised of 16 items, to assess the methodological quality of the review.

Data synthesis and integration of findings

This review will include and synthesise evidence from quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies to provide a comprehensive understanding of the prevalence and associated factors of suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA. A convergent integrated approach will be used to combine findings from the different types of studies. Quantitative and qualitative data will be extracted, analysed, and synthesised separately, and then integrated during the interpretation phase of the review.

Quantitative synthesis

Meta-analysis

We will quantitatively synthesise the prevalence estimates and associated risk factors to estimate pooled prevalence rates and identify significant correlates that increase or decrease the risk of suicidal behaviour in this population group. To do so, we will conduct statistical tests for heterogeneity and publication bias in the included studies. Studies will be eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis if they provide quantitative data on the prevalence, report data from SSA countries, use clear definitions or standardised measures of suicidal behaviour, and include sufficient statistical data to allow calculation of effect size. The meta-analysis will be conducted using JAMOVI V.2.3.28 software. For prevalence data, proportions will be pooled using a random-effects model18 to account for between-study heterogeneity. For associated factors, effect estimates will be extracted and examined for comparability. Where two or more studies report the same factor using comparable measures and statistical estimates, pooled effect sizes will be calculated using a random-effects model. This will only be done if there is a need to proceed to meta-analysis. Where quantitative pooling is not feasible due to methodological or clinical heterogeneity, findings will be synthesised narratively and organised according to the Socio-Ecological Framework. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using I2 statistics, with thresholds interpreted as 0% to 25% as low heterogeneity, 26% to 75% as moderate heterogeneity, and 75% as substantial heterogeneity.18 In a case of high heterogeneity (I²> 75%), sources of variation will be explored via subgroup analysis and meta-regression where sufficient data are available. We will use the I2 statistics to report the percentage of variation across studies that is attributed to heterogeneity.22 Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be conducted based on gender, geographical region or country within SSA and type of suicidal behaviour (suicidal ideation, suicide attempt and completed suicide) to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted, where feasible, by excluding studies assessed as having a high risk of bias and through leave-one-out analyses to determine the robustness of pooled estimates. Funnel plots will be generated to visually inspect for publication bias. Results will be presented using forest plots showing individual study estimates and the overall pooled effect. Tables will be used to summarise characteristics and quality ratings of included studies.

Qualitative synthesis

Qualitative findings will be synthesised using thematic synthesis. Emerging themes related to factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth will be identified, coded and organised according to the Socio-Ecological Framework. Specifically, themes will be categorised into individual, interpersonal, community and societal levels to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the multilevel influences associated with suicidal behaviours in this population.

Mixed-method studies

For mixed methods studies, quantitative and qualitative components will be disaggregated and analysed within their respective categories. The integration will consider both components to avoid duplication and ensure balanced interpretation.

Integration of findings

After independent analysis, a convergent synthesis approach will be used to integrate findings from quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies. Integration will be achieved through a joint display matrix tool (see online supplemental material 3), which is commonly used in mixed-methods research and systematic reviews to visually and thematically integrate findings from quantitative and qualitative studies.72 The matrix will allow researchers to align, compare and contrast findings from different sources side by side in a table format. To facilitate interpretation, associated factors identified across studies will be mapped and organised according to the Socio-Ecological Framework at the individual, interpersonal, community and societal levels. This approach will enable a comprehensive understanding of the multilevel factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this systematic review protocol. This review will synthesise evidence from previously published studies and will not involve the collection of primary data from patients or the public.

Discussion

The proposed systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of existing evidence on suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA. This review is expected to generate important insights into the burden of suicidal behaviours within a population that may experience unique socioeconomic, structural and mental health challenges.36 Specifically, the review seeks to determine the prevalence of suicide and suicidal behaviours and identify the factors associated with these outcomes among unemployed youth in SSA. Given the persistently high levels of youth unemployment across many SSA countries58 and the well-established association between unemployment and poor mental health outcomes,9 38 it is anticipated that the prevalence of suicidal behaviours may be substantial within this population group. Existing evidence suggests that unemployment may expose young people to a range of interacting risk factors operating at individual, interpersonal, community and societal levels,24 including psychological distress, substance use, family and financial pressures, social exclusion, poverty and limited access to mental healthcare services.11 35 47 These challenges may be particularly pronounced in many SSA settings, where high unemployment rates, widespread poverty, inadequate social protection systems and limited employment opportunities may further exacerbate vulnerability to suicidal behaviours. Despite the potential significance of these factors, evidence from SSA remains fragmented73 and has not been comprehensively synthesised. By systematically collating evidence from diverse settings across the region, this review will provide a clearer understanding of the prevalence, associated factors and methods of suicide among unemployed youth in SSA, thereby informing future research, policy development and targeted suicide prevention interventions.

Second, this review seeks to identify the factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA. Guided by the Socio-Ecological Framework, we anticipate that the review will identify a complex interplay of factors operating at individual, interpersonal, community and societal levels.28 These may include psychological factors such as depression, anxiety, hopelessness and substance use24; interpersonal factors such as family responsibilities and limited social support; and broader structural influences, including poverty, financial insecurity, social exclusion, limited access to mental healthcare and persistent unemployment. While some of these factors have been reported globally,24 49 74 their influence may be amplified within SSA due to widespread socioeconomic inequalities, limited social protection systems and constrained mental health resources.12 While factors such as depression, anxiety and substance use may be associated with suicidal behaviour globally,31 unemployed youth in SSA may also be affected by contextual factors such as poverty, structural unemployment, family economic obligations, limited mental health services and social exclusion. These factors may differ in both nature and magnitude from those observed in higher-income settings. Consequently, the review will provide a more nuanced understanding of the context-specific determinants of suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in the region. Such evidence may inform the development of targeted suicide prevention strategies, mental health interventions and employment-support policies, while also identifying gaps that require further research.

Importantly, the findings of this review will be interpreted through a socioecological lens, recognising that suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth are shaped by the interaction of multiple influences operating across different levels of the social environment.74 In the context of SSA, where youth unemployment often coexists with poverty, limited access to mental healthcare, social inequality and constrained employment opportunities, understanding suicidal behaviour requires consideration of both individual vulnerabilities and broader structural determinants.10 57 75 76 By synthesising evidence across these interconnected levels, this review will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviours among unemployed youth in SSA. Such evidence may support the development of multilevel and context-specific interventions that address not only individual mental health needs but also the social and economic conditions that may contribute to suicide risk in this population. Furthermore, the findings may contribute to the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being)77 through efforts to reduce suicide mortality and improve mental health outcomes, and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth) by highlighting the mental health implications of youth unemployment and the importance of promoting meaningful employment opportunities for young people.

Anticipated challenges and limitations

This systematic review is expected to face several challenges and limitations. First, there may be a limited number of studies specifically focusing on unemployed youth in SSA, potentially affecting the completeness and representativeness of the review. One significant challenge is the expected heterogeneity across studies. This is because the included studies may vary in study design, population characteristics, definition of suicidal behaviour, measuring tools, reporting and outcome measures. This heterogeneity may limit comparability and hinder meta-analysis. Language bias is also a concern, as only studies published in English will be included, possibly excluding relevant data published in other regional languages.

As documented by,22 we also anticipate encountering challenges related to methodologies and the use of varying terminology that convey similar meanings, as SSA countries have diverse cultural backgrounds and norms. Suicide is a sensitive issue; we anticipate that we may find challenges in finding more empirical studies being done on suicide among unemployed youth, especially keeping in mind that some of the countries within the SSA still consider suicide a punishable criminal offence.25 26 Additionally, the sensitive nature of suicide-related topics may lead to under-reporting or social desirability bias in primary data sources, thus underestimating the true prevalence of suicidal behaviours.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval is not required since the study will not involve gathering data directly from unemployed youth or any individual, nor will it violate their privacy. The review will exclusively use data from previously published studies. The findings will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and presented at relevant conferences in the field. Plain language summaries of the key findings will be prepared and disseminated to existing peer and social networks. The researchers will only contact authors for clarification purposes or missing data of a study that they wish to include in the review. The review is expected to commence in July 2026 and conclude in June 2027.
