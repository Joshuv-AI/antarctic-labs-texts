Management Strategies and Outcomes in HR+/HER2− Metastatic Breast Cancer Receiving CDK4/6 Inhibitors and Subsequent Therapies
Authors: Katarzyna Pogoda, Hubert Pawlik, Anna Balata, Magdalena Czopowicz, Agata Bak, Iwona Twardowska, Malgorzata Meluch, Maria Wojda, Agnieszka Mlodzinska, Ewa Szombara, Renata Sienkiewicz, Aleksandra Konieczna, Elzbieta Brewczynska, Izabela Lemanska, Anna Majstrak-Hulewska, Anna Gorniak, Anna Niwinska, Zbigniew Nowecki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the real-world outcomes and treatment patterns of cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors in combination with endocrine therapy (ET) for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−) advanced breast cancer (ABC), as well as the use and effectiveness of later-line therapies, in a single tertiary center in Poland.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study included 661 patients who initiated CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy between September 2019 and December 2023. Data were extracted from medical records to assess progression-free survival (PFS), chemotherapy-free survival, and treatment trajectories. Statistical analyses included Kaplan–Meier estimates and Log rank tests for survival outcomes.ResultsThe majority of patients (80%) received CDK4/6 inhibitors as first-line therapy, predominantly combined with aromatase inhibitors. Median PFS was 25.8 months overall, with superior outcomes observed in the first-line setting (29.0 months vs 13.8 months in second-line; p < 0.0001). AI-based combinations outperformed fulvestrant in the first line (38.9 vs 16.8 months; p < 0.0001). Chemotherapy-free survival of patients treated in the first line with CDK4/6 inhibitors reached 39.1 months. After progression on first-line CDK4/6 inhibitors, 71% received second-line treatment (49% chemotherapy, 35% ET); median PFS was 4.2 vs 5.3 months, respectively. Only 31% received third-line treatment (median PFS: 3.1 vs 2.6 months).ConclusionCDK4/6 inhibitors continue to provide substantial clinical benefit in routine practice, with evolving treatment strategies reflecting growing emphasis on individualized, less toxic approaches. A notable shift from chemotherapy towards targeted and endocrine therapies in later lines underscores the changing landscape of ABC management. However, the outcomes in the later lines of therapy are still unsatisfactory. Enhancing biomarker testing is critical for advancing precision oncology in this setting.Keywords: CDK4/6 inhibitors, advanced breast cancer, endocrine therapy, real-world data, treatment attrition, treatment patterns

Introduction

Historically, endocrine monotherapy with an aromatase inhibitor (AI) or fulvestrant in the first-line setting achieved median progression-free survival (PFS) of approximately 13–16 months.1 The outcome of patients with hormone receptor-positive (HR+) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2-) metastatic breast cancer (MBC) has dramatically improved with the introduction of oral cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 (CDK4/6)-targeted therapies. These molecules block the cell cycle of tumor cells. Three CDK4/6 inhibitors, palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib, are approved for the treatment of advanced HR+ HER2- breast cancer (BC) and have been used as first-line treatment. For several years, CDK4/6 inhibitors, in combination with endocrine therapy (ET), have become part of the standard of care for the treatment of patients with HR+ HER2- advanced breast cancer (ABC).

Guidelines recommend all three CDK4/6 inhibitors as treatment options in combination with ET;2–4 while some guidelines designate ribociclib as a category 1 preferred regimen in the first-line setting.2 Each CDK4/6 inhibitor has demonstrated a substantial improvement in first-line progression-free survival (PFS) compared with ET alone, with median PFS ranging from approximately 23 to 28 months versus 13 to 16 months across MONALEESA-2, MONALEESA-7, PALOMA-2, and MONARCH-3 trials. Notably, ribociclib and abemaciclib have also achieved the secondary endpoint of overall survival (OS) in clinical trials.5–8 Variations in patient populations and drug characteristics likely contribute to the observed differences in outcomes across the referenced studies. AIs have served as the backbone of first-line ET. In current clinical practice, and consistent with international guidelines, AI combined with a CDK4/6 inhibitor constitutes the recommended standard first-line regimen for most patients with HR+/HER2- metastatic breast cancer.3,4,9 Fulvestrant, a selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD), functions by promoting estrogen receptor degradation and inhibiting estrogen-mediated signaling pathways.10 For patients whose cancer progresses during or shortly after adjuvant AI therapy, those exhibiting resistance to AI, or those with identified ESR1 mutations, fulvestrant in combination with CDK4/6 inhibitors has shown therapeutic benefit. While the combination of CDK4/6 inhibitors with ET has markedly improved first-line outcomes in patients with HR+/HER2- ABC, optimal therapeutic strategies following disease progression remain a significant clinical challenge. Subsequent lines of treatment are typically associated with diminished efficacy, underscoring the need for more effective, individualized approaches.11

Polish patients living with ABC got access to CDK 4/6 inhibitors-based therapies in September 2019 on a basis aligned with local and international guidelines.2,4 Our study aimed to analyze patient characteristics, treatment patterns, and real-world use of CDK4/6 inhibitors and ET in a tertiary healthcare center in Poland. In addition to evaluating outcomes in the first-line setting, our study provides a comprehensive analysis of treatment patterns and clinical results in later lines of therapy. This includes the assessment of treatment patterns, the incorporation of emerging targeted therapies, and real-world clinical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This study is a single-center, retrospective review of medical records, focused on patients with MBC who received CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapies as part of a routine clinical practice.

Aims

This study aimed to characterize patients and assess the treatment outcomes of CDK4/6 inhibitors - palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib - administered in combination with ET as part of routine clinical management for ABC, as well as to describe the subsequent treatment pathways and outcomes following first-line therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitors.

Patients

All constitutive patients who started treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitors between September 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, were enrolled. A follow-up was conducted to determine patient status as of June 2024. There were no inclusion or exclusion criteria; however, the treatment financed by the state in the drug access program required that the patients have locally advanced or metastatic HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer and an ECOG performance status from 0 to 2, without symptomatic brain metastases and any clinically significant uncontrolled comorbidities that would contraindicate the treatment. CDK4/6 inhibitors were used in patients with unresectable locally ABC, when radical local treatment was unlikely to become feasible despite systemic or radiation therapy. The use of three CDK4/6 inhibitors with AI or fulvestrant depended on the type of earlier treatment and time from (neo-)adjuvant therapy to relapse. Re-treatment with AI was possible after 12 months following adjuvant therapy; such restrictions did not apply to fulvestrant therapy.

Treatment Response Evaluation

The response of the patients to treatment was monitored using computed tomography (CT) according to the physician’s choice. Imaging was performed at a minimum every 3 months, and response evaluation was performed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1; 262 patients experienced disease progression during this time.12

Treatment Paths

Patients were receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-containing therapy as first-line systemic therapy for advanced disease (de novo stage IV cases or recurrences on or after adjuvant ET) or as second-line therapy after progression on prior ET for advanced disease. Hormone sensitivity was assessed using standard definitions, with primary endocrine resistance defined as relapse during the first 24 months of adjuvant ET or progression within 6 months of ET in the metastatic setting, and secondary endocrine resistance defined as relapse ≥24 months after adjuvant therapy or progression ≥6 months after initiating ET for metastatic disease.9 The treatment trajectories of patients who initiated therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitors in the first-line setting were analyzed to determine the proportion of patients who continued the same treatment, transitioned to subsequent lines of therapy, or discontinued treatment entirely. Types of treatments used from the second to fourth lines of therapy were analyzed along with PFS.

Data Collection

Patient records were accessed through the electronic medical record system of the Department of Breast Cancer and Reconstructive Surgery of the National Oncology Institute in Warsaw. Researchers manually reviewed baselines and follow-up notes. A database was developed based on medical records.

The database captured key clinical parameters, including patient demographics and clinical characteristics at treatment initiation, the type of treatments administered, line of therapy, therapy start and end dates, reasons for therapy discontinuation, and treatment outcome.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were provided for all variables of interest. For numeric variables, sample size, median, minimum, and maximum were given. For categorical variables, sample size, number, and percent were provided for each category.

Differences between groups were compared by Pearson’s chi‐squared test or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate, for categorical variables, and by Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney test for continuous variables.

PFS was defined as the time from the beginning of treatment until clinical relapse or death from any cause. Chemotherapy-free survival (CFS) was defined as the time from the beginning of the first-line treatment until the start of chemotherapy or death from any cause. PFS and CFS were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with medians and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The intervention effect was compared using a Log rank test and a stratified Log rank test. P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 and R Studio version 2024.04.

Results

Patient Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

Overall, there were five men among 661 patients in the registry receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor in the first or second line of therapy. Baseline characteristics of included patients are shown in Table 1. Over half of the patients were aged 50–69 years old. There were differences in the age of patients treated with each CDK4/6 inhibitor; the median age of those treated with abemaciclib was 63 (95% CI, 61–65), 60 (95% CI, 57–63) for ribociclib, and 68 (95% CI, 66–69) for palbociclib; p < 0.01. Palbociclib was more frequently used in the group of patients ≥70 years old (43%) compared to ribociclib (21%) and abemaciclib (28%); p < 0.0001. The age of patients treated with palbociclib increased in subsequent years of the analyzed period, from 63 years in 2020 to 75 years in 2023 (p=0.0052). In contrast, the age of patients receiving the other CDK4/6 inhibitors remained stable over time (ribociclib p=0.194, abemaciclib p=0.347).

Patient Characteristics of the Overall CDK4/6 Inhibitor-Treated Cohort, Stratified by First- and second-Line Therapy

Abbreviation: CDK, cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6.

The majority received CDK4/6-based therapy in the first line of treatment. Two-thirds of patients combined CDK4/6 inhibitors with AI (Table 1). There were no significant changes in CDK4/6 inhibitor use according to treatment line (first-line use of abemaciclib - 147/183 [80%]; palbociclib - 180/231 [78%], and ribociclib - 204/247 [83%], p = 0.4389). Ribociclib was more often combined with AI (183/247, 74%) than abemaciclib (117/183, 64%) and palbociclib (140/231, 61%), p = 0.0052. Overall, AI-containing combinations were commonly used in the first-line setting (384/531, 72.3%). In 2019, 69% of patients received AI, whereas in 2023, 73%. In the second line, 74/130 patients (56.9%) received fulvestrant.

Among patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors in the first-line setting, 73 (13.7%) had primary endocrine resistance, whereas 458 (86.3%) had secondary endocrine resistance. A similar distribution was observed in the second-line setting, where 17 (13.1%) patients had primary and 113 (86.9%) had secondary endocrine resistance. The detailed characteristics of these subgroups are presentedin Supplementary Table 1.

Progression-Free Survival with CDK4/6 Inhibitor Therapy

With a data cut-off date of June 15, 2024, the median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 21.0 months. Two hundred sixty-two patients experienced disease progression. For all analyzed patients, independently of the type of CDK4/6 inhibitor, ET, and the line of treatment, the median PFS was 25.8 months (95% CI 22.8–33.4).

Treatment outcomes were evaluated separately by treatment line and by the ET combined with the CDK4/6 inhibitor. The median PFS in the first-line setting was over twice as long as in the second-line: 29.0 months (95% CI 22.5–38.9) vs 13.8 months (95% CI 10.4–19.0), p < 0.0001, independently form used ET (Figure 1A). Patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitor and AI in the first line had longer median PFS (38.9 months [95% CI 30.6 – not reached (NR)]) than patients receiving fulvestrant-containing regimens (16.8 months [95% CI 13.4–22.1]); p < 0.0001 (Figure 1B). PFS according to endocrine sensitivity was additionally assessed among all patients in whom first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy was initiated, irrespective of the endocrine partner. Median PFS of 14.2 months (95% CI, 6.8–37.0) was observed for patients with primary endocrine resistance, whereas a median PFS of 37.2 months (95% CI, 27.6–46.8) was observed for those with secondary endocrine resistance; p < 0.0001 (Figure 1C).

Progression-free survival (A) in patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-based treatment in the first and second line of therapy, (B) in patients receiving CDK4/6 inhibitor-based combinations with aromatase inhibitors or fulvestrant in the first line of therapy, (C) in patients receiving first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor with endocrine therapy, stratified according to primary or secondary endocrine resistance, (D) in patients receiving CDK 4/6 inhibitor-based combinations with aromatase inhibitors or fulvestrant in the second line of therapy.

In the second line, there was no statistically significant difference in median PFS between CDK4/6 inhibitor combined with AI and fulvestrant (13.2 months [95% CI 9.5–18.2] vs 15.2 months [95% CI 9.7–35.5]); p = 0.49 (Figure 1D). AI-containing regimens demonstrated PFS advantage in the first line in comparison with the second line (p < 0.0001), whereas CDK4/6 inhibitor–fulvestrant combinations had similar efficacy regardless of the line of treatment (p = 0.98).

Overall, in patients with visceral metastases, the median PFS was 22.1 months (95% CI 17.2–26.7) and 37.7 months (95% CI 7.2–16.7) in the group without visceral metastases; p = 0.0006. There was no statistically significant difference in median PFS between patients with metastases in bone only and in other locations (36.7 [95% CI 24.6–46.2] vs 25.5 [95% CI 21.9–33.4]), p = 0.32.

Time on treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitors was also assessed. The median duration of therapy was 23.1 months (95% CI, 19.7–26.2) in the first-line setting and 12.1 months (95% CI, 9.6–14.6) in the second-line setting. When analyzed by individual agents, median time on therapy in the first-line setting was 20.8 months (95% CI, 16.3-NR) for abemaciclib, 20.7 months (95% CI, 15.0–24.5) for palbociclib, and 26.6 months (95% CI, 21.3-NR) for ribociclib.

In the second-line setting, median durations were 12.7 months (95% CI, 9.5-NR) for abemaciclib, 9.7 months (95% CI, 7.0–14.6) for palbociclib, and 13.4 months (95% CI, 6.8-NR) for ribociclib.

Dose Reductions During CDK4/6 Inhibitor Therapy

Doses of CDK4/6 inhibitors were reduced in 269 patients (41%), without a significant difference between drugs; in 39% of patients treated with abemaciclib, 45% treated with palbociclib, and 38% treated with ribociclib.

Treatment Patterns After Progression on CDK4/6 Inhibitors

During the observation period, 365 patients (55%) discontinued CDK4/6 inhibitor-based treatment. The primary reason for discontinuation was disease progression, reported in 262 patients (71.8%) followed by death (50 patients, 13.7%) and toxicity (25 patients, 6.8%). Three patients (0.8%) stopped treatment based on their own decision or that of their physician. Other unspecified reasons accounted for 6.8% of discontinuations (25 patients).

An analysis was performed for the subgroup of patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors in the first line (n = 531), for whom comprehensive data on subsequent treatment lines and outcomes were available (n = 198). The treatment trajectories of patients who initiated first-line therapy with CDK4/6 inhibitors are illustrated in Figure 2A. In this cohort, 47.8% of patients remained on first-line therapy throughout the observation period, while 37.3% transitioned to second-line treatment. The distribution of second-line treatments was comparable between bone-only and visceral progression: ET-based therapy was used in 31.0% vs 29.7% and chemotherapy in 48.3% vs 49.5%, respectively, with the remaining patients receiving other therapies.

The median CFS for the overall cohort was 39.1 months (95% CI, 30.9–44.1). In exploratory analyses stratified by CDK4/6 inhibitor, the median CFS was 29.7 months (95% CI, 25.0–NR) for abemaciclib, 36.1 months (95% CI, 26.3–43.2) for palbociclib, and 60.7 months (95% CI, 35.8–NR) for ribociclib (p = 0.033). Among patients who received second-line therapy, most were treated with chemotherapy, most commonly doxorubicin or capecitabine, and ET, with fulvestrant administered in 81.1% of these cases (Figure 2B). Over time, there was a notable decline in the use of chemotherapy, from 72% in 2020 to 47% in 2024, accompanied by an increase in the use of ET (47% in 2024) and targeted therapies (6% in 2024).

PFS in the second-line setting was 5.3 months (95% CI, 4.5–9.3) for patients treated with ET, and 4.2 months (95% CI, 3.2–5.8) for those receiving chemotherapy (p = 0.01) (Figure 3). A subgroup of patients (n = 12) treated with targeted therapies, including alpelisib in combination with fulvestrant or exemestane, and talazoparib, achieved a median PFS of 9.7 months (95% CI, 7.1–not reached). Notably, in the group of patients with a change of BC subtype, ie, confirmed HER2 overexpression in biopsy performed at progression, the median PFS was 19.8 months (95% CI: 5,6–not reached). Overall, the change of BC subtype, gain of HER2 positivity, was confirmed in 9 patients (5, 3, and 1 treated in the second, third, and fourth lines, respectively). Ten patients participated in clinical trials after progression on the first-line treatment (Figure 2).

Progression-free survival in patients receiving chemotherapy and endocrine therapy in the second-line treatment.

In the third line, 33 patients were treated with chemotherapy and 6 with ET (Figure 2). The median PFS was 3.1 months (95% CI: 2,8–not reached) in the chemotherapy group and 2.6 months (95% CI: 0.7–not reached) in the ET group (p = 0.67) (Figure 4). Seventeen patients received fourth-line treatment, with chemotherapy used in most cases (Figure 2).

Biomarker Assessment: PIK3CA and BRCA Status

Genomic testing was infrequently performed: only 5.3% (28/531) of patients underwent somatic PIK3CA mutation testing, and 5.5% (29/531) underwent germline BRCA1/2 mutation analysis.

Discussion

This study assessed patient characteristics and the effectiveness of CDK4/6 inhibitors within the population of patients treated in the tertiary clinical center in Poland. Results showed that CDK4/6 inhibitors are effective for patients with HR+/ HER2- ABC and are largely consistent with results reported in clinical trials.

An AI-based endocrine backbone predominated, in line with local and European guidelines.2,4 Observed outcomes of the first-line treatment with AI-containing combinations exceeded those reported in randomized clinical trials,6,7,13 and for CDK4/6–fulvestrant combinations were similar to those reported in MONALEESA-3 and MONARCH-2 studies.8,14 The difference may come from a smaller proportion of patients with visceral metastases, which were present in half or more patients in clinical trials. However, even at a similar visceral metastatic burden in the Danish cohort of first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor recipients, a median PFS was 35.1 months,15 exceeding the outcomes reported in clinical trials. In the randomized trial, the palbociclib-fulvestrant combination had similar efficacy to palbociclib-letrozole in patients with endocrine-sensitive, HR+ HER2- breast cancer with a hazard ratio of 1.13, p = 0.32, consistently across stratification factors.16 In our study, AI-containing therapy was associated with a longer PFS in the first-line setting compared to the fulvestrant backbone. The magnitude of the benefit of 14 months between these two subgroups is difficult to explain. Treatment line-wise analysis revealed a lack of PFS benefit in subgroups of patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitor-fulvestrant in the first and second lines of therapy. This may be because, in the first-line setting, fulvestrant is typically used in patients who progress on adjuvant AI therapy, a population characterized by more aggressive disease with poorer prognosis.

In our study, patients receiving palbociclib were older than those treated with abemaciclib and ribociclib. Differences in the age of patients receiving different CDK4/6 inhibitors were noticed previously, with older patients treated with palbociclib and ribociclib than with abemaciclib.15 In a pooled analysis of Phase II/III randomized trials, the safety profile of CDK4/6 inhibitors was similar between younger and older patients, except for neutropenia, which was more frequent in patients older than 65. The discontinuation rates among elderly patients are higher than younger patients, with the lowest rates observed for palbociclib. Palbociclib and ribociclib maintained survival benefit in elderly patients.17 Despite being less fit and experiencing a significantly higher number of palbociclib dose reductions and delays, patients ≥70 years had superior OS than the younger group (HR 0.68, p = 0.02).18 Over the years, accumulated evidence supported using palbociclib in the elderly population. In the studied period, from 2019 to 2023, the median age of patients continuously increased, indicating physicians’ preference.17–20 Palbociclib was more frequently selected by clinicians for elderly or frail patients, likely due to its potential to reduce the likelihood of dose interruptions or reductions. Palbociclib provides sustained efficacy while demonstrating a favorable tolerability profile in elderly patients. In the context of balancing survival benefits with tolerability, particularly among older patients, palbociclib represents a rational and practical treatment choice.17

The observed treatment trajectories following first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy underscore the durability of initial treatment. Nearly half of the patients remained on first-line therapy during studied period. This finding aligns with prior reports supporting the long-term efficacy of CDK4/6 inhibition.21 However, the treatment for patients experiencing progression had limited efficacy once resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors develops. There is currently no single standard of care after CDK4/6 inhibitor progression, and multiple options are considered, often yielding only modest effectiveness.11 In our study, transition to second-line therapy occurred in 37.3% of patients, with chemotherapy and ET representing the predominant treatment modalities. Notably, 81.1% of those receiving ET were treated with fulvestrant, consistent with current guidelines recommending its use following progression on a prior endocrine agent. Previously reported median PFS after tumor progression on first-line CDK4/6-ET combination is a few months when chemotherapy or ET alone is used as a subsequent line.11 However, earlier reports indicated that second-line treatment with fulvestrant monotherapy did not yield a statistically significant improvement in PFS compared to chemotherapy (3.71 vs 3.25 months),22 which contrasts with our findings, where ET was slightly more effective than chemotherapy. Importantly, the observed difference, although statistically significant, was clinically marginal. Two antibody-drug conjugates, trastuzumab deruxtecan and sacituzumab govitecan, have demonstrated improved outcomes in heavily pretreated populations and are now emerging as important options in later lines of therapy for patients with endocrine-resistant disease.23–25 Changing clinical practice in the second-line setting is necessary, as our data underline that traditional options like single-agent ET or chemotherapy have limited effectiveness once resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors develops.

The median CFS observed in our study was longer or comparable to those reported previously in patients treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors with AI or fulvestrant in the first-line setting.26–28 The temporal decline in chemotherapy use in the second line of therapy suggests a growing preference for less toxic, more targeted approaches. This is in line with previous reports.22 In the absence of a clear standard regimen after CDK4/6 inhibitor failure, treatment selection is increasingly guided by tumor biology and biomarkers. The uptake of ET and targeted therapies, including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARP), increased, reflecting the impact of emerging evidence and guideline updates on clinical practice.9,29,30 For patients with tumors harboring a PIK3CA mutation, adding alpelisib to ET can significantly improve outcomes – the SOLAR-1 trial showed a median PFS of 11 months with alpelisib–fulvestrant compared to 5.7 months with ET alone in patients with PIK3CA mutations.31 A similar benefit was observed with capivasertib used in combination with fulvestrant in the CAPItello-291 study.32 For selected patients harboring germline BRCA mutations, PARP inhibitors may represent a reasonable alternative to chemotherapy following progression on CDK4/6 inhibitors.33 Another emerging biomarker is ESR1 mutation status.34 However, in Poland, this was limited by low access to biomarker testing and in consequence, tailoring later-line treatments to the tumor’s molecular profile is challenging. Filling this gap is especially important, since the median PFS in the second-line setting was modest for ET and chemotherapy, and the more notable benefit was observed among the small subset of patients receiving targeted therapies. These results support incorporating biomarker-driven strategies into later lines of therapy. The underutilization of genomic testing, PIK3CA, and BRCA1/2 mutations were assessed in only ~5% of patients, representing a missed opportunity for therapeutic personalization. In the third-line setting, PFS outcomes were uniformly poor regardless of treatment modality. The lack of a significant difference in PFS between chemotherapy and ET further underscores the need for novel therapeutic strategies and broader molecular profiling to guide individualized treatment decisions. Expanding access to molecular diagnostics and integrating biomarker-based therapies earlier in the treatment course could substantially improve outcomes for patients with ABC.

Another important consideration after progression on first-line therapy is the possibility of tumor subtype switching and phenotypic evolution. Breast cancers can alter their receptor status over time; for example, a tumor initially classified as HR+/HER2- may later exhibit a different biomarker profile. Receptor discordance between primary and metastatic lesions is well documented. A considerable proportion of patients may show a change in HER2 status.35 Emergence of HER2 positivity opens up new treatment opportunities; the patient can be treated with highly active anti-HER2 targeted therapies, which may substantially improve disease control. In our study, 3.5% of patients receiving subsequent lines of therapy became HER2-positive. Therefore, re-biopsy and receptor re-evaluation at progression are strongly recommended in MBC,36 especially if there is an unexpected early progression or poor response to standard therapy, as this may reveal a different subtype or actionable target. In a patient with a minimal benefit from first-line CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy, an altered tumor phenotype, such as HER2-positive disease, should be suspected. Identifying a shift to HER2-positive or HER2-low disease is particularly important: this finding would prompt a change in therapy, eg, initiation of HER2-targeted treatments, that can lead to significantly prolonged PFS compared to continuing ET or chemotherapy alone.23

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, outcomes observed in real-world settings could be influenced by confounding factors, such as concomitant diseases and medications that were not captured. Furthermore, the retrospective design entails inherent limitations, including the absence of blinded response assessments at predefined intervals and the potential for physician subjectivity in determining treatment regimens. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, all patients received treatment within the framework of the Polish national drug program, which applies standardized inclusion and exclusion criteria. This uniform approach supports consistency in patient selection and treatment delivery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study demonstrates outcomes with CDK4/6 inhibitors combined with ET in routine clinical practice in patients with HR+/HER2- ABC that align with the benefits from pivotal clinical trials. The findings underscore the importance of treatment selection based on patient characteristics, metastatic burden, and prior therapies. CDK4/6 inhibitors offer significant clinical benefits in routine practice, with evolving treatment strategies reflecting an increasing focus on individualized, less toxic approaches. A notable shift from chemotherapy to targeted and endocrine therapies in later lines highlights the changing landscape of ABC management. Our analysis confirms that traditional second-line strategies, including endocrine monotherapy or chemotherapy, offer limited disease control after progression on CDK4/6 inhibitors, underscoring the need for more effective targeted options in this setting. Encouragingly, the therapeutic landscape is rapidly evolving, but these benefits require broad integration of molecular profiling into routine practice. The recent results of treatment with trastuzumab deruxtecan and sacituzumab govitecan provide hope for better outcomes in the journey beyond CDK4/6 inhibition.
