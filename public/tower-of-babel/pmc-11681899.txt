Functional resilience of the neural visual recognition system post-pediatric occipitotemporal resection
Authors: Michael C Granovetter, Anne Margarette S Maallo, Shouyu Ling, Sophia Robert, Erez Freud, Christina Patterson, Marlene Behrmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryNeural representations for visual stimuli typically emerge with a bilateral distribution across occipitotemporal cortex (OTC)? Pediatric patients undergoing unilateral OTC resection offer an opportunity to evaluate whether representations for visual stimulus individuation can sufficiently develop in a single OTC. Here, we assessed the non-resected hemisphere of patients with pediatric resection within (n = 9) and outside (n = 12) OTC, as well as healthy controls’ two hemispheres (n = 21). Using functional magnetic resonance imaging, we mapped category selectivity (CS), and representations for visual stimulus individuation (for faces, objects, and words) with repetition suppression (RS). There were no group differences in CS or RS. However, OTC resection patients’ accuracy on face and object (but not word) recognition was lower than controls’. The neuroimaging results highlight neural resilience following damage to the contralateral homologue. Critically, however, a single OTC does not suffice for typical behavior, and, thereby, implicates the necessary contributions of bilateral OTC for visual recognition.Subject areas: Neuroscience, Sensory neuroscience

Introduction

The recognition of visual stimuli is chiefly governed by bilateral neural representations in posterior occipitotemporal cortex (OTC).1,2,3 Disparate but overlapping regions within OTC are implicated in deriving neural representations of images from categories such as faces, objects, and words.4,5 The development of this brain organization and associated visual competence emerge slowly and dynamically over childhood,6,7 likely driven by plastic processes that are more common at younger ages8,9 and continue to be refined even to age 30 years.10

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies in typically developing (TD) children attest to the changes in the strength and topography of neural representations for visual categories over time. For example, relative to adults, children show diminished neural activation for faces, larger variability in the spatial location of this activation,11 and a greater degree of non-specific activation of cortex outside OTC even when just passively viewing face stimuli.12 Furthermore, with reading acquisition, competition for cortical territory results in the “recycling” of cortex previously selective, perhaps weakly, for other stimuli to become selective for words and perhaps also for faces.7,13 Initially, prior to reading acquisition, faces are represented bilaterally, but, with the acquisition of literacy, the left and right hemispheres ultimately develop biases for words and faces, respectively.14,15,16 Collectively, these studies indicate that the mature pattern of neural representations is a product of cooperative and competitive plastic processes—as well as constraints imposed by connectivity to other regions—that occur over development.17 As a consequence, category-selective neural representations for faces and words—as well as objects18−become more reliably localizable and precise with experience.6 This previous work also highlights the fact that, while visual category representations become somewhat segregated between the two hemispheres by adulthood, specialization of the two hemispheres in early childhood is not as well-defined.6,14,19

The question we address here concerns the malleability of this brain organization during childhood and its sufficiency for visual recognition behavior, and while we examine this in the context of visual cortex, the findings may also have implications for plasticity and/or resilience of neural systems across the brain. Individuals who have undergone surgical removal of OTC in one hemisphere for the management of pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy provide a unique opportunity to investigate topographical adaptivity when all category-selective neural responses must coexist in the single preserved OTC. Because plasticity is more viable in childhood than in adulthood, this investigation may permit inferences about topographic malleability and the relationship between the neural profile and behavior.20,21 Remarkably, fMRI studies in children with a single OTC indicate that the magnitude and location of peak activation is comparable to that observed in age-matched TD controls.22,23,24 Moreover, these patients’ multivariate response pattern of category-selective activation across voxels within a region of interest (ROI) do not differ from that of TD controls, as depicted using category-based representational similarity analysis (RSA).23,25 However, our day-to-day visual recognition depends not on differentiating between categories but, rather, on the ability to discriminate among exemplars within a category (for instance, recognizing the face of one’s parent versus that of one’s teacher). Here, in contrast with our previous work that focused on category selectivity (CS), we examine whether, in a single hemisphere, representations permitting stimulus individuation are also preserved. If the neural signatures for stimulus individuation are unimpaired in pediatric OTC resection patients relative to age-matched controls, such a demonstration would attest to remarkable resilience of cortical organization with a single intact OTC.

The extent to which stimuli are individuated can be gauged by measuring repetition suppression (RS), defined as the reduction of neural activity with presentation of a sequence of identical stimuli compared with a sequence of non-identical stimuli.26,27 Upon repeated presentation of the same stimulus, the observed hemodynamic response is driven lower to a state of habituation or adaptation (fewer neurons are recruited to respond and/or previously active neurons enter a refractory state).28 On the other hand, with successive presentation of unique stimuli, the observed overall hemodynamic signal remains relatively high. By measuring RS, we can infer a neural system’s ability to differentiate individual stimuli within a category29,30: if a voxel shows RS, this indicates that neurons within that voxel selectively are tuned for the individual token. This exemplar-specificity cannot be inferred by the standard practice of averaging the hemodynamic response signal across all voxels and allows a more fine-grained assessment of the neural system at subvoxel resolution.27

The neural processes underlying stimulus individuation appear to emerge over childhood once the CS profile is established: adolescents, but not children, show reliably localizable CS for faces and scenes in OTC, but neither group shows adult-like RS to individual faces or scenes.31 That patients with resections that include OTC have seemingly intact category-selective regions of interest (CS ROIs)22,23,24 does not necessarily imply that the neurons in these regions are tuned for stimulus exemplars. Indeed, while patients with a single OTC perform surprisingly well on recognizing individual faces and words (average accuracy about 85%), their exemplar-specific performance is still statistically inferior to TD controls,32,33,34 perhaps due to compromised neural profiles for stimulus individuation.

The current investigation compares the neural profile of both CS and RS in patients with a single OTC (“OTC patients”) with that of age-matched control patients who have undergone surgery outside of OTC (including patients with focal anterior temporal resections; “control patients”) and TD controls. For all participants, CS and RS are measured in response to faces, objects, and words, and participants’ behavioral abilities are characterized as well. If OTC patients do not evince RS in the TD range, this may offer a direct explanation for patients’ previously reported behavioral difficulties on visual recognition.32,33,34 If RS is intact but visual behaviors are not, this may suggest that the documented behavioral deficits in visual recognition are less a consequence of impairment of the integrity of stimulus-specific neural representations than they are a consequence of constraining processes to a single OTC (and hence, revealing the necessity of contributions from OTC bilaterally). In addition to quantifying the RS profile of the OTC patients, we also examine whether the RS profiles differ depending on which hemisphere is resected, given the left and right hemispheric superiorities for word and face representations that respectively emerge over development6,31; this comparison allows us to test whether category and individual visual representations can emerge in either the left or right hemisphere (LH or RH) depending on which is intact and whether a single intact left or right OTC suffices for behavior that is comparable to controls. Together, the findings will highlight the extent to which the developing, plastic brain subserves changes in or maintenance of the development of neural representations following pediatric cortical resection and the consequences that may bear on behavior.

Results

This investigation was designed to characterize and compare the neural underpinnings and behavioral performance of face, object, and word recognition in OTC patients, control patients, and TD controls (see STAR Methods for a comprehensive description). See Figure 1 and Table 1 for a description of participating patients. First, for each participant, CS amplitude was quantified for each stimulus category both as a means of ensuring that we were able to replicate our previous results of intact CS in OTC resection patients (in a larger sample) but also to identify ROIs within which to explore RS. Second, an fMRI adaptation protocol was deployed to assess neural activation patterns underlying stimulus individuation per category. Note that in the neuroimaging experiments we are only evaluating activity in the patients’ preserved hemisphere; data from TD controls are included separately for their LH and RH. Finally, the participants’ ability to discriminate between visually presented exemplars, separately for each stimulus category, was assessed.

For each dependent measure for the imaging data, the following statistical procedures were applied: (1) model comparisons of linear mixed effects models (LMEMs) with the likelihood ratio test (LRT) to select the most complex model that minimizes the Akaike information criteria (AIC),36 (2) Wald χ2-test on the selected model to determine which predictors (e.g., group, hemisphere) were statistically significant,37 (3) Bayes factor (BF) calculation on each model term to offer evidence either for the null model lacking the factor or for the model containing the factor,38 and (4) for statistically significant interaction terms, post hoc contrasts of estimated marginal means (EMM; with the Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied to p values).39

Additionally, to validate the effect of group on each dependent variable, each model was refit 1,000 times with the group label randomly shuffled with replacement, to simulate a random distribution; the p value was taken to be the proportion of instances in which the test statistic in the simulated distribution exceeded the true test statistic.23,40,41 Last, each patient’s dependent measure was compared to the respective distribution of the TD controls’values using a procedure for testing differences between individual patients against the control group.42 Note: mean-centered age was included as a covariate in all models/tests.

Category selectivity

Participants viewed blocks of stimuli (faces, objects, words, houses, and scrambled objects) while lying in an MRI scanner (Figure 2A). CS ROIs were defined as clusters of voxels in ventral stream visual cortex (as defined by Glasser et al.)44 that were significantly more responsive for each primary category of interest in this study (faces, objects, words) vs. a baseline (the other categories). On independent runs of data, CS amplitude was computed as the median responsiveness (β-weight) for a given category vs. baseline of the voxels in that ROI (Figure 2B).

Repetition suppression

Participants viewed blocks of stimuli (faces, objects, or words) in which the same stimulus was displayed successively (“same” condition), two different stimuli were shown successively alternating (“alternating” condition), or each stimulus displayed in succession was unique (“different” condition; Figure 3A). Initially, an LMEM was fit to test the four-way interaction of block condition (same, alternating, or different) × group × hemisphere × stimulus category on the responsivity within the corresponding CS ROI. The four-way interaction did not survive the model selection (see Table S4 for details), and there were also no significant three-way interactions (group × condition × stimulus category: χ28 = 2.57, p = 0.96, BF = 1.71 × 1010; group × hemisphere × stimulus category: χ24 = 5.04, p = 0.28, BF = 2.03 × 104; group × condition × hemisphere: χ24 = 0.87, p = 0.93, BF = 1.61 × 105; condition × hemisphere × stimulus category: χ24 = 1.29, p = 0.86, BF = 1.30 × 105). Additionally, although there was a significant two-way interaction of group × stimulus category (χ24 = 10.70, p = 0.03), there was strong evidence favoring the null model without the interaction term (BF = 1.37 × 103). Post hoc comparisons showed that there were no differences between groups for any stimulus category, which was confirmed with permutation testing (see Table 2 for EMMs by group × stimulus category; Table S5 for complete post hoc comparison details). There was a significant two-way interaction effect of hemisphere × stimulus category (χ24 = 12.44, p < 0.01, BF = 1.20; see Table S6 for post hoc comparisons) but no other significant two-way interaction effects (group × condition: χ24 = 0.15, p ≈ 1, BF = 2.29 × 105; group × hemisphere: χ22 = 4.76, p = 0.09, BF = 51.06; condition × hemisphere: χ22 = 0.03, p = 0.99, BF = 4.87 × 102; condition × stimulus category: χ24 = 1.22, p = 0.88, BF = 1.37 × 105). Additionally, there was a significant effect of condition (χ22 = 58.82, p < 0.001, BF = 2.60 × 10−9). Post hoc testing demonstrated that the responsivity to the different condition was significantly greater than that for the alternating condition (z = 3.43, p < 0.001) and for the same condition (z = 6.04, p < 0.001), and the responsivity to the alternating condition was significantly greater than that for the same condition (z = 2.61, p = < 0.001), as would be expected with RS,26,27,30 thereby validating the study paradigm. Finally, on individual case-control comparisons, in all but one case (a single right-sided OTC resection patient’s responsivity to different objects in the LH vs. TD controls’ LH), there were no significant differences between any patient and the TD controls (see Table S7 for statistics).

To examine RS specifically, we computed the difference per voxel in the responsivity for the different vs. same conditions (the most extreme comparison) within the predefined CS ROI associated with the stimulus class being presented in the given blocks. We defined the RS magnitude as the median value across voxels (Figure 3C). Initially an LMEM was fit with the three-way interaction of group × hemisphere × stimulus category. Only the three-way interaction did not survive model selection (see Table S8 for details). There was a significant two-way interaction effect of hemisphere x stimulus category (χ22 = 9.04, p = 0.01, BF = 2.05). Post hoc comparisons revealed that RS to words in the LH was significantly greater than RS to faces in the LH (z = 3.84, p < 0.01; as expected on accounts of weighted asymmetry for words in the LH),6 RS to objects in the LH (z = 3.15, p = 0.01), and RS to words in the RH (z = 2.51, p = 0.04). There were no other significant pairwise differences (see Table S9 for complete post hoc comparisons). The two-way interaction of group × hemisphere (χ22 = 2.51, p = 0.28, BF = 48.65) and group × stimulus category (χ24 = 8.45, p = 0.08, BF = 4.48 × 102) on RS magnitude were not statistically significant. As with CS amplitude, and concordant with our analyses on responsivity to the aforementioned adaptation experiment, there was no significant difference in RS magnitude by group (χ22 = 0.34, p = 0.84, BF = 3.41 × 106; see Table 2 for EMMs), as further confirmed by permutation testing (p = 0.86). Moreover, on individual case-control comparisons, in all but one case (an LH resection control patient with lower RS magnitude to faces in the RH vs. TD controls’ LH), there were no significant differences between any patient from the OTC or control patient groups and TD controls (see Table S3 for statistics).

Thus, altogether, as with CS, there were no group differences in RS between OTC patients and control patients or TD controls.

Representational similarity analysis

To scrutinize further the extent to which the neural representations of the OTC patients match those of the other two groups, RSA was conducted. RSA involves computing a second-order correlation (e.g., Spearman’s correlation) between activation-pattern representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) and model RDMs.25 Model RDMs represent the predicted similarity between activation-pattern RDMs computed from the neural profile using dissimilarity metrics (e.g., Pearson’s correlation) and experimental conditions based on a priori hypotheses. Figure 4A displays the average activation RDMs on the fMRI adaptation data for each participant group (note patients’ data are collapsed across hemispheres here due to small sample sizes and, as aforementioned, we know that there are no group by hemisphere differences). Specifically, each participant’s activation RDM was transformed to Fisher’s z score, averaged within the group, and then transformed back to Pearson’s correlation values. To determine the similarity of the RDMs across groups, the rank correlation (Kendall’s τ) of each pair of RDMs was computed. Then, over 5,000 iterations, group labels were shuffled, group average RDMs were computed per group, and pairwise correlations were calculated. All participant groups exhibited similar patterns of activation (see Figure 4B).

We then correlated each participant’s RDM with three theoretical models using, in an assumption-free approach, all ventral stream visual cortex voxels, which is narrowly defined as just posterior regions by the Glasser atlas, on the fMRI adaptation data (Figure 4C). First, a CS model was designed where ventral cortex exhibits CS without RS, with activation patterns across conditions of the same category (same, alternating, or different) showing highest similarity, while patterns of different categories showing lowest similarity. Second, an RS model was constructed, in which voxels show RS but do not distinguish between categories. We theorized that the similarity in a same vs. different comparison would decrease compared to a same vs. same comparison, illustrating the classic RS effect; the similarity in a same vs. alternating comparison was hypothesized to be intermediate. Additionally, we expected that the similarity in an alternating vs. different comparison would fall in between a same vs. same comparison and a same vs. different comparison. Last, a CS-RS model combined both RS and CS, such that RS should be present within a category but not across categories.

An LMEM was initially run predicting model fit (Fisher’s z) from the three-way interaction of group (TD controls, control patients, OTC patients) × hemisphere (LH, RH) × model type (CS, RS, CS-RS; Figure 4D). No interaction effects survived model selection (see Table S10). There was a significant difference in model fit across the three models (χ22 = 807.23, p < 0.001, BF = 7.57 × 1060); post hoc contrasts indicated that the CS model fit significantly better than the RS model (z = 21.91, p < 0.001). The CS-RS model, however, was the best fit for the data, significantly outperforming the CS model (z = 4.71, p < 0.001) and the RS model (z = 26.62, p < 0.001). Importantly, there was no effect of group (χ22 = 0.03, p = 0.99, BF = 1.86 × 102), nor of hemisphere (χ21 = 3.71, p = 0.05, BF = 2.22) on the model fit. Permutation testing confirmed that there was no difference in model fit across groups (p ≈ 1). Moreover, individual case-control comparisons demonstrated that no patient’s model fit fell outside the distribution of model fits for each the LH and RH of TD controls, except for the RS model for one right-resection control patient compared to the distribution of model fits for TD controls’ LH (see Table S11 for statistics).

Overall, the multivariate approach to examining neural activation pattern in OTC patients, control patients, and TD controls revealed no group differences.

An LMEM (see Table S12 for model selection details) was fit predicting task accuracy from the interaction effect of group (TD controls, control patients, OTC patients) × stimulus category (faces, objects, words; Figure 5B); this interaction effect was significant (χ24 = 17.62, p < 0.01, BF = 6.25). Group comparisons demonstrated that OTC patients’ face recognition accuracy was significantly lower than that of both TD controls (z = 4.06, p < 0.001) and control patients (z = 3.67, p < 0.001). Control patients and TD controls did not differ in performance (z = 0.10, p = 0.92), suggesting that the group deficit was specific to resection inclusive of OTC. On object recognition, OTC patients’ accuracy was also significantly lower than that of both TD controls (z = 5.37, p < 0.001) and control patients (z = 3.45, p < 0.01). Again, control patients and TD controls did not differ in performance (z = 2.02, p = 0.08), suggesting that the deficit was specific to OTC patients. There were no group differences on word recognition (and the ceiling effect on the word recognition task is discussed further below; see also Table 2 for EMMs; Table S13 for complete post hoc comparison statistics). Permutation testing confirmed group comparisons (Table S13).

As TD controls have two intact hemispheres, hemisphere could not be modeled as a predictor for accuracy; therefore, an analysis was performed only on the OTC and control patients’ data as they have only a single non-resected hemisphere. Initially, an LMEM predicting accuracy from the three-way interaction of group (control patients, OTC patients) x hemisphere (LH, RH) x stimulus category (faces, objects, words) was fit on only the patients’ data. The three-way interaction did not survive model selection (see Table S14 for details), but there were significant interaction effects of both group x hemisphere (χ21 = 4.32, p = 0.04, BF = 1.03) and group × stimulus category (χ22 = 7.42, p = 0.02, BF = 2.91) on accuracy; there was no interaction effect of hemisphere × stimulus category (χ22 = 0.13, p = 0.94, BF = 56.30). As aforementioned, relative to control patients (who, although they have had a resection, have intact OTC bilaterally), OTC patients had significantly lower accuracy on face recognition (z = 3.42, p < 0.01) and object recognition (z = 3.22, p < 0.01) but not word recognition (z = 0.57, p = 0.57). Decomposing the group × hemisphere interaction revealed that left OTC resection patients had significantly lower accuracy than left resection control patients (z = 3.83, p < 0.01), but otherwise there were no further pairwise differences (see Table S15 for complete post hoc statistics).

Finally, on individual comparisons of each OTC and control patient to the TD control distribution, three out of nine OTC (two preserved LH, one preserved RH) and only one out of 12 control patients (preserved RH) showed significant deficits on face recognition. There were no observed deficits on object recognition (see Table S3 for statistics). On word recognition, one out of nine OTC patients (preserved LH) and no control patients exhibited a deficit. Of note, all OTC patients performed all tasks at above-chance accuracy.

Altogether, the findings imply that OTC patients, as a group, have significantly lower accuracy on both face and object—but not word—recognition, and that this deficit appears specific to surgery that encompasses OTC.

Correlations between neural activation of individual exemplars and behavior

Thus far, we have showed RS of the OTC patients does not differ from that of the control groups and yet, behavioral performance of the OTC patients was poorer than that of controls. One possibility, as we have alluded to previously, is that the one hemisphere of the OTC patients may be equivalent to one hemisphere of the controls but that robust behavior requires the engagement of the joint information distributed across two hemispheres. If so, this would predict a correlation between RS and behavioral accuracy in the controls and in the control patients (bilateral OTC and two hemispheres contribute to behavior). In contrast, we may expect no correlation for the OTC patients who have only one OTC for behavior. To understand this pattern further, we correlated the RS values per condition (faces, objects, and words) for each participant with their behavioral accuracy for the corresponding condition using a bivariate analysis. Because controls presumably use both their left and right OTC for visual recognition, we used the RS values per condition, with the mean across the two hemispheres, which were then correlated with the behavioral accuracy scores. The control patients and OTC patients each had only a single hemisphere, and its RS value was correlated with the corresponding behavioral accuracy.

There was a significant RS-accuracy correlation for the face condition for the TD controls (r = 0.44; p = 0.01) but not for the control patients (r = 0.54; p = 0.07), although in a combined analysis for the two control groups, the correlation was significant (r = 0.48; p < 0.001). While there was no significant RS-accuracy correlation for the object condition for each TD controls and control patients separately (p > 0.05), a combined analysis for the two control groups did reveal a significant correlation (r = 0.34; p = 0.03). There were no significant RS-accuracy correlations for controls or for control patients for words (p > 0.05), although we know that both of these groups performed at ceiling on the word recognition task. Notably, there are no correlations between RS and accuracy for any condition for the OTC patients. Using BF for assessing the evidence for or against a correlation, we note that the correlations between RS and behavioral accuracy for the OTC group had BF10 smaller than 1, indicating the absence of correlation.51 As predicted then, it appears that the OTC in a single hemisphere does not suffice to yield typical behavior and suggests that each of the two hemispheres contribute non-redundant information.52

Discussion

Here, we examined the nature of neural representations underlying visual category recognition and stimulus individuation in individuals who, following childhood resection of OTC for the treatment of drug-resistant epilepsy, develop with only a single OTC. Although we have conducted neuroimaging studies and behavioral investigations previously with this population, we have not examined neural representations of exemplars within categories, nor have we assessed correlations between neuroimaging findings and behavioral accuracy.

The neuroimaging experiments revealed several findings, supported by both univariate and multivariate analyses. First, consistent with prior work,22,23 pediatric OTC patients showed CS amplitudes comparable to those of TD controls and control patients (with surgeries outside of OTC). This suggests that, despite developing with just a single OTC, patients’ non-resected cortex shows distinct neural representations for different visual stimulus categories, and these representations are not differentiable from representations observed in the developing LH and RH in TD individuals.

Second, there were no between-group differences in the magnitude of RS to individual stimulus exemplars measured within the non-resected CS ROIs, defined individually for each participant. RS is often used to characterize the function of perceptual systems in the brain,28 both for assessing the extent of representational overlap between exemplars and for inferring the neural computations giving rise to cognitive behaviors.26 Suppression of the hemodynamic response on fMRI is posited to reflect neural subpopulations’ individual sensitivities to the repetition of exemplars, either through neural “fatigue” from the selective neurons’ delayed refractory periods or neural “tuning” such that neurons that do not represent an exemplar’s features are suppressed to conserve metabolic resources.28 Our finding of comparable RS across the patient and control groups thus suggests that the neural processes subserving individuation between exemplars of the same category in patients’ single, contralesional OTC are within the normal distribution.

A single OTC does not suffice for robust visual recognition

Despite the observed integrity of the neural profile, the OTC patients’ visual recognition ability is statistically inferior to that of controls for face and object recognition, as has been demonstrated previously.32,33,34 The OTC patients’ accuracy was not different from the two other groups for word recognition, but this may have been an artifact of the task being overall simpler than the face and object recognition tasks. The reduction in OTC patients’ accuracy profiles appears to indicate that visual recognition may stringently require the maintenance of representations across two hemispheres to yield typical recognition, or in plain words, two hemispheres are better than one. This claim is bolstered by the fact that, whereas in TD controls and control patients, we observe a correlation between RS and behavioral accuracy for faces and objects (word recognition accuracy is at ceiling and, subsequently, no correlations can be uncovered), we do not see any such correlations in the OTC patients, and the statistical Bayes evidence weighs against a correlation. Thus, the two hemispheres support robust recognition and high accuracy for the TD controls and, in the control patients, presumably by virtue of the two preserved OTCs. At the same time, a single OTC in the OTC patient group can support behavior to some extent (accuracy is not at chance), but a second OTC appears to be required to achieve typical levels of performance.

The current finding of RS-behavior correlations in our control participants is consistent with existing data to this effect. For example, the magnitude of the RS showing repetition-induced reduction of fMRI responses to faces in the fusiform face area is positively associated with identity discrimination performance outside the scanner; this association is also selective, and RS for faces is not present in the extrastriate body area.53 Similar support for RS-behavior correlation for objects has been reported previously in studies that have revealed that the extent of behavioral priming of objects is generally associated with suppression in object-responsive regions.54,55

Two hemispheres and interhemispheric communication

That contributions from bilateral OTC are required suggests that each hemisphere contributes at least some distinct information to support recognition behavior. Many studies have documented differences in the visual encoding properties of the two hemispheres with the LH more tuned to local information and the right hemisphere more tuned to global information.56,57 Alternatively, this distinction may be thought of as arising from different spatial frequency filtering, with the LH tuned to higher spatial frequencies and the RH tuned to lower spatial frequencies.58 If the two hemispheres each contribute some unique information, the joint outcome of both hemispheres requires appropriate interhemispheric connectivity between category (and exemplar) regions of the temporal lobes.

The two hemispheres in TD controls and the non-resected OTC in the control patients likely contribute to the controls’ high recognition accuracy. Interhemispheric connectivity in OTC has been well-documented, especially for face-selective regions,59,60 but for object recognition, as well.61 As we have shown here, one hemisphere in the OTC patients matches the neural profile of the corresponding hemisphere of the controls but, alone, underperforms in terms of accuracy of face and object recognition. Ideally, the exact nature and extent of this connectivity and its role in recognition might be better determined in studies of individuals with callosal agenesis or individuals who have undergone callosotomy, although there are often concurrent neurological challenges in such cases that may hamper such an approach.

Implications for development and plasticity

The competitive interplay underlying the establishment of visual stimulus category representations across the two hemispheres over development has long led to the question of whether both hemispheres are necessary for neural representations to mature sufficiently and optimally. Neuropsychology studies in adults reveal that, at later ages, accommodation of new functions is limited, as even a focal unilateral lesion to left or right OTC in adulthood can result in pronounced recognition deficits for words and faces, respectively.62,63,64 Moreover, these deficits have proven resistant to intervention.65,66,67 At the same time, given that competition for representing visual stimulus categories is dynamic and ongoing throughout development,6,14 it is reasonable to conjecture that, during this period of plasticity, representations for the full array of visual stimulus categories may potentially come to co-exist in a single, developing OTC. Indeed, our prior work demonstrated that patients with only one hemisphere could nonetheless perform visual recognition tasks at above-chance accuracy (on average ∼85%) but still significantly poorer relative to healthy controls, and this held true independent of whether the single hemisphere was the left or the right. The current finding mirrors this prior result very clearly.

Our results demonstrate that following pediatric OTC resection, neural processes of typical development do not appear to have been interrupted, as OTC patients, with their single OTC, evince CS and RS profiles that are within the range of the corresponding hemisphere of controls. Notably, as there is no interaction of group by hemisphere on either CS or RS, this pattern ensues independent of which hemisphere is resected. These findings highlight the non-resected OTC’s resilience to damage to its contralateral homologue and stability of the typical neurodevelopmental trajectory in that hemisphere. At the same time, that pediatric OTC surgery patients show intact CS ROIs with intact RS, but impaired behavior, suggests that their behavioral impairment is likely not caused by an atypicality in the neural computations underlying stimulus individuation per se (at least at the resolution that RS paradigms can afford). In other words, one functional OTC may be sufficient—as all OTC patients performed all tasks at above-chance accuracy—but nonetheless suboptimal for visual recognition. This behavioral deficit replicates our previous finding of behavioral competence of 85% independent of hemisphere in OTC patients relative to matched controls.32 Thus, having two hemispheres—rather than just one, as in the OTC patients—may account for the behavioral differences. The lack of support from the resected hemisphere and/or constraining of function to a single hemisphere following OTC resection may be implicated in the behavioral deficit.

Importantly, the finding that CS, RS, or behavioral performance between OTC patients and controls is independent of hemisphere resected is consistent with findings suggesting that hemispheric specialization is not well-refined early in development. For example, insult to either the LH or RH in early childhood can result in a face recognition deficit,16 but in adulthood, prosopagnosia is more common and more severe after RH than LH damage.68 Similarly, there is more bilateral activation in language regions in children compared with adults whose neural profile is strongly weighted to the LH.69,70

The brain may be resilient to injury at younger ages due to the up-regulation of plasticity. While the current study demonstrates the remarkable stability of one hemisphere forced to develop in isolation, future work is necessary to demonstrate the potential plastic mechanisms subserving this stability. Prior work has demonstrated, for instance, that there are global differences in functional connectivity (FC) in the contralesional hemisphere of this patient population, indicative of plasticity from competition for functional representations.24,71 The mechanisms by which brain-wide FC differences may be implicated in the formation of stimulus exemplar representations remains to be explored. Furthermore, not all neural systems may demonstrate the resilience that we observe here; for example, we know that individuals with resections of parietal cortex show less plasticity of function to the homologous region of the other hemisphere, unlike in the OTC resections in the current cases.72

We should note too that while this study characterizes visual recognition of the largest sample of pediatric OTC patients to date, both in terms of behavioral and neural data, the group of patients was nonetheless heterogenous. This heterogeneity is advantageous in that it suggests that the findings are likely to extend to a diversity of patient cases, and individual case-control comparisons revealed few obvious patterns to suggest that select patient ages or surgery types result in unique behavioral or RS profiles different from the group (also see Robert et al. for similar logic).71 At the same time, further study of larger samples of patients would allow one to determine possible patient-control differences dependent on relevant clinical variables including age at disease onset/surgery or medical etiology. Understanding the relationship of age at disease onset/surgery and behavior/CS/RS profiles will be especially important given the slow maturation of visual category representations that occur over development.11,14,31 It is possible perhaps that while pediatric OTC surgery patients overall do not show differences from controls, that, within this group of patients, those with earlier neural damage may show evidence for greater plasticity than those with later surgeries. Indeed, earlier surgery has been posited to “correct” the developmental trajectory of a child with drug-resistant epilepsy, as evidenced by improved cognitive outcomes following resection.73 It will also be critical for future studies to characterize the nature of neural representations for visual recognition pre- and post-surgery to ascertain whether there are differences in plasticity/resilience as a function of disease etiology vs. surgical intervention.

Altogether, this study demonstrates that patients with pediatric OTC surgery show, relative to control patients and TD controls, deficits in visual recognition behaviors but, remarkably, no differences in CS or RS to visual stimulus exemplars in the contralesional, preserved hemisphere. The findings indicate that while the processes of establishing representations for visual stimuli associated with development may not depend on the presence of OTC bilaterally and are resilient in the context of early unilateral injury to OTC, because these processes are constrained to a single OTC, behavior may be adversely impacted. That is, despite the stability of the neural profile of the preserved hemisphere to support visual recognition, the absence of the other suggests both are likely necessary for optimal behavior.

Future directions

A number of clear and testable predictions emerge from these findings. One immediate question concerns the sufficiency of a single hemisphere for recognition in stimulus domains that are typically bilaterally lateralized. Our findings thus far and some of our previous work focuses on recognition of faces and words that are represented bilaterally but with a weighted asymmetry to the RH for faces and to the LH for words.32,33 A further question concerns the competence of a single hemisphere for representing and recognizing objects and scenes. The performance of the OTC patients is also poorer than the control groups for object recognition, but this remains to be confirmed (in a task that does not have a mnemonic component). The perception of scenes, another bilaterally represented domain, may be worth examining, as well.

We have also argued that hemispheric organization changes over the course of development; here, we have focused on brain-behavior correspondences following childhood cortical resection but whether similar univariate, RSA, and correlation results would be obtained following adult cortical resection of OTC remains to be determined. Based on many neuropsychological studies, we surmise that the neural profile and its association with behavior will differ between childhood and adulthood.62,74,75 Last, we propose that the correlations between the neural pattern and behavior of other cognitive functions outside of the visual system, such as language, be explored as well.76 Such an approach will permit a broader analysis of the extent to which a single hemisphere, during childhood, shows neural integrity but compromised behavior.

Limitations of the study

One key limitation to the study is the patient sample size and heterogeneity. Our sample size was limited by recruitment of our population of interest: posterior OTC resections are not as common surgical interventions for temporal lobe epilepsy as anterior resections,77 and fewer than 5% of epilepsy surgeries utilize posterior quadratic resection/disconnection.78 We acknowledge that the small sample size increases the risk of type II error, and we encourage replication of this work with larger samples in the future. For the current investigation, given the small sample size, we employed statistical testing designed to compare single cases to a control distribution.42,79 Furthermore, as a means of increasing sample size, we included patients with a broad range of characteristics, including age at study participation, age at seizure onset, age at surgery, epilepsy etiology, type of surgical intervention (e.g., focal resection vs. hemispheric surgery), and seizure freedom (detailed in Figure 1 and Table 1). While our heterogenous sample speaks to the generalizability of our findings, we recommend that future work with larger samples determines whether select clinical variables are predictive of postoperative neural signatures underlying visual recognition and corresponding behavior. Etiology is in fact already considered to be a key variable for understanding postoperative cognitive outcomes.80,81 Age at seizure onset and/or surgery may also be important given the plasticity during development8 and the positive effects on cognitive outcome with earlier surgeries.73 Another limitation is that while RS permits a high resolution evaluation of OTC,27 additional measures in future studies may capture differences in computational properties underlying visual stimulus individuation not afforded by this particular fMRI technique. For example, electrocorticography allows more direct demonstration of RS.82 Moreover, pediatric epilepsy surgery patients show differences in FC relative to controls24,71: direct exploration of OTC’s connectivity to other regions could perhaps offer more explanation for patients’ deficits relative to controls and warrants further investigation in future work.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patients (n = 21) with childhood cortical resections or ablations were recruited from the Pediatric Epilepsy Surgery Program at University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh or with assistance from the Pediatric Epilepsy Surgery Alliance (formerly the Brain Recovery Project). Of these patients, nine underwent surgeries encompassing OTC (including six with near to total resection or disconnection of an entire hemisphere but always encompassing OTC). The remaining 12 patients (referred to throughout as control patients) underwent surgeries where OTC was preserved bilaterally (including four with anterior temporal resections). All patients had surgery at age 18 years or younger. Additionally, native-English-speaking, right-handed individuals who had not undergone prior neurosurgery (referred throughout as TD controls) were recruited from the local Pittsburgh community. See Table 1 for individual patient details, Figure 1 for an illustration of the patients' lesions, and Table S16 for individual control details.

To verify the matching of patients and controls specifically with respect to age at time of testing and gender, multinomial regression models were fit predicting group (OTC patients vs. control patients vs. TD controls), with age and gender in separate models. A permutation test was performed in which predictor values were randomly sampled with replacement, and the model was fit on the permuted data, 1000 times. p was calculated as the probability that the absolute value of z for the predictor (for distinguishing OTC patients from each TD controls and control patients) was greater than or equal to the absolute value of z observed. Age was not a significant predictor for distinguishing OTC patients from either TD controls (z4 = 0.18, p = 0.88) or control patients (z4 = 0.18, p = 0.73). Gender was also not a significant predictor for distinguishing OTC patients from either TD controls (z4 = 0.64, p = 0.51) or control patients (z4 = 0.25, p = 0.82).

Adult participants or child participants' guardians gave informed consent, and child participants assented to participate. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Carnegie Mellon University (study approval #2015_00000013) and the University of Pittsburgh (study approval #19090231). All participants received $75 an hour for the imaging portion and $25 an hour for the behavioral portion of the study.

Method details

Neuroimaging experiments

All but four participants' data were acquired with a Siemens Prisma 3T MRI scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Data from the remaining four participants (all TD controls) were collected with a Siemens Verio 3T MRI with a 32-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted whole-brain anatomical data were acquired with a magnetization rapid gradient-echo sequence: 176 slices, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm, repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 1.97 ms, and scan time = 5 min 21 s.

There were two functional scans, one a set of localizers for the selection of CS ROIs22–2422−24 and the other, an adaptation paradigm for the RS portion. On each run of the localizer, participants viewed 15 blocks of stimuli (each separated by approximately 8-s inter-block periods with no stimulus presentation, including one prior to the start of the first block) displayed with the Psychophysics Toolbox (version 3)83 in MATLAB (Key Resources). Each block consisted of 16 stimuli (each shown for approximately 800 ms with a 200-ms interstimulus interval) from one of five categories: faces, objects, words, houses, and scrambled objects (Figure 2A). (Face stimuli were from Righi et al.43) Of the 16 stimuli per block, 15 were unique, with 1 stimulus repeated randomly per sequence. A fixation cross was always displayed at the screen’s center, and participants were instructed to maintain fixation and press a button on a fMRI-compatible glove on detection of a stimulus repeat. Each category block (totaling approximately 16 s) was shown three times per run, in a pre-determined random sequence used for all participants, and all participants completed three runs. Across each of the estimated 6 min 8 s runs, echoplanar imaging (EPI) data were acquired (69 slices, FOV = 212 × 212 mm, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, multiband acceleration factor = 3).

The fMRI adaptation paradigm29 consisted of 6 runs, each comprising 36 "mini-blocks" of approximately 3 s each. In each mini-block, 12 stimuli were presented centrally, each for 250 ms, with 50-ms intervals between stimuli. For each mini-block, three baseline fixation periods without presentation of stimuli—of durations 6, 7.5, and 9 s each—were randomly interspersed. This mini-block design was intended to increase statistical power while limiting the duration that children would be in the scanner. Valid general linear models (GLMs) can be successfully fit to EPI data with this format, including stimulus category mini-blocks that are likewise not all separated by baseline fixation periods.93

Blocks featured one of three stimulus classes: unfamiliar faces (see Table S17 for list of stimulus IDs from the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces stimulus set),45 Greebles (a novel object stimulus class),94 or pseudowords (Figure 3A), all of which were chosen to measure RS without the confound of prior familiarity with specific stimulus exemplars. Furthermore, there were three block conditions: same in which the same exemplar was shown 12 times; alternating for which two individual stimuli were shown in an alternating pattern, six times each; and "different" in which 12 unique stimuli were shown in succession. Across the experiment, 18 unique exemplars were presented for each stimulus category. The same two and 12 exemplars were used for the alternating and different conditions, respectively. On the other hand, four unique exemplars were used for each same condition within a run, but the same four exemplars were used for the same condition across runs (presented in a randomized order per run). Blocks were pseudorandomized such that each of the nine block types (three stimulus categories x three conditions) were presented once before cycling through the block types again. Moreover, two blocks of the same stimulus category did not immediately follow one another. All participants viewed blocks in an identical sequence.

Participants were instructed to fixate on a dot in the center and to indicate with a button press when the dot changed color (from blue to red or red to blue) to encourage fixation. On each run, the dot changed color only once per block type, at a random time during the block. The color change occurred in the same sequence of block types across runs, though block types were randomized in their sequence, limiting the predictability of the dot color change. For three out of the four repetitions of the nine block types within a run, the dot changed color only twice, and on the other, three times, totaling nine color changes across the run. The color change timing was identical across participants.

All participants completed six runs. Across each of the estimated 3 min 36 s runs, EPI data were acquired (68 slices, FOV = 192 × 192 mm, TR = 1500 ms, TE = 30 ms, multiband acceleration factor = 3).

Behavioral experiments

Participants were also assessed on their ability to recognize faces, objects, and words. The Cambridge Face Memory Test adapted for children46 was used (Figure 5A; Key Resources). In the first block, participants viewed a grayscale male face sans hair from three different angles, each for 5 s. Then, in an alternative forced choice task, they were presented with three face pairs (each pair shown at one of three angles) successively and identified which of the two faces they had just seen. This process was repeated for five faces, totaling 15 trials. In the second block, participants studied the previously presented five faces for 20 s, after which they were presented with face pairs (one "old" and one "distractor") and identified which of the two faces was "old" (25 trials). In the final block, after studying the five faces again for 20 additional seconds, participants identified which of the two faces was "old," but in the final 20 trials, faces were presented with Gaussian noise to increase the difficulty of the task. All participants viewed the same stimuli presented in the same sequential order. Accuracy was computed as the mean accuracy across all trials from the three blocks (60 total).

Participants also completed the Cambridge Bicycle Memory Test with grayscale bicycles instead of faces48 (Figure 5B; Key Resources). The experiment structure was identical to that of the Cambridge Face Memory Test (and the Bicycle Memory Test was designed to be equated to the Face Memory Test), except that participants were initially presented with and subsequently studied a total of six, not five, exemplars; and participants identified the exemplar they had previously seen among three, not two, stimuli.

Because there is not a widely available and standardized version of a word recognition test, participants completed a word matching task, previously used in this patient population32 (Key Resources). Pairs of 58 four-letter gray words, chosen to be age-appropriate for young readers, were presented in white Arial font, on a black background. Words in each pair differed by one letter (for example, “tack” vs. “tank”). The second letter differed in 14 pairs, and the third letter differed in 15. As shown in Figure 5C, participants viewed a word centered over fixation for 750 ms (long enough for encoding by children). After a 150 ms interval with just a fixation cross, participants saw a second word centered over fixation for 150 ms. A randomly jittered 1500 to 2500 ms interval separated trials. Participants indicated, via key press, whether the two stimuli were the same or different, for 96 trials (half same, half different). Participants completed 12 practice trials and received feedback, and no direct feedback was provided during the task.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification

Data preprocessing steps were adapted from prior fMRI work with this patient population.24 Anatomical data were defaced with PyDeface,95 and then, using FreeSurfer (v7.1.0; Key Resources), underwent motion correction,96 intensity normalization,97 and skull stripping.98 All fMRI preprocessing and derivation of dependent measures were then completed with Analysis of Functional Neuroimages (AFNI)99,100 (Key Resources). EPI data, which were acquired in oblique orientation, were transformed to a cardinal grid. Volumes were slice-time-corrected. The volume with least motion was aligned to the anatomical data with a local Pearson correlation cost function.101 Data were scaled to a mean of 100 per run and were not spatially smoothed to preserve resolution. Volumes were discarded if the Euclidean norm of motion derivatives exceeded 1 cm or if outlier values (approximately 5.5 median absolute deviations away from the median) were detected in more than 10% of voxels. To optimize registration of brains with complete hemispheric surgery (e.g., minimal residual tissue of the operated hemisphere) in AFNI, after defacement and prior to preprocessing, anatomical data were mirrored102 (Key Resources). Additionally, only EPI data from the preserved hemisphere were analyzed for all patients.

The first two runs of the category localizer data, which were used to define ROIs, were concatenated and preprocessed independently from the third run, which was held out to independently derive CS amplitudes. Separately for the concatenated first two runs and the third run, a GLM was fit with each visual category (faces, objects, words, houses, and scrambled objects) as predictors of a boxcar function. Regressors for motion in each of the six coordinate directions (translation: x, y, and z; and rotation: pitch, roll, yaw) and scanner drift were also modeled. Temporal autocorrelations were regressed out of the data. To restrict analyses to only functionally relevant parcels, each participant’s brain was aligned to the Human Connectome Project Multi-Modal Parcellation (v1) atlas44 in Montreal Neurological Institute space and then back-transformed into native space.24,103 A mask was created, separately in the LH and RH, that only included voxels within the ventral stream visual cortex parcel.44 To correct for multiple comparisons, within this mask for each hemisphere, Monte Carlo simulations of noise datasets (thresholded at p < 0.001) were run 10,000 times to approximate the minimum size of a cluster of significant voxels that would not be expected due to chance alone (at an α of 0.05).104,105 With the first two runs of the data, a CS ROI was then defined, as the collection of clustered positive voxels for the balanced contrast of the β-weights for the given stimulus category against all other categories, above the designated cluster threshold and within the ventral stream visual cortex mask. CS amplitude was defined as the median β-weight across all voxels for a given stimulus category on the third run of data, within the corresponding CS ROI (defined with the first two runs of the data).

fMRI data from the adaptation paradigm were preprocessed as above, except all six runs were concatenated, and a Gamma basis function instead of a boxcar, was convolved with the hemodynamic response function, given the abbreviated block lengths. A GLM was fit to the basis function with each block type (e.g., same/alternating/different x faces/objects/words) as predictors. The median β-weight across every voxel was calculated for each block condition within the preferred CS-ROI (e.g., same faces in the face-selective ROI, different words in the word-selective ROI).

For the behavioral data, mean accuracy was computed per participant on each task. Mean accuracy on a given task was discarded for an individual participant if it was at or below 33% on the face and object recognition tasks (with three response choices) or 50% on the word recognition task (with two response choices). No participants performed below chance on either the face or object recognition tasks. One control patient performed at chance on the word recognition task.

Statistical analysis

For each dependent measure (e.g., CS amplitude, RS, behavioral accuracy), the same statistical approach was applied. First, following the procedures outlined by Fox & Weisberg37 and Brown,36 an LMEM was fit with the complete interaction of relevant predictors (e.g., group, stimulus category, hemisphere) as a fixed effect, mean-centered age as a fixed covariate, and participant modeled as a random intercept. Note that by modeling participant as a random effect, we could model on both LH and RH data from TD controls, while solely LH or RH data from patients. The LRT was used to compare the model with the complete interaction vs. each lower-order model without the complete interaction (e.g., a model with a three-way interaction was compared to a model with all two-way interactions, each model with a two-way interaction was compared to a model without each two-way interaction, etc.; see Tables in supplemental information for listing of all factors in a model and then the selection of the best fit). Models with the highest order interaction were compared to the next most complex model, and so on. In these stepwise comparisons, if two models provided significantly different fits of the data, the one with the lower AIC was tested, and no further comparisons were made. All models included group (the key predictor of interest) and age (the primary covariate, expected to affect each dependent variable).14,32,106 In approximating fixed effects, the maximum log likelihood criterion was optimized,86 and the Bound Optimization by Quadratic Approximation algorithm was applied.107 Wald χ2-tests were employed to calculate p-values for each effect.37 If an interaction effect was statistically significant, post hoc contrasts on the EMMs for meaningful factor comparisons were calculated.108

Two statistical approaches were adopted from prior work with small (though smaller) sample sizes of this patient population.22,23,24 First, the three groups (OTC patients, control patients, and TD controls) were compared to one another using permutation testing. For each model evaluated, group labels were shuffled 1000 times without replacement, and the model was refit. A Wald χ2-test was run on each refitted model, and a p-value was calculated as the proportion of instances across the permutations in which the derived χ2-value on a refitted model was greater than the true χ2-value. In addition, as group is a three-level variable, if the group effect was statistically significant, post hoc contrast testing was performed. On each of the shuffled datasets, a Z score was computed for each pairwise comparison, and the proportion of instances across the permutations in which each Z score was greater than each true Z score was computed. Additionally, to further address the small sample size, an approach developed by Crawford et al.79 to compare individual patient measures to small control samples (while regressing out age) was adopted. Individual comparisons between patients and TD controls were performed separately with data from TD controls' LHs and RHs.

In addition to the above, we computed Pearson correlations between RS values for faces, objects, and words with the corresponding behavioral accuracy for face, object, and word recognition. A bivariate two-tailed test was used, and Bonferroni correction was used within each group’s paired RS-accuracy analysis for faces, objects, and words.

Finally, RSA was conducted to examine the pattern of activation in the ventral stream. For each participant, an activation RDM was constructed by computing the similarity between each block type (e.g., same/alternating/different x faces/objects/words) across all voxels from the ventral stream visual cortex parcel using Pearson correlation. Three model RDMs were then constructed based on properties of the ventral stream related to CS and RS. Each participant’s activation RDM was compared to each of the three model RDMs using Spearman’s correlation. Spearman’s correlation maps for each participant were Fisher transformed. The effects of group, hemisphere, and model type (CS, RS, CS-RS) on the Fisher transformation was analyzed using the same approach as for predicting other dependent measures (e.g., CS amplitude, behavioral accuracy) as described above.

All statistical analyses were performed in R,84 with the exception of the RSA, which was conducted in MATLAB and the RS-behavior correlations, which were conducted in SPSS (Key Resources). For a complete list of R packages used, see Key Resources.37,85,86,87,88,89,90,91,92 Select code was written with the assistance of ChatGPT (Key Resources). α criterion for statistical significance was 0.05. A BF was also calculated by comparing a model with a given effect to a null model without that effect: BFs above 3 or below 0.33 can be interpreted as evidence supporting the null or alternative hypothesis, respectively.38,109 The Benjamini-Hochberg correction39 was applied to p-values of each set of post hoc comparisons and each set of Crawford comparisons (e.g., all patients compared to one hemisphere of the TD controls on a given measure).
