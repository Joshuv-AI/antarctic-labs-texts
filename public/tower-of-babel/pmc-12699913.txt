Comprehensive genomic analysis of the expansin gene family in pecan (Carya illinoinensis) and their transcriptional responses to stresses and fruit abscission
Authors: Gang Wang, Tao Wang, Guo-ming Wang, Zhan-hui Jia, Ji-ping Xuan, Ji-yu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExpansins (EXPs) are important components of the plant cell wall. They are involved in plant growth and development and diverse environmental stress responses by promoting cell-wall loosening and cell enlargement. Although EXPs have been characterized in many plant species, little is known about the EXPs in Carya illinoinensis.MethodsThe CiEXP gene family was systematically analyzed using bioinformatics. RNA-seq data (both from our study and public databases) and qRT-PCR were employed to analyze the expression patterns of the CiEXP family in different tissues, under biotic and abiotic stress, and in persistent versus abscised fruit.ResultsIn this study, a total of 39 EXP genes unevenly distributed on 14 chromosomes were identified in the C. illinoinensis genome, which were classified into four subfamilies (27 CiEXPAs, 3 CiEXPBs, 2 CiEXLAs, and 7 CiEXLBs), and the motif and gene structures were consistent with this subfamily classification. Thirty-six pairs of duplicated genes were identified, suggesting that gene duplication may have contributed to the expansion of the EXP gene family. Collinearity analysis provided further phylogenetic insights into the EXP gene family. Cis-acting element analysis revealed that the promoter regions of the CiEXPs gene were associated with hormone-responsive, plant growth and development, and stress-responsive, particularly ABA response element (ABRE) and MeJA-responsive element. The expression results showed that most CiEXPs exhibited tissue-specific expression patterns, and some CiEXPs were highly responsive to abiotic and biotic stresses. Additionally, most CiEXPA genes, CiEXPB1/2, and CiEXLA2 were up-regulated in persistent fruit.ConclusionsOur study findings enhance the understanding of the CiEXP gene family and facilitate the selection of suitable candidate genes for further study, which lays a foundation for future investigations into the functional roles of specific CiEXPs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07666-5.

Background

Pecan [Carya illinoinensis (Wangenh.) K. Koch], which belongs to the genus Carya (Juglandaceae family) and is native to North America [1], is an economically important nut and woody oil tree worldwide. Despite pecan has only been cultivated in China for about 120 years, it has become a rapidly developing nut crop due to its high nutritional and economic value. However, more than 57% of young fruits abscise within 3–8 weeks post-anthesis [2], and this excessive fruit drop is a primary constraint on yield in pecan production. Consequently, reducing physiological fruit abscission and increasing yield have become important issues in pecan-growing countries.

The breakdown of cell walls and cell separation in the abscission zone are essential for fruit abscission. As a key structural component in plants, the cell wall consists primarily of polysaccharides and is one of the most significant characteristics differentiating plants from animals [3]. Beyond providing mechanical support and rigidity to the plant body, the cell wall plays critical roles in plant growth and development, hormone signaling, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [4]. Plant cells can rapidly modify their shape or structure through coordinated cell wall loosening and expansion, enabling them to transform into various differentiated cells [5, 6]. Among them, the EXPANSIN (EXP) gene family is an important regulator of extension and relaxation of cells in normal plant growth and development as well as stress responses [5]. They break the hydrogen bonds between cellulose microfibrils and hemicelluloses through pH-dependent non-enzymatic activity, thereby regulating the relaxation degree between cell wall components and increasing cell wall flexibility [7]. This mechanism was first identified in an experiment investigating acid-induced extension in cucumber hypocotyls [8].

A typical EXP protein comprises 250–275 amino acids, including two functional domains and a signal peptide with a length of 20–30 amino acid residues at the N-terminal [9, 10]. Domain 1 (N-terminal) is a double-psi beta-barrel (DPBB), which is cysteine-rich, and its core sequence has high homology with the catalytic domain of glycoside hydrolase family 45 proteins (GH45) [11]; Domain 2 (C-terminal) shares 50% similarity with Group-II pollen allergens [11, 12]. According to phylogenetic relationships, plant EXP proteins can be divided into four subfamilies: EXPA (α-expansin), EXPB (β-expansin), EXLA (expansin-like A), and EXLB (expansin-like B) [10, 13]. Among them, EXPAs and EXPBs have been widely studied and primarily involved in cell expansion and plant developmental processes [13, 14], whereas EXLAs and EXLBs regulate hypocotyls elongation, root architecture, and stress response [15–17]. To date, EXP genes have been identified across a wide range of plant species, including encompassing model plants (e.g., Arabidopsis thaliana [10], tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) [18], rice (Oryza sativa) [19]), cash crops (e.g., wheat (Triticum aestivum) [9], maize (Zea mays) [20], sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum) [21], soybean (Glycine max) [22], cotton (Gossypium spp.) [23], potato (Solanum tuberosum) [24], Brachypodium distachyon [25], sugar beet (Beta vulgaris) [26], sweetpotato (Ipomoea batatas) [11]), horticultural plants (e.g., tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) [27], Brassica spp. [28, 29], apple (Malus domestica) [30], banana (Musa spp.) [31], cucumber (Cucumis sativus) [32], netted melon (Cucumis melo) [33], watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) [34], grapevine (Vitis vinifera) [35], Chinese jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) [36], pomegranate (Punica granatum) [37]), forest trees (e.g., Populus [38], Ginkgo biloba [39], moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis) [12]), and primitive plants (e.g., ferns and bryophytes [40]), as well as other like Salvia miltiorrhiza [41].

Previous studies have demonstrated that EXPs participate in nearly all plant growth and development processes related to cell wall relaxation, including seed germination [42], root elongation [43], leaf and stem development [44, 45], pollen tube elongation [46], fruit softening and ripening [47, 48], and fruits abscission [49]. In addition, EXPs are also involved in abiotic stress [50–53], biotic stress [31], and nutrient efficiency [54].

The identification of molecular characteristics of key EXP gene family members will contribute to further understanding of the regulatory mechanisms in plant development and adaptation to environmental stresses [11]. Recently, the available pecan genome database [55] has enabled us to perform a genome-wide analysis of the EXP gene family in C. illinoinensis. In this study, 39 CiEXP genes were identified from the pecan cultivar ‘Pawnee’ genome (PRJNA680556). Phylogenetic relationships, chromosomal location, conserved motifs and domains, gene structure, molecular characteristics, cis-elements, and expression patterns of CiEXPs in different tissues and under various stresses were investigated. This study provides a potential reference for understanding the characteristics of the pecan EXP family and their molecular mechanisms in response to environmental stresses.

Based on previous standardized nomenclature, the CiEXPs were divided into four subfamilies and sequentially named according to their chromosomal position [34]. Physicochemical properties of CiEXPs Nomenclature, peptide lengths, molecular weights (MW), theoretical isoelectric points (pI), grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), instability indices

The amino acid length of CiEXPs ranged from 226 (CiEXPA12) to 286 (CiEXLA1) amino acids (aa), with molecular weight (MW) between 24.16 kDa (CiEXPA12) and 31.09 kDa (CiEXLA1) (Table 1). The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) of the CiEXPs family members ranged from 5.07 (CiEXPB3) to 9.67 (CiEXPA1). Grand Average of Hydropathicity (GRAVY) analysis revealed that 35 CiEXPs were hydrophilic proteins, while 3 CiEXPs were hydrophobic (Table 1). Among them, CiEXPA15 exhibited the strongest hydrophilicity, whereas CiEXPA12 showed the highest hydrophobicity. Additionally, the instability index indicates that most CiEXPs are stable, as their instability indexes were lower than 40 (Table 1). The prevalence of hydrophilic (35/39) and stable (most instability index < 40) CiEXP proteins aligns with their role as extracellular cell wall proteins, which require solubility and structural stability to function in the apoplast.

Phylogenetic analysis of EXP proteins

To evaluate the evolutionary relationships of EXP proteins, a phylogenetic analysis was performed using the complete protein sequences of 39 CiEXPs from C. illinoinensis, 36 AtEXPs from Arabidopsis, and 58 OsEXPs from rice. The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) revealed that all EXPs were clustered into four major subfamilies: α-expansin (EXPA), β-expansin (EXPB), expansin-like A (EXLA), and expansin-like B (EXLB). This classification was based on tree topology, clade support values, and reported studies on EXP classification in Arabidopsis and rice. Accordingly, the four subfamilies in C. illinoinensis were designated CiEXPA (27 members), CiEXPB (3 members), CiEXLA (2 members), and CiEXLB (7 members) (Fig. 1). Furthermore, a comparative analysis of EXP gene family members across 35 reported species showed that the EXPA subfamily had the largest members, followed by the EXPB subfamily, while EXLA and EXLB had the fewest members (Supplementary Table 2).

Motif and gene structure analyses of EXP in C. illinoinensis

The conserved motifs and gene structures within each subfamily of CiEXPs were largely consistent (Fig. 2). Ten types of motifs were identified in CiEXPs (Supplementary Table 3). According to the motif analyses, it was observed that motifs 2 and 6 were present in all CiEXPs proteins, while motifs 7 and 4 were found in most CiEXPs (Fig. 2B). Most proteins in the EXPA subgroup followed the motif arrangement: motif 5–motif 2–motif 8–motif 1–motif 3–motif 6–motif 4–motif 7 (Fig. 2A, B). However, motif 8 was absent in CiEXPA4, CiEXPA6, and CiEXPA22, whereas CiEXPA12 lacked motifs 5, 3, and 4. The proteins in the EXPB subgroup typically comprised motif 5–motif 2–motif 1–motif 3–motif 6–motif 4–motif 7 (Fig. 2A, B), and the motif structure of the member was similar to the EXPA subfamily. In the EXLA subgroup, proteins contained motif 2–motif 10–motif 9–motif 6–motif 4–motif 7 (Fig. 2A, B). For the EXLB subgroup, almost all proteins harbored motifs 2, 10, 9, 6, and 7, though CiEXLB3 uniquely contained motif 5 instead of motif 2 (Fig. 2A, B). Notably, CiEXPA and CiEXPB subgroups commonly contained unique motifs 1 and 3, while motifs 10 and 9 were specific to the CiEXLA and CiEXLB subgroups.

Gene structure analysis revealed that the intron numbers of CiEXP genes varied from 1 to 5, with members of the same subfamily sharing similar exon-intron structures (Fig. 2A, C). For example, the majority of the EXPA members had 2 or 3 introns; the members of EXPBs and EXLAs subfamilies typically possessed 3 and 4 introns, respectively; EXLB subfamily members contain 3 or 4 introns (Fig. 2A, C).

Chromosome localization of CiEXP genes in C. illinoinensis

Chromosome distribution analysis exhibited that 39 CiEXP genes were located on 14 out of 16 chromosomes in C. illinoinensis, with no CiEXP genes found on chromosomes 15 and 16 (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 4). The number of CiEXP genes on each chromosome ranged from 1 to 10. Chromosome 11 harbored the highest number of CiEXP genes (10), chromosome 3, 5, 6, 9, 12 and 13 each contained three CiEXP genes, chromosomes 1, 4, 7, and 8 had two CiEXP genes each, whereas the remaining chromosomes (2, 10, and 14) carried only one CiEXP gene (Fig. 3). In addition, the CiEXPA genes were distributed across 14 chromosomes, three CiEXPB genes were located on chromosomes 3, 4, and 13, two CiEXLA genes were found on chromosomes 5 and 6, six CiEXLB genes were located on chromosome 11, while the remaining one was located on chromosomes 12 (Fig. 3).

Collinearity analysis of CiEXPs in C. illinoinensis

A total of 36 segmental duplicates pairs were identified on 14 chromosomes based on the physical locations of the 39 CiEXPs; each chromosome contained segmental duplicates, and most of which belonged to the EXPA subfamily (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 5). This observation not only provides a plausible explanation for the numerical expansion of the EXPA subfamily but also suggests the functions of EXPA genes in regulating plant development and response to stress than other subfamilies. The Ka/Ks ratios of all duplicates pairs were less than 1 (Supplementary Table 6), indicating that the CiEXP family has undergone strong purifying selection during evolution. The lowest Ka/Ks ratio for CiEXPA2/CiEXPA27 suggests these genes may encode core functions in pecan growth (e.g., cell expansion during fruit development), while the higher ratio for CiEXPA12/CiEXPA13, CiEXPB1/CiEXPB2 implies they may have evolved faster to adapt to species-specific stresses.

Cis-element prediction in CiEXP promoter regions

A total of 438 cis-elements were found in the promoter regions of CiEXP genes (Supplementary Tables 7 − 1), mainly associated with 15 types of biological processes. These were roughly categorized into three major classes based on physiological processes: hormone responsiveness, growth and development, and stress responsiveness (Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 7 − 2). The number of cis-elements in each gene varied significantly (Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 7 − 2). Among them, CiEXPA17 contained the largest number (28) of cis-elements, while no cis-elements were detected in CiEXPA12, CiEXPA13, or CiEXLB5. In terms of quantity, the cis-elements primarily participated in hormone pathways, accounting for 312 elements, including the Abscisic acid (ABA)-responsive elements (ABRE, 114), Auxin-responsive elements (29), Gibberellin (GA)-responsive elements (34), Methyl jasmonate (MeJA)-responsive elements (107), and Salicylic acid (SA)-responsive elements (28). The second category was associated with growth and development (48 elements), including Circadian control elements (13), Endosperm expression elements (4), Meristem expression elements (17), Palisade mesophyll cell differentiation elements (4), and Seed-specific regulatory elements (10). The third category was related to stress responsiveness (74 elements), including Defense and stress-responsive elements (14), Dehydration-, low-temperature-, and salt-stress elements (2), Drought-inducibility elements (32), Low-temperature responsive elements (25), and Wound-responsive element (1) (Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 7 − 2). In summary, the EXPA subfamily had a higher number of cis-elements across all three categories compared to the other subfamilies (Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 7 − 3).

We analyzed the number of major cis-elements in the promoter regions for further functional analysis (Fig. 6). ABREs were the most abundant cis-element. Numerous EXP genes had more than three ABREs in their promoter regions, such as CiEXPA3/8/16/17/19/22/23/26/, CiEXPB1/2/3, CiEXLA2, and CiEXLB1/2/6. The CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif, both involved in MeJA response, were also highly abundant hormone-responsive elements in the CiEXPs promoters. Additionally, the TCA motif and MBS motif are involved in SA response and drought-induced response, respectively, and these elements were also abundant. Notably, some CiEXP genes (CiEXPA3/5/7/8/16/17/19/23/26, CiEXPB3, CiEXLA2, and CiEXLB6) contained multiple response elements in their promoter regions, which can be selected as candidates for further functional and regulatory studies.

Expression pattern of CiEXPs genes in different tissues of pecan

The expression patterns of CiEXP genes were analyzed by qRT-PCR across eight pecan tissues: kernel, male flower, female flower, young fruit, shuck, young root, stem, and young leaf. The results revealed that the expression levels of CiEXP generally varied widely (Fig. 7; Supplementary Fig. 1). Most CiEXP members were significantly expressed in male flowers, female flowers, young fruits, young roots, and stems, but showed low expression in kernels, shucks, and young leaves. Additionally, certain genes displayed distinct tissue-specific expression. For example, CiEXPA19 was predominantly expressed in kernels, while CiEXPA7, CiEXPA15, and CiEXLB7 showed high expression in young leaves. CiEXPA2 and CiEXLB6 had the highest expression levels in male flowers. CiEXPA11, CiEXPA12, CiEXPA13, CiEXPA14, and CiEXPA22 were most abundant in roots (Fig. 7; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Expression patterns of CiEXP genes in response to abiotic and biotic stresses

To further investigate the role of CiEXP genes in abiotic (drought) and biotic (Fusicladium effusum infection) stress responses, we analyzed their expression patterns using publicly available transcriptome data. After excluding five genes with undetectable expression, heatmap analysis revealed that 34 CiEXP genes responded to drought stress (Fig. 8; Supplementary Tables 8 − 1). Among them, the expression levels of CiEXPA13, CiEXLB6, and CiEXLB7 were gradually upregulated, whereas CiEXPA18 was gradually downregulated. Additionally, CiEXPA1/4/6/7/9/16/19/25/26/27, and CiEXPB1 showed significant upregulation followed by downregulation, and the peak of them was discovered at 3 days post-treatment (Fig. 8; Supplementary Tables 8 − 1). Under pathogen infection conditions, 11 genes were not expressed (Fig. 8; Supplementary Tables 8 − 2). Notably, more than half (20) of the EXP genes were highly expressed in the disease-resistant cultivar ‘Jinhua’, while the remaining eight genes were expressed in the susceptible cultivar ‘Pawnee’ (Fig. 8; Supplementary Tables 8 − 2).

Expression patterns of CiEXP genes in persisting and abscised fruit

To elucidate the involvement of CiEXP genes in pecan fruit abscission, we analyzed the expression patterns of 39 CiEXP genes in persistent and abscised fruit using transcriptome data. The results indicated distinct expression profiles between the two types (Fig. 9; Supplementary Table 9). Most CiEXPA genes, along with CiEXPB1/2, and CiEXLA2 showed higher expression in persisting fruits (except CiEXPA11 and CiEXPA20), while CiEXPB3, CiEXLA1, and five CiEXLB genes were significantly upregulated in abscised fruit.

Discussion

In plant cells, the cell wall is an essential and unique structure that regulates cell shape and size, provides mechanical support, and functions as the first line of defense against external environmental stimuli [56]. Expansins (EXPs) proteins can relax plant cell walls and are widely regarded as key regulators of cell wall extension during plant growth. They play critical roles in plant growth, development, and responses to abiotic and biotic stress of a variety of plant species [51, 57].

Characterization of CiEXPs in C. illinoinensis

In this study, 39 CiEXP genes were identified from the C. illinoinensis genome based on the presence of both Pollen_allerg_1 and DPBB_1 domains, fewer than monocots but similar to dicots (Supplementary Table 2), supports divergent EXP family expansion between monocots and dicots, likely driven by differences in cell wall complexity.

The 39 CiEXP were classified into four subfamilies through phylogenetic analysis: 27 CiEXPA, 3 CiEXPB, 2 CiEXLA, and 7 CiEXLB (Fig. 1), consistent with patterns observed in other plant species (Supplementary Table 2). Though the sizes of the EXP subfamily are unevenly distributed in different plant species, the EXPA subgroup consistently contains the most members in all plants. Notably, the CiEXPB members in C. illinoinensis are fewer than in other species, possibly due to differences in cell wall composition and evolutionary divergence.

Despite there are significant variations in CiEXP protein properties, the conserved motifs and gene structures were highly similar within each subfamily (Fig. 2), supporting their evolutionary relatedness and classification. Motif analysis revealed that CiEXPA and CiEXPB proteins commonly contained unique motifs 1 and 3, whereas CiEXLA and CiEXLB proteins typically harbor motifs 10 and 9. This evolutionary conservation may serve as a key criterion for subfamily classification and could play functional roles in plant development. Gene structure analysis showed that most CiEXP genes contain 2–4 introns, whereas CiEXPA12 diverged from this pattern by containing 5 introns.

Evolutionary relationships and collinearity analysis of CiEXPs in C. illinoinensis

The 39 CiEXP genes in C. illinoinensis were unevenly distributed on 14 chromosomes (Fig. 3). Similar unequal distributions have been observed in S. miltiorrhiza [41], sweetpotato [11], potato [24], and sugarcane [21], among others. This suggests that the number of CiEXP genes on each chromosome is independent of chromosome size, likely due to unequal replication of chromosomal segments.

Gene family expansion primarily stems from tandem duplications, large-scale chromosomal segment duplications, and transposition events [58]. Among these, gene duplication is considered a key driver of genome evolution, providing raw genetic materials for new gene emergence and functional diversification [11]. In this study, we identified 36 segmental duplicates pairs (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 5), which may have contributed to the expansion of the EXP gene family. Notably, the duplicated CiEXP genes mainly come from the EXPA subfamily, indicating that expansions of CiEXPA genes may confer adaptive advantages for pecan growth, development, and environmental adaptation. The Ka/Ks ratios of all gene pairs were less than 1 (Supplementary Table 6), indicating that the CiEXP family has undergone strong purifying selection during evolution. This further implies functional conservation among these genes.

Cis-elements analysis of CiEXPs in C. illinoinensis

In plants, the expression patterns of EXP genes are influenced by environmental and internal factors, enabling their involvement in developmental processes and responses to various signals. These expression patterns are likely regulated by corresponding cis-elements in their promoter regions [36]. Our study identified cis-elements in the promoters of CiEXP genes linked to hormone responsiveness, growth and development, and stress response (Figs. 5 and 6; Supplementary Table 7). These findings are consistent with previous reports on EXP gene promoters in other plant species [11, 33, 34], indicating evolutionary conservation of cis-regulatory elements across species. Notably, the number and types of cis-elements varied significantly among CiEXP genes, implying differential regulation through signal-specific pathways. Consistent with our results, most CiEXP promoters contained hormone-related cis-elements, particularly those responsive to ABA and MeJA (Supplementary Table 7), which were the most abundant cis-elements. Recent studies support the role of hormone signaling pathways in regulating CiEXP gene functions [11, 33]. Key cis-elements and their associated functions included: hormone responses (The ABRE, TGA-element, CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif, TATC-box, P-box, and TCA-element); circadian control (circadian element); tissue-specific developmental (the CAT-box, RY-element, GCN4-motif, and CGTCA-motif), abiotic stress responses (MBS, LTR, TC-rich repeats, and WUN-motif). These results suggest that cis-elements may function as essential regulators of hormonal signaling, growth and development, and stress responses in C. illinoinensis.

Expression patterns and functional prediction of CiEXPs in C. illinoinensis

Gene expression profiles are intrinsically linked to biological functions, and characterizing these patterns can provide crucial insights into gene functionality. The functional diversity of EXP genes has been extensively demonstrated, playing pivotal roles in plant growth, development, and responses to diverse stresses through complex regulatory networks. The results of qRT-PCR analyses showed that most CiEXPs exhibit tissue-specific expression patterns (Fig. 7 and Figure Supplementary 1). Differential expression was observed across various tissues: multiple CiEXP members showed higher expression in reproductive (male and female flowers) and vegetative tissues (young fruit, roots, and stems), while CiEXPA19 exhibited kernel-specific expression. Notably, CiEXPA7, CiEXPA15, and CiEXLB7 showed high expression in young leaves, whereas CiEXPA2 and CiEXLB6 had the highest expression levels in the male flower. Additionally, CiEXPA11, CiEXPA12, CiEXPA13, CiEXPA14, and CiEXPA22 were most highly expressed in roots. These findings suggest that different CiEXP genes may play an important role in specific tissues.

EXP genes are known to play critical roles in stress responses, particularly under drought stress. For instance, overexpression of NtEXPA11 modulates plant growth and development and enhances drought resistance in tobacco [50], while overexpression of wild Arachis AdEXLB8 in tobacco also improves tolerance to drought stress [59]. In this study, transcriptome analyses revealed that 34 CiEXP genes responded to drought stress (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 9). More than half (20) of these genes were highly expressed in the disease-resistant ‘Jinhua’ variety, while the remaining eight genes were upregulated in the susceptible ‘Pawnee’ variety (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 10). This suggests that these genes may contribute to stress adaptation and help plants mitigate damage caused by various stresses. Of particular interest are four CiEXP genes (CiEXPA5, CiEXPA8, CiEXLA2, and CiEXLB7) that positively respond to both abiotic and biotic stresses, implying their involvement in the crosstalk between stress signaling pathways. A comprehensive analysis of phylogenetic relationships, evolutionary patterns, cis-elements, and gene expression profiles can provide valuable insights into gene function. It is important to note that this study is primarily based on transcriptome and qRT-PCR data; future functional validation is essential to confirm the roles of candidate genes in fruit abscission and stress tolerance. Based on our results, priority candidates for future functional study include CiEXLA2 and CiEXLB7, which were responsive to drought and scab stress, and CiEXPA2/9/26, which were higher expressed in persistent fruits. These genes represent promising targets for molecular breeding efforts aimed at developing stress-tolerant and high-yielding pecan cultivars.

Conclusions

In this study, 39 EXP genes of C. illinoinensis were identified at the whole genome level. Then we systematically investigated their molecular characteristics, phylogenetic classification, conserved motifs, gene structures, chromosomal distributions, cis-regulatory elements, and collinearity relationships. Moreover, the expression profiling using qRT-PCR and RNA-seq revealed distinct tissue-specific and stress-responsive patterns among CiEXP family members. In summary, this study provides valuable genomic resources for understanding the evolutionary dynamics of plant EXP genes and lays the foundation for selecting candidate genes and investigating their specific functions.

Methods

Identification and characterization of EXP in C. illinoinensis

The genome data and GFF annotation files of pecan cultivar ‘Pawnee’ (C. illinoinensis) were downloaded from the Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/CillinoinensisPawnee_v1_1). Protein sequences of expansins (EXPs) from Arabidopsis and rice were obtained from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) and the Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/), respectively. Then, the Arabidopsis and rice EXP protein sequences were used as query sequences to carry out Blastp search against all C. illinoinensis protein sequences with TBtools software [60], applying an E-value threshold of < 10− 5 to identify candidate proteins. Subsequently, utilizing conserved domains of DPBB_1 (PF03330) and Pollen_allerg_1 (PF01357) derived from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org), a hidden Markov model (HMM) of the EXP was constructed. The putative CiEXPs proteins were further validated using HMM search with the E-value threshold was < 10− 5, and exclude protein sequences that lack these EXP-specific domains. Finally, we manually checked the remaining sequences to ensure they contained the essential EXP domains. The identified CiEXP sequences were analyzed using TBtools to determine their amino acid (aa) length, molecular weights (MW), theoretical isoelectric points (pI), instability indices, aliphatic indices, and grand average of hydropathicity (gravy).

Phylogenetic analysis of EXPs

First, the protein sequences of AtEXPs, OsEXPs, and CiEXPs were aligned using ClustalW with default parameters. Then, the phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbour-joining (NJ) method by MEGA 5.0 software. The detailed parameters were as follows: Poisson model, pairwise deletion, and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized and refined using the iTOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/).

Conserved domains and gene structure analysis of CiEXPs

Conserved motifs in CiEXPs were identified using the MEME online program (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme), with the maximum number of motifs set to 10. The gene structure (exon–intron) distributions of the CiEXP genes were determined based on the coding sequence (CDS) and GFF annotation files from the pecan genome. The results were visualized and analyzed using TBtools software.

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements of CiEXPs

To identify potential regulatory elements, 2 kb promoter regions upstream of the CiEXP coding sequences were extracted from the genome database. Then, these extracted sequences were analyzed using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict cis-regulatory elements (CREs). Finally, the results, including CRE types and numbers, were visualized using TBtools.

Chromosomal location, gene duplication, and collinearity analysis of CiEXPs

The chromosomal locations of CiEXP genes were determined using GFF annotation information in TBtools. Tandem duplication events of CiEXP genes were analyzed using the one-step MCScanX–Super program in TBtools. Genomic collinearity relationships were constructed using Advanced Circos, with visualization based on syntenic gene pairs and their chromosomal coordinates. To assess evolutionary selection patterns, we performed pairwise sequence analysis to calculate non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates for gene pairs, followed by computation of Ka/Ks ratios using TBtools.

Plant materials treatment and gene expression analysis

We collected young leaves, male flowers, female flowers, stems, young fruit, shucks, and kernels from nine healthy randomly selected 10-year-old ‘Pawnee’ pecan trees at the experimental station of the Institute of Botany, Jiangsu Province and Chinese Academy of Sciences (32°18′N; 118°52′ E). Additionally, we grew ‘Pawnee’ seedlings from seeds in controlled growth chamber conditions with a 16/8 h light/dark cycle, and collected young roots from 1-year-old seedlings. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80◦C for RNA extraction.

Total RNA was isolated using a Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using a TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China), then diluted the cDNA 10-fold and stored at −20◦C for real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments. The specific primers of all CiEXP genes were designed using Primer Premier 5.0, with an actin gene (CiActin, CiPaw.03G124400.1) serving as the reference gene for normalization. The detailed list of primers is shown in Supplementary Table 10. The PCR reaction system and amplification program were described in a previous study [61]. Three independent technical and biological repetitions were performed for each sample. The results were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method [62]. A gene was defined as exhibiting tissue-specific expression if its expression level in a target tissue showed a log2 (fold change) > 2 with a statistical significance of P < 0.05 compared to its expression in the lowest-expressing tissue.

Transcriptome expression patterns analysis of CiEXP genes

To investigate CiEXP gene expression patterns in response to fungal infection, we performed RNA-seq analysis on fruit samples from two pecan cultivars: ‘Pawnee’ (displaying scab symptoms on the fruit exocarp) and ‘Jinhua’ (asymptomatic control). Samples were collected at the same developmental stage and sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The sequencing data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Archive with accession number (PRJNA1312143). The publicly available RNA-seq data in response to drought stress were analyzed from the NCBI under BioProject ID PRJNA799663. The experiment subjected one-year-old ‘Pawnee’ grafted seedlings to progressive drought stress by withholding water for 15 days, with the leaf samples collected at 0 (control), 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 days post-treatment [63]. Additionally, expression profiles during fruit abscission were examined using transcriptome data from our laboratory (NCBI BioProject ID: PRJNA1180220). Clean reads were obtained by filtering the raw reads with Trimmomatic v0.39 to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads (Q30 > 90%).

Gene expression levels for all CiEXP genes were quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) using StringTie. The resulting FPKM values were log2-transformed for normalization. Comparative expression patterns across different treatments and developmental stages were visualized as a heatmap using TBtools.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Crop, Armonk, NY, USA). We performed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test to assess significant differences among treatments at three confidence levels (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001). Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) for statistical graphs and TBtools for heatmaps generation.
