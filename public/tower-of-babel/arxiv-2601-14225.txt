Group Fourier filtering of quantum resources in quantum phase space
Luke Coffman, N. L. Diaz, Martín Larocca, Maria Schuld, M. Cerezo

Abstract
Recently, it has been shown that group Fourier analysis of quantum states, i.e., decomposing them into the irreducible representations (irreps) of a symmetry group, enables new ways to characterize their resourcefulness. Given that quantum phase spaces (QPSs) provide an alternative description of quantum systems, and thus of the group’s representation, one may wonder how such harmonic analysis changes. In this work we show that for general compact Lie-group quantum resource theories (QRTs), the entire family of Stratonovich–Weyl quantum phase space representations—characterized by the Cahill–Glauber parameter s s —has a clear resource-theoretic and signal-processing meaning. Specifically, changing s s implements a group Fourier filter that can be continuously tuned to favor low-dimensional irreps where free states have most of their support ( s = − 1 s=-1 ), leave the spectrum unchanged ( s = 0 s=0 ), or highlight resourceful, high-dimensional irreps ( s = 1 s=1 ). As such, distinct QPSs constitute veritable group Fourier filters for resources. Moreover, we show that the norms of the QRT’s free state Fourier components completely characterize all QPSs. Finally, we uncover an s s -duality relating the phase space spectra of free states and typical (Haar-random) highly resourceful states through a shift in s s . Overall, our results provide a new interpretation of QPSs and promote them to a signal-processing framework for diagnosing, filtering, and visualizing quantum resources.

From the previous, it is clear that QPSs and QRTs have a striking similarity: they are built from the exact same ingredients, a group of free operations and a reference free state. This connection has motivated a series of works exploring whether resourcefulness can be detected directly from

Despite the clear connections between QRTs and QPSs, the underlying connection between the Hilbert space of operators

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

\mathbb{G}

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is reflected,
mode by mode, in the different QPS representations. Addressing this gap is the main goal of the present work.

Our main contributions are as follows (see Fig.
1
). First, we show that for any compact Lie-group–covariant QRT (arising from a semi-simple Lie algebra) the SW kernels and all associated QPS representations are completely determined by the group Fourier (irrep) norms (or purities) of the reference free state. Second, we prove that the phase space kernel acts as a genuine group Fourier filter on these purities. That is, the purity in operator space and that in phase space are related by a simple irrep-dependent coefficient which arises from the free state purities. Not only can such filters kill off certain irrep components, but the Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
interpolates between free-adapted, low-pass phase space representations and resource-sensitive, high-pass ones. For
s=-1
the information of near-free states is preserved when going into the QPS, whereas that of high-resource states is mostly suppressed. The converse occurs for
s=1
, as here the information of free and near-free states is suppressed, while the components of resourceful states are amplified. In this way, representing states through irrep purities and a tunable group-Fourier filter is not merely formal, as it tells us which symmetry modes carry resource, and how a given choice of
s
makes a phase space representation free-friendly and smooth (
s=-1
) or resource-sensitive and highly structured (
s=1
). Choosing
s
is therefore a design choice that trades off classical interpretability against sensitivity to quantum resources. Third, we identify an
s
-duality relating the phase space spectra of free states and typical (Haar-random) highly resourceful states. That is, we show that the phase space spectra of free states and typical (Haar-random) highly resourceful states can be related through a shift in
s
. Then we illustrate how the QPS filtering picture clarifies when QPS negativity is or is not a faithful indicator of resource across several QRTs, including spin coherence, multipartite entanglement, and fermionic Gaussianity.

In this section we present several important concepts that will be used throughout the work. First, we will review well-known results of how, under the adjoint action of a unitary group, the space of linear operators of a quantum’s Hilbert space decomposes into subspaces called irreducible representations (irreps). Then, we will review the results of
Bermejo et al. 2025
where we defined projections and purities onto the irreps of operator space. Next, we briefly present the basic ingredients for QRTs
Chitambar and Gour 2019
. Finally, we will recall the SW correspondence to build QPSs, and present as well the irrep decomposition of functions over phase space. In the next section we will see how all these concepts are intimately related.

I.1
Group Fourier decomposition of the operator space

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space, and let
T
be an unitary irreducible representation

1

of a compact Lie group
\mathbb{G}
with associated semi-simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
spanned by the skew-hermitian operators
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}
.

We are interested in characterizing the action of the unitaries in
T(\mathbb{G})
over elements of the space of linear operators
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, i.e.,

T(g)AT^{\dagger}(g)\,,\text{ for $g\in\mathbb{G}$ and $A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})$}.

(1)

For this purpose, we recall the following result
Serre et al. 1977
;
Fulton and Harris 1991
.

Definition 1

(Irrep decomposition of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
)
.

The action of
T(\mathbb{G})
induces a decomposition of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
into irreps as

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}V_{\lambda}\,,

(2)

where the index
\lambda
is finite, and runs over irreps and multiplicity label. For each
V_{\lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
we define a Hermitian orthonormal basis

V_{\lambda}={\rm span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{D_{j}^{\lambda}\}_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}},

(3)

with

\left\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},D_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\,\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\,,

(4)

where
d_{\lambda}=\dim(V_{\lambda})
, and where
\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}=\Tr[A^{\dagger}B]
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product for the operators
A,B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

Definition
1
implies that the space of linear operators decomposes into invariant subspaces under the action of the group. As such, given some
A\in V_{\lambda}
, then
\forall g\in\mathbb{G}
and one has
T(g)AT^{\dagger}(g)\in V_{\lambda}
. Thus, we can simplify the study of Eq. (
1
) by analyzing how the group acts on each irrep.

Indeed, given some unitary
T(g)
, which we assume for simplicity to be of the form
T(g)=e^{\theta\,T(h)}
for
h\in\mathfrak{g}
, we can explicitly build its action over
V_{\lambda}
by studying how it acts over a basis of such a subspace, i.e., by the transformation
T(g)D_{j}^{\lambda}T^{\dagger}(g)
. This is achieved via the so-called adjoint and Adjoint representations on the
\lambda
-th irrep, defined as follows.

Definition 2

(adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{g}
in
V_{\lambda}
)
.

Given an operator
h
in
\mathfrak{g}
, the adjoint representation in the irrep
V_{\lambda}
is the map
\Phi_{\rm ad}^{\lambda}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}\times d_{\lambda}}
and is defined by

\left(\Phi_{\rm ad}^{\lambda}(h)\right)_{jj^{\prime}}=\Tr\left[iD_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\left[dT(h),iD_{j}^{\lambda}\right]\right]\,,

(5)

where
dT
id the Lie algebra homomorphism induced by
T
.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is compact, then
\Phi_{\rm ad}^{\lambda}
is faithful
Somma 2005
. Then, the adjoint representation of the Lie algebra induces–through the exponential map–a representation of the elements of
\mathbb{G}
on
V_{\lambda}
.

Definition 3

(Adjoint representation of
\mathbb{G}
in
V_{\lambda}
)
.

Given a group element
g=e^{\theta h}
of
\mathbb{G}
, where
h\in\mathfrak{g}
,
\Phi_{\rm ad}^{\lambda}
induces the unitary Adjoint representation
\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\lambda}}\right)
given by

\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(g=e^{\theta\,h}\right)=e^{\theta\,\Phi_{\rm ad}^{\lambda}(h)}\,.

(6)

Note that, due to linearity of the adjoint representation the Adjoint representation of some
\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(e^{\sum_{i}\theta_{i}\,h_{i}}\right)
is simply given by
e^{\sum_{i}\theta_{i}\,\Phi_{\rm ad}^{\lambda}(h_{i})}
. Importantly, Definitions
2
and
3
imply

T(g)D_{j}^{\lambda}T^{\dagger}(g)=\sum_{j^{\prime}}\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(g\right)\right)_{jj^{\prime}}D_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\,,

(7)

which faithfully determine how

acts on each irrep of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, via linearity we can fully characterize the transformation of Eq. (
1

At this point, we find it important to note that the notions of adjoint and Adjoint representations may appear
technical at first glance, but they are exactly what allows us to
organize operator space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
into symmetry sectors. In particular,
the adjoint action of
\mathbb{G}
on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is the one under which both the GFD purities and the SW kernels decompose into irreps. Making this action explicit now will let us track, later on, how each irrep
contributes to resource measures in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and to phase-space
functions.

Next, we will use Definition
1
to define the group Fourier decomposition (GFD) of an operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
as the collection of its projections into each irrep. Moreover, we dub the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of such projections as the GFD purities.

Definition 4

(GFD and purities of operators in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
)
.

Given a linear operator
A
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, its projection onto the
\lambda
-th irrep is given by

A_{\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\left\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},A\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}D_{j}^{\lambda}\,.

(8)

The GFD purity on the
\lambda
-th irrep is therefore obtained as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(A)

\displaystyle=\langle A_{\lambda},A_{\lambda}\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}|\left\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},A\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}|\Tr[D_{j}^{\lambda}A]|^{2}\,.

(9)

One can readily verify that since
A=\sum_{\lambda}A_{\lambda}
, then
\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(A)=\langle A,A\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}
, indicating that if
A
is normalized, then the collection of purities
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(A)
forms a probability distribution. Moreover, a straightforward calculation reveals that

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(T(g)AT^{\dagger}(g))=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(A)\,,\quad\forall g\in\mathbb{G}\,,

(10)

meaning that the GFD purities are invariant under conjugation by the unitaries in
T(\mathbb{G})
.

As we discuss in the next section, the GFD purities have been shown to be relevant within the context of QRTs
Bermejo et al. 2025
. Moreover, we will later see in this paper that they are also central to understand QPSs as group Fourier processing filters.

I.2
Quantum resource theories

Here we will define a QRT
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Brandao and Gour 2015
over
\mathcal{H}
in terms of two basic ingredients: a set of operations which are assumed to be easy and free to implement, and a reference state which is assumed to be resourceless. Naturally, all the states that can be obtained by evolving such reference state from free operations are also considered to be resourceless. For our purposes, we will consider QRTs with unitary, i.e., resource-preserving, free operations.

Definition 5

(Free operations and states in Lie-algebraic QRTs)
.

We define the free operations of a QRT as given by the irreducible representation
T
of a compact group
\mathbb{G}
. Then, we take the highest weight state of
T(\mathfrak{g})
as the reference state, which we denote as
\ket{{\rm hw}}
, such that the set of all free states is the orbit
\{T(g)\ket{{\rm hw}}\,,g\in\mathbb{G}\}
.

Note that in Definition
5
, we have chosen the reference state to be the highest weight of the algebra. While a priori this choice seems arbitrary, it turns out that most QRTs are implicitly picking this state
Bermejo et al. 2025
. Indeed, such choice makes the set of free states correspond to the so-called generalized coherent states
Barnum et al. 2003
;
Barnum et al. 2004
;
Perelomov 1977
;
Gilmore 1974
;
Zhang et al. 1990
. This also guarantees that free states minimize generalized uncertainty relations
Delbourgo and Fox 1977
;
Barnum et al. 2004
and that their orbits possess underlying Kähler structures
Kostant and Sternberg 1982
, somewhat making them the “most classical” set of states.

A central problem within QRTs is the identification, quantification and characterization of the resource present in a state
\ket{\psi}
which is not free. For instance, the coherent state fidelity
Regula 2017
;
Bravyi et al. 2019
;
Reardon-Smith 2024

\displaystyle S(\ket{\psi})

\displaystyle=\max_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\left\langle\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi},T(g)\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}T^{\dagger}(g)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\max_{g\in\mathbb{G}}|\langle{\rm hw}|T(g)|\psi\rangle|^{2}\,,

(11)

simply compares
\ket{\psi}
with all coherent states, and is equal to one if
\ket{\psi}
can be obtained by evolving the reference state with some free operation. In addition, there exist irreps in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
whose purities serve as resource quantifiers
Barnum et al. 2003
;
Barnum et al. 2004
. For instance, when
\mathfrak{g}
is simple one can verify that there is some
\lambda^{\prime}
for which
\mathcal{L}_{{\lambda}^{\prime}}={\rm span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{dT(\mathfrak{g})\}
and

\mathcal{P}_{{\lambda}^{\prime}}(\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi})\text{ is maximized iff }\ket{\psi}=T(g)\ket{{\rm hw}}

(12)

for some
g\in\mathbb{G}
.

While Eqs. (
I.2
)–(
12
) can determine whether a state is resourceful or not, the fact that they are single scalar quantities greatly limits the amount of information one can extract from them. To mitigate this issue, the recent work of Ref.
Bermejo et al. 2025
showed that the whole collection of GFD purities carries useful information regarding the structure of the resourcefulness in a given state. Notably, it was shown that free states tend to be supported in lower-dimensional irreps, whereas resourceful states have support in more, and higher-dimensional ones. Such behavior closely mimics that which is observed in classical harmonic analysis.

I.3
Quantum phase spaces

We begin by describing the basic ingredients of QPSs. Notably, their two basic ingredients are exactly the same as those that define QRTs with resource-preserving operations: a unitary representation
T
of a group
\mathbb{G}
, and a reference state. Here we will focus on the case when
\mathbb{G}
is a compact Lie group, and the reference state is the highest weight
\ket{{\rm hw}}
of the associated Lie algebra.

As in QRTs a central object of QPSs is the orbit
\{T(g)\ket{{\rm hw}}\,,g\in\mathbb{G}\}
, but its study is refined by defining the subgroup
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{G}
which stabilizes the reference state, i.e.,
\mathbb{K}
is composed of all the group elements
k
such that
T(k)
leave
\ket{{\rm hw}}
invariant up to a phase
factor. Specifically, one has

T(k)\ket{{\rm hw}}=e^{\phi(k)}\ket{{\rm hw}}\,,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{K}\,.

(13)

Indeed, for all group elements
g\in\mathbb{G}
there exists a decomposition in terms of left cosets as

g=\Omega k\,,\text{ where }k\in\mathbb{K}\,,\text{ and }\Omega\in\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{K}.

(14)

Since any pair of elements,
g=\Omega k
and
g^{\prime}=\Omega k^{\prime}
, will lead to the same state (up to a global phase), we identify free states with points
\Omega
in
\mathcal{X}:=\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{K}
such that

\ket{\Omega}:=T(\Omega)\ket{\rm hw}=T(g)\ket{\rm hw}

(15)

where
g=\Omega k
for some
k\in\mathbb{K}
. From here, one defines the QPS as the homogeneous space
\mathcal{X}:=\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{K}
:

Definition 6

(Quantum phase space)
.

Given a representation
T
of a group
\mathbb{G}
, a reference state
\ket{{\rm hw}}
and its associated stabilizer group
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{G}
, we define the QPS as
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{G}/\mathbb{K}
.

Bite that irreducibility of
T
indicates that the set of free states forms an overcomplete basis of
\mathcal{H}
as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)\ket{\Omega}\!\bra{\Omega}=\openone\,,

(16)

where
d\mu
indicates the uniform invariant measure over the phase space
\mathcal{X}
, normalized so that the right-hand-side of Eq. (
16
) is the identity matrix.

Starting from Definition
6
we can follow the SW correspondence
Brif and Mann 1999
which states that the linear injective mappings between the space of operators on the Hilbert
space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and that of square-integrable functions on the QPS
\mathcal{X}
, denoted as
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, satisfy the following properties:

Definition 7

(Properties of phase space functions)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
\Omega\in\mathcal{X}
. Then, any linear phase space function
F_{A}(\Omega,s)
parametrized by the real-valued Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
satisfies the following properties

(i)

Reality:
F_{A^{\dagger}}(\Omega,s)=F_{A}^{*}(\Omega,s)
,

(ii)

Standardization:
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)\,F_{A}(\Omega,s)=\Tr[A]
,

(iii)

Covariance:
F_{T^{\dagger}(g)AT(g)}(\Omega,s)=F_{A}(g\cdot\Omega,s)
,

(iv)

Tracing:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)F_{A^{\dagger}}(\Omega,s)F_{B}(\Omega,-s)\!=\!\Tr[A^{\dagger}B]
.

Note that the tracing property (guaranteed by Eq. (
16
)) can be expressed as

\langle F_{A}(\cdot,s),F_{B}(\cdot,-s)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}\,,

(17)

with

\langle F,G\rangle_{L^{2}}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)F^{*}(\Omega)G(\Omega)

(18)

Next, we remark that Riesz’s representation theorem states that for any
\Omega
there always exists an operator
\Delta(\Omega,s)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, called the SW kernel, such that

F_{A}(\Omega,s)=\langle\Delta(\Omega,s),A\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}=\Tr[\Delta(\Omega,s)^{\dagger}A]\,.

(19)

Since the tracing property holds
\forall B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, and thanks to injectivity, then we also have

A=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)F_{A}(\Omega,s)\Delta(\Omega,-s)\,.

(20)

The properties of phase space functions enumerated in Definition
7
induce properties on the SW kernels, as explained below.

Definition 8

(Properties of the SW kernels)
.

Let
\Delta(\Omega,s)
be the kernel associated with the SW phase space mapping parametrized by
s
. Then,
\Delta(\Omega,s)
satisfies the following properties

(i)

Hermiticity:
\Delta(\Omega,s)^{\dagger}=\Delta(\Omega,s)
.

(ii)

Resolution:
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)\,\Delta(\Omega,s)=\openone
.

(iii)

Covariance:
\Delta\bigl(g\!\cdot\!\Omega,s\bigr)=T(g)\,\Delta(\Omega,s)\,T^{\dagger}(g).

Because QPSs are not unique (e.g., different
s
parameters in the phase space functions lead to different representations of an operator
A
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
) we can relate different representations as

F_{A}(\Omega,s)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega^{\prime})K_{s,s^{\prime}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})F_{A}(\Omega^{\prime},s^{\prime})\,,

(21)

where

K_{s,s^{\prime}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})=\Tr[\Delta(\Omega,s)\Delta(\Omega^{\prime},-s^{\prime})]\,.

(22)

Indeed, Eq. (
22
) directly implies the relation

\Delta(\Omega,s)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega^{\prime})K_{s,s^{\prime}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})\Delta(\Omega^{\prime},s^{\prime})\,,

(23)

where we can see that setting
s=s^{\prime}
indicates that
K_{s,s}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})
plays the role of a delta function over the QPS
\mathcal{X}
.

Since the homogeneous space
\mathcal{X}
was originally constructed from the Lie group
\mathbb{G}
, it naturally admits an action of
\mathbb{G}
via translations, and hence so does phase space
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
. Therefore, just as we can decompose
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
into irreps, we can do the same for
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
via the Peter-Weyl theorem
Folland 2016
:

Theorem 1

(Irrep decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
)
.

The action of
\mathbb{G}
induces a decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
into irreps as

L^{2}(\mathcal{X})\cong\bigoplus_{\sigma}W_{\sigma}\,,

(24)

where the index
\sigma
runs over all infinitely many irreps and multiplicity label. For each
W_{\sigma}
we define an orthonormal basis of harmonic functions

W_{\sigma}={\rm span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{Y_{j}^{\sigma}(\Omega)\}_{j=1}^{d_{\sigma}},

(25)

with

\left\langle Y_{j}^{\sigma},Y_{j^{\prime}}^{\sigma^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\,\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\,,

(26)

where
d_{\sigma}=\dim(W_{\sigma})
, and where
\langle A,B\rangle_{L^{2}}
denotes the inner product over
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
defined in (
18
).

From here, we can define projections and purities onto the irreps of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
.

Definition 9

(GFD and purities of operators in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
)
.

Given a square integrable function
F(\Omega)
over
\mathcal{X}
, its projection onto the
\sigma
-th irrep is given by

F^{\sigma}(\Omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\sigma}}\left\langle Y_{j}^{\sigma},F\right\rangle_{L^{2}}Y_{j}^{\sigma}(\Omega)\,.

(27)

The GFD purity on the
\sigma
-th irrep is therefore obtained as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\sigma}(F)

\displaystyle=\langle F^{\sigma},F^{\sigma}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\sigma}}\left|\left\langle Y_{j}^{\sigma},F\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right|^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\sigma}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)(Y_{j}^{\sigma})^{*}(\Omega)F(\Omega)\right|^{2}\,.

(29)

In particular, because the phase space mapping is an isomorphism onto its image and further unitary when its image is equipped with the inner product in Equation (
17
), it is a unitary intertwiner. Therefore, the only irreps which can appear in the phase space functions
F_{A}(\Omega,s)
are those in which
A
has component. Then, note that, without loss of generality, we choose a basis of harmonic functions transforming as

Y_{j}^{\lambda}(g^{-1}\cdot\Omega)=\sum_{j^{\prime}}\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(g\right)\right)_{jj^{\prime}}Y_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\,.

(30)

As proved in Ref.
Brif and Mann 1999
the SW kernels take the explicit form

\Delta(\Omega,s)=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s/2}Y^{\lambda}_{j}(\Omega)D_{j}^{\lambda}\,,

(31)

where the index
\lambda
runs over the irreps (and multiplicities) appearing in the decomposition of operator space of Definition (
1
) (that is, only the irreps labels that exist in operator space, appear in the summation). Here,
\tau_{\lambda}
is defined as

\tau_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\bra{\Omega}D^{\lambda}_{j}\ket{\Omega}=Y^{\lambda}_{j}(\Omega)\,.

(32)

In particular, since
\tau_{\lambda}
is independent of
j
and
\Omega
one can pick
\Omega=e
(the identity over the cosets) and any
D^{\lambda}_{j}
in the weight zero subspace of
V_{\lambda}
(i.e., any
D^{\lambda}_{j}
commuting with the representation of the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
). Equation (
32
) implies that we can always express

D^{\lambda}_{j}=\tau_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)Y^{\lambda}_{j}(\Omega)\ket{\Omega}\!\bra{\Omega}\,,

(33)

and concomitantly

\displaystyle\tau_{\lambda}^{-s/2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle Y_{j}^{\lambda},\left\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},\Delta(\cdot,s)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},\left\langle Y_{j}^{\lambda},\Delta(\cdot,s)\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}\,.

Note that combining Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
) directly leads to the covariance property of the SW kernels.

To finish, we note that some SW kernels are of particular interest. For instance, taking
s=-1
one recovers the so-called Husimi Q function

\Delta(\Omega,-1)=\ket{\Omega}\!\bra{\Omega}\,,

(34)

and for
s=0
, one obtains the self-dual Wigner kernel

\Delta(\Omega,0)=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}Y^{\lambda}_{j}(\Omega)D_{j}^{\lambda}\,.

(35)

To finish, we refer the reader to the Appendix where we present a table summarizing all main concepts of the Hilbert space and QPS formulation of quantum mechanics.

II
Results

As previously mentioned, in Ref.
Bermejo et al. 2025
it was shown how the GFD in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
of a pure state
\rho
can characterize its resourcefulness within the context of a QRT (see Definition
5
). One can then wonder if mapping
\rho
to the QPS
\mathcal{X}
, and then performing its GFD decomposition, will lead to a different characterization. That is, we want to understand the differences and similarities which arise from projecting
\rho
onto the irreps of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
in Definition
1
, versus projecting
F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
onto the irreps of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
in Definition
1
.

II.1
Explicit formula for
\tau_{\lambda}
, and its connection to the GFD purity of free states

At first sight, the coefficients
\tau_{\lambda}
appearing in the SW
kernel expansion may look like a technical detail. A key message of
this work is that they are in fact the central object of SW construction of phase space functions, and thus to their interpretation as filters. Once the

\tau_{\lambda}
are known, essentially all irrep-level information in
the QPS is fixed. In particular, we will show (Eq. (
36
)) that

\tau_{\lambda}
can be read off directly from the GFD purities of the
reference free state, and that the same
\tau_{\lambda}

simultaneously control (i) the spectral content of the SW kernels
themselves, (ii) the filters that connect
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)
to
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s))
, and (iii) the inter-
s
conversion kernels and twisted products (Propositions
2

and
3
). This is why the rest of the paper repeatedly returns to
\tau_{\lambda}
:
they are the “master spectrum” from which all group-Fourier structure
of the QPS can be reconstructed. As such, we first find it important to present an explicit formula for this coefficient. Indeed, we note that while Eq. (
32
) provides a generic way to relate
D^{\lambda}_{j}
,
Y_{j}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
and
\tau_{\lambda}
, this result is not particularly constructive. However, we find that the following resul holds.

Proposition 1
.

The coefficient
\tau_{\lambda}
is related to the GFD purity in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
of the free highest weight state
\ket{{\rm hw}}
as

\tau_{\lambda}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\rm hw}\!\bra{\rm hw})}{d_{\lambda}}\,.

(36)

While most of our proofs are presented in the Appendix, we explicitly derive Proposition
1
(as well as other main results) in the main text as the derivation provides key insights. In particular, the following derivation of Proposition
1
contains an explicit recipe for constructing specific SW kernels that will be used in the examples below.

Proof.

We begin by taking
\Omega=e
. Here we find

\bra{e}D^{\lambda}_{j}\ket{e}=\bra{\rm hw}D^{\lambda}_{j}\ket{{\rm hw}}=\begin{cases}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle\,,\quad\text{if $D^{\lambda}_{j}\in V_{\lambda}^{0}$ $\forall\lambda$, }\\
0\,,\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

with
V_{\lambda}^{0}
the diagonal weight zero subspace of
V_{\lambda}
. Note that above, we have simply defined the shorthand notation
\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle=\bra{\rm hw}D^{\lambda}_{j}\ket{{\rm hw}}
for the non-zero elements of the irrep. In particular, let us define as
J_{\lambda}^{0}
the set of indexes such that
D_{j}^{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}^{0}
, and we define as
d_{\lambda}^{0}=\dim(V_{\lambda}^{0})=|J_{\lambda}^{0}|
the dimension of the weight zero subspace of
V_{\lambda}
. As such

\ket{e}\!\bra{e}=\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle D_{j}^{\lambda}\,.

(37)

Next, combining Eqs. (
7
) and (
15
) leads to

T(\Omega)D_{j}^{\lambda}T^{\dagger}(\Omega)=\sum_{j^{\prime}}\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{j,j^{\prime}}D_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\,,

(38)

with
\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}
is the irreducible unitary representation of
\mathbb{G}
defined above. Hence, Eq. (
37
) shows that

\displaystyle\ket{\Omega}\!\bra{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\sum_{j^{\prime}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{j,j^{\prime}}D_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\,.

(39)

From Eq. (
36
) we can identify

Y_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\tau_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\sum_{j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{j,j^{\prime}}\,.

(40)

Using the orthogonality of the harmonic functions of Eq. (
26
), we find

\displaystyle\delta_{l^{\prime}j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle Y_{l^{\prime}}^{\lambda},Y_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)\tau_{\lambda}^{-1}\!\!\sum_{l,j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\!\!\langle D_{\lambda}^{l}\rangle\langle D_{\lambda}^{j}\rangle\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\!\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{l,l^{\prime}}\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{j,j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\lambda}^{-1}\sum_{l,j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D_{\lambda}^{l}\rangle\langle D_{\lambda}^{j}\rangle\frac{\delta_{l,j}\delta_{l^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{d_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\lambda}^{-1}\frac{1}{d_{\lambda}}\sum_{j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle^{2}\delta_{l^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\,,

(41)

where we used the orthogonality of the elements of irreducible representations. The previous allows us to provide the general formula

\tau_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{d_{\lambda}}\sum_{j\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j}\rangle^{2}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\rm hw}\!\bra{\rm hw})}{d_{\lambda}}\,.

(42)

For the special case when the expectation values are independent of
l
, i.e.,
\langle D_{\lambda}^{l}\rangle=\langle D_{\lambda}\rangle
we obtain

\tau_{\lambda}=\frac{d_{\lambda}^{0}}{d_{\lambda}}\,\langle D_{\lambda}\rangle^{2}\,.

(43)

∎

Equation (
36
) in Proposition
1
shows that
\tau_{\lambda}
is simply the GFD purity of the
reference free state
\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}
in the irrep labeled by
\lambda
, divided by the
dimension
d_{\lambda}
of that irrep. Since
\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})=1

for a pure state, the numbers
\{d_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
form a probability
distribution over irreps, and
d_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}
is exactly the fraction of the
operator-space purity of the density matrix
\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}
carried by the sector
V_{\lambda}
.
Equivalently,
\tau_{\lambda}
quantifies how strongly the highest-weight state populates the irrep

\lambda
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
.
To finish, we note that we can combine Eqs. (
31
) and (
42
)

\displaystyle\Delta(\Omega,s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tau_{\lambda}^{-(s+1)/2}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{j^{\prime},j}\right)D_{j}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-(s+1)/2}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\left(\Phi_{\rm Ad}^{\lambda}\left(\Omega\right)\right)_{j^{\prime},j}D_{j}^{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=T(\Omega)\left(\sum_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-(s+1)/2}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle D_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right)T^{\dagger}(\Omega)\,.

(44)

In the last line, we used the covariance of the group action as per Eq. (
38
). Indeed, setting
s=-1
in Eq. (
44
) readily recovers

\displaystyle\Delta(\Omega,-1)

\displaystyle=T(\Omega)\left(\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\lambda}^{0}}\langle D^{\lambda}_{j^{\prime}}\rangle D_{j^{\prime}}^{\lambda}\right)T^{\dagger}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=T(\Omega)\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}T^{\dagger}(\Omega)=\ket{\Omega}\!\bra{\Omega}\,.

II.2
Irrep decomposition of the SW kernel

In this brief section we note that since the SW kernel is an operator in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, we can compute its GFD purities according to Definition
4
. In particular, a straightforward calculation (see the Appendix for a proof) leads to the following result.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Delta(\Omega,s)
be an SW kernel as defined in Eq. (
31
), then its GFD purities in the irreps of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
are

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\Delta(\Omega,s))=\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}d_{\lambda}=\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\rm hw}\!\bra{\rm hw})}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{-s}d_{\lambda}\,.

(45)

Proposition
2
implies that the Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
plays the role of modulating, or weighting, the purities in each irrep. For the special case of
s=0
, the SW kernel has a “uniform” distribution, as the purity on each irrep is proportional to its dimension. Then, for
s=-1
, one recovers the purities of the highest weight state
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})
, which implies as per the results in
Bermejo et al. 2025
that the SW Kernel mostly lives in the lower-dimensional irreps. For
s=1
we obtain the converse, as the kernel is reversed and weighted towards higher-dimensional irreps. Finally, we see that if the highest weight state has no component in a given irrep, then that irrep is killed off when going into the QPS.

II.3
QPS kernels as signal-processing filters

With the previous result in mind, we are now ready to study the irrep decomposition of
F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
. Combining Eqs. (
19
) and (
31
) we obtain

F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s/2}\,Y^{\lambda}_{j}(\Omega)\,\big\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},\rho\big\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}\,.

(46)

Then one can prove the following proposition (see the Appendix for a derivation).

Proposition 3
.

Let
\rho
be a quantum state and
F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
its phase space representation. Then, from Definition
9
, the irrep projections are non-zero only for the irreps
\sigma=\lambda
that appear in the decomposition of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
in Definition
4
. In this case we have

[F_{\rho}]_{\lambda}(\Omega,s)=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s/2}\,Y^{\lambda}_{j}(\Omega)\,\big\langle D_{j}^{\lambda},\rho\big\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}\,,

(47)

and the ensuing purities

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big)

\displaystyle=\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})}{d_{\lambda}}\right)^{-s}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)\,.

(48)

Notably, the previous result shows that the
\lambda
-th GFD purity of

F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
is simply obtained by multiplying the

\lambda
-th purity of
\rho
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
by the factor
\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}
.
Combining this with Proposition
2
, we see that the same
parameter
s
which modulates the purities of the SW kernel in each irrep becomes a
signal-processing-like Fourier filter for the GFD decomposition of an operator in
the QPS
\mathcal{X}
. Concretely, the GFD purities
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}
in

L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
are obtained from those in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
by a pointwise multiplication of
the vectors with entries
\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)
; this is
precisely the action of a diagonal filter in standard Fourier analysis.

Therefore, when
s=0
no filter is applied to the irrep decomposition and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big)=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)
. On the
other hand, for
s=-1
, we know that
\Delta(\Omega,-1)=\ket{\Omega}\!\bra{\Omega}
is a QRT free state,
meaning that it lives predominantly in the smaller-dimensional irreps
Bermejo et al. 2025
. This is, of course, in accordance with the well-known fact that the Husimi Q (
s=-1
) function is the Weierstrass transform of the Wigner quasiprobability distribution, i.e. a smoothing by a Gaussian filter
Schupp 2022
. Then, the SW kernels
\Delta(\Omega,s)
act as low-pass (high-pass) filters
for
s=-1
(
s=1
), where low-dimensional irreps are enhanced (suppressed), and
high-dimensional ones are suppressed (enhanced).

Hence, we can expect that when
s=-1
, if

\rho
is a near-free state in a QRT, then most of its relevant information will
be preserved when mapping from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
to
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
. Conversely, if
\rho
is
highly resourceful (i.e., if it has most of its weight in high-dimensional irreps),
its information will tend to be strongly attenuated in the QPS. The opposite
behaviour occurs for
s=1
, where the information of free and near-free states is
mostly suppressed, while the components of resourceful states in the large irreps
are amplified.

II.4
Duality between free states and random highly resourceful states

In this section we reveal a notable duality between the GFD purities in the QPSs of free states and those of random states. To begin, let us consider the reference free state
\ket{{\rm hw}}
. From Proposition
3

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,-1)\big)=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})^{2}}{d_{\lambda}}\,,

(49)

where we used Eq. (
36
). This shows that, for the reference free state, the QPS purities
at
s=-1
are obtained from the operator-space purities by squaring the
irrep profile and rescaling by
1/d_{\lambda}
. This has two important effects: (i) it nonlinearly sharpens the distribution over irreps
by squaring, further concentrating weight on those sectors where the free state
already has large
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)
, and (ii) it penalizes irreps according to
their dimension via the factor
1/d_{\lambda}
. In other words, the
s=-1
QPS
representation of the reference free state is maximally biased towards the
low-dimensional sectors in which
\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}
is most concentrated in
operator space. The SW kernel is thus “matched” to the structure of the free
state as it preserves and accentuates precisely those irreps that dominate the free
sector, while relegating the rest to the tails of the irrep spectrum.

Then, for
s=1
we obtain

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,1)\big)=d_{\lambda}\,,

(50)

i.e., the GFD purities of the phase space representation at
s=1
are
completely independent of the original irrep profile of the free state and
are determined solely by the irrep dimensions. As such, in this
s=1
QPS representation of
\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}
has a spectrum proportional to the irrep dimension, and thus accentuated towards higher-dimensional ones.

Next, consider a Haar random state
\ket{\psi_H}
, and let us note that we can always express the GFD purity as

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\Tr[(D_{j}^{\lambda})^{\otimes 2}\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}^{\otimes 2}]\,.

(51)

From here we can find that, in expectation value
Mele 2024
;
García-Martín et al. 2025
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}^{\otimes 2}]=\frac{\openone^{\otimes 2}+{\rm SWAP}}{d(d+1)}\,,

(52)

with
\openone
the identity operator in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
{\rm SWAP}
the swap operator acting on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}
. Hence, if
\lambda
is the trivial irrep containing
\openone
we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})]=1/d
, while for every other irrep

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})]=\frac{d_{\lambda}}{d(d+1)}\,.

(53)

That is, the purity of Haar random states is, on average, flat across all irreps (up to normalization).

Using Proposition
3
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}}(\Omega,-1)\big)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,0)\big)}{d(d+1)}\,.

(54)

Moreover, since

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}}(\Omega,0)\big)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})]\,,

we also find

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}}(\Omega,0)\big)=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,1)\big)}{d(d+1)}\,.

(55)

Equations (
54
) and (
55
) show an intriguing connection between the QPS’s GFD purities of the free highest weight states, and the expected QPS’s purities for the most resourceful Haar random states. Indeed, these results are two
instances of a more general identity which is presented in the following proposition, valid for any QRT/QPS based on compact Lie groups.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\ket{\psi_H}
be a Haar random state sampled according to the Haar measure over
\mathcal{H}
, and let
\ket{{\rm hw}}
be the highest weight free state of the QRT. Then, we find that their purities in the QPS satisfy the relation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}}(\Omega,s)\big)\big]=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,s+1)\big)}{d(d+1)}\,.

(56)

Proof.

Using Proposition
3

and Eq. (
53
) we have that for arbitrary
s

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}}(\Omega,s)\big)\big]=\frac{d_{\lambda}\,\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}}{d(d+1)}\,.

On the other hand, for the highest-weight state
\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}
we know that

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})=d_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}
, so

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,s+1)\big)=\tau_{\lambda}^{-(s+1)}\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})=d_{\lambda}\,\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}.

Combining these two expressions yields the compact relation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\big[\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H}}(\Omega,s)\big)\big]=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,s+1)\big)}{d(d+1)}\,.

Equations (
54
) and (
55
) correspond to the
special cases
s=-1
and
s=0
of (
56
).
∎

At this point, we note that Proposition
4
readily implies

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\big[\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})\big]=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})}{\tau_{\lambda}d(d+1)}\,.

(57)

Hence, since a random state
\ket{\psi_H}
can be obtained, without loss of generality, by applying a random (resourceful) unitary from
\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{H})
to
\ket{{\rm hw}}
we find

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}f(\ket{\psi_H})=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{H})}f(U\ket{{\rm hw}})\,.

(58)

Equation (
57
) thus says that the average amount of resource obtained by applying a random unitary to the QRT’s free state is a scaled version of the resource already present in the free state. Moreover, using Markov’s inequality for the random variable
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})
(i.e., the purity of a random state), we find that for any
a>0

{\rm Pr}[\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})\geq a]\leq\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})}{a\tau_{\lambda}d(d+1)}=\frac{d_{\lambda}}{ad(d+1)}\,,

meaning that the purities of the free state, or concomitantly the irrep’s dimension, bound the probability of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{\psi_H}\!\bra{\psi_H})
being larger than a given positive constant.

Then, let us note that Eq. (
56
) has a striking interpretation. It states
that, on average over the Haar measure, the QPS GFD purities of a
highly resourceful state (a typical Haar random pure state) at parameter
s

are proportional to the QPS purities of the reference free state at parameter

s+1
. In other words, shifting the Cahill–Glauber parameter
s\mapsto s+1
maps the
irrep profile of the free state to the “typical” irrep profile of a highly resourceful Haar
states, up to the global factor
1/(d(d+1))
. As such, the extremes of the QRT—free and typical resourceful
states—are thus linked by a simple shift in the kernel parameter
s
rather
than by independent, unrelated spectral data.

From a signal-processing viewpoint, this reveals a kind of duality between free
and Haar-random states mediated by the SW kernels. For the free state

\ket{{\rm hw}}
, the
s=-1
representation is a matched filter that sharply
emphasizes the low-dimensional irreps where
\ket{{\rm hw}}
is concentrated,
while the
s=1
representation completely flattens its profile to

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}(F_{\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}}(\Omega,1))=d_{\lambda}
, independent
of the detailed structure of
\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}}
. Equation (
56
)
shows that this “maximally flattened” spectrum at
s=1
coincides (up to
normalization) with the typical spectrum of Haar states at
s=0
, and
the
s=0
spectrum of the free state, in turn, matches the typical Haar spectrum
at
s=-1
(as in Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
)).

Resource-theoretically, this means that the same family of kernels that is
adapted to the free sector (at
s=-1
) also organizes the spectrum of
highly resourceful random states (Haar random) in a very rigid way that is fully and completely independent of the group and QRT considered. That is, once
s
is
fixed, their typical QPS irrep profile is completely determined by that of the
reference free state at
s+1
. The extremes of the resource spectrum are thus
linked by a one-step shift in
s
: free and Haar-random states are not only
opposites in terms of where their weight sits in operator space, but their QPS
representations are related by a simple, representation-theoretic Cahill–Glauber “
s
-duality”
encoded in the coefficients
\tau_{\lambda}
.

II.5
Additional implications for QPSs

In this section we analyze how Propositions
1
and
2
allow us to reinterpret standard results in the study of QPSs. We note that this analysis is not exhaustive, but rather intends to show the power of our results.

II.5.1
Characterization of the Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
-flow

Here we discuss how the purities
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s))
are expected to change as a function of the continuous Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
. Starting from

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big)=\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)\,,

(59)

and taking the derivative with respect to
s
leads to

\frac{d}{ds}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big)=-\log(\tau_\lambda)\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big)\,.

(60)

Hence, we can understand the
s
-dependence of the irrep components as an exponential flow, where the generator
-\log(\tau_\lambda)
is fixed by the highest weight purities. As such,
the speed at which the
\lambda
-sector is amplified or suppressed as one moves along
s
is controlled by
-\log(\tau_\lambda)
, meaning that the small irreps in which
\ket{\rm hw}
has large purity evolve slowly with
s
, whereas irreps where
\ket{\rm hw}
has small coefficient evolve more rapidly. Thus, the free state purities
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})
not only fix the filters at each
s
, but also the full flow across the SW kernel family. In effect, it is the spectrum of the generator of the deformation between different phase space representations (Husimi, Wigner, etc).

II.5.2
Twisted product and algebra structure in QPS

Up to this point we have mainly discussed the isometry between

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
at fixed
s
, and how their irrep decompositions relate. However, we can define a “twisted product” which endows

L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
with a noncommutative product so that this correspondence becomes an
algebra isomorphism. That is, operator multiplication corresponds to a deformed
product on phase space functions,

\displaystyle F_{AB}(\Omega,s_{1})

\displaystyle=\big(F_{A}\star_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}F_{B}\big)(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!\!d\mu(\Omega^{\prime})\!\int_{\mathcal{X}}\!\!d\mu(\Omega^{\prime\prime})M_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times F_{A}(\Omega^{\prime},s_{2})\,F_{B}(\Omega^{\prime\prime},s_{3}),

where the twisted-product kernel

M_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime\prime})\!=\!\Tr\!\big[\Delta(\Omega,s_{1})\,\Delta(\Omega^{\prime},-s_{2})\,\Delta(\Omega^{\prime\prime},-s_{3})\big]

is completely determined by the SW kernels. In this way, the QPS representation
does not merely match
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
as Hilbert spaces, but as

\mathbb{G}
-covariant algebras
Brif and Mann 1999
.

Expanding each kernel in the harmonic basis as in Eq. (
31
) allows us to write the twisted-product kernel as

M_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime\prime})\!=\!\!\sum_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}}T_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}\!\!\sum_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}C^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}_{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}}\,Y_{j_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}(\Omega)\,Y_{j_{2}}^{\lambda_{2}}(\Omega^{\prime})\,Y_{j_{3}}^{\lambda_{3}}(\Omega^{\prime\prime}),

(61)

where

\displaystyle C^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}_{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}}

\displaystyle=\Tr[D_{j_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}D_{j_{2}}^{\lambda_{2}}D_{j_{3}}^{\lambda_{3}}\big]\,,

(62)

\displaystyle T_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{\lambda_{1}}^{-s_{1}/2}\,\tau_{\lambda_{2}}^{s_{2}/2}\,\tau_{\lambda_{3}}^{s_{3}/2}=\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{1}}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})}{d_{\lambda_{1}}}\right)^{-s_{1}/2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{2}}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})}{d_{\lambda_{2}}}\right)^{s_{2}/2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda_{3}}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})}{d_{\lambda_{3}}}\right)^{s_{3}/2}\,.

(63)

Importantly, the coefficients
C^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}_{j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}}
are purely group-theoretic and arise from the bases of the irreps, whereas the terms
T_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}
are coupling constants which depend only on the GFD purity profile in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
of the highest-weight state.

For large irreps
\lambda
in which the free state has very small purity,
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})\ll 1
so that
\tau_{\lambda}=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})/d_{\lambda}\ll 1
. The couplings then have the following implications. First, if the net exponent of
\tau_{\lambda}
in
T_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}
is positive (e.g., factors like
\tau_{\lambda}^{s_{1}/2}
with
s^{\prime}>0
), channels involving such large irreps are strongly suppressed in the twisted product. In a low-pass/matched regime, products of free or near-free symbols therefore stay confined to the low-dimensional sectors where
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\ket{{\rm hw}}\!\bra{{\rm hw}})
is large, and the symbol algebra has little participation from highly structured high-
\lambda
components. However, if the net exponent is negative (e.g.,
\tau_{\lambda}^{-s/2}
with
s>0
), the same large-
\lambda
channels are potentially enhanced. Thus, whenever
F_{A}
and
F_{B}
carry significant weight in high-dimensional irreps, their twisted product receives strong contributions from these sectors. In a high-pass/mismatched regime the symbol calculus becomes very sensitive to resourceful, high-
\lambda
content, and highly oscillatory, interference-like features are amplified in phase space.

Putting it all together, we find that the highest weight purity profile not only identifies which irreps are “free-like” or “resourceful”, but also, through the factors
\tau_{\lambda}^{\pm}
in the couplings

T_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}^{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}}
, controls how strongly those
sectors interact under the noncommutative product on QPS.

II.6
Norm bounds for QPS representations from highest-weight purities

The relation

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big)=\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho),

(64)

together with the decomposition of the
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
norm into GFD purities (via the Plancherel Theorem
Folland 2016
),

\|F_{\rho}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\langle F_{\rho}(\cdot,s),F_{\rho}(\cdot,s)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{\lambda}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\lambda}\big(F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)\big),

immediately yields universal bounds on the total “power” of any QPS
representation in terms of the coefficients
\tau_{\lambda}
, and hence in
terms of the GFD purity profile of the highest-weight state.

For a general state
\rho
we have

\|F_{\rho}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho),

(65)

which, using
0\leq\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)\leq\Tr[\rho^{2}]
and

\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\rho)=\Tr[\rho^{2}]
, implies

\min_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\,\Tr[\rho^{2}]\;\leq\;\|F_{\rho}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\;\leq\;\max_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\,\Tr[\rho^{2}].

In particular, for a pure state
\rho=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, where

\Tr[\rho^{2}]=1
and
\sum_{\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})=1
, we
obtain the sharper bounds

\min_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}\;\leq\;\|F_{\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\;\leq\;\max_{\lambda}\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}.

(66)

Recalling from Eq. (
36
) that
\tau_{\lambda}=\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\outerproduct{{\rm hw}}{{\rm hw}})/d_{\lambda}
,
these inequalities show that, once the operator-space GFD purities of the
highest-weight state are known, the
L^{2}
norm of any QPS
representation
F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
is confined to a state-independent
interval determined solely by the highest weight profile and the choice of
s
. The
irreps for which
\tau_{\lambda}^{-s}
is largest (smallest) set the maximal
(minimal) possible overall intensity of phase space representations, identifying the symmetry sectors that can dominate or minimally contribute
to the total power of QPS functions across all quantum states.

II.7
Additional connections to QRTs

In the previous section we have discussed how the choice for the Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
serves as an effective filter in the Fourier decomposition of a quantum state
\rho
. Here we provide further interpretation to this phenomenon by realizing that when
s=-1
, then

F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)=\bra{\Omega}\rho\ket{\Omega}\,,

(67)

which is nothing more than the fidelity between
\rho
and the coherent states. That is, from a QRT perspective
F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)
can be understood as a comparison between
\rho
and the orbit of free states with a low-pass filter. Indeed, one can then re-write the coherent state fidelity of Eq. (
I.2
) as

\displaystyle S(\ket{\psi})

\displaystyle=\max_{\Omega\in\mathcal{X}}F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)\,.

(68)

More generally, the irrep decomposition of
F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)
provides a new interpretation of the fidelity between
\rho
and coherent states. In particular, when
F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)
has components in the high-dimensional irreps (despite their suppression as per the filter induced by the
s=-1
kernel), this means that a small change in
\Omega
gets translated into large fidelity changes. That is, high-irrep components can be expected to lead to highly oscilatory and sharp behaviour, meaning that
\rho
varies rapidly as one compares it against the orbit of free states. On the other hand, for near-free states (or in the limiting case of
\rho
actually being free), the fact that
F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)
has support in the lower-dimensional irreps indicates a smoothness in the fidelity between
\rho
and the coherent states. As such, one can expect that small changes in
\Omega
get translated into small and continuous fidelity changes.

Then, we can further push this analysis to other SW kernels with different
s
values via Eq. (
22
). That is,

F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega^{\prime})K_{s,-1}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})\bra{\Omega'}\rho\ket{\Omega'}\,.

(69)

As such,
F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
is related to the fidelity through the group-covariant kernel
K_{s,-1}
which filters out or amplifies particular irrep components. That is, all QPSs are ultimately just comparisons between
\rho
and the orbit of free states obtained via distinct filters as in Eq. (
21
).

IV
Generalization beyond SW kernels

Throughout this work we have focus on QPSs obtained from SW kernels as described in
Brif and Mann 1999
. In this section our analysis of QPS as signal-processing filters to mote general kernels beyond those explicitly parametrized by
s
.

To begin, let us relax Definition
7
of a QPS function as follows: let
\hat{\Delta}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
be a mapping with the following properties

1.

Linearity
: The mapping of
A\to\hat{F}_{A}
via
\hat{\Delta}
is linear,

2.

*
-preserving
: The mapping satisfies
\hat{F}_{A}^{*}=\hat{F}_{A^{\dagger}}
,

3.

Standardization
:
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)\,\hat{F}_{A}(\Omega)=\Tr[A]
,

4.

Covariant
:
\hat{F}_{T^{\dagger}(g)AT(g)}(\Omega)=\hat{F}_{A}(g\cdot\Omega)
,

5.

Duality
: There exists a dual map

\check{\Delta}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
sending
B\to\check{F}_{B}
which satisfies the previous properties and further satisfies
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)\hat{F}_{A^{\dagger}}(\Omega)\check{F}_{B}(\Omega)=\Tr[A^{\dagger}B]
.

Note that properties
1-4
exactly match those in Definition
7
as these do not depend explicitly on
s
, while property
5
is a generalization to recover the inner product. We can again use Reisz’s representation theorem to understand that
\hat{F}_{A}(\Omega)=\Tr[A\hat{\Delta}(\Omega)]
as well as conclude analogous properties in Definition
8
. Questions as to the existence of
\check{\Delta}
are not the concern of this paper, though this is directly from the frame theoretic generalization of QPSs which is discussed in
Ali et al. 2000
and further in the companion work
Coffman et al. 2026
.

Given the previous generalized, one may ask “
What transformations preserve the QPS structure?
”. Indeed, this is equivalent to asking “
What are the most general filters one could consider?
”. To answer this question, let us first analyze the transformation that maps us from the SW kernels
\Delta(\Omega,0)
into a more abstract QPS kernel
\hat{\Delta}(\Omega)
. In particular, the following sequence of identities hold

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{A}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\Tr[A\hat{\Delta}(\Omega)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega^{\prime})F_{A}(\Omega^{\prime},0)\Tr[\hat{\Delta}(\Omega)\Delta(\Omega^{\prime},0)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega^{\prime})F_{A}(\Omega^{\prime},0)\kappa(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})\,,

(100)

where we have defined the real kernel function

\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\kappa(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})=\Tr[\hat{\Delta}(\Omega)\Delta(\Omega^{\prime},0)]\,

(101)

by Hermiticity of
\hat{\Delta}
and
\Delta(\Omega,0)
. One can readily verify that
\kappa
is translation invariant under the action of
G
as

\displaystyle\kappa(g\cdot\Omega,g\cdot\Omega^{\prime})=\kappa(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}),\quad\forall g\in\mathbb{G};\quad\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}.

Therefore, for
\Omega=gH
we always have that
\kappa(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})=\kappa(e,g^{-1}\cdot\Omega^{\prime})
. Thus, we can always define
k(\Omega)=\kappa(e,\Omega)
such that
\kappa(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})=k(\Omega^{-1}\cdot\Omega^{\prime})
where the group action is given by
\Omega^{-1}\cdot\Omega^{\prime}=g^{-1}\cdot\Omega^{\prime}
(note this is well-defined because
\kappa
is symmetric). Note that
k:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathbb{H}
-invariant by construction. Indeed, recall that a translation invariant kernel is always a convolutional kernel, in the sense that

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{A}(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)F_{A}(\Omega,0)k(\Omega^{-1}\cdot\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(F_{A}*k)(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[A\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)k(\Omega^{-1}\cdot\Omega^{\prime})\Delta^{(s=0)}(\Omega)\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr[A(k*\Delta^{(s=0)})(\Omega^{\prime})]\,.

(102)

Hence, the transformation from the
s=0
SW kernel to a generalized kernel is given by convolution with
k
, or
\hat{\Delta}=k*\Delta^{(s=0)}
which is defined pointwise.

The previous analysis has revealed that any generalized kernel
\hat{\Delta}
can be obtained as the convolution of a real
H
-invariant function
k
with any SW kernels (or any generalized kernel). For simplicity, we can always take the reference kernel to be
\Delta(\Omega,s=0)
(as it applies no QPS filtering). Naturally the definition of
\hat{\Delta}
constraints the functions

\int_{\mathcal{X}}d\mu(\Omega)k=1
(the other properties are obeyed by convolution). Leveraging the convolution theorem we see that we can always find a

with these properties such that

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}=(k*\Delta^{(s=0)})=\sum_{\nu}(k*Y_{\nu})D_{\nu}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\nu}k_{\nu}Y_{\nu}D_{\nu}^{\dagger}.

In particular, one only needs to place a modest set of restrictions over

so that the inverse kernel
\check{\Delta}
exists.
Formally, the previous equations shows the large freedom there exists in building free space functions, and thus filters for QPSs, beyond those explicitly parametrized by the SW construction.

V
Discussions

The main takeaway of our results is a strong form of representation-theoretic rigidity for QPSs. Once the GFD purity profile of the reference free state in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is known, essentially all irrep-level information in the QPS is fixed, for all
s
, and even for typical states such as Haar-random pure states. Once this highest-weight profile is fixed, there is no further freedom at the irrep level: all SW kernels, phase space representations, and symbol calculi compatible with the QRT are just different “filterings” of the same underlying group–Fourier data. In this sense, QPSs inherit the same representation-theoretic structure that already organizes resources in operator space, and our work makes this inheritance completely explicit.

From a resource-theoretic perspective, we show that the Cahill–Glauber parameter
s
acquires a precise operational meaning in the form of a tunable group–Fourier filter that interpolates between free-adapted low-pass representations at
s=-1
, an unfiltered spectrum at
s=0
, and
resource-sensitive high-pass representations at
s=1
. For near-free states, the
s=-1
QPS faithfully preserves and accentuates the low-dimensional irreps where they live, while strongly attenuating high-dimensional components. For highly resourceful states, the situation is reversed:
s=1
suppresses the low-dimensional “classical” modes and amplifies high-dimensional irreps, revealing fine-grained, rapidly varying features in the QPS. The
s
-duality in Proposition
4
shows that this filtering picture is not merely qualitative: the average phase space spectrum of a typical Haar-random pure state at parameter
s
is directly proportional to that of the reference free state at
s+1
. Thus, the same family of kernels that is adapted to the free sector also rigidly organizes the phase space spectra of highly resourceful random states.

Our analysis also reinterprets several standard QPS constructions in group–Fourier language. The explicit form of the twisted (star) product shows that its coupling constants factor into a purely group-theoretic part, coming from trace overlaps of irrep basis elements, and a purely resource-theoretic part, given by powers of
\tau_{\lambda}
. This clean separation clarifies when the symbol algebra behaves in a “classical-like” way (when large-
\lambda
channels are suppressed) and when it becomes highly sensitive to resourceful, high-dimensional components (when those channels are enhanced). Likewise, the universal
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
norm bounds expressed in terms of
\tau_{\lambda}
show that the overall “power” of any QPS representation is confined to a narrow interval determined entirely by the highest-weight purities and the chosen
s
, independently of the state being represented.

Looking ahead, our results suggest several directions for further connecting QPSs and QRTs. On the conceptual side, one can ask to what extent other resource quantifiers (e.g., monotones, robustness-type measures, or asymptotic conversion rates) admit natural QPS expressions once the group–Fourier filter structure is taken into account. Since
F_{\rho}(\Omega,-1)
coincides with fidelities against free coherent states, and all other
F_{\rho}(\Omega,s)
are filtered versions thereof, it is natural to expect new QPS-based witnesses and diagnostics that interpolate smoothly between “free-friendly” and “resource-sensitive” regimes as
s
is varied. On the representation-theoretic side, it would be interesting to extend our framework to non-compact or discrete groups.

Finally, our work places the recent revival of QPS ideas in modern quantum information and quantum machine learning on a firm representation-theoretic footing. Recent proposals to use QPSs for simulation, learning, and classical benchmarking rely crucially on how information is distributed over symmetry modes of the system, and our results
show that, for group-covariant QRTs, this mode structure is entirely controlled by the free state’s GFD purities and the choice of
s
. This could open the door to designing QPS-based algorithms and architectures that are explicitly “resource-aware”—for example, filters tailored to suppress irreps that are easy to simulate classically while enhancing those that carry genuinely quantum resources. This can be achieved by changing the reference state, or the measure over
\mathcal{X}
(e.g., adding a character in the average over
\mathcal{X}
changes the projection from trivial irreps, onto other ones). In this way, QPSs are promoted from a largely geometric visualization tool to a principled, representation-theoretic signal-processing framework for diagnosing, filtering, and ultimately exploiting quantum resources in practical information-processing tasks.
