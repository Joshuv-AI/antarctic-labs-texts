Passive-avoidant leadership and psychological distress among Iranian nurses: the mediating role of work–to-family conflict
Authors: Fatemeh Mohammadi, Ghasem Zarei, Hojjatollah Farahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ineffective leadership style of passive-avoidance is consistently linked to detrimental outcomes, particularly in high-stress clinical settings like healthcare. Extensive research demonstrates a link between work-to-family conflict and heightened psychological distress. However, the specific mechanism and sequential pathway through which passive-avoidant leadership translates into psychological distress for nurses, particularly in the cultural context of Iranian healthcare, remains under-explored. This study addresses this gap by formally testing the mediating role of work-to-family conflict.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional, correlational study conducted among 326 official and contractual nurses working in a large hospital in Iran. Data were collected using validated self-report instruments for passive-avoidant leadership, work-to-family conflict, and psychological distress. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) with the Maximum Likelihood Robust (MLR) method was employed using R software (version 4.5.0) to test the direct and indirect relationships between the variables.ResultsPassive-avoidant leadership was significantly associated with psychological distress, directly and indirectly, through work-to-family conflict. Although the effect size was relatively small, the association observed in this Iranian sample appeared somewhat stronger than that reported in several previous cross-sectional studies.ConclusionsThese finding suggests that healthcare leaders may benefit from training in active supervision and that hospital management should consider organizational strategies that help reduce work-to-family conflict and support nurses’ mental health and promote well-being.

Introduction

Leadership is a foundational element in any organization [1]. It directly shapes organizational culture [2], influences employee performance [3], organizational innovation [4], and drives overall success [5]. Leadership can be interpreted and described in various forms [6, 7]. This can be seen in the wide-ranging leadership behaviors that have been developed. Effective leaders employ a variety of approaches. Some of the most effective leadership styles include transformational, transactional, and democratic [8]. The most successful leaders are often those who can adapt their style to suit a situation [7]. This includes the specific needs of their team and the overarching goals of the organization. Positive leadership styles are shown to boost employee motivation, job satisfaction [9], and productivity [3]. Conversely, ineffective leadership leads to detrimental outcomes. These outcomes threaten the well-being of employees and the stability of the organization [10].

Among the various ineffective leadership styles, passive-avoidant leadership is particularly damaging [11]. This style is also known as laissez-faire leadership. It is characterized by a leader’s inaction, avoidance of decision-making, and a general lack of guidance or support for employees [12]. Passive-avoidant leaders fail to address problems when they arise, and also fail to provide constructive feedback [13]. This “benign neglect” is consistently linked to a range of negative outcomes. This includes increased stress [14], lower job satisfaction, poor performance, and a higher incidence of workplace issues like absenteeism and bullying [15].

The consequences of this leadership style are especially pronounced in high-stress, high-stakes environment like healthcare settings [16, 17]. Research suggests that passive-avoidant leadership is more prevalent among nurse managers in some hospitals and nursing homes compared to other sectors. This is a significant concern. In a healthcare context, a lack of clear leadership can escalate workplace issues and undermine team effectiveness [18]. It can also negatively impact both staff and patient outcomes [16]. The absence of supportive leadership leaves nurses feeling unsupported and uncertain. This depletes their psychological resources and increases fatigue [19].

One of the key mechanisms through which passive-avoidant leadership may harm employees is work-to-family conflict (WFC) [20]. This type of conflict arises when job demands are incompatible with family roles [21]. The result is tension and stress. The lack of a leader’s support and guidance contributes to role ambiguity and conflict [22]. These are major sources of psychological strain [23]. From a role boundary perspective [24], weak or absent leadership makes it harder for employees to maintain clear boundaries between work and family domains, thereby intensifying work–family interference. For nurses, who already face demanding schedules, passive leadership can intensify role conflict and significantly heighten work–family interference. Previous studies have established a connection between passive leadership and WFC [20], as well as between WFC and psychological distress [25, 26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that WFC may serve as a key mechanism through which passive-avoidant leadership contributes to psychological distress.

Psychological distress in the workplace—such as stress, anxiety, and depression—can create serious problems for both employees and organizations. It can harm mental and physical health [27], lower productivity [28], and increase the chance of mistakes and accidents [29]. It may also damage the overall work climate and reduce organizational effectiveness [28]. Because of this, identifying and managing psychological distress is necessary for keeping a healthy and efficient workforce. This issue becomes even more important in nursing. The job is highly stressful, and nurses deal with constant pressure. In such an environment, supportive leadership is crucial. It not only protects nurses’ well-being but also helps ensure patient safety.

The existing literature has established links between passive-avoidant leadership and psychological distress [30]. It has also established links between this leadership style and WFC [25, 26]. However, the precise mediating role of WFC in this relationship remains an area that warrants further exploration. To the best of our knowledge, no empirical study to date has tested this mediational pathway. Previous studies, such as Che, Zhou [20], have shown that passive leadership contributes to employee strain through workload and WFC. However, their focus was mainly on burnout and physical symptoms among Chinese employees. Less is known about how passive-avoidant leadership may influence broader indicators of psychological distress, especially stress, anxiety, and depression.

Although many studies in Western and East Asian countries have shown that passive leadership is linked to work-family conflict and psychological distress, this topic has not been studied much in Iran. Nurses in Iran’s public hospitals work under very high pressure. They often deal with heavy workloads, not enough staff, and limited organizational support, these conditions can easily lead to more WFC and higher psychological strain [31, 32]. In addition, the Iranian healthcare system is highly centralized and bureaucratic [33]. Such systems can make the effects of weak or passive leadership even worse. When managers avoid responsibility or fail to support their staff, employees may experience more stress and poorer well-being.

For these reasons, studying this mediation model among Iranian nurses is important. It can help us improve global theories in occupational health psychology by including Middle Eastern contexts. It can also provide useful evidence for designing local policies and interventions that support nurses more effectively. Accordingly, the present study aims to investigate the direct and indirect effects of passive-avoidant leadership on psychological distress among Iranian nurses. It specifically seeks to examine the mediating role of work-to-family conflict in this relationship.

Methods

This section provides details about the research design, participants, measurement instruments, and data collection procedures. The reporting of this cross-sectional observational study was inspired by the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist [34].

Study design

This study was conducted as a cross-sectional, correlational-analytical study. The primary objective was to investigate the predictive relationship among passive-avoidant leadership, work-to-family conflict, and psychological distress of nurses by using the Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) method.

Inclusion criteria

Full-time official or contractual employment in the hospital at the time of the study. Having at least six months of work experience in the hospital (This criterion was determined to ensure that participants were sufficiently familiar with the work environment and job challenges, including WFC.). Willingness and informed consent to participate in the study, which was obtained by confirming at the beginning of the online questionnaire.

Exclusion criteria

Long-term leave (more than one month) or sick leave at the time of data collection. Incomplete responses (more than 10% of unanswered questions) in the questionnaire.

Formal diagnosis of a severe mental disorder or use of psychiatric medication affecting cognitive function, based on the participant’s self-declaration. Part-time or voluntary employment, due to differences in job commitment, workload, and the nature of job conflicts with full-time employees.

Sampling method

Data were collected for the present study through convenience sampling, with the assistance of the hospital research department in distributing the survey link. To determine the sample size, based on the recommendations of Hair Jr, Hult [35] in structural equation modeling, at least 10 to 15 respondents were considered for each estimated parameter. Given the model structure, the minimum sample size was estimated to be 200 people. Finally, in order to increase statistical power and compensate for possible invalid data, 326 complete questionnaires were collected and entered into the analysis. This sample size was assessed as sufficient and reliable for SEM analysis.

Instruments and measures

Passive-avoidant leadership (measured by MLQ)

To measure passive leadership style, the relevant subscale in the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire Instrument (MLQ) was used. This questionnaire, designed by Bass and Avolio [11], consists of 45 questions in its short form (5X-Short). The non-intervention subscale of this questionnaire consists of 8 items that describe the behavior of leaders in avoiding intervention or decision-making. The questions of this scale were answered based on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“frequently, if not always”). The validity and reliability of this scale have been confirmed in numerous studies. In a study by Ekmekci, Camgoz [36], the Cronbach’s alpha of this scale was reported to be 0.84. Also, in a domestic study in the Iranian community by Sadeghi, Samsamy [37], this questionnaire was validated, and the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for this subscale was obtained to be 0.95. In the present study, the reliability of this subscale was also confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.766, that indicates an acceptable internal consistency in this sample.

Work-to-family conflict scale

To measure work-to-family conflict, the relevant subscale in the Multidimensional Work-Family Conflict Questionnaire designed by Carlson, Kacmar [38] was used. This questionnaire has a total of 18 items. The Work-to-Family Conflict subscale of this instrument consists of 9 items that examine the negative effects of work on family life. Responses to this scale were based on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”). The structural validity of this scale was reported using confirmatory factor analysis and its reliability through Cronbach’s alpha in six subscales by Carlson, Kacmar [38]. All subscales had an alpha coefficient above 0.78, indicating adequate internal reliability of the instrument. In Iranian society, Karimi [39] validated this questionnaire and reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91 for the work-to-family conflict subscale. In the present study, the reliability of this subscale was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.914, indicating good internal consistency.

Depression, anxiety, and stress scale (DASS-21)

To measure psychological distress, the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) was used. This instrument, developed by Lovibond [40], is a 21-item questionnaire that assesses three distinct subscales, including: Depression (7 items), Anxiety (7 items), and Stress (7 items). Responses to the items were based on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (“did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applied to me very much, or most of the time”). For each subscale, the scores of the 7 relevant items were summed and then multiplied by two to align with the original DASS-42 scoring. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this scale have been confirmed in numerous studies. Kakemam, Navvabi [41] examined its psychometric properties in a sample of Iranian health professionals, reporting Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.93 for the Depression subscale, 0.79 for Anxiety, and 0.91 for Stress. In the present study, the internal consistency of these three subscales was also confirmed with the following Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, indicating good reliability: Stress: α = 0.863, Depression: α = 0.854, and Anxiety: α = 0.852.

Procedure

Data were collected using online questionnaires. With ethical approval and coordination from the hospital’s research section, the survey link was distributed to eligible employees via the organization’s internal nursing communication channels. Because the link was shared through open channels, the exact number of employees who received or viewed it could not be determined. Considering the total target population of approximately 521 nurses, 326 fully completed responses were obtained, corresponding to an approximate response rate of 62.6%, which should be interpreted with caution due to the unknown actual exposure to the survey link.

Participants were advised to complete the survey individually and during non-working hours to reduce response bias. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to accessing the questionnaire. Confidentiality and anonymity were assured. Data collection took place over two months (June–July 2025). The dataset was collected solely for this study and has not been used in any prior research or publications.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0) [42], and the lavaan package was used for SEM analysis. Also, SPSS software (version 26) [43] was used to prepare statistical descriptions of the variables. In the descriptive statistics section, indicators such as mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis were used to examine the distributional characteristics of the variables. The results showed that the distribution of some dependent variables (stress, depression, anxiety) was not normal; therefore, to increase the accuracy of the estimates, the Maximum Likelihood Robust (MLR) method was used in the model analysis. This method allows the extraction of robust statistics and standard errors in the face of violations of the normality assumption.

To test the research hypotheses SEM was used. The model structure included three main variables (passive-avoidant leadership, work-to-family conflict, psychological distress) and one latent variable (psychological distress) with three indicators (depression, anxiety, stress).

The fit of the model was evaluated using several indices, including the chi-square statistic (χ²), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Because some variables showed non-normality, robust fit indices based on the MLR estimator were also examined.

To examine the mediating role of WFC, direct, indirect and total effects analyses were used. The path coefficients were reported in standardized and unstandardized forms along with standard error, z-statistic and p-value. The indirect effect was assessed through the multiplication of path coefficients and the Bootstrap test.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations

The sample consisted of 326 employees. The majority of the participants were women (65.6%) and married (85.6%). The participants had an average work experience of 13.24 years (SD = 7.30).

Descriptive statistics for the study variables, including means, standard deviations, skewness, kurtosis, and their intercorrelations are presented in Table 1. As shown, the depression, anxiety, stress, and psychological distress variables exhibited significant positive skewness and kurtosis, indicating non-normal distributions. These results supported the use of the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation method in subsequent analyses.

Passive-avoidant leadership was positively and significantly correlated with WFC (r = .132, p = .017) and psychological distress (r = .202, p < .001). Moreover, WFC was positively associated with psychological distress (r = .511, p < .001). As expected, depression, anxiety, and stress showed strong positive correlations with each other and with psychological distress.

Importantly, the demographic variables (Work experience, gender, and marital status) were not significantly correlated with any of the main study variables, including passive-avoidant leadership, WFC, and psychological distress (all p > .05). Therefore, these variables were not included as control variables in the final SEM model.

Structural model fit

The fit of the proposed structural model was assessed using multiple indices. The chi-square statistic was χ² [4] = 7.974, p = .093, indicating that the null hypothesis of exact fit was not rejected. The additional fit indices were as follows: CFI = 0.995, TLI = 0.988, RMSEA = 0.055 (90% CI: 0.000–0.111), and SRMR = 0.017. Overall, these indices indicated an acceptable model fit.

Because some variables showed non-normality, the robust fit indices based on the MLR estimator were also examined. The robust values were close to the conventional indices (robust CFI = 0.995, robust TLI = 0.987, robust RMSEA = 0.057, SRMR = 0.017).

3) Indirect and total effects

Indirect effect: The indirect effect of passive-avoidant leadership on psychological distress through WFC was significant and positive (β = 0.072, p = .012). This effect was tested using the Bootstrap method (with 5000 resamples), which indicated a significant indirect effect. This supports the mediation hypothesis, suggesting that passive-avoidant leadership was indirectly associated with psychological distress through work-to-family conflict. Total effect: The total effect of passive-avoidant leadership on psychological distress (the sum of direct and indirect effects) was also significant and positive (β = 0.228, p < .001). Explained variance (R²): The R² results indicated that 30% of the variance in psychological distress was explained by passive-avoidant leadership and WFC, indicating that these variables accounted for a portion of the variance in psychological distress. In contrast, only 2.1% of the variance in WFC was explained by passive-avoidant leadership, suggesting that additional factors play an important role in shaping WFC.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest a significant and positive association between passive-avoidant leadership and higher levels of psychological distress among nurses, an observation consistent with the model’s proposed associations.

Consistent with prior research, passive-avoidant leadership had a significant positive direct effect on psychological distress (β = 0.156, p < .01). This is consistent with studies showing that leaders who are indifferent, inattentive, or dismissive are associated with higher stress, burnout, and lower well-being among employees [14, 36, 44, 45]. In high-stress environments such as nursing, a lack of guidance and support can make employees feel insecure and unsupported, which may contribute to higher levels of psychological distress.

Importantly, our study also indicates that WFC may play a mediating role in this relationship. The indirect effect of passive-avoidant leadership on psychological distress through WFC was significant (β = 0.072, p < .05).

This suggests that passive-avoidant leadership may be linked to heightened stressors such as role ambiguity and workload, which may make balancing work and family responsibilities more difficult. These findings are properly supported by the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory. Passive leaders, through their inaction and failure to provide support, may contribute to the depletion of employees’ personal resources (e.g., energy, self-efficacy). This depletion may be associated with higher anxiety [46] and may reduce employees’ capacity for self-control, which is essential for managing and demarcating the boundaries between work and family roles [47]. Consequently, the inability to effectively utilize self-control resources may lead to higher WFC. This mechanism is consistent with previous research indicating that passive-avoidant leadership amplifies stressors that then spill over into family life and reduce mental well-being [14, 19, 20].

Our results also highlight the strong association between WFC and psychological distress (β = 0.503, p<.001). This relatively β value suggests that WFC may play an important role in explaining psychological distress in our model.

This finding aligns with prior research that has identified a meaningful association between WFC and mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, and general psychological distress, across various populations [26, 48, 49].

This mechanism can be interpreted within the framework of the Spillover Theory. The theory posits that stress and negative feelings generated in one life role (e.g., work) “spill over” and contaminate the quality of life in the other (family). Specifically, when nurses struggle to balance conflicting demands, the emotional and time pressure they experience may deplete their essential psychological resources. This chronic resource depletion may be associated with higher levels of psychological distress. This suggests that managing WFC may be important for protecting employee well-being.

The model explained approximately 30% of the variance in psychological distress, indicating that the indirect pathway through WFC may be relevant for understanding nurses’ mental health. By contrast, passive-avoidant leadership accounted for only 2.1% of the variance in WFC, which represents a small effect size based on Cohen [50] guidelines. This low R² is theoretically consistent with prior work–family research showing that WFC is a multifaceted construct influenced by a broad set of organizational and individual factors rather than any single leadership style. For example, in a comprehensive meta-analysis, Kossek and colleagues [51] found that general supervisor support showed only a relatively small association with WFC, whereas family-specific supervisor and organizational support demonstrated stronger relationships. Moreover, when family-specific support was included in multivariate models, the effect of general supervisor support became non-significant. These findings suggest that broad leadership or supervisory styles often have limited explanatory power for WFC compared with more domain-specific forms of support.

The modest variance in WFC explained by passive-avoidant leadership in the present study can also be understood in light of the mechanisms through which passive leadership may influence employee outcomes. Passive leaders tend to avoid decision-making, fail to clarify expectations, and provide little support, thereby increasing role ambiguity, role conflict, workload, and other job stressors [19]. These job demands may represent more proximal predictors of WFC than leadership style itself. Consistent with this view, previous research has shown that the association between passive leadership and WFC becomes non-significant once workload is controlled [20]. Similarly, Zhou and colleagues [52] showed that passive leadership amplified the negative effects of illegitimate tasks on psychological detachment and, in turn, on strain-based WFC. Additionally, one meta-analytic study showed that job demands such as role overload, role conflict, job stress, and time demands consistently emerge as strong predictors of WFC, whereas contextual workplace resources such as organizational and supervisor support play more protective but comparatively smaller roles [53].

Consistent with this perspective, Hauser and colleagues [54] found that factors such as overtime work, working during free time, higher employment percentage, presenteeism, shift work, and perceived staffing inadequacy were all significantly associated with higher levels of WFC among care workers. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of WFC in healthcare settings and suggest that structural job demands and staffing conditions may play a particularly important role. Taken together, these findings help explain the small proportion of variance in WFC explained by passive-avoidant leadership in the present study. In nursing contexts, structural job demands and staffing conditions may represent more proximal determinants of WFC than general leadership styles.

A key contribution of this study is that it helps differentiate between the psychological and physical consequences of passive-avoidant leadership within a high-stress nursing context. We support the core relationship between passive leadership, WFC, and employee strain, consistent with the foundational work of Che et al. (2017), who utilized a Chinese nurse sample.

However, a noteworthy finding is the comparatively larger effect sizes observed in the Iranian sample for psychological outcomes:

The direct effect of passive-avoidant leadership on psychological distress (β = 0.156) is substantially larger than the direct effect reported by Che et al. (2017) on physical symptoms (β = 0.07). Similarly, the mediating role of WFC in the pathway to psychological distress (β = 0.072) appears slightly stronger compared to its mediating effect on physical symptoms (β = 0.05) in their study.

This difference in magnitude may indicate that psychological consequences such as stress, anxiety, and depression could manifest more prominently than the physical symptoms reported in prior studies.

The observed difference in effect sizes between the two studies may be interpreted in light of distinct cultural dimensions. Specifically, the stronger psychological consequences in Iran may be linked to the country’s tendency toward higher Uncertainty Avoidance and a relatively more individualistic orientation compared to China’s strong collectivism [55, 56]. Passive-avoidant leadership is often associated with greater role ambiguity and uncertainty due to the leader’s failure to make decisions or provide direction. In the high Uncertainty Avoidance context of Iranian hospitals, this ambiguity may be particularly stressful and may contribute to higher levels of anxiety, stress, and subsequently WFC.

Furthermore, while Chinese culture strongly emphasizes group harmony and collective coping [57], the comparatively more individualistic tendency among Iranian nurses may suggests a greater reliance on clear, individual managerial guidance and a lower inclination to rely solely on group dynamics to mitigate the strain imposed by a passive leader. Consequently, the combination of high intolerance for ambiguity and a greater need for individual guidance may contribute to greater psychological strain when facing the stressor of passive leadership, highlighting how the idiographic context significantly modulates the stressor-strain relationship.

To further contextualize these findings, the structural characteristics of the healthcare systems involved should be considered. Evidence from China suggests a transition toward greater fiscal and administrative decentralization at the municipal level, which has been associated with improved service efficiency [58]. In contrast, the Iranian healthcare system is often described as relatively centralized, with standardized top-down regulations [59]. In such structured environments, nursing staff may have limited professional autonomy and often depend on formal administrative leadership for resource coordination and conflict resolution. Consequently, a passive-avoidant leadership style in this context may create a “managerial vacuum” within the formal hierarchy. Unlike more decentralized systems, where local autonomy may partially compensate for a lack of guidance, the structural constraints inherent in a centralized framework may place additional strain on nurses’ psychological resources. This structural context may help explain the greater magnitude of psychological distress observed in the Iranian sample compared with the findings reported in the Chinese context.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretically, this study adds to the literature by empirically examining the mediating role of work-family conflict in the relationship between passive-avoidant leadership and psychological distress. It provides a more detailed understanding of how passive-avoidant leadership may influence employee well-being and complements prior research linking this style to lower performance, commitment, and safety compliance [60–62].

Practically, the findings have clear implications for healthcare organizations. Leadership development programs should focus on reducing passive-avoidant behaviors, promoting active engagement, and providing clear guidance and support. Studies by Walsh and colleagues [46, 63] suggest that daily mindfulness programs at workplace, have been associated with reductions in negative effects of passive leadership and decrease work-family conflict. Hospitals can also implement policies to reduce work-family conflict, such as flexible schedules, counseling services, or on-site childcare [64, 65]. By addressing both leadership behavior and work-family conflict, organizations may support nurses’ mental health and potentially enhance patient care.

From an organizational perspective, the findings offer important implications for healthcare administrators. While individual-level strategies such as mindfulness programs may help mitigate the immediate psychological strain associated with passive-avoidant leadership [46, 63], the relatively modest variance explained in WFC in the present study suggests that organizations should also prioritize structural and organizational interventions.

To reduce WFC, hospitals may need to focus on staffing and workload management. Evidence suggests that optimizing nurse-to-patient ratios and minimizing excessive overtime are particularly important, as high workload and perceived staffing shortages are major contributors to WFC among nurses [66, 67]. Furthermore, implementing evidence-based shift scheduling—such as predictable rostering and limiting the frequency of night shifts—may help reduce WFC by minimizing irregular working hours and improving schedule predictability [67–69].

Beyond scheduling practices, family-supportive policies such as flexible working hours and access to childcare support may further alleviate pressure on nursing staff [64, 65]. By addressing these structural job demands alongside leadership development initiatives, healthcare organizations may be better positioned to reduce WFC and, ultimately, protect nurses’ psychological well-being while supporting safer patient care.

Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions. Therefore, while our model demonstrates significant relationships, future research could utilize three-wave longitudinal designs to more clearly examine the directionality and investigate how passive-avoidant leadership and WFC interact over time in relation to psychological distress.

Second, we relied on self-report measures, which can sometimes lead to bias. Using data from different sources, like supervisor evaluations, would make future studies stronger.

Another limitation is the relatively small variance in WFC explained by passive-avoidant leadership. While WFC showed a strong association with psychological distress (β = 0.503), passive-avoidant leadership accounted for only a small portion of its variance (R² = 2.1%). This indicates that although passive-avoidant leadership is a significant factor, it is not the only or main predictor of WFC. Future research should explore additional influences such as workload, lack of organizational support, or cultural factors in order to develop a more comprehensive model.

Additionally, the generalizability of the findings is limited because the sample was drawn from a single occupational group (nurses) within one cultural context. Future studies would benefit from including more diverse occupational samples or controlling for variables such as work shifts, job tenure, and demographic characteristics.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings show that passive-avoidant leadership is associated with higher psychological distress among nurses, both directly and indirectly through work-to-family conflict. These results highlight the potential importance of addressing passive leadership behaviors and work-to-family conflict as part of organizational strategies to support employee mental health, particularly in demanding healthcare settings.
