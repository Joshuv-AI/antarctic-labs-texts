Systematic survey of pulsed laser deposition of YBa2Cu3O7 superconductor films on metal tapes
Authors: Sergey Samoilenkov, Sergey Lee, Valery Petrykin, Roman Valikov, Kai Schmidt, Max Fischer, Ralph Delmdahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulsed laser deposition (PLD) of biaxially oriented cuprate superconducting films rapidly progresses from versatile research method to large scale production method. This development is likely the first example when PLD gets to industrial production tool. In this article we have thoroughly reviewed vast literature data on PLD of YBa2Cu3O7 superconductor coatings on buffered metal tapes and compared these observations with our own experimental results. This comprehensive comparison allowed us to determine for the first time practically useful limits of superconducting performance of PLD-derived YBCO tapes in dependence on underlying buffer layer misorientation and film thickness. We also demonstrate that the PLD process has remarkably low power consumption and enables high-rate production without any performance degradation. We elaborate on further development of high power excimer lasers for superconductor tape production.

Introduction

Whenever new perspective material emerges, different thin film deposition methods are comparatively evaluated to make its functional thin films or coatings. Ultimately, the most successful deposition methods get adopted for large-scale production. This complex evaluation process considers desired functional properties, coating thickness, suitable substrates, availability of raw materials, capital expenditure, operating costs, and other factors. As a result of such rigorous technological evolution, chemical vapor deposition became widely spread in semiconductor industry, while e-beam evaporation and magnetron sputtering are often applied in industry to deposit ceramic coatings. There are few if any examples of using Pulsed Laser Deposition (PLD) in a large-scale production, although PLD stands out as a versatile tool for research purposes1, 2, 3 due to precise process control, wide material compatibility and high quality of deposited films. This long-time status quo seems to start changing now with the advance of high temperature superconductor (HTS) tapes technology. The worldwide PLD-based HTS tape production facilities develop incredibly fast and have produced more than 200 kg of extremely complex nano-engineered HTS coating in 2024 alone. Concurrently, PLD starts being applied to commercial production of other inorganic materials, e.g. semiconductors4.

The ‘working horse’ of the state-of-the-art HTS tape technology is the REBa2Cu3O7 superconductor family (RE = rare earth element, REBCO), which was discovered in 19875 as a first superconductor with the transition temperature above the boiling point of nitrogen. REBCO tapes are now becoming the key enabling material across various technologies. Apart from zero resistance, REBCO importance stems from a combination of unique properties: high transition temperature of about 90 K, record-high critical current density (also maintained in very high magnetic field), high mechanical strength, high efficiency of material use, and finally, low cost at mass-production. Recent noteworthy projects include ultra-high field large-size magnet coils for fusion power plants6, high voltage fault current limiters7, power cables for urban grid8, railway9, electrical propulsion systems for aviation10, and applications for space technologies11. The key enabler for this rapid evolvement of novel technologies was the recent availability of long-length HTS tapes, majority of which is currently manufactured with the help of pulsed laser deposition.

In this contribution we cover aspects, which are equally relevant to emerging mass production of HTS tapes, as well as to materials science of REBCO superconductors. For the sake of clarity, we have deliberately restricted our analysis mostly to yttrium-containing YBCO composition (including off-stoichiometric Y-Ba-Cu-O films), grown by PLD method on metal substrates. We have thoroughly collected all available data on PLD of YBCO on metal tapes and other technical substrates published in 1994–2024 timeframe (available in Supplementary materials). To our knowledge, this publication is currently the most comprehensive analysis on PLD of YBCO tapes. The comparison of our results with those obtained by other research groups put our data in context and outlines – for the first time – the possible limits of YBCO material performance. We also demonstrate that the availability of high-power industrial excimer lasers and thoughtful choice of superconductor material were instrumental in getting from research samples to commercially viable product. At last, we speculate that the development of PLD from a research method to a robust industrial reel-to-reel deposition tool can bring about further advances in materials science in nearest future.

Experimental

YBCO superconducting coatings were obtained by pulsed laser deposition in production reel-to-reel machines used by Faraday Factory Japan in regular production. The typical tape architecture was YBCO(2–3 μm)/LMO(50 nm)/MgO(50 nm)/Y2O3(10 nm)/Al2O3(50 nm)/Hastelloy(40 μm). The majority of YBCO tapes in this work were fabricated by in-plume PLD process13 with the target-to-tape distance of 70 to 90 mm depending on the laser power with the visual control of tape surface being inside the plume. For the PLD process we used commercially available high-quality 200 × 8 mm round targets of YBCO composition with 10 to 25% excess of Y2O3 as compared to the stoichiometric composition and density of about 80% of theoretical value. The experimental conditions were finely tuned for every design of the PLD system and laser. In particular, oxygen pressure and process temperature are controlled by the automatic gate valve and temperature controller. After the YBCO deposition, the silver layer was DC sputtered on both sides of the tape and the tapes were annealed in pure oxygen at 1 bar at temperature up to 350 °C to attain full oxygenation of superconductor.

The crystalline quality including crystalline misorientation of buffer and superconductor layers was determined with the SmartLab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). YBCO film thickness was observed by scanning electron microscopy of tapes’ cross-sections. The measurements of critical current along the tape length at liquid nitrogen temperature were performed with TapeStar device (Theva, Germany) with a subsequent calibration by direct current 4-point method. The critical current at high magnetic field and low temperature was determined using facilities of High Field Laboratory at Tohoku University14 and in-house SuperCurrent device (HTS-110, New Zealand).

Results and discussion

Mass transfer efficiency of PLD process

It is of crucial importance for PLD technology to optimize efficiency of laser light conversion to the mass of deposited YBCO coating. We refer to this parameter here as the material transfer efficiency. The laser power is a product of pulse energy and pulse frequency. To improve production speed, we have developed a version of PLD technology to accommodate very high laser power which was not used before for deposition of YBCO coatings (Fig. 2). Our goal was to ensure that the maximum material transfer efficiency is attained without compromising the film quality. For this, we used to optimize deposition parameters, optical instrumentation and maintenance procedures. We have found that several actions were essential for the success: splitting of high energy beam into two or more smaller beams, keeping substrate area larger than the plume footprint, and finally keeping laser beam path free from energy-consuming obstacles (use minimum number of mirrors and lenses).

Material transfer efficiency can be defined either by volumetric or mass transfer rate. We have chosen to use the volumetric rate here because it is the film thickness value that is usually reported in literature rather than the deposited coating mass. It should be kept in mind that the ratio between the volumetric and mass transfer rates is proportional to density which may be affected by film porosity and is not constant.

Figure 3 shows the YBCO volumetric deposition rate vs. laser power. Although with a significant scattering, it can be clearly seen that the deposition rate increases proportionally to the laser power. All published experimental data fall in the range between 0.001 and 1 mm3/s per 1 kW of laser power. There are multiple reasons that may lead to lower efficiency, such as small sample sizes (much smaller than the plume size), large distance between target and substrate, low-efficiency optical schemes, potential influence of laser beam wavelength, and others. Production type PLD machines (like used in our case) are developed to ensure an efficient capture of ablated material with the deposition zone typically being larger than the plume and a distance between target and substrate set as short as possible. In agreement with this understanding, our data belong to the highest boundary of material transfer efficiency. The average deposition rate of 0.45 mm3/s was achieved for the highest value of the laser power used, 600 W. To put this value in perspective: 1 m of 12 mm wide tape is covered with 2.5 μm YBCO layer in just under 70 s. If we suppose that a PLD machine is making superconductor tape 50% of the time (50% of the time is needed to run up/down and maintenance), the annual output would be 236 km per one 600 W laser.

In our experiments, 0.2–0.6 mm3/s volumetric deposition rate translated into 25–75 nm/s average deposition rate. It should be kept in mind that the local deposition rate fluctuates significantly, as the plasma plume is highly non-uniform and is smaller than the actual deposition zone.

Crystallographic texture and superconducting properties of PLD YBCO tapes

Due to its layered structure, YBCO superconductor is an intrinsically anisotropic material. Practically meaningful level of critical current is achieved only when YBCO grains are well aligned both in-plane and out-of-plane. Three main approaches were developed in 1990 s for making biaxially textured template: rolling assisted biaxially textured substrates (RABiTS), inclined substrate deposition (ISD), and ion beam assisted deposition (IBAD) methods. Although in our work we use only the IBAD approach, we keep all three technologies represented in this article to get a representative comparison.

Figure 4 shows critical current density at 77 K and zero external field, versus average degree of in-plane misorientation of the top buffer layer. Several details on this graph deserve a discussion. First, the set of previously published data presents a large scatter. This reflects the complexity of YBCO film growth and underlines multiple factors which need to be thoroughly controlled for high-quality HTS tape production. The properties of YBCO can be affected by one or many of the following factors: textured substrate type15, contamination of YBCO by foreign elements from buffer layer or metal substrate16, non-optimal deposition conditions17, varying grain size and grain aspect ratio18, 19, presence of admixture crystallographic orientations20, secondary phases21, film thickness22, 23, presence of nano-particles on buffer layer surface24, defect landscape25, degree of YBCO oxygenation23, and perhaps by some other less known factors. Although the overview of the complex interplay of all these aspects is beyond the scope of this article, we want to note that on average PLD-derived YBCO films get better crystal texture when grown on IBAD-derived templates, as compared to RABiTS-type substrates with the same degree of in-plane misorientation (see Supplementary for details). The possible explanation is that buffer template grains in IBAD templates are smaller (∼10s of nm26) than the typical grain size of YBCO, while the opposite is the case for YBCO on RABiTS which have the grain size thousand times larger, often reaching tens of microns27. As a result, YBCO layer on IBAD templates improves its texture as the thickness increases28. This mechanism can be explained by the preferential growth of well-aligned grains with low-energy boundaries at the cost of misaligned grains, similar to so-called “self-epitaxy effect”29, which was studied in relation to CeO230 and MgO28 layers.

Secondly, the samples with the highest performance in Fig. 4 obviously set a virtual ‘technology limit’ for a critical current, i.e. what seems to be achievable with the state-of-the-art PLD technology of YBCO tapes on metal substrates. Due to the texture improvement effect described above, relatively large misorientation of the IBAD-type buffer layer still enables high performance of superconductor: some groups were even able to obtain YBCO layers with Jc(77 K, sf) > 1 MA/cm2 if in-plane misorientation of the underlying buffer layer was as high as 14°31, 32. Nevertheless, FWHM values lower than 10° should be regarded as pre-requisite for obtaining high performance superconductor.

Finally, we note that data obtained in our work are close to the ‘technology limit’ of the critical current for any given misorientation angle, proving that the influence of detrimental factors is successfully suppressed, even for high values of deposition rate that we have used.

Thickness dependence

The critical current density decreases with the increase of YBCO layer thickness (Fig. 5). Again, larger thicknesses affect Jc due to many reasons: stronger chemical reaction of superconductor layer with metallic substrate (because of the longer deposition time), larger number of voids, larger fraction of grains with undesirable crystallographic orientation in thicker films22, 33, secondary phase inclusions33, the evolution of dislocation nanostructure34, and many others. Similar to the results described in previous section, we observe here a ‘technology limit’ for attainable performance of PLD-derived YBCO films. It should be noted here that the influence of some of detrimental effects mentioned above can be diminished by formation of multilayers, where YBCO layers are interchanged with thin layers of other material like Y2O3 and CeO235, SrTiO336, and NdBCO37. Such additional layers were shown to preserve higher Jc values in thick YBCO films.

We note here that our data compare rather well to the data published by other research teams, hitting the upper segment of the performance range. It can be concluded that Jc(77 K, sf) of most samples obtained in our work is equal to 75 to 100% of the ‘technology limit’ described above. This direct comparison proves again that the deposition conditions in our process are well optimized and controlled. On the other side, this proximity to the optimum leaves little ‘headroom’ for further improvements within the same technology and new approaches should be looked for.

Finally, Fig. 6 presents a map of critical current density vs. buffer layer misorientation and film thickness. It is clear that the critical current density values obtained in our work are close to the highest level reported: the average Jc(77 K, sf) for our tapes is about 2 MA/cm2, while the highest value for the 2–3 μm YBCO layer thickness and 5–10° in-plane FWHM of the buffer layer was 2.2 MA/cm2 as reported by Pahlke et al.38. It can be expected that the improvement of the buffer layer texture to below 5° may help to improve the critical current density further up to 2.8 MA/cm2 for the same thickness range, as it was previously demonstrated in39. Another viable approach to increase performance of thick YBCO films is (YBCO/CeO2) multi-layer formation40. In this case, the improvement over 3 MA/cm2 level might be expected41.

Film composition and control of pinning landscape

In our work we aimed at the yttrium-rich film composition located at YBa2Cu3O7 – Y2O3 tie line of the phase diagram and described by Y1+2xBa2Cu3O7+3x formula. It is well known that the excess yttrium content leads to high concentration of yttria nanoparticles, which are semi-coherent with the YBCO matrix12. Such nano-particles are known to be effective pinning centres especially at low temperature and high magnetic field42. This is particularly important for 4 to 20 K temperature range which is relevant to magnetic confinement fusion, accelerator magnets, magneto resonance imaging and nuclear magnetic resonance technologies.

Comparing performance of our tapes to the state-of-the-art is not straightforward, because there is only a few published data on critical current at low-temperature and high-field for PLD-derived YBCO tapes. More data are available for YBCO films grown on single crystal substrates though and we have used them here for comparison. It can be seen from Table 2; Fig. 7 that performance of our YBCO tapes compares rather well to single crystal data. It is a striking observation, given that buffer layer misorientation in our tapes is comparatively high (5–10° as compared to single crystal values of < 0.1°), YBCO film thickness is over 2 μm vs. typical values for thin films on single crystals in 100–500 nm range, and finally our tapes were grown at tens of nm/s deposition rate that is about 2 orders of magnitude higher than deposition rate used to grow thin films on single crystals. We believe it is the simplicity of microstructure of YBCO+Y2O3 composite that provides this process robustness.

Let’s discuss different ways to introduce artificial pinning centers in YBCO. In most cases, introduced phases are semi-coherent to YBCO matrix and correspondingly create strain. For example, Y2O3 nanoparticles have a negative lattice mismatch of about − 3% with YBCO in-plane, while for perovskites the in-plane lattice mismatch is positive and varies from + 3 to + 9%. In case of perovskite nanocolumns, the lattice strain is accommodated through the formation of misfit dislocations and may result in an oxygen-deficient YBCO zone, which surrounds each nanocolumn. The accumulation of strain may also make difficult obtaining thick layers, although it has been demonstrated that interleaving the structure of e.g. YBCO + BZO film with thin Ca-doped YBCO layer helps solving this issue54.

In general, all these approaches – nanoparticles, nanocolumns, multilayers – were proven to be useful. It has been demonstrated that one-dimensional nanocolumns work especially well at high temperatures and moderate magnetic fields, producing correlated pinning for B//c field direction. For example, adding double perovskite Ba2Y(Nb, Ta)O6 nanocolumns to YBCO resulted in impressive pinning force (Fp) of 25 GN/m3 at 77 K58. At the same time, Y2O3 nanoparticles act as isotropic 3D pins at low temperature and high magnetic field with reported Fp values of 1,000 GN/m3 at 4.2 K and 16 T47.

Another important aspect is manufacturability. The translation of a lab scale growth process to an industrial equipment requires a robust processing window for consistent high-rate production. It is known however that self-assembly of nanocolumns relies on the delicate interplay between the arrival rate of adatoms and their mobility on the substrate surface. As a result, microstructure and pinning properties of introduced nanocolumns strongly depend on deposition rate58, 59 and deposition temperature60. The growth rate is less of a problem in case of YBCO with Y2O3 nanoparticles, as we have not observed any performance degradation trends vs. deposition rate (see insert in Fig. 5). Finally, to grow a multilayer, it is necessary to use at least two different targets, while YBCO+Y2O3 composite can be grown from a single target. For further details we would suggest checking comprehensive reviews on this topic41, 60, 61, 62, 63.

As it was mentioned above, YBCO+Y2O3 system can accommodate very high PLD growth rates. Figure 8 presents Jc(77 K, sf) plot vs. average growth rate - this plot includes our data along published data. It can be seen that PLD of YBCO demonstrates remarkable quality stability when growth rates varies over 2 magnitudes of order! Some of the data with low Jc are obviously due to other reasons than the growth rate, as described above. Finally, it is important to note that the average growth rate value is significantly lower than the maximum growth rate, as the laser plume during deposition provides non-uniform distribution of the material with the maximum at the centre of the plume.

Energy efficiency of PLD

Although less critical at the initial development stage, energy use for coating production gets a paramount importance at large scale production stage. How much energy is used to produce one unit of product adds both to the initial capital intensity and the product environmental footprint. If energy consumption is large, it may even impose restrictions onto production geographical location. Probably for the first time in literature, we note here that PLD is an extremely energy efficient production tool.

The energy consumption by our PLD machines relative to produced length of superconducting tape is compared to the data available for other processes in Table 3. This value includes energy consumption of PLD process (excimer lasers, heating elements, vacuum equipment), as well as associated water cooling. This is a practical brutto metrics that explains the energy intensity of the process in whole. It’s worthy to note that the total energy intensity of HTS tape production in our work is about 3 kWh per meter of 12 mm wide tape, so PLD of YBCO process stage accounts for about a half of total energy consumption.

Due to its low energy consumption, the PLD-based HTS tape factory can be located even at demanding geographies, while future scale up will likely not be limited by power availability. The production process can be powered by renewable energy with storage.

Future outlook for the PLD technology

The LEAP excimer laser platform has been designed to suit cost-effective three-shift processing with a particular focus on the specifics of volume production via the pulsed laser deposition method. To this end, its discharge design employs capacitively coupled preionization and a maintenance-free solid-state switching module enabling pulse energy and pulse frequency scaling. Today, 300 W LEAP lasers at a wavelength of 308 nm successfully form the backbone of the available YBCO-tape production capacity. To meet the continuously rising tape demand and increase the throughput per PLD system, as opposed to simply multiplying the number of PLD systems, the LEAP 600 W was recently developed65. Ongoing endurance testing at full intensity laser operation so far looks as expected and indicates a significant decrease of the cost-per-watt-ratio for the new LEAP 600 W production laser, further improving the economics of HTS tape production. From a processing results viewpoint, the transition of the PLD process from 300 W to 600 W excimer laser output power has by now been demonstrated by means of a Coherent excimer laser model which has been designed for large panel annealing in the display industry. Interestingly, in much the same way as excimer laser power scaling today drives PLD production capacity of HTS tapes, the smartphone industry has been fuelled by excimer power scaling at just the same wavelength of 308 nm, as all low-temperature polysilicon (LTPS) based smartphones are produced with high power excimer lasers. In this context, it is even more interesting to note that the anticipated areal capacity of YBCO-tape per year required for the largest demand driver magnetic confinement fusion to succeed as an energy source of the future is of the same order of magnitude as the area of excimer laser processed LTPS panels per year. It can be concluded that the excimer laser development will support PLD processing of superconducting YBCO tapes similar to how it has supported the recent display revolution.

Conclusions

Over 20 years of development of PLD of YBCO on metallic tapes resulted in hundreds of scientific publications. Probably for the first time, we have performed a comprehensive review of these data and compared them with observations made in our own work. This allowed us to determine important finding which can be used for planning next steps in science and technology of HTS tapes.

First, the efficiency of pulsed laser deposition of the order of 1 mm3 of YBCO per each 1 kJ of laser energy was consistently achieved. We believe that this value approached what is doable with the state-of-the-art technology.

Next, we have determined ‘technology limits’ for the performance of YBCO superconductor coatings caused by buffer layer misorientation and film thickness. Both our observations on hundreds of samples as well as vast amount of published data are in full alignment with each other on this influence. We think that this consistency is due to fundamental mechanisms of YBCO film growth, which depend essentially on misorientation degree and film thickness.

In contrast, deposition rate does not seem to play any major role in limiting performance of PLD-derived YBCO tapes with Y2O3 nano-particles, at least in the range studied up to now. We show that as the deposition rate was increased by two orders of magnitude on the way from research samples to production, attainable superconducting properties remained essentially stable. Remarkably, up to 0.6 mm3/sec volumetric deposition rate was reproducibly achieved with the performance remaining at highest possible level.

Last but not least, we observed that the PLD process is very energy-efficient with 1.3 kWh needed to produce YBCO coating which is 2.5 μm thick, 12 mm wide, and 1 m long. This low level of energy consumption is crucially important for further scale up of this technology to higher laser power and throughput.
