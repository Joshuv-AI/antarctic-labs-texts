Bianchi Cosmologies in a Thurston-Based Theory of Gravity
Quentin Vigneron, Hamed Barzegar

Abstract
The strong interplay between Bianchi–Kantowski–Sachs (BKS) spacetimes and Thurston geometries motivates the exploration of the role of topology in our understanding of gravity. As such, we study non-tilted BKS solutions of a theory of gravity that explicitly depends on Thurston geometries. We show that shear-free solutions with perfect fluid, as well as static vacuum solutions, exist for all topologies. Moreover, we prove that, aside from non-rotationally-symmetric Bianchi II models, all BKS metrics isotropize in the presence of a positive cosmological constant, and that recollapse is never possible when the weak energy condition is satisfied. This contrasts with General Relativity (GR), where these two properties fail for Bianchi IX and KS metrics. No additional parameters compared to GR are required for these results. We discuss, in particular, how this framework might allow for simple inflationary models in any topology.

A

In this sense, a geometry is an equivalence class of homogeneous Riemannian manifolds.

2.4.2
Spacetime isometry group

While some Bianchi metrics always have continuous isotropies, as shown in the previous section, these symmetries are not necessarily symmetries of the spacetime metric
\boldsymbol{g}
. In other words, the spacetime metric does not necessarily inherit the isometry group of the spatial metric, but, in general, only the minimal group (cf.
[
97
]
). For an isometry of the spatial minimal metric to be an isometry of the spacetime metric, a necessary and sufficient condition is that the shear tensor
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
(see
Section

4.2
for the definition) is invariant by this isometry. Whether or not the shear tensor shares some isotropies with the metric depends on the constraint equations, the matter content and the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
(see, e.g.,
[
54
,
55
,
9
]
). Thus, there is an important distinction to make between the fact that some Bianchi metrics always have more than three continuous symmetries, and the notion of
locally rotationally symmetric
(LRS) metrics used in the literature on Bianchi spacetimes (see, e.g.,
[
26
]
); LRS is a property of the set
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and not the spatial metric
\boldsymbol{h}
alone.

Remark 2.10.

There always exists a subgroup of
{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
that is isomorphic to
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})
, but this does not necessarily imply
{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
. This is the case if the Killing vector generating the infinitesimal isotropy of the metric rotates with time. A simple example is that of
{\rm B_{I}}
metric of the form
h_{ij}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathrm{d}x^{j}=A_{1}(t)\mathrm{d}x^{2}+A_{2}(t)\mathrm{d}y^{2}+A_{3}(t)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
. Its shear tensor is
\sigma_{ij}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathrm{d}x^{j}=\partial_{t}A_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}+\partial_{t}A_{2}\,\mathrm{d}y^{2}+\partial_{t}A_{3}\,\mathrm{d}z^{2}
. The vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{4}=A_{2}(t)\,{y}\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{x}-A_{1}(t)\,{x}\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{y}
is a (time-dependent) Killing vector of isotropy for the metric. However, only its time derivative is a shear collineation. Thus, both
\boldsymbol{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
have a continuous isotropy, but the associated collineation is different.
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{4}
is a shear collineation if and only if
A_{2}(t)=A_{1}(t)
, in which case we say that the set
(\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is locally isotropic (LRS) around the
z
-axis. A similar example can be built for the Nil (
{\rm B_{II}}
) metric.

S\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{M}}\sqrt{-g}\,\left[\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}R_{\mu\nu}-{\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}}\right)g^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\right]\mathrm{d}^{4}x.

(3.1)

Assuming that the matter Lagrangian does not depend on

, i.e.,
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}
is a background/non-dynamical connection, we obtain from this Lagrangian the following field equations

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}R_{\alpha\beta}-\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\kappa\,\left(T_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{T}{2}g_{\alpha\beta}\right)+\Lambda g_{\alpha\beta},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.80095pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.02325pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\nabla^{\mu}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\mu\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.80095pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.02325pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\nabla_{\alpha}\left(g^{\mu\nu}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\right)\!,

(3.3)

where
\kappa\coloneqq 8\pi G
.

The action (

) corresponds to a modified Hilbert action that can be obtained with the procedure

from the Hilbert action. The same applies for the field equation (

). The second field equation (

) corresponds to the conservation of the term
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}-g_{\mu\nu}g^{\alpha\beta}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\alpha\beta}/2
. Very importantly, the two field equations depend on the reference connection
only
via its Ricci curvature

.

3.2
Reference Ricci tensor

A fundamental aspect of topo-GR is the presence of a
reference
Ricci tensor
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. In this section, we first provide its definition, and then consider its consequence.

We assume the
4
-manifold
\mathcal{M}
to be of the form
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
with
\Sigma
being a closed geometric
3
-manifold. Then, we define
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
by the following two conditions:

(i)

it is the Ricci tensor of a
reference
connection
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}
that is complete and torsion-free, and is characterized using the
external Whitney sum
of a connection
\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}
on
\mathbb{R}
and a connection
\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
, i.e.,

\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}\boxplus\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}\,,

(3.4)

(ii)

\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}]
is the maximal Ricci tensor arising from a maximal Riemannian metric on
\Sigma
(cf.
Table

3
).

The external Whitney sum of the two tangent bundles
T\mathbb{R}
and
T\Sigma
is
T\mathbb{R}\boxplus T\Sigma:={\rm pr}_{1}^{*}(T\mathbb{R})\oplus{\rm pr}_{2}^{*}(T\Sigma)
with the Whitney sum of the connections as
\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}\boxplus\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}:={\rm pr}_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}})\oplus{\rm pr}_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma})
where
{\rm pr}_{1}:\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
{\rm pr}_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma
(see, e.g.,
[
50
, Chapter I]
for more details on the external Whitney sum). Hence,
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}
given in (
3.4
)
acts on a given smooth vector field
\boldsymbol{Y}=(\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{R}},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\Sigma})
along an arbitrary smooth vector field
\boldsymbol{X}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{R}},\boldsymbol{X}_{\Sigma})
as

\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{R}}}\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{R}}+\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\boldsymbol{X}_{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{Y}_{\Sigma}
. As a result, we have
{\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}}[\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}]=\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}]\boxplus\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}]={\rm pr}_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}])\oplus{\rm pr}_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}])
, and in particular

{\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}}[\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}]={\rm pr}_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}])\oplus{\rm pr}_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}]),

(3.5)

which acts as
{\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}}[\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}](\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})=\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}](\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathbb{R}},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathbb{R}})+\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}](\boldsymbol{X}_{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\Sigma})
. Since
\mathbb{R}
is 1-dimensional we have
\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\mathbb{R}}]=\boldsymbol{0}
, and
{\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}}[\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}]
is given entirely by
{\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}]
. For this reason we will drop the subscript
Σ
for the connection on
\Sigma
, and use the following notations for the curvature tensors
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Riem}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}}[\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}]
,
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}}[\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}]
,
{\boldsymbol{\overline{{\rm Riem}}}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{{\rm Riem}}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}]
and
{\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}}[\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}_{\Sigma}]
.

Therefore, by
(ii)
,
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
is uniquely defined by the universal covering space of
\Sigma
. In this sense
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, and therefore
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, depend on the (spatial) topology. A direct consequence of this definition is that for Euclidean topologies for which
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}^{3}
and
{\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, hence
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, topo-GR and GR are equivalent. Therefore, the two theories only differ in non-Euclidean topologies.

From the definition (
3.4
), there exists a (reference)
\Sigma
-foliation, an adapted vector basis
\{\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}_{0},\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\}
and dual 1-form basis
\{\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}^{0},\boldsymbol{e}^{i}\}
, i.e., with
\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}^{0}
orthogonal to the foliation and
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
tangent to the foliation, such that the components of the reference Riemann tensor are purely spatial, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=\delta^{\alpha}_{i}\delta^{j}_{\beta}\delta^{k}_{\mu}\delta^{\ell}_{\nu}\,{\bar{R}}^{i}{}_{jk\ell}\,.

(3.6)

In this same basis, the reference Ricci tensor has the form

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&{\bar{R}}_{ij}\end{pmatrix}\!.

(3.7)

Furthermore, the Whitney sum (
3.5
) implies that
{\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}}_{0}
is a collineation for
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Riem}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}_{0}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Riem}}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\ \Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}_{0}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(3.8)

Relation (
3.7
) implies that
\boldsymbol{\bar{e}_{0}}\in\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)
and that
\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)=\{\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}_{0}\}\oplus\ker\left({\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}}\right)
. Therefore, from properties (

) and (

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{0}\,;\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{u}\in\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)\!.

(3.9)

Since only the reference Ricci curvature is present in the field equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), then only equations (
3.7
) and (
3.9
) are relevant for topo-GR. They will be especially important when deriving the 3+1-equations of the theory.

3.3
Discussions

Before diving into the 3+1-decomposition of topo-GR, we provide in this section some interpretations and discussions of the topo-GR framework presented so far.

3.3.1
Motivations for topo-GR and the form of the reference Ricci tensor

The initial motivation for topo-GR is to have a theory that admits a Newtonian limit in any topology, see
[
108
]
. We detail in this paper the motivations for this requirement, especially a few conditions are listed for a modified gravity theory and the additional term present in the Einstein equation to admit this limit. In particular, the new term should not depend on the matter content, but it has to depend explicitly on the spatial topology in a form similar to (
3.7
). Taking this term to be a (reference) Ricci tensor of the form (
3.7
) is the simplest solution we found, essentially because it does not explicitly introduce new parameters. On top of admitting a Newtonian limit in any topology, it was showed in
[
106
]
that topo-GR allows for the construction of a simple curved inflation model, something not possible in GR, thus providing a second—here model-dependent—motivation for the theory.

3.3.2
Interpretation of the field equation

From the field equation (
3.2
), in the absence of matter, the spacetime is not Ricci-flat as in GR, but rather we have
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
which is non-zero for non-Euclidean types of topologies. Therefore, the role of matter is not to directly curve spacetime as in GR, but only to induce a deviation from the “topological curvature”
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. This was rendered in
[
106
, Figure 1]
.

3.3.3
No reference foliation

By definition,
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\nabla}}
and
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Riem}}
define a reference foliation, or a set of reference foliations, which can be reconstructed from a 1-form
\boldsymbol{n}
satisfying
n_{\alpha}\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 4.48613pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.1585pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.12427pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4}}}}\bar{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=0
. However, the information on this foliation is lost when only the reference Ricci tensor
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
is considered, since no preferred 1-form can be constructed from
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
alone, i.e., the kernel of
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
only involves vectors and not 1-forms. Consequently, the field equations of topo-GR, which only feature the reference Ricci curvature, and not the reference Riemann curvature, do
not
involve a reference foliation. As shown in Section
4.1
, the consequence is that in a 3+1-decomposition,
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
always induces the same spatial reference Ricci tensor regardless of the choice of foliation.

But while no preferred foliation is defined, the set
\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)
defines a
reference
subset of the set of vector fields on
\mathcal{M}
. The dimension of that subset depends on the type of spatial topology as we have
\dim\left[\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)\right]=1+\dim\left[\ker\left({\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}}\right)\right]
, from (
3.7
). When
\Sigma
is modeled on one of
\{\mathbb{S}^{3},\ \mathbb{H}^{3},\ \mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il},\ \widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\}
-geometries, we have
\dim\left[\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)\right]=1
and a unique (up to a factor) reference vector field is defined on
\mathcal{M}
. While for the
\mathbb{E}^{3}
-geometry, we have
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and no reference vector fields are defined on
\mathcal{M}
, leading exactly to GR.
Consider the spacetime metric
\boldsymbol{g}
of the theory, one can construct the set of 1-forms dual to
\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)
, which is 1-dimensional for
\{\mathbb{S}^{3},\ \mathbb{H}^{3},\ \mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il},\ \widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\}
-geometries. But these 1-forms do not necessarily fulfill the Frobenius condition needed for them to define a foliation. Therefore, even in the case
\ker\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\right)
is
1
-dimensional, in general, no preferred foliation can be constructed in topo-GR.

3.3.4
Reference curvature for general manifolds
\mathcal{M}

The definition of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
assumes that
\Sigma
is a geometric
3
-manifold. This is the case for the present paper, and for most cosmological models. However, if
\Sigma
is not a geometric
3
-manifold (e.g., a connected sum), then no maximal metric can be defined on it. In this situation, the definition given in
Section

3.2
for
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
does not hold. This happens in particular for asymptotically flat spacetimes, for which spatial sections are infinite. In this situation, we define
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
to be zero, and therefore, we define topo-GR to be equivalent to GR. In this sense, the Schwarzschild metric is an exact vacuum solution of topo-GR.

For more general asymptotic conditions or when
\Sigma
is closed but not modeled on a Thurston geometry, we do not yet have a definition for
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. A possibility, that would include the current definition, would be to define
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
as being the Ricci tensor of a (locally) maximally symmetric Riemannian metric on
\Sigma
, given a choice of boundary conditions (i.e., choice of closed spatial manifold
\Sigma
or choice of asymptotic conditions). For closed non-geometric manifolds, this metric would not be locally homogeneous. However, we are not yet sure if this is a well-defined definition, leading to a unique Ricci tensor, as it does for closed geometric manifolds.

3.3.5
A metric behind
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}

From the definition given in
Section

3.2
, the reference connection and its curvature tensors can be obtained from a metric of the form

{\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}}=\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}_{\mathbb{R}}\boxplus\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{\Sigma}\coloneqq{\rm pr}_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{g}_{\mathbb{R}})\oplus{\rm pr}_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{\Sigma}),

(3.10)

where
\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{\Sigma}
are, respectively, any metric on
\mathbb{R}
and a maximal metric on
\Sigma
. As a consequence, there is a coordinate system in which

\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\pm 1&0\\
0&\bar{h}_{ij}\end{pmatrix}\!,

(3.11)

where
\bar{h}_{ij}
is time-independent. However, the field equations do not depend explicitly on this metric, which is why topo-GR is rather a “bi-connection” theory than a bi-metric theory.

4.4
General results for LSH solutions

We present in this section some results for LSH solutions of topo-GR that are independent of choosing a precise model, i.e., a precise Thurston geometry on which the spatial manifold is modeled.

4.4.1

We can derive a theorem similar to Wald’s theorem
[

for isotropization of LSH solutions in GR in the presence of a positive cosmological constant.

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider an LSH solution of topo-GR with

(i)

a positive cosmological constant

(ii)

matter satisfying the dominant and strong energy conditions,

(iii)

no reference tilt,

(iv)

R-\bar{R}\leq 0
.

If
H>0
initially at time
t_{0}
, then
H>0
for all time
t\geq t_{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\overset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{0}
.
Additionally, for non-tilted perfect fluids, we have
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}-\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\overset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{0}
.

Proof.

Assuming
\boldsymbol{{\bar{\beta}}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the Hamilton constraint (
4.18
) and the Raychaudhuri equation (
4.20
) differ from those of GR only by the presence of
\bar{R}
. The proof is therefore very similar to that of Wald
[
111
]
. In
[
111
]
, the necessary condition for obtaining
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\overset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{0}
was
H^{2}\geq\Uplambda/3
. From the Hamiltonian constraint of GR, this is ensured when
R\leq 0
. In topo-GR,
H^{2}\geq\Uplambda/3
is ensured by the Hamilton constraint (
4.18
) when
R-\bar{R}\leq 0
. For non-tilted perfect fluids, we have
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}-\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\overset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{0}
follows directly from equation (
4.21
) and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\overset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{0}
.
∎

Remark 4.4.

In GR, the fourth condition in
Theorem

4.3
is replaced by
R\leq 0
. This is why for LSH solutions modeled on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
(i.e., Bianchi IX solutions) or
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
(i.e., Kantowski–Sachs solution), a positive cosmological constant do not ensure isotropization of the solution asymptotically. In topo-GR, isotropization is ensured if
R-\bar{R}\leq 0
. As will be shown in Section
5
, this will be fulfilled by
{\rm B_{IX}}
and Kantowski–Sachs metrics. However, surprisingly, for
{\rm B_{II}}
solutions, the condition is not ensured, unless the LRS condition is assumed (
Section

5.7
).

4.4.2
Shear-free LHS solutions

In topo-GR, shear-free (
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{0}
), non-tilted perfect fluids (
\boldsymbol{q}=\boldsymbol{0}=\boldsymbol{\pi}
) solutions exist for all types of topologies. Indeed, for such solutions (
4.19
) and (
4.21
) imply
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
and
\quad\boldsymbol{{\bar{\beta}}}=0
, and the field equations become

\partial_{t}H=-(1+q)H^{2}\,,\\
\Omega_{\rho}=1\,,\\
q=\frac{1}{2}(3w+1)\,.

(4.27)

The shear-free condition implies that the time dependence of the spatial metric
\boldsymbol{h}
in an
Eulerian basis
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\partial}_{t}=\boldsymbol{n}^{\sharp}
, is solely given by a scale factor. In turn, this implies that
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is time independent in the Eulerian basis, automatically fulfilling (
4.22
). The spacetime metric has the form

\boldsymbol{g}=-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}t\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}t+\textsl{a}^{2}(t)\,h_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x^{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x^{j}.

(4.28)

The condition
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
forces
\boldsymbol{h}
to be a maximal metric, unless
\Sigma
is modeled on the
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-geometry. In that case,
\boldsymbol{h}
can have no continuous isotropies, and therefore is not necessarily maximal. This is detailed in
Appendix

A.5
. The field equations (
4.27
) for the scale factor are equal to that of a flat-FLRW model in GR.

The existence of shear-free, non-tilted perfect fluids solutions for all models in topo-GR contrasts with GR for which this type of solutions only exists for Euclidean, spherical, or hyperbolic topologies. In these cases, the solutions are isotropic, as for topo-GR, but the Hamilton constraint in GR still features the curvature parameter. Shear-free solutions in other topologies in GR require the presence of a fluid anisotropic stress
[
64
]
due to the anisotropic Ricci curvature sourcing the shear. Generally, in these GR models, the anisotropic stress is just
ad-hoc
, chosen to be equal to the anisotropic part of the Ricci tensor, but without a physical model of matter giving rise to that stress (see, e.g.,
[
57
]
for a proposition of such a physical model). In particular, when perturbations of the shear-free LSH metric are considered, the anisotropic stress remains a pure background quantity
[
75
,
74
,
76
,
30
]
. Additionally, to get the shear-free solution in GR, the standard approach is to assume that the ad-hoc matter is solely characterized by the anisotropic stress (equal to the anisotropic Ricci curvature), only modifying the evolution equation for the shear, but not the other
3+1
-equations. In topo-GR, the effective density associated to
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
modifies the Hamilton constraint. As a result, the evolution equations for expansion of shear-free solutions in topo-GR do not feature the scalar curvature (as can be seen in (
4.27
) above), while they do so in GR. The direct consequence is that the evolution of the scale factor of shear-free solutions in topo-GR is equivalent to that of flat-FLRW solutions of GR for any BKS type, while in GR it depends on the model, and can feature a bounce or a recollapse.

As a subcase of these solutions, we can consider
\rho=0=p
, leading to (shear-free) static-vacuum solutions, for which the metric is given by

\boldsymbol{g}=-\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}t\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}t+h_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x^{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x^{j},

(4.29)

where, again,
\boldsymbol{h}
is maximal, except for the
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-geometry (Appendix
A.5
). The existence of SH static-vacuum solutions for any topology contrasts strongly with GR, for which this kind of solutions is only possible for Euclidean topologies with the Minkowski metric.

One interesting consequence of the existence of these static solutions is for inflation. In
[
106
]
it was showed that it allows for a well defined Bunch–Davies vacuum for spherical and hyperbolic topologies. This is because in the large-Hubble-radius limit, the perturbation equations become wave equations on a static background (with the Mukhanov–Sasaki equation in particular), hence allowing for canonical quantization. In GR, this is not possible exactly because LHS static-vacuum solutions do not exist for spherical and hyperbolic topologies (see, e.g.,
[
41
]
and references therein). Only after additional (fine-tuned) assumptions on the curvature scale, canonical quantization becomes possible in the large-Hubble-radius limit. In this sense, the existence of static-vacuum solutions in topo-GR makes the construction of inflation and the choice of initial conditions simpler and more natural than in GR. As discussed in
[
106
]
, this result can be viewed as an additional (model-dependent) motivation for the topo-GR theory.

In this context, the fact that in topo-GR static-vacuum solutions exist for all types of topologies suggests that canonical quantization of the inflaton should be feasible in the same way demonstrated in
[
106
]
. If this is true, this would again contrast with GR, for which anisotropic topologies (i.e., different from Euclidean, spherical or hyperbolic) also suffer from the same limitations as spherical and hyperbolic topologies when it comes to quantizing the inflaton
[
75
,
74
]
.

5.1
Relation between the physical metric and the reference curvature

For all models, a prerequisite to be able to compute the field equations is to relate the spatial reference Ricci curvature
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
with the spatial metric
\boldsymbol{h}
. More precisely, given a basis in which
\boldsymbol{h}
is known (e.g., an orthonormal basis), we need to determine the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, using, in particular, its definition as maximal Ricci tensor. The goal of this section is to present the procedure we will follow to tackle this problem. In this section, the maximal and minimal groups
G_{\rm max}
and
G_{\rm min}
are defined as abstract groups, provided in
Table

1
, not belonging to the diffeomorphism group of
\tilde{\Sigma}
.

By definition,
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
is constructed from a maximal Riemannian metric, and therefore there exists a transitive subgroup
\overline{G}_{\rm max}\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
such that
\overline{G}_{\rm max}\cong G_{\rm max}
, where
G_{\rm max}
is one of the maximal groups. Additionally, there exists a transitive subgroup
\overline{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq\overline{G}_{\rm max}
such that
\overline{G}_{\rm min}\cong G_{\rm min}
, where
G_{\rm min}
is one of the minimal groups compatible with
G_{\rm max}
.

Remark 5.1.

There might exist other subgroups
\overline{G}_{\rm max}^{\prime}\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
and
\overline{G}_{\rm min}^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{G}_{\rm max}^{\prime}
such that
\overline{G}_{\rm max}^{\prime}\cong G_{\rm max}
and
\overline{G}_{\rm min}^{\prime}\cong\overline{G}_{\rm min}
, but with
\overline{G}_{\rm max}^{\prime}\not=\overline{G}_{\rm max}
and
\overline{G}_{\rm min}^{\prime}\not=\overline{G}_{\rm min}
. The groups
\overline{G}_{\rm max}^{\prime}
and
\overline{G}_{\rm max}
induce two different families of maximal metrics
\{\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}^{\prime}\}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}\}
on
\tilde{\Sigma}
, both yielding the same Ricci tensor
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. For example, in the case of the
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-geometry, we have
{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})_{0}\cong\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
(where the subscript
0
refers to the connected components to the identity of the group), and there are two families of maximal metrics giving the same Ricci tensor, left and right
{\rm B_{VIII}}
-invariant metrics, respectively. The important point of this remark is that for any subgroups
\overline{G}_{\rm max}
and
\overline{G}_{\rm min}
with the above properties, we can always define a maximal metric
\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}]
and for which
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\bar{h}})=\overline{G}_{\rm max}
and
\overline{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\bar{h}})
.

The physical metric
\boldsymbol{h}
is defined to be a minimal metric. Therefore, there exists a transitive subgroup
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})
such that
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\cong G_{\rm min}
.
Thus, by definition, both
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
are invariant by a transitive subgroup of diffeomorphisms (of
\tilde{\Sigma}
) isomorphic to a minimal group, denoted by
\overline{G}_{\rm min}
and
\hat{G}_{\rm min}
, , respectively, and for a given choice of Thurston geometry we have
\overline{G}_{\rm min}\cong\hat{G}_{\rm min}
. However, since
\overline{G}_{\rm min}
and
\hat{G}_{\rm min}
are isomorphic as subsets of
{\rm Diff}(\tilde{\Sigma})
, in general, they are equal only up to a diffeomorphism. A direct consequence, taking the example of Bianchi groups, is that, in general, a left invariant basis for
\hat{G}_{\rm min}
is not necessarily a left invariant basis for
\overline{G}_{\rm min}
, and vice versa. Therefore, in a basis where the components of
\boldsymbol{h}
are spatially constants, the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
are not necessarily spatially constants, even though the two tensors are locally homogeneous. Therefore, without additional constraints on the relation between the symmetries of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
, a full diffeomorphism freedom remains between the components of these two tensors in a given basis. As presented just below, those constraints come from the field equations.

We assume now that the transitive subgroup
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\subset{\rm Diff}(\tilde{\Sigma})
making the spatial metric locally homogeneous also makes the energy-momentum tensor locally homogeneous, i.e.,
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\rho,p,\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{\pi})
. This is the standard assumptions for LSH models in cosmology. Then, from the properties
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
and
\hat{G}_{\rm BKS}\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{n}^{\sharp}}\boldsymbol{\sigma})
(see
Section

2.4
), the equations (
4.18
) and (
4.26
) directly imply
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
, leading to

\hat{G}_{\rm min}=\overline{G}_{\rm min}\,.

(5.1)

Therefore, from the remark
5.1
, we can consider a maximal metric
\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}]
which is invariant by the group
\hat{G}_{\rm min}
. We shall show in Sections
5.3
to
5.10
that, except for the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, this property is sufficient to fully determine the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
as functions of those of
\boldsymbol{h}
. Note that
a priori

{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\bar{h}})
does not hold and therefore
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
does not hold either. Hence, as for the shear tensor (see
Section

2.4
), in models where the spatial metric has more continuous symmetries than that described by
\hat{G}_{\rm min}
, those symmetries are not necessarily symmetries of the reference Ricci tensor. We discuss this in
Section

6
.

Now, as mentioned in the beginning of this section, the goal is to determine the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
with respect to those of
\boldsymbol{h}
. For Bianchi metrics, this amounts to answering the following question.

Question 5.2
.

What is the expression of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}

in an orthonormal Milnor basis for
\boldsymbol{h}
?

To answer
5.2
, a three-step program will be followed:

(i)

We consider a left-invariant basis
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\}
, in which the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, denoted by
\bar{R}_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}
, are known, and a unit Milnor basis in which
\bar{R}_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}
do not feature free parameters. As explained in
Section

2.5
, these components can easily be obtained from the third column of Table
3
with a rescaling of the basis vectors.

(ii)

We determine the general transformation matrix
\Lambda
, with components
\Lambda_{i}{}^{\bar{i}}
, between the basis
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\}
and a second Milnor basis
\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\}
defined to be orthonormal with respect to
\boldsymbol{h}
, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}=\Lambda_{i}{}^{\bar{i}}\boldsymbol{\bar{e}}_{\bar{i}}\,.

(5.2)

Due to (
5.1
),
\Lambda
is space-independent. We will decompose this matrix into an automorphism
A
of the Lie algebra of
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\}
, and a diagonal transformation
H
. (Note that this decomposition will not be unique in general.)

(iii)

Using
\Lambda
, we compute the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
in the orthonormal basis
\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\}
. We denote them by
{\bar{R}}_{ij}
. We thus have

\bar{R}_{ij}=\Lambda_{i}{}^{\bar{i}}\Lambda_{j}{}^{\bar{j}}\bar{R}_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}\,.

(5.3)

Once this program is fulfilled, the field equations in the orthonormal basis can be computed, using the orthonormal approach detailed in the next section.

For the
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
-geometry, no
G_{\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}}
-invariant basis can be defined. For this geometry we will directly use a canonical coordinate system to determine the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
and
\boldsymbol{h}
, and to express the field equations.

Remark 5.4.

In the literature, Bianchi VII

As shown in Section

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}-\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(n_{1}^{2}-n_{2}^{2}-n_{3}^{2}+n_{2}n_{3}\right){\boldsymbol{e}}^{1}\otimes{\boldsymbol{e}}^{1}\\

\displaystyle\dot{H}

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{1}

13
13

13

The maximal metric is both a

and the physical metric
\boldsymbol{h}
. Therefore,

[

. The only difference to GR is that the scalar curvature is not anymore present in the Friedmann equation.

5.6.2
Field equations

As shown in
Appendix

A.2
, any locally homogeneous vector field is in the kernel of the Ricci tensor. Therefore, we always have
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}\left(\boldsymbol{{\bar{\beta}}},\cdot\right)=\boldsymbol{0}
. Additionally, any locally homogeneous symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor field is transverse, implying

) in the orthonormal basis

and therefore depends only on one variable. The consequence is that the physical metric can be written in the simple form (resembling that of Kantowski–Sachs by replacing

\sinh^{2}x
, representing the hyperbolic fibers)

(5.36)

implying that the (reference) Ricci curvature have the form

\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=-\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x+\sinh^{2}x\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}y\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}y\right).

(5.37)

Then, the field equations of a general LSH solution of topo-GR with perfect fluid on a geometric manifold modeled on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}
are

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\sigma}+\Omega_{\rho}\,,

(5.38a)

\displaystyle\dot{H}

\displaystyle=-(1+q)H^{2}\,,

(5.38b)

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}^{i}{}_{j}

\displaystyle=-3H\sigma^{i}{}_{j}\,,

(5.38c)

with

H=4\frac{\dot{A}}{A}+2\frac{\dot{B}}{B},\quad\left(\sigma^{i}{}_{j}\right)=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\dot{A}}{A}-\frac{\dot{B}}{B}\right){\rm diag}\left(1,1,-2\right),\quad H^{2}\Omega_{\sigma}=\frac{4}{9}\left(\frac{\dot{A}}{A}-\frac{\dot{B}}{B}\right)^{2}.

(5.39)

\Omega_{R-\bar{R}}=0
. These equations are similar to those in GR, with the difference that the spatial curvature is not present anymore. As a consequence, the shear parameter always evolves as

{\rm B_{I}}
metrics.

5.11

In

to

Because

fails for a general LSH solution on the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, the isotropization theorem does not apply for that geometry, unless the LRS condition is assumed. This is a very peculiar and unexpected result. Indeed, the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry is, algebraically speaking, one of the simplest geometry, and, in GR, LSH solutions on this geometry (i.e., Bianchi II solutions) are, dynamically speaking, the second simplest LSH solutions after the Bianchi I solutions. In particular, Wald’s theorem holds for Bianchi II solutions in GR. In this sense, we did not expect any fundamental difference for the Bianchi II solutions between the two theories.
Moreover, four purely geometric properties make LSH solutions of topo-GR on the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry even more peculiar:

(i)

for all geometries apart from the non-LRS
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, when the metric
\boldsymbol{h}
has more local isometries than that imposed by the minimal group, these isometries are always symmetries of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, i.e.,
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
always holds. For the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, this is not the case, as the fourth Killing vector field of the metric is not necessarily a collineation vector field for
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, unless
r_{2}=r_{3}
in (
5.44
).

(ii)

for all geometries except for the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, when
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is maximal, then
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. For the
\{\mathbb{E}^{3},\ \mathbb{H}^{3},\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2},\ \mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}\}
-geometries this is trivially obtained. For the
\{\mathbb{S}^{3},\ \mathbb{S}\mathbbm{ol},\ \widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\}
-geometries, maximality of
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is achieved when
n_{1}=n_{2}=n_{3}
,
n_{2}=-n_{3}
and
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=0
, respectively, and relations (
5.25
), (
5.58
) and (
5.67
) imply
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. But, the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry is the only geometry where this property does not hold, as
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is always maximal but
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}\not=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
by relation (
5.45
), in general.

(iii)

for all geometries other than the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, given a basis in which
\boldsymbol{h}
is known, the components of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
are fully expressed as functions of the components of
\boldsymbol{h}
and the commutation coefficients of the basis. Taking the example of the orthonormal Milnor basis for Bianchi metrics, then
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
is uniquely expressed for each model as a function of
n_{i}
and

\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}

and

.

(iv)

the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry is the only geometry where the absence of reference tilt
\boldsymbol{{\bar{\beta}}}
is needed to get
\div_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{0}
via (
4.23
). Additionally, it is the only geometry for which the evolution equation (
4.22
) for
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
provides equations independent of the other 3+1-equations.

We were unable to identify a definite explanation for the mentioned peculiarity of the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry. We hoped to find a “hidden” geometric or topological condition that would impose either
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
or directly
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
. When the topology is

(see
[
54
,
55
]

{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})

5.7.3
). But for the other closed manifolds modeled on
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
, no such constraint arises, and the peculiarities listed above remain.

We believe that these peculiarities, and especially the first one in the list above, suggest that the definition of
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
should be slightly changed such that either
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
always holds, or perhaps the stronger condition “
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is maximal
\Rightarrow

\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
” always holds. That is, we would eliminate by definition (part of, or all of) the diffeomorphism freedom that might remain between
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
and
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
when both are maximal. Ideally, this redefinition should imply
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
prior to considering the field equation. We recall that in Section
5.1
, we had to use the field equations to show that the Killing vectors related to the
\hat{G}_{\rm min}\subseteq{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})
of the spatial metric are collineations of
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
, leading to (
5.1
).

Imposing
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
is, however, foliation dependent, as it features explicitly the spatial metric
\boldsymbol{h}
. We would rather want this property to hold regardless of the chosen foliation. Therefore, the “redefinition” of
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
would be made such that, as a consequence, the following property would hold.

Property 6.1
.

For
all

\Sigma
-foliations, spacelike with respect to
\boldsymbol{g}
,
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}})
holds, where
\boldsymbol{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
are, respectively, the spatial metric induced by
\boldsymbol{g}
on the foliation and the spatial reference Ricci tensor induced by
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
on the foliation.

Adding
6.1
to the definition of topo-GR would be very artificial. Ideally, we would want that the way
\mathchoice{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 6.15276pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 7.82512pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}{\mathop{}\kern 8.7909pt\mathopen{\vphantom{|}}^{\mathmakebox[0pt][l]{\mathmakebox[r]{4\,}}}}\boldsymbol{\overline{\rm Ric}}
is constructed would encompass both the above property, and the current definition (
3.4
) in terms of external Whitney sum. Even more ideally, we would like this definition not to depend on the spacetime manifold being topologically
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
as assumed in the paper (see the discussion in
Section

3.3.4
). However, we have not yet found a natural and compact way of doing that.

Finally, let us clarify an important point here: the result for
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
and the discussion we just made do
not
mean that the theory is currently ill-defined. Indeed, the system of equations (
5.49
) for
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
is closed, and more generally, the 3+1-system derived in
Section

4.2
is closed. Rather, the results for non-LRS Bianchi II models on the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry contradict the understanding and interpretation we have had so far of the theory, namely that the solutions of the theory are qualitatively the same regardless of which topology is chosen. With this respect, if one wants the topo-GR theory to fully comply with this line of interpretation, one might want
6.1
to result from the definition of the theory.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we have determined the systems of equations for locally spatially homogeneous (LSH) solutions, admitting a Bianchi–Kantowski–Sachs (BKS) metric, of a topology dependent modified gravity theory,
topo-GR
. We focused on non-tilted LSH solutions for closed
3
-manifolds, hence using the correspondence between the Thurston classification and BKS metrics. Except for the
\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il}
-geometry, all models feature the same number of free-parameters as in general relativity (GR). Our four main results are:

(i)

All BKS models admit shear-free exact solutions for perfect fluids with a dynamics equivalent to that of a flat FLRW metric in GR. This contrasts with GR, for which for maximal geometries other than
\{\mathbb{E}^{3},\ \mathbb{S}^{3},\ \mathbb{H}^{3}\}
, shear-free solutions are only possible if the matter has a non-vanishing anisotropic stress (cf., e.g.,
[
63
]
).

(ii)

All BKS models admit a static vacuum solution. The spacetime metric features no lapse and the induced spatial metric is static and corresponds to the maximal metric (apart from the
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-geometry where it can be minimal) related to the spatial topology. In contrast, static vacuum LSH solutions in GR are only possible for the
\mathbb{E}^{3}
-geometry, with the Minkowski metric.

(iii)

For all BKS models, apart from the non-locally rotationally symmetric (non-LRS) Bianchi II model, recollapse is
never
possible when the weak energy condition is fulfilled. In contrast, Bianchi IX solutions (
\mathbb{S}^{3}
-geometry) and Kantowski-Sachs solutions (
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
-geometry) can recollapse in GR (cf., e.g.,
[
11
]
).

(iv)

For all BKS models, apart from the non-LRS Bianchi II model, a Wald-like theorem
[
111
]
holds: a positive cosmological constant ensures isotropization at late times. This differs from GR for which LSH solutions on the
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
geometries do not isotropize without fine-tuning on the value of the initial spatial curvature.

With respect to the recollapse and the isotropization problems, these results show that topo-GR behaves better than GR without introducing additional parameters, with the only exception of the non-LRS Bianchi II model. We discussed in
Section

6
why this latter result is peculiar.

In direct continuation of this work, there are two main perspectives: dynamical analysis and tilted models. Indeed, while we provided some exact solutions for each model, we did not conduct an in-depth analysis of the systems of equations. In this regard, studying the dynamics towards the initial singularity and the presence or absence of chaos will be one of the main follow-ups. Further, while for the
\{\mathbb{E}^{3}\,({\rm with\ Bianchi\ I}),\ \mathbb{H}^{3},\ \mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2},\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}\}
-geometries generic solutions do not feature tilt, for the other geometries, we imposed a vanishing tilt. Generalizing this work to tilted models for the
\{\mathbb{S}^{3},\mathbb{N}\mathbbm{il},\mathbb{S}\mathbbm{ol},\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\}
-geometries would be therefore another main follow-up.

Finally, as discussed in Section
4.4.2
, the existence of static vacuum solutions in any topology could be beneficial for quantizing inflation and defining a Bunch–Davies vacuum in any topology, as was shown in
[
106
]
for
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
\mathbb{H}^{3}
types of topologies. Alongside this potential benefit for inflation, shear-free solutions in topo-GR also suggest the possibility to extend the
\Lambda
CDM model to any Thurston geometry without adding any parameter (other than the spatial curvature already present in the
\Lambda
CDM model) and without changing the background dynamics. In this sense, topo-GR would offer a way to define a universal cosmological model, formally equivalent for any spatial topology.

The maximal number of linearly independent Ricci collineations of a maximal metric on
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-geometric manifolds is
6
and is achieved if and only if
A_{1}=2A_{2}
. In this case, the Ricci tensor is left and right invariant under
\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
, and its collineations are

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{3},\ {\boldsymbol{e}}_{1},\ {\boldsymbol{e}}_{2},\ {\boldsymbol{e}}_{3},

(A.14)

with
{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}})_{0}=\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Remark A.8.

From (
A.12
), we can see that a minimal metric with
A_{1}=A_{2}+A_{3}
(equivalently with
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=0
in an orthonormal Milnor basis) produces the same Ricci tensor as a maximal metric (i.e.,
A_{2}=A_{3}
, equivalently
n_{2}=n_{3}
in an orthonormal Milnor basis) with
A_{1}=2A_{2}
(equivalently with
n_{1}+2n_{2}=0
in an orthonormal Milnor basis).

Proof.

The Ricci tensor of a maximal
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-metric is

\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]=\left(\frac{A_{1}}{2A_{2}}\right)^{2}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}^{1}\otimes{\boldsymbol{e}}^{1}+\frac{A_{1}(A_{1}+2A_{2})}{2A_{2}A_{1}}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}^{2}\otimes{\boldsymbol{e}}^{2}+\frac{\left(A_{2}\right)^{2}-(A_{1}+A_{2})^{2}}{2A_{1}A_{2}}\,{\boldsymbol{e}}^{3}\otimes{\boldsymbol{e}}^{3}\,.

(A.15)

This tensor is always non-degenerate for any value of
A_{1}>0
and
A_{2}>0
, with always two negative eigenvalues and one positive eigenvalue. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]
is a Lorentzian metric and for this reason its maximum number of collineations is
6
. This is achieved if and only if
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]
is an Einstein metric, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}\left[\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]\right]\propto\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]
. Solving the latter equation yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}\left[\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]\right]=\frac{1}{4}\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}],\quad{\rm iff}\ A_{1}=2A_{2}\,.

(A.16)

This shows that for
A_{1}=2A_{2}
, a maximal
\widetilde{\mathbb{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
-metric has
6
linearly independent Ricci collineations. If
A_{1}\not=2A_{2}
, then
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}[\boldsymbol{h}]
is not an Einstein metric and the maximum number of Ricci collineations of
\boldsymbol{h}
is
4
. This is because, on a
3
-manifold, the isometry group of a metric cannot be 5-dimensional (cf., e.g.,
[
110
, Section 1.2]
).

Finally, a direct computation of
\mathcal{L}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}}\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
for all left-invariant vector basis (given in
Table

7
), shows that all
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
are collineations of
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
, and therefore
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is right-invariant, implying
{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}})_{0}=\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
(where the subscript
0
means the connected component of the identity). Interestingly, this implies that
\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
is the Killing form of
\widetilde{{\rm SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
(e.g.
[
71
]
).
∎

A.5.2
Bianchi VIII metrics as Bianchi III metrics

If a metric
\boldsymbol{h}
is at the same time a
{\rm B_{VIII}}
and a
{\rm B_{III}}
metric, then it can be represented in two different orthonormal Milnor bases having the algebra of the former group and the latter group, respectively, hence non-isomorphic algebras. In the Bianchi VIII representation,
n^{\rm VIII}_{2}=n^{\rm VIII}_{3}
is imposed by the presence of a fourth Killing vector additional to the three coming from the
{\rm B_{VIII}}
group. In the Bianchi III representation,
n^{\rm III}_{2}\not=a^{\rm III}
is imposed, otherwise the
{\rm B_{III}}
metric is a
G_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2}}
metric. Since these are two different representations of the same metric, one can relate
n^{\rm VIII}_{1}
and
n^{\rm VIII}_{2}
to
n^{\rm III}_{2}
and
a^{\rm III}
. To do so, it is sufficient to calculate the Ricci scalar
\tr_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{{\rm Ric}}
and the Ricci square
R_{ij}R^{ij}
. Since these are invariant quantities, they must agree in both bases. One finds

n^{\rm VIII}_{1}=\frac{\left|\left(a^{\rm III}\right)^{2}-\left(n^{\rm III}_{2}\right)^{2}\right|}{n^{\rm III}_{2}},\\
n^{\rm VIII}_{2}=\frac{4\,\left(a^{\rm III}\right)^{2}\,n^{\rm III}_{2}}{\left|\left(a^{\rm III}\right)^{2}-\left(n^{\rm III}_{2}\right)^{2}\right|},\\
\forall\,n^{\rm III}_{2}\neq a^{\rm III}.

(A.17)

A.6
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
-geometry

On
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the generators of
G_{\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}}
-invariance are the vector fields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}=\cos y\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{x}-\cot x\sin y\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{y}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}=\sin y\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{x}+\cot x\cos y\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{y}\,,\\
&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3}=\boldsymbol{\partial}_{y}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{4}=\boldsymbol{\partial}_{z}\,,\end{split}

(A.18)

with

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}]=-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3},\quad[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3}]=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\quad[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{3}]=-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},

(A.19)

and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{4}
commutes with all the other vector fields.

A general
G_{\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}}
-invariant (Kantowski–Sachs) metric
\boldsymbol{h}
has the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}=A_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x+\sin^{2}x\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}y\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}y\right)+A_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}z\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}z.

(A.20)

By a direct computation with the Lie derivative along the vector fields (
A.18
), one can prove the following proposition.

Proposition A.9
.

Let
\Sigma
be a closed manifold modeled on the
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
-geometry. Let
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\boldsymbol{n}
and
\boldsymbol{A}
be, respectively, a vector field, a 1-form and a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor on
\Sigma
, all locally
G_{\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}}
-invariant. Then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\partial}_{z}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=c_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}z\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}

\displaystyle=c_{3}\,\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}x+\sin^{2}x\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}y\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}y\right)+c_{4}\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}z\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathrm{d}}z\,,

where
c_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,4
are constants.

A direct consequence of this proposition is

Corollary A.10
.

Given a locally
G_{\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}}
-invariant metric
\boldsymbol{h}
on an
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{S}^{2}
-geometric manifold
\Sigma
, any symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor
\boldsymbol{A}
such that
{\rm Isom}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{h})\subseteq{\rm Sym}(\tilde{\Sigma},\boldsymbol{A})
, is a transverse tensor field, i.e.,
\div_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{A}=0
.
