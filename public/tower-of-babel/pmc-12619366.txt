Influence of prepregnancy BMI and weight gain during pregnancy on pregnancy outcomes in women: a cohort study based in Yunnan, China
Authors: Yating Wu, Xiong Zhang, Zhongming Zhao, Xueji Che, Qian Chen, Min Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and gestational weight gain (GWG) are important determinants of maternal and neonatal health. While there is a link between maternal weight and pregnancy complications, evidence in multiethnic regions with diverse lifestyles and socioeconomic backgrounds is scarce. Yunnan Province, located in southwest china, is a multiethnic region with significant geographic and cultural diversity, providing a unique setting to examine these associations in underrepresented populations. This study examines the impact of pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG on pregnancy outcomes within Yunnan’s diverse cohorts, with the aim of addressing localised knowledge gaps and informing the development of targeted antenatal care strategies.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from 13,221 pregnant women from 25 ethnic groups in Yunnan Province between 2019 and 2023. Prepregnancy BMI was classified according to Chinese standards (underweight: <18.5 kg/m²; normal: 18.5–23.9 kg/m²; overweight: 24.0–27.9 kg/m²; obese: ≥28.0 kg/m²). GWG adequacy was assessed against Institute of Medicine guidelines. Prenatal records, delivery modes, and neonatal outcomes were extracted from provincial maternal health databases. Chi-square tests and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify risk factors. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was employed to quantify the direct and indirect effects to prepregnancy BMI and GWG on outcomes such as birth weight and preterm birth.ResultsThe cohort included 13,221 women with an average age of 20.01 years and an average BMI of prepregnancy of 22.26 kg/m2[standard deviation (SD)3.70] . Nutritional profiles varied: 11.9% were underweight (compared to a national average of 8.2%), and 51.5% had abnormal GWG (20.0% insufficient and 31.5% excessive). Compared to normal weight women, underweight women were at an increased risks of cesarean delivery (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.26–1.63), postpartum haemorrhage (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.10–1.67), low birth weight (OR 4.71, 95% CI: 2.53–8.77), and gestational diabetes (OR 3.58, 95% CI: 1.57–8.17). SEM analysis indicated that prepregnancy BMI directly predicted birth weight (β=0.036, p<0.001), while GWG moderated the overall effect of BMI on premature birth (indirect effect 0.18, p<0.01) but not on birth weight (p=0.89).ConclusionLow prepregnancy weight has been found to be associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as caesarean section, low birth weight and premature delivery. Our findings suggest that prepregnancy BMI influences outcome via dual-pathway effects: it directly influences birth weight and indirectly modulation preterm risk through gestational diabetes. Those findings emphasise the importance of ethnicity-specific GWG monitoring and targeted antenatal care strategies.

Introduction

Maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and gestational weight gain (GWG) are widely recognised as key determinants of maternal and neonatal health. These indicators reflect women’s nutritional status before and during pregnancy, predict fetal growth and development [1], and are important risk factors for adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis based on the 2009 Institute of Medicine (IOM) guidelines found that 47% of pregnant women exceeded GWG recommendations, while 23% had insufficient GWG [3]. Excessive GWG is associated with an increased risk of caesarean delivery, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, postpartum weight retention, macrosomia, and childhood overweight or obesity, whereas insufficient GWG is linked to a higher risk of low birth weight, preterm delivery, and delayed initiation of breastfeeding.

Although the associations between pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG and pregnancy outcomes have been extensively investigated, most of the evidence comes from relatively homogeneous populations in high-income countries or eastern regions of China. Data from multiethnic and socioeconomically diverse settings remain limited. Yunnan Province, located in southwestern China, is home to 25 ethnic minority groups and characterised by complex geography and marked disparities in lifestyle and dietary habits. These factors contribute to varied dietary patterns, lifestyles, and access to healthcare services, which may influence maternal nutritional status and pregnancy outcomes. However, it is unclear to what extent pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG affect maternal and neonatal health in such a diverse and resource-limited context.

The aim of this study was to examine the impact of pre-pregnancy BMI and GWG on pregnancy outcomes among women from diverse ethnic groups in Yunnan Province, China.The findings are expected to inform the development of tailored antenatal care strategies and ethnicity-specific GWG monitoring systems in multiethnic, resource-limited settings by addressing a key knowledge gap.

Materials and methods

Study population

The present study was a retrospective longitudinal cohort study. Data were obtained from the health monitoring database for pregnant women and newborns in an ethnic minority area of Yunnan Province. The sociodemographic characteristics, prenatal examination records, pregnancy outcomes and delivery information of women who gave birth between 2019 and 2023 were collected.

Measurements

The prepregnancy height (m) and weight (kg) were collected at the time of enrollment to calculate the BMI (kg/m2). The BMI standards for Chinese adults were followed [3], pregnant women were categorized as underweight with a BMI of < 18.5 kg/m2, adequate weight with 18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2, overweight with 24.0 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28.0 kg/m2, and obese with a BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2. On the basis of the recommendations of optimal GWG for Chinese women, the range of adequate GWG was 11–16 kg for women with a prepregnancy BMI of < 18.5 kg, 8–14 kg for women with a prepregnancy BMI of 18.5–23.9 kg, and 7–11 kg for women with a prepregnancy BMI of 24–27.9 kg and 5–9 kg for women with a prepregnancy BMI of ≥ 28.0 kg (20). When the weight gain during pregnancy is within the recommended range, it is defined as appropriate weight gain. When the overall weight gain is lower than the lower limit of the range, it is defined as insufficient weight gain. When the total weight gain exceeds the upper limit of the range, it is defined as excessive weight gain.

The primary outcomes were preterm birth, low birth weight (LBW) infancy and macrosomia. The secondary outcomes were cesarean delivery, gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes mellitus. Preterm birth was defined as a gestational age at delivery < 37 weeks. LBW was defined as a birth weight less than 2500 g. Macrosomia was defined as a birth weight greater than 4000 g.

Sociodemographic information, including age, education, ethnicity, nativity and reproductive history, including parity and history of miscarry, was obtained via a standardized handbook of maternal health care at enrollment.

Statistical analysis

The database was established via SPSS 25.0, and statistical analysis was conducted on the collected data. Continuous data were described using means and standard deviations, and for continuous variables, independent samples t tests or one-way ANOVA were used. Categorical variables were analyzed via the chi-square test. A logistic regression model was applied to examine the effects of prepregnancy BMI and GWG (gestational weight gain) on pregnancy outcomes. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Structural equation modeling

Results

A total of 13,221 pregnant women took part in the study. The mean maternal age was 20.01 years (range 18–57 years). The mean prepregnancy BMI was 22.26 kg/m² (SD 3.70), with 60.6% of participants having a normal weight, 11.9% being underweight, 20.1% being overweight and 7.5% being obese. In terms of GWG, 48.9% of participants had an adequate weight gain, 20.0% had an inadequate gain, and 31.1% had an excessive gain. The mean weight before pregnancy was 55.6 kg (SD 9.7) and the mean total GWG at delivery was 11.4 kg (SD 5.0).

Preterm delivery occurred in 1.1% of cases. Modes of delivery included spontaneous vaginal delivery (64.5%), caesarean Sect. (31.5%), and assisted vaginal delivery by midwives (3.9%). Significant differences were revealed in comparisons across BMI categories in terms of maternal age, education level, obstetric history, gestational weight gain (GWG), gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), delivery mode, postpartum haemorrhage and neonatal birth weight. Being underweight before pregnancy was strongly associated with low birth weight, while insufficient GWG was associated with preterm birth, which indirectly affects birth weight. Discussion

Impact of prepregnancy BMI and GWG on delivery mode

Our study demonstrates that both prepregnancy BMI and GWG significantly influence pregnancy outcomes, particularly delivery mode, preterm birth and birth weight. Notably, our findings regarding BMI and delivery mode differ from those of most previous studies. While many studies have shown that a higher prepregnancy BMI is associated with an increased risk of caesarean section — 1.82-fold for obese women and 2.6-fold for severely obese women (BMI > 40) — we found that compared to women with a normal BMI, those who were underweight had a 1.431-fold higher risk, while those who were overweight had a significantly reduced risk [4]. Additionally, GWG showed no significant association with delivery mode, contrary to several reports. These discrepancies may reflect regional differences in lifestyle, dietary habits, and ethnicity. The relationship between GWG and caesarean section remains controversial. For example, a prospective Dutch cohort study found that early pregnancy GWG increased caesarean section risk (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.10–1.29), whereas mid- or late pregnancy GWG showed no association [5].

Unexpected association between inadequate GWG and preterm birth

Contrary to most global studies that have reported an increased risk with both insufficient and excessive GWG [6–8], we unexpectedly found that inadequate GWG was negatively associated with preterm birth (OR = 0.31, 95% CI 0.17–0.56). This result should be interpreted with caution. The small number of preterm deliveries (n = 141, accounting for 1.1% of the sample) potentially limited statistical power, despite the application of Firth’s correction to mitigate the effects of sparse data. Residual confounding factors, such as unmeasured nutritional intervention intensity, placental function or other biological factors, may also have influenced the association. One possible explanation is the compensatory effect of nutritional support provided by the Maternal and Child Health Package programme, which has been implemented in Yunnan since 2018 and provides high-risk pregnant women with inadequate GWG with free micronutrients (e.g. folic acid and iron). This intervention may have mitigated the adverse impact of low GWG on preterm birth by improving placental function [9].

Evidence from other settings has shown that inadequate GWG can lead to preterm birth via reduced uteroplacental blood flow, insufficient plasma volume expansion, micronutrient deficiencies, impaired antioxidant activity and an increased susceptibility to infection and inflammation [7, 8]. Meanwhile, excessive GWG can elevate the risk by inducing a proinflammatory state and promoting fluid retention, which is associated with preeclampsia [10]. However, our findings are consistent with those of a US study which reported a positive correlation between GWG and preterm birth [11, 12], as well as a Peruvian study which suggested a U-shaped association between GWG rate and preterm birth among women with normal or overweight prepregnancy BMI [13]. The heterogeneity in results highlights the need for culturally and regionally contextualised interpretations.

Pre-pregnancy BMI as a determinant of birth weight

With respect to birth weight, we found that prepregnancy BMI was positively associated with the weight of newborns at birth, which is consistent with previous studies. However, mediation analysis revealed no significant indirect effect through gestational weight gain (GWG) (β = 0.0001, p = 0.892). This differs from many Western studies [14] and may reflect Yunnan’s unique dietary context, where pregnant women are exposed to both traditional low-protein diets and high-fat fast foods, which weakens the predictive value of GWG for birth weight [9]. In line with other reports, severe, moderate and mild underweight prior to pregnancy were each associated with an increased risk of intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight and very low birth weight, in a dose-response manner [10, 13]. In our cohort, underweight women were 4.708 times more likely to deliver a low-birth-weight infant, and their newborns’ average birth weight was significantly lower. In our analysis, the influence of prepregnancy BMI on birth weight was predominantly direct, highlighting the importance of optimising maternal weight before conception.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Firstly, it is a large-scale cohort study involving a multiethnic population in a geographically and socioeconomically diverse region of southwestern China. Using a health monitoring database minimised follow-up losses and enabled a comprehensive assessment of maternal and neonatal outcomes. Furthermore, mediation analysis enabled us to quantify the direct and indirect effects of BMI and GWG on these outcomes.

Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective design and reliance on registry data may introduce misclassification bias, particularly with regard to self-reported prepregnancy weight. Secondly, missing covariates (3.2%) may reduce representativeness. Secondly, GWG was assessed only as a total pregnancy value without trimester-specific analysis, which precludes the evaluation of critical periods. Thirdly, unmeasured factors such as detailed dietary intake, physical activity, socioeconomic status and culturally specific health behaviours could contribute to residual confounding. Fourthly, the findings may only be generalisable to regions with similar multiethnic compositions, healthcare access disparities and public health interventions. Notably, the availability of the Maternal and Child Health Package programme (free supplementation of folic acid and iron, etc.) in Yunnan may mitigate GWG-related risks, whereas areas without such interventions may not yield comparable results.

Conclusion

In this study, a structural equation model (SEM) was used for the first time to reveal the complex relationship between prepregnancy BMI and pregnancy outcome in a Yunnan multiethnic cohort. Unlike conventional regression approaches, SEM mediation analysis delineates the direct effect of prepregnancy BMI on birth weight (β = 0.0362, p < 0.001) and its indirect effect on preterm birth via GWG (indirect effect = 0.18, p < 0.01), offering novel insights into the dynamic pathways between nutritional status and pregnancy outcomes. This approach is particularly valuable for multifactorial interaction studies, such as health disparity research in resource-limited settings. This study identified distinct distribution patterns of prepregnancy underweight (11.9%) and abnormal GWG (51.5%) in Yunnan. Against the backdrop of mountainous geography and multicultural coexistence, these findings provide irreplaceable evidence for designing ethnicity-tailored interventions.
