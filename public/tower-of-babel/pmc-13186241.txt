Recent Advances in Nanomedicine for Targeted Phototherapy in Head and Neck Cancer
Authors: Hongshuai Liu, Chen Lin, Wenying Li, Linrong Wu, Zihao Xia, Yi Wu, Yanqi Huang, Shanshan Gu, Junjun Zhang, Zhisen Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHead and neck cancer is the seventh most common malignancy worldwide. Conventional treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, have achieved substantial clinical success. However, their efficacy remains limited due to treatment-associated toxicity and suboptimal therapeutic outcomes. Phototherapy, encompassing photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT), has emerged as a promising anticancer strategy owing to its high selectivity, minimal invasiveness, and low systemic toxicity. These modalities exert antitumor effects through distinct yet complementary mechanisms. PDT utilizes a photosensitizer activated by light of a specific wavelength to generate reactive oxygen species, thereby inducing cytotoxicity. In contrast, PTT employs photothermal agents to convert light energy into heat for tumor ablation. With advances in photosensitizer design, nanotechnology, and immunotherapy, phototherapy has gained increasing attention as a potential alternative for the treatment of head and neck cancer. In this review, we provide a concise overview of the fundamental principles and mechanisms of PDT and PTT, and present recent progress in smart phototherapeutic agents. For example, liposome-based biomimetic nanodelivery systems enable precise regulation of biodistribution and release kinetics, while carbon-based materials and hybrid nanostructures have attracted considerable interest due to their multifunctionality and applicability in both PDT and PTT. Moreover, we discuss recent applications of PTT/PDT in combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and gene therapy for head and neck cancer. We also summarize progress regarding agents approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and ongoing clinical trials. Finally, we highlight the major challenges and future directions for the broader clinical translation of nanomedicine-based targeted phototherapy.Keywords: nanoparticles, photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) comprises a group of malignancies arising from the mucosa of the upper aerodigestive tract, including the oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, paranasal sinuses, and larynx. Collectively, these malignancies represent the seventh most common type of cancer worldwide.1 According to the Global Burden of Disease 2023 study, approximately 1.01 million new cases and 550,000 deaths were reported globally in 2023, underscoring the substantial public health burden of HNC.2 In addition, data from the American Cancer Society indicate that approximately 71,110 new cases and 16,110 deaths were expected in the United States of America in 2024, highlighting the continued regional impact of this disease. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the predominant histological subtype, accounting for more than 90% of cases.3 Although HNC occurs in both sexes, its incidence is 2–4 times higher in males, reflecting sex-related differences in exposure to risk factors. Tobacco and alcohol use remain the most prevalent risk factors worldwide.4 A critical etiological and clinical distinction within head and neck SCC (HNSCC) lies in the presence or absence of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. HPV-positive tumors predominantly arise in the oropharynx and represent a distinct clinical and molecular entity associated with sexual behavior, typically exhibiting enhanced therapeutic responsiveness and more favorable prognosis.5 In the United States of America and Europe, approximately 60–70% of oropharyngeal cancers are attributable to HPV infection, further emphasizing its etiological significance in these regions.6 In contrast, HPV-negative HNSCC is primarily associated with chronic exposure to carcinogens such as tobacco and alcohol. These tumors are biologically and clinically distinct from their HPV-positive counterparts. They often demonstrate greater therapeutic resistance, leading to poorer clinical outcomes and increased risks of recurrence and metastasis.7

Importantly, HNC is characterized by a high rate of locoregional recurrence, with approximately 50% of patients experiencing disease relapse, most commonly within 2 years following initial treatment.8 Despite advances in multimodal therapies, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the overall 5-year survival rate has remained at approximately 50%, indicating limited improvement over recent decades.9 Moreover, these treatments are frequently associated with substantial toxicity and functional impairment, significantly compromising the quality of life of patients. This challenge is particularly pronounced in HPV-negative HNSCC, which exhibits poor responsiveness to conventional therapies and persistently unfavorable prognosis.10 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more effective and better-tolerated therapeutic strategies to improve clinical outcomes in patients with HNC.

Phototherapy, including photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT), has emerged as a promising treatment modality for cancer owing to its high selectivity, minimal invasiveness, and low systemic toxicity.11 Moreover, its low propensity for inducing resistance and capacity for repeated administration render it particularly suitable for recurrent and aggressive HNCs.12 In PDT, photosensitizers generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby inducing tumor cell death, disrupting tumor vasculature, and activating antitumor immune responses.13 In contrast, PTT relies on the photothermal conversion capability of photoabsorbing agents to generate localized hyperthermia, leading to tumor ablation. Both modalities have demonstrated considerable efficacy in preclinical and clinical settings, with relatively mild adverse effects.14 Thus, they have been attracting increasing attention in the management of head and neck malignancies. Despite these advantages, the clinical translation of phototherapy remains constrained by several critical challenges.15 As light propagates through biological tissues, it is absorbed and scattered by endogenous biomolecules (eg, proteins, lipids, DNA, and RNA) This substantially reduces the amount of light reaching the tumor site. Conventional phototherapeutic agents typically operate within the ultraviolet–visible spectrum, which lies outside the optimal biological transparency window (650–950 nm), thereby limiting tissue penetration to less than ~3 mm and restricting treatment efficacy in deep-seated tumors.16 In addition, numerous phototherapeutic agents are characterized by rapid systemic clearance and poor bioavailability, further compromising therapeutic efficiency. The generation of ROS is often hindered by multiple factors, including tumor hypoxia, aggregation-caused quenching of photosensitizers, and insufficient light penetration in tissues.17,18 Furthermore, heat diffusion during PTT may inadvertently damage surrounding healthy tissues, raising concerns regarding the off-target effects of this modality.19

In recent years, advances in nanomedicine and nanobiotechnology have created new opportunities to enhance the efficacy of phototherapy in the treatment of HNCs.20 The potential usefulness of nanoparticles (NPs) in the field of oncology has been extensively investigated owing to their favorable biocompatibility, controllable drug release, and high targeting specificity.21 Notably, nanocarriers enable both passive and active tumor targeting, while facilitating spatiotemporally controlled drug release to modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME). These effects minimize off-target cytotoxicity and improve overall therapeutic outcomes.22 For example, nanocarriers can deliver photosensitizers or photothermal agents selectively to tumor tissues. This process allows for precise regulation of ROS generation, thereby achieving tumor-specific therapeutic effects.23,24 Furthermore, rationally engineered nanoplatforms responsive to the first and second near-infrared (NIR) windows (ie, NIR-I, 650–900 nm and NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm, respectively) have been developed to improve light penetration and therapeutic performance in deep-seated head and neck tumors25 In parallel, oxygen-generating NPs have been designed to alleviate tumor hypoxia by directly releasing oxygen within the TME, thereby enhancing the efficacy of ROS-dependent PDT.26 Collectively, these advances underscore the potential of NP-based strategies to improve the specificity, efficacy, and safety of phototherapy for HNCs.

In this review, we provide a concise overview of the fundamental principles and mechanisms of PDT and PTT, followed by a summary of recent advances in nanomedicine-enabled phototherapy for HNCs. In particular, we highlight the development of second- and third-generation phototherapeutic agents with improved photophysical properties, which have substantially enhanced the specificity and therapeutic efficacy of phototherapy. Furthermore, we discuss the emerging applications of PDT/PTT in combination with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and gene therapy in the treatment of HNCs. We particularly emphasize the profound effects of nanotechnology integration on drug delivery paradigms by enabling enhanced permeability and retention-mediated (EPR-mediated) tumor accumulation, active targeting, and stimuli-responsive activation. We also summarize the current clinical landscape, including agents approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and ongoing clinical trials. Finally, we critically address the key challenges that hinder the broader clinical translation of nanomedicine-based targeted phototherapy in HNCs, as well as outline future research directions. By balancing recent advances with existing translational barriers, the aim of this review is to delineate the realistic opportunities and unresolved challenges in advancing NP-based targeted phototherapy from a promising experimental strategy to a clinically established treatment modality for HNCs.

PDT for HNC

Phototherapy involves the irradiation of photosensitizers to exert its therapeutic effects. Upon absorbing photons, the photosensitizer undergoes one or more energy transitions, moving from its ground state (S0) to an excited singlet state (Sn), and ultimately returns to the S0 via a combination of radiative and nonradiative deactivation pathways. Radiative transitions involve energy dissipation through photon emission, which includes both fluorescence and phosphorescence. Nonradiative transitions encompass processes such as vibrational relaxation, internal conversion, and intersystem crossing.27,28 These mechanisms are fundamental to both PDT and PTT (Figure 1).

Schematic illustration of phototherapy modalities and their mechanisms of action.

PDT is a minimally invasive treatment that relies on a photosensitizer activated by light of a specific wavelength to convert molecular oxygen in the TME into cytotoxic ROS. Through this process, it induces tumor cell apoptosis, damages the vasculature, and activates the host immune system.29 Upon light absorption, electrons in the Sn undergo internal conversion and intersystem crossing, transitioning to a more stable and long-lived triplet state (T1). Subsequently, these electrons can return to the S0 through photon emission (fluorescence/phosphorescence) or vibrational relaxation. This process leads to the generation of ROS through two distinct mechanisms. In the Type I process, T1 forms free radicals through electron transfer, reacting with intracellular substrates such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids. These radicals interact with water or oxygen to generate additional ROS, including superoxide anion (O2•−), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).28 In the Type II process, T1 directly transfers energy to nearby triplet oxygen (³O2), resulting in the formation of cytotoxic singlet oxygen (¹O2), a highly reactive and cytotoxic ROS.30 In recent years, the Type III process has been identified as a potential mechanism of PDT. Unlike the Type I and II processes, the Type III reaction involves a pathway that does not depend on oxygen or ROS intermediates.

Specifically, after absorbing photons, the photosensitizer selectively binds to RNA, efficiently transferring its excitation energy directly to the RNA, leading to its cleavage.31 Notably, this process occurs independently of oxygen, which allows the Type III photosensitizer to circumvent the limitations of traditional PDT in hypoxic tumor regions. While these mechanisms may occur simultaneously, it is widely accepted that most photosensitizers generate ROS predominantly through the Type II process.32 Hence, this process remains the main mechanism driving the antitumor efficacy of PDT.

Studies have shown that three major types of cell death are involved in PDT, namely apoptosis (the most common type), necrosis, and autophagy-associated cell death.33 Notably, mitochondria play a central role in the regulation of apoptosis. PDT can disrupt mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, leading to the release of caspase activators such as cytochrome C and apoptosis-inducing factor.34 In addition, PDT can induce photodamage to the antiapoptotic protein B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), thereby promoting the apoptotic process. Other apoptotic pathways, such as those mediated by calpain (a calcium-dependent protease), have also been reported following PDT.35 As described in the context of ROS generation mechanisms, the efficacy of PDT depends on three essential components, namely the photosensitizer, molecular oxygen, and light. Among these, the photosensitizer plays a pivotal role, as its intrinsic physicochemical properties largely determine the therapeutic outcome.36 An ideal photosensitizer should possess strong absorption in the therapeutic window (typically 650–800 nm), high efficiency in generating ROS, and excellent biocompatibility. To date, numerous photosensitizers and nanoplatforms have been extensively investigated for PDT in the treatment of HNC (Figure 2).

Photosensitizer-Based PDT for HNC

To improve the therapeutic efficacy of PDT, substantial efforts have been devoted to the optimization and evolution of photosensitizers.37 Thus far, photosensitizers have progressed through three generations with distinct physicochemical and biological characteristics (Figure 3). The first-generation photosensitizers, developed from the 1970s to early 1980s, were primarily porphyrin-based compounds that typically required activation by ultraviolet–visible light, which lies outside the first biological transparency window (650–950 nm), thereby resulting in limited tissue penetration. Hematoporphyrin derivative is a representative first-generation photosensitizer;38 however, its clinical application is constrained by poor tissue penetration of excitation light and prolonged systemic retention. Second-generation photosensitizers, developed from the late 1980s, include structurally optimized porphyrin derivatives and other macrocyclic compounds, such as 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and chlorin-based agents.39–42 These photosensitizers exhibit improved activation in the NIR region and enhanced ¹O2 generation capacity, although their tumor selectivity remains suboptimal.43–45 The NIR spectrum is generally divided into the first (NIR-I, 700–900 nm) and second (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) biological windows. Compared with visible light, NIR light offers greater tissue penetration depth, reduced scattering and autofluorescence, and lower phototoxicity to normal tissues.46 Therefore, it is widely regarded as an optimal “biological optical window”.

Schematic illustration of the evolution of phototherapeutic nanoplatforms.

Recent investigations have focused on the development of phototherapeutic agents with maximal absorption in the NIR region to enhance treatment efficacy in patients with HNCs.47 In particular, NIR-II-responsive agents have demonstrated considerable potential for the ablation of deep-seated tumors, such as those located in the pharyngeal region.48 In parallel, nanomaterial-based phototherapeutic strategies have garnered increasing attention, as bioconjugation approaches (eg, peptide functionalization and cell membrane coating) improve biocompatibility, tumor specificity, and precise delivery of conventional photosensitizers49,50 Collectively, these effects enhance the overall therapeutic performance. Porphyrins

Among the numerous photosensitizers, porphyrins and their derivatives possess unique photophysical properties owing to their intrinsic conjugated π-electron systems.67,68 In PDT for cancer, porphyrins have demonstrated remarkable therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, they constitute a substantial proportion of clinically used PDT agents.69 Notably, porphyrin-based photosensitizers encompass several subclasses, including hematoporphyrins,54 metalloporphyrins,70 pheophorbide a,60 and phthalocyanines.59 Among them, hematoporphyrin derivatives Photofrin® and Photosan have been approved by the FDA for clinical use.51,55,56 As a first-generation photosensitizer, Photosan has been utilized in the treatment of oral SCC (OSCC). Chiang et al employed a 640 nm light-emitting diode light source to perform localized Photosan-PDT in a 7,12-dimethylbenz(a)anthracene-induced hamster cheek pouch model with premalignant lesions The results demonstrated that Photosan-PDT effectively treated these lesions without inducing cumulative phototoxicity or scar formation in the buccal mucosa, even after multiple treatment sessions.52 Nevertheless, the clinical application of Photosan remains limited due to cutaneous photosensitivity and insufficient tissue penetration depth. Moreover, due to the large planar structure of porphyrin derivatives, they tend to undergo strong π–π stacking interactions, which reduce their ability to generate ROS.68

To further enhance the tumor-targeting capability of photosensitizers, extensive efforts have been directed toward the chemical modification of porphyrin derivatives and the development of multifunctional, multicomponent systems. For example, Liu et al designed the water-soluble porphyrin derivative Ac-DEVDD-TPP, which can be specifically cleaved by caspase-3 (Casp3) to release an amphiphilic molecule (D-TPP), thereby initiating a Casp3-dependent self-amplifying cascade (Figure 4A) Mechanistically, following cellular uptake, the initially generated ROS are insufficient to induce substantial cytotoxicity; however, they serve as a critical trigger for activating the Casp3-dependent cascade. Upon 660 nm laser irradiation, enzyme-mediated nanofiber assembly promotes the generation of 1O2. This effect amplifies mitochondrial ROS production and induces Casp3 activation and apoptosis. Subsequently, activated Casp3 further catalyzes the formation of additional porphyrin-based nanofibers. This process reinforces ROS generation and apoptotic signaling in a positive feedback loop, ultimately achieving self-amplified PDT. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that Ac-DEVDD-TPP markedly enhances ROS production and tumor cell apoptosis, highlighting its considerable potential for the treatment of OSCC.53 In another study, Zhang et al synthesized a nanoscale photosensitizer coloaded with salvianolic acid B (SalB) and ALA, termed SalB-ALA (Figure 4B) Under NIR irradiation, this nanoplatform generated high levels of 1O2, significantly inhibited the viability of Cal27 cells, and suppressed tumor growth in nude mouse models, exhibiting pronounced antitumor efficacy.61 In summary, porphyrins and their derivatives hold great promise for future clinical applications in PDT. However, further systematic and rigorous evaluation of their biosafety profiles and in vivo antitumor efficacy is required to achieve successful clinical translation.

Chlorin e6 (Ce6)

Ce6 is characterized by strong absorption in the NIR region, enabling deeper penetration into tumor tissues; this property makes it a promising second-generation photosensitizer.71 However, the clinical application of Ce6 remains limited due to its intrinsic hydrophobicity, lack of tumor-targeting specificity, and nonspecific phototoxicity.72

In recent years, NPs have been widely employed as drug delivery carriers for photosensitizers to enhance their stability and prevent premature degradation.73–75 Concurrently, oxygen-generating nanoplatforms have been engineered to alleviate tumor hypoxia by directly releasing oxygen within the TME.76 Ferroptosis is a form of iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven regulated cell death, primarily mediated by Fenton chemistry and iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation processes involving lipoxygenases. Morphologically, ferroptosis is characterized by necrosis-like features, including mitochondrial shrinkage, loss or disappearance of mitochondrial cristae, and eventual plasma membrane rupture.77,78 Notably, excessive ROS generated during ferroptosis can participate in Fenton reactions in the presence of high concentrations of iron ions.79,80 This process facilitates additional ROS amplification and partially mitigates hypoxia within the TME, potentially enhancing further the efficacy of PDT. Xu et al combined the ferroptosis inducer sorafenib (Sor) with the photosensitizer Ce6 to construct self-assembled codelivery Sor–Ce6 nanoparticles (Sor–Ce6 NPs) for the PDT of oral cancer (Figure 4C). The results demonstrated that Sor–Ce6 NPs could continuously supply oxygen via the ferroptosis pathway, thereby markedly alleviating tumor hypoxia, enhancing ROS generation, and significantly downregulating the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Moreover, Sor–Ce6 NPs exhibited favorable in vivo tumor-targeting capability, and both in vitro and in vivo experiments showed that their antitumor efficacy was significantly higher than that of the physical mixture of Sor and Ce6.58 In another study, Tao et al developed a dandelion-like nanosystem (RC-GMN) by co-encapsulating resveratrol and Ce6 into GE11-modified micelles (with GE11 serving as an epidermal growth factor receptor-targeting [EGFR-targeting] ligand) These micelles were subsequently anchored onto a hyaluronic acid-based nanogel (Figure 5A). Experimental results indicated that RC-GMN alleviated hypoxic stress by reducing intracellular mitochondrial oxygen consumption, while GE11 enhanced tumor-targeting specificity and triggered autophagic cell death, thereby significantly improving the efficacy of PDT against OSCC.57 Collectively, these findings highlight the considerable therapeutic potential of nanocarrier-mediated Ce6 delivery systems to improve the clinical outcomes of PDT for OSCC.

Verteporfin

Verteporfin is a benzoporphyrin derivative that was initially approved by the FDA for PDT in the treatment of age-related macular degeneration.81 Subsequent studies revealed that verteporfin can be repurposed for cancer therapy by inducing mitochondrial photochemical damage through the accumulation of ROS.82 In recent years, preclinical investigations have demonstrated the broad antitumor potential of verteporfin across various malignancies, including uveal melanoma,83 breast cancer,84 glioblastoma,85 pancreatic cancer,86 gastric cancer,87 and HNC.64 Its potential usefulness as a photosensitizer for PDT in HNC is currently under investigation, with promising results reported thus far. Yu et al developed a biomimetic nanoscale photosensitizer by loading verteporfin into zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 NPs, followed by coating with M1 macrophage membranes63 This design prolonged systemic circulation time and enhanced tumor-targeting specificity. Under laser irradiation, the system significantly inhibited tumor growth via the photodynamic effect. In another study, Yu et al encapsulated verteporfin into poly (lactic–co–glycolic acid) (PLGA) NPs and further modified them with arginine–glycine–aspartate peptides, forming RPV NPs for PDT of tongue SCC (TSCC) (Figure 5B) Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that RPV NPs exhibited low cytotoxicity, selective accumulation at tumor sites, and significant inhibition of tumor cell proliferation.62 Therefore, verteporfin-mediated PDT represents a promising therapeutic approach for HNC. Future efforts should focus on optimizing verteporfin-based nanoplatform design and further evaluating their translational potential in clinical settings.

Rose Bengal (RB)

RB, a member of the fluorescein dye family, is a well-characterized anionic photosensitizer with a 1O2 quantum yield approaching 76% under 532 nm light irradiation.88 This photosensitizer has been employed in PDT for the treatment of HNSCC and melanoma, as well as in antimicrobial applications.89–91 In recent years, researchers have utilized NPs as carriers for RB to increase its uptake by cancer cells.65,92 Wang et al developed a novel RB–gold nanorod (RB-GNR) nanoplatform, which demonstrated efficient 1O2 generation under 532 nm green light illumination (Figure 5C) The multifunctional RB-GNR system also enhanced RB internalization by cancer cells, resulting in improved photodynamic efficacy. In vivo experiments further confirmed that RB-GNRs preferentially accumulated in tumor tissues and exhibited excellent cellular biocompatibility.66 However, the majority of studies conducted thus far are limited to in vitro and animal model investigations. Hence, further clinical trials are necessary to fully evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of RB-based NP-targeted phototherapy in humans.

Nanomaterial-Based PDT for HNC

Nanotechnology-based PDT enables the efficient and targeted delivery of photosensitizers to tumor tissues.93 In recent years, research efforts have increasingly focused on the development and optimization of third-generation photosensitizers. The aim of these efforts is to increase the targeting specificity and therapeutic efficacy of PDT through molecular structure modification and functionalization strategies.94 For this purpose, an increasing number of researchers have employed nanocarriers to encapsulate and deliver photosensitizers.95 Moreover, NPs often provide additional reactive sites for the conjugation of functional groups, facilitating targeted delivery while preventing photosensitizer deactivation by blood components; these effects improve overall therapeutic performance.96–98 Various nanocarrier systems, including liposomes, chitosan-based materials, carbon-based NPs, and polymeric NPs such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), PLGA, and PLA, have been utilized to achieve precise and efficient photosensitizer delivery. Liposomes

Owing to their excellent biocompatibility, appropriate size, and unique amphiphilic nature, liposomes have been widely employed to encapsulate various types of photosensitizers for PDT in cancer treatment.100,101,110,111 Chen et al developed red blood cell (RBC) membrane-coated TPZ/TPP@RBC-FA liposome NPs loaded with 5,10,15,20-tetraphenyl-21H,23H-porphyrin (TPP-4COOH) and tirapazamine (TPZ) The results revealed that the TPZ/TPP@RBC-FA liposome NPs were selectively taken up by SCC-7 cells. Upon irradiation with 650 nm laser light, molecular oxygen was converted to 1O2, inducing apoptosis and disrupting the RBC membrane, thereby triggering the release of TPZ. Simultaneously, PDT exacerbated intratumoral hypoxia, which in turn activated TPZ to generate cytotoxic free radicals, further enhancing the tumoricidal effect (Figure 6A). Furthermore, this combination therapy induced immunogenic cell death (ICD), which activates immune cells and elicits an antitumor immune response, indicating promising potential for clinical translation.99

Chitosan

Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide derived from chitin, has garnered significant attention because of its excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and permeability.112 In recent years, chitosan-based NPs have been increasingly incorporated into targeted PDT for cancer treatment.113 Zhu et al combined fluorinated chitosan–Ce6 (FC-Ce6) with catalase (CAT) to develop self-assembled nanoparticles (CAT-FC-Ce6 NPs) (Figure 6B). The results demonstrated that the CAT-FC-Ce6 NPs significantly enhanced the transmembrane permeability. Under 650 nm laser irradiation, the NPs effectively delivered CAT into Cal-27 cells, thereby increasing O2 generation and inducing apoptosis.102 Owing to their favorable passive tumor-targeting ability and low dark toxicity, these NPs exhibit considerable potential for applications in PDT.

Carbon-Based Materials

Carbon-based materials such as graphene quantum dots, graphene oxide, and carbon dots (CDs) have been widely utilized in PDT research because of their chemical inertness, excellent photostability, and favorable biocompatibility.104,107,114 Among them, CDs have emerged as a distinctive class of PDT agents characterized by intrinsic photoluminescence and superior biocompatibility.105 Kong et al synthesized CDs via a hydrothermal method, followed by purification through column chromatography to obtain uniform NPs The results revealed that the particle size of the CDs was predominantly in the range of 5–10 nm, with an average zeta potential of 22.98 ± 0.85 Mv. In vivo studies demonstrated that the CDs exhibited good biocompatibility in CAL-27 and UM1 cell lines, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations of 159.1 μg mL−1 and 99.9 μg mL−1, respectively. Upon light irradiation, the CDs generated substantial amounts of ROS, inducing apoptosis. CD-mediated cell death may involve lysosome-associated pathways.103 However, research on the intrinsic photodynamic and anticancer properties of CDs remains relatively limited. Therefore, further exploration of CDs as photosensitizers for PDT in the treatment of HNSCC is warranted.

PEG/PLGA/PLA NPs

PEG is widely employed in drug delivery systems because of its ability to coat the surface of NPs, thereby preventing aggregation and phagocytic uptake. This results in prolonged systemic circulation, reduced immunogenicity, and preservation of NP bioactivity. Importantly, PEG does not interfere with the therapeutic efficacy of the encapsulated agents.109 Moreover, PEG can be conjugated with PLGA to form amphiphilic block copolymer NPs (PEG/PLGA NPs), which help modulate the physicochemical properties of PLGA-based carriers and enhance their tumor-targeting capabilities.108 These features render PEG/PLGA NPs increasingly promising in PDT for cancer treatment. Zhou et al designed folic acid-conjugated PLGA NPs (F/CDPR-NPs) coloaded with cisplatin (DDP) and a pheophorbide for the treatment of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) (Figure 6C) Their findings demonstrated that F/CDPR-NPs extended the drug circulation time and enabled controlled release. Additionally, F/CDPR-NPs exerted pronounced cytotoxic effects on the CNE-1 and HNE-1 NPC cell lines. Under laser irradiation, these NPs significantly reduced the tumor burden, indicating enhanced therapeutic efficacy.106 Overall, the combination of targeted NP delivery systems with PDT offers a promising therapeutic strategy for NPC and potentially other malignancies.

PTT for HNC

PTT is a minimally invasive treatment modality in which photothermal agents absorb light at specific wavelengths—typically in the NIR region—and convert it into heat to effectively suppress or eliminate cancer cells.115 In phototherapy, internal conversion is the primary mechanism for photothermal conversion. During this process, electrons in the excited state relax to the ground state in a nonradiative manner, causing the chromophore to collide with its surrounding environment. A portion of the energy is released as heat, which subsequently diffuses into the surrounding medium, leading to a sharp increase in the local temperature of the tumor.116 Therefore, the goal of PTT is to elevate the temperature in the tumor region, resulting in localized “burning” damage to the malignant tissue without causing immediate irreversible harm to the surrounding healthy tissues.

Moreover, the effects of PTT on tumors are markedly dependent on temperature. Light-induced thermal damage significantly disrupts the integrity of the cell membrane, triggering a substantial influx of calcium ions (Ca²+), which further causes chemical damage. Although at moderate to low temperatures (41–43°C) the heat is insufficient to directly destroy tumor cells, it can lead to protein aggregation and denaturation. Additionally, at this temperature range, the permeability of blood vessels in the tumor area is temporarily increased, thus facilitating improved delivery of drugs and oxygen. Furthermore, heat shock proteins (HSPs) may be activated to protect the cells from thermal stress.117 At slightly higher temperatures (43–45°C), PTT exerts notable effects on tumor cells, such as the reorganization of the cytoskeleton, modulation of gene expression, induction of local oxidative stress, and generation of ROS, resulting in temporary cell inactivation. At temperatures ranging 45–49°C, tumor cells are subjected to substantial thermal stress. This stress compromises the integrity of the cell membrane, leading to leakage of intracellular contents and the initiation of programmed cell death, including heat-mediated apoptosis and necrosis. At temperatures exceeding 49°C, immediate tumor cell necrosis is induced. However, such high temperatures may also cause damage to surrounding healthy tissues (eg, vascular injury and inflammation) and lead to excessive activation of HSPs, thereby potentially suppressing the anticancer activity of the immune system.118–120

Given the principles of cancer PTT, ideal PTT agents should exhibit the following properties: high photothermal conversion efficiency; strong light absorption capacity; low biological toxicity; and excellent biocompatibility.121 Owing to their unique transport properties, biocompatibility, and light-responsive characteristics, various inorganic and organic NPs have been developed as PTT agents or enhancers for HNC.122 Surface modifications can be applied to these NPs to more effectively address the complexities of the biological environment. Modifying their surface potential can prolong the blood circulation time, reduce systemic toxicity, and improve dispersion ability (Figure 7). Moreover, functionalization with tumor-targeting ligands can optimize their tumor-targeting capabilities, thus enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing excessive damage to normal tissues.123

AuNPs

AuNPs have emerged as excellent candidates for PTT because of their favorable biocompatibility, ease of synthesis, and versatile optical properties, including localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), surface-enhanced Raman scattering, and photothermal conversion capabilities.136–138 Among these, AuNPs exhibit superior LSPR performance in the NIR region, making them suitable for deep-tissue tumor ablation. Their potential is further enhanced by the EPR effect, which facilitates their passive accumulation at tumor sites.127,128,139 Conjugation with specific ligands, such as antibodies, can significantly improve the tumor-targeting specificity of AuNPs and reduce their nonspecific distribution in cancer therapy. Hao et al developed Au-HN-1 by modifying gold nanodots with the TSCC-targeting peptide HN-1 (Figure 8A–8E) The results demonstrated that the Au-HN-1 nanosystem effectively targeted TSCC cells, and the selective accumulation of the photosensitizer at tumor sites significantly enhanced the specificity and therapeutic efficacy of PTT. Owing to its stable long-term fluorescence and high X-ray attenuation coefficient, the Au-HN-1 nanosystem could also be utilized for both fluorescence imaging and computed tomography imaging of TSCC.126 Overall, the Au-HN-1 nanosystem represents a promising tool for integrated imaging diagnostics and targeted PTT in TSCC. Despite their considerable therapeutic potential, AuNPs (as inorganic nanomaterials) are inherently nonbiodegradable, raising concerns regarding their long-term biological fate.140 Previous studies have shown that AuNPs with diameters smaller than ~6 nm can be rapidly cleared via renal excretion, whereas larger particles are predominantly sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system.141 This size-dependent biodistribution critically influences their in vivo fate. As a result, AuNPs tend to accumulate in organs such as the liver and spleen, thereby raising concerns regarding potential chronic toxicity.142 Therefore, efficient renal clearance is critical for minimizing long-term toxicity, and future strategies should prioritize the rational design of ultrasmall or biodegradable gold nanoclusters that enable efficient systemic excretion, thereby enhancing overall biosafety.

Carbon-Based Materials

Unlike those of AuNPs, the photothermal effects of carbon-based materials rely primarily on the following synergistic mechanisms: light-induced intermolecular energy transfer; vibrational mode coupling of carbon–carbon bonds within the three-dimensional network framework; and heat release driven by lattice relaxation.143–145 Carbon materials—including carbon nanotubes, graphene, fullerenes, and CDs—have been widely studied in cancer PTT because of their facile functionalization, excellent biocompatibility, and high photothermal conversion efficiency. Among them, nanographene oxide (NGO) has been the most extensively investigated.132–135 Li et al developed the lipid-modulating nanoplatform NGO-SB-204990-DOX (NSD) for the treatment of OSCC This nanoplatform codelivers SB-204990, an inhibitor of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) citrate lyase (ACLY), and doxorubicin. ACLY is highly expressed in various types of cancer, and its inhibition can interfere with lipid metabolism in cancer cells. The results demonstrated that NSD is characterized by a high drug loading capacity, pH- and photothermal-responsiveness, and efficient cellular internalization. In both in vitro and in vivo studies, NSD combined with laser irradiation achieved a tumor inhibition rate as high as 99.4%, indicating that ACLY represents a promising anticancer target and that the NSD nanoplatform is a highly promising drug delivery system.131 In conclusion, carbon-based materials show great promise in the treatment of HNC, offering innovative and effective strategies for targeted PTT. Despite the substantial progress achieved with carbon-based nanomaterials in phototherapy for HNCs, several critical challenges remain to be addressed, particularly regarding their long-term biocompatibility and potential toxicity.146 Addressing these concerns is essential to ensure the safe and effective application of carbon-based materials in clinical practice.

Semiconductor–Metal HNPs

Semiconductor–metal HNPs represent a class of photocatalysts with significant application potential.147 Upon light excitation in oxygenated and aqueous environments, HNPs can generate ROS. In addition, the plasmonic response of either the semiconductor or the metal component leads to localized heating, endowing these hybrid nanostructures with great promise in PTT.148,149 Zhang et al synthesized 131I-labeled bovine serum albumin-modified (BSA-modified) copper sulfide (CuS) NPs (131I–BSA@CuS) Their results showed that 131I–BSA@CuS had a significant therapeutic effect on ARO cells, with a photothermal conversion efficiency of 28.07%. In addition, the NPs exhibited prolonged accumulation at the tumor site.129 In another study, Liu et al synthesized PEG-modified ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide NP conjugated with sialyl Lewis X (USPIO-PEG-sLex) nanostructures using a high-temperature pyrolysis method (Figure 8F–8J) The findings revealed that USPIO-PEG-sLex exhibited excellent dispersibility, robust photothermal conversion performance, high stability, and higher than T2 relaxivity, while specifically targeting E-selectin expression on endothelial cells of tumor neovasculature. Moreover, USPIO-PEG-sLex, serving as a multifunctional nanoplatform, enabled both magnetic resonance imaging and PTT in NPC xenograft models in nude mice, effectively suppressing tumor progression.130 Thus, USPIO-PEG-sLex shows great promise as a novel therapeutic strategy for NPC. However, the application of semiconductor–metal HNPs in HNC PTT remains rare. There is a compelling need for further investigation into HNPs with active targeting capabilities and controlled drug release to unlock their full potential in precise and efficient cancer therapy.

Organic NP-Based PTT

Compared with traditional inorganic NPs, organic NPs exhibit comparable photothermal conversion efficiency while offering the additional advantages of lower toxicity and biodegradability.150 Furthermore, under laser irradiation, organic-based materials can display both photothermal and photoacoustic effects.151 In recent years, organic small molecules have attracted increasing attention as potential alternatives to nanomaterials in the field of PTT. Researchers have aimed to develop photothermal agents that combine high photothermal conversion efficiency with excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability.152 Among the families of organic molecules studied as PTT agents within NPs, two major categories have been identified, namely small molecules and semiconducting polymer NPs.153,154 In this section, we summarized the strategies employed in recent studies and discuss the application prospects of organic NPs in PTT for the treatment of HNC. Indocyanine Green (ICG)

ICG, an FDA-approved NIR fluorescent dye, has been widely applied in phototherapy because of its strong absorption at approximately 800 nm and its combined photothermal effect.161 However, the clinical application of ICG in PTT is limited by its poor photostability, short half-life, and lack of tumor-specific targeting capability. To overcome these limitations, various NP-based ICG formulations have been designed to enhance the stability of ICG, prolong its systemic circulation, and increase its accumulation at tumor sites.156,157 Furthermore, conventional ICG typically exhibits a moderate photothermal conversion efficiency of approximately 47%; however, recent advances in molecular engineering, such as dimerization strategies, have increased this value to as high as ~95% under optimized conditions.162 In comparison, it has been reported that plasmonically coupled AuNP clusters achieve photothermal conversion efficiencies of approximately 84%.163 Collectively, these findings underscore the substantial potential of ICG-based hybrid nanoplatforms for PTT in HNCs. Wang et al developed HNPs (HSA-ICG-DDP NPs) by encapsulating an ICG–DDP coordination complex within human serum albumin (HSA) This system exploits the high expression of the secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich protein in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to mediate endocytosis, thereby improving tumor-targeting efficiency. Upon irradiation with an 808 nm laser, the ICG-mediated photothermal effect induces cleavage of the coordination bond, resulting in the release of DDP and simultaneous generation of ROS, which synergistically increase tumor cell death (Figure 9A). In vivo experiments demonstrated that this nanoplatform significantly inhibited tumor growth, outperforming monotherapy approaches.155 Therefore, HSA-ICG-DDP NPs offer a safe and effective strategy for combinatorial PTT, presenting a promising direction for improved cancer treatment.

Polypyrrole

Polypyrrole has garnered significant attention in tumor PTT because of its outstanding photostability and photothermal conversion efficiency. However, its clinical application is hindered by poor colloidal stability due to its hydrophobic nature, complex synthetic pathways, and potential toxicity.164 To address these limitations, hydrophilic polymer coatings are often employed to enhance dispersion and biocompatibility. Li et al designed a novel polymer—the polymer acceptor acrylate-substituted thiadiazoloquinoxaline-diketopyrrolopyrrole polymer (PATQ–DPP)—for the treatment of NPC The results showed that the PATQ–DPP NPs exhibited absorption in the NIR-II window and demonstrated excellent photothermal properties. Although these NPs displayed pronounced tumor-targeting accumulation in NPC cells, they showed low toxicity to normal tissues. In vivo studies revealed that under NIR laser irradiation, PATQ–DPP NPs effectively accumulated at the tumor site and exhibited potent photothermal effects, significantly inhibiting tumor growth (Figure 9B). Notably, complete tumor elimination was achieved within 18 days post PTT.158 Therefore, PATQ–DPP NPs represent a promising therapeutic approach for NPC. However, their clinical efficacy and safety require further investigation in future studies.

Polydopamine (PDA)

PDA exhibits excellent photothermal conversion efficiency, biocompatibility, and drug-loading capacity.165 Its surface is enriched with active functional groups, such as –OH and –NH2, which facilitate the conjugation of therapeutic molecules, making PDA broadly applicable in the biomedical field.166,167 Chen et al developed Fe3O4@PDA–HCPT NPs that combine the chemotherapeutic agent 10-hydroxycamptothecin (HCPT) with a ferroptosis-inducing mechanism for the treatment of esophageal cancer HCPT functions by inhibiting DNA replication and related processes, thereby inducing apoptosis. The results showed that the Fe3O4@PDA–HCPT NPs possessed excellent photothermal conversion properties, catalytic activity for the Fenton reaction, and controlled drug release capabilities. Under NIR laser irradiation and within the acidic TME, PDA generates heat to ablate tumor cells while simultaneously triggering the release of HCPT and Fe2+/Fe3+ ions to induce apoptosis and ferroptosis. Moreover, Fe3O4@PDA–HCPT NPs allow for magnetic resonance imaging-guided synergistic therapy, effectively suppressing the proliferation of EC1 and EC109 esophageal cancer cells.159 Owing to its favorable biocompatibility, this nanoplatform represents a promising approach for the treatment of esophageal cancer.

Synergistic and Antagonistic Interplay Between ROS Generation and Photothermal Effects

The interplay between ROS generation and thermal effects represents a central yet often underappreciated determinant of the therapeutic efficacy of PDT and PTT.168 In principle, moderate photothermal heating can enhance PDT efficacy by improving tumor perfusion, facilitating oxygen delivery, and increasing cell membrane permeability, thereby promoting photosensitizer uptake and ROS production. Conversely, ROS-mediated oxidative stress can sensitize tumor cells to hyperthermia by impairing mitochondrial function and disrupting cellular stress response pathways, establishing a positive feedback loop between oxidative and thermal damage.169–171 However, this interaction is highly context-dependent rather than universally synergistic. Excessive thermal damage may disrupt the tumor microvasculature and exacerbate local hypoxia, thereby limiting the oxygen levels required for type II photodynamic reactions and ultimately suppressing ROS generation. In addition, severe heat stress can induce protective heat shock responses, partially counteracting ROS-mediated cytotoxicity.172,173 At the material level, photothermal conversion and ROS generation often compete for the same absorbed photon energy; nanomaterials optimized for efficient nonradiative heat dissipation may exhibit reduced triplet-state yield and, consequently, diminished photodynamic efficiency.174,175

Therefore, the relationship between PDT and PTT is not a universally synergistic paradigm. Instead, it should be regarded as a context-dependent, biphasic continuum governed by temperature thresholds, irradiation sequence, oxygen availability, and the photophysical properties of nanoplatforms. These considerations are particularly critical in HNSCC, where stromal fibrosis, heterogeneous perfusion, and hypoxia frequently constrain oxygen-dependent photochemical reactions. Accordingly, future studies should focus on precisely calibrating photothermal intensity and treatment timing to maintain a therapeutic window in which hyperthermia enhances oxygen delivery and photosensitizer activity without inducing vascular collapse or oxygen depletion.

Therefore, combining phototherapy with other therapeutic strategies offers a more effective approach for the treatment of HNC.178,179 In the following section, we highlight recent studies that demonstrated the synergistic effects of NP-based phototherapy when used in combination with other treatments. Combination with Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is a commonly employed modality in the treatment of HNC; however, its efficacy is often limited by the nonspecific toxicity of chemotherapeutic agents due to their lack of tumor specificity.181 Moreover, the complex TME hinders drug penetration and retention within the tumor tissue.183 In addition, tumor recurrence is frequently observed as a result of multidrug resistance, making monotherapy with chemotherapy insufficient to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes.197–199 In recent years, nanotechnology-based delivery systems have provided promising options to address these limitations.200 The combination of chemotherapy with phototherapy has demonstrated significant therapeutic advancements in recent studies, offering a synergistic strategy that enhances treatment efficacy while potentially minimizing systemic toxicity.184

PDT Combined with Chemotherapy

In PDT, the generation of ROS can disrupt the covalent interactions between therapeutic agents and photosensitizers, thereby enabling the controlled release of chemotherapeutic drugs.201 Paul et al developed a NIR–light-activated, oxygen-generating nanomicellar photodynamic–chemotherapy system (mPPCPN Ms) for the treatment of oropharyngeal cancer (Figure 10A) The results demonstrated that this polymer exhibited good biocompatibility and could self-assemble into micellar vesicles, enabling passive targeting and efficient drug delivery to tumor tissues. Under 660 nm laser irradiation, mPPCPN Ms depleted intracellular glutathione, facilitating the reduction of Pt(IV) to its more cytotoxic form Pt(II). Simultaneously, the generated oxygen activated Ce6 to produce ROS, effectively reversing tumor hypoxia within the TME. Moreover, both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that mPPCPN Ms significantly improved tumor oxygenation and enhanced the efficacy of platinum-based chemotherapy, leading to pronounced inhibition of tumor growth.180 Therefore, photoresponsive nanomedicines represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of HNC.

PTT Combined with Chemotherapy

In PTT, the substantial heat generated within tumor tissues facilitates the release of chemotherapeutic agents, thereby increasing therapeutic efficacy.202,203 He et al developed a dual pH- and NIR-responsive nanodrug delivery system (HA/Au–PDA@CZT) for the treatment of HNSCC with combined chemotherapy and PTT (Figure 10B) This nanoplatform achieves tumor targeting by binding to CD44 receptors, which are overexpressed on cancer cells. Under laser irradiation, the LSPR peak of the system redshifts, enabling efficient photothermal ablation of tumors due to its high photothermal conversion efficiency. Simultaneously, the chemotherapeutic agent cabazitaxel (CZT) is released into the acidic TME, which has a lower pH than that of normal tissues, thereby minimizing off-target toxicity. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that HA/Au–PDA@CZT exerts a synergistic effect on PTT and chemotherapy, with notably low cell viability and the smallest tumor volumes observed in treated mice.182 In another study, He et al developed mussel-inspired PLGA/PDA core–shell NPs for combined PTT and chemotherapy (Figure 10C) The results demonstrated that, with the assistance of anti-EGFR antibodies, these NPs could efficiently enter head and neck tumor cells. Following entry, they converted NIR light into heat to trigger drug release from the PLGA core for chemotherapy, while simultaneously ablating tumors through hyperthermia. Notably, due to the unique concentration-dependent peak working temperature of PLGA/PD, overheating or overtreatment could be readily avoided.160 These findings indicate that PTT combined with chemotherapy holds substantial potential for clinical application in the treatment of HNSCC.

Combination with Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment strategy for HNCs and has attracted considerable attention in recent years. Notably, the FDA has approved immune checkpoint inhibitors as first-line therapy for patients with recurrent or metastatic HNSCC.204 However, despite these advances, only approximately 15–20% of patients achieve durable clinical responses to treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors, highlighting an unmet need for strategies to improve therapeutic efficacy.205 Recent studies have demonstrated that phototherapy can elicit systemic antitumor immunity by inducing ICD and activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Figure 11). Moreover, tumor cells inherently express multiple highly immunogenic antigenic epitopes, providing sufficient antigenicity to effectively initiate immune responses.185,186 Under stress conditions, tumor cells release a spectrum of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and proinflammatory cytokines, including extracellular ATP, calreticulin (CRT), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and HSPs (HSP70/90), among others.206,207 The release of these signals promotes the recruitment and maturation of antigen-presenting cells, which subsequently migrate to lymphoid organs to present tumor-derived antigens to T cells. This process initiates tumor-specific cytotoxic responses and activates adaptive antitumor immunity. In addition, tumor cell debris generated during phototherapy-induced cell death can serve as an in situ autologous vaccine, further amplifying systemic immune responses.208–210 Concurrently, phototherapy reshapes the TME into a highly immunogenic state, characterized by increased programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and enhanced infiltration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. It also promotes the reprogramming of M2 tumor-associated macrophages toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype, thereby converting an immunosuppressive “cold” TME into an immunologically active “hot” TME (Figure 12A). These coordinated immunomodulatory effects sensitize tumors to immune checkpoint blockade and other immunotherapeutic strategies.211–213 Accordingly, the combination of phototherapy and immunotherapy holds substantial promise for the treatment of HNSCC.

In this section, we summarized recent advances in photoimmunotherapy (PIT) for HNSCC, highlighting studies in which phototherapeutic agents have been combined with immune adjuvants or immune checkpoint inhibitors to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects.

PDT Combined with Immunotherapy

Although PDT can enhance tumor antigen presentation, the antitumor immune response it induces is often limited.214 Moreover, PDT inevitably causes damage to surrounding healthy cells, leading to the release of self-antigens and subsequent immunosuppressive effects.215,216 Immunotherapy presents multiple ways to counteract the immunosuppression associated with standalone PDT. Yu et al developed phototherapeutic NPs (TSD NPs) with aggregation-induced emission characteristics and a NIR-II fluorescence peak centered at 1000 nm for PIT of OSCC Under NIR laser irradiation, these NPs demonstrated a strong oxygen-independent type I ROS generation capability, along with a high photothermal conversion efficiency of 45.3%. TSD NPs induced ICD and promoted T-cell infiltration, thus stimulating a potent antitumor immune response. Additionally, TSD NPs upregulated the expression of PD-L1 on OSCC cells, thereby enhancing the efficacy of combination therapy with αPD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade. Notably, the synergistic application of TSD NPs and αPD-L1 therapy achieved complete tumor eradication in vivo without causing damage to healthy tissues (Figure 12B). These findings indicate that TSD NPs represent a highly promising strategy for the treatment of OSCC.187

PTT Combined with Immunotherapy

PTT can activate antitumor immune responses; however, when tumor temperatures exceed 45°C, immune activation tends to be suppressed, thereby limiting the extent of immune stimulation.189 Recent studies have shown that PTT-induced immunosuppression can be reversed through combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors.217 Therefore, investigating the combination of phototherapy with other immunotherapeutic agents (eg, immune checkpoint blockade) is essential for achieving optimal antitumor immune activation and enhancing therapeutic outcomes Ran et al prepared organic photothermal NPs (PBDB-T NPs) via a straightforward nanoprecipitation method for PIT of OSCC The results demonstrated that PBDB-T NPs effectively enabled mild-temperature PTT against OSCC and promoted the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, such as CRT and HMGB1. Both in vitro and in vivo studies revealed that rhythmically applied mild-temperature PTT-induced PIT significantly improved the tumor immune microenvironment, triggered ICD, and enhanced antitumor effects in a temperature-dependent manner (Figure 12C). Moreover, this approach improved the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade in preventing tumor recurrence.188 Thus, rhythmic mild-temperature PTT combined with immunotherapy represents a highly promising strategy for the treatment of OSCC in the future.

Combination with Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is a commonly employed treatment modality for HNC; nevertheless, its efficacy is often limited by factors such as insufficient radiation energy deposition, hypoxic TMEs, and toxicity to surrounding healthy tissues.192 By inducing localized hyperthermia, PTT can increase blood flow and promote the generation of ROS, thereby allowing for reduced X-ray doses.218–220 Consequently, the combination of radiotherapy with phototherapy holds significant promise for improving therapeutic outcomes (Figure 13A).

PDT Combined with Radiotherapy

PDT is characterized by inherent challenges, such as oxygen dependence and limited tissue penetration depth. In contrast, high-energy X-rays can penetrate deeply into tumor tissues and eradicate cancer cells located in hypoxic or deeply-seated regions.221 Akbarzadeh et al developed bismuth oxide (Bi2O3) NPs conjugated with folic acid and ALA for combined radiotherapy and PDT in the treatment of NPC Through folate receptor-mediated targeting, the NPs are selectively internalized by tumor cells, increasing their cytotoxic efficacy. In addition, Bi2O3 NPs exhibited pronounced radiosensitizing potential; moreover, the combination of PDT and radiotherapy improved therapeutic efficacy and reduced treatment-related adverse effects, indicating that this synergistic strategy may represent an effective approach for the treatment of NPC.190 Nevertheless, further clinical studies are required to validate its efficacy and safety.

PTT Combined with Radiotherapy

PTT can eradicate radiotherapy-resistant tumor cells even under hypoxic conditions, and its photothermal effects can improve tumor oxygenation, thereby increasing the sensitivity of tumor cells to radiotherapy.222 Arash et al developed alginate-coated iron oxide–gold core–shell NPs (Fe3O4@Au/AlgNPs) and investigated their radiosensitizing effect on human NPC KB cells At 24 h after the administration of NPs, the optimal synergistic anticancer effect was achieved under specific conditions, namely NPs (30 μg mL−1, 4-h incubation) combined with laser irradiation (808 nm, 1 w cm−2 for 5 min) and radiotherapy (6 Gy). At this timepoint, the majority of KB cells were arrested in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, which is the most radiosensitive phase, resulting in a cell survival rate of only 12.3%.191 These findings suggest that the combination of PTT and radiotherapy is a promising option for the treatment of HNC.

Combination with Gene Therapy

The aim of gene therapy is to deliver genetic materials (eg, DNA, RNA, and CRISPR) for the treatment of tumors223,224 However, its therapeutic efficacy is often hindered by challenges such as nucleic acid degradation and low cellular uptake. The integration of phototherapy with gene delivery has emerged as a promising approach, wherein phototherapy nanomaterials can be employed as carriers to load nucleic acids, thereby increasing their stability and targeting ability to achieve improved therapeutic outcomes.193 In recent years, the combination of nanomaterial-based phototherapy with gene therapy has been linked to notable advances in the treatment of HNC.196

PDT Combined with Gene Therapy

ROS-induced PDT-induced lipid membrane disruption—also termed photochemical internalization—can inhibit tumor growth. In addition, gene therapy targets cancer through intracellular mechanisms.225 Therefore, the combination of PDT with gene therapy can enhance antitumor efficacy. Wang et al developed a tripolyphosphate (TPP)-conjugated chitosan NP delivery system for the dual delivery of methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 1-like (MTHFD1L) short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) and ALA in the treatment of OSCC This study revealed that the chitosan-TPP-(shMTHFD1L-ALA) NPs had an average size of approximately 145 nm and that the codelivery of MTHFD1L shRNA and ALA-PDT significantly increased their cytotoxicity to OSCC cells. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that treatment with chitosan-TPP-shMTHFD1L-ALA-PDT induced mitochondrial dysfunction through downregulation of MTHFD1L expression and photodynamic effects, resulting in pronounced proapoptotic activity, ROS generation, and antitumor effects.194 Thus, chitosan-TPP-(shMTHFD1L-ALA) NPs serve as promising multifunctional platforms for the treatment of HNC. Nonetheless, further investigations are required to explore and optimize their performance and therapeutic potential.

PTT Combined with Gene Therapy

The combined application of gene therapy and PTT has been extensively studied over the years. Photothermal effects can modulate gene expression. Moreover, low-power photothermal irradiation can increase cell membrane permeability, thereby facilitating gene delivery without causing significant cellular damage.226 Xu et al developed a polypyrrole–poly(ethylenimine)–siILK fibroin nanocomposite (PPRILK) for combined gene therapy and PTT of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) Integrin-linked kinase (ILK) is overexpressed in PTC and associated with tumor progression. Small-interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting ILK (siILK) can downregulate ILK expression and inhibit tumor lymphangiogenesis and metastasis (Figure 13B). The results demonstrated that PPRILK generated significant heat under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation, effectively ablating tumors. Furthermore, the polypyrrole–poly(ethylenimine) carrier promoted the intracellular delivery of siILK molecules into PTC cells via the EPR effect and a charge-reversal mechanism, thereby assisting PTT in suppressing tumor proliferation.195 Thus, PPRILK holds substantial promise for the combination of gene therapy and PTT in the treatment of PTC.

Clinical Applications of Nanomedicine-Enabled Phototherapy in HNC

The clinical application of phototherapy represents a significant advance in the treatment of HNCs, largely driven by progress in nanotechnology and optical physics. From FDA-approved agents to promising outcomes emerging from ongoing clinical trials, the clinical application of phototherapy in head and neck oncology continues to expand.

FDA-Approved Phototherapeutic Agents

FDA-approved phototherapeutic agents underscore the transformative potential of phototherapy, particularly in oncology and related diseases. Despite encouraging results from both preclinical and clinical studies, only a limited number of agents (eg, Photofrin®, ALA, and Visudyne®) have received FDA approval This limited clinical success reflects the major regulatory and developmental challenges associated with phototherapy. Specifically, the intricate interplay between optical physics, photosensitizer pharmacokinetics, and treatment precision imposes significant barriers to the standardization and optimization of clinical protocols.

Porfimer Sodium

Porfimer sodium (Photofrin®) was the first photosensitizer approved for clinical use in cancer treatment and has been widely utilized in PDT for various malignancies, including head and neck, esophageal, lung, bladder, and cervical cancers.227–229 In head and neck oncology, its application is primarily focused on early-stage superficial lesions of HNSCC, epithelial dysplasia or carcinoma in situ of the oral cavity, oropharynx, as well as selected cases of recurrent or residual disease treated in a salvage setting.230,231 For instance, Ikeda et al reported a complete response rate of 96% (24/25 patients) in the treatment of early-stage OSCC with Photofrin®-mediated PDT.232 In addition, Photofrin®-PDT has been applied as a salvage therapy for recurrent or residual HNSCC. Studies performed by Hosokawa et al (2018, 2020) demonstrated that porfimer sodium-mediated PDT can provide clinically meaningful local control and improvement of quality of life in selected patients with recurrent or residual disease who are unsuitable to undergo further surgery or re-irradiation.233,234

Despite these clinical applications, the use of Photofrin® is limited by several well-recognized drawbacks. For example, it exhibits prolonged retention in the body for more than 2 months. This leads to persistent cutaneous and ocular photosensitivity, which significantly restricts the daily activities of patients. Furthermore, its excitation wavelength of 630 nm results in limited tissue penetration, thereby reducing its efficacy against deep-seated, bulky, or highly invasive head and neck tumors.235

ALA

ALA and its derivatives have been approved by the FDA for fluorescence-guided resection of high-grade gliomas and for PDT of superficial premalignant and malignant skin lesions.236 As a prodrug, ALA is metabolized intracellularly via the heme biosynthesis pathway into protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), which preferentially accumulates in tumor cells owing to dysregulated enzymatic activity and altered mitochondrial metabolism. Upon irradiation with red light (630 nm), PpIX generates ROS, thereby inducing apoptosis and necrosis.237

Clinically, ALA-mediated PDT (ALA-PDT) has demonstrated significant efficacy in oral potentially malignant disorders, including leukoplakia and epithelial dysplasia, as well as in early-stage HNSCC.238,239 Compared with surgery or radiotherapy, ALA-mediated PDT represents a minimally invasive and function-preserving therapeutic option. Importantly, ALA exhibits relatively rapid systemic clearance, resulting in a substantially shorter duration of photosensitivity compared with first-generation photosensitizers, thereby improving patient compliance and enabling repeated treatments.240 In addition, the intrinsic fluorescence of PpIX allows for intraoperative tumor visualization, positioning ALA as a theranostic platform that integrates both diagnostic and therapeutic functionalities.241

However, the clinical application of ALA-based PDT remains constrained by the limited tissue penetration of visible-wavelength light, thereby restricting its therapeutic efficacy primarily to superficial lesions. In addition, tumor hypoxia and heterogeneity in the EPR effect further compromise treatment uniformity and overall efficacy.242 Taken together, the available data demonstrate that ALA-mediated PDT represents a promising precision therapeutic strategy for early-stage and localized head and neck lesions. However, further optimization of light delivery, oxygen modulation, and combination treatment strategies is required to extend its applicability to advanced HNCs.

Verteporfin

Verteporfin (Visudyne®), a benzoporphyrin derivative, is an FDA-approved photosensitizer primarily indicated for the treatment of age-related macular degeneration and choroidal neovascularization associated with pathological myopia.243 In oncological applications, verteporfin is administered intravenously and activated by laser irradiation at a wavelength of 690 nm.244 Recent studies have revealed that, beyond its canonical role as a photosensitizer, verteporfin also exerts light-independent inhibitory effects on the Hippo–Yes-associated protein 1 (Hippo–YAP) signaling pathway. This pathway is critically implicated in the progression, maintenance of cancer stemness, and therapeutic resistance of HNSCC.245,246 Notably, preclinical studies in HNSCC models have demonstrated that verteporfin can suppress tumor cell proliferation, attenuate cancer stem cell phenotypes, and enhance sensitivity to cytotoxic therapies even in the absence of light activation.247 This dual functionality positions verteporfin at the intersection of PDT and targeted molecular inhibition, thereby broadening its therapeutic scope.

Despite these promising attributes, the clinical utilization of verteporfin in head and neck oncology remains limited. Following verteporfin-mediated PDT, commonly reported adverse events include injection-site reactions such as pain, edema, inflammation, bleeding, and discoloration. In addition, patients may experience visual disturbances, including blurred vision, photopsia, decreased visual acuity, and visual field defects. To minimize photosensitivity-related complications, patients are required to avoid direct exposure to sunlight and intense indoor lighting for at least 48h after treatment.248 Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of verteporfin is constrained by the limited tissue penetration of activating light and the heterogeneous oxygenation status of solid tumors—challenges that are particularly pronounced in bulky or hypoxic HNSCC. Intertumoral variability in vascular architecture and drug distribution may further complicate the prediction of treatment response and clinical outcomes.249

Advances in nanotechnology and optical engineering are progressively addressing these limitations. Encapsulation of verteporfin within metal–organic frameworks, polymeric NPs, or other nanocarriers is designed to enhance its tumor accumulation and modulate its pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles.250,251 In parallel, the aim of strategies such as interstitial light delivery and upconversion-based approaches is to extend the effective penetration depth of activating light.252 Collectively, these innovations are expected to improve the therapeutic index of verteporfin and expand its clinical utility beyond ophthalmology to oncology and other disease settings.

Current Barriers to Clinical Translation

Despite the rapid progress of nanomedicine-based targeted phototherapy in the treatment of HNCs, its clinical translation remains constrained by several unresolved challenges (Figure 14).

Clinical translation roadmap for nanomedicine-based phototherapy platforms.

Limited Light Penetration

A primary limitation of conventional PDT lies in the restricted tissue penetration of visible light and NIR-I wavelengths, which typically extends only a few millimeters, thereby limiting the clinical applicability of PDT and PTT in HNCs.259 This challenge is particularly pronounced in anatomically complex regions (eg, nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx), where tumors are often located in deep-seated sites that are difficult to access While superficial lesions in the oral cavity can be readily treated, tumors arising in deeper pharyngeal structures remain largely inaccessible to conventional light-based therapies.254 The NIR-II window offers several advantages, including reduced scattering and attenuation, lower tissue autofluorescence, and minimal phototoxicity, thereby enabling deeper tissue penetration and improved imaging resolution.255 Recent advances in the development of NIR-II-responsive photodynamic and photothermal agents have demonstrated substantial potential to overcome these limitations.25,258 NPs with strong absorption in the NIR-II region, such as rare-earth-doped nanomaterials and semiconducting polymer nanostructures, are emerging as next-generation platforms for the treatment of deep-seated head and neck tumors.260 In addition, advanced phototherapeutic systems are increasingly integrated with ultrasound-based approaches to enhance tumor targeting and therapeutic precision. Ultrasound can promote NP extravasation via acoustic cavitation and facilitate localized drug release, thus enabling greater tissue penetration.261 The combination of phototherapy and ultrasound holds promise for improving spatial precision and safety, ultimately advancing clinical translation.

Tumor Hypoxia

Oxygen is an indispensable component of PDT, serving as a critical substrate for the generation of ROS during tumor ablation. Nonetheless, hypoxia is a prevalent and defining feature of most solid tumors, arising from an imbalance between oxygen supply and consumption driven by rapid tumor growth.262 Specifically, aberrant and dysfunctional tumor vasculature—characterized by structural disorganization, collapse, and poor perfusion—leads to acute hypoxia. Furthermore, increased diffusion distances and sustained metabolic demand contribute to chronic hypoxia during tumor progression.263 In HNCs, this hypoxic microenvironment markedly limits oxygen-dependent ROS generation, thereby attenuating the direct cytotoxic efficacy of phototherapy.

Moreover, hypoxia constitutes a key hallmark of the TME, playing pivotal roles in tumor progression, resistance to phototherapy, and immune modulation. First, PDT intrinsically consumes molecular oxygen during photochemical reactions, and a hypoxic TME directly restricts ROS production; conversely, PDT-induced oxygen depletion further exacerbates intratumoral hypoxia, establishing a vicious cycle.264 Second, hypoxia-driven stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) upregulates protumorigenic mediators, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), and interleukin-8 (IL-8), thereby promoting tumor progression and metastasis. In addition, hypoxia reprograms tumor metabolism toward glycolysis, fostering an acidic and immunosuppressive microenvironment and establishing a feedback loop that accelerates tumor proliferation and therapeutic resistance. Hypoxia-mediated metabolic reprogramming further modulates immune cell function and promotes the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME.265–267 Although strategies such as oxygen-generating nanocarriers and hypoxia-activated prodrugs have been developed, concerns regarding their biocompatibility, biosafety, and scalability continue to hinder clinical translation. Future approaches may focus on targeting the HIF-1 pathway to reprogram the hypoxic tumor metabolism and inhibit angiogenesis, thus suppressing tumor growth and metastasis in HNCs; nevertheless, further investigation is required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and identify the optimal HIF-1 inhibitors.

Limited Reliability of Preclinical Animal Models

A critical challenge in translating phototherapy strategies to HNC treatment lies in the biological discrepancies between preclinical models and human disease. Most experimental studies rely on murine SCC models, including xenograft and syngeneic systems.268 Although these models provide valuable insights into the efficacy of NP-based phototherapy, they are characterized by major inherent limitations. Murine tumors typically exhibit rapid growth, relatively simple stromal architecture, and high vascular permeability, conditions that favor NP accumulation and may overestimate therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, human head and neck tumors are characterized by dense extracellular matrix remodeling, enrichment of CAFs, aberrant vascular architecture, and elevated interstitial pressure, which significantly impede drug penetration and intratumoral distribution.269,270 Furthermore, CAF-mediated extracellular matrix deposition and cytokine signaling promote immune evasion and therapeutic resistance, resulting in a more restrictive TME than that observed in most murine models.271 Therefore, future studies should prioritize the development of more physiologically relevant models that more accurately recapitulate human tumor biology, including advanced in vitro three-dimensional culture systems and organ-on-a-chip technologies that capture the structural and functional complexity of human tissues and the TME.272 Such advances will enable more accurate preclinical evaluation of NP-based therapies in HNCs and improve the predictive value for clinical translation.

Heterogeneity of the EPR Effect in Human Tumors

The EPR effect has long been regarded as a cornerstone of passive drug delivery and is widely exploited in nanomedicine-based PDT and PTT.273 In murine xenograft models, rapidly growing tumors typically exhibit highly permeable vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage, conditions that facilitate the accumulation of NPs.274 However, an increasing body of clinical evidence indicates that EPR-mediated drug delivery in human tumors is highly heterogeneous.275 In HNCs, vascular permeability, stromal density, and perfusion vary substantially across tumor regions and disease stages. These variations result in highly uneven NPs deposition and, consequently, limit the uniform distribution of ROS or heat within tumors.276 Moreover, the dense stromal architecture and fibrosis commonly observed in human HNSCC further restrict the penetration of NPs beyond perivascular regions. As a result, the accumulation of NPs predicted from small-animal models frequently overestimates the achievable intratumoral drug levels in patients.277 Future research should focus on the development of nanoplatforms capable of both efficient tumor accumulation and deep tissue penetration to enhance the overall therapeutic efficacy of phototherapy.

Heterogeneity in HNCs

HPV infection has emerged as an independent etiological factor in HNSCC. The strongest association has been observed in the oropharynx, where HPV16 represents the most prevalent genotype.278 HPV-positive and HPV-negative HNSCC exhibit fundamentally distinct biological behaviors and should be regarded as separate disease entities rather than merely molecular subtypes.279 HPV-negative HNSCC is primarily driven by carcinogen-induced DNA damage, leading to frequent mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as tumor protein p53 (TP53) and cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A). In addition, the composition of immune infiltration differs markedly between HPV-positive and HPV-negative tumors.280 Compared with HPV-negative HNSCC, HPV-positive tumors are characterized by a higher density of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.281 Consequently, HPV-positive HNSCC displays distinct clinical and histopathological features, unique epidemiological patterns, and generally more favorable prognosis. From the perspective of phototherapy, these biological differences are of critical importance. HPV-positive tumors may be more susceptible to immune amplification following ICD, whereas HPV-negative tumors are more likely to be constrained by stromal barriers, hypoxia, and immune exclusion, thereby limiting therapeutic efficacy.282

Scalability, Cost-Effectiveness, and Regulatory Barriers

To enhance therapeutic efficacy, an increasing number of nanomedicine platforms incorporate multiple functional components, including targeting ligands, imaging probes, oxygen-generating catalytic modules, and immunomodulators.283 However, the structural and functional complexity associated with synthesizing such multifunctional NPs and developing light-based systems leads to substantial increases in the cost of production.284 Although these sophisticated designs demonstrate impressive performance in experimental settings, they also markedly complicate large-scale manufacturing and quality control. During scale-up, ensuring batch-to-batch consistency in critical parameters (eg, particle size distribution, surface chemistry, drug loading efficiency, and photophysical responsiveness) remains a major challenge285 These manufacturing constraints hinder compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards and may delay regulatory approval processes. Furthermore, nanomedicine-based phototherapeutic systems are often classified as combination products that integrate drugs with medical devices (eg, laser systems or optical delivery platforms), thereby adding further complexity to regulatory evaluation and clinical trial design286 Establishing standardized manufacturing workflows and scalable purification strategies is essential for broad clinical implementation. In parallel, economic considerations critically influence translational feasibility, as cost-effective and large-scale production is indispensable for global clinical adoption, particularly in resource-limited settings.287 Addressing these manufacturing and regulatory bottlenecks is pivotal for advancing the widespread clinical translation and commercialization of NP-based targeted phototherapy.

Safety and Biocompatibility

Ensuring the biosafety of nanomedicine platforms is critical to minimizing unintended toxicities and enabling their clinical translation. Inorganic nanomaterials, such as AuNPs and carbon-based systems, are inherently non-biodegradable. This characteristic leads to potential long-term accumulation in organs such as the liver and spleen and raises concerns regarding chronic toxicity.288 Although polymeric materials, including polypyrrole and PDA, are generally considered biocompatible and biodegradable, their long-term in vivo fate and potential cumulative toxicity require careful and systematic evaluation.289 Additionally, it has been shown that biomimetic strategies (eg, cell membrane functionalization) enhance the accumulation of such materials in tumors Nevertheless, the intrinsic immunogenicity of biological coatings (eg, derived from cell membranes or viral proteins) may trigger unpredictable immune recognition and clearance mechanisms290 These biosafety concerns further complicate the clinical application of nanomedicine-based phototherapy, as the long-term metabolic behavior, biodistribution, and stability of these materials must be comprehensively characterized. Future studies should prioritize rigorous pharmacokinetic profiling and nanotoxicological assessments to ensure the safety of these materials and facilitate regulatory approval.

Future Research Directions

Photoacoustic Imaging-Guided PTT

The integration of phototherapy with advanced ultrasound technologies represents a promising direction for targeted treatment of HNCs. The NIR-II window offers reduced attenuation, minimal tissue autofluorescence, and low phototoxicity, thereby enabling deeper tissue penetration and improved spatial resolution for imaging.291 Unlike light, ultrasound can penetrate tissues to depths of up to ~10 cm, effectively overcoming the limited penetration associated with PTT and minimizing collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues. In addition, Ultrasound can promote NP extravasation via acoustic cavitation and facilitate localized drug release, thus enabling greater tissue penetration.292 Consequently, photoacoustic imaging-guided PTT enables real-time monitoring of photothermal distribution and facilitates adaptive treatment modulation, thus enhancing therapeutic precision and safety.257,293 These advances pave the way for highly personalized and minimally invasive PTT interventions in HNCs.

Synergistic Effects of Phototherapy and Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy in HNCs is frequently constrained by a profoundly immunosuppressive TME, which is characterized by the upregulation of chemokines such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 22 (CCL22) and CCL28, accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells, polarization of macrophages and neutrophils toward protumorigenic phenotypes, and suppression of T cell and natural killer cell activity.294,295 Within this context, CAFs constitute a dominant stromal cell population that actively supports tumor progression. CAFs promote immune evasion by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), IL-10, and IL-6, as well as by expressing immune checkpoint ligands such as PD-L1 and PD-L2. Moreover, CAFs generate a dense extracellular matrix rich in collagen, fibronectin, and proteoglycans (eg, hyaluronan and versican), forming a physical barrier that limits immune cell infiltration and impedes the penetration of phototherapeutic agents296 In addition, CAF-driven signaling networks facilitate the recruitment and activation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages, which secrete cytokines (eg, IL-6, IL-10) and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive TME and promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype297,298 Of note, PD-L1 (a key immune checkpoint molecule) mediates immune escape by binding to PD-1 on lymphocytes and suppressing T cell activation. Importantly, PD-L1 expression exhibits pronounced spatial heterogeneity across tumor cells, stromal components, and infiltrating immune cells.299 This characteristic complicates its use as a predictive biomarker and reflects the adaptive nature of immune evasion within the TME. As discussed above, the ability of PDT and PTT to induce ICD positions them as powerful immunomodulatory partners capable of converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. This process enhances the responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade and other immunotherapies. Accordingly, the combination of PDT/PTT with immune checkpoint inhibitors, cancer vaccines, or adoptive T cell therapies holds substantial promise for eliciting durable systemic antitumor responses against both primary and metastatic lesions.256,300 Such combinations may ultimately transform phototherapy from a local modality into a systemic therapeutic strategy.

Precision Phototherapy Based on Tumor Molecular Stratification

HNCs exhibit marked heterogeneity in genetic composition, biomarker expression, oxygenation status, and therapeutic response; hence, standardized treatment regimens often fail to meet the needs of individual patients.301 Accordingly, the transition toward personalized and precision medicine is critical for advancing nanomedicine-based targeted phototherapy. Integrative analysis of high-throughput multi-omics data—including genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics—enables the stratification of patients who are more likely to benefit from PDT. In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning approaches can leverage large-scale clinical and molecular datasets, encompassing genetic profiles, tumor characteristics, and treatment histories, to predict optimal phototherapy strategies tailored to specific molecular subtypes.302 Such data-driven precision approaches hold considerable promise for improving therapeutic outcomes and advancing individualized treatment paradigms in HNCs.

AI-Assisted Design of NPs for Phototherapy

AI has emerged as a transformative tool with the potential to rapidly and accurately design novel photosensitizer candidates with low toxicity and high photodynamic activity, based on existing datasets of well-characterized and biocompatible molecules.303,304 Such data-driven approaches enable the efficient identification of optimized molecular structures, thereby facilitating the development of safer and more effective targeted phototherapies for HNCs. These intelligent systems support high-throughput virtual screening, reduce material waste, and accelerate the discovery of high-performance nanomaterials.305 The integration of AI with nanotechnology offers significant opportunities for precision design, enhanced efficiency, and accelerated clinical translation, emphasizing the key role of this interdisciplinary approach in the future of biomedical nanotechnology.

Interdisciplinary Collaboration and Translational Integration

The successful clinical translation of nanomedicine-based phototherapy requires close interdisciplinary collaboration and the integration of expertise from precision medicine, nanotechnology, optical physics, AI, and oncology.306 The convergence of these disciplines has driven the development of multifunctional nanoplatforms that integrate therapeutic efficacy with diagnostic precision. By fostering interdisciplinary integration, aligning technological innovation with clinical needs, and establishing standardized evaluation frameworks, targeted phototherapy for HNCs can be further optimized to achieve improved efficacy, enhanced safety, and broader clinical applicability.

Conclusions and Outlook

Although nanomedicine-based phototherapy remains at an early stage of clinical translation, the field is nonetheless undergoing rapid advances at both the preclinical and technological levels. With the rapid advancement of nanotechnology, nanomedicine-based targeted phototherapy has emerged as a transformative strategy for the treatment of HNCs. This treatment modality leverages the unique physicochemical properties of nanomaterials to significantly enhance the efficacy and specificity of PDT and PTT. In the present review, we summarized recent progress in the development of next-generation photosensitizers, design of multifunctional nanocarriers, and integration of phototherapy with both conventional and emerging therapeutic modalities for the treatment of HNCs.

Despite these impressive scientific advances, the clinical translation of targeted phototherapy remains cautious and is limited by major challenges. A primary concern lies in the potential toxicity associated with nanomedicine platforms.307 Although multifunctional nanoplatforms demonstrate robust performance in preclinical studies, their translation into clinical practice is hindered by regulatory complexity and incomplete safety evaluation. Currently, the biocompatibility and safety profiles of numerous emerging and hybrid nanomaterials remain insufficiently characterized, with a lack of standardized validation for their absorption, distribution, pharmacokinetics, and long-term toxicity. Moreover, complex synthesis processes, challenges in manufacturing scalability, and stringent regulatory requirements further complicate reproducibility and clinical approval. These parameters constitute major bottlenecks for the transition from laboratory-scale synthesis to industrial-scale production. Future efforts should prioritize the harmonization of nanotoxicological assessment, establishment of standardized manufacturing workflows, and development of scalable purification strategies to enable widespread clinical implementation. In parallel, large-scale and cost-effective production is essential for global clinical adoption. Furthermore, innovations in green synthesis and economically sustainable manufacturing processes may play a pivotal role in ensuring accessibility and affordability across diverse healthcare settings. AI also represents a transformative tool for the rational design of nanoplatforms. By leveraging datasets of well-characterized photosensitizer and photothermal agent molecules, AI-driven approaches can optimize key parameters such as optical absorption, quantum yield, and biodistribution, thereby accelerating the development of safer and more effective next-generation phototherapeutic systems for HNCs.308

Another critical challenge lies in the pronounced variability of the EPR effect across patients, tumor types, and different temporal stages of the same tumor. Such heterogeneity limits the predictability and efficiency of NP delivery. In particular, the penetration of NPs into large, poorly perfused, and deep-seated pharyngeal tumors remains inadequate, resulting in the uneven distribution of ROS or heat within the tumor mass. Recent advances in organic–inorganic hybrid nanostructures and biomimetic cell membrane-functionalized platforms have demonstrated improved translational potential by achieving a balance between optical performance and biocompatibility. The development of intelligent multifunctional nanoplatforms responsive to NIR-II light, ultrasound, or X-ray irradiation may further expand the applicability and safety of phototherapy for deep-seated head and neck tumors.309

Most current preclinical studies rely heavily on in vitro two-dimensional cell culture systems and murine subcutaneous tumor models, which fail to recapitulate the physicochemical complexity of the human TME. Moreover, patient-derived xenograft models established in immunodeficient mice lack a functional tumor immune microenvironment, thereby limiting their ability to accurately reproduce human tumor immunobiology. As a result, these models provide only partial insight into therapeutic responses and may overestimate translational efficacy. Future research should prioritize the development of more physiologically relevant models, including advanced three-dimensional in vitro systems and organ-on-a-chip technologies capable of capturing the structural and functional characteristics of human tissues and the TME.310 Such innovations may enable more accurate preclinical evaluation of NP-based phototherapy and improve the predictive power for clinical translation in human tumors.

In addition, the pronounced heterogeneity of HNCs poses a significant challenge to the therapeutic efficacy of photoactivated nanoplatforms. Variations in genetic alterations, molecular phenotypes, and features of the TME can lead to divergent treatment responses, thereby complicating the optimization of phototherapy strategies. AI and machine learning approaches offer powerful tools for processing and learning from large-scale patient datasets. By integrating high-throughput multi-omics data (eg, genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics) these technologies can facilitate the identification of novel predictive biomarkers associated with phototherapy responsiveness311 Such advances may enable the stratification of patients most likely to benefit from phototherapy and pave the way for precision phototherapeutic strategies based on tumor molecular profiling.

Nanomedicine-based targeted phototherapy represents a rapidly advancing field of research in the treatment of HNCs, particularly when synergistically integrated with advances in optical physics. Such convergence holds the potential to transform phototherapy from a promising adjunctive modality into an established and clinically recognized therapeutic strategy. Although the transition from laboratory research to clinical application is likely to be gradual and challenged by substantial obstacles, continued interdisciplinary innovation and translational efforts are expected to drive this evolution. Ultimately, precision and individualized phototherapy enabled by nanomedicine may redefine the therapeutic landscape for HNCs.
