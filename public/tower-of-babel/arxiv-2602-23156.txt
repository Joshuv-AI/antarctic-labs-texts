Coupling of the continuum and semiclassical limit. Part I: convergence of eigenvalues
Matthias Keller, Lorenzo Pettinari, Christiaan J. F. van de Ven

Abstract
. We analyze the semiclassical d d -dimensional Schrödinger operator in the continuum 1 2 ​ Δ + λ N 2 ​ V \frac{1}{2}\Delta+\lambda_{N}^{2}V discretized on a mesh with spacing proportional to 1 / N 1/N . The semi-classical parameter λ N \lambda_{N} is chosen as λ N = N 1 − γ \lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma} , with γ ∈ ( − 1 , 1 ) \gamma\in(-1,1) , which ensures that N N governs both the semiclassical and continuum limit simultaneously. We prove that all eigenvalues of the discrete operator converge to those of the continuum, as λ N → ∞ \lambda_{N}\to\infty . Beyond this semi-classical domain, in the case of the harmonic oscillator, we further discuss the spectral asymptotics for γ ∈ ℝ ∖ ( − 1 , 1 ) \gamma\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1) , thereby fully characterizing the eigenvalue behavior across all possible values of γ ∈ ℝ \gamma\in\mathbb{R} .



1.
Introduction

This is the first part of a work which is devoted
to the analysis of the spectral asymptotics of a discrete Schrödinger operator. Subsequently there is a second part
[
12
]
which will then be devoted to the analysis of the eigenfunction asymptotics in the same limit, including Agmon-type estimates for the ground state.

This first part is focused on the convergence of eigenvalue to the analysis of the energy asymptotics of a
d
-dimensional discrete Schrödinger operator in a
combined
semi-classical and continuum limit. Unlike other approaches in this context
[
6
,
7
,
16
,
13
,
14
]
, we investigate a wide range of scalings, interlacing the semiclassical parameter with the discretization one. This coupling yields a rich variety of asymptotic regimes for the eigenvalues. In particular, we are able to pinpoint the precise region in parameter space where the limiting behavior aligns with the semiclassical limit of a continuum Schrödinger operator
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}+\lambda_{N}^{2}V
in
d
dimensions.
More precisely, we rigorously show that, in the semi-classical regime
\lambda_{N}\gg 1
, with
\lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(-1,1)
the scaling parameter, all eigenvalues of the discrete Schrödinger operator exhibit the same asymptotic behavior as those of the continuum operator
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}+\lambda_{N}^{2}V,

where the smooth, non-negative potential
V
with finitely many non-degenerate minima is strictly positive at infinity. This result confirms the validity and appropriateness of our scaling relation between the semi-classical parameter
\lambda_{N}
and the discretization mesh size
1/N
, showing that it effectively captures the classical limit of the continuous model through the discrete approximation. As already mentioned above analysis of Agmon-type estimates for eigenfunctions are deferred to our forthcoming companion paper
[
12
]
.

In the final part of the paper, we emphasize that our analysis on eigenvalue asymptotics extends beyond the semi-classical regime. It not only identifies the interval
(-1,1)
as the true semiclassical domain, but also classifies four additional regions where the spectral behavior deviates significantly. Framed within this broader context, our study provides a unified understanding of the various limiting regimes and underscores the role of the parameter
\gamma
in determining the spectral asymptotics.

1.1.
Semiclassical analysis and discrete Schrödinger operators

For a positive integer
d\geq 1
, we consider the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Let
V:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a function and define

\displaystyle V_{N}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad x\mapsto V\left(\frac{x}{N}\right).

For a given parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we consider the following discrete Schrödinger operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})

\displaystyle H_{N}f(x):=-\frac{N^{2}}{2}\sum_{|x-y|=1}(f(y)-f(x))+N^{2(1-\gamma)}V_{N}(x)f(x)

For suitable conditions on
V
,
cf.
Assumption
1.1
in Section
1.3
, we analyze the spectral asymptotics as
N\to\infty
for different values of
\gamma
.
In particular, we may identify a total number of five distinct limits with the following characteristics for the eigenvalues, as further discussed in Remark
Remark
, namely

•

\gamma>1
: corresponding to the continuum limit of the free discrete Laplacian, towards the Laplacian
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
;

•

\gamma=1
: corresponding to the continuum limit of
H_{N}
towards the operator
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}+V
;

•

\gamma\in(-1,1)
: corresponding to a semi-classical limit of the operator
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}+\lambda^{2}V
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with main result stated in Theorem
1.2
below;

•

\gamma=-1
: corresponding to a purely discrete model, whose precise features depend on the particular choice of the potential;

•

\gamma<-1
: corresponding to a semiclassical approximation of a discrete model.

Among these regimes, the case
\gamma\in(-1,1)
is of particular interest: it yields not only semiclassical spectral asymptotics, but also allows for the derivation of exact Agmon estimates for the ground state, in the spirit of the continuum results of
[
19
]
. This regime constitutes the main focus of our second work
[
12
]
.

1.2.
Physical interpretation and comparison with literature

We recall the usual semi-classical analysis for a Schrödinger operator in the continuum

\displaystyle\frac{h^{2}}{2}\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}+V\ \ \ \text{on}\ \ L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{2}
is the usual Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
V:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a potential defined as multiplication operator.
The parameter
h
is a scaled and dimensionless version of Planck’s constant, describing a classical theory in the regime
h\to 0
. The behavior of this operator is closely related to the behavior of the rescaled operator with
\lambda=1/h

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}+{\lambda^{2}}V\ \ \ \text{on}\ \ L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(1.1)

when letting
\lambda\to\infty
. This limit is studied by Simon in
[
19
,
20
]
in relation to the time dependent Schrödinger equation.

For a discrete analogue, one typically considers a uniform mesh size
\delta
and introduce a discrete Schrödinger operator
H_{\delta,\lambda}
on
\ell^{2}(\delta\mathbb{Z})
, defined by

\displaystyle H_{\delta,\lambda}f(x)=-\frac{1}{2\delta^{2}}\sum_{|x-y|=\delta}(f(y)-f(x))+\lambda^{2}V(x)f(x).

The continuum limit deals with the analysis of this operator as
\delta\to 0
at fixed
\lambda
. Details on this limit can be found in e.g.
[
6
,
16
]
. In contrast, the classical limit concerns the regime
\lambda\to\infty
, at fixed
\delta
(see e.g.
[
2
]
).

In this work, we couple the parameters
\delta,\lambda>0
as follows. For a given parameter
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we let

\delta_{N}=1/N\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma}.

In view of the above discussion, the corresponding operator is given by

\displaystyle{H_{1/N,N^{1-\gamma}}}f(x)=-\frac{N^{2}}{2}\sum_{|x-y|=1/N}(f(y)-f(x))+\lambda_{N}^{2}V(x)f(x)

on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}/N).
Thus, the limit
\delta\to 0
is not taken independently of
\lambda_{N}
; instead, it is a single coupled limit
N\to\infty
obeying
\delta_{N}=1/N
and
\lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma}
. In other words, the limit
N\to\infty

simultaneously
describes the continuum as well as the semi-classical limit.

To see how
H_{N}
defined above is derived from
{H_{1/N,N^{1-\gamma}}}
we make the following simplification, which, however, does not lead to a loss of generality. Instead of considering the scaled lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}/N
, we work on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, which means that we have to consider the potential
V_{N}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by

V_{N}(x)=V\left(\frac{x}{N}\right),

and furthermore the discrete Laplacian on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
sums over vertices of distance
1
instead of distance
1/N
, which readily gives the operator
H_{N}
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
which we already introduced in the beginning of this section, i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{N}f(x)=-\frac{N^{2}}{2}\sum_{|x-y|=1}(f(y)-f(x))+N^{2(1-\gamma)}V_{N}(x)f(x).

Note that
H_{N}
unitarily equivalent to
{H_{1/N,N^{1-\gamma}}}
via the map
U_{N}:\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\to\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d}/N)
,
U_{N}f(x/N)=f(x)
. In particular, the prefactor
N^{2}
in front of the Laplacian is essential: it represents the approximate second derivative of a function in the continuum.

Remark
.

We compare our results with the current literature, taking
H_{N}
as the starting point.

\gamma<-1
: By rescaling the operator with the global factor
N^{-2}
, one obtains, for
\gamma<-1
, a semiclassical approximation of the discrete model. This limit was analyzed in the sense of Weyl asymptotics for bounded multiplication operators in
[
2
]
, where asymptotic formulas for the number of discrete eigenvalues in the semi-classical limit were obtained. It has been studied in
[
15
]
in the case of finite connected graphs. Regarding the latter work, in case of the harmonic oscillator, we obtain similar results, in the sense that the eigenvalues converge towards the points of the potential, discussed in
cf.
Section
4
. In addition, the case
\gamma=-1
corresponds to a purely discrete model, whose eigenvalue asymptotics coincide with those of the discrete harmonic oscillator,
cf.
Proposition
4.2
.

\gamma\in(-1,1)
: For the specific choice of
\gamma=0
where the operator
H_{N}
approximates the semi-classical limit of (
1.1
), various interesting results have been obtained in case of bounded potentials with sufficient regularity. Examples include the works by Helffer and Sjöstrand
[
4
]
, Kameoka
[
7
]
, Rabinovich
[
17
]
, and Klein and Rosenberger
[
14
]
. In particular, Klein and Rosenberger studied a generalized Laplacian with variable coefficients, which in our setting reduces to the constant-coefficient Laplacian.
Unlike the present work, the spectral estimates for the eigenvalues in these references are obtained using microlocal techniques, and eigenvector asymptotics are derived in terms of the Agmon–Finsler metric
[
7
,
13
]
. A further extension of these asymptotics to eigenfunctions will be presented in our follow-up work
[
12
]
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no literature on this regime for
\gamma\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
, and the case of unbounded potentials has not been studied neither.

\gamma=1
: For
\gamma=1
, i.e., the regime in which the continuum limit of
H_{N}
approximates
\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+V
, convergence results have been obtained in the norm-resolvent sense. In particular, Nakamura and Tadano
[
16
]
prove norm-resolvent convergence under suitable assumptions on the potential, and Kameoka and Nakamura
[
8
]
further extend this analysis to the convergence of resonances. In the present work we only discuss this regime for the harmonic oscillator as particular case of Theorem
2.1
.

\gamma>1
: Since the eigenvalues tend to zero as
O(\lambda_{N})
, we expect the limit to be the Laplacian with purely continuous spectrum, as follows from Theorem
2.1
.

1.3.
Main results on eigenvalue asymptotics

We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues of
H_{N}
as
N\to\infty
for potentials which satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

The following conditions on
V:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are required:

(V1)

V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
V\geq 0
.

(V2)

V
has
1\leq m<+\infty
zeros,
a_{1},\dots a_{m}
, such that the Hessian at these points given by

D^{2}V(a_{l})=((\partial_{i}\partial_{j}V)(a_{l}))_{i,j=1}^{d}

is strictly positive definite for all
l=1,\ldots,m
, i.e.,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
are non-degenerate minima for
V
.

(V3)

V
is strictly positive at infinity, i.e., there exists a
c>0
and
R_{0}>0
such that
|x|>R_{0}
implies
V(x)\geq c
.

Due to Assumption
1.1
(V1), the Hessian of
V
is symmetric and, due to (V2), it has
d
strictly positive eigenvalues at each zero
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
which we denote by
\omega_{i}(a_{l})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
,
l=1,\ldots,m
. Furthermore, the set

\displaystyle\Sigma(V)

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{i}(a_{l})(2n_{i}+1)\mid n_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},l=1,\ldots,m\right\}

can be enumerated as a non-decreasing sequence of eigenvalues counting multiplicities, i.e.,

\displaystyle e_{0}(V)\leq e_{1}(V)\leq\ldots\leq e_{n}(V)\leq\ldots

and it is known that the energy levels
e_{n}(V)
are precisely those obtained from a semi-classical limit starting with
\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}+\lambda_{N}^{2}V
, with
\lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma}
and
\gamma\in(-1,1)
,
cf.

[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
.

We consider the
n
-th eigenvalue of the operator
H_{N}
counting with multiplicity which we denote by
E_{n}(H_{N})
and show the following eigenvalue asymptotics.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
V
be a potential satisfying Assumption
1.1
and
\Sigma(V)=\{e_{0}(V)\leq e_{1}(V)\leq\ldots\leq e_{n}(V)\leq\ldots\}
as above. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\gamma\in(-1,1)
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{E_{n}(H_{N})}{\lambda_{N}}=e_{n}(V),

where
\lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma}
.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we study the discrete harmonic oscillator and prove eigenvalue asymptotics in this special case. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.2
using the results from Section
2
. Finally, in Section
4
we discuss the other regimes of the parameter
\gamma
and provide an overview of the corresponding eigenvalue asymptotics.

Throughout the paper,
C>0
denotes a generic constant which may change from line to line.

2.
Discrete harmonic oscillators

In this section we study the spectrum of the discrete harmonic oscillator. For a vector
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we let

V^{harm}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}},\qquad V^{harm}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(\omega_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}+\ldots\omega_{d}^{2}x_{d}^{2}).

The continuum harmonic oscillator is given by the operator
H^{harm}=\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}+V^{harm}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and has purely discrete spectrum with eigenvalues given by

\displaystyle\sigma(H^{harm})

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{i}(2n_{i}+1)\mid n_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right\}

which we can enumerate as a non-decreasing sequence of eigenvalues counting multiplicities, i.e.,

\displaystyle e_{0}(V^{harm})\leq e_{1}(V^{harm})\leq\ldots\leq e_{n}(V^{harm}){\leq}\ldots.

We denote the
n
-th eigenvalue of the operator
H_{N}
counting multiplicities by
E_{n}(H_{N})
and show the following eigenvalue asymptotics.

Theorem 2.1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\gamma\in(-1,\infty)
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{E_{n}(H_{N})}{\lambda_{N}}=e_{n}(V^{harm}),

where
\lambda_{N}=N^{1-\gamma}
.

Since the harmonic oscillator potential
V^{harm}
is
2
-homogeneous, we make a further simplification to our setting by moving all of the scaling and constants towards the potential. We furthermore start with the one-dimensional case
d=1
since the general case then follows by tensorization of the one-dimensional results.

Specifically, for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we consider the multiplication operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
by the function

v_{\kappa}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\qquad v_{\kappa}(x)=2\kappa^{2}V^{harm}(\kappa x)=\kappa^{4}x^{2}.

We study the operator

H_{\kappa}=\Delta+v_{\kappa}

where
\Delta
is the discrete Laplacian on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
defined as

(\Delta f)(x)=2f(x)-f(x+1)-f(x-1).

The operator
H_{\kappa}
has purely discrete spectrum as
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}v_{\kappa}(x)=\infty
. We denote its
n
-th eigenvalue by
E_{n}(\kappa)=E_{n}(H_{\kappa})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.

Now, letting
\kappa=\sqrt{\omega/N^{1+\gamma}}
, one sees that

H_{N}=\frac{N^{2}}{2}H_{\kappa}

for
H_{N}
in
d=1
and we can derive eigenvalue asymptotics for
E_{n}(H_{N})
from eigenvalue asymptotics for
E_{n}(\kappa)
as
\kappa\to 0
which are stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{E_{n}(\kappa)}{\kappa^{2}}=e_{n}(V).

To prove eigenvalue asymptotics for
E_{n}(\kappa)
as
\kappa\to 0
, we use the approximate eigenfunctions given by Hermite polynomials.
Let
h_{n}
be the
n
-th Hermite polynomial,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, in physicists normalization, i.e.,
h_{n}(y)=(-1)^{n}e^{y^{2}}{\frac{d^{n}}{dy^{n}}}e^{-y^{2}}
, see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 22]
a nd denote for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\varphi(y)=e^{-y^{2}/2},\qquad\Psi_{n}=h_{n}\varphi.

Furthermore, for some
\kappa>0
, we define the scaled Hermite polynomial and the corresponding test function for
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}

\psi_{n}(x)=\Psi_{n}(\kappa x)=h_{n}(\kappa x)\varphi(\kappa x).

The fundamental property of these test functions is that they are approximate eigenfunctions of the operator
\Delta+v_{\kappa}
for small
\kappa
as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\kappa>0
, it holds

(\Delta+v_{\kappa})\psi_{n}(x)=\kappa^{2}\left(2n+1\right)\psi_{n}(x)-R(x,\kappa),

where

R(x,\kappa)=\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{t^{3}}{3!}\Psi_{n}^{(4)}(\kappa x-t)dt=O(\kappa^{4})

and the
O(\cdot)
is uniform in
x
.

Proof.

We expand
\psi_{n}(x\pm 1)=\Psi_{n}(y\pm\kappa)
about
y=\kappa x
with respect to
\kappa

\Psi_{n}(y)-\Psi_{n}(y\pm\kappa)\\
=-\left(\pm\kappa\Psi_{n}^{\prime}(y)+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(y)\pm\frac{\kappa^{3}}{6}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)+\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{t^{3}}{3!}\Psi_{n}^{(4)}(y-t)dt\right),

where the integral term is of order
O(\kappa^{4})
and is uniform in
y
since
\Psi_{n}
is a Schwartz function and thus bounded.
Using that
\Psi_{n}
satisfies the harmonic oscillator equation
[
24
, Section 8.3]

-\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(y)+{y^{2}}\Psi_{n}(y)=\left(2n+1\right)\Psi_{n}(y),

we obtain from the expansion above that

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=2\psi_{n}(x)-\psi_{n}(x+1)-\psi_{n}(x-1)

\displaystyle=2\Psi_{n}(y)-\Psi_{n}(y+\kappa)-\Psi_{n}(y-\kappa)

\displaystyle=-{\kappa^{2}}\Psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(y)+O(\kappa^{4})=\kappa^{2}\left(2n+1-{y^{2}}\right)\Psi_{n}(y)+O(\kappa^{4})

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\left(2n+1-{\kappa^{2}x^{2}}\right)\psi_{n}(x)+O(\kappa^{4}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

2.1.
Eigenvalue upper bound for harmonic oscillators

In this section we prove an upper bound on the
n
-th eigenvalue
E_{n}(\kappa)
of the operator
\Delta+v_{\kappa}
via the min-max principle using the test functions
\psi_{n}
defined above.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\kappa>0
, the
n
-th eigenvalue
E_{n}(\kappa)
of the operator
\Delta+v_{\kappa}
satisfies for
\kappa
small

E_{n}(\kappa)\leq\kappa^{2}(2n+1)+O(\kappa^{3}).

We start by proving the following lemma showing that the test functions
\psi_{n}
are almost orthogonal for small
\kappa
. We denote the scalar product on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Lemma 2.5

(Upper bound)
.

For all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\kappa>0
, there is
C_{n,m}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\psi_{n},\psi_{m}\rangle-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!}{\kappa}\delta_{n,m}\right|\leq C_{n,m}.

Proof.

Using the orthogonality of Hermite polynomials with respect to the weight
\varphi^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{n},\Psi_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}H_{n}(x)H_{m}(x)e^{-\omega x^{2}}dx=\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!\delta_{n,m}.

Now, we want to estimate the difference to the discrete. To this end, we observe that by Poisson summation formula, we have for any
g
in the Schwartz space

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}g(x)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\hat{g}(2\pi k)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}g(x)dx+\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}}\hat{g}(2\pi k).

Applying this to
g=\Psi_{n}\Psi_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n},\psi_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}h_{n}(\kappa x)h_{m}(\kappa x)\varphi^{2}(\kappa x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}h_{n}(\kappa x)h_{m}(\kappa x)e^{-\kappa^{2}x^{2}}dx+\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}}\hat{g}(2\pi k).

Changing variables
y=\kappa x
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}h_{n}(\kappa x)h_{m}(\kappa x)e^{-\kappa^{2}x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}h_{n}(y)h_{m}(y)e^{-y^{2}}dy=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!}{\kappa}\delta_{n,m}.

Finally, we estimate the Fourier coefficients. Note that
\hat{g}
is the Fourier transform of
\varphi(\kappa x)^{2}
times two Hermite polynomials in
\kappa x
, and, therefore,
\hat{g}
is
\varphi(x/\kappa)^{2}/\kappa
times a linear combination of Hermite polynomials in
x/\kappa
up to degree
n+m
.
As,
h_{l}(y)\leq(1+|y|)^{l}
for all
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have for some constants
C>0

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}}|\hat{g}(2\pi k)|\leq C\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{k^{n+m}}{\kappa^{n+m+1}}e^{-({2\pi k}/{\kappa})^{2}}\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{n+m}e^{-y^{2}}dy\leq C,

where we estimated the sum by an integral and substituted
y=2\pi k/\kappa
in the second step. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

We use the min-max principle to estimate the
n
-th eigenvalue
E_{n}(\kappa)
of
H_{\kappa}=\Delta+\kappa^{4}x^{2}
. To this end, we consider the linear span of the first
n+1
test functions
\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{n}
which are clearly in the domain of
H_{\kappa}
and denote it by
W_{n}
. For any
\phi\in W_{n}
, we have
\phi=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\psi_{j}
for some
a_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Using the previous two lemmas, we compute

\displaystyle\langle\phi,(\Delta+v_{\kappa})\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m=0}^{n}a_{j}\overline{a_{m}}\langle\psi_{j},(\Delta+\kappa^{2}x^{2})\psi_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m=0}^{n}a_{j}\overline{a_{m}}\left(\kappa^{2}(2m+1)\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{m}\rangle+O(\kappa^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|^{2}\kappa^{2}(2j+1)\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{j}j!}{\kappa}+\sum_{j,m=0}^{n}|a_{j}||a_{m}|O(\kappa^{4})

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}(2n+1)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{j}j!}{\kappa}+\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|\right)^{2}O(\kappa^{2})\right).

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\|\phi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m=0}^{n}a_{j}\overline{a_{m}}\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{m}\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{j}j!}{\kappa}+\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|\right)^{2}O(1).

Thus, for
\kappa
small enough, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\phi,(\Delta+\kappa^{2}x^{2})\phi\rangle}{\|\phi\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}(2n+1)\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{j}j!}{\kappa}+O(\kappa^{2})\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{j}j!}{\kappa}+O(1)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}(2n+1)+O(\kappa^{3}).

Taking the supremum over all
\phi\in W_{n}
concludes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Eigenvalue upper bound for modified harmonic oscillators

In this section, we address the lower bound on the eigenvalues which is more involved. Indeed, we have to restrict the operator to suitable intervals and estimate the ground state energies on these intervals. To this end, we modify the harmonic oscillator potential by increasing it outside a certain interval at one point to deal with the approximation error. The result is stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6

(Lower bound)
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\kappa>0
, the
n
-th eigenvalue
E_{n}(\kappa)
of the operator
\Delta+v_{\kappa}
satisfies for
\kappa
small

\liminf_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{E_{n}(\kappa)}{\kappa^{2}}\geq 2n+1.

To prove the lower bound, we use the so called Agmon–Allegretto–Piepenbrink theorem. We first recall that a function on
u:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called an
\alpha
-superharmonic
function for an operator
H
on
C({\mathbb{Z}})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
if

(H+\alpha)u\geq 0.

If
-u
is
\alpha
-superharmonic, then
u
is called
\alpha
-subharmonic
and if both
u
and
-u
are
\alpha
-superharmonic, then
u
is called
\alpha
-harmonic
.

Assume now that the restriction of
H
to the compactly supported functions of
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
is symmetric and lower semibounded and, thus, allows for a Friedrichs extension which we denote with slight abuse of notation also by
H
. Let
E_{0}
be the ground state energy of
H
, i.e., the bottom of the spectrum. The Agmon–Allegretto–Piepenbrink criterion, see e.g.
[
10
, Theorem 4.14]
, connects the existence of positive superharmonic functions to lower bounds on the ground state energy for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
in the following equivalence:

(i)

It holds
\alpha\geq-E_{0}
.

(ii)

There exists a strictly positive
\alpha
-superharmonic function.

Clearly, the Agmon–Allegretto–Piepenbrink theorem is only suitable to estimate the ground state energy. However, we can use it to estimate higher eigenvalues by decomposing and restricting our operator to suitable intervals.
These intervals are chosen with respect to zeros of the Hermite polynomials. We then estimate the ground state energy of the restricted operator on these intervals using the Agmon–Allegretto–Piepenbrink theorem and use the ground states on these intervals to construct an approximate eigenfunction functions for the operator on
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

To estimate the ground state energies on these intervals, we again use the approximate eigenfunctions given by the Hermite polynomials. In the interior of these intervals, these functions are indeed approximate eigenfunctions by Lemma
2.3

(\Delta+v_{\kappa})\psi_{n}(x)=\kappa^{2}\left(2n+1\right)\psi_{n}(x)+O(\kappa^{4}).

However, at the boundary of these intervals, the Hermite polynomials vanish across an edge which causes problems. These problems manifest themselves, on the one hand, for the bounded intervals between two zeros and also for the unbounded interval at infinity since the error term
O(\kappa^{4})
Lemma
2.3
is independent of
\psi_{n}(x)
which is a problem if
\psi_{n}(x)
is small.

Here, we employ two different strategies to control the behavior of
\psi_{n}(x)
near the boundaries of the intervals:

•

First, we prove a lower bound on
\psi_{n}(x)
for
x
such that
\kappa x
has distance at least
\kappa
from the zeros of the Hermite polynomial, see Lemma
2.8
below.

•

Second, we modify the functions
\psi_{n}
near the interval boundaries by shifting and rescaling their arguments so that a zero of the Hermite polynomial is located exactly at the boundary vertex outside the interval. At the opposite boundary, the function
\psi_{n}
retains a fixed sign and does not vanish, which allows us to apply the approximate eigenfunction property from Lemma
2.3
directly.

For the unbounded interval, the situation is different. A closer inspection of the error term
O(\kappa^{4})
in Lemma
2.3
shows that, although it also contains a Gaussian factor, the associated polynomial factor is larger than that of
\psi_{n}
and appears with a negative sign. Consequently, for large
x
, the approximate eigenfunction property cannot be applied directly. We address this issue by increasing the potential of the harmonic oscillator. We then show that, for fixed
n
, the appropriately scaled eigenvalues of the harmonic oscillator and of the modified oscillator converge to the same value, since the corresponding eigenfunctions localize near zero.

To pursue these ideas we now discuss how to restrict the operator to intervals and then construct the intervals such that the approximate eigenfunctions vanish on one side of the boundary.

For an interval
I\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
, we consider is the projection

\pi_{I}\colon\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(I),\quad f\mapsto f|_{I}

onto
\ell^{2}(I)
and

\iota_{I}\colon\ell^{2}(I)\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}),\quad f\mapsto\begin{cases}f,&\mbox{on }I,\\
0,&\mbox{else}.\end{cases}

its adjoint which is the canonical injection by continuation by zero.

Now, we construct the specific intervals for fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Observe that the Hermite polynomial
h_{n}
has exactly
n
distinct real zeros which are symmetric about
0
and is even or odd depending on whether
n
is even or odd.
Let
0\leq z_{1}<z_{2}<\ldots<z_{k}
be the non-negative zeros of the
n
-th Hermite polynomial, where

k=\begin{cases}n/2&\text{ if $n$ is even, i.e., $z_{1}>0$},\\
(n+1)/2&\text{ if $n$ is odd, i.e., $z_{1}=0$}.\end{cases}

Moreover, we set
z_{k+1}=\infty
for convenience. By symmetry
-z_{k}>\ldots-z_{1}\geq 0
are zeros of
h_{n}
as well.

The starting point of the interval
I_{1}=I_{1,n}(\kappa)=[a_{1},b_{1}]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
is defined as

a_{1}=a_{1,n}(\kappa)=\left\lfloor{\frac{z_{1}}{\kappa}}\right\rfloor+1.

If
z_{1}=0
(in the case where
n
is odd), then
a_{1}=1
and we set
\beta_{1}=1
. Otherwise, we set

\beta_{1}=\beta_{1,n}(\kappa)=\frac{z_{1}}{\kappa(a_{1}-1)}.

We then define

b_{1}=b_{1,n}(\kappa)=\left\lfloor\frac{z_{2}}{\beta_{1}\kappa}\right\rfloor-1.

We proceed inductively. Given
a_{1}\leq b_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{j-1}\leq b_{j-1}
, and

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{j-1}
, we let

I_{j}=I_{j,n}(\kappa)=[a_{j},b_{j}]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}

be given by

\displaystyle a_{j}=a_{j,n}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{z_{j}}{\beta_{j-1}\kappa}\right\rfloor+1,

\displaystyle\beta_{j}=\beta_{j,n}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{j}}{\kappa(a_{j}-1)},

\displaystyle b_{j}=b_{j,n}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{z_{j+1}}{\beta_{j}\kappa}\right\rfloor-1,

where we set
b_{k}=\infty
and, thus,

I_{k}=I_{k,n}(\kappa)=[a_{k},\infty)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}.

If
n
is even, we further define
b_{0}=b_{0,n}(\kappa)=a_{1}-2

\displaystyle I_{0}=I_{n,0}(\kappa)=[-b_{0},b_{0}]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=\left[{-\left\lfloor{\frac{z_{1}}{\kappa}}\right\rfloor+1},\left\lfloor{\frac{z_{1}}{\kappa}}\right\rfloor-1\right]\cap{\mathbb{Z}},

and
I_{0}=\emptyset
if
n
is odd (i.e.,
z_{1}=0
). Finally, for
j=1,\ldots,k
, we define the negative intervals via

\displaystyle I_{-j}=I_{n,-j}(\kappa)=[-b_{j},-a_{j}]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}

and set
\beta_{-j}=\beta_{j}
.

We summarize the properties of these intervals in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.7

(Properties of the intervals)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
\kappa>0
. Then, the intervals
I_{j}=I_{j,n}(\kappa)
,
j=-k,\ldots,k
defined above are disjoint and satisfy

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}}=\bigcup_{j=-k}^{k}I_{j}\,\dot{\cup}\,\left\{\pm\left\lfloor{\frac{z_{1}}{\beta_{1}\kappa}}\right\rfloor,\ldots,\pm\left\lfloor{\frac{z_{k}}{\beta_{k}\kappa}}\right\rfloor\right\}.

Furthermore, for all
j=1,\ldots,k
, it holds

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}(a_{j}-1))=0=\psi_{n}(-\beta_{-j}(a_{j}-1))

and

1\leq\beta_{j}=1+O(\kappa).

Proof.

The disjointness of the intervals follows directly from their construction. Furthermore, by construction we have for all
j=1,\ldots,k
that
b_{j}
and
a_{j+1}
have distance exactly
2
with the point
\left\lfloor{z_{j+1}}/{\beta_{j}\kappa}\right\rfloor
in between. Thus, the intervals
I_{j}
,
j=-k,\ldots,k
cover all of
{\mathbb{Z}}
except for the points
\pm\left\lfloor{{z_{j}}/{\beta_{j}\kappa}}\right\rfloor
for
j=1,\ldots,k
. This gives the desired decomposition of
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
The fact that
\psi_{n}
vanishes at the points
\pm\beta_{j}(a_{j}-1)
follows
since

\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}(a_{j}-1))=h_{n}(z_{j})\varphi(z_{j})=0.

As for the final statement
\beta_{j}\geq 1
is clear by definition and the upper bound follows by induction. Let us introduce the fractional part of a real number by
x=\lfloor x\rfloor+\{x\}
, where
0\leq\{x\}\leq 1
. For
j=1
, we have by construction since
a_{1}=\left\lfloor{z_{1}}/{\kappa}\right\rfloor+1
that

1\leq\beta_{1}=\frac{z_{1}}{\kappa(a_{1}-1)}=\frac{z_{1}}{z_{1}-\kappa\left\{z_{1}\kappa^{-1}\right\}}\leq{\frac{z_{1}}{z_{1}-\kappa}}=1+O(\kappa).

If the statement holds for
j-1
, then we have since
a_{j}=\left\lfloor{z_{j}}/{\beta_{j-1}\kappa}\right\rfloor+1

\beta_{j}=\frac{z_{j}}{\kappa(a_{j}-1)}=\beta_{j-1}\frac{z_{j}}{z_{j}-\kappa\beta_{j-1}\{z_{j}(\kappa\beta_{j})^{-1}\}}=1+O(\kappa).

This concludes the proof.
∎

We define the restricted operators

\displaystyle H_{j,\kappa}=\pi_{I_{j}}(\Delta+v_{\kappa})\iota_{I_{j}}

on
\ell^{2}(I_{j})
which extend to operators on
C(I_{j})
for
j=-k,\ldots,k
. We will show below that the ground state energies of these operators satisfy the desired lower bound. To do so, the lemma above suggests defining the test functions
\psi_{n,j}
on
I_{j}
via

\psi_{n,j}(x)=|\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}x)|=|\Psi(\beta_{j}\kappa x)|=|h_{n}(\beta_{j}\kappa x)|\varphi(\beta_{j}\kappa x).

We first prove the bounds for
j=-(k-1),\ldots,k-1
. As described above, we first we need a lower bound on
\psi_{n}
away from the zeros of the Hermite polynomial. This is proven in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.8

(Behavior near zeros)
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is
c_{n}>0
such that for all
\kappa>0
small enough and all
j=-(k-1),\ldots,k-1
, it holds

\min_{x\in I_{j}}|\psi_{n,j}(x)|\geq c_{n}\kappa\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\psi_{n,\pm k}(a_{\pm k})|\geq c_{n}\kappa.

Proof.

By symmetry we only have to check the statement for positive
j=1,\ldots,k
.
One directly checks that for
j=1,\ldots,k
,
i=0,\ldots,k-1

\displaystyle|\beta_{j}\kappa a_{j}-z_{j}|=z_{j}\frac{1}{a_{j}-1}\geq\beta_{j-1}\kappa\geq\kappa,

\displaystyle|z_{i+1}-\beta_{i}\kappa b_{i}|=\beta_{i}\kappa+z_{i+1}-\beta_{i+1}\kappa(a_{i+1}-1)=\beta_{i}\kappa\geq\kappa.

So, in particular, for
x\in I_{j}
,
j=-(k-1),\ldots,k-1
, we have that
|\beta_{j}\kappa x-z|\geq{\kappa}
for
z=z_{j}
or
z=z_{j+1}
.

To estimate
\psi_{n,j}
, we recall that
\psi_{n,j}(x)=h_{n}(\beta_{j}\kappa x)\varphi(\beta_{j}\kappa x)
and that the Gaußian
\varphi
has a uniform lower bound on the bounded intervals
I_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,k-1
, say
c>0
.
Moreover, by continuity of
h_{n}
, it suffices to check the lower bound only at the vertices near the zeros of
h_{n}
which are
x=a_{j}
and
x=b_{j}
.
We expand
h_{n}
about
z=z_{j}
or
z=z_{j+1}
to obtain for
x=a_{j}
and
x=b_{j}
respectively, using
h_{n}(z)=0
,

\displaystyle h_{n}(\beta_{j}\kappa x)

\displaystyle=h_{n}^{\prime}(z)(\beta_{j}\kappa x-z)+O((\beta_{j}\kappa)^{2}).

It is well-known that the zeros of
h_{n-1}
are distinct from the ones of
h_{n}

[
1
, Section 22.16(iii)]
,
[
23
, Theorem 3.3.1]
. Thus, by the recursion formula for Hermite polynomials, we have

h_{n}^{\prime}(z)=2nh_{n-1}(z)\neq 0.

Above we have checked that
|\beta_{j}\kappa x-z|\geq\kappa
. So, putting
c_{n}=|h_{n-1}(z)|>0
, we obtain the result for
\kappa
small enough.
∎

Lemma 2.9

(Bounded intervals)
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
j=-(k-1),\ldots,k-1
, the eigenvalues
E_{n,j}(\kappa)
of the operator
H_{j,\kappa}
satisfies for
\kappa
small

\liminf_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{E_{n,j}(\kappa)}{\kappa^{2}}\geq(2n+1).

Proof.

The strategy is to show that
\psi_{n,j}
is an
\alpha
-superharmonic function for
\alpha=(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1)
for given
\varepsilon>0
and
\kappa
chosen small enough accordingly.

By Lemma
2.3
,
\psi_{n,j}
satisfies for
x\in I_{j}\setminus\{b_{j}\}
,

\displaystyle H_{j,\kappa}\psi_{n,j}(x)

\displaystyle=(\Delta+v_{\beta_{j}\kappa})\psi_{n,j}(x)+(v_{\kappa}-v_{\beta_{j}\kappa})\psi_{n,j}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{j}^{2}\kappa^{2}(2n+1)\psi_{n,j}(x)+{\kappa}^{4}(1-\beta_{j}^{4})x^{2}\psi_{n,j}(x)+O(\kappa^{4}).

In particular, the equation is satisfied at
x=a_{j}
as
\psi_{n,j}(a_{j}-1)=0
because of the choice of
\beta_{j}
. Now,
\psi_{n,j}(x)>c_{n}
by Lemma
2.8
above, as well as
\beta_{j}=1+O(\kappa)
by Lemma
2.7
, and
x^{2}\leq b_{j}^{2}
. Thus, for given
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
\kappa>0
small enough such that

(H_{j,\kappa}-(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1))\psi_{n,j}(x)\geq 0.

We are left to check the case of
x=b_{j}
. We observe that
\beta_{j}\kappa(b_{j}+1)\leq z_{j+1}
. Thus,
h_{n}(\beta_{j}\kappa b_{j})
and
h_{n}(\beta_{j}\kappa(b_{j}+1))
have the same sign and so do
\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}b_{j})
and
\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}(b_{j}+1))
which we assume without loss of generality to be positive.
Hence, as
\psi_{n,j}
vanishes outside of
I_{j}

\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})-\psi_{n,j}(b_{j}+1)=\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})=\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}b_{j})\geq\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}b_{j})-\psi_{n}(\beta_{j}(b_{j}+1)).

We obtain from Lemma
2.3

\displaystyle H_{j,\kappa}\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})

\displaystyle\geq(\Delta+v_{\beta_{j}\kappa})\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})+(v_{\kappa}-v_{\beta_{j}\kappa})\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})

\displaystyle=\beta_{j}^{2}\kappa^{2}(2n+1)\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})+{\kappa}^{4}(1-\beta_{j}^{4})b_{j}^{2}\psi_{n,j}(b_{j})+O(\kappa^{4})

from which we conclude
(H_{j,\kappa}-(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1))\psi_{n,j}(x)\geq 0
for small
\kappa
as above. Since
\varepsilon>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small, we conclude the result.
∎

For the unbounded interval
I_{k}
, we have an additional issue because
\psi_{n,k}
becomes superexponentially small and, indeed, as will be clear from the proof below small than the error
O(\kappa^{4})
. So, we need to modify the potential and later also the test function. We will later show that this modification does not change the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues for fixed
n
as
\kappa\to 0
.
To this end, we define for
\delta>1
the modified potential

\tilde{v}_{\kappa}(x)=\begin{cases}v_{\kappa}(x)=\kappa^{4}x^{2},&|x|\neq\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor,\\
\kappa^{-\delta},&|x|=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor.\end{cases}

We then define the modified restricted operators

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\pm j,\kappa}=\pi_{I_{\pm j}}(\Delta+\tilde{v}_{\kappa})\iota_{I_{\pm j}}

and their corresponding eigenvalues by
\tilde{E}_{0,j}(\kappa)
, for
j=-k,\dots,k
. We observe that
H_{\pm j,\kappa}=\tilde{H}_{\pm k,\kappa}
for
|j|<k
and, thus,
E_{n,\pm j}(\kappa)=\tilde{E}_{n,\pm j}(\kappa)
.

Lemma 2.10

(Unbounded intervals)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/2)
.
For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the eigenvalues
E_{n,k}(\kappa)
of the operator
\tilde{H}_{k,\kappa}
satisfies for
\kappa
small

\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{\tilde{E}_{n,k}(\kappa)}{\kappa^{2}}\geq 2n+1.

Proof.

We define the test functions

\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}=\begin{cases}\psi_{n,k}(x),&x\leq x_{\delta}=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor,\\
\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta}),&x>x_{\delta}=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor.\end{cases}

For
x\in I_{k}
with
x<\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}
, we take a closer look at the error term
R(x,\kappa)
in Lemma
2.3

R(x,\kappa)=-\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{t^{3}}{3!}\Psi_{n}^{(4)}(\kappa x-t)dt\\
=-\int_{0}^{\kappa}\frac{t^{3}}{3!}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\binom{4}{j}(h_{n}^{(4-j)}\varphi^{(j)})(\kappa x-t)dt\\
=-\sum_{j=0}^{4}c_{n,j}\int_{0}^{\kappa}t^{3}\varphi(\kappa x-t)(h_{n-4+j}\tilde{h}_{j})(\kappa x-t)dt\,

where, in the last step, we used the recursion formula
h_{k}^{\prime}=2kh_{k-1}
for Hermite polynomials as well as
\varphi^{(j)}=(-1)^{j}\tilde{h}_{j}\varphi
(where
\tilde{h}_{j}
are the Hermite polynomials of degree

in probabilistic normalization)
and
c_{j,n}
are constants. Since the Hermite polynomials
h_{l},\tilde{h}_{l}
are of degree
l
, the dominant term in the sum is the one with

which gives rise to the estimate

R(x,\kappa)\geq-C\kappa^{4}(\varphi h_{n}\tilde{h}_{4})(x)=-C\kappa^{4}(\tilde{h}_{4}\psi_{n})(x)\geq-C\kappa^{8}x^{4}\psi_{n}(x).

We now recall that
\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(x)=\psi_{n,k}(x)
for
x\leq x_{\delta}=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor
and
\tilde{v}_{\kappa}(x)=v_{\kappa}(x)
for
x<x_{\delta}
. Thus,
we have for
x\in I_{k}
with
x<x_{\delta}

as above with the refined estimate on the error in Lemma
2.3
and using
(v_{\kappa}-v_{\beta_{k}\kappa})(x)=\kappa^{2}(1-\beta_{k}^{2})x^{2}
and

\beta_{k}=1+O(\kappa)
from Lemma
2.7
that

(\tilde{H}_{k,\kappa}-(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1))\tilde{\psi}_{n,j}(x)=(H_{k,\kappa}-(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1))\psi_{n,j}(x)\\
\geq\varepsilon\beta_{j}^{2}\kappa^{2}(2n+1)\psi_{n,k}(x)+{\kappa}^{4}(1-\beta_{k}^{4})x^{2}\psi_{n,k}(x)-C\kappa^{8}x^{4}\psi_{n,j}(x)\\
\geq{\kappa}^{2}\left(\varepsilon(2n+1)-C({\kappa}^{3}x^{2}+\kappa^{6}x^{4})\right)\psi_{n,j}(x)\\
{\kappa}^{2}\left(\varepsilon(2n+1)-C({\kappa}^{1-2\delta}+C\kappa^{2-4\delta})\right)\psi_{n,j}(x)\geq 0

for
\kappa
small enough since
\delta<1/2
. Observe that the estimate, in particular, holds at
x=a_{k}
since
\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(a_{k}-1)=0
because of the choice of
\beta_{k}
and the arguments presented in Lemma
2.9
near the zeros of
h_{n}
.
For
x_{\delta}=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor
, we have by construction of
\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}
that

\tilde{H}_{k,\kappa}\tilde{\psi}_{n,j}(x_{\delta})=H_{k,\kappa}\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})+(\tilde{v}_{\kappa}\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}-v_{\kappa}\psi_{n,k})(x_{\delta})+(\psi_{n,k}-\tilde{\psi}_{n,k})(x_{\delta}+1).

By the same argument as above, we have

(H_{k,\kappa}-(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1))\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})\geq 0

for
\kappa
small enough. So, it remains to estimate the last two terms.
Since
\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(x_{\delta})=\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})
, we have

\displaystyle(\tilde{v}_{\kappa}\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}-v_{\kappa}\psi_{n,k})(x_{\delta})=(\kappa^{-\delta}-\kappa^{2(1-\delta)})\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})\geq\kappa^{-\delta/2}\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})

for
\kappa
small enough. Moreover, since
h_{n}(\beta_{k}\kappa x)>0
for
x\in I_{k}
, we have
\psi_{n,k}>0
on
I_{k}
and, thus,

\displaystyle(\psi_{n,k}-\tilde{\psi}_{n,k})(x_{\delta}+1)=\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta}+1)-\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})\geq-\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta}).

Combining these estimates with the refinements of the approximate eigenfunction property above, we obtain for
x_{\delta}=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor

\displaystyle(\tilde{H}_{k,\kappa}-(1-\varepsilon)\kappa^{2}(2n+1))\tilde{\psi}_{n,j}(x_{\delta})\geq\left(\kappa^{-\delta/2}-1\right)\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})\geq 0

for
\kappa
small enough.

Finally, for
x>x_{\delta}=\lfloor\kappa^{-(1+\delta)}\rfloor
, we have
\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(x)=\psi_{n,k}(x_{\delta})
and
\tilde{v}_{\kappa}(x)=v_{\kappa}(x)=\kappa^{4}x^{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{k,\kappa}\tilde{\psi}_{n,j}(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{\kappa}(x)\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(x)\geq\kappa^{2}\kappa^{-2-\delta}\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(x_{\delta})\geq\kappa^{2}(2n+1)\tilde{\psi}_{n,k}(x).

This concludes the proof as
\varepsilon>0
can be chosen arbitrarily small.
∎

Combining the lemmas above, we will now prove the eigenvalue lower bound for the modified oscillator. To do so, we first prove that an eigenfunction to the
n
-th eigenvalue must have
n+1
graph nodal domains, i.e., maximal connected regions where it does not change sign.

We start by showing that for symmetric potentials eigenvalues can have at multiplicity two.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
H=\Delta+v
be a discrete Schrödinger operator on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
with purely discrete spectrum. Suppose that

v(x)=v(-x).

Then, all eigenfunctions are either symmetric or anti-symmetric and, eigenvalues can have at most multiplicity two. Moreover, if an eigenvalue has multiplicity two, then one of the corresponding eigenfunctions is symmetric and the other one is anti-symmetric.

Proof.

Let
f
be an eigenfunction of
H
.
We first observe that if
f
vanishes at zero, then
f
is anti-symmetric. Indeed, by the eigenvalue equation at zero, we have
f(1)=f(-1)
and, thus, by induction
f(x)=f(-x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Otherwise, we can define the symmetric and anti-symmetric parts of
f
by

f_{s}(x)=\frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2},\qquad f_{a}(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}

which are clearly eigenfunctions of
H
with the same eigenvalue as
f
. If
f
is not symmetric or anti-symmetric, then
f_{s}
and
f_{a}
are linearly independent and, thus, the eigenvalue corresponding to
f
has multiplicity at least two.

Assume now that there is are two symmetric eigenfunctions
f
and
g
corresponding to the same eigenvalue. As observed above,
f(0)\neq 0
and
g(0)\neq 0
since they are symmetric. Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
f(0)=g(0)
by rescaling one of the two eigenfunctions. Now, the eigenvalue equation at zero implies that
f(-1)=f(1)=g(1)=g(-1)
. By induction, we obtain that
f(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The same argument applies if there are two anti-symmetric eigenfunctions corresponding to the same eigenvalue since the eigenfunction is inductively determined by its values
f(0)=0
at
f(1)=-f(-1)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We make the following convention for the ordering of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of a symmetric discrete Schrödinger operator.

Convention
.

For a Schrödinger operator with discrete spectrum and symmetric potential we arrange
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
counted with multiplicity in increasing order and choose the corresponding eigenfunctions
f_{n}
to be either symmetric or anti-symmetric.
If an eigenvalue has multiplicity two, i.e.,
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n+1}
, then we choose the symmetric eigenfunction to be
f_{n}
and the anti.symmetric to be
f_{n+1}
.

We want to apply a result for half-line operators
[
25
, Corollary 4.9]
to the symmetric operator on the full line. This result states that if a generic Jacobi operator

Hf(x)=a(x)f(x)-b(x-1)f(x-1)-b(x+1)f(x+1)

defined on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
has discrete spectrum with each eigenvalue having multiplicity at most one, then the
n
-th eigenfunction
has exaclty
n+1
graph nodal domains.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
H=\Delta+v
be a discrete Schrödinger operator on

with purely discrete spectrum and symmetric potential

If

is the

has exactly
n+1
graph nodal domains. In particular, if

Proof.

We first assume that

is an symmetric eigenfunction to the eigenvalue

gives that the restriction of

to

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{+}f(0)=2f(0)-2f(1)+v(0)f(0)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{+}f(x)=2f(x)-f(x-1)-f(x+1)+v(x)f(x),\qquad x\geq 1.

However, to apply the result from
[
25
]
, we need a self-adjoint Jacobi operator. We can obtain such an operator by rescaling the above operator as follows. We define

on

\displaystyle{H_{+}}f(0)=2f(0)-\sqrt{2}f(1)+v(0)f(0),

\displaystyle{H_{+}}f(1)=2f(1)-\sqrt{2}f(0)-f(2)+v(1)f(1),

\displaystyle{H_{+}}f(x)=2f(x)-f(x-1)-f(x+1)+v(x)f(x),\qquad x\geq 2.

and

Thus, we see that for any symmetric eigenfunction

of
H

of the symmetric Jacobi operator

g_{s}

By the lemma above there is at most one symmetric eigenfunction corresponding to a given eigenvalue, hence the eigenvalues of
H_{+}
have multiplicity at most one. We order the symmetric eigenfunctions

of
H

The argument for anti-symmetric eigenfunctions is analogous and varies only in the following details: Since the eigenvalue equation at zero is trivial, we obtain the correpsondence to a half-line operator on
{\mathbb{N}}
rather than

All that is left to prove to conclude is that if

After this preparation, we are now in the position to prove the eigenvalue asymptotics for the modified harmonic oscillator

Proposition 2.13
.

For all

of the operator

\liminf_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{\tilde{E}_{n}(\kappa)}{\kappa^{2}}\geq 2n+1.

Proof.

We compare
\tilde{H}_{\kappa}
with yet another operator

\hat{H}_{\kappa,n}=\tilde{H}_{\kappa}+\sum_{j=-k}^{k}(\tilde{E}_{n,\min}(\kappa)-\tilde{E}_{j,n}(\kappa))1_{I_{j}},

where

is the indicator function of a set
A
and

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{n,\min}(\kappa)=\min_{j=-k,\ldots,k}\tilde{E}_{j,n}(\kappa).

Next, we show that the
n
-th eigenvalue
\hat{E}_{n}(\kappa)
of
\hat{H}_{\kappa,n}
is equal to
\tilde{E}_{n,\min}(\kappa)
.
To this end, we construct a corresponding eigenfunction. Let
\tilde{f}_{j,n}
be the ground states of
\tilde{H}_{j,\kappa}
on the interval
I_{j}
,
j=-k,\ldots,k
with norm

which we extend by zero to
{\mathbb{Z}}
. We define the function

where the coefficients

are chosen such that for

and

.

on the points between the intervals

j=-k,\ldots,k

I_{j}

n

n

2.3.
IMS localization formula

Above we have shown the eigenvalue asymptotics for the modified harmonic oscillator
\tilde{H}_{\kappa}
. To conclude the proof of Theorem

H_{\kappa}
and
\tilde{H}_{\kappa}

[
3
, Chapter 3.1]
. Here, we give a slightly more general version than needed for the one dimensional case since we employ it also later for more general potentials.

As usual, we identify a scalar function on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with its corresponding operator of multiplication on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
. Furthermore, for two bounded operators
A
and
B
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
,
we denote its commutator by

[A,B]=AB-BA.

We call a bounded operator
L
on
C(X)
which is bounded on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
a
Laplace type operator
if its matrix elements satisfy
L(x,y)\leq 0
for all
x\neq y
and
L1=0
.

Lemma 2.14

(IMS localization formula)
.

Let
\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{m}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to[0,1]
be such that
\sum_{j=0}^{m}\eta_{j}^{2}=1
. Then, for any bounded self-adjoint Laplace type operator
L
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
and a potential
V
, we have for
H=L+V

H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\eta_{j}H\eta_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\eta_{j},[\eta_{j},L]].

Furthermore, for all
j=1,\ldots,m

\|[\eta_{j},[\eta_{j},H]]\|\leq 2\|L\|C^{2},

where

C=\sup_{|x-y|=1}|\eta_{j}(x)-\eta_{j}(y)|.

Proof.

The equality follows by direct calculation using

\displaystyle[\eta_{j},[\eta_{j},L]]=\eta_{j}^{2}L+L\eta_{j}^{2}-2\eta_{j}L\eta_{j}

and summing over
j=1,\ldots,m
.

To get the norm estimate we calculate, for
f\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
,

\langle f,[\eta_{j},[\eta_{j},H]]f\rangle=\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x)f(y)L(x,y)(\eta_{j}(x)-\eta_{j}(y))^{2}.

Hence, we have by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle|\langle f,[\eta_{j},[\eta_{j},H]]f\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|f(x)|^{2}|L(x,y)|(\eta_{j}(x)-\eta_{j}(y))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|L(x,y)|\right)C^{2}\|f\|^{2}\leq 2\|L\|C^{2}\|f\|^{2}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.
General potentials

In this section, we extend the eigenvalue asymptotics from the harmonic oscillator potential to more general potentials satisfying Assumption
1.1
.

We first prove the upper bound on the eigenvalues by constructing suitable test functions based on the eigenfunctions of the harmonic oscillator near the minima of the general potential.
The main idea for the lower bound is to use the IMS localization formula to compare the general potential with the harmonic oscillator potential near its minimum.

3.1.
Eigenvalue upper bound for general potentials

Proposition 3.1

(Upper bounds general potentials)
.

4.
Alternative scaling regimes

In this section, we study the eigenvalue asymptotics of the the harmonic oscillator potential for
\gamma\in(-\infty,-1]
in the one-dimensional case
d=1
in contrast to the interval
(1,\infty)
which was studied in Section
2
. The
d>1
cases then follow by tensorization of the former results for
d=1
. These techniques might be generalized to more arbitrary potentials. This goes beyond the scope of the present work.

Let us take
\omega>0
and consider the multiplication operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
by the function

v\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad v(x)=\frac{\omega^{2}x^{2}}{2}.

We denote by

H_{N}=\frac{N^{2}}{2}\Delta+N^{-2\gamma}v

the discrete harmonic oscillator operator and by
E_{n}(H_{N})
its
n
-th eigenvalue counted with multiplicity.

We first analyze the singular value in the parameter space
\gamma=-1
, where
\lambda_{N}=N^{2}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
\gamma=-1
, it holds all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{E_{n}(H_{N})}{N^{2}}=E_{n}(H_{1})

Proof.

For
\gamma=-1
the discrete harmonic oscillator becomes

H_{N}=N^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}+v\right)=N^{2}H_{1}.

Therefore, the statement of the proposition follows immediately.
∎

Next, we analyze the parameter domain
(-\infty,-1)
. We have the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

For
\gamma\in(-\infty,-1)
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{E_{0}(H_{N})}{N^{2|\gamma|}}=0

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{E_{2n}(H_{N})}{N^{2|\gamma|}}=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\frac{E_{2n-1}(H_{N})}{N^{2|\gamma|}}=\frac{\omega^{2}n^{2}}{2},\qquad n\geq 1.

Proof.

For
\gamma<-1
, the harmonic oscillator operator can be written as

H_{N}=N^{2|\gamma|}\left(N^{2-2|\gamma|}\frac{\Delta}{2}+v\right).

It is easier to study the operator
H_{N}/N^{2|\gamma|}
, where the only dependence on
N
is now in the factor
N^{2-2|\gamma|}
front of the discrete Laplacian. Since
\Delta
is bounded, the Laplacian term vanishes as

\lim_{N\to+\infty}N^{2-2|\gamma|}\Delta=0,

where the latter limit is in the
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-norm topology.

We infer by
[
9
, Chapter VIII, Theorem 3.6 & 3.15]
that the eigenvalues of
H_{N}/N^{2|\gamma|}
converge to the eigenvalues of
v
.

The multiplication operator
v
has a purely discrete spectrum with eigenfunctions given by

\delta_{n}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\delta_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x=n\\
0&x\neq n\end{cases},

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In particular,
\delta_{0}
is the unique ground state with energy given by
E_{0}(v)=0
, whereas the
2n
-th and
2n-1
-th excited states are degenerates, having the same energy

E_{2n}(v)=E_{2n-1}(v)=v(n)=\frac{\omega^{2}n^{2}}{2}.

This finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎
