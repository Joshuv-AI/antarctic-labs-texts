1.
Introduction

1.1.
Matroid bingo

Consider the following simplified version of the game of bingo.
Each of the
m
players gets a card on which is printed a subset of
\{1,...,n\}
for some positive integer
n
.
The bingo caller has a box filled with
n
balls, on which the numbers
1
through
n
are written (no duplications).
The caller selects a ball at random, reads out the number, and each player who has that number on their card circles it.
The caller continues this process, selecting balls at random until someone calls out “Bingo!”, signifying that they have circled all the numbers on their card.

The company that manufactures the cards (let us call it Matroid, Inc.) follows three simple rules in the design process:

(B1)

No bingo card is empty. In other words, no one shouts “Bingo!” before the game even starts.

(B2)

Every player has a nonzero chance of winning. In other words, no card is a proper subset of another one.

(B3)

There is no possibility of a tie. In other words, it can never happen that two players shout “Bingo!” at the same time; this guarantees that the game always has a unique winner.

For example, consider the following set of cards (with
m=5
players and
n=8
possible numbers):

Figure 1
.
A valid set of matroid bingo cards.

It is not difficult to check that this particular set of cards satisfies properties (B1) through (B3).

On the other hand, the following is
not
a valid set of bingo cards, because if the bingo caller happens to draw the numbered balls in numerical order
1,2,3
, both players will shout “Bingo!” at the same time.

Figure 2
.
A set of cards for which a tie is possible, and therefore doesn’t comprise a valid bingo game.

Despite the simplicity of the rules, it turns out that this game encodes — in a precise way — the axioms for a
matroid

For this reason, we will refer to the game as

1.2.
Matroids

For the purposes of this paper, we will use the following characterization

Matroids can famously be characterized using numerous different systems of axioms. Axiomatic descriptions that characterize the same objects but in a non-obvious way are known in the field as
cryptomorphisms

Definition 1.1
.

A
matroid

{\mathcal{M}}
is a finite set
E

{\mathcal{C}}
of subsets of
E

(C1)

\varnothing\notin{\mathcal{C}}

(C2)

(C3)

We can readily verify that given the set

Indeed, it is clear that (B1) corresponds to (C1) and (B2) corresponds to (C2), so the only issue is to relate (B3) and (C3).
If

satisfies (C3), then there cannot be a tie in matroid bingo, since if calling out a shared element

and
C_{2}

and
C_{2}

which was already complete before
e
was called out. Conversely, if
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies (B3), then given
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
and
e\in C_{1}\cap C_{2}

C_{1}\cup C_{2}\backslash\{e\}
before all the other numbers, followed immediately by
e

C_{3}
had already been completed. This means precisely that
C_{3}
is a circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
with
C_{3}\subseteq(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\backslash\{e\}
.

The game of matroid bingo was invented by Dima Fon-Der-Flaass (cf.

1.3.
Monotonicity

Our goal in this paper is to explore some questions which arise quite naturally when one starts to think about matroids from the point of view of matroid bingo. For example, take a look at

1.4.
Winning probabilities

Let us formulate things more precisely now. Given a legal set of bingo cards—or, equivalently, the set
{\mathcal{C}}
of circuits of a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
—we denote by
{\mybeta}_{C}
the probability that a given circuit
C
wins the game. By assumption,
{\mybeta}_{C}>0
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and

\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mybeta}_{C}=1.

The following two theorems allow us to calculate these probabilities. We have found the second formula to be more useful, both for computer calculations and theoretical results, but we include both formulas for completeness.
In the statements, and elsewhere in the paper, we write
[n]
as shorthand for the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
Also, for a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
, the maximum size of a subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
which does not contain any circuit is called the
rank
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, and is denoted
r({\mathcal{M}})
.

Theorem A
.

Given a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
on
E=[n]
and a circuit
C
of
{\mathcal{M}}
,

{\mybeta}_{C}=\sum_{k=0}^{|{\mathcal{C}}|-1}\left((-1)^{k}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\},\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\frac{|C|}{|(\cup_{C^{\prime}\in S}C^{\prime})\cup C|}\right).

Theorem B
.

Given a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
on
E=[n]
and a circuit
C
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, we have

{\mybeta}_{C}=\sum_{t=|C|}^{r({\mathcal{M}})+1}{\mybeta}_{C}(t),

where

(1)

{\mybeta}_{C}(t)=\frac{|C|}{n}\cdot|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|\cdot\binom{n-1}{t-1}^{-1}.

Here
{\mathcal{I}}_{C,k}
denotes the collection of all
k
-element subsets
S
of
E\backslash C
such that
C
is the unique circuit contained in
S\cup C
.

Remark 1.2
.

The right-hand side of (
1
) has a simple reformulation in terms of the
Tutte polynomial
of the contracted matroid
{\mathcal{M}}/C
,
cf.
Theorem 2.16
.

Remark 1.3
.

The quantity
{\mybeta}_{C}(t)
appearing in (
1
) is the probability that a given bingo card
C
wins in the
t^{\rm th}
round of the game, where
t=1,2,\ldots,n
. We call these the
timed winning probabilities
.
A matroid bingo game always ends by round
r+1
, where
r
is the rank of the corresponding matroid, so
{\mybeta}_{C}(t)=0
for
t>r+1
. Similarly, the card
C
cannot win before at least
|C|
numbers have been called out, so
{\mybeta}_{C}(t)=0
for
t<|C|
.
See
Figure 5
for an example.

Figure 5
.

Figure 3

A sequence
a_{1},\ a_{2},\ldots,\ a_{n}
of real numbers is
log-concave
if for any three consecutive terms, the middle term squared is at least as big as the product of its neighbors:

a_{i}^{2}\geq a_{i-1}\cdot a_{i+1}.

Combining
Theorem B
with a deep recent result from combinatorial Hodge theory, we prove the following (
Theorem 2.17
).

Theorem C
.

For every matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
and every circuit
C
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, the sequence

{\mybeta}_{C}(1),\ {\mybeta}_{C}(2),\ldots,\ {\mybeta}_{C}(n)

of timed winning probabilities is log-concave.

It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
) that a log-concave sequence
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
of non-negative numbers with no internal zeros
4
4

4

A sequence
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
has
no internal zeros
if whenever
a_{i}=0
for some index
i
, either all terms before it or all terms after it are also zero, i.e., the set of indices

a_{i}\neq 0
forms a contiguous block of integers.

is
unimodal

there exists an index

a_{0}\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{m}
and
a_{m}\geq a_{m+1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}
.
One deduces from this that the sequence

{\mybeta}_{C}(1),\ {\mybeta}_{C}(2),\ldots,\ {\mybeta}_{C}(n)

is unimodal.
Although intuitively plausible, this is a highly non-trivial fact about matroid bingo.

5

1.5.
Upper and lower bounds on winning probabilities

We will prove the following upper and lower bounds for
{\mybeta}_{C}
(
Theorem 2.20
and
Theorem 2.29
):

Theorem D
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid of rank
r
on
n
elements and let
C
be a circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Then,

{\mybeta}_{C}\leqslant\frac{\binom{r+1}{|C|}}{\binom{n}{|C|}}=\frac{(r+1)!\cdot(n-|C|)!}{n!\cdot(r+1-|C|)!}.

Theorem E
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid of rank
r
on
n
elements and let
C
be a circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Let
d=r+1-|C|
. Then,

{\mybeta}_{C}\geqslant\binom{n-d}{|C|}^{-1}=\frac{|C|!(n-d+|C|)!}{(n-d)!}.

Remark 1.4
.

The upper bound in
Theorem D
is sharp. Specifically, for any
n
,
r
, and
d
with
0\leqslant d\leqslant r<n
, there is a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
of rank
r
on
n
elements, together with a circuit
C
of size
r+1-d
in
{\mathcal{M}}
, which achieves the upper bound
(cf.
Section 2.3
).
The lower bound in
Theorem E
is not sharp for
d>0
; however, it is deduced from a stronger bound (
Theorem 2.23
) which is sharp in the same sense as above (cf.
Section 2.3
).

When
d=0
, the upper bound in
Theorem D
and the lower bound in
Theorem E
coincide, so we obtain:

Corollary F
.

If
|C|=r+1
, then
{\mybeta}_{C}=\binom{n}{r+1}^{-1}.

In other words, bingo cards with the maximum possible size
r+1
all have the same winning probability.

1.6.
Monotonicity violations revisited

Returning to our earlier theme of monotonicity violations, we have the following positive results.
In the statements, we say that
monotonicity holds
for a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}

if there is no monotonicity violation among the circuits of
{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,
{\mybeta}_{C_{1}}<{\mybeta}_{C_{2}}
whenever
C_{1},C_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfy
|C_{1}|>|C_{2}|
.

From
Theorem D
and
Theorem E
, we will deduce the following result (
Section 2.4
):

Corollary G
.

If
{\mathcal{M}}
is a matroid of rank
r
and
C_{1},C_{2}
are circuits of
{\mathcal{M}}
with

|C_{1}|=r+1
and
|C_{2}|<r+1
, then
{\mybeta}_{C_{1}}<{\mybeta}_{C_{2}}
.
In other words, there can be no monotonicity violations involving a circuit of maximum size,
r+1
.

Remark 1.5
.

In particular, if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a rank
r

paving matroid
(i.e., a matroid whose circuits all have size
r
or
r+1
), there are no monotonicity violations. A well-known conjecture in matroid theory
[
3
, Conjecture 1.6]
asserts that, from an asymptotic point of view, 100% of all matroids are paving. We therefore conjecture that 100% of all matroids have no monotonicity violation.

We also show that monotonicity always holds when
n\gg r
(
Theorem 2.32
):

Theorem H
.

For fixed
r
, there exists
N=N(r)
so that if
{\mathcal{M}}
is a matroid of rank
r
on
n\geqslant N
elements, then
monotonicity holds for
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Remark 1.6
.

More precisely, one can make the argument below quantitative and show that it is possible to take

N(r)=(r+2)+(r+1)!\,\big\lceil\,e^{3r+\frac{25}{6}}\,\big\rceil

in the statement of the theorem.

1.7.
Equitable matroids

Although axiom (B2) asserts that every player in matroid bingo has a non-zero chance of winning, the game is rarely ‘‘fair’’.
6
6

6

We note, wistfully, that the same can be said for life itself.

In a truly fair game, every player would have an equal chance of winning. We call the matroids for which this holds
equitable
.
7
7

7

The reader should not confuse this notion of “equitable” with other uses of the term in the matroid theory literature, for example in Mayhew’s paper
[
2
]
. One can profitably think of Mayhew’s notion as
basis equitable
and ours as
circuit equitable
. Our notion of equitability appears to be unrelated to the one considered in
[
5
]
.

We summarize our findings about equitable matroids in the following:

Theorem I
.

(1)

If
C_{1},C_{2}
are circuits of a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
and there is an automorphism
\myvarphi
of
{\mathcal{M}}
with
\myvarphi(C_{1})=C_{2}
, then
{\mybeta}_{C_{1}}={\mybeta}_{C_{2}}
. In particular, if the automorphism group of
{\mathcal{M}}
acts transitively on the set of circuits then
{\mathcal{M}}
is equitable.

(2)

There exists an equitable matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
whose automorphism group does not act transitively on the set of circuits.

(3)

Uniform matroids and duals of projective geometries over finite fields are equitable.

It is an interesting and seemingly difficult question to classify all equitable matroids.

2.1.
Background from matroid theory

In this section, we review some definitions and facts from matroid theory which will be used later on.

Definition
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid.

(1)

An

of

(2)

(3)

The

for all subsets

.

(4)

The rank of
E
is called the

[

If

[

.

The following result summarizes one particular consequence of the well-known fact (see

Theorem 2.3

A matroid is determined by any of: (a) its collection of independent sets; (b) its collection of bases; or (c) its rank function.

Let

Remark 2.4

It is easy to see that

If a matroid

Proof.

Since

.

Now let

\bullet

If

\bullet

Thus, for every

Remark 2.6
.

By

(2)

({\mathcal{M}}/T_{1})/T_{2}={\mathcal{M}}/(T_{1}\cup T_{2})=({\mathcal{M}}/T_{2})/T_{1}

({\mathcal{M}}\backslash T_{1})/T_{2}=({\mathcal{M}}/T_{2})\backslash T_{1}

On the other hand, if

Lemma 2.10

2.2.
Formulas for winning probabilities

Theorem 2.15
.

Given a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
on
E=[n]
and a circuit
C
of
{\mathcal{M}}
,

{\mybeta}_{C}=\sum_{k=0}^{|{\mathcal{C}}|-1}\left((-1)^{k}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\},\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\frac{|C|}{|(\cup_{C^{\prime}\in S}C^{\prime})\cup C|}\right).

Proof.

For a circuit
C^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
and a permutation
\mysigma
of
E
, define
t_{C^{\prime}}(\mysigma)
to be the smallest
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
C^{\prime}\subseteq\mysigma(\{1,\ldots,t\})
, and
define
P_{C^{\prime}}
to be the set of all permutations
\mysigma
such that
t_{C^{\prime}}(\mysigma)\leqslant t_{C}(\mysigma)
. Note that it is possible to have a permutation
\mysigma
and circuits
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
with
t_{C_{1}}(\mysigma)=t_{C_{2}}(\mysigma)
(this doesn’t contradict axiom (B3) of the matroid bingo game, because that axiom only applies to the first completed card in the game). Define
P
to be the set of all permutations of
E
.

Note that
P\backslash\cup_{C^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\}}P_{C^{\prime}}
consists of those permutations
\mysigma
for which
t_{C^{\prime}}(\mysigma)>t_{C}(\mysigma)
for all circuits
C^{\prime}\neq C
. So, by definition, we have

(4)

\mybeta_{C}=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot|P\backslash\cup_{C^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\}}P_{C^{\prime}}|=1-\frac{|\cup_{C^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\}}P_{C^{\prime}}|}{n!}.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle, we have

(5)

|\cup_{C^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\}}P_{C^{\prime}}|=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{C}}|-1}\left((-1)^{k+1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\},\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}|\cap_{C^{\prime}\in S}P_{C^{\prime}}|\right).

Concretely, we think of
P
as the set of all possible orders in which the bingo numbers can be called out,
and for a given collection
S
of bingo cards other than
C
, the set
\cap_{C^{\prime}\in S}P_{C^{\prime}}
consists of all orders in which
C
loses to all of the bingo cards in
S
.

We can therefore enumerate the permutations
\mysigma
belonging to
\cap_{C^{\prime}\in S}P_{C^{\prime}}
by:

1. Setting
Y:=(\cup_{C^{\prime}\in S}C^{\prime})\cup C
and choosing a
|Y|
-element subset
X
of
[n]
with
\mysigma(X)=Y
.

2. Choosing an element
e\in C
to be the last element from
Y
to be called out in the order corresponding to
\mysigma
.

3. Choosing an ordering for
Y\backslash\{e\}
.

4. Choosing an ordering for
E\backslash Y
.

This gives the formula:

\displaystyle|\cap_{C^{\prime}\in S}P_{C^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{|Y|}\cdot|C|\cdot(|Y|-1)!\cdot(n-|Y|)!

\displaystyle=n!\cdot\frac{|C|}{|(\cup_{C^{\prime}\in S}C^{\prime})\cup C|}.

Combining this with (
4
) and (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mybeta_{C}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{C}}|-1}\left((-1)^{k+1}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\},\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\frac{|C|}{|(\cup_{C^{\prime}\in S}C^{\prime})\cup C|}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{|{\mathcal{C}}|-1}\left((-1)^{k}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}\backslash\{C\},\\
|S|=k\end{subarray}}\frac{|C|}{|(\cup_{C^{\prime}\in S}C^{\prime})\cup C|}\right).

∎

Theorem 2.16
.

Given a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
on
E=[n]
and a circuit
C
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, we have

{\mybeta}_{C}=\sum_{t=|C|}^{r({\mathcal{M}})+1}{\mybeta}_{C}(t),

where

{\mybeta}_{C}(t)=\frac{|C|}{n}\cdot|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|\cdot\binom{n-1}{t-1}^{-1}.

Here
{\mathcal{I}}_{C,k}
denotes the collection of all
k
-element subsets
S
of
E\backslash C
such that
C
is the only circuit contained in
S\cup C.

Furthermore, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant r({\mathcal{M}}/C)
we have

|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,k}|=|\{I\in{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}/C):|I|=k\}|=[x^{r({\mathcal{M}}/C)-k}]T_{{\mathcal{M}}/C}(x+1,1).

Proof.

For the first part, we prove, equivalently, that if
P_{C}(t)
denotes the set of permutations of
E
in which a given circuit
C
is the first one to be completed, and this happens in round
t
, then

|P_{C}(t)|=|C|\cdot|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|\cdot(t-1)!\cdot(n-t)!.

We can enumerate all such permutations by:

1. Choosing an element
e
to be the last element of
C
called out (in round
t
).

2. Choosing a
(t-|C|)
-element subset
S\subseteq E\backslash C
of additional elements to be called out before circuit
C
wins, or in other words, an element of
{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}
.

3. Choosing an ordering of the elements in
(C\cup S)\backslash\{e\}
.

4. Choosing an ordering of
E\backslash(C\cup S)
.

This gives the formula:

|P_{C}(t)|=|C|\cdot|{\mathcal{I}}_{c,\,t-|C|}|\cdot(t-1)!\cdot(n-t)!.

And dividing by the
n!
total number of permutations of
E
, we obtain:

\mybeta_{C}(t)=\frac{|P_{C}(t)|}{n!}=\frac{|C|}{n}\cdot|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|\cdot\binom{n-1}{t-1}^{-1}.

Since
\mybeta_{C}(t)
is the probability of circuit
C
winning on round
t
, the sum over
t
gives the overall probability of
C
winning.

Note that setting
y=1
in (
3
) shows that the number of independent sets of size
k
in a matroid
{\mathcal{M}}

is given by the coefficient of
x^{r({\mathcal{M}})-k}
in
T_{{\mathcal{M}}}(x+1,1)
.

Claim:
A subset
I
of
E\backslash C
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}/C
iff
C
is the only circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
contained in
I\cup C
.

Assuming the claim, we conclude that

|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,k}|=|\{I\in{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}/C):|I|=k\}|=[x^{r({\mathcal{M}}/C)-k)}]T_{{\mathcal{M}}/C}(x+1,1)

as desired.

To prove the claim, choose
e\in C
and let
J=C\backslash\{e\}
. It follows from
Section 2.1
that
I

is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}/J
iff
I\cup J
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Moreover, since
\{e\}
is a loop of
{\mathcal{M}}/C
,
I
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}/C
iff it is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}/J
.

If
C
is the only circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
contained in
I\cup C
, then
I\cup J
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}
and so
I
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}/C
.
Conversely, suppose
I
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}/C
. If there is another circuit
C^{\prime}
contained in
I\cup C
, then since
I\cup J
is independent in
{\mathcal{M}}
,
C^{\prime}
must contain
e
. But then, by the circuit elimination axiom (C3), there exists a circuit
C^{\prime\prime}
contained in the independent set
I\cup J
, a contradiction. Thus
C
is the unique circuit contained in
I\cup C
.

∎

We now establish
Theorem C
:

Theorem 2.17
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid on
[n]
and let
C
be a circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Then the sequence

{\mybeta}_{C}(1),\ {\mybeta}_{C}(2),\ldots,\ {\mybeta}_{C}(n)

is log-concave.

To prove
Theorem 2.17
we will use (the strong version of) Mason’s conjecture, proved independently in
[
10
, Theorem 4.14]
and
[
9
, Theorem 1.2]
. For the statement,
we say that a sequence
a_{1},\ a_{2},\ldots,\ a_{n}
of real numbers is
ultra log-concave
if the sequence
\frac{a_{1}}{\binom{n}{1}},\ \frac{a_{2}}{\binom{n}{2}},\ldots,\ \frac{a_{n}}{\binom{n}{n}}
is log concave
[
15
, p.316]
.

Theorem 2.18

(Strong version of Mason’s conjecture)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid on
[n]
, and let
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
denote the collection of independent sets of
{\mathcal{M}}
of size
k
.
Then the sequence
|{\mathcal{I}}_{0}|,|{\mathcal{I}}_{1}|,\ldots,|{\mathcal{I}}_{n}|
is ultra log-concave.

Remark 2.19
.

Since subsets of an independent set are independent, it is easy to see that the non-negative sequence
|{\mathcal{I}}_{0}|,|{\mathcal{I}}_{1}|,\ldots,|{\mathcal{I}}_{n}|
has no internal zeros.

Proof of
Theorem 2.17
.

Recall the formula

{\mybeta}_{C}(t)=\frac{|C|}{n}\frac{|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|}{\binom{n-1}{t-1}},

where
{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,k}
is the collection of independent sets of
{\mathcal{M}}/C
of size
k
and
|C|\leqslant t\leqslant n
.
Moreover,
{\mybeta}_{C}(t)=0
for
0\leqslant t<|C|
.

Since
n
and
|C|
are independent of
t
, to prove the theorem it suffices to prove that the sequence

\frac{|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|}{\binom{n-1}{t-1}}

with
t=|C|,|C|+1,\ldots,n
is log-concave.

By
Theorem 2.18
, applied to the matroid
{\mathcal{M}}/C
on
E\backslash C
, the sequence

\frac{|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,k}|}{\binom{n-|C|}{k}}

with
k=0,1,\ldots,r({\mathcal{M}}/C)-|C|
is log-concave. (We don’t need to consider values of
k
larger than
r({\mathcal{M}}/C)
because
|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,k}|=0
for such values.)

Setting
t=k+|C|
shows that the sequence

h(t):=\frac{|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|}{\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}}

with
t=|C|,|C|+1,\ldots,r({\mathcal{M}}/C)
is log-concave.

Now note that for
|C|\leqslant t\leqslant n
, we have

\frac{|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|}{\binom{n-1}{t-1}}=\frac{|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,\,t-|C|}|}{\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}}\cdot\frac{\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}}{\binom{n-1}{t-1}}.

We claim that
g(t):=\frac{\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}}{\binom{n-1}{t-1}}
is log-concave. Indeed,

\frac{g(t+1)}{g(t)}=\frac{\binom{n-|C|}{t+1-|C|}}{\binom{n-1}{t}}/\frac{\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}}{\binom{n-1}{t-1}}=\frac{t}{t+1-|C|}

is non-increasing in
t
(its derivative is
\frac{1-|C|}{(t+1-|C|)^{2}}\leqslant 0
), and so

\frac{g(t)}{g(t-1)}\geqslant\frac{g(t+1)}{g(t)}\Leftrightarrow g(t)^{2}\geqslant g(t-1)g(t+1),

i.e.,
g(t)
is log-concave.

We are now done, because both
f(t)
and
g(t)
are non-negative log-concave sequences, and by
[
14
]
the pointwise (Hadamard) product of two such sequences is again log-concave.(Proof: We have

\displaystyle\bigl(f(k)g(k)\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=f(k)^{2}g(k)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq f(k-1)f(k+1)\cdot g(k-1)g(k+1)

\displaystyle=f(k-1)g(k-1)\cdot f(k+1)g(k+1)

for all
k
.)
∎

2.3.
Upper and lower bounds on winning probabilities

In some of the arguments that follow, we will use the “falling factorial” notation

x^{\underline{k}}:=x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1).

We now prove
Theorem D
and
Theorem E
.

Theorem 2.20
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid of rank
r
on
n
elements and let
C
be a circuit in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Then,

{\mybeta}_{C}(t)\leqslant\frac{|C|}{n}\cdot\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}\cdot\binom{n-1}{t-1}^{-1}

for all
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
, and

{\mybeta}_{C}\leqslant\frac{(r+1)!\cdot(n-|C|)!}{n!\cdot(r+1-|C|)!}=\frac{\binom{r+1}{|C|}}{\binom{n}{|C|}}.

Proof.

Note, from the proof of
Theorem 2.16
, that
|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,k}|
equals the number of
k
-element independent sets in
{\mathcal{M}}/C
. The size of the ground set of
{\mathcal{M}}/C
is
n-|C|
, so an upper bound for the number of independent sets of size
k
is
\binom{n-|C|}{k}
. Substituting this into the formula for
\mybeta_{C}(t)
, we obtain

\mybeta_{C}(t)\leqslant\frac{|C|}{n}\cdot\binom{n-|C|}{t-|C|}\cdot\binom{n-1}{t-1}^{-1}.

Since
\mybeta_{C}(t)=\sum_{t=|C|}^{r+1}\mybeta_{C}(t)
, this gives

\displaystyle\mybeta_{C}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|C|}{n}\sum_{t=|C|}^{r+1}\frac{(n-|C|)!}{(t-|C|)!\cdot(n-t)!}\cdot\frac{(t-1)!\cdot(n-t)!}{(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{|C|\cdot(n-|C|)!}{n!}\sum_{t=|C|-1}^{r}t^{\underline{|C|-1}}.

Let
|C|=r+1-d
with
0\leqslant d<r+1
.
By
[
13
, p.53]
(a discrete version of the fundamental theorem of calculus), for any integer
c\neq-1
we have
\sum_{k=a}^{b}k^{\underline{c}}=\frac{1}{c+1}\left((b+1)^{\underline{c+1}}-a^{\underline{c+1}}\right)
, which gives:

\displaystyle\mybeta_{C}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(n-|C|)!}{n!}\left((r+1)^{\underline{|C|}}-(|C|-1)^{\underline{|C|}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(n-|C|)!}{n!}(r+1)^{\underline{|C|}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r+1)!\cdot(n-r+d-1)!}{n!\cdot d!}.

∎

Remark 2.21
.

This upper bound is sharp, in the sense that given any nonnegative integers
n
,
r<n
, and
d\leqslant r
we can construct a matroid on

of rank
r
with a circuit
C
of size

such that
{\mybeta}_{C}
achieves this bound. Specifically, consider the matroid
U_{d,n-(r+1-d)}\oplus U_{r-d,r+1-d}
and denote the unique circuit of the second summand by
C
. Note that appending any

elements of
C
to any basis of
U_{d,n-(r+1-d)}
will yield a basis of
{\mathcal{M}}
, so
r({\mathcal{M}})=r
as desired. Furthermore, the upper bound on
|{\mathcal{I}}_{C,k}|
is achieved, since
{\mathcal{M}}/C=U_{d,n-(r+1-d)}
, which contains the desired numbers of independent sets, so
{\mybeta}_{C}
achieves the desired upper bound as well.

Remark 2.22
.

If
{\mathcal{M}}
contains coloops, then the bounds in Theorem
2.20
can be improved by applying

and calculating the corresponding bound for

, where

is the set of coloops in

:

Theorem 2.23
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a matroid of rank

on

{\mathcal{M}}
with

for some

and when

To prove

, we will need the following preliminary results.

Lemma 2.24
.

If
a,b,s\geqslant 0
are integers and
a+b<s
, then

\sum_{k=a}^{s-b-1}\binom{k}{a}\binom{s-k-1}{b}=\binom{s}{a+b+1}.

Proof.

By
[
13
, p.169]
, for integers
l,m\geqslant 0
and
n\geqslant q\geqslant 0
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{l}\binom{l-k}{m}\binom{q+k}{n}=\binom{l+q+1}{m+n+1}.

Setting

,

, and

gives

\sum_{k=0}^{s-a-1}\binom{a+k}{a}\binom{(s-a-1)-k}{b}=\binom{s}{a+b+1}.

Re-indexing the sum and cutting off zero terms gives:

\sum_{k=a}^{s-b-1}\binom{k}{a}\binom{s-k-1}{b}=\binom{s}{a+b+1}.

∎

Lemma 2.25

{\mathcal{M}}
is a coloopless matroid of rank

Proof.

Let

Direct proof of Corollary
2.3

If a circuit

denote the event that the first

On the other hand, we have

. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C\textrm{ wins})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}\frac{1}{n-r}\binom{n}{r}^{-1}=\frac{r+1}{n-r}\binom{n}{r}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r+1)\cdot r!\cdot(n-r)!}{(n-r)\cdot n!}=\frac{(r+1)!\cdot(n-r-1)!}{n!}=\binom{n}{r+1}^{-1}.

The following consequence of

Corollary 2.31
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}

be distinct circuits of

Proof.

By

, we know that
{\mybeta}_{C}=\binom{n}{r+1}^{-1}
, and from

{\mybeta}_{C^{\prime}}\geqslant\binom{n-d}{r+1-d}^{-1}
.
So it suffices to prove that

(6)

\binom{n}{r+1}^{-1}\;\leq\;\binom{n-d}{\,r+1-d\,}^{-1}

To prove (

\frac{\binom{n-d}{\,r+1-d\,}}{\binom{n}{r+1}}=\frac{(n-d)!\,(r+1)!}{(r+1-d)!\,n!}=\frac{(r+1)^{\underline{d}}}{n^{\underline{d}}}.

, each factor in

is at least as large

\frac{(r+1)^{\underline{d}}}{n^{\underline{d}}}\;\leq\;1.

(with equality iff

has a unique circuit, so

only when

.
