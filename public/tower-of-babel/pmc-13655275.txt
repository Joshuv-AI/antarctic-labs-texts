Spin and Methodological Quality in Systematic Reviews of Decompression Timing for Central Cord Syndrome
Authors: Marco Napolitano, Andrew Kwon, Aidan Lindgren, Daniel Rusu, Joshua Davood, Clay Beagles, Rajko Vucicevic, Jeffrey C Wang, Raymond J Hah, Ram K Alluri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignMeta-research.ObjectivesTo evaluate the prevalence and types of severe spin and the methodological quality of systematic reviews addressing early versus delayed surgical decompression for central cord syndrome (CCS).MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase were searched for systematic reviews evaluating decompression timing in CCS. Introduction

Central cord syndrome (CCS) is an incomplete cervical spinal cord injury (SCI) pattern characterized by disproportionately greater motor impairment in the upper extremities than the lower extremities, often with variable sensory loss and bladder dysfunction.1-4 CCS is frequently encountered in older adults with pre-existing cervical spondylosis or stenosis after a low-energy hyperextension mechanism (e.g., falls), although high-energy trauma and associated osseous/ligamentous injury can also cause CCS.3,5 Contemporary epidemiologic data underscore both the clinical burden and the heterogeneity of presentation; estimates of CCS vary depending on definitions and cohorts, reflecting ongoing challenges in case definition, prognostication, and risk stratification. 6 This underlying clinical heterogeneity of CCS naturally extends to heterogeneity in management strategies and operative indications. 3

Historically, the recommended management of CCS favored non-surgical treatment and natural recovery, with surgical decompression once recovery with conservative management plateaued. 4 Following developments in surgical technique and reported benefits in the literature, surgical management has assumed an increasingly prominent role in the treatment of CCS. 7 However, one major unresolved question in the treatment of CCS is the optimal timing of surgical decompression when surgery is pursued.

The rationale for early decompression is that prompt relief of ongoing mechanical compression and restoration of spinal cord stability may mitigate secondary injury cascades and provide improved neurologic recovery.8-10 In support of this approach, clinical practice guidelines have suggested consideration of early surgical intervention (commonly ≤24 hours) for acute CCS, while acknowledging limitations in the evidence base. 11 Conversely, others have questioned the necessity and efficacy of early surgical decompression for acute SCI patients, citing profound heterogeneity, potential publication bias, and a lack of robustness in the evidence supporting early intervention. 12 Notably, some cohort data have suggested that timing has no significant therapeutic effect in specific CCS populations. 13 More recent syntheses continue to report mixed findings, in part, because primary studies vary in timing thresholds (e.g., 8, 24, 48, or 96 hours), operative indications, and measured outcomes.14,15

Systematic reviews often guide clinical practice, especially in the setting of conflicting evidence. Accordingly, accurate and unbiased reporting is critical to preserve the integrity of evidence-based care, and review conclusions should accurately reflect the underlying evidence. “Spin” is a term that describes reporting practices that portray a more favorable interpretation of evidence than is justified by the data, including unwarranted clinical recommendations, selective emphasis on positive outcomes, and downplaying of risk of bias, heterogeneity, or imprecision. 16 Importantly, the direction of a review’s conclusion does not itself constitute spin; rather, spin reflects a mismatch between the interpretation or strength of a conclusion and the supporting evidence. Yavchitz et al developed a classification for spin in systematic reviews and meta-analyses with a ranking of spin types, establishing a reproducible framework for identifying the most consequential forms. 17 Spin has been repeatedly documented across orthopaedic and spine research, affecting a substantial proportion of published reviews in certain settings.18,19 Given the present uncertainty surrounding surgical timing in CCS, such patterns of spin underscore the importance of ensuring that review conclusions remain proportionate to the supporting evidence.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and types of severe spin and the methodological quality of systematic reviews and meta-analyses addressing early versus late surgical decompression for CCS. Spin was assessed using the classification framework established by Yavchitz et al 17 and methodological quality was evaluated using AMSTAR-2 criteria. 20 Based on prior studies demonstrating high rates of spin in orthopaedic literature, we hypothesized that spin would be present in at least 40% of the included reviews.

Methods

Search Process

A search was conducted on June 25, 2025, using PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase. To identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses regarding central cord syndrome decompression, the following search was used: ((systematic review OR meta-analysis) AND (central cord syndrome OR central spinal cord syndrome OR central cord injury syndrome) AND (decompression OR surgery OR surgical intervention)). The results from this search were uploaded to Covidence for screening by two independent reviewers, and the duplicates were automatically removed.

Inclusion Criteria and Initial Screening

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were systematic reviews, with or without meta-analysis, that evaluated surgical decompression for central cord syndrome and compared outcomes by timing of decompression. Included articles were required to be published in English and have full-text availability. Two independent reviewers (DR and AL) screened titles and abstracts in Covidence, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Articles passing initial screening then underwent full-text review to establish the final set of included studies. Minor discrepancies were reconciled with brief discussion, and the finalized list was exported for data collection.

Article Review

Two reviewers (MN and AK), following standardized guidance, independently assessed each manuscript for the nine most severe types of spin, as defined by Yavchitz et al in 2016 17 and previous orthopaedic spin studies.19,21-24 For application of the Yavchitz framework, early decompression was designated as the experimental intervention and delayed decompression as the comparator, as several spin categories are defined relative to the experimental intervention. The direction of each review’s conclusion was recorded separately and was not used to determine the presence of spin. In addition to spin classification, extracted review characteristics included year of publication, publishing journal, presence of funding, adherence to PRISMA guidelines, review type, population and eligibility criteria, definitions of early and delayed decompression, primary outcomes, risk-of-bias assessment, certainty or level of evidence, and the direction and strength of each review’s conclusion. A paraphrased rationale for each spin classification and the location of the relevant statement were also recorded. Methodological quality was graded using A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR-2) criteria on a scale from critically low to high. 20 For overall AMSTAR-2 rating purposes, individual items rated as “partial yes” were treated as “yes.” Upon completion, discrepancies were resolved through discussion by the two reviewers to produce a consensus assessment. Interrater reliability for the presence of any spin was assessed using Cohen’s kappa.

Results

The literature search yielded 167 studies from the four databases, of which 48 were identified as duplicates and removed. Of 119 studies that went to title and abstract screening, 11 underwent full-text review; one was excluded for wrong study design, leaving 10 studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria (Figure 1).

Article Characteristics

Included reviews were published between 2010 and 2025 across eight journals. Six of 10 (60%) reported receiving funding, and eight of 10 (80%) reported adherence to PRISMA guidelines. Additional article-level characteristics, including review type, population and eligibility criteria, timing definitions, primary outcomes, risk-of-bias and certainty assessments, conclusion direction and strength, spin classifications, and AMSTAR-2 ratings, are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Spin Bias

Spin bias was present in four of 10 reviews (40%), with 0 to 4 spin types per article (Figure 2). Type 1 spin, defined as clinical recommendations not supported by the review findings, was most common and was present in 4/10 (40%); all spin-positive reviews contained type 1. Spin types 3 and 9 were each present in 2/10 (20%) reviews, and types 5, 7, and 8 were each present in 1/10 (10%) reviews; types 2, 4, and 6 were not observed. Definitions of spin types are provided in Table 1, and article-specific classification rationales and locations of relevant statements are provided in Supplemental Table S1. Overall, six (60%) reviews favored early surgical decompression, three (30%) had mixed or subgroup-dependent conclusions, and one (10%) reported no timing benefit. Conclusion direction was recorded descriptively and was not itself considered evidence of spin. Interrater reliability for identification of any spin was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic with substantial agreement (κ = 0.783) using the Landis and Koch interpretation bands. 25

AMSTAR-2 Rating

Methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR-2. Nine of 10 reviews (90%) were rated critically low and one (10%) was rated low; none were rated moderate or high. All reviews met the criterion for reporting potential sources of conflict of interest. Conversely, none reported the sources of funding for their included studies or provided a list of excluded studies with justification for exclusion. Failure to provide a justified list of excluded studies represents a critical AMSTAR-2 flaw and contributed to the uniformly low methodological ratings across the included reviews. Most other AMSTAR-2 criteria were satisfied by the majority of reviews (Supplemental Table S2).

Discussion

In this evaluation of systematic reviews and meta-analyses comparing early versus late surgical decompression of central cord syndrome, we found that spin bias was present in 40% (4/10) of included articles. Among reviews exhibiting spin, the number of severe spin types ranged from 2 to 4. Type 1 spin, defined by conclusions containing recommendations for clinical practice not supported by the study’s findings, was present in all articles with at least one type of spin. Six reviews favored early decompression, three had mixed or subgroup-dependent conclusions, and one reported no timing benefit. Methodological quality was limited across the included literature, with nine reviews rated critically low and one rated low by AMSTAR-2. Taken together, these findings identify instances in which the strength or framing of review conclusions exceeded the supporting evidence; however, they do not determine the comparative efficacy of early versus delayed decompression.

The marked prevalence of spin bias (40%) observed in the included systematic reviews is consistent with prior work highlighting significant rates of spin across orthopaedic literature. In particular, previous studies investigating spin in orthopaedic spine systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported notable rates of spin, including reviews comparing decompression alone versus decompression with fusion for lumbar stenosis with spondylolisthesis (46.2%) and reviews comparing laminoplasty versus laminectomy with fusion for degenerative cervical myelopathy (64%).19,21 Beyond spine research, spin has also been reported across various orthopaedic subdomains, including orthopaedic trauma randomized controlled trial abstracts, as well as systematic review/meta-analysis abstracts addressing proximal humerus fracture and rotator cuff treatment.18,26,27

Notably, our study found a predominance of type 1 spin, observed in all reviews containing spin. This is especially relevant in the context of the currently available literature for surgical timing in CCS, where primary studies vary substantially in patient selection, threshold of “early” timing, and outcome measurement. Type 1 spin was particularly important because it concerns the transition from evidence synthesis to an actionable clinical recommendation. For example, in the conclusions of one systematic review, the authors present clinical recommendations to “operate <2 weeks after injury” and state that “surgery for traumatic CCS <24 hours after injury appears safe and effective” while acknowledging the “low level of evidence.” 28 They characterized much of the evidence regarding decompression within 24 hours as low or insufficient, yet still issued clinical recommendations favoring earlier surgery. Moreover, for one of its formal recommendations, only one retrospective study was used as the basis. The same review met criteria for type 5 spin because most included therapeutic studies were moderate- or poor-quality level III evidence and several prognostic studies carried a moderately high risk of bias. 28 Type 1 should not, however, be assumed to have the same clinical implications as every other spin category. Whereas type 1 directly increases the actionability of a conclusion, other categories may primarily affect the emphasis placed on efficacy, certainty, or limitations of the underlying evidence.

Types 3 and 9, which concern selective emphasis on favorable efficacy findings and claims of benefit despite reporting limitations, were each present in two reviews. One study rated the seven-study evidence comparing early and late surgery as low certainty, with serious concerns regarding risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision, while the resulting position statement characterized the evidence as supporting better outcomes with surgery within 24 hours for selected patients with structural injury. 29 Similarly, another review emphasized early decompression as safe and potentially beneficial and issued strong recommendations for early surgery in SCI and cervical SCI despite acknowledging substantial variability in CCS-specific surgical timing, pathology-dependent outcomes, and potential response bias in the accompanying surgeon survey. Notably, the authors themselves identified CCS as a possible exception to very early decompression. 30 These examples illustrate that spin may arise from different forms of discordance between the underlying evidence and the strength or emphasis of the resulting conclusion. The present study did not assess whether these different spin types have equivalent effects on physician interpretation or clinical behavior.

Methodological quality was uniformly limited in the included review literature. Across included studies, nine of 10 reviews were rated critically low and one was rated low; none earned a moderate or high AMSTAR-2 rating. The AMSTAR-2 criteria are designed to evaluate confidence in the results of systematic reviews by assessing critical and noncritical methodological weaknesses; “high” indicates zero or one noncritical weakness, whereas “critically low” indicates multiple critical flaws that seriously limit confidence in the review’s conclusions. 20 Recurrent deficiencies included failure to provide a justified list of excluded studies and failure to report the funding sources of included primary studies. Because there was almost no variability in overall AMSTAR-2 classification, these data do not permit meaningful assessment of a relationship between methodological quality and spin. Rather, the findings indicate that important methodological limitations were common across the review literature regardless of spin classification.

The direction of review conclusions should be interpreted separately from the presence of spin. Although six of 10 reviews favored early decompression, three were mixed or subgroup-dependent and one reported no timing benefit. This distribution may reflect the available clinical evidence, differences in patient populations, biological rationale, operative indications, or other characteristics of the literature; the present analysis was not designed to determine why reviews reached a particular treatment direction. Furthermore, several Yavchitz categories are defined relative to the benefit or safety of an experimental intervention. Because early decompression was designated as the experimental intervention in this study, the framework may be more sensitive to overstatement favoring early surgery than to analogous overstatement favoring delayed surgery. Accordingly, the predominance of early-favoring conclusions should not itself be interpreted as spin or as evidence of systematic directional bias.

These distinctions are particularly important when applying conclusions from CCS timing reviews to clinical practice. Decisions regarding decompression timing in CCS are multifactorial and may depend on neurologic status and trajectory, ongoing spinal cord compression, instability, associated injuries, medical readiness for surgery, and available resources. Systematic reviews therefore represent only one component of clinical decision-making. The relevance of spin in this setting is not whether a review favors early or delayed surgery, but whether the certainty and strength of its recommendations remain proportional to the evidence presented. The present findings do not establish that early decompression is ineffective, that spin accounts for the reviews favoring early surgery, or that removal of spin would change the direction of any review’s conclusion.

The article-level assessment also highlights two practical priorities for future reviews of CCS surgical timing. First, several reviews articulated timing thresholds in their conclusions that were narrower or more definitive than those used in the underlying primary studies despite substantial variability in how “early” surgery was defined across the literature. Timing thresholds used in recommendations should remain consistent with those evaluated in the underlying primary studies or should be explicitly justified when a narrower threshold is proposed. Second, recommendations were sometimes presented without parallel discussion of risk of bias, imprecision, or certainty of evidence, potentially inflating confidence in conclusions drawn from predominantly observational data. Greater consistency in timing definitions and closer alignment between evidence certainty and recommendation strength would improve the interpretability of future reviews. Attention to these patterns may help clinicians better contextualize conclusions and avoid extending CCS surgical timing recommendations beyond the limits of the evidence.

This study has several limitations. Most notably, the small number of eligible reviews (n=10) limits the precision and generalizability of the observed prevalence and distribution of spin; these findings should therefore be interpreted descriptively within the available CCS review literature. Second, as previously discussed, there is heterogeneity in the definition of “early surgical decompression” across reviews and their underlying studies. Most studies used a threshold near 24 hours from the injury as early surgery, but other studies had broader or alternative definitions. Third, in our analysis of spin bias, we only reported on the nine most severe types of spin bias described by Yavchitz et al 17 ; other forms of spin were not assessed, and classification retains an element of reviewer judgment. Although interrater agreement was substantial (κ = 0.783), agreement reflects reproducibility between raters rather than the validity of the classifications themselves. In addition, several Yavchitz categories are directionally framed around the experimental intervention; because early decompression was designated as the experimental intervention, the framework may identify overstatement favoring early surgery more readily than comparable overstatement favoring delayed surgery. The evaluated spin types also differ in their potential clinical consequences, and this study did not measure whether spin influenced physician interpretation or treatment decisions. Finally, this meta-research study did not evaluate the comparative efficacy of early versus delayed decompression and cannot determine whether removal of spin would alter the conclusions of individual reviews or whether early decompression is clinically beneficial.

Conclusion

In this meta-research study of 10 systematic reviews, four (40%) met criteria for at least one severe type of spin, most commonly clinical recommendations judged to extend beyond the supporting findings. Methodological quality was limited, with nine reviews rated critically low and one rated low by AMSTAR-2. These findings identify potential mismatches between the strength of some review conclusions and the evidence presented but do not establish that early decompression is ineffective or that spin accounts for the direction of the literature. Future reviews should clearly define surgical timing, report risk of bias and certainty alongside their conclusions, and ensure that recommendation strength remains proportionate to the underlying evidence.
