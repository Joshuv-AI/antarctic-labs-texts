Assessment of the 2D:4D Digit Ratio in Women With Fibromyalgia: Traces of Prenatal Hormonal Exposure
Authors: Emel Sabaz Karakeci, Ramazan Fazil Akkoc, Ayse Bicer Alioglu, Feyza Aksu, Ahmet Karatas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesFibromyalgia is a chronic pain syndrome predominantly affecting women, characterized by widespread pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and cognitive impairment, yet lacks objective diagnostic biomarkers. The 2D:4D digit ratio is considered an indirect marker of prenatal sex hormone exposure, with lower values reflecting greater fetal androgen influence. This study aimed to evaluate the 2D:4D digit ratio in women with fibromyalgia and assess its potential as a noninvasive biomarker.MethodsFifty‐seven female fibromyalgia patients (2016 ACR criteria) and 32 age‐matched healthy female controls were enrolled. Index and ring finger lengths were measured bilaterally using digital calipers, and group comparisons were performed with Welch's t‐test and Mann–Whitney U test.ResultsMean age was comparable between groups (p = 0.685). Both the right 2D:4D ratio (0.991 ± 0.031 vs. 1.022 ± 0.016; p < 0.001; Cohen's d = −1.18) and the left 2D:4D ratio (0.999 [IQR 0.975–1.023] vs. 1.021 [IQR 1.013–1.034]; p < 0.001; Cohen's d = −0.98) were significantly lower in patients.ConclusionWomen with fibromyalgia exhibit significantly lower 2D:4D digit ratios in both hands compared to healthy controls, with the right hand demonstrating greater discriminative power. These findings suggest an association between digit ratios and fibromyalgia, pointing to potential observational links with prenatal hormonal proxy markers that warrant further mechanistic investigation. The right‐hand 2D:4D ratio is simple, painless, and inexpensive to measure, and might therefore be worth exploring as a potential supportive parameter in future research. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings across different populations.

Introduction

When we look carefully at the human hand, we notice that the difference in length between the index finger (the second digit, 2D) and the ring finger (the fourth digit, 4D) varies from person to person. The ratio of these two fingers to one another that is, the 2D:4D ratio may at first glance appear to be a trivial anatomical detail, yet it is in fact a marker thought to carry a silent trace of the sex hormones to which one was exposed in the womb (Jantz 2022; Manning et al. 2025). According to the general consensus, this ratio reflects the balance of androgens and estrogens during the fetal period: the lower the ratio, the more dominant the influence of prenatal testosterone is presumed to be, whereas higher values are suggested to indicate an environment in which estrogen prevailed (Eichler et al. 2023; Manning and Fink 2023). One of the most striking observations supporting this interpretation is that men generally have a lower 2D:4D ratio than women (Koç 2026). Moreover, obtaining this marker does not require a complex laboratory examination; the fact that it can be recorded painlessly and at low cost through a simple digital measurement has made it a practical and appealing tool for researchers (Akyıldız et al. 2024).

The idea that the hormonal environment in the womb may not remain confined to that period alone but may instead lay the groundwork for certain diseases later in life has led to the investigation of the 2D:4D ratio in relation to many different conditions. In particular, autoimmune diseases, which are seen far more frequently in women than in men, have yielded noteworthy findings in this regard. For example, the fact that women with primary Sjögren's syndrome exhibit a markedly lower 2D:4D ratio than healthy individuals has suggested that the influence of androgens during the fetal period may be stronger in these patients (Gür et al. 2026). A similar picture has been observed in women with systemic sclerosis, where low finger ratios have again been associated with increased prenatal androgen exposure (Akkoc et al. 2026). Interestingly, in individuals with hypertension the opposite direction has been found—namely, a higher 2D:4D ratio compared with healthy individuals—and this has been interpreted as an early harbinger of cardiovascular risk (Servi et al. 2025). The trace of the finger ratio is not limited to these diseases alone; it has also been investigated in many conditions of both musculoskeletal and hormonal origin, such as carpal tunnel syndrome (Serin and Emül 2025), joint hypermobility (Zorlular and Zorlular 2026), and polycystic ovary syndrome (Güner Emül and Kaplan Serin 2026).

Among all these diseases, fibromyalgia occupies a distinct place with its own particular character. Manifesting itself through widespread and persistent pain, unrelenting fatigue, disturbed sleep, and mental fogginess, this complex chronic pain syndrome profoundly affects patients' lives (Benjamin et al. 2025). Perhaps its most striking feature is that it predominantly affects women. However, the diagnosis of fibromyalgia today still rests largely on the symptoms reported by the patient; the absence of an objective laboratory indicator to confirm it often leads to delayed diagnosis or even to the condition never being diagnosed at all (García‐Domínguez 2026). It is precisely this gap that has made the search for reliable and readily accessible biomarkers for fibromyalgia an increasingly urgent need (García‐Domínguez 2026).

The fact that fibromyalgia is seen so frequently in women, together with its affinity to other female‐predominant hand disorders such as carpal tunnel syndrome, raises the possibility that sex hormones may play a role in the development of the disease. The 2D:4D ratio, which carries the trace of the prenatal hormonal environment, has been associated with many autoimmune and musculoskeletal diseases that are concentrated in women (Akkoc et al. 2026; Gür et al. 2026), suggesting that this marker also deserves to be examined with respect to fibromyalgia. This study aims to measure the 2D:4D ratio in both hands of patients diagnosed with fibromyalgia, to compare these values with those of healthy individuals, and thereby to investigate whether a simple finger measurement can offer a meaningful, noninvasive clue for fibromyalgia.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Fırat University Non‐Interventional Research Ethics Committee (session number: 2025/16‐07; session date: 06.11.2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in both the patient and control groups. All stages of the research were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Population

This study included 57 female patients aged 18 years or older who were randomly recruited between January 2026 and May 2026 following diagnosis with fibromyalgia according to the 2016 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) diagnostic criteria at the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation clinic of Fethi Sekin City Hospital.

The control group included 32 age‐matched female volunteers aged 18 years or older who had no known chronic disease. Women with a diagnosis of fibromyalgia, chronic widespread pain, or persistent pain lasting longer than 3 months were not included. Those with rheumatic or inflammatory diseases, thyroid or other endocrine disorders, neurological conditions that could affect pain perception, or a history of depression or anxiety requiring treatment were also excluded.

In addition, volunteers with a history of hand trauma, fracture, surgery, congenital hand abnormalities, visible hand deformities, or conditions affecting the hands that could interfere with finger‐length measurements were excluded. Pregnant women were not included. Only volunteers who met these criteria were enrolled as controls.

Digit Length Measurements

Digit length measurements were performed exclusively by a single researcher who was kept blinded to patient and control group information throughout all measurements. To ensure intra‐observer consistency and measurement repeatability, all parameters were measured repeatedly using digital calipers with a precision of 0.01 mm (Servi et al. 2025; Akkoc et al. 2026), and the average value was recorded. During the measurements, both hands of each participant were placed palm‐side up on a flat, hard surface, with care taken to ensure that the dorsal surface of the hand was in a taut and flat position. The distance between the proximal crease on the palmar surface and the fingertip was measured for the second (index) and fourth (ring) fingers. The 2D:4D ratio was calculated by dividing the length of the 2D by the length of the 4D.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was determined prior to the study using an a priori power analysis performed with G*Power version 3.1 software. The analysis was based on the comparison of mean 2D:4D digit ratios between two independent groups. A two‐tailed hypothesis, a Type I error rate (α) of 0.05, a power (1 − β) of 0.90, and a large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.80) were assumed. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 27 participants per group. The sample included in this study, consisting of a total of 89 participants (57 fibromyalgia patients and 32 healthy controls), met the determined minimum requirement and provided sufficient statistical power to detect between‐group differences. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, supplemented by median and interquartile range where appropriate, while categorical variables (e.g., menopausal status) were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test. Due to unequal variances between groups, Welch's t‐test was employed for between‐group comparisons, with Mann–Whitney U tests performed as nonparametric verification. For subgroup evaluations within the patient cohort (e.g., comparing medication users vs. nonusers), standard independent‐samples t‐tests were utilized as appropriate. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to evaluate the discriminative ability of digit ratios, and areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 89 participants were enrolled, comprising 57 patients and 32 healthy controls. The two groups were comparable in terms of age (43.11 ± 8.39 vs. 42.28 ± 10.43 years; p = 0.685), and there was no statistically significant difference in menopausal status between the patient group (29.8% post‐menopausal, n = 17/57) and the control group (21.9% post‐menopausal, n = 7/32) (p = 0.466). No statistically significant difference was detected for the lengths of the right second finger, right fourth finger, or left second finger (all p > 0.10). The left fourth finger was modestly longer in patients than in controls (67.66 ± 4.23 vs. 65.82 ± 3.47 mm; p = 0.039). Distributions of the study variables were inspected using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The left 2D:4D ratio deviated from normality in both groups (p = 0.010 and p = 0.005, respectively) and was therefore analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas the remaining variables were compared using the independent‐samples t‐test. Levene's test indicated unequal variances for the right 2D:4D ratio (p < 0.05), and Welch's correction was applied accordingly.

Both the right and the left 2D:4D ratios were significantly lower in patients than in controls. The right 2D:4D ratio was 0.991 ± 0.031 in the patient group and 1.022 ± 0.016 in controls (Welch t = −6.36, p < 0.001), corresponding to a large effect size (Cohen's d = −1.18). The left 2D:4D ratio showed a comparable pattern, with a median of 0.999 [IQR 0.975–1.023] in patients versus 1.021 [IQR 1.013–1.034] in controls (Mann–Whitney U = 466, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = −0.98). Furthermore, a subgroup analysis within the fibromyalgia cohort revealed no statistically significant differences between patients receiving FM‐related medications (n = 30) and nonusers (n = 27) regarding right 2D:4D ratios (0.986 ± 0.033 vs. 0.996 ± 0.030, respectively; p = 0.252) or left 2D:4D ratios (0.996 ± 0.031 vs. 1.002 ± 0.030, respectively; p = 0.482).

The diagnostic performance of the 2D:4D ratio for discriminating patients from controls was further assessed by ROC curve analysis (Figure 2; Table 2). The right 2D:4D ratio yielded an AUC of 0.812 (95% CI: 0.724–0.894), with an optimal Youden‐derived cut‐off of ≤ 1.000 providing a sensitivity of 64.9%, a specificity of 100.0%, a positive predictive value of 100%, a negative predictive value of 61.5%, and an overall accuracy of 77.5%. The left 2D:4D ratio performed slightly less well, with an AUC of 0.745 (95% CI: 0.637–0.846) at a cut‐off of ≤ 0.995, corresponding to a sensitivity of 59.6%, a specificity of 100.0%, a positive predictive value of 100%, a negative predictive value of 58.2%, and an accuracy of 74.2%. These findings suggest that a lower 2D:4D ratio—particularly on the right hand—may be associated with the disease status.

Discussion

In this study, the 2D:4D digit ratio in both hands of women diagnosed with fibromyalgia was found to be significantly lower than that of age‐matched healthy women. The effect size calculated for the right hand (Cohen's d = −1.18) may be considered large in clinical terms, and the AUC value obtained from ROC analysis reached 0.812, providing high accuracy and 100% specificity in distinguishing patients from controls.

These findings point to a statistically significant observational association between lower 2D:4D digit ratios and fibromyalgia in women. While lower ratios are widely discussed in relation to intrauterine androgen exposure (Eichler et al. 2023; Manning and Fink 2023), our case–control study design demonstrates an observational link rather than a direct causal relationship or underlying biological mechanism.

Our findings are consistent with the pattern of low 2D:4D reported in female‐predominant autoimmune and hormonally driven diseases. In women with primary Sjögren's syndrome, significantly lower 2D:4D ratios were detected in both hands compared with healthy individuals, and it was shown that this reduction stemmed not from a shortened index finger but from an elongated ring finger—a picture consistent with dominant prenatal androgen influence (Gür et al. 2026). A similar finding was obtained in women with systemic sclerosis, where low digit ratios accompanied by large effect sizes (Cohen's d > 2.8) were likewise associated with increased fetal androgen exposure (Akkoc et al. 2026). The common ground shared by these two studies partially overlaps with our observations in the fibromyalgia patient group: whereas in Sjögren's syndrome and systemic sclerosis the ring finger elongation was evident in both hands, in our fibromyalgia cohort this pattern was confined to the left hand, with only the left ring finger being significantly elongated. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism appears to converge across all three conditions—the reduction in digit ratio arises from ring finger elongation rather than index finger shortening, preserving the characteristic trace of prenatal androgenization.

The most recent systematic review and meta‐analysis addressing the relationship between autoimmune diseases and 2D:4D evaluated 20 studies encompassing a total of 6958 patients diagnosed with osteoarthritis, multiple sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, Graves' disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis. It was demonstrated that the overall 2D:4D ratio in the autoimmune disease group was significantly lower than in healthy individuals and that the prevalence of the Type 3 digit pattern (index finger shorter than ring finger) was markedly elevated (Ma et al. 2026). Although fibromyalgia is distinct from classical autoimmune diseases, it shares overlapping biological features, including central sensitization, low‐grade neuroinflammation, and altered immune‐endocrine signaling (Findeisen et al. 2025; Sedda et al. 2025). Therefore, referencing meta‐analytic findings from autoimmune populations (Ma et al. 2026) serves not to imply an autoimmune etiology for fibromyalgia, but to highlight a parallel tendency of low 2D:4D ratios across conditions characterized by systemic immune and neuro‐inflammatory dysregulation. These comparisons should be viewed purely as associative parallels rather than evidence of a shared autoimmune mechanism.

Noteworthy findings also exist in the context of musculoskeletal and connective tissue diseases. The systematic review examining the relationship between carpal tunnel syndrome and 2D:4D demonstrated that a low digit ratio in women is associated with the risk of carpal tunnel syndrome and that this marker may carry value as a potential biomarker (Serin and Emül 2025). A case–control study focused directly on carpal tunnel syndrome showed that intrauterine sex hormone exposure is associated with the severity of this disease in later life (Akyıldız et al. 2024). These findings carry particular significance with respect to the overlapping epidemiological ground between fibromyalgia and carpal tunnel syndrome, given that the prevalence of carpal tunnel syndrome in fibromyalgia patients has been reported to be approximately three times higher than in healthy controls (Sarmer et al. 2002). In the context of joint hypermobility as well, a significant association was identified between low 2D:4D and hypermobility and attributed to the influence of prenatal androgens on skeletal development (Zorlular and Zorlular 2026); this finding also carries contextual importance, given that connective tissue hypersensitivity is frequently raised in fibromyalgia.

It must be emphasized, however, that the 2D:4D ratio does not show a deviation in the same direction across all diseases. In individuals with hypertension, for instance, the opposite direction was found: values higher than those of healthy controls, and this was interpreted as a link between a low prenatal testosterone/high prenatal estrogen environment and cardiovascular risk (Servi et al. 2025). Similarly, a negative association between the left‐hand 2D:4D ratio and fracture risk in postmenopausal osteoporosis has been reported (Uzeli et al. 2026). The systematic review addressing polycystic ovary syndrome reports that digit ratio differences are observed in this condition, but that the results show inconsistency depending on the hand and measurement method (Güner Emül and Kaplan Serin 2026). All these contrasts and variations demonstrate that the 2D:4D ratio can reflect hormonal traces specific to different disease processes, while also serving as a reminder that interpretations must always be evaluated in a disease‐specific manner.

The fact that the right‐hand digit ratio exhibits stronger discriminative power compared with the left (AUC 0.812 vs. 0.745) is an important observation consistent with the literature. The meta‐analysis by Hönekopp and Watson (2010) demonstrated that the sex difference in 2D:4D is more pronounced in the right hand than the left and that the right‐hand measurement may be a more reliable indicator of prenatal androgenization. This finding also converges with observations in cardiometabolic risk research suggesting that right‐hand measurements carry stronger predictive value (Elvan et al. 2025). On the other hand, the significant difference observed in both the right and left hands suggests that prenatal hormonal influence leaves a systematic and bilateral trace reflecting a general hormonal pattern associated with the disease rather than one exclusive to lateral asymmetry.

When evaluating digit ratio findings, it is unavoidable to consider the fundamental methodological and conceptual debates in this field. The question of whether 2D:4D is a valid biomarker of prenatal androgen exposure remains unanswered. A replication study that attempted to verify the relationship between second‐trimester amniotic testosterone and digit ratio failed to demonstrate this association; correlation directions were found to be inconsistent, and it was noted that the available evidence calls into question the premise that second‐trimester sex hormones influence digit ratio development (Richards et al. 2021). The largest GWAS study conducted in 2018 was unable to strongly support the hypothesis that 2D:4D is a direct biomarker of prenatal androgens; while not definitively excluding this possibility, it revealed that the biological mechanisms shaping 2D:4D variation have yet to be fully elucidated (Warrington et al. 2018). Methodological studies synthesizing conflicting findings on the validity of 2D:4D have drawn attention to the fact that measurement technique (soft tissue distortion, indirect measurement, etc.), ethnicity, and genetic background play a determining role in this debate (Lupu et al. 2023). The systematic review compiling cancer research also emphasized that 2D:4D–disease associations follow an inconsistent course depending on topology and laterality, and that this marker should therefore be treated as an associative clue rather than a causal instrument (Fonseca et al. 2022). In the meta‐analysis covering psychiatric disorders, lower 2D:4D ratios were generally found in psychiatric patients, but it was specifically noted that the differences were heterogeneously distributed and that caution was warranted in interpreting the findings (Fusar‐Poli et al. 2021).

Despite these limitations, the high specificity and positive predictive value observed for the right‐hand 2D:4D ratio are worth noting, even though these values likely reflect the case–control design and require confirmation in future studies. The moderate sensitivity (64.9%) is an important limitation, and clearly precludes its use as a stand‐alone screening test. That said, fibromyalgia remains a diagnosis without objective laboratory confirmation (Benjamin et al. 2025; García‐Domínguez 2026), and in this context, a simple, painless, and inexpensive measure like the 2D:4D ratio might still have a modest complementary role when considered alongside other clinical findings—though this possibility would need to be tested more rigorously before any practical recommendation could be made. It is also worth mentioning, as Jeevanandam and Muthu (2016) pointed out, that 2D:4D appears to remain relatively stable throughout adulthood regardless of current hormone levels; if replicated consistently, this property could make it a potentially interesting retrospective marker, distinct in nature from conventional blood‐based tests.

Several methodological limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size imbalance between patients (n = 57) and healthy controls (n = 32), alongside the single‐center design, represents a notable constraint. The relatively small control cohort renders high ROC metrics—specifically the 100% specificity and 100% positive predictive value—statistically fragile, likely reflecting the controlled case–control setting rather than real‐world diagnostic performance. Second, hand dominance was not prospectively recorded using standardized inventories. Although right‐hand 2D:4D is reported in the literature as a more sensitive proxy for prenatal androgen exposure, the absence of formal handedness testing represents a methodological covariate. Third, detailed disease duration and quantitative symptom severity scores (such as the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire) were not prospectively collected, limiting the ability to investigate potential symptom‐severity correlations. Fourth, although our subgroup analysis demonstrated no significant differences in 2D:4D ratios between patients receiving FM‐related medications (n = 30) and nonusers (n = 27) (p > 0.05 for both hands), and menopausal status was comparable between groups (p = 0.466), direct serum sex‐hormone levels were not assayed. Fifth, body mass index (BMI) was not prospectively recorded for the entire cohort; however, a sub‐analysis on an available subset (n = 17 patients, n = 22 controls) revealed no significant group difference (27.02 ± 2.88 vs. 25.45 ± 3.33 kg/m2, p = 0.131), suggesting BMI was unlikely to be a major confounder in this sample. Finally, as our study included only female participants and ethnicity influences digit ratios (Lupu et al. 2023), these findings warrant independent validation in larger, multi‐center, and ethnically diverse populations incorporating longitudinal follow‐up and direct prenatal hormonal evaluations.

Among the strengths of the study, the blinded measurement protocol performed by a single researcher using digital calipers with no knowledge of group allocation may be counted; this approach provides a methodological advantage that reduces measurement error and potential bias (Servi et al. 2025; Akkoc et al. 2026; Hönekopp and Watson 2010).

In conclusion, the lower 2D:4D ratios observed in fibromyalgia patients—most notably in the right hand—add a modest piece of observational evidence to the broader literature regarding digit ratios in female‐predominant conditions. This finding is consistent with patterns reported in primary Sjögren's syndrome (Gür et al. 2026), systemic sclerosis (Akkoc et al. 2026), and carpal tunnel syndrome (Serin and Emül 2025; Akyıldız et al. 2024). However, in light of ongoing debates regarding the biological validity of 2D:4D (Richards et al. 2021; Warrington et al. 2018; Lupu et al. 2023), these findings must be interpreted strictly at an associative level rather than a causal one, serving as an observational clue that warrants confirmation in future mechanistically oriented studies.
