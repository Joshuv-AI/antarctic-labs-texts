Correcting the record on “Publication integrity and industry influences on the editorial process: a case study”
Authors: James P Rathmell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2024, Anesthesiology published a systematic review and meta-analysis of liposomal bupivacaine together with four editorials presenting both strong support for its conclusions and critical perspectives, with the editorial authors’ industry affiliations and financial interests disclosed. A subsequent case study in Research Integrity and Peer Review characterized this presentation as a “retraction in situ” and questioned whether organizational and industry relationships compromised editorial independence. This Correspondence presents additional facts relevant to evaluating those conclusions. The research article remained intact. The coordinated presentation was commissioned by the Editor-in-Chief, while a senior editor serving as acting Editor-in-Chief independently managed peer review and made the publication decisions for all four editorials. Before the case study was published, its authors had submitted to Anesthesiology a manuscript advancing a similar critique. That manuscript underwent multiple rounds of external assessment, senior editorial-board review, and discussion by the full editorial board; its rejection was not unilateral. During my tenure, neither the American Society of Anesthesiologists nor Pacira BioSciences participated in or influenced the 2024 publication decisions for the research article or accompanying editorials, or the disposition of the authors’ later submission. Greater procedural clarity could have been achieved through timelier publication, fuller disclosure of organizational relationships, and complete recusal of the Editor-in-Chief from consideration of the later submission. These are legitimate procedural lessons, but they do not establish external influence or editorial misconduct. The publication record instead reflects the transparent presentation of scientific disagreement, not a disguised retraction.Keywords: Editorial independence, Peer review, Publication ethics, Competing interests, Scientific disagreement, Editorial decision-making, Industry influence, Liposomal bupivacaine

To the Editor

In a case study published in Research Integrity and Peer Review, [1] Kharasch and colleagues raise a legitimate question: journals and editors should meet the same standards of transparency, conflict management, and accountability they impose on authors. I agree and welcome scrutiny of my decisions as Editor-in-Chief. The case study, however, omits material facts about the editorial process, conflates the decision to solicit competing viewpoints with the separate decisions to review and accept them, and uses a chronology of publicly known events to insinuate organizational and industry influence without evidence. Serious allegations about editorial integrity require the same distinction between observation and inference that journals demand in scientific reports. The record warrants correction. This response is limited to editorial decisions made during my tenure as Editor-in-Chief and does not address or draw conclusions about interactions between the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) and Anesthesiology before I assumed that role.

What the publication record actually shows

In May 2024, Anesthesiology published the systematic review and meta-analysis by Hussain, Speer, and Abdallah in full [2]. The article concluded that liposomal bupivacaine and plain local anesthetics had similar analgesic effectiveness for abdominal fascial plane blocks and that the evidence did not support a preference for liposomal bupivacaine. The journal did not retract, correct, qualify, or place an expression of concern on the article; it remains part of the scientific literature. Acceptance signified that the work was sufficiently sound and informative to publish, not that the journal or every editor endorsed every method, inference, or interpretation.

The four accompanying editorials were not four attacks on the meta-analysis. My editorial explained why the journal solicited multiple perspectives and stated that peer reviewers had deemed the meta-analysis worthy of publication [3]. Fettiplace strongly supported its conclusion [4]. Habib, Gadsden, and Carvalho accepted the findings but questioned their generalizability across heterogeneous operations and imperfect block techniques [5]. Slonin, DiGiorgi, and Yu, all Pacira BioSciences employees, offered a methodological critique focused on rescue analgesia, heterogeneity, trial quality, and pooling [6]. Together, the package provided a procedural explanation, a strong defense, a clinically focused counterinterpretation, and the manufacturer's critique—not a coordinated effort to discredit the research.

The phrase “retraction in situ” is memorable, but it does not accurately describe what occurred. Retraction formally signals that an article’s findings are unreliable [7]. The Hussain article was published intact; one accompanying editorial strongly endorsed its central conclusion; the critical pieces were clearly labeled as opinion; and readers were invited to assess the competing reasoning. Scientific criticism does not become a disguised retraction merely because it appears at the time of publication.

Solicitation, peer review, and acceptance were distinct decisions

I decided that this unusual controversy warranted a coordinated presentation of competing viewpoints, and I commissioned the editorials. I explained that decision in my contemporaneous editorial [3]. Because I authored one of the pieces, a senior editor served as acting Editor-in-Chief for the package, independently managed all four editorials, and made the publication decisions. I did not adjudicate my own editorial. All four pieces underwent external peer review by the same reviewers, who assessed the package together, considered its scientific content and balance, and recommended acceptance. Their identities remain confidential under ordinary peer-review practice.

Commissioning a viewpoint is not the same as deciding that it is publishable. The manufacturer-authored editorial was not requested by Pacira or ASA; it was openly attributed, externally reviewed with the other perspectives, and accepted by the acting Editor-in-Chief. The identical acceptance dates reflect the planned simultaneous publication of a coordinated package, not evidence of improper intervention. The case study repeatedly blurs these distinct functions and thereby turns a disclosed editorial choice—to present relevant disagreement—into an insinuation of compromised decision-making.

The publicly available peer-review history for the case study published in Research Integrity and Peer Review does not indicate that the decisive process facts—who handled, reviewed, and accepted the editorials—were independently confirmed [8]. Those facts materially affect the ethical inference: the Editor-in-Chief solicited the viewpoints, but a senior editor serving as acting Editor-in-Chief independently managed peer review and made the publication decisions.

Disclosed conflicts are not proof of external influence

Industry employment requires disclosure and management but is not an automatic bar to authorship: major journals publish perspectives by industry scientists when their expertise is relevant and their relationships are transparent [9–11]. The Pacira authors' affiliations appeared on the first page of their editorial; the competing-interest statement disclosed their executive roles, stock ownership, stock options, and Pacira's payment of the open-access charge [6]. Relationships of authors in another editorial were similarly reported [5]. Readers therefore had the information needed to judge the arguments, with external review providing an additional safeguard.

Pacira’s publicly announced $2.5 million grant to the ASA Charitable Foundation and its participation in an ASA industry-supporter program reasonably invite scrutiny [12, 13]. Neither involved a payment to Anesthesiology, its editorial office, or me. I had no personal financial relationship with Pacira. No ASA leader or Pacira representative participated in or influenced the review or acceptance decisions for the meta-analysis or the editorials. In retrospect, my competing-interest statement should have acknowledged ASA's organizational relationships with Pacira, particularly because my editorial discussed Pacira's prior litigation against ASA. That omission created an avoidable appearance problem, but it was not evidence of influence; no such influence occurred.

The case study presents no evidence that ASA influenced editorial decision-making, that Pacira was promised an editorial, that its grant was linked to journal content, or that any editor or reviewer was pressured. Likewise, my statement that the journal would heed the courts' "direction" referred to the courts' reasoning that scientific disputes should be tested through scholarly discourse; it did not claim that a court had ordered the journal to publish four editorials [3, 14]. Publishing the research and competing interpretations honored that principle and protected editorial independence from the chilling effect of prior litigation.

Viewed against this factual record, the case study’s juxtaposition is seriously misleading. It opens with the “classic case” of an editor who reportedly received more than $20 million directly from a device manufacturer and then presents the events at Anesthesiology as another “illustrative case” of journal conflict [1]. Both circumstances warrant disclosure and scrutiny, but they are not ethically analogous. Direct personal compensation of that magnitude, coupled with allegations of favorable product coverage, is fundamentally different from an organizational relationship that provided no personal benefit to the editor and did not influence any editorial decision. Conflating these circumstances obscures distinctions essential to any meaningful analysis of competing interests.

The later submission was not rejected unilaterally

Before publication in Research Integrity and Peer Review, the authors had submitted to Anesthesiology a manuscript advancing a similar critique. The case study’s account of how Anesthesiology evaluated that submission omits material facts [1]. The manuscript received three external reviews, and revisions underwent further assessment. The authors were repeatedly invited to pose specific questions in a focused letter, with assurance of a published response, and were ultimately provided an edited version suitable for resubmission. They did not take that route. Without disclosing confidential review communications, the persistent point of impasse was not whether the journal would publish criticism, but whether allegations of improper motive, organizational influence, and self-interest could be stated without evidentiary support.

Before the final decision, an additional senior editorial-board member reviewed the complete record. I then convened an ad hoc video conference of the full board, explained the chronology and proposed decision, answered questions, and invited contrary views. The board supported the decision not to accept the manuscript. I remained formally involved and signed the decision correspondence. Complete recusal, with another editor serving throughout as handling editor and acting Editor-in-Chief, would have provided cleaner procedural separation and been preferable. It is nevertheless inaccurate to characterize the outcome as a decision I made alone or in my own interest. That procedural shortcoming does not, by itself, establish that the decision was self-protective.

International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidance addresses post publication criticism and author response but does not identify a corresponding right to contemporaneous prepublication rebuttal to invited editorials [15]. Anesthesiology maintained that mechanism and subsequently published a comment on the meta-analysis by Pace and a reply from Hussain and Abdallah [16, 17].

Conclusion

There are fair criticisms of the process. Publication took too long. An explicit disclosure of ASA's organizational relationships with Pacira would have improved transparency. Complete recusal from the later submission criticizing my actions would have avoided an appearance problem. I accept those criticisms as guidance for improving editorial practice. They do not validate the case study's more serious claims.

Editorial independence was the controlling principle throughout. During my tenure, neither ASA nor Pacira participated in, directed, or influenced the handling or disposition of the research article, the accompanying editorials, or the case study authors' later submission. The case study presents no evidence of such influence, and the editorial process described here does not support that inference. The case study instead relies on an incomplete record, conflates separate editorial functions, and converts assumptions about motive into allegations of compromised integrity. Legitimate scrutiny should be welcomed; serious accusations should rest on verified facts.

Despite these procedural shortcomings, the journal published the research, a vigorous defense of its conclusions, and critical perspectives, leaving readers to weigh the evidence and judge the arguments for themselves. That record is more accurately understood as the presentation of scientific disagreement—not a “retraction in situ” or evidence of external influence on editorial decision-making.
