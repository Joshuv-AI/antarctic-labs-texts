Shape-fit 3D printed mineralized membrane for guided bone regeneration in long bone defects
Authors: Wenbin Jiang, Yichen Zhan, Yuxuan Chen, Jiaming Sun, Qiuning Lin, Qiang He, Junpeng Ma, Bingkun Bao, Zhenxing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Segmental large-sized long bone defects remain a significant challenge in clinical practice. The standard treatment, involving autologous or allogeneic graft implantation, is often insufficient due to the limited availability of donor bone. As an alternative therapy for long bone defects, guided bone regeneration (GBR) presents a promising approach, effectively enhancing bone augmentation by preventing bone defects from soft tissue infiltration and facilitating osteoblast migration. This technique employs a biomaterial membrane as a protective barrier on the bone surface, promoting osteogenesis. This study proposed a GBR membrane designed specifically for long bone defects for fabrication. A photocurable hydrogel and light-processing 3D printing, based on 3D-scanned long bone models, were utilized to achieve fabrication. A mineralization-inducing compound was also incorporated into the material to enhance cell adhesion and osteogenesis. The 3D-printed GBR membranes demonstrated precise attachment to the long bone surface, confirming successful fabrication. Introduction

Segmental large-sized long bone defects resulting from trauma, infection, or bone tumors frequently lead to bone non-union in clinical settings [1, 2]. Failed healing can cause deformity and disability, imposing a substantial burden on patients and public health. Several factors contribute to the complexity of bone defect healing. First, the migration of cells from bone marrow to the defect site is insufficient for large-sized defects [3]. Second, osteogenesis of bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) is suppressed within the injured area [4, 5]. Third, soft tissue infiltration obstructs new bone formation [6, 7]. Autologous or allogeneic bone graft implantation remains the standard treatment of long bone defects in clinical practice. However, this approach necessitates surgical extraction of donor bone, and the availability of graft material is limited [3]. Tissue-engineered bone implants have been developed as alternatives to autologous or allogeneic bone grafts. Despite its potential, the fabrication of tissue-engineered bone is time-consuming and expensive [8]. Additionally, infiltration by fibroblasts and other connective tissue cells can compromise the scaffold integrity, inhibiting new bone formation [9, 10].

In clinical practice, guided bone regeneration (GBR) is a highly effective and easily implemented approach for treating alveolar bone loss [11]. This technique involves applying a biomaterial membrane over the bone defect site to prevent soft tissue infiltration and facilitate cell migration [12, 13]. The membrane is a structural guide for new bone formation while inhibiting soft tissue growth [14]. To apply GBR in segmental large-sized long bone defects, a membrane that conforms to the long bone surface and supports cell adhesion is essential.

Traditional materials for GBR, such as polytetrafluoroethylene and collagen, are manufactured into flat sheets through molding, pressing, or electrospinning [15, 16]. However, these flat membranes cannot attach precisely to the surface of bone. In large-sized bone defects, the gap between the membrane and the bone becomes significant, limiting their effectiveness. Additionally, hydrogels have been explored as GBR materials in research. Gelatin and polyacrylamide have been utilized to fabricate flexible GBR membranes [17, 18], while chitosan hydrogel-reinforced graphene oxide has been developed as a bilayer membrane with greater mechanical strength than collagen [19]. Despite these advancements, most hydrogels either lack sufficient mechanical strength or are restricted by processing methods such as molding or solvent evaporation. Moreover, their applications are limited to small-area defects [20]. Materials with superior mechanical properties that can be tailored to customized size and shape for large-sized bone defects present a more effective solution.

A photocurable hydrogel with exceptional mechanical properties has been recently reported [21]. This hydrogel consists of o-nitrobenzyl alcohol (NB)-terminated tetra-armed polyethylene glycol (PEGNB) and highly rigid methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA). The hydrogel crosslinks under ultraviolet (UV) light and is compatible with digital light processing (DLP) 3D printing. Due to the soft-hard microstructure interface formed during polymerization, the hydrogel exhibits high mechanical stability after printing [21]. Based on these advantages, PEGNB/HAMA was selected to fabricate a 3D-printed arch-shaped GBR membrane to conform precisely to a long bone surface.

To enhance osteogenic cell migration and reinforce osteogenesis, PEGNB/HAMA was combined with Zeolite imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), a metal-organic framework compound that induces surface mineralization [22, 23], to modify the hydrogel surface. The incorporation of ZIF-8 modifies surface roughness, wettability, and protein affinity [22]. This modification is anticipated to enhance BMSC adhesion and promote osteogenesis, consistent with previous findings that zinc ions released from ZIF-8 can regulate osteogenesis through BMP2/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, while simultaneously promoting angiogenesis and immune modulation [24–26].

In this study, DLP-printed PEGNB/HAMA and ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA membranes were fabricated for GBR in long bone (Fig. 1). Compared to the collagen membrane, the DLP-printed hydrogel membrane adhered precisely to the rabbit long bone. The precipitation of calcium phosphatase on the ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA surface was confirmed, and its effects on BMSCs were evaluated in vitro. Rabbit radius bone defects were used as experimental models, in which arch-shaped GBR membranes were successfully applied, leading to enhanced bone regeneration. The DLP-printed mineralized hydrogel membrane presents a promising approach for GBR in long bone defects.

Results

DLP printing of hydrogel GBR membrane

Prior to DLP printing, the printability of ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA hydrogels was evaluated. Photographs of precursor solutions with different ZIF-8 concentrations (0%, 2%, 5%, and 10%) demonstrated uniform dispersion of ZIF-8 within the hydrogels (Fig. 2a). Moreover, SEM imaging combined with elemental EDS mapping of Zn was carried out on cross-sections of the printed membranes, clearly revealing a homogeneous spatial distribution of ZIF-8 throughout the PEGNB/HAMA network (Fig. S12).

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) and XRD were employed to confirm the presence of ZIF-8 within the photocured hydrogel. The FT-IR spectrum exhibited characteristic absorption frequencies at 1081 and 1145 cm− 1, along with absorption bands in the ranges of 900–1350 and 1350–1500 cm− 1 (Fig. 2b). Based on a previous study [27], these spectral features correspond to the stretching and planar bending of the imidazole ring in ZIF-8. Additionally, the XRD spectrum of the photocured hydrogels revealed a peak pattern consistent with hydrogels containing ZIF-8 and ZIF-8 powder (Fig. 2c), confirming its incorporation. In addition to confirming ZIF-8 incorporation, FT-IR analysis comparing the uncured precursor and the photocured hydrogel further verified the effectiveness of the photocrosslinking process: the characteristic C = C stretching peaks corresponding to the norbornene groups in PEGNB and the methacrylate groups in HAMA (in the range of 1680–1620 cm-¹) were clearly visible in the uncured precursor but disappeared after UV-induced crosslinking, indicating successful consumption of the reactive double bonds and the formation of a stable polymer network (Fig. S10). Rheology analysis confirmed that the gel points of PEGNB/HAMA and composite hydrogels occurred within 5 s (Fig. 2d), indicating the rapid and efficient one-step photo-polymerization of the hydrogels. Regarding printing resolution, both PEGNB/HAMA and ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA were successfully printed with a precision of 200 μm (Fig. 2e). Circular and acute-angled shapes were successfully printed, demonstrating the high shape fidelity of DLP printing.

The mechanical properties of the printed hydrogels were evaluated, revealing that the strength of 2% and 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA exceeded 0.5 MPa (Fig. 2f), ensuring the stability of the GBR membrane. Previous mechanical studies have shown that collagen membranes such as Bio-Gide® possess tensile strengths on the order of 1 MPa under hydrated conditions, while dense PTFE membranes such as Cytoplast™ display lower tensile values but still meet clinical requirements for barrier function rather than load bearing. In addition, compressive testing was conducted on both the bulk hydrogel cylinders and the DLP-printed membrane constructs to further evaluate their structural robustness. The PEGNB/HAMA hydrogel cylinders exhibited a compressive modulus of approximately 0.24 MPa, whereas the thinner DLP-printed membrane samples showed a compressive modulus of approximately 0.03 MPa, which is expected given their reduced thickness and structural geometry. Cyclic compression over 100 loading-unloading cycles demonstrated that the hydrogel cylinders retained approximately 88% of their initial modulus, while the membrane constructs retained approximately 86%, indicating good elastic recovery and mechanical stability under repeated deformation for both sample types. It should be noted that, unlike load-bearing implants, GBR membranes primarily act as flexible barrier structures that prevent soft-tissue invasion and maintain a protected osteogenic microenvironment, rather than serving as mechanical supports comparable to cortical bone. The mechanical performance of our printed PEGNB/HAMA membranes thus falls well within this clinically accepted range and is sufficient for their intended biological role.

Subsequently, GBR membranes were fabricated based on 3D scanning models of the rabbit radius bone (Fig. 2g). CAD models for DLP printing were designed according to the bone size and diameter determined from the 3D scans (Fig. 2h). Arched GBR membranes measuring 2.5 cm in length with a maximum internal diameter of 5 mm were successfully printed using both PEGNB/HAMA and ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA hydrogels (Fig. 2i, Fig. S13). Compared to a collagen GBR membrane, DLP-printed hydrogel GBR membranes exhibited superior conformity to the rabbit radius bone surface, whereas the collagen membrane displayed significant gaps (Fig. 2j).

Mineralization of the hydrogel surface

ZIF-8 was utilized to induce the mineralization of the hydrogel surface, and the surfaces of PEGNB/HAMA and ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA hydrogels (containing 2%, 5%, or 10% wt ZIF-8) were compared after immersion in simulated body fluid (SBF). Changes in hydrogel surface appearance were observed (Fig. S3). SEM was used to examine the microstructure of the hydrogel surface (Fig. 3a). After SBF immersion, microparticles were detected on the ZIF-8/P/H hydrogel surface, whereas no particles were observed on the surface of the hydrogel without ZIF-8 (PEGNB/HAMA). The density of microparticles increased with higher ZIF-8 concentration. The microstructural difference between hydrogel immersed in deionized water and those in SBF confirmed that the particles resulted from the interaction between ZIF-8 and SBF, providing evidence of the mineralization process [22]. EDS was performed to analyze the elemental composition of the hydrogel after SBF immersion, revealing an increased percentage of calcium and phosphorus on the hydrogel surface in the presence of ZIF-8. (Fig. 3b). ICP-OES was used to quantify the concentrations of calcium and zinc in the solution samples at different time points (Fig. 3c). Additionally, elemental mapping images demonstrated the distribution of calcium and phosphorus (Fig. 3d). Quantitative analysis of the SEM images was further performed using ImageJ to calculate the mineralized area fraction and particle number on the hydrogel surface. The results showed that both mineralized area fraction and particle number increased progressively with higher ZIF-8 concentrations, with the 10% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA group exhibiting the highest values among all groups (Fig. S14; p < 0.0001). The results indicated that ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA facilitated the deposition of calcium phosphate while releasing zinc into the solution. Notably, the concentration of calcium and zinc in the solution exhibited a noticeable plateau after day 4, indicating a slowdown in the release process rather than a complete cessation.

The protein absorption, surface roughness, and hydrophilicity of the hydrogel surfaces were characterized. The absorption of serine protein (Fig. 3e) and fibrinogen (Fig. 3f) on the mineralized hydrogel surface was significantly higher than that on the PEGNB/HAMA surface (P < 0.01), indicating that mineralization enhanced the protein affinity of the hydrogel. AFM was employed to assess the surface roughness (Fig. 3g), revealing an increase in roughness following ZIF-8-induced mineralization. Besides, the static contact angle was measured to evaluate the hydrophilicity of the hydrogel surface (Fig. 3h), indicating that mineralization reduced the hydrophilicity of the hydrogel surface.

BMSC adhesion and viability on the mineralized surface

ZIF-8-induced mineralization was confirmed to enhance protein adsorption, increase surface roughness, and reduce hydrophilicity, properties that are closely associated with cell adhesion on the material surface [25, 26, 28]. Given the critical role of BMSC adhesion and migration in GBR [29], the behavior of BMSCs cultured on the hydrogel surface was examined. Calcein-AM staining demonstrated that ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA effectively supported BMSC proliferation (Fig. 4a). The CCK-8 assay quantitatively confirmed that BMSC viability was significantly increased on 2% and 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA compared to PEGNB/HAMA (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4b). Phalloidin staining of F-actin and DAPI staining of cell nuclei were used to visualize the BMSC morphology (Fig. 4c). BMSCs on 2% and 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA exhibited a well-spread morphology, whereas those on PEGNB/HAMA were round or triangle shape with minimal thin protrusions. Quantitative analysis of actin density was conducted using ImageJ software (Fig. 4d-e) and confirmed that BMSCs exhibited significantly higher actin densities on mineralized hydrogel compared to PEGNB/HAMA, with the highest values observed on 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA (P < 0.01).

Promotion of osteogenesis by extracts of mineralized hydrogels

Extracts of PEGNB/HAMA and ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA were prepared to evaluate the osteogenic potential of BMSCs influenced by hydrogel extracts. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and activity analysis were conducted after 7 days of osteogenic induction, revealing that more BMSCs entered the early osteogenic stage when cultured with ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA extracts (Fig. 5a-b). ARS staining and pigment quantification were conducted after 14 days of induction to evaluate the formation of inorganic bone matrix by osteoblasts (Fig. 5a and c). ARS assays indicated that ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA extracts enhanced the transition of BMSCs into the late osteogenic stage. During BMSC differentiation into osteoblasts, collagen was secreted as the primary organic component of the bone matrix. Collagen secretion was also assessed to evaluate osteogenesis, revealing that the extract of 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA significantly promoted osteogenesis compared to osteogenic medium (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5d). Furthermore, the extent of osteogenesis was analyzed using mRNA and protein markers to determine the optimal ZIF-8 concentration. ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA extracts enhanced osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 5e-f, Fig. S4). Overall, the 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA extract notably increased the expression levels of most osteogenesis-related mRNA and protein markers at days 7 and 14. Consequently, 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA was selected to fabricate GBR membranes for in vivo experiments.

GBR in rabbit radius bone defects

Based on the size and morphology of the rabbit radius bone, a 3D model of the GBR membrane was designed, and GBR membranes were fabricated using hydrogels through DLP printing. The DLP-printed membranes were successfully implanted during surgery between the soft tissue and bone defects (Fig. S5). Bone regeneration was assessed at 12 weeks post-surgery following GBR membrane implantation. X-ray imaging and 3D reconstruction demonstrated substantial bone defect recovery with GBR membranes application, whereas non-union of bone was observed in the bone defect untreated defects (Fig. 6a and S5). CTAn software analyzed BV and BS areas (Fig. 6b). The quantified values of BV, BS, BV/TV, and Tb.Th were ranked as follows: ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA, PEGNB/HAMA, and blank control. Histological analysis was conducted by staining specimen sections with H&E and MT to visualize the tissue structures (Fig. 6f). In the absence of GBR, significant soft tissue infiltration was observed within the bone defects. In contrast, new bone was evident within the bone defect area with GBR membranes. These findings indicated that GBR effectively prevented soft tissue infiltration while promoting bone regeneration, with ZIF-8 mineralized GBR membranes exhibiting enhanced osteogenic effects in the rabbit radius bone. Moreover, H&E staining of kidney, liver, and muscle tissues was performed to evaluate the potential toxicity of the GBR membrane. No signs of inflammation or necrosis were detected in any of the tissue sections (Fig. S7).

IHC staining for bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), osteocalcin (OCN), and runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) was performed to assess bone regeneration. Analysis of stained tissue sections and their quantification demonstrated that ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA GBR membranes notably elevated the expression of BMP2, OCN, and RUNX2 in bone defects (Fig. 7a-d, Fig. S9). Additionally, IHC staining of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a marker of fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and vascular smooth muscle cells, revealed the presence of α-SMA-positive blood vessels in the defect area. In the GBR-treated groups, α-SMA-positive structures were primarily localized along the newly formed bone surface, whereas in the defect sites without GBR, these structures were more dispersed within the defect region (Fig. S8), confirming that GBR effectively prevents soft tissue infiltration and supports bone regeneration. Additionally, IF staining of macrophage markers was conducted to evaluate the effect of GBR on the microenvironment. Pro-inflammatory macrophage (M1 macrophage) exhibited cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86) positivity, while pro-regeneration macrophage (M2 macrophage) was characterized by Cluster of Differentiation 163 (CD163) expression [30, 31]. A lower presence of M1 macrophage and a higher presence of M2 macrophage were observed in bone defects with GBR (Fig. 7e). Furthermore, ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA membrane significantly increased the M2 macrophage ratio while reducing the M1 macrophage ratio (P < 0.01) (Fig. 7f-g). The IF results demonstrated that the mineralized GBR membrane promoted the microenvironment to support regeneration.

Discussion

Currently, research on GBR membranes primarily focuses on enhancing functionality, including antibacterial properties [32, 33], homeostasis maintenance [34], and tissue adherence [18, 35]. Although significant advancements in bone regeneration have been reported, these GBR membranes have predominantly been applied to alveolar bone loss and cranial bone defects. As a clinically validated treatment [11, 36], GBR holds the potential for broader application in large bone defects [12]. For successful implementation in long bone defects, GBR membranes must attach closely to the bone; otherwise, they fail to prevent the infiltration of soft tissue. Accordingly, precision in shape and size is crucial for the effective application of GBR in long bone defect treatment.

It is important to highlight that our design integrates two synergistic advantages that have not been simultaneously achieved in prior PEGNB/HAMA hydrogels or existing 3D-printed GBR membranes. First, the DLP-printed PEGNB/HAMA architecture enables highly shape-conformable, defect-specific membranes capable of matching long-bone curvature with high fidelity-an essential requirement for maintaining stable coverage along extended defect segments. Second, the incorporation of ZIF-8 introduces an in situ mineralization mechanism that not only provides osteoconductive surface cues but also delivers sustained Zn²⁺-mediated immunomodulation, fostering a pro-regenerative osteoimmune microenvironment. Unlike previously reported PEG or HA-based hydrogels, which primarily act as passive physical barriers, and unlike polymeric GBR membranes that rely on external coatings or particulate additives [33, 35], our system couples structural precision with dynamic biochemical activation within a unified, digitally engineered construct. To our knowledge, this synergistic integration has not been previously reported for large-segment GBR applications.

In this study, we employed DLP printing and PEGNB/HAMA hydrogel to fabricate shape-fit GBR membranes. Photocurable hydrogels have been extensively studied in tissue regeneration; however, PEGNB/HAMA offers superior printability and ease of storage and use compared to other photocurable hydrogels. For instance, printing GelMA requires maintaining a temperature of 37 °C to ensure low viscosity [37], whereas PEGNB/HAMA can be printed at room temperature while maintaining excellent flowability. Therefore, layer collapse and shape infidelity are rarely observed during PEGNB/HAMA printing. Furthermore, the addition of ZIF-8 demonstrated that it did not compromise 3D printability, allowing PEGNB/HAMA to be readily modified. This synthetic hydrogel exhibits superior scalability due to its advantageous production, processing, and material properties.

The ability of GBR membranes to guide bone regeneration depends not only on their physical properties but also on their capacity to regulate the surrounding biological microenvironment. Our study demonstrates that the incorporation of ZIF-8 into the PEGNB/HAMA hydrogel enhances osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immune modulation, consistent with previous studies on the role of zinc ions in bone regeneration. The mineralized GBR membrane facilitated the deposition of calcium phosphate on its surface (Fig. 3b-d), promoting BMSC adhesion and proliferation through integrin-mediated signaling pathways [38]. Similar findings have been reported by Ranjbar et al. [39], who showed that bioactive surface coatings significantly improved BMSC attachment and osteogenic differentiation. The increased expression of osteogenic markers, such as BMP2, RUNX2, and OCN (Fig. 7a-d), suggests that the membrane provides a favorable niche for osteoblastic differentiation, potentially via BMP/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin signaling [24–26]. This aligns with the findings of Huang et al. [40], who demonstrated that zinc-containing biomaterials promote osteogenesis through activation of the BMP2 pathway.

Moreover, zinc ions released from ZIF-8 have been reported to support endothelial cell function and neovascularization, which is critical for bone repair [41, 42]. Consistently, our study observed vascular structures in GBR-treated defects (Fig. 6f), indicating that the mineralized membrane may stimulate angiogenesis through VEGF upregulation, which is in agreement with the angiogenic effects of zinc-based biomaterials reported by Yuan et al. [43]. Additionally, our immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the membrane modulates macrophage polarization, increasing the M2/M1 ratio (Fig. 7f-g). This shift toward a pro-regenerative immune environment may be attributed to zinc-mediated inhibition of NF-κB signaling [44]. Our results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that zinc can promote M2 macrophage polarization, thereby enhancing tissue repair [45].

Advancements in 3D printing will further accelerate the clinical translation of GBR membranes. In this study, the size of the 3D printing model was constrained by the capacity of the DLP printer. However, larger membranes are required for application in large animal experiments to facilitate clinical translation. Previous research by Duan et al. [46] has shown that larger GBR membranes significantly improve defect healing in large-animal models, further supporting the necessity of scaling up our current design. Additionally, the rapid prototyping technique offers a viable approach for membrane production. For example, volumetric printing notably reduces 3D printing time to a few minutes [47, 48], enabling immediate fabrication of membranes in medical emergencies. These developments highlight the potential for next-generation GBR membranes to be customized for patient-specific applications, improving the efficacy of bone regeneration strategies.

In comparison with widely used commercial GBR membranes such as Bio-Gide® (collagen) and Cytoplast™ (PTFE), as well as PCL-based synthetic membranes, our DLP-printed PEGNB/HAMA-ZIF-8 constructs offer several distinct advantages. Collagen membranes, although clinically established, often lack geometric stability and show limited conformability to the curved long-bone surface [49], while PTFE membranes are non-resorbable and provide minimal biological activity. By contrast, our shape-fitting hydrogel membrane combines precise anatomical conformity, tunable mechanics appropriate for non-load-bearing GBR applications, and ZIF-8-induced mineralization that simultaneously enhances osteogenesis and immunomodulation. From a translational standpoint, the fabrication workflow is compatible with scalable, GMP-oriented production: DLP printing enables reproducible, patient-specific geometries derived directly from medical imaging, and ZIF-8 incorporation can be controlled with high batch-to-batch consistency.

It is also noteworthy that GBR membranes are already widely used in dental and maxillofacial practice, and recent clinical investigations have explored composite and hydrogel-based membranes with enhanced bioactivity, reflecting increasing clinical interest in next-generation GBR materials [50]. Although our ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA system has not yet undergone clinical testing, its core components-including PEG and HA-based hydrogels as well as zinc-releasing biomaterials-have demonstrated favorable safety profiles in preclinical studies, supporting its translational promise. Going forward, direct comparison with clinically used collagen membranes will further strengthen the clinical relevance of this platform. Moreover, because synchronizing membrane degradation with the rate of new bone formation is crucial for long-term success, comprehensive in vivo degradation studies will be an important focus of our future work.

Conclusion

The 3D printability of the composite hydrogel ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA was validated, demonstrating the feasibility of using DLP 3D printing to fabricate hydrogel GBR membranes. 3D scanning and 3D printing provide an effective solution for producing customized membranes for long bone defects. The incorporation of ZIF-8, along with immersion in simulated body fluid, induced surface mineralization of PEGNB/HAMA hydrogels, enhancing BMSCs adhesion and proliferation. Moreover, ZIF-8 facilitated the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The 3D-printed GBR membrane was successfully applied to rabbit radius defects, effectively preventing soft tissue infiltration, preserving the regenerative microenvironment, and promoting bone tissue repair. Consequently, the combination of photocurable hydrogel and DLP printing presents a promising strategy for GBR in long bone defects.

Methods and materials

Preparation of hydrogel precursor solution

The detailed synthesis protocols and 1H NMR characterization of HAMA and PEGNB are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S1 and Fig. S2). The PEGNB/HAMA hydrogel precursor solution was prepared by gently dissolving lyophilized PEGNB, HAMA, and lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) photoinitiator in deionized water to achieve a homogenous solution. The final composition of the solution consisted of 10% PEGNB (wt), 2% HAMA (wt), and 0.6% LAP (wt). The precursor solution was stored at 4 ℃. The ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA precursor solution was obtained by incorporating ZIF-8 powder into the PEGNB/HAMA precursor hydrogel, followed by vortex mixing. The weight ratio of ZIF-8 was set at 2%, 5%, or 10%, depending on the designated experimental groups. The ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA solution was freshly prepared before use.

DLP-based 3D printing

The 3D printing was performed using a DLP printer (EFL-BP-8600, EFL, China). The 3D models were designed using CAD software and saved as STL files. STL files, which were sliced along the Z-axis. The 3D printing was conducted by projecting patterned UV light onto the precursor solution, curing it layer by layer with a thickness of 50 μm. For the PEGNB/HAMA precursor solution, the UV light intensity was set at 14 mW/cm3, with an exposure time of 55 s. For the ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA precursor solution, the UV light intensity was 15 mW/cm3, with an exposure time of 65s.

Characterization of hydrogels and polymers

Spectroscopy

The UV-Vis absorption spectra of precursor solutions were recorded using a spectrometer (Lambda 35, PerkinElmer, USA). To assess monomer conversion during photocuring, FT-IR spectra of the uncured precursor and the photocured hydrogel were recorded and compared. For Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, samples were photocured under 395 nm UV light for 20 min in a mold with dimensions of 8 mm in width, 16 mm in length, and 1 mm in depth. FT-IR analysis was performed using an FT-IR spectrophotometer (Nicolet iS50R, Thermo Scientific, USA).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) test

XRD analysis was performed using an X’Pert3 Powder diffractometer (PANalytical, Netherlands). The samples were scanned in the 2θ range of 5° to 60° at a scan speed of 2°/min with a step size of 0.02°. The X-ray source was a Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Data were collected in Bragg-Brentano reflection mode, and phase identification was conducted using JADE software with the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) database.

Rheological test

Rheological tests were performed as previously described [21]. A photorheometer (HAAKE MARS III) with a parallel-plate geometry (P20 TiL, 20 mm diameter) was used to perform dynamic rheology experiments. Under light systems (OmniCure Series 2000) at 25 ℃, the test was conducted in time sweep oscillatory mode with 10% strain (CD mode) at 1 Hz, using a gap range of 0.1–0.5 mm for 180 s. The gel point was determined at the time when the storage modulus (G′) exceeded the loss modulus (G″).

Mechanical test

The tensile modulus and strength were evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron 3365, USA). The samples were 10 mm in width, 20 mm in length, and 1 mm in height. Tests were conducted at a tensile rate of 1 mm/min, and the stress-strain curves were recorded. The tensile modulus was determined from the linear region of the stress-strain curve to ensure accurate mechanical characterization.

Mineralization of ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA

Induction of mineralization

Following polymerization, ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA was immersed in SBF after removing the uncured hydrogel. SBF was prepared as previously reported [51] by dissolving NaCl (7.996 g), NaHCO3 (0.350 g), KCl (0.224 g), K2HPO4·3H2O (0.231 g), MgCl2·6H2O (0.305 g), CaCl2 (0.278 g), Na2SO4 (71 mg), and (CH2OH)3CNH2 (6.057 g, Sinopharm, China) in 900 mL deionized water at 37 ℃. The pH was adjusted to 7.4 using HCl or NaOH, and the final volume was brought to 1 L with deionized water. The prepared SBF was stored at 4 ℃. For surface mineralization characterization, cubic samples were immersed in SBF for 10 days. Additionally, 1 mL aliquots from the SBF immersing 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA were collected at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 days for inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) analysis. Materials intended for cell experiments were cured in either 48- or 6-well plates and subsequently immersed in SBF for 5 days, with SBF refreshed on day 3. GBR membranes designated for animal experiments underwent SBF-induced mineralization for 10 days, with media replaced every 3 days.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping

Lyophilized samples were gold sputter-coated (10 nm thickness) and analyzed using a TESCAN MIRA LMS (Czech) SEM system. Imaging was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a working distance of 10 mm, with magnifications set at 10,000× to ensure high-resolution surface characterization of the GBR membrane and mineralized hydrogel Subsequently, EDS was activated to analyze the elemental composition.

ICP-OES analysis

Liquid samples collected during the mineralization process were preserved for ICP-OES testing to quantify zinc and calcium concentrations. The collected 1 mL SBF aliquots were analyzed without dilution to accurately reflect the in situ ion concentrations. To ensure consistency, the remaining SBF solution was gently mixed before each sampling to maintain homogeneity. Samples were acidified with nitric acid to decompose organic components, and the analysis was performed using an ICP-OES instrument (Agilent 5110 ICP-OES, Germany).

Protein absorption

The assessment of protein absorption on the hydrogel surface was adapted from a previously reported method [52]. Hydrogel samples were cured in 24-well plates and immersed in SBF for 5 days. Before protein incubation, the samples were equilibrated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and rinsed three times. For serum protein absorption, 400 µL of BSA solution (5% wt, in PBS) was added to each well and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. Subsequently, the protein solutions were removed, and the samples were rinsed five times. Protein absorption on the hydrogel surface was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Beyotime, China). The fibrinogen solution (Beyotime, China) (1 mg/mL in PBS) was used for fibrinogen adsorption, following the same evaluation process as serum protein adsorption.

Water contact angle

The hydrophobicity of mineralized hydrogel samples was evaluated using an OCA 20 contact angle goniometer (Dataphysics, Germany). A CCD video camera, equipped with a lens and mounted on the viewing stage, was used to measure the angle between the liquid droplet and the solid surface.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) scanning

The surface morphology of mineralized samples was analyzed using an atomic force microscope (AFM, SPM9700, Shimadzu, Japan). Samples were first lyophilized and then mounted onto glass slides using double-sided adhesive tape to ensure stability during scanning. AFM imaging was performed in tapping mode to capture high-resolution topographical features of the sample surface.

Cell viability and adhesion on the curing hydrogel

Cell isolation and cell culture

BMSCs were isolated from the bone marrow of SD rats within 7 days after birth following a previously described method [53]. Briefly, after euthanasia via the spinal dislocation method, the rats were immersed in 75% alcohol for 30 min. The femur and tibia were extracted, and the cartilage at both ends of the bone was removed. The bone marrow cavity was repeatedly flushed with a culture medium until the bone appeared white. Finally, the collected bone marrow tissue was seeded into a culture dish containing 6 mL of culture medium (L-DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin). Cultures were incubated at 37 ℃, with the medium changed every 3 days. BMSCs used in the experiments were in passages 3–5.

Calcein-AM staining and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

A total of 0.1 mL of PEGNB/HAMA, 2% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA, 5% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA, and 10% ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA hydrogels (n = 6) were added to the well of a 48-well plate, photocured for 10 min and subjected to mineralization for 5 days. Subsequently, the plates were sterilized in 75% alcohol under UV lights for 12 h. BMSCs were seeded onto the hydrogel at the density of 4,000 cells per well. On day 3 and 7, BMSC viability was assessed. The culture medium was discarded, and cells were stained using a calcein-AM cell viability assay kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were observed under a confocal microscope (Ni-E, Nikon, Japan). Cell viability was quantified using the CCK-8 (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader (BioTek, Agilent, USA).

Nucleus and F-actin staining

Six-well plates for cell culture were prepared using the same method described above, except using 0.3 mL of hydrogel per well. BMSCs were seeded onto the hydrogel at the density of 4 × 105 cells per well. On day 7, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, USA). The cell nucleus and actin filaments were stained with DAPI and Alexa Fluor 555-labeled phalloidin (Beyotime, China), respectively. Stained cells were observed under a confocal microscope. For quantitative analysis of actin density, ImageJ software (NIH, USA) was used to measure the fluorescence intensity of actin staining. Actin density was calculated as the mean fluorescence intensity per unit area, providing an objective assessment of cytoskeletal organization on different hydrogel surfaces. Three independent experiments (n = 3) were conducted, and for each sample, at least five randomly selected fields of view were analyzed to ensure statistical reliability.

Osteogenesis assay

Osteogenesis induction

Photocured and mineralized hydrogel samples were immersed in L-DMEM culture medium at 37 ℃ for 3 days to obtain hydrogel extracts. Subsequently, 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin were added to the extracts for cell culture. BMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well for alkaline phosphatase (ALP), Alizarin red S (ARS) staining, and collagen secretion analysis. A total of 4 × 105 cells per well seeded in 6-well plates for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Western blot assays. BMSCs were cultured in L-DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 3 days to achieve 80%−90% confluence. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with hydrogel extracts supplemented with 1 mM dexamethasone (Sigma), 10 mM ascorbate (Sigma), and 1 M β-glycerophosphate (Sigma) to induce osteogenesis.

ALP staining and activity assessment

An ALP evaluation was conducted on BMSCs following 7 days of osteogenic induction. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained using an ALP color development kit from Beyotime (n = 4). Cellular proteins were extracted using cell lysis buffer without inhibitors (Beyotime) and analyzed with an ALP assay kit from Beyotime (n = 6). The reaction solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 405 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Collagen secretion test

Collagen secretion was evaluated in BMSCs following 7 days of osteogenic induction. The relative amount of collagen secreted in the culture medium was quantified (n = 6) using a hydroxyproline assay kit (Solarbio, China). The medium was transferred into 5 mL centrifuge tubes and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ARS staining and semiquantitative analysis

The ARS assay was performed on BMSCs following 14 days of osteogenic induction. The cells were fixed and stained using an ARS staining solution (Cyagen, China) (n = 6). Subsequently, the stained cells were washed with cetylpyridinium chloride (Sigma) to extract the pigment for semiquantitative analysis. The absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Osteogenic messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression

Osteogenic marker expression was analyzed on days 7 and 14 of osteogenesis. mRNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (ELK Biotechnology) and treated with 1 st Strand complementary DNA Synthesis Super Mix (ELK Biotechnology). The expression levels of Alp, Runx-2, and Bglap were quantified through real-time PCR using SYBR Green PCR SuperMix (ELK Biotechnology) on the QuantStudio™ reaction system (Thermo Fisher). β-Actin served as the endogenous control. The primers are listed in Table 1 (Supplementary Information).

Osteogenic protein expression

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor (Servicebio, China). The protein solution was mixed with 5× loading buffer (Servicebio) and boiled for 5 min. Subsequently, the proteins were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking, the PVDF membrane was incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with anti-Runx2 (Affinity), anti-BMP2 (Servicebio), and anti-actin (Servicebio). After washing, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody and treated with an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Servicebio). Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Clinx, China).

Surgery and treatment of rabbit radius bone defects

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Number [2019] IEC (S2117) was approved by Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology for the studies involving animals.) A total of 15 male New Zealand rabbits (age: 6 months, weight: approximately 2.5 kg) were randomly assigned into three groups: (i) blank control (n = 5), (ii) PEGNB/HAMA (n = 5), and (iii) ZIF-8/PEGNB/HAMA (n = 5). The rabbits were intravenously anesthetized with tenetamine hydrochloride and zolazepam hydrochloride (Zoletil®50, Virbac, France). The skin on the anterior side of the upper limbs was prepared, and a 3 cm incision was made along the radius. Subsequently, the muscle was separated to expose the radius. A total of 1.4 cm defect was created by removing the bone and periosteum tissue. Depending on the assigned group, the defect area was either covered with GBR membrane or left untreated. The membrane extends slightly beyond (0.55 cm) both ends of the 1.4 cm defect, enabling it to conform closely to the bone surface while spanning the defect region. This design not only ensures stable positioning but also prevents the membrane from inadvertently filling the defect space, which could otherwise interfere with the bone regeneration process (Fig. S5a). Afterward, the muscle and skin were sutured, and penicillin was administered via intramuscular injection. The surgery was performed under the sterile conditions. Rabbits were housed individually in standard laboratory cages with free access to water and food.

Evaluation of bone repair in rabbits

Micro-computed tomography (CT) analysis

Radial bone defect repair in rabbits was evaluated through micro-CT scanning and histological analysis. Twelve weeks post-surgery, rabbits were euthanized using an overdose of anesthesia, after which the radius was dissected. To facilitate micro-CT imaging and ensure accurate bone regeneration analysis, the hydrogel membrane was carefully removed before scanning. The dissected radius was then fixed in paraformaldehyde for 48 h prior to micro-CT analysis. Scanning was performed using a Bruker SkyScan 1276 system with a voxel size of 9 μm, an X-ray source voltage of 60 kV, and a current of 166 µA. A 0.5 mm aluminum filter was applied to optimize image contrast, and scanning was conducted with a rotation step of 0.4°. Image reconstruction was performed using NRecon software (Bruker), while volumetric and morphometric measurements, including bone volume (BV), bone surface (BS), BV-to-tissue volume ratio (BV/TV), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), were quantified using CTAn software (Bruker). Thresholding was carefully adjusted to distinguish newly formed bone from soft tissue, ensuring an accurate assessment of bone regeneration.

Histological analysis

Following micro-CT scanning, the samples were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 4 weeks, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, and embedded in paraffin. The samples were then sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices along the longitudinal axis in the center for further analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome (MT) staining were performed to evaluate tissue structure, while immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses were conducted to assess the expression and distribution of key osteogenic and immune markers. Specifically, IHC staining was performed to evaluate BMP2, OCN, RUNX2, and α-SMA, whereas IF staining was used to detect CD86 and CD163. H&E, MT, and IHC-stained sections were examined and scanned using a Nikon Eclipse Ni microscope (Japan), while IF-stained sections were scanned using the Panoramic 250 Flash III system (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and analyzed with CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH, Hungary). The IHC and IF analyses were conducted using primary antibodies against CD86 (ab955, Abcam, 1:200), CD163 (ab182422, Abcam, 1:200), RUNX2 (ab76956, Abcam, 1:200), BMP2 (AF355, R&D Systems, 1:100), and OCN (ab13418, Abcam, 1:200). Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature. IHC-stained sections were examined using a Nikon Eclipse Ni microscope (Japan), while IF-stained sections were scanned using the Panoramic 250 Flash III system (3DHISTECH, Hungary) and analyzed with CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH, Hungary). Quantitative analysis of BMP2, OCN, RUNX2, CD163, and CD86 expression was performed using ImageJ (NIH) software, with integrated density measurements normalized to background intensity to minimize variability. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to assess significant differences between groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 by IBM Corp. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and student’s t-test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
