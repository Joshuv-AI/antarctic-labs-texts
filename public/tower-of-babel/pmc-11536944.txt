Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells overexpressing stromal cell- derived factor 1 aid in bone formation in osteoporotic mice
Authors: Yanghao Wang, Ya Xiao, XinYu Yang, Fei He, Jun Hu, Guang Yang, Weizhou Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis is characterized by low systemic bone mineral content and destruction of bone microarchitecture. Promoting bone regeneration and reversing its loss by infusion of exogenous bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) is a potentially effective treatment for osteoporosis. However, their limited migration to target organs reduces the therapeutic effect of the cells. Stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF1) is a chemokine that induces targeted cell migration through the SDF1/CXCR4 (C-X-C chemokine receptor 4) axis and can induce migration of exogenous mesenchymal stem cells to sites of high SDF1 concentration. There are no studies on BMSCs overexpressing SDF1 (SDF1-BMSCs) in osteoporotic mice in vivo. We aimed to investigate if the increased SDF1 concentration facilitated cell migration to the bone.MethodsWe used lentivirus to construct BMSCs overexpressing SDF1 or knocking down CXCR4. We verified the proliferation ability of the cells in vitro using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) and 5-Bromodeoxyuridinc (BrdU), the migration ability of the cells using Transwell, and the osteogenic and lipogenic ability of the cells using osteogenic and lipogenic induction solutions. In in vivo experiments, we induced osteoporosis in 72 female mice by ovariectomy and injected different groups of cells via the tail vein. Femoral tissue samples were collected for a fixed time, and the osteogenic and homing abilities of the cells were verified by MicroCT and tissue section staining.ResultsWe successfully demonstrated that high expression of SDF1 promoted cell proliferation and migration in vitro, without affecting their cell differentiation ability. In an ovariectomized mouse model, SDF1-BMSCs were more likely to be home to the femur than the BMSCs, had a better pro-osteogenic ability, and had higher expression of Wnt-1. Blocking the SDF1/CXCR4 axis reduced the homing of exogenous mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to the femur and their osteogenic capacity.ConclusionsSDF1-BMSCs can further promote bone formation by increasing the number of cells homing to the femur in osteoporotic mice. Our study shows that stem cells can promote their proliferation and home to the femur via the SDF1/CXCR4 axis and further help bone formation via Wnt-1 signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-07957-2.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a disease characterized by low systemic bone mineral content and destruction of bone microarchitecture, which further leads to increased bone fragility and risk of fracture. Postmenopausal osteoporosis is the most common type of osteoporosis [1, 2], which increases the susceptibility of the bone to fractures and causes deformity, disability, and even death. It has serious repercussions on the physiological health and quality of life of a majority of middle-aged and elderly women [3, 4]. Therefore, it is important to actively pursue effective treatment methods for osteoporosis to improve the bone health and quality of life of middle-aged and elderly individuals.

Osteoporosis is associated with a significant reduction in stem cells and therefore the osteogenic capacity in vivo [5]. Studies have found that bone repair and reversal of bone loss can be promoted by introducing exogenous stem cells [6]. However, for osteoporosis, the number of cells migrating to the target organ post-stem cell transplantation is limited [7]. Active exploration of how to promote the efficient migration and homing of stem cells is important to apply stem cell therapy for osteoporosis.

Stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF1) is an important chemokine that regulates the biological behaviors of stem cells, such as residency, mobilization, migration, and homing [8]. The SDF1/CXCR4(C-X-C chemokine receptor 4) axis plays a crucial role [9, 10], especially in injured tissues. Here, the local cellular SDF1 expression is increased by inflammatory stimuli and other factors, thereby increasing the binding to CXCR4 receptors on the stem cell surface, their subsequent mobilization, and homing to the injured tissue [11, 12]. Similarly, increased SDF1 expression in osteoporotic bone tissue promotes stem cell migration into the bone [13]. However, with tissue repair and adaptation, local SDF1 expression gradually decreases [14]. This leads to a decrease in the migratory effect of stem cells, a process that does not demonstrate significant efficacy in treating chronic diseases such as osteoporosis through simple infusion of exogenous MSCs. Therefore, we focused on whether BMSCs overexpressing SDF1 can promote cell homing to the bone due to increased concentration of SDF1 in the bone, and thus enhance the pro-osteogenic effect.

Materials and methods

SDF1 and CXCR4 lentiviral transduction and its expression analysis

BMSCs were extracted and cultured as described previously [15]. Further, they were inoculated in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and infected with lentivirus containing overexpressed SDF1(Hanheng Bio, titer of 1 × 108 PFU/mL, MOI = 75), knockdown CXCR4(Hanheng Bio, titer of 1 × 108 PFU/mL, MOI = 50) or control vector (NC) (Hanheng Bio, titer of 1 × 108 PFU/mL, MOI = 75) with polybrene (Hanheng Bio) at a final concentration of 8 µg/mL for 72 h. After transfection, puromycin(5 µg/mL) was added to each well after transfection to screen for BMSCs stably expressing SDF1 and NC.

RNA from BMSCs, NC-BMSCs, SDF1-BMSCs, and shCXCR4-BMSCs(Using short hairpin RNAs to knockdown CXCR4 expression in BMSCs) was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme Biotech). Amplification was done in a 20 µL reaction volum Lentivirus containing SDF1 overexpression or CXCR4 knockdown was used to infect bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Post-transduction, PCR and Western blot analyses were employed to assess changes in the expression of SDF1 and CXCR4 at the mRNA and protein levels, respectively.

The PCR reaction mixture was denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, and the cDNA template was amplified as follows: denaturation at 95 °C and annealing at 60 °C for 1 min for 40 cycles.

Protein was extracted from the three cell lines using a protein extraction kit (Beyotime, Bioz, Inc.), and protein concentration was estimated using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Beyotime, Bioz, Inc.). Equal amounts of protein were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk, and secondary antibodies containing horseradish peroxidase (HRP, Servicebio) were conjugated at 20 ℃ after incubation with SDF1 primary antibody (1:500, Bioss Antibodies), CXCR4 primary antibody (1:5000, Abcam), β-actin primary antibody (1:1000, Bioss Antibodies, Woburn, MA, USA), and GAPDH primary antibody (1:10000, PROTEINTECH). Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system(GE Healthcare Life Sciences), and the grayscale images were quantified using the ImageJ software [16]. Lentivirus containing SDF1 overexpression or CXCR4 knockdown was used to infect bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Post-transduction, PCR and Western blot analyses were employed to assess changes in the expression of SDF1 and CXCR4 at the mRNA and protein levels, respectively. A normal control group (NC-BMSCs), transduced with a lentiviral vector not containing the gene of interest, served as a control to compare transduction efficiency and changes in cellular functions.

Transwell migration assay

The four types of BMSCs were inoculated in the upper chamber of the Transwell assembly (Corning Inc.) at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL (Serum-free medium). The lower chamber contained a medium with 15% serum. After 48 h, the chambers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. The number of migrating cells was determined by counting five random fields of view per well using a microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). To confirm the specificity of the SDF1/CXCR4 axis in Transwell migration assays, this study utilized a CXCR4 knockdown group. In our research, we did not employ blocking antibodies or inhibitors to ascertain the specificity of the SDF1/CXCR4 axis in migration analysis.

BrdU and CCK8 cell proliferation assay

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well. Each well was incubated with 10 µL BrdU (ThermoFisher) for 72 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. BrdU primary antibody – 300 µL (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology) was added to each well, incubated overnight, and labeled with the corresponding secondary antibody (1:1000, Proteintech). Cells were counterstained with DAPI, mounted using glycerol, and images were acquired using a fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Japan).

100 µL (1 × 104 cells/well) of the cell suspension was added to a 96-well plate and incubated for 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 days, following which 10 µL CCK8 solution (Beyotime) was added at the respective time points and incubated for 3 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. In the CCK-8 and BrdU assays, to ensure that the observed effects are due to the overexpression of SDF1 and not other variables, the following specific control groups were used:

BMSCs: bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells Group

NC-BMSCs: negative control bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells group

SDF1-BMSCs: bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells overexpressing SDF1 group

High absorbance in the CCK-8 assay signifies greater cell viability, while the BrdU assay measures proliferation through anti-BrdU antibody binding and detection methods like flow cytometry or immunofluorescence. The implementation of these controls and assays ensures that the effects are specifically due to SDF1 overexpression and accurately assesses cell viability and proliferation post-transduction.

Validation of osteogenic and lipogenic differentiation ability of the cells

The osteogenic differentiation was evaluated, after 14 days of osteogenic induction (Cyagen Biosciences), using the alizarin red staining method. Initially, cells are fixed in 70% ethanol for one hour. Subsequently, they are rinsed with distilled water three times. The staining process involves the application of a 2% Alizarin Red S solution at a pH of 4.1–4.3 and an incubation period of 20 min at room temperature. Excess stain is removed by rinsing with distilled water. The presence of red calcium deposits, indicating mineralization, is observed under a microscope.

The lipogenic differentiation ability was evaluated using Oil Red O staining after 21 days of lipogenic induction (Cyagen Biosciences). Cells are fixed with 10% formalin for 30 min, followed by a rinse with 60% isopropanol. The staining is performed with an Oil Red O solution at a concentration of 0.3% in isopropanol for 10–15 min. After staining, cells are rinsed with 60% isopropanol and then with distilled water. The presence of red-stained lipid droplets, indicative of adipogenesis, is examined under a microscope. These quantification methods provide a means to assess the extent of mineralization and adipogenesis in a quantitative and group percentage-controlled manner. The key quantification indicators are the absorbance values at 405 nm for Alizarin Red Staining and 500 nm for Oil Red O Staining, which correlate with the levels of mineralization and adipogenesis, respectively. The quantification of Alizarin Red Staining begins with the destaining process, where 10% acetic acid is added to the stained cells and incubated for 30 min. The solution is then transferred to a new tube, heated at 85 °C for 10 min, cooled, and neutralized with 10% ammonium hydroxide. The amount of calcium-bound dye is measured by determining the absorbance at 405 nm using a spectrophotometer. For the quantification of Oil Red O Staining, destaining is carried out by adding 100% isopropanol to the stained cells and incubating for 10 min. The dye dissolved in isopropanol is collected, and its quantity is determined by measuring the absorbance at 500 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Cell homing assay

Ovariectomized mice were randomly divided into 3 groups of 12 mice each: DiD-labeled BMSCs group, DiD-labeled SDF1-BMSCs group, and DiD-labeled SDF1-sh-BMSCs group. 5 µL/mL DiD solution (ThermoFisher) was added to the cell suspension (1 × 106/mL). The mixture was then incubated for 20 min and centrifuged. After removing the supernatant, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and were counted after resuspending in PBS. The cell infusion volume was 2 × 106 cells per mouse via tail vein injection. Our euthanasia drug is pentobarbital. The injectable dose of pentobarbital is 135 mg/kg. Pentobarbital has a long shelf life and rapid action. It is capable of causing smooth unconsciousness in rodents. Three mice in each group were cervically dislocated and executed 4 h, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d after cell infusion. The soft tissues of both lower extremities were quickly peeled under light-protected conditions, and the bilateral femurs of the mice were obtained and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The femurs were then directly frozen, sectioned (20 μm), counterstained with DAPI, and glycerol mounted, and images were obtained using inverted fluorescence microscopy (Nikon, Japan). In the study, the homing efficiency of the injected cells in the femoral tissue was quantified using DiD labeling of the cells. Post-cell infusion, the distribution of labeled cells in the cortical and trabecular bone of the femur was observed under a fluorescence microscope. The quantity of injected cells was quantified by counting the number of positive cells at different time points, such as 4 h, 3 days, 7 days, and 14 days post-infusion. Additionally, the injected cells were marked with the fluorescent dye DiD, which allowed for easy identification of the infused cells under a fluorescence microscope.

Bone mass restoration experiment in osteoporotic mice

All experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Review Committee of Kunming Medical University. The program complies with the ARRIVE guidelines. Twelve-week-old C57BL/6 female mice (weight, 23 ± 3 g) were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Kunming Medical University. The ovariectomy model was constructed as described previously [17]. Female mice were selected for all animals, which is necessary to simulate postmenopausal osteoporosis.

Mice were randomly divided into five groups of six each: sham-operated (Sham), ovariectomy (OVX), OVX + BMSCs (BMSCT), OVX + SDF1-BMSCs (SDF1-BMSCT), and OVX + SDF1-sh-BMSCs (SDF1-sh-BMSCT) (n = 6). Additionally, we used histological sections with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining. H&E staining revealed a loose bone matrix structure of the entire trabecular bone, while Masson’s staining showed a decrease in collagen density. Combining these two indices, we determined the successful establishment of the osteoporosis model in ovariectomized mice. Mice in each group underwent tail vein infusion with 2 × 106 cells in 200 µL PBS, 1 month after the ovariectomy. The sham and OVX groups were infused with 200 µL PBS.

The optimal timing for cell infusion was determined based on both the progression of osteoporosis in the ovariectomy (OVX) mouse model and the expected therapeutic window for cell-based interventions. In the OVX mouse model, significant bone loss and the establishment of osteoporosis typically occur within one month post-ovariectomy. This timeframe is well-documented in the literature and provides a consistent and relevant model for studying postmenopausal osteoporosis [18, 19]. By one month, the mice exhibit clear signs of bone density reduction and structural deterioration, mimicking the osteoporotic condition seen in postmenopausal women. Preliminary experiments and literature reviews indicated that cell therapy administered at one month post-ovariectomy can effectively home to the damaged bone areas, initiate repair processes, and enhance bone regeneration. The timing also aligns with the typical therapeutic cycle observed in similar studies, where cell-based treatments are administered after the disease model has been fully established [20, 21].

Femoral tissue sectioning and staining

Mice in all groups were sacrificed by cervical dislocation 1 month after cell infusion. Before euthanasia at the cervical dislocation site, we anesthetized the mice by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (70 mg/kg) to prevent suffering. The lower limb femurs were obtained post-separation of limb soft tissues and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Femurs were decalcified in 0.5 M Ethylene Diamine Tetraacetic Acid (EDTA) for 24 h and then frozen Sect. (10 μm) were obtained. The sections were stained using an HE kit and Masson staining kit(Solarbio, Beijing, China), sealed with neutral resin, and images were obtained using light microscopy.

The frozen sections were thawed and blocked with 3% BSA. Primary antibodies (SDF1, 1:100; Wnt, 1:100; Bioss Antibodies) were added. Post overnight incubation, the sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (HRP conjugate, 1:350). Diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic solution (Biosharp) and hematoxylin re-staining were added dropwise, the neutral resin was blocked, and images were obtained using light microscopy. In this study, several critical steps were undertaken to ensure the consistency and reproducibility of staining outcomes: Standardization of staining protocols facilitated a uniform staining process. Fixation and decalcification procedures maintained consistent tissue architecture. Cryosectioning preserved the integrity and morphology of the tissues. Quantitative analysis of grayscale in images using ImageJ software provided an objective assessment of staining results. Multiple sections from the same sample, processed synchronously under identical conditions, ensured the reproducibility and consistency of staining outcomes.

Micro CT scan

The fixed femurs were scanned in a Micro CT (Ping Sheng Medical) machine tube with a radiation source tube voltage of 90 kV, a current of 60 µA, a frame rate of 20, and a DSD set to 395 mm using Cruiser software. The femur was analyzed using Avatar3 software, using the proximal femoral growth plate as a reference point. The region of the metaphysis was selected to determine the bone volume density (BV/TV), number (N), bone mineral density (BMD) of the trabeculae (Tb), and the bone mineral density (BMD), thickness (Th), a ratio of area to total area (Ar/Tr.Ar) of the cortical bone(Ct). A decrease in these values compared to normal mice confirmed the successful construction of our ovariectomized model, indicating a reduction in bone mass following ovariectomy.

Statistical analysis

We have conducted extensive research on the sample size for the experimental group, supported by a thorough review of relevant literature. Based on the findings from our preliminary studies and a comprehensive analysis of the existing literature, we have determined the appropriate sample size. The sample sizes for each experimental group were determined based on prior studies and preliminary data that indicated the expected effect sizes. For our in vitro experiments, we ensured sufficient replicates (n = 3 to 5) to obtain reliable and reproducible results, accounting for biological variability. For in vivo studies, sample sizes were chosen considering ethical guidelines, previous literature on similar studies, and the expected magnitude of changes. Specifically, we included 12 mice per group for the cell homing assay and 6 mice per group for the bone mass restoration experiment. This approach allowed us to balance the need for statistical power and ethical use of animals. Power analyses were conducted during the planning phase of the study to ensure that our sample sizes were adequate to detect significant differences between groups. Using standard assumptions for power (1-β) of 0.8 and an alpha level (α) of 0.05, we calculated the required sample sizes to detect medium to large effect sizes based on pilot data and previous studies. For in vitro assays, we expected at least a 20% difference in outcomes such as cell proliferation and migration, and for in vivo experiments, we aimed to detect at least a 30% difference in bone volume and cell homing efficacy. These power analyses ensured that our study was adequately powered to detect significant differences, minimizing the risk of Type II errors and enhancing the reliability of our findings. The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA, www.graphpad.com) for data processing and quantification of results. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test whether the data conformed to normality and was expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-tailed t-test was used to compare the differences between the two groups. For the comparison of multiple groups of data, data that conformed to a normal distribution with equal variances were compared using one-way ANOVA and those with statistical differences were then compared using the “Least-Significant Difference test”. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, we used the Kruskal-Wallis test to compare the statistical significance of each group and Dunn’s test for the difference between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Successful generation of SDF1-BMSCs and SDF1-sh-BMSCs

SDF1 expression was increased in SDF1-BMSCs at both mRNAs (P < 0.0001) and protein levels (P < 0.0001) compared with BMSCs (Fig. 1A). Compared with SDF1-BMSCs, CXCR4 expression was significantly decreased in SDF1-sh-BMSCs at both mRNAs (P < 0.0001) and protein levels (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we selected sh3 as the CXCR4 knockdown cell line for the next experiment. In contrast, the expression levels of SDF1 and CXCR4 in NC-BMSCs were not statistically different from those in BMSCs (P ≥ 0.05).

Lentiviral transduction of SDF1 enhances cell proliferation and migration

The CCK-8 cell proliferation assay (Fig. 1G) showed no significant difference in the proliferation rate between BMSCs and SDF1-BMSCs on days 0 and 1 in culture (P ≥ 0.05). However, the proliferation rate of SDF1-BMSCs was significantly higher than that of BMSCs alone on day 3 (P = 0.0230), 5 (P = 0.0074), and 7 (P = 0.0011). Meanwhile, the proliferation rate of SDF1-sh-BMSCs was significantly lower than that of SDF1-BMSCs at day 5 (P = 0.0035) and 7 (P = 0.011). The results of the cell proliferation assay (BrdU method) showed that (Figs. 1F and 2), the proportion of cells possessing red fluorescence was significantly increased in SDF1-BMSCs compared with BMSCs (P = 0.0028); There was no difference in proliferation rates among SDF1-sh-BMSCs, NC-BMSCs and BMSCs (P ≥ 0.05). Transwell results showed (Fig. 1D and E) that SDF1-BMSCs migrated an increased number of cells compared to BMSCs (P < 0.0001), and the number of migrating cells was not statistically different between NC-BMSCs and BMSCs (P ≥ 0.05). SDF1-sh-BMSCs migrated the least number of cells.

Overexpression of SDF1 does not affect cell differentiation

All four cell lines showed red calcium nodules and were capable of osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 1C). The cells showed orange-red lipid droplets and the ability to differentiate into lipids (Fig. 1C). The differentiation potential of the four cell strains was not significantly different (Fig. 1C).

Overexpression of SDF1/CXCR4 promotes the homing of BMSCs to the femur

Starting at the 4th hour after cell infusion, nucleated cells labeled with DiD were observed near the cortex and trabecular bone of the femoral epiphysis (Fig. 3). The number of cells homing to the femur was approximately the same for both (P = 0.9427). At days 3 (P < 0.0001), 7 (P < 0.0001), and 14 (P < 0.0001) after cell infusion, SDF1-BMSCs increased the number of positive cells homing to the femur of osteoporotic mice compared to BMSCs. SDF1-sh-BMSCs homed to the femur of osteoporotic mice had the lowest number of positive cells, which was most pronounced at day 14.

BMSCs accelerate bone gain in the cancellous bone of mice via SDF1/CXCR4

Bone volume changes in isolated femoral tissues of mice were measured using small animal Micro CT (Fig. 4). The bone volume of the femur in the OVX group was significantly lower than that in the sham group (P < 0.0001). The BMSCT group showed a significant increase in the bone volume of the femur when compared with that in the OVX group (P = 0.0006, P = 0.0001). The SDF1-BMSCT group also showed an increase in the femur bone volume compared to that of the BMSCT group (P = 0.0136, P = 0.0284). Among them, the SDF1-sh-BMSCT group had poorer bone volume recovery compared to the two groups with infused cells. The bone volume changes mentioned above were mainly related to the volume density and thickness of the trabeculae, while the differences in the measured parameters related to cortical bone were not statistically significant (P ≥ 0.05).

SDF1 improves femur microarchitecture and promotes Wnt-1 signaling

Hematoxylin and eosin staining of paraffin sections of the bone tissues showed that there were no major differences in the morphology of the femur in each group of mice (Fig. 5). Masson staining showed that mice in the OVX group had decreased (P < 0.0001) while those in the cell transplantation group showed increased bone formation and mature collagen fibers near the femoral growth plate compared to mice in the sham group. Additionally, an increase in bone formation and mature collagen fibers was observed near the femoral growth plate in the SDF1-BMSCT group compared to that of the BMSCT group, though this difference was not statistically significant (P ≥ 0.05). However, there was a significant decrease in bone formation and mature collagen fibers near the femoral growth plate in the SDF1-sh-BMSCT group compared with the SDF1-BMSCT group in mice (P = 0.0042).

The immunohistochemical staining of femoral sections (Fig. 5) showed that the expression of SDF1 was significantly higher in the femur of mice in the OVX group than in the Sham group (P < 0.0001), while it was significantly lower in the BMSCT group than in the OVX group (P = 0.0489). Notably, the SDF1 expression levels in the SDF1-BMSCT group were similar to those in the SDF1-sh-BMSCT group and significantly higher than the BMSCT group (P = 0.0302). There was a low expression of Wnt-1 in the femurs of osteoporotic mice, and there was some increase in local Wnt-1 concentration in the femurs of mice after cell treatment (P = 0.0489). a further promotion of Wnt-1 expression in tissues was observed only with SDF1-BMSCs (P = 0.0142).

Discussion

The role of MSCs is thought to be mediated by their differentiation and secretion of multiple cellular factors in the affected microenvironment. However, the efficiency of MSC transplantation and homing rates are lower [22, 23]. SDF1 is a chemotactic cytokine, and studies have shown that SDF1-BMSCs have better migration ability in vitro [24, 25]. It is inconclusive whether SDF1-BMSCs will home more and further play a bone-producing role in osteoporotic mice. Therefore, this study was conducted to investigate the homing and bone formation capabilities of SDF1-BMSCs in osteoporotic mice.

Previously, orthopedic research on SDF1 focused more on bone defects or fractures rather than osteoporosis. Many studies have confirmed that BMSCs overexpressing SDF1 reduce mineral deficiency near the defect site during the early stages of bone healing [14]. However, little attention has been paid to the homing effect that SDF1 can generate. This study focuses on the role of BMSCs overexpressing SDF1 in osteoporosis, with particular emphasis on the generation of homing effects.

Some studies have confirmed that MSCs overexpressing CXCR4 promote bone formation in osteoporotic rats [26]. Our study, however, focuses on the overexpression of SDF1 and includes a control group with knockdown of CXCR4 expression, considering both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Another study showed that systemic injection of exogenous bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells into a mouse model of osteoporosis can promote an increase in bone formation. However, this study specifically targets age-related osteoporosis, not postmenopausal osteoporosis, and does not include molecular-level experiments [27]. We filled this gap through further experiments on molecular targets and research on postmenopausal osteoporosis.

SDF1 is a CXC chemokine and CXCR4 is a cell surface receptor for SDF1 that mediates the homing and migration of stem cells [28]. Previous studies have shown that increased levels of circulating SDF1 promote the mobilization of MSCs, thereby accelerating the cell cycle, activating cells, and promoting cell migration [29–32]. The results of our in vitro cellular experiments showed that SDF1 overexpression promoted the migration of BMSCs. The enhanced cell migration capacity could be an autocrine effect of SDF1 or it may play a role in promoting the activation of BMSCs [33]. We found that overexpression of SDF1 improved stem cell survival and proliferation, and the aforementioned cellular phenotypes may act through the SDF1/CXCR4 axis. In addition, null-loaded lentiviral transfection did not affect the expression of SDF1, cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation abilities.

It has been demonstrated that promoting stem cell homing in the bone can improve bone loss caused by osteoporosis [34]. Infusion of BMSCs via the tail vein increases femoral bone mass in osteoporotic mice [35, 36]. The method modified BMSCs by overexpressing the SDF1 gene, which contributed more BMSCs to the femur and enhanced bone repair without changing the total amount of infused cells.

The classical signals for osteogenesis is the Wnt signaling pathway [37]. Osteoblast differentiation is predominantly regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin signaling (canonical Wnt pathway), which, together with bone morphogenetic proteins, acts as the master regulator of osteogenesis. This pathway determines the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into mature osteoblasts rather than chondrocytes and adipocytes, playing a crucial role in bone tissue formation [38]. Mouse genetics have confirmed the importance of canonical Wnt signaling in bone homeostasis, with pathway activation leading to increased bone mass and strength, and inhibition resulting in decreased bone mass. Genome-wide association studies have further highlighted the significance of Wnt signaling in bone health, making it a target for therapeutic interventions to restore bone strength in patients at risk of fractures [39]. In this study, we initially explored the expression levels of Wnt-1 in the femur after cell therapy and found that the native expression of Wnt-1 was high, which may be related to the selective expression of osteogenesis-related signals after cell therapy. Stem cells exhibit osteoblastic differentiation and bone formation through the Wnt signaling pathway in vitro [33]; however, in vivo, studies are lacking in this regard. Therefore, it is necessary to further validate the expression of key proteins and genes downstream of this pathway in mouse models to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms of osteogenic signaling in bone tissue after SDF1-BMSCs treatment.

In summary, we constructed SDF1-BMSCs to strengthen the altered migration and proliferation ability of MSCs. The homing and pro-osteogenic abilities of SDF1-BMSCs were explored in osteoporotic mice, which may play corresponding roles through the SDF1/CXCR4 axis. This study projects the idea of injecting stem cells with enhanced SDF1 expression into osteoporotic animals which may aid in targeting cells to the affected bones for recovery (Fig. 6, www.biorender.com). This study is expected to provide new perspectives for modifying cells and promoting cell homing and function.
