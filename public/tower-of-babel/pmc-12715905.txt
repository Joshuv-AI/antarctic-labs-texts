Challenging prehospital paradigms: bystanders, data-driven strategies, and interprofessional collaboration in transforming trauma care
Authors: Michael D Christian, Lorenzo Gamberini, Alberto Cucino, Marius Rehn
Published: 2025

Suppose you trained as a clinician at any point over the past half-century. In that case, chances are you were taught to reach for a hard cervical collar, Kendrick Extrication Device (KED), or a short spine board, and to recite “A–C–B–C” when approaching motor vehicle collisions (MVCs). Generations of prehospital providers have been drilled on the paramount importance of spinal precautions after crashes. This philosophy traces back to the 1940s, when J. L. Moir wrote in the BMJ [1]:

In all cases where there is a history of accident or injury to the neck or backbone with pain and shock, even though there is no paralysis of arms and/or legs, treat as follows: (i) warn the patient not to try to move; (ii) keep him warm; (iii) send for the doctor…do nothing more till the doctor comes.

By the 1960s, this doctrine had become embedded in prehospital training across North America and, eventually, much of the world [2–4]. For many paramedics and firefighters, these techniques are not only considered foundational truths but also badges of professional pride—ritualized in training, reinforced through examinations, and celebrated in competitions such as the World Rescue Challenge.

In recent months, readers of SJTREM will have seen a remarkable series of papers from the EXIT Project [Table 1], now part of IMPACT – The Centre for Post-Collision Research, Innovation and Translation, led by Dr. Tim Nutbeam. These studies, the culmination of 18 years of work, mark a paradigm shift in prehospital medicine. Their implications for patient outcomes may rival the revolution brought by Dr. Peter Safar’s introduction of modern cardiopulmonary resuscitation in the 1960s.

Lessons from the past

The field owes much to Dr. Kossuth’s seminal 1965 article, The Removal of Injured Personnel from Wrecked Vehicles. Five enduring lessons emerge from his work that illuminate how the EXIT project has corrected decades of misdirected practice.

Innovation must start with the patient

Kossuth and colleagues interviewed crash survivors and were struck by the pain and discomfort caused by extrication with unsplinted fractures. Their conclusion—that splinting before removal reduced suffering—was consistent with the principle of primum non nocere (first do no harm). Yet good intentions ossified into rigid doctrine. Immobilization was pursued at all costs—even when it delayed extrication or compromised the management of life-threatening injuries.

While the aim was noble and the intent pure, the solution fixated upon by Kossuth and perpetuated by many others failed to consider alternative means to the same ends, patient comfort and experience. High-performing prehospital services, such as London’s Air Ambulance, have recognized for several decades the importance of balancing both patient comfort and the need for speed in extricating critically injured patients. Their solution, however, was to provide advanced (physician-paramedic) trauma teams with the skillset to assess and manage these complex patients and achieve the dual aim of patient comfort and rapid extrication through the safe and well-governed delivery of procedural sedation in these situations [5].

Innovators must live the work

Kossuth’s perspective was that of an observer, informed by television footage, anecdotes, and military training exercises. Too often, policy for prehospital care has been dictated by clinicians with little exposure to the realities of the roadside. As Heffernan notes in Wilful Blindness, the gap between those who set policy and those who deliver care is a recurrent cause of systemic failure [6]. This highlights the potential harm that can result from physician leaders in prehospital care who operate from afar without the experience and ongoing exposure to practicing prehospital care directly.

By contrast, Dr. Nutbeam’s work was born from lived experience as a prehospital physician. He witnessed contradictions between priorities at the roadside and in the trauma bay, and recognized the potential harm. This led directly to the EXIT project.

Evidence must replace hearsay

In his article, Kossuth states, “we are all familiar with the hearsay reports of spinal injuries that resulted in transection of the cord during removal from the vehicle”. Dr Nutbeam has described the literature related to spinal cord injuries following extrication as “zombie statistics”, data that has been quoted and perpetuated as facts for which no original source can ever be found. Dr Nutbeam's rigorous methodological approach to both the individual studies and the overall program of work is a key strength of the EXIT project and an example to be followed for future innovations in prehospital care.

The role of bystanders

In his paper, Kossuth recognized the role that bystanders can play on the scene of accidents. However, he focused on the potential negative impact that untrained laypersons conducting extrication might have on patients. The EXIT group has highlighted not only the need for updated guidance for emergency dispatchers to provide appropriate instructions to bystanders on the most suitable timing and manner for extricating patients from vehicles, but also the willingness of bystanders to help and the potential benefits they can deliver to patients through early assessment, triage, and first aid.

Multidisciplinary teams drive real progress

Kossuth began his article lauding the benefits delivered by a multidisciplinary team approach to his research to improve the safety of motor vehicles and prevent injury or deaths from collisions. Similarly, the EXIT project has demonstrated the strengths of multidisciplinary teams conducting research in prehospital care. Not only was the EXIT project team composed of multidisciplinary health professionals (physicians, paramedics and nurses), but it also included firefighters, research methodologists, experts in biomechanics, automotive engineers, regulators and statisticians. Recently, they have expanded their team further via IMPACT to include experts in communication and knowledge translation, producing excellent resources such as this animated video that explains the findings of the project (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hquLBLjkhpA). The success of the EXIT project team highlights not only the benefits of multidisciplinary research teams, but also the importance of drawing its membership from front-line providers who practice prehospital medicine or have a role in the response to MVCs. There is little doubt that the grounding of the EXIT team in the day-to-day realities of the situations they are dealing with enabled them to develop pragmatic solutions to these complex issues.

A new benchmark for prehospital innovation

The EXIT project offers a blueprint for how innovation in prehospital medicine should proceed: patient-centred, clinically grounded, evidence-driven, and multidisciplinary. It replaces outdated, anecdote-driven practices with rigorous science and authentic clinical insight.

The challenge ahead

Changing practice globally will not be easy. Spinal immobilization is embedded in training, tradition, and professional identity. Evidence alone is insufficient; culture change, leadership, and education will be required. But the EXIT team has provided evidence, consensus statements, recommendations, and resources necessary to help systems navigate this change.

Conclusion

The EXIT project represents a watershed moment in prehospital medicine. Like the introduction of CPR, its true measure will be lives saved and suffering reduced. It has also set the standard for how future innovations in prehospital and retrieval medicine should be pursued.
