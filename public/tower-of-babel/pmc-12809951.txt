CLAP-HMM: a biologically constrained deep learning framework for resistance gene prediction in long DNA sequences
Authors: Liuyan Wang, Yingfan Xu, Xuemei Guan, Shanchun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Resistance (R) genes play a critical role in defending against pathogenic infections, and identifying novel R genes is a key component of resistance breeding. However, current computational methods face significant limitations when predicting R genes from ultra-long, highly repetitive genomic sequences. In this study, we propose a deep learning framework, CLAP-HMM, which employs a CNN-LSTM-Attention fusion strategy to extract gene expression patterns from long DNA sequences and accurately predict R genes. Our approach introduces protein function hints as biological priors to guide the HMM module in structural state modeling, enabling precise identification of R gene boundaries. Comparative experiments demonstrate that CLAP-HMM outperforms existing methods in discovering more potential novel R genes, offering promising prospects for uncovering biologically meaningful functional genes and advancing our understanding of disease resistance mechanisms.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-025-01086-x.

Background

Resistance (R) genes enable plants to defend against pathogens or insects, forming the foundation of disease-resistance breeding [1]. However, functional validation through transformation or knockout experiments is time-consuming, costly, and often limited by tissue-specific reproducibility, making comprehensive screening of candidate genes highly challenging under realistic biological contexts [2–4].

With the advancement of computational biology, sequence-based computational models are theoretically capable of overcoming these challenges [5–7]. Classical models such as AUGUSTUS [8], Resfams [9], and EuGene [10] rely on sequence features such as start and stop codons, intron-exon boundaries, and reading frames to predict gene structures. Subsequent studies incorporated protein-level information to refine gene models, improving biological relevance [11, 12]. For instance, NBSPred [13] employed a support vector machine to predict NBS-LRR resistance proteins through domain pattern recognition, and similar frameworks have been adopted for specific gene families across plant genomes. Nevertheless, these traditional approaches depend heavily on handcrafted features and predefined templates, making them sensitive to sequence length and intron complexity. Their performance tends to degrade for long genes with non-canonical splicing sites, leading to boundary ambiguity and omission of key functional motifs.

The recent rise of deep learning has introduced new possibilities for predicting functional elements within complex biological sequences [14, 15]. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have achieved notable performance in a variety of tasks related to gene expression prediction from DNA sequences [16–19]. For example, DeepARG [20], DeepGO [21], and DeepGOPlus [22] leverage CNNs to extract local sequence patterns and integrate gene ontology or functional annotation signals, thereby improving antibiotic resistance gene classification and large-scale protein function prediction. Similarly, PICRUSt2 [23] expanded reference-based inference for metagenomic gene function profiling. However, these models share a common limitation, namely their reliance on short and fixed-length input windows. As a result, they typically consider only sequence regions within approximately 20 kb upstream or downstream of transcription start sites, which hampers their ability to model long-range dependencies between distal regulatory elements and target genes, thereby reducing their effectiveness in long-sequence functional gene prediction tasks [24].

To overcome the limitations in capturing long-range dependencies, Long Short- Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been widely adopted for genomic annotation, protein sequence prediction, and non-coding RNA function analysis [25–29]. For instance, ATTCry [30] and DSA-Transformer [31] both integrate LSTM with attention mechanisms, enabling richer sequence representations and improving prediction accuracy in protein function prediction tasks. Additionally, several studies have explored hybrid networks that combine CNNs and LSTMs to simultaneously capture local conserved motifs and global dependencies [32]. DeepFormer integrates CNNs with a Flow-Attention mechanism to model cross-region information flow, while BiLSTM-CRF frameworks demonstrate the potential for precise boundary annotation [33, 34]. Despite the progress achieved by these computational approaches in various biological sequence prediction tasks, still lack the capacity to capture boundary in gene structures. Some studies take a different route by incorporating Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) to represent latent state transitions along sequences, as exemplified by [35–37]. Nonetheless, these methods often lack tight integration with biologically meaningful functional cues, making it difficult to constrain redundant state paths or correct false-positive predictions effectively.

In this study, we present a dual-channel disease-resistance gene identification framework that integrates biological constraint information—termed CLAP-HMM (CNN-LSTM-Attention enhanced Predictor guided by HMM) designed to predict structurally complex novel resistance genes in long-sequence species such as trees. The model employs a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) to extract local sequence features, a bidirectional LSTM to capture long-range dependencies, and an attention mechanism to enhance the interpretability of key feature regions. Simultaneously, ProHint functional cues are introduced as biological constraints on structural states, guiding an HMM-based framework that integrates deep learning with state transition modeling to achieve precise delineation of gene structural boundaries. Systematic evaluations on R gene datasets from multiple tree species reveal that CLAP-HMM surpasses current mainstream methods in both structural localization accuracy and functional prediction consistency, exhibiting robust generalization. This tool provides a novel, biologically interpretable approach for the accurate identification of resistance genes in long genomic sequences of higher plants.

Methods

Construction of datasets

In this study, resistance gene (R gene) sequences from seven tree species, Populus nigra, Populus euphratica, Populus trichocarpa, Populus alba, Eucalyptus grandis, Salix purpurea, and Salix viminalis were selected as the research objects. These species were selected because they represent diverse evolutionary backgrounds and span multiple genera with distinct repertoires of resistance genes. They also have high quality reference genomes and well annotated resistance gene datasets, which provide a reliable foundation for training. Moreover, the tree species included in this study contain long and repetitive resistance gene regions, making them well suited for evaluating the ability of the model to capture complex patterns across full length genomic sequences. The genomes of these species are characterized by ultra-long sequences, redundant introns, and a high proportion of repetitive elements, representing typical examples of “long genomic sequences.” These features make them suitable model systems for studying complex gene structure modeling and resistance gene prediction. The corresponding gene sequences were obtained from the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and subsequently converted into FASTA format and standardized for further analysis.

To ensure the quality of gene sequence data and maintain consistency for model training, we first applied length-based filtering to all gene sequences collected from the raw datasets. After extracting the target DNA sequences of 7 species, we calculated the length of each sequence. The sequences with lengths between 1,000 and 6,000 bp were retained, while the remaining sequences were eliminated. We found that resistance-related genes are predominantly distributed within this length range, including well-characterized families such as NBS-LRR [38]. Additionally, this range increases the likelihood of retaining complete gene models and reduces the inclusion of truncated fragments or tandem duplications, which could introduce noise into model training.

In the dataset, positive samples correspond to genes that have been experimentally verified to possess disease resistance functions, while negative samples were randomly selected from genes not annotated as resistance-related, ensuring a roughly balanced number of positive and negative instances. All gene sequences were numerically encoded using one-hot encoding and standardized to eliminate potential biases in base frequency distributions, thereby enhancing the consistency of feature representation. The final cross-species disease resistance gene prediction dataset comprised a total of 12,630 gene sequences across the seven woody species, including 6,293 positive and 6,337 negative samples. The average sequence length was 2,083 base pairs. The dataset was split into training and test sets using an 8:2 ratio.

The overall framework of CLAP-HMM

CLAP-HMM is a deep learning framework featuring dual-channel feature extraction, consisting of four main components: sequence feature extraction, protein function hinting, feature fusion, and state classification modules, as shown in Fig. 1. First, in the sequence feature extraction channel, a hybrid CNN-LSTM-Attention framework is employed to model the input DNA sequences at multiple levels, effectively capturing both local and global features within the sequence. This results in high-dimensional sequence feature vectors. Simultaneously, ProtHint v2.3.0 performs protein-level functional annotation on the genomic sequences, generating structured functional hint vectors. These vectors serve as biological priors to guide the model alongside sequence-based features. In the feature fusion module, feature vectors from the sequence-based channel and the ProtHint derived functional channel are first subjected to Z-score normalization. A Gated Concatenation mechanism is then applied to integrate the heterogeneous features into a unified joint feature space, enabling comprehensive representation learning. Finally, a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) is adopted as the classifier to perform hidden state discrimination. The joint feature vectors are used for binary classification (0: non-resistant, 1: resistant), thereby enabling accurate identification of potential disease resistance genes.Fig. 1The overall framework of CLAP-HMM. Sequence feature extraction channel

To effectively extract structural feature information embedded in genomic sequences, we adopt a hybrid CNN-LSTM-Attention model that integrates local pattern recognition, contextual dependency modeling, and critical region awareness. This design enhances the model’s capacity to represent potential functional sites and regulatory signals. The convolutional module consists of three 1D convolutional layers with kernel sizes of [5, 3, 3] and filter numbers of [32, 64, 128], respectively, designed to capture local patterns across the input sequences. The second stage, functional scoring, evaluates the quality of the generated hints using metrics that measure the evolutionary conservation of genomic fragments, such as Alignment of Entire Exon (AEE), Intron Border Alignment (IBA), and Intron Mapping Coverage (IMC). These metrics help ensure that only high-confidence, biologically relevant hints are retained for use in the model.

For further technical details, including the formulas and the specific scoring functions, please refer to Supplementary Material S1.

Model training and hyperparameter setting

All experiments in this study were conducted using the TensorFlow 2.8 framework and trained on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU’s. During model training, DNA sequences were encoded in 6-mer format. A 5-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. The Adam optimizer was used, with a batch size of 64 and an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−3, and the learning rate attenuated to half of the original value after every 10 rounds of training to prevent the model from jumping out of the optimal solution.

Results

Interpretability analysis of sequence feature recognition

To interpret the model’s focus on genomic sequences, we applied the DeepLIFT (Deep Learning Important FeaTures) algorithm [40], which assigns nucleotide resolution contribution scores by backpropagating the influence of model predictions to the input. This highlights regions most relevant to the prediction.

Unlike gradient-based methods, DeepLIFT avoids vanishing gradients by comparing activations between the actual and reference inputs. It attributes activation changes layer-by-layer, ultimately mapping scores to individual nucleotides. Positive, negative, or near-zero scores indicate whether a nucleotide promotes, suppresses, or negligibly affects the prediction for the positive class (e.g., high expression or resistance). The contribution maps are visualized in Fig. 2, showing nucleotide-level contributions around the transcription start site (TSS) and transcription termination site (TTS).Fig. 2Nucleotide resolution significance scores near transcription start site (TSS) and transcription termination site (TTS)

Nucleotide resolution significance scores near transcription start site (TSS) and transcription termination site (TTS)

The results demonstrate that the model consistently exhibits strong attention to specific sequence fragments across different species. These attention regions are predominantly located upstream and downstream of the TSS and TTS. In the TSS analysis, a markedly enhanced response is observed in the downstream region (+1 to + 500 bp), suggesting that the model has captured regulatory features around promoters and adjacent cis-elements. For TTS, the model shows high-contribution regions upstream (−1000 to 0 bp), reflecting its ability to recognize termination signal-related sequence motifs. Meanwhile, we calculated the overlap between the top 5% most important loci identified by DeepLIFT and known motifs such as promoter regions and transcription factor binding sites. The results (Supplementary Material S2) show a 60% overlap with statistically significant enrichment (p = 0.0059, Odds Ratio = 2.33), indicating that the model captures biologically relevant sequence features.

These findings indicate that the model can accurately focus on functionally significant regulatory regions. The learned sequence features possess clear biological relevance, thereby validating the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed model.

Discussion

In this study, our results demonstrate that integrating deep representation learning with biologically informed structural constraints can substantially improve the detection of full length resistance (R) genes from long and complex genomic regions.

As a supervised approach, a comprehensive evaluation of CLAP-HMM confirms its superior performance across all metrics, with this improvement stemming from how its architecture aligns with the known properties of R genes. The CNN-LSTM-Attention module captures multilayered sequence signals, allowing the model to integrate motif level features with broader structural patterns that often extend beyond local sequence windows. The addition of functional hints from protein level annotation introduces priors that reflect evolutionary and biochemical constraints, improving the model ability to distinguish genuine R gene signatures from background variation. The HMM component further contributes by shaping predictions according to plausible state transitions along gene structures, which is particularly important for long sequences containing interspersed domains, partial motifs, or irregular spacing. These enhancements help the model approximate the hierarchical organization of real R genes, providing interpretability and biological coherence that purely data driven architectures often lack.

The effectiveness of CLAP-HMM is further supported by its ability to generalize across species. The architecture captures structural regularities and functional features that are conserved in R genes, allowing the model to maintain predictive capability even in species with divergent evolutionary backgrounds. Attention distributions correspond closely with known functional regions, including promoters, conserved domains, and terminator regions, highlighting that the model not only encodes predictive sequence patterns but also aligns with biologically interpretable features. This combination of generalization and interpretability underscores the capacity of model to approximate real gene organization across diverse genomic contexts.

In summary, the strong performance of CLAP-HMM in long-sequence R gene prediction stems from its innovations in feature extraction, biological information integration, and attention mechanism design. These advantages deepen our understanding of the complex relationship between gene structure and function and offer a useful reference for resistance gene identification, functional gene annotation, and molecular breeding in plants.

Nonetheless, CLAP HMM has several limitations that require further examination. First, the combination of multiple deep learning modules with probabilistic state modeling increases computational demands, especially when the model is applied to genome wide prediction in large and repetitive forest genomes. Specifically, using our minimum computational resources, completing a full training analysis requires approximately 3–4 days. Second, although the model performs well across genera within woody species, its scalability to non tree species remains insufficiently assessed. This is mainly because our study focuses on long R genes in complex woody genomes, whereas herbaceous plants, crops, or microorganisms often have more compact genomes or markedly different R gene architectures. Therefore, developing models with improved transferability that can accurately predict resistance genes across diverse gene architectures will be a key focus of future work. Third, the HMM component depends on predefined structural boundaries (e.g., resistant vs. non-resistant regions), which may limit its effectiveness in handling atypical domains or irregular splicing patterns. Future studies will focus on reducing computational costs and improving cross species adaptability by incorporating graph based priors, semi supervised structure recognition, and extending the framework to other gene families with different structural or regulatory characteristics [42].
