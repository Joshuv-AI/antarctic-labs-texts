The Potential Protective Effects of Resveratrol Against Cabazitaxel-Induced Oxidative Stress and Cardiotoxicity in Rats
Authors: Hadeer Khaled, Ahmed Farag, Ahmed Elfadadny, Takashi Tanaka, Hany Hassan, Zongru Li, Ahmed Mandour, Ryou Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cabazitaxel, an anti-prostate cancer agent, is associated with dose-dependent cardiotoxicity mediated by oxidative stress. This study investigated cabazitaxel-induced cardiac injury, the utility of intraventricular pressure gradients for early detection, and the potential protective effects of resveratrol. Twenty male Sprague Dawley rats were allocated into four groups: control, therapeutic cabazitaxel (0.5 mg/kg/week, intraperitoneally), toxic cabazitaxel (1.5 mg/kg/week, intraperitoneally), and toxic cabazitaxel + resveratrol (1.5 mg/kg/week cabazitaxel + 10 mg/kg/day resveratrol, intraperitoneally) for 28 days. Cardiac function was assessed via conventional echocardiography, electrocardiography, and intraventricular pressure gradients under anesthesia. Blood samples and heart tissues were collected for further biochemical and histopathological evaluation. Toxic cabazitaxel administration induced significant RR prolongation and bradycardia compared with controls (RR: 0.21 ± 0.02 vs. 0.18 ± 0.02 s; heart rate: 285.88 ± 31.04 vs. 330.40 ± 27.24 bpm), accompanied by aortic dilatation (aortic diameter: 3.73 ± 0.29 vs. 3.28 ± 0.21 mm), reduced acceleration time to ejection time ratio (0.21 ± 0.04 vs. 0.32 ± 0.12), increased derivatives of reactive oxygen metabolites: 394.75 ± 63.47 vs. 324.80 ± 15.56 U.CARR), elevated platelet counts (121.10 ± 26.48 vs. 81.65 ± 6.49 104/µL), decreased hemoglobin levels (14.26 ± 2.02 vs. 17.66 ± 1.014 g/dL), along with structural myocardial damage. Resveratrol co-administration reduced derivatives of reactive oxygen metabolites (372.40 ± 33.02), normalized aortic diameter, and improved acceleration time to ejection time ratio (3.09 ± 0.40 mm; 0.28 ± 0.06), enhanced electrocardiographic parameters (RR: 0.17 ± 0.01 s; heart rate: 352.24 ± 20.567), restored hemoglobin and platelet counts (15.94 ± 1.39 g/dL; 98.38 ± 28.41 104/µL), and preserved myocardial architecture. However, intraventricular pressure gradient indices remained elevated, and persistent thinning of the left ventricular posterior wall at end-diastole and end-systole was observed. Introduction

Cardiovascular complications associated with cancer therapy constitute a significant contributor to both oncological and cardiovascular mortality, thereby critically affecting patients’ prognosis and overall quality of life [1]. Although numerous oncological treatment regimens are known to exert varying degrees of cardiotoxicity, chemotherapy-induced cardiac injury remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among long-term cancer survivors [2, 3]. Taxane-based chemotherapeutic agents, including paclitaxel, docetaxel, and cabazitaxel (CBZ), have emerged as cornerstone treatments for advanced prostate cancer since the early 2000 s [4]. CBZ, the most recent semi-synthetic taxane derivative, was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2010 for the management of castration-resistant prostate cancer [5]. Despite its therapeutic efficacy, CBZ is associated with multiple serious adverse effects, including neutropenia, hypersensitivity reactions, gastrointestinal disturbances, and renal toxicity [5–7]. Notably, although the clinical use of CBZ continues to expand, current evidence regarding its toxicity profile—particularly its potential cardiotoxic properties—remains limited and insufficiently characterized [8].

Substantial research efforts have focused on identifying effective therapeutic strategies to prevent or mitigate cardiovascular damage induced by chemotherapeutic agents [9]. Among the various emerging approaches, dietary polyphenols with potent antioxidant properties—particularly naturally derived antioxidants—have attracted significant interest as promising candidates for cardiac protection [10]. Resveratrol (RES), a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound abundant in grapes and berries, exhibits a broad spectrum of biological activities [11]. Experimental studies across several animal models have demonstrated that RES reduces the formation of reactive oxygen species and ameliorates oxidative damage in cardiac tissue [12, 13]. In addition, RES is widely recognized for its diverse pharmacological effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antihypertensive, anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, and cardioprotective actions [14, 15]. Oxidative stress is a pathological state arising from a disruption of the equilibrium between the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the capacity of endogenous antioxidant systems to neutralize or eliminate these reactive intermediates through detoxification and degradation pathways [16]. A substantial body of evidence indicates that oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases [17]. Among the biomarkers associated with oxidative status, derivatives of reactive oxygen metabolites (d-ROMs) represent intermediate products generated in the early stages of oxidative signaling cascades [18]. For this reason, d-ROMs are considered reliable indicators of early oxidative damage and may offer a valuable opportunity for timely therapeutic intervention [19]. Conversely, the Biological Antioxidant Potential (BAP) assay is applied to assess the antioxidant capacity of plasma, reflecting the major circulating non-enzymatic antioxidants—including vitamin C, vitamin E, uric acid, and bilirubin—that collectively form the primary barrier against oxidative insult [20, 21].

Previous studies have suggested that the cytotoxicity of CBZ is largely attributable to its ability to promote excessive ROS generation while simultaneously suppressing endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems [22].

Ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) are commonly used echocardiographic indices to assess cardiac function [23]. However, these parameters lack sensitivity for detecting early contractile impairment associated with chemotherapy-induced cardiac dysfunction. Notably, a decline in EF in cancer patients typically occurs at advanced stages of cardiac dysfunction, often necessitating interruption of chemotherapy and adversely affecting survival outcomes [24]. Therefore, the identification of a more sensitive and earlier diagnostic index for chemotherapy-related cardiac dysfunction remains critically needed. While the diagnosis of chemotherapy-induced cardiac dysfunction has largely focused on systolic impairment, growing evidence indicates that diastolic dysfunction occurs earlier and may serve as a precursor to overt systolic failure [25–27] Consequently, increasing emphasis has been placed on assessing diastolic function as a reliable, noninvasive approach to the early detection of cardiac dysfunction. Color M-mode echocardiography (CMME) allows assessment of intraventricular pressure gradients (IVPG), representing early diastolic pressure differences within the left ventricle when apical pressure falls below basal pressure. IVPG derived from CMME offers a reproducible, less invasive alternative for evaluating diastolic function compared with catheterization [28–30]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate IVPG as an early indicator of CBZ-induced cardiotoxicity. Although IVPG has been investigated in other chemotherapy models, its diagnostic value in taxane-related cardiac dysfunction remains unexplored, and data on CBZ-associated oxidative stress and early functional impairment are still limited. Therefore, the present study was designed to comprehensively assess the impact of CBZ administration on cardiac performance, with special emphasis on the dose-response relationship, using advanced diagnostic modalities such as IVPG. Additionally, this study investigates the influence of CBZ on oxidative stress biomarkers, including d-ROMs and BAP pathways in cardiac tissue. A secondary aim is to evaluate whether RES exerts therapeutic and cardioprotective effects that attenuate CBZ-induced cardiac injury. We hypothesized that CBZ induces early cardiac dysfunction, detectable through IVPG assessment and oxidative stress biomarkers, and that RES administration may attenuate these alterations.

Materials and Methods

Blinding and Experimental Design

The present study was designed as a single-blind experimental investigation. Animals were randomly allocated to the experimental groups using a simple randomization method, with numbered identifiers drawn manually by the principal investigator (first author) before the initiation of the experimental procedures. Each animal had an equal probability of assignment to any group. Outcome assessments were conducted in a blinded manner to minimize potential bias. ECG recordings and analyses were performed in a blinded manner by an independent investigator who was unaware of group allocation. Histopathological evaluation was performed by an experienced pathologist who was unaware of group allocation. Furthermore, biochemical analyses were conducted using coded samples to ensure an objective and unbiased assessment.

Animals and Experimental Design

A total of 20 adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (6 months old), weighing 677.36 ± 50.19 g, were used in this study. Animals were housed under controlled laboratory conditions (temperature: 20 °C; 12 h light/12 h dark cycle) with ad libitum access to water and 250 g of food per group daily.

Rats were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n = 5per group):

CBZ (MedChemExpress, USA) was administered intraperitoneally (IP) once weekly for four consecutive weeks at doses of 0.5 mg/kg (therapeutic CBZ group), and 1.5 mg/kg (toxic CBZ and toxic CBZ + RES groups) [8, 31]. RES (Fluorochem Ltd., United Kingdom) dissolved in DMSO was administered I.P. at a dose of 10 mg/kg daily, as previously described [32–34]. At the end of the experimental period, all animals underwent conventional echocardiography, Color M-mode echocardiography (CMME), and ECG. Subsequently, rats were euthanized under isoflurane overdose, and cardiac tissues were harvested for histopathological evaluation (Fig. 1).

Blood Sampling

Two blood samples were collected from each rat. One sample was collected in a heparinized tube for plasma separation and subsequent measurement of d-ROMs and BAP, and the second sample was collected in an EDTA tube for complete blood count (CBC) analysis.

Complete Blood Count

CBC parameters were evaluated using an automated hematology analyzer (Celltac α MEK-6558, Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan).

Measurement of Plasma d-ROMs, BAP, and Oxidative Stress Index

Plasma levels of d-ROMs and BAP were quantified using a commercial oxidative–reductive analyzer kit (REDOXLIBRA, WISMERLL, Whismar Co. Ltd./Wismar Research Institute, Tokyo, Japan). d-ROMs values were expressed in Carratelli units (U.CARR), whereas BAP values were expressed in µmol/L.

The oxidative stress index (OSI) was computed as the ratio of oxidant to antioxidant levels, representing the systemic imbalance between reactive oxygen species production and antioxidant capacity [35].

Conventional Echocardiography

Conventional echocardiography, CMME, and ECG were conducted on each rat on the same day using a ProSound F75 ultrasound system equipped with a 12 MHz transducer and joint ECG (Hitachi-Aloka Medical Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Measurements adhered to the guidelines of the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) [36, 37]. Isoflurane anesthesia was used to ensure minimal stress during image acquisition.

Tissue Doppler imaging was used to determine systolic (s′) and early diastolic (e′) velocities at the lateral and septal mitral annulus. E/lateral e′ and E/septal e′ ratios were calculated accordingly. Aortic flow measurements were used to compute ACCT/ET [38].

Color M-Mode Echocardiography for Intraventricular Pressure Gradients

Intraventricular pressure gradients (IVPG) were assessed non-invasively using Color M-mode echocardiography (CMME). The ultrasound probe was positioned to acquire IVPG measurements from the left apical four-chamber view, ensuring clear visualization of the entire mitral inflow pathway extending to the left ventricular (LV) apex. CMME settings were optimized according to Ohara et al. (2012) [39, 40]. Sweep speed was adjusted to 300 mm/s, with a − 64 color base shift to enhance Nyquist limits. For the analysis of intraventricular pressure gradient (IVPG), key temporal parameters were extracted from conventional echocardiographic recordings, including the interval between aortic valve opening and closure, the duration from the onset of the Q wave to the initiation of early mitral inflow (E wave), and the time from the onset of the Q wave to the peak of the E wave. These measured values were subsequently entered manually into the MATLAB analysis program for computation. To improve measurement reliability, five consecutive cardiac cycles with high-quality recordings were selected for analysis, and the mean value obtained from these measurements was used for each animal. Images were stored for offline analysis in MATLAB (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Electrocardiography

ECG recordings were obtained using needle electrodes connected to Power Lab hardware (ML880 Power Lab 16/30, AD Instruments) and analyzed via Lab Chart Pro software (Lab Chart v8, AD Instruments) [43]. Animals were anesthetized using isoflurane delivered via a nose cone to minimize movement during the procedure. The rats were placed in the supine position, with all limbs secured separately to maintain adequate separation. The forelimbs and left hind limb were used for ECG recording in standard limb leads, while the right hind limb was connected to a ground electrode. The ECG parameters, including RR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval, were measured from the lead II recordings and analyzed. ECG recordings were obtained for 3 min for each animal in all experimental groups. The QT-corrected value was calculated using Bazett’s formula [44], according to the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:QTc\:=\:\frac{QT}{\sqrt{RR}}$$\end{document}

Histopathological Examination

Hearts were dissected immediately post-euthanasia and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Samples were processed using an automated tissue processor (LEICA TP 1020, Biosystem, Tokyo, Japan), sectioned at 5 μm thickness, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological sections were examined under light microscopy, and images were captured using CellSens Standard software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For each sample, multiple non-overlapping fields were randomly selected and analyzed at consistent magnification. A blinded histopathologist evaluated the sections for mononuclear cell infiltration, interstitial edema, myocyte necrosis, and structural disorganization of cardiac fibers. Histopathological evaluation was performed based on a previously validated semi-quantitative scoring system developed by Shi et al. [45], commonly used to assess myocardial injury.

Each parameter was scored independently using a semi-quantitative scale: 0 = no detectable lesions; 1 = mild (minimal focal involvement); 2 = moderate (multifocal involvement affecting a limited area); 3 = severe (extensive involvement); 4 = very severe (diffuse and widespread lesions). To enhance reliability, scoring was performed in a blinded manner, and a subset of sections was re-evaluated independently to ensure consistency of observations. The overall histopathological score for each section was calculated by summing the scores of all evaluated parameters. Nine sections per group were analyzed, and the mean score was calculated for statistical [45].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows (Version 16.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Multiple group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. A two-way ANOVA was used to analyze graphics illustrating the time course of changes. Correlations between d-ROMs and conventional echocardiographic parameters were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation and linear regression, and the coefficient of determination (R²) was computed. A post hoc analysis was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4, the University of Kiel, Germany) to achieve 86% power based on the observed effect size. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California).

Results

General Manifestations and Toxicity Profiling

Throughout the experimental period, animal attitude, mental status, general reactions, discharges, body weight (BW), and food consumption were continuously monitored. Intraperitoneal administration of CBZ elicited a transient abdominal constriction response that appeared immediately after injection. Rats in the toxic CBZ group progressively demonstrated lethargy, reduced locomotor activity, weakness, and marked body weight loss. No gross morphological abnormalities of the body surface and no mortality were observed in any group.

Body weight data are expressed in Fig. 2 . Rats in the toxic CBZ and toxic CBZ + RES groups exhibited significantly greater BW loss (− 8.0133 ± 6.08 and − 7.6433 ± 6.09 g, respectively; P < 0.05), whereas both the control (4.8500 ± 3.42 g) and therapeutic CBZ (1.0183 ± 4.73 g) groups displayed a continuous BW gain throughout the study.

Food intake patterns across the experimental duration are shown in Fig. 3 . A marked fluctuation in daily food consumption was observed in all CBZ-treated rats relative to controls. During the first three weekly CBZ injections, food intake was markedly reduced in both the toxic CBZ and toxic CBZ + RES groups. Specifically, the toxic CBZ group demonstrated significant reductions during the first (62.43 ± 12.21 g; P = 0.0001), second (51.96 ± 30.94 g; P = 0.0001), and third (104.67 ± 17.51 g; P = 0.007) doses compared with controls (116.33 ± 16.97, 133.14 ± 24.10, and 127.58 ± 8.91 g, respectively). Similarly, the toxic CBZ + RES group exhibited reduced food intake during the first (88.43 ± 13.77 g; P = 0.001), second (73.64 ± 17.86 g; P = 0.0001), and third (83.84 ± 15.88 g; P = 0.0001) doses relative to controls.

Following the fourth CBZ dose, the therapeutic CBZ group also displayed reduced food intake (89.16 ± 10.19 g; P = 0.02) compared with controls (102.33 ± 11.81 g), whereas both the toxic CBZ and toxic CBZ + RES groups recovered to near-normal food consumption following the final treatment.

Effects of Cabazitaxel and Resveratrol on Cardiac Mass Indices

The Heart weight (HW) and body weight (BW) are illustrated in Fig. 4 . A significant decrease in HW was detected in both the toxic CBZ group (1.72 ± 0.15 g; P = 0.009) and the CBZ + RES group (1.70 ± 0.08 g; P = 0.003), in comparison with the control group (1.95 ± 0.03 g). Additionally, BW demonstrated a pronounced decline in the toxic CBZ group (617.22 ± 39.95 g; P < 0.0001), CBZ + RES group (597.76 ± 46.29 g; P < 0.0001), and therapeutic CBZ group (681.90 ± 37.40 g; P = 0.004), compared with the control group (765.66 ± 27.63 g). Notably, no significant alterations in HW were detected in the therapeutic CBZ group, indicating partial structural preservation of cardiac mass under the therapeutic dosing regimen.

Impact of Cabazitaxel and Resveratrol on Oxidative Stress and Antioxidant Status

The oxidative stress profile, including d-ROMs, BAP, and OSI across all experimental groups, is summarized in Table 1. A significant elevation in d-ROMs and OSI was detected in the toxic CBZ group (394.75 ± 63.47; 16.67 ± 3.05, respectively) compared with the control group (324.80 ± 15.56; 13.00 ± 1.41) (p < 0.05 and p = 0.05, respectively), confirming the strong oxidative imbalance accompanying toxic CBZ exposure.

Co-administration of resveratrol attenuated this oxidative burden. The CBZ + RES group exhibited lower d-ROMs and OSI values (372.40 ± 33.02; 13.60 ± 2.30) than the toxic CBZ group, although the differences did not reach statistical significance compared with the control (p = 0.10 and p = 0.69, respectively).

Across all groups, no significant variation in BAP levels was detected, suggesting that CBZ-induced oxidative stress was driven primarily by excessive ROS production rather than by impairment of the antioxidant buffering capacity.

Hematological Alterations Following Cabazitaxel and Resveratrol Treatment

The hemogram analysis presented in Fig. 5 demonstrated notable hematological dysregulation in response to toxic CBZ administration. The toxic CBZ group showed a significant decrease in red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), and hematocrit (HCT) values (672.20 ± 111.45; 14.26 ± 2.02; 36.48 ± 5.66, respectively; p = 0.005), accompanied by a marked elevation in PLT (121.10 ± 26.48; p < 0.05) when compared with the control group (RBCs: 812.80 ± 39.78; Hb: 17.66 ± 1.01; HCT: 44.18 ± 2.99; PLT: 81.65 ± 6.48). In the toxic CBZ + RES group, RBCs (716.40 ± 41.40) and HCT (39.04 ± 3.21) remained significantly reduced compared with the control (p < 0.05). Moreover, Hb concentration and PLT were restored toward normal levels (15.94 ± 1.39; 98.38 ± 28.413), demonstrating the partial protective effect of RES against CBZ-induced Hematological toxicity.

Conventional Echocardiographic Assessment of Cardiac Function

Conventional echocardiographic parameters are presented in Table 2. In rats treated with toxic doses of CBZ, a significant increase in aortic outflow diameter during diastole (AODd) was observed (3.73 ± 0.29; p < 0.05) compared with the control group (3.28 ± 0.21). Conversely, ACCT/ET, left ventricular posterior wall thickness during diastole (LVPWd), and during systole (LVPWs) were markedly reduced (0.21 ± 0.04; 1.97 ± 0.27; 2.72 ± 0.38, respectively; p < 0.05) relative to their corresponding control values (0.32 ± 0.12; 2.49 ± 0.37; 3.24 ± 0.26).

In the CBZ + RES group, echocardiographic impairments were less pronounced. Significant reductions persisted only in LVPWd (1.80 ± 0.20) and LVPWs (2.61 ± 0.22; p < 0.05), while other parameters, including AODd and ACCT/ET, showed no significant deviation from the control group.

Correlation and Regression Analysis Between Oxidative Stress and IVPG Indices

The correlations between the oxidative stress biomarkers d-ROMs, BAP, and OSI and the IVPG parameters are summarized in Table 4. A significant positive correlation was observed between d-ROMs and total IVPG (r = 0.47), mid-to-apical IVPG (r = 0.58), and mid-IVPG (r = 0.74). In addition, OSI showed positive correlations with both mid-to-apical IVPG and mid-IVPG, indicating that higher oxidative stress levels were associated with greater intraventricular pressure gradients. In contrast, BAP showed no significant correlation with any IVPG parameter.

Linear regression analysis further demonstrated that d-ROMs and OSI had significant predictive effects on total IVPG, mid-to-apical IVPG, and mid-IVPG. Specifically, d-ROMs explained 20%, 38%, and 55% of the variance in these parameters (R² = 0.20, 0.38, and 0.55, respectively), whereas OSI accounted for 34%, 29%, and 38% of the variance (R² = 0.34, 0.29, and 0.38, respectively). These findings collectively highlight a strong link between systemic oxidative stress and impaired intraventricular diastolic hemodynamics.

Histopathological Evaluation of Cardiac Tissue

Histological analysis of cardiac tissue is presented in Fig. 7 . In the control group, myocardial architecture was well-preserved, with cardiomyocytes exhibiting centrally located, large, euchromatic nuclei and minimal artifacts from tissue handling. In contrast, the toxic CBZ group displayed severe structural alterations, including clear demarcation between perfused and non-perfused myocardial regions. H&E staining revealed extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, interstitial edema, and ischemic necrosis, along with distorted and fragmented cardiomyocytes, reflecting profound myocardial injury.

Histopathological examination of the CBZ + RES group demonstrated substantial improvement, with reduced mononuclear cell infiltration, absence of edema, and better preservation of myocardial fiber organization. Similarly, the therapeutic CBZ group exhibited milder lesions than the toxic CBZ group (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In the present study, food consumption initially decreased but gradually returned to near-normal levels by the end of the experimental period, consistent with previous reports [46]. In contrast, body weight declined rapidly following CBZ administration, consistent with prior findings [46, 47]. This reduction likely reflects the systemic toxicity of CBZ. Indeed, body weight change is a well-established indicator of antitumor agent–induced systemic toxicity [48]. Our findings also demonstrated a significant reduction in heart weight (HW) in the toxic CBZ group, consistent with previous studies using doxorubicin [49]. Although RES has been shown to attenuate doxorubicin-induced HW loss, it did not fully restore HW in the current study. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in experimental parameters, including pretreatment duration, RES dose, rat age, and route of administration. The pronounced decline in HW and BW in CBZ-treated rats may also be linked to enhanced catabolic processes and reduced caloric intake, resulting in decreased appetite, fatigue, and overall systemic stress [50]. These findings highlight the severity of CBZ-induced systemic and cardiac toxicity. The primary mechanism underlying chemotherapy-induced cardiac injury is the overproduction of ROS, which promotes cellular damage and disrupts normal physiological responses, a condition broadly defined as oxidative stress [51]. To explore the mechanisms and pathways underlying CBZ-induced cardiac alterations, we assessed oxidative stress and antioxidant status using d-ROMs, OSI, and BAP, which are composite, dynamic markers of oxidative stress and have gained considerable attention in recent years [52]. Our results demonstrated a significant elevation of d-ROMs and OSI in the toxic CBZ group relative to other experimental groups, consistent with previous studies reporting increased d-ROMs in doxorubicin-treated rats [53, 54]. Interestingly, RES co-administration effectively normalized d-ROMs and OSI values, highlighting its potent antioxidant activity [55–57]. No statistically significant differences in BAP levels were observed compared with controls, indicating that overall antioxidant capacity was largely preserved. This observation aligns with prior studies and may reflect a compensatory upregulation of endogenous antioxidants in response to heightened ROS production [53, 54, 58]. The hematopoietic system is highly sensitive to chemical toxicity and serves as an important indicator of pathological alterations. Consistent with Liu et al. (2018), our study demonstrated a significant decline in RBCs, hemoglobin (Hb), and hematocrit (HCT) in the toxic CBZ group, likely reflecting impaired erythropoiesis, inhibition of heme synthesis, or oxidative damage to RBCs [59, 60]. CBZ has been reported to bind RBCs, leading to hemolysis and anemia [61]. Interestingly, platelet counts were elevated in the toxic CBZ group, which may reflect a dose-dependent effect of CBZ or inflammatory cytokine–mediated thrombopoiesis [46, 62–64]. Elevated platelet levels can increase the risk of thrombosis, highlighting a potential systemic impact of CBZ beyond direct hematological toxicity. Co-administration of RES attenuated CBZ-induced hematological toxicity, as indicated by restoration of Hb levels and PLT counts relative to the CBZ toxic group. These protective effects are likely mediated through antioxidant activity, enhanced erythroid maturation, and stabilization of RBC membranes against oxidative stress [65–68].

ECG is a critical tool for detecting cardiac injury. In this study, CBZ administration induced notable ECG abnormalities, including prolonged RR intervals and bradycardia, consistent with previous reports in doxorubicin-treated rats [69–71]. Prolonged R intervals reflect delayed ventricular repolarization and a negative chronotropic effect [72], while the RR interval directly determines heart rate. Our findings indicate that CBZ-induced bradycardia resulted from an extended RR interval, reflecting impaired cardiac electrical activity. Importantly, co-administration of RES effectively mitigated these ECG alterations, restoring both RR interval and heart rate toward normal levels, highlighting its cardioprotective potential against CBZ-induced electrophysiological disturbances.

Taxanes are widely used in the treatment of prostate cancer and are associated with cardiotoxic effects, including heart block, hypertension, arrhythmias, and congestive heart failure [73, 74]. Although the overall incidence is relatively low (2.3–8% with docetaxel and paclitaxel) [75], the risk remains clinically significant, particularly when taxanes are combined with other cardiotoxic agents [76]. The precise mechanisms underlying taxane-induced cardiotoxicity are not fully elucidated; however, proposed mechanisms include disruption of the cardiac Purkinje fiber system and impairment of autonomic regulation of cardiac electrical activity [77]. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammation, and ferroptosis contribute to paclitaxel-induced cardiac injury [78], while docetaxel has been shown to promote oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction through increased cellular apoptosis [79]. CBZ administration induced structural and functional cardiac alterations, including reductions in LVPWd and LVPWs, consistent with previous findings in doxorubicin-treated rats [80], where such decreases were early indicators of cardiac remodeling. The observed decrease in ACCT/ET likely reflects multiple factors, including reduced LV posterior wall thickness, compensatory prolongation of systolic duration in response to increased systolic load [81–83]. RES co-administration partially restored AOD and ACCT/ET ratios, indicating partially improved cardiac function. Previous studies support RES as a therapeutic agent that attenuates cardiac remodeling, pressure overload, fibrosis, and hypertrophy [84, 85].

During early diastole, IVPG produces a suction effect that promotes blood transfer from the left atrium to the left ventricle, accounting for nearly 80% of ventricular filling [86]. Therefore, IVPG represents a reliable parameter for longitudinal evaluation of diastolic function in both healthy and pathological conditions across humans and animal models of various ages [87], including rat models [88]. Our study demonstrated elevated total, mid-to-apical, and mid-IVPG in both the toxic CBZ and CBZ + RES groups. Mid and mid-to-apical IVPG reflect elastic recoil and active ventricular relaxation, while basal IVPG is primarily influenced by left atrial pressure [89, 90]. The observed elevations suggest impaired ventricular relaxation and early diastolic dysfunction. While such changes may also be associated with increased atrial pressure, E/e′—a well-established indicator of left atrial pressure [91, 92] —remained unchanged in the present study. Furthermore, both EF and FS remained within normal ranges, indicating preserved systolic function. In contrast, IVPG indices were significantly elevated, suggesting the presence of early ventricular dysfunction preceding overt systolic impairment. These findings suggest that IVPG may serve as a sensitive marker of early alterations in cardiac function before the onset of overt clinical manifestations in our model. RES treatment did not fully normalize IVPG indices, likely due to the short treatment duration, which was adequate to reduce remodeling but insufficient to achieve complete cardiac recovery. These findings support IVPG as a sensitive early marker of CBZ-induced cardiac toxicity before overt functional impairment.

Histopathological analysis revealed severe myocardial damage in the toxic CBZ group, characterized by necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and interstitial edema, confirming pronounced cardiotoxicity, similar to the previous studies on doxorubicin in rats [71, 72]. In contrast, RES-treated hearts exhibited well-preserved myocardial architecture, with minimal inflammation and edema, indicating significant cardiac protection, consistent with the previous study of RES in rats [93]. These findings support the notion that resveratrol mitigates CBZ-induced cardiac injury, likely through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, thereby preserving myocardial structure and integrity.

Conclusions

In our study, cabazitaxel (CBZ) induced dose-dependent systemic and cardiac toxicity in this experimental model, as evidenced by increased oxidative stress, hematological alterations, and measurable impairment in cardiac structure and function. Notably, alterations in IVPG parameters were observed alongside conventional echocardiographic indices, suggesting sensitivity to early or subclinical cardiac dysfunction. Co-administration of resveratrol (RES) demonstrated a potential protective effect against CBZ-associated cardiac injury, as indicated by improvements in oxidative stress markers, selected functional parameters, and histopathological findings.

Limitations

Despite the comprehensive evaluation of CBZ-induced cardiotoxicity and the potential protective effects of resveratrol, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was relatively small, which may have limited the statistical power to detect minor functional changes, particularly in echocardiographic and IVPG parameters. However, post hoc effect size and power analyses were performed, indicating adequate statistical power for the study results, supporting the reliability of the findings. The absence of a dedicated vehicle control group represents a potential limitation; however, the solvent concentration used was within ranges accepted in scientific research. Intracardiac catheterization was not performed to obtain direct hemodynamic measurements. Detailed inflammatory or coagulation biomarkers were not assessed, which could provide further insight into the mechanisms underlying cardiac and hematological alterations. The short duration of resveratrol treatment and the use of a single-dose regimen may have limited its full cardioprotective potential. We believe that future studies with larger sample sizes, extended treatment durations, multiple dosing strategies, and the incorporation of detailed molecular and hemodynamic assessments are necessary to enhance the robustness of these findings.
