Loneliness is linked to psychological pain in both euthymic patients and healthy controls: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mayssam Shahine, Manon Malestroit, Philippe Courtet, Emilie Olié
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological pain is a subjective distressing emotional experience associated with suicidal vulnerability independently of depressive symptom severity. Although it has been described in both clinical and non-clinical populations, little is known about psychosocial factors associated with psychological pain in euthymic patients with mood disorders and in non-clinical individuals.ObjectiveTo investigate clinical and psychosocial factors associated with psychological pain in euthymic and non-clinical samples, with a particular focus on loneliness and childhood trauma.MethodsWe included 133 euthymic patients (Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (IDS-C 30) < 11 and Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) < 7) and 84 non-clinical participants. Psychological pain was defined using the Physical and Psychological Pain Visual Analog Scale (PPP-VAS) and suicidal ideation with the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS). Loneliness and anxiety were measured respectively by the University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA) and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-T). Childhood trauma was evaluated using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ). Analyses were conducted separately in each group.ResultsBoth samples were predominantly female (76% in euthymic participants and 69% in non-clinical sample) with a mean age of 40 ± 14 and 37 ± 12 years respectively. Only loneliness was associated with psychological pain in both euthymic (β = 0.058, p-value = 0.030) and non-clinical samples (β = 0.063, p-value = 0.053). Childhood trauma moderated this association in non-clinical participants (β = -0.007, p-value = 0.029, adjusted effect size R² = 0.09).ConclusionLoneliness appears to be a key psychosocial correlate of psychological pain in both euthymic and non-clinical populations. These findings support the importance of considering interpersonal factors in the assessment of psychological pain beyond acute mood episodes and to develop interventions targeting loneliness to reduce psychological pain and related negative consequences.Clinical Trial number (Health Data hub reference): 23272722 (date: 26-03-2025)This study was conducted in accordance with the French Reference Methodology MR-004 (CNIL) for research using health data that does not involve human participants. According to this framework, registration in a clinical trial registry is not required. The study was registered in the public directory of the Health Data Hub, as required for MR-004 studies, on the 26-03-2025.

Background

Psychological pain, often referred to as psychache, is a deeply distressing subjective emotional experience characterized by feelings such as hopelessness, shame, guilt, emptiness, and loneliness [1–3]. Initially described by Shneidman as the “ache of the mind” [4], psychological pain has since been conceptualized as a persistent and aversive emotional state reflecting a discrepancy between one’s perceived self and desired self [5]. Although a universally accepted definition is still lacking, psychological pain is generally considered a subjective, non-physical form of suffering that plays a central role in emotional distress [6].

Psychological pain has been widely documented in several psychiatric disorders, including borderline personality disorder [3], major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder [1]. Beyond its association with psychopathology, it has also been proposed as a key mechanism underlying suicidal risk. Shneidman suggested that suicide may represent an attempt to escape unbearable psychological pain [4]. Consistent with this view, numerous studies have shown strong associations between psychological pain and suicidal ideation (SI) as well as suicide attempts (SA), independently of depressive symptom severity [7–10].

Importantly, psychological pain is not restricted to acute psychiatric states [11]. It has been reported in both clinical and non-clinical populations, suggesting that it reflects a broader emotional dimension rather than a symptom limited to psychiatric disorders. Epidemiological studies indicate that a substantial proportion of individuals who die by suicide have no documented psychiatric diagnosis, highlighting the importance of identifying psychosocial factors contributing to psychological distress beyond traditional diagnostic categories [12].

One particularly relevant context for studying psychological pain is euthymia in mood disorders. Euthymia is generally defined as the absence of clinically significant depressive or manic symptoms [13, 14]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that euthymic patients may still experience persistent cognitive, emotional, and social difficulties [11]. These residual vulnerabilities include impaired social cognition, functional impairments, and heightened sensitivity to psychosocial stressors, all of which may contribute to ongoing psychological distress despite symptomatic remission [15, 16].

Several psychosocial vulnerability factors may contribute to the development and maintenance of psychological pain. According to theoretical frameworks such as the stress–diathesis model and the interpersonal theory of suicide, both distal and proximal factors influence suicidal vulnerability [17, 18]. Childhood traumatic experiences represent distal risk factors that can increase long-term vulnerability to emotional dysregulation, loneliness, and suicidal behaviors [19]. In parallel, interpersonal factors such as loneliness—a subjective feeling arising from perceived discrepancies between desired and actual social relationships—have been strongly associated with psychological distress and suicidal behaviors [20, 21]. Evidence also suggests that childhood trauma may contribute to loneliness in adulthood, thereby increasing psychological distress [22–24].

Taken together, these findings suggest that psychological pain may emerge from complex interactions between developmental and interpersonal vulnerabilities. However, few studies have examined these factors simultaneously in both clinical and non-clinical populations. In particular, it remains unclear whether similar psychosocial factors contribute to psychological pain in euthymic patients and individuals without psychiatric disorders.

The present study therefore aimed to investigate psychosocial factors associated with psychological pain in two distinct populations: euthymic individuals with a history of mood disorder and non-clinical participants. Specifically, we first aimed to characterize the sociodemographic, clinical, and psychosocial profiles of both samples. Second, we examined the association between perceived loneliness and psychological pain. Finally, we tested whether childhood trauma moderated the relationship between loneliness and psychological pain.

Methods

Participants

The participants’ data were extracted from previous studies conducted by our team in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (ID-RCB n° 2016-A00276-45 (CPP Sud Méditerranée V) / NCT02829671 conducted from 16/06/2016 to 15/03/2019; (ID-RCB n°2020-A02123-36 (CPP Ile de France VI) / NCT04714671) conducted from 27/01/2021 to 01/02/2024; ID-RCB n°2018-A03127-48 (CPP Ile de France III) / NCT03898648) conducted from 16/06/2021 to 17/09/2021; (ID-RCB n°2016-01267-44 (CPP Sud Méditerranée IV) / NCT03052855): from 01/02/2017 to 20/07/2017. ID-RCB n°2019-A01277-50 (CPP Ile de France I) / NCT04137458): from 05/03/2020 to 16/12/2021). The use of data for the current study was approved by the Local Ethic Committee of the Academic Hospital of Montpellier with the registration number 2025-02-210. Participants gave their written informed consent.

Euthymic participants were recruited in the Department of Psychiatric Emergency and Post-Psychiatric Emergency, University Hospital of Montpellier, France. The euthymic sample consisted of a total of 133 adults (> 18 years old), with a lifetime major depressive episode according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition (DSM-5) criteria. Euthymia was defined as the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology IDS-C 30 < 11, and the Young Mania Rating Scale YMRS < 7. Non-included participants are those with a lifetime diagnosis of schizoaffective disorders, schizophrenia or presence of psychotic features.

The non-clinical sample consisted of 84 participants recruited through public press announcements and public spaces postings. They had no lifetime history of psychiatric disorder and no history of suicide attempt.

Measures of sociodemographic, clinical and social characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, educational level, professional status) were collected through clinical evaluation. Smoking habits as well as history of psychiatric hospitalizations were recorded.

Current psychopathology was assessed by a trained clinician according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition criteria [25]. Severity of suicidal ideation within past month was assessed with the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) [26], using 5 dichotomous items (1 = wish to be dead, 2 = nonspecific active suicidal thoughts, 3 = suicidal thoughts with methods, 4 = suicidal intent, and 5 = suicidal intent with plan). Each item was scored No (absence) or Yes (presence). If participants responded No to the first 2 items, the total score is 0. Lifetime history of SA was recorded during the clinical interview.

Maximal psychological pain within last 15 days was assessed using the self-administered Physical and Psychological Pain-Visual Analog Scale (PPP-VAS) [27], from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain). A higher score indicates higher psychological pain [28].

Loneliness was measured using the French version of the University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA) [29]. This self-reported 20-items questionnaire evaluates the subjective feelings of loneliness and social isolation as well as satisfaction and dissatisfaction concerning social ties. Questions are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with values being “Never, Rarely, Sometimes and Often”. The total score ranges between 20 and 80 with higher scores indicating higher loneliness [30].

Trait anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-T) [31, 32]. This self-reported scale includes 20 items with a 4-point Likert response to questions formulated as statements. Responses range from Almost Never to Almost Always [33] with higher mean scores indicating a stronger presence of anxiety.

Childhood trauma was assessed using the French validated version of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [34]. This self-reported questionnaire includes 28 items presented as statements and are rated on a 5-point scale with responses ranging from 1 = Never True to 5 = Very often true. The total score ranges between 25 and 125 with higher scores indicating higher traumatic exposures [35].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted separately in euthymic patients and healthy controls. Psychological pain was analyzed as a continuous variable. Association between loneliness and psychological pain was first examined using univariate linear regression, followed by multivariable models adjusting for age, sex, and clinically relevant covariates (trait anxiety, childhood trauma, and borderline diagnosis in patients; age and sex only in controls). Standardized coefficients were reported to facilitate interpretation. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 2 indicating the absence of multicollinearity. To examine whether childhood trauma modified the association between loneliness and psychological pain, a loneliness × trauma interaction term was tested in each group (predictors mean-centered). Collinearity was assessed using VIF and model assumptions were verified graphically. Analyses were conducted in R 4.5.2 [36].

The euthymic patients’ sample was predominantly female (76%) with a mean age of 40 ± 14 years. Nearly half of the participants were single (49%), 57% were unemployed and 43% were non-smokers. Almost 60% had a history of psychiatric hospitalizations. Regarding psychopathology, 64% were diagnosed with bipolar disorder, 11% with eating disorder, 11% with borderline personality disorder, 6% with alcohol or substance use disorder and 21% had a history of suicide attempts. No participants report current suicidal ideation.

In the non-clinical sample, 69% were female with a mean age of 37 ± 12 years. Most were in a relationship (60%), 71% were unemployed and 58% were non-smokers. No participants reported current suicidal ideation (Table 1).

Euthymic patients: Univariate and multivariable analysis

In univariate analyses, loneliness was significantly associated with psychological pain (β = 0.068, Standard Error SE = 0.022, p-value = 0.0026) whereas childhood trauma was not (β = 0.01, p-value = 0.55). In a multivariate model including age, sex, trait anxiety, childhood trauma and borderline personality disorder, loneliness remained significantly associated with psychological pain (β = 0.058, SE = 0.026, p-value = 0.030, β standardized = 0.236) (Table 2). No other covariate reached significance and collinearity diagnosis indicated no inflation of variance (all VIF < 1.5). The full model explained 12% of variance (R²=0.119, adjusted R² = 0.071). These results suggest that, in euthymic patients, perceived loneliness is the strongest predictor of psychological pain, independent of demographic and clinical covariates.

Non-clinical sample: Univariate and multivariable analysis

In univariate analyses, loneliness was significantly associated with psychological pain (β = 0.06, p = 0.036) whereas childhood trauma was not (β = 0.03, p-value = 0.26) (Table 3). After adjustment for age, sex, and childhood trauma, the association between loneliness and psychological pain was trending towards significance (β = 0.063, SE = 0.032, p-value = 0.053, standardized β = 0.24). The overall explanatory power of the model was modest (R² = 0.09; adjusted R² = 0.04) (Table 3).

Role of childhood trauma: moderation analysis

In euthymic patients, no significant interaction between loneliness and childhood trauma was observed (p-value = 0.81), indicating that the effect of loneliness on psychological pain did not vary according to trauma.

In healthy controls, we found a significant interaction between loneliness and childhood trauma (β = -0.007, p-value = 0.029), i.e. association between loneliness and psychological pain was weaker among individuals reporting higher trauma. Although the effect size was modest (adjusted R² = 0.09), this finding suggests that childhood trauma may modulate the impact of loneliness on psychological pain in non-clinical populations.

Discussion

The present study investigated psychosocial factors associated with psychological pain in two distinct populations: euthymic individuals with a history of mood disorder and non-clinical participants. The main finding is that perceived loneliness was consistently associated with higher levels of psychological pain in both samples. This association remained significant after adjustment for relevant demographic and clinical variables in euthymic patients and showed a similar trend in the non-clinical sample. In contrast, childhood trauma was not directly associated with psychological pain. However, a modest moderating effect of childhood trauma on the relationship between loneliness and psychological pain was observed in non-clinical participants.

The association between loneliness and psychological pain observed in both samples is consistent with a growing body of literature highlighting the close relationship between social disconnection and emotional suffering. Loneliness reflects a subjective discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships and has been repeatedly linked to psychological distress, depressive symptoms, and suicidality [20, 21]. The present findings support the hypothesis that loneliness may represent an important psychosocial correlate of psychological pain across both clinical and non-clinical contexts. These results are also consistent with the concept of social pain, which refers to the emotional distress associated with perceived social exclusion or rejection [37, 38]. Using a social exclusion paradigm and ecological momentary assessment, Olié et al. (2021) found that orbitofrontal activity during social exclusion was linked to daily experiences of psychological pain in euthymic patients with a history of suicide attempts [39]. Furthermore, lonely individuals exhibit reduced volume in key brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus and insula -areas involved in psychological pain [40–42]. Although these findings support our results, it should be noted that they cannot be generalized to all populations, as studies using social paradigms follow a specific protocol and neuroimaging studies cannot validate causality.

Alternatively, in non-clinical populations, high loneliness is primarily linked to non-psychiatric factors such as relationship status (e.g. separated, widowed), living arrangements (living alone), specific socio-demographic profile (e.g. migration, fewer years of education), professional status (e.g. unemployment and financial difficulties) [43, 44].

Another potential mechanism linking loneliness and psychological pain involves emotional regulation processes. Emotional dysregulation is characterized by difficulties in identifying, processing, and regulating emotions, as well as poor impulse control [45]. It has been independently associated with both loneliness [46] and psychological pain [47]. Negatively regulating one’s emotions may lead to interpersonal difficulties, increasing interpersonal rejection, social isolation, and perceived inability to overcome emotional distress [45, 48]. However, factors such as emotional dysregulation or cognitive impairments could be potential confounders rather than explicative, highlighting the need of prospective studies.

In contrast to our expectations, childhood trauma was not directly associated with psychological pain in either sample. Several explanations may account for this finding. First, the overall level of reported childhood trauma in both samples was relatively low to moderate, which may have limited statistical power to detect direct associations. It is also possible that protective factors such as social support/support systems, resilience and hope buffer the impact of childhood traumatic experiences [49]. However, these factors were not directly assessed in our study.

Interestingly, a modest moderating effect of childhood trauma on the association between loneliness and psychological pain was observed in the non-clinical sample. This result suggests that psychological pain may be more strongly determined by other vulnerability factors beyond loneliness. A theoretical explanation is through increased vulnerability to negative cognitive bias, negative perceptions of the surrounding environment, mistrust and pessimism, increasing psychological pain [11, 50]. Another mechanism may involve insecure attachment styles as well as low perceived social support that partially mediates the association between childhood maltreatment and depressive symptoms [51]. It should be noted that although these factors may be plausible explanations to the weakened association, the measures of psychological pain, the population, the study design and the relative outcomes in the literature differ from our study.

In the euthymic sample, no moderation effect was observed. This may reflect the presence of other clinical factors associated with mood disorders that contribute to psychological pain independently of childhood adversity. Despite being in euthymic state, they may experience persistent cognitive and social impairments that contribute to psychological pain [15]. Also, loneliness may result from self-stigmatization and perceived discrimination, leading to social withdrawal, interpersonal difficulties, and heightened psychological stress [52, 53]. Therefore, persistent deficits in social cognition, even during remission are possible explanations for the absence of childhood trauma effect [54]. This is relevant in literature that included samples of euthymic patients and studies may vary in methodology, therefore findings cannot be generalized to other states or psychiatric disorders.

Although causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional nature of our study, the findings highlight the importance of identifying factors associated with loneliness in clinical and non-clinical populations. Literature suggests that perceived social support can act as a protective factor against loneliness and psychological distress [55, 56]. One promising approach is social prescribing, where individuals are referred to community groups and activities. Among reported outcomes were increased social connectedness, self-esteem, sense of purpose, achievement, belongingness, social well-being and reduced loneliness [57, 58].

Limitations

Our study presents several limitations. First, the sample sizes are small and the study is cross-sectional. Therefore, the samples may not be representative of euthymic and healthy populations, the statistical power is low and results cannot be generalized or considered causal. Second, our study used self-reported measurement tools to assess loneliness, psychological pain and childhood trauma which introduces recall bias. Third, childhood adversity was assessed using the CTQ, which focuses primarily on abuse and neglect and does not capture other forms of early-life stress such as family dysfunction, bullying, or socioeconomic adversity [59]. It is therefore important to consider the complex nature of childhood traumatic experiences, particularly in regards to their cumulative nature, type, frequency and dose-responsive impact on physical, mental and social consequences [60].

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies loneliness as a main dimension underlying psychological pain in both euthymic and non-clinical samples. Our findings highlight the importance of considering social factors in psychological pain, particularly in non-clinical populations who may be overlooked in clinical settings. Future research should investigate the mechanisms linking loneliness and psychological pain using longitudinal designs and larger samples, and examine whether interventions targeting social connectedness may help reduce psychological distress in vulnerable individuals.
