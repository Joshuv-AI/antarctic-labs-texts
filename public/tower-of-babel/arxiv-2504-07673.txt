nimblewomble: An R package for Bayesian Wombling with nimble
by Aritra Halder, Sudipto Banerjee

Abstract
This exposition presents nimblewomble , a software package to perform wombling, or boundary analysis, using the nimble Bayesian hierarchical modeling language in the R statistical computing environment. Wombling is used widely to track regions of rapid change within the spatial reference domain. Specific functions in the package implement Gaussian process models for point-referenced spatial data followed by predictive inference on rates of change over curves using line integrals. We demonstrate model based Bayesian inference using posterior distributions featuring simple analytic forms while offering uncertainty quantification over curves.



1  Introduction

Detecting regions of rapid change is an important exercise in spatial data science as they harbor effects not easily explained by predictors incorporated into a spatial regression model for point-referenced spatial data. For example, environmental health scientists are often keen on identifying regions where exposure levels display rapid change or sharp gradients. Formal statistical detection of such regions can lead to data-driven discoveries of latent risk factors and other predictors that drive the rapid change in exposure surfaces.
This exercise is referred to as wombling
(
Womble, 1951
;
Gleyze et al., 2001
;
Banerjee, 2010
)
. Wombling results in curves that track regions of interest. Identifying such regions serves as crucial guides for interventions. For example, to determine whether natural features such as rivers or mountain ranges represent significant zones of rapid change in weather patterns; To identify significant boundaries (over geographic areas) in disease rates, such as cancer, or to track variations in access to health care across geographical regions. Measurement scales of the spatial data usually dictate the methods required for wombling. For areal data, boundaries delineate neighboring regions
(
Gao et al., 2023
;
Wu and Banerjee, 2025
, see, e.g.)
. Predictive inference is sought for
smooth
curves. We evaluate spatial gradients along a curve while assessing its candidacy for a boundary
(
Banerjee and Gelfand, 2006
;
Qu and Niu, 2021
;
Halder et al., 2024
, see, e.g.,)
, which requires specifying the smoothness of the spatial process
(
Kent, 1989
;
Banerjee et al., 2003
, see, e.g.)
.

In this software package, we are concerned with point-referenced wombling. Developing an easily accessible software that implements Bayesian wombling for use by the wider scientific community is faced with several challenges. Perhaps, the most severe being the need for two-dimensional quadrature to enable posterior inference. Our contributions here lie in the use of analytic closed forms for posteriors that require at most one-dimensional quadrature, greatly easing the computational burden and efficient Bayesian inference for hierarchical spatial models
(
Banerjee et al., 2014
, see, e.g.)
via
nimble

de Valpine et al. (2017)
within the
R

R Core Team (2021)
statistical environment.

Several
R
packages exist for point-referenced spatial modeling, with
spBayes

Finley et al. (2007)
and
R-INLA

Lindgren and Rue (2015)
being more widely used. However, they do not address boundary analysis, or wombling, in any capacity. In recent years,
nimble
has found increased use in Bayesian modeling applications
(
Turek et al., 2016
;
Ponisio et al., 2020
;
Goldstein and de Valpine, 2022
, see, e.g.)
. Notable
R
-packages that use nimble include
BayesNSGP

Risser and Turek (2020)
and
nimbleEcology

Goldstein et al. (2024)
. The Bayesian hierarchical framework in
nimblewomble
is similar to
spBayes
. We take advantage of the
one line call and execute
feature of
nimble
to develop Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms for fitting Gaussian process (GPs). This makes the underlying code for
nimblewomble
easily accessible and customizable for wider use.

We demonstrate our developments using the Matérn class of covariance kernels
(
Abramowitz et al., 1988
, see, e.g.,)
. They are a popular choice in the literature for GPs
(
Rasmussen and Williams, 2005
, see, e.g.,)
. They feature a fractal parameter that provides explicit control over process smoothness. Our package offers three choices for the fractal parameter allowing for flexible process specification. Gradient estimation is done on a grid. We show an example of generating an equally spaced grid. Users can also specify a grid of their choice. Wombling requires a curve, we use contours for that purpose. We demonstrate the procedure for obtaining contours using the
raster
package. Alternatively, a curve of choice can also be used. Our vignettes show an example that uses the
locator()

spBayes
can also be used for wombling with
nimblewomble
. The same kernel needs to be used in both packages to ensure valid inference.

The ensuing discussion describes the necessary methodological details in brief. We begin with a general overview of the functions within
nimblewomble
.

Section

3  Spatial Processes for Rates of Change

We consider
\{Y({\bm{s}}):{\bm{s}}\in\mathscr{S}\subseteq\mathfrak{R}^{2}\}
to be a univariate weakly stationary random field with zero mean and a positive definite covariance
K({\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime})={\rm Cov}(Y({\bm{s}}),Y({\bm{s}}^{\prime}))
for locations
{\bm{s}},{\bm{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{R}^{2}
. Mean square smoothness
(
Stein, 1999
, see, e.g.,)
at an arbitrary location
{\bm{s}}_{0}
requires
Y({\bm{s}}_{0}+h{\bm{u}})=Y({\bm{s}}_{0})+h{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})+h^{2}{{\bm{u}}^{\otimes 2}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})+o(h^{3}||{\bm{u}}||^{3})
, where,
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\in\mathfrak{R}^{2}
is an arbitrary vector of directions,
{\bm{\partial}}
is the gradient operator,
{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{x}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\frac{\partial}{\partial s_{y}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}=(\partial_{x}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\partial_{y}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}))^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
,
\otimes
is the Kronecker vector product. Hence,
{\bm{u}}^{\otimes 2}=\left(u_{1}^{2},u_{1}u_{2},u_{2}u_{1},u_{2}^{2}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
and
{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}={\bm{\partial}}\otimes{\bm{\partial}}
. Note that
{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
is the vectorized Hessian. The processes
{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
and
{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
govern rates of change in
Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
. The
gradient
or, first order rate of change, is captured by
{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
while,
curvature
is captured by
{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
.
Mean square differentiability

(
Banerjee and Gelfand, 2003
, see)
of the first and second order for
Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
guarantees that
{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
and
({\bm{u}}\otimes{\bm{v}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
are well-defined respectively, for any set of direction vectors
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in\mathfrak{R}^{2}
. We note that the entries of
{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
contain duplicates, both
\partial^{2}_{xy}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s_{x}\partial s_{y}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
and
\partial^{2}_{yx}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})
are included. To avoid singularities that arise from duplication, we work with
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})=\left(\partial_{xx}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\partial_{xy}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\partial_{yy}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
comprised of only unique derivatives.

Statistical inference is devised for the joint process,
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{*}Y({\bm{s}})=\left(Y({\bm{s}}),{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
. Note that in
\mathfrak{R}^{2}
,
{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}})
and
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}})
are
2\times 1
and
3\times 1
vectors respectively. Validity of the inference is considered at length in
Banerjee et al. (2003)
;
Halder et al. (2024)
. The process
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{*}Y({\bm{s}})
is also weakly stationary with a cross-covariance matrix of order
6\times 6
given by,

{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{*}}({\bm{\Delta}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}K({\bm{\Delta}})&{\bm{\partial}}K({\bm{\Delta}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}&\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}K({\bm{\Delta}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\\
-{\bm{\partial}}K({\bm{\Delta}})&-{\bm{\partial}}^{2}K({\bm{\Delta}})&-\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{\Delta}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\\
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}K({\bm{\Delta}})&\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{\Delta}})&\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{4}K({\bm{\Delta}})\end{array}\right),

(1)

where
{\bm{\Delta}}={\bm{s}}-{\bm{s}}^{\prime}
,
{\bm{\partial}}K({\bm{\Delta}})
is a
2\times 1
vector of gradients,
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}K({\bm{\Delta}})
is a
3\times 1
vector of unique curvatures,
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{\Delta}})
is a
3\times 2
matrix of third derivatives,
{\bm{\partial}}^{2}K({\bm{\Delta}})
is the
2\times 2
Hessian and
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{4}K({\bm{\Delta}})
is a
3\times 3
matrix of fourth order derivatives. Evidently, for
{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{*}}({\bm{\Delta}})
in
eq.

1
to be valid all entries need to be well-defined.

Let
Y({\bm{s}})\sim GP(0,K(\cdot;{\bm{\theta}}))
denote a Gaussian process (GP) where
K({\bm{\Delta}};{\bm{\theta}})={\rm Cov}(Y({\bm{s}}),Y({\bm{s}}^{\prime}))
with process parameters
{\bm{\theta}}=\{\sigma^{2},\phi\}
. We will denote
K({\bm{\Delta}};{\bm{\theta}})=K({\bm{\Delta}})
to ease notation. The covariance function satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}a_{j}K({\bm{\Delta}}_{ij})>0
for any collection of coordinates
\{{\bm{s}}_{i}:i=1,\ldots,N\}
. Under isotropy we have
K({\bm{\Delta}})=\widetilde{K}(||{\bm{\Delta}}||)
. Let
{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}=\left(y({\bm{s}}_{1}),\ldots,y({\bm{s}}_{N})\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
be the observed realization over
\mathscr{S}
,
\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}
be the
N\times N
covariance matrix with entries
K({\bm{s}}_{i},{\bm{s}}_{j})
,
i,j=1,\ldots,N
and
{\bm{s}}_{0}
an arbitrary location of interest. The joint distribution is as follows:

{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\,|\;{\bm{\theta}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{N+5}\left({\bm{0}}_{N+5},\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}&{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{1}&{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{2}\\
-{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}&-{\bm{\partial}}^{2}K({\bm{0}})&-\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{0}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\\
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}&\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{0}})&\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{4}K({\bm{0}})\end{array}\right)\right),

(2)

where,
\mathcal{N}_{d}
denotes the
d
-variate Gaussian distribution,
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{1}=\left({\bm{\partial}}K(\delta_{10})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\ldots,{\bm{\partial}}K(\delta_{N0})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
,
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{2}=\left(\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}K(\delta_{10})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\ldots,\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}K(\delta_{N0})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
and
\delta_{i0}={\bm{s}}_{i}-{\bm{s}}_{0}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. The resulting posterior predictive distribution for rates of change at
{\bm{s}}_{0}
is
P({\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})=\int P({\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0}),\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},{\bm{\theta}})\;P({\bm{\theta}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})\;d{\bm{\theta}}
. Posterior sampling proceeds in a one-for-one fashion corresponding to posterior samples of
{\bm{\theta}}
. From
eq.

2
the resulting full conditional distribution is obtained as follows:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\\
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Y({\bm{s}}_{0})\end{array}\right)\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{5}\left(-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{1}\\
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{2}\end{array}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-{\bm{\partial}}^{2}K({\bm{0}})&-\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{0}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\\
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{3}K({\bm{0}})&\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{4}K({\bm{0}})\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{1}\\
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{2}\end{array}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{1}\\
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{2}\end{array}\right)\right).

(3)

We use the Matérn class of kernels,
K(||{\bm{\Delta}}||,{\bm{\theta}})=\sigma^{2}\Gamma(\nu)^{-1}2^{1-\nu}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\phi||{\bm{\Delta}}||\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\phi||{\bm{\Delta}}||\right)
, where
K_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind
Abramowitz et al. (1988)
featuring a fractal parameter
\nu
that controls process smoothness, a spatial range parameter
\phi
and an overall variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
.

4  Spatial Wombling

The wombling exercise seeks posterior predictive inference on line integrals

{\bm{\Gamma}}(C)=\left(\int_{C}{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}})\;d{\bm{s}},\int_{C}{{\bm{u}}^{\otimes 2}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}})\;d{\bm{s}}\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},

(4)

where
C
is a curve of interest to the investigator. Average wombling measures are defined as
\overline{{\bm{\Gamma}}}(C)={\bm{\Gamma}}(C)/\ell(C)
, where
\ell
is the arc-length measure. For closed curves we replace
\int
with
\oint
in
eq.

4
. The choice of direction is crucial when measuring rates of change. The curve
C
typically tracks a region of rapid change in the reference domain and hence, the direction normal to
C
is naturally of interest. We denote the normal to
C
at
{\bm{s}}
by
{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}})
and set
{\bm{u}}={\bm{n}}({\bm{s}})
in the line integrals of
eq.

4
. The curve
C
is deemed to be a
wombling boundary
if any entry of
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C)
is large. Focusing on the choices for
C
, not all curves ensure the existence of
{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}})
at every
{\bm{s}}
. Parametric smooth curves offer some respite in that regard. We work with
C=\{{\bm{s}}(t)=(s_{1}(t),s_{2}(t)):t\in\mathcal{I}\subset\mathfrak{R}\}
. As
t
varies over
\mathcal{I}
,
{\bm{s}}(t)
traces out
C
. We assume
||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||\neq 0
which ensures
{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}})=||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||^{-1}\left(s_{2}^{\prime}(t),-s_{1}^{\prime}(t)\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
is well-defined.

The arc-length,
\ell(C)=\int_{\mathcal{I}}||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||\;dt
. For parametric curves
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C)
can be expressed as,
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C)=\left(\int_{\mathcal{I}}{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}}(t))||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||\;dt,\int_{\mathcal{I}}{{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))^{\otimes 2}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}Y({\bm{s}}(t))||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||\;dt\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
. Let
\mathcal{I}=[0,t^{*}]
, and the curve traced out over
\mathcal{I}
be denoted as
C_{t^{*}}
. Statistical inference for
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C_{t^{*}})
follows from
{\bm{\partial}}Y({\bm{s}})
and
\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Y({\bm{s}})
being GPs, as seen in
eq.

3
,
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C_{t^{*}})\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}({\bm{0}}_{2},{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*},t^{*}))
, where
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*},t^{*})
is a
2\times 2
matrix with entries

k_{ij}(t^{*},t^{*})=(-1)^{i}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}{\bm{a}}_{i}(t_{1})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{i+j}K({\bm{\Delta}}(t_{1},t_{2}))\;{\bm{a}}_{j}(t_{2})\;||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t_{1})||\;||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t_{2})||\;dt_{1}\;dt_{2},

(5)

where
{\bm{a}}_{1}(t)={\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))
,
{\bm{a}}_{2}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{2}\;{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))^{\otimes 2}
, with
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&&&\\
&1&1&\\
&&&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
being an elimination matrix and
{\bm{\Delta}}(t_{1},t_{2})={\bm{s}}_{2}(t)-{\bm{s}}_{1}(t)
for
i,j=1,2
. Predictive inference on
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C)
requires

{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},{\bm{\Gamma}}(C_{t^{*}})\,|\;{\bm{\theta}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{N+2}\left({\bm{0}}_{N+2},\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}&{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\\
{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*})&{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*},t^{*})\end{array}\right)\right),

(6)

where
{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bm{\gamma}}_{1}(t^{*})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\gamma}}_{N}(t^{*})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is an
N\times 2
matrix with entries

\gamma_{i}(t^{*})=\left(\int_{0}^{t^{*}}{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}K({\bm{\Delta}}_{j}(t))\;||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||\;dt,\int_{0}^{t^{*}}{{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))^{\otimes 2}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{\otimes 2}K({\bm{\Delta}}_{j}(t))\;||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||\;dt\right)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},

(7)

where
{\bm{\Delta}}_{j}(t)={\bm{s}}(t)-{\bm{s}}_{j}
. Posterior predictive inference proceeds one-for-one (similar to
eq.

3
) using
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C_{t^{*}})\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(-{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*})\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*},t^{*})-{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*})\Sigma_{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mathscr{G}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)
.

In practice, modern computing environments store curves as a set of points. As a result, it suffices to demonstrate wombling for rectilinear approximations to smooth curves where predictive inference is performed iteratively on segments. We show the inference for one generic segment. Let
C=\{{\bm{s}}(t_{0}),{\bm{s}}(t_{1}),\ldots,{\bm{s}}(t_{n_{p}})\}
, then the
i
-th segment,
C_{t_{i}}=\{{\bm{s}}(t)={\bm{s}}(t_{i-1})+t{\bm{u}}_{i}:t\in[0,t_{i}]\}
,
t_{i}=||{\bm{s}}(t_{i})-{\bm{s}}(t_{i-1})||
and
{\bm{u}}_{i}=t_{i}^{-1}({\bm{s}}(t_{i})-{\bm{s}}(t_{i-1}))
. Clearly,
||{\bm{u}}_{i}||=1
,
||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t)||=1
and the normal to
C_{t_{i}}
is
{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\perp}=(u_{i2},-u_{i1})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
. For predictive inference on
{\bm{\Gamma}}(C_{t_{i}})
, note that we need
{\bm{\Delta}}(t_{1},t_{2})=(t_{2}-t_{1}){\bm{u}}_{i}
in
eq.

5
and
{\bm{\Delta}}_{j}(t)={\bm{\Delta}}_{i-1,j}+t{\bm{u}}_{i}=({\bm{s}}_{i-1}-{\bm{s}}_{j})+t{\bm{u}}_{i-1}
in
eq.

7
.

Wombling with Closed Forms

We acknowledge that
eq.

5
requires 2-dimensional quadrature which is computationally expensive to evaluate. We work with the Matérn kernel for which closed form analytic expressions exist for the entries of
{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}_{\bm{\Gamma}}(t^{*},t^{*})
improving on

;
Halder et al. (2024)
(see Theorems 1 & 2 in the Supplement). Apart from reduced computation time resulting from the reduction of a surface integral to closed-form expressions, the Matérn class is uniquely suited for wombling, featuring a dedicated fractal parameter that controls process smoothness and thereby guaranteeing validity of posterior inference on derivatives and line integrals
(
Halder et al., 2024
, see, e.g.,)
. Our
R
-package,
nimblewomble
features Matérn kernels with
\nu=\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2}
and
\infty
(squared exponential).

5  Bayesian Hierarchical Models

A Bayesian hierarchical model is specified as follows:

Y({\bm{s}})=\mu({\bm{s}},{\bm{\beta}})+Z({\bm{s}})+\epsilon({\bm{s}}),

(8)

where
\mu({\bm{s}},{\bm{\beta}})={\bm{x}}({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\beta}}
,
Z({\bm{s}})\sim GP(0,K(\cdot;\sigma^{2},\phi))
and
\epsilon({\bm{s}})
is a white noise process (i.e.,
\epsilon({\bm{s}}_{i})\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,\tau^{2})
over any finite collection of locations). The process parameters are
{\bm{\theta}}=\{\sigma^{2},\phi,\tau^{2}\}
. Predictive inference for
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{*}Z({\bm{s}})
evaluates
P({\bm{\partial}}Z({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Z({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})=\int P({\bm{\partial}}Z({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\widetilde{{\bm{\partial}}}^{2}Z({\bm{s}})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}},{\bm{\theta}})\;P({\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},{\bm{\theta}})\;P({\bm{\theta}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})\;d{\bm{\theta}}\;d{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}
. Similarly for the wombling measures,
P({\bm{\Gamma}}_{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}(C_{t^{*}})\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})=\int P({\bm{\Gamma}}_{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}(C_{t^{*}})\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}},{\bm{\theta}})\;P({\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}},{\bm{\theta}})\;P({\bm{\theta}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})\;d{\bm{\theta}}\;d{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}
. A customary
collapsed
posterior, that is generated by marginalizing
{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}
from the likelihood
(
Finley et al., 2019
, see, e.g.)
, for
{\bm{\theta}}
is specified as follows:

P({\bm{\theta}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}})\propto U(\phi\,|\;a_{\phi},b_{\phi})\times IG(\sigma^{2}\,|\;a_{\sigma},b_{\sigma})\times IG(\tau^{2}\,|\;a_{\tau},b_{\tau})\times\mathcal{N}_{N}({\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\,|\;{\bm{\mathrm{X}}}{\bm{\beta}},\Sigma+\tau^{2}{\bm{\mathrm{I}}}_{N}),

(9)

where
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}{\bm{\mathrm{R}}}_{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}(\phi)
, with
{\bm{\mathrm{R}}}_{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}(\phi)
being the correlation matrix corresponding to
K(\cdot;\sigma^{2},\phi)
,
U(\cdot\,|\;)
is the uniform distribution and
IG(\cdot\,|\;)
is the inverse-gamma distribution. Hyper-parameters are chosen such that a weakly informative prior is specified on
{\bm{\theta}}
. Posterior samples for
{\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}
and
{\bm{\beta}}
are generated one-for-one corresponding to posterior samples of
{\bm{\theta}}
using a Gibbs sampling scheme.

Posterior sampling in
eq.

9
is straightforward in
nimbleCode
as seen in the code for
gp_model
below, which forms the core of our
gp_fit
function (see
Table

1
). We use hyper-parameter settings that result in weakly informative inverse gamma priors for
\sigma^{2}
and
\tau^{2}
. We take advantage of the
one-line call and execute
feature of
nimble
using the
buildMCMC
and
runMCMC
functions to obtain posterior samples from
eq.

9
thereby, fitting
eq.

8
.

#################################
# Collapsed Metropolis-Hastings #
# for covariance parameters #
#################################

gp_model <- nimbleCode({
# Priors #
phi ~ dunif(0, 10)
sigma2 ~ dinvgamma(shape = 1, rate = 1)
tau2 ~ dinvgamma(shape = 2, rate = 1)

# Initialization #
mu[1:N] <- zeros[1:N] # vector of 0s
cov[1:N, 1:N] <- kernel(dists[1:N, 1:N], phi, sigma2, tau2)

# Likelihood #
y[1:N] ~ dmnorm(mu[1:N], cov = cov[1:N, 1:N])
})

7  Further Computational Details

We provide further details about sensitivity to prior choices, convergence and scalability that affect computational aspects that may arise in practical applications for our package.

Alternative prior choices and sensitivity

Although there are numerous precedents for our prior choices
(
Banerjee and Gelfand, 2006
;
Loro et al., 2023
;
Halder et al., 2024
, see, for e.g.,)
, alternative priors are available for use to the interested investigator
(
Gelman, 2006
, see, for e.g.,)
. In reference to the code snippet and discussion in
Section

0.5
there are several alternative choices available for
\sigma
, for example, the half Cauchy, the uniform and the folded-noncentral-
t
distributions. Within
NIMBLE
, the uniform and half Cauchy distributions are easily implemented (
sigma
\sim
dcauchy(0, 1) T(0, )
, where the
T(0,)
truncates the Cauchy) while, the folded-noncentral-
t
requires some effort (using
dt_nonstandard()
). The resulting posterior is robust to these alternative choices. The algorithm for posterior sampling under these complex priors is
automatically determined
by
NIMBLE
once the model is specified. Under such complex non-conjugate priors a Metropolis Hastings algorithm is usually chosen by
NIMBLE
, which is shown in the console when fitting the model. The inference for gradients remains unaffected since it is done one-for-one using the posterior samples for
\sigma^{2}
,
\tau^{2}
and
\phi
.

Convergence diagnostics
The posterior MCMC samples are stored as an
mcmc
object that communicates well the
R
-package
coda
. Running
traceplot(mc_sp)
produces trace plots to assess convergence. The
gp_fit()
function also contains a
nchains
argument which defaults to 1, increasing this to
\geq 2
allows for computing
\widehat{R}
using the
gelman.diag()
function for further assessment. We checked posterior convergence using trace plots which showed satisfactory evidence for convergence.

Scalability
Addressing scalability for the package we repeated the model fit under increasing sample sizes. The model fit takes: 18.37 secs. for
N=100
, 2.52 mins. for
N=500
and 16.39 mins. for
N=10^{3}
. Setting
N=100
, for a grid of size 400, generating posterior samples for gradients takes 10.02 mins. and for a curve containing 257 points, posterior samples for wombling measures takes 32.12 mins. Using a coarser representation of grids and curves (less points) improves the run-time when working with larger
N
. These computations were performed on a 12
th
Gen. Intel ®Core™i9-12.9K processor with 24 cores running Ubuntu OS and 96GB of RAM.

9  Summary

We have developed an easy-to-use software for boundary analysis or, wombling under a Bayesian framework. We hope that it will find use in many applications, for example usage see
Banerjee and Gelfand (2006)
;
Halder et al. (2024)
. The
sp_ggplot
function also features an option to supply shape-files for more mainstream geostatistical applications. Further examples are available in the GitHub repository:
arh926/nimblewomble/
. Boundary analysis requires the investigator to pre-select curves. Identifying such curves in context of the application often proves crucial for detecting differential behavior in the response variable.
Nimble
facilitates an accessible MCMC framework that is immensely helpful in developing the statistical inference for rates of change and boundary analysis.

Future developments can proceed along many directions. We hope to expand the software to include spatiotemporal wombling
(
Halder et al., 2024
;
Halder et al., 2026
, see, e.g.,)
. Similar frameworks can be developed for generalized linear models, particularly focusing on zero-inflated models
(
Finley et al., 2011
;
Halder et al., 2020
;
Halder et al., 2021
;
Halder et al., 2023
, see, e.g.)
which provide a more realistic setting for analyzing raw gene-expression counts. We also plan to include code for inference on directional data considering Bayesian inference for the direction of maximum gradient and curvature
(
Wang and Gelfand, 2014
;
Wang et al., 2018
, see, e.g.,)
. Finally, developments in wombling have relied on the assumption of stationarity and isotropy. In hindsight, the assumptions yielded simplified mathematical expressions. Smoothness for nonstationary processes can be developed
(
Paciorek and Schervish, 2006
, see, e.g.)
depending on the smoothness of location specific covariances. Investigations for process smoothness can also be considered for (geometrically) anisotropic processes
(
Allard et al., 2016
, see, e.g.,)
–we now define
K(||\Delta||)=\widetilde{K}(r)
, where
r=\sqrt{\Delta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}A\Delta}
, with a positive definite matrix,
A
corresponding to rotations and translations. The derivatives are then taken with respect to the radial profile,
r
. Finally, while this package employs MCMC for model fitting, we recognize opportunities to incorporate alternative, possibly faster, machine learning tools such as Bayesian predictive stacking
(
Zhang et al., 2025
;
Pan et al., 2025

that are implemented in the
spStack

Pan and Banerjee, 2024
, see, for e.g.,)
package on CRAN into future editions of

Theorem 1
.

On each rectilinear segment,
C_{t^{*}}=\{{\bm{s}}_{0}+t{\bm{u}}:t\in[0,t^{*}]\}
, where
{\bm{u}}
is a unit vector and
{\bm{u}}^{\perp}
is its normal, the terms in the cross-covariance matrix are

\begin{split}k_{ij}(t^{*},t^{*})&=(-1)^{i}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}{\bm{a}}_{i}(t_{1})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{i+j}K({\bm{\Delta}}(t_{1},t_{2}))\;{\bm{a}}_{j}(t_{2})\;||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t_{1})||\;||{\bm{s}}^{\prime}(t_{2})||\;dt_{1}\;dt_{2},\\
&=(-1)^{i}\;t^{*}\int_{-t^{*}}^{t^{*}}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{i+j}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{j}\;dx,\quad i,j=1,2,\end{split}

where
{\bm{a}}_{1}(t)={\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))
is the normal to the segment,
{\bm{a}}_{2}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{2}\;{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))\otimes{\bm{n}}({\bm{s}}(t))
, where
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&&&\\
&1&1&\\
&&&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is an elimination matrix and
{\bm{\Delta}}(t_{1},t_{2})={\bm{s}}_{2}(t)-{\bm{s}}_{1}(t)
.

Proof.

Considering the parametric segment,
C_{t^{*}}=\{{\bm{s}}_{0}+t{\bm{u}}:t\in[0,t^{*}]\}
, where
{\bm{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
is a unit vector,
||{\bm{u}}||=1
,
{\bm{u}}^{\perp}
is its normal,
{\bm{u}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{u}}^{\perp}=0
,
{\bm{a}}_{1}(t)={\bm{a}}_{1}={\bm{u}}^{\perp}
and
{\bm{a}}_{2}(t)={\bm{a}}_{2}=\mathcal{E}_{2}({\bm{u}}^{\perp}\otimes{\bm{u}}^{\perp})
are free of
t
, and
{\bm{\Delta}}(t_{1},t_{2})=(t_{2}-t_{1}){\bm{u}}
. The integrand in
k_{ij}(t^{*},t^{*})=(-1)^{i}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}\int_{0}^{t^{*}}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{i+j}K((t_{2}-t_{1}){\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{j}\;dt_{1}\;dt_{2}
, depends only on
(t_{2}-t_{1})
. Making a change of variable–define
x=t_{2}-t_{1}
,
y=t_{2}+t_{1}
, implying
\frac{x+y}{2}=t_{2}
and
\frac{y-x}{2}=t_{1}
. The Jacobian is
\frac{1}{2}
. Hence,
0\leq y+x\leq 2t^{*}
and
0\leq y-x\leq 2t^{*}
. This implies
0\leq y\leq 2t^{*}
and
-t^{*}\leq x\leq t^{*}
. Making the substitution above reduces,
k_{ij}(t^{*},t^{*})=\frac{(-1)^{i}}{2}\int_{0}^{2t^{*}}\int_{-t^{*}}^{t^{*}}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{i+j}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{j}\;dx\;dy=(-1)^{i}\;t^{*}\int_{-t^{*}}^{t^{*}}{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{i+j}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{j}\;dx
. Setting
i=j=1
, produces the scenario in
Banerjee and Gelfand (2006)
.

Theorem 2
.

For the Matérn kernel, with
\nu=3/2
, the variance of the gradient based wombling measure is
k_{11}(t^{*},t^{*})=2\sqrt{3}\sigma^{2}\phi t^{*}G\left(1,\sqrt{3}\phi t^{*}\right)
. In case
\nu=5/2
, the cross-covariance matrix for the wombling measures based on spatial gradient and curvature requires
k_{11}(t^{*},t^{*})=\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{3}\sigma^{2}\phi\;t^{*}\left\{G\left(1,\sqrt{5}\phi t^{*}\right)+G\left(2,\sqrt{5}\phi t^{*}\right)\right\}
,
k_{22}(t^{*},t^{*})=10\sqrt{5}\sigma^{2}\phi^{3}\;t^{*}G(1,\sqrt{5}\phi t^{*})
and
k_{21}(t^{*},t^{*})=-k_{12}(t^{*},t^{*})=0
, where
G\left(a,\frac{x}{b}\right)=\int_{0}^{t^{*}}x^{a-1}e^{-\frac{x}{b}}dx
is the lower incomplete Gamma function with shape parameter
a
and scale parameter
b
. For the Gaussian kernel,

{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}(t^{*})=2\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\phi\;t^{*}\left\{2\Phi\left(\sqrt{2}\phi t^{*}\right)-1\right\}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&6\phi^{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cdf for the standard Gaussian probability density.

Proof.

Using
Theorem

1
, if
\nu=3/2
, then we obtain
k_{11}(t^{*},t^{*})=3\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\;t^{*}\int_{-t^{*}}^{t^{*}}e^{-\sqrt{3}\phi|x|}\;dx=2\sqrt{3}\sigma^{2}\phi t^{*}\;G(1,\sqrt{3}\phi t^{*})
. If
\nu=5/2
, then
k_{11}(t^{*},t^{*})=\frac{5}{3}\sigma^{2}\phi\;t^{*}\int_{-t^{*}}^{t^{*}}(1+\sqrt{5}\phi|x|)\;e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi|x|}\;dx=\frac{2\sqrt{5}}{3}\sigma^{2}\phi\;t^{*}\left\{G\left(1,\sqrt{5}\phi t^{*}\right)+G\left(2,\sqrt{5}\phi t^{*}\right)\right\}
. For terms
k_{21}
and
k_{12}
, let us consider
a_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{3}K(x{\bm{u}})\;a_{2}=\frac{25}{3}\sigma^{2}\phi e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi|x|}|x|\big\{{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{1}{\bm{a}}_{2}-\sqrt{5}\phi|x|\;{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{2}\;{\bm{a}}_{2}\big\}
, where

{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3u_{1}&u_{2}&u_{1}\\
u_{2}&u_{1}&3u_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right),\quad{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}^{3}&u_{1}^{2}\;u_{2}&u_{2}^{2}\;u_{1}\\
u_{1}^{2}\;u_{2}&u_{2}^{2}\;u_{1}&u_{2}^{3}\end{smallmatrix}\right).

The first and the second term both reduce to 0. Hence,
k_{21}(t^{*},t^{*})=-k_{12}(t^{*},t^{*})=0
. Next,
{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{4}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{2}=\frac{25}{3}\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}\;e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi|x|}\big\{{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{3}\;{\bm{a}}_{2}-\sqrt{5}\phi\;|x|\;{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{4}\Big(\sqrt{5}\;\phi\;|x|+1\Big)\;{\bm{a}}_{2}\big\}
, where

{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{3}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&3\end{smallmatrix}\right),\quad{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{4}(x^{\prime})=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}6\;u_{1}^{2}-\;x^{\prime}\;u_{1}^{4}&(3-x^{\prime}\;u_{1}^{2})\;u_{1}\;u_{2}&1-x^{\prime}\;u_{1}^{2}\;u_{2}^{2}\\
(3-x^{\prime}\;u_{1}^{2})\;u_{1}\;u_{2}&1-x^{\prime}\;u_{1}^{2}\;u_{2}^{2}&(3-x^{\prime}\;u_{2}^{2})\;u_{1}\;u_{2}\\
1-x^{\prime}\;u_{1}^{2}\;u_{2}^{2}&(3-x^{\prime}\;u_{2}^{2})\;u_{1}\;u_{2}&6\;u_{2}^{2}-\;x^{\prime}\;u_{2}^{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right).

After some algebra,
{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{4}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{2}=25\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}\;e^{-\sqrt{5}\phi|x|}\big\{1-2\sqrt{5}\;\phi\;|x|\;{\bm{u}}_{1}^{\perp}\;{\bm{u}}_{2}^{\perp}\;({\bm{u}}_{1}\;{\bm{u}}_{2}+{\bm{u}}_{1}^{\perp}\;{\bm{u}}_{2}^{\perp})\big\}
. Observe that
{\bm{u}}_{1}^{\perp}={\bm{u}}_{2}
and
{\bm{u}}_{2}^{\perp}=-{\bm{u}}_{1}
, substituting we get
k_{22}(t^{*},t^{*})=10\sqrt{5}\sigma^{2}\phi^{3}\;t^{*}G(1,\sqrt{5}\phi t^{*})
.

For the Gaussian kernel,

k_{11}(t^{*},t^{*})=2\sigma^{2}\phi^{2}\;t^{*}\int_{-t^{*}}^{t^{*}}e^{-\phi^{2}x^{2}}\;dx=2\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\phi\;t^{*}\left\{2\Phi\left(\sqrt{2}\phi t^{*}\right)-1\right\}
.
Note that
{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{3}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{2}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}\;e^{-\phi^{2}x^{2}}x\left\{{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{1}{\bm{a}}_{2}-2\phi^{2}x^{2}\;{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{2}\;{\bm{a}}_{2}\right\}
. Again, the first and second terms equate to 0 implying
k_{21}(t^{*},t^{*})=-k_{12}(t^{*},t^{*})=0
. Next,
{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\partial}}^{4}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{2}=4\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}\;e^{-\phi^{2}x^{2}}\big\{{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{3}\;{\bm{a}}_{2}-2\phi^{2}\;x^{2}\;{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{4}(2\phi^{2}x^{2})\;{\bm{a}}_{2}\big\}
. After some algebra,
{\bm{a}}_{2}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\;{\bm{\partial}}^{4}K(x{\bm{u}})\;{\bm{a}}_{2}=12\sigma^{2}\phi^{4}\;e^{-\phi^{2}x^{2}}\big\{1-4\;\phi^{2}\;x^{2}\;{\bm{u}}_{1}^{\perp}\;{\bm{u}}_{2}^{\perp}\;({\bm{u}}_{1}\;{\bm{u}}_{2}+{\bm{u}}_{1}^{\perp}\;{\bm{u}}_{2}^{\perp})\big\}
.
Substituting, we get
k_{22}(t^{*},t^{*})=12\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\pi}\phi^{3}t^{*}\left\{2\Phi\left(\sqrt{2}\phi t^{*}\right)-1\right\}
resulting in the required expression for
{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}(t^{*})
.
∎

Theorem

2
interestingly shows that the posited cross-covariance matrix,
{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}(t^{*})
, reduces to a
variance-covariance matrix
. It has a simpler form for the Gaussian case when compared to Matérn with
\nu=\frac{5}{2}
. Finally, considering the covariance between
Z({\bm{s}}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,N
and the wombling measures—in the Gaussian case, closed-forms are available
(see, e.g.,
Halder et al., 2024
, end of Section 3)
. The inferential exercise of wombling does not require quadrature when using a Gaussian kernel however, only one-dimensional quadrature is required for the same when using a Matérn kernel.
