Machine learning to predict distant metastasis risk and prognostic analysis in T1–2 esophageal cancer: a study based on the SEER database
Authors: Pubo Shi, Pengjie Yang, Haibo Wu, Changhong Lian, Benben Zhu, Yong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe risk of distant metastasis (DM) in T1–2 esophageal cancer (EC) is frequently underestimated, and the prognosis for patients who develop metastasis remains poor. Currently, there is a lack of effective tools based on routine clinical data to accurately identify high-risk patients preoperatively or to perform subsequent prognostic stratification for those with metastatic disease. This study aimed to develop an integrated tool to preoperatively identify T1–2 EC patients at high risk of DM and stratify prognosis for metastatic cases, providing evidence-based clinical support.MethodsThis retrospective study utilized data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, including 12,928 patients. The cohort was split into training and testing sets (7:3). In the training set, univariate and multivariate logistic regression (LR) were first applied to initially identify factors associated with DM. Feature selection was subsequently conducted using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and the Boruta algorithm. Variables consistently selected by both methods and confirmed as independent predictors were retained for model construction. Next, 12 machine learning algorithms were applied to build prediction models. Model interpretability was achieved using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), calibration curves, precision-recall (PR) curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) curves. For patients who developed DM, a prognostic nomogram was constructed based on factors identified through Cox regression and evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), AUC, calibration curves, and DCA.ResultsAmong the DM prediction models, the Naive Bayes classifier (NBC) model achieved AUC values of 0.740 and 0.750 in the training and testing sets, respectively, indicating favorable discriminative ability. Additionally, it exhibited the highest sensitivity among all models and was therefore selected as the final prediction model. SHAP analysis identified N stage as the most important predictive factor, with N stage, primary site, and tumor size being confirmed as risk factors for DM, whereas T stage and older age were associated with reduced risk. In the prognostic analysis, race, T stage, chemotherapy, marital status, and median household income were independent prognostic factors for metastatic patients. The resulting overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) nomograms demonstrated good discrimination, with C-indexes of 0.653 and 0.654 in the testing set, respectively, along with satisfactory calibration and clinical net benefit.ConclusionsWe developed a dual-strategy risk assessment tool that predicts DM in T1–2 EC and provides individualized survival estimates for metastatic patients, offering a practical framework for stratified clinical decision making and precision oncology.Keywords: Machine learning, esophageal cancer (EC), distant metastasis (DM)

Key findings

• In this study, we developed and validated a novel prediction model for distant metastasis (DM) in T1–2 esophageal cancer (EC), and established prognostic nomograms for patients who developed DM. These models demonstrated favorable predictive and prognostic performance, which may facilitate individualized risk stratification and precision therapy for T1–2 EC.

What is known and what is new?

• The risk of DM in T1–2 EC is often underestimated, and patients who develop DM have a poor prognosis. Currently, few effective tools based on routine clinical data exist for early identification of high-risk DM in T1–2 EC, and neither for prognostic stratification of metastatic patients.

• In this study, we constructed a dual-strategy risk assessment tool based on the SEER database. Machine learning algorithms were used to predict the risk of DM in patients with T1–2 EC, while prognostic nomograms for overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were established to provide individualized survival evaluation for patients with DM.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In this study, machine learning models were employed to accurately assess the risk of DM in T1–2 EC, providing evidence to assist clinicians in identifying high-risk individuals and tailoring individualized treatment strategies. For patients with DM, prognostic nomograms were applied to evaluate OS and CSS, providing personalized guidance for subsequent treatment. Such a comprehensive risk assessment model covering early intervention to advanced management provides methodological support for the implementation of stratified and precision medicine in EC.

Introduction

According to the 2022 Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) report, esophageal cancer (EC) ranks as the 11th most commonly diagnosed cancer and the seventh leading cause of cancer death worldwide, with approximately 511,000 new cases and 445,000 deaths estimated in 2022 (1). For EC, radical esophagectomy is the standard treatment for localized disease (2). However, once distant metastasis (DM) occurs, the cancer is classified as stage IV and the treatment goal shifts to palliation. In this context, surgery is generally avoided, and management focuses instead on systemic therapy to control disease, alleviate symptoms, and prolong survival (3). The prognosis for patients with DM is substantially worse than for the without (4). This marked disparity in outcomes underscores the critical importance of identifying, at an early disease stage, those patients who harbor a high risk of DM. T1–2 EC are anatomically localized lesions confined within the muscularis propria (5). In clinical practice, however, the risk of occult DM in this seemingly localized group of patients is consistently underappreciated. Thus, performing timely and accurate metastatic risk assessment for this specific T1–2 cohort is essential to inform precise clinical management and improve patient outcomes.

Accurate risk stratification for DM remains a significant clinical challenge. Conventional clinicopathological parameters, such as depth of invasion, lymphovascular invasion, and histological grade, offer limited predictive value, with insufficient sensitivity and specificity to guide individualized prognosis (6-8). Although advanced imaging modalities, including positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) and molecular techniques like liquid biopsy, have demonstrated progress, their utility in the preoperative risk assessment of patients with T1–2 disease is constrained by notable limitations. PET-CT exhibits inadequate sensitivity for detecting occult micrometastases, while liquid biopsy faces multiple practical barriers in low tumor burden settings, such as high cost, lack of standardization, and variable detection sensitivity (9-12). Consequently, there is currently no widely adopted, robust tool capable of integrating multidimensional data to reliably identify T1–2 EC patients at high risk of DM. In this context, machine learning offers a novel approach to address this challenge. This study utilizes readily available clinicopathological, demographic, and treatment-related variables. Through machine learning algorithms, we integrate and analyze multidimensional data, including patient age, T/N stage, tumor size, location, differentiation grade, histologic type, and socioeconomic factors. This approach aims to uncover the complex, nonlinear interactions and latent predictive signals within these conventional variables (13,14). Consequently, it enables the construction of an individualized risk prediction model based on routine clinical data without relying on specialized tests. Such a model holds the potential to achieve a more precise quantification of DM risk, thereby providing a more actionable foundation for clinical decision-making.

Patients with DM from EC exhibit marked heterogeneity in clinical outcomes, influenced by a complex interplay of factors including primary tumor characteristics, metastatic patterns, treatment response, and patient-related variables (15,16). Notably, the prognosis, determining factors, and optimal management strategies for the distinct subgroup of patients who develop DM after an initial diagnosis of localized disease remain inadequately characterized. Current evidence indicates that the median overall survival (OS) for metastatic EC remains poor even with contemporary systemic therapy, and it remains unclear whether patients with DM originating from T1–2 primary tumors follow distinct survival trajectories or exhibit differential treatment responses (17,18). Therefore, developing a prognostic tool that integrates clinicopathological, therapeutic, and sociodemographic features, specifically tailored for patients with DM derived from T1–2 EC, holds significant clinical and scientific value.

This study develops an integrated risk assessment tool designed to address two pivotal aspects of EC management. The first component aims to identify patients with T1–2 disease who harbor a high risk of occult metastasis at the preoperative stage. The second component focuses on delivering refined prognostic stratification for those who have already developed metastatic disease. Together, these tools provide evidence-based quantitative support throughout the clinical continuum, from early therapeutic decision-making to advanced patient management. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0432/rc).

Methods

Study population and characteristics

Data for this retrospective study were retrieved from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (https://seer.cancer.gov/), an internationally recognized authoritative public database for cancer epidemiological research established and maintained by the National Cancer Institute (NCI). From 17 regions covered by the SEER database, we retrospectively extracted data on 73,782 histologically confirmed EC patients diagnosed between 2004 and 2022, of whom 17,255 were with T1–2 stage. After excluding samples with incomplete data, 12,928 subjects were finally included and randomly divided into a training set and a testing set at a 7:3 ratio. Additionally, patients with DM in this cohort were allocated to training and testing sets at the same ratio. The detailed workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. Since all SEER data had undergone de-identification, no additional ethical approval was required. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Flow diagram of the study population. M, metastasis; N, node; SEER, Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results.

The following 14 clinicopathological and demographic variables were selected for analysis: age (<65 and ≥65 years), sex (female, male), race (White, Black, other), marital status (married, unmarried), American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) T stage (T1, T2), AJCC N stage (N0, N1, N2, N3), tumor size (<2, 2–<4, 4–<6, ≥6 cm, unknown), primary site (upper, middle, lower esophagus, overlapping lesion), differentiation grade (I, II, III, IV, unknown), histology (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma), radiotherapy (no/unknown, yes), chemotherapy (no/unknown, yes), median household income (<$50,000, $50,000–<60,000, $60,000–<70,000, ≥$70,000), and rural-urban continuum (<250,000, 250,000–<1 million population, ≥1 million population, other). Radiotherapy and chemotherapy were excluded from the DM prediction model, and the remaining 12 preoperative baseline variables were used for modeling. All 14 variables were included in prognostic modeling for patients with DM.

DM was defined as involvement of distant organs or lymph nodes. OS considered death from any cause, while cancer-specific survival (CSS) included only deaths attributable to EC.

Development and evaluation of machine learning models

To develop a machine learning model for predicting DM in patients with T1–2 EC, we first performed Spearman correlation analysis on the variables, treating a correlation coefficient >0.7 as indicating high collinearity. Based on the training set, univariate and multivariate logistic regression (LR) analyses were then conducted to preliminarily identify factors associated with DM. Subsequently, variable selection was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and the Boruta algorithm. Features consistently identified by both methods were retained to construct the final prediction model.

We employed 12 machine learning algorithms to construct predictive models, including categorical boosting (CatBoost), Rangr, support vector machine (SVM), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), decision tree (DT), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), Bayesian networks (BNs), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), LR, Naive Bayes classifier (NBC), neural network (NNET), and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost). All models underwent internal validation via five-fold cross-validation. A comprehensive evaluation was performed using both visual analytical curves and quantitative metrics. The overall discriminative ability was assessed by plotting the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calculating the area under the ROC curve (AUC). To evaluate performance on the minority class, the precision-recall (PR) curve and its corresponding area under the curve were utilized. Calibration curves were examined to verify the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed risk. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to quantify the clinical net benefit of the models. Additionally, model performance was evaluated using the following metrics: Accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, and precision. The optimal model was selected based on a synthesis of the graphical results and quantitative metrics obtained from the training set and testing set.

To enhance the interpretability of the machine learning model, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, which aims to address the common “black-box” limitation in clinical prediction and make model outputs more understandable and acceptable to clinicians (19,20). Based on the Shapley value concept from game theory, this method quantifies the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, thereby identifying key predictors associated with the risk of DM. Specifically, we employed the well-performing NBC model and used the SHAP approach to rank feature importance. The analysis indicated that features with positive SHAP values increased the risk of DM in EC patients, whereas those with negative values had a protective effect. Furthermore, two representative patient cases were selected to provide individualized interpretation of DM risk using SHAP values, visually demonstrating how different features contributed to risk in specific cases.

Development and evaluation of prognostic models

In the prognostic analysis, potential prognostic variables were first screened using univariate Cox regression (P<0.05). Significant variables were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression model to identify independent prognostic factors, which were then utilized to construct a nomogram. The predictive performance and clinical applicability of the nomogram were evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), AUC, calibration curves, and DCA.

Statistical analysis

This study described categorical variables using frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were conducted with R software (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and FreeStatistics v2.4.0. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included a total of 12,928 patients diagnosed with EC from the SEER database. Among them, 2,821 patients (21.8%) had DM, while 10,107 (78.2%) did not. The patients were randomly allocated into a training set (n=9,050) and a testing set (n=3,878) in a 7:3 ratio. The study population consisted of 55.7% of patients aged ≥65 years, with a predominance of males (78.9%) and White individuals (85.7%); 59.2% were unmarried. Regarding clinical stage, 72.2% were classified as T1 and 62.3% as N0. Pathologically, 39.1% were grade II, 69.6% had tumors located in the lower esophagus, and adenocarcinoma was the most common histological type (69.8%); 19.2% of tumors measured 4–<6 cm in size. Radiotherapy was administered to 54.5% of patients, while 58.8% did not receive chemotherapy. In terms of socioeconomic indicators, 67.7% of patients had a median household income exceeding $70,000, and 52.5% resided in a rural-urban continuum with a population of at least 1 million. The training and testing sets remained well-balanced across all baseline characteristics (all P>0.05), supporting the reliability of subsequent model validation. Variable selection for machine learning models

Potential collinearity among the measured parameters was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis (Figure 2A). To identify independent risk factors for DM in EC patients, variables with a significance level of P<0.05 in univariate LR were included in a multivariate LR model. Univariate analysis indicated that age ≥65 years, histology, T stage, and rural-urban continuum were protective factors against DM [odds ratio (OR) <1, 95% confidence interval (CI) <1, P<0.05]. In contrast, male patients, primary site, grade, N stage, and tumor size were identified as significant risk factors associated with an increased DM risk (OR >1, 95% CI >1, P<0.05). Multivariable LR analysis revealed that age ≥65 years, T stage, and histology were independent protective factors associated with a reduced risk of DM in EC patients (OR <1, 95% CI <1, P<0.05). Conversely, male patients, primary site, Grade, N stage, and tumor size were identified as independent predictors of an increased risk of DM (OR >1, 95% CI >1, P<0.05) (Table 2). Subsequently, two feature selection methods, namely LASSO regression and the Boruta algorithm, were applied to the clinical variables (Figure 2). LASSO regression identified five variables, whereas the Boruta algorithm selected 10. Through the integration of both approaches, five key prognostic features were determined, including T stage, N stage, age, tumor size, and primary site.

Development and evaluation of machine learning models

Based on seven predictors, we developed and compared twelve machine learning models on the training set, including CatBoost, Ranger, SVM, XGB, DT, LightGBM, GBM, BNs, KNN, LR, NBC, NNET, and AdaBoost. This study systematically evaluated the predictive performance of twelve machine learning models for DM in patients with T1–2 EC. ROC curve analysis indicated that the NBC model demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability in both the training and validation sets, with AUC values of 0.740 and 0.750, respectively (Figure 3A,3B). To address the class imbalance caused by the low proportion of positive samples, PR curve analysis was further conducted, revealing that the NBC model also performed notably well under such imbalanced conditions (Figure 3C,3D). Calibration curve analysis suggested that this model exhibited favorable predictive calibration (Figure 3E,3F). DCA further supported that the NBC model provided relatively high clinical net benefit, indicating its potential value in assisting diagnosis (Figure 3G,3H). Based on a comprehensive assessment of multiple metrics, including AUC, accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and specificity, and considering that the prediction of DM in EC demands high sensitivity to minimize missed diagnoses, the NBC model demonstrated relatively robust and acceptable overall performance in predicting DM of EC. Accordingly, the NBC model was selected as the preferred predictive model in this study (Table 3).

Model interpretation and feature importance

To visualize the contribution of each feature variable within the NBC model, we employed the SHAP method. The SHAP feature importance ranking plot (Figure 4A) and the beeswarm plot (Figure 4B) collectively illustrate the global contribution of all features. In these visualizations, the horizontal axis represents the SHAP value, indicating both the magnitude and direction of a feature’s contribution to the prediction, while the vertical axis ranks the features in descending order based on their mean absolute SHAP value, providing an intuitive hierarchy of their relative importance. In our model, the N stage possessed the highest SHAP value, and this identified it as the most critical factor influencing DM in patients with T1–2 EC. To further elucidate the model’s interpretability at the individual level, we present two representative cases. The first patient, who did not develop DM, was associated with low SHAP values across features and a low prediction score of 0.279, consistent with a low estimated probability of DM (Figure 4C). In contrast, the second patient, who developed DM, demonstrated high SHAP values and a correspondingly high prediction score of 0.627, reflecting a high model-assessed likelihood of the event (Figure 4D).

A total of 2,600 patients (92.2%) died during the study, with EC being the cause of death in 2,440 cases (86.5%). Comparison between the training and testing sets showed no statistically significant differences in any baseline characteristics except for age distribution (P<0.05), confirming good comparability between the two groups.

To assess the predictive performance and clinical applicability of the models, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation using an independent test set. For OS, the C-index was 0.672 (95% CI: 0.66–0.69) in the training set and 0.652 (95% CI: 0.63–0.68) in the testing set. The AUCs for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS prediction were 0.809, 0.768, and 0.744 in the training set, and 0.802, 0.754, and 0.697 in the testing set, respectively (Figure 6A,6B). Calibration curves showed good agreement between observed and predicted survival probabilities at all three time points (Figure 6C-6H). DCA curves further indicated a positive net clinical benefit (Figure 6I-6N). For CSS, the C-index was 0.673 (95% CI: 0.66–0.69) in the training set and 0.654 (95% CI: 0.63–0.68) in the testing set. The corresponding AUCs for 1-, 3-, and 5-year predictions were 0.823, 0.771, and 0.746 for the training set, and 0.797, 0.754, and 0.700 for the testing set (Figure 7A,7B). Calibration was also satisfactory, and DCA curves supported the clinical utility of the CSS model (Figure 7C-7N). In summary, the prediction models for OS and CSS demonstrated good discrimination, calibration, and clinical usefulness, confirming their reliable performance.

Discussion

EC is a highly aggressive malignancy with persistently high global incidence and mortality rates (21,22). While patients with T1–2 stage disease can achieve favorable local control through surgery and other localized treatments, DM remains a leading cause of treatment failure and reduced survival (23,24). Common sites of metastasis include the liver, lungs, and bones, and once metastasis occurs, treatment options become considerably limited, often resulting in poor prognosis (15). In current clinical practice, there is a lack of reliable tools to effectively identify high-risk individuals for metastasis at an early stage, as well as a shortage of models for precise prognostic stratification in patients who have already developed metastasis.

Based on multivariate regression and feature selection using LASSO and Boruta algorithms, we identified five independent predictors of DM in patients with T1–2 EC. Using these variables, we developed and compared 12 machine learning models. The NBC model demonstrated relatively balanced and stable predictive performance across both training and testing sets. SHAP interpretability analysis quantified and visualized feature contributions, confirming that N stage, T stage, tumor size, primary site, and age were the core factors influencing risk stratification. For patients who developed DM, Cox regression identified independent prognostic factors for OS and CSS, including race, T stage, chemotherapy, marital status, and median household income. A prognostic prediction model was subsequently constructed. Together, these models form an integrated assessment framework spanning from preoperative DM risk prediction to post-metastatic survival stratification. In clinical practice, imaging evaluation serves as the core basis for identifying patients at high risk of DM, and the DM risk prediction model can provide valuable supplementary support to further improve the accuracy of risk assessment. The prognosis model for patients with DM provides individualized survival estimates to support clinical decision-making, trial enrollment, and patient communication.

Our analysis confirms that the N stage of the primary tumor is the most critical driver in predicting DM in patients with EC. Guo et al. point out that a higher N stage directly reflects the ability of cancer cells to break through the confines of the primary lesion, thereby acquiring enhanced invasive and metastatic potential (25). Studies corroborating our findings indicate that N stage is an independent high-risk factor for the development of lung, bone, and liver metastases in EC (26-28). Furthermore, research in other tumor types supports this general pattern. For example, a large-scale real-world study using machine learning to predict DM in colorectal cancer similarly identified N stage as the most influential predictor among all clinicopathological features (14). This reinforces the universal relevance of N staging in evaluating metastatic risk across solid tumors. Furthermore, tumor size, as a direct reflection of tumor burden, also demonstrated significant predictive value for metastatic risk in this study. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating a positive correlation between tumor diameter and the probability of microvascular invasion and lymphatic metastasis (29,30). In the present study, we observed that patients with T1 stage EC had a higher risk of DM than those with T2 stage disease, a finding inconsistent with the conventional understanding that may be explained by multiple pathological and biological mechanisms. First, despite limited invasion depth, some T1 stage tumors demonstrate more aggressive biological behaviors such as poor differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion, which confer early invasive and metastatic potential and significantly increase DM risk (31,32). Second, as T1 stage tumors are restricted to the mucosa or submucosa with abundant blood and lymphatic vessels, they can easily form occult micrometastases undetectable by conventional imaging and pathology, leading to underestimated metastatic risk (33,34). Variations in clinical management also contribute to this difference. T2 stage patients more often receive intensive preoperative systemic therapy including neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy, which controls tumor burden and inhibits micrometastases, thus lowering metastasis risk (35). Conversely, treatment for T1 stage disease is relatively conservative without intensive preoperative systemic intervention, allowing occult micrometastases to progress and resulting in a higher risk of DM (36). This pattern is not unique to EC, as a SEER-based study on early-stage gallbladder carcinoma similarly reported a higher risk of DM in T1 compared to T2 patients (37). Furthermore, age was inversely associated with DM in this study. This may imply that younger patients tend to exhibit more aggressive tumor biological behaviors, whereas older patients harbor relatively indolent tumors (38). Nevertheless, age demonstrated limited predictive value for DM in the present cohort.

Based on the DM risk prediction model established in this study, more proactive perioperative comprehensive therapy may be considered for T1–2 EC patients assessed as high risk. For those with high-risk clinicopathological features, including large tumor size, suspected lymph node involvement, or poor differentiation, appropriate radiotherapy and chemotherapy might serve as a reasonable treatment option, which could help reduce metastatic risk and potentially improve long-term prognosis (39-41). For patients classified as low-risk for DM using our model, clinicians may carefully consider these findings when deciding whether to omit unnecessary neoadjuvant therapy, providing evidence for individualized surgical decision-making (12).

The prognostic model for patients with DM integrates multidimensional indicators, including race, T stage, chemotherapy, marital status, and median household income, to establish a comprehensive assessment framework. Extensive research has shown that sociodemographic variables such as household income and marital status independently influence cancer prognosis (42,43). By incorporating this critical dimension into quantitative analysis, the model not only confirms the independent role of social determinants in the prognosis of EC with DM but also enables a more comprehensive characterization of patient survival risk beyond traditional biological evaluation. This tool can simultaneously identify patients requiring intensive treatment due to high disease aggressiveness and those needing targeted intervention due to limited social support, thereby advancing prognostic assessment toward a more holistic and personalized paradigm.

This study has several merits. First, the use of a large, population-based cohort of 12,928 EC patients from the SEER database provides a representative sample and ensures adequate statistical power for model development. Second, through a systematic comparison of multiple machine learning algorithms, the NBC model was identified as exhibiting optimal predictive performance for this specific task. Third, the incorporation of the SHAP framework enhanced the transparency of the prediction process and improved the interpretability of the results. Finally, by focusing on T1–2 stage EC patients with DM, the study developed a prognostic prediction model for this subgroup, establishing a coherent analytical pipeline from high-risk identification to post-metastasis outcome assessment.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the predictive performance of the NBC model warrants further improvement in future research. Second, the model was developed and internally validated solely using retrospective SEER data, lacking external validation from independent cohorts, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, the retrospective design inherently carries risks of selection and information bias. For future work, it is recommended to further optimize the sampling strategy, validate and refine the model using multi-center prospective data to enhance its clinical utility.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated an integrated risk assessment tool employing a dual-stratification strategy to support decision-making throughout the management of EC. This study established a machine learning model to assess the risk of DM in patients with T1–2 EC. It may serve as a complementary tool to imaging and other routine examinations, facilitating the identification of high-risk individuals for active therapeutic intervention. For patients who have developed metastasis, a prognostic nomogram is provided to estimate OS and CSS, offering individualized guidance for advanced treatment strategies and clinical trial enrollment. By establishing a continuous risk‑assessment framework spanning from early intervention to advanced‑stage management, this work provides methodological support for implementing stratified and precision medicine in EC.
