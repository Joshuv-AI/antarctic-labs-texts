When Coordination Is Avoidable: A Monotonicity Analysis of Organizational Tasks
Harang Ju

Abstract
Organizations devote substantial resources to coordination, yet which tasks actually require it for correctness remains unclear. The problem is acute in multi-agent AI systems, where coordination overhead is directly measurable and routinely exceeds the cost of the work itself. However, distributed systems theory provides a precise answer: coordination is necessary if and only if a task is non-monotonic , meaning new information can invalidate prior conclusions. Here we show that a classic taxonomy of organizational interdependence maps onto the monotonicity criterion, yielding a decision rule and a measure of avoidable overhead (the Coordination Tax). Multi-agent simulations confirm both predictions. We classify 65 enterprise workflows and find that 48 (74%) are monotonic, then replicate on 13,417 occupational tasks from the O*NET database (42% monotonic). These classification rates imply that 24–57% of coordination spending is unnecessary for correctness.

Introduction

Coordination is a fundamental cost of organization. Meetings, approval chains, status updates, and cross-functional reviews all exist because organizations assume that coordination is necessary. Yet no formal criterion distinguishes the coordination that is necessary for correctness from the coordination that is not. The cost of this ambiguity is substantial and growing. Multi-agent AI systems, which inherit their coordination topology from the organizations they assist, now devote 40–60% of total compute to coordination overhead
[
18
]
, and the overhead grows super-linearly with group size
[
6
]
. Because these systems make coordination costs directly countable in tokens, they also provide the first clean empirical setting for testing theories of coordination necessity. The standard response is to optimize coordination by improving communication, streamlining processes, or adding coordination technology. But optimization assumes coordination is necessary. What if much of it is not?

Whether coordination is necessary depends on the structure of the task itself. Thompson’s
[
20
]
interdependence taxonomy classifies tasks by the coordination demands their structure imposes. Pooled tasks contribute independently to a common outcome and need only uniform rules. Sequential tasks pass output from one unit to the next and require planning and scheduling. Reciprocal tasks require mutual adjustment among units, the most costly coordination form. Thompson observed that pooled and sequential interdependence dominate, with reciprocal interdependence concentrating at critical integration points. This taxonomy remains foundational in organizational theory. NK fitness landscapes formalized interdependence for search and adaptation
[
12
,
14
]
, but no formalization has yielded computability guarantees for when coordination is necessary. Yet as Puranam
[
17
]
argues, the concept of interdependence remains under-theorized relative to its importance in organizational design.

Distributed computing has identified the precise structural property that determines when coordination is necessary. The CALM theorem (Consistency As Logical Monotonicity) proves that a computation can execute without coordination if and only if it is monotonic, meaning new inputs never retract previously derived outputs
[
9
,
1
]
. Non-monotonic computations, those where new information can invalidate prior conclusions, provably require coordination for correctness. The theorem has been applied extensively to databases
[
4
]
and recently unified with other coordination criteria over Lamport histories
[
10
]
, but never to organizational task analysis. CALM governs correctness, not quality. A monotonic task may still benefit from coordination for coherence, but correctness never requires it.

This paper proves a formal correspondence between Thompson’s taxonomy and CALM’s monotonicity criterion. The Thompson-CALM Bridge Theorem (Theorem
1
) yields a decision rule: pooled interdependence maps to monotone (coordination-free) specifications, sequential interdependence maps to monotone specifications under causal ordering, and reciprocal interdependence maps to non-monotone (coordination-required) specifications. A companion result, the Feedback Boundary (Proposition
2
), resolves when sequential-with-feedback becomes reciprocal, a classification that Thompson’s taxonomy leaves ambiguous and that organizational science has long recognized
[
3
]
but never formalized. The Coordination Tax (Equation
1
) then quantifies the avoidable overhead.

We apply this framework to two complementary corpora. We classify 65 enterprise workflows across all 13 categories of the APQC Process Classification Framework
[
2
]
and find that 74% are monotonic, meaning they can execute coordination-free without sacrificing correctness. We replicate on 13,417 occupational tasks from the O*NET 29.1 database
[
16
]
spanning 22 SOC major groups and find 42% monotonic. These classification rates imply that 24–57% of coordination spending is unnecessary for correctness. Multi-agent AI simulations across three model families validate the predictions empirically, confirming that monotonic tasks preserve 100% validity without coordination while non-monotonic tasks fail completely without it.

Taken together, these results connect several lines of organizational theory. The Bridge Theorem provides the formal underpinnings for Thompson’s
[
20
]
verbal classification and advances Malone and Crowston’s
[
15
]
program of characterizing dependencies by providing a formal criterion for when coordination is necessary. The Coordination Tax, in turn, identifies a new category of transaction cost
[
7
,
21
]
that is not merely excessive but structurally unnecessary. The practical implication for both is a tiered architecture, whether for human organizations or AI systems, where task topology (not authority structure) determines coordination intensity.

Theory

Theoretical Bridge Between Interdependence and Monotonicity

Coordination is required only when local actions can invalidate globally correct outcomes. Distributed systems theory formalizes this condition using monotonicity: a task is monotonic if adding new information cannot invalidate previously correct conclusions. Organizational theory classifies tasks by patterns of interdependence instead. We show that these two traditions identify the same boundary for when coordination is required for correctness.

Establishing the correspondence requires three definitions. We define correctness as the property that a system’s outputs satisfy a specified constraint under all admissible execution orders. We define coordination as any mechanism that enforces ordering or synchronization to prevent inconsistent intermediate states. A task requires coordination if correctness cannot be guaranteed under asynchronous, unordered execution. Formal definitions appear in SI Text S1.

Thompson distinguishes pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence. Pooled tasks aggregate independent contributions, and their outputs combine via a join-semilattice
[
19
]
. Sequential tasks require ordered handoffs along Lamport’s happened-before order
[
13
]
, where each agent’s computation is non-retractive. Reciprocal tasks involve mutual adjustment among agents, and the dependency graph contains a cycle with constraining mutual adjustment.

CALM provides the complementary criterion from distributed systems: a program can execute without coordination if and only if it is monotonic
[
1
,
9
,
10
]
. The underlying distinction is the same in both traditions. Outputs that only accumulate can be merged freely, while outputs that can retract require coordination to remain consistent. The following theorem establishes the connection.

Theorem 1

(Thompson-CALM Bridge)
.

Under deterministic task evaluation, reliable message delivery, and eventual consistency in state propagation, let
T
be a multi-agent task. Then:

(a)

Pooled interdependence
\implies
the task specification is monotone (coordination-free).

(b)

Sequential interdependence
\implies
the task specification is monotone under causal ordering (coordination-free with causal delivery).

(c)

Reciprocal interdependence
\implies
the task specification is non-monotone (coordination required).

Proofs appear in SI Text S2. For (a), sub-task monotonicity and the join-semilattice property guarantee that aggregate output only grows. For (b), the composition of non-retractive functions is non-retractive, so causal delivery suffices. For (c), a shared budget counterexample shows that one agent’s proposal can invalidate another’s, violating monotonicity. The two traditions converge on the same boundary.

The Bridge Theorem classifies pure sequential tasks as monotonic, but sequential pipelines often contain feedback loops (code review, quality inspection, editorial passes). The Feedback Boundary resolves whether feedback pushes a task across the monotonicity boundary.

Proposition 2

(Feedback Boundary)
.

Let
T
be a sequential task with feedback from a downstream agent to an upstream agent.

(a)

If the feedback is additive (the upstream agent incorporates new information without retracting prior output), the specification remains monotone.

(b)

If the feedback is retractive (the upstream agent revises or replaces prior output), the specification is non-monotone.

Additive feedback preserves the non-retractive property established in Theorem
1
(b), and retractive feedback violates it. This distinction maps onto Argyris and Schön’s
[
3
]
boundary between single-loop refinement and double-loop revision, recognized in organizational science but never formalized in coordination-theoretic terms. In our corpus, 9 of 65 tasks fall into this category.

The Coordination Tax

The Bridge Theorem identifies
which
tasks need coordination. The Coordination Tax quantifies how much coordination overhead is avoidable when the classification is ignored. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a portfolio of
n
tasks with non-monotonic fraction
f\in[0,1]
and coordination overhead multiplier
c>1
. If every task receives uniform coordination, the unnecessary fraction of total spending is

T(f,c)=\frac{(1-f)(c-1)}{c}

(1)

Equation (
1
) represents an upper bound on avoidable coordination overhead under a regime in which all tasks receive uniform coordination regardless of structural necessity.

The derivation is straightforward. Uniform cost
nc
minus selective cost
n(1{-}f{+}fc)
equals
n(1{-}f)(c{-}1)
; dividing by
nc
gives
T
. As
f\to 0
(no tasks need coordination),
T\to(c{-}1)/c
(nearly all coordination is wasted); as
f\to 1
(all tasks need coordination),
T\to 0
(all is justified). We now estimate
f
and
c
from two empirical corpora.

Results

We test the Bridge Theorem’s predictions in three steps: classifying enterprise workflows, replicating on occupational tasks, and validating experimentally with multi-agent simulations.

Most Enterprise Workflows Are Monotonic (74%)

Our first corpus draws from the APQC Process Classification Framework
[
2
]
, the most widely used enterprise process taxonomy (maintained since 1992). We classify 65 workflows at Levels 2–3 (team-level workflows with 2–5 agents), enumerating all eligible processes as an exhaustive census across all 13 APQC categories (see Materials and Methods).

), of which 39 are fully coordination-free and 9 require only causal ordering. The remaining 17 (26.2%) require full coordination. The pattern spans all 13 APQC categories rather than concentrating in a few domains.

Breaking the result down by APQC category, we find systematic variation (Table

Financial Resources ranks lowest (57%) because most of its tasks involve allocating shared budgets or credit across competing claims.

To understand what drives the 26% that require coordination, we examine the non-monotonic tasks individually and find that nearly all 17 involve allocation of shared finite resources (budget, headcount, capacity, inventory). The pattern echoes Thompson’s observation that reciprocal interdependence clusters at critical integration points
[
20
]
, and CALM explains why. Shared finite resources introduce negation (one agent’s allocation constrains another’s), which breaks monotonicity. Reclassifying all borderline cases as non-monotonic still yields 60–74% monotonic, so the finding is robust to classification uncertainty. In practical terms, three-quarters of enterprise workflows can execute correctly without any coordination mechanism.

Nearly Half of Occupational Tasks Are Monotonic (42%)

To test whether monotonicity prevalence extends beyond enterprise workflows, we classify 13,417 core task statements from the O*NET 29.1 occupational database
[
16
]
spanning 22 SOC major groups (Figure
1
). Of these, 5,564 (41.5%, 95% CI [40.6, 42.3]) are monotonic. The rate is lower than APQC’s 74%, which reflects the difference between team-level workflows (APQC) and individual work activities (O*NET). APQC processes decompose naturally into multi-agent sub-tasks, while O*NET tasks describe what a single worker does. The O*NET classification characterizes the inherent coordination structure of each task under multi-agent decomposition.

Figure
1
shows the breakdown by SOC group. Monotonic tasks appear in every group, from Life and Physical Science (52%) to Management (32%). Groups dominated by independent observation and analysis (sciences, sales, healthcare support) rank highest, while Management ranks lowest because it concentrates resource allocation, staffing, and cross-unit negotiation. Full per-group results with 95% Wilson confidence intervals appear in SI Table S3. The scope of coordination avoidance thus extends well beyond enterprise workflows to the broader occupational landscape.

Simulations Confirm the Monotonicity Boundary

To test the Bridge Theorem’s predictions experimentally, we run multi-agent simulations where coordination can be cleanly toggled and costs are directly measurable in tokens (see Materials and Methods). In the coordinated condition, an orchestrator plans assignments and passes intermediate outputs between agents. In the uncoordinated condition, agents receive only their sub-task descriptions and their outputs are concatenated without review. We select ten tasks spanning the monotonicity spectrum and run each under both conditions across three models (GPT-4.1 mini, Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5; 10 repetitions at temperature 0).

Discussion

We show that coordination is not required for correctness across a substantial fraction of tasks. The Bridge Theorem identifies which tasks those are, the Coordination Tax quantifies the avoidable overhead, and simulations confirm both predictions with a clean binary result. Across two independent corpora, 42–74% of tasks are monotonic and 24–57% of coordination spending may be structurally unnecessary. This creates a tension, because organizations routinely coordinate as a precaution, yet the theory shows that for a large fraction of tasks precaution adds cost without improving correctness.

The Bridge Theorem formalizes Thompson’s
[
20
]
taxonomy by proving that each interdependence type corresponds to a specific monotonicity class with known computability properties. The contribution is distinct from CALM itself, which identifies monotonicity as the computational boundary but says nothing about organizational task structure. The Bridge Theorem maps Thompson’s categories onto that boundary, yielding predictions about task portfolios that CALM alone does not make. Organizations can now use Thompson’s categories as a prescriptive decision rule with computability guarantees, not merely as a descriptive taxonomy.

The Bridge Theorem also advances the research program that Malone and Crowston
[
15
]
outlined. They defined coordination as “managing dependencies among activities” and proposed that progress would come from characterizing dependency types and the coordination processes needed to manage them. The Bridge Theorem addresses the prior question: given a dependency type, is coordination necessary at all? By mapping each of their dependency types to a monotonicity class, it provides a formal criterion that their framework lacked (the full mapping appears in SI Text S4).

Beyond the theoretical contribution, the Coordination Tax isolates a category of overhead that neither Coase
[
7
]
nor Williamson
[
21
]
identified, namely overhead that is structurally unnecessary for correctness. Standard transaction cost analysis asks how to reduce coordination costs, but the Coordination Tax asks which costs should not exist at all. Unlike most transaction costs, which resist ex ante estimation,
T(f,c)
can be computed from two observable quantities. The 24–57% range assumes uniform coordination, an idealization that provides an upper bound. Organizations that already coordinate selectively will realize smaller savings, but the formula identifies the gap between uniform and optimal allocation. The Tax also suggests a strategic response: invest in modularity. Baldwin and Clark
[
5
]
showed that modular architectures reduce coordination by encapsulating interdependence within modules. In CALM terms, well-designed modularity converts non-monotonic tasks into monotonic sub-tasks with a thin non-monotonic integration layer, reducing
f
and with it the tax.

These findings prescribe a tiered architecture for any multi-agent system, human or artificial. Tier 1 tasks (pooled/monotonic, 60% of our APQC corpus) need only fire-and-forget execution with merge via join. Tier 2 tasks (sequential, 14%) need only causal delivery. Only Tier 3 tasks (reciprocal, 26%) need the full mutual-adjustment coordination that current organizations and AI frameworks apply uniformly. For three-quarters of enterprise work, agents (human or artificial) can operate as flat peers, not as managed subordinates in a hierarchy. This tiered architecture carries a further guarantee. CALM proves that monotonic computations maintain consistency and availability under partition
[
9
]
. In organizational terms, partition corresponds to distributed or asynchronous work. Monotonic tasks tolerate it without sacrificing correctness, while non-monotonic tasks force a trade-off between responsiveness and alignment.

Current multi-agent AI frameworks inherit their coordination topology from the organizations they assist, replicating managerial hierarchies by default. The Bridge Theorem prescribes the reverse. Agent topology should mirror the task’s dependency structure, not the organization’s authority graph. The O*NET results extend this implication to the broader economy. With 42% of occupational tasks monotonic, the scope for coordination avoidance spans every occupational category. The difference between APQC (74%) and O*NET (42%) reflects the unit of analysis, but both figures suggest substantial room for coordination reduction.

Several boundary conditions constrain the scope. CALM guarantees correctness, not quality, so independently written sections may be valid yet stylistically inconsistent, so coordination for coherence may still be worthwhile on monotonic tasks. We adopt correctness as the criterion precisely because it admits a formal boundary; broader criteria would require domain-specific judgment that resists formalization. Misclassification risk is asymmetric (a false monotonic label endangers correctness; a false non-monotonic label adds unnecessary overhead), so systems should default to coordination when uncertain. The 74% prevalence holds at APQC Levels 2–3; coarser decomposition pushes toward non-monotonic, finer toward monotonic (60–74% under any reclassification). Our classification is static; tasks can shift mid-execution, and runtime reclassification is future work. LLM agents are stochastic and may not faithfully implement a monotone specification; pairing classification with output validation remains essential. Our simulations enforce binary validity (VALID/INVALID); in practice, coordination failures often produce gradual quality degradation or rework rather than the clean 0% boundary observed in the experimental sandbox.

The Bridge Theorem shows that whether a task requires coordination is a computable property of its structure. Across two independent corpora, 42–74% of tasks are monotonic and provably do not require coordination for correctness. For organizations and multi-agent systems alike, task topology rather than organizational convention can now determine coordination intensity. Coordination is not wasteful in general, but applying it uniformly wastes resources on tasks where it cannot improve correctness. As both organizations and AI systems grow in complexity, understanding when coordination is necessary may prove as important as improving coordination itself.

Materials and Methods

APQC corpus construction.

We draw our primary task corpus from the APQC Process Classification Framework
[
2
]
, the most widely used enterprise process taxonomy. We define tasks at Levels 2–3 (2–5 agents, team-level workflows). A process qualifies if it decomposes into 2+ agent sub-tasks, involves information processing, produces a defined output, and spans industries. We enumerate all eligible processes as an exhaustive census, yielding 65 tasks across all 13 APQC categories (SI Table S2). For each task, we classify interdependence structure using Thompson’s taxonomy, apply the Bridge Theorem to predict CALM classification, and verify against five heuristic tests (SI Text S3). Borderline cases default to non-monotonic.

O*NET classification procedure.

To scale beyond the 65 APQC tasks, we validate an LLM classifier against the author-classified APQC corpus. We use GPT-4.1 mini and Claude Sonnet 4.5, each under two prompting conditions (3 runs at temperature 0), achieving
\kappa\geq 0.88
and
\geq
95% accuracy (SI Table S1). Every misclassification labels a monotonic task as non-monotonic (safe bias). Following Eloundou et al.
[
8
]
, who used GPT-4 to annotate O*NET tasks for LLM exposure, we apply the validated classifier (GPT-4.1 mini, heuristic condition) to 13,417 core task statements from the O*NET 29.1 occupational database
[
16
]
spanning 22 SOC major groups.

Simulation design.

Ten tasks span the monotonicity spectrum (four monotonic, two requiring ordering only, four non-monotonic) under two conditions. In the coordinated condition, an orchestrator LLM plans assignments, passes intermediate outputs, and reviews for consistency. In the uncoordinated condition, agents receive only their sub-task descriptions and outputs are concatenated mechanically. An LLM-as-judge evaluator reasons step-by-step against pre-specified validity criteria before issuing a VALID or INVALID verdict. We run 10 repetitions at temperature 0 across three models (GPT-4.1 mini, Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.5). Full task specifications, validity criteria, and token overhead ratios appear in SI Text S8.
