Prognostic model for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma treated with transarterial therapy plus TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies
Authors: Tong Yuan, Junjie Liu, Xing Lv, Guan Tan, Ronghua Zhu, Yi Zhou, Pengbo Zhang, Lin Xue, Chunyu Zhang, Zhiyong Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransarterial therapy, including transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), has long been a cornerstone in the management of unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC). In parallel, the emergence of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies has reshaped the systemic treatment landscape. Recently, the combination of transarterial therapy with TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies has shown promising efficacy in treating uHCC, but lacks a standardized prognostic model.MethodsWe retrospectively included 243 patients with uHCC treated with transarterial therapy plus TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies, divided into training (n = 169) and validation (n = 74) cohorts. Within the training cohort, Cox regression identified factors associated with overall survival (OS), forming a scoring model. Model performance was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration plots, and the concordance index.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified hepatitis B virus infection, largest tumor size pre-treatment, baseline neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and spleen volume at 6 weeks post-treatment as independent prognostic factors for OS. The HLNS score was constructed and stratified patients into low- and high-risk categories, with median OS of 34.6 vs. 7.3 months (p < 0.001), and objective response rates of 49.6% vs. 23.8% (p = 0.006). The model’s performance was corroborated across both the validation and entire cohorts.ConclusionThe HLNS score provides a useful tool for personalized prognostication and risk classification of uHCC patients treated with transarterial therapy plus TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04154-w.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the therapeutic landscape for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) has evolved markedly [1]. The integration of programmed death-1 (PD-1) blockade with anti-angiogenic agents has been established as a primary treatment regimen for uHCC patients [2]. In recent years, transarterial therapies combined with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and anti-PD-1 antibodies have shown manageable toxicity and superior tumor response rates compared to monotherapy, along with improved long-term survival outcomes [3]. Frequently employed transarterial approaches encompass transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) [4]. TACE involves the delivery of chemotherapy drugs through an arterial route with selective embolization, while HAIC administers chemotherapy without embolization.

However, the efficacy of combining transarterial therapy, TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies varies widely among patients, largely due to significant heterogeneity in factors such as systemic inflammatory status, liver function, and tumor burden [3]. This variation emphasizes the necessity for prognostic tools capable of identifying which patients are most likely to respond favorably to such treatments. Therefore, identifying patients likely to benefit from triple therapy will aid clinical decision-making for uHCC.

Although biomarkers have been explored for predicting responses to anti-PD-1 monotherapy or targeted combination therapy in HCC [5–7], validated models for the increasingly common triple therapy involving transarterial treatment are still lacking. Clinical parameters, including systemic inflammatory status [8], tumor markers [9], tumor burden [10], and spleen variables [11], have been demonstrated to correlate closely with survival outcomes in HCC patients and are potential candidates for prognostic model development. This study aims to establish and test a survival prediction and risk stratification framework tailored to uHCC patients treated with a combination of transarterial therapy, TKIs, and anti-PD-1 antibodies.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study received ethical approval from the institutional review board (IRB) of Tongji Hospital and was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. IRB approval was obtained before the initiation of data cleaning and statistical analysis. All clinical data were de-identified prior to analysis, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design. We reviewed the clinical records of uHCC patients treated with transarterial therapy (TACE or HAIC) in combination with TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies between April 2020 and September 2024 at the Hepatic Surgery Center of Tongji Hospital. Inclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosed with HCC based on imaging or pathology [12]; age ≥ 18 years; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) of 0–1; Child-Pugh score A or B; and presence of at least one measurable lesion as defined by the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) [13]. In addition, all patients were evaluated as having uHCC and were deemed suitable for triple therapy according to multidisciplinary team (MDT) recommendations. uHCC was defined as either: (i) surgical unresectability, where safe and curative resection was not feasible due to tumor size, location, multifocality, or inadequate future liver remnant; or (ii) oncological unresectability, where resection was technically possible but expected to result in inferior prognosis compared with non-surgical strategies [14]. The choice between TACE and HAIC was made at the discretion of the treating physician, based on tumor characteristics and the patient’s overall condition. Patients with one of the following conditions were excluded: prior antitumor treatment before starting triple therapy; receipt of other therapies during the combination treatment; presence of other malignancies; lack of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans pre- and post-treatment; and incomplete medical or follow-up data.

Treatment protocol

The treatment regimen administered to all patients included TACE or HAIC in conjunction with TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies. The frequency of TACE sessions was determined by the cumulative iodine oil load and the degree of enhancement of liver cancer lesions. The FOLFOX4 chemotherapy regimen was employed for HAIC and was delivered at three-week intervals. TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies were administered within one week after the first transarterial therapy. The TKIs employed were lenvatinib (12 mg/day for individuals ≥ 60 kg; 8 mg/day for < 60 kg), apatinib (250 mg/day), donafenib (200 mg twice daily). As part of the immunotherapy regimen, patients received one of several anti-PD-1 antibodies: fixed-dose regimens included toripalimab, sintilimab, and pembrolizumab at 200 mg every three weeks, while tislelizumab was given at 240 mg with the same frequency. Camrelizumab dosing was weight-dependent, with either 200 mg for patients ≥ 50 kg or 3 mg/kg for those < 50 kg. Details of specific TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies used in this study are summarized in Table S1. Subgroup analyses of overall survival (OS) according to specific TKI + anti-PD-1 antibody combinations were performed, and the corresponding Kaplan–Meier curves are provided in Figure S1.

Data collection and study objectives

Patients underwent imaging and blood tests before treatment and at 6 weeks post-treatment. The final follow-up occurred in April 2025. The medical records provided the following data: age, gender, liver cirrhosis, platelet-to-spleen length ratio, hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection status, body mass index (BMI), ascites, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), largest tumor size, tumor number, macrovascular invasion, extrahepatic metastasis, Child-Pugh score, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), spleen thickness, spleen length, and spleen volume at baseline and 6 weeks after treatment. HBV infection was defined as positivity for hepatitis B surface antigen. For HBV-positive patients, baseline HBV DNA levels and the use and timing of nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy were recorded. HBV DNA values reported as < 100 IU/mL were classified as suppressed (below the lower limit of quantification), whereas higher values were considered indicative of detectable viremia. Liver cirrhosis was diagnosed based on characteristic imaging features suggestive of cirrhosis observed on CT or MRI.

The primary endpoint was OS, which was defined as the duration from the initiation of treatment to death from any cause. Secondary endpoints consisted of the objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR), providing further evaluation of therapeutic efficacy. The ORR represented the percentage of individuals attaining either complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) maintained for a minimum of four weeks, while DCR encompassed both ORR and stable disease (SD). Treatment responses were independently assessed by two radiologists with 20 years of experience using mRECIST criteria specific to HCC.

Spleen volume assessment

Spleen volume was quantitatively assessed using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI at the designated evaluation time points, and changes were calculated. The volume was derived using the formula: spleen volume = 30 + 0.58 × (Lmax × Thhilum × Hvert) [7], where Lmax is the maximum diameter on any transverse section and Thhilum is the thickness at the hilum. Hvert denotes the vertical height of the spleen, assessed by the number of consecutive CT or MRI slices covering the spleen. To ensure reliability, spleen measurements and volume calculations were independently performed by two radiologists blinded to other patient data. In cases of disagreement, the final spleen measurements were determined by averaging the two readers’ measurements. Inter- and intra-observer reproducibility were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), which showed good inter-observer agreement (ICC = 0.848, 95% CI 0.799–0.885) and excellent intra-observer agreement (ICC = 0.931, 95% CI 0.912–0.946).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were summarized as means with standard deviations and were assessed using the independent samples t-test. Categorical data were reported as counts and proportions, with intergroup differences evaluated through Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests based on the sample characteristics. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate survival curves, and differences across groups were tested using the log-rank method. All eligible patients who initiated triple therapy between April 2020 and September 2024 were included in the entire cohort. A 7:3 random allocation was employed to assign patients to the training and validation cohorts for model development and evaluation. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to derive hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Significant predictors from the univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were included in the multivariate model to identify factors independently associated with OS. Linearity assumptions for continuous variables were examined using restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions with four knots, and nonlinearity was assessed by Wald χ² tests. To mitigate overfitting and assess the robustness of variable selection, we performed a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression as a sensitivity analysis. The optimal penalty (λmin) was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients at λmin were recorded and compared with the set of predictors from the primary multivariable Cox analysis. Given the concordance between approaches, we report coefficients (HRs and 95% CIs) from the prespecified primary multivariable Cox model and used these to construct the nomogram, while LASSO results are provided as supplementary robustness evidence.

A prognostic nomogram was subsequently constructed using these independent predictors. Risk scores were computed for individuals in the training, validation, and overall cohorts by applying the regression coefficients derived from the multivariate model. Model discrimination was measured via the concordance index (C-index), and its time-based predictive validity was tested using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. To ensure robustness, internal validation of the model was conducted in the entire cohort using both 10-fold cross-validation and 1000-bootstrap resampling. To further assess temporal generalizability, we additionally defined a temporal validation subset within the entire cohort, consisting of patients who initiated triple therapy on or after 1 January 2023. During the study period, indications for triple therapy, HAIC/TACE techniques, and the systemic agents used at our center remained broadly stable. No major shifts in treatment practice occurred after 2023 that would be expected to systematically bias the temporal validation. The HLNS model derived from the original training cohort was then applied to this post-2023 subset without refitting. Model performance in the temporal cohort was evaluated using the C-index and time-dependent ROC curves. Calibration curves were further applied to illustrate agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Statistical significance was determined using two-sided tests, with a threshold p < 0.05. All statistical computations were performed in R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A cohort of 243 patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria was enrolled in this study. As shown in Fig. 1, random allocation resulted in 169 patients assigned to the training cohort and 74 to the validation cohort. In both cohorts, the mean age was approximately 54 years; the majority of patients were male; and most had preserved liver function (Child–Pugh A) with an ECOG PS of 0. Macrovascular invasion, multiple tumors, and BCLC stage C were common, reflecting the advanced disease status of the study population. The mean baseline spleen volume was 375.40 cm³ in the training cohort and 358.87 cm³ in the validation cohort, and spleen volume increased after triple therapy in most patients. HBV infection was present in 146 patients (86.4%) in the training cohort and 59 patients (79.7%) in the validation cohort. Among 205 HBV-positive patients, baseline HBV DNA was < 100 IU/mL in 88 (42.9%), whereas 117 (57.1%) had detectable viremia. Of the 185 HBV-positive patients who received nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy, 54 (29.2%) had started antiviral treatment before the diagnosis of HCC, and 131 (70.8%) initiated therapy after HCC diagnosis but before the start of triple therapy (Table S2). Detailed baseline demographics and clinical parameters for both cohorts are presented in Table 1. The median duration of follow-up was 26.3 months (95% CI: 24.4–33.8) for the entire cohort, with the training and validation cohorts having median follow-up times of 27.8 months (95% CI: 24.3–34.6) and 25.9 months (95% CI: 22.5–36.3), respectively. The median OS for all patients was 22.0 months (95% CI: 15.8–29.5), with survival probabilities of 83.9% at 0.5 year, 62.8% at 1 year, and 46.9% at 2 years (Table S3). The training cohort showed a median OS of 23.4 months (95% CI: 16.9–34.6), whereas the validation cohort exhibited 15.0 months (95% CI: 11.1–26.9). No significant difference in survival outcomes was identified between the training and validation cohorts (Table S3).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses in the training cohort

A total of 23 variables potentially associated with OS were initially evaluated using univariate Cox regression in the training cohort (n = 169). Among them, 11 variables showed statistically significant associations with OS and were subsequently entered into the multivariate analysis. The final multivariate model identified HBV infection (HR = 2.50, p = 0.039) as a significant categorical predictor. In addition, three continuous variables, including largest tumor size (HR = 1.08, p = 0.006), NLR (HR = 1.15, p = 0.011), and spleen volume at C2 (6 weeks after treatment initiation; HR = 1.003, p = 0.033) were found to be independent survival predictors. These four factors were used to construct the prognostic model (Table 2).

To examine whether the inclusion of continuous predictors as linear terms was appropriate, RCS analysis was performed. RCS analysis showed that all continuous predictors approximately satisfied the linearity assumption, except for NLR (p = 0.032) and PLR (p = 0.020), which exhibited mild nonlinear trends (Table S4). PLR was not significantly associated with OS in the univariate analysis and was therefore not entered into the multivariable Cox model. As for NLR, although it showed a mild deviation from linearity, the overall functional form was approximately monotonic without distinct inflection points (Figure S2). Alternative parameterizations, including log-transformed NLR, did not materially improve model discrimination or calibration compared with the linear specification. Therefore, NLR was retained as a linear term in the final model for parsimony and interpretability.

To further confirm the robustness of variable selection and minimize potential overfitting, a LASSO Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed as a sensitivity analysis (Figure S3). At the optimal penalty parameter (λmin = 0.0607), eight variables with non-zero coefficients were identified. These variables were subsequently assessed by univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Table S5). Consistently, HBV infection, largest tumor size, NLR, and spleen volume at C2 remained independent prognostic factors for OS, demonstrating that the variable selection process was stable and reproducible across methods.

Development and evaluation of the HLNS score

Based on the identification of HBV infection, Largest tumor size, NLR, and Spleen volume at C2 as key prognostic factors, we developed a nomogram, named the HLNS score, to estimate individual survival probabilities at 0.5, 1, and 2 years (Fig. 2). Time-dependent ROC analyses confirmed the model’s strong predictive power at 0.5, 1, and 2 years, with AUCs of 0.80, 0.78, and 0.77 for the training cohort; 0.73, 0.73, and 0.77 for the validation cohort; and 0.76, 0.75, and 0.76 for the entire cohort (Fig. 3). The HLNS-based Cox model aligned well with the Kaplan-Meier survival estimates, while calibration plots showed high concordance between predicted and actual outcomes (Fig. 4). The HLNS score also demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability, with C-index values of 0.72, 0.68, and 0.70 in the training, validation, and entire cohorts, respectively. Further internal validation of the HLNS score in the entire cohort using 10-fold cross-validation yielded a C-index of 0.69 and mean AUCs of 0.75, 0.73, and 0.73 at 0.5, 1, and 2 years, respectively. In addition, 1000-bootstrap resampling confirmed the robustness of the HLNS score, with an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.70 and time-dependent AUCs of 0.77, 0.75, and 0.76 at 0.5, 1, and 2 years, respectively (Table S6). To further assess temporal generalizability, the temporal validation cohort (n = 96) achieved a C-index of 0.71 and time-dependent AUCs of 0.76, 0.70, and 0.76 at 0.5, 1, and 2 years, respectively (Table S6, Figure S4), indicating good temporal stability and calibration.

Correlation between HLNS score and treatment response

Analysis of the training cohort revealed that patients with lower HLNS score had a significantly improved ORR of 49.6%, nearly double that of the high-score group (23.8%) (p = 0.006). The DCR also favored the low-score group, reaching 97.6% versus 83.3% in the high-score group (p = 0.002) (Table S7). Similar trends in ORR were observed in both the validation and entire cohorts, highlighting a strong association between the HLNS score and treatment response across all patient groups.

Discussion

Several clinical trials targeting uHCC patients have demonstrated that combining transarterial therapy with TKIs and anti-PD-1 antibodies yields promising tumor responses and acceptable toxicity [15–17]. Nevertheless, individual prognoses show considerable variability, shaped by factors like systemic inflammation, liver status, and tumor burden [18]. Effective risk assessment and accurate prognosis prediction for these patients are essential for guiding treatment decisions.

This study focused on uHCC patients undergoing a triple therapy regimen that included TKIs, anti-PD-1 antibodies, and either TACE or HAIC. Key baseline factors, such as HBV status, largest tumor size, and NLR, along with the post-treatment variable spleen volume at C2, were identified as critical prognostic variables. Using multivariate Cox regression analysis, we developed the HLNS score model. Among the training cohort, individuals classified into the low HLNS score group had a median OS of 34.6 months, compared to 7.3 months in the high HLNS score group, indicating a significant survival disparity. Notably, the HLNS score also exhibited good performance and discriminative ability in the validation cohort. This nomogram can predict individual survival probabilities early during treatment. Moreover, we observed that the HLNS score stratification was generally consistent with imaging-based treatment responses. These findings are of substantial clinical importance. First, the HLNS score is constructed from routine clinical parameters, making it easily applicable in real-world clinical settings, with straightforward model construction. Second, the HLNS score could serve as a valuable tool for clinicians to pinpoint patients with a higher likelihood of achieving favorable outcomes from triple therapy.

Previous studies have pointed out that liver status, laboratory markers, and tumor characteristics are prognostic factors for HCC patients [19]. Recently, several prognostic models have been developed, including the CRAFITY score for HCC patients receiving immune monotherapy, as well as the MAPS-CRAFITY and LANS scores for those treated with combined anti-PD-1 and targeted therapy [5–7]. However, as our retrospective cohort lacked routine C-reactive protein (CRP) testing and sarcopenia assessment, direct comparison with CRAFITY or MAPS-CRAFITY scores was not feasible. Given the availability of corresponding variables, we compared the HLNS and LANS scores and found that the HLNS model achieved superior predictive performance (C-index = 0.70 vs. 0.58; AUCs at 0.5, 1, and 2 years = 0.76 vs. 0.70, 0.75 vs. 0.61, 0.76 vs. 0.57; Figure S5). These results further confirm the robustness and discriminative advantage of the HLNS score for patients receiving triple therapy. However, the clinical utility of the HLNS score depends not only on its statistical performance but also on how it differs from existing tools and how it can be implemented in routine practice.

Beyond these comparative performance metrics, the HLNS score also differs conceptually from previously proposed models such as CRAFITY, MAPS-CRAFITY, and LANS. It was specifically derived in patients receiving triple therapy, whereas prior scores were developed in cohorts treated with immune monotherapy or doublet regimens without HAIC or TACE. From a clinical standpoint, the HLNS score can be used as a simple decision-support tool at the first on-treatment evaluation. Using the predefined cutoff, patients in the low-score group have favorable predicted survival and may continue triple therapy with standard surveillance intervals. When deep or durable responses are observed, these patients could also be considered for treatment de-escalation or conversion to potentially curative approaches. In contrast, patients in the high-score group have poor predicted outcomes despite triple therapy. For these patients, clinicians may choose closer imaging and laboratory monitoring, more frequent multidisciplinary review, and early consideration of treatment modification, enrolment in clinical trials, or alternative systemic regimens. In this way, the HLNS score complements existing staging systems and radiologic response assessments and helps tailor treatment intensity and follow-up strategies to individual risk profiles.

In the context of advanced HCC immunotherapy, HBV status is considered a factor that may affect treatment outcomes. For instance, the IMbrave150 study confirmed the clinical efficacy of atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab (T + A regimen) in uHCC patients, and subgroup analyses suggested that HBV-related HCC patients might benefit more from this treatment [20]. Similarly, the combination of pembrolizumab and lenvatinib has demonstrated a high ORR and survival benefits in HBV-related HCC patients [21]. However, our study identified HBV infection as an independent risk factor in patients receiving triple therapy. Initially, we attributed this primarily to the potential risk of HBV reactivation due to undergoing TACE or HAIC treatment, but we recognize that this explanation may have been overly absolute [22, 23]. In fact, the majority of HBV-positive patients in our cohort received antiviral therapy with nucleos(t)ide analogs (Table S2), in accordance with current international and Chinese guidelines [24–26]. Previous real-world studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that prophylactic antiviral therapy can significantly reduce the risks of HBV reactivation, treatment interruption, and HBV-related hepatic failure, thereby improving treatment safety and efficacy [27, 28]. Consistent with this evidence, no clinically significant HBV reactivation–related hepatic failure events were observed in our study. Nevertheless, HBV infection still emerged as an independent adverse prognostic factor, which may reflect the long-term effects of viral infection on the immune microenvironment and its complex interactions with systemic therapy responses. These findings underscore the importance of routine HBV DNA monitoring and timely initiation of antiviral therapy for HBV-related HCC patients undergoing triple therapy, while also highlighting the need for future studies to further elucidate the impact of HBV status on immunotherapy and combination therapy outcomes.

Tumor burden is a critical prognostic factor, as larger tumors are more frequently associated with vascular invasion, extrahepatic metastases, and an increased risk of liver failure [29]. At the same time, tumor burden also significantly affects the efficacy of immunotherapy [10]. Patients with lower tumor burden at baseline generally experience more favorable outcomes. As tumor burden increases, the tumor microenvironment becomes increasingly infiltrated by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which fosters immune escape and immunosuppression [30]. Additionally, larger tumors release more immune-suppressive cytokines compared to smaller tumors [31]. Therefore, largest tumor size pre-treatment was prioritized in model development and became a key component of the HLNS score.

NLR is a significant peripheral blood biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and immune function. It has gained widespread application in prognostication and treatment response prediction for various malignancies [32]. High NLR is consistently linked to unfavorable treatment responses and decreased survival in HCC, regardless of whether patients receive surgery, TACE, targeted agents, or immunotherapy [8, 33]. Liu et al. [34] reported that an NLR ≥ 4 independently predicted worse survival in HCC patients with metastases treated using TACE plus lenvatinib and PD-1 blockade. Consistently, our nomogram model demonstrated that increasing baseline NLR was associated with decreasing survival benefits from triple therapy. In the spline analysis, the association between NLR and OS showed only mild deviation from linearity. Because log-transformation did not materially improve model performance, we modeled NLR in its original scale. By contrast, PLR was not significantly associated with OS in the univariate Cox analysis and was therefore not included in the final model, as adding a non-prognostic variable would not provide clear gains in predictive performance.

Spleen volume has gained increasing attention as a relevant prognostic biomarker in HCC [11]. Additionally, correlations between immune therapy response and baseline spleen volume as well as post-treatment spleen volume increase have been reported in various cancers, including HCC [35–37]. In our cohort of HCC patients receiving triple therapy, only spleen volume at 6 weeks post-treatment was significantly correlated with survival outcomes, while post-treatment increases in spleen volume were not associated with survival. This aligns with prior findings where absolute spleen volume at first follow-up was predictive of prognosis, while increases or decreases alone did not show consistent survival patterns [38]. This discrepancy may be related to measurement errors in spleen volume based on imaging assessments. Grouping patients solely based on spleen volume increase may fail to accurately reflect true physiological changes, thus affecting the accuracy of the analysis. Mo et al. [7] categorized patients by whether their spleen volume increased >20.05 cm³ at 6 weeks post-treatment, finding this increase to independently predict OS in those receiving combined anti-PD-1 and anti-angiogenic therapy. In our cohort, baseline spleen volume and spleen volume at 6 weeks post-treatment were strongly correlated (Pearson r = 0.93), suggesting that splenomegaly was largely a stable feature among these patients (Figure S6). Nevertheless, only spleen volume at 6 weeks post-treatment retained independent prognostic significance in the multivariate Cox model, indicating that spleen volume measured after initial treatment may more accurately reflect treatment-related alterations in the immune microenvironment. Increasing spleen volume may indirectly reflect the accumulation of MDSCs in the spleen during treatment [38]. Patients with larger spleen volumes at 6 weeks post-treatment may have more MDSCs accumulating in the spleen. MDSCs are immature myeloid cells with immune-suppressive and pro-angiogenic capabilities, promoting tumor progression [39]. The relatively high mean spleen volume observed in this study is consistent with the clinical background of the cohort, in which most patients had HBV-related cirrhosis, which may contribute to splenomegaly [40]. On the other hand, the tumor itself may contribute to splenomegaly [41, 42]. Prior reports have similarly documented enlarged spleen volumes among HCC patients with cirrhosis, supporting the representativeness of our findings [7, 43].

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective, single-center nature and modest sample size may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the HLNS score was derived in an HBV-dominant Chinese cohort in which chronic HBV infection and HBV-related cirrhosis were the predominant etiologies. Baseline characteristics such as the prevalence of HBV infection, tumor size, and the proportion of multifocal disease were consistent with those reported in previous studies, supporting the representativeness and generalizability of our cohort within the Chinese setting [44–46]. However, in many Western regions, HCV infection, alcohol-related liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis are the main causes of HCC. These etiologic differences are associated with distinct patterns of liver fibrosis, systemic inflammation, and immune microenvironment, which may influence the prognostic impact of HBV status, NLR, tumor burden, and spleen volume. As a result, the current coefficients and cutoff of the HLNS score may not be directly transportable to HCV-related or alcohol-related HCC populations without recalibration. Until external validation is available, the HLNS score should therefore be applied with caution in non-HBV–predominant settings and used as a complementary decision-support tool rather than a standalone determinant of treatment choices. Third, the study exclusively involved treatment-naive patients, while real-world clinical settings often include individuals who may have received prior treatments before starting triple therapy. Accordingly, future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts and more diverse patient populations, including those with different etiological backgrounds and prior treatments, are warranted to confirm the robustness and clinical utility of the HLNS score. Fourth, the present study specifically focused on patients with uHCC who were deemed suitable for triple therapy by MDT evaluation. Therefore, our findings should not be extrapolated to all single-lesion or BCLC stage A patients, particularly those who remain eligible for potentially curative options such as resection or transplantation. Finally, spleen volume in this study was assessed using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI cross-sectional measurements rather than three-dimensional reconstruction. Although this approach has been validated and widely used in prior studies, potential measurement errors and reader-dependent variability cannot be entirely excluded [7, 35, 37]. Nevertheless, the formula relies on simple linear measurements that can be obtained on routine CT or MRI workstations, which makes integration of the HLNS score into everyday clinical workflows feasible, provided that measurement protocols are standardized and readers receive appropriate training. Future prospective studies incorporating semi-automated segmentation or volumetric reconstruction techniques may further reduce measurement variability and strengthen the robustness and scalability of the HLNS score [47].

To summarize, we have developed the HLNS score, a new prognostic tool designed to predict outcomes in uHCC patients receiving triple therapy involving transarterial therapy, TKIs, and anti-PD-1 antibodies. The model, based on four easily accessible clinical factors, provides reliable risk stratification and survival predictions. It offers valuable support for clinicians in tailoring treatment plans to individual patients, improving decision-making in real-world clinical practice.
