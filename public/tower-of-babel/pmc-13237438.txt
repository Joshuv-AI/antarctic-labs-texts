Successful allogeneic stem cell transplantation for a patient with high titers of donor-specific antibody after multiple desensitization procedures
Authors: Kiyotaka Izumi, Kazuki Yoshioka, Yuto Ueda, Bunta Kakihara, Kensuke Nakao, Tomoya Kitagawa, Kenichi Nagai, Katsuhiro Io
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 66-year-old woman who was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia, myelodysplasia-related underwent allogeneic peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (PBSCT) from her human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-mismatched daughter after primary induction failure. The patient had a high donor-specific antibody (DSA) titer to the mismatched HLA antigen and received multiple desensitization treatments including rituximab, intravenous immunoglobulin, and random platelet concentrate transfusions, which resulted in a slight decrease in the DSA titer. The donor was a poor mobilizer and the patient could have received a limited number of CD34-positive cells in the PBSCT with a relatively high CD34-negative fraction. Although the graft rejection was a great concern, the patient achieved delayed but successful engraftment and complete remission. The extent of the DSA reduction was greatest after transplantation, indicating that a relatively greater number of infused CD34-negative cells had a certain effect on antagonizing the DSAs. The DSAs were detected even after engraftment, suggesting that other factors, such as HLA expression levels and HLA epitopes, might affect the engraftment outcome.

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) from human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-mismatched related donors is an alternative treatment option for patients requiring immediate transplantation or those who do not have suitable donor sources. Previous studies have shown that donor-specific anti-HLA antibodies (DSAs) are a risk factor for graft failure and are associated with poor overall survival1-3, indicating the need for desensitization before allo-SCT. Although several methods for desensitization have been reported4-6, no standard protocol exists, and great variations among institutions exist, partly because of the feasibility of the desensitization methods.

Here, we report our experience of successful engraftment after allo-SCT achieved in a patient with refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML) who had high DSA titers.

Case Presentation

A 66-year-old woman with no remarkable past medical history was admitted to our hospital presenting with fever, dyspnea, and abnormal white blood cells (WBC) in the peripheral blood. Her hemoglobin level was 5.7 g/dL, WBC count was 320 × 109/L, and platelet count was 109 × 109/L. The leukocyte differential count showed 60.5% in the WBCs were blasts. The lactate dehydrogenase level was 244 IU/L, and the uric acid level was 5.6 mg/dL. Bone marrow examination revealed that 67.9% of the nucleated cells were leukemic blasts. Multilineage myelodysplasia was also observed. Flow cytometry showed that the leukemic cells were positive for CD34, CD13, CD33, CD117, CD64, and cytoplasmic MPO, and negative for HLA-DR, CD19, and CD56. FLT3-ITD/TKD, NPM1, and KIT mutations were absent. G-banded karyotyping showed 47,XX,add(5)(q22),del(7)(q22q32),+del(11)(p11.2),add(17)(p11.2)[18] / 48,XX,add(5)(q22),del(7)(q22q32),+del(11)(p11.2),add(17)(p11.2),+mar[2]. The patient was diagnosed with AML, myelodysplasia-related according to the 5th World Health Organization classification. Chest computed tomography revealed consolidations in both lungs, indicating pneumonia. The patient received remission induction chemotherapy with daunorubicin (DNR) and cytarabine arabinoside (Ara-C) concomitantly with broad-spectrum antibiotics for the pneumonia. Although a slight reduction in the blast count was observed, leukemic blasts were persistently observed in the peripheral blood. Bone marrow examination on day 14 of DNR + Ara-C induction revealed that 36.4% of the nucleated cells were residual leukemic cells, showing a refractory course. Recognizing the immediate need for allo-SCT, we searched for related donors. As the patient had no available HLA-identical siblings, an HLA-mismatched SCT from her daughter was scheduled. HLA typing of A, B, C, and DRB1 loci showed one-locus mismatch in the graft-versus-host and the host-versus-graft directions (Table 1). An anti-HLA antibody test revealed a high DSA titer to the mismatched A*24:02 allotype. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was 22183. In addition, she showed broadly positive results for class I and II HLA antigens, precluding the feasibility of cord blood transplantation. Since the patient had few matched unrelated donors in the Japan Marrow Donor Program and no time to wait for donor selection, we decided to proceed with allo-SCT from her daughter. We initiated DSA desensitization and new anti-leukemia chemotherapy.

Treatment was switched to a combination of venetoclax (Ven) and azacitidine (AZA). However, bone marrow aspiration 14-days after the Ven + AZA therapy revealed that 49% of the nucleated cells were leukemic blasts. We subsequently administered a combination of etoposide (100 mg/m2, 5 days) and high-dose Ara-C (1 g/m2, 5 days) to control her leukemia. Although the patient was unable to achieve a morphologic leukemia-free status, her blast count decreased to 8.0%. We did not expect further benefits from the chemotherapy and decided to proceed with allo-SCT. For DSA desensitization, we administered rituximab (375 mg/m2 on days -28, -21, -14, and -7) and intravenous immunoglobulin (20 g on days -6 to -2). Plasma exchange was avoided due to severe thrombocytopenia. We tried to obtain platelet concentrates expressing DSA-targeted HLA (DSA-PC), but we could not prepare a platelet product in-house. Also, we could not obtain HLA-selected platelet concentrate (PC) expressing A*24:02 from the blood bank of the Japanese Red Cross Society. Considering the relatively high allele frequency of A*24:02 in the Japanese population (36.5%, according to Ikeda et al.7), we transfused 90 units of PC from random donors, expecting that approximately one-third of them would be positive for A*24:02. We started to transfuse PCs on day -1 and completed them on the early morning of day 0.

The patient received fludarabine (150 mg/m2) and myeloablative dose of intravenous busulfan (12.8 mg/kg) as a conditioning regimen. The total body irradiation was not performed. Peripheral blood stem cells (PBSCs) were mobilized from the donor using granulocyte colony-stimulating factor in the usual manner. Unexpectedly, because of the low proportions of CD34-positive cells (around 0.1%), we could obtain 2.01 × 106/kg of CD34-positive cells after PBSC harvest performed on four consecutive days. The total number of nucleated cells was 2.01 × 109/kg, all of which were transfused. Prophylaxis for graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) included post-transplant cyclophosphamide (40 mg/kg on days 3 and 4), tacrolimus, and mycophenolate mofetil. Neutrophil and platelet engraftment were delayed but ultimately achieved. A neutrophil count of > 0.5 × 109/L and a platelet count of > 20 × 109/L without platelet transfusion were achieved on days 21 and 42, respectively. No acute GVHD symptoms were observed. A bone marrow examination performed on day 32 showed no residual leukemic cells. Chimerism analysis showed complete donor chimerism. The patient was discharged on day 70. The patient remained in complete remission for more than 1 year after the allo-SCT.

We measured the DSA titers, expressed as MFI, over a number of time points. The titer remained high (MFI, 20212) on the day of transplantation, even though the patient received multiple desensitization treatments. Although graft rejection was a concern, the DSA titer had decreased to 8201 on day 32 after transplantation following neutrophil engraftment. The DSA level continued to decline gradually and reached a clinically insignificant range approximately 6 months after transplantation. Discussion

Here, we present a patient with refractory AML who underwent allo-SCT with a high titer of DSA and finally achieved engraftment. Although the relatively high titer of remaining DSAs after desensitization and the limited availability of CD34-positive cells at transplantation raised great concern about graft failure, the patient finally achieved neutrophil and platelet engraftment. The patient's clinical course indicated insufficient efficacy of the performed desensitization methods. The fact that the extent of the DSA reduction was the largest after transplantation indicated that a relatively greater number of infused CD34-negative cells had a certain effect on antagonizing the DSAs. However, the DSAs were detected even after engraftment, suggesting that other factors affected engraftment outcomes.

Transfusion of DSA-PC8-11 or an irradiated donor buffy coat5 has been reported to be an effective method for DSA absorption. Theoretically, Infusion of donor-derived CD34-negative cells at transplantation has the same effect on DSA reduction as these procedures. However, DSA-PC or irradiated donor buffy coat is not always available, as in the present patient. In such cases, infusion of a large number of donor cells at transplantation may be a feasible alternative option.

A meta-analysis showed that the patients with DSA positivity had a 7.47-fold increased risk of graft failure compared with patients without DSA positivity12. Another study reported that patients with high-titer DSAs (MFI > 20,000) had a significantly lower engraftment rate5. On the other hand, Hagino et al. reported cases in which neutrophil engraftment was achieved after allo-SCT with a high DSA titer (MFI > 5,000)13. They pointed out that the measurement of DSA using flow cytometry with HLA antigen-coated beads could detect antibodies with no clinical significance and that antibodies to one HLA can bind another HLA through shared eplets, which influences the MFI value. In the present case, a steady decrease in DSA titer was observed, indicating some biological significance.

Previous studies have shown that the HLA-A mRNA expression varied among alleles14. The protein expression of some A*24:02 allotypes are reportedly low15. If the expression level of an HLA antigen is low, the avidity of the DSAs may decrease. In such cases, the biological effect of the DSAs may not be significant. In the present case, we could not determine the expression level of A*24:02 on donor cells; one possible explanation is that the donor might have had low expression of the A*24:02 allele, and the DSAs had less impact on engraftment.

In conclusion, we reported a patient with AML who achieved successful engraftment despite a high DSA titer. Although transfusion of a relatively large number of CD34-negative cells partly contributed to the reduction of DSAs, the titer remained high after engraftment. Investigation of the expression levels of HLA antigens and the analysis of HLA epitopes might enable accurate estimation of DSA's impact.
