Early coconut oil exposure suppresses chronic experimental Crohn’s disease via myristic acid and enhanced regulatory T cells
Authors: Alex Rodriguez-Palacios, Abigail Raffner Basson, Paola Menghini, Jessica Ezeji, Ashley Trotter, Nitish Rana, Wendy Goodman, Ganapati H. Mahabaleshwar, Wei Xin, Theresa T. Pizarro, Colleen M. Croniger, Alexander Miron, Yehe Liu, Michael W. Jenkins, Mark S. Sundrud, Ilya R. Bederman, Fabio Cominelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Public health advisory guidelines caution against the consumption of saturated fats, especially coconut oil (CO), yet CO is widely consumed in regions with low inflammatory bowel disease prevalence (IBD) and is often perceived as beneficial by IBD patients. CO is rich in medium-chain saturated fatty acids, notably lauric acid (C12:0) and myristic acid (C14:0) which are negligible in mammalian tissues without dietary intake. Because IBD is linked to saturated fats consumption, here we tested and unexpectedly found that CO and its saturated fatty acids are beneficial suppressing intestinal inflammation in SAMP1/YitFc mice, a chronic IBD model of Crohn’s disease-like ileitis. In young mice, the early exposure to a high-dose CO-based diet prevented ileitis in 63.64% of animals (95%CI = 30.79-to-89.07%). Importantly, comparable protection was observed at lower doses amenable for human translation. Although CO did not reverse established disease in adult mice, when combined with steroids, it prolonged the period of steroid-induced remission illustrating its therapeutic benefits in IBD. Disease prevention was associated with myristic acid and correlated with anti-inflammatory microbiota shifts and enhanced regulatory T-cell responses, without adverse effects. Findings challenge current dietary advisories against CO and support reevaluation of CO and myristic acid as anti-inflammatory dietary adjuvants for the prevention and management of established IBD.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic condition characterized by inflammation of the digestive tract, with Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) being the two main subtypes. CD is distinct in its presentation, featuring transmural inflammation and multifocal cobblestone-like structures, predominantly in the ileum, which contribute to disease severity and treatment challenges1,2. While the precise cause of CD remains unknown, it is believed to arise from genetic susceptibility to dysregulated immune responses to gut microbiota, environment, and dietary factors.

Dietary fat composition plays a critical role in the regulation of intestinal inflammation3. Long-chain saturated fatty acids (LC-SFA), found abundantly in animal lard, are known for their association with worsened IBD symptoms3,4, whereas omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) have shown protective effects5,6. In contrast, the impact of medium-chain fatty acid (MCFA) saturated fats from plant origin, such as coconut oil (CO, Cocos nucifera L.), remains unclear in IBD.

Despite advisory statements recommending against CO in the human diet due to its high saturated fat (SF) content7–10, the consumption of CO is widely favored in numerous societies and it is favored by IBD patients in our community for the traditionally perceived benefits of CO in overall health11. Supporting its widespread acceptability12, the United States is a major consumer of CO, with 30% of US households reporting using CO as a food ingredient13. With a growing consumption projection (CGR 5.82% to 2033), it is critical to reassess the potential positive effects of CO and its safety margin in IBD. Although significant changes have been observed for high-density lipoprotein (HDL-) cholesterol (‘good cholesterol’), metanalyses of clinical trials on CO and cardiovascular risk factors indicate that CO has no negative effects in humans; specifically, glycemia, adiposity, systemic inflammation, or in low-density lipoprotein (LDL-) cholesterol (‘bad cholesterol’) or triglycerides14–17, regardless of what oils are used as controls16.

Coconut oil is rich in MCFA18, particularly lauric acid (LA) and myristic acid (MA), which differ from LC-SFA in terms of host metabolism and immunoregulatory effects. Despite concerns about the high saturated fat content in CO, emerging evidence suggests that MC-SFA has anti-inflammatory effects. Notably, exclusive liquid enteral nutrition (EEN) formulations, rich in MCFA, have been shown to reduce gut inflammation in pediatric CD5,6,18. Despite the benefits, unfortunately, EEN remains impractical for adult CD patients due to taste and compliance. Regarding the potential benefits of CO in adult CD, a recent case report illustrates that CO was effective at helping control the disease in a surgical patient with fistulizing CD complicated by diversion colitis19. Since most CD patients (70–80%) develop chronic inflammation manifested as multifocal cobblestone inflammatory lesions in the ileum mucosa (ileitis)20, we aimed at determining the impact of CO in a mouse model of CD-like cobblestone ileitis. Herein, we used SAMP1/YitFc mice as a model because it develops cobblestone lesions spontaneously in 100% of mice by 6 weeks of age, which progress over time to damage up to 80% of the ileal mucosa by 24–30 weeks of age due to cobblestone lesion expansion21. Here, we investigated the effect of CO in preventing and treating such a predictable pattern of CD-like ileitis in young and adult mice, fed under various circumstances.

Herein, we show that CO at a low dose, and MA, but not LA or palmitic acid (C18:0, predominant in lard), were associated with disease prevention in SAMP mice as a chronic multifocal model of CD. Coconut oil promoted an anti-inflammatory microbiota, increased T regulatory (Treg) cells in lymphoid organs, and enhanced Treg cell-mediated suppression of effector T cell responses. Notably, no adverse effects were observed in mice, and beneficially, the consumption of a low-dose CO diet promoted a carbohydrate-based energy metabolism instead of a fat-based response, indicating that CO at low doses could be used for the prevention and therapy of CD. Our data indicate that replacing lard as an ingredient in the ‘American Diet’ with a low dose of CO, or supplementing our diet with MA, might be beneficial in CD.

Results

Overview of study design controlling for confounders

In SAMP mice, cobblestone lesions expand within the ileum in a pattern resembling forest-fire dispersion, whereby small inflammatory foci enlarge eccentrically over time. Using this established model of spontaneous ileitis, we first tested the effects of a purified high-fat coconut oil (HF-CO) research diet (36% w/w; 58% kcal from fat; Research Diets, Inc., D12331) by comparing it with the standard normal diet (ND) used in our facility (7% w/w fat; 14.9% kcal from fat). The ND, a grain-based chow formulated with pork lard and soybean oil as fat sources (RMH-P3000, LabDiet), contains substantially higher and more complex fiber content than purified research diets, as reported in the literature22,23.

A comparative analysis of the FA composition of the diets further highlights these dietary differences. The CO research diets are enriched in medium-chain fatty acids (MCFAs), whereas the lard/soybean oil fat sources used in the grain-based chow are predominantly composed of long-chain fatty acids (LCFA). As noted in Fig. 1a, b, the FA profiles for these diets are substantially different, in terms of saturation, and length and relative abundance. Because differences in fiber amount and composition (not only fat content or saturation) are recognized sources of confounding effects on metabolism, gut morphology, and microbiome composition when comparing chow and purified high-fat diets22,23, in independent mouse experiments, we then evaluated the effects of CO relative to other fat sources within purified research diet frameworks. Specifically, we compared HF-CO and low-fat CO (LF-CO) diets with matched purified cocoa butter-based control diets at high and low doses (HF-CB and LF-CB) manufactured concurrently by the same vendor (Research Diets, Inc.), thereby minimizing variability unrelated to fat source.

Finally, to further disentangle fat-specific effects from those attributable to diet formulation and fiber content, we tested candidate anti-inflammatory FA derived from CO using purified food-grade supplements added and mixed directly to the grain-based ND. This complementary approach allowed us to assess whether the observed effects on ileitis due to CO, via FA mechanisms, were independent of the fiber content and overall composition of the chow diet.

To reduce microbiome variability, all experiments used an inter-subject pre-experimental fecal microbiota homogenization (IsPreFeh) protocol before diet administration24, in which pooled feces from 4-week-old mice were gavaged to all animals to normalize the gut microbiota across cages and mice. Mice were also housed individually to avoid cage-cluster effects, thus increasing statistical power (n = 10–12 per diet) in the experiments. Two weeks later, mice were randomly assigned to diets (Fig. 1c).

Early high-dose coconut oil suppresses development of chronic ileitis

The main objective of our diet study25 was to compare our regular rodent chow diet (ND) to a highly caloric diet containing a high dose of CO (HC-CO) to determine if the overall ecological multi-ingredient effect of two different diets could influence (worsen or prevent) the development of multifocal cobblestone ileitis in SAMP mice (i.e., prevention study) (Figs. 1c and 2a). To determine the effect of long-term HF-CO administration, we offered the diet to cohorts of young mice (6-week-old) for 24 weeks, which is an age that represents ~80% of the SAMP lifespan in our mouse research facility. To further assess the interaction between HF-CO diet and host genetics, we compared the diet effects in young AKR/J mice, which do not suffer IBD or SAMP ileitis, and which are the putative parental inbred control line to SAMP26 mice.

After 24 weeks in the feeding trial, we found that SAMP and AKR mice consumed comparable amounts of HF-CO diet (Fig. 2b). Compared to the ND diet, both SAMP and AKR mice on HF-CO had higher body weights. Of nutritional interest, by the end of the study, the body weight of SAMP mice on HF-CO was similar to the body weight of AKR mice consuming the ND diet (Fig. 2c, d). This suggests that in the context of CD, where mice may experience malnutrition or reduced body weight gain as animals age27, a HF-CO diet may help restore normal weight. Since an HF-CO diet could modulate glucose homeostasis28,29, we conducted five glucose tolerance tests throughout the study. In terms of intestinal inflammation, early lesions of cobblestone-like ileitis develop in 100% of SAMP mice by 6 weeks of age, which steadily and predictably worsen over time21. Remarkably, the HF-CO diet prevented the onset of CD-like ileitis in most SAMP mice (63.64%, 7/11; Fisher exact p < 0.004, Binomial exact 95% CI = 30.79–89.07%) when examined at 30 weeks of age and compared to the ND-fed mice.

Equally important, we found that the HF-CO diet did not induce intestinal histological or biochemical (myeloperoxidase; MPO activity26,30) inflammation in AKR/J mice of similar age. Of the SAMP mice that exhibited a small number of cobblestone lesions, stereomicroscopy (3D-SMAPgut analysis21) showed that these lesions were smaller compared to those of SAMP mice fed the ND diet (8.7 ± 3.0%, n = 4; vs. 40.1 ± 5.2%, n = 12; p < 0.05; overall 78.3% reduction of CD-ileitis affected mucosa). Histologically, SAMP on HF-CO had total inflammatory scores that were 7.9 times lower compared to ND (0.8 ± 0.1 vs. 6.3 ± 0.3, n = 11; p < 0.001; Fig. 2f–h). Supporting that the findings were reproducible, a correlation analysis between histological and stereomicroscopic scores showed a high level of technical and data reproducibility and agreement (slope 4.17, R2 = 0.8817)21. Since such a level of prevention in SAMP ileitis had not been seen in SAMP mice, we next performed a microsatellite analysis across 98 PCR markers to verify that SAMP mice were indeed SAMP mice. Analysis confirmed that the SAMP mice had the SAMP genotype as described31 (see satellites map in ref. 32 and overview of chromosomal distribution of loci known to be unique to SAMP in Supplementary Fig 1).

To further assess the effect of diet on ileal epithelial integrity, cobblestone and non-affected tissues were dissected from the ileum of mice with SM at the end of the study and used ex-vivo to measure transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) at the time of dissection and after 1 h of incubation in cell culture medium21. As illustrated in Fig. 2i, the HF-CO diet shifted the TEER phenotype of cobblestone lesions toward that of healthy ileum in AKR mice and away from the high-TEER phenotype observed in cobblestones from SAMP mice fed the ND diet, documenting a protective role in CD-like ileitis.

We then tested whether a diet shifted MLN immune responses toward a less inflammatory phenotype via CD3/CD28-stimulation (to promote T cell proliferation and cytokine production) of freshly collected cells from the mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN). We found that stimulated cells from HF-CO mice produced ~10.36% less TNF-α after 72 h of incubation compared to cells from mice fed the ND diet. This reduction was observed in both SAMP and AKR mice (9.28% and 11.44%, respectively), indicating that CO has anti-inflammatory effects in MLN lymphocytes independent of mouse line (sign test P < 0.05; mean ± SD for SAMP in HF-CO 12.04 ± 1.95 and ND 13.27 ± 1.42, and for AKR in HF-CO 14.89 ± 0.89 and ND 16.82 ± 1.06). Although similar findings were observed for IL-10, the significance of reduced IL-10 in the pathogenesis of SAMP ileitis remains unclear. No additional cytokines were measured to further define the impact of the CO diet on MLN cytokine kinetics.

Mass spectrometry and lipidomic analyses determined that the fecal excretion profiles of FA in both diets were comparable for AKR and SAMP mice, indicating that the anti-inflammatory effect of the HF-CO diet in SAMP is not due to a different absorption pattern of CO fatty acids in SAMP (Supplementary Fig. 2). To determine whether the dose of CO or other types of plant fats would have comparable effects in SAMP mice, we next conducted experiments with diets that were designed with comparable caloric content (4.07–5.56 kcal/g, see Supplementary Table 2), but using two different fat ingredients of distinct plant origins, i.e., CO and cocoa butter (CB).

Crohn’s-like chronic ileitis is also prevented by low-dose coconut oil

To assess whether CO retains its anti-inflammatory effects at a lower concentration (7%, kcal 14.9%) comparable to the ND diet, we repeated our prevention model study in 6-week-old SAMP mice. To assess the effect of CO as a dietary ingredient, isocaloric diets were designed to vary only in the type and concentration of fat, using cocoa butter (CB) as a control plant-based fat ingredient. We used CB to evaluate whether another plant-based fat could replicate the effects of CO, which is also plant based. The CB has high palmitic acid content similar to lard/soybean oil and lacks medium-chain fatty acids, which are abundant in CO. Groups of SAMP mice were divided into four distinct dietary treatments (n = 6–8 mice/group) and fed one of the following diets: high-fat CO (HF-CO), low-fat CO (LF-CO), high-fat CB (HF-CB), or low-fat CB (LF-CB), starting at 6 weeks of age and continuing for 24 weeks.

Histology, 3D-SMAPgut analysis, and fecal MPO measurements confirmed that the HF-CO diet significantly reduced the severity of SAMP ileitis compared to the HF-CB diet (MW P < 0.05). Notably, the LF-CO diet showed comparable benefits to HF-CO (Fig. 2j), demonstrating that the CO protective mechanism does not require a high dose to prevent the development of CD-like ileitis in mice.

To complement histological scoring, ileal MPO was quantified in ileal tissues as an objective biochemical marker of mucosal inflammation in SAMP ileitis as we previously demonstrated when we validated the study power of our 3D-stereomicroscopic scoring system to assess SAMP ileitis21. Pairwise analyses confirmed that HF-CB had significantly higher inflammation scores compared to both CO diets (high and low content, P < 0.05), supporting that the anti-inflammatory effect of CO occurs regardless of the dose in the diet (Fig. 2k). Based on these findings, subsequent experiments were focused on CO, given that its anti-inflammatory effect occurred at high and low doses (HF-CB was pro-inflammatory).

Coconut oil prolongs steroid remission in advanced chronic ileitis

Having established the preventative effects of the CO diet on ileitis development in young SAMP mice, we next tested if the same HF-CO diet could therapeutically reduce established ileitis in 30-week-old adult SAMP mice26. Histology and stereomicroscopy showed that HF-CO alone had no significant effect on ileitis. This suggests that the CO-induced anti-inflammatory mechanisms, which prevent ileitis development, are either insufficient to reverse established disease or not similarly activated with aging.

Steroids are widely used as frontline immunosuppressant therapy to manage CD activity or flares33,34. However, a major challenge in CD therapy is the inability of medications to maintain steroid-induced CD remission after steroid withdrawal. Thus, we next asked if CO might augment or maintain the immunosuppressive effects of steroids in adult SAMP mice with advanced ileitis following a short therapeutic course of steroids (Fig. 3a). For this, we developed a 4-week ‘remission and recurrence’ model to simulate a case scenario where patients with CD flares receive steroids to induce remission. In this model, adult SAMP mice on a lard-based ND diet received short-acting dexamethasone (DEX, 1 mg/kg/day, intraperitoneally, for 5 days) to induce remission, while control SAMP mice received no treatment. Compared to the no-treatment group, DEX effectively reduced histological ileitis at 7 days, confirming its ability to induce remission21,35,36. However, ileitis severity returned by day 14 (DEXw2) and remained unchanged by day 28 (DEXw4), demonstrating rapid recurrence after discontinuation of steroids (Fig. 3b) as is common in human CD patients37–40. Using this ‘remission and recurrence’ SAMP ileitis model, we found that concurrent use of our HF-CO diet for 6 weeks (Fig. 3c) extended the DEX-induced remission period to 35 days, measured by body weight, MPO (feces, spleen, ileum), and ileal histology scores (Fig. 3c–e). This suggests that combining steroids with CO produces a synergistic sustained extension of remission beyond the effects of steroids alone.

Although we observed a correlation between higher initial body weight and increased ileitis severity (slope = 0.55, R² = 0.62, p = 0.07), as previously reported in humans where a higher body mass index is associated with worse CD6,41,42, multivariable regression confirmed that the beneficial effect of HF-CO on ileitis was independent of the body weight in mice at the start of the study (p = 0.03) indicating that CO is beneficial to all mice (Fig. 3f), lean or overweight, regardless of BMI.

To examine how the combination of HF-CO and DEX influences gut microbiota, we performed 16S rRNA fecal microbiome sequencing. This analysis revealed that the dietary effects of ND and HF-CO were independently derived from the effect of DEX, consistent with prior findings36. Figure 3g demonstrates how animals consuming the HF-CO clustered together after DEX and HF-CO administration. As expected, these data also confirmed that our fecal homogenization protocol (IsPreFeH) was effective at controlling cage-to-cage variability, providing homogeneous gut microbiota in mice prior to testing the diets21,36.

Coconut oil induces durable anti-inflammatory gut microbiota

To determine whether the effects of the HF-CO diet were due to stable, anti-inflammatory changes in the gut microbiota that could be transmissible to germ-free (GF) mice, we conducted fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments into GF-SAMP mice. Feces from SPF-SAMP mice fed the HF-CO diet for 24 weeks were pooled and orally administered to young GF-SAMP mice, maintained on the ND diet. Studies assessing the effect of diet on MPO as a marker of intestinal inflammation, and those assessing how diet rapidly and reproducibly alters the human gut microbiome, indicate that a single day is sufficient to identify changes in fecal markers in gnotobiotic mice transplanted with gut microbiota. Therefore, 3 days post-FMT, mice fed the ND were euthanized, and intestinal tissues (ileal and colonic) were analyzed for acute histological inflammation and MPO activity.

Mice colonized with mouse fecal microbiota derived from donors fed the HF-CO diet for 24 weeks exhibited significantly lower colonic MPO concentrations compared with mice receiving microbiota from ND-fed donors (M-W p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 3). As expected for germ-free mice at this age, no histologic or stereomicroscopic evidence of colitis or ileitis was observed. MPO activity in ileal tissues did not differ significantly between groups. However, the reduction in colonic MPO activity detected within three days following microbiota transplantation indicates that the protective effects of the HF-CO diet may be mediated by stable, anti-inflammatory modifications of gut microbiota induced by CO, which retained functional activity after transfer into recipient GF mice fed an ND diet.

Given the uniqueness of the microbial patterns observed in SAMP mice, we next performed DNA-based microbiome analysis using fecal and cecal samples in a separate experiment involving young SAMP mice. This study evaluated whether the prevention of ileitis depended on dietary CO dose, comparing ND, CB, and CO-supplemented diets administered for 24 weeks. During initial experiments in SAMP and AKR mice, we determined that animals fed high-fat diets frequently had insufficient distal ileal contents for reliable sampling of intestinal ingesta and for extraction of consistently high-quality microbial DNA. Cecal samples were therefore selected for sequencing analyses, as they yielded reproducible DNA quantities and quality. With this biological limitation, fecal samples were used as a proxy for colonic microbiota, whereas cecal samples were used as a proxy for communities of the distal small intestine.

Microbiome 16S DNA analysis showed that both CO and CB significantly altered the gut microbiome composition in mice. Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) analysis revealed that most unique, diet-specific OTUs were in the ND (n > 215), while substantially fewer OTUs were detected in HF diets, with comparably slight differences observed for feces (n < 40) and ceca (n < 78; Fig. 4c). Overall, there was a noted reduction in OTUs, indicating that both HF-CO and CB diets reduce microbial diversity compared to the control ND diet. Remarkably, the effects of the LF diet paralleled the effects of the HF diets, although, as illustrated in multivariable (PCA) analysis, the effects due to CO were opposite for some species compared to the effects of CB (Fig. 4d and e). The effect of CO was more significant on the cecum microbiome than on the fecal microbiome. At the species level, CO caused a reduction in Bacillales, Verrucomicrobiales, Caulobacteriales, Enterobacteriales, and Pseudomonadales, compared to a profile rich in Stramenopiles and Lactobaccilales for ND (Fig. 4f–i).

Coconut oil prevents dysbiotic phylum expansion in humanized mice

To date, no causal relationship between the human gut microbiota and CD has been definitively established. However, CD has been associated with expansion of Bacteroidota (formerly Bacteroidetes) and Proteobacteria (Pseudomonadota) accompanied by a relative reduction in Firmicutes, resulting in a decreased Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidota (F:B) and Firmicutes-to-Proteobacteria (F:P) ratios43. To assess the impact of CO on this microbial balance, germ-free SAMP (GF-SAMP) mice were transplanted with fecal microbiota from a previously characterized healthy human donor44 and subsequently fed the HF-CO or ND control diet. As a safety test and to verify that HFCO did not promote Bacteroidota expansion, this human fecal donor was selected because it has consistently had a relatively low abundance of Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria compared to Firmicutes. Quantitative PCR45 confirmed stability of the transplanted human microbiota for these phyla for 14 days post-transplant. After confirmation, 16S rRNA sequencing performed on the inoculum and the transplanted mice demonstrated that HF-CO feeding for 21 days did not promote Bacteroidota or Proteobacteria overgrowth in transplanted mice (Fig. 4j), despite the predisposition of SAMP mice to favor Bacteroidota overgrowth in SPF conditions21,44–46. Analysis of the F:B and F:P normalized ratios demonstrated that at the phylum level, the HF-CO diet was safe but did not have a major phylum differential effect compared to the ND diet and SPF mice (post hoc ANOVA pairwise p > 0.3), which indicated that the beneficial effects of HF-CO in gut inflammation in humans could be due to changes in a more define set of species reorganization within these three phyla in the human gut microbiota or due to changes in other phyla.

To integrate both compositional impact and directional change, we evaluated HF-CO-induced microbial shifts using fold-change thresholds (≥2 or ≤0.5) while weighting taxa by their mean relative abundance, interpreting in parallel the changes on the humanized diet due to HF-CO and ND diet, using SPF mice ND data as the mouse microbiota referent. Analysis revealed that HF-CO drives a dominant expansion of Actinobacteria, almost entirely attributable to Bifidobacteriales, with high fold-change enrichment (7-11X vs. ND; 5-20X vs. SPF) that exceeded levels in the human donor. Bifidobacterium animals and B. pseudolongum were top-ranked species in the analysis and phylum (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). In contrast, Bacteroidota exhibited coordinated suppression, driven by depletion of high-abundance Prevotellaceae/Bacteroidales OTUs (≤0.25X vs. ND and ≤0.05X vs. SPF). Firmicutes underwent internal restructuring within the phylum rather than uniform expansion, with enrichment of Bacilli and select Clostridiaceae (specifically, C. perfringens) counterbalanced by inhibition of abundant Ruminococcaceae, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. Notably, taxa commonly presumed to be pathogenic, including Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota, were not enriched by HF-CO and instead remained low. Together, this indicates that CO does not promote expansion of microbial groups classically associated with CD pathogenesis but instead modifies the community through selective enrichment of Actinobacteria and inhibition of expected Bacteroidota overgrowth.

Because SAMP ileitis is associated with early Treg dysfunction, we hypothesized that CO prevents ileitis by improving impaired Treg responses in SAMP mice. High-fat lard diets exacerbate colitis in DSS-treated C57BL/6 mice and in Treg adoptive transfer models, demonstrating that dietary fat composition influences T-cell-mediated intestinal inflammation49. After iterative testing of fatty acid (FA)-supplemented normal diet (ND) formulations to ensure palatability and consistent intake, four diets were prepared with defined individual supplementation of LA, MA, or PA, or a mixture (FA-MIX). Supplement concentrations were calibrated to achieve an average daily fatty acid intake of 793 mg LA, 300 mg MA, or 15 mg PA per mouse, assuming a consistent food consumption of 5 g per mouse per day (hereafter referred to as ND + LA, ND + MA, and ND + PA diets). These supplemented doses correspond to <50% of the respective fatty acid amounts ingested by mice fed HF-CO in the initial studies.

Assessing the effect of each FA, a prevention study in young mice after 12-weeks of dietary supplementation of the ND diet with the three FAs (half the length of the first 24-week-long experiments) revealed that adding MA for a shorter period was sufficient to prevent cobblestone ileitis in SAMP mice (ND + MA vs. ND; 0.2 ± 0.44 vs. 5.5 ± 2.6; M-W p = 0.015). In context, such a reduction in SAMP ileitis yielded histological ileal inflammation scores that were comparable to those of AKR mice in the same ND + MA diet (M-W p = 0.28). In contrast, offering ND + LA or ND + PA to SAMP mice had no effect on the development of ileitis compared to mice consuming the non-supplemented ND diets (3.8 ± 1.4 vs. 5.5 ± 2.6, M-W p = 0.30 for MA, and 3.4 ± 3.5, vs.5.5 ± 2.4, M-W p = 0.44 for PA; Fig. 6b–e). Biochemically, SAMP mice fed the ND + MA had a marginally significant reduction in ileal MPO inflammatory activity compared to ND (Log2 5.0 ± 2.1 vs. 8.1 ± 1.2 log2; M-W p = 0.071, with 1000 Montecarlo simulations, M-W p < 0.05, Fig. 6f).

From a safety perspective, the supplementation of these diets with any FAs, or their mixture (FA-MIX, for 12 weeks did not induce histological inflammation or increased activity of MPO in the ileal tissues in healthy ARK mice, indicating the safety of these FAs in mice at the doses administered. Perhaps due to palatability, the supplementation of FAs resulted in lower body weight and adipose tissue compared to ND, but the effect was comparable across all FAs (MA, LA, and PA, Fig. 6g and h).

Assessing the effect of these FAs in adult SAMP ileitis, the same experiment with 24-week-old mice showed that the supplementation did not reduce the severity of already established cobblestone ileitis (Fig. 6i). In adult SAMP mice with established disease, therapeutic FA supplementation with MA did not reduce the severity of advanced cobblestone ileitis using histology and stereomicroscopy. In this cohort, some animals were euthanized before the planned study endpoint due to a lack of response by 48 weeks of age, which represents an advanced age in SAMP mice. These findings reflect the similar findings observed when we added the wholesome CO to the ND diet in SAMP mice. The supplementation was beneficial to young mice, but not adult mice. Together, studies with young and adult mice indicate that MA is one of the primary anti-inflammatory mediators in CO that help prevent the cobblestone ileitis in susceptible mice.

To further characterize the local immune environment of established cobblestone lesions, we used confocal imaging of adult FoxP3-GFP SAMP mice to compare the distribution of Treg cells in the ileum within cobblestone lesions and their adjacent non-affected ileal tissue. Imaging demonstrated markedly reduced Treg densities within cobblestone lesions relative to neighboring villi (Fig. 7b and c). The mechanisms responsible for the reduced accumulation of Treg cells within cobblestone lesions remain unclear.

After 24 weeks of feeding (~80% of the SAMP lifespan), plasma lipid profiling (mass spectrometry) revealed diet-dependent but not disease-specific effects. CO feeding modestly increased cholesterol and phospholipids across mouse lines without selective exacerbation in SAMP mice (Fig. 8b). Notably, AKR mice on HF-CO maintained plasma cholesterol within normal laboratory ranges (105.4 mg/dl; range 44–129 mg/dl), while SAMP mice showed a non-significant upward trend (188.2 vs. 108.2 mg/dl; K–W p = 0.05). Compared with HF-CB, SAMP mice fed HF-CO had 34.3% lower cholesterol, suggesting a comparatively safer lipid profile for CO (CO 179.6 vs. CB 273.3 mg/dl, MW p = 0.1). Although triglycerides were higher and more variable with HF-CO, these changes were not associated with hepatic or vascular pathology (CO 758.7 ± 177.7 vs. CB 312.7 ± 52.9 mg/dl; MW p = 0.1). A multivariable PCA analysis of all plasma lipids showed clear separation by mouse line under the ND diet and the HF-CO diets, while LF-CO feeding induced a modest shift toward ND-like profiles without overriding mouse line-specific metabolic signatures (Fig. 8c). Notably, LF-CO-fed SAMP mice clustered closer to AKR mice, emphasizing the safety nature of a LF-CO dose approach.

Finally, to assess whole-body energy metabolism dynamics as a mechanism of adaptation to long-term diets, we performed 24-h indirect calorimetry and energy expenditure study of mice at the end of the feeding period. Food intake was reduced in high-fat groups regardless of fat source, and VO₂, VCO₂, and energy expenditure were similar between CO and CB at matched fat concentrations (Fig. 8d and e). Interestingly, the LF-CO and CB diets produced a higher respiratory quotient, consistent with preferential carbohydrate utilization, whereas high-fat versions of both diets indicate mice had a metabolic profile of fat-based energy utilization, indicating that LF-CO diets are metabolically preferred. Collectively, data also indicate that the long-term CO does not induce adverse metabolic stress, even at high doses, and support safe energy utilization in mice prone to ileitis.

To further assess metabolic safety, we also evaluated the effects of supplementation with individual fatty acids (myristic, lauric, and palmitic acids) on parameters assessing overall organ adaptation and plasma lipid metabolism using mass spectrometry (liver and spleen weights, plasma cholesterol, triglycerides, lipoproteins, phospholipids, and non-esterified fatty acids). Collectively, plasma metabolite analysis indicates that long-term supplementation with MA does not promote metabolic alterations in SAMP mice that are beyond what is observed in control mouse lines not affected by IBD (AKR or B6) consuming PA or LA supplemented diets (see statistical narrative details in Supplementary Materials).

Discussion

Crohn’s disease remains incurable, and durable remission is difficult to achieve due to high rates of treatment failure, relapse, and medication-associated toxicity. Here, we identify a diet-modifiable pathway that robustly suppresses chronic ileitis in a genetically susceptible mouse model of Crohn’s disease. Specifically, replacement of dietary lard with CO, or supplementation with its principal medium-chain fatty acid, myristic acid, prevented the development of cobblestone ileitis and prolonged steroid-induced remission in SAMP mice. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized anti-inflammatory role for MA in chronic intestinal inflammation and motivate its evaluation as an adjunctive strategy in Crohn’s disease.

Our studies demonstrate that CO-rich diets consistently prevent the onset of cobblestone ileitis in SAMP mice, a model uniquely suited to studying 3-dimensional inflammatory lesional patterns (cobblestones) akin to human CD21,54. Notably, CO prevented disease progression in most mice, regardless of dietary fat concentration, and significantly prolonged steroid-induced remission. These findings are unprecedented, as no prior intervention has fully prevented SAMP ileitis in our laboratory despite nearly three decades of experimentation. Myristic acid, the primary MCFA in CO, was identified herein as the key component associated with preventing ileitis, even at lower doses than those found in the initial HF-CO diet. This finding is of major clinical value because it indicates that humans may benefit from CO at low doses, which will ensure that CO consumption equivalencies stay below a SFA desirable range below 10% of total caloric intake55. For context, the beneficial effect observed with our low-CO diet was with a diet using CO at a dose that contributed to 7% of the total caloric intake in mice.

Steroids remain the primary treatment for inducing remission in CD, yet their limitations, non-responsiveness (20–37%), relapse within a year (29%), steroid dependency (36%), and significant side effects33,56–59, highlight the potential benefits of using CO as a low-risk strategy to complement modern medicines. Our findings indicate that incorporating CO into dietary regimens could help address these challenges by extending remission durations and potentially reducing steroid dependency in practice.

The beneficial effects of CO in SAMP mice as a model of CD were linked to multiple mechanisms, including immune modulation, microbiome alterations, and systemic metabolic safety. In our study, CO-fed to SAMP mice exhibited increased Tregs in mesenteric lymph nodes, reduced dysbiosis, and a shift towards a Firmicutes-dominant anti-inflammatory microbiota (reduced F:B and F:P ratios), including expansion of beneficial genera such as Bifidobacteria/Lactobacilli40–42,60,61, which was also reproduced in mice transplanted with human gut microbiota. An additional observation supporting an immune-mediated mechanism was that responses to dietary fatty acids differed between young mice and adult mice with established ileitis. Adult mice supplemented with FAs exhibited concurrent expansion of Teff cells, which coincided with the markedly reduced Treg densities observed within cobblestone lesions of adult mice fed the ND diet. Because the protective effects of CO and MA in young mice were associated with enhanced Treg responses, the paucity of Treg cells within established lesions may limit the capacity of these dietary interventions to modulate local inflammation. These findings suggest that the efficacy of CO may depend not only on its immunomodulatory properties but also on the presence of regulatory cell populations within affected tissues. The mechanisms underlying such findings remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Reduced microbial diversity has been associated with CD remission and mucosal healing in children and adults treated with liquid-exclusive enteral nutrition formulations, which are rich in MCFA62–67. As noted, CO is rich in MCFAs. Therefore, findings align with studies showing that exclusive enteral nutrition, rich in MCFA, reduces inflammation in IBD in humans. Recent preliminary studies in dogs with severe clinical IBD (inflammatory protein-losing enteropathy) showed improvement of clinical signs when consuming a liquid diet rich in MCFA68. Our study notoriously expands on earlier studies reporting anti-inflammatory effects of CO in acute models of chemically or genetically induced intestinal inflammation68,69. In BALB/c mice offered dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) in the drinking water to induce colitis (4%), the oral administration of virgin CO exerted anti-inflammatory effects in a 12-day trial using young 4–6-week-old mice70. In mice with IL-10 deficiency, or mice with trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS)-colitis, the replacement of dietary long-chain FA with MCFA decreases the incidence of spontaneous or chemically induced colitis68,71.

While MA was protective against ileitis, lauric acid (LA) and palmitic acid (PA) were less effective or neutral, highlighting the specificity of MA in modulating inflammation and remission quality in chronic ileitis. Caution is required when extrapolating these findings to humans, as dietary surveys have reported associations between MA intake and ulcerative colitis in some pupulations72. Nevertheless, regulatory evaluations indicate that MA does not pose a health risk as a food ingredient73,74. MA is widely present in commonly consumed foods and has long been used in the food and cosmetic industries, with no evidence of mutagenicity or clinically relevant toxicity at typical intake levels73,74. Consistent with these assessments, we observed no evidence of hepatic steatosis or adverse cholesterol effects, even with long-term high-fat CO diets. Moreover, meta-analyses of human studies report no adverse effects of CO consumption on glycemia, adiposity, LDL cholesterol, or triglycerides14–17. Because protection coincided with shifts in microbiota composition and regulatory T-cell responses, we interpret these findings as reflecting multifactorial host-microbiome interactions rather than a single dominant mechanism. While we assessed diet-induced changes in immune cell populations by flow cytometry across multiple CD-relevant models, comprehensive cytokine profiling was not performed, representing a mechanistic limitation of the study. Future studies could incorporate comprehensive molecular analyses to better define the mechanisms underlying the modification of ileitis by this diet.

Although our findings demonstrate the potent anti-inflammatory effects of CO in multifocal cobblestone CD-like ileitis when used in animals for a great fraction (80%) of the animal’s lifespan (24 weeks of use over 30 weeks of life), it is important to highlight that direct extrapolation to human IBD has limitations in inferring the long-term requirements for effect or safety of CO-saturated fatty acids. The SAMP mouse model provides a valuable platform for studying CD-like ileitis but does not fully replicate the complexity of human CD, including lifestyle factors, environmental influences, and comorbidities such as cardiovascular diseases. In addition, although a balanced 1:1 male:female design was used and sex was included in the analyses with no significant effects observed, the study was not specifically powered to detect sex-dependent differences, which should be considered when interpreting these findings in future studies. Further research should explore the dose–response relationship of CO and MA, the interplay between immune regulation and microbiota shifts, and the efficacy of CO in diverse genetic and environmental contexts.

To provide context in terms of patient perception and acceptability of CO in the diet, we recently conducted a hospital-based survey of dietary behaviors in CD outpatients and control individuals in our facilities. Overall, participants with IBD ranked CO as the ingredient perceived as the healthiest among a list of dietary items offered to participants to rank11,46. CO received the highest average health score, ranking above soy-based foods, stevia, and artificial sweeteners, suggesting a potential preference or perceived benefit in the context of digestive health. A similar perception was observed among control participants, who ranked CO as the item with the second-highest health score. Despite this widespread acceptance and belief in benefits, the effects of CO and its primary component, MA, on intestinal inflammation in humans remain understudied.

As part of the present study, we also objectively examined the scientific literature to determine if clinical trials had been purposefully conducted to determine the effectiveness of CO in human IBD. Using a focused approach, we conducted a scoping review of studies and clinical trials in PubMed investigating the effect of CO, MA, or LA on human health, nutrition, and intestinal inflammation or IBD. Overall, we observed that although CO has been investigated since the 1940s, with a six-fold increase in research interest over the past decade, there is scarce evidence for its effect in human IBD. Between MA and LA, LA has received the most research attention since the 2010s. Despite growing interest in CO health effects, most studies have focused on metabolism and nutrition14 rather than on intestinal inflammation. Overall, studies on CO, LA, or MA related to inflammation remain a small fraction of the literature (1.9%, 140/7463 papers, with 3047 on CO), and clinical trials are scarce. Of 91 human trials examining CO, MA, and LA, only 7 clinical trials have assessed the impact of MA on inflammation, but none have focused on chronic intestinal inflammation or diseases such as IBD. In selected trials, MA supplementation did not alter serum MA levels but improved metabolic or inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein, leptin, and adiponectin ratios, suggesting the need to define how such additives alter inflammation independently of blood levels75,76. Experimental studies in animals suggest that MA-containing diets modulate inflammation via mechanisms involving adipose tissue-derived stromal vascular cells and insulin resistance77, indicating that MA could influence IBD through mechanisms that remain unexplored.

To date, it is unclear to what extent CO is beneficial to the gut health of humans in regions where CO is widely produced and consumed, and where the prevalence of IBD is significantly lower than in the USA (e.g., Southeast Asia, 27.2-fold lower prevalence of IBD vs. North America)78. Our experimental studies demonstrate that dietary supplementation with coconut oil or MA safely prevents Crohn’s disease-like ileitis in young, pre-disease SAMP mice and supports maintenance of remission in steroid-treated adult mice. Notably, MA supplementation was protective in young SAMP mice, suggesting context-dependent anti-inflammatory effects that may be most effective early in disease or during remission induction. Despite these promising preclinical results, clinical evidence supporting the use of CO or MA in human IBD remains scarce. Nevertheless, further studies are warranted, particularly because a substantial fraction of patients with IBD believe CO is beneficial to gut health11, which may facilitate acceptance and implementation of such dietary supplementation strategies.

Our focused scoping review of the literature identified few clinical trials examining MA or CO in inflammatory conditions, and none addressing chronic intestinal inflammation or Crohn’s disease specifically. Although MA supplementation has been reported to improve systemic metabolic and inflammatory markers in selected human studies, serum MA levels often remain unchanged, indicating that tissue-level or immune-mediated mechanisms may underlie biological effects. These observations underscore the need for mechanistic clinical studies designed to assess intestinal, immunologic, and microbial-mediated effects on the prevention of chronic intestinal inflammation.

Although our steroid-remission and withdrawal experiments were conducted using a high-dose CO diet, long-term high-dose CO intake cannot be recommended clinically at this time. Instead, future human studies should evaluate low- and high-dose regimens, including tapered strategies in which short-term high-dose administration is followed by low-dose maintenance, particularly given our finding that low-dose CO was sufficient to suppress CD-like inflammation in vivo. In parallel, supplementation with MA alone warrants investigation as a more mechanistic and potentially safer dietary intervention. Carefully designed clinical trials incorporating longitudinal monitoring of inflammatory markers, lipid profiles, and microbiome composition will be essential to define the therapeutic window, safety, and clinical utility of CO- and MA-based strategies for managing chronic intestinal inflammation.

Methods

Most methods used in this study are based on detailed protocols previously published by our group describing the use of stereomicroscopy to assess intestinal inflammation in mice, particularly SAMP mice. Accordingly, the methodologies applied here were performed as previously described21, especially those used to evaluate cobblestone ileitis, which in mice typically presents as three-dimensional agglomerates of intestinal inflammatory foci. When applicable, the descriptions below closely follow the methods, or are described verbatim as reported in our previous publications21,44,79.

Animals for experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Case Western Reserve University. Human fecal samples used for laboratory experiments were obtained from volunteers who donated discarded stool specimens to the Cleveland Digestive Diseases Research Core Center Biorepository. Samples were provided to investigators as deidentified, non-human-subject research specimens under protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Case Western Reserve University and University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center. To assess the impact of CO on cobblestone ileitis, herein we used primarily the SAMP1/YitFC (SAMP) mouse line, which we demonstrated is the only existing animal model affected with the three-dimensional pattern of chronic intestinal inflammation that resembles the ‘cobblestone’ appearance similar to that of human CD26. The SAMP mouse line maintained in our laboratory originated from a breeding pair obtained from Japan in the 1990s26. The genetic characteristics and strain differentiation of this mouse line, which originated from inbreeding and selection of AKR/J mice, have been previously described using microsatellite analyses31,32. AKR/J and C57BL/6J mice were derived from in-house breeding colonies that are regularly refreshed with breeder stock obtained from The Jackson Laboratory.

More recently, we determined that segmental ileitic lesions occur in essentially 100% of SAMP mice and that the characteristic cobblestone lesions are unique to this strain. Notably, these lesions have not been observed in other mouse models of spontaneous ileitis, including C57BL/6J mice carrying the TNFΔARE mutation (B6TNFΔARE)21. Therein, cobblestone ileitis in mice predicts with 100% accuracy the genome of the SAMP mouse line. More recently, our group has precisely characterized over 83 unique genomic features that are distinct from the SAMP line herein reported as 99% inbred compared to the genetic diversity of the other 13 well-established mouse lines sequenced by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute. Age/sex-matched (littermate controls) inbred germ-free (GF) and specific-pathogen-free (SPF) SAMP and C57BL/J6 (B6; SAMP reference genome) mice were obtained from the Cleveland Digestive Diseases Research Center, Mouse Models Core. In this study, the word spontaneous refers to when disease appears naturally as animals age, and inducible refers to when disease results within a predicted period following the administration of an agent deemed necessary to trigger inflammation. Sex-matched balanced groups of male:female ratios (1:1) were used across experiments, which were implemented according to validated protocols and guidelines defined to minimize artificial microbiome heterogeneity and promote reproducibility and study power44,79. Animals were humanely euthanized by CO2 narcosis. Unless otherwise specified, mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions on a 12-h light–dark cycle in a centrally controlled environment with constant temperature (23 °C) and humidity. Animals were housed in positively ventilated Allentown shoebox cages and provided ad libitum access to irradiated standard laboratory chow (5P00 Prolab RMH 3000; LabDiet), containing porcine-derived fat preserved with butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) and 6.8% crude fat as determined by acid hydrolysis. Fine aspen bedding and pine shavings were used as bedding material during the feeding trials to prevent consumption of bedding material. Aspen bedding has been shown in our laboratory not to influence the 3D-stereoenterotype of ileitis in SAMP mice46. All the mouse strains have paired colonies concurrently housed in separate complexes (facility A and B), which we previously showed maintain the cobblestone ileitis phenotype. All procedures were approved by Case Western Reserve University’s (CWRU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care guidelines.

Protocol of fecal homogenization (IsPreFeh)

To enhance experimental reproducibility and control for cage-to-cage microbiome variability, random microbiota distribution, and housing-associated confounding effects, a fecal homogenization protocol was implemented as previously validated in our laboratory to increase the homogeneity of the gut microbiota across animal subjects80. In brief, fresh fecal pellets from all SPF mice enrolled in the study were pooled, homogenized in pre-reduced sterile saline under anaerobic conditions, and re-administered within 30 min to each animal by oral gavage once daily for three consecutive days, one week before initiation of the experiments. This approach was used to standardize microbiota exposure across animals and improve microbiome reproducibility throughout all diet studies.

Controlling for cyclical bias

Unlike previous studies demonstrating that IBD can be transmissible by the gut microbiota, we have shown that SAMP ileitis does not result in ileitis in control AKR mice cohoused for 24 weeks since weaning46. To control for the cyclical effect of microbiome bias in the bedding of animals, all studies described herein were conducted with individually housed mice maintained on non-edible Aspen wood shavings (to prevent nutritional confounders). Cages were changed weekly.

Germ-free animals

The fecal microbiota colonization experiments described herein were tested by housing age- and sex-matched (male:female 1:1 ratio) inbred GF SAMP mice re-derived from Taconic Biosciences. In brief, the SAMP GF mouse colony (Cleveland Digestive Diseases Research Core Center; CDDRCC, Mouse Models Core) is maintained in high-efficiency particulate air-filtered pressurized isolators in the Animal Resource Center (ARC) ultra barrier facility, at CWRU School of Medicine. GF positive-pressurized HEPA rigid isolators (Plas-Labs Inc™ HEPA filtered isolation glove boxes with maximum capacity of 12–14 cages; 4–5 mouse cages/isolator, 1–5 adult mice/cage) are located inside an ultra-barrier pressurized HEPA-grade facility. Animals are housed in wire-topped polycarbonate shoebox cages (~30 cm L; 15 cm W; 15 cm H) in a 12 h:12 h light:dark cycle. Autoclaved GF-grade 40–50 kGy irradiated pellet food (PMI Nutrition Int’l., LLC., Labdiet® Charles River. Vac-Pac Rodent 6/5 irradiated, 5% kcal% fat) or autoclaved (Prolab RMH 3000; porcine animal-derived fat preserved with BHA; 6.8% content by acid hydrolysis) diets and water in bottles are provided ad libitum. All mice inside both pressurized isolators and NesTiso sets were routinely tested using standard culture-based microbiological procedures and Gram-staining81.

Human fecal material transfer experiment

We used GF SAMP mice (described above) transplanted with human microbiota isolated from a healthy non-IBD control to assess the effect of dietary fat on ileitis severity. Frozen human feces were stored and obtained under an approved IRB protocol (IRB# NHR-11-22) through the CDDRCC Biorepository Core, at CWRU, as previously described95. At the start of the experiment, each mouse received a total of 2 oral gavages (0.20 mL/10 g of body weight; 108–109 CFU/mouse). On the day of transplantation, donor inocula aliquots were thawed at room temperature (~6 min thaw/aliquot) and administered within <10 min after fully thawing.

Mouse diets

Several diets were tested using AKR and SAMP mice. Semipurified murine diets prepared by Research Diets Inc., New Brunswick, NJ, are described in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Mice were randomly assigned to 1 of 5 diets. Three control groups were fed a low-fat diet (lard vs. coconut vs. cocoa butter) and three HF groups (lard vs. coconut vs. cocoa butter) for 3 weeks or 24 weeks. Laboratory rodent pellets, standard to our facility (Labdiet® Rat/Mouse 18%VacPac-5LQ6, Charles River, USA) served as the control diet (Supplementary Table 1). All diets were vacuum-packed and double-irradiated to reach GF standards. All mice were allowed free access to food and water. Body weight and food intake were determined weekly.

Blinding and randomization

Sample processing and data analyses were performed in a blinded manner whenever feasible. Blinding during animal handling and diet administration was not possible in all cases for the animal experimenters because the use of HF-CO or CB experimental diets differed visibly in fat content and physical appearance. This limitation and the measures taken to minimize bias have been clarified79,82,83. Animals were randomized a priori in all experiments based on a predefined list of available animals. Whenever possible (e.g., during analysis of test tubes containing samples or in experiments where diets were indistinguishable, such as the meal + FA supplementation studies), blinding was maintained throughout data collection, analysis, and image preparation using non-informative codes that were revealed only after analyses were completed.

High-fat diet experiments containing coconut oil

SAMP mice were fed a high-fat diet (HF) (kcal-16% protein, 58% saturated fat-coconut oil, 26% carbohydrate, 0 ppm choline) or a standard rodent diet (ND) for 24 weeks starting at 6 weeks of age. AKR control mice were fed the same HF and ND diets. Body weight and glucose levels were monitored weekly and monthly, respectively. Ilea were directly examined using a stereomicroscopic technique to calculate the percentages of abnormal mucosa described by our group21. Harvested ilea were examined using a stereomicroscopic pattern profiling protocol of gut health (3D-SMAPgut)21 to measure the % of abnormal mucosa, and total inflammatory scores (based on histologic indices of chronic, active, and villous distortion) were determined at the time of sacrifice (30-week-old mice). Ileal total inflammatory scores (based on histologic indices of chronic, active, and villous distortion) were determined at the time of sacrifice (30 weeks). In a separate experiment, GF SAMP with spontaneous colitis (n = 9) were transplanted with fecal matter from either 26-week-old SAMP HF or SAMP ND; control GF SAMP received only sterile PBS (n = 6 for all groups). After 3 days, mice were sacrificed and colon tissues examined for the severity of colitis by MPO assay.

Because the HF-CO (plant-based) and ND (lard-based) diets varied in numerous aspects, including the source and concentration of fat, in additional experiments, we controlled for all dietary ingredients. We only used plant sources of fat to replace the potential pro-inflammatory effect of lard in ileitis84, and to avoid treatment selection bias. To avoid lard or animal fats, we used cocoa butter (CB) as a plant source of saturated long-chain fatty acids. Diets had equal fractions for all macronutrients and varied only in the plant-derived sources of fat.

We aimed at determining the role of CO as a preferential source of medium-chain fatty acids, compared to CB as a preferential source of long-chain fatty acids. We used as a baseline diet Surwit’s rodent diet85 with 11 or 58 kcal% fat, using soybean oil, sucrose, maltodextrin, and corn starch as the balancing ingredients to adjust the four diets to an equivalent overall kcal content of 5557 kcal (see Supplementary Table 2). Overall, the FA composition for the CO oils as dietary ingredients is palmitic acid 31.6% (long-chain FA), and medium-chain FAs lauric 158.7%, myristic 60%, caprylic 25.7%, capric 19.7%, and caporic 2%. The composition of these FAs for CB is palmitic 37.7%, and 0% for lauric, myristic, caprylic, capric, and caproic. For comparison, soybean oil FA composition is primarily oleic acid (C18:1) (Omega-9) with ~11% palmitic, with non-detectable amounts of myristic, lauric, caprylic, capric, and caproic86.

Individual fatty acid mouse diets

Mice were randomly allocated to receive a rodent diet (P3000, 14.9% kcal fat, Harlan diets) supplemented with one of three fatty acids; lauric, myristic or palmitic acid (79 g fatty acid/1 kg diet; 156–197 mg/fatty acid per day assuming 4–5 g/day intake, Sigma Aldrich USA), or a mixture of all three fatty acids combined. Mice did not eat when FA were 100% of the diet. The fatty acid supplementation represented 50% of that found in the rodent coconut oil diet. Non-supplemented laboratory rodent diet (P3000, 14.9% kcal fat, Harlan diets) served as the control. Investigation of the effect of individual fatty acids encompassed three separate experiments. Experiment one; groups of sex-matched male and female 4-week-old SPF SAMP (before ileitis onset) and AKR control mice (6 mice/group) were fed either a lauric, myristic or palmitic acid supplemented diet, or control diet for 24 weeks. Mice fed a non-supplemented laboratory diet served as controls. Experiment two: Groups of sex-matched male and female 24-week-old SPF SAMP mice (established cobblestone ileitis, 6 mice/group) were fed either a lauric or myristic acid-supplemented diet for 24 weeks. Experiment 3: Groups sex-matched male and female 12- and 24-week-old SPF SAMP, AKR, and C57BL/6 control mice (4 mice/group) were fed a standard laboratory rodent diet supplemented with a mixture of 3 fatty acids (lauric, myristic, and palmitic acid) for 12 weeks. All mice were maintained on non-edible Aspen bedding (2–3 mice/cage), and underwent a fecal homogenization (as described above) one week prior to the start of their respective experiment. Body weights and food intake were recorded weekly in all experiments. Mice were kept on a 12 h:12 h light:dark cycle in species-appropriate temperature/humidity-controlled room and maintained in AAALAC-accredited Animal Research Center rooms at Case Western Reserve University (CWRU). Animals were humanely euthanized by C02 narcosis. All described procedures were approved by the IACUC and the Institutional Review Board at CWRU, in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Dexamethasone remission SAMP ileitis model

We first developed and validated a 5-week-long SAMP ileitis recurrence and remission model using systemic dexamethasone (DEX) to achieve remission of ileitis in SAMP mice. Then, we used the model to make dietary changes at the start of DEX treatment. Prior to dietary supplementation, we used a protocol of fecal homogenization (IsPreFeh) as described above. Mice were then divided into two groups of sex-matched adult SPF SAMP mice (30-week-old, advanced ileitis). One group was treated with DEX intraperitoneally for 5 days and maintained on a regular diet (DEX-ND diet), and the other was treated with the same DEX regimen but maintained on a high-content coconut oil diet (DEX-CO diet). Fecal, ileal, and spleen tissue samples were collected for the determination of myeloperoxidase activity. Histology and stereomicroscopic profiling of 3D-inflammatory patterns of ileitis were used at the end of the feeding trial (weeks 2–5) to assess intestinal inflammation.

Indicators of metabolic function

Basal and fasting glucose concentrations (6-h daytime fasting) were measured in blood collected via tail vein using a OneTouch Ultra2 Blood Glucose meter (LifeScan, Milpitas, CA, USA).

Quantification of intestinal inflammation postmortem

Intestinal tissue samples (terminal ilea, colon) were collected at sacrifice for blinded stereomicroscopy 3-D pattern profiling (3D-SM)21 and histological assessment of formalin-fixed tissues using validated methodology21,87. Inflammatory indices for histological villous distortion, active/chronic inflammation, and total inflammation were assessed in a semi-quantitative fashion. Ilea were removed from mice to determine the presence of stereomicroscopic (SM) abnormalities on the mucosal surface, and for histology, and to detect using the SMAPgut protocol as described21. Bouin’s, or neutral buffered formalin, PBS, and RNA-later fixed tissues were used as fixative. Tissue lesions were scored manually. Images and image-based analysis were conducted using ImageJ, which is a public domain Java image processing program developed at the NIH.

Histological inflammation scores

Histological evaluation of inflammation severity for SM correlation analysis was determined in hematoxylin and eosin-stained 5 µm-thick sections using a semi-quantitative scoring system88. As described21, ileum inflammation scores were given for the severity (ranging from 0 to 4) for 5 indices of inflammation severity (0–3). Total scores resulted from adding the individual scores21.

Stereomicroscopy and decimal scoring system

To characterize the integrity of the intestinal mucosa and quantify the microscopic features of the cobblestone lesions in SAMP, we used 3D-SMAPgut, a method that has been validated technically and mathematically as a suitable alternative strategy to murine ileal endoscopy and colonoscopy. The final scoring system used enabled the description of the 3D appearance of the intestinal mucosa in greater detail, which then served for mathematical model of lesion progression. See details in publication21, which includes an annotated form and criteria to assess the last 10 cm of the mouse ileum and the entire colon. In this study, we used those analytical principles to examine the entire small and large intestine (~30 cm).

Microsatellite analysis of DNA to verify that mice responsive to CO were SAMP mice

We used a microsatellite analysis protocol developed for SAMP mice differentiation and the identification of senescence-accelerated genes (Supplementary Fig. 1). Genomic DNA was isolated from tail biopsies or tissue samples from SAMP1/Fc mice and used for microsatellite-based strain authentication. A panel of polymorphic microsatellite markers distributed across all autosomes and the X chromosome was analyzed to assess the genetic background of the mice. The marker positions had been assigned according to recombination distances reported in the Mouse Genome Database. PCR amplification was performed using locus-specific primers, and allele sizes were determined by automated fragment analysis. SAMP1/Fc allele sizes were compared with AKR/J and C57BL/6J, two reference inbred strains representing major laboratory mouse lineages used in a previous study, including AKR/J, C57BL/6 J, C3H/HeJ, DBA/2J, BALB/cJ, NZB/BINJ, NOD/LtJ, and SM/J. Markers were classified as AKR-like, shared with non-AKR reference strains, or unique to SAMP1/Fc when the observed allele size was not detected in any reference strain. SAMP mice have notable differences in selected genomic regions on Chrs 4, 14, 16, and 1731,32.

MPO activity and rescaling for SM-scoring purposes

Tissues obtained by SM-dissection were tested for MPO activity89 as described88, with important modification in the acquisition of data and interpretation of time series spectrometry changes in absorbance over 5 min of incubation at 28 °C using 96-well plate formats, as described88. Instead of inferring the data from only three time-point measurements as commonly reported, which had a great sample-to-sample variability due to incubation or loading-of-reagent artifacts, we automatically recorded the OD changes every 30 s to create a kinetic curve from which the steepest slope was identified to extract slope information needed to make precise quantifications.

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER)

To quantify the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured using stereomicroscopy-dissected intestinal tissue segments mounted on porous membranes, as previously described by our laboratory in SAMP mice ileal explants21. Briefly, rinsed tissue samples were positioned apical side up on 0.4-µm membranes and secured in custom sandwich mounts to define a standardized exposure area. Assembled mounts were placed in transwell inserts, submerged in culture medium, and verified by stereomicroscopy. TEER was recorded using an EVOM voltmeter immediately after assembly and following incubation at 37 °C.

qPCR enumeration of 16S-based microbial communities relative to B-actin

We quantified specific bacterial communities at the family level relative to the abundance of β-actin (host-cell gene marker) or total 16S DNA using DNA extracts from SM dissected tissues and real-time qPCR. DNA extraction from fresh and fixed tissues was performed using a Qiagen kit (Gentra Systems, Minneapolis, MN), following the recommendations for bacteria, and after a period of tissue digestion with proteinase K90. Pure DNA quantification was conducted with fluorescence and Qubit, and then adjusted to equimolar concentrations using PCR-grade water. Gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing of representative PCR products were used to verify amplification specificity. Host β-actin was used for normalization, and data were also examined using total bacterial 16S normalization. Quantitative PCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche) and validated primers. Relative bacterial abundance was calculated from Ct values. qPCR has been previously validated for sensitive quantification of bacterial targets in fresh and preserved intestinal tissues91,92. Cross-contamination during tissue dissection with SM was minimized by using new or sterilized instruments for sample collection.

Tissue collection and single-cell preparation for flow cytometry analysis

Spleens and mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) were harvested into cold wash buffer (HBSS + 1% FBS) or RPMI containing FBS and maintained on ice. Single-cell suspensions were prepared within 1 h of harvest to preserve viability. For each sample, a 70 µm cell strainer was placed in a 5 cm dish and pre-wetted with 5 mL cold wash buffer. One spleen or MLN was transferred to the strainer and gently dissociated using a 3- or 5-cc syringe plunger with minimal pressure (~20 s), allowing cells to pass through the strainer into the dish. Cells were transferred to conical tubes and kept on ice. Cells were washed twice with 20 mL cold wash buffer. Red blood cells were not lysed. Following the final wash, the cell suspension was passed through a 40 µm strainer, centrifuged, and the cells were counted. With respect to surface staining and controls, for each sample, 1 × 106 cells were aliquoted into three separate 5 mL round-bottom FACS tubes (total 3 × 1 × 106 cells per sample). Cells were washed with 1 mL wash buffer and resuspended in ~100 µL residual wash buffer. Fc receptors were blocked by adding 5 µL Fc block (from the staining kit), followed by incubation at room temperature (RT) for 10–15 min. Without washing, surface antibodies were added at manufacturer-recommended concentrations for 1 × 106 cells and incubated for 30 min at RT protected from light. The following tubes were prepared: Tube 1 (surface isotype control): anti-CD4 + CD25 isotype (Rat IgG1–APC). Tube 2 (Foxp3 isotype control): anti-CD4+ anti-CD25 (APC), Tube 3 (Foxp3 stain): anti-CD4+ anti-CD25 (APC). Cells were washed twice with 1 mL wash buffer. Fixation, permeabilization, and intracellular Foxp3 staining. After the final surface wash, pellets were fully resuspended by vortexing. Cells were fixed/permeabilized by adding 1 mL of 1× Fix/Perm solution (prepared per kit instructions), vortexing, and incubating for 30 min at RT protected from light. Two milliliters of 1x permeabilization buffer were then added to each tube, cells were centrifuged, and supernatants were discarded. Cells were resuspended in ~100 µL permeabilization buffer, and intracellular antibodies were added at manufacturer-recommended concentrations for 1 × 106 cells: Tube 1: no intracellular stain. Tube 2: Foxp3 isotype control. Tube 3: anti-Foxp3. Samples were incubated for 30 min at RT protected from light. Cells were washed by adding 2 mL permeabilization buffer, vortexing, centrifuging, and discarding supernatant, followed by a final wash with 2 mL wash buffer. Cells were resuspended in 300 µL wash buffer and stored at 4 °C protected from light until acquisition. Samples were acquired within 2–3 days without appreciable loss of signal. Samples were acquired on a BD FACSAria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences; instrument ID P0060) using standard single-color compensation controls. Data were analyzed using BD FACSDiva software (version 8.0.1). Regulatory T cells were identified as CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ events. Positive gates were defined using the CD25-APC isotype control for surface staining and the Foxp3 isotype control for intracellular staining. The quantification of the analyzed populations was determined by hierarchical gating using forward- and side-scatter parameters to exclude debris, doublets were excluded by FSC-A/FSC-H gating, and lymphocytes were identified by FSC/SSC characteristics. CD4+ cells were then gated, and subset purity (e.g., CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) was confirmed using isotype controls and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls where applicable. Additional lymphocyte subsets were quantified using fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD44 and CD62L, applying the same gating strategy and isotype-based control approach. Absolute cell counts and normalized percentages were calculated relative to the parent gated population. Cell population abundance was quantified both as absolute cell counts and as normalized percentages relative to the parent gated population (e.g., CD4+ lymphocytes). Additional subsets, including CD44+ and CD62L+ populations, were quantified using the same gating hierarchy and control strategy. Reported frequencies represent gated populations after exclusion of non-lymphocyte and non-singlet events. Sequential hierarchical gating strategy. Initial gates were established on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters to identify the lymphocyte population and exclude debris based on size and granularity. Doublets were excluded using FSC area versus height discrimination. Within the singlet lymphocyte gate, CD4+ cells were identified, and subsequent gates were applied to define CD25+ and Foxp3+ populations. Boundaries between positive and negative populations were established using isotype controls and fluorescence-based thresholds. All gating parameters were applied consistently across samples within each experiment. Representative gating plots illustrating the strategy and gate placement are provided in the manuscript figures to document data quality and reproducibility.

Microbiome effect of coconut diet on mice transplanted with human gut microbiota

To determine whether our coconut oil (CO) diet exerted specific positive or negative effects on bacteria present in a human fecal inoculum, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on fecal samples from the healthy human donor and from 24-week-old germ-free (GF) SAMP mice following transplantation. Fecal samples were collected after 21 days and compared across three groups: transplanted mice fed a high-fat coconut oil diet (HF-CO), transplanted mice fed a normal diet (ND), and specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice maintained on ND. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were normalized and expressed as relative abundance based on the total number of taxa detected per sample. To reduce mouse-to-mouse variability and avoid inflation of statistical variance caused by zero-count taxa, species were included in the analysis only if detectable DNA was present in all HF-CO-fed mice. Taxa were excluded if any transplanted mouse had zero counts for that species. A bacterium was classified as enriched or inhibited by the CO diet only if its average relative abundance in HF-CO–fed transplanted mice exceeded (or was lower than) its abundance in (i) the human donor inoculum, (ii) transplanted mice fed ND, and (iii) SPF mice fed ND. To quantify diet-associated effects, mean read counts were calculated for each group, and enrichment or inhibition was ranked using ratio-based comparisons between HF-CO- and ND-fed transplanted mice.

Statistical analysis

In preparation for the experiments' execution, we complied with the ARRIVE93 reporting checklist and complementary strategy themes to reduce artificial study heterogeneity and irreproducibility, as we reported79,82,83. The methods here described are largely based on methods previously validated and published in our laboratory21. Over several years, various cohorts of animals were examined in repeated experiments (at least independent duplicated experiments, and single-caged animals to prevent data clustering) with well-defined dietary conditions to increase external validity, incorporating methodologies previously reported83.

The number of animals used in most experiments was based on a priori sample size estimations derived from our previously published studies using the same SAMP mouse model, dietary interventions, and histological ileitis endpoints assessed at 24 weeks of age, which consistently detected biologically meaningful differences. To prevent data clustering and maximize statistical power, animals were housed individually in all experiments, as previously justified79,82,83. In those prior studies, SAMP mice fed a standard rodent control diet (ND; lard-based) historically exhibited histological ileitis scores of ~18–20 with standard deviations of 6–8. Using the STATA power means function, and assuming a two-sided alpha = 0.05, 80% power, and a biologically expected meaningful effect corresponding to a 50% increase or reduction in ileitis severity, we estimated that ~7–12 mice per group would be required. Accordingly, most dietary intervention experiments included 8–12 mice per group. Following the initial studies with HF-CO, post hoc power calculations based on the larger-than-anticipated treatment effects demonstrated that selected proof-of-concept and validation experiments, including microbiome engraftment studies, could be adequately performed using 4–6 mice per group. These smaller cohorts were therefore used for descriptive and confirmatory studies where large effect sizes had already been established. In all cases, the exact number of animals analyzed is reported for each experiment. To further validate the impact of the CO diet in the SAMP ileitis model, experiments were repeated under varying conditions (seasons), including different doses and control diets, to ensure that observed effects were not due to chance.

Quantitative data are presented as mean values ± SEM as appropriate. Frequency data (percentages) were calculated as the proportion of positive observations divided by the total sample size (x/N) and expressed as proportions (%). Exact 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for proportions were calculated using the binomial (Clopper–Pearson) method, with normal-approximation estimates used for comparison.

Unless otherwise indicated, all statistical analyses (e.g., T-tests or Mann–Whitney) were two-tailed, and all reported P values are based on two-sided tests. Box plots show the median (center line), the interquartile range (box; 25th−75th percentiles), and the minimum and maximum values (whiskers). Individual data values are also shown to further illustrate data dispersion. Parametric tests were used when assumptions were met. Log transformation was performed for MPO analysis to improve normalization. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the unpaired t-test. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA or 2-way ANOVA as appropriate. Pairwise comparisons computed accounted for adjusted family errors and statistics (p-values). Kruskal–Wallis or two-tailed Mann–Whitney tests were used when data were not normally distributed based on residual analysis. All inferential and normality assumptions of data and statistics were conducted using GraphPad primarily for simple univariate analysis, and R or STATA for regression statistics or visualizing multivariable sets. PCA visualization methods used in the final figures were produced using STATA, or using the open-source software Orange datamining (https://orangedatamining.com), or the heatmap Broad Institute software Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus). Violin plots were also used for visualization of data distribution, as reported. Nonparametric correlation analyses were used to evaluate associations between primary inflammation outcomes (MPO, histology, MPO Log2, SM) and fecal or plasma variables (microbiome, metagenome, mass spectrometry), controlling for diet. Multivariable regression and biplot analyses identified contributors to data heterogeneity explaining mouse segregation by diet. Logistic regression assessed the impact of diet on gene ontology and pathway analyses across microbiota conditions. Binary analysis (presence/absence of taxa) of microbiome data, excluding low-abundant taxa (threshold < 0.1), was used to verify taxa engraftment in mice as described94. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Nonparametric tests were used when appropriate. When p-values were within 0.003 of the 0.05 threshold and considered biologically relevant, exact p-values were reported, and findings were described without stating statistical significance. In selected cases, 1000 Monte Carlo simulations were performed to estimate effect size and study power. P values are presented as numeric items whenever feasible in the graphs for ease of interpretation. For the analysis of Firmicutes:Bacteroidota (or F:Proteobacteria) ratios, when the sample size was small for one group (3–8 mice/group), data were analyzed after lognormal transformation using Brown–Forsythe and Welch one-way ANOVA tests, followed by Holm–Šídák multiple-comparison testing to control for family-wise error (alpha = 0.05). The degrees of freedom were reported for regressions when appropriate.
