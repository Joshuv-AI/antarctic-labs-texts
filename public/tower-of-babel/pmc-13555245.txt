Age-Related Differences in Chronic Rhinosinusitis: Inflammatory Profiles and Surgical Outcomes in Older Adults and Non-older Adults
Authors: Hee Sung Chae, Joo Hyung Lee, Minheon Kim, Eun Kyung Jeon, Young-Ha Lee, Eun Jung Lee
Published: 2026

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a heterogeneous inflammatory condition that substantially impairs quality of life [1]. Aging has been associated with immunosenescence, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and altered host–microbe interactions; however, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study [2,3]. Although several studies have reported age-related differences in CRS inflammatory profiles and clinical outcomes, the effects of aging on type 2 inflammatory endotypes and surgical outcomes remain incompletely understood.

We retrospectively analyzed adults who underwent endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) for CRS at a tertiary referral center between January 2020 and December 2024. CRS was diagnosed according to the criteria established in the European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps 2020 [4]. Patients with fungal sinusitis, tumors, mucoceles, or other non-CRS conditions were excluded. Participants were categorized as older adults (≥65 years) or non-older adults (<65 years). Type 2 CRS was defined according to the Japanese Epidemiological Survey of Refractory Eosinophilic CRS (JESREC) criteria (JESREC score ≥11 plus tissue eosinophils ≥70 per high-power field) [5,6]. Sensitization to staphylococcal enterotoxin A (SEA) and B (SEB) was evaluated using serum-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels. Postoperative recurrence was defined as revision ESS.

Among 416 patients, 79 (19.0%) were classified as older adults. CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) was significantly less prevalent in older adults (Table 1). Overall, 17.8% of patients met the criteria for type 2 CRS, a proportion lower than that reported in several previous Asian cohorts, possibly because of the stringent diagnostic criteria used in this study. Specifically, the definition required both a high JESREC score and marked tissue eosinophilia. In addition, inclusion of only surgical patients from a tertiary referral center may have contributed to differences in disease spectrum relative to prior studies. When stratified by decade, type 2 CRS prevalence peaked among patients in their 40s and generally declined with advancing age (Supplementary Table 1). After adjustment for asthma and other covariates, age remained independently associated with a lower likelihood of type 2 CRS (Fig. 1A) and a reduced risk of postoperative recurrence (Fig. 1B).

Peripheral blood eosinophil counts correlated with tissue eosinophil levels (Spearman’s ρ=0.4246, P<0.001), supporting concordance between systemic and local inflammation; however, eosinophil levels did not differ significantly between age groups (ANOVA P=0.6529 for peripheral eosinophil counts, P=0.3316 for tissue eosinophil counts). Older patients demonstrated numerically lower total serum IgE levels than non-older patients, although the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.636) (Table 1). Sensitization to SEA and SEB was identified in 10.3% and 11.3% of patients, respectively, without significant age-related differences. During follow-up, 19.5% of patients required revision surgery. Recurrence rates declined with advancing age (Supplementary Table 2). In multivariable logistic regression analyses treating age as a continuous variable, age remained independently associated with a lower risk of recurrence after adjustment for baseline type 2 status and other covariates (Fig. 1B).

These findings suggest that CRS in older adults may represent a distinct clinical phenotype characterized by a lower prevalence of nasal polyps, reduced type 2 inflammatory burden, and fewer postoperative recurrences. Aging has been associated with immunosenescence, including altered T-cell responses and reduced T-helper 2 polarization. Epidemiological studies have also reported declining asthma prevalence and diminished IgE-mediated responses with advancing age, which may partly explain the lower prevalence of type 2 CRS observed in our older cohort. Previous histopathological studies have demonstrated reduced tissue eosinophilia and a relative shift toward neutrophil-dominant inflammation with aging [7]. Potential mechanisms include attenuated type 2 immune signaling, reduced local IgE activity, age-related epithelial barrier alterations, and chronic bacterial colonization favoring non-type 2 inflammatory pathways [8]. Because no histological or mechanistic analyses were performed, the proposed biological mechanisms underlying these age-related differences in CRS should be considered speculative.

A male predominance was observed in both age groups, consistent with previous studies conducted in Korea. Smoking was not significantly associated with CRS in our analyses (P=0.121); however, its indirect contribution to sex-related differences in CRS prevalence cannot be excluded [9]. We initially hypothesized that the lower prevalence of type 2 CRS among older patients would be accompanied by reduced systemic and local eosinophilic activity. Notably, eosinophil levels did not differ significantly by age group, suggesting that aging may influence the qualitative expression rather than the magnitude of type 2 inflammation [10].

Previous studies have linked staphylococcal enterotoxin sensitization with the severity of eosinophilic CRS [11]. In the present cohort, however, SEA/SEB sensitization was not associated with age, suggesting that aging may modulate downstream inflammatory expression rather than sensitization itself. Because type 2 CRS is more prone to recurrence, the age-related reduction in type 2 disease provides a biologically plausible explanation for the lower revision rates observed among older adults. Thus, older patients may have a lower likelihood of type 2 CRS and recurrence, whereas younger patients with a greater type 2 inflammatory burden may require closer postoperative monitoring. An alternative explanation is that type 2 inflammation may accelerate disease progression, leading to earlier surgical intervention rather than reflecting a true protective effect of aging. This interpretation is supported by previous studies suggesting that allergic or type 2 inflammatory conditions are associated with more rapid CRS progression [12]. Therefore, the inverse association between age and revision surgery should be interpreted cautiously. Although differences in healthcare utilization, follow-up intensity, and surgical extent may contribute to heterogeneous findings across populations, the consistent inverse association between age and recurrence observed in this cohort supports the possibility of a meaningful biological effect.

Clinically, these findings support consideration of patient age when counseling individuals regarding inflammatory endotype and postoperative prognosis. Older patients may have a lower likelihood of type 2 CRS and a reduced risk of revision surgery, whereas younger patients with a higher type 2 inflammatory burden may warrant closer postoperative surveillance and targeted adjunctive therapy when appropriate.

This study has several limitations. First, this retrospective single-center study may be subject to selection bias. Second, detailed medication history, surgical extent, and systemic atopic status were not consistently available in the retrospective dataset. Although total serum IgE levels were available for a subset of patients, no statistically significant age-related difference was identified. Given the limited availability of IgE data and the retrospective nature of testing, these findings should be interpreted cautiously when considering the relationship between aging and systemic type 2 inflammatory activity. Third, the study population consisted exclusively of patients undergoing ESS, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader CRS population. Fourth, recurrence was defined on the basis of revision surgery and therefore may underestimate disease recurrence managed medically. Because follow-up duration and completion may have varied within this retrospective cohort, potential bias in recurrence assessment cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the large sample size and the consistency of findings across decade-based strata strengthen the validity of the results.

In conclusion, CRS in older patients was characterized by a lower prevalence of type 2 inflammation and reduced postoperative recurrence. Age remained independently associated with these outcomes after adjustment for asthma, supporting the possibility of an age-related CRS phenotype. Recognition of these differences may facilitate more individualized evaluation and management, although prospective endotype-based studies are needed to further refine treatment strategies across age groups.
