Fluid flow-induced deformation dynamics under biaxial loading in anisotropic (layered) analogues of unconventional reservoirs: insights from physical model experiments and numerical simulations
Authors: P N R L Sudhishna, Tridib Kumar Mondal, Sourav Mondal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding fluid-driven fracture propagation in anisotropic, layered geological formations is critical for optimizing resource recovery in unconventional reservoirs. In this study, we present a novel experimental and numerical investigation into fluid flow-induced deformation dynamics under biaxial loading using analogue models of shale and reservoir rock. A custom-designed low-pressure biaxial compression system is developed to simulate realistic subsurface conditions while allowing real-time, high-resolution visualization of fracture evolution in low-modulus elastic and viscoelastic materials. Gelatin-based analogues, representing both isotropic and anisotropic formations, are employed to mimic geological heterogeneity and elasticity. Fracturing experiments are conducted using water, SAE 140 oil, and oil–toluene mixtures injected at a constant rate of 1 ml/min under controlled loading conditions (confining load: 1.23 bar; top load: 0.52 bar). The evolving fracture geometries are analysed using ImageJ, focusing on metrics such as aspect ratio and fracture length. Numerical simulations based on a phase field damage model are performed to predict fracture initiation and propagation, showing strong agreement with experimental results and qualitative consistency with the analytical Khristianovic–Geertsma–de Klerk (KGD) model. Our integrated approach provides critical insights into the role of fluid viscosity, heterogeneity and loading conditions on fracture dynamics. The experimental setup also supports studies relevant to CO₂ sequestration and gas hydrate dissociation. This work advances the mechanistic understanding of fracture processes in layered formations, offering insights for field-scale hydraulic fracturing and sustainable subsurface resource management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55767-4.Keywords: Biaxial loading, Layered heterogeneous media, Fracturing, Phase field damage, Unconventional reservoirSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Shale rocks with high porosity, often contain significant hydrocarbons; however, their extraction is challenging due to their low permeability1. Understanding fracture propagation and fluid flow behaviour in such media is therefore critical, as enhanced recovery often relies on induced fractures to improve permeability. Hydraulic fracturing has become the primary technique to enhance and recover oil and gas, particularly in unconventional shale reservoirs2,3. Beyond oil and gas recovery, hydrofracturing is also applied in subsurface applications such as geothermal energy extraction and nuclear waste disposal under confined stress conditions. In geothermal energy systems, rock permeability is increased in hot, dry rocks and low-permeability formations to facilitate heat extraction4,5. Similarly, in the context of CO2 sequestration, the hydraulic fracturing technique studies are used to examine fracture evolution in host rocks, ensuring long-term containment and stability of deep geological repositories6,7.

The propagation of fluid induced fractures under various loading conditions such as uniaxial, biaxial, and triaxial stress, is fundamental in understanding the deformation of rock and fracture evolution8,9. Recent studies are mostly based on laboratory investigations, have primarily focused on high-modulus materials such as sandstone, concrete blocks, and resin specimens of varying sizes under a biaxial compression test apparatus10–14.

Similarly, several authors have investigated fracture growth in a sandstone and resin samples using a conventional triaxial compression system integrated with Computer Tomography (CT) scanning equipment and biaxial loading frames equipped with X-ray transmission windows to visualize the 3D fracture formation over experimental time15–17. However, a major research gap persists regarding the continuous monitoring of fracture propagation or formation evolution over time, particularly in low modulus or heterogeneous mediums. Different fracture morphologies, such as wing fractures, nonlinear fractures, and disk-shaped fractures, commonly form under biaxial compression across both homogeneous and layered rock samples, regardless of host rock type11,13,14,18. With increasing load, nonlinear fractures typically appear on the surfaces of layered blocks categorized into tensile opening in intact rock, shear and tensile fractures along pre-existing joints, and mixed-mode fractures resulting from combined tensile and shear stresses19.

Extensive experimental and numerical research has addressed three-dimensional fracture growth under biaxial loading. Validation of observed fracture patterns in lab and field studies has often relied on numerical modelling methods such as finite element and boundary element methods20,21, extended finite element method (XFEM), finite element (FE) techniques13,22,23, and the Cohesive Zone Method (CZM)24,25. Each of these approaches has specific advantages and limitations in capturing 3D fracture propagation. The Finite Element Method (FEM) and the Extended Finite Element Method (XFEM) provide robust frameworks for simulating complex 3D fracture geometries, but their application to 3D domains requires significant computational resources. However, Boundary element methods are well known for their ability to simulate large-scale models and computational efficiency, but are less suitable for solving heterogeneous materials and non-linear constitutive behaviour. The Cohesive Zone Method (CZM) provides detailed insights into fracture zones and initiation mechanisms but requires predefined crack paths and may become computationally demanding in 3D applications.

Recent phase field model approaches have significantly improved the ability to simulate hydraulic fracture propagation in complex geological formations. From previous studies, it is evident that the authors have investigated the interactions of fluid fracture interface interactions in the poroelastic homogeneous rock using a variational phase field approach54,56; meanwhile, others, have analysed fracture initiation, rock failure behaviour, and multi-layer propagation under anisotropic stress fields55. These numerical simulations will provide the energy-based fracture criteria and preferentially propagated fracture pathways; however, they rely on assumptions about material interface properties and the evolution of fracture toughness58–60. By comparing numerical simulations with the analogue experiments in the present framework, the experiments provide an opportunity to directly visualize the evolution and growth of fluid fracture in a heterogeneous medium under controlled biaxial stress conditions. Thus, these observations will provide a physical validation and complementary insights for future phase field simulations.

The layered gelatin analogue medium is structurally heterogeneous, consisting of isotropic layers (each homogeneous) with contrasting Young’s moduli, rather than being intrinsically anisotropic like shale.

The current study offers significant advantages for modelling hydraulic fracture propagation in the matrix, particularly its ability to capture fracture initiation and complex propagation behaviour in phase field modelling. In this framework, fracture evolution is implicitly represented by a scalar damage field, which is governed by a characteristic length scale parameter (lint) and a fracture aperture. The direct measurement of fracture aperture, mesh resolution, and a parameter that is particularly, sensitive to permeability can be complicated by diffuse representation, leading to uncertainty in aperture-dependent flow calculations. These challenges have been addressed in this study through the adopted modelling strategy, which balances numerical robustness and physical realism in simulating coupled solid-fluid interactions.

However, these models failed to predict the complex fracture behaviour or pattern observed in experiments. The phase field model has recently been adopted to simulate fracture initiation and propagation under applied stress conditions. This approach effectively captures fracture evolution even in complex media such as laminated layered formations or sandstone samples from unconventional reservoirs, showing improved alignment with experimental observations18,26–28. Despite advances, a significant research gap remains in the real-time monitoring of fluid-driven fractures under biaxial compression at laboratory scale. Most prior work has focused on analysing the final fracture pattern rather than the temporal evolution of fractures.

To address these limitations, the present study introduces a novel, indigenous low-pressure biaxial loading system specifically designed to investigate the dynamic evolution and capable of real-time visualization of three-dimensional fluid-driven fractures in analogue materials mimicking geological media. While previous experiments or relevant studies predominantly relied on homogeneous rock samples under uniaxial or triaxial confinement, but the developed biaxial system can operate under both uniaxial and biaxial loading, enabling us to systematically isolate the coupled effects of stress non-uniformity and material heterogeneity on fracture initiation and penetration arrest, deviation of the fractures across the layers. Thus, the system accommodates low Young’s modulus samples (< 1 MPa), including plastics, polymers, resin, and viscoelastic materials. Gelatin is chosen as the analogue medium in the present work, due to its excellent transparency and mechanical properties, closely replicating geological formations, enabling clear visualization of internal layering and facilitates the observation of fracture initiation, shape, and propagation direction during deformation. Different gelatin composition (contrasting Young’s modulus) is used for different layers (creating a gradient or inducing heterogeneity in the material properties). Furthermore, by fluid injection with varied viscosities (water, oil, and oil-toluene mixtures for creating the hydro fracture), further help in resolving fluid-solid coupling effects from interface-controlled mechanics. This approach reveals the previously, unresolved mechanisms such as material heterogeneity, deviation of fracture at the interface, layer interface reactivation under low modulus or toughness conditions for hydro fracture evolution. In contrast to numerical phase field approaches, which requires the calibration of fracture energy and regularization parameters like, length scale parameter (lint), the present framework, provides controlled experimental evidence to evaluate fracture transition mechanism and offers quantitative validation for modelling approaches.

Computational details

In the present study, numerical simulations enhance experimental findings by expanding a wide range of parameters beyond lab-scale limitations and providing insights into the internal parameters, including stress distribution at the fracture tip and damage evolution, which are challenging to measure during laboratory experiments. This integration helps improve the reliability and applicability of findings by bridging the gap between laboratory and field-scale conditions. With this aim, numerical simulations are carried out using the phase field method implemented within the COMSOL Multiphysics v6.3® platform [License #16078497], utilizing the CFD, Structural Mechanics, and Nonlinear Structural Materials modules. A two-dimensional square domain measuring 0.1 m × 0.1 m is constructed (Fig. S-1, supplementary material), with a narrow inlet channel of dimensions 0.001 m × 0.004 m positioned centrally along the top boundary. The domain’s material properties, listed in Table 1, are used as input parameters for the simulation. Boundary conditions include vertical loading on the top surface (to represent lithostatic pressure) and horizontal confinement on the sides (simulating in-situ stress). The computational mesh comprises 30,000 quadrilateral elements, with a maximum element size of = 4 mm (Fig. S-1, supplementary material). No initial fractures are introduced in the domain. A mesh independency check is conducted by varying the maximum element size evaluating its effect on maximum fracture width and length over time as shown in Fig. S-1 (supplementary material). The simulations are particularly sensitive to the intrinsic length scale of the phase field model, denoted as , which governs the transition zone of damage. A sensitivity analysis for the model parameter , is performed and represented in Fig. S-2 (supplementary material). Following previous studies33, this parameter is set as /2. To accurately capture steep gradients—especially near the fluid injection point, where stress concentration is highest-a refined mesh is applied in that region. This mesh refinement is essential for enhancing solution accuracy. Non-uniform meshing is employed throughout the domain, a common strategy in numerical modelling to maintain low Courant numbers, promote solution stability, and ensure convergence of the simulation.

Experimental design and procedure

Materials

In this study, a custom-designed biaxial loading apparatus is utilized to explore the progression of fractures within a soft medium. The experimental material consists of commercially available gelatin derived from porcine skin, possessing a bloom strength in the range of 200–225 g, chosen for its elasticity and high optical clarity. To enhance both the transparency and mechanical strength of the gelatin matrix, sucrose is incorporated into the mixture. For the fluid injection tests, two types of dyed fluids are prepared: SAE 140 oil (procured from Reliance Industries Limited) blended with an oil-soluble dye (LOBAChemie), and distilled water tinted with liquid food colouring (PAPILON brand). These fluids allow for clear visualization of fracture paths. A standard 18-gauge butterfly needle (Top brand-winged infusion set) with a rounded (flat) tip is employed as the injection port (with a depth of 0.4 cm, inserted into sample) to deliver the fluids into the gelatin block, enabling controlled initiation and monitoring of fracture development.

Gelatin analogue

Gelatin has been widely utilized in earlier studies as an analogue material for investigating fluid-induced fractures, dyke intrusions, and magma-driven crack propagation35,37–43. Scaling these laboratory analogue models to field conditions is achievable through appropriate mathematical modelling techniques. The mechanical behaviour of gelatin is characterized using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM) in accordance with ASTM D638 standards44. Stress–strain data obtained from these tests are employed to determine the Young’s modulus of the gelatin samples (Fig. 1a). The Poisson’s ratio value (reported in Table 1) of the gelatin used in this study is assumed to be 0.45as we are using gelatin powder made from porcine skin from previous literature35,36 and was not measured directly in our experiments. Generally, for gelatin materials, Poisson’s ratio can be taken from 0.45 to 0.5. It should also be noted that other parameters, such as fracture toughness and viscoelastic relaxation characteristics, which are important for capturing the evolution of fracture, were not experimentally determined here but referred from previous literature35,45. Future work will be focused on the measurement of these parameters to further strengthen the accuracy of the fracture propagation studies in gelatin-based analogue models.

Sample preparation

A solid elastic matrix for homogeneous and heterogeneous media is prepared using various gelatin, water, and sucrose concentrations. Initially, we prepare the gelatin solution by heating water to 60 °C and slowly adding gelatin powder. Once gelatin powder is thoroughly mixed in the water, we add sucrose to enhance its transparency and strength. The solution is poured into a 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm mold to form a layer of the desired height and is then stored at 5 °C for approximately 14 h. We continue the process with different concentrations of gelatin in the solution, resulting in varying stiffness for the solidified gelatin layers. Since the mechanical properties of gelatin are sensitive to temperature, experiments are conducted at a controlled laboratory temperature of 22–25 °C.

Experimental test procedure and rheology analysis of the injected fluid

All components are mounted on an aluminium guide plate, featuring a hydraulic jack connected to the piston and capable of withstanding pressures up to 10 bar. Now, we attach the Perspex block to the piston and place it into the confinement plate, along with the weighing balance. The plate is movable, and the hydraulic jack attaches the hydraulic piston to the bottom.

The hydraulic jack, filled with hydraulic oil, has a capacity of 0.5 tons. The setup mechanism involves first inserting the sample into the Perspex block and then sealing it with a piston. Place this total compartment onto the weighing balance and then onto the confinement plate. The weighing balance (up to 150 kg) gives a reading of the total weight of that component. Now, connect the compressor line, as well as the fluid injection line, to the hydraulic press plate and piston.

Adjust the air pressure and fluid inlet flow rate accordingly. Using the handle of the hydraulic jack, pump the oil until the hydraulic piston begins to move the plate, ensuring that the piston connected to the fluid line comes into contact with the top confinement plate. Thus, the top load is applied to the sample through the piston movement, and the nylon plate with a needle is automatically placed into the sample to a depth of 0.4 cm, selected to ensure consistent fracture initiation across all experimental runs. Simultaneously, the weighing balance measures the top load while the hydraulic jack facilitates the piston movement. Ultimately, the fluid injection and loading conditions will fracture the sample, allowing two high-resolution cameras positioned at the setup side and front views to record the fracture as shown in Fig. 2. In the present experimental configuration, direct viewing or imaging of fracture evolution or propagation, is not feasible in top or bottom views, due to practical constraints associated with the loading frame, the sealing of the injection system, and the need to maintain stable biaxial boundary stresses during fluid injection. Two cameras were set up on the accessible other views (faces), enabling continuous visualization of fracture evolution and propagation over time throughout the experiment. Meanwhile, this setup cannot directly capture out-of-plane fracture growth in top- and bottom-view images. However, incorporating the utilization observations of other views, the subsequent anticipated three-dimensional fracture morphology, and complementary phase‑field simulations provides a robust framework for interpreting the dominant fracture propagation modes.

It may appear counter-intuitive here, that since the applied load on the top surface is the smallest; therefore, the normal direction of the horizontal plane corresponds to the minimum principal stress, which can typically would lead fracture propagation in the horizontal direction. However, it may be noted that the vertical loading magnitude is relative to the confined load. So, even if the top loading is apparently low, it is relatively not small compared to the confined loading.

Results and discussions

In this study, we used water, oil, and a mixture of oil and toluene as fracturing fluids at a constant flow rate of 1 ml/min under a continuous load of confining load of 1.23 bar and a top load of 0.52 bar in a biaxial loading system to determine fluid-driven fractures in various solid elastic mediums such as homogeneous, heterogeneous (layered) of dimensions 10 cm x 10 cm x 10 cm. The injection flow rate was fixed to 1 ml/min, as our main objective was to investigate fracture propagation in various heterogeneous media rather than studying the effect of flow rate. It is to be noted that initial trial runs performed with lower and higher flow rates are not reported here, which resulted in excessive fluid line pressures and unstable fracture formation, whereas at 1 ml/min, stable and reproducible conditions were ensured for all experimental conditions. Pressure was not measured during the experiments because, at these very low flow rates and with soft gelatin analogue materials, the recorded pressures were extremely low and negligible. Therefore, the comparison with numerical predictions focuses on fracture length, aperture, and propagation patterns. The experimental results and model predictions are validated against the classical Khristianovic–Geertsma–de Klerk (KGD) model47,48 for all the scenarios. The phase field model predictions agree with the KGD predictions, and details are presented in the Figs. S-3 through S-5 (supplementary material).

Observations across all experimental cases indicate that, under high confining and low top loading conditions, fracture propagation is more towards the lateral direction than the vertical growth, and fractures are arrested in the heterogeneous matrix. However, fracture growth is not governed by far‑field stress contrast alone at the laboratory scale. Due to the presence of free surface boundary conditions and the finite dimensions of the sample, leading to local stress redistribution and rotation of the principal stress directions, especially when the fracture dimensions become comparable to the sample thickness. The stresses induced near the boundaries within this framework, may locally dominate over the applied stress contrast and promote lateral fracture growth, even where the classical hydraulic fracturing theory would predict vertical propagation61,62. Accordingly, strong pore‑pressure gradients and stress concentrations near the injection inlet can play a key role in controlling the fracture initiation and propagation, leading to orientations governed by local stresses rather than far‑field stresses63,64. In such low absolute stress conditions, typical of laboratory experiments, fracture propagation may further evolve in a three-dimensional and mixed-mode manner such that the final observed fracture geometry may reflect the integrated fracture evolution rather than the initial propagation direction assumed in two-dimensional models65,66.

The selected high value of confining load has been chosen to ensure that when a significant top load is applied, the elastic matrix (gelatin) experiences deformation in the initial stages before fluid injection. This approach allows a better understanding of the material’s behaviour under stress. Earlier studies have reported that variations in elastic modulus within layered shales or laminated rocks tend to arrest fractures and promote lateral rather than vertical propagation49,50. Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrate that such heterogeneities in layered media significantly influence the final fracture length under biaxial loading conditions, particularly when injected with a high-viscosity fluid ( = 1050–2500 mPa·s). However, in homogeneous media, the fracture tends to be elliptical, particularly with the further injection of high- or low-viscosity fluid under biaxial loading. Similarly, previous research has shown similar circular shapes in resin and sandstone samples during biaxial fracture studies11.

The phase field model adequately captures the overall behaviour of fracture growth. Meanwhile, a few differences are observed compared to the experimental observations, specifically, cases involving low-viscosity fluids or high modulus layers, for example, in Fig. 10. These variations arise from layer heterogeneity, irregular fracture geometries at layer interfaces, the effect of fluid viscosity, and negligible experimental uncertainties. For example, low-viscosity fluids tend to accelerate fracture growth in low modulus layers or in the vertical confinement of high-viscosity cases, which in turn highlights the sensitivity of fracture propagation to local elastic contrasts and fluid–structure interactions. Generally, fluids with a high-viscosity nature tend to yield more stable fractures, whereas low-viscosity fluids often lead to irregular fractures, branching, or tip instabilities. Experimentally propagated fracture widths are different from numerical results due to complex, asymmetric fracture geometries and limitations in measuring width along the entire fracture length. Because of these variations in the fluid and elastic properties of the layered media, they influence fracture length, aperture, and propagation speed, and partially explaining the observed deviations between numerical simulations and experiments. These findings highlight that the developed fracture plays a dominant role in fluid viscosity and the applied stress conditions over material heterogeneity in governing fracture evolution. These findings enhance our understanding of fracture mechanics in layered media, enabling the design of effective hydraulic fracturing strategies and the development of predictive models suitable for geologically complex formations.

Impact of viscosity of the fluid in different elastic mediums

The produced fracture patterns explicitly demonstrate the mechanical interpretation of the differences between water, oil, and oil - toluene mixtures. The underlying mechanics of observed fracture patterns arise from the fluid’s viscosity, which helps shift the equilibrium between the fluid pressure distribution within the fracture, the resistance to fluid leak-off in the gelatin matrix under biaxial loading, and stress intensity around the fracture tip. Low-viscosity fluids, such as water, generate a high-pressure front that rapidly transfers pressure to the fracture and leads to a sharp increase in the Mode I stress intensity factor. These result in fast, unstable propagation, tip instabilities, and irregular or asymmetric geometries, as observed in the Figs. 3, 4 and 5. While high viscous fluids such as SAE 140 oil, on the other hand dissipates pressure gradients along the fracture length, reducing local pressure at the crack tip and thereby slowing the fracture front’s acceleration. Thus, it produces slower, more stable propagation, and more symmetric fracture shapes are observed in Figs. 6, 7 and 8. The intermediate viscosity of oil - toluene mixture exhibits transitional behaviour, the pressure front progress steadily, by avoiding the instabilities typical of low viscous fluids, resulting in smoother growth while remaining sensitive to modulus contrasts in layer stiffness (Figs. 9, 10 and 11). Under biaxial loading, as lateral confinement is high, viscous resistance delays the process of fluid injection into high modulus layers and promotes lateral fracture growth. Thus, viscosity plays a key role in whether fractures arrest at modulus interfaces or propagate through the layers. Overall, viscosity does not affect the rate of propagation, but it also actively changes the stress field near the tip, controlling the stability of fracture growth, and governing the balance between the vertical penetration and lateral deviation is more effective in heterogeneous media. These mechanical characteristics governs the redistribution of pressure and modify tip stresses, as reflected quantitatively in the temporal evolution of fracture length across all tested fluids and elastic media due to fluid viscosity. Comparison of the phase field modelling results with (modified) KGD model

The primary aim of this study is not to create a new numerical formulation, but rather to showcase the effectiveness of the phase field approach by calibrating and validating it against controlled hydrofracture experiments in layered gelatin models. Unlike earlier studies that mainly concentrated on uniform domains, our focus is specifically on utilizing experimental data from elastic anisotropic or layered heterogeneous analogue materials. To evaluate the predictive capability of the model, the simulated fracture propagation is compared with experimentally measured fracture length, aperture, and propagation time, and further across various elastic media, by providing a quantitative validation of the experimental results against the outcomes of phase field modelling.

Additionally, these results are evaluated in conjunction with the well-established Khristianovich-Geertsma-de Klerk (KGD) model47,48, as detailed in the supplementary material. The KGD formulation used here is only as a baseline reference for the fracture behaviour within each layer. Specifically, the Eq. (21) is not a classical KGD solution, it is a KGD inspired, layer-wise plain-strain scaling applied locally within each layer in the layered matrix, assuming homogeneous to each layer, viscosity- dominated analytical benchmark. The constraints of the experimental setup are broadly consistent with the assumptions underlying plane -strain conditions. KGD‑type model and its variants assume plane‑strain conditions, which are applicable for fractures propagating in laterally extensive and vertically confined media, rather than as a model intended to predict the fully evolved fractures in this study. Thus, the fractures develop with a large aspect ratio, so that opening deformation dominates over variations along the propagation direction, within each layer. However, the stiffness contrast between adjacent layers, together with lateral confinement, limits deformation normal to the layering. Consequently, the fracture system as a whole evolves in three- dimensional form; in-plane stresses govern the mechanical response within the interior of each layer. In this context, the KGD‑based scaling is mainly used as a reference for comparing phase field simulations, rather than as an attempt to reproduce the full three-dimensional complexity that develops near layer boundaries. Comparing the KGD model to the phase field simulation, we observed that the model is unable to capture layer heterogeneity after fracture propagation has occurred. At present, it is not feasible to further validate the viscosity-dominated or toughness-dominated regimes. The irregular fracture geometry and anisotropic layered structure of the gelatin medium violate the assumptions of these analytical models. Still, the KGD-based benchmarking provides a reliable basis for assessing the accuracy of the phase field model for fracture evolution in our analogue system. Integrating layered material heterogeneity, fluid–structure interaction, and direct experimental validation offers new insight into hydrofracture growth in soft geological analogues. This approach establishes a modelling strategy that is both quantitatively benchmarked and experimentally grounded.

Relevance of the findings to field-scale and engineering applications

The outcomes of the current study demonstrate the role of fluid viscosity and heterogeneity of the gelatin analogue mediums for fracture propagation under biaxial stress in controlled laboratory conditions, relevant to real-world applications. For instance, in the CO₂ sequestration scenario, the phase change of CO₂ in the reservoir (from gaseous to supercritical state) results in a substantial reduction in fluid viscosity (from ~ 0.02–0.1 mPa·s)51,52, which is considerably lower than the SAE 140 oil used in our experiments. Despite this difference, the observed fracture behaviour with high viscous oil, such as fracture growth, direction, and impact of elastic heterogeneity, can still provide qualitative insights into how lower-viscosity fluids behave at the field scale. In shale gas reservoirs, natural fractures play an important role in the propagation of fluid-driven fractures through layered section53 with pre-existing fractures. However, our current experiments on intact gelatin layers do not capture these interactions. This discussion frames our findings relevant to CO₂ sequestration and shale gas development scenarios by highlighting their applicability and limitations. Future work will be focused on pre-fractured anisotropic analogue media to mimic actual field-scale conditions and further bridge the gap between laboratory-scale observations and engineering applications.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrates the capabilities of a customized low-pressure biaxial loading system for observing the dynamic evolution of fluid-driven fractures in analogue materials, providing valuable insights into fracture mechanics under controlled stress conditions. With real-time monitoring and 3D visualization, the biaxial loading system offers a powerful tool for capturing fracture propagation, enhancing our understanding of fracture behaviour under biaxial stress regimes. Following are the key outcomes of the study.

The experiments highlight the contrasting effects of fluid viscosity on fracture dynamics: water, with its low viscosity, promotes rapid and linear fracture propagation, while oil, a high-viscosity fluid, induces slower, more intricate fracture patterns. We observe the same behaviour when we use a mixture of oil and toluene.
