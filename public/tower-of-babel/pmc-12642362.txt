Capillary leak syndrome in sepsis: the role of intrinsic coagulation pathway activation
Authors: Li Shu, Jin-Lian Jiang, Yun-Fen Chen, Ya Chen, Huan Chen, Ying-Hua Chen, Wei-Wei Zhong, Zhong-Qin Pan, Xue Xiao, Yi-Huai He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coagulation system is closely linked to the inflammatory response and kinin system activation in sepsis, but its contribution to sepsis-associated capillary leak syndrome (SACLS) remains elusive. This study explored the underlying role of the coagulation system in SACLS.MethodsPatients with sepsis were recruited to evaluate the early predictive potential of coagulation function-related indicators for capillary leakage. Patients with hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) and scrub typhus, conditions featuring SACLS as a typical manifestation, were selected to validate the predictive value of coagulation-related indicators in SACLS. The levels of coagulation factors in patients with sepsis were analyzed to elucidate the altered mechanisms in the coagulation system. Additionally, rat SACLS models were established via an intravenous injection of lipopolysaccharide to corroborate the changes and potential effects of the coagulation system during SACLS.ResultsProthrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) exhibited predictive potential for diagnosing capillary leak syndrome in patients with sepsis. APTT displayed higher sensitivity than PT in detecting SACLS at an early stage. Compared with the findings in patients with sepsis and normal APTT, the activities of coagulation factor Ⅺ (FXI) and FXII in plasma and levels of complement 3 and 4 in serum were significantly decreased in patients with APTT prolongation (all P < 0.05) and negatively correlated with APTT prolongation (all P < 0.05). FXI and FXII activities were decreased in the plasma of SACLS model rats (P < 0.001). Conversely, FXII and complement levels were elevated in the lungs (both P < 0.05), accompanied by elevated bradykinin levels (P < 0.001).ConclusionsAPTT is a sensitive indicator for predicting SACLS. APTT prolongation in plasma might be attributable to the enrichment of FXII in the microcirculation. The microcirculatory enrichment of FXII might activate the kinin and complement systems, contributing to the process of capillary leakage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12069-w.Keywords: Activated partial thromboplastin time, Sepsis-associated capillary leak syndrome, Coagulation factor XII, Bradykinin, Complement system

Background

Sepsis refers to life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated immune response to severe infection by bacteria, viruses, fungi, and other pathogens [1]. In 2017, sepsis affected nearly 50 million people globally, resulting in approximately 11 million deaths. The mortality rate of sepsis is nearly 20%, representing a major global public health problem [2]. Sepsis is a common trigger of capillary leak syndrome (CLS), termed sepsis-associated capillary leak syndrome (SACLS). CLS, characterized by systemic edema, hypoalbuminemia, hypotension, and hemoconcentration, results from increased vascular permeability secondary to endothelial injury [3]. In SACLS, bacterial toxins and inflammatory mediators inflict widespread endothelial damage, enabling the leakage of intravascular fluid and proteins into tissues. These changes results in refractory hypotension, hypovolemic shock, and ischemic end-organ dysfunction [4]. Early recognition and prompt intervention are crucial to reducing the mortality associated with SACLS; however, identifying SACLS in its initial stages remains a clinical challenge.

CLS progresses through three stages, namely prodromal, leakage, and recovery, but the prodromal stage in SACLS might be obscured by sepsis. The observable course of SACLS can be roughly divided into two stages. In the initial leakage phase, impaired capillary integrity causes the rapid extravasation of plasma into the interstitial space [5]. During this stage, associated complications are frequent, including compartment syndrome (50%), acute renal failure (14%), thrombosis/pulmonary embolism (14%), cardiac tamponade (9%) [6], and myocardial and cerebral edema [7, 8]. The recovery stage commences 48 h to 1 week after the onset of shock, during which capillary repair halts further leakage and the exuded fluid is reabsorbed back into the circulation. Consequently, early identification is crucial for timely intervention and the prevention of mortality in SACLS.

Two pathways facilitate the transfer of intravascular substances into the interstitial space: transcellular and paracellular. Capillary leakage occurs predominantly through the paracellular route. The vascular endothelium consists of three components: endothelial glycocalyx (EGL), endothelial cells, and intercellular junctions. The vascular endothelium plays a vital role in maintaining vascular integrity and barrier function, regulating vascular tone and permeability, modulating immune responses, and inhibiting thrombosis. Both excessive inflammation and coagulation dysfunction can impair the integrity of the vascular endothelium, compromising vascular integrity [9]. In sepsis, bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and inflammatory mediators disrupt the EGL. For instance, reactive oxygen species (ROS) cause increased cellular oxidative stress, triggering inflammation and vascular damage. Degradation of structural barriers such as the EGL, exposure of protein substances on the vascular endothelial basement membrane, platelet activation, and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps can activate factor XII (FXII) into its active form FXIIa, thereby activating factor XI (FXI) and a cascade of coagulation factors. These processes results in the full activation of the intrinsic coagulation pathway and thrombus formation. Furthermore, FXIIa interacts with the kinin system, forming a positive feedback loop that leads to the sustained activation of endogenous coagulation and kinin systems and increases bradykinin (BK) release. This promotes the inflammatory response, vasodilation, and vascular permeability [10]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), the levels of which are elevated during inflammation, disrupts endothelial junctions, destabilizes blood vessels, and further increases permeability [11, 12]. In the presence of high VEGF levels, angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) promotes endothelial activation, antagonizing the barrier-stabilizing effects of Ang-1 [13]. These pathways increase vascular leakage together in SACLS [14].

In summary, sepsis can progress to SACLS, causing shock and potential death through complex mechanisms. However, the early clinical diagnosis of SACLS remains challenging because of a lack of effective diagnostic modalities. The current gold standard involves quantifying vascular permeability changes using exogenous markers such as radiolabeled albumin (ALB) or bioimpedance measurements. Although accurate, these techniques are impractical for widespread adoption because of their procedural complexity and cost. The diagnosis of idiopathic CLS is based on refractory hypotension, hemoconcentration, and hypoalbuminemia excluding other secondary factors. By contrast, SACLS in critically ill patients such as those with sepsis is more complex, and currently, there is no unified diagnostic standard. Symptoms of intravascular hypovolemia, peripheral edema, and hemodynamic instability are clinically diagnosed as CLS [15]. Theoretically, when capillary leakage occurs, hemoglobin (HGB) levels should increase. However, patients with sepsis often experience anemia attributable to various factors, including chronic diseases, nutritional deficiencies, inflammatory processes, hemolysis, thrombosis, acute massive transfusion, inhibition of erythropoietin production, reduced bone marrow responsiveness, and drug reactions. Consequently, HGB levels can vary significantly [16–18]. Prior research suggested that, based on the increase in hematocrit (HCT) levels and decrease in ALB levels observed in capillary leakage, an increase in the difference between HCT and ALB levels (HCT-ALB) might reflect the severity of capillary leakage in sepsis. A high HCT-ALB might better diagnose severe infectious diseases, and it is associated with mortality risk in older patients [19–24]. Identifying simple biomarkers to predict SACLS has important clinical significance for reducing the mortality rate associated with this condition.

Coagulation abnormalities are common in sepsis, manifesting as subclinical activation or diffuse thrombotic–hemorrhagic complications. Disseminated intravascular coagulation, doubling mortality risk, occurs in 30%–50% of patients with severe sepsis [25]. Endothelial damage, inflammation, and coagulation factor depletion underlie sepsis-associated coagulopathy [26, 27]. Bacterial components prompt tissue factor (TF) expression on monocytes and endothelial cells, triggering extrinsic pathway activation [28, 29]. Reduced activity of anticoagulant substances, such as protein C, protein S, thrombomodulin, antithrombin, and TF pathway inhibitor, also promotes the formation of microthrombi [30–34]. Neutrophil extracellular traps can hydrolyze inter-endothelial junctions, thereby exacerbating permeability and inducing TF expression [35, 36]. The generation of thrombin leads to endothelial cell activation and the release of von Willebrand factor, which promotes platelet (PLT) adhesion and aggregation, prevents the inactivation of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), and promotes thrombosis [37].

The inextricable relationship between disordered coagulation and endothelial dysfunction likely influences vascular leakage in sepsis, but the specific mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated the relationship between coagulation abnormalities and capillary leakage in sepsis through three complementary approaches. First, we conducted a retrospective analysis to explore coagulation parameters as predictive indicators of SACLS. Additionally, we selected patients with hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) and scrub typhus to verify the predictive value of coagulation function-related indicators in SACLS. Second, by detecting changes in coagulation factor levels in patients with sepsis, we examined the potential mechanism underlying coagulation function and capillary leakage. Finally, we employed an LPS-induced rat model to delineate the mechanism of coagulation function in the occurrence of SACLS, thereby exploring the predictive utility and underlying role of the coagulation system in SACLS.

Methods

Clinical research

Cohort 1: 356 patients with sepsis with or without CLS

To investigate the relationship between relevant indicators and capillary leakage in patients with sepsis, we enrolled 457 patients with sepsis and clear etiologies (presence of fever symptoms, positive blood culture for pathogens or positive for hantavirus and Orientia scrub typhus antibodies) who were admitted to the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University and Suining Central Hospital in Sichuan Province between March 2017 and March 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 18 years or older at the time of admission, confirmed sepsis (presence of fever symptoms and positive pathogen test), and complete clinical data. We excluded 101 patients based on the following criteria: pregnancy, poisoning, anticoagulation treatment within 1 week prior to admission, hematological or other malignant tumors, severe trauma or bleeding disorders, allergic or immunological disease, and severe chronic liver, kidney, or heart failure. The remaining 356 patients (163 men and 193 women) were included in the study. Patients were divided into the CLS and non-CLS groups according to the presence of capillary leakage symptoms (edema, hypovolemia, systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg) during hospitalization.

We collected the following data for all patients at the time of admission: age, sex, pathogens, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and signs of CLS (such as progressive systemic and mucosal edema and serosal effusion). We also measured the following blood test indicators at the time of admission: HGB, HCT, PLTs, ALB, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen (FIB), d-dimer, capillary leakage index (CLI) [the ratio of C-reactive protein (mg/dL) to albumin (g/L) × 100], and HCT-ALB.

Cohort 2: 44 patients with HFRS or scrub typhus

A specific subset of 44 patients from Cohort 1 had either HFRS (n = 30) or scrub typhus (n = 14). As these conditions characteristically feature SACLS as a typical manifestation, this subgroup was selected to validate the predictive value of coagulation-related indicators specifically for SACLS. In addition to the standard data collected for Cohort 1, SOFA scores were recorded for these 44 patients at the time of admission (assessed at different stages of the disease course upon admission). The SOFA score is a widely used clinical score that assesses the extent of organ dysfunction based on six organ systems: respiratory, coagulation, liver, cardiovascular, central nervous, and renal. Furthermore, dynamic measurements of PT and APTT were performed throughout the course of fever progression in these patients. Given the availability of complete SOFA score data on admission, we analyzed the correlation between the SOFA score and APTT at admission.

Cohort 3: 49 patients with sepsis for coagulation factor analysis

A separate cohort of 49 patients with sepsis was recruited specifically to measure coagulation factor activity. These patients were hospitalized in the Department of Infectious Diseases of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University and the Department of Infectious Diseases of Suining Central Hospital in Sichuan Province from May 2023 to October 2023. Patients 18 years or older who fulfilled the 2016 Sepsis 3.0 sepsis diagnostic criteria for sepsis, which includes the presence of infection and SOFA ≥ 2 points, were included [1]. We exclude patients meeting any of the following criteria: younger than 18 years, pregnancy, poisoning, anticoagulant therapy within 1 week prior to admission, hematological or other malignancies, severe trauma or bleeding disorders, allergic or immunological diseases, and severe chronic liver, kidney, or heart failure. We divided the patients into two groups based on the presence of APTT prolongation. We collected the following data from all patients at the time of admission: age, sex, respiratory rate, SOFA scores, signs of CLS (such as progressive systemic and mucosal edema and serosal effusion), PT, APTT, FIB, d-dimer, coagulation factor activities (FII, FV, FVII, FVIII, FIX, FX, FXI, FXII), complement 3 (C3), and C4. This clinical study was approved by the medical ethics committee of our hospital (KYLLKS20230119), and informed consent was obtained from the patients prior to the collection of blood samples.

Animal model and treatment

Healthy male Sprague-Dawley rats (specific pathogen-free, 6–8 weeks old, 250 ± 30 g) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [SCXK (Jing) 2019-0008]. The rats were housed in a pathogen-free facility with a controlled temperature of 20–24 °C, unlimited access to food and water, and a 12-h/12-h light/dark cycle. After 1 week of acclimation, the rats were randomly assigned to different experimental groups using a random number table. Unless otherwise noted, at least 12 Sprague-Dawley rats per group were used for all studies. All animal studies were approved by the Animal Laboratory Studies Ethics Review Committee of Zunyi Medical University (zyfy-an-2023-0227).

We randomly divided 96 healthy Sprague-Dawley rats into the sham control groups (rats injected with saline; 0, 24, 48, and 72 h; n = 12/time point, total of 48 rats) and experimental groups (0, 24, 48, and 72 h; n = 12/time point, total of 48 rats). The size of the cages was 595 × 435 × 200 mm. Prior to injection, rats were deprived of food and water for 6 h. The rats in the experimental groups were injected with LPS (3.0 mg/kg) via the tail vein to induce SACLS. At 24, 48, and 72 h after injection, an intraperitoneal injection of 5% pentobarbital sodium (5 mg/kg; specification: 0.1 g per vial, batch number: 20230116, approval number: National Medical Products Administration H31021725, Shanghai Xinya Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) was performed to induce anesthesia. After successful anesthesia, the peripheral blood of rats was collected to test coagulation function; plasma coagulation factor activity; BK, complement, HGB, and ALB levels; PLT counts, and other indicators. Lung tissue was collected for HE staining pathology examination. After tissue collection, euthanasia was performed using the cervical dislocation method.

HGB and HCT levels and PLT counts

A fully automated blood analyzer (XN-9000, Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) was used to determine HGB and HCT levels and PLT counts.

ALB and inflammatory marker levels

ALB levels were measured using a fully automated blood analyzer (AU5800, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). CRP and PCT levels were quantified using fully automated specific protein analyzers (PA300, Genrui, Shenzhen, China; VIDAS® B.R.A.H.M.S PCT, bioMérieux, Lyon, France).

BK and complement levels

The BK content in rat serum and lung tissue was assessed using the BK Content Detection Kit (ZC-36714, Shanghai Zhuocai Biotechnology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. An enzyme immunoassay reader (Shanghai Gene Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the absorbance of BK at 450 nm, and the serum BK content was calculated according to the kit instructions. Serum complement levels were determined using the Complement 3/4 Detection Kit (MACCURA Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Chengdu, China). Rat C3a levels in lung tissue were measured using the Rat Complement Fragment 3 A (C3a) ELISA Kit (RA20070, Bioswamp Life Science Lab, Wuhan, China).

Coagulation parameters

Coagulation indicators, including PT, TT, APTT, FIB, and d-dimer, were assessed using a coagulation method and automatic coagulation analyzers (STARMax, Stago, Asnières-sur-Seine, France; CN-5100, Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). Plasma samples collected in 3.2% sodium citrate anticoagulated tubes (Siemens, Munich, Germany; blood-to-anticoagulant ratio = 9:1) were used to measure the activities of FII, FV, FVII, FVIII, FIX, FX, FXI, and FXII (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics products, Germany).

Lung histopathological analysis

Lung tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The stained sections were imaged using a panoramic slide scanner, and the images were analyzed using Case Viewer v2.2 software (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

Determination of the lung wet/dry weight ratio

After euthanasia under anesthesia, the right lung was excised, rinsed with saline, blotted dry on filter paper, and weighed (wet weight). The lung tissue was then dried in an oven at 80 °C until constant weight was achieved, after which it was reweighed (dry weight). The wet/dry weight ratio was calculated as the quotient of wet weight over dry weight.

Determination of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cell differentials and total protein concentration

Rats were euthanized under anesthesia and secured in a supine position. After alcohol disinfection of the furred area, a midline incision was created to expose the trachea, bronchi, and both lungs. The right main bronchus and heart were ligated with suture silk. A No. 7 sterile medical catheter needle was inserted into the upper trachea and secured, its tip was positioned at the opening of the left main bronchus. The inner stylet was withdrawn, and warmed (37℃) saline (0.8 mL per lavage, repeated 2–3 times) was slowly injected via a syringe to perform unilateral lavage of the left lung. The recovery of BALF exceeded 80%. Following collection, BALF was centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 0.5 mL of PBS. Cell counts were determined using an automated cell counter. Supernatants were analyzed for total protein content via the bicinchoninic acid assay using a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Lung tissue protein expression

Western blotting was conducted according to standard procedures using the lysates of lung tissue samples from rats. The tissues were solubilized in lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China). To determine the protein concentration, 40 µg of each sample were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Then, the membranes were incubated with FXII antibody (PA5-116703, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) overnight or for 5 h. The relative protein expression was reflected as the gray value of the proteins, and anti-mouse β-actin primary antibody (sc-81178; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) was used as the internal control. Images were captured using a gel documentation system (ChemiScope 6000, Clinx, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Count data were expressed as numbers (%) and compared using the χ2 test. Normality tests and homogeneity of variance analysis were conducted for the data in each group. Measurement data with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and compared using an independent-samples t-test and ANOVA. Measurement data with a non-normal distribution were expressed as the median (interquartile range) and compared using a non-parametric (Mann–Whitney U) test. The correlation of grade data was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was used to analyze the sensitivity and specificity of coagulation indicators in predicting capillary leakage, and the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of cohort 1 patients

Of 356 patients with sepsis included in the study, the median age was 61 (48, 73) years. To standardize the timeline across all participants regardless of the hospital admission date or study enrollment, day 1 was consistently defined as the day of fever onset for each patient. At baseline, age and fever duration did not significantly differ between the CLS (n = 121) and non-CLS groups (n = 235). The main pathogens were gram-negative bacteria (57.30%), followed by gram-positive bacteria (25.56%), viruses and other pathogens (13.48%), and fungi (3.65%). Escherichia coli was the most common pathogen in both groups, and there was no significant difference in the pathogen distribution between the two groups (P > 0.05, χ2 = 6.021, Table 1). However, the SOFA score was significantly higher in the CLS group than in the non-CLS group (P = 0.009).

Differences in experimental indicators of cohort 1 patients

To account for the variation in the normal ranges of APTT and PT across different devices, this study analyzed the difference of each parameter (APTT and PT) from the upper limit of its respective normal reference standard, hereafter termed the APTT D-value and PT D-value. The CLS group exhibited significantly higher APTT and PT D-values than the non-CLS group (both P < 0.001). Notably, although the non-CLS group had negative D-values for both PT and APTT, indicating a relative shortening of clotting times, these measured values consistently remained within the established normal reference ranges. Furthermore, the CLS group also had significantly higher d-dimer levels (P = 0.006), and a larger HCT-ALB than the non-CLS group (P < 0.001). Conversely, the CLS group had significantly lower FIB (P = 0.004), HGB (P = 0.006), ALB (P < 0.001), and HCT levels (P = 0.006) than the non-CLS group. PLT counts (P = 0.164), PCT (P = 0.891) and CRP levels (P = 0.755), and CLI (P = 0.129) did not differ between the CLS and non-CLS groups (Table 2).

We used ROC curves to evaluate the predictive value of coagulation indicators (APTT D-value, PT D-value, d-dimer, HCT-ALB, and combined PT and APTT) for CLS. The optimal cutoff for each parameter was determined by analyzing the ROC curve and selecting the threshold corresponding to the highest Youden index (Fig. 1).

Coagulation indicators and their predictive value for cohort 2 patients

We analyzed the changes in APTT and PT over time in patients with HFRS (n = 30) and scrub typhus (n = 14) to assess their relationships with the occurrence of CLS (Tables 4 and 5). We obtained the change curves through median analysis. APTT initially increased during early time points, normalized on day 7, and subsequently increased again at later time points. PT prolongation manifested only 7 days after fever development. Patients with CLS had a significantly longer APTT than those without CLS. the median APTT prolongation in patients with CLS 3–6 days after onset was 8.15 s. PT prolongation was not obvious in the early stage, and it only became significant after 10 days of fever. Therefore, PT was not as sensitive as APTT for predicting CLS in the early stage (Fig. 2A, B). Analysis of the correlations among APTT, PT and SOFA scores in this patient cohort revealed significantly positive correlations of both APTT (r = 0.700, P < 0.001) and PT (r = 0.497, P = 0.001) with SOFA at admission (Fig. 2C, D). The correlation was stronger for APTT than for PT. Furthermore, a stronger correlation between APTT and SOFA scores was observed during the first 6 days after disease onset than over the 23-day period (r = 0.735, P < 0.001, Fig. 2E). By contrast, no significant enhancement of the correlation between PT and SOFA scores was detected when limiting analyses to the initial 6-day window versus the full 23-day observation period (r = 0.503, P = 0.003; Fig. 2F).

Potential mechanisms of APTT changes in cohort 3

Forty-nine hospitalized patients with sepsis were selected, including 31 men and 18 women aged 18–94 years (median, 59 years). The median SOFA score was 2 (2, 5). Patients were then stratified into two groups based on the presence or absence of APTT prolongation. No significant differences were observed regarding sex, age, or fever duration between the two patient groups. Patients in the prolongation group (45.45%, 10/22) had a higher incidence of CLS than those in the non-prolongation group (18.52%, 5/27, P = 0.042). We measured coagulation function based on coagulation factor activities (FII, FV, FVII, FVIII, FIX, FX, FXI, FXII) and complement levels (C3, C4) in this cohort.

The extension of APTT was positively correlated with the SOFA score (r = 0.426, P = 0.002, Fig. 3A). FXII activity was significantly reduced in patients with sepsis, whereas the activities of coagulation factors and complements were not significantly changed (Table 6). APTT had significant negative correlations with FXI and FXII activities and C3 and C4 levels (r = − 0.362, − 0.355, − 0.605, and − 0.377, respectively, all P < 0.05) but not with FII, FV, FVII, FVIII, FIX, and FX activities (P > 0.05, Fig. 3B, C).

Discussion

In sepsis, endothelial injury and inflammatory response caused by pathogenic microorganisms promote the activation of exogenous and endogenous coagulation pathways. The activation of FXII in the intrinsic coagulation pathway promotes the activation of the complement and kinin systems, thereby aggravating inflammation. Therefore, the coagulation system is involved in the pathogenesis of sepsis, but its contribution to SACLS remains elusive. This study explored the predictive value and mechanistic role of the coagulation system in SACLS. The findings revealed that prolonged PT and APTT were associated with leakage and shock in SACLS, with APTT demonstrating superior predictive ability. By analyzing coagulation factor activity in patients with sepsis, prolonged APTT was found to be related to reduced FXII and FXI activities in plasma. In the SACLS rat model, prolonged plasma APTT was also associated with decreased FXII activity. However, FXII accumulated in microvessels, and local BK and C3a levels were increased. Collectively, these findings suggest that APTT is an early indicator of impending vascular leakage and hypoperfusion in sepsis. The prolongation of APTT might be attributable to the enrichment of FXII in capillaries, and the locally enriched FXII in capillaries might increase vascular permeability by activating the kinin and complement systems.

Microvascular endothelial dysfunction is the fundamental link in the occurrence of CLS, as confirmed by numerous studies [38]. The substantial amounts of cytokines and inflammatory mediators produced in patients with sepsis damage capillary endothelial cells and disrupt intercellular connections, leading to the destruction of vascular barrier function and a significant increase in capillary permeability. Protein-rich fluid leaks from the intravascular space to the interstitial space, causing a decrease in intravascular colloid osmotic pressure and an increase in interstitial colloid osmotic pressure. Consequently, more water is retained within tissues, resulting in interstitial edema. In addition to cytokines, coagulation disorders play a crucial role in the development of multiple-organ failure, and they are closely associated with mortality [25]. Modern medicine suggests that the main pathways leading to sepsis-related coagulation disorders involve inflammatory cells, coagulation activation, and vascular endothelial damage [39]. In this study, we analyzed the correlations between coagulation indicators and capillary leakage in patients with sepsis and found that PT and APTT have good predictive value for microcirculatory capillary leakage. When analyzing the changes of PT and APTT over time in sepsis through HFRS and scrub typhus, the prolongation of APTT appeared earlier, lasted longer, and recovered more slowly than that of PT. This might reflect an imbalance in the intrinsic coagulation pathway that predominates in the early stages of SACLS. Some studies indicated that APTT is more markedly prolonged in the high-lactate group in sepsis, and the mortality of sepsis and septic shock is closely related to hyperlactatemia [20, 40]. In the SACLS rat model, APTT was prolonged in the experimental group at 24 h, consistent with clinical data.

As an acute-phase reactant, FIB promotes permeability and coagulation by binding to cellular integrins. Elevated FIB levels are associated with adverse outcomes in sepsis [41]. FIB is continuously activated into fibrin under the action of thrombin, which can lead to decreased FIB levels in the presence of impaired liver synthesis. Our study found that patients with capillary leakage had significantly lower FIB levels than those without leakage, possibly reflecting increased consumption. d-dimer, a fibrin degradation product, is elevated in the presence of recent and ongoing intravascular coagulation and fibrinolysis. Some studies suggested that d-dimer levels > 2.0 µg/mL on admission are associated with increased mortality and that they should be included in the sepsis risk stratification [42]. Although d-dimer is related to leakage, our study found that its predictive performance was lower than those of PT and APTT, which might be related to the increase in coagulation and inhibition of fibrinolysis in early sepsis [43]. Inflammatory activation is an important mechanism of capillary damage. The strength of the inflammatory response largely determines the degree of capillary endothelial damage and level of leakage [44]. However, in our study, we found that the levels of the inflammatory markers PCT and CRP did not differ significantly between the CLS and non-CLS groups. Some studies also found that CRP and PCT have poor predictive performance for 30-day all-cause mortality in sepsis [45]. PLTs have a hemostatic effect, and they can induce endothelial permeability by adhering to endothelial cells, releasing extracellular vesicles rich in inflammatory factors, and activating inflammasomes [46]. However, our study found no significant correlation between reduced PLT counts and vascular leakage in sepsis. PLT counts in sepsis often exhibit a biphasic pattern characterized by an initial decrease during the first 1–4 days followed by an increase. The lack of this biphasic response results in persistent thrombocytopenia, which is associated with increased 28-day mortality. Therefore, serial monitoring of biphasic PLT trends has better prognostic implications [47]. The changes in PLT counts in rats in our study verify this view.

PT reflects the activity of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, mainly involving FVII, FX, FV, and FII. In sepsis, the activation of TF causes increased consumption of FVII and prolongation of PT. However, the reactive release of TF pathway inhibitors can partially alleviate the prolongation of PT. Conversely, increased APTT reflects the activity of the intrinsic coagulation pathway, primarily involving FVIII, FIX, FXI, and FXII. Although FXII deficiency does not increase the risk of bleeding, it has outstanding immunomodulatory properties, and it is closely associated with inflammation and activation of the kinin system.

Inflammation triggers the intrinsic coagulation cascade through FXII binding to endothelial receptors and neutrophil extracellular traps. FXIIa promotes the BK and kallikrein production, stimulating inflammation through positive feedback that is not controlled by normal surface regulation. Similarly, in HFRS, both FXII-dependent contact activation and complement activation increase vascular permeability, and APTT is elevated before antibodies can be detected [48]. Delayed PT prolongation is attributable to progressive inflammation leading to thrombosis, the consumption of coagulation factors, and decreased synthesis of coagulation factors because of liver involvement. Thus, early APTT prolongation heralds impending leakage and shock, whereas late PT prolongation parallels disease severity. The study found that compared to PT, APTT displayed better sensitivity and specificity in predicting hypocoagulability in sepsis and septic shock [49]. In this study, the analysis of plasma coagulation factor activity in patients with sepsis and rats with SACLS revealed reductions in FXI and FXII activities, suggesting that the prolongation of APTT in ASCLS might be caused by the decreased circulating activities of FXI and FXII.

FXII occupies a critical position interfacing coagulation, fibrinolysis, inflammation, and immunity. Although it appears dispensable for hemostasis, pathological FXII overactivity triggers hereditary angioedema attacks via uncontrolled BK production [50]. Negatively charged surfaces promote the activation of the intrinsic coagulation pathway via auto-activated FXIIa during infection [51, 52]. FXIIa elicits vasodilation, permeability, and swelling alongside prothrombotic and proinflammatory consequences. Kallikrein reciprocally activates FXII and cleaves C3 to intersect with complement pathways also driving inflammation in sepsis [53–55]. We observed that FXI, FXII, and complement were more rapidly consumed in patients with sepsis and prolonged APTT. It was expected that in severe FXII deficiency, BK and anaphylatoxin levels would be reduced, leading to reduced inflammation and leakage. However, the continued decreases in PLT counts and ALB levels, inflammatory cell infiltration in lung tissue, alveolar hemorrhage and exudation, and interstitial edema confirmed the aggravation of inflammation and leakage. The increased levels of FXII, C3a, and BK in the lung tissue of rats in the LPS injection group suggest that FXII activates the complement and kinin systems in the local microcirculation, exacerbating inflammation and vascular leakage. It is evident that although the level of FXII in the circulation is low, its accumulation in microvessels might aggravate local pathology [56]. In acute respiratory distress syndrome, FXIIa and BK are locally enriched in pulmonary capillaries and are associated with mortality [57].

Overall, extensive interactions among the contact system, coagulation, inflammation, and immunity might be involved in the pathogenesis of sepsis. Elucidating the drivers and significance of pathological FXII redistribution might reveal therapeutic strategies to stabilize uncontrolled inflammation and vascular leakage.

Regarding the limitations of this research, this study was a retrospective analysis with a limited sample size and potential selection bias. The early predictive value of coagulation indicators, especially APTT, for capillary leakage and shock in sepsis requires further verification by prospective studies with larger sample sizes. However, clinicians should closely monitor APTT prolongation in patients with sepsis for comprehensive assessment and timely intervention.

Conclusion

APTT is a sensitive indicator for predicting SACLS. The prolongation of APTT in plasma might be attributable to the enrichment of FXII in the microcirculation, resulting in a decrease in its plasma levels. The microcirculatory enrichment of FXII might activate the kinin and complement systems, contributing to the process of capillary leakage.
