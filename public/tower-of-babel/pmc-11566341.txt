A phase III study to evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of fasinumab in patients with pain due to osteoarthritis of the knee or hip
Authors: Stephen J. DiMartino, Jingning Mei, Thomas J. Schnitzer, Haitao Gao, Simon Eng, Christine Winslow, Tina Ho, Kenneth C. Turner, Hazem E. Hassan, Yamini Patel, John D. Davis, Ngan Trinh, Angela Manley, Garen Manvelian, Michael Fetell, Ned Braunstein, Gregory P. Geba, Paula Dakin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPain associated with osteoarthritis (OA) is frequently disabling; treatments are often ineffective or intolerable. Fasinumab selectively inhibits nerve-growth factor and has shown efficacy for the management of OA pain.MethodsIn this randomized, double-blind, phase III safety study, patients with moderate-to-severe OA pain and history of inadequate pain relief received placebo or fasinumab (at 1, 3, 6, and 9 ​mg every 4 weeks [Q4W] and 1 and 6 ​mg every 8 weeks [Q8W] for 52 weeks). Primary safety endpoints included adverse events, adjudicated arthropathies (AAs), and joint replacements (JRs). Co-primary endpoints of an efficacy sub-study were change in Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) pain and physical function scores. During the study, higher fasinumab doses were discontinued for safety; 1 ​mg doses continued.ResultsOf 13,945 patients screened, 5331 were randomized; 1074 were included in the efficacy sub-study. AAs and JRs occurred in all groups. Increased severity of disease at baseline was associated with higher rates of AAs and JRs. A dose-dependent risk of AA or JR was observed for fasinumab; in the 1 ​mg groups, only a small percentage of patients with JR had prior AA. Fasinumab significantly improved WOMAC pain and physical function scores compared with placebo; least squares mean differences versus placebo were −1.22 and ​−1.20 for 1 ​mg Q4W and −0.73 and ​−0.74 for 1 ​mg Q8W, respectively (P<0.001).ConclusionAAs and JRs showed a dose relationship to fasinumab and were associated with baseline OA status. Fasinumab achieved statistically significant improvements in WOMAC pain and physical function scores compared with placebo.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, progressive disease manifested by breakdown of articular cartilage, meniscal damage, subchondral bone changes, osteophyte formation, and synovitis. Knees and hips are commonly affected [1]. OA pain can become chronic and disabling, reducing function and negatively impacting quality of life [[1], [2], [3]].

Currently available analgesics often do not provide adequate pain relief or are limited by toxicity/intolerability [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the mainstay of treatment in mild-to-moderate OA, but their use is limited not only by common toxicities like gastrointestinal distress and hypertension, but also by serious, sometimes life-threatening effects like gastrointestinal bleeding as well as cardiovascular and renal complications [8]. Opioids have been used when NSAIDs are ineffective/intolerable. However, side effects particularly in older patients, can be problematic, causing drowsiness, dizziness, and motor imbalance, increasing the risk of falls; misuse and abuse are also serious concerns [9,10]. Guidelines now recommend against strong opioids to manage chronic OA pain [11].

Nerve growth factor mediates pain signaling by binding to its receptors on nociceptive neurons [12]. Fasinumab, an investigational, recombinant, human, immunoglobulin G4 isotype monoclonal antibody, inhibits nerve growth factor by preventing engagement with its receptor [13,14]. Fasinumab has demonstrated statistically and clinically significant improvements in pain and physical function in patients with moderate-to-severe chronic OA pain [15]. This phase III study (NCT02683239) compared the long-term safety of fasinumab with placebo. Efficacy was assessed in a prespecified and fully powered sub-study.

Methods

Study design

This multi-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase III study conducted in North America, Europe, Latin America, Asia-Pacific, and South Africa, consisted of a ≤30-day screening period, a 7–10-day pre-randomization period, a 52-week treatment period, a 20-week follow-up period, and an end-of-study call ∼1 year after the last dose (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study flow diagram. Study flow diagram. EOS, end of study; EOT, end of treatment.

The study characterized the safety profile of several doses/regimens of fasinumab during and following 52 weeks of exposure. A sub-study characterized the efficacy of fasinumab over the first 16 weeks of treatment. Safety concerns prompted discontinuation of doses >1 ​mg every 4 weeks (Q4W), resulting in a change in study design and enrollment of three randomized, placebo-controlled cohorts: (1) a high-dose safety cohort in which dosing was prematurely discontinued, (2) an efficacy sub-study cohort evaluating 1 ​mg Q4W and 1 ​mg every 8 weeks (Q8W) and higher doses prematurely terminated before all patients had received 52 weeks of exposure, and (3) a 1 ​mg safety cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1). Patients for whom dosing was prematurely terminated were asked to remain in the study to complete the 20-week follow-up period and end-of-study call. See Supplementary methods.

Safety data were collected for all patients during the treatment (end of treatment/week 52) and follow-up periods (end of 20-week follow-up visit/week 72). Only data on joint replacements (JRs) were collected until the end-of-study phone call.

Patients

Patients were males and females, aged ≥18 years, with a diagnosis of OA of the knee or hip based on American College of Rheumatology criteria and radiologic evidence of OA (Kellgren-Lawrence grade [KLG] ≥2) for the index joint at screening. Patients also had a Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) average pain subscale score ≥4 in the index joint at both screening and baseline visits (on a scale of 0–10), and a history of inadequate pain relief from acetaminophen, inadequate pain relief from or intolerance to ≥1 oral NSAID, and inadequate pain relief from, intolerance to, unwillingness to take, or no access to opioids. Patients stopped their standard-of-care OA pain medications during the pre-randomization period (7–10 days pre-baseline). All patients gave informed consent to be involved in the study.

Treatment and protocol amendments

Randomization within each cohort was performed at baseline according to a predetermined central randomization scheme, stratified by baseline KLG (2–3 or 4) and geographic region (North America, Latin America, Europe, or Asia-Pacific/South Africa). Changes in the conduct of the study were implemented by protocol amendments (see Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary methods).

Safety evaluation

Safety was monitored by Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc.'s Safety Monitoring Team, an independent Data Monitoring Committee, and an independent Arthropathy Adjudication Committee. Safety assessments included monitoring of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), adverse events of special interest (AESIs), vital signs, and radiologic and laboratory tests. AESIs included adjudicated arthropathies (AAs), peripheral sensory adverse events (AEs) requiring neurology or other specialty consultation, sympathetic nervous system (SNS) dysfunction, and JRs.

Arthropathies and JRs

Details concerning monitoring for joint-related AEs, including exclusionary findings on imaging, the AA definitions, and methodology for detection of AAs, are in the Supplementary methods.

Efficacy endpoints

The co-primary endpoints for the efficacy sub-study were change from baseline to week 16 in WOMAC pain and physical function subscale scores [16]. For this study, WOMAC pain and physical function scores were between 0 and 10. Secondary endpoints included change from baseline to week 16 in patient global assessment (PGA) for OA score, and percentage of patients with ≥30 ​% improvement in the WOMAC pain subscale score. Other efficacy endpoints were change from baseline to week 16 in the average weekly walking index joint pain score, and patient response using the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology Clinical Trials-Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OMERACT-OARSI) responder criteria (Supplementary methods).

Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity

See Supplementary methods for pharmacokinetic and immunogenicity methodology.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis for efficacy was planned for when the 16-week data were available for all randomized patients in the efficacy sub-study (primary efficacy analysis). The week 72 analysis was planned for when the last patient in the 1 ​mg safety cohort completed their last visit. The final analysis of long-term safety was performed when all follow-up data, including 100-week data for JRs, were available for all randomized patients. Additional ad hoc interim safety analyses were performed to support regulatory authority and/or internal decision-making.

The full analysis set for the evaluation of long-term safety (FAS-LTS) for patient disposition, demographics, baseline disease characteristics, and history of analgesic use included all randomized patients in the overall study and was based on treatment allocated. The safety analysis set for long-term safety (SAF-LTS) included all randomized patients who received any study drug and was based on treatment-as-received. The efficacy analysis set included all randomized patients in the efficacy sub-study (FAS-sub-study) and was based on intent-to-treat. The safety analysis set for the efficacy sub-study included all randomized patients in the efficacy sub-study who received any study drug during the efficacy sub-study and was based on treatment-as-received.

Incidence rate ratios, difference estimates, and two-sided 95 ​% confidence intervals were computed using generalized estimating equations with a log-linked Poisson model with terms for treatment-as-received and each randomization stratum. Exposure-adjusted incidence rates with 95 ​% confidence intervals were calculated as the number of events divided by duration of observation period. The number and percentage of patients with AEs, SAEs, TEAEs, AESIs, images requiring arthropathy adjudication, and confirmed AAs were summarized by treatment for the SAF-LTS. Sub-types and outcomes of AAs were also summarized.

Primary efficacy variables and continuous secondary efficacy variables were analyzed using a multiple imputation approach with a mixed-effect model for repeated measures for the FAS-sub-study. A hierarchical procedure was used to control the overall Type-1 error rate at 0.05 for the two co-primary endpoints and key secondary endpoints across the two dose regimens (1 ​mg Q4W tested first, then 1 ​mg Q8W; see Supplementary methods). Analysis of categorical secondary efficacy variables employed the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel approach, stratified by randomization.

Time-to-event endpoints were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method.

Results

Patient population

From February 17, 2016 (first patient screened), to June 15, 2021 (last participant received end-of-study call), 13,945 patients were screened and 5331 were randomized (FAS-LTS population; Fig. 2). Patients were enrolled into three consecutive cohorts: high-dose safety (n ​= ​2491), efficacy sub-study (n ​= ​1074), and 1 ​mg safety (n ​= ​1766) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Adverse events

AEs are presented in Table 2. Overall, 88.5 ​% of patients in the placebo group and 89.8 ​%, 92.6 ​%, and 93.6 ​% of patients in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups reported ≥1 ​AE to the end-of-follow-up visit (week 72), respectively. Most AEs were related to musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders. The proportions of patients who reported ≥1 SAE were 13.8 ​% in the placebo group and 14.3 ​%, 18.1 ​%, and 24.6 ​% in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups, respectively; the most common SAEs were related to musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders, and surgical and medical procedures.

Twenty-five patients had an AE leading to death during the on-study period (i.e., occurring any time after the first dose of study drug and up to end of the 20-week follow-up visit); the proportion of patients was comparable between the placebo (0.6 ​%) and fasinumab groups (0.3 ​%, 0.6 ​% and 0.5 ​% in the 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W and 6 ​mg Q8W groups, respectively).

The proportion of fractures on-study to the end of follow-up was similar across the placebo, fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, and fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W groups (3.3 ​%, 2.5 ​%, and 3.3 ​%, respectively), and slightly higher in the fasinumab 6 ​mg Q8W group (4.5 ​%).

The proportion of patients with peripheral sensory AESIs was 2.3 ​% in the placebo group, and 6.4 ​%, 6.4 ​% and 4.5 ​% in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups, respectively. Events were predominantly paresthesia and carpal tunnel syndrome, and were mild/moderate. Most events of paresthesia were recovered/resolved or were recovering/resolving by the end of the on-study period.

One patient receiving fasinumab 6 ​mg Q8W reported peripheral coldness further described as feelings of cold feet. Onset of the event was day 324 and the event resolved ∼2 weeks later. No other AESIs of SNS dysfunction were reported.

Adjudicated arthropathies and joint replacements

AAs were seen in all treatment groups. A dose-dependent increase in AAs was observed across the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups. (Table 3). Adjusting for observation time, AA incidence rates (per 100 patient-years) for the placebo and fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups were 1.6, 4.2, 5.5, and 10.8, respectively. The incidence rate difference (95 ​% CI) versus the placebo group at week 72 was 3.6 (2.1, 5.0) for fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 4.8 (3.2, 6.4) for 1 ​mg Q4W, and 11.5 (9.6, 13.4) for 6 ​mg Q8W. Trends for AAs were similar across the three cohorts.

Rapidly progressive OA type 1 (RPOA-1; subtype indicating joint space narrowing) accounted for most AAs (∼68.9–84.2 ​% overall). The percentages of patients with RPOA-1 were 5.8 ​%, 7.3 ​%, and 13.8 ​% in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups, respectively, versus 2.4 ​% in the placebo group.

Rapidly progressive OA type 2 (RPOA-2; subtype indicating a region of bony collapse) was the second most common AA subtype (0.9 ​%, 1.8 ​%, and 2.4 ​% in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W and 6 ​mg Q8W groups, respectively, vs. 0.2 ​% in the placebo group). These included patients who also met the criteria for destructive arthropathy (DA). Patients with DA (indicating severe damage unequivocally observed on x-ray including severe bony collapse, bone loss, or fragmentation) were reported in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W (0.1 ​%), 1 ​mg Q4W (0.5 ​%), and 6 ​mg Q8W (2.0 ​%) groups but not in the placebo group. Most DAs were associated with RPOA-2.

The distribution of joints with AAs was similar across treatment groups. The most common joint affected was the knee (∼67–77 ​% of AAs in each treatment arm), followed by the hip (∼14–26 ​%). Joints with clear evidence of disease on x-ray (i.e., KLG ≥2) at baseline were at greater risk for AA and DA (Supplementary Table I). Altogether in the fasinumab 1 ​mg groups, 13/3441 knee/hip joints with baseline KLG 0–1 developed an AA. In 9/13 joints, moderate-to-severe pain (WOMAC pain scale score ≥4) was observed, suggesting the presence of pathology (possibly soft tissue) not detected on baseline radiography. Generally, when an AA was identified in a joint with a baseline KLG of 0–1, RPOA-1 was the most identified sub-type. AAs were predominantly detected on routine imaging as opposed to being prompted by worsening symptoms; 6.8–19.4 ​% of AAs in the fasinumab groups and 14.3 ​% in the placebo group were prompted by worsening symptoms.

JRs were seen in all treatment groups. A dose-dependent increase in JRs was observed across the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups. Adjusting for observation times, the JR incidence rate (per 100 patient-years) for the placebo and fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups at week 100 were 3.8, 5.1, 7.2, and 10.6, respectively. The incidence rate differences (95 ​% CI) versus placebo were 1.2 (−0.2, 2.6) for fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 3.4 (1.8, 4.9) for 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6.8 (5.2, 8.4) for 6 ​mg Q8W. Trends for JRs were similar across the three cohorts, but the proportion of patients with JRs was higher in all treatment groups, including placebo, in the efficacy sub-study cohort (data not shown). A Kaplan-Meier analysis comparing the 1 ​mg arms with concurrently randomized placebo participants demonstrated that the rate of JRs in the 1 ​mg Q8W arm overlapped with placebo throughout, while separation of the 1 ​mg Q4W arm from placebo began at approximately week 72 (after the follow-up period; Supplementary Fig. 2).

The distribution of location of JRs was similar across treatment groups. The most common location was the knee (62.4–80.2 ​%), followed by the hip (17.8–44.4 ​%). Most joints that had a JR had pre-existing pathology at baseline, as most were seen in the index joint (>60 ​%) for all treatment groups, including placebo. Even among non-index joints that were replaced, a large proportion had clear evidence of disease on baseline imaging (KLG ≥2; Supplementary Table I). The median times to first JR were 366.5 days in the placebo group and 450.0, 470.0, and 450.0 days in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups, respectively.

Subgroup analysis

To explore whether different phenotypes of OA (particularly generalized OA) might show different levels of risk, a post hoc subgroup analysis based on the number of joints with radiographic OA at baseline was conducted. OA-impacted joints were defined as any hip or knee with baseline KLG ≥2 and any shoulder with a baseline score of mild, moderate, or severe. The minimum number of joints with radiographic OA was one; the maximum was six. Three subgroups were arbitrarily designated: 1–2, 3–4, and 5–6 joints with radiographic OA at baseline. Subgroup analyses of WOMAC pain and physical subscale scores by baseline index joint, KLG (2–3 and 4), geographic region, age, sex, race, weight, and body mass index showed a consistent benefit in favor of fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W and Q8W over placebo (data not shown). The subgroup with baseline KLG 4 for the index joint showed a greater treatment response than those with KLG 2–3 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Patient Global Assessment

Baseline PGA scores were similar across groups. Fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W and 1 ​mg Q8W resulted in statistically significant improvements in PGA score versus placebo; the LS mean differences from baseline to week 16 were −0.27 (P ​= ​0.0025) and −0.20 (P ​= ​0.0230), respectively.

Weekly walking index joint pain score

Results of the average weekly walking index joint pain score demonstrated that significant pain relief was rapid, with differences versus placebo achieving nominal P ​< ​0.05 within a few days of treatment, maintained until week 16 for all fasinumab doses (Fig. 4b); doses of fasinumab >1 ​mg did not provide incremental efficacy. The treatment effect observed with fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W was maintained from baseline to week 16; the treatment effect of fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W waned towards the end of each 8-week dosing interval.

OMERACT-OARSI

Based on the OMERACT-OARSI responder criteria, a greater proportion of patients in the fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W and 1 ​mg Q8W groups than in the placebo group were responders (66.8 ​% and 59.5 ​% vs. 43.5 ​%, respectively; nominal P ​< ​0.001).

Pharmacokinetics of fasinumab

For patients in the long-term safety study and efficacy sub-study (PKAS-sub-study), mean fasinumab trough concentration in serum for the 1 ​mg Q8W and 1 ​mg Q4W groups achieved steady-state by week 8, maintained through weeks 32 (1 ​mg Q8W) and 52 (1 ​mg Q4W) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Accumulation of fasinumab in serum for the 1 ​mg Q4W and 1 ​mg Q8W groups was 1.36 and 1.40, respectively. At weeks 60 and 72, mean fasinumab concentrations in serum were below the lower limit of quantification in both groups.

For patients in the PKAS-sub-study, mean fasinumab concentrations in serum increased in a dose-proportional to slightly greater than dose-proportional manner during the 16-week efficacy assessment period for the 1 ​mg Q8W, 1 ​mg Q4W, 3 ​mg Q4W, and 6 ​mg Q8W groups (data not shown).

Immunogenicity of fasinumab

The incidence of treatment-emergent anti-drug antibodies was low and comparable in all fasinumab (1.1 ​%; 42/3867) and placebo (1.2 ​%; 58/5020) groups; all titers in the fasinumab treatment groups were low (<1000). No neutralizing antibodies were detected in any patient. Immunogenicity did not affect the concentration-time profile of fasinumab in serum.

Discussion

This is the first study to explore the long-term (up to 52 weeks of treatment) safety of fasinumab in patients with difficult-to-treat OA. All participants had moderate-to-severe pain and inadequate response and/or intolerance to traditional OA pain treatments; nearly three-quarters (70.7 ​%) had previously taken opioids to manage their OA pain. This is higher than what has been observed in other studies looking at management of OA pain [18] and is consistent with a difficult-to treat population.

Overall, the safety profile was consistent with what has been observed with fasinumab and the class of anti-NGF compounds in general. There were no signals for SNS dysfunction, no imbalance of deaths, and no imbalance of fractures between the placebo and treatment groups. There was a higher incidence of peripheral sensory neuropathy in the fasinumab groups, but this was generally mild and reversible.

While AAs and JRs were seen in all groups, including placebo, a clear dose-dependent risk was observed across the fasinumab groups. This contrasts with the efficacy findings, which showed no relationship for doses >1 ​mg Q4W. RPOA-1 was, by far, the most frequently reported AA. The clinical significance of RPOA-1, however, is unclear. RPOA-1 is not typically detected following worsening of symptoms and a previous study (manuscript in progress) showed that, despite an increased number of RPOA-1 events in the fasinumab group relative to the control groups, there was no between-group difference in the incidence of JRs. In that study, RPOA-1 joints predominantly consisted of two groups: (1) joints with full-thickness articular cartilage defects at baseline (∼50 ​%; therefore at high risk of JR at the study start); and (2) joints with new or worsening partial thickness articular cartilage defects.

The incidence of AAs in patients treated with fasinumab was positively associated on a per-joint basis with more severe joint damage at baseline (KLG ≥2), as were most JRs (KLG 3–4) (Supplementary Table I). Of the small number of AAs that occurred in KLG 0–1 joints at baseline most were described as causing moderate-to-severe pain, suggesting underlying pathology despite no clear evidence of damage on x-ray (manuscript in progress). Furthermore, in a post hoc subgroup analysis, a higher total body baseline burden of radiographic OA was associated with an increased risk of AA, DA, and JR in fasinumab-treated but not placebo-treated patients (Fig. 3). Therefore, at the level of both the individual joint (severity of OA in the affected joint) and the patient (total number of joints with OA), more OA was associated with a higher risk of AA, DA, and JR in patients treated with fasinumab. This suggests that underlying OA is a pre-requisite for manifestation of these events and that patients with a lower burden of radiographic OA (1–2 joints; ∼60 ​% of the study population) may be at a lower risk for joint-related AEs associated with fasinumab. However, given the duration of the study, one cannot rule out the possibility that joints with less damage at baseline could take longer for progression to manifest.

In the 16-week efficacy sub-study, fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W and Q8W demonstrated statistically significant and clinically meaningful superiority over placebo, consistent with previous findings [14,15]. Improvements in pain and physical function were seen as early as week 1 and maintained through week 16. The efficacy of fasinumab 1 ​mg Q8W attenuated near the end of the dosing interval, suggesting that a Q4W interval may better maintain efficacy. The study also showed that fasinumab was effective, possibly more so, in patients with advanced structural damage (those with baseline KLG 4).

Key limitations of this study include the multiple amendments terminating dosing in the higher fasinumab dose groups, the enrollment of patients in distinct cohorts that examined different doses of fasinumab and that had different randomization schemes, thus potentially confounding the ability to assess the safety data across the different cohorts, and the exploratory nature of the subgroup analysis.

In a difficult-to-treat population of patients with pain due to OA, AAs and JRs occurred in all treatment groups but were more frequent in fasinumab-treated patients and appeared more often in patients with structural damage at baseline. Fasinumab 1 ​mg Q4W and 1 ​mg Q8W achieved statistically significant improvements at week 16 in WOMAC pain and physical function subscale scores compared with placebo.
