Hybrid Machine learning-based modeling to predict and optimize the compressive strength of electric arc furnace slag-modified concrete
Authors: Md Alhaz Uddin, Md Habibur Rahman Sobuz, Md Kawsarul Islam Kabbo, Ibrahim Y Hakeem, Ratan Lal, Ali Masria, Sani Aliyu Abubakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing worldwide population has increased the use of electric arc furnaces (EAF), resulting in a surge of EAF slag and a huge environmental concern. EAF slag’s complex physical qualities have a considerable impact on concrete’s mechanical performance, mainly its compressive strength (CS). This study introduces a novel framework for forecasting the CS of EAF slag concrete that uses advanced machine learning models such as Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), AdaBoost (ADB), Random Forest (RF), Hybrid XGB-RF, and Hybrid XGB-ADB. A full dataset of 730 samples was meticulously created, containing essential input parameters such as binders, aggregates, and other necessary variables, with CS as the desired outcome. Based on the findings, the XGB model showed highest accuracy, with an test R2 of 0.951, MAPE of 1.128, and RMSE of 1.393 MPa, indicating its potential for dependable performance forecasting. In addition, the hybrid XGB–RF model also demonstrated strong predictive accuracy, achieving an R2 value of 0.947 during the testing phase. Furthermore, explainability tools such as SHapley Additive ExPlanations (SHAP) and partial dependence curves (PDCs) identified the curing period and content of cement as the most influential factors to predict the CS of EAF slag concrete. The methodologies and outcomes of this study will help to reduce reliance on resource-intensive experimental methods, pave the way for efficient, precise, and ecologically conscientious concrete design.Keywords: Arc furnace slag concrete, Compressive strength prediction, Hybrid machine learningSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In contemporary research, there has been a marked increase across the globe emphasis placed on the combination of waste materials into concrete and mortar1–4. Research has demonstrated that incorporating extra ingredients into cement-based concrete improves its properties and significantly aids in environmental conservation5–9. The manufacturing of steel, which is a significant industrial activity, results in the production of a by-product known as slag10,11. This by-product has been successfully exploited in the building industry to reduce its impact on the environment11–13. Besides this, construction and demolition waste has also gained significant attention in the development of modified sustainable concrete, with notable progress in Asian, European and Middle East (such as Najran city) regions14–17. Blast furnace slag (BFS), electric arc furnace oxidizing slag (EAF), and ladle furnace slag (LFS) represent typical forms of slag that are frequently utilized in the construction industry18–22. Some of these slag types are derived from the procedures that are used to manufacture steel. Researchers from all over the world have investigated the possible applications of electric arc furnace (EAF) slag in civil engineering and construction23–25. This is due to the enormous quantities of EAF slag that are produced, which are estimated to constitute between 10 and 15% of the total weight of steel production26.

Rondi, et al.27 studied the utilization of EAF slag as a complete substitute for aggregates in concrete and discovered that higher specific density and water absorption than concrete using natural aggregates. However, the slag showed reduced volumetric expansion and an acceptable quantity of contaminant leaching. Sezer and Gülderen28 found that concrete incorporating EAF slag as coarse aggregate exhibited reduced water penetration depth during pressure testing; however, its use as fine aggregate resulted in higher penetration depths compared to conventional limestone aggregate mixtures. González-Ortega, et al.29 found that water absorption within concrete containing EAF slag was lower at 48 h compared to conventional concrete, with absorption reducing as limestone coarse particles were replaced by EAF slag. Santamaría, et al.30 observed that concrete incorporating EAF slag exhibited greater resistance to water penetration under pressure compared to combinations employing natural aggregates. Their findings also demonstrated that steel rebars embedded in conventional concrete had higher electrochemical corrosion rates than those in EAF slag-based concrete, according to ASTM C-876. The existence of free CaO in EAF slag, which contributes to Ca-ion leaching, was thought to be favorable for steel reinforcing preservation over time. de Lima31 conducted carbonation depth tests under accelerated and laboratory circumstances and discovered that replacing typical fine aggregates with EAF slag lowered carbonation depth. However, replacing coarse particles with EAF slag resulted in deeper penetrations than in the reference concrete. Ortega-López, et al.32 investigated water absorption via capillarity in EAF slag-based concrete with fiber reinforcement and without fiber reinforcement that had been moist-cured for 90 days and found substantial differences. ARRIBAS GARCIA33 investigated steel bar corrosion in EAF slag concrete exposed to a sea environment for a year and discovered that these specimens had similar corrosion behavior to those made with limestone aggregates.

In concrete research, machine learning (ML) has become established as a potent statistical technique for the prediction of critical attributes such as strength, durability, and workability, resulting in increased attention34–37. Without the need for human involvement, ML models can discover trends and make intelligent predictions based on historical data38–41. These algorithms excel in analyzing nonlinear and multidimensional correlations between multiple parameters, resulting in very accurate forecasting42–44. Compared to traditional experimental methods, ML-driven approaches improve the precision of concrete property predictions by reducing errors45–47. Several machine learning (ML) methods, including Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB)48, LightGBM (LGB)49, Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM)50, random forest (RF)51, Decision Trees (DT)52, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)53, AdaBoost54, and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)55, have been used in studies. In addition to standalone algorithms, researchers have created hybrid models that integrate the best features of different methodologies to improve forecast accuracy56–58. Recent studies from Najran city using date palm fibers in high-performance concrete also highlighted the potential of regional waste materials and the need for ML-based models to capture their strength behavior59. Other techniques, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)60, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)61,62, and multi-model based approaches, have been investigated for their ability to simulate the nonlinear and complex interactions that influence tangible attributes63,64. These advances highlight the critical role of machine learning (ML) in increasing the predictive accuracy and operational efficiency of predictive models for concrete strength. Güçlüer, et al.65 found that Decision Tree (DT) models performed well, with strong correlation (R² = 0.86) and low mean absolute error (MAE = 2.59), especially for samples cured for 28 days. Ensemble models such as GS-XGB obtained over 99% accuracy in 28-day CS predictions. These models are further supplemented by user-friendly software platforms that aim to simplify material design and testing operations66–68. The Categorical Gradient Boosting (CatBoost) approach performed well with an R2 of 0.966 and an RMSE of 3.06 MPa, indicating its potential to simplify assessments and reduce experimental costs69. With an R2 of 0.9356 and an RMSE of 5.5604 MPa, the GB_PSO model optimized for recycled concrete outperformed individual methods. Furthermore, evaluations stressed the importance of cement content in maximizing strength70,71.

In another study, Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) proved highly effective in predicting the compressive strength of concrete with Electric Arc Furnace (EAF) slag, with a minimum Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 2.20% and a minimum Mean Squared Error (MSE) of 46.95, demonstrating MLR’s robustness in such applications72. Furthermore, the Grey Model (GM) emerged as a powerful tool for prediction of compressive strength, achieving remarkable results with a minimum MAPE of 0.9% and MSE of 8, and outperforming ANN, particularly with limited data—GM successfully predicted strength with as few as four data points, demonstrating superior performance for scenarios with constrained datasets73. These findings show the potential of ML-based models for improving concrete strength prediction and providing efficient, reliable, and data-adaptive solutions to the construction sector.

Although numerous studies have successfully applied machine learning techniques to predict the CS of conventional concrete, limited attention has been given to concrete incorporating EAF slag. Existing investigations on EAFS-based concrete are often constrained by small datasets, or a limited number of input variables. Moreover, few studies have integrated advanced interpretability tools to systematically explain the influence of individual mixture components on strength development. To address these gaps, this study develops a large and structured dataset comprising 730 samples collected from published literature, incorporating eight critical input parameters. The novelty of this research lies in multiple primary aspects. First, it establishes one of the most comprehensive datasets specifically focused on EAFS-modified concrete for CS prediction. Second, it performs a systematic comparative assessment of standalone ensemble models (ADB, RF, and XGB) alongside hybrid stacking-based models (XGB-ADB and XGB-RF), which enables a robust evaluation of predictive performance. Third, it integrates parametric modeling techniques, including SHAP analysis and partial dependence plots (PDPs), to quantify feature importance and interpret nonlinear interactions among mixture constituents. Through this comprehensive framework, the study advances predictive modeling for EAFS-based concrete and provides interpretable, data-driven insights to support the design of high-performance and environmentally sustainable construction materials.

Research methodology

The most useful machine learning model for forecasting the CS of EAFS-based concrete was determined by testing five alternative approaches, including two hybrid models. The following machine learning techniques were applied in this work: (1) ADB, (2) XGB, (3) RF, (4) XGB-ADB, and (5) XGB-RF. The dataset was divided such that 80% was used for training, 10% for testing and remaining 10% for validation. Here, the validation set was employed to further verify model robustness and consistency on unseen data, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting. All analyses were conducted using the Anaconda Navigator distribution, where an integrated Jupyter Notebook environment (version 7.2.2) was employed for workflow execution. The programming language used was Python (version 3.12.7). Furthermore, widely recognized and well-documented Python libraries were utilized for different stages of the study. Data preprocessing and numerical computations were performed using NumPy and Pandas, while visualization tasks were carried out using Matplotlib and Seaborn. ML model development, training, and evaluation were implemented using Scikit-learn, along with model specified standard libraries. In addition, SHAP library was employed for model interpretability analysis. Figure 1 depicts the overall workflow of the ML implementation, with detailed step-by-step representations of the process.

The dataset used in this study consists of 730 data points compiled from previously published peer-reviewed literature. Furthermore, the statistical attributes of all the input and output features have been provided in Table 1. The collected studies were carefully screened to ensure consistency in reported units, testing standards, and curing conditions. All CS values were converted to MPa, and mixture proportions were standardized to the same unit to maintain dimensional uniformity across the dataset. Furthermore, several preprocessing steps were performed prior to model development. The dataset was first examined for missing, duplicate, or incomplete entries, and inconsistent samples were removed to preserve data integrity. Only experimental results obtained under comparable laboratory conditions were retained. These conditions included standard curing regimes and conventional CS testing procedures. This filtering process minimized variability arising from incompatible experimental protocols and ensured that the dataset represented similar mechanical and environmental conditions.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGB)

XGB represents an advanced machine learning algorithm renowned for its exceptional capacity to generate precise forecasts. The main basis of its boosting method is to train iteratively learners progressively, with each learner emphasizing on rectifying the mistakes made by its predecessor. During this procedure, each succeeding model is fine-tuned to the preceding one’s residual errors, and these iterative adjustments are combined to improve the model’s overall forecast accuracy. This step-by-step correction greatly strengthens the projections74. Furthermore, XGB uses effective resource management for both CPU and memory, resulting in faster computation and shorter processing times. Adaptive boosting (ADB)

As seen in Fig. 2(b), ADB Regression is a sophisticated ensemble learning technique intended to improve regression job accuracy. This technique combines multiple weak regression models into a cohesive ensemble, creating a more reliable and robust prediction system75. Unlike conventional methods, ADB Regression adapts dynamically to diverse data patterns. During each cycle, the algorithm adjusts the weights of erroneously classified examples, compelling following models to focus on improving predictions for these difficult contexts. The final ensemble aggregates individual models using weighted contributions, reflecting their performance. This adaptive process fosters continual improvement, enabling ADB Regression to handle complex datasets and variable interactions effectively. Its capacity to yield precise and dependable predictions makes it particularly suited for scenarios involving challenging data structures and intricate patterns76,77.

Random forest (RF)

RF is an integrated-models learning approach that amplifies model performance by mixing numerous decision trees, each trained on a distinct subgroup of the original data via “bagging” or bootstrap aggregation. Bagging entails sampling with replacement, in which certain samples appear several times while others are omitted, hence reducing model variance by averaging predictions from these independent trees, as explained by Breiman78, shown in Fig. 2(c). RF computes splits using a randomly selected subset of features at each node, reducing computational load and increasing performance, making pruning unnecessary. Hybrid machine learning modeling

This paper describes a fusion approach that combines machine learning techniques to improve predicted accuracy using ensemble modeling. Two advanced variants, XGB-ADB and XGB-RF, were created using stacking techniques. The XGB-ADB model incorporates the strengths of two gradient-boosting techniques, XGB and ADB, to increase accuracy by addressing complex data patterns together. During the first stage, each base learner was independently trained using the same training dataset and hyperparameters. After training, the predicted compressive strength values produced by the base learners were extracted and used as new input features for the second stage of the stacking process. In the final stage, a meta-learner (Linear regression model) was trained on these prediction outputs to generate the final compressive strength estimate. This approach allowed the hybrid models to combine the individual strengths of each algorithm while reducing the limitations associated with standalone models.

In this study, hyperparameter tuning was performed to improve model accuracy and increase generalization ability. Each ML algorithm was configured within a predefined search space, and combinations of influential parameters were systematically evaluated to identify the most effective configuration. This approach enabled the evaluation of multiple ML models under consistent conditions and ensured a fair comparison among algorithms. The selection of optimal hyperparameters was based on minimizing prediction error while maintaining consistency between training and validation performance. SHapley additive explanations (SHAP)

SHAP values have acquired widespread recognition in recent years as an effective way for evaluating machine learning model predictions79. This technique is well-known for its capacity to explain complex black-box models. SHAP applies Shapley values, which originated in collaborative game theory, to machine learning80. In machine learning, SHAP refers to features as “players” and the model’s output as “reward.” Each feature is assigned a score indicating its relevance to the total forecast80. This methodology not only finds the most important traits but also explains how these factors influence model outcomes81. Visualization techniques, such as summary plots and feature importance graphs, expand their utility by providing explicit insights into model behavior and decision-making processes82.

Partial dependence plots (PDPs)

With all other parameters held constant, PDPs are frequently employed to investigate the link between certain features and machine learning model predictions. These graphs can demonstrate if a feature’s relationship to the model’s output is linear, nonlinear, or influenced by interactions with other variables83. PDPs improve model transparency by isolating the effect of specific parameters, making them an essential tool for data-driven decision-making. They let users decide if the relationship between an input feature and the model response is simple or complex. This method computes the average predicted output for specific feature values over the whole dataset, maintaining consistency while isolating the feature’s influence. The approach can be extended to investigate the effects of two traits at the same time. PDPs are powerful interpretive tools that provide precise information about how individual or combination characteristics influence model predictions.

Evaluation metrics for prediction performance

Three different statistical techniques were used at three phases to evaluate the algorithms’ prediction accuracy: training, validation, and testing. Root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) are these factors that determine statistical performance. The degree to which the forecast and the experimental result match is also assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2). The evaluation metrics were selected because they provide complementary and comprehensive insights into regression model performance. RMSE penalizes larger prediction errors more severely, which makes it effective for identifying models sensitive to high-magnitude deviations in CS. MAE offers a direct and interpretable measure of average absolute error and MAPE expresses prediction error in percentage terms, enabling relative error comparison across different strength ranges and improving interpretability for engineering applications. Furthermore, R2 evaluates the proportion of variance in CS explained by the model, thereby indicating goodness-of-fit and predictive consistency84,85. Predictive accuracy of ML models

Figure 6 presents the scatter plots of observed versus predicted compressive strength values for all developed models. The plots demonstrate the degree of agreement between experimental measurements and model predictions. Its performance remains acceptable, with R2 values of 0.856 (training), 0.836 (testing), and 0.816 (validation). However, the reduced coefficients indicate weaker predictive strength relative to XGB. The RF model improves prediction accuracy compared to ADB and performs competitively with XGB. It achieves R2 values of 0.981 for training, 0.926 for testing, and similarly high consistency during validation. This demonstrates its suitability for compressive strength estimation.

Figure 11 demonstrates how each input variable influences arc furnace slag concrete’s compressive strength. Both a decision tree and a multi-layer perceptron were used to perform the partial dependency analysis. As expected, the concrete strength increases with a higher curing period, cement content, and superplasticizers as shown in Fig. 11(a), positively impacting the compressive strength. The compressive strength steadily increased from 33 to 48 MPa, a 45% rise, as the cement content was raised from 200 to 500 kg/m3. Additionally, the use of arc furnace slag led to an overall 12% improvement in compressive strength, increasing from 37.2 to 41.8 MPa at a proportion of 160 kg/m3 (Fig. 11(d)). The increase in compressive strength at moderate EAF slag contents can be attributed to the rough surface texture, angular shape, and higher hardness of slag particles, which improve interfacial bonding and internal friction within the concrete matrix. However, excessive EAF slag replacement may reduce strength because of higher porosity, and greater water absorption.

Practical applicability of the developed models

The developed ML models can be directly applied as a decision-support tool for the design and optimization of EAF slag-based concrete mixtures. Instead of relying solely on repeated laboratory trial batches, practitioners can input the proposed mixture proportions, including cement, EAF slag, SCMs, aggregates, water, superplasticizer dosage, and curing duration, into the trained model to rapidly estimate the expected CS. From a practical perspective, the developed framework can support engineers during both the preliminary design and optimization stages. A conceptual workflow for field implementation should involve four steps. First, the user defines the available material properties and target CS. Second, the selected mixture proportions are entered into the trained model. Third, the model predicts the expected CS and highlights the relative importance of each variable. Finally, the user can refine the mixture proportions until the predicted performance satisfies the project requirements. This approach can provide a practical pathway for integrating sustainable waste materials into concrete production while maintaining mechanical performance and reducing dependence on extensive experimental testing.

Conclusions

This study developed and evaluated multiple machine learning models to predict the compressive strength of EAF slag–based concrete using an extensive dataset compiled from published literature. The most important findings of this study are as follows:

All five machine learning models showed excellent performance and great generalization abilities, making them extremely dependable for forecasting the CS of EAF slag concrete. The XGB model has the lowest average error values across all stages, with the highest prediction accuracy (R2 values of 0.951 for testing, and 0.945 for validation), indicating its robustness and reliability. Hybrid models (XGB-ADB and XGB-RF) outperformed single ADB and RF models, with R² values up to 0.947. These new models lowered prediction errors dramatically, demonstrating a gain in accuracy over previous methods.

Limitations and future studies

Although this study reveals some significant outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. The dataset was compiled from published experimental studies, which may contain variability in material properties, testing standards, curing conditions, and mix design procedures. Although efforts were made to ensure consistency, uncontrolled heterogeneity may influence model generalization beyond the considered parameter ranges. In addition, the models were trained on predefined input variables, and other potentially influential factors such as chemical composition variations of EAF slag, and long-term durability indicators were not incorporated. Future research should focus on expanding the dataset with real time experimental results. In addition, considering chemical composition based parameters, and validating the models through independent laboratory testing would greatly enhance the generalizability. The development of deployable graphical user interface (GUI) tools and real-time optimization frameworks for practical field applications would further enhance the applicability of the proposed modeling approach.
