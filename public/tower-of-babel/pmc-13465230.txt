Health-related quality of life and its associated factors among patients with neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder in China: a questionnaire survey analysis
Authors: Wenxuan Jiang, Shihan Yan, Shitong Xie, Jing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of Chinese Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) patients, and to explore the influencing factors.MethodsAn online survey was conducted among patients with NMOSD in China from May to June 2024. Data on socio-demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, healthcare resource utilization, and HRQoL measured by EQ-5D-5 L were collected. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with HRQoL.ResultsA total of 336 patients with NMOSD (mean age 40.4 years; 89.3% female) were included in the analysis. The EQ-5D-5 L utility value was 0.665 ± 0.335 (mean ± SD). NMOSD patients most frequently reported problems in anxiety/depression whereas self-care was least affected; the proportion reporting no problems was 14.9% and 75.9%, respectively. According to multivariable linear regression analyses, older age, a greater number of comorbidities and chronic diseases, functional impairment (disability, difficulty walking), and higher caregiving needs were independently associated with lower HRQoL, whereas higher treatment satisfaction score and higher income (≥ 5000 CNY) were associated with higher HRQoL. Relapse status was not independently associated with HRQoL after adjustment for disease duration and current functional impairment.ConclusionsChinese NMOSD patients experience substantially decreased HRQoL. Functional impairment and caregiving dependence are the main determinants of HRQoL, highlighting the need for patient-centered management beyond relapse prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02560-6.Keywords: EQ-5D-5L, Health-related quality of life, Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, China

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is an autoimmune disease of the central nervous system (CNS) that primarily affects the optic nerve and spinal cord [1]. It shows a pronounced female predominance, accounting for approximately 90% of adult patients [2, 3]. The global prevalence of NMOSD varied from 0.7 to 10 per 100,000 individuals, being lowest in Australia and New Zealand and highest in Afro-Caribbean region [2, 4, 5]. However, the prevalence of NMOSD in China remains unclear [6]. NMOSD typically causes visual impairment, motor dysfunction, pain, and cognitive impairment, imposing significant medical and economic burdens on patients [7–9]. The impact of NMOSD on daily functioning, work or study performance, and social participation significantly impairs health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in patients with NMOSD [10–12].

There has been increasing attention to the HRQoL of individuals living with NMOSD. Studies conducted in Europe, Latin America, and Asia consistently demonstrate that NMOSD imposes a profound negative effect on HRQoL compared with both the general population and patients with multiple sclerosis (MS), another autoimmune disease of the CNS resulting in symptoms including visual disturbances and mobility limitations [8, 13–20].

A cross-sectional study in Argentina using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) revealed significantly lower HRQoL scores among NMOSD patients than healthy controls, with greater disability and longer disease duration being independently associated with lower HRQoL [19]. A multicenter study from Thailand using the EQ-5D-3 L reported comparable health utility scores between NMOSD and MS patients (mean 0.41), with pain, mobility limitations, and anxiety/depression being the most affected domains [16]. Studies from Germany and UK assessed HRQoL using the EQ-5D-5 L instrument. This new version offers improved sensitivity and reduced ceiling effects compared to the original EQ-5D-3 L [21]. Utilizing the EQ-5D-5 L, these studies identified anxiety, depression, fatigue, and pain as substantial contributors to HRQoL decline, even among clinically stable patients [8, 14].

To date, research on HRQoL among Chinese NMOSD patients remains scarce, with one study using the Multiple Sclerosis Quality of Life-54 (MSQOL-54) in a cohort of 73 NMOSD patients from West China Hospital [15]. The study found that anxiety, fatigue, depression, and disability were independent predictors of lower HRQoL. However, the generalizability of these findings is limited by its single-center design, limited sample size, while the use of a non–preference-based measure restricts comparability with other studies and limits its application in health-economic evaluation.

A broader, multi-regional assessment is essential to comprehensively evaluate HRQoL and its associated factors among NMOSD patients in China. Moreover, previous research in China has not applied generic preference-based measures such as the EQ-5D-5 L, which enable the estimation of health utility values and facilitate international comparisons. The objective of this study was to evaluate HRQoL among Chinese NMOSD patients using the EQ-5D-5 L and to explore factors associated with HRQoL.

Methods

This study has been reviewed and approved by the Academic Ethics Committee at Tianjin University (TJUE-2024-269). Informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to the survey.

Study design and participants

An online survey was conducted from May to June 2024 in collaboration with CHINA MS, NMOSD, MOGAD+ COMMUNITY, which is the first nationwide patient organization for individuals with demyelinating diseases in China and has been supported by and has collaborated with the Multiple Sclerosis International Federation (MSIF) since 2014. Eligible participants were required to meet the following criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) clinically diagnosed with NMOSD; (3) provided electronic informed consent after reading the study information. To improve representativeness, quota sampling was employed with a prespecified target of 90% female participants, consistent with the epidemiological profile of NMOSD [2, 3]. The survey was distributed through two primary channels: (1) direct invitations containing the questionnaire link posted in the organization’s WeChat patient groups; (2) recruitment notices with the questionnaire link promoted via its official WeChat subscription account.

The structured questionnaire was developed by researchers from Tianjin University following a review of the literature and was subsequently revised after consultation with Chinese clinicians to improve its clinical relevance for NMOSD. The questionnaire contained four sections: (1) socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education, income); (2) clinical characteristics (e.g., time since diagnosis, time since treatment, relapse status, number of comorbidities); (3) healthcare resource utilization (HRU) such as annual hospitalizations and annual outpatient visits; (4) EQ-5D-5 L. All completed questionnaires were reviewed by the research team. In cases of missing data, outliers, or logical inconsistencies, the corresponding patients were promptly re-contacted to verify and correct their entries. This process ensured the maximal accuracy of the final dataset.

Measures of socio-demographic characteristics

Socio-demographic information consisted of sex, age, ethnic group, education level, insurance type, personal monthly income and region.

Measures of clinical characteristics and HRU

Clinical information consisted of BMI category, uncorrected visual acuity, lifestyle, years since NMOSD diagnosis, years since NMOSD treatment initiation, disability status, difficulty walking, relapse status, number of comorbidities, number of chronic diseases, NMOSD-related loss of work or study status, caregiving needs and treatment satisfaction score. Patients were categorized according to BMI based on the Chinese adult (≥ 18 years) weight classification criteria specified in the Weight Management Guidelines (2024 Edition) issued by the National Health Commission of China. Uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) was measured with a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart (5-point scale). UCVA was measured separately for each eye, and the poorer UCVA of the two eyes was used for analysis. Disability status was defined based on possession of an official disability certificate issued by the Chinese government. Difficulty walking was defined based on self-reported impairment in walking ability. Relapse status was defined based on participants’ self-reported history of clinically confirmed acute NMOSD attacks from the time of NMOSD diagnosis to survey completion. Participants who reported no clinically confirmed acute attacks during this observation period were categorized as the relapse-free group, whereas those who reported one or more clinically confirmed acute attacks were categorized as the relapse group. Participants with no reported attacks were categorized as relapse-free group, those with ≥ 1 reported attacks were categorized as relapse group. NMOSD-related loss of work or study status was defined as self-reported discontinuation of education, unemployment, or early retirement attributable to NMOSD. Participants reporting any of these conditions were categorized as “Yes”, whereas those reporting none were categorized as “No”. Caregiving needs were assessed by asking participants to estimate the proportion of time they required assistance from others in their daily lives over the past three months, categorized as none, approximately 20%, 50%, 80%, or 100% of the time. Treatment satisfaction was evaluated using a single-item numerical rating scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction. HRU consisted of annual hospitalizations and annual outpatient visits.

Measures of HRQoL

HRQoL of patients was assessed using the EuroQol Five-Dimensional Questionnaire, Five-Level Version (EQ-5D-5 L) which comprises five dimensions including mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has 5 levels: no problems (level 1), some problems (level 2), moderate problems (level 3), severe problems (level 4), and extreme problems (level 5) that higher levels indicate more severe issues within each dimension [21]. Patients’ HRQoL was expressed in health utility values calculated using the established Chinese EQ-5D-5 L value set. The theoretical range of values is from − 0.391 to 1.0 (-0.391 = worst health, 1 = full health) [22].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patients’ characteristics and the distribution of HRQoL. Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables, while means and standard deviations (SD) were reported for continuous variables. For comparisons of Socio-demographic, clinical characteristics and HRU between the relapse group and the relapse-free group, F-tests were used for continuous variables, and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate, were used for categorical variables. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to compare unadjusted HRQoL across different socio-demographic and clinical groups.

A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors contributing to HRQoL among Chinese NMOSD patients. The EQ-5D-5 L health utility value was included as the dependent variable in the model and all socio-demographic, clinical characteristics and HRU listed in Table 1 were included as independent variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 16.0 software.

Results

NMOSD patients’ characteristics

A total of 349 questionnaires were received. After removing 8 duplicate submissions and 5 responses without informed consent, 336 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis. Women accounted for 89.3% (300/336) of participants, close to the prespecified 90% female quota. Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates the population screening procedure for the study.

The social and clinical characteristics of the 336 participants who completed the survey are shown in Table 1. Of the total sample, 204 (60.7%) were in the relapse group and 132 (39.3%) were relapse-free. The mean (SD) age of the total sample was 40.4 (12.5) years. More than half of the patients had a normal body weight (53.6%), and fewer than half did not have a bachelor’s degree (46.4%). The mean (SD) uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) of the total sample was 4.2 (0.9), with significant difference between groups (P = 0.006).

Significant group differences were observed in insurance type and personal monthly income. Compared with relapse-free patients, those in the relapse group were less likely to be covered by Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (56.4% vs. 70.5%; P = 0.013). In addition, relapse patients reported significantly lower personal monthly income than relapse-free patients (P = 0.005), characterized by a higher proportion of patients with no income (41.7% vs. 31.8%) and a lower proportion of those with high income (≥ 5000 CNY (Chinese Yuan): 16.7% vs. 31.8%).

Patients in the relapse group had a significantly longer disease duration and treatment duration compared with relapse-free patients (years since diagnosis: 7.5 ± 5.4 vs. 2.7 ± 2.3; years since treatment initiation: 8.1 ± 6.0 vs. 2.8 ± 2.6; both P < 0.001). In terms of functional status, patients in the relapse group were more likely to report disability (22.1% vs. 6.1%, P < 0.001), difficulty walking (48.0% vs. 33.3%, P = 0.011), and NMOSD-related loss of work or study status (38.7% vs. 22.0%, P = 0.002). Caregiving needs also differed significantly between groups (P = 0.018), with a higher proportion of relapse patients reporting caregiving needs for 50% or more of the time, whereas relapse-free patients more often reported lower levels of caregiving needs. Treatment satisfaction score, annual hospitalizations and outpatient visits were comparable between groups.

HRQoL of NMOSD patients

Table 2 presents the unadjusted EQ-5D-5 L utility values stratified by patients’ characteristics. Among the Chinese NMOSD patients, the value of EQ-5D-5 L utility was 0.665 ± 0.335 (mean ± SD). Higher utility values were observed among patients with higher educational attainment, higher personal monthly income, and coverage by the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (all P ≤ 0.001), with a clear positive gradient across income categories. Significant regional differences in utility values were observed (P = 0.003). Lifestyle-related characteristics were also associated with differences in utility values (P < 0.001), with lower utility values observed among patients reporting no lifestyle behaviors compared with those reporting at least one such behavior.

Significant differences in EQ-5D-5 L utility values were observed across functional and disease-related characteristics. Patients without disability, without difficulty walking, and without NMOSD-related loss of work or study status had substantially higher utility values than those with these status (all P < 0.001). Utility values decreased progressively with increasing caregiving needs (P < 0.001). In addition, relapse-free patients exhibited significantly higher utility values than relapse patients (0.728 ± 0.321 vs. 0.625 ± 0.338 P = 0.006).

Distribution of HRQoL among NMOSD patients

The distribution of EQ-5D-5 L dimensions across levels is shown in Fig. 1. Among these dimensions, the overall NMOSD patients reported the poorest health status in anxiety/depression, with only 14.9% reporting no problems (level 1). This was followed by pain/discomfort, in which only 19.3% of patients reported no problems. For usual activities and mobility, 54.2% and 53.0% of patients, respectively, reported no problems performing usual activities or walking about. The most favorable outcomes were observed in self-care, with 75.9% of patients reporting no problems with washing or dressing themselves. Additionally, 6.3% (21/336) of NMOSD patients reported the EQ-5D-5 L full health state (11111).

Patients in the relapse group reported a higher proportion of problems (levels 2–5) than relapse-free patients across all five dimensions. The proportions of patients reporting no problems (level 1) in the relapse group versus the relapse-free group were as follows: mobility (45.6% vs. 64.4%), self-care (72.1% vs. 81.8%), usual activities (48.5% vs. 62.9%), pain/discomfort (17.2% vs. 22.7%), and anxiety/depression (12.7% vs. 18.2%). The largest between-group differences were observed in the mobility and usual activities dimensions, indicating a greater impairment in physical functioning among relapse patients.

Factors associated with HRQoL of NMOSD patients in multivariable analysis

Table 3 presents the results of the multivariable linear regression analysis. Linear regression was adopted for comparability with prior EQ-5D studies and because the EQ-5D-5 L utility value was treated as a continuous outcome. Dichotomization of the utility score would have led to information loss and required an arbitrary cutoff in the absence of an established gold standard. After adjustment, older age, a greater number of comorbidities, and a greater number of chronic diseases were independently associated with lower EQ-5D-5 L utility values, whereas a higher treatment satisfaction score was associated with higher utility values. Compared with patients with no income, those with a personal monthly income of ≥ 5000 CNY had significantly higher utility values. Several lifestyle-related factors, including regular physical activity, daily meat consumption, and late bedtime, were independently associated with higher utility values.

Functional impairment showed the strongest associations with HRQoL. Disability and difficulty walking were both independently associated with substantially lower utility values (both P < 0.001). In addition, utility values decreased progressively with increasing caregiving needs, with the largest negative effects observed among patients requiring caregiving 80% or 100% of the time (both P < 0.001). Relapse status, defined as any history of clinically confirmed acute NMOSD attacks since NMOSD diagnosis, was not independently associated with utility values after adjustment for disease duration and current functional impairment.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed HRQoL among Chinese patients with NMOSD using the EQ-5D-5 L and explored a comprehensive set of associated factors. We found that NMOSD patients in China experienced substantial impairment in HRQoL. The EQ-5D-5 L utility value of Chinese NMOSD patients in our study was 0.665 ± 0.335 (mean ± SD), which is much lower than the Chinese population norm (0.946 ± 0.096) [23]. Among the factors that affected HRQoL, disability status, difficulty walking, and caregiving needs were the strongest independent determinants of HRQoL. To our knowledge, this study is the first nationwide online study to assess HRQoL among NMOSD patients in China using the generic preference-based measures EQ-5D-5 L, and to explore the potential factors influencing HRQoL.

The overall HRQoL observed in our study is broadly consistent with findings from previous international research, while also suggesting that differences across countries may be partly explained by variations in patient characteristics and measurement approaches. Specifically, compared with our Chinese cohort (mean age 40.4 years; mean 5.6 years since first attack; mean EQ-5D-5 L utility 0.665), the UK cohort was older (mean age 53 years), had a longer disease duration (mean 12 years since first attack), and had a more severe disability distribution, with only 26% of patients in the mild disability group (Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) ≤ 4.0) and 24% already in EDSS ≥ 7.0 categories [8]. These characteristics likely contributed to its lower mean utility value of 0.54. The German study reported a utility value of 0.693, which was close to our finding, although that cohort was somewhat older (median age 50 years), had a longer disease duration (median 7 years), and had a median EDSS score of 3.5 [14]. Importantly, the German study also included patients with MOGAD, which limits direct comparability with our pure NMOSD cohort. The Thai study reported a substantially lower utility value (0.41), but direct comparison is less straightforward because it used EQ-5D-3 L rather than EQ-5D-5 L and applied a country-specific Thai value set [16].

The mean utility value in our Chinese cohort fell within the moderate range, likely because, despite the relatively younger age of the participants, a considerable proportion still experienced functional impairment and dependency on care. This interpretation is supported by our regression findings, in which disability, difficulty walking, and caregiving needs were the strongest independent predictors of lower utility values. In addition, anxiety/depression and pain/discomfort were the most affected EQ-5D dimensions in our sample, suggesting that both physical and psychological burdens contributed to the moderate overall utility level.

Across the five EQ-5D dimensions, anxiety/depression and pain/discomfort emerged as the most severely affected domains. Fewer than one in five patients reported no problems in either dimension, underscoring the substantial psychological and pain-related burden of NMOSD. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that anxiety, depression, and chronic pain are highly prevalent among NMOSD patients and contribute substantially to impaired quality of life, even in the absence of acute relapses [10–12]. The unpredictable disease course, fear of relapse-related disability, and long-term treatment burden may predispose patients to psychological distress, while neuropathic pain related to spinal cord and optic nerve damage often persists chronically [12].

Relapse status was associated with lower HRQoL in unadjusted analyses but was not independently associated with utility values after adjustment for functional status and caregiving needs. This finding suggests that the impact of relapse on HRQoL may be largely mediated through its long-term functional consequences rather than through relapse occurrence itself. Consistent with this interpretation, previous studies have demonstrated that longer disease duration is significantly associated with sustained disability and deterioration in quality of life [19].

Some lifestyle-related variables, including staying up late and daily meat intake, were independently associated with higher HRQoL in the multivariable analysis. These findings should be interpreted cautiously. Previous studies have shown that HRQoL in NMOSD is strongly influenced by physical disability, psychiatric distress, pain, and social participation [10, 24]. Therefore, the observed associations in our study may reflect better overall functional status, greater social participation, or other unmeasured aspects of daily living, rather than indicating that such behaviors themselves improve HRQoL. In addition, given the cross-sectional design of this study, temporal sequence cannot be established. Accordingly, these findings should be understood as correlational rather than causal [25]. Future research is warranted to further explore the relationship between behavioral patterns and quality of life among patients with NMOSD.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the use of a web-based survey in this study for data collection may have limited participation among certain demographic groups, particularly individuals with limited access to or familiarity with digital platforms, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, the questionnaire data were self-reported by patients, and disability-related variables, including disability status and walking difficulty, were not assessed using standardized clinical instruments such as the EDSS, which may have restricted a comprehensive and precise assessment of disease severity. Future studies should incorporate standardized clinical assessment tools to improve the validity of measurement. Third, although quota sampling was applied to reflect the known female predominance of NMOSD, the online recruitment strategy may still limit population representativeness. Finally, the questionnaire did not collect information on recent relapse or relapse frequency. As a result, relapse status was defined only according to any history of attacks since diagnosis, which may have been too broad to fully reflect the influence of current disease activity on current HRQoL. Future studies should incorporate more detailed relapse-related measures.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Chinese patients with NMOSD experience substantial impairment in HRQoL, particularly in psychological well-being and pain. Functional impairment and caregiving dependence are the primary determinants of reduced HRQoL, while relapse status is not associated with HRQoL after adjustment for disease duration and current functional impairment. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive, patient-centered management strategies that extend beyond relapse prevention to address long-term functional outcomes, psychological health, and caregiving support in NMOSD.
