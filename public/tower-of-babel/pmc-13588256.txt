Nanomedicine for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: From Pathophysiological Mechanisms and Disease Heterogeneity to Precision Therapy
Authors: Wei Li, Wei Guo, Hao Wang, Jinyan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a life-threatening form of acute respiratory failure characterized by diffuse alveolar injury, severe hypoxemia, and high mortality. Despite advances in supportive care, effective pharmacological interventions remain limited owing to the complex pathophysiology of ARDS, poor pulmonary drug accumulation, systemic adverse effects, and substantial disease heterogeneity. Increasing evidence indicates that distinct clinical and biological subphenotypes of ARDS exhibit differential inflammatory responses, molecular signatures, and therapeutic susceptibilities, underscoring the need for precision medicine approaches. Nanomedicine has emerged as a promising strategy to address these challenges. Through tunable physicochemical properties, surface functionalization, and microenvironment-responsive designs, nanomaterials can enhance drug stability, improve pulmonary targeting, and enable controlled drug release within injured lung tissues. Moreover, nanotherapeutic platforms can exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, barrier-protective, anticoagulant, and antifibrotic effects through passive and active targeting, biomimetic delivery systems, and stimuli-responsive mechanisms. In this review, we summarize the current understanding of ARDS heterogeneity and its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, discuss the limitations of existing therapeutic approaches, and comprehensively examine recent advances in nanomaterial-based therapies and targeted drug delivery systems for ARDS. We further highlight key challenges associated with biosafety, large-scale manufacturing, regulatory approval, and clinical translation. Collectively, this review provides a framework for the rational development of precision nanomedicines and outlines future directions for improving therapeutic outcomes in patients with ARDS.Keywords: immunothrombosis, nanozymes, biomimetics, targeted delivery, drug delivery systems, precision medicine

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe form of acute respiratory failure frequently encountered in intensive care units and remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among critically ill patients. Sepsis is one of the leading extrapulmonary causes of ARDS and is associated with particularly poor clinical outcomes, with mortality rates approaching 40% in severe cases. ARDS is characterized by diffuse alveolar damage resulting from direct or indirect pulmonary insults, leading to increased alveolar–capillary permeability, pulmonary edema, severe hypoxemia, and impaired gas exchange. Histopathologically, ARDS is characterized by diffuse alveolar edema, hyaline membrane formation, alveolar hemorrhage, and inflammatory cell infiltration.1,2 Despite advances in supportive care, ARDS continues to pose a substantial clinical challenge. A major obstacle to effective treatment is its pronounced biological and clinical heterogeneity. Variability in etiology, disease severity, inflammatory responses, imaging characteristics, genetic susceptibility, biomarker profiles, and treatment responsiveness contributes to diverse clinical trajectories and outcomes. Current management relies primarily on supportive interventions, including lung-protective mechanical ventilation, prone positioning, inhaled pulmonary vasodilators, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and adjunctive anti-inflammatory therapies. Emerging approaches such as cell-based therapies and lung transplantation have shown promise in selected patients; however, their broader clinical application remains limited. To date, no pharmacological therapy has consistently demonstrated a significant survival benefit in ARDS, highlighting the need for more effective and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Nanomaterials have emerged as promising platforms for disease diagnosis and therapy owing to their unique physicochemical properties, including nanoscale dimensions, tunable surface characteristics, high drug-loading capacity, and versatile functionalization potential. These features enable targeted drug delivery, controlled drug release, enhanced imaging performance, and reduced off-target toxicity. Nanotechnology-based approaches have demonstrated considerable success across multiple biomedical applications, including molecular imaging, biosensing, infectious disease diagnosis, and targeted therapy.3–5 In particular, nanocarrier systems such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles (NPs), and other engineered nanoplatforms can improve drug accumulation at disease sites, enhance therapeutic efficacy, and minimize systemic adverse effects.6,7 Furthermore, theranostic nanoplatforms integrating diagnostic and therapeutic functions have enabled real-time monitoring of drug distribution and treatment responses.8

The application of nanomaterials in acute lung injury (ALI) and ARDS has expanded rapidly in recent years. Nanotechnology-based platforms have been developed for targeted diagnosis, drug delivery, and microenvironment-responsive therapy. Through mechanisms including inflammatory mediator neutralization, pathogen elimination, attenuation of oxidative stress, immune modulation, and restoration of alveolar–capillary barrier integrity, nanomaterials offer new opportunities for the precise diagnosis and treatment of ARDS. In this review, we summarize current understanding of ARDS heterogeneity and pathophysiology, discuss the limitations of existing therapeutic approaches, and examine recent advances in nanomaterial-based therapeutic strategies and targeted drug delivery systems for ARDS. We further highlight key challenges and future directions for the clinical translation of nanomedicine, with the goal of providing a framework for the rational design of next-generation nanotherapeutic platforms for ARDS.

ARDS: Clinical Heterogeneity and Pathophysiological Mechanisms

Evolution of the Definition and Diagnostic Criteria of ARDS

Since Ashbaugh et al first described ARDS in 1967, its clinical definition has undergone several important revisions, reflecting a progressively deeper understanding of the disease. Initially, ARDS was defined primarily on the basis of clinical manifestations and pathological findings, including reduced lung compliance, pulmonary edema, and hyaline membrane formation9 (Figure 1). Subsequently, the lung injury score was proposed, and parameters such as positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), PaO2/FiO2 ratio, bilateral lung infiltration shown on chest X-ray, and absence of increased pulmonary capillary pressure were included to quantify the severity of the disease, thereby improving the consistency of diagnosis and the predictive ability of mortality.10–12 In recent years, non-invasive respiratory support methods such as SpO2/FiO2 ratio, lung ultrasound examination, and high-flow nasal oxygen therapy have also been incorporated into the ARDS diagnostic framework, emphasizing the continuous assessment of oxygenation dysfunction.2,13

Schematic overview of the major milestones in the evolution of ARDS definitions, including the original description by Ashbaugh et al,9 the Lung Injury Score (LIS), the American–European Consensus Conference (AECC) definition, the Berlin definition, the Kigali modification, and recent updates incorporating noninvasive respiratory support modalities.10–17

Despite these advances, current definitions still have limitations, including inter-observer variability in imaging interpretation and reduced accuracy of the SpO2/FiO2 ratio under specific conditions, such as poor peripheral perfusion, oxygen saturation levels exceeding 97%, and darker skin pigmentation.14,15 Ongoing efforts are exploring the integration of artificial intelligence-assisted image analysis, lung ultrasound, and modified scoring systems, such as the Radiographic Assessment of Lung Edema score, to improve diagnostic accuracy.16,17

Clinical and Biological Heterogeneity of ARDS

Etiology-Based Classification of ARDS

ARDS can be broadly classified according to the source of the initial injury (Figure 2). Direct (pulmonary) ARDS results from primary insults to the lung, including pneumonia and aspiration of gastric contents, features include alveolar epithelial cell (AEC) injury, leakage of protein-rich fluid into the alveolar space, surfactant inactivation, alveolar collapse, hyaline membrane formation, and focal pulmonary consolidation.18 In contrast, indirect (extrapulmonary) ARDS arises from extrapulmonary insults that induce a systemic inflammatory response, such as severe non-pulmonary sepsis, and major trauma with shock. In this subtype, pulmonary vascular endothelial injury predominates, leading to increased capillary permeability and interstitial edema.19,20 These two forms of ARDS differ in biomarker profiles, disease severity, and clinical outcomes.19

Severity Stratification Based on PaO2/FiO2 Ratio and Its Clinical Significance

According to the Berlin definition, ARDS is classified into three severity categories based on the ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to inspired oxygen fraction (PaO2/FiO2) (Figure 2): mild (200 mmHg < PaO2/FiO2 ≤ 300 mmHg), moderate (100 mmHg < PaO2/FiO2 ≤ 200 mmHg), and severe (PaO2/FiO2 ≤ 100 mmHg). This classification has important prognostic value, with in-hospital mortality increasing progressively from approximately 27% in mild ARDS to 45% in severe ARDS.21 Beyond prognostic stratification, the Berlin classification also serves as a valuable enrichment tool for clinical trials.22

Inflammatory Subphenotypes of ARDS

Among biological classification systems, inflammatory subphenotyping has received the strongest support. Using latent class analysis, two subtypes are identified: the hyperinflammatory subtype and hypoinflammatory subtype. They show different mortality and distinct responses to fluid management, PEEP settings, corticosteroids and prone positioning.23

Emerging Subphenotypes and Genetic Susceptibility

Additional trajectory-based subphenotypes (good-prognosis, hyperinflammatory shock, organ-dysfunction/metabolic-disorder) have been validated and exhibit distinct responses to PEEP and fluid strategies.24 Genetic variants, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme insertion/deletion (influencing angiotensin II levels) and ABO blood group (affecting von Willebrand factor and ICAM-1), contribute to individual differences in ARDS risk and severity, suggesting that endothelial dysfunction and coagulation abnormalities may vary among genetically defined subgroups.25–27

Major Pathophysiological Mechanisms Underlying ARDS

Uncontrolled Inflammation and Immune Dysregulation

During sepsis, pathogen-associated molecular patterns and damage-associated molecular patterns activate pattern-recognition receptors (eg, Toll-like receptors (TLRs)), triggers downstream signaling pathways, such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase cascades, resulting in excessive production of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6 (Figure 3). At the same time, the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome further exacerbates the inflammation. A large number of neutrophils infiltrate, releasing proteases, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). NETs can damage alveolar epithelial and endothelial cells, promote the polarization of M1 type macrophages, and also serve as a scaffold for microthrombosis. The increase in NETs is associated with the severity of the disease.28,29 Alveolar macrophages shift towards an inflammatory M1 phenotype, inhibiting the reparative M2 phenotype, and maintaining the inflammatory microenvironment.30 Additionally, excessive inflammation induces T cell apoptosis and functional exhaustion. Th17 cells cooperate with neutrophils to amplify inflammation. Regulatory T cell and IL-10 produce B cells with insufficient function, leading to immunosuppression and increased susceptibility to secondary infections.31

Alveolar–Capillary Barrier Dysfunction

The disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier is the core pathological event of ARDS, leading to increased permeability and refractory pulmonary edema. The main pathological physiological changes include endothelial and epithelial damage as well as impairment of alveolar fluid clearance32 (Figure 3). Activated immune cells release ROS and proteases, directly damaging alveolar epithelium and endothelium. Uncontrolled inflammation and oxidative stress promote the degradation of intercellular connection proteins and widening of the intercellular space, resulting in increased vascular permeability and protein-rich plasma leakage.33–35 Additionally, the functions of epithelial sodium channel, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and aquaporin 5 are severely impaired, and the alveolar fluid clearance ability decreases.36,37 The injury of type II AECs leads to a reduction in surfactant synthesis, exacerbating alveolar collapse and gas exchange disorders.38 These changes collectively result in the accumulation of proteinaceous pulmonary edema fluid, which is the main pathological basis of hypoxemia and respiratory failure.

Programmed Cell Death Pathways in ARDS

In ARDS caused by sepsis, damage to AEC and structural cells directly leads to barrier dysfunction. The mechanism involves various programmed cell death pathways such as apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, which are interconnected and converge into integrated death programs such as PANoptosis, forming a “death network” that amplifies tissue damage and inflammation39 (Figure 3). Oxidative stress and DNA damage activate the endogenous mitochondrial apoptotic pathway in AEC (mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3/7 activation mediated by Bax/Bak Bcl-2-associated X/Bcl-2 antagonist/killer), and exogenous apoptosis can be initiated through death receptors such as Fas. Excessive apoptosis damages epithelial integrity and promotes edema fluid accumulation.40,41 Pyroptosis is triggered by activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and leads to caspase-1/4/11 and gasdermin D cleavage, forming membrane pores that cause cell lysis and release IL-1β and IL-18, exacerbating the inflammatory cycle;42,43 Necroptosis mediates membrane rupture and damage-associated molecular pattern release through the RIPK1–RIPK3–MLKL pathway, aggravating lung injury;44,45 Ferroptosis is triggered by impaired antioxidant defense of GPX4 under iron overload and oxidative stress, resulting in lipid peroxidation, release of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signals, and is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction.46 These programmed cell death pathways jointly promote epithelial loss, amplification of inflammation, and disease progression.

Coagulation Activation and Tissue Repair Disorders in ARDS

In sepsis-associated ARDS, the inflammatory microenvironment induces the pulmonary microvascular endothelium to switch to a pro-coagulant phenotype, with increased expression of tissue factor and decreased expression of thrombomodulin, thereby promoting thrombin generation and fibrin deposition47 (Figure 3). At the same time, neutrophils release NETs, which serve as scaffolds for platelet adhesion and fibrin deposition, promoting immune thrombosis, while the degradation of thrombomodulin weakens the anticoagulation pathway, forming an inflammatory-thrombus positive feedback loop.48 The increase in microthrombus load leads to a significant decrease in pulmonary capillary perfusion, exacerbating ventilation/perfusion imbalance and hypoxemia.

The abnormal repair after lung injury is mainly characterized by the regeneration disorder of type II AECs. Under inflammatory conditions, the proliferation and differentiation of type II AECs are impaired, and the basement membrane is continuously exposed49 (Figure 3). Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) drives fibroblasts to differentiate into myofibroblasts, and combined with platelet-derived growth factor, dual oxidase and other mediators, promotes excessive deposition of extracellular matrix and collagen remodeling49,50 Local macrophages present a pro-fibrotic phenotype, and their phagocytic function decreases, resulting in insufficient clearance of apoptotic cells, continuous release of pro-fibrotic signals, maintaining fibroblast activation, and ultimately forming irreversible fibrous scars.51

Current Therapeutic Strategies and Clinical Limitations in ARDS

Broad-Spectrum Anti-Inflammatory Therapy

Broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory drugs, especially glucocorticoids, exert therapeutic effects through potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory actions (Figure 4). The application of glucocorticoids in ARDS remains controversial, with treatment efficacy influenced by dose, administration time, treatment duration, and patient selection. Current evidence supports the use of glucocorticoids as an adjunctive treatment in the early stages of ARDS (typically within 7 to 14 days after onset), especially for patients with an excessive inflammatory phenotype.52 However, delayed initiation (>14 days) increases the risk of secondary infection and mortality, and requires individualized assessment.53

Current therapeutic strategies for ARDS. Overview of current pharmacological interventions for ARDS, including corticosteroids, targeted anti-inflammatory therapies, pulmonary vascular modulators, barrier-protective therapies, anticoagulants, neuromuscular blocking agents, natural products and traditional Chinese medicine formulations, and other investigational therapeutic approaches.

Targeted Anti-Inflammatory Therapy

Targeted anti-inflammatory therapy aims to regulate specific inflammatory pathways involved in the pathogenesis of ARDS, potentially offering higher precision compared to broad-spectrum immunosuppressants (Figure 4). Anti-IL-6 receptor antibodies (tocilizumab) combined with hormones improve survival rates in severe COVID-19-related ARDS,54 Interferon-β stabilizes the endothelial barrier and reduces leakage by upregulating the CD73/adenosine pathway.55

Lung Vascular Regulators

Inhaled NO selectively dilates blood vessels in the ventilated area, improving oxygenation, but it only brings short-term physiological benefits and cannot improve mortality, the number of days without ventilator use, or other major clinical outcomes, and is only used as a salvage treatment for refractory hypoxia56 (Figure 4).

Barrier Protection and Repair Therapy

KGF, a growth factor for keratinocytes, promotes epithelial proliferation and shows significant potential in vitro studies, but it does not reduce mechanical ventilation time in clinical trials and may even increase mortality57 (Figure 4).

Anticoagulation Therapy

Heparin primarily exerts anticoagulant effects by enhancing antithrombin activity and also has anti-inflammatory properties (Figure 4). It can act by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory mediators.58 Heparin aerosol administration may increase the number of days without ventilator use, but it has not been proven to significantly reduce mortality.59 In specific populations with COVID-19, systemic anticoagulation is beneficial for patients with hypercoagulable subtypes.60

Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) are commonly used during the early phase of moderate-to-severe ARDS (typically within the first 48 h) to improve patient–ventilator synchrony and reduce ventilator-induced lung injury (Figure 4). Beyond these physiological effects, emerging evidence suggests that NMBAs may also exert direct immunomodulatory actions.61 Clinical studies have indicated that early administration of NMBAs may improve outcomes in selected patients.62

Emerging and Investigational Therapeutic Strategies

In vitro experiments have shown that natural products and traditional Chinese medicine preparations have the potential to treat ARDS, but there is still a lack of strong evidence from large-scale randomized controlled trials.63 Several other pharmacological candidates have also been evaluated in ARDS. Statins demonstrated anti-inflammatory and endothelial-protective effects in experimental models but failed to show consistent benefits in large clinical trials of non-COVID-19 ARDS.64,65 Similarly, neutrophil elastase inhibitors have shown a strong mechanistic rationale; however, Phase II studies have not demonstrated definitive clinical efficacy, suggesting that therapeutic benefits may be restricted to specific patient populations or treatment windows.66,67 In contrast, β2-adrenergic receptor agonists, such as salbutamol, were associated with increased mortality in clinical trials, leading to discontinuation of their development for ARDS treatment.68

Challenges of Conventional Drug Delivery in ARDS

The management of ARDS, particularly sepsis-associated ARDS, remains heavily dependent on supportive care centered on mechanical ventilation. Although mechanical ventilation effectively improves oxygenation and reduces respiratory distress, considerable interpatient variability in ventilatory tolerance and disease characteristics limits the effectiveness of standardized ventilation strategies. Moreover, ventilation-associated complications, including pneumothorax, volutrauma, and atelectasis, further increase treatment complexity. Pharmacological therapies also face substantial limitations (Figure 5). For example, glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive agents may increase the risk of secondary infections, while potential hepatic and renal toxicities may limit their long-term or high-dose use.53,69 Furthermore, the inflammatory response in ARDS is highly dynamic and heterogeneous, making it difficult for a single therapeutic strategy to precisely regulate both systemic and local inflammation. Consequently, despite advances in supportive care and pharmacological interventions, ARDS continues to be associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.

Major limitations of current pharmacological therapies for ARDS. Key challenges associated with current ARDS therapies, including limited targeting efficiency, systemic toxicity, poor pulmonary bioavailability, disease heterogeneity-associated variability in therapeutic responses, and the lack of effective antifibrotic interventions.

Limited Targeting Efficiency and Systemic Toxicity

A major limitation of conventional systemic administration is the inability to selectively accumulate therapeutic agents at sites of lung injury. Only a small fraction of administered drugs reaches the alveolar space or pulmonary interstitium at therapeutically effective concentrations. As a result, higher systemic doses are often required, increasing the risk of off-target toxicity. For example, although high-dose glucocorticoids can suppress inflammation, they may also increase the incidence of secondary infections, hyperglycemia, and muscle atrophy.53 Similarly, intravenous antibiotics such as polymyxins often achieve suboptimal concentrations in alveolar fluid, whereas dose escalation may significantly increase nephrotoxicity, particularly in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS who frequently exhibit multiple organ dysfunction.70,71

Poor Pulmonary Bioavailability and Rapid Clearance

The lungs possess multiple physiological barriers that limit drug penetration and retention. These include the airway mucus layer, mucociliary clearance system, alveolar epithelial tight junctions, and pulmonary surfactant layer. While these barriers are essential for maintaining pulmonary homeostasis, they also impede efficient drug delivery to injured tissues. In addition, the extensive pulmonary vascular network facilitates rapid absorption of drugs into the systemic circulation, resulting in accelerated clearance and reduced residence time at the target site.

During ARDS, drug clearance may be altered significantly. A population pharmacokinetic study of methylprednisolone in patients with ARDS showed that plasma protein binding rate in patients with ARDS (46% ± 11%) was significantly lower than ARDS in healthy individuals (72% ± 4%) (p < 0.001), and the clearance rate of methylprednisolone increased in a time-dependent non-linear manner after the start of treatment. The initial clearance rate (CLo) was 13.2 ± 2.4 L/h, and it increased to the maximum value (CLmax) of 25.0 ± 3.6 L/h after about 7 days.72

The bioavailability of inhalation administration is impaired. Results from the multicenter randomized controlled trial of inhaled dornase alfa for COVID-19-related ARDS (COVIDORNASE trial, NCT04355364) demonstrated that the drug bioavailability decreased during ARDS.73 This study showed that inhaled dornase alfa failed to significantly enhance DNAase activity or reduce NET-related markers in the patient’s plasma and airway secretions. The failure of the phase II clinical trial of inhalation polyethylene glycol (PEG)-adrenal medulla (SEAL trial, NCT04417036) might also be related to the problem of drug delivery. The trial was prematurely terminated due to the ineffectiveness of inhalation PEG-adrenal medulla.74 This study did not directly measure the drug concentration in lung tissue, but the lack of clinical efficacy after inhalation administration suggested that the polyethylene glycolization did not effectively overcome the physical barriers of pulmonary surfactant proteinic liquids and edema on aerosol deposition and penetration.

The failure of pulmonary surfactant replacement therapy also indicates the same problem. Multiple Phase III clinical trials of surfactant for adult ARDS did not show mortality benefits.75,76 The insufficient delivery of surfactant in damaged alveoli might be an important reason for the failure of these phase III multicenter clinical trials.77

Overall, the pathological changes of ARDS not only reduce the efficiency of drug delivery to the lungs but also accelerate the clearance of the drugs. Only by simultaneously addressing the problems of low pulmonary bioavailability and rapid drug clearance can promising drugs exert their expected therapeutic potential.

Therapeutic Challenges Associated with Disease Heterogeneity

ARDS is a highly heterogeneous syndrome, characterized by substantial variability in etiology, inflammatory responses, disease progression, and treatment responsiveness. During the course of the disease, patients may transition through distinct pathological stages, including hyperinflammation, immune suppression, and tissue repair or fibrosis. Consequently, the same therapeutic intervention may exert beneficial effects during one stage but prove ineffective or even harmful during another. For example, aggressive anti-inflammatory therapy may be advantageous during the early hyperinflammatory phase but may exacerbate immune dysfunction during later stages.

Furthermore, differences in underlying etiologies, including bacterial infections, viral infections, and trauma, as well as variations in host genetic background, contribute to diverse immune responses and dominant pathological mechanisms. Some patients exhibit cytokine-driven inflammation, whereas others demonstrate prominent endothelial injury, coagulation abnormalities or fibroproliferative remodeling. This biological heterogeneity is considered a major factor underlying the failure of many ARDS clinical trials and the inconsistent efficacy of current pharmacological therapies.

Lack of Effective Antifibrotic Therapies

Although current treatment strategies primarily focus on improving survival during the acute phase of ARDS, effective therapies for preventing or reversing pulmonary fibrosis remain lacking. Persistent fibroproliferative remodeling can result in long-term impairment of pulmonary function, reduced exercise capacity, and diminished quality of life among survivors. The absence of effective antifibrotic interventions therefore represents a major unmet clinical need in ARDS management.

Collectively, these limitations have driven increasing interest in advanced drug delivery systems, particularly nanomaterial-based therapeutic platforms, which offer the potential to improve pulmonary targeting, enhance drug bioavailability, reduce systemic toxicity, and enable personalized treatment strategies for heterogeneous ARDS populations.

Nanomedicine Platforms for ARDS Therapy

Classification and Biomedical Applications of Nanomaterials in ARDS

Nanomaterials have demonstrated broad application potential in biomedicine owing to their unique physicochemical properties and precise targeting capabilities. Based on their composition and functional characteristics, nanomaterials can be broadly classified into organic, inorganic, carbon-based nanomaterials and composite nanomaterials (Figure 6). These platforms possess diverse therapeutic functions, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, drug delivery, and oxygen transport capabilities.

Organic Nanomaterials

Organic nanomaterials mainly include liposomes, polymeric NPs (eg, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) [PLGA]), and protein-based NPs. These materials exhibit excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ease of surface functionalization.78,79 As versatile drug carriers, they can efficiently deliver anti-inflammatory agents, nucleic acids, and other therapeutics to pulmonary inflammatory lesions or immune cells, thereby enabling targeted delivery, controlled release, and reduced systemic toxicity. However, the clinical translation of organic nanomaterials for the treatment of ARDS still faces multiple challenges. The stability of liposomes is poor, and they are prone to aggregation and drug leakage during storage.80 In addition, the shear forces during aerosol administration may disrupt lipid particle structures and cause premature drug release.81 The drug loading capacity of polymer NPs is generally low, typically below 10% (w/w),82 which represents a major limitation of their use as drug delivery systems. The degradation products of some polymers may induce local inflammatory responses.83,84 Moreover, during scale-up from laboratory to industrial production, particle size distribution, drug loading capacity, and release profiles may also undergo significant changes. Furthermore, unified quality control standards and reference materials are currently lacking.85,86 Collectively, these factors limit clinical translation and application of organic nanomaterials.

Inorganic Nanomaterials

Inorganic nanomaterials mainly include metal NPs, such as gold and silver NPs; metal oxide NPs, such as cerium oxide, zinc oxide, and iron oxide; and mesoporous silica NPs.87,88 These materials can serve as targeted drug carriers, and some inorganic nanomaterials also possess intrinsic antibacterial and enzyme-like catalytic activity.89 Some inorganic nanomaterials can directly scavenge ROS without loading drugs, and the high specific surface area and adjustable pore size of mesoporous silica can bring high drug loading capacity (up to 30–50%).89 For example, gold NPs are promising antibacterial agents that can exert antibacterial effects through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of bacterial metabolism and communication.90 Studies have shown that peptide-modified gold NPs can effectively inhibit the TLR signaling pathway and protect against LPS-induced ALI in mice.91 Cerium oxide NPs have mixed valence states (Ce3⁺/Ce4⁺), which can mimic the activities of superoxide dismutase and catalase, effectively eliminate reactive oxygen and alleviate oxidative lung injury.92 Amorphous Ce–Mn–O bimetallic oxide NPs can simultaneously activate superoxide dismutase (SOD)1 and SOD2, and effectively alleviate oxidative stress and lung inflammation in ARDS mouse models through the Keap1/Nrf2/SOD axis.93 Mesoporous silica NPs, owing to their high specific surface area, adjustable pore size, and good biocompatibility, have been widely investigated for targeted drug delivery in ARDS treatment.94,95 However, these materials also raise important safety concerns. Some inorganic nanomaterials are non- biodegradable or degrade extremely slowly, and their long-term retention in lung tissue may induce chronic inflammation and fibrosis.96 Mesoporous silica NPs administered intravenously or intraperitoneally mainly accumulate in the liver, spleen, and lungs, and some studies have reported lethal toxicity.97 In addition, inorganic nanomaterials are easily recognized and cleared by the mononuclear phagocytic system, seriously weakening their pulmonary delivery efficiency, and the narrow window between the therapeutic dose and the toxic dose further limits their clinical application.98,99 In summary, inorganic nanomaterials show clear advantages in the treatment of ARDS, but their biological safety and pharmacokinetic challenges are also real and severe, which constitute major barriers to clinical translation.

Carbon-Based Nanomaterials

Carbon-based nanomaterials mainly include fullerenes, carbon quantum dots, carbon nanotubes, carbon nanofibers, graphene, and diamond-like carbon.100 These materials, owing to their unique sp2 hybridized carbon frameworks, excellent photoelectric properties, high specific surface areas, and good biocompatibility, show considerable potential in the integrated diagnosis and treatment of ARDS.100,101

Carbon dots are one of the most rapidly developing carbon-based nanomaterials in the field of ARDS nanomedicine. Carbon dots are a type of zero-dimensional carbon nanomaterial typically less than 10 nm, possessing tunable photoluminescence properties, excellent biocompatibility, and intrinsic enzyme-like activity.101,102 Carbon dots can act as nanoscale enzyme mimics with superoxide dismutase-, catalase-, and peroxidase-like activities, effectively eliminating ROS1.101 In recent years, various functionalized carbon dots have been explored for ARDS treatment: platinum-doped emodin carbon dots can alleviate lung injury induced by sepsis through targeting the gut microbiota-palmitic acid-fasospasm axis;103 Carbon dots can also be incorporated into ROS-responsive drug delivery systems.101

Graphene and its derivatives, including graphene oxide (GO), also show potential for ARDS treatment. GO can induce invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells to produce TGF-β, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects in iNKT cell-mediated sepsis models.104 Fullerene C60 inhibits oxidative stress-mediated cellular apoptosis and autophagy by regulating the cytochrome c/caspase-3/beclin-1/IL-1α/HO-1/p53 signaling pathway, suggesting its potential for ARDS treatment.105

However, the pulmonary safety concerns associated with these materials remain major obstacles to their clinical translation. Animal studies have shown that inhalation of carbon nanotubes can induce granulomatous inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis, and may also impair the function of regulatory immune cells.106 Following intratracheal instillation, GO can induce persistent lung granuloma formation for up to 90 days, while GO with different physicochemical properties and sizes can elicit inflammatory responses of varying severity.107 In addition, the sizes, layers, oxidation degree, and surface functional groups of carbon-based nanomaterials obtained through different preparation methods vary greatly, resulting in their biological instability, and the degradation, metabolism, and clearance pathways of these materials in the body remain unclear.108

Composite Nanomaterials

Composite nanomaterials represent a rapidly emerging direction in nanomedicine. By integrating organic and inorganic components, these systems combine and often surpass the advantages of individual materials. Representative examples include metal–organic frameworks and graphene–polymer composites. Such hybrid platforms enable targeted and controlled drug release, microenvironment-responsive delivery, multimodal combination therapy, and biosensing applications for the early detection of ARDS-related biomarkers.109–111 The complexity of composite nanomaterials is a major constraint on their clinical translation. The integration of multiple components results in complex preparation processes and creates substantial challenges in scaling up from laboratory synthesis to large-scale production in accordance with GMP standards. These complex manufacturing processes and high production costs pose severe economic barriers to commercialization.112 Composite nanomaterials comprise multiple components, and the regulatory classification and approval pathways for them as single pharmaceutical entities remain unclear.113

Overall, nanomaterials can enhance pulmonary drug targeting, improve therapeutic stability, enable controlled drug release, and increase the therapeutic index of loaded agents. Furthermore, their ability to integrate diagnostic and therapeutic functions offers a promising strategy for addressing major limitations of conventional ARDS therapies, including poor delivery efficiency, limited specificity, and systemic toxicity.

Design Principles and Therapeutic Mechanisms of Nanomaterials

The therapeutic potential of nanomaterials in sepsis-associated ARDS is primarily derived from two complementary properties. First, certain nanomaterials possess intrinsic biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory functions, allowing them to directly modulate disease processes. Second, nanomaterials serve as intelligent drug delivery platforms capable of achieving precise targeting, controlled release, and enhanced accumulation at sites of lung injury. Together, these characteristics provide a foundation for the development of multifunctional nanotherapeutic systems that simultaneously enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce off-target effects.

Nanomaterial-Based Therapeutic Strategies for ARDS

Certain nanomaterials function not merely as passive carriers but also as active therapeutic agents. Through their intrinsic physicochemical properties, these bioactive nanomaterials can directly modulate key pathological processes involved in ARDS, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune dysregulation (Figure 7).

Antioxidant and Nanozyme-Based Therapies

Excessive production of ROS by activated neutrophils and macrophages is a major contributor to oxidative tissue injury in sepsis-induced ARDS. To address this challenge, a variety of inorganic nanomaterials have been engineered to exhibit enzyme-like catalytic activities, commonly referred to as nanozymes, which can efficiently scavenge ROS.114 For example, cerium oxide NPs possess a mixed Ce3⁺/Ce4⁺ valence state that enables SOD- and catalase-like activities, facilitating the removal of superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide while reducing oxidative lung injury.115 Niemiec et al linked cerium oxide NPs (CNP) with anti-inflammatory microRNA-146a to generate CNP-miR146a-1. In a mouse model of bleomycin-induced ALI, intratracheal administration of CNP-miR146a (1 μg/50 μL) increased local miR-146a levels in lung tissue without elevating systemic miR-146a levels. This treatment achieved an injury-protective effect by modulating local leukocyte recruitment in lung tissue and reducing inflammation and oxidative stress.115 Polydopamine NPs (PDA NPs) are rich in phenolic hydroxyl groups and can directly scavenge free radicals while catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2. In a mouse model of ALI induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a single intravenous injection of PDA NPs with a particle size of approximately 150 nm at a dose of 5 mg/kg was administered. The results showed that PDA NPs significantly reduced excessive ROS through their phenolic hydroxyl structure, significantly reducing OS levels in lung tissue (about 60% lower than the control group) and alleviating neutrophil infiltration.116 In a mouse model of acute kidney injury induced by cisplatin, PDA-coated manganese ferrite NPs (PDA@MF NPs) were administered intravenously as a single dose of 4 mg/kg. PDA@MF NPs could continuously catalyze the decomposition of endogenous H2O2 in the renal hypoxic microenvironment, generating O2, alleviating tissue hypoxia, and scavenging multiple ROS species to improve renal function.117 In addition, functionalized β-cyclodextrin NPs incorporating ROS scavengers such as Tempol and phenylboronic acid derivatives can simultaneously eliminate multiple ROS species, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress-induced apoptosis.118

Immunomodulatory and Anti-Inflammatory Nanotherapies

Effective control of inflammation and restoration of immune homeostasis are essential for improving outcomes in ARDS. Certain nanomaterials can directly interact with immune cells and modulate inflammatory responses through surface-engineered functionalities. For example, glycosylated or peptide-modified NPs can bind to pattern-recognition receptors on macrophages, including mannose receptors, thereby facilitating targeted delivery and promoting macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, ultimately attenuating excessive inflammatory responses.119 Liposomes formulated with dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC), a major component of pulmonary surfactant, and non-lamellar lipid nanovesicles prepared using dioleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE) have also demonstrated therapeutic potential. DPPC-DOPE nanovaccine aerosols have been reported to enhance the resistance of pulmonary surfactants to inflammatory mediator-induced inactivation, thereby supporting alveolar stability and lung function.120,121 In addition, highly hydrophilic polymeric nanogels can reduce nonspecific immune cell activation and exhibit anti-inflammatory properties, highlighting their potential as immunomodulatory nanotherapeutics for ARDS.122

Targeted Nanomedicine Delivery Strategies for ARDS

Passive and Active Targeting Based on ARDS Pathophysiology

ARDS is characterized by profound alterations in the pulmonary microenvironment, including disruption of endothelial barrier integrity, increased vascular permeability, and dynamic changes in cellular phenotypes. During disease progression, intercellular junctions between pulmonary vascular endothelial cells become disrupted, resulting in increased vascular leakage. Simultaneously, activated endothelial cells, recruited immune cells (eg, neutrophils and macrophages), and injured AECs exhibit elevated expression of specific adhesion molecules, receptors, and surface antigens. By exploiting these pathophysiological characteristics, nanomaterials can selectively accumulate within injured lung tissues, thereby increasing local drug concentrations while reducing nonspecific uptake by reticuloendothelial organs such as the liver and spleen. Consequently, targeted nanomedicine delivery can enhance the efficacy of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and barrier-protective therapies while minimizing systemic toxicity (Figure 8).

Passive Targeting

NPs with diameters typically ranging from 10 to 200 nm can exploit the enhanced permeability and retention-like effect associated with inflamed pulmonary tissues. Increased vascular permeability facilitates the extravasation and retention of NPs within the inflammatory microenvironment of the lungs, resulting in preferential accumulation at sites of injury123 For example, animal studies of ALI have shown that cationic liposomes and polymeric NPs of appropriate size can accumulate at significantly higher levels in inflamed lungs than in healthy lung tissue.124,125 Recent studies have further expanded the application of passive targeting strategies for ARDS treatment. Men et al developed lung-specific selenite lipid NPs (Dex/DOSEH) for treating endotoxin-induced ALI. During endotoxin-induced lung injury, inflammation increases the permeability of lung tissue vessels, and the lipid NPs selectively accumulate at this site, thereby enhancing the pulmonary delivery of dexamethasone. Dex/DOSEH produced significant anti-inflammatory therapeutic effects in an ALI model.126

Active Targeting

Active targeting is achieved through surface modification of NPs with ligands that specifically recognize adhesion molecules, receptors, or antigens expressed within injured lung tissues.127 Common targeting ligands include antibodies and antibody fragments, such as anti-intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and anti-platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1) antibodies, which target activated pulmonary vascular endothelial cells. Peptide- and protein-based ligands have also been widely investigated, including arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) peptides targeting integrins, mannose targeting macrophage mannose receptors, and pulmonary surfactant protein-derived peptides targeting AECs. In addition, small-molecule ligands such as sialic acid can target E-selectin expressed on activated endothelial cells. Zamora et al found that in lung inflammatory diseases, such as ARDS, large numbers of neutrophils accumulating within the pulmonary vasculature can “intercept” the NPs originally designed to target vascular endothelial cells, thereby substantially reducing active targeting efficiency. This finding provides important insights into the design of active targeting strategies. In particular, when designing nanomedicines targeting the pulmonary vascular endothelium, the potential competition between neutrophils and endothelial cells for targeting ligands under inflammatory conditions should be considered.128 Liu et al constructed an apoptotic neutrophil-mediated drug delivery system (T-NP@ApoNEs), that exploits the inflammatory homing properties of apoptotic neutrophils to achieve targeted delivery to the pulmonary inflammatory microenvironment. In addition to delivering anti-inflammatory drugs, this system may facilitate immunomodulation through macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, thereby providing dual therapeutic functions.129

Microenvironment-Responsive and Stimuli-Triggered Drug Release

Inflammatory lesions in ARDS exhibit distinct microenvironmental characteristics that differ markedly from those of healthy tissues, including acidosis, excessive production of ROS, and overexpression of specific enzymes, such as neutrophil elastase and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). By incorporating stimulus-sensitive materials or cleavable chemical linkages, nanocarriers can be engineered to release their therapeutic payloads selectively upon exposure to these pathological cues. Such microenvironment-responsive delivery systems enable intelligent, on-demand drug release at sites of inflammation, thereby enhancing efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. Consequently, the development of these nanoplatforms has significantly improved the precision and safety of ARDS pharmacotherapy.

pH-Responsive Drug Delivery

pH-responsive nanocarriers are typically constructed using acid-sensitive materials or linkers, such as poly(β-amino ester) and hydrazone bonds. Upon exposure to the mildly acidic microenvironment of inflamed tissues or intracellular endosomal compartments, these materials undergo hydrolysis or structural transformation, thereby accelerating drug release and improving local therapeutic efficacy.130,131 Qiao et al developed a hybrid biomineralized nanovacuum, that enabled responsive drug release in response to the acidic pH of the inflammatory microenvironment. This system was constructed by biomineralizing the surface of liposomes, which significantly enhanced dexamethasone accumulation in lung tissue, reducing systemic adverse effects in an ARDS model.132

Li et al designed a pH-responsive nanospray. This nanospray triggered drug release in the acidic microenvironment of inflamed lung tissue. It was administered directly to the lungs through inhalation, and it demonstrated excellent therapeutic effects in the ARDS model.133

ROS-Responsive Drug Delivery

ROS play important roles in host defense against pathogens and exogenous stimuli; however, excessive ROS production contributes significantly to tissue injury and oxidative stress in ARDS.134 Accordingly, ROS scavenging has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Nanocarriers incorporating ROS-sensitive linkers, such as thioketal bonds and phenylboronic ester derivatives, can undergo stimulus-triggered degradation in ROS-rich environments, resulting in controlled drug release. In addition, materials with intrinsic antioxidant properties, such as polydopamine NPs, can simultaneously facilitate drug delivery and scavenge ROS, thereby providing synergistic therapeutic benefits.123,135 Muhammad et al developed ROS-responsive polymeric NPs with high drug-loading capacity. By incorporating ROS-sensitive chemical bonds, the rapid release of dexamethasone was triggered in the high-ROS environment at the site of ARDS inflammation, thereby achieving precise regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment.136 Li et al developed a pH-responsive nanospray that triggers drug release in response to the acidic microenvironment of inflamed lung tissue. This nanospray is administered directly to the lungs through inhalation and demonstrates excellent therapeutic effects in the ARDS model.133

Enzyme-Responsive Drug Delivery

Activated inflammatory cells in ARDS release a variety of proteolytic enzymes, creating opportunities for enzyme-triggered drug delivery.137 To exploit this feature, peptide sequences specifically cleavable by inflammatory proteases, such as neutrophil elastase, can be incorporated into nanocarriers as molecular “gatekeepers” or responsive linkers.138 Enzymatic cleavage of these peptide segments triggers localized drug release within inflamed tissues, thereby improving delivery specificity and therapeutic efficiency.

Biomimetic Nanomedicine and Cell-Based Delivery Systems

During the development of ARDS, immune cells, particularly neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages, are actively recruited to sites of pulmonary inflammation. In addition, various cell types secrete extracellular vesicles, such as exosomes, which serve as natural mediators of intercellular communication. By exploiting these biological processes, researchers have developed biomimetic and cell-based drug delivery systems capable of achieving highly efficient and intelligent targeting of inflamed lung tissues. These strategies help overcome key challenges associated with conventional NPs, including immune recognition, rapid blood clearance, and limited targeting efficiency. Furthermore, certain biomimetic platforms, such as mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes, possess intrinsic therapeutic activities that can modulate inflammation and promote tissue repair. Zhao et al developed a biomimetic drug NP system encapsulating macrophage membranes (Mφ@Ast-NPs) for the treatment of ALI through nasal administration. This system exploits the inflammatory homing properties of macrophage membranes to deliver astilbin (Ast) to inflamed lung tissue, demonstrating significant anti-inflammatory effects in ALI models.139 Wang et al developed glycyrrhizin-loaded glycyrrhizin protein NPs that reprogram macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype by activating the STAT6/IRF4 signaling pathway, thereby alleviating the improvement of sepsis-induced ARDS. This study not only achieved targeted delivery of dexamethasone but also synergistically promoted the transformation of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype through the immunomodulatory activity of glycyrrhizin.140 A membrane fusion drug delivery system based on coiled-coil lipopeptides (CPE and CPK) can directly release drugs into the cytoplasm through fusion between liposomes and target cell membranes, thereby bypassing lysosomal encapsulation and enzymatic degradation associated with conventional endocytosis pathways. In an LPS-induced ALI model, this system delivered aurantiflorin and effectively reduced the secretion of inflammatory factors, regulated macrophage polarization, and protected barrier function.141

Cell Membrane-Coated Biomimetic NPs

Cell membranes derived from neutrophils, macrophages, platelets, or other cell types can be coated onto NP cores to generate biomimetic nanocarriers. These systems inherit membrane proteins from the source cells, including integrins, selectin ligands, and other adhesion molecules, thereby mimicking the inflammatory homing and immune-evasion properties of native cells.142,143 In a mouse model of LPS-induced acute inflammatory liver injury, a single tail vein injection of neutral granule membrane-coated NPs (NM-NPs; without drug loading) produced significant anti-inflammatory effects. NM-NPs captured chemokines and prevented their interaction with inflammatory endothelial cells, thereby inhibiting the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages to the inflammatory site. Following uptake by phagocytic cells, such as macrophages, they changed the cell phenotype by altering phosphatidylserine and were cleared through the lysosomal degradation pathway.144

In an ALI mouse model, aerosol inhalation of platelet membrane vesicle-coated poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) NPs (PM@Cur-RV NPs), co-loaded with curcumin and resveratrol, was administered. After inhalation, the NPs effectively accumulated in the lungs, and the pulmonary vascular permeability was significantly reduced; the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were decreased, and the infiltrating macrophages polarized towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This study confirmed that the platelet membrane coating strategy can achieve precise targeted treatment of lung inflammation by mimicking the inherent inflammatory homing characteristics of platelets.145

Extracellular Vesicle-Based Delivery Systems

Extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes derived from MSCs, endothelial progenitor cells, or macrophages, have emerged as promising natural drug carriers.146 Owing to their low immunogenicity, excellent biocompatibility, and intrinsic targeting capabilities, exosomes can efficiently transport therapeutic proteins, nucleic acids, and small-molecule drugs to injured tissues. In a male SD rat model of sepsis-induced ARDS (LPS), adipose-derived MSC-derived exosomes (EXAD) combined with exogenous mitochondria (mitoEx) provided effective protection against lung injury. The animals were divided into five groups: control, ARDS-SS model, EXAD monotherapy, mitoEx monotherapy, and EXAD + mitoEx combination therapy. The animals were sacrificed 120 h after ARDS induction. Flow cytometric analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid indicated that the combined treatment group had the lowest levels of inflammatory cells (Ly6G⁺/CD14⁺/CD68⁺/CD11b/c⁺/myeloperoxidase⁺) and albumin levels among all treatment groups. The biomarkers of inflammation (TLR-4/MMP-9/IL-1β/TNF-α), oxidative stress (NOX-1/NOX-2/p22phox/oxidized protein), and mitochondrial damage (cytoplasmic cytochrome C/dynamin-related protein 1) in the combined treatment group were significantly lower than those in the monotherapy groups, while the expression of antioxidants (Nrf-1/Nrf-2) and cell junction proteins (ZO-1/E-cadherin) was significantly higher than that in the monotherapy groups.147 Inhaled administration of overexpressed CD24 gene-engineered exosomes (EXO-CD24) to patients with mild to moderate COVID-19-related ARDS showed therapeutic effects in phase Ib/Ia and phase IIb clinical trials. The study included 126 patients with ARDS. The results showed that EXO-CD24 significantly reduced cytokine and chemokine secretion, improved respiratory distress symptoms, and improved oxygenation in patients. This study demonstrated that the gene-engineered exosome platform EXO-CD24 provided a promising therapeutic strategy for the high-inflammatory state of ARDS.148

Cell-Mediated “Piggyback” Delivery Systems

Cell-mediated delivery approaches utilize circulating immune cells as biological carriers for therapeutic NPs. In this strategy, NPs are engineered to be preferentially internalized by activated neutrophils or monocytes through optimization of surface charge, protein corona composition, and ligand presentation.149,150 Following cellular uptake, these “carrier cells” transport the NPs to inflamed pulmonary tissues during their natural migratory process, enabling highly specific delivery and localized drug release within the injured lung microenvironment.

Pulmonary Delivery Routes of Nanomedicines in ARDS

Administration Routes and Their Advantages and Limitations

Systemic Intravenous Administration

Intravenous (IV) injection is one of the most widely used administration routes in clinical practice. Following entry into the systemic circulation, NPs can accumulate within injured lung tissues through mechanisms including enhanced vascular permeability-mediated retention, active targeting, and cell-mediated delivery. Studies have demonstrated that the accumulation of various nanocarriers, such as cationic liposomes and cyclodextrin NPs, is significantly increased in inflamed lungs compared with healthy lung tissues, thereby facilitating targeted drug delivery.125,132,151

A major advantage of systemic IV administration is its suitability for critically ill patients, particularly those receiving invasive mechanical ventilation, while also enabling simultaneous intervention against systemic inflammation (Figure 9). However, a substantial proportion of intravenously administered NPs is still captured by the mononuclear phagocyte system, particularly in the liver and spleen, thereby reducing delivery efficiency to the lungs.

Comparison of systemic intravenous administration, local pulmonary administration, and combined delivery strategies, highlighting their respective advantages, limitations, and potential therapeutic applications in ARDS. Local Pulmonary Administration

Local pulmonary delivery through nebulized inhalation enables direct deposition of NPs within the respiratory tract and alveolar regions.152 Following deposition, nanomaterials can interact directly with pulmonary tissues, penetrate biological barriers, and be taken up by AECs, thereby prolonging retention within inflamed lung regions. This administration route enables high local drug concentrations to be achieved at the sites of injury while minimizing the systemic exposure, thereby reducing adverse effects associated with therapies such as corticosteroids.

Combined Delivery Strategies

To maximize therapeutic efficacy, several studies have explored the combination of intravenous and intrapulmonary administration. For example, intravenously delivered NPs preferentially target the pulmonary vascular endothelium, whereas intratracheally administered NPs primarily interact with the alveolar epithelium. The combination of these approaches may facilitate more comprehensive restoration of the alveolar–capillary barrier and enhance therapeutic outcomes through synergistic mechanisms. Nevertheless, local and combined delivery strategies also face several challenges. In patients with severe respiratory failure, excessive airway secretions, or mechanical ventilation, the efficiency and reproducibility of aerosol delivery may be compromised. In addition, maintaining NP stability during nebulization, including prevention of aggregation, structural disruption, and premature drug release, remains a significant technical challenge.

Emerging Delivery Strategies

Alternative administration routes and delivery technologies, including transdermal delivery, ultrasound-assisted delivery, and electroporation-mediated delivery, are also being explored as potential approaches for nanomedicine administration. Although these strategies remain largely experimental, continued technological advances may expand their future applicability in ARDS treatment.

For the treatment of ARDS, the choice of nanomedicine delivery route is particularly important because critically ill patients often require mechanical ventilation. Intravenous administration, being relatively independent of the respiratory state, has become a stable and reliable route in the intensive care unit and is also the most widely used approach in current research on nanomedicines for ARDS.153,154 However, intravenous administration is subject to the common limitations of systemic NP delivery, including sequestration by the liver and spleen, which can substantially reduce pulmonary targeting efficiency. Only a small fraction of intravenously administered NPs reaches the pulmonary microvasculature, and an even smaller fraction can cross the pulmonary endothelial barrier and enter the alveolar space, potentially resulting in lower pulmonary delivery efficiency than that achieved by other administration routes.155,156 Nebulized inhalation directly crosses the vascular endothelial barrier, and the drug acts directly on the surface of the alveolar epithelium. This route may substantially increase local pulmonary bioavailability while reducing systemic exposure.155,157 This advantage may be particularly important when the pulmonary alveolar-capillary barrier is relatively intact in the early stage of ARDS.156,157 However, as the disease progresses, large amounts of proteinaceous exudates and edema fluid accumulate in the alveoli, forming a physical barrier covering the epithelial surface, severely weakening the deposition and penetration efficiency of the aerosol particles.155,156 Moreover, mechanical ventilation alters normal airway airflow and particle transport, resulting in considerable variability in the amount of aerosol that ultimately reaches the lungs during administration.155,156 Consequently, recent studies have begun to explore sequential or combined strategies of intravenous and inhalation approaches, attempting to find the optimal balance point between systemic coverage and local concentration.158,159

Factors Influencing Pulmonary Targeting and Delivery Efficiency of Nanomedicines

The therapeutic efficacy of nanomedicines in ARDS, is influenced by multiple factors. The complex inflammatory microenvironment, characterized by excessive production of cytokines, proteolytic enzymes, and inflammatory mediators, profoundly affects the biodistribution, cellular uptake, and therapeutic performance of NPs.160,161 In addition to disease-associated pathological changes, treatment-related factors, including the timing of intervention and route of administration, as well as the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials, such as size, shape, surface charge, hydrophilicity, and surface functionalization, can significantly influence pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, and cellular interactions, ultimately affecting therapeutic outcomes116,162 (Figure 10).

Influence of Physicochemical Properties of Nanomaterials

The physicochemical properties of nanomaterials are critical determinants of their biological behavior and therapeutic efficacy.

Particle Size and Shape

Particle size strongly influences drug release, cellular uptake, biodistribution, and pharmacokinetic behavior.163,164 For intravenous administration, NPs ranging 100–200 nm are generally considered favorable for accumulation within inflamed tissues because they can more readily traverse permeable vascular barriers while avoiding rapid renal clearance and excessive splenic filtration. For inhalation-based delivery, NPs are typically formulated into aerosol particles with aerodynamic diameters of 1–5 μm to facilitate deposition within the distal airways and alveolar regions while maintaining nanoscale primary particle dimensions to promote cellular uptake. The influence of NP morphology on ARDS therapy remains incompletely understood.165 Nevertheless, spherical NPs are generally internalized more efficiently by endothelial cells, whereas elongated or rod-shaped particles exhibit distinct interactions with immune cells. For example, macrophages often display reduced uptake of elongated particles, whereas rod-shaped polymeric NPs may be preferentially phagocytosed by neutrophils, potentially providing opportunities for cell-selective targeting in neutrophil-dominant inflammatory conditions.166

Surface Charge and Hydrophilicity

Surface charge significantly influences interactions between NPs and biological membranes. Positively charged NPs exhibit enhanced binding to negatively charged cell membranes, thereby facilitating endocytosis by endothelial and epithelial cells. Several studies have demonstrated improved pulmonary accumulation and cellular uptake of cationic nanocarriers in ARDS models.167,168 However, other investigations have reported preferential accumulation of anionic nanostructured lipid carriers within inflamed pulmonary tissues, highlighting the complexity of charge-dependent biological interactions.169 In contrast, neutral or negatively charged surfaces, particularly those modified with PEG, can reduce nonspecific protein adsorption, prolong circulation time, and decrease clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Highly hydrophilic surface modifications may also reduce unintended immune activation induced by the nanomaterials themselves.

Surface Modification and Functionalization

Surface engineering using antibodies, peptides, carbohydrates, and other targeting ligands is a key strategy for achieving active targeting.170–172 For example, mannose-modified NPs can selectively target alveolar macrophages through mannose receptor-mediated uptake, whereas RGD peptides can target activated endothelial cells with elevated integrin expression. PEGylation remains one of the most widely used approaches for improving colloidal stability, prolonging circulation time, and enhancing immune evasion.

Material Composition and Biocompatibility

The composition of nanomaterials directly influences their safety profile and biological interactions. Liposomes composed of pulmonary surfactant-mimetic lipids, such as DPPC, exhibit excellent biocompatibility and may provide synergistic therapeutic benefits.159,173 Biodegradable polymers, including PLGA, can be metabolized following drug delivery and are generally considered safe.174 Inorganic nanomaterials, such as mesoporous silica and cerium oxide NPs, offer unique advantages in drug loading and antioxidant activity;92,175,176 however, their long-term retention, biodegradability, and biosafety require careful evaluation. Experimental studies have also shown that highly hydrophilic nanogels can attenuate LPS-induced immune activation and help restore immune homeostasis, suggesting that surface hydrophilicity plays an important role in determining the immunomodulatory properties of nanocarriers.122

Impact of the Pulmonary Microenvironment on Nanomaterial Delivery

NPs administered to the lungs must overcome multiple biological barriers, including airway mucus, pulmonary surfactants, epithelial and endothelial barriers, the interstitial space, and innate immune defense mechanisms.177 The pathological alterations associated with ARDS can further complicate NP delivery. Alveolar injury, pulmonary edema, and structural disruption of the alveolar–capillary barrier may alter NP deposition, penetration, and cellular uptake, thereby affecting therapeutic efficiency.

Precision Nanotherapy Strategies for ARDS

The clinical and biological heterogeneity of ARDS provides a basis for guiding precision nanotherapy.

Different Inflammatory Subtypes of ARDS and Nanotherapy

The hyperinflammatory and hypoinflammatory subtypes of ARDS differ substantially in inflammatory mediator levels, immune cell infiltration, and therapeutic responsiveness. These differences may necessitate distinct nanotherapeutic strategies.

For hyperinflammatory ARDS, therapeutic strategies should focus on limiting excessive inflammatory mediators reducing the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, promoting neutrophil apoptosis and macrophage-mediated clearance, and eliminating excessive ROS. The inhibition of immune cell infiltration can control uncontrolled inflammatory response. Neutrophils adhere to the inflammatory endothelium through the PSGL-1/P-selectin interaction, which is key to the massive infiltration of neutrophils in ARDS. The P-selectin binding platform LA NPs, after loading curcumin, form LA/Cur NPs. This NP reduces neutrophil infiltration by interfering with the PSGL-1/P-selectin interaction, inhibits inflammation through the NF-κB pathway, and clears excessive ROS, ultimately alleviating ALI in vivo.178 NETs play an important role in ARDS inflammatory damage. In vitro experiments showed that neutrophils isolated from critically ill patients with ARDS, after treatment with lung homing nanoliposomes, exhibited a significant anti-NETosis effect and reduced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines −15-19. In the LPS-induced ALI mouse model, lung homing nanoliposomes improved the pathological changes in lung tissue and early respiratory function.179 Neutrophil apoptosis and macrophage phagocytosis are the key to the physiological resolution of inflammation. Inspired by the physiological resolution of inflammation, two NPs were developed, neutrophil-targeted apoptosis recovery NPs (AR NPs) and macrophage-targeted phagocytosis recovery NPs (ER NPs), collectively referred to as AER NPs. The first accelerates the programmed apoptosis of inflammatory neutrophils by AR NPs, and then the macrophage-targeted ER NPs restore the phagocytic function of macrophages. Sequential treatment can effectively promote inflammation resolution.180 ROS scavenging represents another strategy for interrupting the vicious cycle between oxidative stress and inflammation. Chen et al developed a biomimetic and adaptive nanomedicine HCeOx-D@PM (7,8-dihydroxyflavone-loaded hollow mesoporous cerium oxide, covered with platelet membrane) that significantly improved the survival rate of ARDS model animals. HCeOx-D@PM achieves targeted delivery to damaged lung tissue through the platelet membrane shell, and achieves efficient drug loading, active oxygen (ROS) clearance, and penetration of the alveolar-capillary barrier. This drug inhibits inflammation by clearing accumulated ROS, reducing damage to AT2 cells, and responding to caspase-3 activation for adaptive release of 7,8-dihydroxyflavone, promoting AT2 cell proliferation and achieving protective effects in ALI.181

For hypoinflammatory ARDS, nanotargeted therapy may instead focus on restoring immune homeostasis by promoting tissue repair rather than broadly suppressing inflammation. Loaded with dexamethasone, the glycyrrhizin protein NPs (Dex@GNPs) can reprogram macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype by activating the STAT6/IRF4 signaling pathway. In the mouse model of sepsis-induced ARDS, they effectively alleviated lung inflammation and restored the integrity of the alveolar-capillary barrier.140

Nanotherapy for Different Pathophysiological Targets of ARDS

The pathogenesis of ARDS involves multiple interrelated targets, including damage to vascular endothelial and AECs, activation and accumulation of immune cells. Accordingly, nanotherapeutic platforms have been developed to target different cellular and molecular components of ARDS.

Targeting vascular endothelial cells: During ARDS, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) is highly expressed on activated pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, making it a potential target for active NP delivery.182

Targeting AECs: The protection and promotion of their regeneration is the key to the repair in the later stage of ARDS. Chen et al reported that the proliferative capacity of AT2 cells are impaired in the inflammatory environment, which may contribute to persistent alveolar dysfunction and respiratory failure in ARDS. HCeOx-D@PM nanomedicine responds to caspase-3 release to promote proliferation and promotes the proliferation of AT2 cells.181

Targeting neutrophils cells: During ARDS, excessive neutrophil accumulation in the lungs, contribute to persistent inflammation injury. Curcumin-loaded NPs, such as LA/Cur NPs, have been shown to alleviate ALI by reducing local neutrophil recruitment and NET formation.178,183 Liu loaded hesperidin into ROS-responsive liposomes and fused them with activated neutrophil membranes to form a lipid-integrated bilayer structure (TK-NLP). This nanomedicine utilizes the natural inflammatory tendency of the neutrophil membrane to achieve active targeting of the pulmonary inflammatory microenvironment, and releases its cargo in response to elevated ROS levels, effectively reducing neutrophil infiltration and inflammatory factor secretion. In the ALI mouse model induced by LPS, after intravenous injection of TK-NLP, this nanosystem specifically aggregated at the inflammatory site, reducing neutrophil infiltration and inflammatory factor secretion, and alleviating barrier dysfunction, exerting protective effects on ALI.184

Targeting alveolar macrophages cells: Nanotherapeutic can exert anti-inflammatory effects by regulating the polarization state of M1/M2 in the ALI microenvironment. CM@Nar-NPs, Ber-lipo bioactive lung-targeting lipid nanodrugs, and engineered biomimetic nanocapsules DHA@ANeu-DDAB all achieve this by regulating the polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype to inhibit pulmonary inflammation and promote ALI repair.185–187

FDA-Approved Nanoplatforms: Lessons for ARDS Nanomedicine Development

Although no specific nanomedicine has been approved for the treatment of ARDS, several FDA-approved nanocarrier platforms provide valuable technological and translational precedents. Arikayce, an inhaled liposomal amikacin formulation, was approved in 2018 for the treatment of Mycobacterium avium complex lung disease, demonstrating the clinical feasibility of inhaled liposomal drug delivery for pulmonary diseases.188,189 The clinical success of lipid NP mRNA vaccines Comirnaty® (approved in 2021) and Spikevax® (approved in 2022) further confirmed the application potential of nanocarriers in lung-related diseases.190 Additionally, FDA-approved polymer nanocarriers, such as Eligard (approved in 2002, PLGA matrix), and albumin-bound nanocarriers, such as Abraxane (approved in 2012), further established the clinical safety of biodegradable and protein-based nanocarriers.191 These approved nanomedicines provide a technical foundation for the development of nanotherapies for ARDS.

Reasons for Low Clinical Conversion Rate of Nanomaterials in the Treatment of ARDS

Although nanomedicines for ARDS have achieved beneficial results in preclinical studies, the clinical conversion rate remains low, and might be related to the following:

Scale-up production and GMP compliance: The preparation of nanomedicines involves complex processes. During the scaling-up from laboratory milligram levels to industrial kilogram levels, particle size distribution, drug loading, and release curves often undergo significant changes.192,193 Purification methods, endotoxin control, long-term storage stability, and GMP-related regulatory requirements may constitute additional obstacles for industrial production.194

Batch-to-batch stability and quality control: The synthesis of nanocarriers is a highly process-dependent process. If precise control is lacking in each step, significant batch-to-batch variability can occur, leading to unpredictable biological effects.195

Long-term biological safety and in vivo fate: As exogenous materials, require careful evaluation of their long-term safety, biodistribution, metabolism, and clearance after administration. In particular, the prolonged long-term retention of some inorganic and carbon-based nanomaterials in lung tissue may contribute to chronic inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis.196

Non-specific hepatic and splenic accumulation: Most of the NPs administered intravenously are captured by Kupffer cells in the liver and macrophages in the spleen, substantially reducing the fraction that reaches the intended target organ.197,198

Challenges and Future Perspectives of Nanomedicine for ARDS

Despite the considerable promise of nanomedicine for sepsis-associated ARDS, several challenges must be overcome before successful clinical translation can be achieved. One major obstacle is the increasing complexity of advanced nanoplatforms. To improve targeting, controlled drug release, and therapeutic efficacy, nanomedicines often incorporate multiple functional components, such as targeting ligands, stimulus-responsive linkers, and biomimetic coatings. Although these features enhance performance, they also complicate manufacturing, reduce reproducibility, and create barriers to large-scale production and regulatory approval. Safety remains another critical concern. While many organic nanomaterials exhibit favorable biocompatibility, the long-term biosafety of certain inorganic NPs remains incompletely understood. Their biodistribution, biodegradation, metabolic fate, and potential to induce chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, or fibrosis require further investigation. In addition, the biological effects of NP degradation products remain largely unknown. Delivery efficiency also remains suboptimal. Even with active-targeting strategies, many intravenously administered NPs are sequestered by the mononuclear phagocyte system, particularly in the liver and spleen, limiting accumulation within injured lung tissues. Similarly, inhaled NPs must overcome mucus barriers, mucociliary clearance, and phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages, all of which reduce pulmonary retention and therapeutic efficacy. Another limitation is the inability of many current nanoplatforms to adapt to the dynamic progression of ARDS. Disease-associated changes in oxidative stress, protease activity, inflammatory mediators, and pH evolve over time, whereas most “smart” nanomaterials rely on single-stimulus or simple on–off release mechanisms. Future research should focus on developing clinically translatable nanomedicines that balance functionality with simplicity, scalability, stability, and safety. Cascade-responsive delivery systems capable of responding sequentially to disease-stage-specific signals may improve therapeutic precision. Biomimetic approaches, including cell membrane-coated NPs and extracellular vesicle-based delivery systems, offer additional opportunities to enhance targeting while minimizing immune clearance.

The differences between cell culture and animal models and the complex pathophysiological conditions of the human body also contribute to the limited reproducibility and predictive value of preclinical nanomedicine studies. Wan et al used 3D inkjet bioprinting technology to construct an artificial lung tissue structure containing endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, and successfully established an ALI in vitro model through LPS stimulation. This platform allows flexible adjustments of cellular composition to recapitulate the pulmonary inflammatory microenvironment involving different immune cells, providing a 3D in vitro model that is closer to the real situation in the body for the preclinical evaluation of nanomedicines for ARDS.199 Ultimately, integrating ARDS endotyping, precision medicine, and intelligent nanotechnology may facilitate the development of personalized nanotherapeutic strategies and accelerate translation from bench to bedside.

Conclusion

ARDS is a highly complex and heterogeneous syndrome characterized by diverse etiologies, inflammatory phenotypes, disease stages, and genetic backgrounds. Although current therapeutic approaches, including corticosteroids, targeted anti-inflammatory agents, anticoagulants, and barrier-protective therapies, provide benefits in selected patients, their overall efficacy remains limited by nonspecific biodistribution, low pulmonary bioavailability, systemic adverse effects, and variability in treatment responses. Nanomedicine offers a promising strategy to address these limitations. Nanocarriers can selectively accumulate within inflamed lung tissues through enhanced vascular permeability and can further improve targeting through surface-engineered ligands directed toward activated endothelial cells, immune cells, and injured AECs. Microenvironment-responsive systems that respond to pH, ROS, or enzymatic stimuli enable controlled drug release at sites of injury, while biomimetic platforms, including cell membrane-coated NPs and extracellular vesicle-based delivery systems, enhance targeting specificity and reduce immune clearance. Beyond their role as drug carriers, certain nanomaterials possess intrinsic therapeutic activities, including nanozyme-mediated antioxidant effects and immunomodulatory functions that can scavenge ROS, regulate inflammation, and promote tissue repair. These unique properties position nanomedicines as promising platforms for precision therapy in ARDS.

Future advances in ARDS endotyping, biomarker-guided patient stratification, intelligent nanomaterial design, and clinically translatable delivery systems are expected to accelerate the development of personalized nanotherapeutic strategies. By integrating mechanistic insights into ARDS heterogeneity with next-generation nanotechnology, nanomedicine has the potential to improve therapeutic efficacy and long-term outcomes in patients with ARDS.
