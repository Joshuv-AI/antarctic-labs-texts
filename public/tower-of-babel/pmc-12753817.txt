GC-MS characterization of bioactive compounds from Lippia multiflora Moldenke (Verbenaceae) flower essential oils and their antibiofilm potential against resistant bacteria
Authors: Ablassé Rouamba, Moussa Compaoré, Yahaya Zoungrana, Eli Compaoré, Martin Kiendrebeogo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial resistance to antibiotics is a global health challenge, partly due to biofilm formation, which protects bacteria against drugs and the immune system. This study investigated the potential of essential oils from Lippia multiflora flowers to inhibit bacterial biofilms, offering a natural alternative to address bacterial resistance. Antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus mutans was assessed using the agar diffusion method on Luria Bertani agar. The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the serial liquid dilution process and the minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) by the bacterial growth assay on Luria Bertani agar. Biofilm inhibition was tested spectrophotometically at 590 nm using the crystal violet assay, antioxidant activity was performed using the DPPH method, and phytochemical composition was determined by gaz chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis. The essential oils produced larger inhibition zones against S. aureus (28.38 ± 0.23 mm) and S. mutans (24.5 ± 0.25 mm) compared with standard antibiotics (gentamicin disc, 13.5 ± 0.07 mm for S. aureus; oleandomycin disc, 16.5 ± 0.05 mm for S. mutans). MIC was ranged from 0.10 ± 0.00% for both S. aureus and S. mutans, and MBC was not achieved within the tested range (≤ 0.10%). The essential oils inhibited biofilm formation more effectively than salicylic acid (p < 0.001), with inhibition rates of 59.97 ± 0.68% for S. aureus and 71.63 ± 0.15% for S. mutans. Essential oils showed stronger antioxidant activity (DPPH IC50 value of 0.23 ± 0.02 µg/µL) compared with the ascorbic acid (DPPH IC50 value of 0.33 ± 0.01 µg/µL). GC–MS analysis identified 22 terpenes, including major components β-caryophyllene (43.55%), germacrene D (16.44%), elemol (10.33%) and eucalyptol (6.41%). These results demonstrated that the essential oils of L. multiflora flowers are a potent source of antibacterial compounds.Keywords: Bacterial resistance, Biofilm, Essential oil, Lippia multiflora, Gas chromatography-mass spectrometrySubject terms: Health care, Microbiology

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance is a major public health concern1. It occurs when bacteria evolve to withstand drugs designed to kill them, making infections more difficult to treat and increasing risks of severe illness and death2. Bacteria develop resistance through mechanisms such as enzymatic inactivation of antibiotics, efflux pumps that expel drugs, and biofilm formation that reduces drug penetration3. Biofilms are structured microbial communities encased in a protective matrix, which makes them far more tolerant to antibiotics, disinfectants, and immune defenses compared to free-floating (planktonic) bacteria4,5. Biofilm-associated tolerance arises from many factors such as the transfer of virulence factors, the exopolysaccharide matrix that impedes antibiotic diffusion, and other not yet fully understood mechanisms. Bacteria in biofilms can be up to 1,000 times more resistant than their planktonic counterparts6. The close proximity of cells within biofilms facilitates genetic exchange and communication, enhancing resistance and virulence factors production.

Streptococcus mutans, a major contributor to dental plaque and tooth decay, forms biofilms that protect it from immune defenses and antimicrobial agents7,8. This biofilm-forming ability is central to its virulence. Similarly, S. aureus, a Gram-positive pathogen responsible for infections ranging from skin lesions to pneumonia and endocarditis, is notorious for biofilm-associated resistance9. Both species represent clinically relevant targets for new antibiofilm therapies. Bacterial biofilm formation mechanisms and its contribution to enhance bacterial resistance is not fully understood by researchers, that complicates the development of effective antibiofilm drugs for combatting bacterial resistance.

Current research seeks to better understand biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance, while also developing novel therapeutic strategies. Promising approaches include phage therapy, novel effective antibiotics, and natural compounds that inhibit biofilm development10,11. This current research was designed to promote the fight against bacterial resistance through the development of new derived plant antibiofilm compounds.

Lippia multiflora is an aromatic plant belonging to the Verbenaceae family whose leaves and flowers were consumed as tea in the West Africa regions12. Its essential oils have demonstrated antibacterial properties in several studies, attributed to compounds such as linalool, 1,8-cineole, and citral13. Previous studies have shown that L. multiflora essential oils possess antimicrobial activity against various bacteria, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains14. It has been successfully tested against bacteria such as S. aureus and Escherichia coli, two common pathogens and the demonstrated antibacterial properties have been attributed to some secondary metabolites like monoterpenes and phenols14. While its antimicrobial activity against bacteria such as S. aureus and E. coli has been reported, little is known about the antibiofilm potential of its flower essential oils, particularly against resistant strains such as S. mutans and S. aureus. This research focused on the antibacterial and antibiofilm investigation of the essential oil of L. multiflora flowers against resistant bacteria.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

DPPH, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), Luria Bertani (LB) medium, p-iodonitrotetrazolium (INT), agar, ascorbic acid, tween 80 and salicylic acid were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Germany), while methanol and acetic acid were purchased from Pro Labo (France). All reagents are an analytical grades (purity grades ˃ 0.98%). Catalog numbers are available upon request.

Plant material collection

The flowers of wild L. multiflora were harvested in September 5th, 2022 at Loumbila, located 25 km from Ouagadougou (12°31’5.39˝N/1°22’8.39ʺW). Plant collection complied with institutional, national, and international ethical standards, including the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture. A voucher specimen (IC-922) was deposited at the UFR/SVT herbarium, identified by Prof. Amadé Ouédraogo, a botanist of the University Joseph KI-ZERBO. Flowers were shade-dried at room temperature (approx. 28 °C, 5 days) and ground into powder before stored at 4 °C until extraction.

Essential oils extraction

Essential oils were extracted by hydrodistillation in a Clevenger-type apparatus for 4 h (modified from15). 100 g of flower powder was distilled with 1,500 mL distilled water. The distillate was separated and dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove residual water, then stored at 4 °C in amber vials. The extraction yield was 1.11 ± 0. 10% across independent batches (n = 3).

Organoleptic characteristics and physicochemical parameters determination

Color and appearance were recorded visually, and odor was evaluated by inhalation. Relative density was determined at 25 °C using a pycnometer, following AFNOR standards NFT 75.11. Each experiment was tested in triplicate (n = 3).

DPPH quenching assay

Antioxidant activity was measured using the DPPH radical scavenging assay16. Essential oils stock (1% v/v in methanol) was serially diluted into seven concentrations. Each 100 µL sample was mixed with 200 µL of DPPH solution (20 mg/L in methanol). After 15 min at room temperature, absorbance was recorded at 517 nm against a methanol blank. Ascorbic acid served as positive control. % inhibition was calculated as [(A control – A sample)/A control] × 100, and IC50 values were derived by non-linear least-squares fits (R² > 0.99). Experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Antibiotic susceptibility test

Antibacterial activity was tested by agar diffusion assay17. Bacterial inocula were plated in petri dishes containing LB agar at 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL. Whatman discs were impregnated with 10 µL of essential oil (100% in tween 80). Cefotaxime (30 µg) and oleandomycin (10 µg) served as positive controls, and DMSO discs as negative controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and inhibition zone diameters were recorded. Experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Determination of minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC)

In order to choose a non-bactericidal and a non-bacteriostatic concentration of the essential oils for the antibiofilm investigation, the MIC and the MBC of the essential oils were determined as described previously18. The MIC was determined by using the serial liquid dilution process. A half-fold serial dilution of the essential oils in LB containing 1% DMSO was performed in wells of a sterile 96-well plate, and 30 µL of bacterial inoculum (1.5 × 108 CFU) was added to each well. The plate was incubated for 18 h at 37 °C, and 30 µL of 0.2 mg/mL p-iodonitrotetrazolium (INT) was added and incubated for an additional 30 min. The MIC was deduced from the first well in the series devoid of bacterial growth, marked by the absence of the pink INT color.

For the determination of the CMB, samples were taken from the wells without bacterial growth at the INT and placed in petri dishes containing LB-agar growth medium. The petri dishes were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and the CMB was determined from the dish containing the lowest concentration of essential oils without visible bacteria. Salicylic acid was used as positive control and MIC and MBC were tested in triplicate (n = 3).

Antibiofilm assay

Biofilm inhibition was assessed using the crystal violet assay19. Inocula (1.5 × 10^8 CFU/mL) were incubated in 96-well plates with sub-MIC concentrations of essential oils (0.05% v/v) or salicylic acid (100 µg/mL) for 18 h at 37 °C. Wells were washed with distilled water to remove planktonic cells, fixed with 100 µL methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, washed to removed the excess cristal violet, and solubilized with 100 µL acetic acid. Absorbance was read at 590 nm. DMSO 1% served as the negative control and salicylic acid as positive control. % inhibition = [(A control – A sample)/A control] × 100. Each condition was tested in triplicate (n = 3).

GC-MS analysis of essential oils

GC–MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 8860 GC-FID with DB-Wax column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) coupled to a 5977 MSD. Injection volume was 1 µL (20 µL/mL solution in hexane), split ratio 1:3 (MSD), 2:3 (FID). Helium was the carrier gas at 1.0 mL/min. Oven program: 40 °C (2 min), ramp 10 °C/min to 260 °C, hold 10 min (total 34 min). Injector temperature was 250 °C. Mass spectra were recorded at 70 eV. Linear indices [Ix= 100n (tx-tn)/(tn+1-tn)] are defined where tx, tn, tn+1 are net retention times. Compounds were identified by comparison with NIST library available as a part of NIST/EPA/NIH Mass Spectral database and the compounds RI (PEG), the CAS numbers are verified20. Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean value of several independent repetitive experiments (n = 3) ± standard deviation. The degree of significance between the results was verified by One Way ANOVA analysis of variance followed by Newman Keuls post-test. A statistical difference was observed at P < 0.05.

Organoleptic characteristics and the physicochemical parameters of the essential oils

Relative density at 25 °C was 0.9449 ± 0.0009 g/mL (n = 3), within the AFNOR NFT 75.11 range for Lippia spp. (0.897 ± 0.006 average). Colour is pale yellow; appearance is clear, mobile liquid; odour is mint-camphoraceous with herbaceous notes (sensory panel, n = 3). Minor deviations from AFNOR descriptors likely reflect harvest season and pedoclimatic factors.

Anti-DPPH activity

Antioxidant testing showed EO IC50 = 0.23 ± 0.02 µg/mL (n = 3), significantly lower (better antioxidant activity) than ascorbic acid IC50 = 0.33 ± 0.01 µg/mL; unpaired Newman Keuls-test, mean difference = 0.10 µg/mL (95% CI 0.05–0.15), P < 0.001. IC50 values were derived by non-linear least-squares fits (R² > 0.99). These finding suggested the presence of strong antioxidant compounds in the EO or a synergistic effect of the oils phytoconstituents.

Discussion

The essential oils possess antibacterial properties due to their chemical composition, particularly their volatile compounds, which can inhibit bacterial growth and multiplication13,14. These findings, obtained in vitro, suggest that L. multiflora flower essential oils have antibacterial and antibiofilm activities that warrant further investigation in relevant in vivo models. No conclusions about clinical prevention, treatment, or immunomodulation can be drawn from the present data. Indeed, previous studies showed that essential oils have the properties of inhibiting bacterial growth by preventing the multiplication of bacteria, the formation of spores, the synthesis of their toxins and the organization of their plasma membrane21. They could also have the properties of enhancing the action of antibiotics and neutralizing the mechanism of bacterial resistance22. Strategies targeting the formation of S. mutans and S. aureus biofilms, particularly by interfering with the metabolism of exopolysaccharides (EPS), offer promising pathways for preventing infections caused by these bacteria. These strategies aim to disrupt EPS synthesis, degrade existing EPS, or interfere with the regulatory systems controlling their production. S. mutans uses glucosyltransferases (Gtf) to synthesize glucans from sucrose8. Inhibiting these enzymes, either directly or by targeting their regulatory pathways could reduce the amount of produced EPS, thereby impeding the biofilm formation. EPS, particularly glucans, form a matrix that binds S. mutans cells to each other and to the tooth surface, contributing to the biofilm formation and protection. Enzymes such as glucanases could degrade this matrix, disrupting the biofilm and potentially making the bacterium more susceptible to other treatments23. Previous data on the antibiofilm activity of L. multiflora flowers EOs are not available. Given the high β-caryophyllene content, we hypothesize that specific oils constituents may interfere with biofilm pathways (e.g., S. mutans glucosyltransferases or adhesion factors). However, the present study did not assess Gtf activity, EPS quantification, or target binding; these mechanisms remain to be tested (e.g., enzyme assays, EPS biomass, qPCR of gtfB/C/D, or ligand-binding studies in the target species). Indeed, molecular docking studies identified the β-caryophyllene as a potent inhibitor of enterococcal surface protein (Esp) involved in the initial adhesion and the formation of biofilm24.

Futhermore, The essential oils exhibited growth-inhibitory activity (MIC 0.10% v/v for both species), while MBC was not achieved within the tested range; thus bactericidal activity was not established. Future time-kill and MBC assays across wider ranges, plus standardized comparisons (e.g., checkerboard FIC with antibiotics), are required. In previous studies, L. multiflora leaves EOS have exhibited antibacterial activity against several bacterial strains such as S. aureus (MIC = 1.2 ± 0 mg/mL), S. enterica (MIC = 4.2 ± 0.1 mg/mL) and S. dysenteria (MIC = 4.4 ± 0.1 mg/mL), the major compound was P-cymene (21, 3%)14. Similarly to the antibiofilm activity, the β-caryophyllene would be responsible of the bacterial growth inhibition activity of EOs, Indeed, in previous studies, β-caryophyllene showed higher inhibitory activity on the growth of S aureus than the antibiotic kanamycin25. In addition, β-caryophyllene showed antibacterial activity against S. mutans, especially at concentrations above 0.078%26. It has been also suggested that β-caryophyllene could be an alternative to chlorhexidine, a commonly used antiseptic, in the prevention and treatment of periodontal diseases27.

Previous researches have shown that antioxidant molecules could modulate biofilm formation by interfering with signaling and communication mechanisms between bacteria, thus preventing the biofilm formation. For example, antioxidants like ascorbic acid could degrade the extracellular matrix of the biofilm, making bacteria more vulnerable to antimicrobial treatments28. By weakening the biofilm, antioxidants could potentially increase the effectiveness of antibiotics, allowing better penetration and more effective action against bacteria. In this current study, the essential oils showed better antioxidant activity than ascorbic acid. Antioxidant activity (DPPH) could co-occurre with antibiofilm effects, but causality was not tested. Dedicated experiments (ROS modulation, antioxidant controls at equipotent IC50, biofilm imaging) are needed to determine whether redox effects contribute to biofilm inhibition.

Limitation

This study is limited to in vitro assays; we did not perform time-kill studies, cytotoxicity/selectivity testing, mechanistic validation (EPS/Gtf), or in vivo/clinical evaluations. Moreover, Gtf activity, EPS quantification, or target binding must assessed for more interpretation of the biofilm inhibition mechanisms.

Conclusion

L. multiflora flower essential oils showed in vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm activities against S. aureus and S. mutans. Given its β-caryophyllene-rich profile, it is a promising candidate for further preclinical evaluation, including safety, mechanism, and efficacy in relevant models.
