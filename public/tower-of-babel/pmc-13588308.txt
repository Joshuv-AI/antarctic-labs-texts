Knowledge, Practice and Perceived Barriers of Healthcare Provider Adherence to the Notifiable Disease Surveillance System (NDSS) in Government Hospitals and Primary Healthcare Centers in Jazan, Saudi Arabia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Moatasem F. Hakmi, Alaa Mathkour, Yousef Zahrani, Rayan I. Binduhayyim, Saad S. Alqahtani, Muhammad Z. Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:To assess and compare healthcare providers’ understanding of the Notifiable Disease Surveillance System (NDSS), alongside reported and perceived challenges, across public hospitals and primary healthcare centers in Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted between 10 January 2025 and 30 December 2025 among health care providers at Jazan hospitals and primary healthcare centers (PHCs) (n = 420). Data were collected via structured self-administered questionnaire that included the following sections: demographic data, knowledge of the NDSS, reporting practices, competency of the Health Electronic Surveillance Network (HESN) and perceived barriers. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to analyze the data.Results:Most participants knew the purpose of the NDSS (78.1%) and knew the diseases that needed to be reported immediately (71.9%). Only 50% reported notifiable diseases to health authorities, but 77.4% reported suspected or confirmed cases during the previous year. Only 24% of the participants reported receiving formal HESN training, and 59% reported poor knowledge of HESN reporting. Nurses and laboratory staffs exhibited significantly higher noncompliance rates than physicians and public health workers were. The most significant obstacles were high workload (68.6%), poor supervision (71.7%) and a lack of communication infrastructure (59.5%).Conclusion:In Jazan, healthcare providers reported fair knowledge of the NDSS, but optimal reporting was lacking. Support for the implementation of NDSS through strengthening practical surveillance training, improving digital reporting competency and strengthening institutional support can enhance the performance of NDSS and preparedness for outbreaks.

Introduction

Public health surveillance serves as the foundational backbone of modern healthcare systems. Continuous monitoring is carried out through organized steps such as collecting health data and examining them for patterns. Findings are then interpreted and shared with relevant public health stakeholders. Planning and implementation are supported through this process. The evaluation of interventions is also made possible through surveillance activities. Reducing disease burden and preventing death remain the central goals, while population health outcomes improve [1]. Technology alone does not define whether surveillance is effective. Timely and usable information must be produced so that actions can follow. Decisions on disease prevention and control are strongly shaped by this type of evidence [23].

Among surveillance components, the notifiable disease surveillance system (NDSS) holds a key position. Specific diseases are targeted because reporting is mandatory due to outbreak risk or serious health impact [4]. Disease trends can be monitored through the NDSS, and early response measures can be triggered. The information produced by notifications enhances community health policies and decision-making. This system also helps in mobilizing resources during emerging health crises. Preparedness for epidemics is strengthened as reporting becomes more systematic. NDSS implementation also contributes to international obligations, including international health regulations [56].

For many years, disease notification has mostly been left to healthcare professionals. The greatest responsibility for reporting has traditionally been assigned to physicians and nurses because they are usually the first to meet patients [78]. Laboratories have assumed more of a prominent role over time. Cases have been progressively confirmed using laboratory networks, and notifiable conditions reported through laboratory systems have increased the quality and completeness of data [910]. Without frontline providers, notification cannot be successful. Cases suspected or confirmed must be detected properly and reported immediately [11]. Reporting is a legal requirement in both public and private healthcare in Saudi Arabia. It must be notified even in cases where laboratory confirmation is still pending, but there is clinical suspicion [1213]. Simultaneously, confirmed laboratory diagnoses should be reported directly to health authorities. This forms a two-fold reporting channel to increase the sensitivity of surveillance [14].

Healthcare workers themselves determine the effectiveness of NDSS. As compliance is directly driven by their knowledge and attitudes. Delayed reporting or underreporting may occur because of weak awareness or understanding of responsibilities. This undermines the ability of the system to regulate the spread of diseases and allocate resources efficiently [1516]. In many parts of the world, reporting compliance continues to be a major challenge. It is usually more apparent in low- and middle-income countries where the infrastructure is restricted and training is not necessarily available [1718]. The opposite is the case in high-income countries, which have increasingly embraced electronic systems of surveillance. These tools facilitate reporting and help manage the data more quickly and accurately. In contrast, many LMICs still use manual or semiautomated methods. This enhances the chances of human error and loss of data [41920].

Saudi Arabia is a developed, high-income nation with a strong infrastructural system of public health. Significant advances in surveillance have been achieved in the past few decades. The Ministry of Health introduced the Primary Health Care model in the 1980s. This model focuses on the prevention and control of communicable diseases. Primary healthcare facilities were placed as the initial point of contact with the communities [21]. The New Model of Care was advocated more recently by the national health transformation agenda. The importance of preventive services was also emphasized, and the role of PHCCs in the health system was also reinforced [22]. Repeated threats of infectious disease influenced these reforms. Some of them include MERS-CoV, dengue fever, and malaria. The number of such diseases has been higher in southern regions, such as Jazan [23242526].

Jazan is a province found in southwestern Saudi Arabia and is located toward international borders. It places unique pressures on public health. Population mobility driven by cross-border movement. Climatic conditions also facilitate the transmission of vectors. These factors contribute to the spread of communicable diseases and the need for early detection systems [2627]. To optimize monitoring, the Health Electronic Surveillance Network (HESN) was introduced. It is an internet-based tool that is created to aid in reporting cases and investigating outbreaks. It also performs immunization tracking [28]. The Deputy Ministry of Public Health is the central office of HESN. It connects healthcare providers with national authorities by providing secure real-time reporting channels [22]. However, the implementation of HESN has not entirely resolved the compliance issue. The issue of timeliness, completeness, and provider engagement has been observed in past studies [2930].

Globally, several barriers to NDSS compliance have been identified, Including, inadequate training is a major issue. Reporting guidelines may be unclear or unknown. Certain providers believe that notifying is the duty of nurses. Reduced motivation and engagement may also be experienced because of the absence of feedback on reporting [1617313233]. In 2018, a Saudi Arabian study reported similar challenges. However, the investigation had geographic limitations and did not target geographical locations with high burdens, such as Jazan [34]. The evidence in this area is still scant. Critical evaluations of knowledge practice patterns and perceived hindrances have not been commonly reported.

This study was undertaken to address these identified literature gap by evaluating healthcare professionals working across public hospitals and primary care facilities in Jazan, Saudi Arabia. Knowledge and reporting practices are evaluated. Perceived barriers to NDSS compliance are also explored.

Methods

The investigation followed an analytical cross-sectional framework. The investigation was carried out across public hospitals and primary care centers located in Jazan. According to the Ministry of Health's annual statistical report, Jazan has 21 ministry-operated hospitals and 165 primary healthcare centers [35].

Study population

Healthcare personnel employed at secondary-care hospitals and community-based primary care facilities across Jazan were invited to participate. The sample population included physicians, nursing staff, laboratory specialists, and public health practitioners.

The inclusion criteria included eligible participants consenting healthcare professionals employed in public-sector hospitals or primary care facilities within Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. This group included physicians, nursing personnel, laboratory practitioners, and public health specialists. However, the exclusion criteria included healthcare providers working outside the selected government facilities, administrative staff without clinical or surveillance responsibilities, interns or students, and participants who submitted incomplete questionnaires.

Sampling technique

Participants were recruited using a nonprobability convenience sampling method. Healthcare workers stationed at government-run hospitals and PHC centers throughout Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, were the focus of recruitment. Participants who were available during the data collection period and agreed to participate were invited to complete the questionnaire.

Sample size

We used a 95% confidence interval and a 5% margin of error, with a 50% prevalence used to make the calculation as conservative as possible. The computations were carried out using Raosoft software, and a minimum sample size of 384 respondents was obtained. To reduce the impact of incomplete responses, the final recruited sample was increased to 420 participants.

Data collection tool

A structured self-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. The instrument was developed by revising measures used in earlier research from Riyadh, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, and South Africa [3416]. The questionnaire items were refined to reflect the regional healthcare context, the HESN+ surveillance platform, and the occupational categories represented among participants. The questionnaire was translated into Arabic to make it suitable for the target participants. Items were designed to measure sociodemographic information knowledge reporting practices, NDSS compliance and perceived barriers to notification. The finalized questionnaire consisted of 43 questions covering respondents’ background characteristics, their approaches to reporting diseases, competencies related to notification and case handling, understanding of prevention and professional training, and views regarding the NDSS. The instrument underwent pretesting with a limited group of health professionals to examine question clarity, language suitability, response time, and the digital form's operational reliability. Based on their feedback, minor wording and formatting changes were made before the final distribution. The pretest participants were not included in the final analysis.

Skill items were rated on a 10-point scale, where 1 indicated very low skill and a greater need for support or training, while 10 indicated very high skill and no need for additional support. For descriptive presentation, the responses were grouped into low- and very high-skill categories. The total notification and case-management score ranged from 7 to 70, while the prevention and training score ranged from 4 to 40. Higher scores indicated better self-reported skills.

Data collection procedure

The questionnaire was created through Google Forms. A web link was shared electronically with eligible participants. Distribution was performed using email and social media channels such as WhatsApp.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Numerical variables were summarized using the mean and standard deviation. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using an independent sample t test. One-way ANOVA was applied when more than 2 groups were compared. Categorical variables are described as frequencies and percentages. Potential predictors of the study outcomes were examined through linear regression analysis. The results were considered to be statistically significant when the two-sided p value was less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The research protocol received approval from the ethics committee at King Khalid University before data collection commenced. All participants provided informed consent before enrollment, and their privacy was protected throughout the research process. No identifying personal details were collected, and participant names were not recorded.

A total of 101 participants (24%) reported receiving Health Electronic Surveillance Network Plus (HESN+) training. The mean (±standard deviation) work experience was 4.75 (±4.8) years.

The perception scores differed significantly by professional category and age (both p < 0.001). Males had slightly higher perception scores than females did (p = 0.045). The NDSS training and formal epidemiology training were also associated with higher perception scores (both p < 0.001). No significant differences were found by health care sector (p = 0.979) or facility type (p = 0.499).

The multivariable analysis revealed that nurses (p < 0.001) and medical laboratory staff (p = 0.001) had significantly lower scores for notification and case management than physicians did. More positive views of the NDSS were significantly associated with greater professional experience (p = 0.034) and stronger disease-notification and case-management competencies (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study identified a clear gap between health care providers’ knowledge of NDSS requirements and their self-reported reporting practices.

The majority of the respondents knew the purpose of the NDSS. More than half of the respondents (4/5) identified its intent correctly at a rate of 78.1%. This finding was matched by a similar percentage (71.9%) who admitted that some of the diseases need to be reported urgently. However, only half of the providers facing notifiable conditions in the previous year who reported them. This lack of connection is common in the surveillance literature and has been noted in several environments. Instituting rules does not necessarily lead to compliance. Riyadh-based Saudi research found similar discrepancies in terms of the identification of reporting requirements, but notifications were not fully realized [3436]. Similar results have also been found in South Africa. The idea of surveillance can be appreciated theoretically, but underreporting can continue in everyday practice, as the surveillance practice is not entirely supported by the systems in place [16]. The current findings are more alarming because the exposure rates are high in Jazan. More than three quarters of the respondents (77.4%) reported that they had experienced notifiable cases during the past year. This finding is an indication that chances for notification were common. The low reporting level does not appear to be explained by limited exposure to notifiable cases. It may instead reflect gaps in reporting practices and facility-level support.

There was another concern, timeliness. Only 25% of the participants chose to report immediately within the first 4 hours, whereas an equal percentage reported notification in the last 72 hours. The diffusion of responses indicates a variability in response to reporting schedules. Similar delays have been reported in Taiwan and Nigeria, thus uncertainty about reporting channels and a lack of tools were closely associated with the late notifications [3738]. This finding differs from other studies in which systems are developed to support staff. Effective reporting channels and supervision are beneficial for enhancing rapid notification and outbreak responses [30]. The observed delays could thus be an indication of a work environment in which surveillance competes unsuccessfully with clinical responsibilities. This explanation is reinforced by the fact that workload is a widely reported barrier. Delay is not a small technical issue in an area such as Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Outbreaks move rapidly and early detention constitutes the gap between containment and escalation [26]. Therefore, even when reporting latencies are typical at other locations, their effects may be worse in this environment.

Among the most eye-opening results of the research is linked to the Health Electronic Surveillance Network. The HESN was adopted to update the notification process and enhance performance in the area of national surveillance. However, in ordinary practice the system appears to be rarely used. Not all providers felt that they could use the platform. More than half (57.9%) rated their skill level as low. Even worse, 59% of the providers said that they were unaware of how to use HESN to notify authorities of cases. This finding aligns with previous evidence from Saudi Arabia indicating the weak adoption of HESN because of the lack of training and irregular user participation [2834]. Similar challenges have been outlined in primary health care informatics more generally, where training gaps and system underutilization minimize the utility of digital tools [29]. In contrast, in other environments, electronic reporting has been demonstrated to be effective when the system is user friendly and training is integrated into normal workforce development processes [30]. Thus, access to technology may not be the key problem; rather integration appears to be the challenge. The benefit of a surveillance platform may be limited when health care providers are not sufficiently familiar with its use. The HESN might be theoretically but not practically useful. This finding implies that national investment will not meet its full benefit in digital surveillance unless hands-on training and technical support are continually provided.

There were professional differences as well. Doctors and health workers had higher knowledge and compliance scores than nurses and laboratory personnel did. This trend is in line with past studies conducted in Saudi that indicated that physicians were more aware of regulations and definitions of cases. This advantage is probably due to their diagnostic role [3439]. Public health workers can also be anticipated to do well since their training and their day-to-day activities are closely associated with surveillance activities [16]. Of particular interest is the poor performance of the nurses. The highest number of respondents in this study were nurses, accounting for 36.9% of the total participants. This implies that their reporting knowledge or skills, which have slight weaknesses, can massively impact overall NDSS performance. The outcomes of laboratory personnel are also important. Laboratories are becoming increasingly important in enhancing the quality of surveillance, as confirmed diagnoses and laboratory reporting increase data completeness [910]. Laboratory-based reporting in well-functioning surveillance systems also contributes to reducing overdependence on physicians as the sole provider of notification. The current findings indicate that laboratory professionals in Jazan might not be well integrated into notification processes. There may be a lack of role clarity, inadequate training, or poor coordination. This is unlike in the world where the strengthening of laboratories has been encouraged as a priority in surveillance.

One of the most practical performance determinants proved to be training. Only 24% of the participants received HESN training. Fewer than half of respondents (46.2%) had completed a formal qualification or structured training in epidemiology or disease monitoring. These figures show an apparent capacity gap. The same trends have been observed in other places, where poor levels of training coverage translate to poor underreporting and inconsistent levels of compliance [163440]. This also conforms to the broader perspective that surveillance can rely on a trained workforce and not just policies or platforms [15]. Reporting in an environment where training on surveillance is often enhanced. In this research, training seems to be intermittent. Institutionalization of skill building may not be a reality. Disease surveillance concepts are not the only concepts of HESN that limit training needs. It also involves reporting steps of operations. The fact that HESN has low confidence is thus an indicator of a lack of exposure as well as reinforcing. Electronic surveillance assessments indicate that its use increases with regular training, which is practical and facilitated by technical support [30]. In the case of Jazan, the shift should be made to apply the competency to active awareness.

The obstacles mentioned by the participants identified the importance of the organizational structure. A total of 68.6% of the respondents chose workload as a significant challenge. A total of 71.7% reported a lack of supervision. Access to communication tools was limited among 59.5% of the respondents. These results are quite similar to those of previous studies in other countries that identified time pressure administrative load and low support as the 2 factors that were always associated with underreporting [3132]. Contrasting better reporting performance is usually observed when facilities offer monitoring and feedback. When health care workers receive feedback on their reports, their motivation is enhanced, and compliance is more routine [30]. The feedback did not seem to be regularly available in the current study. This lack can influence attitudes toward reporting as unnecessary additional work but not as a valuable contribution to the health of the population. The strong focus on weak supervision is also indicative of a larger problem. There can be a lack of accountability mechanisms. When expectations are reinforced, reporting is enhanced. Supervision is most important in work-intensive environments.

Notification can also be slowed down by infrastructure constraints. The essential items in operating surveillance are communication gadgets and effective reporting channels. These are lack of timeliness and completeness, where environments that are more digitally integrated note reduced barriers in infrastructure, which indicates that practical barriers can be eliminated through the enhancement of connectivity and access. This is especially true in regard to Jazan since fast action is crucial owing to the regional communicable disease burden [26]. An investment in access to internet availability systems and tools that help with reporting could thus have a direct effect on the performance of NDSSs.

The problem here was not the attitude toward the NDSS. In this research, 61.4% of the respondents answered that they would be ready to participate in the surveillance activities. It is a good observation and indicates that the need to report disease is recognized by many health care professionals. The same findings have been documented in other countries with the widespread participation of health care workers in surveillance despite the lack of strong reporting practices [16]. However, willingness by itself was not enough to ensure compliance. A positive attitude did not necessarily lead to actual reporting behavior. This means that one cannot easily be motivated without a reporting system that is easy and realistic to utilize. Hands-on training can aid in the development of confidence and competence. Supervision and frequent monitoring should be employed to promote accountability. Consciousness alone will not correct the NDSS gaps in Jazan. Proper facility-level support is required to enable health care providers to report cases easily and regularly.

Strengths and limitations

This paper has numerous strengths. The 420 participants provided a good foundation on which comparisons could be made. The variety of health care occupations thus increased the sample's breadth. By sourcing data from both hospitals and primary health care centers in Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, we aimed to capture compliance patterns across diverse facility types. The evaluation was thorough as well. Knowledge levels were analyzed and not measured in isolation but in relation to actual reporting behavior. Barriers were delved into detail, and this ensured that gaps such as weak HESN competency and low training exposure were identified.

Limitations also have to be taken into account. The design was a cross-sectional design, so cause and effect could not be determined. Self-reporting of practices might not have been accurate to actual behavior, resulting in bias. There is a possibility that some respondents have not recalled events correctly, resulting in recall bias. Social desirability may have led to the overreporting of good practices by others. Convenience sampling was used to lower the extent to which the outcomes can be generalized to all health care providers in the area. Although the questionnaire was adapted from previous studies and was pretested for clarity and functionality, internal consistency reliability, such as Cronbach's alpha, was not assessed in the present study. This should be considered when interpreting the scale-based findings.

These limitations notwithstanding, the study is informative. The results reveal actual drawbacks, which can be used to implement specific measures to improve the performance of the NDSS in Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

Public health implications

The results of this research offer practical guidance for enhancing the surveillance of communicable diseases in Saudi Arabia. High-risk environments, such as Jazan, are even more in need. Failure to report on time or delayed surveillance eliminates its early warning capability. Outbreaks can be identified later than desired. The investigation of public health can be delayed. Prevention can also be taken too slowly, which could increase the general risk.

System-level strengthening is necessary to enhance better NDSS compliance. The awareness of individuals is not sufficient to enhance reporting performance. HESN training and surveillance should no longer be considered options in workforce development. Better supervision at the facility level is essential for enhancing accountability and follow-up. Infrastructure also relies on reporting because communication tools and access to the system need to be good. Practice should have feedback mechanisms to ensure that health care providers are not bored and that the worth of notification is clear to them.

The priority groups should be considered when interventions are designed. Weaker reporting competency should receive special attention. Nurses and laboratory personnel are of particular concern, as enhancing their reporting can enhance NDSS sensitivity and timeliness. The increase in NDSS in Jazan may result in a faster response to outbreaks and the enhancement of disease control. It could also promote national preparedness and resilience in Saudi Arabia's population health.

In conclusion, we assessed health care professionals at governmental hospitals and PHC facilities in Jazan, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, to investigate their adherence to the Notifiable Disease Surveillance System. All were knowledge-reporting behaviors and perceived barriers. The knowledge of NDSS goals and anticipated reporting schedules was fairly adequate. However, there was no uniformity in notification practices. Underreporting and late reporting were common even in cases of constant exposure to suspected or known notifiable diseases. There was a strong discrepancy between what the providers knew and what they practice was therefore apparent.

A number of factors may explain this lack of compliance. Participants were generally limited in their ability to use the Health Electronic Surveillance Network. Exposure to training was also wanting. Additionally, reports of high workloads, poor supervision and a lack of communication facilities were many institutional constraints. Enhancing the NDSS in Jazan will involve organized capacity building, enhanced skills in electronic reporting and enhanced facility-level facilitation to make reporting timely and complete. To achieve enhanced preparedness to face the outbreak and better control of communicable diseases, such changes are needed.
