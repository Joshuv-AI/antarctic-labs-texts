Reply to González González et al.: The contribution of bacteria to kidney stone formation
Authors: William C. Schmidt, Rena Yang, Henry L. Schreiber, Aaron Celestian, Gerard C. L. Wong, Kymora B. Scotland
Published: 2026

We thank González González et al. (1) for their thoughtful comments on our work (2). They note that more work on how our findings relate to extant nucleation mechanisms will be valuable. We agree. In fact, we note that there are nucleation pathways that have not been sufficiently considered in extant work, and that the field can benefit from broader inquiry. Below we address the various points raised in the comment.

That urine is not sterile is the original motivation of our study. It is known that renal pelvic urine cultures correlate to postoperative infectious complications. We agree that additional investigation into the presence of bacteria in preoperative midstream and renal pelvic urine cultures can potentially help understand how the urinary microbiome contributes to stone formation. However, there is only partial concordance between urine and stone cultures. Additionally, the species culturable in this manner may not reflect the actual species present within the stones. Our strategy to get the most direct readout of stone-resident bacterial species is to culture directly from the stones themselves. Moreover, we also expect the presence of microbes classified as “viable but nonculturable” (VBNC) (3) within the stringent environments of kidney stones. We wholly agree that the full spatiotemporal dynamics of the distinct microbiomes at all loci relevant to nephrolithiasis will be valuable.

At present, it is not generally known for kidney stones exactly what the ‘earliest’ mineral nidus is, nor how many different kinds of mineral nidi there are, their respective compositions or structures (4). For example, mature kidney stones are often aggregates of smaller stones rather than simply overgrowth on existing nuclei, which has been one of the dominant paradigms. This observation is not surprising given the complex environment of the kidney, which can potentiate entropy-driven depletion interactions. To survive in the kidney, bacteria need to maintain a 4 order of magnitude Ca concentration difference with their external environment despite their low metabolism. Our work here [consistent with the work of others (5)] shows that bacterial extracellular DNA release, which solves this problem, is strongly amplified in the physiochemical conditions of urine. We can show how the structure of extracellular DNA in particular is a good template for direct nucleation of Ca oxalate itself. We are continuing our studies of stone morphology and internal architecture using state-of-the-art materials science techniques for the same reasons noted by González González et al., and are expanding our investigations to include studies at the crystallite level to evaluate the role of extracellular DNA in nucleation. We agree that the classical theory of Randall’s plaques and intratubular plugging can account in part for the formation of some calculi (4). Our presentation of a role for bacteria opens up a pathway of exploration into a unifying theory of nephrolithiasis.
