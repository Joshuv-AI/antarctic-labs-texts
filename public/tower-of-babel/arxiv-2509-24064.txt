Abstract
. In this paper we introduce the edge inducibility problem. This is a common refinement of both the well known Kruskal–Katona theorem and the inducibility question introduced by Pippenger and Golumbic. Our first result is a hardness result. It shows that for any graph G G , there is a related graph G ′ G^{\prime} whose edge inducibility determines the vertex inducibility of G G . Moreover, we determine the edge inducibility of every G G with at most 4 4 vertices, and make some progress on the cases G = C 5 , P 6 G=C_{5},P_{6} . Lastly, we extend our hardness result to graphs with a perfect matching that is the unique fractional perfect matching. This is done by introducing locally directed graphs , which are natural generalizations of directed graphs.



1.
Introduction

Given a graph
G
, determining the maximum number of copies or induced copies of
G
in a graph
H
with some combinatorial restrictions lies at the heart of extremal graph theory. Two well-known problems falling under this description are the Kruskal–Katona theorem and the inducibility question. In this paper, we study a new and natural question in the common refinement of these problems.

To make our discussion rigorous, for graphs
G
and
H

N(G,H)
and
N_{\ind}(G,H)
to be the number of copies and induced copies of
G
in
H

c_{\mathcal{H}}(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup\{N(G,H):H\in\mathcal{H}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\ind_{\mathcal{H}}(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup\{N_{\ind}(G,H):H\in\mathcal{H}\},

where

is some family of graphs.

The inducibility question of Pippenger and Golumbic
[

asks to determine

for the most natural family of graphs

Finally, our attention is directed to the new problem that we suggest. Indeed, the above discussion naturally leads to the following question.

Question 1.2

For a graph

In this paper, we are interested in studying Question

(1.1)

(1-o_{G}(1))\left(\frac{m}{e(G)}\right)^{\alpha^{*}(G)}\leq\eind(G,m)\leq\frac{(2m)^{\alpha^{*}(G)}}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}.

This is an immediate corollary of Theorem

Definition 1.3

For a graph
G

\eind(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert\eind(G,m)}{(2m)^{\alpha^{*}(G)}}\in[0,1].

We remark that this graph parameter was implicitly studied by Bollobás, Nara, and Tachibana
[
6

for the case of balanced complete bipartite graphs.

Our first result, perhaps surprising, shows that the edge inducibility problem—i.e. determining
\eind(H)

Theorem 1.4

Suppose that
G
is a graph, then there is a graph

such that

\frac{\ind(G)}{2^{v(G)}v(G)!}=\frac{\eind(G^{\prime})}{|\Aut(G^{\prime})|}.

Let us note that this is indeed a hardness-type result, as resolving the edge inducibility yields a solution to the vertex inducibility.
That said, for natural graphs, this problem seems more tractable than the vertex inducibility. Supporting this, in

To be more specific, the graphs where the argument can be generalized to are precisely graphs

For any graph

Due to its technicality, we only remark that a locally directed graph is a generalization of a directed graph, and we postpone its formal definition until

The first is a lower bound on

Let

2.1.
Notations

Throughout the paper, we use the following standard graph theoretic notations. For a graph
G
, we write
V(G)
to denote its vertex set, and write
v(G)
to denote its number of vertices. Furthermore, we write
E(G)
for the edge set of
G
and write
e(G)
to denote the size of
E(G)
.

We also use the following standard notions from fractional graph theory.
Suppose that
G
is a graph. A function
\alpha\colon V(G)\to[0,1]
is said to be a
fractional independent set
of
G
if for every
uv\in E(G)
we have
\alpha(u)+\alpha(v)\leq 1
.
The
fractional independence number
of
G
, denoted by
\alpha^{*}(G)
, is the maximum of
\sum_{v\in V(G)}\alpha(v)
, running over all fractional independent sets
\alpha
of
G
.
Lastly, a function
w\colon E(G)\to[0,1]
is called a
fractional matching
of
G
if for every
v\in V(G)
, we have
\sum_{u:uv\in E(G)}w(uv)\leq 1
.
If the equality holds at every vertex, then we call it a
fractional perfect matching
.

Finally, we will also use some notations regarding homomorphisms and embeddings when it is more convenient for us.
Let
G,H
be two graphs.
We say that a function
\varphi\colon V(G)\to V(H)
is a
homomorphism
from
G
to
H
if for every
uv\in E(G)
, we have
\varphi(uv)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\varphi(u)\varphi(v)\in E(H)
.
An injective homomorphism
\varphi
is called an
embedding
.
If an embedding
\varphi
from
G
to
H
furthermore satisfies
\varphi(uv)\not\in E(H)
for every
uv\not\in E(G)
, we call
\varphi
an
induced embedding
.
We denote by
E_{\ind}(G,H)
the number of induced embeddings from
G
to
H
, and note that
E_{\ind}(G,H)=\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert\cdot N_{\ind}(G,H)
.
Let us remark that with this notation, for every graph
G
we have

\eind(G)=\limsup_{m\to\infty}\max_{H:e(H)=m}\frac{E_{\ind}(G,H)}{(2m)^{\alpha^{*}(G)}},

which we sometime use.

2.2.
Shannon entropy

For any discrete random variable
X
, its support
\supp(X)
is the set
\{x\mid\mathbb{P}(X=x)>0\}
. Throughout this paper, every random variable mentioned is discrete and has a finite support, i.e. the size of its support is finite. Now, we may introduce the definition of Shannon entropy of a random variable.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a random variable
X
, its Shannon entropy is given by

\mathbb{H}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in\supp(X)}-\mathbb{P}(X=x)\log_{2}\mathbb{P}(X=x).

For any sequence of random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
, the joint entropy
\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
is the entropy of the random tuple
(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
.

We also define the conditional entropy of
X
given
Y
,

\mathbb{H}(X\mid Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{H}(X,Y)-\mathbb{H}(Y).

Next, we state some properties that are frequently used in
Section

5.2
. We refer the readers to
[
2
,
17
]
for more details about entropy. First, we have the uniform bound which helps us connect the number of objects we are counting and the entropy of random variables.

Proposition 2.2

(Uniform bound)
.

For any random variable
X
, we have

\mathbb{H}(X)\leq\log_{2}\left\lvert\supp(X)\right\rvert,

where equality holds if and only if
X
is a uniform random variable on
\supp(X)
.

Next, we have the chain rule, which follows from the telescoping sum of conditional entropy.

Proposition 2.3

(Chain rule)
.

For any random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
, we have

\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})=\mathbb{H}(X_{1})+\mathbb{H}(X_{2}\mid X_{1})+\dots+\mathbb{H}(X_{k}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{k-1}).

We also need the following two inequalities about entropy.

Proposition 2.4

(Dropping conditioning)
.

For any random variables
X,Y,Z
, we have

\mathbb{H}(X\mid Y,Z)\leq\mathbb{H}(X\mid Y).

Proposition 2.5

(Subadditivity)
.

For any random variables
X,Y,Z
, we have

\mathbb{H}(X,Y\mid Z)\leq\mathbb{H}(X\mid Z)+\mathbb{H}(Y\mid Z).

The last proposition we need is the mixture bound. This bound plays an important role in the recent development of proving the Kruskal–Katona theorem and Turán’s theorem with the entropy method
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Here, we only state a simplified version that we need. The full statement and proof can be found in
[
9
]
.

Definition 2.6

(Mixture)
.

Given random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
, we say that
Z
is a mixture of
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
if
Z
is of the form
X_{\mathbf{i}}
, where
\mathbf{i}\in[k]
is an independent random index.

Proposition 2.7

(Mixture bound)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
be random variables with mutually disjoint supports. That is,
\supp(X_{i})\cap\supp(X_{j})=\varnothing
holds for any distinct
i,j\in[k]
. Then there exists a mixture
Z
of
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
such that

2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1})}+\dots+2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{k})}\leq 2^{\mathbb{H}(Z)}.

2.3.
Simple bounds on the edge inducibility

We conclude this section with the following lemma, which as mentioned in the introduction, is a simple corollary of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For every graph
G
without isolated vertices and a nonnegative integer
m
we have

(1-o_{G}(1))\left(\frac{m}{e(G)}\right)^{\alpha^{*}(G)}\leq\eind(G,m)\leq\frac{(2m)^{\alpha^{*}(G)}}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}.

Proof.

Note that
\eind(G,m)\leq c(G,m)
always, and thus Theorem
1.1
implies the required upper bound.
For the lower bound, let
\alpha\colon V(G)\to[0,1]
be a fractional independence set achieving
\alpha^{*}(G)
. Let
(V_{v}:v\in V(G))
be a sequence of mutually disjoint sets satisfying
\left\lvert V_{v}\right\rvert=\left\lfloor\left({m}/{e(G)}\right)^{\alpha(v)}\right\rfloor
. Define
G^{*}
to be the graph with vertex set
\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}V_{v}
and edge set consisting of all pairs
xy
, where
x\in V_{v}
and
y\in V_{u}
such that
uv\in G
. Note that

e(G^{*})=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\left\lvert V_{v}\right\rvert\left\lvert V_{u}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{uv\in E(G)}\left(\frac{m}{e(G)}\right)^{\alpha(v)+\alpha(u)}\leq m.

Moreover,

N_{\ind}(G,G^{*})\geq\prod_{v\in V(G)}\left\lvert V_{v}\right\rvert=(1-o_{G}(1))\left(\frac{m}{e(G)}\right)^{\alpha^{*}(H)}.

This implies the required lower bound.
∎

By definition, we immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.9
.

For every graph
G
without isolated vertices,

\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}{(2e(G))^{\alpha^{*}(G)}}\leq\eind(G,M)\leq 1.

3.
Edge inducibility is harder than vertex inducibility

The starting point for our discussion is the edge inducibility problem for
P_{4}
, which is notably the only 4-vertex graph for which vertex inducibility in the sense of Pippenger and Golumbic
[
31
]
is not completely understood. We think of
P_{4}
as the union of a matching and an edge; for each of these components (matching and edge) we know the extremal host graph construction (matching and complete graph, respectively). A natural question would be whether we can suitably “stitch together” these two constructions into an extremal host graph for
P_{4}
.

Example 3.1

(Edge inducibility for
P_{4}
)
.

One natural construction for the host graph
H
is to start with a complete graph
K_{t}
, for
t
to be chosen, and then attach an
s
-star to each vertex of the
K_{t}
(see
Figure

1
). Sometimes this graph can be written using the Corona product
[
16
]
as
K_{t}\circ\overline{K_{s}}
.
In this case,
e(H)=st+\binom{t}{2}
. The number of embeddings of
P_{4}
in
H
can be counted by selecting an edge in the
K_{t}
and then choosing a vertex in each of the stars at the endpoints, for a total of
2\cdot\binom{t}{2}\cdot s^{2}
. By taking
t\ll s
, and letting
s,t\to\infty
, we can check that
\eind(P_{4})\geq\frac{E_{\ind}(P_{4},H)}{(2e(H))^{2}}\to\frac{1}{4}
.

On the other hand, we may also note that taking every alternate edge of
P_{4}
gives a matching on two edges. We may easily upper bound the number of induced embeddings of
P_{4}
in the host graph
H
by the number of ordered pairs of edges, giving
E_{\ind}(P_{4},H)\leq m^{2}-m
. That is,
\eind(P_{4})\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{m^{2}-m}{(2m)^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}
showing that the lower bound is in fact tight and we have
\eind(P_{4})=\frac{1}{4}
.

This example suggests why the edge inducibility problem for
P_{4}
is more tractable than the vertex inducibility problem for
P_{4}
, since it is in fact in some sense equivalent to the vertex inducibility problem for an edge. A moment of thought reveals that this same kind of argument shows that for general graphs we expect the edge inducibility problem to be basically at least as difficult as the vertex inducibility problem. Indeed, for any graph
G
, construct
G^{\prime}
by attaching a pendant edge to each vertex of
G
. The next theorem shows that the edge inducibility problem for
G^{\prime}
can be reduced to the vertex inducibility problem of
G
.

Theorem 3.2

(Edge inducibility is “as hard as” vertex inducibility)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices, and let
G^{\prime}
be obtained by attaching a pendant edge to each vertex of
G
. Then

\frac{\ind(G)}{2^{n}n!}=\frac{\eind(G^{\prime})}{|\Aut(G^{\prime})|}.

Proof.

For the upper bound on
\eind(G^{\prime})
, the main idea is that “pinching” the pendant edges of
G^{\prime}
in the extremal host graph
H^{\prime}
gives
G
. As such, for every host graph
H_{m}^{\prime}
with
m
edges, that satisfies
N_{\ind}(G^{\prime},H^{\prime}_{m})=\eind(G^{\prime},m)
, we construct an auxiliary host graph
H_{m}
on
m
vertices. This is done as follows: each edge
e
of
H_{m}^{\prime}
corresponds to a vertex
v_{e}
of
H_{m}
, and the edge set of
H_{m}
is defined to be
v_{e}v_{f}
for
e,f\in H_{m}^{\prime}
such that
e\cap f=\varnothing
, and there is an edge of
H^{\prime}_{m}
intersecting both
e
and
f
. This way, every induced copy of
G^{\prime}
in
H^{\prime}_{m}
gives rise to an induced copy of
G
in the host graph
H_{m}
that we constructed. By accounting for automorphisms of
G^{\prime}
and the fact that
\alpha^{*}(G)=n
, we get

\displaystyle\eind(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G^{\prime})\right\rvert N_{\ind}(G^{\prime},H_{m}^{\prime})}{(2m)^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{m\to\infty}\left(\frac{n!\cdot N_{\ind}(G,H_{m})}{m^{n}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G^{\prime})\right\rvert}{2^{n}n!}\right)

\displaystyle=\ind(G)\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G^{\prime})\right\rvert}{2^{n}n!}.

To prove the lower bound on
\eind(G^{\prime})
, we draw inspiration from Example
3.1
. We start with the extremal host graph
H
for the vertex inducibility of
G
, and add a star with
s
vertices to each vertex of
H
to form the host graph
H^{\prime}
for
G^{\prime}
. More precisely, let
H
be the extremal host graph for the vertex inducibility of
G
on
t
vertices. As in Example
3.1
, for some
s
to be chosen we define
H^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}H\circ\overline{K_{s}}
. Now, each induced copy of
G
in
H
corresponds to
s^{n}
many induced copies of
G^{\prime}
in
H^{\prime}
, and the total number of edges in
H^{\prime}
is bounded above by
e(H^{\prime})\leq\binom{t}{2}+st
. By taking
s\gg t
so that
s
grows sufficiently fast relative to
t
, and letting
t\to\infty
, we can bound the number of induced copies of
G^{\prime}
in
H^{\prime}
to get

\mathrm{eind}(G^{\prime})\geq\left\lvert\Aut(G^{\prime})\right\rvert\cdot\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{s^{n}\cdot\ind(G,t)}{\left(2\cdot\left(\binom{t}{2}+st\right)\right)^{n}}=\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G^{\prime})\right\rvert\cdot\ind(G)}{2^{n}n!},

as desired.
∎

4.
Generalization: local digraph

In the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, the strategy to relate the edge inducibility of some graph to the vertex inducibility of some other graph is the following.
We think of edges in a host graph
H
as vertices in some other auxiliary graph
H^{\prime}
.
In addition, for any two edges
e,f\in E(H)
with
e\cap f=\varnothing
, we may record whether there are any edges going across
e
and
f
using edges of
H^{\prime}
.
However, this transformation going from
H
to
H^{\prime}
is somewhat lossy, as there are actually four possible edges going between
e
and
f
.
The motivation of considering locally directed graphs is to fully retain information from
H
when transforming to
H^{\prime}
in the hope that we can generalize
Theorem

3.2
further.
It turns out that with the correct setup, we not only can generalize the upper bound on the edge inducibility in
Theorem

3.2
, but we can also extend the argument for the lower bound.

4.1.
Locally directed graphs

In this subsection, we first define locally directed graphs and then define some operations that will be useful later.

A directed graph can be thought of as a graph where for each edge, it is an out-edge at one endpoint and an in-edge at the other endpoint.
If we allow an edge to be an out-edge at each endpoint, or an in-edge at each endpoint, we obtain what we call a
locally directed graph
.
To avoid confusion and for our convenience, we will use plus and minus signs instead to specify the “direction” of an edge at a vertex.

Definition 4.1

(Locally directed graph)
.

A
locally directed graph
, or
local digraph
, is a multigraph with no self-loops with the following additional data: for each vertex
v
and each edge
e
incident to it, the pair
(v,e)
has either a positive sign or a negative sign.
Let
\textup{sgn}(v,e)
be the sign of
e
at
v
.

Two local digraphs
G,G^{\prime}
are thought to be isomorphic if there are bijections
f=(f_{V},f_{E})
where
f_{V}\colon V(G)\to V(G^{\prime})
and
f_{E}\colon E(G)\to E(G^{\prime})
so that it is an isomorphism between multigraphs and also for any two edges
e,e^{\prime}\in E(G)
with some vertex
v\in e\cap e^{\prime}
, we have
\textup{sgn}(v,e)=\textup{sgn}(v,e^{\prime})
if and only if
\textup{sgn}(f_{V}(v),f_{E}(e))=\textup{sgn}(f_{V}(v),f_{E}(e^{\prime}))
.

It turns out that each isomorphism class of local digraphs has a representation in graphs and a representation in digraphs, and both will be useful for us.
We first begin by defining the graphical representation.

.

, where

In the opposite direction, for any graph

, we may reverse the process and define a local digraph with

Definition 4.4

(Double cover)
.

Let

Its

4.2.
Locally directed acyclic graphs

Having defined what local digraphs are, we now study the local-digraph analog of acyclicity.
Later, we will show that this is the property that a local digraph would naturally possess if it comes from a graph with a perfect matching being its unique fractional perfect matching.
Along the way, we will also prove some useful properties of acyclic local digraphs.

Definition 4.5

(Locally directed acyclic graph)
.

Given a local digraph
G
. A
locally directed closed walk
in
G
is a walk
v_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}v_{2}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{t-1}}v_{t}\xrightarrow{e_{t}=e_{0}}v_{t+1}=v_{1}
with
\textup{sgn}(v_{i},e_{i})\neq\textup{sgn}(v_{i},e_{i-1})
for all
i\in[t]
.
We call
G
a
locally directed acyclic graph
or
LDAG
if it does not have any locally directed closed walk.

We would like to warn the readers that unlike the case for graphs or digraphs, having a locally directed closed walk is different from having a locally directed closed cycle, a closed walk that uses no repeated vertices.
For example, the local digraph in
Figure

6
has a directed closed walk
514523
, yet the only cycles in the local digraph are
514
and
523
, both of which are not locally directed at
5
.
However, it is true that each locally directed closed walk can be reduced to a locally directed closed walk that uses each vertex at most twice.
We will also omit the proof of this claim as this will not be used, though it can be extracted from the proof of
Proposition

4.6
.

Next, we show that acyclicity is preserved under taking double covers.

Proposition 4.6
.

A local digraph is acyclic if and only if its double cover is acyclic.

Proof.

We first show that if a local digraph has a locally directly closed walk, then its double cover has a directed closed walk.
To show this, it suffices to consider the double cover of a locally directed closed walk
v_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}v_{2}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{t-1}}v_{t}\xrightarrow{e_{t}=e_{0}}v_{t+1}=v_{1}
.
Now set
s_{i}=\sgn(v_{i},e_{i})
for each
i\in[t]
and set
s_{t+1}=s_{1}
.
Then one of the edges that
e_{i}
is lifted up to is
v_{i}^{s_{i}}\to v_{i+1}^{-\sgn(v_{i+1},e_{i})}
, which is simply
v_{i}^{s_{i}}\to v_{i+1}^{s_{i+1}}
as
s_{i+1}=\sgn(v_{i+1},e_{i+1})\neq\sgn(v_{i+1},e_{i})
.
Therefore we have found a directed closed walk
v_{1}^{s_{1}}\to v_{2}^{s_{2}}\to\cdots\to v_{t}^{s_{t}}\to v_{1}^{s_{1}}
, as desired.
Reversing each step, we can also see that each directed closed walk in the double cover projects down to a locally directed closed walk in the original local digraph.
∎

Now we can transfer properties of acyclic digraphs, also called DAGs, to properties of LDAGs.
One property that we need regards what is known as
topological sorting
.

Definition 4.7

(Topological sort)
.

For any LDAG
G
, an ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of
V(G)
is a
topological sort
of
G
if there is a choice of signs
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
such that for any
1\leq i<j\leq n
and any edge
e
between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, we have
\textup{sgn}(v_{i},e)=s_{i}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Every LDAG admits a topological sort.

Proof.

Let
G
be an LDAG with
n
vertices, and let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2n}
be a topological sort of the double cover
\overline{G}
of
G
, so that each directed edge goes from
u_{i}
to
u_{j}
for some
j>i
.
For each
v\in V(G)
, delete the later appearance of
v^{+}
and
v^{-}
in the ordering
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2n}
.
Suppose that
v_{1}^{s_{1}},\ldots,v_{n}^{s_{n}}
is the resulting ordering.
We claim that any edge
e\in E(G)
between
v_{i},v_{j}
with
i<j
must satisfy
\textup{sgn}(v_{i},e)=s_{i}
, which would imply that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
is a topological sort for
G
.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that
\sgn(v_{i},e)=-s_{i}
for some
1\leq i<j\leq n
and edge
e
between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
.
Then we know that there is a directed edge in
\overline{G}
going from
v_{j}^{\sgn(v_{j},e)}
to
v_{i}^{-\sgn(v_{i},e)}=v_{i}^{s_{i}}
.
However, by the choice of the ordering and the signs, we know that
v_{j}^{\sgn(v_{j},e)}
does not appear earlier than
v_{j}^{s_{j}}
, which appears later than
v_{i}^{s_{i}}
in the topological sort of
\overline{G}
.
This is a contradiction, as desired.
∎

Now we can prove the main proposition of this subsection, which explains why we are interested in LDAG.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
G
be a graph and
M
be a perfect matching.
Then
M
is the only fractional perfect matching of
G
if and only if
\ldg(G,M)
is acyclic.

Proof.

We first show that if there is another fractional perfect matching, then
\ldg(G,M)
is not acyclic.
Let
f\colon E(G)\to[0,1]
be such that
\sum_{e\ni v}f(e)=1
for every
v\in V(G)
.
Now whenever
f(e)=1
for any edge
e
in the matching
M
, remove both its endpoints together with all edges that use any of the two endpoints from
G
, and also remove it from
M
.
We repeat this process until we end up with a graph
G^{\prime}
and a perfect matching
M^{\prime}
where
f(e)<1
for every
e\in M^{\prime}
.
Then we see that
\ldg(G^{\prime},M^{\prime})
is an induced subgraph of
\ldg(G,M)
as local digraphs, and so it suffices to show that
\ldg(G^{\prime},M^{\prime})
is not acyclic.
Note that since
f(e)<1
for every
e\in M^{\prime}
, any
v\in V(G^{\prime})
is incident to some edge not in
M^{\prime}
.
As a consequence, for any vertex
v\in V(\ldg(G^{\prime},M^{\prime}))
and sign
s\in\{\pm\}
there exists an edge
e\in E(\ldg(G^{\prime},M^{\prime}))
with
\sgn(v,e)=s
.
However, this shows that there cannot be any topological sort for
\ldg(G^{\prime},M^{\prime})
as no vertex can be the first in the ordering, which shows that
\ldg(G^{\prime},M^{\prime})
is not acyclic by
Proposition

4.8
.

Now suppose that
\ldg(G,M)
is not acyclic, which shows that there is a locally directed closed walk in
\ldg(G,M)
.
Suppose that
v_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}v_{2}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{t-1}}v_{t}\xrightarrow{e_{t}=e_{0}}v_{t+1}=v_{1}
is the locally directed closed walk, where each
v_{i}\in V(\ldg(G,M))
corresponds to the edge
v_{i}^{+}v_{i}^{-}
in the matching
M
.
Let
u_{i}=v_{i}^{\sgn(v_{i},e_{i-1})}
and
w_{i}=v_{i}^{\sgn(v_{i},e_{i})}
for each
i\in[t]
.
By the definition of
\ldg(G,M)
, we know that the edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{t}
in
\ldg(G,M)
corresponds to the edges
w_{1}u_{2},w_{2}u_{3},\ldots,w_{t}u_{1}
in
G
.
By the definition of locally directed closed walk, we see that
u_{i}
and
w_{i}
must be two different endpoints of some edge in
M
, showing that
u_{i}w_{i}\in M
.
Note that this shows that
u_{1}w_{1}\cdots u_{t}w_{t}u_{1}
is an alternating closed walk for the matching
M
, which means that we can swap some weight on
M
using the alternating walk to get another fractional perfect matching.
To be more specific, let
c\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be the function that counts, for each edge
e
, how many times it appears as an edge in the walk
u_{1}w_{1}\cdots u_{t}w_{t}u_{1}
, and for every
\varepsilon>0
let
f_{\varepsilon}:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
f_{\varepsilon}(e)=\varepsilon c(e)
if
e\not\in M
, and
f_{\varepsilon}(e)=1-\varepsilon c(e)
if
e\in M
.
It is easy to verify that
f_{\varepsilon}
is another fractional perfect matching for all
\varepsilon\leq\left(\max_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\right)^{-1}
.
∎

We end with an auxiliary lemma that will be useful later when we would like to show that iterated blowup preserves acyclicity.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2}
be two LDAGs, and let
v
be a vertex of
G_{1}
.
Let
G
be a graph where, starting from
G_{1}
,
v
is blown-up to a set of size
v(G_{2})
in
G_{1}
, and
G_{2}
is put on this set.
Then
G
is an LDAG as well.

Proof.

Suppose that the vertex set
V(G)
of
G
can be written as
(V(G_{1})\backslash\{v\})\cup\{v^{u}:u\in V(G_{2})\}
, where
G
induced on
\{v^{u}:u\in V(G_{2})\}
is isomorphic to
G_{2}
via the bijection
v^{u}\mapsto u
for each
u\in V(G_{2})
.
Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}
be a topological sort of
G_{1}
(where
s=v(G_{1})
) and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}
be a topological sort of
G_{2}
(where
t=v(G_{2})
), which exist by
Proposition

4.8
.
Suppose that
v_{i}=v
.
Then it is clear that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1},v^{u_{1}},\ldots,v^{u_{t}},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{s}
is a topological sort of
G
, showing that
G
is an LDAG once again by
Proposition

4.8
.
∎

4.3.
From edge inducibility to vertex inducibility

In this section, we will generalize
Theorem

3.2
to any graph with a perfect matching that has no other fractional perfect matchings.
It turns out that the edge inducibilities of such graphs are related to vertex inducibilities of certain local digraphs.
We will thus have to define vertex inducibility for local digraphs.

Definition 4.11

(Vertex inducibility and acyclic vertex inducibility for local digraphs)
.

Let
G
be any local digraph.
For any local digraph
H
, let
N_{\ind}(G,H)
be the number of induced local digraphs of
H
that are isomorphic to
G
.
The
vertex inducibility
of
G
is

\ind(G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max_{v(H)=n}\frac{N_{\ind}(G,H)}{\binom{n}{v(G)}}

where the maximum ranges through all local digraphs of size
n
.
Its
acyclic vertex inducibility
is denoted by
\aind(G)
, and is defined by the same expression except that
H
is required to be acyclic.
In particular, if
G
is not an LDAG, then
\aind(G)=0
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
G
be a graph and
M
be a perfect matching in
G
so that
M
is a unique fractional perfect matching.
Then

\aind(\ldg(G,M))\leq\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}\eind(G)\leq\ind(\ldg(G,M)).

Proof.

We first prove the second half of the inequality.
The proof is similar to that of
Theorem

3.2
.
Suppose that
H
is a graph with
m
edges.
We will construct a local digraph
H^{\prime}
with
m
vertices such that every induced copy of
G
in
H
gives rise to many distinct induced copies of
\ldg(G,M)
in
H^{\prime}
. This is done as follows.
For each
e\in E(H)
, let
v_{e}
be a vertex in
H^{\prime}
and if
e=\{u,v\}
, fix any bijection
\sgn_{e}\colon\{u,v\}\to\{\pm\}
.
For any edges
e,e_{1},e_{2}\in E(H)
where
e_{1},e_{2}
are disjoint and
e
goes between
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, we create an edge
\tilde{e}
between
v_{e_{1}},v_{e_{2}}
.
We also set

\sgn(v_{e_{i}},\tilde{e})=\sgn_{e_{i}}(e_{i}\cap e)

for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
From this construction and the definition of
\ldg(G,M)
, in any induced copy of
G
in
H
, the vertices corresponding to the unique perfect matching of the induced copy induce a local digraph isomorphic to
\ldg(G,M)
in
H^{\prime}
.
Therefore, we know that

\frac{\left\lvert M\right\rvert!\cdot N_{\ind}(\ldg(G,M),H^{\prime})}{m^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}\geq\frac{\left\lvert M\right\rvert!\cdot N_{\ind}(G,H)}{m^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}=\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert N_{\ind}(G,H)}{(2m)^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}.

By the definitions of edge inducibility for graphs and vertex inducibility for local digraphs, we get that

\ind(\ldg(G,M))\geq\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}\eind(G),

as desired.

To show the first half of the inequality, we begin with an LDAG
H
with
n
vertices.
We will construct a graph
H^{\prime}
with many induced copies of
G
corresponding to indueced copies of
\ldg(G,M)
in
H
, though the number of edges of
H^{\prime}
would not be
n
.
Instead, we will construct a graph
H^{\prime}
with
(n+o_{n;k\to\infty}(1))k
edges where
k\in\mathbb{N}
is a sufficiently large positive integer.
To do so, let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be a topological sort of
H
, and also relabel the signs so that
\sgn(v_{i},e)=+
for any
e
going between
v_{i},v_{j}
and
i<j
.
Take the graphification
\gr(H)
of
H
, and let
M_{H}
be the perfect matching
\{v_{1}^{+}v_{1}^{-},\ldots,v_{n}^{+}v_{n}^{-}\}
.
Then any induced copy of
\ldg(G,M)
in
H
corresponds to an induced copy of
G
in
\gr(H)\cup M_{H}
where the unique matching
M
of
G
is mapped to a submatching of
M_{H}
.
We can also, without losing any induced copies of
G
, assume that
\gr(H)\cup M_{H}
is simple.
The graph
H^{\prime}
will then be chosen to be an appropriate blowup of
\gr(H)\cup M_{H}
constructed as follows.
Fix some rational numbers
0<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}<1/2
, and let
k
be a large positive integer such that
k^{\alpha_{i}}
is also a positive integer for any
i\in[n]
.
Let
H^{\prime}
be the blowup of
\gr(H)\cup M_{H}
where
v_{i}^{+}
is replaced by an independent set of size
k^{\alpha_{i}}
and
v_{i}^{-}
is replaced by an independent set of size
k^{1-\alpha_{i}}
.
Then the number of edges in
H^{\prime}
is at least
nk
and can be upper bounded by

nk+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}k^{\alpha_{i}}\left(k^{\alpha_{j}}+k^{1-\alpha_{j}}\right)=(n+o_{n;k\to\infty}(1))k.

This shows that
e(H^{\prime})=(n+o_{n;k\to\infty}(1))k
.
Moreover, for any induced copy of
G
in
\gr(H)\cup M_{H}
whose unique perfect matching
M
is a subset of
M_{H}
, it is blown-up to
k^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}
induced copies in
H^{\prime}
.
Therefore,

N_{\ind}(\ldg(G,M),H)\leq\frac{N_{\ind}(G,H^{\prime})}{k^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}=(1+o_{n,\left\lvert M\right\rvert;k\to\infty}(1))(2n)^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\cdot\frac{N_{\ind}(G,H^{\prime})}{(2e(H^{\prime}))^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}.

By taking
k
to infinity, we see that

N_{\ind}(\ldg(G,M),H)\leq\frac{(2n)^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}\eind(G),

and so

\frac{\left\lvert M\right\rvert!\cdot N_{\ind}(\ldg(G,M),H)}{n^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}}\leq\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}\eind(G).

By the definition of acyclic vertex inducibility, we see that
\aind(\ldg(G,M))\leq\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\left\lvert\Aut(G)\right\rvert}\eind(G)
, as desired.
∎

We remark that now

becomes a corollary of this theorem.

.

For now, we first apply

to prove

.

Proof of

.

We first note that

for any LDAG

In this subsection, we prove

Proof of
Theorem

1.8
.

Let
H
be a graph with
m
edges. We sample an induced embedding of
C_{5}
,
(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})\in V(H)^{5}
uniformly at random, so that
X_{1}X_{2},X_{2}X_{3},X_{3}X_{4},X_{4}X_{5},X_{5}X_{1}
are the only edges among these five vertices. It follows that

\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})=\log_{2}E_{\ind}(C_{5},H).

Together with the uniform bound
\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\log_{2}(2m)
, it is now sufficient to prove

2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})+\log_{2}250\leq 5\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2}).

This is because substituting the uniform bound gives

2\log_{2}E_{\ind}(C_{5},H)+\log_{2}250\leq 5\log(2m),

which implies
\eind(C_{5})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{250}}
.

To upper bound
2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{5})
, we use the chain rule and symmetry to get

2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})=2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2})+2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3})+2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})+2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5}).

To proceed, we need the following two claims to bound the conditional entropies. These two claims are both proved by applying the mixture bound,
Proposition

2.7
.

Claim 5.3
.

\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5})\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{5})\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2})-\log_{2}2.

Proof.

The first inequality is simply dropping conditioning, so we will focus on the second inequality.
Note that
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})
and
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{4},X_{3})
have disjoint supports. This is because
X_{2}X_{3}
is always an edge and
X_{2}X_{4}
is always not an edge. By the mixture bound, we have

2\cdot 2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})}\leq 2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2},A,B)},

where
(X_{1},X_{2},A,B)
is a mixture of the two random tuples
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})
and
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{4},X_{3})
. Taking logarithms, we get

\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})+\log_{2}2\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2},A,B).

Since
(X_{3},X_{4}\mid X_{1})
is symmetric, we know that
(X_{1},X_{3},X_{4})
and
(X_{1},A,B)
have the same distribution. Thus, we have

\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2},A,B)\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1},A,B)+\mathbb{H}(X_{2}\mid X_{1})=\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{3},X_{4})+\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2}),

where the first inequality follows from the definition of conditional entropy and dropping condition.
Therefore, we may combine the inequalities above and get

\mathbb{H}(X_{2}\mid X_{1},X_{3},X_{4})=\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})-\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{3},X_{4})\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2})-\log_{2}2.

By symmetry, we have

\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{5})=\mathbb{H}(X_{2}\mid X_{1},X_{3},X_{4}),

and this completes the proof.
∎

Claim 5.4
.

\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1})-\log_{2}5.

Proof.

For
1\leq i\leq 5
, let
Y_{i}
be a conditional independent resampling of
X_{i}
conditioning on
X_{i+1},X_{i+2},X_{i+3}
, where the indices are considered modulo
5
.
We claim that the supports of
(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq 5
are mutually disjoint.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that

(x_{1},\dots,x_{5},y)\in\supp(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{i})\cap\supp(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{j}).

By symmetry, we may assume without loss of generality that
i=1
and
j\in\{2,3\}
. Since
(x_{1},\dots,x_{5},y)\in\supp(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{1})
, we know that
yx_{2}
is an edge but
yx_{3},yx_{4}
are not edges. If
j=2
, then we know that
yx_{3}
must be an edge since
(x_{1},\dots,x_{5},y)\in\supp(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{2})
, which is impossible. If
j=3
, then
yx_{4}
must be an edge since
(x_{1},\dots,x_{5},y)\in\supp(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{3})
, which is also impossible.

Therefore, we may apply the mixture bound and get

\sum_{i=1}^{5}2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y_{i})}\leq 2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5},Y)},

where
Y
is a mixture of
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{5}
. Factoring out
2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})}
from both sides and using the chain rule, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{5}2^{\mathbb{H}(Y_{i}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{5})}\leq 2^{\mathbb{H}(Y\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{5})}.

Note that the marginal distributions of
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{5}
and
X_{1}
are all the same, so we have

\mathbb{H}(Y\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{5})\leq\mathbb{H}(Y)=\mathbb{H}(X_{1}).

From the definition of
Y_{i}
, we know that

\mathbb{H}(Y_{i}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{5})=\mathbb{H}(X_{i}\mid X_{i+1},X_{i+2},X_{i+3})=\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}).

Thus, we have

5\cdot 2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})}\leq 2^{\mathbb{H}(X_{1})},

and the claim follows from taking logarithms on both sides.
∎

Finally, we are ready to bound
2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})
.
Recall that

2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})=2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2})+2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3})+2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})+2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5}).

Using
5.3
and
5.4
, we get

\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5})\leq\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2})-\log_{2}2,

and

2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})+\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5})\leq 3\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})\leq 3\mathbb{H}(X_{1})-3\log_{2}5.

In addition, we may simply drop conditioning and get

2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2},X_{3})\leq 2\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2}).

Lastly, combining the inequalities above, we get

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{5})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2})+3\mathbb{H}(X_{1})+3\mathbb{H}(X_{1}\mid X_{2})-\log_{2}250

\displaystyle=5\mathbb{H}(X_{1},X_{2})-\log_{2}250.\qed

6.
Concluding remarks and open problems

6.1.
Locally directed graphs

In this paper, we define locally directed graphs to generalize
Theorem

3.2
to
Theorem

4.12
, which turns out to be useful in understanding edge inducibilities of many graphs.
We remark that there seem to be many interesting problems one can ask about locally directed graphs that may or may not be related to edge inducibility itself.
Perhaps the most interesting connection we are aware of now is that deciding the vertex inducibility of
LDC_{3}
, the locally directed cycle of length
3
, is equivalent to the following conjecture by Erdős and Sós
[
12
]
.

Conjecture 6.1

(Erdős–Sós)
.

Any
3
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices with bipartite link at every vertex has at most
(\frac{1}{4}+o(1))\binom{n}{3}
edges.

A simple observation that might have not been written down formally is that this conjecture is equivalent to that
\ind(LDC_{3})=\frac{1}{4}
(this was communicated by Maya Sankar to the fourth author).
We remark that
LDC_{3}
is not acyclic, and thus
\ind(LDC_{3})
does not give an upper bound on any edge inducibility of any graph via
Theorem

4.12
.
This suggests that the vertex inducibility problem of locally directed graphs should already be interesting on its own, and it can be extremely challenging even for small graphs.
Let us mention that if we instead work with directed host graphs, then it is a classical result (dating back to as early as
[
25
]
) that the vertex inducibility of a directed cycle of length
3
is exactly
\frac{1}{4}
.
This also shows that problems regarding locally directed graphs can be fundamentally harder than those for directed graphs.

Continuing this theme, in the proof of
Theorem

4.13
, we showed that
\ind(LDP_{3})\leq\frac{2}{3}
and
\aind(LDP_{3})\geq\frac{10}{31}
.
It would also be interesting to determine
\ind(LDP_{3})
and
\aind(LDP_{3})
themselves.
The best lower bound we have for
\ind(LDP_{3})
is
\frac{2}{5}
coming from the iterated blowup of
LDC_{4}
, and it is tempting to conjecture that the lower bounds are tight for both
\ind(LDP_{3})
and
\aind(LDP_{3})
.
For comparisons,
\ind(P_{3})=\frac{3}{4}
was already known by Pippenger and Golumbic
[
31
]
, and for directed path
DP_{3}
on
3
vertices, Hladký, Král’ and Norin announced that
\ind(DP_{3})=\frac{2}{5}
in 2018 (see
[
10
]
).
We may also define
\aind(DP_{3})
similarly to how we define acyclic vertex inducibility for local digraphs, where now we require the host graph to be acyclic.
In this case, we can prove that
\aind(DP_{3})=\frac{1}{4}
, and a proof is provided in
Appendix

A
.

6.2.
Tightness of
Theorem

4.12

Let
G
be a graph with a perfect matching
M
that is the unique fractional perfect matching.

Theorem

4.12
gives that
\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\Aut(G)}\eind(G)\geq\aind(\ldg(G,M))
, and it is tight when
\aind(\ldg(G,M))=\ind(\ldg(G,M))
.
It would be interesting to know whether the equality always holds.

Question 6.2
.

If
G
is a graph with a perfect matching
M
that is the unique fractional perfect matching, is it always true that

\frac{2^{\left\lvert M\right\rvert}\left\lvert M\right\rvert!}{\Aut(G)}\eind(G)=\aind(\ldg(G,M))?

6.3.
Edge inducibilities of small graphs

In this paper, the special cases that are not completely settled are
P_{6}
and
C_{5}
.
We have conjectured in
1.7
that the edge inducibility of any cycle
C_{k}
with
k\geq 4
should match the one given by balanced blowups of
C_{k}
.
For
P_{6}
, we make the following bold conjecture.

Conjecture 6.3
.

The edge inducibility of
P_{6}
is equal to
\frac{5}{372}
.

For general paths, we do not have a good candidate for conjectures, which is similar to the case of the vertex inducibility of paths.

6.4.
Upper tails for induced counts

We conclude with another motivation mentioned in the introduction. Indeed, there is a tight connection between the upper tail problem and the problem of estimating
c_{\mathcal{H}}(G)
for
\mathcal{H}
being the family of
n
-vertex and
m
-edge graphs. As was shown by the second author
[
11
]
, studying
\ind_{\mathcal{H}}(G)
for the same family
\mathcal{H}
can shed light on the upper tail problem for induced counts. We believe that studying the edge inducibility problem is the first step towards studying this problem, similarly to the case of
c_{\mathcal{H}}

[
1
,
15
,
22
]
.

Let us highlight that the breakthrough paper of Harel, Mousset, and Samotij
[
18
]
has two major steps towards applying a union bound type argument. The first is to gain control on the upper tail probability via the existence of “seeds”. The second is to analyze the seed structure. The first step in
[
18
]
is not available for induced counts; that said, following their suggestion, Theorem 2.1 in
[
11
]
extends their first step to induced counts. This leaves the main task of the second step. In both works, evaluating
c_{\mathcal{H}}(G)
and
\ind_{\mathcal{H}}(G)
are central tools in analyzing seeds and counting a compressed variant of them, enabling the use of the union bound.
