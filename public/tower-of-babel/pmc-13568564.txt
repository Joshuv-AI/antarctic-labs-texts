Sintilimab‑related cystitis/ureteritis in high‑grade endometrial stromal sarcoma: A case report and structured literature review
Authors: Shuhan Yang, Ruike Gao, Daorui Li, Wenguang Shi, Xuedong Yang, Peiqi Shi, Leilei Qi, Weina Li, Ying Zhang, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are cutting‑edge therapies in cancer treatment; however, they may trigger unique immune‑related adverse events (irAEs). Although irAEs can affect various organs, ICI‑induced cystitis/ureteritis has rarely been reported. We present a 44‑year‑old female patient with high‑grade endometrial stromal sarcoma who developed severe urinary frequency, urgency, and dysuria after three cycles of sintilimab combined with nab‑paclitaxel and carboplatin. Empirical antibiotic therapy failed to relieve her symptoms. Imaging revealed diffuse thickening of the bladder and ureteral walls with marked peri‑vesical and peri‑ureteral exudation. After treatment with methylprednisolone, the patient’s symptoms improved dramatically. A subsequent urine culture grew Enterococcus faecalis, and the addition of piperacillin-tazobactam resulted in further symptom resolution. Following symptom resolution, she was rechallenged with sintilimab and developed only mild urinary tract symptoms. Subsequently, due to disease progression, treatment was switched to tislelizumab, and the urinary symptoms did not recur. The patient’s urinary symptoms were sintilimab‑related cystitis/ureteritis with possible concomitant or partially suppressed bacterial infection. This case, together with a structured literature review of other immunotherapy‑related cystitis/ureteritis cases and subsequent ICI use, provides a practical reference for the diagnosis and management of ICI‑related urological irAEs and for subsequent anti‑tumor treatment.

Introduction

ICIs have revolutionized the therapeutic landscape for a broad spectrum of advanced malignancies; however, their increasing use has been accompanied by a corresponding rise in irAEs. Sintilimab, a fully human immunoglobulin G4 monoclonal antibody that selectively disrupts the interaction between programmed cell death‑1 (PD‑1) and its ligands PD‑L1 and PD‑L2, has demonstrated clinically meaningful efficacy across multiple tumor types, including lymphomas, non‑small‑cell lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and gastric adenocarcinoma.1 Nevertheless, experience with sintilimab in endometrial stromal sarcoma (ESS) remains extremely limited, and urological toxicities attributable to this agent have not previously been reported or systematically characterized in ESS. This report is the first to present a case of sintilimab‑related cystitis/ureteritis in a patient with ESS. We also provide a structured literature review of ICI‑induced cystitis/ureteritis to facilitate better recognition, monitoring, and management of such irAEs and to inform the use of ICIs in ESS treatment.

Case presentation

A 44‑year‑old woman was admitted to the First People’s Hospital of Qiqihar, Heilongjiang Province, in October 2024 with abnormal uterine bleeding and a presumptive diagnosis of uterine fibroids. Her medical history was notable for nephrotic syndrome. She had no prior exposure to tobacco or alcohol and no documented hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or viral hepatitis. Her mother had been diagnosed with endometrial carcinoma. On 23 October 2024, she underwent laparoscopic total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo‑oophorectomy. Histopathological examination revealed a high‑grade spindle‑cell neoplasm consistent with high‑grade endometrial stromal sarcoma (HGESS); both adnexa were tumor‑free. Immunohistochemistry showed: P16 (+), SMA (−), desmin (−), AR (weak, 10%), ER (−), CD10 (−), p53 (wild‑type pattern), Cyclin D1 (+), and Ki‑67 index 60%. Post‑operative staging PET/CT on 26 November 2024 demonstrated hypermetabolic tissue at the vaginal cuff (SUVmax 9.0), a hypermetabolic right pelvic‑wall lymph node, and multiple osseous metastases involving T4, T7, L4 transverse processes, the right seventh rib, left ilium, and left pubis. The patient was assigned FIGO 2023 stage IVB and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 1.

She was subsequently referred to Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. Two cycles of conventional chemotherapy were administered on 8 January and 7 February 2025: epirubicin 80 mg d 1 plus ifosfamide 2 g d 1–3 every 3 weeks. Restaging imaging after cycle 2 showed progressive disease (PD) with a 35% increase in pelvic lymph‑node diameter. On 19 March 2025, a combination regimen of sintilimab plus nab‑paclitaxel and carboplatin was initiated: sintilimab 200 mg d 1, nab‑paclitaxel 200 mg d 1 and 100 mg d 8, carboplatin AUC 5 (0.4 g) d 1, repeated every 21 d. For cycles 2 and 3, the d‑8 nab-paclitaxel dose was escalated to 150 mg. Restaging after two cycles demonstrated stable disease (SD). Denosumab was administered concomitantly for osteoclast inhibition.

On 1 June 2025, 10 d after cycle 3 (sintilimab plus nab‑paclitaxel and carboplatin), the patient developed severe urinary frequency, urgency, and end‑voiding pain (Numeric Rating Scale [NRS] 5/10). She presented to the Department of Urology, Peking University First Hospital, on 2 June. A diagnosis of acute lower urinary‑tract infection was made, and she was prescribed oral levofloxacin 500 mg once daily plus indometacin suppositories as needed. After 3 d of antibiotic therapy, the patient reported only partial improvement (NRS 3/10). On 6 June, she was re‑evaluated at Guang’anmen Hospital. She still complained of urinary frequency, urgency, and end‑voiding pain (NRS 3/10). Physical examination showed a soft abdomen, no percussion pain in the renal area, no lower abdominal bulging, no tenderness or rebound tenderness, and no vulvar abnormalities. Urinary system ultrasound showed adequate bladder filling before voiding with no obvious abnormal echoes; post‑void residual urine volume was approximately 24 mL. Complete blood count, C‑reactive protein, and renal/liver functions were normal. After hospitalization, intravenous levofloxacin (0.5 g once daily) was continued. For pain, half an indomethacin suppository was given as needed. After 4 d, the patient’s urethral spasm and end‑voiding pain did not further improve. Antibiotics were changed to intravenous cefazoxime 2.0 g every 12 h. After 7 d (by 17 June), two sets of urine cultures showed no bacterial growth, but a urine routine revealed an increase in white blood cells compared with previous results. Antibiotics were again changed to cefazoxime plus oral azithromycin 0.25 g once daily. On 20 June, she experienced four breakthrough pain episodes (NRS 7/10) requiring intramuscular bucinnazine hydrochloride. To further clarify the condition, a CT scan of the urinary system was performed, which showed changes in the bladder and surrounding area suggestive of inflammatory lesions, with possible involvement of both ureters (left more severe). Contrast‑enhanced pelvic MRI demonstrated thickening of the bladder wall, involvement of the pelvic segments of both ureters, multiple peri‑vesical exudates, and high‑signal foci on DWI at the posterior bladder wall (pre‑treatment images in Figure 1). Discussion

Diagnostic process

The diagnostic process of this case highlights key decision points for establishing a correct diagnosis in the context of immunotherapy. The patient initially presented with urinary frequency, urgency, and terminal dysuria. Empirical treatment for acute lower urinary tract infection yielded a poor response. At this juncture, the diagnostic workup did not stop at conventional reasoning but progressively clarified immune‑related cystitis and ureteritis based on the following criteria: ①urine culture results: two consecutive sets of urine cultures showed no bacterial growth; ②imaging findings: CT and MRI revealed bladder wall thickening, bilateral ureteric involvement in the pelvic segment and perivesical exudation; ③response to steroids: rapid and sustained symptom relief following glucocorticoid therapy; ④cystoscopy/biopsy: cystoscopic findings of inflammatory mucosal elevation in the trigone, with biopsy pathology showing chronic inflammation of the urothelium and formation of von Brunn nests. Before establishing this diagnosis, the clinical team systematically ruled out several differential diagnoses. Contrast‑enhanced pelvic MRI and CT showed no signs of tumor recurrence or metastasis around the bladder or ureters, thereby excluding tumor involvement. The patient had received only sintilimab, nab‑paclitaxel, carboplatin, and denosumab, with no history of intravesical instillation or known exposure to drugs that can cause chemical cystitis, thus ruling out drug‑induced chemical cystitis. Urinary tract CT imaging revealed no calculi, excluding stone‑related symptoms. Based on these rigorous diagnostic criteria and a thorough process of differential exclusion, the clinical team was able to precisely initiate glucocorticoid therapy and dynamically monitor infectious parameters.

Importantly, although the clinical picture and the rapid steroid response strongly supported an immune‑mediated process, the possibility of concomitant bacterial infection was not entirely excluded. This complexity was later underscored by the positive urine culture for E. faecalis after antibiotic washout, which suggested that a partially suppressed infection may have coexisted with the immune‑related inflammation. Therefore, we prefer to characterize this event as sintilimab‑related cystitis/ureteritis with possible concomitant or partially suppressed bacterial infection, rather than a purely immune‑related process.

Literature review of 23 cases

To better understand ICI‑induced urinary toxicities, we performed a structured literature review. We searched PubMed and Web of Science from inception to May 2026 using the keywords: (“immune checkpoint inhibitor” OR “PD‑1” OR “PD‑L1” OR “sintilimab” OR “pembrolizumab” OR “nivolumab”) AND (“cystitis” OR “ureteritis” OR “urinary tract irAE”). We included original case reports and case series reporting ICI‑associated cystitis or ureteritis in cancer patients. We identified 23 previously reported cases, which are summarized in Table 2 (detailed case‑by‑case data are provided in the Supplementary Materials). Among the 23 reported cases of immune‑related cystitis associated with ICIs, PD‑1 inhibitors were the predominant type, including nivolumab (7 cases), pembrolizumab (7 cases), sintilimab (5 cases), tislelizumab (2 cases), and atezolizumab (2 cases). Additional cases involved toripalimab and the combination of nivolumab plus ipilimumab. Three cases of sintilimab‑related cystitis have been documented, occurring in gastric cancer (2 cases) and lung cancer (3 cases: 1 adenocarcinoma and 2 squamous cell carcinomas), all of which presented with typical cystitis symptoms. The patient in the present case had advanced endometrial stromal sarcoma and received sintilimab combined with chemotherapy (nab‑paclitaxel plus carboplatin). This represents the first report of sintilimab‑related cystitis in uterine sarcoma, thereby further extending the tumor spectrum in which this agent can induce immune‑related cystitis. Of note, although the patient received concurrent chemotherapy, chemotherapy alone is not typical for causing isolated terminal dysuria accompanied by imaging findings of inflammatory changes in the bladder wall and ureters. Combined with multidisciplinary consultation that ruled out tumor metastasis, infection, calculi, and other factors, the clinical evidence supports sintilimab as the causative agent.

Symptom presentation

A summary of the literature indicates that the most common symptom of immune‑related cystitis is gross or microscopic hematuria (over 50% of cases), followed by urinary frequency, urgency, and dysuria. Some patients may also present with flank pain, fever, or diarrhea. According to CTCAE grading, grade 3 manifestations include severe pain and hematuria requiring intervention.3 In all cases, urinary tract infection must be excluded before the diagnosis can be established. The core symptoms in the present case were severe terminal urethral pain (maximum NRS 7) accompanied by urinary frequency, but notably without significant hematuria throughout the disease course. This differs from the high incidence of hematuria reported in the literature, indicating that the clinical spectrum of immune‑related cystitis is broad; severe dysuria may serve as a primary or even presenting symptom, and the absence of hematuria does not rule out the diagnosis.

Urine bacteriology culture and treatment

The coexistence of immune‑related cystitis and bacterial cystitis increases the difficulty of diagnosis and treatment. In the present case, initial urinalysis revealed progressively elevated leukocyte counts with negative urine cultures. Empirical anti‑infective therapy yielded a poor response. Subsequently, when immune‑related cystitis was suspected, antibiotics were discontinued, and methylprednisolone was initiated, after which the patient’s symptoms improved. To further alleviate symptoms and exclude bacterial infection, a repeat urine culture was performed 3 d after antibiotic withdrawal. After 3 d of culture, Enterococcus faecalis was identified; the addition of the sensitive antibiotic piperacillin‑sulbactam resulted in further symptom relief. Previous studies have demonstrated that following a single dose of empirical antibiotic therapy, the urine culture positivity rate rapidly declines from 99% pre‑administration to 35% (after the first post‑dose void).22 After a three‑day washout period, drug concentrations in the body and urine fall to sub‑inhibitory levels, allowing residual bacteria that were suppressed but not eradicated to resume proliferation, thereby converting the culture to positive. Thus, the initial negative urine culture, followed by a positive culture after methylprednisolone therapy, is attributable to bacterial growth suppression during the preceding antibiotic use. This course suggests that when concomitant immune‑related cystitis and bacterial infection are suspected in a patient receiving antibiotics, antibiotics should be withheld for at least 3 d prior to urine culture to better define bacterial infection status and guide antibiotic use, thereby alleviating the patient’s symptoms. Furthermore, withholding antibiotics for this period does not exacerbate the patient’s symptoms.

Beyond the diagnostic implications, the therapeutic response in this case also merits a balanced appraisal of the relative contributions of corticosteroids and antibiotics. The rapid and substantial improvement after methylprednisolone – NRS 7/10 dropped to 2/10 within 4 d – strongly supports a dominant anti‑inflammatory effect targeting immune‑mediated bladder and ureteral inflammation. The subsequent addition of piperacillin–tazobactam after the identification of E. faecalis likely contributed to the complete resolution of residual symptoms. This stepwise pattern – rapid major improvement followed by slower complete resolution – suggests that corticosteroids addressed the primary immune‑driven pathology, while antibiotics eliminated a coexisting bacterial component that may have been partially suppressed but not eradicated by the earlier empirical therapy. Rather than attributing the outcome to either intervention alone, we consider that they likely acted in a complementary and sequential manner: corticosteroids provided the initial and decisive control of the immune‑mediated inflammation, while antibiotics ensured eradication of the bacterial component, together leading to the patient’s full recovery. This interpretation aligns with the overall clinical picture and reinforces the importance of dynamic microbiological monitoring and individualized treatment adjustment in patients with suspected ICI‑related urological irAEs.

Imaging findings

In the literature, commonly used imaging modalities include ultrasound, CT urography (CTU), magnetic resonance urography (MRU), and PET/CT. Major positive findings include bladder wall thickening, mucosal enhancement or edema, ureteral dilatation or hydronephrosis, ureteral wall thickening, and perinephric exudation. In some cases, ultrasound may show no significant abnormality, whereas CT or MRI can reveal typical inflammatory changes. In the present case, initial urinary tract ultrasound showed no obvious abnormalities, but contrast‑enhanced pelvic MRI demonstrated bladder wall thickening, involvement of the bilateral pelvic segments of the ureters, surrounding exudation, and a DWI hyperintense lesion in the posterior bladder wall, findings fully consistent with the typical imaging features summarized in the literature. Bilateral ureteral involvement is noteworthy; in the literature, seven cases exhibited ureteral dilatation or wall thickening. The present case represents a more severe manifestation, but timely corticosteroid intervention prevented renal function impairment. This course indicates that a negative ultrasound does not exclude immune‑related cystitis; in clinically highly suspicious cases, imaging should be escalated to CT or MRI to avoid missed diagnosis.

Timing of symptom onset

The time to onset of immune‑related cystitis reported in the literature varies widely, with the earliest onset occurring after the first cycle and the latest after the seventh cycle. However, most cases occur between the third and sixth cycles, with a median onset around the fourth cycle. This pattern provides a clear time window for clinical monitoring. In the present case, the patient developed terminal dysuria 10 d after the third cycle of sintilimab, which closely aligns with the peak period (cycles 3–6) reported in the literature. This feature suggests that when a patient develops new urinary symptoms around the third cycle of ICI therapy, immune‑related cystitis should be prioritized in the differential diagnosis without waiting until later cycles.

Rechallenge with immune agents

The literature shows considerable heterogeneity in the management of rechallenge with ICIs after an episode of cystitis. Some patients did not resume ICIs after discontinuation; some experienced recurrence upon rechallenge, but the recurrence remained sensitive to corticosteroid therapy; a few patients successfully tolerated rechallenge, with the key being slow tapering of corticosteroids and close monitoring. In other patients, switching to a different ICI (e.g., from toripalimab to pembrolizumab) still led to recurrence, whereas switching to a targeted agent with a different mechanism (e.g., alectinib) resulted in symptom resolution. According to the ASCO guidelines (2018 and 2021 update) and CSCO guidelines, grade 3–4 toxicities generally warrant permanent ICI discontinuation, with the exception of endocrinopathies that are manageable with hormone replacement.2,23,24 Based on this principle, we predefined the criteria for ICI discontinuation as recurrence of grade ≥ 3 symptoms or development of other severe irAEs. After treatment with methylprednisolone (1–2 mg/kg), the symptoms in this case significantly improved. During the fourth cycle of anti‑tumor treatment, the clinical team decided to re‑challenge the original regimen. The decision was based on the patient having achieved symptom resolution (NRS 0–1/10). Monitoring included daily symptom assessment and regular urinalysis. The recurrence was mild (NRS 2–3/10) and manageable with short‑term antibiotics and continued steroids. This course is consistent with the literature experience of recurrence upon rechallenge, but overall it was manageable.

Of note, the patient in this case later switched to tislelizumab plus anlotinib due to disease progression, and no further urinary symptoms occurred after the switch. This differs from some reports in the literature and suggests that cross‑reactivity between different PD‑1 inhibitors may exhibit individual variability. For patients who are forced to discontinue the original ICI due to immune‑related cystitis but still have an indication for immunotherapy, a cautious attempt to switch to another ICI represents an alternative strategy that requires careful individual assessment and close monitoring.

Nephrotic syndrome predisposes ICI cystitis

The patient had a past medical history of nephrotic syndrome. Studies have demonstrated that patients with nephrotic syndrome exhibit an imbalance in T‑cell subsets, characterized in particular by a reduced number and impaired function of regulatory T cells (Tregs), as well as a shift in the Th2/Treg and Th17/Treg balance toward a pro‑inflammatory orientation.25,26 Furthermore, nephrotic syndrome is often accompanied by abnormal activation and consumption of the complement system, with persistent proteinuria leading to urinary loss of complement regulatory proteins and consequently impairing the capacity to regulate the alternative complement pathway.27 Under normal physiological conditions, the bladder wall is rich in Treg cells expressing high levels of PD‑1, and these Treg cells constitute a key subpopulation responsible for maintaining the local immunosuppressive microenvironment of the bladder. Following ICI therapy, blockade of the PD‑1/PD‑L1 pathway attenuates the suppressive function of Tregs, leading to marked expansion of cytotoxic T cells, particularly CD8+ T cells, within bladder tissue and subsequent attack on the bladder urothelium.28 Case reports have provided histopathological evidence that in patients who develop ICI‑associated cystitis following pembrolizumab treatment, the bladder urothelium exhibits abundant CD8+ T cell infiltration accompanied by significant upregulation of PD‑L1 expression.18

It should be noted that the patient refused cystoscopy at disease onset due to severe pain; therefore, bladder histopathological results were unavailable, and direct evidence of CD8+ T cell infiltration or PD-L1 expression could not be obtained. However, in this patient, active proteinuria was already present as early as May 2023 (i.e., prior to ICI therapy), suggesting ongoing complement consumption and preexisting immune network dysregulation, which may have increased susceptibility. A meta‑analysis confirmed that cancer patients with pre‑existing autoimmune diseases have a significantly increased risk of developing irAEs of any grade following ICI therapy.29 Although the available data are insufficient to determine whether the aforementioned factors directly contributed to the development of grade 3 cystitis/ureteritis in this patient, or to what extent. Nonetheless, they suggest that patients with a history of nephrotic syndrome should remain vigilant for immune-related bladder toxicity when receiving ICIs.

Immunotherapy in HGESS

ESS is a rare malignancy. From a therapeutic perspective, standard treatment consists of total hysterectomy with or without bilateral salpingo‑oophorectomy.30 The roles of lymphadenectomy, adjuvant radiotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy remain controversial and are typically determined on a case‑by‑case basis, taking into account tumor stage, grade, patient comorbidities, and life expectancy.30 ICIs are a novel class of antineoplastic agents. Studies have shown that HGESS exhibits an immune‑hot tumor microenvironment, and patients with upregulation of genes involved in immune‑related pathways may be more likely to derive clinical benefit from immunotherapy; therefore, immunotherapy may be considered when chemotherapy proves ineffective or after metastasis has occurred.31–33 Currently, according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, pembrolizumab may be used in patients with recurrent uterine sarcoma and extensive metastasis who have high tumor mutational burden (TMB‑H); however, it is not a preferred regimen.34 The treatment of HGESS may follow that of uterine sarcoma in general. Nevertheless, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in HGESS is currently mostly at the clinical trial stage, as exemplified by studies evaluating the efficacy of nivolumab alone or nivolumab plus ipilimumab in patients with advanced or metastatic endometrial tumors.35 In the present case, the patient received a regimen of sintilimab combined with nab‑paclitaxel and carboplatin as second‑line therapy. The progression‑free survival from the initiation of first immune‑chemotherapy to disease progression was longer than that achieved with first‑line conventional chemotherapy. This finding provides a reference for the clinical application of immunotherapy in HGESS and suggests that, for patients with HGESS who are chemotherapy‑resistant and have a background of potential immune activity, ICIs combined with chemotherapy may represent a salvage treatment option worth exploring.

Conclusion

This case report describes sintilimab-related cystitis/ureteritis in a patient with high-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma with possible concomitant or partially suppressed bacterial infection, representing the first such report in uterine sarcoma. The diagnosis was supported by imaging findings of diffuse bladder and ureteral wall thickening, dramatic response to corticosteroids, and cystoscopic biopsy showing chronic inflammation. The presence of Enterococcus faecalis on subsequent culture suggests a possible concomitant or partially suppressed bacterial infection. Key lessons include: (1) early recognition of ICI-related urological irAEs when urinary symptoms arise around cycles 3–6 with poor response to antibiotics; (2) prompt exclusion of infection and obstruction through urine cultures and imaging; (3) use of contrast-enhanced CT or MRI when ultrasound is negative but clinical suspicion remains high; (4) corticosteroid responsiveness as a diagnostic and therapeutic cornerstone; and (5) careful individualized decision-making regarding ICI rechallenge, with close monitoring and consideration of alternative ICIs in select cases. This report, together with our structured literature review, provides a practical reference for the diagnosis and management of ICI-related urological irAEs and for subsequent antitumor treatment.
