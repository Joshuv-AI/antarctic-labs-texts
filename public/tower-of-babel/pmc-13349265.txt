Correspondence: Sarcopenia and its Cognitive Consequences: Insights from an Urban Indian Cohort
Authors: Nagma Sheenam, Ravi Gaur, Nitesh M Gonnade, TK Abins
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Joseph and colleagues titled “Sarcopenia and its cognitive consequences: Insights from an urban Indian cohort”.[1] The paper investigates the association between sarcopenia and cognitive performance using data from the Tata longitudinal study of aging (TLSA) and provides valuable information in the Indian context.

The study included cognitively normal adults (clinical dementia rating = 0) aged ≥45 years from the TLSA cohort. Restricting analyses to cognitively unimpaired participants may introduce collider bias, as both sarcopenia and cognition influence study inclusion, potentially attenuating or distorting observed associations. Excluding individuals with mild cognitive impairment or dementia may therefore underestimate the true relationships. In addition, TLSA participants were predominantly urban, highly educated (median education = 15 years), and relatively (mean age = 62 years), which limits generalizability to older adults, rural populations, or those with lower socioeconomic status. Survival bias may also be present if individuals with more severe sarcopenia or cognitive impairment were lost to follow-up.

The authors assessed sarcopenia using only handgrip strength and calf circumference, classifying participants into healthy, intermediate (one abnormal), and sarcopenia (both abnormal) groups. This approach diverges from the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 framework, which defines possible sarcopenia by low strength or physical performance, sarcopenia by low muscle mass plus low strength, and severe sarcopenia when all domains are impaired. Omitting muscle mass and physical performance measures risks misclassification and attenuation of associations. The introduction of an intermediate category further complicates interpretation, as its relationship to AWGS-defined phenotypes is unclear.[2] Although the lack of Indian cutoffs is acknowledged, the implications of assessing only two components are not fully explored.

The screening tool for sarcopenia was derived from the original study by Shinya Ishii et al.,[3] in which classification was based on three parameters, including age. The omission of this key variable in the present study represents a deviation from the validated protocol and may compromise the applicability of the normative cutoff values.

The statistical analysis section states that pairwise deletion was used to handle missing data. Pairwise deletion can produce different sample sizes for each analysis and yields unbiased estimates only when data are missing completely at random, a strong and rarely satisfied assumption. In cross-sectional studies, missingness often relates to age, health status, or socioeconomic factors. More robust techniques such as multiple imputation or full information maximum likelihood would have retained all available data and reduced bias. The authors should report the proportion of missing values for each variable and conduct sensitivity analyses using appropriate imputation methods.

The generalized linear models (GLMs) were adjusted for age, gender, education, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. However, Table indicates significant between-group differences in body mass index (BMI), C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides, total cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein. BMI and lipid profiles are strongly associated with both sarcopenia and cognition and could act as confounding variables. In addition, physical activity, nutritional status, socioeconomic status, and medication use (for example, statin use, which the authors note was related to CRP levels) were not included in the models. Failure to adjust for these factors may result in residual confounding and overstate the independent effect of sarcopenia on cognitive performance.

The study evaluated multiple cognitive outcomes (Hindi Mental State Examination [HMSE], Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-III ACE-III] total score, and five ACE-III subdomains) using unadjusted and adjusted GLMs. Although Bonferroni correction was applied to the Spearman correlations, no adjustment was made for the numerous regression-based comparisons. Conducting multiple uncorrected tests increases the risk of type I error. Applying a family-wise or false discovery rate correction (e.g., Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg) across all cognitive outcomes would provide more conservative estimates of significance. Without such control, some reported associations may be due to chance.

The authors report using generalized linear models to examine associations between sarcopenia status and cognitive scores; however, the specific distributional family and link function are not described. For continuous cognitive outcomes, a Gaussian model with an identity link would be appropriate, provided assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity are satisfied, and it would be helpful if the authors clarified whether diagnostic checks, such as residual plots or influence diagnostics, were performed. In addition, handgrip strength and calf circumference are likely to be correlated with age and BMI, raising the possibility of multicollinearity if these variables were entered simultaneously into multivariable models. Reporting variance inflation factors or other collinearity diagnostics would allow readers to better assess the stability and robustness of the reported estimates.

By analyzing only cognitively intact, urban participants, the study may underestimate the strength of the association between sarcopenia and cognition. Individuals with sarcopenia and cognitive impairment are likely underrepresented in the TLSA cohort. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences (an issue acknowledged by the authors), but the aforementioned biases may also explain part of the observed associations. The results should therefore be interpreted with caution and should not be generalized to the broader Indian population without further validation.
