Surgical Resection of Lumbar Spine Osteoblastoma With Secondary Aneurysmal Bone Cysts Through a Combined Transpsoas and Posterior Approach: A Case Report
Authors: Enliang Chen, Daxuan Liang, Xiaodong Li, Xiaobao Zou, Chenfu Deng, Junlin Chen, Rencai Ma, Xiangyang Ma, Junjie Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAggressive lumbar osteoblastoma with secondary aneurysmal bone cysts is rare and may involve both posterior elements and vertebral bodies. When extensive multilevel involvement is present, a combined posterior and transpsoas approach can facilitate complete tumor resection, restore spinal stability, and achieve favorable neurological and functional outcomes with low recurrence risk.Keywords: aggressive osteoblastoma, aneurysmal bone cysts, transpsoas approach, vertebrectomy

Introduction

Primary spinal column tumors, including malignant pathologies and benign lesions, are rare in the general population. First described by Lichtenstein [1] in the 1950s, osteoblastomas are rare primary bone tumors that are commonly located in the posterior elements involving the pedicle and lamina and tend to predominate during the second decade of life [2]. Aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) are rare, benign lesions of the bone, and approximately 8%–30% of ABCs arise in the spine, mostly in the thoracic and lumbar regions [3, 4]. Osteoblastoma and ABCs individually comprise less than 1% of bone tumors [5, 6]. The combination of osteoblastoma and ABCs in the lumbar spine is a relatively rare occurrence in an already rare subclass of tumors [7].

We describe a case of aggressive osteoblastoma with ABCs of both the vertebral body and the posterior elements of L4 and L5, including a summary of the radiographic findings, relevant pathology, and surgical intervention. A six‐month clinical and radiographic follow‐up is presented. Given the rarity of lumbar osteoblastoma combined with ABC involving two adjacent vertebrae and the limited literature on minimally invasive lateral approaches in such cases, this report highlights a novel combined surgical strategy and its clinical outcomes.

Case History/Examination

A 12‐year‐old girl underwent surgical treatment for an aneurysmal bone cyst in the L4 vertebral body in another hospital in August 2021, and the postoperative pathological results showed that the lesion was consistent with osteosarcoma. Two months after the operation, the mass was palpated again at the original surgical site in the waist, so she presented to our hospital for treatment. The pathological slides from other hospitals were borrowed for consultation, suggesting that aggressive osteoblastoma with aneurysmal bone cysts was considered.

On examination, a protruding mass approximately 5 cm in diameter was seen at the level of the spinous process of the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae in the lower waist. The mass was soft, had an unclear boundary, and had a hard texture, and the skin had a slightly elevated temperature and was tender (+). The muscle strengths of the right lower extremity iliopsoas muscle, left lower extremity iliopsoas muscle, and remaining muscles were assessed to be grade IV, grade III, and grade V, respectively. The sensations and muscle tension of both lower extremities were normal. The patient reported no issues with urinary or fecal continence.

Preoperative X‐ray showed dense nodular shadows in the L4 vertebral body and paravertebral soft tissue, and dense clump‐like shadows were seen in the posterior soft tissue at the L4‐5 level. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) showed osteolytic destruction of the L4‐5 vertebral body and appendages with multilocular cyst formation, and tumor recurrence was considered. A 1.5‐T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan showed a large extraspinal soft tissue mass containing large flow voids infiltrating the body of the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae and compressing the spinal canal (Figure 1). PET/CT results showed that the bone destruction of the L4 and L5 vertebral bodies and their appendages was accompanied by a perifocal soft tissue mass with slightly elevated metabolism, and tumor recurrence was considered. PET/CT imaging of other parts of the body showed no obvious abnormalities.

Methods

We decided to completely excise the recurrent tumor in the patient. To prevent excessive bleeding, an autologous blood transfusion machine was used during the operation. After anesthesia administration, the patient was placed in the prone position, and the mass was exposed through a midline incision along the spinous process from L1 to S2. A large mass at the L4–L5 level was observed. The mass was lifted with curved forceps and separated by a nerve dissector. Then, the mass and the laminae of L4 and L5 were completely removed, and a pathological biopsy was performed. Posterior surgery was used to remove the remaining L3 caudal lamina, bilateral facet joints, and transverse processes of both L4 and L5. Subsequently, bilateral L1, L2, L3, and S1 pedicle screws and sacroiliac screws were implanted. The dural sac and nerve root were carefully retracted with a nerve root retractor. Then, discectomy of the L3/4 and L5/S1 intervertebral discs was performed. Two titanium rods of appropriate length were selected and placed into the U‐shaped groove of the pedicle screws on both sides, and the nuts were tightened. Two matching horizontal connection devices were used for reinforcement (Figure 1).

Then, the patient was adjusted into a true right lateral position, with the left hip and knee flexed. Adhesive plaster was used to appropriately fix the patient. Fluoroscopy was used to localize the index level. After a 10 cm incision was made across the index level, the autogenous iliac crest was extracted first. Then, blunt dissection of the external and internal oblique muscles, transverse abdominal muscle, and transverse fascia was performed. Afterwards, the surgeon reached into the retroperitoneal space with a finger, and a blunt dilator was used to penetrate the psoas muscle. The target area of L4 and L5 was located and exposed with a retractor, and then the segmental artery was identified and ligated or coagulated with a bipolar cautery. Separate exposures through the psoas muscle were used to first access the disc spaces above and below the corpectomy level (L3/4 and L5/S1) to allow for complete discectomies and endplate preparation. After incising the anterior longitudinal ligament, the anterior and middle columns of L4 and L5 were further released and removed intact. A cylindrical titanium cage of an appropriate length filled with autogenous iliac crest and an allograft was then implanted and reinforced with three screws (Figure 2).

Conclusion and Results

The total surgical time was 11 h, and the blood loss volume was 3000 mL. Postoperatively, the patient experienced significant clinical improvement. The muscle strength of the left iliopsoas improved from grade III preoperatively to grade IV at discharge and reached grade V at 7‐month follow‐up. The right lower extremity muscle strength recovered to grade V. No new neurological deficits were observed. The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score decreased from 8/10 preoperatively to 2/10 at 1 month postoperatively and 0–1/10 at the 7‐month follow‐up. The patient regained independent ambulation within 4 weeks after surgery. At the 7‐month follow‐up, she resumed normal daily activities without limitations.

Radiographs and CT imaging at 7 months demonstrated solid arthrodesis, satisfactory cage positioning, and no loosening of instrumentation (Figure 3). Importantly, there was no radiographic or clinical evidence of tumor recurrence.

Aggressive osteoblastoma with arterial hemangioma bone cysts in the lumbar spine is rare. In addition to the traditional en bloc technique for complete resection of the tumor, a transpsoas approach combined with a posterior approach may also be considered.

Discussion

Osteoblastoma is a rare bone tumor that accounts for less than 1% of all primary bone tumors and 30% to 40% of all spinal tumors [5, 8]. Around 20% to 40% of osteoblastomas occur in the spine with an equal distribution among the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar levels [9]. These tumors frequently occur in the posterior elements of the spine, including the pedicles [5]. Typically, patients report progressive, low‐grade, dull, aching pain, which is generally unresponsive to aspirin or other non‐steroid anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Although osteoblastoma does not produce high‐intensity localized night pain associated with osteoid osteoma [10], it can produce spinal instability and neurologic manifestations because of the destruction of surrounding bony structures and compression of the spinal cord and vertebral arteries [11]. The general histologic features of osteoblastoma are characterized by cellular osteoblastic tissue with active intercellular production of osteoid material and primitive woven bone and fibrovascular stroma, and in 89% of cases there is minimal or no mitotic activity [12].

Aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) are non‐neoplastic, primarily vascular lesions characterized by thin‐walled, blood‐filled cystic cavities [9]. ABCs are most common in patients younger than 20 years but have been reported in patients aged 2–69 years [13, 14]. Similar to osteoblastomas, ABCs affect males slightly more often than females, and their most common symptom is local pain [5]. On gross examination, the lesion consists of a soft fibrovascular core containing blood‐filled cyst‐like cavities surrounded by a bone shell [3, 15]. On histologic examination, fibroblasts, multinucleated osteoclast‐like giant cells, immature trabecular bone, hemosiderin granules, macrophages, and thin capillary vasculature are typically observed [15, 16].

In 1988, Martinez and Sissons reviewed 61 cases of osteoblastoma, finding a related ABC in 4 cases [17]. More recently, a few cases on osteoblastoma associated with ABCs in the sacrum, cervical spine (subaxial and odontoid), and skull were reported [17, 18]. Nonetheless, although the combination of osteoblastoma and aneurysmal bone cyst may be seen in 10%–15% of cases of primary bone neoplasms, the occurrence of both in the lumbar spine is rare [19].

Typically, en bloc resection is the standard of care for primary spinal column tumors [20]. The goal of en bloc tumor resection is to provide local oncological control and prevent the seeding of tumor cells into surrounding tissues [21]. Thus, complete surgical excision of both ABCs and osteoblastomas, which minimizes the possibility of recurrence, as well as preoperative embolization of the ABCs to reduce their inherently high risk of bleeding, can be reasonably considered the gold standard of treatment [5].

After surgical intervention has been indicated, the surgeon must preoperatively plan the optimal surgical approach to adequately address the pathology while minimizing potential complications [22]. However, given most surgeons' familiarity and comfort with the approach, the posterior approach remains the conventional technique for en bloc resection of spinal tumors. However, in a case series of using an open, transpedicular approach for the treatment of spinal metastases with spondylectomy, decompression, and circumferential fusion, Bilsky et al. reported a complication rate of 48%, including two infections and three mortalities within 30 days [23]. Another study of 67 patients who underwent posterior decompression and fusion for spinal tumors reported a 19.4% wound complication rate and a 16% infection rate [24].

Since Ozgur et al. first described extreme lateral interbody fusion (XLIF) in 2006, the technique has become a less invasive alternative to conventional anterior and posterior approaches for interbody fusion [25]. In fact, corpectomy in the lumbar spine using the transpsoas approach is a direct extension of the XLIF technique. However, there are few reports on the application of this technique in spinal tumors. In 2010, Uribe and colleagues reported significant improvements over traditional, open surgical techniques with the LLIF approach for thoracic tumors, with a mean blood loss of 291 cc and LOS of 3 days in 21 consecutive patients with an average of 21 months of follow‐up [26]. In a series of three patients treated with the XLIF approach for neurofibroma removal, Dakwar et al. reported a mean blood loss of 150 mL, operative time of 85 min, and LOS of 2 days. There were no complications, and the patients achieved significant improvements in pain and function [27].

In the present case, a sole posterior or lateral approach was insufficient due to extensive involvement of both posterior elements and vertebral bodies across two levels (L4–L5). Therefore, a combined posterior and transpsoas approach was strategically employed. Compared with conventional en bloc spondylectomy performed entirely through a posterior approach, our combined technique allowed direct visualization and complete resection of posterior tumor components, safe anterior column corpectomy through a minimally invasive lateral corridor, improved reconstruction of spinal stability with circumferential fusion, and potential reduction in manipulation of neural structures during anterior column removal. While lateral approaches such as XLIF/LLIF have been reported for degenerative conditions and selected spinal tumors, their application in aggressive benign tumors involving two lumbar vertebrae remains extremely rare. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of combined aggressive osteoblastoma with secondary aneurysmal bone cysts involving two adjacent lumbar vertebrae resected using a staged posterior–transpsoas strategy.
