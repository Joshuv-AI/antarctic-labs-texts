Cryo-EM structures of heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 channel suggest mechanisms of inward rectification and channel blockage
Authors: Yingjie Ning, Xiaoyu Zhou, Yimin Zhang, Nanhao Chen, Qianbei Guo, Chenxia Gu, Didi Xu, Ran Cui, Jiayi Wang, Mengdan Li, Dongxue Yang, Chen Song, Jie Yu, Zhaobing Gao, Jingpeng Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInwardly rectifying potassium (Kir) channels play key roles in regulating membrane potential and potassium transport through voltage-dependent inhibition by cytoplasmic Mg²⁺ and polyamines. Despite decades of extensive studies, the structural basis for polyamine-mediated inward rectification remains unclear. Here, we present cryo-EM structures of the heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 channel—which is critical for brain and kidney function and whose dysfunction causes EAST/SeSAME syndrome—in its apo state and in complex with spermine, a channel blocker named VU0134992, and EHop-016, an inhibitor identified in this study. The structures of Kir4.1/5.1, in an opposite 2:2 heterotetrameric assembly, suggest an inner-ring blockage mechanism, where polyamines and channel blockers with different stoichiometries bind in a membrane-parallel orientation at the upper site of the transmembrane central cavity. Together with electrophysiology and molecular dynamics simulations, our findings provide mechanistic insights into inward rectification, channel inhibition, and the pharmacology of Kir channels.Subject terms: Ion channels in the nervous system, Cryoelectron microscopy, Structural biology

Introduction

Inwardly rectifying potassium (Kir) channels are critical for maintaining resting membrane potential and potassium homeostasis, affecting neuronal signaling, heart rate, insulin release, hearing and kidney function1. Kir4.1 (encoded by KCNJ10) and Kir5.1 (encoded by KCNJ16), members of the K+ transport subfamily of Kir channels, are highly expressed in the brain, kidney and inner ear2–5. Kir4.1 forms either homotetramers alone or heterotetramers with Kir5.1 in a 4-5-4-5 order6. Heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 displays distinct functional properties compared to homomeric Kir4.1, including larger unitary conductance, stronger inward rectification, higher pH sensitivity and diverse intracellular regulations2,6–10. Mutations in KCNJ10 cause EAST/SeSEAM syndrome, an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by epilepsy, ataxia, sensory hearing loss and renal salt-wasting tubulopathy11,12, whereas dysfunction of Kir5.1 displays an overlapping but distinct disease spectrum13,14. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of Kir4.1 or Kir4.1/5.1 has been shown to produce diuresis, natriuresis, kaliuresis and antidepressant effects in animal models15,16, underscoring their potential as therapeutic targets for neurological and cardiovascular diseases.

Inward rectification is the property of Kir channels that enables greater K⁺ influx than efflux, driven by voltage-dependent blockages from intracellular Mg²⁺ and polyamines1. While Mg²⁺ induces fast blocking and unblocking, polyamines mediate a slower time-dependent inhibition with steeper voltage dependence17,18. Among natural polyamines, spermine exhibits the highest potency and steepest voltage-dependent blockage of Kir channels19,20. Electrophysiology and mutagenesis studies have identified a key acidic residue responsible for strong inward rectification, known as “D/N site” or “rectification controller”21–23, located approximately halfway inside the central cavity of the transmembrane domain. Structural studies have provided insights into Mg2+ binding24, and the regulation of Kir channels by intracellular lipids25,26, G proteins25,27 and ATP/ADP28–30. However, the structural basis of polyamine-mediated inhibition remains unclear. Furthermore, the interaction between Kir channels and positively charged channel blockers, which may share similar binding and inhibitory mechanisms with polyamines31, is not well understood, limiting our understanding of channel blockage and potential drug development.

In this study, we combine cryo-EM structural biology, electrophysiology and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to elucidate the structures of Kir4.1/5.1 channels alone or in complex with spermine and a channel blocker named VU013499216. Furthermore, we identify EHop-016, a known Rac GTPase inhibitor with anti-cancer effects32, inhibiting both Kir4.1 and Kir4.1/5.1 with IC50s of 0.80 ± 0.05 μM and 3.20 ± 0.18 μM, respectively, and determine the EHop-016-bound Kir4.1/5.1 structure. Our findings reveal two spermine, two VU0134992 or one EHop-016 bind to the upper site of the central cavity in an unexpected membrane-parallel orientation, forming inner rings to block the channel pore. These results provide a structural framework for understanding heteromerization, inward rectification and channel-blocking mechanisms of Kir4.1/5.1 channels and provide insights for therapeutic and rational drug development of Kir channels.

Results

Functional characterization and overall structure

Homomeric Kir4.1 channels display intermediate inward rectification, whereas heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 channels, tested with either a tandem construct of Kir4.1-Kir5.1 or co-expression of Kir4.1 and Kir5.1, display significantly stronger inward rectification (Fig. 1a–c). To facilitate structural studies, we utilized truncated constructs comprising residues 25–368 of Kir4.1 and 20–355 of Kir5.1, with well-behaved biochemical behavior and electrophysiological properties comparable to the full-length Kir4.1/5.1 (Fig. 1a–c, Supplementary Fig. 1a). While investigating the channel blockage mechanism is best achieved using open-state structures, our initial attempts to capture the structures of Kir4.1/5.1 bound with PIP2, a conserved Kir channel activator, in either mild detergent micelles (glyco-diosgenin, GDN) or lipid nanodiscs yielded highly flexible conformations that precluded high-resolution reconstruction. In contrast, PIP2-free Kir4.1/5.1 could be well-resolved with clear side-chain densities. Reasoning that blockers might still access the central cavity via structural fluctuations, we proceeded to determine the structures of Kir4.1/5.1 in the absence of PIP2. Because preparations in GDN produced slightly better reconstructions than those in lipid nanodiscs, we determined the final cryo-EM structures of the channel alone, or in complex with spermine, VU0134992, or EHop-016, in GDN micelles without PIP2. These structures were resolved at overall resolutions ranging from 3.1 Å to 3.5 Å (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–5). The maps are of high quality to allow the de novo model building of both protein and ligands.

Kir4.1 and Kir5.1 form a heterotetramer in a 2:2 ratio, adopting an opposite assembly with overall dimensions of approximately 115 × 65 × 70 Å (Fig. 1d, e). The overall structure shares the conserved Kir channel architecture24,25, containing a transmembrane domain (TMD) with two transmembrane helices (outer helix TM1 and inner helix TM2), an ion selectivity filter and a short pore helix, and the β-strand-rich cytoplasmic domain (CTD) formed by the N- and C- termini. Given that Kir5.1 is more homologous to Kir2.1 than Kir4.1 (Supplementary Fig. 7), Kir4.1/5.1 exhibits significant structural differences from homomeric Kir4.1, incorporating substantial structural features of the Kir2 subfamily. The most striking feature of Kir4.1/5.1 is the structural heterogeneity at both cytoplasmic subunit interfaces, namely Kir5/4 interface and Kir4/5 interface, and the gating sites along the ion permeation pathway (Fig. 1d–j). Kir channels typically harbor a primary gating site located at the cross-bundle region of TM2 and a regulatory gating site at the G-loop of CTD24,25. In Kir4.1/5.1, the TM2 gating site is formed by L166 in Kir4.1 (equivalent to M180 in Kir2.1) and I165/L169 in Kir5.1 (equivalent to I176/M180 in Kir2.1) (Fig. 1f, g, Supplementary Fig. 7). These three pairs of residues, with side-chain densities well resolved (Supplementary Fig. 1), form a compact and stepwise arrangement, resulting in a narrow transmembrane gate (Fig. 1f–i). The G-loop, which functions as the ion constriction site in the CTD, adopts a rectangular shape (Fig. 1j), with subunit distances of ~8 Å (Kir5.1) and ~14 Å (Kir4.1), comparable to homomeric Kir2.1 and Kir4.1 channels15,33, respectively. Two ion-like densities are observed under the G-loop. In Kir2.133,Kir2.224, and Kir3.225, additional ion constriction sites between the G-loop and the cytoplasmic entrance of CTD form an “upper ring” and a “lower ring” (Supplementary Fig. 8), highlighting the critical role of the CTD in Mg2+ and polyamine binding and regulation17,33–36. However, in Kir4.1/5.1, this region is wide open and does not contain this constriction site (Fig. 1f, j, Supplementary Fig. 8).

Distinct cytoplasmic interfaces of heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1

The CTD interface regulates Kir channel function by modulating gating kinetics, inward rectification and pH sensitivity37,38. Intriguingly, Kir4.1/5.1 possesses two types of CTD interfaces: a canonical Kir4/5 interface similar to that in Kir2, Kir3 and Kir6 channels, and an atypical Kir5/4 interface resembling that of homomeric Kir4.1. The classical Kir4/5 interface involves two pairs of electrostatic interactions (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 7d). E227 (Kir4.1) forms sandwich-like contacts with the guanidine group of R193 and R309 (Kir5.1) at 3.0 Å and 3.8 Å, respectively, inducing a ~ 90° bend in the β5-β6 strand of Kir4.1 at residue R230 (Fig. 2a). This bend facilitates a second interaction between the carboxyl group of E225 (Kir4.1) and the main-chain nitrogen of D194 and G195 (Kir5.1) at 2.8 Å and 3.1 Å (Fig. 2a). These electrostatic pairs (E-R/R) are conserved in Kir2, Kir3 and Kir6 channels24–30. Furthermore, R230 (Kir4.1) lies close to D312 and E315 (Kir5.1), suggesting a potential third stabilizing contact. In contrast, the Kir5/4 interface lacks these interactions (Fig. 2b). R230 (Kir5.1) and H190/E310 (Kir4.1)—equivalent to E227(Kir4.1) and R193/R309 (Kir5.1) in the Kir4/5 interface—are too far away to form stable interactions (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 7d). As a result, the β5-β6 strand of Kir5.1 remains in a straight conformation (not bended), preventing the formation of the second pair of interactions, similar to that observed in homomeric Kir4.1 (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Notably, residues at this atypical interface, including R230 (Kir5.1), H190 (Kir4.1) and E310 (Kir4.1), have been extensively characterized and demonstrated to regulate heteromeric assembly, channel gating and pH regulation38,39. The structural observation, together with previous electrophysiological studies, supports the notion that dynamic rather than static interactions at the Kir5/4 interface are critical for coupling structural rearrangements to functional regulation.

To test whether disruption of the canonical Kir4/5 interface (E-R/R pair) affects heteromeric assembly, we employed a split-intein trans-splicing assay (Fig.2c–e)40. Complementary split inteins (IN and IC) were inserted into the N-terminus of Kir4.1 and the C-terminus of Kir5.1, respectively, each flanked by an ER-retention motif (ERr), ensuring that only assembled heteromers traffic to the plasma membrane (Fig.2c). Upon subunit co-assembly, the juxtaposed inteins undergo post-translational trans-splicing, excising the intein sequences and covalently joining Kir4.1 and Kir5.1 to generate a Kir4.1-5.1 tandem detectable by immunoblotting at ~120 kDa (Fig.2e). Electrophysiology confirmed that this system reconstitutes WT Kir4.1/5.1 currents (Fig.2d). Introducing the E227R (Kir4.1) mutation did not reduce formation of the 120 kDa Kir4.1-5.1 tandem, which was absent when either subunit was expressed alone. Moreover, whole-cell recordings of cells expressing the Kir4.1E227R/Kir5.1 tandem construct revealed a modest reduction in inward rectification, yet overall channel behavior remained comparable to that of WT Kir4.1/5.1 (Fig.2f). These findings demonstrate that disruption of the canonical Kir4/5 electrostatic interaction does not significantly impair heteromeric assembly and function of Kir4.1/5.1, indicating that additional structural determinants beyond the E/R-R interaction also contribute to the formation and stability of the Kir4.1/5.1 heteromer.

Structure of Kir4.1/5.1 bound with spermines

To investigate the structural basis for Kir-spermine interaction, we determined the structure of Kir4.1/5.1 in the presence of spermine (Supplementary Fig. 2). Comparison of the spermine-bound and apo structures revealed that the major conformational differences localize to the central cavity of the transmembrane domain. In the apo state, the cavity is occupied by discrete, spherical densities, likely corresponding to bound ions, reminiscent of those observed in the Kir2.2 crystal structure24. By contrast, in the spermine-bound map, with either C1 or C2 symmetry during data processing, two continuous, linear densities were observed in the upper central cavity, suggesting the binding of two spermine molecules (Supplementary Fig. 6). To further ensure that these densities do not represent an averaged signal from a single spermine bound to one site, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations initialized with either two spermines or a single spermine, based on the cryo-EM map (Supplementary Fig. 9). Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and binding free energies were calculated for all simulation trajectories to assess the stability of protein-ligand interactions. In the two-spermine simulation, the Kir4.1/5.1 backbone reached the equilibrium state at the RMSD values of roughly 3 Å. At the same time, both spermine molecules remained stably bound within the 200-ns simulation, exhibiting RMSD values of approximately 1 Å, with the corresponding binding free energies of −19.3 and −22.1 kcal/mol (Supplementary Fig. 9). In contrast, in the single-spermine simulation, the molecule underwent substantial displacement and failed to align with either of the spermine densities observed in the cryo-EM map. The calculated binding free energies (−14.6 and −16.2 kcal/mol) further indicate a weaker affinity in the single-spermine simulation. Overall, the cryo-EM and MD simulation analyses demonstrate that Kir4.1/5.1 can accommodate two spermine molecules within the central cavity, a configuration that is energetically more favorable than a single spermine.

The upper site contains three layers of residues (Fig. 3a, c). Residues at the bottom of the selectivity filter and pore helix (layer 1) and the rectification controller residues (layer 3) determine the dimension of negatively charged cavity along the electric field, while residues primarily on TM2 (layer 2) determine the cavity dimensions parallel to membrane and create a rectangular-shaped cavity with approximate dimensions of 16.5 × 20 Å (measured from Cα atoms of C158 in Kir5.1 and T155 in Kir4.1, respectively) (Fig. 3b). Spermine molecules, with a length of ~15Å, orient more toward Kir4.1 to fit in the longer side of the cavity, with its amine groups interacting with T154, T155 and E158 on TM2. Mutations of E158 (Kir4.1) to alanine or asparagine result in a weaker rectification, supporting its role as the rectification controller in Kir4.1/5.1 (Supplementary Fig. 10). Kir4.1T154A, which weakens the interaction with the terminal amines of spermine, reduces inward rectification (Supplementary Fig. 10), consistent with previous findings showing the weaker binding affinity caused by modification of the terminal amines of spermine41,42. In addition, residues surrounding the pocket without direct interactions with spermines contribute to the channel function. Alanine mutations of the aromatic residue on TM1(Kir4.1F75A/Kir5.1WT or Kir4.1WT/Kir5.1Y81A), and the residues at the bottom of selectivity filer (Kir4.1T128A/Kir5.1T131A) were not functional, suggesting they are essential for the structural integrity of the upper site or ion permeation (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 10). Moreover, oxidation of C158 (Kir5.1), which lies on layer 2 and defines the shorter side of the upper cavity, inhibits channel function and mediates the S-glutathionylation of heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 by intracellular oxidants7. The binding of spermine also prevents methyl thiosulfhydryl modification of cysteine mutations on the upper site instead of the lower site of the central cavity41,43. Because these spermine-interacting residues also line the ion-permeation pathway, they likely modulate inward rectification not only through blocker binding but also by influencing ion flow and channel gating. To further assess the contribution of polyamines, we inhibited intracellular polyamine synthesis using difluoromethylornithine (DFMO) and methylglyoxal bis (guanylhydrazone) (MGBG) (Supplementary Fig. 11)44. Polyamine depletion produced slightly weaker yet still pronounced inward rectification (Supplementary Fig. 11c), consistent with recent findings that Kir4.1/5.1 rectification arises from both classical polyamine/Mg²⁺-dependent and intrinsic K⁺-flux-driven mechanisms37,45. Nevertheless, the structural and functional analyses demonstrate that residues in the upper cavity are important for spermine binding, inward rectification and channel function.

Structure of Kir4.1/5.1 bound with VU0134992

To date, the only reported structure bound with a channel blocker is homomeric Kir4.1; however, the density for the inhibitor presents as a bulky density in the central cavity only when C4 symmetry was applied, thus hindering the observation of inhibitor-channel stoichiometry and specific interactions15. To gain a mechanistic understanding of the Kir4.1/5.1 channel inhibition, we employed a well-characterized Kir4.1 inhibitor, VU0134992 and solved its cryo-EM structure in complex with Kir4.1/5.1 channels. VU0134992, with a length of ~15 Å similar to spermine, inhibits Kir4.1 and Kir4.1/5.1 with IC50s of 1.76 ± 0.07 μM and 14.9 ± 0.70 μM, respectively (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Fig. 12), comparable to previous functional characterizations16. We initially observed a continuous oval donut-shaped density occupying the upper site of the central cavity, in a membrane-parallel orientation similar to spermine (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Subsequent 3D local classifications focused on the TMD, using either RELION46 or CryoSPARC47 programs, resulted in two binding forms of VU0134992, namely straight binding and curly binding (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 3,4). In both binding modes, the tetramethylpiperidine nitrogen and the amide nitrogen of VU0134992 interact with E158 of Kir4.1(Fig. 4d, e). In the straight form, VU0134992 overlaps well with spermine, forming an extended conformation flanking the long side of the upper cavity in a clockwise direction (Fig. 4d). By contrast, in the curly conformation, VU0134992 assumes the opposite orientation and bends to accommodate the narrower side of the upper cavity (Fig. 4e). MD simulation results agree well with the cryo-EM maps, suggesting both forms can stably bind to the upper cavity, with the curly form exhibiting a lower binding free energy to Kir4.1/5.1 (−20.3 ± 3.1 and −19.8 ± 2.8 kcal/mol), compared to the straight form (−15.4 ± 1.6 and −14.6 ± 2.2 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Fig. 9). The pore radius at the VU0134992 in the straight and curly forms are 0.8 Å and 1.3 Å, respectively, indicating the channel blockage due to the narrow constriction sites and positive charge of the inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. 13). Mutations of E158 of Kir4.1 to alanine or glutamine results in reduced potencies of VU0134992, with IC50s shifting to 56.8 ± 6.30 μM and 57.7 ± 6.52 μM, respectively, while alanine mutations of other residues lining the upper cavity demonstrate subtle effect on channel inhibition by the ligand (Supplementary Fig. 12). Intriguingly, corresponding to a missense single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) c.497T > A (L166Q) in Kcnj10 in the ClinVar database, Kir4.1L166Q/Kir5.1L169Q displays a normal inward rectification but a disruption in the inhibition by VU0134992 (Supplementary Fig. 12). This observation suggests that gating residues near the intracellular entrance of the central cavity are critical for blocker access, although the precise mechanism would need further investigation. Nevertheless, VU0134992 binds to the upper cavity of Kir4.1/5.1 in the same membrane-parallel orientation and 2:1 stoichiometry (ligand to Kir4.1/5.1 tetramer ratio) as spermine, suggesting it may share the “inner-ring blockage” mechanism to prevent ion conductance.

Identification of EHop-016 as a Kir4.1/5.1 channel blocker

Inspired by the “inner-ring blockage” with 2:1 stoichiometry, we wondered whether it is a general mechanism underpinning Kir4.1/5.1 inhibition by other chemical ligands. To identify chemotypes with desirable potencies for Kir4.1/5.1, we performed a pilot-scale screening incorporating small molecules gained from our previous study in developing Kir4.1-specific inhibitors15. Using a thallium flux assay as previously described15, the inhibition against Kir4.1/5.1 channels was assessed across compounds. We identified EHop-016, a known Rac GTPase inhibitor with anti-cancer effects32, substantially inhibiting both homomeric Kir4.1 and heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 channel activities (Fig. 4a, c, Supplementary Fig. 14). Dose-response analysis with whole-cell electrophysiology revealed the IC50s for Kir4.1 and Kir4.1/5.1 channels were 0.80 ± 0.05 and 3.20 ± 0.18 μM, respectively, underscoring its greater potency for both channels compared with VU0134992. Structurally, EHop-016 featured a complex multi-ring structure and a longer flexible chain, suggesting the compatibility for a large ligand binding pocket. The length of EHop-016 (~19 Å) is significantly longer than VU0134992 and spermine (~15 Å for each), which exceeds to long side of the upper cavity and is too large to adopt a 2:1 stoichiometry. Therefore, we used the C1 symmetry in cryo-EM data processing and observed a density in the Kir4.1/5.1 structure for one Ehop-016 binding (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 5). Ehop-016 is highly positively charged with six nitrogen atoms and a single Ehop-016 molecule interacts with two E158 residues of both Kir4.1 subunits and one N161 residue from a Kir5.1 subunit, forming a “U-shaped ring blockage” with a pore radius of ~0.5 Å at the site of EHop-016 (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 14). Consistent with the strong interaction observed in the structure and more potent inhibition by EHop-016 in electrophysiology, MD simulations further show that one EHop-016 molecule stably binds to the upper site with a much lower free binding energy of −28.5 ± 4.8 kcal/mol compared to spermine and VU0134992 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Mutations of residues surrounding EHop-016 also indicate the critical role of E158 in EHop-016 inhibition, while different from VU0134992, Kir4.1L166Q/Kir5.1L169Q does not affect the inhibition by Ehop-016 (Supplementary Fig. 14). This finding does not contradict the role of gating residues in blocker entry but suggests that hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic substitutions at these sites may alter interactions depending on blocker structure.

Ligand-induced conformational changes

The structures of Kir4.1/5.1 in the apo and inhibitor-bound states were determined in the absence of the channel activator PIP2, exhibiting structural features for a closed conformation, with extended TMD-CTD separation and a narrow TM gate (Supplementary Figs. 13, 15). The spermine- and EHop-016-bound structures display similar overall structures compared to the apo state, while VU0134992 induces modest conformational changes in Kir4.1/5.1 (Supplementary Figs. 13, 15). The binding of VU0134992, in either straight or curly conformation, induces a similar asymmetric expansion of TMD, with greater movement observed at the TM gating site of L166 (Kir4.1) compared to L169 (Kir5.1). This expansion is likely a structural consequence of accommodating two large VU0134992 molecules within a relatively narrow upper site. The ligand-induced structural changes reinforce the interactions between ligands with Kir4.1/5.1 and suggest that Kir4.1/5.1 is capable of undergoing asymmetric gating properties.

Discussion

Inward rectification of Kir4.1/5.1

Kir4.1/5.1 exhibits two distinct modes of inward rectification: a classical extrinsic mechanism, mediated by voltage-dependent block by intracellular Mg²⁺ and polyamines1,17,18, and a recently reported intrinsic mechanism driven by K⁺ flux37,45. The latter was identified in excised inside-out patches recorded in the absence of polyamines and Mg²⁺, without exogenous PIP₂ supplementation, and under varying K[out]/K[in] gradients. Under these conditions, Kir4.1/5.1 channel closes at positive voltages but can be activated at negative voltages in a K+-dependent manner, maintaining strong inward rectification even when PIP₂ levels are low. Supplementation with 10 μM PIP2 keeps Kir4.1/5.1 open at positive potentials and restores voltage-dependent block by spermine, indicating a reduction of intrinsic inward rectification and the re-emergence of extrinsic, polyamine-dependent inward rectification. Because both modes of rectification are tightly coupled to channel gating and ion permeation, they are expected to be influenced by modulators of channel gating, such as ions, PIP2 and pH. In this study, while our analysis primarily focused on the extrinsic mechanism and provided structural, functional and computational evidence for spermine and inhibitors binding to Kir4.1/5.1, we also found that polyamine depletion reduces but does not eliminate strong inward rectification, supporting the coexistence of both mechanisms (Fig. 5a). It is thus reasonable to propose that extrinsic and intrinsic inward rectifications operate simultaneously under physiological conditions, with their relative contributions likely determined by the cellular context, including intracellular pH and the availability of PIP2, polyamines and Mg2+.

An “inner-ring blockage” model for Kir4.1/5.1 channel inhibition.

a Schematic representation of extrinsic (top) and intrinsic (bottom) inward rectification mechanisms. When PIP2 is abundant, Kir4.1/5.1 remains open at positive voltages and is blocked by intracellular positively charged particles, particularly Mg2+ and polyamines (extrinsic inward rectification). When PIP2 levels are low, Kir4.1/5.1 close at positive voltages but can be activated at negative voltages by the K+ influx, maintaining inward rectification even in the absence of polyamines (intrinsic inward rectification). The structures obtained in this study represent blocker-bound, PIP2-free states under depolarized and symmetrical pH conditions (0 mV and pH 8). b The binding modes of spermine (pink), VU0134992 (green and orange), and EHop-016 (blue) within the central cavity are depicted as cartoon representations. Layer 2 of the central cavity, formed by C158 (Kir5.1) and T154 (Kir4.1), has a length of approximately 19 Å (distance between Cɑ of T154) and a width of approximately 16 Å (distance between Cɑ of C158). The binding of these small molecules is primarily dictated by the spatial constraints of the cavity, as well as the influence of the negatively charged E158 (Kir4.1) and the polar N161 (Kir5.1).

An “inner-ring blockage” model for extrinsic inward rectification of Kir channels

Although the molecular mechanism of polyamine-mediated, voltage-dependent inward rectification has been extensively characterized, direct structural evidence for this process has remained elusive. This limitation likely arises from the technical difficulty of capturing long, flexible spermine molecules bound at the fourfold symmetry axis within the large hydrophilic central cavity of homomeric Kir channels. We reasoned that a heteromeric Kir4.1/5.1 channel, with its reduced symmetry and asymmetric subunit composition, would provide a greater likelihood of visualizing spermine binding. We determined closed-state structures of PIP2-free Kir4.1/5.1 bound to spermine and two inhibitors, hypothesizing they access the central cavity via transient structural “breathing” motions48. Since potassium channels routinely pass larger hydrated ions through restricted gates—analogous to oxygen entering hemoglobin—permeation of these bulky blockers is thermodynamically feasible49,50. Furthermore, our electrophysiological data showing that gating mutations (Kir4.1L166Q/Kir5.1L169Q) alter blocker inhibition strongly support the gate’s role in governing entry. Together, these observations robustly support a model where large blockers enter the cavity during transient structural fluctuations, yet whether these blockers affect other gating factors, such as PIP2 binding, warrants further investigation.

In this study, we captured two spermine molecules blocking the upper cavity in an unexpected membrane-parallel orientation. This binding mode was further validated by structural studies of Kir4.1/5.1 bound with two chemically distinct channel blockers and MD simulation analysis. These observations not only reinforce the previously proposed “cavity trapping” or “deep rectification” models17,18—where polyamines occupy the upper cavity to produce steep voltage dependence—but also reveal a more detailed multi-site interaction network within the upper cavity. Therefore, we propose an “inner-ring blockage” model for polyamine-dependent rectification. In this model, spermine molecules occupy the upper cavity in a staggered arrangement, resembling a “track-and-field stadium”. Two spermine or two VU0134992 molecules, both with a length of ~15 Å, can occupy the 2nd and 4th positions in a “4 × 100 meter relay”, while two VU0134992 molecules can alternately occupy the 1st and 3rd positions (Fig. 5b). By contrast, the bulkier EHop-016 (~19 Å in length) binds as a single molecule, spanning the inner field in the upper cavity (Fig. 5b). These findings provide a structural framework for rational design of upper-site channel blockers applicable not only to Kir4.1/5.1 but potentially to other Kir subfamilies. These upper-site blockers should (1) carry a net positive charge, (2) have appropriate size, length and geometry to fit the upper cavity, and (3) possess positively charged or hydrophilic groups with optimally spaced linkers to interact with the rectification controller. Such insights could inform future structure-based drug design—for instance, derivatives of VU0134992 with larger molecular size, optimized linkers, or tandem arrangements may achieve enhanced binding affinity, blocking potency, and subtype specificity.

Homomeric Kir4.1 retains ~10-15% residual conductance even at high concentrations of spermine51. This residual current has been attributed to partial spermine permeation through the ion selectivity filter. If spermine binds to Kir4.1 in a similar horizontal orientation as observed in Kir4.1/5.1, the larger upper cavity of Kir4.1, compared to that of Kir4.1/5.1, might permit limited K⁺ passage, providing an alternative explanation for the discrepancy between the residual current or intermediate rectification and the presence of the strong rectification controller residue E158. Consistent with this notion, Kir4.1 can be fully blocked by bulkier inhibitors such as VU0134992, EHop-016, and philanthotoxin51, as shown in this and previous studies. Nevertheless, this hypothesis warrants further structural and functional validation.

Limitations of this study and other possible mechanisms in inward rectification

Under our cryo-EM sample conditions (0 mV, 150 mM K⁺, pH 8.0), spermine and channel blockers stably occupy the upper cavity parallel to the membrane plane. However, polyamines may adopt alternative binding orientations, stoichiometries or mechanisms in other Kir subfamilies or under different physiological conditions where pH, polyamine concentrations, ionic gradients, and membrane potentials fluctuate. Recent MD simulations of Kir2.2 indicate that spermine can bind between the rectification controller and selectivity filter at intermediate voltages (+185 to +215 mV) and translocate through the selectivity filter at higher voltages (>+215 mV)52. It is therefore possible that the binding site, stoichiometry and orientation of polyamines could change upon membrane potential. Moreover, since our electrophysiological measurements were conducted in the whole-cell configuration, the current–voltage profiles of both WT and mutant Kir4.1/5.1 channels likely reflect combined contributions from intrinsic and extrinsic inward-rectification processes. Because our current structures represent closed, PIP₂-free states, we cannot exclude the possibility that polyamines or inhibitors modulate PIP₂ binding of Kir4.1/5.1 during depolarization, analogous to the PIP₂-shielding mechanism proposed for Kv7 channels53.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

The gene encoding residues 25 to 368 of human Kir4.1 (Uniprot ID: P78508) and residues 20 to 355 of human Kir5.1 (Uniprot ID: Q9NPI9) was cloned into the pEG BacMam vector54. Kir5.1 was expressed with N-terminal tags including an 8 x His tag, the yellow fluorescent protein (mVenus), and a 3C protease site, and Kir4.1 was expressed with N-terminal tags including two Strep-Tag II, the cyan fluorescent protein (mCerulean) and a 3 C protease site. Kir4.1 and Kir5.1 were co-expressed in HEK293F cells cultured in SMM 293TI medium supplemented with 1% FBS at 37 °C. For co-transfection, 0.5 mg Kir4.1 and 0.5 mg Kir5.1 plasmids were pre-incubated with 4 mg polyethylenimine (PEI) in 40 ml fresh SMM 293TII medium for 20 min. The mixture was then added to 800 ml of HEK293F cells at a density of 2.0 × 106 cells per mL. The final concentration of 10 mM sodium butyrate was added after 12 h of transfection, and the temperature was adjusted to 30 °C. The cells were collected 48 h after transfection and solubilized with buffer A containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM KCl, 1% (w/v) n-Dodecyl-β-D-Maltoside (DDM), 0.2% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS), 0.8 μM aprotinin, 2 μg/ml leupeptin, 2 mM pepstatin A, 1 mM PMSF for 2 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 186,009 g for 1 h to remove insoluble cell debris, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and incubated with Strep-Tactin resin at 4 °C for 1 h. The resin was washed with buffer B containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM KCl, 0.01% glyco-diosgenin (GDN). Proteins were then eluted with buffer B plus 5 mM desthiobiotin and incubated with TALON resin for 30 min at 4 °C. The TALON resin was subsequently washed with buffer C containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM KCl, 0.01% GDN, 10 mM imidazole. The Kir4.1/5.1 complex was eluted with buffer D containing 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM KCl, 0.01% GDN, 200 mM imidazole, and digested with 3 C protease on ice for 2 h to remove N-terminal tags. The mixture was then applied to the Superose 6 10/300 GL column pre-equilibrated with buffer B. The peak fractions from size exclusion chromatography (SEC) were collected, concentrated and used for cryo-EM sample preparation.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

For cryo-EM sample preparation, Kir4.1/5.1 were incubated without or with 1 mM spermine, 1 mM VU0134992, and 1 mM EHop-016, respectively, for 1 h on ice before grid freezing. Quantifoil 1.2/1.3 Au 300 mesh grids were glow-discharged for 35 s before use. A 4 µL aliquot of protein sample was applied to a glow-discharged grid under conditions of 4 °C and 100% humidity, followed by rapid vitrification in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot.

Cryo-EM data were collected on a 300 kV FEI Titan Krios microscope equipped with a Gatan K3 camera and an energy filter. Movies were acquired in the super-resolution mode at a pixel size of 0.5275 Å or 0.415 Å, using a dose rate of approximately 20 e⁻/pixel/second and a total electron dose of 50 e⁻/Å². The defocus range was set between −1.0 and −2.0 μm.

Cryo-EM data processing

All movie stacks were processed in CryoSparc (version 4.6.0) unless otherwise stated47. Movies were motion corrected by 2x bin and contrast transfer function parameters estimated. Micrographs with CTF fit resolution better than 4.5 Å were accepted for further processing.

For the apo Kir4.1/5.1 dataset, particles were picked from 6776 micrographs by blob picker and template picker, then subjected to several rounds of 2D classification. 1,337,289 particles with good features were finally selected and extracted in a box of 400 pixels with a binning factor of 2. Then, a good reference model was generated after several rounds of ab initio reconstruction with selected particles. Heterogeneous refinement with C1 symmetry was performed, resulting in 336,364 particles with clearly resolved transmembrane helices. Particles were extracted in a box of 400 pixels without binning for non-uniform refinement with C2 symmetry. To obtain better transmembrane helices with clear side chains, local refinement using a transmembrane domain mask was performed with C2 symmetry. The resolution of the final map was 3.13 Å (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Other datasets were processed similarly. For the Kir4.1/5.1-spermine dataset, 3,252,090 particles were selected from 9352 micrographs after several rounds of 2D classification. 930,068 particles were accepted after several rounds of hetero refinement for 3D classification with C1 symmetry, and then a class of 15.4% bad particles was rejected. The remaining 786,927 particles were first subjected to non-uniform refinement and local refinement with C1 symmetry, during which two clear spermine densities were observed in the inner cavity. To further improve the overall map quality, the same set of 786,927 particles was reprocessed using C2 symmetry, yielding a final map at 3.18 Å resolution. (Supplementary Fig. 2).

For the Kir4.1/5.1-VU0134992 dataset, 2,148,772 particles were selected from 11,441 micrographs after several rounds of 2D classification. 756,677 particles were retained after several rounds of heterogeneous refinement and 3D classification, and non-uniform refinement with C1 symmetry revealed two distinct ligand-bound classes. To further improve the overall resolution, class1 (straight conformation) with 200,895 particles and class2 (curly conformation) with 195,697 particles were performed by non-uniform refinement and local refinement with C2 symmetry, respectively, resulting in the resolution of 3.37 Å and 3.35 Å for class1 and class2. Meanwhile, 756,677 particles were imported to Relion 3.1 for 3D classification with C1 symmetry46, and two of the same classes as above were classified. Class1 with 338,486 particles and class2 with 284,106 particles were performed by non-uniform refinement with C2 symmetry, respectively, resulting in the resolution of 3.38 Å and 3.56 Å for class1 and class2 (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4).

For the Kir4.1/5.1-EHop-016 dataset, 2,153,163 particles were selected from 10,924 micrographs after several rounds of 2D classification. 281,339 particles were accepted by several rounds of heterogeneous refinement with C1 symmetry for non-uniform refinement and local refinement with C1 or C2 symmetry. The resolution of the final map was 3.55 Å (C1) and 3.37 Å (C2), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5). To illustrate the EHop-016 interaction with Kir4.1/5.1, the C1 map was used in structural analysis.

Model building and structure refinement

The high quality of cryo-EM maps of Kir4.1/5.1 allows model building in COOT55, with initial models generated by alphafold256. For all datasets, maps from local refinement with a focus on the TMD region have a much higher quality of densities and were thus used for the model building of TMD. For the TMD-CTD linker region and CTD, the overall cryo-EM maps were used. Kir4.1/5.1 in the apo state or in complex with spermine and VU0134992 were built using the C2 refined maps, while EHop-016-bound Kir4.1/5.1 were initially built with the C2 refined map, and further with the C1 refined map for the adjustment of ligand and protein residues. Spermine and other channel blockers were built based on the cryo-EM densities and the calculations from MD simulations. The models were refined against the overall cryo-EM maps of each dataset in Phenix (v1.20.1-4487)57. Maps and structures were visualized using ChimeraX58.

Electrophysiology

Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 growth medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under standard conditions (5% CO2, 37 °C). Flp-in CHO cells stably expressing the Kir4.1/5.1 tandem dimers generated in our previous work15 were maintained in F12 growth medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 700 μg/mL Hygromycin B and 1 mM BaCl2. For expression of wild-type or mutant Kir4.1/5.1 channels, CHO cells were transiently co-transfected with equivalent amounts (2000 ng, respectively) of plasmids encoding indicated N-terminally mCherry-tagged human Kir4.1 cDNAs and N-terminally YFP-tagged human Kir5.1 cDNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following 18 to 24 h incubation, transfected cells were plated onto poly-D-lysine-coated coverslips for whole-cell patch-clamp recordings.

For electrophysiological experiments, macroscopic currents were recorded in the voltage-clamp mode at room temperature using an Axopatch-700B amplifier (Axon), filtered at 2 kHz and digitalized at 20 kHz with a Digidata 1440 A interface (Axon). Borosilicate glass electrodes pulled by the P-1000 puller (Sutter Instrument) had resistances of 2–3 MΩ when filled with the standard pipette solution composed of (in mM) 140 KCl, 5 NaCl, 1 MgCl2, 1 CaCl2, 10 HEPES and 10 EGTA (pH 7.2 adjusted with KOH). The standard bath solution had the compositions of (in mM) 115 NaCl, 30 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 glucose and 10 HEPES (pH 7.4 adjusted with NaOH). A BPS perfusion system (ALA Scientific Instruments) was included for perfusion of the bath solution and application of drugs. The current-voltage (I-V) relationships for mutant Kir4.1/5.1 channels were examined under a voltage-step protocol, which consists of steps from the holding potential of −40 mV to voltages ranging from −80 mV to +80 mV in 20-mV increments for 500 ms, and analyzed with current amplitudes at the end of the step pulses plotted versus voltages. The rectification ratio was calculated with current amplitudes at indicated voltages normalized to the value at −80 mV. To assess responses of Kir4.1/5.1 mutants to small molecules VU0134992 and EHop-016, cells were stepped to −80 mV for 100 ms from −40 mV holding potential, followed by ramping to +90 mV at the rate of 0.34 mV/ms. VU0134992 and EHop-016 were dissolved in DMSO to make stock solutions and freshly diluted in the bath solution prior to recordings (ensuring the final concentration of DMSO was less than 0.1%, which had no effect on whole-cell results). Dose-response curves were fitted with a four-parameter Hill equation, Y=bottem+(top−bottom)/(1+10logIC50−X×hillslope), where the IC50 value is the drug concentration producing half-maximum inhibitory responses at −80 mV. All data are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and n represents the number of cells where electrophysiological data were obtained. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA analysis, and Dunnett’s post hoc test.

Generation of split-intein and ER-retention constructs

For the Kir4.1-encoding plasmid, the original start codon was removed, and the N terminus of Kir4.1 was ligated with a linker (ARSMEAS). After confirming that the linker did not impair channel activity with whole-cell recordings, the DnaE-C sequence (PRIKIATRKYLGKQNVYDIGVERDHNFALKNGFIASNCFN) and the ER retention/retrieval motif (LLDALTLASSRGPLRKRSVAVAKAKPKFSISPDSLS) were synthesized and introduced into the plasmid. The plasmid encoding Kir5.1 with an N-terminal YFP tag was modified at the C-terminus. The original stop codon was removed, and the C terminus of Kir5.1 was appended with a linker (GSAGSAAGSGEFAS), the addition of which was verified not to affect the functionality of the Kir4.1/5.1 heteromer. Following this, the DnaE-N sequence (CLSYETEILTVEYGLLPIGKIVEKRIECTVYSVDNNGNIYTQPVAQWHDRGEQEVFEYCLEDGSLIRATKDHKFMTVDGQMLPIDEIFERELDLMRVDNLPN) and the ER retention/retrieval motif (LLDALTLASSRGPLRKRSVAVAKAKPKFSISPDSLSPRKKFQ) connected by an additional linker (PINQPANTHERPR) were generated and ligated into the vector. The sequence of the final constructs was verified using Sanger sequencing.

Polyamine depletion experiment

CHO cells transiently expressing Kir4.1/5.1 heteromeric channels were treated with various concentrations of difluoromethylornithine (DFMO, 0.1 mM or 2.5 mM) combined with methylglyoxal bis(guanylhydrazone) (MGBG, 0.3 mM), followed by incubation for 24 h in the incubator prior to recordings.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Because the binding pocket locates in the transmembrane gate in the Kir4.1/5.1 ion channel, only the transmembrane region of the protein was considered in the simulations, with residues 57–175 from Kir4.1 and residues 60–175 from Kir5.1. The neutral termini, namely the acetylated N-terminus (ACE) and C-terminal methylated carboxylate (CT3), were added to the N-terminal and C-terminal of all chains, respectively. The protonation states of the acidic and basic residues were determined based on the pKa values calculated by the PROPKA3 program59. According to the pH value in the experiments, all the glutamates and aspartates were deprotonated, and all the lysines were protonated. The simulation systems were set up by CHARMM-GUI60,61. Here, all three different molecules, namely EHop-016, VU0134992, and spermine, were parameterized by CGenFF62 in CHARMM-GUI with the CHARMM General forcefield. The position of the membrane bilayer was determined by the PPM web server63, and the membrane was comprised by CHOL, POPC, POPE, POPG, POPI, and PSM with the ratio 20:32:16:11:5:16. In total, 361 lipid molecules were used to construct the lipid bilayer, and later, their behavior were described by the CHARMM36 all-atom additive forcefield64. At the same time, about 15 thousand TIP3P65 water molecules were added on both sides of the membrane bilayer, and about 40 potassium ions were inserted to balance the total charge of the system. Finally, the AMBER simulation input files were generated for the following molecular dynamics (MD) simulations66.

The classical MD simulations were carried out with a multi-step process to relax the systems and study the binding of different ligands in the Kir4.1/5.1 protein. First, the whole systems were minimized for five thousand steps with the protein and ligand atoms restraints (10 kcal/mol/Å2), and the lipid atoms restraints (2.5 kcal/mol/Å2). Second, a heating process followed by five equilibrium processes was carried out to heat the system to 300 K and equilibrate the density to 1 kg/L. The restraints were gradually decreased to zero during the equilibrium processes. Next, three replicates of 200 ns MD simulations were run to study the binding of the ligands. During the simulations, the Langevin thermostat algorithm with a collision frequency of 1.0 ps−1 was used to control the system temperature, and the Monte Carlo barostat was applied to control the semi-isotropic pressure67. The nonbonding cutoff value was gradually switched off from 10 to 12 Å with the switching function. The particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method was selected to describe the long-range summation of the electrostatic interaction. Bonds involving hydrogen were constrained by the SHAKE algorithm68. Finally, the trajectories in the last 50 ns MD simulations were used to calculate the binding free energy by the MM/GBSA algorithm69. All the simulations were conducted with AMBER2470.
