Abstract
Computational wave imaging (CWI) extracts hidden structure and physical properties of a volume of material by analyzing wave signals that traverse that volume. Applications include seismic exploration of the Earth’s subsurface, acoustic imaging and non-destructive testing in material science, and ultrasound computed tomography in medicine. Current approaches for solving CWI problems can be divided into two categories: those rooted in traditional physics, and those based on deep learning. Physics-based methods stand out for their ability to provide high-resolution and quantitatively accurate estimates of acoustic properties within the medium. However, they can be computationally intensive and are susceptible to ill-posedness and nonconvexity typical of CWI problems. Machine learning-based computational methods have recently emerged, offering a different perspective to address these challenges. Diverse scientific communities have independently pursued the integration of deep learning in CWI. This review delves into how contemporary scientific machine-learning (ML) techniques, and deep neural networks in particular, have been harnessed to tackle CWI problems. We present a structured framework that consolidates existing research spanning multiple domains, including computational imaging, wave physics, and data science. This study concludes with important lessons learned from existing ML-based methods and identifies technical hurdles and emerging trends through a systematic analysis of the extensive literature on this topic.

The common feature is the forward wave equation, and the common goal is the reconstruction of physical properties from wave measurements. Waves are ubiquitous in our world; prominent examples include the propagation of sound through the atmosphere, waves in the ocean, and seismic waves in the earth. Well-established physical models provide detailed descriptions of how these waves are influenced by the materials through which they propagate. Computational wave imaging (CWI) aims to invert these models, and to extract detailed information about these materials, including properties such as density and bulk modulus, using measurements of wave signals (amplitudes, phases, and travel-times) taken on the outer boundaries of these spaces.

A common set of computational challenges emerges from the shared foundation of wave physics across diverse imaging problems. One prominent challenge is the pervasive ill-posedness encountered in CWI problems, arising from the inherently limited data coverage, i.e. attempting to characterize a 3D volume with measurements confined to its 2D surface. To mitigate ill-posedness, conventional physics-based methods introduce techniques such as regularization, but selecting an appropriate regularization term can be a non-trivial task. Additionally, numerical optimization methods for solving CWI problems have their own difficulties: (1) the accuracy of the solution can be highly contingent on the initial guess; and (2) the computational cost can be prohibitive. Traditional physics-based CWI solvers often require many evaluations of the forward model before achieving convergence, presenting substantial computational burden. Furthermore, cycle-skipping is a well-documented issue in CWI
[
13
]
that occurs when the timing of the modeled waveform (predicted by the inverted solution) deviates by more than half a cycle from that of the observed waveform. The goal of this paper is to provide a review of contemporary methods in CWI, with a focus on common challenges and methods. In particular, we explore both the opportunities and difficulties inherent in the application of ML to enhance and expedite the solution of CWI problems.

ML has significantly influenced the evolution of CWI techniques. This synergy is particularly evident in the emerging trend of integrating fundamental physics principles with ML algorithms to advance CWI methods. Governing Wave Physics

CWI relies on partial differential equations (PDEs) tailored to various wave types and propagation media. In Earth sciences, the acoustic wave equation is commonly used in exploration geophysics
[
14
]
, while the elastic wave equation is fundamental in earthquake seismology
[
15
]
. In medical imaging, the acoustic wave model is frequently employed in ultrasound computed tomography
[
16

, whereas a coupled elastic-acoustic wave model is used for elastography and transcranial ultrasound
[

. This paper predominantly features the acoustic wave equation due to its effective representation of underlying wave physics and its simplicity in formulation. Computational methods developed using the acoustic wave model can naturally extend to more complex wave physics models, including elastic, anisotropic, and visco-acoustic waves (c.f. Appendix

II-A

Acoustic Wave Equation

The acoustic wave equation describes the propagation of compressional (P) waves through a non-lossy medium with homogeneous density; specifically

\nabla^{2}p(\bm{r},t)-\frac{1}{V_{P}^{2}(\bm{r})}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}p(\bm{r},t)=s(\bm{r},t)\,,

(1)

where

is the velocity map (
i.e.

\bm{r}

is the source term that specifies the location and time history of the source, and
p(\bm{r},t)

Computational Wave Imaging and Physics-based Methods

III-A

Forward Modeling

Physics-based methods are broadly implemented in various CWI applications. Generally speaking, these methods are computationally expensive, and require careful regularization. However, they tend to be more generalizable and robust to measurements contaminated with noise than data-driven methods.
The forward modeling of CWI is usually posed as

\bm{d}=f(\bm{m})+\bm{\eta}\,,

(2)

where
\bm{d}
represents the observed data,
f(\cdot)
is the forward model,
\bm{m}
represents the material properties we wish to infer, and
\eta
represents measurement noise. For acoustic forward modeling, the observed data
\bm{d}
consists of time traces of the pressure wavefield
p(\bm{r},t)
at discrete receiver locations
\bm{r}
(usually on the exterior of the region whose properties we are attempting to probe), while
\bm{m}
corresponds to the velocity map
V_{P}(\bm{r})
.
In fields such as exploration geophysics and medical ultrasound, the source term
s(\bm{r})
can usually be assumed to be known since the sources are controlled, and the source function is typically directly measurable
[
18
]
. When the source term is unknown, such as in earthquake seismology, specialized methods are required, including joint inversion or source-independent inversion
[
19
]
to infer the properties of the medium.

A variety of numerical techniques have been employed for forward modeling. Finite difference methods
[
20
,
21
]

[

,
23
,
24

can enhance numerical stability and reduce numerical dispersion. Finite element methods
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
are well suited to irregular geometries and complex material properties. Pseudospectral methods
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,

have proven effective for problems that require high precision in spatial derivatives. Finally, spectral element methods
[

,
34
,
35
,
36

combine the advantages of spectral methods and finite elements, offering both high accuracy and flexibility in complex scenarios.

III-B

Physics-based Wave Imaging

CWI aims to estimate the material property
\bm{m}
from the observed data
\bm{d}
; that is, it aims to find an inversion operator
g
so that
\widehat{\bm{m}}=g(\bm{d})
approximates the actual property. A minimal requirement for this approximate model is that the forward propagation based on this model should approximate the observed data; that is,
f(\widehat{\bm{m}})\approx\bm{d}
. This implies that
f(g(\bm{d}))\approx\bm{d}
and for this reason,
g
is informally referred to as the
inverse
of
f
. But directly inverting
f
is generally infeasible due to the ill-posed nature of CWI. To address these challenges, CWI is often formulated as the solution to an optimization problem. The unknown material properties are then estimated as the minimizers
\widehat{\bm{m}}
of a penalized nonlinear least squares problem and can be written as
[
37
,
38
,
39
]

\widehat{\bm{m}}=\underset{\bm{m}}{\operatorname{argmin}}~\left[\left\|\bm{d}-f(\bm{m})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\,r(\bm{m})\right],

(3)

where
\left\|\bm{d}-f(\bm{m})\right\|_{2}^{2}
is the data fidelity term with
||\cdot||_{2}
denoting the
\ell_{2}
norm, and
r(\cdot)
is the regularization term with coefficient
\gamma
. Common choices for
r(\bm{m})
are Tikhonov regularization or total-variation (TV) regularization, where Tikhonov regularization has the form
r(\bm{m})=\|H\bm{m}\|^{2}_{2}
, where
H
is a high-pass filter, while TV regularization uses the
\ell_{1}
norm of the spatial gradient
[
40
]
. The choice of regularization reflects the user’s bias regarding the physical form of the model of the intervening medium: the Tikhonov term biases toward smooth models, while TV regularization promotes piecewise smooth estimates.

IV
Deep Learning for Computational Wave Imaging Problems

In this section, we discuss ML-based methods for CWI, categorizing them by supervision and learning strategies as illustrated in Fig.
2
. Supervision strategy pertains to the use of labels and data, while learning strategy involves integrating physics into ML model design. Techniques are typically classified by supervision strategy in computer vision and ML, and by learning strategy in computational imaging and physics. We present these categorizations side-by-side for a comprehensive understanding across different communities. Additionally, we classify methods based on data fidelity consistency: data-consistent methods ensure model outputs closely align with actual measurements, enhancing trustworthiness and generalizability, while non-data consistent methods may produce quantitatively incorrect yet realistic estimates.

Semi-supervised Methods

Fully supervised learning is a straightforward approach to applying ML to CWI problems, but it is often difficult to obtain an adequate number of labeled samples. The acquisition of unlabeled waveform data, in contrast, is usually less challenging. For instance, in seismic FWI, distributed acoustic sensing (DAS)
[
46
]
enables efficient collection of a large volume of unlabeled acoustic data. Various semi-supervised models have been developed
[
47
,
48
,
49

50
]
to alleviate the scarcity of labeled samples.

Specifically, Gómez
et al.

[
47
]
illustrate how semi-supervised learning can be adapted to solving CWI. The high-level idea is to adapt the training set to the particular waveform
\bm{d}
that was measured, by incrementally generating more data samples that are close to the measured data. The first step is to learn a reconstruction operator
g_{0}
from the waveform-data manifold to velocity-map manifold using the original pairwise data. From the measured data, use
g_{0}(\bm{d}+\eta)
to create a new batch of models
\bm{m}
, and then apply
f
to those models to create a new batch of labeled samples. The training set is augmented with the new labeled samples, and a new regression
g_{1}
is fit. With each iteration, more labeled samples are in the regime of the measured sample, and the performance of the resulting
g
is improved for the waveform
\bm{d}
of interest.

In general, semi-supervised methods can be viewed as a strategy for generating “pseudo labels” using unlabeled data or other auxiliary information. For example, the above work directly derives velocity maps from unlabeled waveform data to support a supervised learning approach. Similarly, Feng
et al.

[
49
]
utilize available well log profiles—which are detailed recordings of geological formations encountered during drilling—to generate additional “pseudo labels” for velocity maps. In contrast, Cai
et al.

[
51

also produce “pseudo labels” from unlabeled waveform data, but they employ a Cycle-GAN architecture to facilitate the learning process. Sun
et al.

[
50
]
generate “pseudo labels” by extrapolating low-frequency velocity components, which serve as initial estimates for conventional physics-based CWI approach.

In the most extreme case, no labels are available. For instance, in USCT, acquiring ultrasound waveforms is generally straightforward and does not require prior knowledge of the target velocity maps. Similarly, in seismic FWI, DAS offers an efficient means of data collection. To overcome such a challenge, there are methods built on a completely unsupervised learning strategy to solve various CWI problems
[
54

11

55

et al.

[
11

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{m}}

\displaystyle=g_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\bm{d})\;,

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;\bm{\theta}^{*}

The major difference is that, since labels are not available, UPFWI defines the loss function in the data space rather than in the velocity space. To do so, the velocity model estimated by the encoder-decoder network is mapped to measurement data by propagating the wave-equation forward model. Numerically, the continuous full-wave equation is discretized using finite differences and incorporated in the loss function as a differentiable operator. The rationale for this is that, similar to model-based FWI, a good match between the measured and simulated waveform indicates a reasonable reconstruction of the physical parameters from the encoder-decoder network. IV-B

Machine Learning Methods by Learning Strategy

CWI, unlike typical ML problems such as natural language processing, is governed by underlying physics. We are thus presented with an opportunity to incorporate critical physics and prior knowledge to potentially improve the performance of ML models in solving CWI problems
[
3
]
. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, different approaches have been developed to incorporate physics into ML; we distinguish the following four:

•

Simulations/Measurements-Driven.
The method is built on a data-driven loss as in Eq. (
4
), and physics knowledge is implicitly represented via simulations and/or measurements.

•

Physics-Aware.
The method is built on a data-driven loss, but physics knowledge is introduced through explicit domain-aware regularization.

•

Parameterized Solution.
The method is built on a physics-based loss as in (Eq.
3
) and ML is used to learn a parameterization of the physical property.

•

Plug and Play Priors.
The method typically makes use of a physics-based loss function, together with a generic denoising operator (possibly ML-based) that may not be designed/trained for the specific problem of interest.

IV-B
1
Simulations/Measurements-Driven Methods

Full-physics simulations and measurements from experiments represent a major source of data for many ML methods for solving physics problems including CWI. Here, simulation data refers to synthetic waveforms generated by virtual imaging. This is achieved by applying the forward model in Eq. (
1
) to various realizations of the medium properties spanning the physical space of interest. The measurements are these data being acquired through lab or controlled experimental setup. Particularly, given the significant recent increase in available computing power, a very large number of pairwise realizations and waveforms becomes available, enabling fully supervised models as provided in Eq. (
4
). Here, we use the model (called “Fourier-DeepONet”) developed by Zhu
et al.

[
59
]
as an example to illustrate how simulations can be utilized to train ML models for solving CWI problems as shown in Fig.

. Fourier-DeepONet was developed to enhance the generalization ability of waveform sources including both frequency range and source locations. It builds on two major components: DeepONet (deep operator networks)
[
61
]
and U-FNO (a block combining Fourier neural operator and U-net)
[
62
]
. Particularly, source parameters (i.e., frequencies and source locations) are employed as the inputs of the trunk net to facilitate the data-driven model with varying source frequencies and locations. The branch net and trunk net encode the waveform data and source parameters, respectively. They are linear transformations lifting inputs to high dimensional space. A few U-FNO blocks were then utilized as decoders to produce high-resolution imaging output.

Fully supervised learning strategies, as discussed in the section on
Fully Supervised Methods
, prove highly effective in utilizing the large volume of simulation-data pairs. Beyond the above-mentioned method, a substantial body of research has explored simulation/measurement driven methods for FWI

, USCT

Another method that has proven effective in enhancing generalization is the “CNN Correction after Direct Inversion” approach, as proposed by Jin

IV-B
2
Physics-Aware Methods

This group of methods incorporates physical knowledge, such as the wave equation, through the use of explicit regularization. Several papers have demonstrated the benefits of using the wave equation to regularize the inversion
[
74
,
11
,
75
,
60
,
53
,
76
]
. Particularly, Gupta
[
60
]
proposes to jointly solve both forward and imaging problems by leveraging an invertible neural network as shown in Fig.
4b
. The idea is to leverage an invertible U-net to learn invertibility in the latent space where the input and output dimensions are strictly equal. Encoder and decoder blocks are added on both sides of the input and target domains to learn meaningful latent representations. Then, the input data are passed to a latent encoder, and the outputs of this encoder are fed to the invertible U-net. Finally, the outputs from the invertible network are decoded to make a prediction in the target domain. As demonstrated by Gupta
[
60
]
, this network architecture comes with a major benefit in that it provides flexibility in learning both forward and inverse transformations with a single architecture and further constrains each other during the training, thus the network provides both enhanced imaging and forward modeling predictions.

Incorporating the governing wave equation into ML models has effectively enhanced their performance. Various network structures and parameterization schedules have been explored. Ren
et al.

[
74
]
develop SWINet, a waveform inversion network that utilizes wave equation-based cells, treating the velocity map as a trainable parameter. Similarly, Yang and Ma
[
76
]
introduce FWIGAN, which merges the wave equation with a Wasserstein GAN framework and also considers velocity as trainable. Rasht-Behesht
et al.

[
75
]
propose a method using a physics-informed neural network
[
77
]
to solve wave propagation and constrained wave inversion simultaneously, producing the velocity map from the inversion network. Key challenges for all those methods include the need for an accurate initial guess to start the process, which can be tricky, and the substantial computational demands of solving the wave equation, particularly for three-dimensional problems.

Parameterization Solution Methods

Physics knowledge can be represented by the parameter space of the neural networks. Ulyanov
et al.

[
58
]
develop the deep image prior, a type of convolutional neural network, to learn useful prior knowledge via re-parameterization. A similar idea has been developed to solve CWI problems as shown in Fig.

. In particular, velocity maps are parameterized while the forward model is used to calculate the associated waveform and obtain the data misfit. Mathematically, parameterization can be posed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{m}}

\displaystyle=g_{\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{d})}(\bm{\beta}),\;\mathrm{~~~~~~~s.t.}\;\bm{\theta}^{*}(\bm{d})=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(f(g_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{\beta})),\bm{d})\,,

(10)

where
\bm{\beta}
is a random tensor drawn from a normal distribution;
i.e.
,
\bm{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
g_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\cdot)
is a network model used to parameterize the velocity maps.

Parameterization solution methods, building on conventional physics-based approaches, inherit similar challenges. Typically, these methods require a well-defined initial velocity map, and factors like regularization and optimization techniques (e.g., gradients and step sizes) significantly influence their convergence. Specifically, crucial to Eq. (
10
) are the selection of
\bm{\beta}
and the network structure as in

. Early work by Wu and McMechan
[

samples

[

The discretization assumption limits the output to fixed sizes, restricting transferability. Overcoming these drawbacks, Zhu
et al.

[

develop a new strategy that integrates the wave equation for regularization, eliminating the pre-training step and addressing the ill-posed nature of CWI problems. More recently, Sun

introduce an approach that employs a continuous implicit function via neural networks to represent velocity maps, accommodating arbitrary sizes.

Plug and Play Methods

The Plug and Play Priors (PPP) method
[
79
,
80
,
81
]
, which is widely used in computational imaging, was originally proposed as an approach to exploiting state-of-the-art image denoisers such as BM3D
[
82
]
to solve more general inverse problems, but now also represents a convenient approach to exploiting a learned model, such as the DnCNN denoiser
[
83
]
, without the need to train the model for the specific inverse problem of interest.

It is based on proximal optimization algorithms
[
84
]
,
such as the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
[
85
,
86
,
87
]
, a fundamental component of which is the scaled proximal operator of a function
h({\bm{m}})

[
88
, Ch. 6]
, defined as

\prox_{\alpha h}(\bm{u})=\argmin_{\bm{m}}\;\alpha h(\bm{m})+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{m}-\bm{u}\|_{2}^{2}\;.

(11)

The ADMM algorithm for solving a problem of the form
\argmin_{\bm{m}}h(\bm{m})+r(\bm{m})
, where
h(\cdot)
is typically a data-fitting term such as

h(\bm{m})=\left\|\bm{d}-f(\bm{m})\right\|_{2}^{2}
, and
r(\cdot)
typically represents a prior/regularization term, consists of the iterations

\displaystyle\bm{m}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{m}}\;h(\bm{m})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bm{m}-\bm{y}^{(k)}+\bm{u}^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle=\prox_{\rho^{-1}h}\big(\bm{y}^{(k)}-\bm{u}^{(k)}\big)

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{y}}\;r(\bm{y})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bm{m}^{(k+1)}-\bm{y}+\bm{u}^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\prox_{\rho^{-1}r}\big(\bm{x}^{(k+1)}+\bm{u}^{(k)}\big)

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{u}^{(k)}+\bm{m}^{(k+1)}-\bm{y}^{(k+1)}\;,

where
\rho
is an algorithm parameter that determines the rate of convergence,
\bm{y}
is an auxiliary variable, and
\bm{u}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint
\bm{y}=\bm{m}
. The PPP method was inspired by the observation that the proximal operator has the form of a maximum a posteriori (MAP) denoiser, motivating its replacement with a state-of-the-art image denoiser. While the interpretation as a minimization algorithm is no longer tenable under this substitution, it can be viewed as a fixed point algorithm that has been shown to converge under a variety of conditions
[
80
,
89
,
90
]
.

PPP have been effectively adapted to solve CWI problems by integrating useful prior knowledge either through model-based methods or learning-based approaches. Aghamiry
et al.

[
91
]
utilize PPP with the BM3D regularization algorithm and a proximal Newton method to address CWI ill-posedness challenges. However, their method struggles with the complexity of realistic noise. To address this limitation, learning-based methods have been developed that employ neural networks as denoising operators. For example, Fu
et al.

[
92
]
show that deep CNNs can offer more effective regularization compared to traditional methods like total variation regularization. Similarly, Izzatullah
et al.

[
93
]
design a CNN-based denoiser to sample from a regularized target posterior distribution, an approach refined to enhance uncertainty quantification in FWI.

IV-C

Emerging Trends in Machine Learning Techniques for Computational Wave Imaging

In this section, we examine two important advancements in ML-enhanced CWI methods: Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) and Generative AI (GenAI) Models. These developments offer significant opportunities to further enhance CWI techniques.

IV-C
1
Uncertainty Quantification (UQ)

Uncertainty is a fundamental feature of CWI and of inverse problems at-large: not only do we wish to infer the model-parameters, but we also want to quantify the uncertainty associated with this inference, reflecting the degree of confidence we have in the inversion.
CWI problems are typically ill-posed and non-convex, often yielding non-unique or suboptimal solutions. Furthermore, real-world waveform measurements are generally compromised by acquisition noise from equipment, environmental disturbances, and other sources. These uncertainties are often categorized as epistemic (model-related) and aleatoric (data-related) uncertainties
[
94
]
. Epistemic uncertainty arises from unknown or unmodeled physics, such as the excitation pulse, detector characteristics, acoustic attenuation, density variations, and nonlinear effects. In contrast, aleatoric uncertainty reflects the inherent variability in the data itself. UQ mitigates these issues by providing predictive distributions and assessing prediction confidence.

In recent years, the Bayesian inverse problem has emerged as the most comprehensive and systematic framework for formulating and solving inverse problems with quantified uncertainties
[
95
,
96
,
97
,
98
,
99
]
. However, the solution of Bayesian inverse problems is extremely challenging; when the forward model is complex and the parameter dimension is large, Bayesian inversion becomes prohibitive with standard methods. Exploration of the posterior distribution using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling is often computationally infeasible for CWI and often requires advanced sampling algorithms that leverage curvature information (geometric MCMC)
[
100
,
101
,
102
,
103
]
. Variational inference approaches introduce a parametrized approximation of the posterior distribution and solve a high dimensional optimization problem to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the approximated and true posterior
[
104
]
. However, in the context of CWI, variational inference approaches have been limited to two-dimensional synthetic problems
[
105
]
and entail significant computational cost due to the “curse of dimensionality” in approximating a probability distribution and its expectations for a high-dimensional parameter space.

ML offers new opportunities to enable UQ for CWI by use of novel network architectures and approaches, such as normalizing flows
[
106
,
107
]
, deep probabilistic imaging
[
108
]
, and generative AI (see next section), that can help to break the course of dimensionality and enable high-dimensional UQ. Here, we explore UQ estimation using neural networks, specifically through a method known as InvNet_UQ
[
109
]
. This approach focuses on aleatoric uncertainty and incorporates Simultaneous Quantile Regression (SQR) within a CNN framework
[
94
]
. InvNet_UQ employs a novel joint quantile regression loss function to enhance CWI by improving model reliability and quantifying prediction confidence. The network parameters of InvNet_UQ are obtained through the following optimization

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\argmin_{\theta}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{pqr}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{r}E_{\tau\approx U[0,1]}[\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(g_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{d}_{k},\tau)(i,j),\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}_{k}(i,j))]\right\},

(13)

where
k
denotes
k
th sample in the dataset,
(i,j)
denotes the pixel location, and the pinball (quantile) loss
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
, defined below, is designed to capture the discrepancy between predicted and actual values across different quantile levels of
{0\leq\tau\leq 1}
.

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\mathbf{m}(i,j),\hat{\mathbf{m}}(i,j))=\begin{cases}\tau(\mathbf{m}(i,&j)-\hat{\mathbf{m}}(i,j))\\
&\text{if ${\mathbf{m}(i,j)-\hat{\mathbf{m}}(i,j))\geq 0}$ },\\
(1-\tau)&(\hat{\mathbf{m}}(i,j)-\mathbf{m}(i,j))\\
&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(14)

Provided with a significance level
{\alpha}
, the estimated imaging uncertainty can be computed from the (
{1-\alpha}
) prediction interval around the median

u_{\alpha}(\mathbf{d}):=\hat{g}\left(\mathbf{d},1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)-\hat{g}\left(\mathbf{d},\frac{\alpha}{2}\right).

(15)

Liu
et al.
shows that data-driven models with UQ excel in handling contaminated data, including noisy or erroneous measurements. Their approach not only boosts confidence in inversion tasks but also refines training sets, enhancing model performance. Unlike Bayesian neural networks, InvNet_UQ does not necessarily require the image prior to be a Gaussian distribution, potentially providing more accurate uncertainty bounds compared to other methods. Beyond the use of quantile regression for UQ, Monte Carlo dropout—a technique favored for its simplicity—is also widely used in CWI. Zhu
et al.

[
56
]
implement it by adding dropout layers to their network. Similarly, Schankee
et al.

[
110
]
use a U-net architecture, employing both Monte Carlo dropout and bootstrap aggregating for uncertainty estimation. Yablokov et al.
[
111
]
use a multi-layer fully connected network and Monte Carlo simulations to estimate uncertainties.

IV-C
2
Generative AI (GenAI)

GenAI models excel in representing probability distributions across diverse data domains and present new opportunities for CWI, and computational imaging in general. By learning an accurate characterization of the model parameter prior distribution, GenAI can 1) enhance the solution of the Bayesian inverse problem and UQ
[
112
,
113
,
114
,
115
]
; 2) reduce ill-posedness of CWI by constraining the model parameter to belong to a high-probability manifold
[
116
,
71
,
117
]
; and 3) boost the performance of learning methods by generating synthetic data to augment training sets
[
64
]
.

GenAI approaches can be broken down into two broad categories: sample-based approaches and score-based approaches.
Sample-based approaches, such as Variational Auto-Encoders
[
118
]
(VAEs) and Generative Adversarial Models
[
119
]
(GANs) learn the push forward from an easy-to-sample latent distribution (e.g., a multivariate
i.i.d.
Gaussian) to the data distribution. Once trained, they allow for
direct
(and computationally efficient) sampling. GANs, for example, were successfully employed to reduce the ill-posedness of CWI in the work of Zhang and Lin
[
71
]
and
Mosser
et al.

[
116
]
by leveraging the GAN latent space as a nonlinear dimension reduction technique, while VAEs were successfully employed in Yang
et al.

[
64
]
for data augmentation. However, it is worth noticing that GAN and VAE approaches are generally limited to small/medium scale problems and may suffer from the so-called
mode collapse
phenomenon
[
120
,
121
,
122
]
, which prevents them from fully capturing the distribution of the data.
Score-based approaches overcome this by directly learning the Stein score of the data distribution (i.e. the gradient of the logarithm of the probability distribution function), which can be used in iterative sampling algorithms such as MCMC
[
123
]
. In particular, diffusion models
[
124
,
125
,
126
]
are now recognized as state-of-the-art in GenAI because of their high-quality sample generation, scalability to high-dimensional distributions, stability, and simplicity.

Wang
et al.

[
114
]
use a diffusion model for solving CWI problems with quantified uncertainties. This method extends Song
et al.
’s
[
126
]
approach for solving linear inverse problems (like CT and MRI image reconstruction) using diffusion models to the more challenging CWI problem, which is governed by a highly non-linear forward model. In this approach, a diffusion model is first constructed from velocity model data. Diffusion models consist of two components: a
forward
diffusion process that uses a stochastic ordinary differential equation (SDE) to map the data distribution to a fixed noise distribution, and a
reverse
diffusion process that maps the noise distribution to the data distribution. In particular, Wang
et al.

[
114
]
adopt the variance preserving (VP) SDE
[
127
]
for the forward diffusion process, which can be written as

d\mathbf{m}_{t}=-\frac{\beta_{t}}{2}\mathbf{m}_{t}dt+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}d\mathbf{w}_{t},\quad t\in(0,T)

(16)

where the parameter
\beta_{t}
controls the noise variance, the initial condition
\mathbf{m}_{0}
of the SDE is the velocity model, and
d\mathbf{w}_{t}
is the noise introduced. The corresponding reverse diffusion process depends on the Stein score of the probability density of
\mathbf{m}_{t}
, which can be approximated by training the parameters
\theta
of a vector-valued neural network
s_{\theta}(\mathbf{m}_{t},t)
using the cost function

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{m}_{t}|\mathbf{m}_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{m}_{0}}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(\mathbf{m}_{t},t)+\frac{\mathbf{m}_{t}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds}\mathbf{m}_{0}}{1-e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)ds}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\,dt,

(17)

where
\lambda
is a positive weighting function.
Once trained, the network can generate new samples from the prior distribution of the model parameter, as the solution of another SDE (reverse diffusion process) as

d\mathbf{m}_{t}=\left(-\frac{\beta_{t}}{2}\mathbf{m}_{t}-\beta_{t}s_{\theta}(\mathbf{m}_{t},T-t)\right)dt+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}d\mathbf{w}_{t},\quad t\in(0,T),

(18)

using white noise at the initial condition.
To condition the sample generation to the measurement data, Wang
et al.

[
114
]
include an FWI update after each step of discretized SDE solver to enhance data consistency.

Acoustic and Industrial Ultrasonic Imaging

Acoustic and industrial ultrasonic imaging is widely used for inspecting and monitoring engineered structures. Its applications range from detecting small defects in 3D printed materials to identifying structural issues in large airplane wings and locating pipeline leaks. The process involves using transmitters to emit signals and receivers to capture these waves post-propagation through the structure. Analyzing signal characteristics like arrival time, amplitude, and distortion through computed tomography helps create images representing structural properties such as sound speed, density, and temperature.

VI
Lessons Learned

Traditional physics-based CWI methods face three challenges: (1) non-unique solutions, (2) multiple local minima, and (3) high computational demands. In this section, we aim to provide lessons learned and analyses concerning these crucial issues.

VI-A

Ill-posedness and Non-uniqueness Solution

The challenge of ill-posedness, primarily caused by incomplete acquisition coverage and the vast dimensionality of the solution space, is a fundamental aspect of CWI problems. Traditional methods, such as regularization or preconditioning, can be effective, but usually require careful design of the penalty function to encode the user’s preferences for which of the non-unique solutions to the unregularized problem to favor. Thus, selecting and optimizing the right regularization function to balance data fidelity and regularization terms, as in Eq. (
3
), can be challenging. In contrast, emerging ML-based methods offer alternative strategies to mitigate ill-posedness. Among the opportunities enabled by ML-based methods, we highlight the following approaches.

•

Learning the Null-Space Component of the Image from Labeled Data.
To address the challenge of non-uniqueness in CWI solutions, fully supervised models trained on carefully curated datasets are effective. A typical example of this is InversionNet
[
41
]
, which uses training datasets constructed in a pairwise way to ensure a direct one-to-one correspondence between velocity maps and waveform data. This strategy confines the model’s predictive capabilities to the data distribution of the training set, thereby alleviating the ill-posedness of CWI problems. However, a potential drawback is the model’s tendency to overfit to its training dataset, which can limit its generalization ability. These issues, along with computational cost and generalization capability, will be further discussed in subsequent sections.

•

Learning a Low-Dimensional Embedding of the Model Parameter Space.
An alternative strategy for reducing the null space involves transitioning the learning process to a lower-dimensional space. Feng
et al.

[
172
]
provide a compelling illustration of this approach. Instead of performing inversion within the original, high-dimensional solution space, their method employs a sophisticated encoder-decoder framework to navigate a significantly reduced solution space (i.e., the latent space). This projection technique not only mitigates the inherent ill-posedness associated with CWI but also uncovers intriguing relationships between data and labels, such as an unexpected near-linearity
[
172
,
10
]
.

•

Plug and Play Priors.
This particular regularization approach, rooted in the traditional CWI framework, is elaborated upon in the section on
Plug and Play Methods
. Its key distinction lies in its ability to derive the regularization term directly from data, moving away from the conventional practice of employing a predefined analytical regularization term. This data-driven approach substantially amplifies the efficacy of the regularization, offering a notable improvement and flexibility in addressing the challenges inherent in CWI.

•

Implicit Regularization through Neural Network Parameterization.
As discussed in the earlier section on
Parameterization Solution Methods
, the choice of a neural network for parameterizing solutions has a significant impact on the structure and exploration of the solution space. Following a similar idea, in the context of CWI, Zhu
et al.

[
56
]
find that reparameterizing the velocity map using neural networks implicitly introduces a form of regularization. This regularization can improve CWI’s effectiveness by shaping the solution space in a way that is informed by the neural network’s architecture, leading to more robust imaging results.

VI-B

Nonlinearity and Nonconvexity: Conventional vs. Data-driven CWI

Conventional CWI, as shown in Eq. (
3
), is nonlinear and nonconvex, presenting challenges for local first-order optimization methods, like accelerated (Nesterov or momentum) gradient or nonlinear conjugate gradient methods, which struggle to guarantee convergence to the global minimum. The nonlinearity is particularly pronounced for long-wavelength background velocities compared to short-wavelength impedance contrasts, which gives rise to the so-called
cycle-skipping
phenomenon. In conventional model-based CWI, several approaches have proven successful in alleviating cycle-skipping. Frequency continuation
[
173
]
is a multiscale approach that leverages the low-frequency information content in the waveform data to build an initial guess of the sought-after material properties and then sequentially refine them using higher-frequency information. Adaptive FWI
[
174
]
defines a new data fidelity term that, by introducing a learnable convolutional filter mapping the predicted data into the measured data, can overcome the cycle-skipping phenomenon. Optimal transport-based FWI
[
175
]
defines a new data fidelity term based on the quadratic Wasserstein metric to correctly correct for cycle-skipping. However, most of the “Data Consistent” ML approaches surveyed here (see Fig.
2
) adhere to the traditional
\ell_{2}
-based CWI loss function, and are therefore also susceptible to cycle-skipping. Besides a few notable exceptions (e.g.
[
176
]
), the use of more advanced and robust data-fidelity terms remains largely unexplored for ML-based CWI.

VI-C

Computational Cost Analysis

In assessing computational cost (time and computing resources) of CWI algorithms, it is important to distinguish training (up-front effort to train an inverse model) from inference (application of the inverse model or of the inference algorithm to obtain a characterization of the medium
\mathbf{m}
from measured waveform data
\mathbf{d}
). For traditional physics-based methods, there is no training per se, and all of the effort is in the inference. For most ML methods, the training stage is by far the most expensive, and the inference is typically much quicker.
For applications in which a single inference is needed, the comparison of these two approaches should consider the total of training and inference. But for monitoring applications (such as carbon sequestration
[
130
,
133
]
or tsunami warning
[
177
,
178
]
), a single training stage can support multiple inferences, and for those cases faster inferences can be a real advantage. A detailed case study comparing the computational costs of these approaches is provided in Appendix
D
.

•

Model Generalization.
We refer to generalization as the model’s performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) data. Traditionally, ML models assume that training and testing data share the same distribution, an assumption that only approximately holds in real-world CWI scenarios, highlighting the need to explore generalization to OOD data. Generalization strategies are categorized into data-centric and model-centric approaches. Data-centric methods enhance datasets via augmentation, cleaning, and feature engineering, while model-centric techniques improve model performance with strategies like regularization and UQ. Preliminary studies have shown promise; for example, employing the OpenFWI dataset
[
179
]
demonstrates that large-scale models could boost generalization
[
180
]
. Gómez
et al.

[
47
]
improve OOD performance with a physics-consistent data augmentation method. Additionally, hybrid approaches that combine data and physics knowledge have been explored to enhance generalization
[
11
,
56
,
60
,
181
]
. Despite progress, challenges remain in integrating relevant physics with ML models, choosing suitable network architectures, and ensuring access to high-quality training data. These issues require continued research focus within the community.

•

Model Interpretability.
The topic of model interpretability is of particular significance for deep neural networks known for their “black box” nature. In CWI, understanding the rationale behind a model’s predictions can be as helpful as the predictions themselves. Interpretability enables us to grasp why certain predictions are made, making it an invaluable tool for debugging, improving model performance, and identifying biases. While tools like UQ, as mentioned earlier, can aid in interpreting deep models, extensive research on this aspect has been conducted in the broader ML/AI community
[
182
,
183
]
. Surprisingly, the CWI field has seen relatively little exploration in this area, pointing to a promising direction for future research and the potential for significant advancements in CWI through enhanced interpretability.

•

Imaging Quality Assessment.
Implementing effective quality control plays a crucial role in evaluating successful applications of CWI. Ultimately, image quality should be assessed based on the utility of the image to perform a specific task. For example, in the context of carbon sequestration, CWI may allow the detection of an underground gas plume; in medical USCT, CWI can support breast cancer screening and diagnostics tasks; and, in industrial ultrasonic CWI, can allow the non-destructive detection of defects. However, a growing body of evidence is showing that, while ML-based approaches can improve conventional metrics of image accuracy (such as mean square error, MSE; mean absolute error, MAE; or structural similarity, SSIM), they can result in diminished task performance
[
184
,
185

186

[
187
,
188
]
. Due to this discrepancy, there is a growing interest in assessing the task performance of proposed image reconstruction methods by use of statistical signal detection theory and numerical observer methods
[
184
,
189
,
190

Wave Equations

In the field of wave-based imaging, researchers have developed a spectrum of wave equations, each engineered to capture the nuances of wave propagation through materials with distinct characteristics. These models account for a range of material properties, from elasticity as delineated by Levander

Nevertheless, the complexity of real-world materials often defies such simplicity, possessing idiosyncratic properties that necessitate more sophisticated wave equations. This discussion presents a curated selection of wave equations, each bespoke to the distinct acoustic signatures of a variety of materials.

Constant Density Acoustic Wave Equation

An acoustic medium refers to a fluid that is compressible, non-viscous, and lacks shear strength, examples of which include air and water. Minor localized displacements within the fluid will propagate as an acoustic wave, commonly referred to as a compressional (P) wave. The acoustic wave equation for a medium with no density variation is given by

Appendix C
Technical Interconnections

Our paper discusses eight different machine-learning-based CWI methods. These methods are interconnected, as illustrated in Fig.
11
. Broadly, the methods can be categorized into two groups: data-consistent methods (Fig.
11
, Left) and non-data-consistent methods (Fig.
11
, Right). The Plug & Play Priors and Parameterization methods fall into the data-consistent category, enforcing data consistency during optimization. The remaining methods are classified as non-data-consistent.

Appendix D
Numerical Comparison of Computational Costs

To compare computational costs (training and inference) of some if the methods surveyed, we report a benchmark performed on the Fault Family dataset from OpenFWI
[
179
]
, with models processing input waveforms of dimensions
5\times 1000\times 70
to produce
70\times 70
velocity maps. Computational costs are assessed using metrics like Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) and processing time, which includes both training and inference phases. Memory costs are evaluated by model parameter size and peak memory usage. Experiments use NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs over 120 epochs, with FLOPs and parameter counts calculated using the
ptflops
Python package. Data-driven methods showcase their efficiency and scalability via batch processing.

Cost analysis for data-driven CWI methods, detailed in Table
III
, highlights significant training expenses. Models that incorporate forward modeling, like UPFWI
[
11
]
and invertible networks
[
60
]
, incur higher costs. For comparison, these are measured against the costs of a traditional physics-based approach in Table
IV
, using a physics method
[
214
]
that operates in the time domain with a multiscale approach, escalating frequencies as iteration losses drop below 0.01. Systematically increasing training data, while maintaining constant test sample size and the number of training epochs, shows that costs for InversionNet increase with larger training datasets, eventually exceeding those of the traditional method. This computational comparison, while not mathematically rigorous, underscores an important point: the substantial data requirements of ML models significantly contribute to this cost increase. While ML-based CWI methods can in some cases exceed the costs of traditional techniques, their post-training inference efficiency may offer a cost-effective advantage.

Appendix E
Open-Source Software and Data Availability

Accessible open data resources facilitate research on wave imaging challenges. Below, we provide a few open datasets for different wave imaging problems.

•

Seismic Full-Waveform Inversion.
We find two open accessible datasets:
OpenFWI

[
179
]
and
\mathbf{\mathbb{E}^{FWI}}

[
215
]
, of which
OpenFWI
was designed for seismic imaging through the application of acoustic wave equations, and
\mathbf{\mathbb{E}^{FWI}}
was tailored for seismic imaging employing elastic wave equations. Particularly,
OpenFWI
comprises 12 datasets that have been synthesized from a variety of sources. These datasets span a wide range of geophysical domains, including interfaces, faults, CO
2
reservoirs, and more. They also encompass different geological subsurface structures, such as flat and curved formations, and offer varying amounts of data samples. Within the
OpenFWI
collection, you can find both P-wave velocity models and seismic data. In contrast,
\mathbf{\mathbb{E}^{FWI}}
encompasses 8 distinct datasets. Notably, the seismic dataset within
\mathbf{\mathbb{E}^{FWI}}
includes both vertical and horizontal components, providing a more comprehensive view of the seismic information. Additionally, the velocity models in
\mathbf{\mathbb{E}^{FWI}}
incorporate both P-wave and S-wave velocities, offering a richer dataset for waveform inversion studies. Both of these valuable datasets can be accessed through the following URLs:
https://openfwi-lanl.github.io/
for
OpenFWI
and
https://efwi-lanl.github.io/
for
\mathbf{\mathbb{E}^{FWI}}
. The source code for both InversionNet and VelocityGAN can be accessed on the GitHub repository at
https://github.com/lanl/OpenFWI
, and to facilitate researchers in their learning process, a comprehensive Colab Tutorial is additionally made available for convenient access via this link:
https://colab.research.google.com/drive/17s5JmVs9ABl8MpmFlhWMSslj9_d5Atfx?usp=sharing
.

Acoustic and Industrial Ultrasound.
In industrial settings, events such as crack propagation, shocks, impacts, or other events generate Acoustic Emission (AE) signals, which can be measured via passive listening. In Ref. [
[
216
]
], acoustic measurements were collected on bolted joints to detect events where the bolts were untightened, which resulted in AE signals. The data set, ORION-AE, containing the time-series measurements can be accessed via
https://github.com/emmanuelramasso/ORION_AE_acoustic_emission_multisensor_datasets_bolts_loosening
. Stiller et al. utilizes impact based AE measurements to identify damage modes in composites under compression. Analysis software and datasets can be accessed via
https://github.com/DominikStiller/tudelft-damage-identification
.
Active interrogation is also a popular method for detecting defects or damage in industrial structures. In Ref. [
[
217
]
], a CNN is used to detect cracks based on acoustic phased-array measurements. The source code and dataset can be accessed via
https://github.com/iikka-v/ML-NDT
. Ref. [
[
218
]
] provides a finite element method simulation package to simulate acoustic NDT measurements based on Pulse-echo, Through-Transmission, and Linear Scanning. The source code to generate synthetic data can be accessed via
https://github.com/mmolero/SimNDT
.
In addition to industrial measurements, there has been a push to classify sounds found commonly in everyday life, such as cars, animal calls, construction, music, etc. Google has extracted 10-second audio clips from YouTube videos to compile AudioSet, a labeled data set with 632 distinct event classes that is accessible via
http://research.google.com/audioset/index.html
. In Ref. [
[
219
]
] New York University presents the UrbanSound8k dataset containing 8732 4-second audio clips with labels of events common in urban environments. This dataset can be accessed via
https://urbansounddataset.weebly.com/urbansound8k.html
.

Medical Ultrasound Tomography.
In Refs. [
[
66
,
220
]
], the authors develop a three-dimensional anatomically realistic numerical phantom of the female breast and a high-fidelity three-dimensional imaging model of ring-array USCT breast ultrasound. The goal of the generated numerical breast phantoms (NBPs) and corresponding simulated measurements is to provide a dataset to 1) support the development and assessment (using objective measurement of image quality) of model-based and learned image reconstruction methods and 2) conduct virtual imaging trials to guide the design of new USCT breast imaging systems. Compared to other acoustic NBPs in the literature, the ones in Ref. [
[
66
]
] are particularly relevant to the development of new learning-based USCT image reconstruction method since they 1) comprise realistic structures and acoustic properties; 2) include lesions and/or other pathologies; and 3) are representative of the stochastic variability in breast size, shape, composition, anatomy, and tissue properties observed in a specified cohort of to-be-imaged subjects.
Leveraging tools developed and released by the Virtual Imaging Clinical Trials for Regulatory Evaluation (VICTRE) project
[
221
,
222
]
of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the NBPs in Ref. [
[
66
]
] correspond to the four different levels of breast density defined according to the American College of Radiology’s (ACR) Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS)
[
223
]
: A) Breast is almost entirely fat, B) Breast has scattered areas of fibroglandular density, C) Breast is heterogeneously dense, and D) Breast is extremely dense. Each NBP is a 3D voxelized map consisting of ten tissue types: fat, skin, glandular, nipple, ligament (connective tissue), muscle, terminal duct lobular unit, duct, artery, and vein. In the framework of the authors, large ensembles of stochastic NBPs with realistic variability in breast volume, shape, fraction of glandular tissue, ligament orientation, tissue anatomy, can be generated by controlling input parameters and selecting the random seed number. A variety of lesions (e.g., circumscribed or spiculated masses) can also be inserted at physiologically plausible locations. Tissue-specific acoustic properties (density, SOS, and AA) are then stochastically assigned to each tissue structure within physiological ranges and acoustic heterogeneity within fatty and glandular tissues is also modeled as spatially correlated random fields. Realization of these numerical phantoms and corresponding simulated measurements can be downloaded from Refs. [
[
224
,
225
,
226
]
], and additional NBPs can be generated using the open-source software in Ref. [
[
227
]
].
