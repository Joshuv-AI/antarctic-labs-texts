Safety and efficacy of niosomal and conventional tranexamic acid/niacinamide vs. hydroquinone creams in melasma: A randomized, double-blind, case-controlled clinical trial
Authors: Parisa Ghasemiyeh, Negin Fazelzadeh Haghighi, Ladan Dastgheib, Sara Ranjbar, Soliman Mohammadi-Samani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Melasma is a common hyperpigmentary skin disorder. Because of its high prevalence and serious complications, including negative psychiatric effects, early diagnosis and treatment would be essential. This randomized, double-blind, case-controlled clinical trial compared the efficacy of niosomal tranexamic acid (TXA) 2%/niacinamide (NCA) 2% cream and conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream with topical hydroquinone (HQ) 4% cream as a gold standard in melasma patients. A total of 99 patients were randomly assigned to three groups: Group A received niosomal TXA/NCA cream, Group B received conventional TXA/NCA cream, and Group C received HQ cream for three months. Each patient attended five dermatologist visits to evaluate clinical efficacy. All treatment groups showed a considerable reduction in melanin index and modified melasma area and severity index (mMASI) scores, as well as a notable improvement in patients’ quality of life. Although both niosomal TXA/NCA cream and conventional TXA/NCA cream were as effective as HQ cream in management of melasma, adverse reactions and relapse were observed in HQ group. Niosomal or conventional TXA/NCA creams are as effective as HQ 4% cream and due to less serious adverse reactions they would be better choices than HQ-containing preparations in hyperpigmentary disorders. Introduction

Melasma is a chronic, relapsing, and multifactorial hyperpigmentary skin disorder that presents as light brown, dark brown, or gray-brown spots or patches on the face1. Melasma lesions commonly appear on the cheeks, forehead, chin, upper lips, and nasal bridge2. Also, extrafacial melasma is considered as a rare subtype of melasma that can affect patients’ neck, forearm, and sternum3. Melasma is more common (roughly nine-fold) in females, but it can occur in males, as well. The main causes of melasma are prolonged unprotected sun exposure and ultraviolet (UV) radiation4. Additionally, genetic factors, including gene polymorphisms, hormonal changes, thyroid disorders, pregnancy, and medications including combined oral contraceptives (OCPs) contribute to its development5,6. The prevalence of melasma varies between 1.5 up to 33% depending on population and geographic location, while its prevalence among pregnant women is considerably higher, ranging from 15 to 50%7.

Ultraviolet radiation increases melanin production by melanocytes through the induction of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, corticotropin, interleukin-1 (IL-1), and endothelin-1 (ET-1)8. Additionally, prolonged UV exposure leads to chronic dermal inflammation by activating fibroblasts9. Furthermore, fibroblasts may induce melanogenesis through the secretion of stem cell factors10. Melanin accumulations occurs in the dermis, epidermis, or both, in this regard, melasma can be classified as dermal, epidermal, or mixed type7,11.

Melasma is associated with substantial psychiatric disorders12, including depression, anxiety, and adjustment disorder13. In this regard, the results of a recent systematic review found that depression was considerably more prevalent in melasma patients compared to a healthy control group with odds ratio (OR) of 1.677. The study reported that the prevalence of depressive disorder and depressive symptoms among melasma patients were 24.2% and 45.1%, respectively14. Melasma also impacts the patients’ quality of life. In this regard, numerous psychometric questionnaires including Melasma Quality of Life scale (MelasQoL) and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) are proposed to assess patients’ quality of life, before and after treatment in melasma studies15. Given these adverse psychological effects, early diagnosis and management of melasma are essential.

The melanin index (MI) is defined as the average proportion of melanin concentration to total pigment concentration16. MI is considerably higher in melasma-affected skin and is commonly assessed before, during, and after treatment17. Additionally, modified melasma area and severity index (mMASI) is one of the most widely used tools for quantitatively assessing melasma severity before, during, and after melasma treatment18. The mMASI score ranges from 0 (clear) to 24 (most severe) calculated by summing the scores of four facial areas including cheeks, forehead, chin, and upper lips18.

Since UVA and UVAB radiation are primary contributors to melanogenesis and melasma, consistent and timely sunscreen application is essential for patients19. In addition, cosmetic makeup and application of tinted sunscreens or foundations can help camouflage melasma lesions20. Topical therapeutic agents remain the mainstay of melasma treatment. Topical hydroquinone (HQ) and triple combination cream (4% HQ, 0.01% fluocinolone acetonide, and 0.05% tretinoin) are considered first-line treatments and the gold standards for managing melasma21,22. According to the American Academy of Dermatology guideline, hydroquinone-containing creams are considered as the most common first-line therapeutic agents for melasma management23. In addition, based on the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology, hydroquinone is the most common first-line agent for melasma treatment, as well, that can be administered either monotherapy or in combination with other therapeutic agents24,25. Hydroquinone, also known as dihydroxybenzene, is a tyrosinase inhibitor that blocks the conversion of DOPA to melanin26. It affects melanosome formation, melanization, degradation, and melanocyte necrosis, therefore, it can be effective in melasma treatment at concentrations of 2 to 5% applied topically once daily at bedtime for at least three months21. Although HQ is the gold standard and the most commonly prescribed depigmenting agent for melasma management, its effects may be reversible and concerns regarding its adverse reactions including skin irritation, erythema, contact dermatitis, hypochromia, nail discoloration, and ochronosis persist21. Consequently, other topical depigmenting agents including azelaic acid, niacinamide (NCA), cysteamine, ascorbic acid (vitamin C), kojic acid, arbutin, resorcinol, glycolic acid, and tranexamic acid (TXA) have been investigated in melasma treatment20,27. Several systemic agents including oral TXA, melatonin, procyanidin, β-carotenoids, and Polypodium leucotomos extract have been explored in melasma treatment and studies indicated that these agents provide some benefit and are generally well tolerated in melasma patients28. Finally, procedural treatments such as laser and light therapies, including Q-switched lasers, nonablative fractional laser (NAFL), and intense pulsed light (IPL), are alternative options for melasma management29.

Niacinamide, also known as nicotinamide, is an active form of vitamin B3 with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties30. Niacinamide reduces melanogenesis by decreasing melanosome transfer to keratinocytes making it effective for melasma treatment. Topical NCA is generally applied at concentrations of 4–5%, twice daily, alone or in combination with other depigmenting agents in patients with melasma20. Tranexamic acid, an anti-plasmin and anti-fibrinolytic agent, has been investigated in melasma treatment through various routes of administration including topical, oral, and microinjection31. Due to its potential adverse reactions, particularly thromboembolic events, the topical route is preferred. Topical TXA, usually at concentration of 5%, inhibits melanin synthesis by interfering with melanocyte-keratinocyte interaction and reducing α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone levels and therefore it would be beneficial in melasma treatment32,33.

Administration of a combinational topical preparation containing TXA and NCA with different mechanism of actions in melasma treatment would be promising and may result in synergistic clinical effectiveness. Moreover, as it has been reported, encapsulation of active pharmaceutical ingredients in topical drug delivery systems, especially niosomes, can result in increased skin penetration, enhanced cutaneous bioavailability and drug deposition, and extended drug release pattern over time. Therefore, encapsulation of active pharmaceutical ingredients within the niosomes may result in the same efficacy at lower concentrations34,35. To the best of our knowledge, there is no published data regarding the efficacy and safety of topical niosomal TXA/NCA cream in melasma management. In this regard, in this double-blinded, case-controlled clinical trial, the efficacy and safety of topical niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% cream and topical conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream were compared with topical HQ 4% cream as a gold standard and the first-line agent in melasma treatment.

Results

Demographic data

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 129 patients were screened, and 99 patients were included based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Each group (A, B, or C) consisted of 33 patients receiving topical niosomal TXA/NCA cream (group A), conventional TXA/NCA cream (group B), or HQ cream (group C). Among the included patients, 98% were female and 2% were male. The average duration of melasma before enrollment was approximately eight years and approximately 53% of the patients had positive family history for melasma. The majority of patients had centrofacial and mixed type melasma with moderate severity. Patient demographic data, baseline mAMSI score, average MI, and MelasQoL results before treatment initiation are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Based on the results, a significant correlation was observed between melasma severity and each of baseline MI and mMASI score values with P-values of 0.034 and < 0.001, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, there was no significant difference (P-value > 0.05) between the patients assigned to groups A, B, and C in terms of demographic data and baseline assessments before treatment initiation.

As shown in Fig. 1, of the 99 patients enrolled, 77 completed the three-month trial. In addition, of these 77 patients who completed the trial, 69 also participated in short-term follow up assessments after treatment discontinuation. A Per-Protocol (PP) analysis was conducted on participants who completed the trial. Comparison with the Intention-to-Treat (ITT) analysis showed no statistically significant differences between the two approaches, indicating that missing data had minimal impact on the overall findings.

Treatment assessment

Patients were visited by the dermatologist in monthly intervals and mMASI score, MI of cheeks, chin, forehead, and upper lips, and patients and physicians’ IGA were assessed and documented. As shown in Table 1, a significant reduction in average MI and mMASI score was observed (P-values < 0.001) after 1, 2, and 3 months of treatment initiation in all groups. However, there was no significant difference between any of these groups in terms of the percentage of MI reduction or the percentage of mMASI score reduction over the time (P-value > 0.05). Since all treatment groups showed a similar pattern of improvement (using MANOVA test; P-value > 0.05), the issue of multiplicity is not a concern. As it is obvious in Table 1, the percentages of reduction in MI and mMASI score were increased over time in all treatment groups. Therefore, continuing treatment for at least 3 months can result in the optimum clinical response with minimum mMASI score and MI values in patients with melasma.

Furthermore, the visual changes in melasma lesions before and after the treatment course were evaluated using standard photography, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. As it is obvious in Fig. 2, considerable changes were achieved after the treatment course in terms of fading of melasma lesions and reduction of skin darkness in groups A and B who received niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA creams, respectively. Substantial improvement was also achieved in some of the patients in group C who were received HQ cream, but the fading of melasma lesions was reversible after discontinuation of treatment in HQ receiving group. In addition, some of the patients in group C showed no considerable changes in melasma lesions, and some of them even became worse than the initial condition, as shown in Fig. 2.

In addition to objective measures of treatment response, subjective outcomes were assessed using a pre- and post-treatment MelasQoL questionnaire. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in MelasQoL score after three months of treatment in all groups compared to baseline. Therefore, according to Table 2, melasma treatment in any of these groups resulted in significantly better quality of life and lower MelasQoL questionnaire score with P-values of 0.014, < 0.001, and 0.005 in groups A, B, and C, respectively. However, there was no significant difference among different treatment groups in terms of response to therapy and quality of life after treatment (P-value > 0.05). Moreover, a significant correlation was observed between MelasQoL and mMASI scores assessed after 3-month treatment course (P-value of 0.001 and Pearson Correlation of 0.368). Therefore, better clinical response after treatment course and lower mMASI score values was accompanied by higher quality of life with lower MelasQoL scores. In addition, a significant correlation was also obvious between mean MI and MelasQoL score after 3-month treatment course (P-value of 0.005 and Pearson Correlation of 0.321). In this regard, lower melanin indices were correlated with higher quality of life and lower MelasQoL scores in patients with melasma. Interestingly, these correlations between mMASI score and MI with MelasQoL were not statistically significant at baseline levels, before treatment initiation (P-value > 0.05) which emphasize on the significant impact of appropriate anti-hyperpigmentary treatment regimens on melasma patients’ quality of life.

Investigator’s global assessment (IGA) of melasma lesion improvement and treatment efficacy was documented by the dermatologist at each follow-up visit and the results are presented in Table 2. In addition, the patients’ opinion regarding the treatment efficacy was documented and the results are shown in Table 2. According to the results presented in Table 2, both IGA and patient opinion showed incremental trends over time during the treatment course, confirming the efficacy of the evaluated preparations in melasma patients. The results of paired Sample T-test revealed that only in niosomal TXA/NCA group, patients’ IGA at month 2 was significantly higher than month 1 (P-value < 0.001) and at month 3 was significantly higher than month 2 (P-value of 0.021). While in groups B and C, increments of IGA at month 3 were not statistically significant (P-value > 0.05). In addition, based on the dermatologists’ point of view, IGA changes overtime were not significant in HQ group (P-value > 0.05), while both niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA groups showed statistically significant incremental trends (P-values of 0.016 and 0.017, respectively). Therefore, based on the IGA results, either niosomal or conventional TXA/NCA creams were superior to HQ cream in terms of melasma lesions improvement over the time.

Short-term follow up assessments after treatment discontinuation

In this study, patients were visited by the dermatologist after one month of treatment discontinuation to assess both the objective outcomes including mMASI and MI, and also subjective outcomes including IGA and patients’ opinion. According to the results, in majority of the patients, especially those in niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA creams groups, outcomes were even improved after treatment discontinuation and no symptom worsening was occurred in these groups. As it is obvious in Supplementary Table S2, mMASI score significantly reduced after treatment discontinuation in niosomal TXA/NCA cream group (P-value of 0.038). The same results were obtained for those received conventional TXA/NCA cream, however, no significant reduction in mMASI score was obvious after treatment discontinuation (P-value = 0.465). Those who received HQ cream showed an increase in mMASI score after treatment discontinuation but this increment was not significant (P-value = 0.343). The results of MI assessment revealed that in all groups of patients, average MI showed a significant declining trend (P-value < 0.05), even after treatment discontinuation.

Adverse drug reactions

The incidence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) was significantly higher in the HQ group compared to the other groups (P-value = 0.033). As shown in Table 3, the severity of ADRs following HQ administration was moderate to severe including hypersensitivity reactions, severe burning and redness, and ochronosis, while in niosomal TXA/NCA group, only one case reported mild burn and in conventional TXA/NCA group one case reported mild to moderate burning and redness. These findings suggest that both niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA creams are superior to HQ cream in terms of ADRs frequencies.

Discussion

This double-blinded, case-controlled clinical trial demonstrated that melasma patients in all treatment groups (A, B, or C) exhibited considerable clinical improvement. These treatments resulted in a substantial reduction in MI and mMASI scores, as primary outcomes, during follow-up, as well as a notable improvement in patients’ quality of life, as assessed through the MelasQoL questionnaire, as a secondary outcome. Although no significant difference was obvious between these 3 different treatment groups in terms of clinical effectiveness for melasma lesion, however, patients in niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA groups encountered with significantly lower ADRs in comparison to those in HQ group.

Melasma is a chronic hyperpigmentary disorder, and the findings of this study align with previous research indicating a strong female predominance36. In addition to the important role of sunlight and UV radiation in melasma development, genetic factors, family history, and polymorphisms also contribute to its pathogenesis5,37. These findings also revealed that 52.6% of the participants had positive family history for melasma that is the same to previous positive family reports in close relationships including patients’ mother or sister. The mean duration of melasma in these cases was about 8 years and more than half of the included patients had positive history for previous topical or systemic anti-hyperpigmentary therapy, laser therapy, or mesotherapy and about 42% of them reported no response, 30% reported relapse, and 16% reported incomplete response to these therapies. Therefore, about half of the assessed patients had somewhat refractory melasma38. In the current study, most of the assessed patients had mixed type melasma, centrofacial pattern, and moderate severity. These results were compatible with the findings of the previous studies in which the mixed type was the most prevalent melasma type followed by epidermal and dermal types using Wood’s Lamp technique for diagnosis39. In addition, the results of another study revealed that the centrofacial pattern was the most common melasma pattern among the patients followed by malar and mandibular patterns40,41.

Melasma can considerably affect patients’ quality of life, therefore, MelasQoL questionnaire was utilized to assess the quality of life at baseline, before treatment initiation, and after the treatment course. In the current study, patients’ baseline MelasQoL score was 44.51 which was compatible with the results of previous studies with baseline MelasQoL scores of 44.40, 42.49, and 39.97 in Brazilian, Spanish, and Indonesian populations, respectively42. The average mMASI score of these patients before treatment initiation was 6.02 which was compatible with the results of a previous study on Indian population with baseline mMASI score of 5.5243. The average baseline MI in the current study was 849.31. The results of a previous study on Chinese population with melasma reported a baseline MI value of 711.844, this difference can be attributed to the patients’ nationality, ethnicity, and geographic region that can substantially affect MI45.

The results of the current study revealed that after only one month of treatment with any of niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% cream, conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream, or HQ cream, a significant reduction in both mMASI score and MI values were obvious. The clinical effectiveness with each of these therapeutic regimens increased significantly overtime and reached to the maximum effect after the end of three-month treatment course. Group A, which were treated with niosomal TXA/NCA cream, twice daily for 3 months, resulted in 18.6% reduction in MI and 66.72% reduction in mMASI score which were the highest values among the others and therefore resulted in the best clinical responses. However, these differences between any of groups A, B, or C were not statistically significant (P-value > 0.05). In this regard, niosomal TXA/NCA cream with even less than the one-half of conventional cream concentrations showed statistically same results which confirm the potential effect of niosomes on enhanced skin penetration and dermal-epidermal specific targeting. Also, the clinical effectiveness of these preparations were statistically same as HQ cream 4% which is considered as the state of the art and the first-choice treatment regimen for melasma10. The results of a previous study in Thai population with melasma who were treated with topical NCA 4% cream resulted in 22.47% reduction in mMASI score after 12 weeks of treatment46. In addition, the results of a previous split-face, double-blinded, clinical trial on the effectiveness of topical TXA cream 5% revealed that although topical TXA resulted in reduction in MI and MASI score after 12 weeks of treatment, however, the results failed to show a statistically significant difference between TXA and placebo in their depigmenting potential47. Moreover, the results of a meta-analysis of clinical trials revealed that topical administration of TXA alone was associated with about 39.51% reduction in mean mMASI/MASI score after 12 weeks of treatment48. Therefore, combining TXA with NCA, either in niosomal formulation with lower concentrations (TXA 2%/NCA 2%) or in conventional formulation with higher concentrations (TXA 5%/NCA 4%) resulted in much higher efficacy, in comparison to each one alone, with 66.72% and 62.40% reduction in mMASI scores for niosomal and conventional creams, respectively, after the same duration of treatment. In addition, the investigated combinational formulations resulted in 18.60% and 14.62% reduction in MI after topical administration of niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA formulations, respectively which were comparable to the results of HQ cream, as a positive control and first-line treatment choice, with 14.78% reduction in MI after 3 months of treatment. While the results of a previous clinical trial on the efficacy of topical NCA 4% alone or in combination with picosecond laser toning revealed no significant reduction in MI after 12 weeks of treatment46. Moreover, the results of another clinical trial revealed that the patients who were received topical TXA 3%/NCA 3% formulation in combination with microneedling showed only 4.94% reduction in mean MI after 12 weeks of treatment which was not statistically significant49. Therefore, in addition to the selection of suitable combinational therapeutic agents with various mechanism of actions for melasma management, preparation of optimum formulations with suitable skin permeation potential and appropriate concentrations of each ingredient would profoundly affect the clinical response rate.

The MelasQoL questionnaire results revealed that patients allocated to any of groups A, B, or C had a statistically significant reduction in MelasQoL score after 3 months of treatment which indicated an improved quality of life after pertinent treatment course. These results were compatible with the results of a recent clinical trial on the effectiveness of a combinational gel formulation containing NCA 10%, magnesium ascorbyl phosphate 5%, and hyaluronic acid 5% in comparison to HQ 4%, which also showed a significant reduction in MelasQoL score in both groups after 60 days of treatment50. Since any of these treatments regimens were associated with significant reduction in both MelasQoL, MI, and mMASI score values, significant correlations were observed between MI and mMASI score with MelasQoL in the current study (P-value < 0.05). These findings were consistent with the results of a previous systematic review and meta-analysis which reported a significant relationship between mMASI score and MelasQoL in 5 out of 14 studies that were assessed in that meta-analysis42.

The results of the current study revealed that no significant increase in mMASI score or MI was occurred one month after treatment discontinuation in any of the different treatment groups A, B, or C. However, a slight increase in mMASI score was obvious in patients received HQ 4% cream, one month after treatment discontinuation. While those who were received niosomal TXA/NCA cream showed a significant declining trend in mMASI score, even after treatment discontinuation. The results of a previous clinical trial on the effectiveness of methimazole 5% cream in comparison to HQ 4% cream in melasma patients also revealed a higher relapse rate in the HQ group51. Moreover, the results of another randomized controlled trial revealed that although addition of oral TXA to HQ cream was associated with higher clinical response with lower mMASI score values, no significant reduction in relapse rate was seen52, therefore, administration of topical preparations, other than HQ, with high clinical efficacy and low relapse rate would be more beneficial.

Based on the results, treatment with either niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% or conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% creams were not associated with serious ADRs, however, treatment with HQ 4% cream was associated with significantly higher adverse drug reactions including moderate to severe hypersensitivity reactions, severe burning, and allergic contact dermatitis that required prompt discontinuation of treatment or intervention in therapy. These results were consistent with the results of a recent clinical trial on the efficacy of silymarin cream in comparison to HQ 4% cream for melasma management which reported significant ADRs in HQ group53. The results of another clinical trial on the efficacy of TXA 5% cream in comparison to HQ 2% cream in melasma patients showed significantly higher rates of ADRs, including erythema and irritation in HQ group, while no ADR was reported with TXA cream54. Hydroquinone ADRs are mostly concentration and also time dependent, therefore, higher concentrations and longer duration of therapies may be associated with more irritation, erythema, burning, allergic contact dermatitis, nail discoloration, hypochromia, and ochronosis55. Therefore, based on the results of the current study, topical TXA/NCA cream, either in niosomal formulation with lower concentrations or in conventional formulation with higher concentrations, would be an appropriate surrogate for HQ in melasma treatment with the same clinical effectiveness and reduced ADRs.

According to the results of the current study treatment with either niosomal or conventional TXA/NCA creams was resulted in an improvement in mMASI score and MI overtime which was statistically significant at each time point, i.e. the reduction in both mMASI score and MI at 2-month point was significantly higher than one-month point and at 3-month point was significantly higher than 2-month point, therefore it seems that the prepared TXA/NCA creams, either in niosomal or conventional formulation, can be used long life in combination with sunscreens to achieve optimal clinical response and also to avoid melasma relapse.

Since both niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% cream and conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream demonstrated comparable clinical effectiveness against melasma lesions, niosomes may serve as an efficient drug delivery system for topical applications56. This approach could allow us to use lower active ingredient concentrations, reducing the risk of ADRs, while simultaneously lowering formulation costs.

Study limitation

Some of the enrolled patients failed to complete the trial due to the personal reasons or adverse reactions, resulting in a reduced sample size.

We should mention that no potential bias was occurred in this trial since patient evaluation was performed through the instrumental techniques and standard photography (Skin Color Catch and VisioFace® 1000D instruments) and the investigating dermatologist was blinded to the type of preparation assigned to each patient.

In conclusion, both niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA creams are as effective as HQ 4% cream in melasma management through the reduction in mMASI and MI scores and improve patients’ self-satisfaction and quality of life by a reduction in MelaosQoL score. Also, due to significant less serious adverse reactions of both niosomal and conventional TXA/NCA creams in comparison to HQ cream, they would be superior choices than HQ-containing preparations in hyperpigmentary disorders although all treatment groups showed comparable efficacy. Niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% cream was as effective as conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream, therefore, encapsulation of these active pharmaceuticals within the niosomes as topical drug delivery systems resulted in the same efficacy at lower concentrations.

Materials and methods

Clinical research ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences [Ethics approval ID: IR.SUMS.REC.1402.050] and registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) which participates in the WHO International Clinical Trial Registry Platform [IRCT approval ID of IRCT20220609055116N1 and approval date of 07/23/2023]. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. Informed consent was obtained to publish the information/image(s) in an online open access publication.

Study design and patient population

This double-blinded, case-controlled clinical trial was conducted at the Dermatology Clinic of Shahid Faghihi Hospital, Shiraz, Iran, from August 2023 to September 2024. Inclusion criteria were male and female patients aged 18–60 years old and had melasma for at least six months. Exclusion criteria were patients with thyroid disorders, those taking OCPs, pregnant and lactating women, patients using oral or topical corticosteroids, those who were on other oral, topical, or procedural therapies for melasma within 1 month prior to the study entry, patients with a history of hypersensitivity reactions to HQ, TXA, or NCA, and those with other hyperpigmentary disorders including lichen planus.

The sample size was selected as 102 patients using MI and mMASI scores as primary outcomes with power of 80% and alpha of 0.05 using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). In this regard, a total of 129 patients were initially screened and 99 patients were included based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients were randomly assigned to three groups (A, B, C) with 33 patients in each group receiving the designated treatment. Randomization was performed based on the block randomization technique using Sealed Envelope™ software [Sealed Envelope Ltd. 2024. Create a blocked randomization list. Available from: https://www.sealedenvelope.com/simple-randomiser/v1/lists] (Block size: 6). A total of 17 blocks (Block size: 6) were generated by the software. Each block contained two cases from each treatment groups (A, B and C), and a unique code was assigned to each preparation. The first author (P.G.) and the corresponding author (S.M.S.) generated the random allocation sequence. The investigating dermatologist (N.F.H.), who was blinded to the types of treatments, enrolled the patients, and one of the co-authors (S.R.), who was blinded to the types of treatments, assigned the participants to interventions. Group A received topical niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% cream; group B received topical conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream; and group C received topical HQ 4% cream (HQ 4% cream). Niosomal TXA 2%/NCA 2% cream and conventional TXA 5%/NCA 4% cream were formulated and assessed in house. While HQ 4% cream was obtained from the market (Pars Darou® Pharmaceutical Co.). Patients were trained to apply their relevant treatment twice daily (except for the HQ group, where cold cream was used in the morning (to maintain blinding) and HQ cream was used once daily at bedtime) for three months. All patients included in this study were advised to apply sunscreen with an SPF of at least 30 or higher every 2 to 3 h during daylight, both during and after the treatment courses. This study was double blinded, ensuring that both patients and the dermatologist were unaware of the treatment assignments. All preparations including niosomal TXA/NCA cream, conventional TXA/NCA cream, marketed HQ cream, and cold cream were packed in the same jars to maintain blinding. In this regard, HQ creams were repacked from their original tubes to the same containers as other preparations. Each patient underwent five dermatological visits: (1) Baseline visit: Melasma diagnosis and inclusion in the study, (2) Month 1: One month after treatment initiation, (3) Month 2: Two months after treatment initiation, (4) Month 3: Three months after treatment initiation, and (5) Follow-up visit: One month after treatment discontinuation. Two jars of creams were assigned to each patient at each clinical visit to be used for 1 month, one of the jars was labeled as “Day cream” and another one was labeled as “Night cream”. For patients assigned to either conventional or niosomal TXA/NCA cream groups, Day and Night creams were the same. While for those assigned to HQ cream group, Day cream was cold cream (with no active ingredient for melasma management) and Night cream was HQ 4% cream. Patients were advised to keep the preparations refrigerated.

Before treatment initiation, demographic data were collected, including gender, age, marital status, education, number of pregnancies, melasma duration, family history of melasma, history of previous therapies, and response to previous treatments. Additionally, patients were completed the MelasQoL questionnaire. Moreover, the dermatologist assessed melasma severity, melasma type, MI of cheeks, chin, forehead, and upper lips using Skin Color Catch instrument (Delfin Technologies, Kuopio, Finland). Baseline melasma severity was determined based on the dermatologist’s clinical evaluation of melasma lesions and also based on mMASI score calculation at the first dermatology visit before treatment initiation. Standard photography was performed from the front-view, left, and right sides using VisioFace® 1000D (Courage-Khazaka Electronic, Köln, Germany) and mMASI scores of the patients were calculated before treatment initiation by the dermatologist. During monthly follow-up visits, standard photography was performed and MI and mMASI scores were assessed as primary outcomes (objective tools), and the investigator’s global assessment (IGA) was documented by the dermatologist as secondary outcome (subjective tool). According to the IGA scoring system, a score of 1 indicated no effect (no visible change in pigmentation), a score of 2 indicated a mild effect (decrease in visible pigmentation, but there is still some visible edge), a score of 3 indicated a moderate effect (marked decrease in visible pigmentation, but there is still some visible edge), and a score of 4 indicated an excellent effect (complete loss of visible abnormal pigmentation). Moreover, the patients’ opinion regarding the efficacy of the preparations administered was assessed as secondary outcome (subjective tool) over the time at each follow-up visit and documented as score 1: no effect, score 2: mild effects, score 3: moderate effects, and score 4: excellent effects. After the end of three-month therapy duration, patients were assessed in terms of aforementioned parameters and also the MelasQoL questionnaire, as secondary outcome (objective tool), was once again filled by each patient. All these aforementioned parameters, except for MelasQol, were also assessed one month after treatment discontinuation.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical analysis, IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used and p-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. In this study, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Paired Sample T-test, Repeated Measure ANOVA test, and Chi-Square test were used for data analysis. In addition, Multiple Imputation (MI) using 20 imputations was conducted to handle missing data (22 out of 99 cases, 22%).
