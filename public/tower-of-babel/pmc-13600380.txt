Utility of Nasopharyngeal Aspirate Over Other Upper Airway Sampling Approaches for Cellular Immunology During Respiratory Viral Infection
Authors: Sara Falck-Jones, Björn Österberg, Eric Åhlberg, Julia Svensson, Afandi Charles, Meng Yu, Avinash Padhi, Vijay Joshua, Jeanette Grundström, Ryan Falck-Jones, Max Bell, Lena Bergqvist, Anna Smed-Sörensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly immune responses to respiratory viruses in the upper airways, including recruitment of innate immune cells such as monocytes and dendritic cells, dictate disease development. Still, unlike soluble biomarkers, comparative evaluations of upper respiratory sampling methods for immune cell analysis are limited. We longitudinally collected matched nasopharyngeal aspirates, nostril swabs, nasal curettes, and blood from patients with influenza-like illness and controls. Among the methods tested, nasopharyngeal aspirates yielded the highest numbers of viable immune cells, including monocytes and dendritic cells, while causing similar sampling discomfort and blood contamination, making it superior for longitudinal collection of immune cells from the upper airways.Keywords: dendritic cells, influenza, monocytes, respiratory immunology

Respiratory viruses, including influenza A and B virus, respiratory syncytial virus, human metapneumovirus, and SARS-CoV-2, cause significant morbidity and mortality [1]. The COVID-19 pandemic reinforced the importance of studies of airway immune responses, and local respiratory events, including interferon production and monocyte and dendritic cell (DC) recruitment, were connected to disease severity [2–8]. Thus, to understand mechanisms driving respiratory viral diseases, it is vital to study the primary site of infection—the upper airways. Despite anatomic differences in composition and function of immune cells within the airways [2, 9, 10], the understanding of upper airway immune responses remains incomplete, highlighting the importance of comparisons between sampling methods and sites. Efforts have been made to investigate nasal soluble proteins, including cytokines and antibodies [11–13]. Analyses of nasal immune cells are rare, partly due to difficulties of obtaining sufficient cell numbers, and functional analyses are almost completely lacking [12]. Methods to noninvasively collect immune cells from the human upper airways include nasopharyngeal aspirate (NPA), nostril swab (NSW), nasopharyngeal (NPH) swab, and curette [10, 12, 14, 15].

Here, upper airway sampling methods were compared side-by-side to identify a noninvasive method to study immune response dynamics during human viral infection. Among these methods, NPA proved most effective, yielding sufficient viable immune cells for flow cytometric analysis and functional assays.

METHOD

Sample Collection

This study sampled adults with influenza-like illness without immunosuppression and healthy controls (HCs; adults without respiratory illness or immunosuppression). NPH swabs were collected for viral polymerase chain reaction diagnostics (FLOQswabs; Copan Diagnostics). Nasal curettes (Rhino-Pro; Arlington Scientific) were used to scrape the inferior turbinate mucosa (1 per nostril) and collected in 1 mL of R2 medium (RPMI + 2% FCS + 1.5mM DTT). For NPA, a suction catheter connected to a mucus trap (Mediplast) was inserted into the nasopharynx and suction applied (20 kPa, 2 minutes). The catheter was rinsed with 3 mL of R2 medium. NSWs (FLOQswabs) were collected by swabbing the nostril and added to 1 mL of R2 medium. Blood was collected in EDTA tubes. Samples were processed within 2 hours or discarded if visibly blood contaminated.

Sample Processing and Flow Cytometry

Samples were processed and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated as previously described [14]. NSWs were vortexed in R2 medium before the swabs were removed. Respiratory cells and PBMCs were counted and viability assessed with trypan blue (Sigma) and an automated Countess cell counter (Invitrogen). Cells were stained fresh with Live/Dead Blue (Invitrogen), incubated with human FcR blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec), and stained with antibodies (Supplementary Table 2). Cells were washed and fixed with paraformaldehyde and acquired on an LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed by FlowJo software 10.5.3 (TreeStar).

For stimulations, 107 fresh cells/mL (PBMC or NPA; total volume, 200 μL) were cultured in a U-bottom 96-well plate in medium (RPMI, L-glutamine, penicillin streptomycin, 10% FCS) with or without lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 100 ng/mL) for 16 hours, and supernatants were collected.

Cytokine Analysis

For stimulation supernatants, a custom-made Luminex assay (LXSAHM-10; R&D Systems) and a Bio-Plex 200 analyzer (BIO-RAD) were used to measure TNF and IL-6 (among others), according to the manufacturer's protocol. TNF and IL-6 ELISA DuoSet kits (R&D Systems) were used to measure levels in plasma, NPA, and NSW.

Statistics

Data were analyzed in RStudio running R version 4.5 and GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Medians were compared with a Wilcoxon rank sum or Kruskal-Wallis test. Pairwise comparisons were performed while controlling the false discovery rate. Correlations were assessed with Spearman rho. A 5% significance level was used and analyses were 2-tailed. Cell counts were obtained by multiplying the total cell count from the automated cell counter with the frequency of the cell subset out of live CD45+ cells.

Study Approval

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (approval 2015/1949-31/4). Written consent was obtained from all participants; for sedated patients, presumed will was confirmed with their primary contact, and retrospective consent was obtained after recovery.

RESULTS

NPA Is Superior to Obtain Viable Cells From the Upper Airways vs NSW and Nasal Curette

Respiratory samples—including NPH swabs for viral diagnostics as well as nasal curettes, NPA, NSW, and blood—were collected longitudinally from patients with influenza-like illness (n = 54) and compared with HCs (n = 33; Figure 1A and 1B). Viral etiology was mixed (Supplementary Table 1, Figure 1C). NPA yielded significantly more live cells than NSW and curettes in patients (Figure 1D) and HCs (Figure 1E), with higher viability in patients (Figure 1F and 1G). NPH swab cell counts showed lower numbers of viable cells vs matched NPA (Figure 1H). NPA cell numbers were significantly elevated when compared with curette and NSW cells over time (Figure 1I).

A numerical rating scale was used to assess sampling discomfort, pain, and induction of tear flow, showing similar levels across sampling methods except NPH swabs (Figure 1J, Supplementary Figure 1A and 1B). Blood contamination in respiratory samples was comparable (Figure 1K and 1L).

NPA Contains More Monocytes and DCs Than NSW and Curette

Innate immune cells including monocytes and DCs are rapidly recruited to the airways during respiratory viral infections and respond to viruses by secreting cytokines and initiating adaptive responses [12]. Previous studies have demonstrated a substantial increase in monocyte and DC subsets in the nasal mucosa during acute human respiratory viral infections [3,14, 15] , and we therefore focused on these cells. Monocyte and DC subsets were identified from lineage− (CD19−CD20−CD3−CD56−CD66−) HLA-DR+ cells (Figure 2A and 2B, Supplementary Figure 1C–E). Analysis of frequencies of NPA, NSW, and blood samples from patients and HCs showed that classical monocytes were the most abundant Lin-HLA-DR+ cells in all samples (Figure 2C and 2D). Since some samples had few events in the Lin-HLA-DR+ gate, a threshold of 99 events was set to avoid unstable frequency estimates, and curettes were excluded from the analysis due to an insufficient number of samples that could be used for subsequent frequency analysis (Supplementary Figure 2A).

When we compared the calculated Lin-HLA-DR+ cell numbers (Lin-HLA-DR+ frequency out of live CD45+ cells multiplied with total cell count from the initial cell count), cell counts were higher in NPA vs the matched patient curette and NSW and the HC NPA (Figure 2E). These numbers were higher than total events obtained in the flow cytometer (Supplementary Figure 2A). In HCs, Lin-HLA-DR+ numbers were similar across sample types (Supplementary Figure 2B). Patient monocyte and DC counts were also higher in NPA vs curette and NSW (Figure 2F and 2G, Supplementary Figure 2C–G). Although the main focus was on monocytes and DCs, a few samples were stained by a separate flow cytometry panel to identify T (CD3+) and B (CD19+) cells (Supplementary Figure 2H). Like monocytes and DCs, higher numbers of T and B cells were found in NPA (Figure 2H and 2I).

NPA and NSW supernatants allowed for detection of IL-6 and TNF in patients, with similar levels in NPA and plasma, whereas NSW had lower concentrations than plasma (Figure 2J and 2K). In 2 NPA samples, the functional response to LPS (TLR4 ligand) was assessed (Figure 2L and 2M). Baseline production of IL-6 and TNF was higher in NPA when compared with PBMCs. Upon LPS stimulation, PBMCs and, to some extent, NPA cells responded with increased cytokine production.

DISCUSSION

To unravel immunologic events driving disease severity in human respiratory viral infections, sampling the primary site of infection is important. However, upper airway sampling in humans is challenging and typically generates few viable cells, limiting the possibilities to use flow cytometry and functional assays [12]. Thus, there is a need for comprehensive comparisons of different upper airway sampling methods.

We show that NPA from influenza-like illness patients generated higher cell numbers when compared with nasal curettes and NSWs, providing enough cells for flow cytometry and in some cases functional assessment in vitro. Specifically, NPA could successfully be used to distinguish monocyte and DC subsets in patients who were infected, and we found increased levels of several cell subsets, consistent with previous observations [14]. Importantly, NPA could be used to obtain enough cells even from HCs to perform flow cytometry. The limited cell yield from curettes and NSWs could be caused by differences in sampling method and differences in anatomic compartment. A limitation of the study is that we mainly studied monocytes and DCs and not other cell types that are important during respiratory viral infections. However, a limited set of stainings with a different flow cytometry panel showed higher numbers of T and B cells in NPA.

Since NPH swabs were used for clinical polymerase chain reaction, they were not stained for flow cytometry, which is a limitation. NPH swabs have been utilized in recent studies to examine immune cell populations [2, 9]. However, we found that NPA generated higher numbers of cells with greater viability and less sampling discomfort than NPH swabs.

In summary, this study supports NPA as a robust noninvasive method to study local immune dynamics in patients with influenza-like illness. Importantly, NPA could be utilized to study local immune responses to circulating respiratory viruses as well as future pandemic viruses.
