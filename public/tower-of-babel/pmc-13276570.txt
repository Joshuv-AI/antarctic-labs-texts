Global distribution and genomic characteristics of Shigella: A genomic epidemiological study

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First systematic depiction of the global spatiotemporal genomic distribution of Shigella.•Elucidation of the relationship between serotype and sequence type in Shigella, and their intercontinental distribution patterns.•Molecular evidence for the transnational transmission of Shigella provided by core-genome multilocus sequence typing.•Prediction of a diverse set of antimicrobial resistance genes harbored by Shigella based on genomic analysis.

Introduction

Shigella is a genus of non-motile, non-flagellated, facultative anaerobic Gram-negative bacteria that serves as the primary pathogen of shigellosis (commonly known as bacterial dysentery). In developing countries, bacterial dysentery primarily threatens the health of children under five years of age and remains the second leading cause of diarrheal mortality in this population.1,2 In developed countries, Shigella infections are also prevalent among specific populations such as men who have sex with men (MSM), people living with HIV, the homeless, and international travelers.3, 4, 5

Shigella can be divided into four species or subgroups: Shigella dysenteriae (Group A), Shigella flexneri (Group B), Shigella boydii (Group C), and Shigella sonnei (Group D). These species exhibit distinct epidemiological characteristics. S. dysenteriae, the first discovered and most virulent species, includes the lethal serotype Sd1, which caused four pandemics but has declined globally since 2010.6, 7, 8 S. boydii was initially confined to the Indian subcontinent but has since been detected in returning travelers, suggesting its distribution may be influenced by international travel.9 Currently, S. flexneri and S. sonnei dominate, with S. flexneri more prevalent in developing countries and S. sonnei in developed ones. However, in transitional economies and regions with improving water quality, such as China, Thailand, and Vietnam, S. sonnei is gradually replacing S. flexneri.10 With the exception of S. sonnei, which has only one serotype, the other three Shigella species each comprise multiple serotypes and subtypes: S. dysenteriae has 15 serotypes, S. flexneri has at least 20 serotypes, and S. boydii has 19 serotypes.6 This serotype diversity poses a central challenge for Shigella vaccine development. Due to the lack of cross-protective immunity among serotypes and marked geographical heterogeneity, no Shigella vaccine has been approved for widespread use to date.11 Current late-stage vaccine candidates primarily target S. flexneri 2a, 3a, and 6, and S. sonnei.11 Emerging serotypes such as SF1c in Indonesia, Pakistan, and northern Vietnam, as well as SFXv in China,12,13 together with non-mainstream serotypes such as SF3a and SFX in Tanzania and SF1a in Pakistan, have been reported, suggesting that current vaccines may still not adequately cover some serotypes in high-burden regions.14 Traditional serological methods, constrained by limited sample sizes and narrow geographic coverage, have often yielded results that reflect only the predominant serotypes, and have failed to fully capture the regional distribution of serotype diversity.

Treatment of moderate to severe shigellosis primarily relies on antibiotics. According to the 2005 World Health Organization guidelines, ciprofloxacin was recommended as first-line therapy, with ceftriaxone and azithromycin as alternatives.15 However, Shigella resistance has expanded from early agents such as sulfonamides and tetracyclines to current first-line fluoroquinolones.10 The emergence of multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant strains poses a severe clinical challenge, exacerbated by global trade and travel.16 A systematic review reported that gyrA-S83L was the most prevalent quinolone resistance gene in S. sonnei, mphA was the most common macrolide resistance gene in S. flexneri, and sul2 was the most frequently detected folate synthesis inhibitor resistance gene in S. boydii and S. dysenteriae.17 Recently, a novel extensively drug-resistant S. sonnei strain carrying blaDHA-1 and blaCTX-M-15 was reported in the United States of America and appears to be spreading beyond MSM populations into the broader population.18 Additionally, outbreaks of multidrug-resistant S. sonnei harboring blaCTX-M-27 plasmids have been reported in Australia, Spain, Switzerland, and other countries.19, 20, 21

As sequencing costs decline, whole genome sequencing is increasingly applied to Shigella surveillance, enabling precise subtyping and resistance profiling. However, integrated analyses across countries and time periods remain limited. In this study, we integrated genomic data from 68 countries across six continents spanning 1914−2024 to systematically analyze the spatiotemporal source profile of Shigella genomes, the geographical dynamics of major serotypes and sequence types (STs), and the regional and temporal variations in carriage rates of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs).

Materials and methods

Construction and quality control of genomic dataset

The self-tested dataset comprised 298 assembled Shigella genomes (193 S. flexneri, 105 S. sonnei) collected from seven regions in China between 1980 and 2017. All genomes have been deposited in National Center of Biotechnology Information under BioProject numbers PRJNA903515, PRJNA230538, and PRJNA820478, and have been published previously.13,22,23 This dataset contains experimentally validated serotype information, among other data, and was primarily used to validate the concordance between predicted and phenotypic results, as well as to perform integrated analyses with the public dataset (Table S1).

The public dataset consisted of 41,891 assembled Shigella genomes and their related annotation files were collected from the National Center of Biotechnology Information genome database (as of January 1, 2025) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/). Python scripts were used to extract metadata, including collection date, country, host, and serotype or serovar. Dataset quality control: Genomes were included only if they had definitive collection date, geographic location, and host information. Remaining genomes were assessed using CheckM v1.2.2 (Australian Centre for Ecogenomics, The University of Queensland, Brisbane, Australia),24 retaining those with ≥ 95% completeness and ≤ 5% contamination. Serotypes were predicted using ShigaPass v1.5.0 (Institut Pasteur, Paris, France).25,26 and ShigEiFinder v1.3.5 (LanLab, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia),27 with predictions validated against available known serotype data. For genomes lacking original serotype information, predictions were accepted only when both tools agreed; discordant results were resolved by adopting ShigEiFinder predictions. Genomes with unknown or non-Shigella serotypes were excluded. The final core dataset comprised 17,360 Shigella genomes from 61 countries spanning 1914−2024 (Table S1).

Antimicrobial resistance gene analysis

ARGs were detected using RGI v6.0.4 (CARD, McMaster University, Hamilton, Canada) with reference to the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (https://card.mcmaster.ca/home) (identity > 80%; coverage > 80%).

Population structure analysis

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of the four Shigella species was conducted using MLST v2.23.0 (Torsten Seemann, University of Melbourne, Melbourne, Australia),28 applying the scheme from the PubMLST website (https://pubmlst.org/) based on seven housekeeping genes (adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, recA) to determine their STs. Core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) analysis was conducted using chewBBACA v3.3.9 (Universidade de Lisboa, Lisbon, Portugal).29 with the Escherichia/Shigella cgMLST scheme from the EnteroBase database (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/). A minimum spanning tree was constructed using GrapeTree v1.5.0 (University of Warwick, Coventry, United Kingdom).30

Statistical analysis

Data were managed in Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, United States of America), with categorical variables summarized as counts (n) and proportions (%). The Kappa consistency test was applied to a randomly selected subset of 3,000 serotype results to assess serotype prediction concordance between ShigaPass and ShigEiFinder. Kappa values were interpreted as: < 0.00 (less than chance), 0.00−0.20 (slight), 0.21−0.40 (fair), 0.41−0.60 (moderate), 0.61−0.80 (substantial), and 0.81−1.00 (almost perfect) agreement. To assess ST-serotype associations, after excluding unknown serotypes or STs, 151 samples were randomly selected for each species (S. flexneri, S. boydii, and S. dysenteriae) based on the minimum sample size. Fisher’s exact test was used for analysis. Cramér's V was calculated to quantify the strength of association, defined as weak (V < 0.1), moderate (0.1 ≤ V < 0.3), relatively strong (0.3 ≤ V < 0.5), and strong (V ≥ 0.5). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analysis of temporal trends in ARGs utilized the Cochran-Armitage trend test (two-sided and one-sided; α = 0.05). The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controlled the false discovery rate, considering results with an adjusted p < 0.05 as significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software v4.5.0 (Vienna, Austria). Stacked bar charts, line charts, and pie charts were generated with ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/) (accessed on 22 November 2025).

Results

The global spatiotemporal distribution of Shigella genomic data

In this study, the spatiotemporal analysis reflects the geographic isolation regions and collection times of samples as recorded in the submitted Shigella genomic metadata, rather than true epidemiological trends. The full dataset comprised 42,189 genomes: S. sonnei (n = 21,719), S. flexneri (n = 19,278), S. boydii (n = 863), and S. dysenteriae (n = 329). Collection dates ranged from 1914 to 2024, with 47.41% lacking temporal information. Among dated samples, 99.12% were collected after 2000, and analysis was stratified into six periods: before 2000 (n = 196), 2000−2004 (n = 319), 2005−2009 (n = 1,465), 2010−2014 (n = 2,482), 2015−2019 (n = 8,581), and 2020−2024 (n = 9,145). From 2005 onwards, the number of genomic samples increased significantly, fluctuating markedly from 2020 to 2024, with a decreasing trend from 2020 to 2022 followed by an increasing trend from 2023 to 2024 (Table S2). In terms of species differences, the numbers of S. sonnei and S. flexneri alternated in dominance, whereas S. boydii and S. dysenteriae stayed low throughout (Fig. 1A). Geographically, genomic samples were collected from 68 countries across six continents, with 24.37% lacking geographic information (Fig. 1B). The four species showed different spatiotemporal patterns of sample collection (Fig. S1A−D). Before 2010, the majority of S. flexneri samples were collected from Asia (66.56%) and Africa (14.11%), while most S. sonnei samples were collected from Asia (50.00%) and South America (18.71%). After 2015, the samples of all four species were predominantly collected from Europe (70.92%). At the country level, the United States of America was the primary country where S. flexneri and S. sonnei samples were collected, while the United Kingdom was the primary country where most S. boydii and S. dysenteriae samples were collected (Fig. 1C). To further understand the differences in the origin of Shigella genomic samples between countries of different economic statuses, four representative countries with substantial data and species coverage were selected: Two developed countries (the United Kingdom and the United States of America) and two developing countries (China and Bangladesh) (n = 27,197). In the United States of America and the United Kingdom, both S. flexneri and S. sonnei accounted for substantial proportions of collected samples, with S. sonnei predominating and the number of S. flexneri increasing over time (Fig. S2A, B). In contrast, the composition of genomic samples in developing countries differed: In China, the number of S. sonnei increased steadily, while the number of S. flexneri decreased continuously; in Bangladesh, despite transient rises in the number of other species during 2015−2019, S. flexneri remained overwhelmingly dominant (74.70%) throughout (Fig. S2C, D).Fig. 1Global spatiotemporal distribution of genomic data for the four Shigella species. Serotype characteristics and population structure of Shigella

Analysis of 17,360 Shigella genomes with complete metadata identified 166 STs and 50 serotypes (Table S3). Kappa consistency tests showed almost perfect agreement for all pairwise comparisons: between original serotype results and ShigaPass (concordance rate: 96.93%, Kappa value: 0.924), between original serotype results and ShigEiFinder (concordance rate: 96.53%, Kappa value: 0.914), and between ShigaPass and ShigEiFinder (concordance rate: 97.63%, Kappa value: 0.941). Among 7,679 S. flexneri genomes, 18 serotypes were identified. SF2a was predominant in Africa (69.02%), SF1b in North America (24.44%), and SF3a in Oceania and Europe (> 15.00%). Strong geographic preferences were observed: SFY predominated in South America (44.44%), while SF2b and SFXv were more prevalent in Asia (Fig. 2A). Among 9,197 S. sonnei genomes, only a single serotype was identified. Among 332 S. boydii genomes, 18 serotypes were identified. SB2 and SB4 were most common, with SB2 highest in Africa (50.00%) and SB4 in Europe, Asia, and Africa (> 18.00%). SB8 and SB19 showed strong geographic preference, with highest proportions in Asia (≥ 15.00%) and North America (≥ 15.00%), respectively (Fig. 2B). Among 152 S. dysenteriae genomes, 13 serotypes were identified. Common serotypes included SD2, SD3, and SD4. SD2 was highest in Asia (75.00%), SD4 in South America (50.00%), and SD3 in Europe (25.71%), with sporadic distribution of these serotypes in North America and Africa. Based on MLST, 92 STs were identified among 7,679 S. flexneri genomes. ST245 was the most frequent, accounting for relatively high proportions among genomes from all continents. ST145 accounted for relatively high proportions among genomes from Europe and Oceania. ST1025 was present among genomes from Europe, Asia, and Oceania, but not from other continents (Fig. 2D). In S. sonnei, 35 STs were identified among 9,197 genomes, with ST152 dominant across all six continents. In S. boydii, 20 STs were identified among 332 genomes. ST145 accounted for high proportions of genomes from Europe, Africa, and Asia (> 35.00%). ST243 accounted for the highest proportion among genomes from North America (55.00%). ST5475 was present among genomes from Europe (15.48%) but not from other continents (Fig. 2E). In S. dysenteriae, 20 STs were identified among 152 genomes. ST148 was the dominant sequence type among genomes from Europe, while ST288 was dominant among those from Asia (Fig. 2F).

A comprehensive analysis of STs and serotypes showed that 16 serotypes each corresponded to a single ST. Among S. flexneri, only the SF1d serotype exhibited a one-to-one correspondence with ST245. SF2a (92.79%), SF1b (98.81%), and SF3a (75.69%) mainly corresponded to ST245, while SF6 (85.09%) corresponded to ST145 (Fig. 3A). Among S. boydii, eight serotypes each corresponded to a single ST, including SB5, SB8, SB10, among others. Distribution of antimicrobial resistance genes in Shigella

The landscape of antimicrobial resistance genes

Analysis of 17,360 Shigella genomes revealed that 17,314 (99.74%) carried at least one ARG, with the exception of 46 S. boydii genomes that lacked any detected ARGs. Broader ARG diversity was observed in S. flexneri and S. sonnei compared to S. boydii and S. dysenteriae (Table S4). Tetracycline ARGs had the highest overall carriage rate (99.26%), reaching 100% in S. flexneri, S. sonnei, and S. dysenteriae. Aminoglycoside ARGs were also highly prevalent, particularly in S. sonnei (98.00%). S. sonnei showed higher carriage of sulfonamide and fluoroquinolone ARGs, while S. flexneri had higher carriage of β-lactam and macrolide ARGs (Table 1). Carriage of ARGs from ≥ 3 drug classes was widespread, with an overall carriage rate of 96.20% (Table 1). The most prevalent β-lactam ARGs were blaOXA-1, blaTEM-1, and blaCTX-M-15. Quinolone resistance-associated genes were primarily represented by plasmid-borne qnrS1 and qnrB10, along with QRDR mutations (Fig. 5 and Table S4). The predominant ARGs for macrolides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, trimethoprim, and sulfonamides are shown in Fig. 5. Geographic and temporal trends in ARG carriage rates

The analysis revealed distinct geographical variations in ARG carriage rates among the four Shigella species. S. flexneri maintained high ARG carriage rates across all continents, with particularly high rates of β-lactam and macrolide ARGs in Europe and elevated rates of quinolone, macrolide, and β-lactam ARGs in Asia (Table 2). S. sonnei had high quinolone ARG carriage rates in Europe, Asia, and Oceania, but low rates in South America and Africa (Table 2). S. boydii exhibited high carriage of β-lactam ARGs, mainly in Europe and Asia, whereas high carriage in S. dysenteriae was concentrated in Africa. This geographical divergence was also seen in some ARGs: blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-27 predominated in Europe, while blaCTX-M-14 and blaCTX-M-55 were more common in Asia. The colistin resistance-associated gene mcr-1.1 was detected almost exclusively in strains from China (Table 2 and S5). Carriage rates of ARGs from ≥ 3 drug classes showed significant increasing trends over time for all four species (Table 3). Cochran-Armitage trend tests revealed that β-lactam and quinolone ARG carriage increased significantly across all species, while macrolide ARG carriage (mphA, ermB) increased significantly only in S. flexneri and S. sonnei genomes. The increase in carriage rates of quinolone resistance-associated genes mainly involved plasmid-mediated genes and chromosomal target site mutations. In S. flexneri, significant increases were observed for the plasmid-mediated genes qnrS1 (Z = 10.460, p < 0.001) and qnrB4 (Z = 3.535, p < 0.05), as well as the target mutation gyrA-D87N (Z = 13.570, p < 0.001). In S. sonnei, significant increases were observed for target mutations parC-S80I (Z = 25.845, p < 0.001), gyrA-D87G (Z = 25.354, p < 0.001), and gyrA-S83L (Z = 12.317, p < 0.001), along with the plasmid-mediated gene qnrS1 (Z = 23.224, p < 0.001). In S. boydii, a significant increase was observed only for the target mutation gyrA-S83L (Z = 3.304, p < 0.05). In S. dysenteriae, significant increases were observed for both the plasmid-mediated gene qnrS1 (Z = 4.478, p < 0.001) and the target mutation gyrA-S83L (Z = 3.886, p < 0.001). Discussion

Based on 42,189 Shigella genomes, this study comprehensively describes the distribution characteristics of global Shigella genomic sample origins. It is important to emphasize that these observations reflect the submitted genomic samples, which are shaped by national surveillance capacities, sequencing priorities, and data-sharing policies, rather than directly representing true changes in shigellosis incidence or species-specific disease burden in these countries. To analyze the dynamics of shigellosis, standardized surveillance data should be used. The dominance of S. sonnei and S. flexneri in this dataset aligns with their epidemiological status as the primary pathogenic species. The distribution of S. boydii appears to be no longer restricted to the Indian subcontinent, as suggested by our data showing that strains have been collected and sequenced across multiple continents. Given the current increase in international travel and cross-border trade, S. boydii may have achieved transregional distribution through these pathways, warranting attention to its transmission risk. The scarcity of S. dysenteriae genomes is consistent with its low endemic trend globally.8 The marked increase in genomic samples of Shigella since 2005 coincides with the development of high-throughput sequencing, but the rapid acceleration during 2015−2019 is attributable to several factors. First, the United Kingdom has progressively integrated whole-genome sequencing into routine Shigella surveillance since 2015, facilitating strain sequencing and data sharing, thereby directly increasing the proportion of genomic data from this region.31 Second, sexual transmission among MSM has emerged as a key pathway for extensively drug-resistant Shigella spread; frequent outbreaks in this population have increased clinical sample volumes in some countries, leading to more Shigella isolates being sequenced.32,33 Additionally, heightened research focus on multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant Shigella has indirectly contributed to the accumulation of genomic data. The number of genomic samples fluctuated markedly between 2020 and 2024, which may be related to the preventive measures implemented to control the COVID-19 pandemic. Some scholars suggest that measures such as social distancing, hand hygiene, and travel restrictions indirectly led to a reduction in gastrointestinal pathogen transmission.34 After the pandemic subsided and daily social activities returned to normal, the risk of gastrointestinal pathogen transmission rebounded. The resumption of strain sequencing then led to a clear rebound in genomic sequence data of Shigella isolates. Differences exist in the origins of Shigella genomic samples between countries of different economic statuses. In developed countries such as the United States of America and the United Kingdom, the increase in the number of S. flexneri samples may be partly attributed to enhanced surveillance for S. flexneri and travelers returning from high-incidence regions, who may introduce the pathogen and facilitate limited local transmission.35,36 In the data from China, the number of S. sonnei samples has steadily increased and gradually surpassed that of S. flexneri, consistent with the shift in species distribution driven by rapid economic growth and improved sanitation.37, 38, 39 The data from Bangladesh align with the epidemiological trend of S. flexneri as the predominant species in developing countries.40

The serotype distribution and sequence typing of Shigella exhibit complex diversity. Serotypes targeted by current vaccines, such as S. flexneri SF2a and SF1b, are widely distributed among isolates globally. However, we observed regional differences in the distribution of prevalent and non-prevalent serotypes. This suggests that current vaccine candidates may inadequately cover local serotypes in some high-burden regions. Therefore, assessing regional serotype diversity and incorporating it into vaccine development and global deployment strategies is essential.41 In terms of genomic typing, the single-clone dominance of S. flexneri and S. sonnei, contrasting with the lack of dominant clonal types in S. boydii and S. dysenteriae, is consistent with previous reports. The strong association between serotypes and STs warrants further investigation, particularly for dominant types with stable combinations. Previous cgMLST analysis of > 4,000 Shigella strains demonstrated its utility for laboratory surveillance and international outbreak monitoring, facilitating the shift from phenotypic typing to routine whole-genome sequencing.42 Our cgMLST analysis further suggests that cross-border transmission of S. flexneri and S. sonnei may be more extensive and sustained, warranting vigilance regarding their potential risk of international spread.

ARG analysis reveals a severe resistance situation at the genotypic level, with especially high carriage of genes conferring resistance to traditional agents like tetracyclines and aminoglycosides.43,44 S. sonnei and S. flexneri carried higher proportions of ARGs against clinically commonly used quinolones and macrolides, warranting vigilance. The carriage of ARGs exhibited geographic variation, which may be influenced by regional antimicrobial use strategies and circulating species. S. flexneri in Europe exhibited higher carriage rates of β-lactam and macrolide ARGs. This pattern may be linked to antimicrobial pressure from the treatment of sexually transmitted infections in the MSM population, among whom shigellosis cases in Europe are predominantly concentrated. Widespread use of azithromycin and ceftriaxone for gonorrhea treatment may exert sustained selective pressure, contributing to elevated ARG carriage in this region.45 The high quinolone ARG carriage in Asian S. sonnei aligns with the regional resistance trend driven by gyrA mutations.10 According to previous reports, CTX-M β-lactamase subtypes exhibit marked regional variation, with blaCTX-M-2 predominating in Japan, blaCTX-M-3 and blaCTX-M-14 widespread in China, and blaCTX-M-15 dominating in India.46, 47, 48 Our findings are consistent with these regional patterns. The presence of the colistin resistance-associated gene mcr-1.1 in S. sonnei warrants attention, as colistin is a last-resort antimicrobial for treating multidrug-resistant Gram-negative infections.49 This phenomenon has also been reported in previous studies in China, where mcr-1-carrying S. sonnei strains were detected during waterborne outbreaks; these strains exhibited resistance to ceftriaxone, azithromycin, and colistin, posing a potential threat to global public health.50,51 The carriage rates of several key ARGs increased significantly over time, indicating that the accumulation of ARGs in Shigella genomes is continuously increasing. If sustained, this accumulation could increase the risk of emerging phenotypic resistance, potentially limit empirical treatment options and raising the risk of treatment failure. Therefore, strengthening genomic-based resistance surveillance is essential for timely detection of resistance dynamics, optimization of empirical therapy, and implementation of effective prevention and control strategies.

This study has several limitations. Only 17,360 high-quality genomes were analyzed due to incomplete metadata and variable data quality, which may limit the comprehensiveness of Shigella genomic characterization. Genomic data were predominantly from developed countries, while high-burden regions such as Asia and Africa had limited data. This data imbalance limits the generalizability of our findings to these high-burden regions. In addition, potential batch effects from different sequencing platforms and assembly methods were not evaluated, which may affect cgMLST and ARG detection. Our analyses of serotypes and ARGs were primarily based on in silico predictions, and ARG carriage does not always equate to functional resistance. Furthermore, this study did not examine the role of mobile genetic elements in ARG dissemination, leaving key mechanisms of resistance transmission unaddressed. Therefore, future experimental validation is needed, including phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing and functional characterization of key genes.

Conclusions

This study provides a valuable spatiotemporal genomic profile of Shigella, enhancing the understanding of the temporal and geographical dynamics of its serotypes, population structure, and antimicrobial resistance genes, with a particular focus on the understudied species S. boydii and S. dysenteriae. The regional distribution of predominant serotypes can inform vaccine development strategies, while trends in resistance gene carriage support resistance surveillance. Strengthening and improving genomic surveillance of Shigella therefore remains essential for guiding future research and public health efforts.
