Analytic Torsion from Chern-Simons theory via the ( 2 , 0 ) (2,0) -theory on Dicyclic Orbifolds of S 3 S^{3}
Emil Albrychiewicz, Andrés Franco Valiente, and Ori Ganor

Abstract
The Witten index of the ( 2 , 0 ) (2,0) -theory compactified on spaces of the form S 3 / Γ × S 2 S^{3}/\Gamma\times S^{2} , with a freely acting group Γ \Gamma , and with external string sources implemented via timelike surface operator insertions, is expressed in terms of Ray-Singer torsion of S 3 / Γ S^{3}/\Gamma and characters of irreducible representations of Γ \Gamma . We compute it explicitly for the Dicyclic groups Γ = Dic k \Gamma=\text{Dic}_{k} . The torsion and characters are generally irrational numbers, but they nicely combine to an integer index. Alternatively, the Witten index can be computed from Chern-Simons theory on S 2 S^{2} , and Ray-Singer torsion on S 3 / Dic k S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k} is thus computable from Chern-Simons theory. The matching of the Witten index calculated by these dual approaches reveals new details about the partition function of the ( 2 , 0 ) (2,0) -theory with surface operators.

. The prescription involves splitting the integration over
c,\bar{c}
modes into contributions from the isotropy group
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and the orthogonal complement of
\text{Ker}\,d_{A}
. Using the nilpotency of
d_{A}
, the space of adjoint-valued 0-forms
\Omega^{0}(X_{5},\mathfrak{su}(2))
can be Hodge decomposed as

\displaystyle\Omega^{0}(X_{5},\mathfrak{su}(2))=\text{Ker}\,d_{A}\oplus\text{Im}\,d_{A}^{\dagger}=\text{Lie}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\oplus(\text{Ker}\,d_{A})^{\perp},

(33)

where the second equality uses the fact that
(\text{Ker}\,d_{A})^{\perp}=\text{Im}\,d_{A}^{\dagger}
. The integral over
\text{Lie}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, where the integrand is independent of these modes, yields the volume of the isotropy group
\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
. The remaining integral is then restricted to the orthogonal component
(\text{Ker}\,d_{A})^{\perp}
, effectively removing the zero modes from the determinant. In summary, we replace
\det(\Delta_{A}^{0})
with:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})}\det\left(d_{A}^{\dagger}d_{A}\big|_{(\text{Ker}\,d_{A})^{\perp}}\right).

(34)

We still need to compute
\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, which is the volume of the subgroup of constant gauge transformations
\mathcal{H}_{A}
that preserve
A
.
\mathcal{H}_{A}
is isomorphic to a subgroup
H_{A}
of the gauge group SU
(2)
, but its volume should be calculated using a different metric from that of SU
(2)
, and as a result it depends on the volume of the manifold
\text{vol}(X_{5})
. To see this, we will study how
\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
varies under rescaling of
\text{vol}(X_{5})
.
\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
is calculated using a metric on the tangent space of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, which is the space of covariantly constant adjoint-valued
0
-forms
\varepsilon
on
X_{5}
, whose norm is given by

\int_{X_{5}}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}\sqrt{g}d^{5}x,

where
\|\varepsilon\|^{2}
is the local Killing form on
\mathfrak{su}(2)
, and
\sqrt{g}d^{5}x
is the volume form on
X_{5}
.
Thus, the norm of
\varepsilon
scales like a factor of
\text{vol}(X_{5})^{1/2}
. Consequently,
\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\text{vol}(X_{5})^{\dim H_{A}/2}\text{vol}(H_{A}).

(35)

For the trivial flat connection, the isotropy group is SU
(2)
, as all gauge transformations preserve the connection. For nontrivial abelian flat connections, the isotropy group is the maximal abelian subgroup of SU
(2)
, which corresponds to the maximal torus U
(1)
. For non-abelian flat connections, such as those associated with the dihedral group Dic
k
, the isotropy group reduces to the center of SU
(2)
, which is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

The second type of zero modes we must address are the zero modes of physical (non-ghost) fields. We have seen near (
27
), from the study of the nontrivial cohomology groups of the universal cover of
X_{5}
, that only the
0
-form and
2
-form fields can have zero modes. Specifically, in the case of abelian flat connections we have fermionic zero modes associated with the 0-form
\rho
and the 2-form
\chi
. The standard approach to address such zero modes is to insert BRST-invariant operators into the path integral, which absorb the zero modes, leading to a nonzero correlation function. However, the BRST transformations of
\rho
and
\chi
do not allow for simple BRST invariant operators that can soak up these modes. More importantly, there is no clear physical intuition about what types of more complicated BRST-invariant observables might be constructed to achieve this.
Without any operator insertions, the partition function vanishes identically for all abelian flat connections, since those have fermionic zero modes. For example, when
X_{5}=S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k}\times S^{2}
, all flat connections are abelian, as discussed in §
2
. It is not hard to check that loop effects cannot remove these zero modes. In the case where
X_{5}=S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}\times S^{2}
, however, non-abelian flat connections do exist. For those non-abelian flat connections, the zero modes are lifted because there are no covariantly constant 0-forms or 2-forms. For example, if a covariantly constant
\rho
satisfying
d_{A}\rho=0
existed, its value
\rho(p)
at some point
p\in X_{5}
would have to commute with the holonomy group of
A
, which is isomorphic to a Dic
k
subgroup of SU
(2)
, and no elements of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
are invariant under the entire group. With the zero modes removed, we can proceed to compute the partition function.

As first observed by Schwarz in
[
70
]
and later extended by Witten in
[
71
]
, the partition function for certain topological gauge theories can be computed using the Ray-Singer analytic torsion. This method applies to cases where the gauge fields define flat connections
A
on a bundle
E
over the manifold
X_{5}
. In particular, the Ray-Singer torsion is related to the volume of the moduli space of flat connections. The torsion
\tau(X_{5},A)
on a smooth
d
-dimensional manifold
M
is a topological invariant that is defined as

\displaystyle\tau(M,A)=\prod_{p=0}^{d}(\text{det}^{\prime}\Delta_{A}^{p})^{-(-1)^{p}\frac{p}{2}},

(36)

where
\det^{\prime}
indicates that zero modes of the twisted Laplacians were removed. However, this approach in computing the partition function holds only when flat connections are both irreducible and isolated. In our case, the non-abelian flat connections are indeed isolated and irreducible, but the abelian flat connections are isolated but not irreducible, since their isotropy group is not in the center of the gauge group as discussed above. In the latter case, even though it does not contribute to the partition function because of the zero modes discussed above, in order to compute correlation functions (with the zero modes absorbed) one has to introduce a metric-dependent factor, leading to the modified torsion
[
72
]

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}(X_{5},A)=(\text{vol}(X_{5}))^{\dim H_{A}}\tau(X_{5},A),

(37)

where
\tau(X_{5},A)
depends solely on the topology of
X_{5}
.

Simplifying the expression for the partition function (
28
), we find:

\displaystyle Z(X_{5})={\mathcal{C}}\sum_{A}\frac{1}{\text{vol}(\mathcal{H}_{A})}\frac{\det^{\prime\frac{5}{4}}\Delta_{A}^{0}\det^{\prime\frac{1}{4}}\Delta_{A}^{2}}{\det^{\prime\frac{3}{4}}\Delta_{A}^{1}},

(38)

where
{\mathcal{C}}
is a normalization constant which usually is fixed by imposing that trivial flat connection gives partition function equal to unity. (See also
[
73
]
for a recent discussion of normalization of the measure in the context of localization.) Since for us trivial flat connection does not contribute, we fix this normalization to be
{\mathcal{C}}=1
using a different argument that will be presented in §
5
. In our case, only non-abelian flat connections
A
are of interest,
\dim H_{A}=0
in (
35
) (since
H_{A}
is finite), and there are no zero modes, so
\det^{\prime}
can be replaced with
\det
.
Comparing (
38
) and (
36
), we finally arrive at:

\displaystyle Z(X_{5})={\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\text{nonab $A$}}\frac{\sqrt{\tau(X_{5},A)}}{|H_{A}|},

(39)

where the (finite) sum is taken over all allowed (non-abelian) inequivalent flat connections. The metric dependence, in the torsion definition for non-irreducible flat connections, cancels with the corresponding factor from (
35
), leaving the partition function expressed in terms of topological invariants.

When
X_{5}
is a product manifold with a rank 1 gauge bundle defined over it, this result can be further simplified. If
M
and
N
are oriented compact manifolds without boundary, and
N
is simply connected, the torsion of the product manifold satisfies
[
46
,
74
]
:

\displaystyle\tau(M\times N,A)=\tau(M,A)^{\chi(N)}\tau(N)^{\chi(M)},

(40)

where
\chi
is the Euler characteristic. Applying this to the case
X_{5}=S^{3}/\Gamma\times S^{2}
, we find that the partition function evaluates to

\displaystyle Z(S^{3}/\Gamma\times S^{2})={\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\text{nonab $A$}}\frac{\tau(S^{3}/\Gamma,A)}{|H_{A}|},

(41)

since
\chi(S^{2})=2
and the Euler characteristic of the odd-dimensional manifold
S^{3}/\Gamma
vanishes. This represents a significant simplification: to study the partition function in five dimensions, we only need to compute the torsion of the three-dimensional manifold
S^{3}/\Gamma
over all admissible choices of flat SU
(2)
connections
A
. In particular, we only need to study the finite number of flat connections which are classified by group homomorphisms
\Gamma\rightarrow
SU
(2)
[or
\Gamma\rightarrow\text{SO}(3)
], up to conjugation. In the next section, we compute these torsions explicitly.

4
Ray-Singer Torsion for Spherical Orbifolds

In
[
75
]
and
[
76
]
, it was shown that for a closed manifold, the Ray-Singer analytic torsion is equal to Reidemeister torsion, the latter being computed using functional determinants of boundary operators on a co-chain complex. The Reidemeister torsion for the quotient space
S^{2n+1}/\Gamma
was computed in
[
45
]
and later used for topological field theories on quotient spaces in
[
77
]
.

We start with a formula for Reidemeister torsion on a
d
-dimensional manifold
M
given in
[
75
]
:

\displaystyle\log\tau(M,\mathbf{1})=\sum\limits_{p=0}^{d}(-1)^{p+1}\left(\log V_{p}(M)+\log O_{p}\right),

(42)

where
\mathbf{1}
indicates that we compute the torsion for a trivial representation of the fundamental group, or equivalently a trivial flat connection. In this formula
O_{p}
denotes

\displaystyle O_{p}=|\text{Tor}\,H^{p}(M,\mathbb{Z})|,

(43)

which is the cardinality of the torsion part of the integer cohomology group. The
\log V_{p}
factors vanish if there are no zero modes, which is the only case we are interested in. They are related to the volume dependent part that appears in (
37
), and we kept them for completeness. We will now present their values for a specific example, which is a quotient space
M=S^{d}/\Gamma
(for any dimension
d
, but for us only
d=3
is relevant). The only non zero Betti numbers are
b_{0}=b_{d}=1
and we find
V_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{vol}(M)}}
and
V_{d}=\sqrt{\text{vol}(M)}
(which are the norms of a 0-form and a harmonic top form).

4.1
Ray-Singer Torsion for
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{k}

We begin by presenting results for the Ray-Singer torsion for
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{k}
since these results will later be used to compute the torsion that we actually need for
\Gamma=\text{Dic}_{k}
. Choosing
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{k}
, for an odd dimensional Lens space

for

, since

\displaystyle\text{Tor}\,H^{p}(M,\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}_{k},\;\text{for}\;0<p<d.

(44)

\displaystyle H^{i}(S^{2n+1}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\mathbb{Z})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{Z}&\text{for $i=0,2n+1$,}\\

\displaystyle\log\tau(S^{2n+1}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\mathbf{1})=\log{\left[V(S^{2n+1}/\mathbb{Z}_{k})\right]}-n\log{k}.

(48)

In general, one can use representation theory to argue

\displaystyle\log{\tau}(S^{3})=\log{\tau}(S^{3}/\Gamma,\mathbf{1})+\sum_{\rho\notin\mathbf{1}}\dim(\rho)\log\tau(S^{3}/\Gamma,\rho),

where
\rho
counts inequivalent nontrivial representations of
\Gamma
.
4
4

4

has been originally computed by Ray in

. Ray gave a formula for the torsion for any orbifold

acts freely. An improved approach was used in
[

where the generator

is represented by

\rho_{m}(\mu)=e^{\frac{2\pi im}{k}},

(50)

with representations
\rho_{m}
labeled by an integer

, we get nontrivial inequivalent representations of

\displaystyle\log\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\rho_{m})=\frac{2}{k}\sum\limits_{p=0}^{k-1}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k-1}e^{2\pi imp/k}e^{-2\pi ilp/k}\log\left[2\sin\left(\frac{\pi l}{k}\right)\right].

(51)

Here,

labels conjugacy classes of
\Gamma

. Summing over

fixes

\displaystyle\log\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\rho_{m})=2\log\left[2\sin\left(\frac{\pi m}{k}\right)\right].

(52)

As a quick check on formula (

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho\notin\mathbf{1}}\log\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\rho)=2\sum\limits_{m=1}^{k-1}\log\left[2\sin\left(\frac{\pi m}{k}\right)\right]=2\log\prod\limits_{m=1}^{k-1}\left[2\sin\left(\frac{\pi m}{k}\right)\right]=2\log{k}.

This can be seen to match (

All our fields are in the adjoint representation

\displaystyle\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{m}=\rho_{2m}\oplus\mathbf{1}\oplus\rho_{-2m},

Therefore, for the

\displaystyle\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\lambda_{m})=\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\rho_{2m})\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\mathbf{1})\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k},\rho_{-2m})=\frac{16}{k}\sin^{4}\left(\frac{2\pi m}{k}\right),

4.2
Ray-Singer Torsion for
\Gamma=\text{Dic}_{k}

Now, we can move on to the case of interest
\Gamma=\text{Dic}_{k}

42

\displaystyle\log\tau(S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k},\mathbf{1})=\log\left[{V(S^{3})/4k}\right]-{2}\log{2},

(56)

where we also used the fact that the order of Dic

is

that are invariant under
\Gamma

\Gamma

can be represented as the union of a cyclic subgroup isomorphic to

cyclic subgroups isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{4}

[

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
subgroups. Overall, one finds
[

78
]

\displaystyle\log\tau(S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k},\mathbf{1})=\frac{1}{4k}\left[2k\log\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2k},\mathbf{1})+4k\log\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{4},\mathbf{1})-2k\log\tau(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2},\mathbf{1})\right],

which recovers (
56

For nontrivial representations of Dic

[

where

where

We will now derive a closed formula for

for

The Witten indices that we found in (

The Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind,

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle T_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=2xT_{n}(x)-T_{n-1}(x),

\displaystyle U_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,

(93)

\displaystyle U_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=2x,

\displaystyle=2xU_{n}(x)-U_{n-1}(x).

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{5}(X)

base of the fibration as
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with coordinate
z=z_{1}/z_{2}
(in the patch
z_{2}\neq 0
). Note that
z
is invariant under
r
. The generator
s
then acts as

s:z\longrightarrow-\frac{1}{\bar{z}},

and on the fiber it acts by an orientation reversal.
A fundamental region of the action of
s
on
z
is
|z|\leq 1
.

We now parameterize
S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2k}
by
\theta,{\phi},{\varphi}
through

z_{1}=e^{i({\phi}+\frac{{\varphi}}{2k})}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2},\qquad z_{2}=e^{i\frac{{\varphi}}{2k}}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\qquad(0\leq{\phi},{\varphi}<2\pi,\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi),

(96)

so that
z=e^{i{\phi}}\tan\tfrac{\theta}{2}
, and we can consider
{\varphi}
as a coordinate on the
S^{1}
fiber. Then,
s
acts as

\displaystyle s:{\phi}\rightarrow{\phi}+\pi,\qquad{\varphi}\rightarrow-{\varphi}-2k{\phi},\qquad\theta\rightarrow\pi-\theta.

(97)

Note that the action on the base (
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
) of the fibration reverses the orientation. The fundamental region
|z|\leq 1
, with the identification
\theta\sim\theta+\pi
, becomes the real projective space
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
. The metric on
S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
can now be written as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left\{d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}\left[d{\varphi}+2k\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)d\phi\right]^{2}\right\}.

(98)

6.2
Gauge Bundles on
\mathbb{RP}^{2}

To proceed with the plan of dimensionally reducing the
(2,0)
-theory on
S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
to 5d SYM on
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, and then further shrinking
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
to zero, we will now study flat
SU(2)
connections on
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
. More precisely, by flat connections, we mean locally trivial
SU(2)
gauge fields
A
on the disc parameterized by
\theta\leq\pi/2
and
0\leq{\phi}<2\pi
[the coordinates defined in (
96
)], with suitable boundary conditions that identify antipodal points parameterized by
{\phi}
and
{\phi}+\pi
on the disc’s boundary at
\theta=\pi/2
[see (
97
)].

The orientation reversal of the Hopf fiber, which supplements the identification of antipoldal points in (
97
), amounts to charge conjugation on fields, which acts as minus the transpose on hermitian matrices, like the components of the gauge field
A
. Thus, the components
A_{\phi}
and
A_{\theta}
of the 5d gauge field
A
satisfy the following boundary conditions at
\theta=\pi/2
:

A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi)=-A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t},\qquad A_{\theta}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi)=A_{\theta}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t},

(99)

where the
(+)
sign on the
A_{\theta}
component is due to the combination of the
(-)
sign in the charge conjugation
-(\cdots)^{t}
and the
(-)
sign in the orientation reversal of the
\theta
direction in (
97
). We can, in principle, include an additional
{\phi}
-dependent gauge transformation on the RHS of each equation in (
99
). Such a gauge transformation can introduce a nontrivial ‘t Hooft magnetic flux, but other than that, its effects can be absorbed in a redefinition of the gauge field [we will expand on this point below (
107
)]. Which combination of ‘t Hooft magnetic fluxes is allowed will be addressed in §
6.5
, but for the time being we will keep all the options.

We will require
A_{\phi}
and
A_{\theta}
to be continuous for
0<\theta\leq\pi/2
, but allow the holonomy around the origin (
\theta=0
) to be
\pm{\mathbf{I}}\in\text{SU}(2)
, with the
(-)
sign corresponding to nontrivial ‘t Hooft magnetic flux. We will show below that, on the disc, such locally trivial gauge fields that satisfy the boundary conditions (
99
) are characterized by a constant element
{\Omega}\in\text{SU}(2)
which satisfies

{\Omega}^{\star}{\Omega}=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\mathbf{I}},

(100)

where the integers
{\nu}\in\{0,1\}
are known as the ‘t Hooft magnetic flux along
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, and
{\mathbf{I}}
is the identity matrix. Roughly speaking,
{\Omega}
is the holonomy of a gauge field along a loop that represents the nontrivial element of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{RP}^{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and represents the leftover global data that cannot be gauged away. A precise definition will be given in (
105
).

Let
{\Phi}(\theta,\phi)
be any adjoint-valued field in the theory. We can choose a gauge that locally trivializes the gauge field along
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, and in this gauge the components of the covariant derivative
\partial_{\mu}{\Phi}-i[A_{\mu},{\Phi}]
reduce to
\partial_{\mu}{\Phi}
, for
\mu=\theta
or
\mu={\phi}
. In this gauge, the field
{\Phi}
satisfies the boundary condition

{\Omega}^{-1}{\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\phi+\pi){\Omega}=\pm{\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\phi)^{t}.

(101)

The
(\pm)
sign is determined by the action of the orientation reversal of the Hopf fiber on the field and by the action of orientation reversal of the
\theta
direction. For example, for the components of the gauge field that are not along
\theta
, the sign is
(-)
; while for
A_{\theta}
, the sign is
(+)
. Equation (
101
) can be taken as an implicit definition of
{\Omega}
for now, and more details will be presented in §
6.3
.

We can consider solutions
{\Omega}_{1},{\Omega}_{2}\in\text{SU}(2)
of (
100
) as gauge equivalent if there exists
\Lambda\in\text{SU}(2)
such that

{\Omega}_{2}=\Lambda^{-1}{\Omega}_{1}\Lambda^{\star}.

(102)

Furthermore, when the gauge group is
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, we will additionally consider
{\Omega}
and
-{\Omega}
as equivalent.

We may investigate the solutions to (
100
) by choosing a Lie algebra basis given by the standard Pauli matrices
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
. For
{\nu}=0
, all solutions to (
100
) are equivalent, by (
102
), to one of the two solutions
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
.
For
{\nu}=1
, all solutions are equivalent to
\pm i
multiple of a Pauli matrix, say,
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
.
For gauge group
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, the
\pm
signs can be dropped, and there is one solution up to equivalence for each ‘t Hooft magnetic flux.

We will now define
{\Omega}
more precisely, and derive (
100
) and (
102
). These details are not needed for the rest of the paper, and readers who accept (
100
) and (
102
) can skip to §
6.4
.

6.3
Definition of
{\Omega}
and Its Properties

We begin with the boundary conditions (
99
). If
A
is a flat connection on
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, we can find a local trivialization such that

A_{\phi}=i{\Upsilon}^{-1}\partial_{\phi}{\Upsilon},\qquad{\Upsilon}(\theta,{\phi})\in\text{SU}(2),\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2},\qquad 0\leq{\phi}<2\pi,

(103)

where
{\Upsilon}
is the gauge transformation that trivializes
A
. To allow for the possibility of a nontrivial ‘t Hooft magnetic flux along
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, we remove
\theta=0
and modify the condition of
2\pi
periodicity in
{\phi}
to

{\Upsilon}(\theta,\phi)=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\Upsilon}(\theta,\phi+2\pi),\qquad{\nu}\in\{0,1\},

(104)

where
{\nu}
is the magnetic flux.
From now on we consider
{\Upsilon}(\theta,\phi)
only at
\theta=\pi/2
and omit the argument
\theta
, for simplicity.
From (
99
), we find

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi}\left[{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}\right]=\partial_{\phi}{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}+{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)\partial_{\phi}{\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)A_{\phi}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}-i{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)A_{\phi}({\phi})^{t}{\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}=0,

and therefore
{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}
is independent of
\phi
and we set

{\Omega}={\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t}.

(105)

Since
{\Omega}
is a constant element of
\text{SU}(2)
, we have at
\theta=\pi/2
:

{\Omega}={\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}={\Upsilon}({\phi}+2\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{t}=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\Upsilon}(\phi){\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{t},

where we have used (
104
).
Therefore,

{\Omega}^{\star}{\Omega}=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{\star}{\Upsilon}({\phi})^{\dagger}{\Upsilon}({\phi}){\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{t}=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\mathbf{I}},

(106)

which proves (
100
).
Note that (
103
) does not require
A
to be a flat connection, since we only used the
{\phi}
component, and
{\nu}
is a topological quantum number of the field configuration.

We can modify (
99
) by allowing another gauge transformation
{\Pi}
defined in the vicinity of
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
,

{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)+i{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}\partial_{\phi}{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)=-A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t},

(107)

and in the case where the gauge group is
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, we will allow

{\Pi}({\phi}+2\pi)=(-1)^{{\mu}}{\Pi}({\phi}),

(108)

for some
{\mu}\in\{0,1\}
, but we will see below (
111
) that
{\mu}=0
is required.
From (
107
) we also find

{\Pi}({\phi})^{-1}A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}){\Pi}({\phi})+i{\Pi}({\phi})^{-1}\partial_{\phi}{\Pi}({\phi})=-A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}-\pi)^{t},

(109)

and plugging this back into (
107
) we get

\displaystyle{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)+i{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}\partial_{\phi}{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)=-A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t}

\displaystyle=-\left[-{\Pi}({\phi})A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}-\pi)^{t}{\Pi}({\phi})^{-1}-i\partial_{\phi}{\Pi}({\phi}){\Pi}({\phi})^{-1}\right]^{t}

\displaystyle={\Pi}({\phi})^{\star}A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi})^{t}+i{\Pi}({\phi})^{\star}\partial_{\phi}{\Pi}({\phi})^{t}.

(110)

If this is to hold for an arbitrary gauge field
A_{\phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi)
, we must require

{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)=(-1)^{{\lambda}}{\Pi}({\phi})^{t},\qquad\text{for some ${\lambda}\in\{0,1\}$},

(111)

so that
{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}=(-1)^{{\lambda}}{\Pi}({\phi})^{\star}
.
Equation (
111
) now implies that
{\mu}=0
in (
108
).

We now trivialize
A
again, locally, as in (
103
).
Now, from (
107
) we find that
{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}
is independent of
{\phi}
and we set

{\Omega}={\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t}.

(112)

We will now compute
{\Omega}^{\star}{\Omega}
. Since
{\Omega}
is a constant element of
\text{SU}(2)
, we have at
\theta=\pi/2
:

{\Omega}={\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi})^{t}={\Upsilon}({\phi}+2\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+2\pi){\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{t}=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\Upsilon}({\phi}){\Pi}({\phi}){\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{t},

and therefore

{\Omega}^{\star}{\Omega}=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{\star}{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{\star}{\Upsilon}({\phi})^{\dagger}{\Upsilon}({\phi}){\Pi}({\phi}){\Upsilon}({\phi}+\pi)^{t}=(-1)^{{\nu}+{\lambda}}{\mathbf{I}}.

(113)

Let
{\Phi}(\theta,{\phi})
be any adjoint-valued field in the theory, which is required to satisfy twisted boundary conditions similar to (
107
)

{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}{\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)={\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t},

(114)

and after the gauge transformation by
{\Upsilon}
, these are modified to

{\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)^{-1}{\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi)^{-1}{\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Pi}({\phi}+\pi)={\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t}{\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t}{\Upsilon}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{\star},

which reduces to

{\Omega}^{-1}{\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi}+\pi){\Omega}={\Phi}(\tfrac{\pi}{2},{\phi})^{t}.

(115)

[Generally, when we allow a nontrivial action of orientation reversal on the fields, we get (
101
).]

Overall, we have seen that a flat
\text{SU}(2)
connection
A
defines a constant
{\Omega}\in\text{SU}(2)
via (
112
) [in terms of
{\Upsilon}
defined in (
103
)], and for gauge group
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
we should project
{\Omega}
to
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
For a constant
\Lambda\in\text{SU}(2)
[or
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
],
{\Upsilon}
and
\Lambda{\Upsilon}
define the same
A
in (
103
), and so we can consider
{\Omega}_{1},{\Omega}_{2}\in\text{SU}(2)
as gauge equivalent, in this context, if

{\Omega}_{2}=\Lambda^{-1}{\Omega}_{1}\Lambda^{\star}.

(116)

We now show that, up to gauge equivalence,
{\Omega}\in\text{SU}(2)
uniquely defines a flat connection
A
that satisfies the boundary conditions (
99
), where uniqueness is up to the obvious fact that
\pm{\Omega}
define the same flat connection. What we need to show is that there exists a continuous
{\Upsilon}(\theta,{\phi})
that satisfies (
105
) and for which (
103
) satisfies the boundary conditions (
99
). We set
{\Upsilon}(\pi/2,0)={\mathbf{I}}
and
{\Upsilon}(\pi/2,\pi)={\Omega}
, which satisfies (
105
) at
\phi=0
. We then choose an arbitrary
{\Upsilon}(\pi/2,{\phi})
in the range
0\leq{\phi}\leq\pi
that equals
{\mathbf{I}}
at
{\phi}=0
and equals
{\Omega}
at
{\phi}=\pi
, and we extend it to the range
\pi\leq{\phi}\leq 2\pi
by using (
105
), setting
{\Upsilon}(\pi/2,{\phi})={\Omega}{\Upsilon}(\pi/2,{\phi}-\pi)^{\star}
.
Using (
100
), at
{\phi}=2\pi
we find that
{\Upsilon}(\pi/2,2\pi)=(-1)^{{\nu}}{\mathbf{I}}
. If
{\nu}=0
we can now extend
{\Upsilon}
continuously to the entire disk
0\leq\theta<\pi/2
, using the fact that
\text{SU}(2)
is simply connected. And if
{\nu}=1
we can extend
{\Upsilon}
to
0<\theta<\pi/2
keeping the boundary condition
{\Upsilon}(\theta,2\pi)=-{\Upsilon}(\theta,0)
, which corresponds to a connection
A
with nontrivial ‘t Hooft magnetic flux. Similarly, since
\text{SU}(2)
is simply connected,
{\Pi}(\pi/2,{\phi})
that satisfies (
108
) with
{\mu}=0
, can be extended continuously to
0\leq\theta<\pi/2
. We can then gauge transform all the fields by this continuously defined
{\Pi}
on the disk. Since
{\Pi}
also satisfies (
111
), such a gauge transformation will eliminate
{\Pi}
entirely from (
107
).
Thus, the flat connections are characterized by the constant
{\Omega}\in\text{SU}(2)
up to gauge equivalence and
{\Omega}\sim-{\Omega}
.

6.4
The 5d Chern-Simons Term

In general, the
A_{1}

(2,0)
-theory compactified on a circle fibration over a base space
Y_{5}
which is a Riemannian manifold equipped with a curvature
\omega
[which is a
2
-form on
Y_{5}
so that the first Chern class is
c_{1}=[\omega]\in H^{2}(Y_{5},\mathbb{Z})
] reduces to
\mathrm{SU}(2)
SYM on
Y_{5}
with an extra Chern-Simons term proportional to
[
86
]

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{Y_{5}}\omega\wedge\tr(A\wedge dA+\tfrac{2}{3}A\wedge A\wedge A).

(117)

As a quick warmup, we will investigate the form of this Chern-Simons term in the case of
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}\times(S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2k})
.
Since the lens space
S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2k}\cong L(2k,1)
is a circle fibration over
S^{2}
, we take the base manifold to be
Y_{5}=\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}\times S^{2}
, where the second
S^{2}
factor is the base of the Hopf fibration
L(2k,1)\rightarrow S^{2}
.
Let
{\varepsilon}
be the area
2
-form on the second
S^{2}
factor, normalized so that
\int_{S^{2}}{\varepsilon}=1
. Thus
\omega=2k{\varepsilon}
.

All this was for
S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{2k}
, but to get to
S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
we need to further quotient by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
that is generated by the inversion
s
in (
97
). This quotient does not introduce any uncontrollable fixed points and replaces
Y_{5}
in (
117
) with
Y_{5}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}\times(S^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. We can identify
S^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with the disc
0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2
, so that the Chern-Simons term becomes

\frac{k}{2\pi}\int_{Y_{5}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}}{\varepsilon}\wedge\tr(A\wedge dA+\tfrac{2}{3}A\wedge A\wedge A)\,.

(118)

Note that
\omega
is invariant under the action of
s
, because the first Chern class of the fibration changes sign under orientation reversal of the fiber, and also
{\varepsilon}
changes sign under orientation reversal of the base. In (
118
), we have slightly abused the notation, since the
2
-form
{\varepsilon}
cannot be integrated over the non-orientable
S^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, and what we actually mean by the integral in (
118
) is an integral over
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
times the disc
\theta<\pi/2
, so that
\int{\varepsilon}
over the disc evaluates to
1/2
.

Alternatively, the Chern-Simons term (
118
) arises from the effect of the shift by
-2k{\phi}
in the action of
s
on
{\varphi}
in (
97
). In general, a translation along the Hopf fiber corresponds in 5d SYM to the symmetry associated with the instanton number as the conserved charge
[
86
,
87
,
88
,
89
,
18
]
, and so when we construct
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
from the disk
\theta<\pi/2
by gluing the arc of the boundary with, say,
0\leq{\phi}<\pi
to the arc with
\pi\leq{\phi}<2\pi
, we must insert a term

\frac{k}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}d{\phi}\wedge\tr\left(A\wedge dA+\frac{2}{3}A\wedge A\wedge A\right),

(119)

along the cut.

6.5
3d Chern-Simons and Gauge Bundles

We now shrink
S^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\mathbb{RP}^{2}
to zero and reduce the Chern-Simons term (
119
) from 5d to 3d.
Let
A
now denote only the 3d part of the gauge field, along
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
, and we assume that it is independent of the point in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
i.e. we keep the zero mode.
According to the discussion below (
101
), this means that

{\Omega}^{-1}A{\Omega}^{\star}=-A^{t}.

(120)

We will now substitute the solutions of
{\Omega}
from §
6.2
.
For
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
, every element of the
\mathfrak{su}(2)
Lie algebra satisfies an equation similar to (
120
), and therefore the 3d gauge algebra is the full Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
.
For
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
, on the other hand, the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
that solves (
120
) is the C
artan subalgebra generated by
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
, and the 3d gauge algebra is therefore
\mathfrak{u}(1)
.

The 5d term (
119
) reduces to a 3d Chern-Simons action,

\frac{k}{4\pi}\int\tr\left(A\wedge dA+\frac{2}{3}A\wedge A\wedge A\right),

(121)

where
\tr
is in the
2
-dimensional representation. We will now proceed separately with the two cases
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
and
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
, starting with the former.

The term (
119
), for
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
, describes 3d
\mathfrak{su}(2)
Chern-Simons at level
k
, but the level is shifted because of a well-known
1
-loop effect due to the fermionic degrees of freedom. Massive gluinos join the gauge fields of the level
k
Chern-Simons term to form a supersymmetric Chern-Simons theory with
\mathcal{N}=2
SUSY. For
\mathfrak{su}(2)
gauge group, after integrating out the gluinos at low-energy, the supersymmetric theory at level
k
becomes pure Chern-Simons theory at level
(k-2)

[
90
,
91
,
92
,
93
,
94
,
28
]
. The shift in level occurs because of a mismatch in the number of positive and negative eigenvalues of the Dirac operator. For completeness, we will show the calculation of the index of the Dirac operator explicitly in Appendix
A
, which can be read after the fermions are discussed in §
6.7
.

For the case
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
, the gauge group is
\text{U}(1)
, and the
\tr
in (
121
) gives an extra factor of
2
. This time there is no
1
-loop contribution to the level, and so the total level is
2k
. We will parameterize the low-energy 3d gauge field by

A={\mathfrak{a}}{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}.

(122)

So far, we discussed the Lie algebras, and now we will discuss the Lie groups. Based on (
102
), the question is: for a given
{\Omega}
what is the group of
\Lambda\in\text{SU}(2)
that satisfies

\Lambda^{-1}{\Omega}\Lambda^{\star}=\pm{\Omega},

(123)

where we included a
(\pm)
to allow for
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge group?
For
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
, every
\Lambda\in\text{SU}(2)
satisfies
\Lambda^{-1}{\Omega}\Lambda^{\star}={\Omega}
, and the 3d gauge group is the full
\text{SU}(2)
. (We will discuss below the ‘t Hooft electric and magnetic flux restrictions.)

For
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
,

\Lambda=\Lambda_{1}(\alpha)\equiv{\mathbf{I}}\cos\alpha+i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}\sin\alpha,

for any
\alpha
, satisfies (
123
) with the
(+)
sign, while for the
(-)
sign in (
123
) we have

\Lambda=\Lambda_{2}(\alpha)\equiv i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{1}\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{3}.

(124)

If the 5d gauge group is
\text{SU}(2)
then the 3d gauge group is the set of
\Lambda_{1}(\alpha)
’s, which is isomorphic to
\text{U}(1)
. In this case, the 3d reduction of the gauge sector is simply
\text{U}(1)_{2k}
Chern-Simons theory. If the 5d gauge group is instead
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, then the 3d gauge group is the union of the
\Lambda_{1}(\alpha)
’s and
\Lambda_{2}(\alpha)
’s, which is equivalent to the a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
quotient of the semidirect product
\mathbb{Z}_{4}\ltimes\text{U}(1)
. The
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
factor in the last expression can be taken as the element
\Lambda_{2}(0)=i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{1}
. A constant gauge transformation
\Lambda=\Lambda_{2}(0)
acts on the 3d
\text{U}(1)
gauge field
{\mathfrak{a}}
in (
122
) as
{\mathfrak{a}}\rightarrow\Lambda^{-1}{\mathfrak{a}}\Lambda=-{\mathfrak{a}}
, and if
\text{U}(1)
is taken to be a Cartan subgroup of
\text{SU}(2)
, then we can identify the action of
\Lambda_{2}(0)
with the nontrivial element of the Weyl group. An additional
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
quotient is required to account for the relation
\Lambda_{2}(0)^{2}=\Lambda_{1}(\pi)
, and the resulting gauge group is therefore
[\mathbb{Z}_{4}\ltimes\text{U}(1)]/\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\text{Pin}^{-}(2)
.

What is left to discuss is which combination of electric and magnetic fluxes are allowed.
For this purpose it is useful to think about the 6d space
S^{1}\times S^{2}\times S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
as a
T^{2}
fibered over
S^{2}\times\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, where
T^{2}\cong S^{1}_{t}\times S^{1}_{H}
is the product of the time circle that we now temporarily denote by
S^{1}_{t}
, and the Hopf fiber that we temporarily denote by
S^{1}_{H}
. Then, the
(2,0)
-theory reduces locally to 4d
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM, and the difference between the dimensional reduction of §
5

Our next task is to reduce the 6d Surface operators introduced in §

times the

{\mathfrak{n}}

If we are in the
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}

{\mathfrak{n}}

\displaystyle\left(2\cos 2\oint{\mathfrak{a}}\right)^{\mathfrak{n}},

(127)

in the path integral, where we have used (

in (

6.7
5d Fermions

Next, we study the fermionic sector of the effective 3d field theory on
S^{1}\times S^{2}
in two steps.
In the present subsection we develop the 5d effective action, and in §
6.8
we will reduce the action to 3d on
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
with either
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
or
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}

{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}

Let us first reduce a free chiral 6d spinor
\Psi
on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}\times S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
to 5d, on the Hopf fiber.
The R-symmetry twist, as in §
3

(5)
. We therefore single out the
\mathbb{R}

S^{2}\times S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}

S^{1}\times S^{2}\times\mathbb{RP}^{2}

Let
{\Gamma}^{I}
(

(

The 6d spinor satisfies the chirality condition

and satisfy
{\gamma}^{12345}=1
. Now we twist so that the R-symmetry background gauge field will match the spin connection in directions

\Psi_{\alpha}^{a}
, with

an R-symmetry index, becomes
\Psi_{\alpha}^{\beta}
, which can be interpreted as a

{\gamma}^{\mu}

\Psi={\tilde{\rho}}{\mathbf{I}}+{\tilde{\psi}}_{\mu}{\gamma}^{\mu}+{\tilde{\chi}}_{\mu\nu}{\gamma}^{\mu\nu},

,

{\tilde{\chi}}_{\mu\nu}
are scalar, vector, and tensor 6d fermionic fields, but their components
\mu,\nu=1,\dots,5
are missing the time direction.
Note that in 5d,
{\gamma}^{\mu\nu\sigma}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\alpha\beta}{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}
, so there is no need for a

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle+\frac{2k}{{\mathfrak{b}}\sqrt{h}}\epsilon^{ijkl}{\tilde{\chi}}_{{\varphi}i}\partial_{j}{\tilde{\chi}}_{kl}+2{\tilde{\chi}}^{ij}\partial_{i}{\tilde{\psi}}_{j}+2k^{2}\left[\frac{1}{{\mathfrak{b}}^{2}}+\tan^{2}\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]{{\tilde{\chi}}_{\varphi}}{}^{i}\partial_{i}{\tilde{\psi}}_{\varphi}

6.8
3d Fermions

We now reduce from 5d to 3d by shrinking
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, and we need to separate the discussion of
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
[with 3d gauge group
\text{SU}(2)
or
\text{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
] from the discussion of
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
[with 3d gauge group
\text{Pin}^{-}(2)
or
\text{O}(2)
].

For
{\Omega}=\pm i{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
, it can be shown that the equations of motion of the Lagrangian (
132
) with boundary conditions (
133
)-(
134
) have no zero modes, but there is a mismatch between Kaluza-Klein modes with positive and negative masses that shift the Chern-Simons level from
k
to
(k-2)
. The details are left for Appendix
A
.

For
{\Omega}=\pm{\mathbf{I}}
, there is no level shift, but there is a pair of fermionic zero modes. The Witten index, however, does not vanish because the modes interact with the Chern-Simons gauge fields, as we will see below.
We begin by finding the modes of the fermionic fields on
S^{1}\times S^{2}\times\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, with boundary conditions (
133
)-(
134
), which survive the low-energy reduction to
S^{1}\times S^{2}
.
We separate the components to those proportional to
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
and those proportional to a linear combination of
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{3}
.
Applied to the field
{\tilde{\rho}}
, for example, the components proportional to
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
are antisymmetric matrices and satisfy the boundary conditions
{\tilde{\rho}}(p)=-{\tilde{\rho}}({\tilde{p}})
. They, therefore, do not survive the reduction to

. The components that do survive are proportional to a linear combination of
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{3}
, which does not commute with the 3d
\text{U}(1)
gauge field (which is proportional to
{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{2}
). The field
{\tilde{\rho}}
therefore reduces to a pair of charged fermionic scalars of charges
\pm 2
. Similarly, the components of
{\tilde{\chi}}_{ij}
and the components of
{\tilde{\psi}}_{i}
with
i,j
along
S^{2}
also reduce to charged fermions. The remaining components
{\tilde{\chi}}_{{\varphi}i}
,
{\tilde{\chi}}_{\theta i}
,
{\tilde{\chi}}_{{\varphi}\theta}
,
{\tilde{\chi}}_{{\phi}i}
,
{\tilde{\psi}}_{\varphi}
,
{\tilde{\psi}}_{\phi}
,

1

2
-forms on

, whether they are symmetric or antisymmetric matrices.

8
Conclusions and Further Directions

In this paper, we built on the work of Bak and Gustavsson
[
44
]
, combined with results of Chang and Dowker
[
78
]
on the Ray-Singer torsion of spherical orbifolds, to compute the Witten index of various systems obtained from the 6d (2,0)-theory associated to gauge group SU
(2)
. We compactified the 6d theory on 5-manifolds
X_{5}
constructed from spherical orbifolds with a SUSY preserving twist, and with possible insertions of surface operators playing the role of external sources. A connection between the values of the Ray-Singer torsion and the characters of representations of the orbifold groups, in terms of which the Witten index is expressed, ensures that the result is always an integer.

Our main example is for the manifold
X_{5}=S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}\times S^{2}
, where
\text{Dic}_{k}
is the dicyclic group (of order
4k
), and in this case the results for the Witten index also allow us to postulate conjectures regarding fusion relations among external string-like sources, represented by timelike surface operators. We also discussed
S^{5}/\mathbb{Z}_{k}
and
S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{k}\times S^{2}
, where the Witten index is zero, because of fermionic zero modes. Extensions to
S^{5}/\Gamma
and
S^{3}/\Gamma\times S^{2}
with other (non-abelian) finite orbifold groups
\Gamma
are possible future developments of this work.

Our initial strategy, which follows
[
44
]
, was to reduce the problem to a partition function of topologically twisted 5d SYM on
X_{5}
, containing gauge fields, bosonic and Grassmann odd 0-forms, 1-forms and 2-forms which participate in a 5d “Fermionic Chern Simons” term in the action. Supersymmetry ensures that the partition function localizes on gauge field configurations that are flat connections, and a study of fermionic zero modes shows that only irreducible non-abelian flat connections contribute. The simplest nontrivial case is then
X_{5}=S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}\times S^{2}
, since for
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
quotient, all representations are abelian. Using expressions developed in
[
44
]
for the one-loop contribution in terms of the Ray-Singer analytic torsion, we recast the partition function as a sum (
41
) over non-abelian representations of Dic
k
torsions (associated with a flat connection) times products of characters of group elements. We simplified the torsion results of
[
78
]
for
S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
to a compact expression (
63
), and we used it to confirm that the Witten index is indeed given by an integer.
Recurrence relations among the Witten indices for various numbers of timelike surface operator insertions lead to conjectural fusion rules for the external string sources associated with those operators. These relations are expressed as combinations of Chebyshev polynomials.

Moreover, we were able to rederive the same expressions for the Witten indices from a dual perspective that led to 3d Chern-Simons theory with two different sectors, labeled by an SU
(2)
element
{\Omega}
that determines boundary conditions on fields, and gauge groups that depend on the parity of
k
. Thus, the results obtained in this work provide a physical explanation for the existence of “nice” closed form expressions for the Ray-Singer analytic torsion for
S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
, and they follow from the dimensions of Hilbert spaces of Chern-Simons theory on
S^{2}
with quark sources, as computed in
[
2
]
. The fusion relations among surface operators then must follow from the basic fusion relations of Wilson loops in Chern-Simons theory, and can be expressed in terms of the
N_{ijk}
fusion coefficients and Verlinde’s formula
[
1
,
2
]
. This connection between Chern-Simons theory and analytic torsion on
S^{3}/\text{Dic}_{k}
has, as far as we know, not been reported before in the literature.

It is interesting to reflect on how the expression (
86
), derived from analytic torsion in §
5.2
, and the derivation of the same formula from Chern-Simons theory in §
6
are connected. The factor
\cos\left(\frac{2m\pi}{k}\right)
in (
86
) arises from a certain character of
\text{Dic}_{k}
in a representation labeled by
m
, while the same factor in §
6
can be traced back, via
[
2
]
, to an eigenvalue of a Wilson loop operator acting on ground states of
\text{SU}(2)
Chern-Simons theory on
T^{2}
. We therefore see that the connection hinges on the observation by Vafa and Witten
[
40
]
about the connection between the partition function of
\mathcal{N}=4
Super-Yang-Mills theory on
\mathbb{R}^{4}/\Gamma
and characters of an affine Lie algebra associated to the Dynkin diagram of
\Gamma
, which in turn was obtained through earlier results of Nakajima
[
95
,
96
]
. In particular, the number of ground states of Chern-Simons theory on
T^{2}
is related to the number of representations of
\Gamma
. (See also a recent proof by Kojima and Tachikawa
[
97
]
of the equality of the number of embeddings of
\Gamma
in
\text{SU}(n)
and in its Langlands dual group
\text{SU}(n)/\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, as well as other Langlands dual pairs.)

In the process of matching the results that were obtained in §
5
via analytic torsion with the results that were obtained in §
6
Chern-Simons theory, we were led to assume that a certain sector of the low-energy
\text{U}(1)
Chern-Simons theory with nontrivial ‘t Hooft magnetic flux should be counted with a
(-)
sign in the total Witten index [see (

{\mathfrak{m}}=1
on
S^{2}
to the sector without it, and there is no way to continuously deform the fields of the
{\nu}=0
[for which the solutions of (
100
) lead to a
\text{U}(1)
gauge group] to the fields of
{\nu}=1
, and so the explanation for this effect seems to originate in 6d.

Looking ahead, our analysis may be naturally extended in several directions. In particular, one could study the smooth orbifolds
X_{5}=S^{5}/\Gamma
for the non-abelian
\Gamma
’s that appear in (
8
). Such an extension is expected to involve similar techniques, namely the computation of the Ray-Singer torsion in the background of flat connections associated to representations of
\Gamma
. Our results can also be examined in the context of 4d
\mathcal{N}=2

\text{Class}\;\mathcal{S}
theories
[
23
,
24
]
, by first compactifying on
S^{2}
(with a topological twist), leading to a gapped 4d theory. In that case, the Witten index gives information about the nontrivial topological nature of this gapped system.

We have worked with an SU
(2)
gauge group, because for SU
(N)
with
N\geq 3
the fermionic 0-forms will always have zero modes. This is because there are no irreducible representations of
\text{Dic}_{k}
of dimension higher than
2

\mathbf{R}
of
\text{Dic}_{k}
there are nontrivial elements of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(N)
(in which the 0-form takes values) that are invariant under the adjoint action of
\text{Dic}_{k}
(embedded in SU
(N)
via
\mathbf{R}
).

As another possible extension, one may consider more general surface operators
[
98
]
. It would be interesting to find explicit operators that have nontrivial matrix elements between different ground states, and a natural candidate would be a surface operator wrapped on
S^{2}
. It would also be interesting to explore the effect of the braiding group on the Wilson loops of the effective 3d theory, although the structure might not be stable under coupling constant deformation, and it might not be visible in the dual picture with the analytic torsion. In general, such studies could reveal a richer structure in the spectrum of BPS states and their associated indices.
