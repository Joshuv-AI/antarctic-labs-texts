Synthesis of azole-fused thiazinethiones using a carbon disulfide surrogate via C–N/C–S tandem coupling and cyclization under reusable magnetic Cu-MOF-74 catalysis
Authors: Thi Hong Hoang, Quynh Nhu Tran, Xuan Tu Nguyen, Do Minh Hoang Vo, Pham Duy Quang Dao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An operationally advantageous catalytic protocol for the synthesis of benzo[4,5]imidazo- or imidazo[1,2-c][1,3]thiazinethiones, a class of compounds holding significant potential for biological and pharmacological applications, was reported. Utilizing magnetic Cu-MOF-74 as a reusable heterogeneous catalyst, a sequential C–N/C–S coupling and intramolecular cyclization strategy was developed using a carbon disulfide surrogate that avoids direct handling of CS2. The substrate scope was broad, accommodating a variety of electronic and structural variations, including electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, alongside complex cyclic and alkyl-substituted motifs. Furthermore, evaluation against MCF-7 breast carcinoma cells demonstrated that these synthetic compounds exhibited notable anti-cancer activity, with selected derivatives showing potent concentration-dependent growth inhibition.

Introduction

Benzimidazole- and imidazole-fused heterocycles are widely recognized as privileged N-containing scaffolds in medicinal chemistry due to their ability to confer unique electronic and hydrogen-bonding capabilities.1–8 The incorporation of these nuclei into fused polycyclic systems increases molecular rigidity and modulates physicochemical profiles, thereby enabling effective interactions with a broad range of biological targets and enhancing binding affinity, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic behavior.9 Consequently, these derivatives have attracted considerable attention for decades, exhibiting a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities. Numerous studies highlight their significant anticancer and cytotoxic effects against various tumor cell lines, alongside strong antimicrobial activity, including potent antifungal and antibacterial efficacy against microorganisms such as Candida albicans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Escherichia coli.10,11 Beyond antimicrobial and antiviral properties, these compounds demonstrate notable anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antioxidant, anticoagulant, and antidiabetic activities, as well as enzyme-inhibitory functions.12–19 In addition, thiocarbamate and dithiocarbamate derivatives represent another important class of sulfur-containing compounds with diverse biological and pharmacological properties. These systems have been extensively investigated owing to their remarkable anticancer, antimicrobial, enzyme-inhibitory, and antioxidant activities.20–25 In particular, metal complexes of dithiocarbamates have shown promising cytotoxic activity and have been widely explored as potential therapeutic agents.26,27 The incorporation of sulfur-containing functional groups, such as thiocarbamates, into bioactive heterocyclic frameworks is an effective strategy to enhance biological activity and develop novel chemical entities. Benzimidazole-fused thiazinethione scaffolds have recently attracted increasing attention in medicinal chemistry because they seamlessly integrate two privileged heterocyclic pharmacophores into a single molecular framework.28–30 Motivated by these attractive structural and biological features, considerable effort has been devoted to developing efficient synthetic approaches toward dithiocarbamate-containing scaffolds. In 2010, Ohno and co-workers reported the synthesis of 3,4-dihydro-2H,6H-benzo[e]pyrimido[1,2-c][1,3]thiazine-6-thione (PD404182) using carbon disulfide CS2 as the thiocarbonyl source in the presence of NaH.31 Meyer-Almes and co-workers synthesized a series of dithiocarbamate derivatives exhibiting promising biological activities.32,33 Later, Mizuhara and co-workers developed a Cu(OAc)2-catalyzed protocol for the reaction of 2-phenyl-1,4,5,6-tetrahydropyrimidine with CS2, although the reaction proceeded in modest yields.34 More recently, Jiang and co-workers reported a greener synthetic approach employing CS2 generated in situ from K2S and chloroform under Cu(OAc)2 catalysis, providing an alternative and environmentally benign strategy for the synthesis of dithiocarbamate-containing compounds.35 As part of our ongoing research interest in the synthesis of biologically active N,S-heterocyclic compounds, we recently reported the construction of benzo[4,5]imidazo[1,2-c][1,3]thiazinethiones via C–N/C–S coupling and cyclization reactions of 2-bromoaryl benzimidazoles with CS2 under strongly alkaline conditions.36 However, these synthetic methods suffer from several notable drawbacks, including the requirement for transition-metal catalysts and ligands, potential product contamination with toxic trace metals, the use of strong alkali in stoichiometric quantities, the handling of volatile and toxic CS2, and a restricted substrate scope. Therefore, the development of an operationally straightforward and efficient protocol for the synthesis of benzo[4,5]imidazo[1,2-c][1,3]thiazinethiones that minimizes toxic waste remains a critical objective. In contrast to the well-established of research addressing MOF-catalyzed synthesis of N-heterocycles and traditional cross-couplings, the utilization of metal–organic frameworks for constructing sulfur-containing heterocycles via C–S bond formation represents a nascent and underexplored avenue in heterogeneous catalysis.37–39 Our protocol replaces the direct handling of volatile carbon disulfide CS2 and stoichiometric strong bases with a safer carbon surrogate and a magnetically recyclable mag-Cu-MOF-74 catalyst, thereby significantly reducing the chemical and metal contamination associated with constructing these privileged N,S-heterocycles. Herein, we report an efficient synthetic protocol for constructing azole-fused thiazinethione scaffolds via a tandem C–N/C–S coupling and cyclization reaction of 2-bromoaryl/vinyl azoles with a safer carbon disulfide surrogate, utilizing a reusable mag-Cu-MOF-74 magnetic catalyst as a key novelty (Scheme 1).

Experimental

General information

1H (500 MHz) and 13C NMR (125 MHz) spectra were recorded on a Bruker UltraShield Plus 500 spectrometers using CDCl3 as the solvent. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) data were obtained on an X500R QTOF system using electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive mode. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker D8 Advance Eco diffractometer using a Cu source. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction data were collected using a Bruker D8 QUEST diffractometer. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained by a Hitachi HT 7700. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer Frontier NIR/MIR spectrometer. Melting points were determined using a Stanford Research Systems MPA100 automated melting point apparatus. Products were purified by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on Kieselgel 60 GF254 glass-backed plates. Starting materials 1a–o were synthesized from corresponding aldehyde and o-phenylenediamine using NaHSO3 in H2O.40 Compounds 1p–t were prepared by reacting the respective aldehydes with benzil or diacetyl in ethanol in the presence of NH4OAc and acetic acid.41 The magnetic Cu-MOF-74 was prepared based on a reported method.42 All other commercially available organic and inorganic reagents were used as received without further purification.

General procedure for the synthesis of 2 and catalyst recyclability

A 10 mL autoclave was charged with 1 (0.3 mmol), mag-Cu-MOF-74 (0.018 g, 0.03 mmol), l-proline (0.0069 g, 0.06 mmol), K2S (0.066 g, 0.6 mmol), CHCl3 (0.24 mL, 3 mmol), Cs2CO3 (0.195 g, 0.6 mmol), and DMF (3 mL). The reaction vessel was degassed, sealed under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the mixture was stirred at 100 °C for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the magnetic catalyst (mag-Cu-MOF-74) was separated using an external magnet. The resulting mixture was filtered through a short silica gel column, eluting with ethyl acetate to remove inorganic salts. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure to afford the crude product, which was purified by preparative thin-layer chromatography (chloroform/MeOH, 99 : 1) to yield compound 2. For catalyst recycling experiments, the recovered mag-Cu-MOF-74 was washed sequentially with methanol (3 × 20 mL) and distilled water (2 × 30 mL). The catalyst was then dried under vacuum at 100 °C for 12 h before being employed in subsequent reaction cycles under identical conditions. All synthesized compounds 2 were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy, and newly synthesized derivatives were further analyzed by HRMS (Fig. S1–S19, see SI).

Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay

Cytotoxic activity of the samples was evaluated using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay, following established procedures widely adopted by the National Cancer Institute (NCI, USA) and the College of Pharmacy, University of Illinois at Chicago, USA.43,44 The assay was performed using the human breast carcinoma cell line (MCF-7). MCF-7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with l-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), penicillin–streptomycin–fungizone (PSF), non-essential amino acids (NEAA), and 10% bovine calf serum (BCS). Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) was used for cell detachment. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as the vehicle for sample preparation, while phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), trichloroacetic acid (TCA), Tris base, sulforhodamine B (SRB), and acetic acid were used for cell fixation, washing, and staining procedures. Ellipticine was employed as the positive control. Absorbance was measured using a microplate ELISA reader at a wavelength of 495-515 nm. Cell survival (CS) was calculated according to the following eqn (1):1where ODsample is the absorbance of treated cells, ODday0 is the absorbance measured immediately after cell seeding, and ODvehicle control is the absorbance of vehicle-treated cells (DMSO). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Samples exhibiting cell survival below 50% were considered active and were further evaluated to determine the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). IC50 values were determined via nonlinear regression analysis of the logarithmic concentration-response curves using TableCurve software. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated with the different compound at difference concentrations for 24 h.

Results and discussion

The reaction of 2-(2-bromophenyl)-1H-benzo[d]imidazole (1a) was selected as the model system to optimize the reaction conditions using mag-Cu-MOF-74 as the catalyst. Initial treatment of 1a with mag-Cu-MOF-74 (10 mol%), 2,2′-bipyridyl, LiOH, K2S, and CHCl3 in CH3CN at 80 °C for 24 h afforded the desired product 6H-benzo[e]benzo[4,5]imidazo[1,2-c][1,3]thiazine-6-thione (2a) in 48% isolated yield with 62% conversion of 1a (Table 1, entry 1). Changing the solvent to DMF significantly improved both the conversion and product yield to 84% and 53%, respectively (Table 1, entry 2). In contrast, DMSO resulted in only a moderate yield, while no desired product was obtained when dioxane was employed (Table 1, entries 3 and 4), indicating that DMF is the optimal solvent for this transformation. The reaction temperature was subsequently investigated in DMF. Increasing the temperature from 80 °C to 100 °C markedly improved the reaction efficiency, yielding 2a in 63% with 91% conversion (Table 1, entry 5). A further increase temperature to 120 °C provided only a marginal improvement in the isolated yield (Table 1, entry 6). Reducing the reaction time from 24 h to 16 h and 8 h resulted in significantly lower product yields and conversions (Table 1, entries 7 and 8), confirming that a prolonged reaction time is essential for efficient product formation. The stoichiometry of the sulfur source was also evaluated. The molar ratio of K2S to 1a significantly affected the yield of 2a, which increased as the ratio was raised to 2 (Table 1, entries 9 and 10). Upon screening different ligands, the desired product was obtained in 75% isolated yield with complete conversion of 1a when l-proline was used (Table 1, entry 11), demonstrating that l-proline is more favorable for this transformation. This is likely attributed to its unique bifunctional nature, which facilitates optimal interaction with the catalytic copper centers and assists in the necessary proton-transfer steps during the tandem coupling and cyclization process.45–47 The base was then screened using l-proline as the ligand. Replacing LiOH with KOH or K2CO3 resulted in lower product yields (Table 1, entries 12 and 13), whereas Cs2CO3 proved to be the most effective, affording 2a in 81% isolated yield with complete conversion of 1a (Table 1, entry 14). CsF also promoted the reaction but resulted in a lower yield and incomplete conversion of 1a (Table 1, entry 15). Finally, reducing the catalyst amount decreased the yield to 41% despite a moderate conversion of 64% (Table 1, entry 16), highlighting the essential role of mag-Cu-MOF-74. When the catalyst loading was increased to 20 mol%, the product was obtained 83% yield (Table 1, entry 17). This minor 2% increase compared to 10 mol% (81% yield) falls within the limits of experimental error and is statistically insignificant. Given that doubling the catalyst loading provides no meaningful benefit, 10 mol% was established as the optimal catalyst loading. Although not included in Table 1, the target product 3a was not obtained in the absence of the mag-Cu-MOF-74 catalyst, clearly demonstrating that the magnetic Cu-MOF-74 is indispensable for facilitating the C–N/C–S tandem coupling and cyclization process.,

Following the optimization of the reaction conditions, the substrate scope was investigated using a diverse array of 2-(2-bromoaryl)- and 2-(2-bromovinyl)azoles 1 and several representative results are summarized in Table 2. The effect of electron-donating and -withdrawing substituents on bromoaryl moiety 1b–g was successfully coupled and cyclized to obtain the corresponding product 2b–g. The introduction of a methyl group 1b did not affect the reaction, affording 2b with a similar yield, indicating that alkyl substituents are well tolerated. On the other hand, methoxy-, halogen-, dimethoxy-, methylenedioxy-substituted substrate 1c–g resulted in lower yields. In addition, treatment of 2-(1-bromonaphthalen-2-yl)-1H-benzo[d]imidazole (1h) with cyanamide also afforded coupling and cyclization product 2h in good yield. To probe the electronic influence of substituents on the benzimidazole moiety, disubstituted derivatives 1i and 1j were examined under the reaction conditions. While the dichloro-substituted substrate 1i failed to yield product 2i, the dimethyl-substituted substrate 1j smoothly delivered compound 2j in 88% yield. These contrasting outcomes indicate that electron-donating alkyl groups facilitate the cyclization process, whereas multiple electron-withdrawing chloro substituents severely inhibit reactivity. Evaluation of cyclic substrates 1k–m revealed that varying the carbocycle size had a negligible effect on reaction efficiency, consistently affording the corresponding products 2k–m in 83-87% yields. The molecular structure of 2l was indisputably established by single-crystal X-ray crystallography (CCDC 2579902, see SI).48 In contrast, acyclic analogues 1n and 1o delivered compounds 2n and 2o in slightly diminished yields. This outcome indicates that cyclic systems benefit from favorable conformational constraints that promote cyclization, whereas the specific chain length of the acyclic substrates exhibits minimal influence on overall reactivity. Subjection of imidazole substrates 1p and 1q to the optimized conditions afforded the corresponding products 2o and 2q, demonstrating that substitution on the imidazole ring has a negligible effect on reaction efficiency. Conversely, examination of aryl-substituted imidazole derivatives 1r–t revealed that the methyl-substituted analogue provided the highest yield of product 2r, whereas the methoxy- and fluoro-substituted derivatives (2s and 2t) proved less effective, indicating that methyl substitution is more favorable than methoxy or halogen groups. While the protocol efficiently accommodates a diverse range of starting materials 1, certain limitations were observed. The dichlorinated substrate 1i completely failed to yield the target product, which can be attributed to the strong steric hindrance and pronounced electronic deactivating effects imposed by the two chlorine atoms on the aromatic ring, hindering the critical cyclization steps.49 On the other hand, the fluorinated derivative 2t was obtained in lowest yield, likely due to electronic and steric properties unique to that specific substitution pattern that impede optimal reaction turnover.

The large specific surface area and highly porous structure of Cu-MOF-74 enhance catalytic activity by providing maximum exposure of fully accessible unsaturated copper centers that act as active sites for reactant molecules.50–52 The magnetic catalyst mag-Cu-MOF-74 was readily separated from the reaction mixture using an external magnet and recycled over multiple cycles without a significant loss in performance, the yield of product 2a remained consistent across successive runs (80%, 79%, 79%, and 78% for the first through fourth reuses, respectively) (Fig. 1). To assess the reusability and structural stability of the catalyst, both fresh and recovered catalyst (fourth cycle) was characterized using powder XRD, FT-IR, and TEM analyses. The XRD pattern of the recycled catalyst remained identical to those of the fresh sample, indicating no phase changes or structural degradation (Fig. 2). Similarly, the FT-IR spectra confirmed the retention of key functional groups and coordination bonds (Fig. 3). Microscopic analyses (TEM) demonstrated that the morphology and particle distribution of mag-Cu-MOF-74 were maintained core–shell structure without notable agglomeration or collapse (Fig. 4). These results establish mag-Cu-MOF-74 as a durable heterogeneous catalyst capable of sustained performance.

According to related literature,35 a proposed pathway for the synthesis of 2a from 1a commences with the generation of dichlorocarbene from chloroform with the assistance of a base, which inserts into the N–H bond of 1a and subsequently loses a chloride anion to afford the iminium intermediate 3. In the presence of K2S and a copper catalyst, intermediate 3 undergoes nucleophilic attack and intramolecular cyclization via organocopper intermediate 4 to yield intermediate 5. Treatment of 5 under basic conditions promotes the elimination of HCl to generate the reactive carbon carbene intermediate 6. Finally, intermediate 6 couples with a sulfur source containing a trisulfur radical anion, which generated from oxidation of K2S,53,54via intermediate 7 to furnish the target product 2a (Scheme 2).

The inhibitory effects on cell growth and corresponding IC50 values of selected compounds (2a, 2g, 2k, 2p and 2r) were evaluated against MCF-7 Breast carcinoma cells using the SRB viability assay, with doxorubicin (DOX) as the comparison standard. The tested compounds demonstrated concentration-dependent growth inhibition against the cancer cells (Fig. 5). The efficacy order was 2p (IC50 = 49.71 ± 4.14 ppm) ∼ 2r (IC50 = 50.87 ± 1.61 ppm) > 2a (IC50 = 80.46 ± 2.20) > 2g (IC50 = 126.15 ± 6.80 ppm) > 2k (IC50 = 327.16 ± 3.54 ppm). Furthermore, at higher concentrations (100–200 ppm), compounds 2a, 2g, 2p, and 2r achieved significant inhibition rates, with 2p and 2r exhibiting the highest efficacy. Specifically, these two analogues outperformed the rest of the series, showing inhibition levels comparable to or exceeding 70–80% at 150–200 ppm. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis reveals that fusion with the benzothiazinethione core and specific substitutions on the imidazole ring heavily influence cytotoxicity. For instance, compounds bearing the 1,3-benzodioxole group 2g consistently showed higher inhibition percentages than the six-membered ring analogue 2k across matching concentrations. 6H-benzo[e]benzo[4,5]imidazo[1,2- c][1,3]thiazine-6-thione (2a) displayed superior cytotoxic activity compared to both 2g and 2k. The imidazole-fused thiazinethiones 2p and 2r show that enhanced inhibitory profiles compared to the benzimidazole-fused analogues. Thus, these findings provide initial insights for further modification and optimization of these heterocyclic scaffolds.

Conclusions

In summary, an efficient synthetic protocol for benzimidazole- and imidazole-fused thiazinethiones was developed via a recyclable magnetic Cu-MOF-74-catalyzed C–N/C–S coupling and cyclization of 2-(2-bromoaryl)- and 2-(2-bromovinyl) azoles with K2S/CHCl3, utilizing a practical sulfur surrogate. Moreover, the evaluated synthetic compounds demonstrated preliminary in vitro cytotoxic activity against MCF-7 breast carcinoma cells, providing a useful foundation for further pharmacological evaluation and structural optimization. This methodology offers notable advantages over existing approaches, including superior yields, catalyst recyclability, and the use of safer sulfur surrogate. Given the current scarcity of reports on MOF-catalyzed C–S coupling, extending this magnetic Cu-MOF-74 system to the synthesis of a broader class of sulfur-containing heterocycles represents a valuable direction. Further exploration into the construction of complex polynuclear N,S-fused heterocycles using this protocol is currently underway.
