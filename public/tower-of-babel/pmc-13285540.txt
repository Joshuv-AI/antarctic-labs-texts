Evaluation of the efficacy and safety of ethacizine in the management of premature atrial and ventricular contractions – a prospective study (ETHAPRO)
Authors: Nika Kuridze, Kakhaber Etsadashvili, Eteri Minadze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPremature atrial contractions (PACs) and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are among the most frequently observed arrhythmias in clinical practice. They represent early depolarizations originating from the atria or ventricles, respectively, and are often detected in both healthy individuals and patients with cardiovascular disease.Patients and methodsIn the ETHAPRO study, 98 patients (58 males, 40 females; mean age 45 years) with either PACs (n = 46) or PVCs (n = 52) were enrolled. All participants had an ectopic burden of >1000 premature beats per 24 h despite prior antiarrhythmic therapy. Patients were treated with ethacizine (50 mg b.i.d.) and were followed at 30–45 days (short-term) and 150–210 days (long-term). The endpoints were a reduction in ectopic burden to ≤1000 beats per 24 h, symptomatic improvement and safety assessments.Results and conclusionsEthacizine treatment led to significant reductions in PACs (88.9%/ 91.7%; p < 0.001) and PVCs (82.8%/ 84.8%; p < 0.001). A total of 89.0% of PAC patients and 86.0% of PVC patients achieved the predefined target. Symptomatic improvement was reported in 91.0% of patients with PAC and 85.0% of patients with PVC. Ethacizine demonstrated robust and sustained antiarrhythmic efficacy with a favorable safety profile in patients with refractory PACs and PVCs.KEYWORDS: Ethacizine, premature atrial contractions, premature ventricular contractions, antiarrhythmic therapy, prospective study

Introduction

Premature atrial contractions (PACs) and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are common arrhythmias whose prevalence increases with age and underlying cardiovascular disease [1,2]. PACs are frequently detected in the general population, often asymptomatically, while PVCs are less common but occur more frequently in older individuals and in patients with structural heart disease [3]. Although often benign, frequent PACs and PVCs can cause symptoms such as palpitations, dyspnea, and fatigue [4]. In certain cases, they may contribute to more severe complications, including the worsening of underlying heart disease, cardiomyopathy, cerebral ischemia, and increased mortality [5–9]. The management of these arrhythmias typically involves the use of antiarrhythmic agents [10], but treatment is often challenging, particularly in patients who do not respond to first-line therapies.

Ethacizine, a class Ic antiarrhythmic drug, is primarily indicated for the treatment of arrhythmias, including refractory supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias [11]. Ethacizine blocks fast sodium channels, slowing conduction and prolonging refractoriness [12,13].

However, despite its use in Eastern European countries, there is a scarcity of rigorous clinical data assessing its efficacy and safety in treating patients with PACs and PVCs who do not respond adequately to other antiarrhythmic medications.

The ETHAPRO study aims to fill this gap by evaluating the efficacy and safety of ethacizine in a cohort of patients with refractory PACs and PVCs.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective study was conducted at our institution, over four years from 2018 to 2022. This was an investigator-initiated prospective observational study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. A prerequisite for inclusion in the study was obtaining written informed consent from the participants, and all participants provided signed informed consent prior to enrollment. A total of 98 patients were enrolled in the study, including 58 males (59.2%) and 40 females (40.8%). The participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 72 years, with a mean age of 45 years. Each participant had a confirmed diagnosis of either premature atrial or premature ventricular contractions, as verified through 24-hour ECG Holter monitoring. A total of 46 patients with PACs and 52 patients with PVCs were included in this study. To be eligible for inclusion in the study, patients were required to have previously undergone treatment with at least one of the following antiarrhythmic medications: bisoprolol, metoprolol, sotalol, verapamil, amiodarone, propafenone, beta-blocker + amiodarone, and/or beta-blocker + propafenone (Table 1). Prior antiarrhythmic therapy included amiodarone either as monotherapy or in combination with beta-blockers, reflecting regional prescribing practices in selected patients with symptomatic PACs or PVCs refractory to initial treatment. Catheter ablation was not routinely indicated in most patients due to the absence of a high ectopic burden (>10%/24 h). In addition, some patients declined invasive treatment and preferred further pharmacological therapy.

Despite prior treatment, all enrolled individuals exhibited a persistent ectopic burden exceeding 1000 premature beats per 24 h, which is considered a clinically significant threshold requiring further intervention. The exclusion criteria encompassed all possible diagnoses or clinical conditions representing contraindications to class 1c antiarrhythmic drugs, as well as extrasystoles associated with conditions such as electrolyte imbalances, anemia, hiatal hernia, and anxiety disorders. Patients with structural heart disease were not included in the study, as ethacizine, similarly to other class Ic antiarrhythmic agents, has limited use in this population. Additionally, due to the small sample size, subgroup analysis based on comorbidities was not performed because it would lack sufficient statistical reliability. Furthermore, individuals younger than 18 years and pregnant women were excluded from the study. Statistical analyses were performed using a paired samples t-test to evaluate within-subject (pre–post) changes in clinical outcomes. The normality assumption for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated no significant deviations from normality (all p > 0.05). No missing data were observed; therefore, all participants were included in the paired analyses. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Intervention protocol and study endpoints

Participants included in the study had been receiving various antiarrhythmic therapies prior to the initiation of ethacizine. Due to insufficient efficacy, these medications were discontinued, and ethacizine was subsequently initiated in all enrolled patients at a standardized oral dosage of 50 mg twice daily, ensuring uniform therapeutic exposure across the study population. Throughout the treatment period, the patients were subjected to rigorous clinical monitoring to evaluate both the efficacy of antiarrhythmic therapy and the emergence of any potential adverse effects. Follow-up assessments were conducted over two distinct timeframes: a short-term follow-up period spanning 30 to 45 days and a long-term follow-up period extending from 150 to 210 days after initiation of treatment with ethacizine.

The primary efficacy endpoint was defined as reduction of the ectopic burden to a clinically acceptable threshold of ≤1000 premature beats per 24-hour period. This parameter was objectively assessed using 24-hour ECG Holter recordings, allowing precise quantification of ectopic activity over time. The secondary endpoints included a percentage reduction in PACs and PVCs during short- and long-term follow-up, as well as visual analog scale (VAS)-based symptom improvement and overall safety evaluation during long-term follow-up. Symptom severity was evaluated using a standard VAS ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 10 (worst imaginable symptoms), on which patients indicated their perceived symptom intensity.

Results

Efficacy in reducing premature atrial contractions

The study demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in the burden of premature atrial contractions following ethacizine therapy, observed consistently during both the early and late follow-up periods. In the early follow-up phase, treatment with ethacizine led to a substantial decline in the mean number of PACs, decreasing from a baseline value of 6,877 (7.6%) ± 2,432 (2.7%) to 761 (0.75%) ± 341 (0.3%). This corresponds to a relative reduction of 88.9%, which was statistically highly significant (t[45] = 13.336, p < 0.001). Notably, this therapeutic benefit was not only sustained but was also further enhanced over time. During the late follow-up period, the mean PAC count was reduced even further to 574 (0.5%) ± 294 (0.3%), yielding a 91.7% reduction relative to baseline (t[45] = 15.124, p < 0.001), thereby confirming the durability of the antiarrhythmic effect of ethacizine (Figure 1).

The maximum individual reduction in PAC frequency reached 93.8% in the early phase and 94.3% in the late phase, underscoring the robustness and long-term efficacy of the treatment in the study population. Importantly, by the end of the follow-up period, 89.0% of patients had achieved the predefined therapeutic target, characterized by a reduction in PAC frequency to ≤1000 within a 24-hour ECG Holter monitoring period.

Subjective improvement, determined by patient-reported outcomes, was reported in 91.0% of patients with PACs (95% CI: 83.0–99.0%). The mean VAS score significantly improved from 7.5 ± 1.0 to 3.0 ± 1.9 (p < 0.001).

These findings suggest that ethacizine is effective in acutely suppressing atrial ectopy and maintaining its clinical benefits over time in most patients with symptomatic or high-burden PACs.

Efficacy in reducing premature ventricular contractions

In patients presenting with frequent premature ventricular contractions, ethacizine demonstrated a substantial reduction in PVCs across both early and late follow-up periods. During the early follow-up phase, the mean number of PVCs declined markedly from a baseline value of 3,205 (3.6%) ± 1,145 (1.3%) to 550 (0.5%) ± 194 (0.2%). This represents a relative reduction of 82.8%, which was statistically significant (t[51] = 8.284, p < 0.001), highlighting the prompt onset of antiarrhythmic efficacy following the initiation of therapy.

Notably, this effect was sustained over time and showed a slight further improvement during late follow-up, where the mean PVC count further decreased to 490 (0.4%) ± 181 (0.2%). This corresponds to an 84.8% reduction compared to the baseline (t[51] = 8.553, p < 0.001), indicating durable suppression of ventricular ectopy with ongoing ethacizine treatment (Figure 2).

The maximum individual response reached an 94.8% reduction in PVC burden during the early phase and 95.0% in the late phase, emphasizing the consistent efficacy of this drug across the study cohort.

By the conclusion of the late follow-up, 86.0% of the patients successfully met the predefined therapeutic goal of reducing PVC frequency to ≤1000 ectopic beats over a 24-hour monitoring period.

Subjective improvement was reported in 85.0% of patients with PVCs (95% CI: 75.0–95.0%). A significant reduction in mean VAS score was observed, from 7.7 ± 1.1 to 2.7 ± 1.8. (p < 0.001).

These findings affirm the capacity of ethacizine to provide significant and sustained control of symptomatic or high-burden PVCs in most treated individuals.

Safety profile

Ethacizine was associated with a low incidence of treatment-related findings in the study population. Although infrequent (4.1%), adverse events included clinically significant arrhythmias requiring drug discontinuation. The observed adverse events included one case of second-degree atrioventricular block (type I) and one episode of sustained ventricular tachycardia, as well as instances of asymptomatic nonsustained polytopic ventricular tachycardia. Additionally, a case of transient ST-segment elevation was noted during routine 24-hour Holter monitoring, although it remained clinically silent and resolved spontaneously without intervention (Table 2). Ethacizine was discontinued in all cases. No patient was excluded from the study due to QRS or QTc interval prolongation.

Discussion

Ethacizine, a class Ic antiarrhythmic drug, was developed in the Soviet Union in the early 1980s and remains primarily used in several Eastern European countries [14]. Ethacizine has been evaluated in several clinical studies, where it demonstrated consistent efficacy in suppressing both supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, thereby supporting its role as a versatile class Ic antiarrhythmic agent [15–19]. Despite decades of clinical use, ethacizine remains largely absent from clinical guidelines and international cardiology discourse, which is partly due to the lack of modern, large-scale studies. Nevertheless, emerging data, including our current prospective analysis, reaffirm its therapeutic potential in the management of supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias.

Our study represents a prospective analysis of ethacizine in the treatment of PACs and PVCs, providing valuable insights into its clinical efficacy and safety profile. The findings demonstrate that ethacizine significantly reduces the frequency of premature contractions in patients who do not respond to other antiarrhythmic medications. The observed reduction in PACs and PVCs, as well as the sustained therapeutic effect over time, suggests that ethacizine is a reliable option for the long-term management of extrasystolic arrhythmias.

Importantly, this study also highlights the potential role of ethacizine in achieving the widely accepted therapeutic target of ≤1000 extrasystoles per 24 h. Both patients with PAC and PVC experienced a reduction to below this threshold in most cases, particularly during the late follow-up period, supporting its long-term efficacy.

The efficacy of ethacizine has been confirmed in various clinical studies. The drug may be particularly suitable for patients without structural heart disease. In a study by Tsaregorodtsev et al. ethacizine significantly reduced ventricular ectopy in 65.0% and prevented atrial fibrillation recurrence in 50.0% of patients [20]. Hrebenyk and Masliy conducted a retrospective analysis of 30 years of clinical experience with ethacizine in patients with various arrhythmias, including those with comorbidities such as diabetes and COPD. Among the 74 long-term users, ethacizine was highly effective, particularly for ventricular ectopy (78.9%) and atrial fibrillation control (61.7%). It was especially effective in vagal, bradycardia-dependent arrhythmias [21]. In a study by Tsaregorodtsev et al. ethacizine was identified as the most effective agent for treating ventricular arrhythmias, including premature ventricular contractions, in patients without structural heart disease. Among patients receiving antiarrhythmic therapy, ethacizine had a positive therapeutic effect in 69.7% of cases. The reported side effect rate was relatively low (16.7%), and the treatment was associated with a significant improvement in the quality of life, particularly in mental health scores. These results support ethacizine as a valuable therapeutic option for appropriately selected patients [22]. In a study by Volkov and Strona, ethacizine (150 mg/day for 30 days) demonstrated significant antiarrhythmic efficacy in patients with ischemic heart disease and ventricular arrhythmias. The average reduction in PVC burden among 29 patients with ventricular ectopy was 82.8%. The drug was generally well-tolerated, and subtherapeutic doses (75 mg/day) achieved comparable efficacy with fewer side effects [23]. Notably, ethacizine has demonstrated a beneficial effect in patients with atrial fibrillation. In a study by Kokina et al. ethacizine administered concomitantly with beta-blockers was associated with positive outcomes in maintaining sinus rhythm following electrical cardioversion in high-risk patients. The study compared the efficacy of ethacizine and amiodarone over a 6-month follow-up period and found no statistically significant differences in rhythm control or arrhythmia-free survival. These findings suggest that ethacizine may serve as an effective alternative to amiodarone, particularly in cases where the latter is contraindicated [24]. In a prospective study conducted at the Latvian Center of Cardiology, ethacizine (100 mg/day) was assessed for its efficacy and safety in maintaining sinus rhythm following electrical cardioversion in patients with persistent atrial fibrillation. Among the 62 enrolled patients, those receiving ethacizine exhibited a lower recurrence rate of atrial fibrillation than those in the propafenone group (55.6% vs. 72.5%, p = 0.05). No significant changes were observed in the QT interval duration, left ventricular ejection fraction, laboratory parameters, or 24 h. ECG Holter monitoring results. Ethacizine was well tolerated and associated with no major adverse events, supporting its potential role in the early post-cardioversion phase for rhythm stabilization [25].

Mechanistically, ethacizine blocks open calcium channels in a voltage-, frequency-, and concentration-dependent manner, consistent with a use-dependent mechanism, without significant interaction with resting or inactivated channels [26]. Compared to other class Ic agents such as flecainide and propafenone, ethacizine exhibits modest activity on calcium channels and weak anticholinergic effects, potentially enhancing its efficacy in vagally mediated arrhythmias. Notably, its pharmacokinetic profile includes a relatively short half-life (4.63 h.) [27], enabling easier reversibility in case of adverse events. From a safety standpoint, our study identified adverse effects in 4.1% of the patients. The events included second-degree atrioventricular block, sustained ventricular tachycardia, asymptomatic nonsustained polytopic ventricular tachycardia, and ST-segment elevation, which were noted during routine 24-hour ECG Holter monitoring, all of which were transient and manageable. These outcomes are consistent with historical safety data, including the prospective study by Sychev et al. which demonstrated good tolerability in a large cohort of patients without significant structural heart disease [28].

The analysis by Lei et al. emphasizes the need for subclass IC antiarrhythmic drugs to be stratified according to updated electrophysiological classifications [29]. Such initiatives are crucial for drugs such as ethacizine, enabling its position in modern pharmacological classification. Additionally, some authors note the underrepresentation of ethacizine in international databases despite its wide usage and favorable efficacy-toxicity ratio. This underscores the need for renewed global investigations, particularly randomized controlled trials, to reassess the value of ethacizine in current arrhythmia management strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, we were unable to compare ethacizine with other class Ic antiarrhythmic agents, such as flecainide, due to their limited availability during the study period. Second, the study was conducted at a single center and included a relatively small sample size. In addition, the observational design without a control group or randomization may introduce potential selection bias. Furthermore, the potential confounding effects of previous or concomitant therapies cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Ethacizine may represent a valuable therapeutic option in carefully selected patients with high-burden ectopy refractory to conventional therapy, although randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.
