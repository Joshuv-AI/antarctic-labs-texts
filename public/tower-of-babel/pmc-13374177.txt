A sweet danger: the silent link between hyperinsulinemia and gastric cancer
Authors: Pasqualina Laurenziello, Margherita Luongo, Francesca Lospinoso Severini, Sabino Russi, Geppino Falco, Carlo Calabrese, Simona Laurino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperinsulinemia (HI), a compensatory response to insulin resistance commonly associated with obesity and preceding Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, represents a critical link between metabolic dysfunction and both cancer risk and progression. While its systemic effects are well-documented, its specific impact on gastric cancer (GC) risk and prognosis requires deeper investigation. HI promotes oncogenesis by activating the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways, which are often dysregulated in GC through the overexpression of insulin receptor isoform A as well as oncogenic mutations in their key regulatory proteins, leading to increased cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and therapy resistance. In addition, the intricate crosstalk between these cascades and other signaling pathways, including NF-kB, JAK/STAT, WNT/β-catenin, fosters a pro-inflammatory milieu, the transition to mesenchymal phenotype, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. This review synthesizes two decades of research to elucidate the biochemical mechanisms by which elevated insulin levels provide the necessary stimuli and metabolic energy for tumor survival. We further evaluate clinical management strategies, emphasizing the potential of dietary interventions and pharmacological approaches to modulate the course of disease. Notably, a meta-analysis of existing literature indicates that metformin use is strongly associated with a reduced GC risk (Hazard Ratio, HR: 0.67, p = 0.008), and better outcomes including lower recurrence (HR: 0.61, p < 0.0001), all-cause mortality (HR: 0.61, p = 0.0002), and cancer-specific mortality (HR: 0.80, p = 0.0003). Despite the limitations of our findings, our review indicates that the pharmacological control of HI, and the underlying mutational pattern and pathway alterations it promotes, should be considered relevant in oncological management of GC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-026-02693-8.

Hyperinsulinemia: a putative spy of metabolism dysregulation

Metabolism, recognized as the set of chemical reactions occurring for energy production and biosynthesis [1], is pathologically altered in cancer cells to facilitate their proliferation and survival [2, 3]. This condition was identified for the first time by Warburg who observed that malignant cells preferentially use glycolysis for energy production even in sufficient quantities of oxygen [4], a phenomenon now termed as “Warburg effect” [3]. He hypothesized that the high rate of glycolysis was the result of mitochondrial damage, but subsequent studies have shown that cancer cells have functional mitochondria [5, 6]. However, cancer cells prefer aerobic glycolysis because it converts glucose to lactate 100 times faster than tricarboxylic acid cycle [7], provides a carbon source for macromolecules biosynthesis [8], and reduces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production favoring anoikis inhibition [9].

Physiologically, blood glucose balance is a process coordinated by the hormones insulin and glucagon [10]. Unhealthy lifestyles and diet play a critical role in metabolic dysfunction. Indeed, the consumption of calorie-dense ultra-processed foods, salt, sugar, and fat is responsible for high basal or fasting insulin levels [11], along with weight gain and obesity [12]. This state of overnutrition also triggers adipose tissue to release free fatty acids, ROS, and inflammatory cytokines, which in turn induce systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. This metabolic impairment prompts a critical compensatory rise in insulin levels, leading to HI [13, 14]. Clinical reports underscore that obese subjects exhibit higher circulating insulin levels both at baseline and four hours postprandially compared to healthy controls, in whom insulin levels typically return to baseline within two hours [15, 16]. HI can also be congenital or related to other factors such as ethnicity. Indeed, African Americans have lower sensitivity to insulin, regardless of obesity and fat distribution [17].

Based on this evidence, research on the role of HI in cancer has grown up. Insulin, underlying insulin resistance, and the consequent higher blood glucose levels provides the stimuli and the energy for cancer cells to promote their proliferation and survival [16]. Here, we summarized literature regarding HI and cancer with a particular focus on GC, a disease still with poor data and that needs deeper investigations.

Insulin and its signaling pathways

Insulin plays an important role in the metabolism of carbohydrates, lipids and proteins. It is synthesized by the β cells of the Langerhans’ pancreatic islets as a single-chain prohormone called preproinsulin. Then it is converted in the endoplasmic reticulum into proinsulin and, subsequently, in the secretory granules of the Golgi apparatus, undergoes proteolytic cleavage where the C-peptide is removed, leaving the A and B chains linked by disulfide bonds to constitute the mature insulin molecule [18, 19]. Insulin binds its receptor, composed by alpha and beta subunits encoded by the insulin receptor gene (INSR) [20]. The α subunit (135 kDa) is located in the extracellular domain, while the β subunit (95 kDa) includes extracellular, transmembrane, and intracellular regions. The latter has the tyrosine kinase domain and regulatory regions [18]. The two subunits are connected by a single disulfide bridge [20].

The insulin receptor (IR) has two isoforms A and B, the first of 1370 amino acids (IR-A) and the second of 1382 amino acids (IR-B). Isoform A lacks exon 11 (36 bp) which confer the ability to recognize and bind not only insulin but also insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) with greater preference for IGF-2 [21]. IR-A is most commonly expressed in fetal tissues, brain, ovaries, spleen and testes, and various cancer cells promoting cell growth, proliferation and survival. The IR-B is most commonly expressed in the liver, pancreatic β-cells, muscles, adipose tissue, and kidney cells mainly regulating glucose homeostasis [20]. When IR-A and IR-B are co-expressed, homodimers or hybrid (IR-A/IR-B) receptors can be formed [22]. IR isoforms can also form heterodimers with insulin-like growth factors receptor (IGF1R) generating hybrid IR-A/IGF1R and IR-B/IGF1R receptors [23] that bind IGF-2 with higher affinity than insulin [22]. In many neoplasms, IR-A is preferentially overexpressed contributing to tumor cell stemness, tumor development, and resistance to targeted therapies [24, 25]. The IR signaling is mediated by insulin receptor substrates (IRSs), a family of six cytoplasmic adaptor proteins. IRSs activate upstream receptors through their pleckstrin homology and the phosphotyrosine binding domains and are subsequently phosphorylated by receptor kinases to form binding sites for downstream proteins [26]. Following ligand binding, IR orchestrates an intricate signaling network of canonical and non-canonical axes. The PI3K/AKT pathway functions as a master regulatory axis in which AKT, in coordination with the functionally distinct multiprotein complexes mTORC1 and mTORC2, regulates several cellular processes including cell growth and survival [27]. Phosphorylated IRS1/2 recruit PI3K that catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-triphosphate (PIP3). This, in turn, promotes the dual activation of AKT by PDK1 at Thr308 and by mTORC2 at Ser473 [28]. Once active, AKT triggers mTORC1 by removing the molecular brake imposed by Tuberous sclerosis 2 (TSC2) protein. Downstream mTORC1 signaling promotes protein translation, nucleotide synthesis and autophagy suppression through the phosphorylation of 4EBP1 translation initiation factor and S6K1 kinase [29, 30]. In a hyperinsulinemic context, this network shifts toward malignant progression through complex feedback mechanisms and pathways interconnection. A critical regulatory mechanism involves chronic mTORC1 activation, which triggers S6K1-mediated negative feedback loop. This process induces the serine phosphorylation of IRS-1, tagging it for proteasomal degradation and ultimately fueling a state of insulin resistance. IRS-1 inhibition is further amplified by ERK1/2 activity, strengthens the crosstalk between the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways [31]. Furthermore, the constitutive overactivation of this axis in cancer is often driven by mTORC2-mediated AKT compensatory activation and by the loss or inhibition of PTEN, the negative regulator that dephosphorylates PIP3 back to PIP2, resulting in increased cell growth [28, 32, 33].

Upon insulin-IR binding, the MAPK/ERK pathway triggers the phosphorylation cascades RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK that culminates in the ERK1/2 nuclear translocation, where it phosphorylates several transcription factors involved in cell proliferation and survival [20, 28, 34]. Due to their interconnection, the abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways allows the inhibition of one to be followed by the activation of the other, thereby ensuring the continuous transmission of survival signals [31, 34]. Beyond these canonical axes, hyperinsulinemia engages a non-canonical network that functions as a molecular bridge to chronic inflammation and chemoresistance [35, 36]. At this divergence point, AKT directly governs glucose metabolism and cellular plasticity by inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), thereby promoting glycogen storage and β-catenin stability [37]. The resulting nuclear accumulation of β-catenin drives the expression of mesenchymal markers and the multidrug resistance protein 1 (MDR1) [38, 39], favoring a chemoresistant phenotype. Additionally, AKT inactivates FoxO transcription factors, further deregulating gluconeogenic and adipogenic gene expression [40].

Moreover, under hyperinsulinemic stimulation, AKT also directly phosphorylates the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, triggering the degradation of IκB and the subsequent nuclear translocation of NF-κB [36, 41]. This process fosters a pro-inflammatory microenvironment through the secretion of cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 and promotes a mesenchymal phenotype through the expression of Snail and Twist. NF-κB also upregulates the anti-apoptotic factors Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL expression [36]. Another pivotal consequence of hyperinsulinemia is the functional suppression of AMPK, which physiologically acts as a metabolic brake. Hyperinsulinemia-mediated AKT activation inhibits AMPK via phosphorylation and further unlocks mTORC1, which leads to dual oncogenic effect. On one hand, mTORC1 promotes the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of SREBP1 that drives de novo lipogenesis to provide the substrates for tumor growth [42]. On the other hand, it favors a glycolytic phenotype through the stabilization of HIF-1α. This accumulation is boosted by the concomitant AMPK inhibition, which prevents HIF-1α degradation [43, 44]. Concurrently, phosphorylated IRS1/2 recruit JAK1/2 to the receptor complex, expanding the spectrum of insulin signaling. Activated JAK2 phosphorylates STAT3, which translocate to the nucleus and drives the transcription of genes involved in immune evasion and cell cycle progression [35, 39]. The JAK/STAT pathway induces SOCS3 protein expression that mediates the degradation of IR and IRSs, stoking insulin resistance [45]. Overall, the HI acts promoting cell growth via mitogenic pathways while constructing a metabolic and anti-apoptotic environment that makes conventional therapies ineffective.

Systemic insulin activity

The intricate signaling between HI and cancer is based on the hormonal communication between the pancreas, the pituitary gland, and the liver. A central role is played by the Insulin/GH/IGF-1 axis, a regulatory system designed to maintain cellular growth and energy balance. Physiologically there is a precise feedback loop that modern dietary habits can disrupt, creating a biochemical environment that favors cellular proliferation.

Insulin levels are fundamental to maintain the equilibrium of the insulin, growth hormone (GH) and IGF-1 axis, which plays a very important role in tumor promotion and progression [46]. Insulin from pancreas and GH from pituitary gland stimulate the hepatic synthesis of IGF-1, which in turn suppresses their secretion through a negative feedback. When the system is in equilibrium, in the early post-prandial period, GH is the main stimulator of IGF-1 secretion, which increases insulin receptor sensitivity and suppresses its proteolysis [47]. Conversely, in the late post-prandial period, IGF-1 attenuates GH-mediated insulin resistance and reduces insulin secretion by the pancreas [48].

In modern society, excessive food and sugar intake, as well as energy surplus, can be responsible for the imbalance of this axis that leads to greater insulin secretion, which in turn induces a high secretion of IGF-1 and consequently a suppression of GH synthesis [49, 50]. According to Cornford et al. this condition can also develop following few days of overeating [47]. Since GH antagonizes the action of insulin, chronic alteration of its levels causes insulin resistance and reduces IR receptors [51]. At the same time, physiological insulin levels are necessary to control the expression of human liver growth hormone receptors and consequently regulate circulating IGF-1 levels (Fig. 2) [52]. Screening and diagnosis of insulin resistance

Comprehensive assessment and diagnosis of insulin resistance rely on a combination of clinical and laboratory evaluations, reflecting a progressive shift from conventional glycemic markers to dynamic and surrogate indices. Standard laboratory tests, including fasting plasma glucose (FPG), oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), and glycated hemoglobin, provide complementary information on basal glucose regulation, postprandial glucose handling, and long-term glycemic control over the preceding two to three months [57].

FPG represents a simple and direct measure, typically executed after an overnight fast of at least eight hours. Although universally accepted cutoffs remain lacking, fasting insulin levels exceeding 8 μIU/mL are commonly interpreted as indicative of hyperinsulinemia [58, 59]. The OGTT, when combined with insulin measurements, is a dynamic assessment facilitating early detection of peripheral or postprandial insulin resistance [60, 61].

Beyond these conventional approaches, the hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp (HEC) and intravenous glucose tolerance test (IVGTT) remain the gold standards for quantifying insulin sensitivity. HEC measures whole-body insulin-mediated glucose disposal by maintaining euglycemia through a variable glucose infusion during continuous insulin administration [62, 63]. The IVGTT offers an alternative dynamic method, bypassing gastrointestinal absorption and the incretin effect to provide a more direct evaluation of pancreatic β-cell function and peripheral insulin action [63, 64]. Simplified IVGTT-based indices have been proposed to improve feasibility for clinical and epidemiological applications [64]. Given the close interplay between insulin resistance and lipid metabolism, lipid-derived surrogate markers have garnered increasing attention. Ratios, such as triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol and composite indices like the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, have demonstrated strong predictive value for insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk, often outperforming fasting glucose alone [65–67].

Among emerging clinical tools, the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR) and the Adipose Insulin Resistance Index (Adipo-IR) have gained prominence. METS-IR integrates fasting glucose, triglycerides, body mass index, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to estimate insulin resistance without the need for direct insulin measurements [68]. Adipo-IR, calculated as the product of fasting insulin and free fatty acids (AdipoIR = fasting insulin × fasting FFA), specifically reflects insulin action in adipose tissue [63, 69].

Quantitative surrogate indices derived from fasting biochemical parameters remain widely used. The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and the Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI) leverage fasting glucose and insulin levels, with QUICKI demonstrating a more linear correlation with gold-standard clamp techniques across a broad spectrum of insulin sensitivity [61, 70, 71].

Protein and hormonal biomarkers also contribute valuable insight. Fasting C-peptide provides a stable measure of endogenous insulin secretion [72], while low adiponectin levels strongly correlates with systemic insulin resistance [73]. Sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) has emerged as an early marker of metabolic dysfunction, particularly in women, where reduced SHBG levels are often associated with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) [74, 75].

Collectively, these clinical assessments, laboratory tests, and surrogate indices offer practical, cost-effective, and complementary approaches for the evaluation, diagnosis, and monitoring of insulin resistance in both research and routine clinical settings [63, 76].

Hyperinsulinemia and cancer

The relationship between metabolic dysfunction and oncogenesis has become a focal point of modern epidemiological research. While obesity has long been recognized as a significant risk factor for various malignancies, recent evidence suggests that HI may serve as a driver of cancer development and progression [77]. By acting as a potent growth factor, insulin can stimulate cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis, creating an environment promoting tumor growth [78]. The following studies provide an overview on how HI correlates with increased risk and poorer outcomes across different cancer types. Lukanova et al. observed, in three cohorts of patients from different Nations, that chronic HI was associated with increased endometrial cancer risk (Odds Ratio, OR: 4.76, 95% CI: 1.91–11.8]) [79]. Similar results came from the meta-analysis by Formica et al. in which high insulinemia levels were found associated with increased risk of breast cancer recurrence (OR = 1.573, 95% CI = 1.009–2.453) [80]. Pandeya et al. found a 2.55-fold increased risk of prostate cancer in subject with higher fasting insulin levels (> 6.10 μU/ml) [81]. Hyperinsulinemia was also identified as the variable that significantly distinguished colon cancer patients with metabolic syndrome from patients without [82]. Gunter et al. performed a study in which they observed that women with fasting insulin levels falling in the top two quartiles were at higher risk of breast cancer regardless of body weight [83]. Tsujimoto et al. evaluated HI as cancer death risk factor in obese and non-obese population. They found a doubled risk of death in all cancer patients with HI as compared with those without. Moreover, they also found an HR of 1.89 in the non-obese population [84]. Moreover, Morales-Oyarvide et al. observed that patients with high dietary insulin load and index had worst disease-free survival, recurrence free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) than those with lower ones [85]. Wang et al. through a meta-analysis found a higher relative risk of all-cause and breast cancer mortality in insulin users as compared to non-users (HR: 1.52 and 1.33). They also found increased recurrence risk (HR = 1.43) associated with insulin use [86]. Overall, these findings highlight the critical role of insulin as a systemic mediator of cancer risk. Spanning from increased susceptibility to endometrial and prostate cancers to significantly higher recurrence rates in breast cancer patients, the data consistently point toward HI as a central clinical challenge. However, caution in the interpretation of this evidence is required, particularly regarding the different metabolic interventions administered to patients. The limitations of these studies rely on the fact that patients requiring insulin typically present with longer-standing diabetes, greater metabolic exhaustion, and higher systemic inflammation than those managed with insulin-sensitizers [88]. Indeed, a large prospective study on 3,649 non-diabetic patients with high-risk non-metastatic breast cancer failed to demonstrate a consistent survival benefit for metformin, suggesting a pivotal role of underlying patient’s metabolic fitness rather than the pharmacological agent itself [89]. This discrepancy highlights the inability to molecularly stratify patients, which precludes a precise assessment of tumor heterogeneity in retrospective cohorts. Conversely, prospective designs allow for the identification of specific biomarkers, such as IR-A overexpression and its hybrid complexes with IGF1R. Under systemic hyperinsulinemia, these molecular features can promote mitogenic signaling in malignant cells, suggesting the effectiveness of metabolic interventions related to patient’s molecular profile [90, 91]. In the view of precision oncology, another pivotal issue is the bidirectional interplay between HI and the tumor mutational status. Indeed, HI is increasingly recognized as driver of genomic instability and increased mutation rates [92, 93]. HI acts as a systemic selective pressure that preferentially supports the expansion of clones harboring specific oncogenic events, particularly those involving the PI3K/AKT pathway [94]. In this scenario, HI exerts a dual oncogenic effect. On one hand, it provides a sustained ligand-mediated stimulus that synergizes with oncogenic mutations to activate abnormal downstream survival signals. On the other hand, HI promotes the oxidative stress and metabolic shifts that lead to further mutational burden [93, 95, 96]. In these mutation-defined scenario, insulin-driven signaling can amplify replication stress and alter cell cycle checkpoints, facilitating further genomic instability and resistance to therapy. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that HI-induced feedback loops can effectively bypass the pharmacological inhibition of PI3K carrying oncogenic mutations, allowing tumors with high mutational loads to escape targeted therapies [94]. It is noteworthy that in cancers characterized by high mutational complexity, including GC, systemic metabolic dysfunction acts as a selective pressure that shapes clonal evolution and tumor heterogeneity [97–100]. Therefore, integrating metabolic control into the oncological workflow is no longer optional but a fundamental requirement for the success of precision medicine.

Hyperinsulinemia and gastric cancer

This evidence strengthens the role of HI as a systemic driver of multi-organ oncogenesis. To provide a comprehensive state-of-the-art, we reviewed the literature from the past 20 years examining the link between HI and GC. This neoplasia, strongly associated with obesity and diet, still represent a relevant clinical problem that needs deeper investigation [101]. Our PubMed search, conducted using the specific string ("gastric cancer") AND (hyperinsulinemia), identified 91 relevant papers. Among them, we focused on those distinguishing HI as the primary independent driver of gastric carcinogenesis, given its functional link to insulin resistance. Although insulin resistance describes the systemic metabolic state, it is the chronic elevation of circulating insulin levels that exerts direct mitogenic effects, promoting tumor cell proliferation and potentially influencing the gastric microenvironment. By focusing specifically on HI, we aim to clarify its role not only as a biomarker for risk stratification but also as a distinct metabolic target in the management of GC patients. Gastric cancer is characterized by a multifactorial etiology, which includes genetic and environmental factors such as diet, smoking, Helicobacter Pylori and Epstein-Barr virus infection [102]. It is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death [103], which advanced and metastatic stages have a 5-year survival of 5%−20% and a median overall survival lower than 1 year [104, 105]. Current treatment includes surgery with adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemo and/or radiotherapy [106]. Treatment failure and disease recurrence are still the major challenges in GC. Indeed, Jiao et al. observed that among 229 patients, 22.7% had a median time recurrence of 17.7 months. About 80% and 97% of recurrence occurred during 2 years and 5 years from surgery, respectively. The 42.6% of patients had distant metastases, 36.8% had locoregional recurrence, and 33.8% peritoneal recurrence [107]. Perseghin et al. from 1990–1991 subjected a survey to the population of Lombardy (Italy), the Cremona study, to collect information about presence of diabetes, fasting glucose and insulin concentration, body composition (weight, height and waist circumference), blood glucose, lipid profile, and blood pressure. After fifteen years, through the Regional Health Registry, they evaluated the vital status, the time and the causes of death. Overall, they recorded 495 death events, of which 221 were related to cardiovascular diseases and 180 were cancer-related. Individuals with serum insulin falling in the highest quintile had cancer mortality risk 62% higher. Moreover, gastrointestinal cancer mortality was 161% higher regardless of diabetes, obesity/visceral obesity, and metabolic syndrome [108]. In 2015, Hidaka et al. assessed whether plasma levels of insulin, C-peptide, and blood glucose were associated with GC risk. They performed a prospective study on 36,745 subjects 40 to 69 years old enrolled across 11 public health centers in Japan. They administered a questionnaire to the participants to collect information about lifestyle, sociodemographic characteristics, personal and family history, smoking habits, eating habits, and physical activity. In addition, based on diabetes plasma biomarkers (plasma insulin, C-peptide, and blood glucose), they calculated the HOMA-IR index and Homeostatic Model Assessment of Beta Cell Function index. Samples were collected between 1990 and 1995, and patients were followed up until the end of 2004. They found that plasma insulin was positively associated with an increased risk of GC as well as C-peptide and HOMA-IR, while no association was observed for blood glucose [109]. Heckl et al. analyzed 467 primary GCs obtained from patients who had undergone total or partial gastrectomy between 1997 and 2009 for adenocarcinoma of the stomach or esophagogastric junction to evaluate whether IR was expressed in cytoplasm or membrane of cancer cells (cCC-IR and mCC-IR) and vasculature (V-IR). They performed immunohistochemistry with antibodies directed to CD31, mucin 2, mucin 5, CD10, HER2 and IR (both isoforms). From these studies, it is observed that V-IR, mCC-IR and cCC-IR were present in 97.0%, 87.6% and 95.7% of GC samples, respectively. IR expression was also found associated with HER2 positivity. Moreover, patients with high V-IR and mCC-IR-high had lower OS and TSS than V-IR-low and mCC-IR-low [110]. Kwon et al., in a prospective case–control study evaluated whether insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was associated with early GC. They enrolled 63 patients, aged from 18 to 75 years, with early gastric cancer (EGC) and age- and sex-matched control group (1:1). They excluded all subjects who had active malignancies other than GC or a cancer history. At the end of the observation, through a multivariate analyses, they demonstrated that elevated hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and HOMA-IR were related to the development of EGC even in the absence of overt diabetes [111]. In addition, Amiry et al. examined the link between GC and both dietary insulin index and load in the Afghanistan population. They recruited 90 patients with newly diagnosed GC and 180 controls, both with age ranging from 20 to 75 years. Participants filled out a food frequency questionnaire containing foods or mixed dishes typically consumed by this population. Dietary intake, during the year before enrollment, of participants was examined in both GC and control group. At the end of the study, they highlighted the diet with a higher insulin potential as independent GC risk factor [112]. Saisana et al. in 2022 evaluated whether insulin and its receptor promote GC. Using samples from metastatic patients, they observed IR and IGF1R genes expression in all tissue samples, an association of their high expression with poor prognosis, and a differential expression of IR, but not IGF1R, in tumor as compared with normal gastric tissue. The insulin gene expression, on the other hand, was absent in the majority or it was weakly expressed [113].

To provide the molecular mechanisms by which HI promotes GC, we collected experimental evidence sustaining the key role of canonical insulin-related signaling pathways (Fig. 4). In particular, we focus on the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways, which are known to be regulated in GC through the overexpression of IR‑A. Li et al. demonstrated that, in BGC-823 GC cells, IGF-1 promotes EMT by ZEB2 transcription factor up-regulation, which depends on PI3K/AKT signaling [114]. IGFs were also found to promote vascular formation in vitro by inducing VEGF-A and -C expression [115]. Cells expressing a dominant negative IGF-R (IGF-R/dn) showed reduced activation of both AKT and MAPK pathway, with reduced vascular formation. Moreover, they demonstrated that the simultaneous targeting of IGF-R and VEGF pathway can be clinically useful. Saisana et al. have identified the IGF1-R as a critical survival and growth driver in triple-negative gastric cancer (TNGC). In models lacking common oncogenic targets (HER2, FGFR2, or MET), IGF-1 signaling activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to prevent apoptosis and the RAS/RAF/MAPK pathway to stimulate cell proliferation. Genetic knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of IGF1R significantly reduces tumor growth and cell cycle progression, particularly in KRAS-addicted or BRAF-impaired subsets [116]. In another work, they evaluated the effects of insulin stimulation on GC cell lines finding concentration-dependent autophosphorylation of IGFRs and increased AKT, ERK1 and ERK2 phosphorylation. Interestingly, IR inhibition by both drug or gene knock-down prevented cell growth and division, and promoted caspase-dependent cell death [113]. Survivin was identified as a crucial mediator of IGF-1-induced EMT in GC. IGF-1 signaling triggers the activation of the p-ERK and p-AKT pathways, subsequently upregulating survivin and EMT-related biomarkers, including N-cadherin, MMP2, and Snail. Clinical data confirms that survivin expression is prevalent in GC and correlates significantly with advanced tumor stage and metastasis. Notably, silencing survivin effectively abrogates IGF-1-induced cell migration and invasion, suggesting that targeting this specific signaling axis could inhibit the metastatic progression of gastric malignancies [117]. Elevated insulin levels have also been identified as a key driver of chemoresistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) in GC. Research involving SGC7901, MKN45, and MKN28 cell lines demonstrates that insulin significantly promotes tumor cell proliferation while simultaneously decreasing the cytotoxic efficacy of 5-FU. This resistance is mediated by the upregulation of P-glycoprotein (P-gp), a well-known multidrug resistance transporter. Clinical findings support this, showing higher P-gp expression in GC tissues from obese patients compared to non-obese patients, suggesting that hyperinsulinemia may diminish the effectiveness of standard chemotherapy [118]. Overall, these findings suggest that targeting the IGF1R axis offers a viable precision medicine strategy for GC patients who currently lack standard targeted options.

Role of hyperinsulinemia in gastric cancer tumor microenvironment

Beyond the described cell-intrinsic effects responsible of the malignant phenotype of tumor cells, HI stimulation mediates of tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling to promote immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic niche that reduces therapeutic efficacy [119, 120]. The specific roles of stromal cell populations, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), are central to this remodeling. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing has identified a subpopulation of IGF1+ CAFs that drive chemoresistance and recurrence by promoting the EMT through IGF1-α6β4 integrin binding [121]. This mechanism is strengthened by IGFBP7, which mediates a bidirectional communication between GC cells and CAFs, driving a more aggressive and infiltrative phenotype [122]. In detail, Zhao et al. through integrated bioinformatics analysis revealed that elevated IGFBP7 expression consequent to its low methylation status is associated with advanced disease stages and significantly poorer patient survival, marking it as an independent risk factor. This protein is involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and increased infiltration of TAMs [122]. Metabolic reprogramming also contributes significantly to immune evasion. Indeed, high levels of IGF2BP3 are associated with a worse prognosis, increased tumor infiltration, and the accumulation of lactate within the tumor microenvironment that impairs CD8+ T cells anti-tumor activity [123]. Additionally, CAF-secreted lysyl oxidase activates the TGFβ/IGF1 signaling pathway, which drives GC cell migration, EMT, and glycolysis. This process culminates in histone H3K18 lactylation, which promotes PD-L1 transcription and potential resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors [124].

Management of hyperinsulinemia in gastric cancer

The strong association between HI and GC fosters biomedical research to identify new strategies to reduce circulating insulin levels that influence the different cancer hallmarks. Both dietary and pharmacology approaches have been proposed. The first are based on ketogenic (KD) and low glycemic index/load (LGI/LGL) diets, the latter on the administration of insulin sensitizers. By lowering glucose and insulin, KD triggers hepatic ketone bodies (KBs) production. Healthy cells utilize KBs for survival whereas cancer cells are often unable to metabolize them due to reduced ketolytic enzyme expression and impaired monocarboxylate transporter function [131, 132], as schematized in Fig. 6. Dietary strategies to reduce circulating insulin levels in gastric cancer

Otto et al. treated GC mice models with two different dietary regimens: standard and KD. They found that mice fed with KD showed a reduction in glucose and insulin levels with delayed tumor growth. In particular, KD group had a strong tumor growth retardation in the first 20 days and in five cases tumors did not grow. In detail, the mean survival time of animals in the KD group was 34.2 ± 8.5 days, in the standard diet (SD) group 23.3 ± 3.9 days, and the tumors of the animals in the KD group showed larger necrotic areas (35.4%) than the tumors in the SD group (13.6% in median, p = 0.0074). They also observed a reduction of tumor vascularization in KD group than SD group [134].

Xiao et al. treated 110 GC adults male Wistar rats with KD alone and in the presence of different doses of curcumin (200 and 400 mg/kg) and Oldenlandia diffusa extract (ODE) (200 and 400 mg/kg). KD supplemented with omega-3 fatty acids and medium-chain fatty acids reduced levels of malondialdehyde, IL-1B, TNF-alpha, NF-kB, VEGF, AKT/mTORC1. Concomitantly, there was an increased activity of antioxidant enzymes and expression of NRF2 in the GC tissue. KD in presence of curcumin and ODE was more effective than KD alone [135].

On the other hand, the LGI/LGL diet is based on postprandial glycemia following carbohydrates intake. Indeed, postprandial glycaemia depends on glycemic index (GI), rate of blood sugar increase, and glycemic load (GL), amount of blood sugar increase, of a food or meal [136].

Bertuccio et al. assessed the risk of GC in relation to GI and GL through a case–control study conducted between 1997 and 2007 in the metropolitan area of Milan (Italy). The study observed a direct association of high GI and GL with GC risk. The odds ratios (ORs) in the highest vs lowest quintile comparison were 1.9 (95% CI: 1.0–3.3) for GI and 2.5 (95% CI: 1.3–4.9) for GL [137]. Similar findings came from the study by Augustin et al., in which ORs for upper three quartiles of dietary GL were 1.44 (95% CI: 1.11–1.87), 1.62 (95% CI: 1.24–2.12) and 1.94 (95% CI: 1.47–2.55) [138].

Pharmacological agents to reduce circulating insulin levels and their impact on gastric cancer

Kato et al. observed that metformin inhibited cell proliferation in GC cells both in vitro and in vivo in athymic nude mice carrying xenografts. They demonstrated the blockade of the cell cycle in the G0-G1 phase caused by a decrease of cyclins G1 and D1, Cdk4 and Cdk6. They also observed that metformin promoted the decrease of retinoblastoma protein, EGFR and IGF1R phosphorylation [139]. Han et al., in vitro on GC cells and in vivo on nude mice xenotransplants, observed that metformin selectively induced apoptosis in tumor cells, but not in non-tumor cells. They attributed this event to survivin levels reduction following increased AMPK phosphorylation and decreased mTOR phosphorylation [140]. Zhu et al. evaluated the synergistic effects of oxaliplatin and metformin in GC cells. They treated SGC-7901 and SNU-16 cell lines with oxaliplatin and metformin alone or in combination, observing a synergic effect on proliferation reduction and apoptosis promotion as compared with the two drugs used alone. In addition, drugs combination promoted a reduction in Bcl-2 levels [141]. Similarly, Yu et al. observed that metformin treatment, alone or in combination with other drugs such as cisplatin and rapamycin, significantly reduced tumor growth and metastasis. They demonstrated a concomitant increase of AMPK and PTEN activity with suppression of mTOR/p70S6K pathway and other genes involved in invasion and migration [142]. In 2024, Wang et al. observed that the administration of metformin significantly suppressed tumor growth in mice xenografts by decreasing phosphorylation levels of STAT1 and PRMT1 [143]. Asghariazar et al., observed that combined treatment with metformin and 5-FU had a greater cytotoxic effect than 5-FU alone by reducing the expressions of WNT5A, MDR1, P-gp and CD44 [144].

Another class of drugs that belongs to the insulin sensitizers and that gained attention of researchers are the thiazolidinediones [145]. Cheng et al. evaluated the effect of rosiglitazone (ROSI) in nude mice that had been injected with the SGC-7901 human GC cell line. Taking ROSI over 42 days led to reduction of tumor volume and decreased lymphangiogenesis as compared with controls [146]. Wang et al. observed that BGC-823 cells treated with troglitazone showed an up-regulation of NAG-1 as well as inhibition of cell growth [147]. Lin et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of the Taiwanese population that included 32,891 T2DM patients, observing that the OS was lower in patients who were thiazolidinediones users (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.43–1.26, p = 0.27) [148]. On the contrary, Chang et al. stated that thiazolidinediones was not associated with GC risk (pioglitazone OR: 0.68, p > 0.05 and rosiglitazone OR: 0.74, p > 0.05) [149]. Another FDA‑approved drug against T2DM is Canagliflozin, a sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, that compared with metformin monotherapy improves insulin resistance in individuals with T2DM [150, 151]. In the context of GC, this drug acts as a metastatic suppressor through inhibition of the well‑known oncogene HDAC6 [152]. The natural triterpenoid compound Pachymic Acid induces cell death in vitro and in vivo mainly through ferroptosis by downregulating the tyrosine kinase receptor PDGFRB, which is frequently overexpressed in GC [156]. The pharmacological effects of different categories of Cucurbitacin have been studied in GC, and it has been shown that these molecules not only inhibit the proliferation of GC cells, but also promote autophagy and apoptosis, and improve the response to chemotherapy by inhibiting the AKT, JAK2/STAT3, and CIP2A/PP2A/mTORC1 signaling pathways [157–159]. Isoliquiritigenin reduces GRP78‑mediated cancer stemness and acts by blocking proliferation and inhibiting the migration and invasion of MKN28 cells through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [160]. Gallic acid could represent a new immunomodulatory agent, as it has been shown to restore the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells, which are often dysfunctional in GC [161]. Co-treatment with curcumin/curcuminoids and chemotherapeutic agents significantly increases the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents in GC cells through the induction of oxidative stress and apoptosis [162]. Moreover, curcumin is under investigation in the NCT02782949 clinical trial to evaluate its role in preventing GC in patients with chronic atrophic gastritis or gastric intestinal metaplasia [169]. Berberine acts by inhibiting the IL‑6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway and induces cytostatic autophagy in vitro and in vivo by targeting the AKT and MAPK/mTOR/p70S6K pathways in GC [163, 164]. Chromomycin A2 has been shown in vitro to reduce GC cells proliferation and increase sensitivity to TRAIL‑induced apoptosis [165]. Kim WJ et al. demonstrated that dehydroabietic acid is a potential anticancer agent in GC cell lines, inducing apoptosis through decreased expression of survivin [166]. In vitro and in vivo, Wogonin has shown beneficial effects on GC by acting on proliferation, migration, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis through inhibition of the JAK‑STAT3 signaling pathway [167].

While direct clinical evidence in GC remains limited, these natural agents share molecular targets implicated in GC progression and in HI-associated carcinogenesis. Their capacity to reduce insulin levels, improve insulin receptor sensitivity, and modulate downstream oncogenic signaling, provides a biologically plausible rationale for exploring them as complementary strategies in GC prevention and treatment, particularly in patients with HI.

Meta-analysis of the association between metformin intake and gastric cancer risk and outcomes

Given the growing interest in the metabolic determinants of GC and the potential anticancer properties of antidiabetic agents, we sought to clarify the impact of metformin on GC incidence and clinical outcomes. After examining the biological rationale linking insulin signaling, insulin‑lowering strategies, and metformin’s putative antitumor mechanisms, we conducted a meta-analysis to quantitatively summarize the available clinical evidence. This approach allowed us to integrate hazard ratio (HR) from eligible clinical studies and provide an updated, comprehensive assessment of the association between metformin use and GC risk, recurrence, and survival.

We systematically searched PubMed Library for clinical articles evaluating the effect of metformin on the GC risk and patients’ prognosis from inception to May 2025. The search query used was: (“gastric cancer”) AND (metformin). Among the 114 articles retrieved, we excluded non-clinical (in vitro or murine models) and those lacking HR. Moreover, we also removed duplicates identified in other systematic reviews. Overall, the random-effects model meta-analysis sustains the protective effect of metformin use against GC risk, recurrence, and all-cause deaths. While substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed, publication bias was not significant. Specifically, GC risk was 33% lower in metformin users as compared with non-users with between-study heterogeneity of 73.61% (Q = 18.12, p = 0.001; Egger’s test: p = 0.91) (Fig. 7A, Figure S1A). A similar protective effect was found considering all-cause and cancer-specific mortality (Fig. 7C-D, Figure S1B-C), with HRs of 0.61 and 0.80, respectively. Heterogeneity for these outcomes was 97.15% (Q = 154.24, p < 0.0001, Egger’s test: p = 0.85) and 68.64% (Q = 15.82, p = 0.01; Egger’s test: p = 0.36), respectively. Regarding GC recurrence, metformin use significantly mitigates the risk of approximately 40% (Fig. 7D and Figure S1D), with no evidence of between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, Q = 0.51, p = 0.92; Egger’s test: p = 0.54).

Kim et al. retrospectively investigated this association in the 2004 Korean National Health Insurance Claims Database, randomly selecting 100,000 patients diagnosed with T2DM, regular metformin users or non-users. Patients diagnosed with GC before or within the first six consecutive months of metformin use were excluded. They observed that T2DM metformin users for more than 3 years had a significantly reduced risk of GC (adjusted HR: 0.57; 95% CI: 0.37–0.87; p = 0.009). Interestingly, they also found that GC risk was doubled in insulin users compared to non-users, regardless of taking metformin [170]. Similar results were obtained by Tseng et al. that observed a significant reduction of GC risk (HR: 0.45) in association with metformin treatment, even more the intake was longer than 2 years [171]. Cheung et al. investigated whether metformin reduced the GC risk in 7,266 H. pylori-eradicated diabetic patients. They demonstrated that metformin use was associated with a 51% reduction in GC risk in dose and duration dependent manner [172]. Seo et al. analyzed the chemopreventive potential of different drugs (aspirin, metformin, and statin) and GC. They observed metformin use, was not significantly associated with GC risk (HR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.59–1.23; p = 0.40). On the contrary, statin and aspirin use reduced risk of GC [173].

Metformin administration has been found to impact outcome of GC patients. Chung et al. evaluated the effects of metformin in patients with GC and T2DM post-gastrectomy. They recruited 2,400 patients with GC (1,749 without and 651 with T2DM) undergone surgery and subgrouped into metformin users and non-users. Metformin intake reduced the risk of cancer recurrence (25% vs 37%) and mortality in patients with GC and T2DM. In addition, metformin prolonged cancer-specific survival in patients with stage I-III GC and T2DM [174]. Lee et al., in a study involving 1,974 GC T2DM patients, observed that resected patients using metformin had a decreased recurrence, cancer-specific mortality, and all-cause mortality risk (RFS: HR = 0.864, 95% CI, 0.797–0.937; CSS: HR = 0.865, 95% CI, 0.782–0.958; OS: HR 0.870, 95% CI, 0.801–0.945) [175]. Lacroix et al., in a Belgian GC population, observed a reduction of all-cause mortality in metformin users, and that its use before cancer diagnosis was associated with a significant improvement in OS (adjusted HR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.57–0.98, p = 0.04) [176]. Seo et al., in a study conducted on 2,187, observed that metformin improved the survival of GC patients with T2DM with a remarkable effect in patients with T4 or N0 disease [177]. Baglia et al. enrolled 2,890 patients, including breast, colorectal, lung and stomach cancer. They found that metformin use was associated with better cancer-specific survival (CSS), while the use of insulin and sulfonylureas was associated with worse one [178]. Chen et al. evaluated whether metformin use and good glycemic control could reverse GC. They enrolled 1,692 patients with GC and observed that the 5-year CSS rate was 47.0% in the T2DM group and 58.0% in the non-T2DM group. Specifically, in the T2DM group, the metformin use subgroup was associated with CSS and progression-free survival greater than 5 years compared to the metformin non-users subgroup. In addition, the 5-year CSS rate was 47.5% in the good glycemic control subgroup and 44.1% in the poor glycemic control subgroup [179]. Dulskas et al., estimated survival in GC patients with T2DM who used several antihyperglycemic drugs. They enrolled 8,423 patients and observed that exposure to antihyperglycemic drugs showed no difference on survival of GC patients. However, survival analysis in antihyperglycemic drug users group revealed better survival for metformin users as compared with diabetic patients using sulfonylurea [180]. Zheng et al., in a study involving 1,140 diabetic patients who developed GC between 2005–2018, observed that the 5-year survival was 18.3% in metformin users and 9.4% in non-users. In particular, metformin users had a reduced risk of both disease-specific mortality (adjusted HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.67–0.93) and all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.68–0.90) [181]. In the FIESTA study, 3,012 patients with GC who underwent gastrectomy were enrolled. It was observed that antidiabetic therapies were significantly associated with improved prognosis and median survival time (120.9 vs 22.3 months, p < 0.001 for antidiabetics). Moreover, patients with GC who received metformin had a better survival compared to patients who received other antidiabetics (metformin HR: 0.16, other antidiabetics HR: 0.78) [182]. Cho et al. also highlighted the association of metformin intake and reduced GC mortality in diabetic patients [183]. Similarly, Joo et al. revealed that the use of metformin improved the prognosis of T2DM GC patients, reducing risk of GC-related and all-cause deaths (HR: 0.80, CI: 95% 0.78–0.82) [184].

Conclusions and future directions

The growing recognition of insulin signaling as a driver of tumor progression and chemoresistance suggests that lowering its circulating levels is a viable metabolism-targeted combination therapeutic strategy. However, clinical integration requires multifaceted, tailored approaches based on improved patient stratification and trials combining dietary interventions or insulin-sensitizing agents with standard therapeutic regimens. Currently, the principal barriers to clinical validation are the scarcity of randomized trials specifically in GC, as well as the disease’s intrinsic molecular and metabolic heterogeneity, which can result in highly variable therapeutic responses. Consequently, the synergistic roles of comorbidities and HI remain insufficiently addressed in clinical settings. This is particularly concerning given rising obesity rates across all age groups, which promotes HI-driven tumor aggressiveness and complicates the clinical management. In this context, dietary approaches aimed at reducing HI are increasingly relevant. Our meta-analysis confirms the association between HI and GC risk and outcomes, as well as the utility of metformin in patients with GC and T2DM. However, given the retrospective nature of current evidence, prospective randomized trials are needed. Such studies are essential to validate insulin-modulating interventions and to bridge the gap between metabolic dysregulation and oncological treatment strategies.
