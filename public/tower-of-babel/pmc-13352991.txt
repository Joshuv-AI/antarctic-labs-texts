All-in-one: a CGRP-encapsulated Rh-GelMA supramolecular dual-network hydrogel orchestrating immune-vascular coupling for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Xianglong Zhou, Jianhui Xiang, Hanhong Fang, Yang Liu, Fulin Zhou, Jiheng Xiao, Weicheng Chen, Haoran Zhou, Guohui Liu, Xiao Lv, Yiqiang Hu, Liming Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic wounds present a complex pathological microenvironment that severely impairs healing. To address this challenge, we developed a supramolecular double-network hydrogel (CGRP@Rh-GelMA) consisting of a self-assembled rhein network and an in situ UV-crosslinked gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) framework. This design endowed the hydrogel with strong mechanical robustness, wet-tissue adhesion, and sustained dual-drug release of rhein and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP). In vitro, CGRP@Rh-GelMA exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and potent reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging capacity. The hydrogel was also associated with reduced neutrophil extracellular trap (NETosis)-related markers, including myeloperoxidase (MPO) and citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit), as well as a shift in macrophage phenotype toward a reparative M2-like state, thereby contributing to a pro-healing immune microenvironment. In addition, CGRP@Rh-GelMA enhanced endothelial cell migration and vascular network formation in vitro, indicating pro-angiogenic potential. In a diabetic rat wound model, CGRP@Rh-GelMA accelerated wound repair, accompanied by improved immune microenvironment remodeling and neovascularization, resulting in enhanced granulation tissue formation, more organized collagen deposition, and re-epithelialization. Proteomic profiling further revealed inflammation-related pathway changes consistent with suppression of NF-κB signaling and NETosis. Collectively, these findings suggest that CGRP@Rh-GelMA is a mechanically robust and multifunctional biomaterial platform for diabetic wound healing by coordinately promoting immune and vascular repair responses.Graphical AbstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04491-2.

Introduction

Diabetic wounds represent a major global health challenge, with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) affecting approximately 19 million individuals annually. Nearly 20% of these cases progress to lower-limb amputation, making DFUs the leading cause of non-traumatic limb loss worldwide [1, 2]. This condition severely reduces patient quality of life and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden on healthcare systems [3].

Delayed healing in diabetic wounds is driven by a sustained inflammatory cycle rooted in immune microenvironment dysregulation. Persistent hyperglycemia promotes excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, triggering oxidative stress that disrupts cellular signaling and impairs tissue repair [4]. This oxidative stress sustains an abnormally high M1/M2 macrophage ratio and prevents the transition to the proliferative phase [5, 6]. Simultaneously, excessive neutrophil recruitment triggers neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETosis), characterized by web-like DNA scaffolds enriched with citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) [7]. By degrading the extracellular matrix and releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), NETosis amplifies inflammatory signaling, promotes a marked M1-like macrophage phenotype, and aggravates tissue injury [8, 9]. This sequential process establishes a self-reinforcing inflammatory cycle that markedly delays wound healing. Disrupting this loop and restoring immune homeostasis are essential for enabling functional regeneration.

Natural bioactive molecules have attracted significant attention in wound repair due to their multitarget activities and biocompatibility. Rhein, a natural anthraquinone derivative from Rheum officinale, inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation and scavenges hydroxyl radicals, conferring combined anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities [10, 11]. However, its high hydrophobicity results in poor aqueous solubility and short-term tissue retention, hindering sustained therapeutic delivery [12, 13]. To overcome this limitation, rhein was induced to self-assemble via intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions, leading to the formation of a supramolecular hydrogel [14]. This stable three-dimensional network markedly improves apparent solubility and retention, enabling controlled release for long-term mitigation of oxidative stress and inflammation.

Nevertheless, controlling inflammation and oxidative stress alone is insufficient for complete functional regeneration. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy reduces nerve fiber density and neuropeptide secretion, thereby impairing the neuro-immune-vascular regulation required for wound repair [15, 16]. Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), a key sensory neuropeptide, is significantly downregulated in diabetic wounds [17]. Previous studies suggest that CGRP may be associated with an M2-like macrophage phenotype and enhanced endothelial migration and proliferation through the CALCRL/RAMP1 receptor complex and related downstream pathways [18, 19]. However, its short half-life and rapid degradation limit sustained therapeutic delivery [20]. Meanwhile, although multifunctional hydrogels and advanced biomaterial-based drug delivery systems have been increasingly developed, many reported platforms still mainly emphasize structural optimization or simple co-delivery of bioactive agents [21–23]. Therefore, a hydrogel that integrates bioactive matrix formation, inflammatory microenvironment remodeling, and sustained neuropeptide-mediated repair may provide a more coordinated strategy for diabetic wound healing.

Based on these insights, we integrated CGRP into the rhein supramolecular hydrogel to achieve localized, synchronous dual-drug delivery. To overcome the mechanical instability inherent to single-network hydrogels under wound exudate and mechanical stress, we incorporated gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) via in situ ultraviolet (UV) photocrosslinking [24]. The resulting covalent network interpenetrates with the rhein supramolecular framework, markedly improving mechanical strength, anti-swelling capacity, and tissue adhesion [25].

The resulting CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogel combines structural stability with immune microenvironment remodeling and pro-angiogenic potential. Rhein is associated with suppression of NETosis and scavenging of ROS. CGRP supports a shift in macrophage phenotype toward a reparative state and enhances angiogenesis-related responses. Meanwhile, GelMA provides a robust and adhesive scaffold for sustained local delivery and wound sealing. This multifunctional double-network platform is therefore designed to modulate the pathological microenvironment of diabetic wounds and shows strong translational potential. Results

Preparation and structural characterization

To construct a multifunctional wound-healing platform with structural stability, intrinsic bioactivity, and sustained localized drug delivery, we fabricated a CGRP-loaded supramolecular double-network hydrogel (CGRP@Rh-GelMA) through stepwise self-assembly followed by UV-induced photocrosslinking (Fig. 1a). Rhein, GelMA, and CGRP were first dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using heating and ultrasonication to obtain a homogeneous precursor solution [14]. Upon cooling, rhein molecules self-assembled into an initial physically crosslinked network, forming a uniform and stable hydrogel. GelMA was then photocrosslinked under UV irradiation to form a secondary covalent network, yielding a composite hydrogel with enhanced mechanical stability. Macroscopic observation (Fig. 1b) showed that the rhein hydrogel formed a dark orange-red gel after gelation, whereas GelMA produced a clear and stable gel after photocrosslinking. The CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogel retained its intact dark orange-red structure without collapse under gravity, indicating good gel integrity.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Fig. 1c and Figure S1) further supported the progressive aggregation of rhein molecules over 100 ns. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) stabilized after the initial structural rearrangement, indicating that the assembly reached a relatively stable state. Meanwhile, the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and radius of gyration (RG) gradually decreased, suggesting reduced solvent exposure and increased compactness of the aggregates. The hydrogen-bond (H-bond) number remained at a relatively high level throughout the simulation, supporting an important role for hydrogen bonding in assembly stabilization. Together, these results indicate that rhein molecules can effectively aggregate in an aqueous environment within 100 ns. Intermolecular interaction analysis (Fig. 1d) confirmed that π-π stacking between anthraquinone rings, together with hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, were the primary forces driving self-assembly. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 1e) revealed rhein-gel nanofibers consistent with the MD simulation results, supporting the supramolecular structure. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra of Rh-sol, Rh-gel, and CGRP@Rh-GelMA (Fig. 1f) exhibited characteristic anthraquinone absorption peaks, verifying the retention of rhein’s functional groups after gelation.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed to further characterize the chemical structures of the hybrid hydrogel (Fig. 1g). Rhein displayed a broad O-H stretching band (~ 3442 cm⁻¹) and a carboxylic C = O band (~ 1706 cm⁻¹). GelMA showed a proteinaceous amide envelope (amide I near ~ 1650 cm⁻¹). In the hybrid hydrogel, the ~ 1700 cm⁻¹ band was attenuated, while a paired carboxylate signature emerged, accompanied by broadening of the amide I band. These changes indicate rhein deprotonation and ionic/hydrogen-bonding interactions with GelMA, thereby supporting the formation of a stable interpenetrating network. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging (Fig. 1h) revealed that CGRP@Rh-GelMA possessed a porous microstructure throughout the network. By contrast, the Rh/GelMA mixture before UV-induced photocrosslinking exhibited a less defined microstructure (Figure S2), further supporting the structural contribution of UV-crosslinked GelMA to the final double-network architecture. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and elemental mapping (Fig. 1i, j) demonstrated a uniform distribution of carbon (C), oxygen (O), and nitrogen (N) across the hydrogel, indicating homogeneous dispersion of both organic and peptide components.

To further evaluate the delivery behavior and matrix stability of the hydrogel, we examined its in vitro degradation and release profiles (Figure S3). Compared with GelMA alone, CGRP@Rh-GelMA exhibited a slower degradation trend, with significantly higher mass remaining at day 10, indicating enhanced structural stability of the double-network hydrogel. Meanwhile, both CGRP and rhein showed sustained release over 10 days, with CGRP displaying a slightly faster cumulative release than rhein. Notably, only limited mass loss was observed during the early stage, whereas appreciable drug release had already occurred, suggesting that the initial release was mainly diffusion-driven. As degradation progressed over time, gradual matrix erosion further contributed to the later sustained-release phase. These results support that drug release from CGRP@Rh-GelMA was associated with, but not solely governed by, hydrogel degradation.

These characterization results confirm the successful fabrication of a supramolecular double-network hydrogel with high structural stability, uniform composition, preserved bioactive functional groups, and sustained dual-drug delivery capability, enabling the localized release of bioactive therapeutic molecules.

Physicochemical properties and biocompatibility assessment

Macroscopic evaluation (Fig. 2a) revealed that the CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogel exhibited excellent injectability and reversible sol-gel transitions triggered by either mechanical shear or temperature variation. In the injectability test (Fig. 2a-1), the hydrogel was smoothly extruded from a syringe without clogging, demonstrating its suitability for minimally invasive application to irregular wound surfaces. In the phase-transition test (Fig. 2a-2), gentle shaking applied as shear force induced gel-to-sol conversion, while mild heating similarly transformed the gel into a flowable sol suitable for injection. Upon subsequent cooling or removal of shear, the hydrogel regained its original gel state. Incorporation of a UV-crosslinked GelMA secondary network further improved its macroscopic structural stability after photocrosslinking (Figs. 2a and 3).

Rheological characterization confirmed desirable mechanical properties. Shear-thinning behavior was observed, with viscosity decreasing as shear rate increased (Fig. 2b), facilitating injection while maintaining post-application mechanical integrity. Frequency sweep measurements (Fig. 2c) showed that the storage modulus (G′) consistently exceeded the loss modulus (G″), indicating that the hydrogel network was predominantly elastic. Time-sweep analysis (Fig. 2d) demonstrated stable viscoelasticity over extended periods, and step-strain tests (Fig. 2e) verified rapid structural recovery after repeated high-strain deformations.

Biocompatibility was comprehensively assessed at the cellular, hematological, and systemic levels. Hemocompatibility testing via incubation with rat erythrocytes (Fig. 2f) showed that all hydrogel groups—including GelMA, Rh-GelMA, and CGRP@Rh-GelMA—induced minimal hemolysis (< 5%), comparable to the PBS negative control group (Con) and markedly lower than the deionized water (DD water) positive control group, indicating acceptable blood compatibility according to the commonly used hemolysis criterion [26]. Cytocompatibility was evaluated using HL-60, RAW264.7, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays (Fig. 2g-i) indicated no significant cytotoxicity toward HL-60 or RAW264.7 cells in any hydrogel group. Notably, CGRP-loaded hydrogels significantly enhanced HUVEC proliferation compared with the other hydrogel groups, suggesting potential pro-angiogenic bioactivity. Live/dead fluorescence staining (Fig. 2j and Figure S4) revealed predominantly viable (green) cells with intact morphology across all groups, while quantitative analysis of survival rates further confirmed the good cytocompatibility of all hydrogel formulations. Histological examination of major organs by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2k) showed normal tissue architecture with no pathological changes, confirming systemic biocompatibility.

Consequently, these results demonstrate that CGRP@Rh-GelMA possesses excellent physicochemical properties, structural recoverability, and comprehensive biocompatibility across multiple biological levels. These attributes establish a robust foundation for its application as an advanced therapeutic wound dressing for diabetic wound repair.

Antibacterial activity and ROS scavenging capacity

Diabetic wound repair is impeded by two major pathological factors—bacterial infection and excessive oxidative stress. Persistent infection not only triggers amplified inflammatory responses but also drives pathological accumulation of ROS, critically impairing tissue regeneration [27]. Developing a multifunctional wound dressing with both potent antibacterial and effective ROS-scavenging capabilities is thus of great importance for restoring microenvironmental homeostasis and promoting healing.

We systematically evaluated the antibacterial effects of CGRP@Rh-GelMA against two common diabetic wound pathogens, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) [28]. Colony-forming unit (CFU) assays (Fig. 3a, b) demonstrated marked reductions in bacterial colonies in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group compared with the Con group, confirming broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3f, g) further showed that the inhibition rates against both MRSA and E. coli were significantly higher in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group than in the Con group. Live/dead bacterial fluorescence staining (Fig. 3c, d) revealed predominantly dead bacteria (red fluorescence) with markedly diminished green fluorescence after treatment, consistent with effective bacterial eradication.

This potent antibacterial effect is attributed primarily to rhein, whose anthraquinone structure disrupts bacterial membrane permeability and interferes with energy metabolism through multiple mechanisms [29, 30]. Importantly, the double-network hydrogel architecture facilitates sustained rhein release at therapeutic concentrations, providing long-term anti-infection protection throughout the wound-healing process.

Given the critical role of pathological oxidative stress in diabetic wounds, an inflammation model was established using lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages to simulate oxidative stress conditions. Fluorescent probe assays using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) were first performed: LPS-stimulated Con group cells showed strong green fluorescence, whereas hydrogel-treated cells exhibited markedly attenuated signals, suggesting high ROS-scavenging capacity (Figure S5). Flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 3e) further corroborated these findings: substantial intracellular ROS accumulation was observed in LPS-stimulated Con group cells, whereas the proportion of ROS-positive cells was markedly reduced in both the Rh-GelMA and CGRP@Rh-GelMA groups. Quantitative results (Fig. 3h) confirmed that the ratio of ROS-positive cells in the rhein-containing hydrogel groups was significantly lower than that in the Con group. Consistently, supplementary concentration-gradient experiments further showed that Rh-containing hydrogels reduced intracellular ROS levels in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure S6). As a natural anthraquinone derivative, rhein exerts antioxidative effects through multiple mechanisms, including direct scavenging of ROS, chelation of metal ions to inhibit Fenton reactions, and interruption of lipid peroxidation [10]. The double-network design preserves the phenolic hydroxyl active sites of rhein and sustains antioxidative activity through controlled release.

Consequently, these data demonstrate that CGRP@Rh-GelMA possesses potent, broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and highly efficient ROS-scavenging capability. By simultaneously addressing infection and oxidative damage through rhein-mediated antibacterial and antioxidative actions, the hydrogel establishes a favorable biochemical environment for chronic wound healing.

Remodeling of the immune microenvironment

Immune dysregulation in diabetic wounds is characterized by persistent activation of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, impaired transition toward a reparative M2-like state, and excessive NETosis. Together, these pathological features create a hostile immune microenvironment that is a key driver of impaired wound repair [31, 32]. We systematically investigated the effects of CGRP@Rh-GelMA on key immune cell phenotypes and its overall capacity to remodel the wound immune microenvironment.

The M1/M2 macrophage balance is pivotal for the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative repair phase. Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4a, b) revealed that LPS-stimulated cells in the Con group exhibited strong inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)-positive green fluorescence with minimal red CD206 signal, reflecting a prototypical M1-dominant phenotype. In contrast, both Rh-GelMA and CGRP@Rh-GelMA treatments markedly reduced iNOS expression and increased the proportion of CD206-positive cells, with CGRP@Rh-GelMA inducing a more pronounced shift toward a reparative M2-like state, indicating synergistic immune modulation between CGRP and rhein. Quantitative fluorescence-intensity analysis (Fig. 4e, f) confirmed that the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group significantly lowered iNOS intensity and elevated CD206 signal compared with the Con group.

Flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 4c, g, h) further validated these phenotypic shifts: CGRP@Rh-GelMA significantly reduced the proportion of CD86⁺ M1 macrophages, increased CD206⁺ M2 macrophages, and raised the M2/M1 ratio, representing a clear immune shift from a pro-inflammatory toward a more reparative phenotype. Notably, Rh-GelMA alone showed some M1-suppressive effects, likely owing to its antioxidative and anti-inflammatory activities. In contrast, incorporation of CGRP was associated with further enhancement of M2-associated macrophage marker expression, suggesting a shift toward a more reparative M2-like state in this hydrogel system. This combined regulation markedly improved the M2/M1 ratio and may help promote the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase.

Excessive NETosis is another major pathological driver of chronic inflammation and tissue injury in diabetic wounds [7]. To examine inhibition of NETosis, a phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-stimulated HL-60 cell model was used to mimic pathological NET formation. Immunofluorescence co-staining (Fig. 4d) revealed abundant MPO/H3Cit-positive web-like structures in Con group cells, consistent with the classical “DNA scaffold-protein modification” morphology of NETosis [33]. In contrast, the Rh-GelMA and CGRP@Rh-GelMA groups showed sparse, fragmented NET-like structures, indicating substantial inhibition of NET formation. Quantitative image analysis (Fig. 4i, j) demonstrated that both rhein-containing hydrogel groups significantly reduced MPO-positive and H3Cit-positive area coverage relative to the Con group. No statistical difference was detected between the Rh-GelMA and CGRP@Rh-GelMA groups, suggesting that rhein is the principal active component mediating NETosis suppression.

In summary, CGRP@Rh-GelMA remodels the wound immune microenvironment through two coordinated effects: (1) a shift in macrophage phenotype toward a reparative M2-like state in the presence of CGRP; and (2) suppression of NETosis-related changes in the presence of rhein. This coordinated regulation shifts the wound immune state from a pathological pro-inflammatory profile toward a more reparative profile, thereby reducing inflammatory injury and supporting downstream regenerative processes, including angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and re-epithelialization.

Promotion of vascular endothelial cell migration and angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is a pivotal process in guiding diabetic wounds from the inflammatory phase into the proliferative phase, as it directly determines the efficiency of nutrient delivery and clearance of metabolic waste [34]. The migratory, proliferative, and vascular-network self-assembly capabilities of endothelial cells form the fundamental cellular basis for new blood-vessel formation [35]. To comprehensively evaluate the angiogenic effects of CGRP@Rh-GelMA, we employed a multi-platform approach, including wound-healing assays, Transwell migration assays, Matrigel tube-formation assays, and immunofluorescence analysis of angiogenic markers.

In the wound-healing assay (Fig. 5a, e), no significant differences in scratch width were observed among groups at 0 h, confirming comparable baseline conditions. At 24 h, the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group showed a markedly narrower scratch width than the Con and GelMA groups, indicating accelerated migration onset. By 48 h, the scratch area in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group was nearly closed, whereas obvious gaps remained in the Con group. Directed chemotaxis was assessed by Transwell migration assays (Fig. 5b, f), in which CGRP@Rh-GelMA significantly increased endothelial cell migration through microporous membranes into the lower chamber compared with the Con group. Although Rh-GelMA exhibited moderate enhancement, migration was highest in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group, suggesting a synergistic role of CGRP in promoting cytoskeletal remodeling and directional motility.

In Matrigel assays (Fig. 5c, g, h), at 2 h, CGRP@Rh-GelMA induced rapid formation of well-defined tubular structures with significantly higher junction counts and total branch lengths than the other groups. At 8 h, treated cells displayed mature, highly interconnected vascular-like networks, with junction counts and total branch lengths markedly greater than those in the Con group. In contrast, the Con group formed scattered short tubular fragments lacking connectivity, whereas the GelMA and Rh-GelMA groups showed intermediate tubular network formation.

Immunofluorescence staining for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Fig. 5d, i) revealed bright, evenly distributed fluorescence in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group. Quantitative analysis showed that VEGF intensity was significantly higher in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group than in the Con group.

In summary, CGRP@Rh-GelMA significantly enhances endothelial migration, accelerates vascular-network formation, and upregulates VEGF, thereby supporting neovascularization and improved perfusion in diabetic wounds. Together with its immune microenvironment-modulating effects, CGRP@Rh-GelMA exhibits coordinated anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic activities that help ameliorate the ischemic and hypoxic microenvironment in diabetic wounds. These in vitro findings provide phenotypic and functional support for subsequent in vivo validation.

Acceleration of wound healing in a diabetic rat model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogels in vivo, we established a clinically relevant type 2 diabetic rat wound model. The model was induced by feeding rats a high-fat diet for 5 weeks followed by a low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) injection (30 mg/kg), according to a previously reported method [36]. Fasting blood glucose was measured on 3 consecutive days starting 72 h after STZ injection, and rats with fasting blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L for 3 consecutive days were considered successfully established as insulin-resistant type 2 diabetic models (Figure S7). A full-thickness skin defect (10 mm × 10 mm) was then created on the dorsal region of the rats (Fig. 6a), mimicking the pathological characteristics of chronic diabetic wounds.

Macroscopic observation (Fig. 6b, c) showed that wounds in the Con group exhibited slow healing, with persistent open defects. In contrast, wounds treated with CGRP@Rh-GelMA displayed the most rapid healing among all experimental groups: by day 3, the wound edges had already contracted, with evident granulation tissue formation and reduced exudate; by day 7, the wound bed was largely covered by newly formed epithelium; and by day 10, closure was almost complete. Schematic healing diagrams (Fig. 6d) depict the progressive wound-area reduction across days 0, 3, 7, and 10, clearly illustrating that CGRP@Rh-GelMA achieved the fastest and most complete repair compared with the other groups.

Histological analysis further revealed that CGRP@Rh-GelMA improved both the rate and quality of tissue regeneration. By day 10, H&E staining (Fig. 6e) showed that the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group exhibited a continuous and intact neoepidermis with multilayered squamous epithelium and early stratum corneum formation, underlain by dense and regularly arranged extracellular matrix (ECM) with minimal inflammatory cell infiltration. These histological features were more similar to those of normal skin than those observed in the other treatment groups (Figure S8). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated enhanced re-epithelialization and increased granulation tissue thickness in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group compared with the Con group (Figure S9). In the Con group, the neoepidermis was discontinuous and thin, and the dermis was loose and edematous, with abundant lymphocyte and neutrophil accumulation, indicative of unresolved inflammation. Scar-width measurements (Fig. 6f) showed significantly narrower scars in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group than in the Con group. Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 6g, h and Figure S8) demonstrated organized collagen fibers parallel to the skin surface, with significantly greater collagen deposition in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group than in the Con group. Compared with normal skin, the collagen architecture in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group more closely resembled a physiological arrangement, with denser and more regularly aligned collagen bundles, whereas collagen fibers in the Con group appeared disorganized and nodular, typical of pathological scarring.

Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6i-k) for angiogenesis-related markers revealed abundant CD31-positive microvascular networks in CGRP@Rh-GelMA-treated wounds, with significantly increased CD31-positive area relative to the Con group. α-Smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) staining showed higher α-SMA-positive signals in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group, indicating active wound contraction and ECM remodeling. Myofibroblasts are key cellular mediators of wound contraction and ECM remodeling, and their activation and recruitment are essential for effective wound healing [54].

In summary, CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogels exhibited outstanding therapeutic effects in the diabetic rat wound model. The in vivo results were highly consistent with the preceding in vitro findings, supporting that the hydrogel facilitates diabetic wound repair through coordinated regulation of inflammatory microenvironment remodeling, angiogenesis-related responses, and tissue remodeling, thus providing a robust experimental basis for its translational potential.

PCA score plots revealed tight clustering of biological replicates within each group (Fig. 7a), confirming high experimental reproducibility. A total of 969 proteins were differentially expressed, including 808 downregulated and 161 upregulated proteins; downregulated proteins accounted for more than 83% of the total. Volcano plots (Fig. 7b) highlighted a predominance of downregulated targets, suggesting that CGRP@Rh-GelMA primarily modulated inflammatory responses through suppressive changes in protein expression. Hierarchical heatmaps (Fig. 7c) demonstrated clear intergroup segregation, reflecting marked proteomic remodeling.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of downregulated proteins (Fig. 7d) identified significant enrichment in categories including protein-containing complex binding and NADPH oxidase complex. Since NADPH oxidase is a major source of ROS in neutrophils, reduced enrichment of this complex is consistent with attenuated oxidative stress and a potential reduction in early NETosis initiation. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of downregulated proteins (Fig. 7e) further revealed significant suppression of two key inflammation-related pathways, namely NF-κB signaling and neutrophil extracellular trap formation. NF-κB is a central regulator of inflammatory responses and controls the transcription of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. The neutrophil extracellular trap formation pathway is associated with the generation of cytotoxic extracellular traps enriched in MPO and H3Cit, which have been implicated in persistent tissue injury in chronic wounds.

Taken together, the GO analysis suggests a reduction in ROS-generating enzymatic activity, whereas KEGG enrichment indicates suppression of central inflammatory signaling and NETosis-related pathways. Specifically, NADPH oxidase-related activity, NF-κB signaling, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation were concurrently downregulated. This pattern supports an anti-inflammatory proteomic profile in the wound tissue. By reducing oxidative stress at its source, CGRP@Rh-GelMA may attenuate upstream triggers of NETosis. In parallel, suppression of NF-κB signaling may help limit inflammatory amplification, while reduced neutrophil extracellular trap formation may alleviate secondary tissue injury. Together, these changes are consistent with a shift in the wound microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory state toward a more reparative state. Reinforcing these findings, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the NF-κB signaling and neutrophil extracellular trap formation pathways (Fig. 7f) revealed significant negative enrichment, indicating that pathway-associated proteins were globally shifted toward lower expression levels. By capturing overall pathway-level trends rather than isolated protein changes, GSEA further supported the KEGG results and strengthened the evidence for broad suppression of inflammation-related pathways.

To further validate the proteomic findings at the tissue level, we performed immunofluorescence analysis of representative inflammatory markers. Macrophage phenotype assessment (Fig. 7g-i) showed significantly reduced expression of the M1 marker iNOS and markedly increased expression of the M2 marker CD206 in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group, indicating a shift in macrophage phenotype toward a more reparative M2-like state. Analysis of neutrophil infiltration and neutrophil extracellular trap formation (Fig. 7j-l) further supported the proteomic findings: the numbers of MPO-positive and H3Cit-positive neutrophils were significantly reduced in the CGRP@Rh-GelMA group, and neutrophil extracellular trap-like structures were markedly diminished. These in vivo tissue-level changes were consistent with the proteomic results and closely mirrored the in vitro expression patterns observed in Fig. 4, thereby linking pathway-level changes with cellular behavior and tissue phenotype.

Consequently, proteomic profiling systematically revealed inflammation-related molecular changes associated with CGRP@Rh-GelMA-mediated remodeling of the wound immune microenvironment. By coordinately modulating oxidative stress-, inflammatory signaling-, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation-related processes, the hydrogel was associated with immune changes favorable for tissue repair.

Overall, these integrated findings suggest that CGRP@Rh-GelMA promotes immune microenvironment remodeling through multi-level suppression of oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation. By reshaping immune-related molecular and cellular responses, the hydrogel establishes a microenvironment favorable for tissue regeneration. Together with the preceding in vitro and in vivo evidence, this work provides an integrated framework linking material design, molecular changes, and therapeutic efficacy, and supports the potential of CGRP@Rh-GelMA as a promising strategy for diabetic wound treatment.

Discussion

Chronic, non-healing diabetic wounds remain a major global public health challenge because of their highly interconnected pathological network, including persistent oxidative stress, sustained inflammation, immune microenvironment imbalance, and impaired angiogenesis, all of which ultimately stall tissue regeneration [31, 37]. Conventional therapies, constrained by single-target action or limited regulatory breadth, have proven inadequate for disrupting this complex loop, underscoring the need for a multidimensional and systemic intervention strategy.

To address this challenge, we rationally engineered a CGRP@Rh-GelMA supramolecular double-network hydrogel that remodels the diabetic wound microenvironment at the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels, thereby accelerating repair. The system first establishes a reduced-inflammatory background through broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, efficient ROS scavenging, and reduced MPO and H3Cit expression, which together are associated with suppression of NETosis and alleviation of inflammatory amplification. Building on this foundation, it was associated with a shift in macrophage phenotype from a pro-inflammatory M1-like state toward a reparative M2-like state, contributing to immune homeostasis; in parallel, it enhanced angiogenesis-related responses and microvascular network formation, thereby improving perfusion and wound closure. The hydrogel also exhibited excellent biocompatibility and in vivo safety, strengthening its translational potential. Notably, this platform is distinct from conventional co-delivery hydrogels in that it does not merely combine two bioactive agents, but instead couples early inflammatory microenvironment regulation with subsequent CGRP-mediated immune remodeling and angiogenic repair within a unified material system.

The core material innovation lies in its double-network structural design and the dual functional role of rhein within this system. Rhein molecules self-assemble via π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding into a supramolecular framework without chemical modification, allowing rhein to function not only as a therapeutic agent but also as a bioactive structural network that significantly improves apparent solubility and tissue retention, thereby enabling sustained release [14]. This approach preserves rhein’s inherent broad-spectrum antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidative bioactivities to the greatest extent possible [13]. However, diabetic wound environments, characterized by persistent exudate and mechanical stress, readily destabilize single-network hydrogels, causing premature swelling, erosion, burst release, and loss of drug efficacy. To overcome this limitation, GelMA was photocrosslinked in situ to form a covalent network interpenetrating the supramolecular structure. This synergistic architecture offers multiple benefits: (1) mechanical stability and conformal sealing—GelMA’s elastic modulus provides structural support, while rhein’s non-covalent “sacrificial bonds” dissipate mechanical energy, enhancing crack resistance while retaining injectability and self-healing capacity. Derived from natural gelatin, GelMA’s RGD motifs facilitate close tissue adhesion and “seamless closure,” suppressing interfacial micromovement and establishing a stable barrier. (2) controlled dual-drug release—refined pore-size distribution and reduced erosion rate limit burst release of rhein and CGRP, ensuring prolonged dosing and sustained regulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. (3) tissue-regeneration support—biodegradable GelMA provides a cell-adhesive scaffold that promotes infiltration, migration, ECM remodeling, and angiogenesis, while rapid in situ photocrosslinking enables clinical adaptability and patient-specific application.

Chronic diabetic wounds often remain arrested in the inflammatory phase, with excessive and sustained NETosis representing a central pathological driver [11]. While NETosis confers antimicrobial protection in acute infection, its prolonged presence in diabetic wounds causes extensive collateral damage [55]. Histones and proteases released during NETosis directly injure endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and the ECM; moreover, they act as DAMPs and persistently activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby reinforcing a “damage → inflammation → further damage” feedback cycle [56, 57]. NETosis deposits also form a dense physical barrier, hindering immune cell infiltration and vascular reconstruction, further delaying healing [38]. Mechanistically, NETosis is a highly ROS-dependent cascade: ROS bursts activate MPO, which triggers peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)-mediated histone H3 citrullination, leading to chromatin decondensation and extracellular trap release [42–44]. Natural bioactive compounds have drawn increasing attention for their multitarget NETosis-inhibitory activity. For example, Bletilla striata polysaccharides (BSP) suppress PAD4 enzymatic activity, reducing NETosis formation and improving lung inflammation in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [42]. Icariin inhibits PADI2-mediated neutrophil infiltration and NETosis, thereby restraining urothelial carcinoma progression [43]. Coptis chinensis-derived extracellular vesicle-like nanoparticles (Cc-ELNs) modulate neutrophil function and NETosis release, promoting intestinal mucosal regeneration [44]. These studies highlight the therapeutic promise of natural agents in NETosis regulation, although their mechanistic application in diabetic wound repair remains underexplored.

In this work, rhein, a lipophilic anthraquinone derived from Rheum officinale, aloe, and senna leaves, was selected as the core bioactive component because of its well-documented antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, antitumor, and neuroprotective properties [10, 13, 45]. Our data demonstrate that rhein effectively scavenges ROS, thereby helping attenuate NETosis initiation at its source. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed reduced MPO activity and H3Cit expression, accompanied by reduced NETosis-related changes and accelerated diabetic wound closure. Proteomic profiling further revealed pathway changes consistent with suppression of NF-κB signaling and NETosis, which may help disrupt the inflammatory feedback loop, reduce secondary tissue injury, and create a microenvironment favorable for immune reprogramming and angiogenesis. Notably, Xu et al. [46] reported that emodin, another rhubarb-derived anthraquinone, suppresses BCL-10/MALT1 complex expression, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activation and NETosis while preventing pathological overactivation of inflammatory and coagulation systems. This aligns with and reinforces our findings, suggesting a shared pharmacological basis among anthraquinone derivatives in NETosis regulation and offering theoretical support for incorporating such natural compounds into diabetic wound therapy.

Beyond inflammation control, diabetic wounds are further complicated by chronic hyperglycemia‑induced peripheral neuropathy and vasculopathy [2]. Long-term metabolic dysregulation damages peripheral nerves, diminishing local sensory and motor function, impairing immune regulation, and restricting blood supply, thereby stalling wound healing [47]. Recent studies underscore the pivotal role of sensory nerves in orchestrating immune modulation and angiogenesis during tissue repair [17]. CGRP, a key sensory neuron-secreted neuropeptide, has been reported to participate in immune regulation and vascular repair through the CALCRL/RAMP1 receptor complex and related downstream signaling pathways. Previous studies have suggested that CGRP may contribute to a shift in macrophage phenotype toward a reparative state and enhance endothelial proliferation and migration [47, 48]. In the present study, our in vitro and in vivo data showed that the CGRP-containing hydrogel was associated with reduced iNOS expression, increased CD206 expression, enhanced endothelial migration and tube formation, elevated VEGF expression, and increased CD31-positive microvascular density. These findings support the view that sustained CGRP delivery contributes to immunomodulatory and pro-angiogenic effects in our hydrogel system. However, the present study mainly provides phenotypic and functional evidence, and the precise receptor-mediated and downstream molecular mechanisms of CGRP action require further investigation.

Despite these promising results, further investigation is warranted. High-resolution single-cell multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics could help dissect the dynamic interactions between rhein and CGRP across immune cell subsets. Optimization of degradation kinetics may further improve microenvironment-responsive drug release and long-term safety. Integration of advanced imaging techniques may also enable real-time wound monitoring, thereby accelerating the personalized application of this platform.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a CGRP@Rh-GelMA supramolecular double-network hydrogel that achieves efficient diabetic wound repair by integrating immune microenvironment remodeling with vascular regeneration. This work establishes an integrated evidence chain, from material innovation and structural characterization to biological-effect evaluation and therapeutic validation, demonstrating the platform’s capacity for multidimensional regulation. The rhein supramolecular network preserves and delivers antibacterial, antioxidative, and anti-inflammatory activities, while CGRP supplementation is associated with improved immune-vascular repair coordination, a shift toward a reparative M2-like macrophage phenotype, and pro-angiogenic responses. The GelMA covalent network confers mechanical robustness, seamless wound sealing, and controlled dual-drug release, ensuring sustained therapeutic efficacy. By synergizing anti-infection, oxidative stress mitigation, inflammation suppression, immune reprogramming, and angiogenesis promotion, CGRP@Rh-GelMA promotes granulation tissue formation, organized collagen deposition, re-epithelialization, and durable wound closure. Its excellent biocompatibility and in situ formability further enhance its clinical applicability. Overall, this multifunctional hydrogel platform systematically reshapes the pathological network of chronic diabetic wounds, offering strong translational potential for precision regenerative medicine.

Experimental section

Preparation of CGRP@Rh-GelMA

GelMA powder (EFL-GM series, Engineering For Life, China) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to prepare a 10% (w/v) solution containing 0.5% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP). The solution was prepared at 37 °C in the dark to avoid premature photoinitiator activation. Rhein powder (MedChemExpress, China) was dissolved in PBS to obtain a 4 mg/mL solution, to which calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP; MedChemExpress, China) was added at a final concentration of 10 µg/mL. Mild heating and ultrasonication were applied to induce rhein self-assembly into a primary supramolecular network. Subsequently, the rhein/CGRP precursor solution was mixed with the GelMA/LAP solution and photocrosslinked under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation to form a secondary covalent network, yielding the CGRP@Rh-GelMA supramolecular double-network hydrogel.

Materials characterization

The macroscopic morphology of the hydrogels was recorded using a digital camera. Rhein self-assembled nanofiber morphology was observed via TEM (JEM-2100 F, JEOL, Japan). Hydrogel samples were freeze-dried, sputter-coated with gold, and examined by SEM (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) to evaluate microstructural and pore features. EDS was performed to determine elemental composition and distribution.

UV-Vis absorption spectra were measured using a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) over the range of 200–800 nm. FT-IR spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a scan range of 4000 –400 cm⁻¹ and a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.

The self-assembly behavior of rhein molecules was simulated using GROMACS 2021.3 with the CHARMM36 force field. The molecular configuration of rhein was obtained from the PubChem database and geometry-optimized using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-311G level [49]. Partial atomic charges were calculated using the Multiwfn program [50]. Topology files for rhein were generated via the ACPYPE web server [51, 52]. A cubic simulation box (10 × 10 × 10 nm³, ~ 50,000 atoms) was constructed containing 100 rhein molecules randomly dispersed in water, generated using Packmol [53]. A 1.0 nm cutoff distance was adopted for van der Waals and short-range Coulomb interactions, and long-range electrostatics were computed using the particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method. Following energy minimization, the system was equilibrated in the NVT ensemble at 298 K using the V-rescale thermostat and subsequently in the NPT ensemble at 1 bar using the Berendsen barostat for 100 ps each. A 100 ns production run was then performed under NPT conditions with a 2 fs time step. The trajectory was analyzed using Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) and GROMACS built-in analysis tools. In addition to structural snapshots at 0, 30, 60, and 100 ns, the RMSD, SASA, H-bond number, and RG were calculated to evaluate the dynamic aggregation behavior, compactness, and stability of the rhein assemblies in an aqueous environment.

Rheological measurements were performed using an MCR302 rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with 25 mm parallel plates at a gap of 1 mm. Tests were conducted at 25 °C unless otherwise stated. Frequency sweeps were performed at 1% strain over 0.1–100 rad/s. Strain sweeps were conducted at 10 rad/s over 0.1–1000% strain. Shear-thinning properties were assessed by measuring viscosity across shear rates from 0.001 to 100 s⁻¹. Cyclic strain tests alternated between 1% and 100% strain (each held for 60 s and repeated for three cycles) to evaluate structural recovery and antifatigue performance.

The in vitro degradation behavior of the hydrogels was further evaluated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. GelMA and CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogels with identical initial weights were prepared, lyophilized, and weighed to obtain the initial dry weight (W₀). At predetermined time points (1, 2, 3, 7, and 10 d), samples were collected, gently rinsed, lyophilized again, and weighed to obtain the remaining dry weight (Wₜ). The mass remaining was calculated as Wₜ/W₀ × 100%.

The in vitro release profiles of rhein and CGRP from CGRP@Rh-GelMA were determined in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C under gentle shaking. Hydrogel samples with identical volumes were immersed in release medium. At predetermined time points (1, 2, 3, 7, and 10 d), aliquots of the supernatant were collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh prewarmed PBS. Rhein concentration in the release medium was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu, Japan) according to previously described analytical conditions, and cumulative release was calculated based on the total amount initially loaded in the hydrogel. CGRP concentration was measured using a CGRP ELISA kit (ABE-GP5412, COBIO, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cumulative release percentage was calculated relative to the initial CGRP loading.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 murine macrophages (CL-0190, Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA).

HL-60 cells (human promyelocytic leukemia cell line, CL-0110) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, and the culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, CP-H082) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in Ham’s F-12 Nutrient Mixture (F-12; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and the medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, the cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin/EDTA solution (Gibco, USA) for passaging.

Biocompatibility

Peripheral blood was collected from rats and stirred gently with a glass rod to remove fibrinogen and obtain defibrinated blood. Erythrocytes were washed 2–3 times with 0.9% sodium chloride solution to prepare a 4% red blood cell suspension. Deionized water, PBS, and different hydrogel extracts were added to the erythrocyte suspension, respectively. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 60 rpm for 1 h. After incubation, the supernatants were collected by centrifugation, and hemolysis was observed and photographed.

Cells were seeded at 5 × 10³ cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured with different hydrogel extracts for 2 days. At the predetermined time point, CCK-8 (MedChemExpress, China) solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by measurement of absorbance at 450 nm.

Cells were cultured with PBS or various hydrogel extracts for 48 h and then stained with a Calcein-AM/PI staining kit (Beyotime, China) for 15 min. Under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), live cells displayed green fluorescence, whereas dead cells displayed red fluorescence.

Antibacteria in vitro

MRSA and E. coli were purchased from BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC, China). Bacteria were cultured in LB nutrient broth (Biosharp, China) at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm overnight until reaching the logarithmic growth phase. After culture, bacterial suspensions were washed twice with PBS and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for subsequent experiments.

A 100 µL aliquot of bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was co-incubated with PBS or various hydrogel extracts for 3 h. After incubation, the co-culture solution was diluted 500-fold, and 100 µL of the diluted suspension was evenly spread onto LB agar plates. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, bacterial colony growth was photographed and CFUs were counted. Colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ software.

A 100 µL aliquot of bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was co-incubated with PBS or various hydrogel extracts for 3 h. After incubation, bacteria were collected by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min) and resuspended in 200 µL PBS. The bacterial suspension was stained using a LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Viability Kit (L7012, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA): SYTO 9 (green fluorescence) labeled live bacteria, and propidium iodide (PI, red fluorescence) labeled dead bacteria. After staining for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, 10 µL of the stained bacterial suspension was dropped onto a glass slide and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). The ratio of live to dead bacteria was quantified using ImageJ software.

Macrophage polarization in vitro

After different treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and washed three times with PBS. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min, cells were blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. For iNOS staining, cells were incubated with rabbit anti-iNOS antibody (Proteintech, 18985-1-AP, China) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Phalloidin-iFluor 555 (red, Beyotime, C2203S, China) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, green, Beyotime, A0423, China) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. For CD206 staining, cells were incubated with mouse anti-CD206 antibody (Proteintech, 60143-1-Ig, China) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Phalloidin-iFluor 488 (green, Beyotime, C2201S, China) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647, red, Beyotime, A0428, China) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After nuclear staining with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, cells were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Macrophage polarization status was assessed by flow cytometry. Briefly, cells from different groups were washed with PBS, collected by pipetting, centrifuged (1000 rpm, 5 min), and resuspended in PBS. Subsequently, rat anti-CD86-PE antibody (ABclonal, A27409, China) or rabbit anti-CD206-APC antibody (ABclonal, A26785, China) was added and incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. After washing with PBS, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA).

After different treatments, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After washing three times with PBS, green fluorescence was observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), or fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ or FlowJo software, respectively.

Netosis production in vitro

After different treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and washed three times with PBS. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min, cells were blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, rabbit anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) antibody (CST, 14569, USA) and mouse anti-H3Cit antibody (CST, 14269, USA) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, green, Beyotime, A0428, China) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647, red, Beyotime, A0468, China) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After nuclear staining with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, cells were observed and photographed under a laser confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 780, Germany). NETosis area and fluorescence colocalization were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Angiogenesis in vitro

HUVECs were cultured in 6-well plates until reaching 90% confluence, and a uniform scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. The wells were gently washed three times with PBS to remove detached cell debris, followed by the addition of culture medium containing 2% FBS and different treatments. At 0, 24, and 48 h, scratch images were captured at the same positions using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). Scratch width was measured using ImageJ software, and the wound closure rate was calculated as follows: wound closure rate (%) = (W₀ - Wₜ) / W₀ × 100%, where W₀ represents the initial scratch width and Wₜ represents the scratch width at a specific time point.

A HUVEC suspension (2 × 10⁴ cells/200 µL serum-free medium) was seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size, Corning, USA), while 500 µL complete medium containing different treatments was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, China) for 20 min. After washing with PBS to remove excess dye, images were captured in five random fields under an inverted microscope, and migrated cells were counted using ImageJ software.

A 96-well plate was precoated with Matrigel matrix (50 µL/well, Corning, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow gelation. HUVECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/100 µL) suspended in culture medium containing different treatments were seeded onto the Matrigel surface. After incubation for 2 and 8 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, images of tubular structure formation were captured using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). ImageJ software was used to analyze the total branch length and number of junctions of vessel-like structures to quantify tube-formation capacity.

HUVECs (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded on gelatin-coated coverslips. After cell adhesion, the cells were cultured with different treatments for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and washed three times with PBS. After permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min, cells were blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, mouse anti-VEGF antibody (Proteintech, 66828-1-Ig, China) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647, red, Beyotime, A0468, China) and Phalloidin-iFluor 488 (green, Beyotime, C2201S, China) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After nuclear staining with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, cells were observed and photographed under a laser confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880, Germany). The relative fluorescence intensity of VEGF was quantified using ImageJ software.

Animal models

All animal procedures were performed in strict compliance with animal welfare guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology ([2024] IACUC Number: 4824). To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CGRP@Rh-GelMA hydrogel in vivo, a type 2 diabetic rat wound model was established. Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (180–220 g; Hubei Biont Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China) were acclimatized for 1 week before modeling. The rats were then fed a high-fat diet (60% kcal fat; Hubei Biont Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China) for 5 weeks, followed by intraperitoneal injection of low-dose streptozotocin (STZ, 30 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) freshly dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5), according to a previously reported method [36]. Blood glucose concentration was measured from tail vein blood using a blood glucose monitoring system based on an amperometric electrochemistry assay (Accu-Chek Performa strips and glucometer, Roche Diagnostics, Australia). Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was recorded at five time points: before high-fat diet feeding (day − 42), after 5 weeks of high-fat diet feeding (day − 7), and on 3 consecutive days starting 72 h after STZ injection (days − 4, -3, and − 2) following 12 h fasting. Rats with FBG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L for 3 consecutive days were considered to have been successfully established as insulin-resistant type 2 diabetic models, as further supported by pancreatic H&E staining and serial fasting blood glucose monitoring (Figure S7).

Two weeks after successful diabetes induction, rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). After shaving, the dorsal area was sequentially disinfected with iodophor and 75% ethanol. Full-thickness skin defects measuring 10 mm × 10 mm (including epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous tissue) were created on the rat dorsum using a sterile biopsy punch. The day of wound creation was designated as day 0. Wounded rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 3/group) for different treatments and then covered with transparent medical dressings (3 M Tegaderm, USA). Dressings were changed every 2 days, and observations continued until day 10. Digital photographs of the wounds were taken on postoperative days 0, 3, 7, and 10, and wound areas were measured using ImageJ software. The wound healing rate was calculated as follows: wound healing rate (%) = (A₀ - Aₜ) / A₀ × 100%, where A₀ represents the initial wound area and Aₜ represents the wound area at a specific time point.

Histological analysis

At the predetermined time point (day 10), rats were euthanized with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital, and wound tissues (including approximately 3 mm of surrounding normal skin margin) as well as major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and pancreas, were completely excised. Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections with a thickness of 4 μm for wound tissues and 5 μm for organ tissues were prepared using a microtome (Leica RM2016, Germany) for subsequent histological staining and immunofluorescence analysis.

After deparaffinization and rehydration, paraffin sections were sequentially stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated with 1% acid alcohol for several seconds, and counterstained with eosin for 3 min. After dehydration and clearing, sections were mounted with neutral balsam. Wound tissue sections were observed using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) to evaluate morphological changes, including epithelial regeneration, dermal remodeling, inflammatory cell infiltration, and neovascularization. Scar width (defined as the distance from the normal dermis at one wound edge to the contralateral normal dermis) was measured using ImageJ software. Major organ sections were scanned using a NanoZoomer® S360 digital scanner for panoramic imaging, and tissue structural integrity, cellular morphology, and potential pathological changes in each organ were evaluated under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) to assess the biosafety of the materials.

After deparaffinization and rehydration, paraffin sections were processed using a Masson’s trichrome staining kit (Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequential staining with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin, Ponceau-fuchsin, and aniline blue was performed. Collagen fibers appeared blue, nuclei black, and muscle fibers and cytoplasm red. The percentage of collagen deposition area was quantified using ImageJ software, and the orderliness of collagen fiber arrangement was evaluated.

After deparaffinization and rehydration, paraffin sections underwent antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, sections were washed three times with PBS. After permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min, sections were blocked with 5% BSA (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the following primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C: anti-CD31 antibody for the angiogenesis marker (Proteintech, 11265-1-AP, China), anti-α-SMA antibody for the myofibroblast-related marker (Proteintech, 14395-1-AP, China), rabbit anti-iNOS antibody for the M1 macrophage marker (Proteintech, 18985-1-AP, China), mouse anti-CD206 antibody for the M2 macrophage marker (Proteintech, 60143-1-Ig, China), rabbit anti-MPO antibody for the neutrophil marker (CST, 14569, USA), and mouse anti-H3Cit antibody for the NETosis marker (CST, 14269, USA). After washing with PBS, goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, green, Abcam, ab150113, UK) and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 555, red, Abcam, ab150078, UK) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After nuclear staining with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, sections were observed and photographed under a laser confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880, Germany). The positive staining area or positive cell number for each marker was quantified using ImageJ software.

For histological quantification, at least three representative sections were analyzed for each animal. For each section, three to five randomly selected, non-overlapping fields within the wound region were captured at identical magnifications. Quantitative parameters (e.g., scar width, collagen deposition area, and positive fluorescence area or cell number) were measured using ImageJ software. For each animal, values from multiple fields and sections were averaged to generate a single biological replicate. Group data were then calculated based on the mean values of three independent animals per group (n = 3). All image acquisition and quantification were performed in a blinded manner.

Proteomics analysis

On postoperative day 10, wound tissues from each group (n = 3) were harvested, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and finely pulverized to facilitate protein extraction. The purity of the extracted proteins was assessed, and protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Proteins underwent sequential reduction of disulfide bonds, alkylation to stabilize cysteine residues, and enzymatic digestion using sequencing-grade trypsin. The resulting peptides were purified and desalted using Strata X C18 solid-phase extraction columns (Phenomenex, USA).

Purified peptide samples were reconstituted in ammonium solution and subsequently subjected to nanoflow separation using an EASY-nLC 1200 liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The eluates were introduced into an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operating in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. Proteomic measurements and DIA data acquisition were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (China).

Raw mass spectrometry files were processed and quantified using Spectronaut software with reference to the UniProt Rattus norvegicus protein database. Differentially expressed proteins were identified according to a fold change ≥ 1.5 combined with p < 0.05. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses were performed using Gene Ontology (GO) classification and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping to explore the biological processes and signaling pathways involved in CGRP@Rh-GelMA-mediated wound repair.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For in vitro experiments, n represents the number of independent biological replicates, each performed using independently prepared cell cultures and hydrogel samples. Multiple technical measurements (e.g., different fields of view or wells) were averaged within each biological replicate before statistical analysis. For in vivo experiments, n represents the number of independent animals per group. For proteomic analysis, three independent biological samples were analyzed per group. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test.
