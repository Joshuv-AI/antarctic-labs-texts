Dissociable Handwriting Domains in Parkinson's Disease?
Authors: Beyza Nur Cetin, Selcuk Comoglu, Aycan Cemil Ulker, Halil Onder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHandwriting abnormalities in Parkinson's disease (PD) extend beyond classical micrographia and may involve multiple graphomotor domains with distinct clinical and mechanistic correlates.ObjectivesTo investigate whether distinct handwriting domains demonstrate dissociable relationships with differential diagnostic performance, cognitive function, motor severity, and dopaminergic responsiveness.MethodsWe evaluated tablet‐based handwriting kinematics in 60 PD and 58 essential tremor (ET) patients using a standardized sequential writing task performed on a digital tablet. Spatial, temporal, pressure‐related, and decrement measures were analyzed together with cognitive performance, levodopa responsiveness, and multivariate clinical associations.ResultsCompared with ET, PD patients demonstrated significantly smaller final‐segment handwriting area (991 ± 582 vs. 1623 ± 710, p < 0.001, d = 0.97), lower mean pressure (0.69 ± 0.17 vs. 0.81 ± 0.13, p < 0.001, d = 0.80), and higher prevalence of progressive reduction ≥10% (58.3% vs. 10.3%, OR = 12.13). Area ratio showed the strongest univariate discriminative performance (AUC = 0.814), whereas multivariate regression identified final‐segment area (β = −0.52, p < 0.001) and mean pressure (β = −0.31, p = 0.003), but not decrement measures, as independent predictors of PD. Within the PD group, smaller final‐segment handwriting area independently correlated with greater motor severity (β = −0.39, p = 0.004) and poorer visuospatial performance (β = +0.27, p = 0.028), whereas pressure‐related measures demonstrated greater levodopa responsiveness. In contrast, decrement measures showed comparatively weak independent associations with motor severity, cognition, and dopaminergic responsiveness.ConclusionsHandwriting abnormalities in PD appear multidimensional and demonstrate partially dissociable relationships with motor severity, visuospatial performance, levodopa responsiveness, and differential diagnostic performance. These findings suggest that different handwriting domains may reflect complementary aspects of graphomotor dysfunction rather than a unitary phenomenon.

Parkinson's disease (PD) is characterized by progressive motor dysfunction involving bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and impaired motor automaticity. Among fine motor abnormalities, handwriting impairment represents one of the most recognizable and clinically relevant manifestations of PD. Although micrographia is not included in formal diagnostic criteria, it has long been considered a characteristic feature of PD and may occasionally precede more overt motor symptoms. 1 , 2 , 3

Traditionally, micrographia has been conceptualized primarily as a reduction in writing size. 1 However, increasing evidence suggests that handwriting abnormalities in PD extend beyond simple spatial decrement and involve multiple partially dissociable kinematic domains, including temporal slowing, reduced pressure generation, impaired motor scaling, and progressive decrement across sequential movements. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 These observations support the emerging view that graphomotor dysfunction in PD may reflect complex abnormalities involving motor execution, movement sustainability, and sensorimotor integration rather than a unitary phenomenon. 1 , 5 , 7 , 8

Recent advances in tablet‐based handwriting analysis have enabled objective quantification of fine motor performance with high temporal and spatial precision. 1 , 5 , 9 Previous studies demonstrated that digital handwriting parameters may distinguish PD from healthy controls and potentially reflect disease‐related motor dysfunction. 8 , 9 , 10 However, several important limitations remain. Most prior studies focused primarily on comparisons with healthy controls, relied on relatively limited feature sets, or evaluated handwriting abnormalities as a single construct. 8 , 9 , 10 Furthermore, the relationships between distinct handwriting domains, dopaminergic responsiveness, cognitive correlates, and differential diagnosis remain incompletely understood. 3 , 6 , 8

Progressive micrographia has attracted particular interest as a potentially characteristic feature of PD. 4 , 9 , 11 Although several studies reported promising discriminative performance of progressive decrement measures, 8 , 9 the clinical and pathophysiological significance of these abnormalities remains uncertain. Previous reports suggested that progressive micrographia may demonstrate relatively weak associations with overall motor severity and variable responsiveness to levodopa therapy, 6 , 8 , 9 raising the possibility that sequential decrement phenomena may reflect partially nondopaminergic or higher‐order motor control mechanisms.

Another important limitation of the literature is the relative scarcity of studies comparing PD with essential tremor (ET), despite ET representing one of the most common differential diagnostic challenges in movement disorders practice. 8 While several studies have investigated handwriting abnormalities in PD, most primarily focused on comparisons with healthy controls, whereas relatively fewer studies evaluated the potential role of handwriting analysis in differentiating PD from ET under clinically relevant diagnostic conditions. 8 , 9

In this context, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate multidimensional handwriting kinematics in PD and ET using a standardized tablet‐based paradigm. Specifically, we investigated spatial, temporal, pressure‐related, and sequential decrement measures together with their relationships to clinical severity, cognitive performance, and levodopa responsiveness. We further explored whether distinct handwriting domains demonstrate partially dissociable clinical and mechanistic profiles, potentially reflecting complementary aspects of graphomotor dysfunction in PD.

Methods

This cross‐sectional observational study was conducted at a tertiary movement disorders center and included patients diagnosed with Parkinson's disease (PD) and essential tremor (ET). All patients with Parkinson's disease fulfilled the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for Clinically Established Parkinson's disease. 12 Essential tremor was diagnosed according to the 2018 International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society Consensus Statement on the Classification of Tremors. 13 Only patients fulfilling the criteria for essential tremor were included; patients classified as ET plus were excluded. The study was approved by the Ankara Etlik City Hospital Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to clinical and handwriting assessments. A total of 118 participants were enrolled, comprising 60 patients with PD and 58 with ET. All participants were evaluated by neurologists specialized in movement disorders. Baseline demographic, cognitive, and handwriting characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

Motor severity in PD patients was assessed using the MDS‐UPDRS Part III both in the practically defined OFF state, following at least 12 h of dopaminergic medication withdrawal, and in the ON state after administration of a suprathreshold levodopa dose sufficient to achieve a clinically meaningful motor response. 14

All participants underwent a standardized neuropsychological assessment including the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), 15 forward and backward digit span, the Stroop test, 16 and the Judgment of Line Orientation (JLO) test, 17 assessing global cognition, attention/working memory, executive function, and visuospatial abilities, respectively. Neuropsychological testing was performed under clinically appropriate conditions to ensure adequate patient cooperation and valid test performance, rather than being restricted to a predefined medication state. Detailed neuropsychological assessment results are additionally presented in Table S1.

Handwriting performance was evaluated using a digital graphic tablet (Wacom Intuos Pro‐Large) and a stylus pen. Participants were instructed to write a standardized sequence of 20 consecutive lowercase “e” letters. For each letter, quantitative parameters including horizontal size, vertical size, area, writing duration, and pressure were automatically extracted using dedicated software as previously described. 18 These parameters were analyzed across three segments: the initial segment (first five letters), the final segment (last five letters), and the entire writing sequence (20 letters). Derived measures included area ratio (initial segment/final segment) and the presence of progressive reduction (≥10%). The principal handwriting domains were predefined as final‐segment spatial impairment, pressure‐related abnormalities, and progressive decrement phenomena for subsequent multivariable analyses and conceptual interpretation.

Group comparisons between PD and ET were performed using appropriate parametric or non‐parametric tests, and effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d or odds ratios. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of handwriting parameters (Table 2). Independent predictors of PD were subsequently assessed using multivariate logistic regression analysis including last five letters area, mean pressure, area ratio, and age (Table 3). Variables included in the multivariable model were selected a priori based on the predefined principal handwriting domains (final‐segment area, pressure, and area ratio), together with age as a potential confounder. Progressive reduction was not entered because it represents a dichotomized form of the area ratio. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R 2.

To further explore the potentially dissociable clinical and mechanistic correlates of the principal handwriting domains, a hypothesis‐driven multivariable linear regression model was constructed within the PD group using final‐segment handwriting area as the dependent variable and motor severity, visuospatial function, executive function, and age as independent variables (Table S4).

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26. Data distribution was assessed prior to selecting parametric or nonparametric tests. Group comparisons were summarized using effect sizes (Cohen's d or odds ratios, where appropriate). Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman coefficients with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Multivariable logistic and linear regression analyses were hypothesis‐driven, with predictor variables selected a priori. Multicollinearity (VIF) and model fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R 2 for logistic regression) were assessed. ROC analyses were internally validated using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

PD and ET groups did not differ significantly regarding age (61.6 ± 13.4 vs. 59.1 ± 11.2 years, p = 0.29) or global cognitive performance (Table 1). Disease duration was longer in ET (9.2 ± 7.8 vs. 5.7 ± 4.9 years, p = 0.031, d = 0.54), whereas MMSE, verbal fluency, and executive function measures were comparable between groups. Extended neuropsychological testing similarly revealed no significant differences across attention, memory, or processing speed domains (Table S1).

Core handwriting measures demonstrated substantial between‐group differences (Table 1). Compared with ET, PD patients exhibited significantly smaller last five letters area (991 ± 582 vs. 1623 ± 710, p < 0.001, d = 0.97), lower mean pressure (0.69 ± 0.17 vs. 0.81 ± 0.13, p < 0.001, d = 0.80), and markedly higher prevalence of progressive reduction ≥10% (58.3% vs. 10.3%, OR = 12.13). Area ratio was also significantly increased in PD (1.66 ± 3.41 vs. 0.86 ± 0.61, p < 0.001, d = 0.32).

ROC analysis identified area ratio as the strongest univariate discriminator between PD and ET (AUC = 0.814, 95% CI: 0.74–0.88), followed by last five letters area (AUC = 0.773) and last five horizontal size (AUC = 0.766) (Table 2). Progressive reduction ≥10% demonstrated high specificity (89.7%) but lower sensitivity (58.3%). Pressure‐related measures showed moderate discriminative performance (AUC = 0.729–0.737). Stratified bootstrap internal validation using 1000 resamples yielded closely comparable AUC estimates, supporting the robustness of the principal discriminative findings (Table 2). Importantly, among patients with mild PD (UPDRS ≤32), area ratio retained good diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.77, p = 0.0003) (Table S2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that last five letters area (standardized β = −0.52, p < 0.001) and mean pressure (β = −0.31, p = 0.003) independently differentiated PD from ET, whereas area ratio did not retain independent significance after adjustment (β = +0.18, p = 0.118). Age did not significantly contribute to the model (β = +0.04, p = 0.641). Model diagnostics demonstrated acceptable model performance without evidence of problematic multicollinearity (Table 3).

Within the PD group, levodopa administration predominantly improved pressure‐related handwriting measures (Table 4). Mean pressure increased from 0.69 ± 0.17 to 0.75 ± 0.15 (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.49), while last five pressure increased from 0.70 ± 0.17 to 0.75 ± 0.15 (p < 0.001, d = 0.45). In contrast, size‐related measures demonstrated comparatively limited responsiveness, with only modest improvement observed in last five letters area (991 ± 582 vs. 1093 ± 604, p = 0.047, d = 0.17). Area ratio showed only small changes after levodopa administration (1.66 ± 3.41 vs. 1.06 ± 0.32, p = 0.015, d = 0.21), whereas progressive reduction frequency itself did not significantly change (p = 0.286).

Correlation analyses demonstrated that smaller handwriting size was associated with greater motor severity, particularly regarding last five horizontal size and UPDRS‐III total score (ρ = −0.46, p = 0.0002), axial severity (ρ = −0.44, p = 0.0004), and tremor burden (ρ = −0.41, p = 0.0010) (Table S3). Longer writing duration correlated with greater motor impairment (ρ = +0.37, p = 0.0040). Additionally, visuospatial performance measured by JLO correlated with writing duration parameters (ρ = −0.27 to −0.29), whereas age demonstrated modest associations with reduced handwriting size (ρ = −0.28 to −0.31). The principal associations remained significant after false discovery rate adjustment.

Within the PD group, multivariate linear regression analysis demonstrated that greater motor severity independently predicted smaller final‐segment handwriting area (β = −0.39, p = 0.004), whereas better visuospatial performance (JLO) independently predicted relatively preserved final‐segment handwriting area (β = +0.27, p = 0.028). Age (β = −0.21, p = 0.071) and Stroop duration (β = −0.14, p = 0.184) showed weaker associations. The overall model demonstrated moderate explanatory performance (adjusted R 2 = 0.31). Model diagnostics demonstrated no evidence of problematic multicollinearity (Table S4).

In contrast, exploratory regression analyses using area ratio as the dependent variable demonstrated comparatively weak explanatory performance (adjusted R 2 = 0.09), and no investigated clinical or cognitive variable emerged as a consistent independent predictor. Together with the differential levodopa responsiveness and distinct clinical associations observed across handwriting domains, these findings support partially dissociable clinical and mechanistic correlates underlying final‐segment size reduction, pressure modulation, and sequence‐level decrement phenomena, as conceptually summarized in Figure 1.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that handwriting abnormalities in PD are multidimensional rather than representing a unitary manifestation of micrographia. Although sequential decrement measures provided the strongest univariate discrimination between PD and ET, they did not remain independently associated with PD after multivariable adjustment and showed comparatively limited relationships with motor severity, cognition, and levodopa responsiveness. In contrast, final‐segment handwriting area and pressure‐related measures demonstrated more coherent clinical associations, linking motor severity, visuospatial performance, and dopaminergic responsiveness. Collectively, these findings suggest that different handwriting domains provide complementary clinical information rather than simply representing different measures of the same graphomotor abnormality. Progressive micrographia has traditionally been regarded as one of the most characteristic handwriting abnormalities in PD since the original descriptions of micrographia by McLennan et al. 2 Subsequent tablet‐based studies further demonstrated that sequential decrement measures may provide meaningful discriminative information for differentiating PD from healthy controls and other movement disorders. 9 , 19 However, the clinical and pathophysiological significance of progressive decrement phenomena remains incompletely understood, with previous studies reporting heterogeneous associations with motor severity and dopaminergic responsiveness. 3 , 6 Notably, Eklund et al found that although progressive micrographia contributed to differentiating PD from ET, decrement‐related measures were not associated with striatal DAT‐SPECT binding, while spatial handwriting measures demonstrated stronger discriminative performance. 8 Similarly, Zham et al reported relatively limited levodopa responsiveness of progressive micrographia measures compared with other handwriting parameters. 6 Our findings are consistent with these observations. Although progressive decrement measures demonstrated strongest univariate discriminative performance, they lost independent significance in multivariate differential diagnostic models and were not independently associated with motor severity, cognition, or levodopa responsiveness.

Collectively, these findings suggest that progressive micrographia may have greater diagnostic than clinical relevance. However, an alternative, and not mutually exclusive, interpretation is that the observed differences between handwriting measures reflect different components of the broader bradykinesia construct rather than truly independent graphomotor abnormalities. Bradykinesia encompasses abnormalities in movement speed and amplitude as well as sequence effects, and these components may vary across patients and respond differently to dopaminergic treatment. 20 , 21 Progressive micrographia may particularly represent a manifestation of the sequence effect, which appears less consistently responsive to levodopa than movement speed or amplitude. 6 , 21 Thus, our findings should be interpreted as evidence of partially differentiated, but potentially overlapping, components of the broader bradykinetic phenotype.

Previous neuroimaging studies have implicated supplementary motor, cerebellar, and basal ganglia networks in progressive micrographia. 5 , 22 Given that these networks have also been implicated in motor sequencing and gait automaticity in PD, future studies may determine whether progressive micrographia is more closely associated with specific sequence‐dependent motor functions, including axial manifestations, than with overall motor severity. 23 , 24

In contrast to progressive decrement measures, final‐segment handwriting area showed more consistent associations with clinically relevant outcomes, including motor severity and visuospatial performance. Smaller final‐segment area was independently associated with higher UPDRS‐III scores, whereas better JLO performance predicted relatively preserved handwriting size during the later phases of writing execution. These findings suggest that preservation of handwriting amplitude during sustained writing depends on multiple functional domains rather than motor impairment alone. The independent association with JLO performance raises the possibility that visuospatial processes contribute to maintaining writing size throughout sequential handwriting.

The relationship between micrographia and disease severity has also been inconsistent across previous studies. While some investigations demonstrated associations between reduced handwriting size and bradykinesia or overall motor severity, 3 , 10 others reported relatively weak or inconsistent correlations between global micrographia measures and clinical severity indices. 8 , 9 One possible explanation for these discrepancies may relate to methodological differences in how handwriting amplitude is quantified. Average or global handwriting area may be substantially influenced by interindividual baseline writing habits, motivational factors, compensatory effort, attentional modulation, age‐related variability, or technical task‐related fluctuations during the initial phases of writing execution. 25 In contrast, our findings suggest that evaluating handwriting performance during the later phases of sequential execution may provide a more clinically informative measure of sustained motor scaling impairment. This methodological distinction may partly explain the heterogeneous associations between micrographia and disease severity reported in previous studies.

The association between final‐segment area and JLO performance is particularly noteworthy. Handwriting production is a complex process requiring continuous visuospatial monitoring, online motor adjustment, and integration of perceptual and motor planning networks. 22 Previous conceptual frameworks similarly suggested that PD‐related handwriting abnormalities may involve higher‐order perceptual‐motor processes in addition to pure bradykinetic impairment. 22 Our findings are consistent with this concept and raise the possibility that preservation of visuospatial abilities contributes to more stable maintenance of writing amplitude during prolonged sequential execution. Interestingly, progressive decrement measures did not demonstrate similar cognitive associations, further supporting the possibility that distinct handwriting domains may reflect partially different functional processes.

In contrast to progressive decrement measures, pressure‐related abnormalities demonstrated clearer responsiveness to dopaminergic modulation. Previous studies investigating levodopa effects on handwriting primarily focused on spatial or temporal kinematic parameters, whereas pressure‐related abnormalities have largely been evaluated for their diagnostic utility rather than their treatment responsiveness. 3 , 6 , 26 , 27 Although Eklund et al. examined handwriting abnormalities in relation to nigrostriatal dopaminergic integrity using DAT‐SPECT, and Zham et al evaluated ON–OFF changes in spatial handwriting measures, neither study specifically addressed levodopa‐related changes in pressure dynamics. 6 , 8 In our cohort, levodopa administration was associated with significant improvement in pressure‐related measures, whereas progressive decrement indices remained comparatively unchanged. These findings raise the possibility that pressure regulation is more closely related to dopaminergic motor function than progressive decrement. More broadly, the differential responses of individual handwriting domains to levodopa suggest that quantitative handwriting analysis may help characterize distinct functional components of graphomotor impairment in PD.

Taken together, our findings and the existing literature support a multidomain framework of PD‐related graphomotor dysfunction, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1. Previous conceptual frameworks have suggested that handwriting impairment in PD extends beyond simple reduction in writing size and encompasses disturbances in motor scaling, movement automaticity, force regulation, sequential execution, and visuospatial‐motor integration. 22 , 26 , 28 Our findings further extend this framework by demonstrating that these graphomotor domains show distinct relationships with differential diagnostic performance, cognitive correlates, and dopaminergic responsiveness. Within this framework, pressure‐related abnormalities may be more closely related to dopaminergic motor function, whereas final‐segment handwriting area appears to reflect sustained motor scaling supported by both motor and visuospatial processes. In contrast, progressive decrement measures demonstrated strong univariate discriminative performance but limited associations with motor severity, cognition, and levodopa responsiveness, suggesting that they capture a different dimension of graphomotor dysfunction. Consistent with previous neuroimaging observations, future studies integrating quantitative handwriting analysis with neuroimaging and neurophysiological techniques may further refine this multidomain model. 5 , 23

Several strengths of the present study should be acknowledged. Unlike many previous handwriting studies primarily focusing on healthy control comparisons, isolated kinematic domains, or univariate diagnostic classification approaches, our study simultaneously evaluated differential diagnostic performance against ET, cognitive correlates, levodopa responsiveness, and multivariable clinical associations within a multidomain handwriting framework. In addition to univariate discrimination analyses, regression‐based approaches enabled hypothesis‐driven examination of the potentially dissociable clinical and mechanistic correlates of progressive decrement, final‐segment spatial impairment, and pressure‐related abnormalities in relation to motor severity, visuospatial performance, cognition, age, and dopaminergic responsiveness. This multidomain approach provided a more integrated characterization of graphomotor dysfunction beyond the traditional unitary concept of micrographia.

Some methodological considerations should be acknowledged when interpreting the present findings. First, the cross‐sectional design limits conclusions regarding longitudinal evolution of handwriting abnormalities and their progression over time. Second, although our operational definition of progressive micrographia using initial‐to‐final segment ratios may provide a clinically practical and relatively robust representation of sequence‐level deterioration, alternative slope‐based approaches used in previous studies may capture more complex continuous decrement trajectories during handwriting execution. 8 , 19 Third, because all PD patients were evaluated under chronic dopaminergic treatment conditions, long‐duration levodopa response effects cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies including drug‐naïve patients and more rigorously defined OFF‐state paradigms may provide clearer insights into the primary dopaminergic versus nondopaminergic mechanisms underlying distinct handwriting domains. In addition, larger cohorts may further improve the stability of regression‐based mechanistic analyses. Finally, although the regression‐based analyses were specifically designed to explore potentially dissociable mechanistic correlates of different handwriting domains, the absence of direct neurophysiological or neuroimaging measurements precludes confirmation of the underlying biological mechanisms. Accordingly, the proposed mechanistic framework should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. Further validation studies, including disease duration‐matched cohorts, addressing normative interindividual variability and tablet‐based measurement reproducibility may help refine the clinical applicability of quantitative handwriting analysis.

Although several handwriting measures demonstrated moderate discriminative performance, quantitative handwriting analysis should currently be regarded as a complementary research tool rather than a standalone diagnostic test. Further prospective multicenter validation will be required before routine clinical implementation can be considered.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that handwriting abnormalities in PD are multidimensional and exhibit distinct relationships with motor severity, cognition, dopaminergic responsiveness, and differential diagnostic performance. Progressive decrement, final‐segment spatial impairment, and pressure‐related abnormalities showed partially dissociable clinical profiles, suggesting that different handwriting domains provide complementary information regarding graphomotor dysfunction. Notably, although progressive decrement demonstrated strong univariate discriminative performance, its limited multivariable associations with motor severity, cognition, and dopaminergic responsiveness suggest that this handwriting feature may have greater diagnostic utility than clinical or biological specificity. Future studies integrating quantitative handwriting analysis with neuroimaging or neurophysiological measures, and extending comparisons to atypical and secondary parkinsonian syndromes, may further clarify the biological basis and clinical relevance of these distinct handwriting domains.
