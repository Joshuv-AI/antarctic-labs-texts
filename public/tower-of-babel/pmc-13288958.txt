Tysabri Observational Program (TOP): long-term safety and effectiveness of natalizumab treatment in relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis over 15 years
Authors: Helmut Butzkueven, Ludwig Kappos, Heinz Wiendl, Maria Trojano, Tim Spelman, Andres Greco, Zhaonan Sun, Tyler Lasky, On behalf of the TOP Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis Tysabri Observational Program (TOP) final analysis evaluated the 15-year safety and effectiveness of natalizumab treatment in patients with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS).MethodsThis multinational, real-world observational study assessed natalizumab-associated serious adverse events, annualised relapse rates (ARRs) and disability progression/improvement in patients with RRMS. These outcomes were evaluated in subpopulations receiving short- (1–2 years) versus long-term (≥10 years) natalizumab and in patients who switched from intravenous to subcutaneous (SC) natalizumab formulation. The probability of conversion to non-active secondary progressive multiple sclerosis (SPMS) was assessed in patients who remained on versus discontinued natalizumab after ≥1 year.ResultsAs of November 2023, TOP enrolled 6319 patients. Median time on natalizumab was 4.13 years. There were no new safety signals after up to 15 years of treatment. Marked, sustained reductions in pretreatment ARR occurred with natalizumab independent of baseline disease indicators (eg, Expanded Disability Status Scale score). On natalizumab, the ARR decreased by 91.5% after 15 years, relative to the year before baseline. At 15.5 years, cumulative probabilities of 24-week confirmed disability progression and improvement were 48.5% and 38.8%, respectively. Long-term natalizumab treatment significantly decreased ARR compared with short-term treatment. Switching from intravenous to SC formulation did not affect ARR after 1 year post-switch. The cumulative probability of converting to non-active SPMS was significantly lower in patients remaining on natalizumab compared with those who discontinued (0.22 vs 0.29, respectively).ConclusionsFollow-up of over 15 years did not reveal new safety concerns and confirmed sustained real-world effectiveness of natalizumab in patients with RRMS (NCT00493298).Keywords: DEMYELINATING DISEASES, MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS, NEUROIMMUNOLOGY

Introduction

Natalizumab, as demonstrated by both clinical trials and real-world observational studies, is a highly efficacious treatment for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS).1,3 The Tysabri Observational Program (TOP), which began in 2007, is the largest real-world study of patients with RRMS who initiated treatment with natalizumab.4 Interim TOP analyses conducted at 5 and 10 years supported and confirmed natalizumab’s well-established safety profile and robust real-world effectiveness.4 5

Over time, TOP real-world analyses have elucidated natalizumab treatment-related clinical sequelae and outcomes in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS).6,9 As a long-term observational study, TOP has explored important clinical and patient-related considerations with natalizumab treatment, including treatment optimisation using extended interval dosing,10 characterisation of disability improvement,8 outcomes in patients who discontinue natalizumab11 and the impact of natalizumab on employment status.9

Data from these milestone studies and other TOP analyses, collected from 2007 through 2023, have guided the use of natalizumab in real-world clinical practice. This final study analysis elucidates the long-term safety and efficacy of natalizumab, including cumulative probabilities of confirmed disability progression (CDP) to Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) milestones.12

Building upon the 10-year interim analysis,4 this final 15-year analysis of TOP includes a larger population and longer duration of natalizumab exposure. In addition to reporting on long-term safety and effectiveness, the 15-year analysis explores outcomes in patients switching from intravenous to subcutaneous (SC) administration of natalizumab and conversion to non-active secondary progressive MS (SPMS) in patients who remain on natalizumab compared with patients who discontinue therapy. Collectively, the 15-year data from TOP extend and expand our knowledge of the long-term safety and effectiveness profile of natalizumab treatment.

Methods

Patients

Patients were enrolled at 402 centres across Argentina, Australia, Canada, Mexico and Europe (online supplemental etable 1). All patients with RRMS were therapy-naive to natalizumab at treatment initiation and met the local prescription criteria specified in the Tysabri label.

Study design

TOP was a multinational, multicentre, observational study of the safety and efficacy of natalizumab and also included continued safety and efficacy data collection post-natalizumab. Eligible patients had a confirmed diagnosis of RRMS, were natalizumab-naive or had received ≤3 doses of natalizumab in their lifetime at the time of enrolment. Study design details have been previously described.5 This 15-year final analysis includes data from the time of study initiation (July 2007) through the last patient visit on 1 November 2023.

Endpoints and assessments

A web-based, TOP-specific electronic case report form was used to capture data. Demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded at enrolment. Additional assessments were performed approximately every 6 months at regular clinic visits and included natalizumab treatment status; use of concomitant disease-modifying therapy (DMT), antineoplastic medications, immunosuppressive medications and systemic steroids; EDSS score; relapse; anti-JC virus (JCV) antibody status and serious adverse events (SAEs), including serious infections and malignancies. MS relapses fulfilling the criteria of an SAE were not considered an SAE in this study. Patients were encouraged to remain in the study if they discontinued natalizumab.

The primary endpoint was long-term safety (incidence and pattern of SAEs) in patients who received natalizumab. SAEs occurring during natalizumab treatment or within 6 months of discontinuation were included, except for progressive multifocal leucoencephalopathy (PML), which was included when it occurred at any time after the first dose of natalizumab. Malignancies and opportunistic infections were considered SAEs. SAE incidence rates per 1000 person-years were calculated for PML, serious herpes infections, malignancies and, overall, for each Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred term (PT). The incidence of SAEs (treatment-related or any) over the entire study period was summarised by the system organ class (SOC) and PT.

Secondary endpoints included clinical relapses, as well as CDP and confirmed disability improvement (CDI), as determined by EDSS score. Clinical relapses were captured as annualised relapse rate (ARR), distribution of the total number of relapses over 15 years, time to first relapse and proportions of patients with and without relapse. A clinical relapse was defined as a new or recurrent neurological symptom not associated with fever lasting ≥24 hours and followed by 30 days of stability or improvement. CDP was defined as an increase, confirmed 24 weeks later, of ≥0.5 points from a baseline EDSS score of ≥6, ≥1 points from a baseline EDSS score of ≥1 to <6 or ≥1.5 points from a baseline EDSS score of 0 (consistent with the metric used in previous TOP analyses). CDI was defined as a decrease of ≥1 point from baseline score, confirmed 24 weeks later, in patients with baseline EDSS scores ≥2. CDP or CDI could occur up to 84 days after the last dose of natalizumab.

ARR and EDSS effectiveness outcomes were evaluated in two subpopulations of TOP patients: (1) in patients treated with natalizumab over the long term compared with the short term; (2) in patients who switched from intravenous (300 mg)1 13 to SC natalizumab (two 150 mg in prefilled syringes).13 In some ex-US markets, in addition to intravenous natalizumab, the SC natalizumab formulation is available for patients with RRMS.14 Nearest-neighbour propensity-score matching was performed using preidentified covariates: age, sex, EDSS at baseline, MS duration and number of prior DMTs.

In subpopulation 1, long-term patients received ≥10 years of continuous natalizumab (without treatment gaps). Short-term patients received natalizumab for 1–2 years of continuous treatment (without reinitiation of natalizumab after a treatment gap). CDP and CDI were also evaluated in these populations. In subpopulation 2, SC natalizumab was administered for ≥12 months (n=474) (figure 1). ARR pre-SC and post-SC administration was calculated based on the closest study visit on or before the intravenous to SC switch date (ie, index date). The mean annual increase in EDSS scores was compared between the periods of four clinical visits pre-switch and two visits post-switch. The time between two adjacent visits was <1 year. Safety outcomes were also collected in this population.14

In addition, a third subpopulation analysis was conducted to evaluate the conversion to non-active SPMS in patients who remained on natalizumab versus those who discontinued treatment (figure 1).7 Patients who discontinued natalizumab were required to have ≥1 year of natalizumab treatment and could not have converted to SPMS prior to discontinuing treatment. EDSS scores were re-baselined after the last reported relapse. Common eligibility criteria for both cohorts included (1) ≥3 non-missing EDSS scores, including a baseline EDSS score and ≥2 follow-up EDSS scores and (2) ≥3 non-missing EDSS scores re-baselined after the last relapses, including a re-baselined EDSS score after the last relapses and two follow-up EDSS scores, with follow-up visits at least 3 months apart. Each patient in the discontinued group was matched with candidates who had longer natalizumab exposure. The index date for these candidates was the date they reached the same exposure duration as the corresponding discontinued patient. A propensity score model matched patients who continued natalizumab with those who discontinued, controlling for covariates at the index date. The covariates were the last EDSS prior to the index date, age and MS duration at the index date and ARR in the 12 months prior to the index date. Conversion to non-active SPMS was defined as an increase of ≥1 point from an EDSS score ≤5 or ≥0.5 point from an EDSS score ≥5.5, that occurred in the absence of relapse, was confirmed ≥3 months later and resulted in an overall EDSS score ≥4, along with a minimum pyramidal functional system score ≥2.7 15 A sensitivity analysis was conducted in a subgroup of patients to compare the time to non-active SPMS conversion in those who stayed on natalizumab and those who discontinued treatment for reasons other than PML or safety concerns.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient baseline characteristics. The overall safety and effectiveness populations were defined as all patients who met study criteria and received ≥1 dose of natalizumab. ARRs were analysed using a Poisson model with a robust covariance matrix. Propensity score matching with the nearest neighbour method was performed separately for each of the two cohorts: long-term versus short-term and stayed-on-natalizumab versus discontinued-natalizumab, using preidentified baseline covariates. The covariates were described in the Endpoints and assessments section. Cumulative probabilities of first relapse, disability progression, disability improvement and conversion to non-active SPMS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.16 Summary statistics of the recurrent CDP analysis are included in this manuscript. The analysis of associations between baseline covariates (such as baseline disease activity and prior DMTs) and natalizumab efficacy has been previously published4 and was thus out of scope of the present analysis.

Results

Patient enrolment and baseline characteristics

As of November 2023, 6319 patients were enrolled in TOP (table 1). At baseline, the overall population had a median disease duration of 8 years (range 1–44) and a median (range) EDSS score of 3.5 (0–7.5). At the time of study enrolment, 2815 patients of 6913 (44.5%) were natalizumab-naive, 22.5% had received one prior dose of natalizumab, 17.5% had received two prior doses of natalizumab, and 15.4% had received three prior doses of natalizumab. Total natalizumab exposure in TOP was 31 008.05 person-years, including the number of doses received prior to enrolment. Anti-JCV antibody status at baseline was recorded in 1993 patients (31.5%), of whom 829 patients (41.6%) were anti-JCV antibody positive and 1164 (58.4%) were anti-JCV antibody negative.

Safety

Non-serious events were not in the scope of this study; only SAEs are summarised. Overall, 1202 of 6319 patients (19%) experienced ≥1 SAE (table 3), in which the SAE in 330 (5.2%) patients was considered by the investigator to be related, probably related, possibly related (or unknown) to natalizumab treatment. SAEs were listed by SOC and MedDRA PT. The most common SAEs were infections and infestations, with an incidence of 2.4%; of these, the most frequently reported were PML (1.1%), pneumonia (0.5%), urinary tract infection (0.5%) and herpes zoster (0.4%).

Confirmed PML was documented in 60 patients (0.9%); in 59 (98.3%) patients, PML was considered by the investigator to be natalizumab-related (online supplemental etable 3). 56 (93.3%) of the 60 confirmed PML cases had a complete onset date, and 51 (91.1%) out of 56 cases occurred in patients receiving natalizumab for more than 2 years.

Of the 60 confirmed PML cases, 17 cases (28.3%) had both positive JCV serostatus and prior immunosuppressant use, 37 cases (61.7%) did not have prior immunosuppressant use, and for six cases (10%), the status of immunosuppressant use was unknown. In cases with both positive JCV serostatus and prior immunosuppressant use, the outcome was reported as recovered in seven (11.6%), recovered with sequelae in five (8.3%), not recovered in three (5%) and fatal in two (3.3%). 59 (98.3%) of the 60 cases with confirmed PML were anti-JCV antibody positive, and the anti-JCV antibody status was negative in one case (1.7%). Of the 60 confirmed PML cases, there were two cases of JCV-associated granule cell neuropathy. Of the 60 confirmed cases of PML, 43 (71.7%) were symptomatic, while 17 cases (28.3%) were reported as asymptomatic during PML diagnosis.

PML was reported as fatal for five cases (five events), not recovered/not resolved for 20 cases (20 events), recovered/resolved with sequelae in 17 cases (17 events), recovered/resolved in 18 cases (18 events) and unknown for two cases (two events) (online supplemental etable 3). Of the 60 confirmed cases of PML, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) was reported in 52 cases (86.7%); no IRIS occurred in three cases (5%), while for the remaining five cases (8.3%), IRIS status was not reported.

Overall, a total of 184 malignancy events were reported in 175 patients (2.8%) (online supplemental etable 4). The incidence of malignancies, regardless of causality, was 5.8 events per 1000 person-years (95% CI 5 to 6.7). Breast cancer (52 cases) was the most frequently reported malignancy. The incidence rate of breast cancer events in the overall population was 1.58 per 1000 person-years (95% CI 1.19 to 2.06).

In the overall population, a total of 49 events of serious herpes infections were reported in 45 patients. The most common PT terms were herpes zoster (n=24), genital herpes (n=4) and oral herpes (n=4). 29 of the 45 serious herpes cases were considered related to natalizumab.

Other SAEs of interest in this population included immune system disorders, which occurred in 100 patients (1.6%). Over the course of the study, there were 59 reported IRIS cases (52 of which were PML-related) and 28 hypersensitivity cases (table 3).

A total of 51 deaths (0.8%) were reported during the study (table 3) associated with 64 fatal SAEs (online supplemental etable 5), 16 of which were considered related or possibly related to natalizumab treatment by the investigator. Notably, one case ID could be associated with ≥1 fatal SAE. The 16 related or possibly related fatal SAEs were PML (six events), IRIS (three events), metastatic breast cancer (two events), accident, autonomic nervous system imbalance, metastases to meninges, MS relapse and aspiration pneumonia (one event each).

In TOP patients who received SC natalizumab (n=474) for ≥12 months, a total of 11 SAEs were identified in 11 patients (2.3%); no PML cases were reported.

Effectiveness

Relapses

During over 15 years of follow-up, the on-natalizumab ARR was 0.17 (95% CI 0.16 to 0.18), a 91.5% reduction from the ARR of 2 (95% CI 1.97 to 2.02) in the year prior to starting natalizumab (p<0.0001). Patients had large reductions in pretreatment ARR, regardless of the number of prior relapses, baseline EDSS score, prior immunosuppressant (IS) use or the number of DMTs used prior to natalizumab initiation. However, a greater reduction in ARR post-treatment was observed in patients with a lower baseline EDSS score (≤3 vs >3), fewer relapses in the year prior to treatment (≤1 vs >1) or fewer prior DMTs (0 vs 1, 0 vs ≥2 and 1 vs ≥2). The cumulative probability of first relapse after first natalizumab infusion at 1 year was 3.71%, 22.03% at 5 years, 37.99% at 10 years and 46.01% at 15.5 years. In the overall population, the ARR decreased from 2 at baseline to 0.25 at 1 year of natalizumab treatment; ARRs remained ≤0.17 from 5 to >15 years (figure 2). In patients on SC natalizumab for ≥12 months (n=474), ARR was not significantly different 1 year prior to SC administration (0.1, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.14) compared with 1-year post-SC administration (0.09, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.12).

ARR during each year of natalizumab treatment. ARRs were analysed using a Poisson model, with logarithms of natalizumab exposures (during the year) as an offset. ARR, annualised relapse rate.

Disability

At 15.5 years, the cumulative probability of CDP was 48.5% (figure 3). In the recurrent CDP analysis, 4881 participants with baseline EDSS and at least two follow-up EDSS assessments were included with a mean (median) follow-up time of 8.5 (9.7) years. Overall, 1718 (35.20%) patients had at least one CDP event; there were a total of 2570 CDP events. Among the 4881 participants with a baseline EDSS and ≥2 follow-up EDSS assessments, 3163 had 0 CDP events, 1133 had 1 CDP event, 390 had 2 CDP events, 134 had 3 CDP events, 50 had 4 CDP events and 11 had 5 CDP events. The mean number of CDP events was 0.53 throughout the follow-up time in this cohort. At 15.5 years, the cumulative probability of CDI was 38.8% (figure 2). Across all study timepoints, the cumulative probability of sustained improvement increased during treatment with natalizumab. Median EDSS scores were stable in natalizumab-treated patients over the 15-year follow-up (ie, EDSS score of 3.5 at baseline and year 15). Among patients administered SC natalizumab, there were 16 CDP events pre-SC natalizumab and 27 CDP events post-SC natalizumab (p=0.31, based on log-rank test).

Short-term versus long-term natalizumab treatment

Baseline characteristics for patients on short-term (1–2 years) versus long-term (≥10 years) natalizumab treatment are shown in online supplemental etable 6. ARR increased after treatment discontinuation in the short-term natalizumab treatment group compared with the long-term treatment group (p<0.05, year 1 to year 10) (online supplemental efigure 1). However, neither group had ARRs on treatment that exceeded pretreatment ARRs. As shown in online supplemental efigure 2, the mean EDSS score increased over 14 years in the short-term natalizumab treatment cohort but remained stable in patients on long-term treatment (p=0.645 at baseline; <0.05 from year 1 to year 14). Disability analyses showed that the probability of 24-week CDP was significantly increased in the short-term treatment group versus the long-term cohort (p<0.0001); the cumulative probability of 24-week CDI was no different between groups (online supplemental efigure 3).

In patients who received long-term natalizumab treatment, the most common reasons for treatment discontinuation were positive anti-JCV antibody status (11.3%), physician decision (4.3%) and patient decision (3.1%). The most frequently cited reasons for treatment discontinuations in the short-term natalizumab treatment group were positive anti-JCV antibody status (38.2%), patient decision (24.4%), other (19%), medication switch (17%), physician decision (13.3%) and insufficient efficacy (12.9%).

Transition to non-active SPMS

The analysis of conversion to non-active SPMS in patients with RRMS who discontinued or remained on natalizumab included a total of 3257 patients (figure 1). Of these, 1769 patients who stayed on natalizumab were matched to 1769 patients who discontinued natalizumab; 441 patients who discontinued for reasons other than PML or safety concerns were included in the sensitivity analysis. Baseline characteristics were generally similar in patients who stayed on natalizumab and those who discontinued. The median (IQR) exposure to natalizumab was 118.2 months (IQR 83.2–146.9 months) in patients who stayed on natalizumab, 39.6 months (IQR 24.4–68.6 months) in patients who discontinued for any reason and 35 months (range 22.8–56.6 months) in patients who discontinued for reasons other than PML or safety concerns. The median (IQR) total follow-up time was 133.1 months (IQR 111–157.1 months) in patients who discontinued natalizumab for any reason and 121.7 months (IQR 95.8–157 months) in patients who discontinued for reasons other than PML or safety concerns.

As shown in figure 4, after 12 years of follow-up, the cumulative probability (95% CI) of converting to non-active SPMS was lower in patients who stayed on natalizumab (0.22, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.24) than in those who discontinued treatment (0.29, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.33, p=0.021). The cumulative probability (95% CI) of converting to non-active SPMS was also lower in patients who stayed on natalizumab (0.21, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.26) than in those who discontinued treatment for reasons other than PML or safety concerns (0.34, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.40, p<0.0001; figure 5).

Discussion

The 15-year final analysis of TOP, the largest real-world study of patients with RRMS treated with natalizumab, reinforces the established safety profile and robust efficacy of natalizumab.4 5 Safety findings were consistent with those reported in the 10-year interim TOP analysis; no new safety signals were identified.4 Over the course of this 15-year study, >80% of patients who initiated natalizumab treatment had no reported SAEs. The incidence of ≥1 SAE in this study is consistent with data from pivotal clinical trials of natalizumab.2 17 Additionally, the incidence of the most common SAEs was low: PML was reported in 1.1% of patients and serious herpes infection in 0.7% of patients. Low incidence rates were also observed for malignancy (2.8%). The incidence rate (cases) for all malignancy types was 5.28 per 1000 person-years (95% CI 4.53 to 6.13) and comparable to the GLOBOCAN18 incidence rates for the countries represented in TOP (5.64, 95% CI 5.63 to 5.64). Breast cancer was the most common neoplasm, which occurred at a rate of 1.58 per 1000 person-years. The relative increase in the rate of malignancy in this study is confounded by the increased risk of cancer over time and participant ageing.19 The incidence of SAEs in patients who switched to SC from intravenous natalizumab was also low; none of the SAEs in this subpopulation was considered related to natalizumab treatment.

Natalizumab-treated patients exhibited significant disease control over the course of the study, as evidenced by on-natalizumab relapse rates and disability data. Compared with the year prior to starting natalizumab, there was a significant 91.5% reduction in ARR in natalizumab-treated patients during the course of the 15-year TOP study. Notably, substantial improvements in ARR were seen within the first year of natalizumab treatment, and ARR remained low over the 15-year study period (≤0.17 from years 2 to ≥15). Patients had marked decreases in pretreatment ARR, regardless of the number of prior relapses, prior IS use, number of DMTs used prior and EDSS score category at baseline. In addition, ARR remained low and stable in the subgroup of patients who switched from intravenous to SC natalizumab administration.

Substantial improvements across disability outcomes with a cumulative probability of 24-week CDI at 15.5 years of 38.8% provide further support for the long-term efficacy of natalizumab.

The effectiveness of natalizumab in three subpopulations of patients was analysed in this study: those who received long-term natalizumab treatment versus short-term natalizumab treatment, those who converted to non-active SPMS after continuous natalizumab treatment versus natalizumab treatment discontinuation and those who switched from intravenous to SC natalizumab. Analyses of treatment duration showed that, overall, patients who continued natalizumab treatment for ≥10 years had better outcomes versus those who stopped treatment after 1–2 years. Patients who remained on natalizumab beyond 2 years were significantly less likely to experience relapses than those who discontinued. EDSS scores also remained stable in patients on long-term treatment compared with those on short-term treatment. However, comparison of EDSS scores beyond 14 years was limited by small sample sizes, thereby resulting in wider SD. Patients who discontinued natalizumab and switched to another therapy also had higher rates of disability over time.

A similar trend was observed with the cumulative probability of conversion to non-active SPMS over 12 years between patients who continued natalizumab treatment versus those who discontinued treatment. Conversion to non-active SPMS was significantly lower in patients who stayed on natalizumab compared with those who discontinued after ≥1 year of treatment. This was also true when comparing patients who stayed on natalizumab versus those who discontinued after ≥1 year for reasons other than PML or concerns about safety. These data support the clinical effectiveness of maintenance of high-efficacy natalizumab treatment for RRMS, when appropriate, to significantly delay MS disease progression.

Mean EDSS scores remained stable in natalizumab-treated patients over the long course of this study among patients switching from intravenous to SC natalizumab for at least 12 months. Collectively, the TOP final data provide a comprehensive overview of the use of natalizumab in patients with RRMS in real-world clinical practice. Data from safety and efficacy endpoints in this study build on the previous 10-year interim analysis4 with a larger patient population (6319 vs 6148 patients), a longer natalizumab exposure duration (median 4.1 vs 3.3 years) and a longer follow-up time (median 123 vs 62 months).

Effectiveness outcomes from this observational study, including outcomes in subpopulations of interest, offer comprehensive information to help clinicians make more informed treatment decisions for their patients with RRMS. Attrition bias is a limitation of observational studies (due to unknown confounders). However, findings from the TOP 10-year interim analysis4 suggest that this is unlikely because comparable outcomes were observed in 8-year completers and patients who could have potentially received 8 years of natalizumab treatment had they not discontinued natalizumab. Another limitation of this study is that descriptive MRI was only collected at baseline and was not included in the TOP protocol. Because TOP was a real-world study involving varying sizes of clinical practices, there was inherent variability of MRI data acquisition among these sites; this would hinder generalisability and causal inference.

In summary, this final analysis of long-term observational data reinforces the substantial MS disease control associated with natalizumab. Substantial improvements in ARR were seen within the first year of natalizumab treatment, and ARR remained low over the duration of follow-up. Patients had large reductions in pretreatment ARR regardless of the number of prior relapses, prior IS use, baseline EDSS score category or number of DMTs used prior to natalizumab initiation; disability remained stable over the follow-up period. Safety outcomes indicated low rates of serious herpes infections and malignancies that were comparable to those observed in the general population. The safety results from TOP are consistent with the current safety profile of natalizumab. Since TOP includes patients from many regions, including Europe, North America, South America and Australia, differing standards of clinical practice were represented in this cohort, which included varying practices for PML monitoring, varying IS use for patients with MS and varying reimbursement and access to natalizumab. Additionally, TOP includes patients with varying baseline demographic and disease characteristics. These varying key features of TOP may allow the results of this study to be generalised to patients with MS in the countries under study and those with similar natalizumab availability.
