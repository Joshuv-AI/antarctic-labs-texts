Triglyceride-glucose index and abnormal blood pressure in adults aged over 70 years: real-world data evidence
Authors: Yongcheng Ren, Dongdong Qi, Hang Yin, Yuxuan Han, Jing Zhang, Hao Chen, Lei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting studies on the association between the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) and hypertension often rely on simplified hypertension classifications and provide limited evidence specific to the elderly population (aged ≥ 70 years). This study aims to explore the relationship between the TyG index and different subtypes of abnormal blood pressure in older adults.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022 using random cluster sampling, including 12,690 permanent residents aged ≥ 70 years from rural communities. Blood pressure was measured by uniformly trained staff using calibrated electronic sphygmomanometers under resting conditions. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was analyzed both as a continuous variable and in quartiles. Multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for age, sex, education level, marriage, smoking, alcohol consumption, family history of diabetes, family history of hypertension, heart rate, and body mass index, were used to assess the associations between the TyG index and hypertension subtypes. The discriminatory ability of the TyG index was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with adjustment for the same set of covariates.ResultsThe overall crude prevalence of hypertension (HTN) was 55.9%. The prevalence rates of systolic hypertension (SHTN), grade 1 hypertension (HTN-I), and grade 2 hypertension (HTN-II) were 37.6%, 37.5%, and 18.3%, respectively. Participants in the highest TyG index quartile had a significantly increased risk of all hypertension subtypes compared to those in the lowest quartile. Trend analysis showed that each 1-unit increase in the TyG index was associated with increased risks of HTN (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.18–1.63), SHTN (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.11–1.60), HTN-I (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.55), and HTN-II (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.22–1.89) (P for trend < 0.01). ROC curve analysis determined optimal TyG cutoff values for predicting HTN (8.96), SHTN (8.96), HTN-I (8.96), and HTN-II (9.11), but modest discriminatory ability of the TyG index for these abnormal blood pressure subtypes (all P < 0.001), with areas under the curves (AUCs) ranging from 0.56 to 0.58.ConclusionAn elevated TyG index is independently associated with abnormal blood pressure in the elderly, with a particularly strong association observed for HTN-II. Although its discriminatory power as a standalone diagnostic tool is limited, the index may serve as a useful auxiliary factor for hypertension risk assessment in this population.

Abnormal blood pressure, exacerbated by global population aging, constitutes a major public health challenge worldwide [1–4]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to enhance strategies for the prevention and management of hypertension, particularly among the elderly population [5, 6]. Insulin resistance (IR) is a pathophysiological cornerstone of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. It plays a significant role in the development of atherosclerosis and is strongly associated with an unfavorable prognosis in cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [7–9]. However, the complexity of direct IR assessment limits its applicability in routine clinical practice and large-scale epidemiological studies. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, derived from fasting triglyceride (TG) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels, has consequently emerged as a well-validated, reliable, and convenient surrogate marker for IR [10, 11].

Substantial evidence links the TyG index to hypertension. A recent meta-analysis involving over 200,000 participants reported a 53% increased risk of hypertension in individuals within the highest TyG index category compared to those in the lowest [12]. Similarly, a study in children and adolescents demonstrated a significant association, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.63 (95% CI: 1.26–2.11) [13]. Despite this evidence, current research has notable limitations. Many studies analyze the TyG index primarily as a categorical variable, failing to explore the continuous dose-response relationship with hypertension risk [14–16]. Furthermore, hypertension is often treated as a binary outcome, neglecting critical subtypes such as isolated systolic hypertension (ISH), grade 1 hypertension (HTN-I), and grade 2 hypertension (HTN-II). Adults aged 70 years and older represent a rapidly growing segment of the population with disproportionately higher risks of hypertension-related complications, frailty, and multimorbidity. However, most previous studies have focused on younger elderly (60–69 years), leaving a critical evidence gap for the oldest old, who may require distinct prevention and management strategies [17, 18].

Although several recent studies have explored hypertension subtypes or progression, these have primarily involved younger or mixed-age populations and often used longitudinal designs. To our knowledge, few studies have exclusively focused on community-dwelling adults aged 70 years and older using a large, cross-sectional sample. Our study addresses this gap by providing population-based evidence on the association between the TyG index and multiple hypertension subtypes specifically in the elderly. This study is novel in that it exclusively focuses on adults aged 70 years and older in rural China, utilizes a large community-based sample, and simultaneously evaluates the association between the TyG index and multiple hypertension subtypes, providing new insights for risk stratification in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Sample size calculation

A priori sample size calculation was performed for an unmatched cross-sectional study design with a binary outcome, utilizing specialized software (Mark Woodward, The George Institute International Health; Lesley Francis, MIS Consultants Pty Ltd). The calculation was based on the following assumptions: a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-tailed), a statistical power (1-β) of 80% (β = 0.20), a projected outcome prevalence of 18% in the reference (non-exposed) group, a detectable odds ratio (OR) of 1.5, and an allocation ratio of 0.5 (exposed to non-exposed). The initial computation indicated a minimum sample size of 1,023 participants. To mitigate the design effect and potential bias associated with the cluster sampling method, the calculated sample size was inflated by 10%, yielding a final target of 1,126 individuals. However, the actual recruitment process, governed by our random cluster sampling design, aimed to enroll all eligible permanent residents aged ≥ 70 years from the designated community health service centers during the 2022 study period. We did not set a strict recruitment cut-off near the minimum target. Instead, to obtain a larger, more stable, and representative sample of this elderly community population—and to ensure sufficient statistical power for subsequent subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex, hypertension subtypes)—we included all eligible individuals throughout the study period. The study ultimately enrolled 12,690 participants, which exceeds the minimum requirement and ensures adequate power for the planned statistical analyses.

Study participants and data collection

A cross-sectional study was conducted using a random cluster sampling design (In 25 communities, 18 were selected through stratified cluster sampling based on the age distribution of the total population). The sampling frame consisted of local community health service centers. All permanent residents aged 70 years or above in the designated areas during 2022 formed the target population. Potential participants were excluded based on the following criteria: a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease, malignant tumors, tuberculosis, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; current use of specific medications known to frequently promote weight gain (e.g., olanzapine, quetiapine, glucocorticoids); disorders of hormone metabolism; or incomplete health information. After screening, a final cohort of 12,690 subjects was enrolled.

A standardized protocol was followed for data collection. All personnel received centralized training to ensure consistency, and all equipment was calibrated before each measurement. The data collected encompassed four domains:

Demographic characteristics

Age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, and family history of diseases.

Behavioral risk factors

Current smoking status and alcohol consumption habits. Alcohol consumption was assessed, but moderate intake and specific types (e.g., wine) were not separately analyzed.

Anthropometric measurements

Height, body weight, resting heart rate, and blood pressure. Blood pressure was measured by uniformly trained staff using calibrated electronic sphygmomanometers under resting conditions. Participants rested in a seated position for at least 5 min before measurement. Following a standard protocol, three BP readings were obtained for each participant at 1-minute intervals. To ensure reading stability, we required the difference in systolic BP between two consecutive readings to be less than 20 mmHg. If this criterion was not met, additional measurements were taken until a pair of consecutive readings meeting this stability criterion was obtained. Finally, the average of the systolic and diastolic BP readings from this qualified pair of measurements (typically the second and third readings) was used for final data analysis and hypertension definition.

Biochemical parameters

Fasting blood glucose, triglyceride (TG) levels, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

Data on demographic characteristics, behavioral risk factors, and medical history were collected through direct interviews with participants using standardized questionnaires. Anthropometric measurements and blood samples for laboratory tests were obtained during the study visit according to standardized protocols. All measurements were conducted on the same day for each participant. Information on antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering medications, as well as kidney function and additional metabolic parameters, was not available in the dataset and thus could not be included in the analyses. This limitation is addressed in the Discussion section.

Definitions of key variables

Hypertension and its subtypes were defined according to the National Manual for Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension at the Grassroots Level (2020 Edition) [19]. The criteria were as follows:

Hypertension (HTN): Systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, or a self-reported physician diagnosis of hypertension coupled with current use of antihypertensive medication.

Grade 1 Hypertension (HTN - I): SBP 140–159 mmHg and/or DBP 90–99 mmHg, or treated participants with controlled BP (< 140/90 mmHg).

Grade 2 Hypertension (HTN - II): SBP 160–179 mmHg and/or DBP 100–109 mmHg.

Isolated Systolic Hypertension (SHTN): SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and DBP < 90 mmHg.

Triglyceride - glucose (TyG) index, a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, was calculated using the formula: TyG = Ln [Fasting Triglycerides (mg/dL) × Fasting Plasma Glucose (mg/dL) / 2]. (Fasting Triglycerides: mg/dL=mmol/L × 88.5; Fasting Plasma Glucose: mg/dL=mmol/L × 18.)

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the characteristics of the study participants. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the Pearson chi-square test. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and group means were compared using the independent samples t-test.

To quantify the strength of the association between the TyG index (analyzed both as a continuous and categorical variable) and different subtypes of abnormal blood pressure, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The regression models were adjusted for the following covariates selected a priori based on clinical relevance: age, sex, education level, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, family history of diabetes, family history of hypertension, heart rate, and body mass index (BMI).

Furthermore, the discriminatory ability of the TyG index for identifying individuals with different blood pressure abnormalities was evaluated by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The optimal cutoff point for the TyG index was identified as the value that maximized the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05.

No statistically significant differences were observed between males and females in terms of age, family history of diabetes or hypertension, prevalence of impaired fasting glucose (IFG), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), body mass index (BMI), overall hypertension (HTN), heart rate (HR), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), or fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels (all P > 0.05).

Conversely, significant sex differences were identified for several variables. A higher proportion of males had attained an education level of senior high school and above (29.2% vs. 22.6%, P = 0.001) and were married or cohabiting (90.2% vs. 85.7%, P = 0.018). Notably, the prevalence of smoking (13.9% vs. 0.6%) and alcohol consumption (15.5% vs. 0.5%) was substantially higher in males than in females (both P < 0.001).

Regarding cardiometabolic indicators, the mean TyG index was 8.86 ± 0.63 and was significantly higher in females than in males (8.94 ± 0.60 vs. 8.76 ± 0.66, P < 0.001). Females also exhibited significantly higher levels of systolic blood pressure (SBP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) compared to males (all P < 0.001), although the absolute differences were small for some parameters.

The overall prevalence of hypertension (HTN) was 55.9%. The prevalence of Grade 1 hypertension (HTN-I) was 37.5%, with no significant difference between sexes (37.2% in males vs. 37.7% in females, P = 0.141). In contrast, the prevalence of both Grade 2 hypertension (HTN-II) (19.8% vs. 16.5%, P < 0.001) and isolated systolic hypertension (SHTN) (40.4% vs. 34.0%, P = 0.019) was significantly higher in females.

Relationship between the TyG index and the risk of different grades of abnormal blood pressure in the elderly

The distribution of different blood pressure abnormalities varied significantly across the TyG index quartiles (Fig. 1). The prevalence of hypertension (HTN), grade 1 hypertension (HTN-I), and grade 2 hypertension (HTN-II) exhibited a stepwise increase with ascending TyG index quartiles (Q1 to Q4). Specifically, the prevalence in Q4 was the highest, reaching 61.6% for HTN, 40.4% for HTN-I, and 21.3% for HTN-II. In contrast, the prevalence of systolic hypertension (SHTN) was 33.7% in Q1, 34.5% in Q2, peaked at 42.5% in Q3, and slightly decreased to 39.8% in Q4, though the overall trend remained significant (P for trend < 0.001).

For each 1-unit increase in the TyG index, the likelihood of having HTN, SHTN, HTN-I, and HTN-II increased by 39% (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.18–1.63), 33% (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.11–1.60), 30% (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.55), and 52% (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.22–1.89), respectively (P for trend < 0.01 for all) (Table 2).

Predictive value of the TyG index for different grades of abnormal blood pressure in the elderly

ROC curve analysis revealed that the TyG index had statistically significant predictive ability for HTN, SHTN, HTN-I, and HTN-II (all P < 0.001). The areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) were 0.567 (95% CI: 0.544–0.590), 0.565 (95% CI: 0.539–0.590), 0.562 (95% CI: 0.536–0.587), and 0.577 (95% CI: 0.548–0.606), respectively. The optimal cutoff values of the TyG index for predicting HTN, SHTN, HTN-I, and HTN-II were 8.96, 8.96, 8.96, and 9.11, respectively. Among these, the TyG index exhibited the highest specificity (72.54%) for predicting HTN-II and the highest sensitivity (47.27%) for predicting SHTN (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study reveals a high prevalence of hypertension among Chinese elderly individuals aged ≥ 70 years, with an overall rate of 55.9%. Specifically, the prevalence HTN-I and HTN-II were 37.5% and 18.3%, respectively. Notably, SHTN was more prevalent in women than in men, with rates exceeding 30% in both groups. Our findings further demonstrate a significant positive association between the TyG index and the risk of various abnormal blood pressure subtypes in this elderly population. After adjusting for multiple confounders, each 1-unit increase in the TyG index was associated with a substantially elevated risk of HTN (39%), SHTN (33%), HTN-I (30%), and HTN-II (52%), exhibiting a significant dose-response relationship (P for trend < 0.001).

Hypertension represents a growing global public health challenge [3, 20]. In China, the situation is particularly pressing. According to the 2023 China Cardiovascular Health and Disease Report, there are approximately 245 million adults with hypertension, yet the awareness, treatment, and control rates remain suboptimal, with the control rate being 12.9%. Evidence from a large meta-analysis of 61 prospective studies indicated that blood pressure levels are log-linearly associated with the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) across a wide range. Notably, for individuals aged 40–69, each 20 mmHg increase in systolic blood pressure (SBP) or 10 mmHg increase in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) from 115/75 mmHg is associated with a doubling of the risk of fatal stroke or ischemic heart disease [12]. As an age-related condition, the prevalence of hypertension rises significantly with age. Against the backdrop of China’s moderately aging population—with over 200 million people aged ≥ 65 years, accounting for 14.2% of the total population by the end of 2021—the high prevalence (55.9%) identified in our study highlights the critical importance of early prevention, detection, and management of hypertension in the elderly as a cornerstone strategy for curbing the CVD epidemic [21].

It is noteworthy that our study observed a pattern warranting further discussion: the prevalence of isolated systolic hypertension (SHTN) peaked in the third TyG index quartile (Q3) and showed a slight decrease in the highest quartile (Q4). This trend appears inconsistent with the generally positive, dose-response relationship observed between the TyG index and other hypertension subtypes. In a cross-sectional study, such a non-linear association may reflect the complex pathophysiology or methodological factors specific to the elderly population. First, this could be related to survival bias: among individuals aged ≥ 70 years, those in Q4 represent the subgroup with the most severe metabolic dysregulation, likely accompanied by a higher burden of comorbidities, frailty, and cardiovascular mortality risk. This may lead to an under-representation of the highest-risk individuals with both extreme TyG levels and severe SHTN in our cross-sectional community sample, potentially resulting in an apparent decrease in SHTN prevalence in Q4. Second, differences in treatment may play a role: participants in Q4, due to more pronounced metabolic abnormalities, might have received earlier and more intensive antihypertensive treatment (particularly medications targeting systolic BP) in clinical practice, leading to controlled BP and thus non-classification as SHTN at the time of the survey. Our lack of systematic data on specific medications precludes direct testing of this hypothesis, underscoring the previously acknowledged limitation of not adjusting for drug therapy. Furthermore, SHTN in the elderly is primarily attributed to increased large artery stiffness, whereas the TyG index more directly reflects insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation. At the extreme end of the spectrum, the predominant pathophysiological mechanisms for these two conditions may differ among individuals, suggesting disease heterogeneity [22]. Managing older hypertensive patients is inherently challenging, requiring comprehensive consideration of factors such as arterial stiffness, comorbidities, frailty, and specific BP phenotypes [23]. Therefore, the relationship between metabolic markers and specific BP phenotypes (like SHTN) in the elderly must be interpreted with caution, considering population heterogeneity, treatment status, and potential survival bias. Statistical variability also cannot be entirely ruled out. Given the inherent limitations of the cross-sectional design, the above explanations remain speculative and require validation in prospective studies.

The TyG index, a well-established surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR) [24], has been increasingly linked to hypertension in recent research [25, 26]. A 2023 Chinese cohort study (n = 8,209) reported that individuals in the highest TyG index quartile had significantly increased risks of developing HTN-I (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.38–2.13), HTN-II (OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.27–2.38), and SHTN (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.31–2.11) compared to those in the lowest quartile [27]. Furthermore, a higher baseline TyG index, as a continuous variable, was associated with an increased risk of progression from normotension to HTN-I (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.16–1.65) and SHTN (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.04–1.56) [27]. Another 6-year follow-up study in a Chinese rural population found that increases in both absolute and relative TyG index values were associated with higher hypertension risk (OR 1.14 and 1.15, respectively), and the TyG index partially mediated the association between obesity and hypertension [28]. Additionally, a 2023 cross-sectional study involving 15,464 participants demonstrated that each 10-unit increase in the TyG-BMI index was associated with a 31% increased risk of hypertension (adjusted OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.25–1.37) [29]. Our findings are consistent with this growing body of evidence. After multivariate adjustment, we observed that elderly individuals in the highest TyG quartile had significantly increased risks of HTN, SHTN, HTN-I, and HTN-II compared to those in the lowest quartile, with the strongest association noted for HTN-II (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.28–2.98). The robust dose-response relationship further strengthens the evidence for a potential causal link. The underlying mechanisms linking IR, as reflected by the TyG index, to hypertension may involve several pathways [30–32]. IR can lead to hyperinsulinemia, which stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and adrenergic activity, resulting in increased catecholamine levels, vascular smooth muscle hypertrophy, and consequent blood pressure elevation. Furthermore, IR can activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), promoting angiotensin II release. Elevated angiotensin II fosters aldosterone secretion, leading to sodium retention, plasma volume expansion, and potentially mineralocorticoid receptor-mediated hypertension.

ROC curve analysis in our study yielded optimal TyG index cutoff points of 8.96 for HTN, SHTN, and HTN-I, and 9.11 for HTN-II. However, the areas under the curves (AUCs) were all below 0.6, indicating limited discriminatory power for clinical diagnosis on its own. This supports a key perspective: the primary clinical utility of the TyG index is not to replace direct and accurate blood pressure measurement. Instead, its value lies in serving as an integrative metabolic marker reflecting insulin resistance, providing insight into the pathophysiology of hypertension. Specifically, in clinical practice, the TyG index may be useful for: (1) identifying individuals at a higher future risk of developing hypertension due to metabolic abnormalities at a population level or among high-risk groups, enabling earlier primary prevention; and (2) among individuals already with hypertension, particularly those with a high TyG index, suggesting that their hypertension may be associated with more significant metabolic disturbances, thereby encouraging clinicians to conduct a more comprehensive metabolic assessment and consider treatment strategies with greater metabolic benefits. Thus, the TyG index should be viewed as an important complementary piece of information to blood pressure measurement, not a replacement, its primary value lies in assessing associations for epidemiological research and risk stratification, rather than for individual diagnosis in clinical practice.

This study examined the cross-sectional association between the TyG index and abnormal blood pressure phenotypes in a large community-based cohort exclusively comprising older adults aged ≥ 70 years. The novelty of this work lies in its exclusive focus on the very elderly (≥ 70 years), its very large community-derived sample size, and the simultaneous evaluation of multiple hypertension subtypes. With global population aging, the management of hypertension in older adults, particularly the prevention and management of hypertensive heart disease (HHD), has become a critical public health concern [23]. Older hypertensive patients often present with arterial stiffness, multiple comorbidities, frailty, and specific BP phenotypes like white-coat or masked hypertension, complicating their assessment and risk stratification [23]. In this context, identifying easily obtainable metabolic markers like the TyG index, which reflects insulin resistance, holds potential for early risk assessment and phenotyping in the elderly. Our finding of a significant association between an elevated TyG index and an increased likelihood of overall hypertension and its subtypes—especially grade 2 hypertension—in this population adds evidence from a large, community-dwelling elderly cohort. Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, the robust association suggests that attention to metabolic dysregulation, such as insulin resistance, may be an important consideration in the management of hypertension in older adults, particularly in more severe forms.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted considering the following limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal inferences between the TyG index and hypertension, despite revealing significant associations. Second, although the sample size was adequate, participants were recruited from community-dwelling elderly in specific regions, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader elderly population across China; validation in larger, more diverse cohorts is warranted. Third, the exclusive focus on individuals aged ≥ 70 years enhances the specificity of the results for this age group but simultaneously limits their direct applicability to younger populations. Fourth, this study did not adjust for markers of renal function. Chronic kidney disease is a common correlate of both hypertension and insulin resistance in the elderly and represents a significant potential confounder. Due to incomplete information required for calculating the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in the community health data, we could not include it in our models. This may lead to residual confounding in estimating the TyG-hypertension association. Finally, an important methodological limitation of this study is that we did not adjust for the use of glucose-lowering (e.g., insulin, metformin) and lipid-lowering medications (particularly statins, fibrates). These medications directly lower fasting blood glucose and/or triglyceride levels, thereby influencing the calculated TyG index value. Due to incomplete recording of medication information in the community health data, we could not include it in our multivariable adjustment models. This may introduce bias in estimating the true association between the TyG index and abnormal blood pressure and limits the interpretability of the findings. Future prospective studies should systematically collect medication information and rigorously adjust for it in the analyses. Additionally, this study employed a multivariate logistic regression model, using the odds ratio (OR) to measure the association strength between the TyG index and hypertension phenotypes. It is important to note that the overall prevalence was 55.9%—the OR may overestimate the relative risk (RR). Although this does not affect the determination of whether an association exists or its direction, it means that the reported OR values (e.g., an OR of 1.39 for HTN per 1-unit increase in the TyG index) may overestimate the actual strength of the association.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms a substantial burden of hypertension and a significant, dose-response association between a higher TyG index and various hypertension subtypes, especially grade 2 hypertension, among community-dwelling Chinese adults aged ≥ 70 years. While multivariable logistic models indicated strong association strengths, receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated its limited individual discriminatory performance. Therefore, the TyG index is a useful biomarker reflecting insulin resistance and associated with hypertension, suitable for population-level risk assessment and pathophysiological inquiry; however, its modest discriminatory accuracy means it should not replace blood pressure measurement as a clinical diagnostic tool but may serve as a complementary indicator within a risk assessment framework.
