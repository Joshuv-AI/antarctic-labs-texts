Investigation of Tearing Mode Stability Near Ideal Stability Boundaries Via Asymptotic Matching Techniques
Richard Fitzpatrick

Abstract
A number of improvements to the TJ toroidal tearing mode code [Phys. Plasmas 31 , 102507 (2024)] are documented. The TJ code is also successfully benchmarked against the STRIDE toroidal tearing mode code [Phys. Plasmas 25 , 082502 (2018)]. Finally, the new capabilities of the TJ code are used to investigate the stability of tearing modes in tokamak plasmas as an ideal stability boundary, associated with either an external-kink or an internal-kink mode, is approached. All elements of the tearing stability matrix are found to tend to infinity as an ideal stability boundary is approached. Furthermore, as the stability boundary is approached, the eigenfunctions of the various tearing modes in the plasma, which are decoupled by sheared plasma rotation, are all found to morph into that of the marginally-stable ideal mode. However, the growth-rates and real frequencies of the various “ideal-tearing-modes” are different from one another. Moreover, the growth-rate of the ideal-tearing-mode that reconnects magnetic flux at the rational surface that lies closest to the edge of the plasma is the one that tends to a very large value as the stability boundary is approached. A relatively simple test for ideal stability that is capable of detecting stability boundaries for external-kink and internal-kink modes, even in the presence of a very close-fitting ideal wall, is described and verified.



I
Introduction

The calculation of the stability of a tokamak plasma to tearing perturbations is most efficiently formulated as an asymptotic
matching problem in which the plasma is divided into two distinct regions.
[
1
]
In the “outer region”, which comprises most
of the plasma, the tearing perturbation is described by the equations of linearized, marginally-stable, ideal magnetohydrodynamics (a.k.a. the “ideal-MHD” equations).
However, these equations become singular on “rational” magnetic flux-surfaces at which the perturbed magnetic field resonates with the equilibrium field. In the “inner region”, which
consists of a set of narrow layers centered on the various rational surfaces, non-ideal-MHD effects such as plasma inertia, plasma resistivity,
plasma viscosity, diamagnetic flows, the ion sound radius, and perpendicular and parallel energy transport become important.
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
The resistive layer
solutions in the various segments of the inner region must be simultaneously asymptotically matched to the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region to produce a matrix
dispersion relation that determines the growth-rates and rotation frequencies of the various tearing modes to which the plasma is subject.
[
7
,
8
]
(See Sect.
A.6
.)

In general, the determination of the ideal-MHD contributions to the elements of the tearing mode dispersion relation is an exceptionally challenging computational task.
[
9
,
13
,
14
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
17
,
19
,
11
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]

One way of greatly reducing the complexity of this task is to employ an inverse aspect-ratio expanded plasma equilibrium.
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
In such an equilibrium,
the metric elements of the flux-coordinate system can be expressed analytically in terms of a relatively small number of flux-surface functions,
which represents a major simplification.
[
7
]
Another significant advantage of an inverse aspect-ratio expanded equilibrium is that the magnetic perturbation in the plasma can be efficiently
matched to an exterior vacuum solution that is expressed as an expansion in toroidal functions.
[
11
]
(See Sect.
C
.) The aspect-ratio expansion approach to the tearing mode stability problem was previously implemented in the T3
[
9
]
and T7
[
11
]
codes, and has recently been reimplemented in an improved form in the TJ code,
which is described in Ref.
28
.

The first aim of this paper is to document three major improvements to the TJ code.

As is well known, tearing modes in tokamak plasmas become extremely
unstable as an ideal stability boundary is approached.
[
16
,
29
,
30
]
When investigating this phenomenon, it is imperative to know the exact location
of the stability boundary. Appendix
B
explains how the the ideal-MHD solutions in the outer region, calculated by the TJ code, can be repurposed to
construct a complete set of marginally-stable ideal eigenfunctions, and how these eigenfunctions can then be used to calculate the total ideal perturbed potential
energy,
\delta W
, of all possible ideal modes. Of course, the ideal stability boundary corresponds to the point at which the smallest possible value of
\delta W
passes through
zero.
[
31
,
32
]

Previously, the TJ code either allowed for fixed boundary calculations, or calculations in which the plasma is surrounded by a vacuum region. Appendix
C
describes a
new capability that allows for the presence of a perfectly conducting wall in the vacuum region. The radius of the wall, relative to the plasma, can be varied.

Previously, the TJ code only calculated the “tearing stability matrix” that emanates from the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region. However, this is only half of the tearing stability problem.
In order to determine the growth-rates and rotation frequencies of possible tearing modes, it is necessary to combine the data contained in the tearing stability matrix with data obtained from resistive layer solutions
in the various segments of the inner region. Appendices
D
and
E
describe a new resistive layer model that has been implemented in the TJ code. The model takes into account
plasma resistivity, plasma inertia, electron and ion diamagnetic flows, the decoupling of the electron and ion fluids on lengthscales below the ion sound radius, perpendicular
momentum transport, parallel and perpendicular energy transport, and the stabilizing effect of average magnetic field-line curvature.

The second aim of the paper is to benchmark the results of the TJ toroidal tearing mode code against results from the STRIDE
[
23
]
toroidal tearing mode code. This benchmarking
exercise is described in Sect.
II
.

The final aim of the paper is to combine all of the new features of the TJ code in an investigation of the stability of tearing modes in tokamak plasmas as
an ideal stability boundary is approached. We are particularly interested in understanding the similarities and differences when the ideal stability boundary in question is associated with
an
external-kink
mode and an
internal-kink
mode. This investigation is described in Sect.
III
.

Finally, the paper is summarized, and conclusions are drawn, in Sect.
IV
.

II.2
Plasma Equilibrium

In the TJ code, the plasma equilibrium is determined by the shape of the plasma boundary, which is taken to be circular in this paper, as well as two profile functions.
The lowest-order safety-factor profile is written
[
28
]

q(r)=\frac{q_{0}\,\nu\,(r/a)^{2}}{1-[1-(r/a)^{2}]^{\nu}},

(1)

and the normalized (see Sect.
A.1
) pressure profile takes the form

p(r)=\frac{\beta_{0}}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{2}\right]^{p_{p}}.

(2)

Here,
r
is the flux-surface label with the dimensions of length that is defined in Sect.
A.2
. Moreover, the magnetic axis corresponds to
r=0
, and the plasma
boundary to
r=a
. Note that
a
is the effective inverse aspect-ratio of the plasma (given that all lengths are normalized to the major radius of the magnetic axis,
R_{0}
—see Sect.
A.1
.)
Furthermore,
q_{0}=q(0)
is the safety-factor on the magnetic axis, and
\beta_{0}
is the central plasma beta.
The value of
\nu
is adjusted to obtain the desired value of the safety-factor at the plasma boundary,
q_{a}=q(a)
.

In the STRIDE code, we make use of a slightly modified form of the
lar

large-aspect-ratio, circular cross-section analytic equilibrium. This equilibrium is also characterized by two profile functions.
The
normalized parallel current profile,
\sigma={\bf J}\cdot{\bf B}/B^{\,2}
, is written

\sigma(r)=\frac{2}{q_{0}}\left[1-\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{2}\right]^{p_{\sigma}}.

(3)

whereas the normalized pressure profile takes the form

p(r)=\frac{\beta_{0}}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{2}\right]^{p_{p}}.

(4)

In order to obtain (almost) the same equilibrium in the two codes (each of which is based on an expansion of the Grad-Shafranov equation in terms of the inverse aspect-ratio of the plasma), we choose common values of
q_{0}
,
p_{\sigma}
,
\beta_{0}
, and
p_{p}
, and then
adjust the parameter
\nu
in the TJ code until both codes have the same value of the safety-factor at the plasma boundary,
q_{a}
. This procedure ensures that the locations of the various rational surfaces in the plasma are
almost identical in both codes. All of the benchmark tests described in this section calculate the stability of
n=1
tearing modes in free-boundary plasma equilibria (i.e., plasmas surrounded by a vacuum region that contains no ideal wall). The TJ calculations include all poloidal harmonics in the range
m=-10
to
m=+20
.

III
Tearing Mode Stability Near Ideal Stability Boundaries

III.1
Tearing Mode Dispersion Relation

Consider the stability of a tokamak plasma to a tearing perturbation that is characterized by the toroidal mode number
n
. (See Sect.
A.4
.)
Suppose that there are
K
rational surfaces in the plasma at which the perturbation resonates with the equilibrium magnetic field. (See Sect.
A.5
.)
Let
{\Psi}_{k}
,
{\Delta\Psi}_{k}
, and
{\Delta}_{k}
be the reconnected helical magnetic flux, the helical current sheet density, and the
tearing-parity layer response parameter, respectively, at the
k
th rational surface. (See Sects.
A.5
and
A.6
.) Note that all of these
quantities are, in general, complex. The matrix tearing-mode dispersion relation takes the form (see Sect.
A.6
)

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1,K}(E_{kk^{\prime}}-{\Delta}_{k}\,\delta_{kk^{\prime}})\,{\Psi}_{k^{\prime}}=0

(5)

for
k=1,K
. Here,
E_{kk^{\prime}}
is the Hermitian tearing stability matrix determined from the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region.
Moreover,
{\Delta\Psi}_{k}={\Delta}_{k}\,{\Psi}_{k}
for
k=1,K
. Note that, in this paper, we are neglecting the twisting-parity responses of the
various segments of the inner region to the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region in favor of the, generally, much larger tearing-parity responses.
[
21
]

In fact, the neglect of the twisting-parity responses is not a good approximation in very high-
\beta
plasmas,
[
30
]
but is justified in the relatively low-
\beta

plasmas considered in this paper.

The tearing-parity layer response parameter at the
k
th rational surface takes the form (see Sect.
E.5
)

{\Delta}_{k}=S_{k}^{\,1/3}\,\hat{\Delta}_{k}(\gamma_{k})+{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}},

(6)

where

{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}=-\sqrt{2}\,\pi^{3/2}\,D_{R\,k}\,\frac{r_{k}}{\delta_{d\,k}}.

(7)

Here,
r_{k}
(see Sect.
A.2
),
S_{k}
(see Sect.
D.3
),
D_{R\,k}
[see Eq. (
235
)], and
\delta_{d\,k}
(see Sect.
E.5
) are the minor radius, Lundquist number, resistive interchange parameter, and critical layer width above
which parallel thermal transport forces the electron temperature to be a flux-surface function, respectively, at the
k
th rational surface. Moreover,
\hat{\Delta}_{k}(\gamma_{k})
is
the
{\cal O}(1)
complex layer response parameter obtained by solving resistive layer equations in the segment of the inner region centered on the
k
th rational
surface. (See Sect.
D
.) This quantity is a function of the complex growth-rate of the tearing mode,
\gamma_{k}
, as seen in the local E-cross-B frame at the surface.

In general, the elements of the
E_{kk^{\prime}}
matrix, the
\hat{\Delta}_{k}
values, and the
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}
values are all
{\cal O}(1)
, whereas the

S_{k}^{\,1/3}
values are much greater than unity. In this case, the only way of finding a solution of the tearing mode dispersion relation, (
5
), is
to choose
\gamma_{k}
, for some particular value of
k
, in such a manner that
|\hat{\Delta}_{k}|\ll 1
. However, because of sheared E-cross-B and diamagnetic rotation in the plasma,
this choice of
\gamma_{k}
is generally such that
|\hat{\Delta}_{k^{\prime}}|\sim{\cal O}(1)
for
k\neq k^{\prime}
.
[
11
]
In this situation, the
K\times K

matrix tearing mode dispersion relation, (
5
), decouples to give
K
independent dispersion relations of the form
[
11
]

S_{k}^{\,1/3}\,\hat{\Delta}_{k}(\gamma_{k})=E_{kk}-{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}

(8)

for
k=1,K
. Here, the
k
th decoupled dispersion relation corresponds to a tearing mode that only reconnects helical magnetic flux at the

k
th rational surface. The complex growth-rate of this mode only depends on the resistive layer physics at the
k
th rational surface. The
real quantity
E_{kk}
is the effective tearing stability index of the mode.
[
1
]
Moreover, the real quantity
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}
is the critical
value of
E_{kk}
above which the mode becomes unstable.

The marginal stability point of the
k
th tearing mode is
E_{kk}={\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}
, rather than
E_{kk}=0
,
[
1
,
2
]
because of the
stabilizing effect of average equilibrium magnetic field-line curvature in tokamak plasmas.
[
35
,
36
]
Note that our expression, (
7
), for

{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}
differs from that of Refs.
35
and
36
because
\delta_{d\,k}
is the critical layer width above which
parallel thermal transport dominates perpendicular transport, which has no dependence on
S_{k}
, rather that the much smaller resistive
layer width, which scales as
S_{k}^{\,-1/4}
.
[
37
,
38
]
This fact resolves a mystery in tearing mode stability theory. According to Refs.
35
and
36
,
large hot tokamak plasmas should be much more stable to tearing modes than small cold tokamak plasmas, because the latter plasmas are characterized by much
larger Lundquist numbers, and the critical value of the tearing stability index,
E_{kk}
, above which the tearing mode that reconnects
magnetic flux at the
k
th rational surface becomes unstable scales as
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}\propto S_{k}^{\,1/4}
. In fact, large tokamak plasmas are only moderately more
stable to tearing modes than small plasmas. The reason for this is that when parallel transport is taken into account,
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}
, in large tokamak
plasmas is of order unity, rather than much greater than unity, and does not depend on
S_{k}
. Thus, the stabilizing effect of average magnetic field-line curvature is a moderate effect in
large tokamak plasmas, rather than a dominant effect. Nevertheless, this moderate stabilizing effect is important. For instance, it is virtually impossible to find a
sensible tokamak equilibrium in which
E_{kk}
for the
m=2/n=1
tearing mode is not positive. However, the classical
m=2/n=1
tearing mode is usually observed to
be stable in large tokamak plasmas. The reason for this is that
E_{kk}
does not generally exceed
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm crit}}
for the
m=2/n=1
mode in such plasmas.

III.2
Approach to an Ideal Stability Boundary

The matrix tearing mode dispersion relation, (
5
), can be written in the alternative form
[
11
]

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1,K}F_{kk^{\prime}}\,{\Delta\Psi}_{k^{\prime}}={\Psi}_{k}

(9)

for
k=1,K
, where
F_{kk^{\prime}}
is the inverse of
E_{kk^{\prime}}
. Now, a marginally-stable ideal mode is characterized by
{\Psi}_{k}=0
for
k=1,K
. In other words, the
mode does not reconnect helical magnetic flux at any rational surface in the plasma. (The mode is marginally-stable
because our ideal-MHD solution in the outer region neglects plasma inertia.) Thus, for such a mode,

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1,K}F_{kk^{\prime}}\,{\Delta\Psi}_{k^{\prime}}=0

(10)

for
k=1,K
. The only way of finding a non-trivial solution of the previous equation is if the determinant of
F_{kk^{\prime}}
is zero. In general, the elements of
the
F_{kk^{\prime}}
matrix remain finite as
{\rm det}(F_{kk^{\prime}})\rightarrow 0
.
[
11
]
Hence, we deduce that, as an ideal stability boundary is approached
[i.e., as
{\rm det}(F_{kk^{\prime}})\rightarrow 0
], the elements of the
E_{kk^{\prime}}={\rm adj}(F_{kk^{\prime}})/{\rm det}(F_{kk^{\prime}})
matrix all become
infinite
. This implies, from Eq. (
8
), that all
of the decoupled tearing modes in the plasma simultaneously become extremely unstable.
[
16
,
29
,
30
]

III.3
Determination of Ideal Stability Boundary

The DCON ideal stability code
[
33
]
implements two tests for ideal stability. The first test is for fixed-boundary ideal modes (i.e., modes in which the
radial plasma displacement is constrained to be zero at the plasma boundary). This test involves calculating the perturbed ideal plasma potential energy
matrix,
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}(r)
(see Sect.
B.7
), in the region lying between the magnetic axis and the equilibrium magnetic
flux-surface whose label is
r
. If
{\rm det}(\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}^{\,-1})=0

for any value of
r
lying in the range
0\leq r\leq a
then the plasma is unstable to a fixed-boundary ideal mode. The second test is for
free-boundary ideal modes (i.e., modes in which the radial plasma displacement is not constrained to be zero at the plasma boundary
because the magnetic perturbation extends into the vacuum region surrounding the plasma). This test involves calculating the Hermitian perturbed ideal plasma potential energy
matrix,
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}
, for the whole region lying between the magnetic axis and the plasma boundary, and adding to it the Hermitian vacuum energy matrix,
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}
, in order to
form the Hermitian total energy matrix,
\underline{\underline{W}}=\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}+\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}
. If any of the real eigenvalues
of the total energy matrix are negative then the plasma is unstable to a free-boundary ideal mode. As described in Sect.
B
, the TJ code
implements the relatively straightforward second test, rather than the much more complicated first test. One question that we would like to address in
this paper is whether the second test is sufficient to detect ideal stability boundaries associated with
m=1/n=1
internal-kink modes.

III.4
Approach to External-Kink Ideal Stability Boundary

III.4.1
Plasma Equilibria

In this section, we shall investigate the behavior of
n=1
tearing modes as an ideal stability boundary associated with an external-kink mode is approached.

For the sake of simplicity and clarity, all of the calculations described in this section are performed for circular poloidal cross-section plasma equilibria that contain two rational surfaces: namely, the
m=2/n=1
and the
m=3/n=1
surfaces (a.k.a. the
q=2
and the
q=3
surfaces).
The equilibria are characterized by
q_{0}=1.5
,
q_{a}=3.6
,
p_{p}=2.0
, and
a=0.2
. The calculations include all poloidal harmonics in the range
m=-10
to
m=+20
.

Resistive layer quantities are calculated with the following plasma parameters (see Sect.
D.2
) that are broadly characteristic of a high-field tokamak fusion reactor:

B_{0}=12\,{\rm T}
,
R_{0}=2\,{\rm m}
,
n_{0}=3\times 10^{20}\,{\rm m}^{-3}
,
T_{e\,{\rm edge}}=100\,{\rm eV}
,
\alpha=0.5
,
Z_{\rm eff}=2
,
M=2
,
and
\chi_{\perp}=0.2\,{\rm m}^{-3}
. Note that the electron and ion temperatures in the plasma are assumed to be equal to one another, as is likely to be the case in a
fusion reactor. Moreover, the
perpendicular momentum/energy diffusivity,
\chi_{\perp}
, is assumed to be spatially uniform, for the sake of simplicity.

III.4.2
Ideal Stability of Free-Boundary Modes

Figure
5
shows the variation of the smallest eigenfunction of the free-boundary (i.e., with no ideal wall in the vacuum region surrounding the plasma),
n=1
,
total ideal energy matrix,
\delta W
, and the corresponding eigenvalues of the plasma and vacuum energy matrices,
\delta W_{p}
, and
\delta W_{v}
, with the
central plasma beta,
\beta_{0}
. It can be seen that

decreases monotonically with increasing

. This decrease is due
to a corresponding decrease in the plasma energy,

, rather than the positive definite vacuum energy,

(which actually increases).
At low

values, the total energy is positive, indicating that the plasma equilibrium is ideally stable.

,
32
]
However, the
total energy passes through zero at
\beta_{0}=0.0134
, which implies that the plasma equilibrium is ideally unstable for
\beta_{0}>0.0134
,
and that the marginal stability boundary corresponds to

.

III.4.3
Free-Boundary Tearing Stability Matrix

Figure
6
shows the variation of the elements of the free-boundary,
n=1
, tearing stability matrix with
\beta_{0}
. In accordance with the discussion in
Sect.
III.2
, the elements all tend to infinity as the ideal stability boundary is approached. This implies that the decoupled tearing modes that
only reconnected magnetic flux at the

surface (a.k.a. the
2/1
mode) and the

III.4.4
Growth and Rotation of Free-Boundary Tearing Modes

IV
Summary and Conclusions

This paper documents three major improvements to the TJ toroidal tearing stability code.
[
28
]
The first improvement is a repurposing of the
ideal-MHD solutions in the outer-region in order to determine the eigenvalues of the ideal perturbed potential energy matrix. (See Appendix
B
.)
This new capability enables TJ to accurately identify ideal stability boundaries. The second improvement allows for the presence of
a perfectly conducting wall in the vacuum region surrounding the plasma. (See Appendix
C
.) This new capability permits the TJ code
to calculate the stabilizing effect of a perfectly conducting wall on both ideal and resistive modes. The third improvement is the addition of
a resistive layer model that enables TJ to determine the growth-rates and rotation frequencies of the various tearing modes to which a
given plasma equilibrium is subject. (See Appendices
D
and
E
.) The layer model incorporates
plasma resistivity, plasma inertia, electron and ion diamagnetic flows, the decoupling of the electron and ion fluids on lengthscales below the ion sound radius, perpendicular
momentum transport, parallel and perpendicular energy transport, and the stabilizing effect of average magnetic field-line curvature.

Section
II
describes a successful benchmarking exercise of the TJ code against the STRIDE
[
23
]
toroidal tearing mode code.

Finally, in Sect.
III
, the new capabilities of the TJ code are used to investigate the stability of tearing modes in tokamak plasmas as
an ideal stability boundary, associated with either an external-kink or an internal-kink mode, is approached. A number of conclusion can be drawn
from this investigation.

The first conclusion is that, when using the asymptotic matching approach to determining tearing mode stability, it is necessary to
solve both halves of the problem, and then combine them together, in order to gain a complete picture. In other words, only calculating the tearing
stability matrix from the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region can be misleading because, when the resistive layer solutions in the
various segments of the inner region are incorporated into the calculation, it turns out that modes with larger tearing stability indices do not
necessarily have larger growth-rates.

The second conclusion is that a realistic resistive layer model must be employed in the calculation.
In particular, the conventional single-fluid resistive-MHD layer model is completely inadequate, because it fails to take into account the fact that
the ion and electron fluid velocities are significantly different from one another as a consequence of diamagnetic flows. As is well known,
diamagnetic flows strongly influence both the growth-rates and rotation frequencies of tearing modes and resistive-kink modes (which is
what tearing modes become when they are very unstable).
[
2
]
Another problem with a single-fluid resistive-MHD model is that,
unless it takes parallel thermal transport into account, it significantly overestimates the stabilizing effect of average magnetic
field-line curvature on tearing modes.
[
37
,
38
]
Of course, parallel thermal transport in tokamak plasmas is
non-diffusive in nature, because the mean-free-path between collisions is very much longer than the dimensions of the plasma.
[
40
,
41
]

This fact needs to be taken into account in the layer model, otherwise the calculation of the stabilizing effect of average magnetic
field-line curvature will give incorrect results. (See Sect.
E.5
.)

The third conclusion is that, for a given toroidal mode number, a tokamak plasma is subject to as many linearly independent tearing modes as
there are rational surfaces in the plasma. Suppose that there are
K
surfaces. The
K
tearing modes are coupled together by the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region (as a
consequence of the Shafranov shifts and shaping of equilibrium magnetic flux-surfaces). However, under normal circumstances,
when the layer solutions in the inner region are taken into account,
this coupling is overridden by sheared E-cross-B and diamagnetic flows, giving rise to
K
decoupled tearing modes that only
reconnect magnetic flux at one particular rational surface in the plasma, and whose growth-rates and rotation frequencies are determined by
the layer solution at this surface.
[
11
]

The fourth conclusion is that, as an ideal stability boundary associated with either an ideal external-kink or an internal-kink
mode is approached, all of the elements of the tearing stability matrix tend to infinity, and the eigenfunctions of the
K
decoupled tearing modes
all morph into the eigenfunction of the marginally-stable ideal mode. However, the growth-rate
and rotation frequency of each ideal-tearing-mode is determined by the layer physics at the rational surface at which it reconnects
magnetic flux. We find that the growth-rate of the ideal-tearing-mode whose reconnection surface lies closest to the edge of the plasma
is the one that tends to a very large value as the stability boundary is approached, even when the boundary is that associated
with an internal-kink mode.

The fifth conclusion is as follows. The off-diagonal elements of the tearing stability index parameterize the coupling of the
various tearing modes due to the ideal-MHD solution in the outer region. The fact that these elements tend to infinity as
an ideal stability boundary is approached implies that the coupling becomes very strong in this limit. Hence, as the boundary is
approached, we would eventually expect a bifurcation in the equilibrium plasma rotation profile to be triggered by the nonlinear electromagnetic
torques that develop at the rational surfaces.
[
11
,
42
]
This bifurcation would cause the various ideal-tearing-modes to
lock together to form a single ideal mode that is more unstable than any of the original modes. Note, however, that this is a strictly nonlinear effect.

The final conclusion is that the relatively simple second test for ideal stability, described in Sect.
III.3
, that involves calculating
the eigenvalues of the sum of the perturbed ideal plasma potential energy and vacuum energy matrices,
[
33
]
is perfectly adequate for
determining the ideal stability boundaries associated with both external-kink and internal-kink modes, even when an ideal wall lies
very close to the plasma boundary. The only case that the second test cannot deal with is the unrealistic case of determining
the ideal stability boundary of an internal-kink mode when the wall exactly corresponds to the plasma boundary. This conclusion suggests
that the much more complicated first test for the ideal stability of fixed-boundary modes,
[
33
]
described in Sect.
III.3
, is generally unnecessary.

Let

\phi

,
and whose symmetry axis corresponds to the symmetry axis of the axisymmetric toroidal plasma equilibrium.

,

Consider a tokamak plasma equilibrium whose magnetic field takes the form

Equilibrium force balance requires that

where

the equilibrium plasma current density.

All perturbed quantities are assumed to vary toroidally as

According to Eqs. (47), (78), (99), and (101) of Ref.

\displaystyle{\cal J}\,b^{r}

\displaystyle b_{\phi}

\displaystyle=x(r,\theta)=n\,z(r,\theta)=n\sum_{m}z_{m}(r)\,\exp(\,{\rm i}\,m\,\theta),

(15)

Here, the (not necessarily positive) integers

Furthermore,

According to Sect. V.B of Ref.

harmonics of the solutions behave locally as

\displaystyle\psi_{m\,k}(r_{k}+x)

\displaystyle=A_{L\,k}\,|x|^{\nu_{L\,k}}+A_{S\,k}\,|x|^{\nu_{S\,k}},

(16)

\displaystyle Z_{m\,k}(r_{k}+x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{L\,k}}{L_{0}}\,A_{L\,k}\,|x|^{\nu_{L\,k}}+\frac{\nu_{S\,k}}{L_{0}}\,A_{S}\,|x|^{\,\nu_{S\,k}},

where

\displaystyle\nu_{L\,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{-D_{I\,k}},

(18)

\displaystyle\nu_{S\,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{-D_{I\,k}},

(19)

\displaystyle D_{I\,k}

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{2\,(1-q^{2})}{s^{2}}\,r\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right]_{r_{k}}-\frac{1}{4},

(20)

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{L_{m_{k}}^{\,m_{k}}}{m_{k}\,s}\right)_{r_{k}},

(21)

\displaystyle L_{m_{k}}^{\,m_{k}}(r)

\displaystyle=m_{k}^{2}\,c_{m_{k}}^{\,m_{k}}(r)+n^{2}\,r^{2},

(22)

\displaystyle c_{m_{k}}^{\,m_{k}}(r)

\displaystyle=\oint|\nabla r|^{-2}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi},

(23)

and
s(r)=d\ln q/d\ln r

\psi_{m_{k}}(r_{k}-x)=\psi_{m_{k}}(r_{k}+x)

Moreover,

is the coefficient of the “small” solution. Furthermore,

Mercier interchange parameter (which needs to be negative to ensure stability to localized interchange modes),

It is helpful to define the quantities

\displaystyle{\Psi}_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{k}^{\,\nu_{L\,k}}\left(\frac{\nu_{S\,k}-\nu_{L\,k}}{L_{m_{k}}^{\,{m_{k}}}}\right)^{1/2}_{r_{k}}A_{L\,k},

(24)

\displaystyle{\Delta\Psi}_{k}

\displaystyle=r_{k}^{\,\nu_{S\,k}}\left(\frac{\nu_{S\,k}-\nu_{L\,k}}{L_{m_{k}}^{\,m_{k}}}\right)^{1/2}_{r_{k}}2\,A_{S\,k},

(25)

at each rational surface in the plasma. Here, the complex parameter
{\Psi}_{k}
is a measure of the reconnected helical magnetic flux at the

th rational surface, whereas
the complex parameter
{\Delta\Psi}_{k}
is a measure of the strength of a localized current sheet that flows parallel to the equilibrium magnetic field at the surface.
The net toroidal electromagnetic torque acting on the plasma is

T_{\phi}=2\pi^{2}\,n\,\sum_{k=1,K}{\rm Im}({\Psi}_{k}^{\ast}\,{\Delta\Psi}_{k}).

(26)

According to Sect. VIII.D of Ref.

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1,K}\left(E_{kk^{\prime}}-{\Delta}_{k}\,\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\right){\Psi}_{k^{\prime}}=0

(27)

for
k=1,K
,
where
E_{kk^{\prime}}
is an Hermitian matrix determined from the solution of the ideal-MHD equations in the outer region, and
{\Delta}_{k}\equiv{\Delta\Psi}_{k}/{\Psi}_{k}
is
a complex quantity that characterizes the tearing-parity response of the resistive layer at the
k
th rational surface to the outer solution.
In general,
{\Delta}_{k}
is a function of the growth-rate and phase-velocity of the reconnected helical magnetic flux at the surface.

Appendix B
Calculation of Ideal Stability

B.1
Plasma Perturbation

The perturbed magnetic field associated with an ideal perturbation is written
[
31
,
32
]

{\bf b}=\nabla\times(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\times{\bf B}),

(28)

where
\xi
is the plasma displacement. According to Eqs. (2), (25), (26), and
(101) of Ref.
28
,

\displaystyle{\cal J}\,b^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}-{\rm i}\,n\,q\right)y={\rm i}\,\psi(r,\theta),

(29)

where

\displaystyle y(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=f\,\xi^{r}.

(30)

Furthermore, Eqs. (38), (39), (48), and (78) of Ref.
28
yield

\displaystyle b_{\theta}=-\frac{\alpha_{g}}{{\rm i}\,n}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}-{\rm i}\,n\,q\right)y+\alpha_{p}\,R^{\,2}\,y+{\rm i}\,\frac{\partial z}{\partial\theta},

(31)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r\,dP/dr}{f^{2}},

(32)

\displaystyle\alpha_{g}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{dg/dr}{f}.

(33)

Thus,

{\bf B}\cdot{\bf b}-\xi^{r}\,\frac{dP}{dr}=B^{\theta}\,b_{\theta}+B^{\phi}\,b_{\phi}-\xi^{r}\,\frac{dP}{dr}=\frac{{\rm i}\,f}{\cal J}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}-{\rm i}\,n\,q\right)\left(\frac{\alpha_{g}}{n}\,y+z\right).

(34)

B.2
Plasma Potential Energy

The perturbed ideal-MHD force operator in the plasma takes the well-known form
[
31
,
32
]

{\bf F}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})=\nabla({\Gamma}\,P\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})-{\bf B}\times(\nabla\times{\bf b})+\nabla(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P)+{\bf J}\times{\bf b},

(35)

where
{\Gamma}=5/3
is the plasma ratio of specific heats.
The perturbed plasma potential energy in the region lying between the magnetic flux-surfaces whose labels are
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
can be written
[
32
]

\displaystyle\delta W_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\oint\oint\left[{\Gamma}\,P\,(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast})\,(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})+\nabla\times(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\times{\bf B})\cdot{\bf b}+(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast})\,(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P)\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\left.+{\bf J}\times\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\cdot{\bf b}\right]{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi.

(36)

Now,

\displaystyle{\Gamma}\,P\,(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast})\,(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot({\Gamma}\,P\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\cdot\nabla({\Gamma}\,P\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}),

(37)

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\times{\bf B})\cdot{\bf b}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot[(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\times{\bf B})\times{\bf b}]+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\times{\bf B}\cdot\nabla\times{\bf b},

(38)

\displaystyle(\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast})\,(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P)-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\cdot\nabla(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P),

(39)

so

\displaystyle\delta W_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\oint\oint\left\{\nabla\cdot[{\Gamma}\,P\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\times{\bf B})\times{\bf b}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\cdot[\nabla({\Gamma}\,P\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})-{\bf B}\times(\nabla\times{\bf b})+\nabla(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P)+{\bf J}\times{\bf b}]\right\}{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi,

(40)

which yields

\displaystyle\delta W_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\oint\oint{\cal J}\,\nabla r\cdot[{\Gamma}\,P\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\times{\bf B})\times{\bf b}+\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\cdot\nabla P]\,d\theta\,d\phi\right)_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\oint\oint\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\cdot{\bf F}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})\,{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi,

(41)

or

\displaystyle\delta W_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\oint\oint{\cal J}\,\xi^{r\,\ast}\left({\Gamma}\,P\,\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}-{\bf B}\cdot{\bf b}+\xi^{r}\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right)d\theta\,d\phi\right]_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\oint\oint\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{\ast}\cdot{\bf F}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})\,{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi,

(42)

where use has been made of the fact that
{\bf B}\cdot\nabla r=0
. However, in the TJ code, the marginally-stable ideal-MHD plasma perturbation in the outer region is calculated assuming that
{\bf F}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})={\bf 0}
and

\nabla\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}=0
.
[
28
]
Thus, making use of Eqs. (
30
) and (
34
), we obtain

\delta W_{12}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\left[-{\rm i}\,y^{\ast}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}-{\rm i}\,n\,q\right)\left(\frac{\alpha_{g}}{n}\,y+z\right)d\theta\,d\phi\right]_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}},

(43)

which reduces to

\delta W_{12}=\pi^{2}\left[\sum_{m}\,y_{m}^{\ast}\,(m-n\,q)\left(\frac{\alpha_{g}}{n}\,y_{m}+z_{m}\right)\right]_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}},

(44)

where
y(r,\theta)=\sum_{m}y_{m}(r)\,\exp(\,{\rm i}\,m\,\theta)
.

Now, according to Eq. (98) of Ref.
28
,

\displaystyle y_{m}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\psi_{m}}{m-n\,q}\right)_{r}.

(45)

Thus, we get

\delta W_{12}=\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}

(46)

where

\displaystyle\chi_{m}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\tilde{k}_{m}\,\psi_{m}+Z_{m}}{m-n\,q}\right)_{r},

(47)

\displaystyle\tilde{k}_{m}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(k_{m}+\frac{\alpha_{g}}{n}\right)_{r}=\left[\frac{\alpha_{g}\,(m\,q\,c_{m}^{\,m}+n\,r^{2})+\alpha_{p}\,m\,d_{m}^{\,m}}{m^{2}\,c_{m}^{\,m}+n^{2}\,r^{2}}\right]_{r},

(48)

and use has been made of Eq. (100) Ref.
28
. Here,
\tilde{k}_{m}(r)
is real, and

\displaystyle d_{m}^{\,m}(r)

\displaystyle=\oint|\nabla r|^{-2}\,R^{\,2}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}.

(49)

B.3
Magnetic Axis

Let the
\psi_{m}(r)
and the
\chi_{m}(r)
be solutions of the ideal-MHD equations in the outer region that are well behaved at
r=0
.
Such solutions vary as
r^{|m|}
. (For the special case
m=0
,
\chi_{0}\sim 1
and
\psi_{0}=0
.) It follows that
[
28
]

\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{0}=0.

(50)

Hence,

\delta W_{p}(r)=\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{r}

(51)

is the perturbed plasma potential energy in the region lying between the magnetic axis and the magnetic flux-surface whose label is
r
.

B.4
Rational Surfaces

At the
k
th rational surface,
r=r_{k}
, the non-resonant components of
\psi_{m}
and
\chi_{m}
are continuous, the
large solution is absent (i.e.,
{\Psi}_{k}=0
), but the small solution is present (i.e.,
{\Delta\Psi}_{k}=0
). (See Sect.
A.5
.)
It is easily shown that
\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}
remains finite at
r=r_{k}
(which would not be the case if
{\Psi}_{k}\neq 0
), and
[
28
]

\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{r_{k-}}^{r_{k+}}=0.

(52)

In other words, there is no contribution to the perturbed potential energy from the surface. Thus, Eq. (
51
) holds even when the region between the
magnetic axis and the flux-surface whose label is
r
contains rational surfaces.
Thus, the total plasma potential energy is

\delta W_{p}=\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{a_{-}},

(53)

where
r=a_{-}
corresponds to an equilibrium magnetic flux that lies just inside the plasma-vacuum interface.

B.5
Toroidal Electromagnetic Torque

The flux-surface averaged toroidal electromagnetic torque acting on the plasma is
[
28
,
32
]

\displaystyle T_{\phi}

\displaystyle={\rm i}\,n\,\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\frac{Z_{m}^{\ast}\,\psi_{m}-\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,Z_{m}}{m-n\,q}\right)_{a_{-}}={\rm i}\,n\,\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\chi_{m}^{\,\ast}\,\psi_{m}-\psi_{m}^{\,\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{a_{-}}

\displaystyle=2\,n\,{\rm Im}(\delta W_{p}),

(54)

where use has been made of Eqs. (
47
) and (
51
).
However, according to Eq. (
26
), this torque must be zero for ideal solutions, which are characterized by
{\Psi}_{k}=0
(i.e., zero reconnected magnetic
flux at the various rational surfaces in the plasma).
[
28
]

It follows that
the total plasma potential energy,
\delta W_{p}
, is necessarily a real quantity.

B.6
Marginally-Stable Ideal Eigenfunctions

We can construct a complete set of marginally-stable ideal eigenfuctions in the outer region from the ideal-MHD solutions in the outer region calculated by the TJ code as follows:

\displaystyle\psi_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{i}(r)

\displaystyle=\psi_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{a}(r)-\sum_{k=1,K}\psi_{m\,k}^{u}(r)\,{\Pi}_{k\,m^{\prime}}^{a},

(55)

\displaystyle Z_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{i}(r)

\displaystyle=Z_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{a}(r)-\sum_{k=1,K}Z_{m\,k}^{u}(r)\,{\Pi}_{k\,m^{\prime}}^{a}.

(56)

Here,
m
labels the poloidal harmonic of the solution,
m^{\prime}
labels the solution itself, and
k
labels the various rational surfaces in the plasma. There are as many solutions as there are poloidal harmonics included in the calculation. All other quantities are defined in Sects. VIII.B–VIII.D of Ref.
28
.
It follows
from the definitions of the various quantities in the previous two equations that the reconnected magnetic fluxes at the rational surfaces in the plasma
associated with the ideal eigenfunctions are all zero (i.e.,
{\Psi}_{k}=0
for all
k
). We can also write

\chi^{i}_{m\,m^{\prime}}(r)=\left(\frac{\tilde{k}_{m}\,\psi^{i}_{m\,m^{\prime}}+Z_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{i}}{m-n\,q}\right)_{r},

(57)

where use has been made of Eq. (
47
).

B.7
Plasma Energy Matrix

We can write a general ideal solution just inside the plasma-vacuum interface as

\displaystyle\psi_{m}(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}}\psi_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{i}(a)\,\alpha_{m^{\prime}},

(58)

\displaystyle\chi_{m}(a_{-})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}}\chi_{m\,m^{\prime}}^{i}(a_{-})\,\alpha_{m^{\prime}},

(59)

where the
\alpha_{m}
are arbitrary complex coefficients. Note that the
\psi_{m}(r)
are continuous across the plasma-vacuum interface, whereas the
\chi_{m}(r)
are generally discontinuous.
The previous two equations can be written more succinctly as

\displaystyle\underline{\psi}

\displaystyle=\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\alpha},

(60)

\displaystyle\underline{\chi}

\displaystyle=\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\alpha},

(61)

where
\underline{\psi}
is the column vector of the
\psi_{m}(a)
values,
\underline{\chi}
is the column vector of the
\chi_{m}(a_{-})
values,

\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}
is the matrix of the
\psi_{mm^{\prime}}^{i}(a)
values,
\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}
is the matrix of the
\chi_{mm^{\prime}}^{i}(a_{-})
values,
and
\underline{\alpha}
the column vector of the
\alpha_{m}
values.
Equation (
53
) then becomes

\delta W_{p}=\pi^{2}\,\underline{\psi}^{\dagger}\,\underline{\chi},

(62)

or

\delta W_{p}=\pi^{2}\,\underline{\alpha}^{\dagger}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\alpha}.

(63)

The fact that
\delta W_{p}
is real implies that

\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}=\underline{\underline{\chi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}.

(64)

Let us define the “perturbed plasma potential energy matrix”,
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}
, such that

\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}=\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}.

(65)

It is easily seen that

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}=\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i},

(66)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\chi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}=\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}^{{\dagger}}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}.

(67)

Making use of Eq. (
64
), we obtain

\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}=\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}^{{\dagger}}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i},

(68)

which implies that
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}
is an Hermitian matrix.

B.8
Total Potential Energy

The total perturbed potential energy of the plasma-vacuum system can be written
[
31
,
32
]

\delta W=\delta W_{p}+\delta W_{v},

(69)

where

\delta W_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a+}^{\infty}\oint\oint{\bf b}^{\ast}\cdot{\bf b}\,{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi

(70)

is the perturbed potential energy of the surrounding vacuum.
[
31
,
32
]
Here,
r=a_{+}
corresponds to an equilibrium magnetic flux-surface that lies just outside the plasma-vacuum interface.

B.9
Vacuum Potential Energy

In the vacuum region, we can write the curl- and divergence-free perturbed magnetic field in the form

{\bf b}={\rm i}\,\nabla V
,
where

\nabla^{2}V=0
.
Hence, the vacuum potential energy is

\displaystyle\delta W_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a+}^{\infty}\oint\oint\nabla V\,\cdot\nabla V^{\ast}\,{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a+}^{\infty}\oint\oint\nabla\cdot(V\,\nabla V^{\ast})\,{\cal J}\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\oint\oint{\cal J}\,\nabla r\cdot\nabla V^{\ast}\,V\,d\theta\,d\phi\right)_{a_{+}},

(71)

assuming that
V(r,\theta)\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow\infty
if there is no wall surrounding the plasma, or
\nabla V\cdot d{\bf S}=0
if a perfectly-conducting wall
surrounds the plasma.
But, Eq. (210) of Ref.
28
implies that

{\cal J}\,\nabla V\cdot\nabla r=\psi,

(72)

so we deduce that

\delta W_{v}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\oint\oint\psi^{\ast}\,V\,d\theta\,d\phi\right)_{a_{+}}=-\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,V_{m}\right)_{a_{+}},

(73)

where

V(r,\theta)=\sum_{m}V_{m}(r)\,\exp(\,{\rm i}\,m\,\theta)
.
However, making use of Eq. (214) of Ref.
28
, we get

\delta W_{v}=-\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,\chi_{m}\right)_{a_{+}},

(74)

where

\chi_{m}=\frac{Z_{m}}{m-n\,q}.

(75)

Note that the previous equation is consistent with Eq. (
47
) because, according to Eq. (
48
),
\tilde{k}_{m}=0
in the vacuum region, given that
\alpha_{g}=\alpha_{p}=0
in the vacuum.

Combining Eqs. (
53
), (
69
), and (
74
), we deduce that

\delta W=\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{m}\psi_{m}^{\ast}\,J_{m}\right)_{\epsilon},

(76)

where

J_{m}=-\left[\chi_{m}\right]_{a_{-}}^{a_{+}}.

(77)

B.10
Boundary Current Sheet

Now,
\alpha_{p}=\alpha_{g}=0
at the plasma-vacuum interface, assuming that the equilibrium current is zero there,
which implies that
k_{m}=\tilde{k}_{m}=0
at the interface, where use has been made of Eq. (
48
),
as well as Eq. (100) of Ref.
28
. It follows from Eqs. (78) and (99) of Ref.
28
, combined with Eq. (
75
), that

x_{m}=n\,\chi_{m}

(78)

at the plasma-vacuum interface, where
x(r,\theta)=\sum_{m}x_{m}(r)\,\exp(\,{\rm i}\,m\,\theta)
.
Suppose that there is a perturbed current sheet on the interface.
Thus, if
{\bf K}
is the current sheet density then Eqs. (66) and (67) of Ref.
28
suggest that

\displaystyle{\cal J}\,K_{m}^{\,\theta}

\displaystyle=n\,J_{m},

(79)

\displaystyle{\cal J}\,K_{m}^{\,\phi}

\displaystyle=m\,J_{m},

(80)

where use has been made of Eqs. (
77
) and (
78
).
Let us write

{\bf K}={\rm i}\,\nabla J\times\nabla r,

(81)

where
J(\theta,\phi)=J(\theta)\,\exp(-{\rm i}\,n\,\phi)
, which ensures that
\nabla\cdot{\bf K}=0
. It follows from (A8) and (A9) of Ref.
28
that

\displaystyle{\cal J}\,K^{\theta}

\displaystyle={\rm i}\,\frac{\partial J}{\partial\phi},

(82)

\displaystyle{\cal J}\,K^{\phi}

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\,\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta}.

(83)

Hence, if we expand the previous two equations as Fourier series in
\theta
then we reproduce Eqs. (
79
) and (
80
), which implies
that the
J_{m}
defined in Eq. (
77
) are the Fourier components of the
J(\theta)
function introduced in Eq. (
81
).

As described in Ref.
32
, the marginally-stable ideal eigenfunctions feature a perturbed current sheet on the vacuum-plasma interface because they do not satisfy the
perturbed pressure-balance boundary condition.
However, these current sheets are entirely fictitious. The true ideal eigenfunctions (which, unlike the marginally-stable ideal eigenfunctions, take plasma inertia into account)
satisfy the pressure-balance boundary condition (and, therefore, have no associated current sheets). Current sheets are permitted because the marginally-stable ideal eigenfunctions
are “trial solutions” used to determine ideal stability, rather than actual physical solutions.

B.11
Determination of Ideal Stability

According to Eq. (215) of Ref.
28
, combined with Eq. (
75
),

\chi_{m}(a_{+})=\sum_{m^{\prime}}\,H_{m\,m^{\prime}}\,\psi_{m^{\prime}}(a),

(84)

where the “vacuum matrix”,
H_{mm^{\prime}}
, is Hermitian. (See Sect. VI.G of Ref.
28
.)
Hence, it follows from Eq. (
77
) that

J_{m}=\chi_{m}(a_{-})-\sum_{m^{\prime}}H_{m\,m^{\prime}}\,\psi_{m}(a).

(85)

Making use of Eq. (
76
), we can write

\delta W=\pi^{2}\,\underline{\psi}^{\dagger}\,\underline{J},

(86)

where

\underline{J}=\underline{\chi}+\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}\,\underline{\psi}.

(87)

Here,
\underline{J}
is the column vector of the
J_{m}
values,
and the “perturbed vacuum energy matrix”,
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}
, is the Hermitian matrix of the
-H_{m\,m^{\prime}}
values.
Making use of Eqs. (
60
), (
61
), and (
65
), we get

\underline{J}=\underline{\underline{W}}\,\underline{\psi},

(88)

and

\delta W=\underline{\alpha}^{\dagger}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,(\underline{\underline{\chi}}_{\,i}+\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i})\,\underline{\alpha}=\underline{\alpha}^{\dagger}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}^{{\dagger}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\underline{W}}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\alpha},

(89)

where

\underline{\underline{W}}=\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}+\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}.

(90)

Note that
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}
and
\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}
are both Hermitian, so
\underline{\underline{W}}
is also
Hermitian. Thus the “perturbed total energy matrix”,
\underline{\underline{W}}
, possesses real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors,
\underline{\beta}_{m}
. Let
(\underline{\beta}_{m^{\prime}})_{m}=\beta_{m\,m^{\prime}}
, and let
\underline{\underline{\beta}}
be the matrix of the
\beta_{mm^{\prime}}
values. It follows that

\underline{\underline{\beta}}^{\dagger}\,\underline{\underline{\beta}}=\underline{\underline{1}}
.
We conclude that there are as many linearly independent ideal eigenmodes of the plasma as there are poloidal harmonics included in the calculation. The
m
th
eigenmode has the associated energy

\delta W_{m}=\delta W_{p\,m}+\delta W_{v\,m},

(91)

where

\displaystyle\delta W_{m}

\displaystyle=\underline{\beta}_{m}^{\,{\dagger}}\,\underline{\underline{W}}\,\underline{\beta}_{m},

(92)

\displaystyle\delta W_{p\,m}

\displaystyle=\underline{\beta}_{m}^{\,{\dagger}}\,\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,p}\,\underline{\beta}_{m},

(93)

\displaystyle\delta W_{v\,m}

\displaystyle=\underline{\beta}_{m}^{\,{\dagger}}\,\underline{\underline{W}}_{\,v}\,\underline{\beta}_{m}.

(94)

Note that
\delta W_{m}
,
\delta W_{p\,m}
and
\delta W_{v\,m}
are all real quantities. Moreover,
\delta W_{p\,m}
and
\delta W_{v\,m}
can be interpreted as the
plasma and vacuum contributions to
\delta W_{m}
, respectively. Finally,

\delta W=\sum_{m}|\hat{\alpha}_{m}|^{2}\,\delta W_{m},

(95)

where
\underline{\hat{\alpha}}=\underline{\underline{\beta}}^{\dagger}\,\underline{\underline{\psi}}_{\,i}\,\underline{\alpha}
.
As is well known, the plasma is ideally unstable if
\delta W<0
for any possible ideal peturbation.
[
31
,
32
]

Thus, given that the
\hat{\alpha}_{m}
are arbitrary, we deduce that if any of the
\delta W_{m}
are negative then the plasma is ideally unstable.

Appendix C
Vacuum Solution

C.1
Toroidal Coordinates

Let
\mu
,
\eta
,
\phi
be right-handed toroidal coordinates defined such that (see Sect.
A.2
)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh\mu}{\cosh\mu-\cos\eta},

(96)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\eta}{\cosh\mu-\cos\eta}.

(97)

The scale-factors of the toroidal coordinate system are

\displaystyle h_{\mu}

\displaystyle=h_{\eta}=\frac{1}{\cosh\mu-\cos\eta}\equiv h,

(98)

\displaystyle h_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh\mu}{\cosh\mu-\cos\eta}=h\,\sinh\mu.

(99)

Moreover,

{\cal J}^{\prime}\equiv(\nabla\mu\times\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\phi)=h^{3}\,\sinh\mu.

(100)

C.2
Perturbed Magnetic Field

The curl-free perturbed magnetic field in the vacuum region is written
{\bf b}={\rm i}\,\nabla V
,
where

\nabla^{2}V=0
.
The most general solution to Laplace’s equation is

\displaystyle V(z,\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}(z-\cos\eta)^{1/2}\,U_{m}(z)\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\,m\,\eta},

(101)

\displaystyle U_{m}(z)

\displaystyle=p_{m}\,\hat{P}_{|m|-1/2}^{\,n}(z)+q_{m}\,\hat{Q}_{m-1/2}^{\,n}(z),

(102)

where
z=\cosh\mu
, the
p_{m}
and
q_{m}
are arbitrary complex coefficients, and

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{|m|-1/2}^{\,n}(z)

\displaystyle=\cos(|m|\,\pi)\,\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(|m|+1/2-n)\,a^{\,|m|}}{2^{\,|m|-1/2}\,|m|!}\,P_{|m|-1/2}^{\,n}(z),

(103)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{|m|-1/2}^{\,n}(z)

\displaystyle=\cos(n\,\pi)\,\cos(|m|\,\pi)\,\frac{2^{\,|m|-1/2}\,|m|!}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(|m|+1/2+n)\,a^{\,|m|}}\,Q_{|m|-1/2}^{\,n}(z).

(104)

Here, the

C.5

According to Eq. (

Appendix D
Resistive Layer Model

D.1
Introduction

The resistive layer model implemented in the TJ code is the three-field model described in Ref.
6
. This model is a further development of
the models derived in Refs.
4
and
5
, and is ultimately based on the four-field model of Ref.
3
.
The model includes plasma resistivity, plasma inertia, electron and ion diamagnetic flows, the decoupling of ion and electron flows on lengthscales below the ion sound
radius, and perpendicular momentum and energy transport. The three fields in the model are the (normalized) perturbed helical magnetic flux,
\psi
, the (normalized)
perturbed electrostatic potential,
\phi
, and the (normalized) perturbed electron number density,
N
. The model makes use of a low-
\beta

approximation that decouples the (normalized) parallel ion velocity,
V
, from the system of equations (thereby converting a four-field model into
a three-field model). Reference
47
explains how the fourth field can be incorporated into the model. We choose not to do this
because the fourth field is only important at high-
\beta
, and an aspect-ratio expanded equilibrium is, by definition, a relatively low-
\beta

equilibrium.

D.2
Plasma Parameters

The values of the following plasma parameters must be specified in order to calculate layer quantities:

•

B_{0}
- the toroidal magnetic field-strength at the magnetic axis (T).

•

R_{0}
- the major radius of the magnetic axis (m).

•

n_{0}
- the electron number density at the magnetic axis (
{\rm m}^{-3}
).

•

\alpha
- the electron density profile is assumed to be
n_{e}(r)=n_{0}\,(1-r^{2}/a^{2})^{\alpha}
.

•

T_{e\,{\rm edge}}
- the electron temperature at the plasma-vacuum interface (eV).

•

Z_{\rm eff}
- the (assumed spatially uniform) effective ion charge number.

•

M
- the ion mass number.

•

\chi_{\perp}
- the (assumed spatially uniform) perpendicular momentum/energy diffusivity (
{\rm m}^{2}/{\rm s}
).

Ricatti Transformation

Equation (
167
) is most conveniently solved by means of a Ricatti transformation.
[
49
,
50
]

Let

W(p)=\frac{p}{\hat{Y}_{e}}\,\frac{d\hat{Y}_{e}}{dp}.

(184)

Equation (
167
) transforms to give

\frac{dW}{dp}=-\frac{A^{\prime}}{p}\,W-\frac{W^{\,2}}{p}+\frac{B}{A\,C}\,p^{3},

(185)

where

A^{\prime}=\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{k}+{\rm i}\,Q_{e\,k}-p^{2}}{\hat{\gamma}_{k}+{\rm i}\,Q_{e\,k}+p^{2}}.

(186)

According to Eqs. (
172
), (
174
), and (
179
), this equation must be solved subject to the boundary condition that

W(p)=x_{k}-\sqrt{b_{k}}\,p^{2}

(187)

at large
p
and large
D_{k}
, or

W(p)=-1+x_{k}\,p-\sqrt{b_{k}}\,p^{3}

(188)

at large
p
and small
D_{k}
, and

W(p)=-1+\frac{\pi\,p}{\hat{\Delta}_{k}}

(189)

at small
p
.
The method of solution is to launch a solution of Eq. (
185
) from large
p
, subject to the boundary condition (
187
) or (
188
), as appropriate, and
integrate it to small
p
. We can then deduce the value of
\hat{\Delta}_{k}
from Eq. (
189
).

Appendix E
Asymptotic Matching Between Outer and Inner Regions

E.1
Introduction

Consider the segment of the inner region that is centered on the
k
th rational surface. Let the surface lie at
r=r_{k}
, and possess the
resonant poloidal mode number
m_{k}
. According to Sect.
A.5
, in the limit
r\rightarrow r_{k}
, the asymptotic behavior of the resonant, tearing-parity, component of the perturbed helical magnetic
flux,
\psi
, [see Eq. (
13
)] in the outer region is

\psi_{m_{k}}(x)=A_{L\,k}\,|x|^{-\nu_{k}}+A_{S\,k}\,|x|^{\,1+\nu_{k}},

(190)

where
x=r-r_{k}
,
\nu_{k}=-1/2+\sqrt{-D_{I\,k}}
, and

{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{2\,r_{k}^{1+2\,\nu_{k}}\,A_{S\,k}}{A_{L\,k}}.

(191)

Here,
D_{I\,k}
is the ideal interchange parameter defined in Eq. (
20
), and
{\Delta}_{k}
is the layer response parameter that appears in the
tearing mode dispersion relation, (
27
). On the other hand, according to Sect.
D.4
, the asymptotic behavior of the resistive layer
solution in the segment of the inner region, as it approaches the outer region, is

\psi_{m_{k}}(x)=B_{L\,k}+B_{S\,k}\,|x|,

(192)

where

{\Delta}_{k}=\frac{2\,r_{k}\,B_{S\,k}}{B_{L\,k}}.

(193)

It can be seen that the solutions (
190
) and (
192
) only match if
\nu_{k}=0
. The reason for the mismatch is because the outer solution takes average magnetic field-line curvature into
account,
[
35
,
36
]
whereas the layer solution does not. The aim of this appendix is to describe a method for resolving the mismatch.

E.2
Pressure Flattening

In the immediate vicinity of the rational surface, the equation that governs the resonant component of
\psi
in the outer region reduces to
[
28
]

\frac{d^{\,2}\psi_{m_{k}}}{dx^{2}}=\frac{\nu_{k}\,(1+\nu_{k})}{x^{2}}\,\psi_{m_{k}},

(194)

where

\nu_{k}\,(1+\nu_{k})=-D_{I\,k}-\frac{1}{4}=\left[\frac{2\,(1-q^{2})}{s^{2}}\,r\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right]_{r_{k}}.

(195)

Following Ref.
51
, let us suppose that the pressure gradient is locally flattened in the vicinity of the rational surface in such a manner that

\frac{dP}{dx}=\left(\frac{dP}{dr}\right)_{r_{k}}\,\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+\delta_{k}^{\,2}}.

(196)

Here, it is assumed that
\delta_{k}\ll r_{k}
. Equation (
194
) transforms to give

(1+X^{2})\,\frac{d^{\,2}\psi_{m_{k}}}{dX^{2}}=\nu_{k}\,(1+\nu_{k})\,\psi_{m_{k}},

(197)

where
X=x/\delta_{k}
.

The most general tearing-parity solution of Eq. (
197
) in the limit
|X|\rightarrow 0
takes the form

\psi_{m_{k}}(X)\simeq\hat{B}_{L\,k}+\hat{B}_{S\,k}\,|X].

(198)

We can define

{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm in}}=\left(\frac{r_{k}}{\delta_{k}}\right)\frac{2\,\hat{B}_{S\,k}}{\hat{B}_{L\,k}}.

(199)

On the other hand, the most general tearing-parity solution of Eq. (
197
) in the limit
|X|\rightarrow\infty
is written

\displaystyle\psi_{m_{k}}(X)

\displaystyle=\hat{A}_{L\,k}\,|X|^{-\nu_{k}}+\hat{A}_{S\,k}\,|X|^{1+\nu_{k}}.

(200)

We can define

{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm out}}=\left(\frac{r_{k}}{\delta_{k}}\right)^{1+2\,\nu_{k}}\,\frac{2\,\hat{A}_{S\,k}}{\hat{A}_{L\,k}}.

(201)

A comparision between Eqs. (
190
)–(
193
) and (
198
)–(
201
) reveals that the solution to Eq. (
197
) successfully interpolates
between the resistive layer solution and the outer solution. It now remains to determine the relationship between
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm in}}
and

{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm out}}
.

E.3
Connection Formula

Suppose that we launch a “large” solution of Eq. (
197
),
\psi_{L}(X)=X^{-\nu_{k}}
,
from large
X
, and integrate to
X=0
. Let

\displaystyle\psi_{L}(0)

\displaystyle=a_{LL},

(202)

\displaystyle\frac{d\psi_{L}(0)}{dx}

\displaystyle=a_{SL}.

(203)

Next, suppose that we launch a “small” solution,

\psi_{S}(X)=X^{1+\nu_{k}}
,
from large
X
, and integrate to
X=0
. Let

\displaystyle\psi_{S}(0)

\displaystyle=a_{LS},

(204)

\displaystyle\frac{d\psi_{S}(0)}{dx}

\displaystyle=a_{SS}.

(205)

The most general solution is

\psi_{m_{k}}(X)=\hat{A}_{L\,k}\,\psi_{L}(X)+\hat{A}_{S\,k}\,\psi_{S}(X).

(206)

It follows that

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{L\,k}

\displaystyle=a_{LL}\,\hat{A}_{L\,k}+a_{LS}\,\hat{A}_{S\,k},

(207)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{S\,k}

\displaystyle=a_{SL}\,\hat{A}_{L\,k}+a_{SS}\,\hat{A}_{S\,k}.

(208)

Thus, Eqs. (
199
) and (
201
) yield the connection formula

\left(\frac{\delta_{k}}{r_{k}}\right)\frac{{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm in}}}{2}=\frac{a_{SL}+a_{SS}\,(\delta_{k}/r_{k})^{1+2\,\nu_{k}}\,({\Delta}_{k\,{\rm out}}/2)}{a_{LL}+a_{LS}\,(\delta_{k}/r_{k})^{1+2\,\nu_{k}}\,({\Delta}_{k\,{\rm out}}/2)}.

(209)

It remains to calculate the coefficients
a_{LL}
,
a_{LS}
,
a_{SL}
, and
a_{SS}
.

E.4
Analytic Solution

Let
\psi_{m\,k}=(1+X^{2})\,\phi
and

) transforms to give

(1-z^{2})\,\frac{d^{\,2}\phi}{dz^{2}}-4\,z\,\frac{d\phi}{dz}-[2-\nu_{k}\,(1+\nu_{k})]\,\phi=0.

(210)

Now, the Legendre functions
Q_{\nu_{k}}(z)
and

[

(1-z^{2})\,\frac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}-2\,z\,\frac{dw}{dz}+\nu_{k}\,(1+\nu_{k})\,w=0.

Let
w^{\prime}=dw/dz

(1-z^{2})\,\frac{d^{2}w^{\prime}}{dz^{2}}-4\,z\,\frac{dw^{\prime}}{dz}-[2-\nu_{k}\,(1+\nu_{k})]\,w^{\prime}=0.

It is clear from a comparison of Eqs. (

[

\displaystyle Q_{\nu_{k}}(z)

\displaystyle Q_{\nu_{k}}^{\prime}(z)\simeq-\frac{\pi^{1/2}\,(1+\nu_{k})\,{\Gamma}(1+\nu_{k})}{2^{1+\nu_{k}}\,\Gamma(3/2+\nu_{k})}\,z^{-2-\nu_{k}}+{\cal O}(z^{-4-\nu_{k}})

So, at large

\displaystyle(1+X^{2})\,Q_{\nu_{k}}^{\prime}({\rm i}\,X)\simeq{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\,\nu_{k}\,\pi/2}\,\frac{\pi^{1/2}\,(1+\nu_{k})\,\Gamma(1+\nu_{k})}{2^{1+\nu_{k}}\,\Gamma(3/2+\nu_{k})}\,X^{-\nu_{k}}.

E.5
Matching Formula

A large aspect-ratio tokamak equilibrium is characterized by
|\nu_{k}|\ll 1
. In this limit, Eqs. (
209
) (
221
), (
222
), (
227
), and (
228
) yield

{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm out}}={\Delta}_{k\,{\rm in}}+\frac{\pi\,\nu_{k}\,r_{k}}{\delta_{k}}.

(229)

It remains to specify the pressure-flattening width,
\delta_{k}
. Following Refs.
37
and
38
, we identify this width with the critical layer width above
which parallel thermal transport forces the electron temperature to be a flux-surface function. This critical layer width is given by
[
40
,
41
]

\displaystyle\delta_{d\,k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{8}\left(\frac{\chi_{\perp}}{\chi_{\parallel}}\right)^{1/4}\frac{r_{k}}{(r_{k}\,s_{k}\,n)^{1/2}},

(230)

\displaystyle\chi_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{\parallel}^{\rm smfp}\,\chi_{\parallel}^{\rm lmfp}}{\chi_{\parallel}^{\rm smfp}+\chi_{\parallel}^{\rm lmfp}},

(231)

\displaystyle\chi_{\parallel}^{\rm smfp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1.581\,\tau_{ee\,k}\,v_{t\,e\,k}^{\,2}}{1+0.2535\,Z_{\rm eff}},

(232)

\displaystyle\chi_{\parallel}^{\rm lmfp}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,R_{0}\,v_{t\,e\,k}\,r_{k}}{\pi^{1/2}\,n\,s_{k}\,\delta_{d\,k}},

(233)

\displaystyle v_{t\,e\,k}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\,T_{e\,k}}{m_{e}}\right)^{1/2}.

(234)

The previous five equations can be solved via iteration. In fact, if we choose
\delta_{k}=\delta_{d\,k}/(2\sqrt{\pi})
, identify
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm out}}
with the

{\Delta}_{k}
appearing in the tearing mode dispersion relation (
27
), identify
{\Delta}_{k\,{\rm in}}
with the
S_{k}^{1/3}\,\hat{\Delta}_{k}

obtained from the solution of the layer equations (see Appendix.
D
), and identify
\nu_{k}
with minus the resistive interchange stability parameter,
[
35
,
36
]

D_{R\,k}=\left(\frac{2\,q^{2}}{s^{2}}\,r\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right)_{r_{k}}\left[1-\frac{1}{q_{k}^{\,2}}+\frac{q_{k}^{\,2}\,s_{k}}{r_{k}^{\,4}}\int_{0}^{r_{k}}\left(\frac{r^{3}}{q^{2}}-2\,R_{0}^{\,2}\,r^{2}\,\frac{dP}{dr}\right)dr\right]

(235)

[rather than
-D_{I\,k}-1/4
—see Eq. (
195
)],
[
55
]

then we obtain

{\Delta}_{k}=S_{k}^{1/3}\,\hat{\Delta}_{k}-\sqrt{2}\,\pi^{3/2}\,D_{R\,k}\,\frac{r_{k}}{\delta_{d\,k}}.

(236)

This formula reproduces one given (but, unfortunately, not derived) in Ref.
37
that has been successfully benchmarked against the XTOR code. The final term on the right-hand side
of the previous equation represents the stabilizing effect of average magnetic field-line curvature on tearing modes in tokamak plasmas.

It should be understood that the local flattening of the pressure profile considered in this section is not real, but rather a proxy for modeling the influence of large parallel thermal conductivity on the so-called
Glasser curvature stabilization term.
[
35
,
36
,
38
]
Furthermore, the neglect of parallel transport in the resistive layer model
described in Appendix
D
is justified on the assumption that the layer width is much less than the flattening width,
\delta_{k}
.
