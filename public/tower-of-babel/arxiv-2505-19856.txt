Beyond the Electric Dipole Approximation: Electric and Magnetic Multipole Contributions Reveal Biaxial Water Structure from SFG Spectra at the Air-Water Interface
Louis Lehmann, Maximilian R. Becker, Lucas Tepper, Alexander P. Fellows, Álvaro Díaz Duque, Martin Thämer, Roland R. Netz

Abstract
The interpretation of sum-frequency generation (SFG) spectra has been severely limited by the absence of quantitative theoretical predictions for multipole contributions. The previously unknown magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole contributions are determined by the bulk media but appear in all experimental SFG spectra, obscuring the connection between measured spectra and interfacial structure. We present the simulation-based framework that predicts the full set of multipole contributions to the SFG spectrum. This framework also yields depth-resolved spectra, enabling the precise localization of spectroscopic features. Applied to the air-water interface, our approach achieves quantitative agreement with experimental spectra for different polarization combinations in both the bending and stretching regions. Multipole contributions are crucial for correctly interpreting SFG spectra: in the bending band, the electric dipole contribution is of an intensity similar to the magnetic dipole contribution, and both are dominated by the electric quadrupole contribution. In the OH-stretch region, the electric quadrupole contribution is found to be responsible for the mysterious shoulder at 3600 cm − 1 3600\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-1} . Crucially, subtracting the multipole contributions isolates the second-order electric dipole susceptibility, which is a quantitative probe for interfacial orientational anisotropy. This electric-dipole susceptibility reveals a pronounced biaxial ordering of water at the air-water interface. By resolving a fundamental limitation of the interpretation of SFG spectroscopy, our framework allows for the detailed extraction of interfacial water ordering from SFG spectra.

III
Conclusion

Accounting for multipole contributions is essential for interpreting SFG spectra and leads to quantitative agreement between simulations and experimental data. In the bending band, the electric dipole contribution has an amplitude comparable to the previously neglected magnetic dipole contributions, but the full signal is dominated by the electric quadrupole contribution.
The shoulder appearing at
3600\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-1}
in the stretch band has been attributed to orientational anisotropy
Ji et al. 2008
;
Stiopkin et al. 2011
, or to a combination mode involving intermolecular coupling
Suzuki, Nojima, and Yamaguchi 2017
, as summarized in the review by Tang
et al.

Tang et al. 2020
.
Our results demonstrate that the shoulder primarily originates from electric quadrupole contributions, which do not reflect interfacial structure but rather depend on bulk properties.
After subtracting multipoles, the anisotropic orientation of interfacial molecules is indeed described by the second-order electric dipole susceptibility, revealing strong biaxial water ordering at the air-water interface.
This biaxial structure is composed of a layering of essentially three orientational species, namely an inwards-oriented, a planar-oriented, and an outwards-oriented species.
Since SFG spectra are significantly influenced by bulk-specific multipole effects, it is crucial to account for and subtract these contributions to interpret experimental spectra in terms of interfacial orientations. This can be achieved by subtracting a reference spectrum with the same bulk medium
Fellows et al. 2025
, or by using theoretical predictions, like those provided in this work.

IV.1
Prediction of Locally Resolved SFG Spectra

We consider a planar interface between two media. The system is homogeneous in the
xy
plane but inhomogeneous along the
z
dimension.
The system is illuminated by two monochromatic light sources with frequencies
\omega^{\mathrm{VIS}}
and
\omega^{\mathrm{IR}}
that induce an electric current density
j_{i}^{(2)}(z,t)
, oscillating with the sum frequency
\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}=\omega^{\mathrm{IR}}+\omega^{\mathrm{VIS}}
.
Here
\omega^{\mathrm{IR}}
is in the IR range and invokes equifrequent oscillation of the nuclei, while
\omega^{\mathrm{VIS}}
is in the optical frequency range.
The resulting second-order electric current density is determined by the second-order response of a time-dependent perturbation expansion
Mukamel 1995

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}j_{i}^{(2)}(z,t)=-i\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}e^{-i\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}t}\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}\left(z\right)\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{VIS}}_{j}\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{IR}}_{k}+c.c.\,,

(7)

where
i
,
j
,
k\in\{x,y,z\}
are Cartesian coordinate indices.
Here
\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
is the second-order response profile, the tilde denotes a Fourier transformation, i.e.
\tilde{\phi}(\omega)=\mathrm{FT}[\phi(t)](\omega)
,
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime\prime}
stand for the real and imaginary parts and
c.c.
denotes the complex conjugate.
The external field amplitude
\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{i}
corresponds
Stern and Feller 2003
;
Bonthuis, Gekle, and Netz 2011
;
Bonthuis, Gekle, and Netz 2012
;
Bonthuis and Netz 2013
;
Gekle and Netz 2014
to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{i}=\left(\delta_{ix}+\delta_{iy}\right)\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{i}+\delta_{iz}\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{i}\,,

(8)

where
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{i}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{i}
are the amplitudes of the electric E-fields and the electric D-fields

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{\alpha}}_{i}(z,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{i}(z)e^{-i\omega^{\mathrm{\alpha}}t}+c.c.

(9)

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{\alpha}}_{i}(z,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{i}(z)e^{-i\omega^{\mathrm{\alpha}}t}+c.c.\,,

(10)

oscillating with the frequency
\omega^{\alpha}

\alpha\in\{\mathrm{SFG},\mathrm{VIS},\mathrm{IR}\}
and
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
Hence, the symbol
\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{i}
is a placeholder for spatially constant electric D-fields or E-fields, depending on the polarization. The external fields are given by
{F^{\mathrm{\alpha}}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\alpha}}_{i}e^{-i\omega^{\mathrm{\alpha}}t}+c.c.}
. By expressing the theory with respect to these fields, we avoid locality approximations.
We note that
\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}(z)
is distinct from the second-order susceptibility

which defines the response to electric E-fields.
Here, we only consider the signal arising from the interface on the length scale at which the external fields are spatially constant.
External field gradients lead to so-called

;
Fellows et al. 2025

The SFG spectrum follows by integration over
z
as

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{(2)}_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\,e^{-i\Delta k_{z}z}\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}(z)\,,

(11)

where

Here we utilize the additional approximation

. At the air waiter interface, this is a good approximation, as a typical value for
\Delta k_{z}

IV.2
Molecular Multipole Contributions

The second-order electric current density in Equation (
7
) can be decomposed into molecular multipole contributions according to

\displaystyle j^{(2)}_{i}(z,t)=j^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{i}(z,t)+j^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}_{i}(z,t)+j^{(2,\mathrm{M})}_{i}(z,t)+...\,,

(12)

where D, Q, and M stand for electric dipole, electric quadrupole, and magnetic dipole, respectively.
The multipole contributions to the second-order current density are defined as
{j_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{D})}(z,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{i}(z,t)}
,
{j_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}(z,t)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}_{ij}(z,t)}
, and
{j_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{M})}(z,t)=\Epsilon_{ijk}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}m^{(2)}_{k}(z,t)}
, where
\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{i}(z,t)
and
\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}_{ij}(z,t)
are the electric dipole and electric quadrupole density,
r_{i}
is the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
Cartesian coordinate of a vector position,
m^{(2)}_{i}(z,t)
is the magnetic dipole density and
\Epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol
Jackson 1975
. Higher-order molecular multipole contributions in Equation (
12
), do not contribute to the experimentally detectable spectrum
\tilde{S}^{(2)}_{ijk}
in Equation (
11
), in the limit
z\Delta k_{z}=0
and consequently need not be considered
Guyot-Sionnest and Shen 1988
.
Using a timescale separation approximation the second-order electric dipole density can be dissected into the contributions due to electric dipole
\Rho^{\mathrm{DS}}_{i}(\bm{r},t)
and electric quadrupole densities
\Rho^{\mathrm{QS}}_{ij}(\bm{r},t)
, acting as sources. The second-order electric dipole density
\Rho_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{D)}}(z,t)
can be dissected into the pure electric dipole contributions
\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{i}(z,t)
induced by
\Rho^{\mathrm{DS}}_{i}(\bm{r},t)
, and the electric dipole - electric quadrupole cross contribution
\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{DQ})}_{i}(z,t)
, induced by
\Rho^{\mathrm{QS}}_{ij}(\bm{r},t)
.
As shown in SI Section V D, these contributions can be defined by

\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{i}(z,t)=\frac{1}{A}\int\mathrm{d}x\int\mathrm{d}y\\
\left[\Rho^{\mathrm{DS}}_{i}(\bm{r},t)+\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}^{\prime}\tilde{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}_{ij}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)F_{j}^{\mathrm{DS}}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t)\right]

(13)

and

\displaystyle\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{DQ})}_{i}(z,t)=\frac{1}{A}\int\mathrm{d}x\int\mathrm{d}y\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}^{\prime}\tilde{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}_{ij}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)F_{j}^{\mathrm{QS}}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t)\,,

(14)

where
F_{i}^{\mathrm{DS}}(\bm{r},t)
and
F_{i}^{\mathrm{QS}}(\bm{r},t)
are the external electrostatic fields induced by the densities
\Rho^{\mathrm{DS}}_{i}(\bm{r},t)
and
\Rho^{\mathrm{QS}}_{ij}(\bm{r},t)
,
A
is the interfacial area and
\tilde{s}^{\mathrm{NL}}_{ij}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},t)
is a nonlocal, linear and instantaneous response function.
We note that the decomposition
\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{i}(z,t)
=
\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{i}(z,t)+\Rho^{(2,\mathrm{DQ})}_{i}(z,t)
, does not enter the calculation of
\tilde{S}^{(2)}_{ijk}
and is only used for interpretation.
We define the corresponding electric current densities
j^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{i}(z,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Rho_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}(z,t)
and
j^{(2,\mathrm{DQ})}_{i}(z,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Rho_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{DQ})}(z,t)
.
The second-order response profile is decomposed in analogy to Equation (
12
)
into

\displaystyle\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}(z)

\displaystyle=\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{ijk}(z)+\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}_{ijk}(z)+\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{M})}_{ijk}(z)

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{ijk}(z)

\displaystyle=\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{ijk}(z)+\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{DQ})}_{ijk}(z)\,,

(16)

where each contribution
\tilde{s}^{(2,\beta)}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
is defined as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}j_{i}^{(2,\beta)}(z,t)=-i\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}e^{-i\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}t}\tilde{s}^{(2,\beta)}_{ijk}\left(z\right)\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{VIS}}_{j}\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{IR}}_{k}+c.c.\,,

(17)

where
\beta\in\{\mathrm{DD},\mathrm{DQ},\mathrm{D},\mathrm{Q},\mathrm{M}\}
. Consequently, the spatially integrated multipole contributions to the SFG spectrum are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{(2,\beta)}_{ijk}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\,e^{-i\Delta k_{z}z}\tilde{s}^{(2,\beta)}_{ijk}\left(z\right)\,.

(18)

While the total current
j^{(2)}_{i}(z,t)
does not depend on the choice of the molecular origin, the contributions in Equation (
12
) do
Byrnes, Geissler, and Shen 2011
;
Hirano and Morita 2022
.
To determine the optimal position of the molecular origin, we consider the SFG signal from an interface with isotopically oriented molecules
\tilde{S}^{(2,\mathrm{ISO})}_{ijk}
, which we call an isotropic interface.
This isotropic interface is created by cutting bulk water at an arbitrary

z
-position into two halves.
Consequently, boundary contributions created by the change in density at the interface are present, but the molecules’ orientational distribution is inversion symmetry.
We test three molecular origins for the calculations of multipoles and find that if we choose the center of mass as the molecular origin
{\tilde{S}^{(2,\mathrm{ISO})}_{ijk}\approx\tilde{S}^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}_{ijk}+\tilde{S}^{(2,\mathrm{M})}_{ijk}}
does hold in good approximation.
Thus, choosing the center of mass as the expansion center ensures that the electric dipole contribution is solely due to molecular orientation, as discussed in detail in SI Section VI.
In contrast, choosing a molecular center, different from the center of mass, introduces significant boundary contributions in the electric dipole contribution
\tilde{S}^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{ijk}
.
Moreover, the center of mass maximizes the decoupling of molecular translations, vibrations, and rotations
Eckart 1935
;
Herzberg 1945
;
Wilson, Decius, and Cross 1980
.
The theory of our multipole decomposition is described in SI Sections II and III. There, we also derive the constitutive relations, including nonlinear multipolar source terms, using the Lorentz field approximation in planar geometry, based on works by Mizrahi and Sipe
Mizrahi and Sipe 1986
and Hirano and Morita
Hirano and Morita 2024
.
These equations are solely needed to aid the interpretation of experimental spectra.

IV.3
Fluctuation-Dissipation Relations within the Born-Oppenheimer Approximation

We assume that the VIS field interacts non-resonantly with the system, meaning it does polarize the molecules but does neither excite higher electronic levels, nor influence the nuclei trajectories.
This approximation is valid since the VIS field oscillates too rapidly to influence the motion of the nuclei yet slowly enough to act adiabatically on the distribution of the electrons. In this limit the SFG signal arises from a first-order perturbation expansion with respect to the external field from the IR light source.
The molecular multipoles are determined by the equations

\displaystyle\mu^{n}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{n,\mathrm{DD}}_{ij}(t)f^{n}_{jk}(t)F^{\mathrm{VIS}}_{k}(t)

(19)

\displaystyle Q^{n}_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{n,\mathrm{QD}}_{ijk}(t)f^{n}_{kl}(t)F^{\mathrm{VIS}}_{l}(t)\,.

(20)

where
\mu^{n}_{i}(t)
and
Q^{n}_{ij}(t)
are the induced electric dipole and electric quadrupole moments of the n
th
-molecule, and
\alpha^{n,\mathrm{DD}}_{ij}(t)
gives the electric dipole moment and
\alpha^{n,\mathrm{QD}}_{ijk}(t)
the electric quadrupole moment induced by a local electric field acting on the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
molecule. The local field factor
f_{ij}^{n}(t)
transforms an external field
F_{i}(t)
into the local E-field acting on the n
th
molecule
{E^{n}_{i}(t)=f_{ij}^{n}(t)F_{j}(t)}
and is determined in a self-consistent manner such that the multipoles induced by
E^{n}_{i}(t)
produce the field
E^{n}_{i}(t)-F_{i}(t)
.
It is demonstrated in SI Section V C that it is incorrect to solve separate self-consistent field equations for the VIS and the SFG field in the time domain, as proposed previously
Morita and Ishiyama 2008
;
Ishiyama and Morita 2009
;
Morita 2018
.
Rather, if a timescale-separation approximation is applied, Equations (
19
) and (
20
) each split into components at SFG and VIS frequencies, as described in SI Section V C.
The polarization contributions of the second-order response profiles, defined in Equation (
17
), are determined by a first-order perturbation expansion, for which we introduce the fluctuation-dissipation relations

\displaystyle s^{(2,\mathrm{D})}_{ijk}(z,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\Theta(t)}{Ak_{B}T\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sum\limits_{n}^{N_{\mathrm{mol}}}\left\langle\alpha^{n,\mathrm{DD}}_{il}(t)f^{n}_{lj}(t)\delta\left[z-z^{n}(t)\right]P_{k}(0)\right\rangle

(21)

\displaystyle s^{(2,\mathrm{Q})}_{ijk}(z,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta(t)}{Ak_{B}T\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sum\limits_{n}^{N_{\mathrm{mol}}}\left\langle\alpha^{n,\mathrm{QD}}_{izl}(t)f^{n}_{lj}(t)\delta\left[z-z^{n}(t)\right]P_{k}(0)\right\rangle,

(22)

where
\langle...\rangle
denotes ensemble averaging,
T
is the temperature,
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant,
\theta(x)
denotes the Heaviside function and the z-position of the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
molecule is denoted as
z^{n}
. These expressions omit the off-resonant hyperpolarizability, which can be included as shown in SI Section V, but does not contribute to the imaginary part of SFG spectra.
Equations (
21
) and (
22
) follow from time-dependent perturbation theory using the perturbation Hamiltonian
{H_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=-P_{i}F^{\mathrm{IR}}_{i}(t)}
, where
P_{i}
is the total system’s dipole moment. Hirano and Morita
Hirano and Morita 2022
suggest the perturbation expansion using the modified perturbation Hamiltonian
{H_{\mathrm{int}}^{+}(t)=-P^{+}_{i}F^{\mathrm{IR}}_{i}(t)}
, where
P^{+}_{i}(t)
is the dipole moment of the upper half of the simulation box. It is demonstrated in SI Section V F that this introduces a spurious contribution from the interface between the two halves. We note that while we treat electric dipole and electric quadrupole contributions in a precise manner, we approximate magnetic dipole contributions by considering only the leading order term of an expansion, as outlined in SI Section V E.
Furthermore, we only consider electrostatic coupling between the molecular multipoles, meaning we neglect the weak coupling between the nonlinear magnetic dipoles and the dielectric material.
We do not compute the position-resolved profile of magnetic dipole contributions but only the overall contribution to the SFG signal, which can be extracted from a simulation of a bulk system, according to a fluctuation-dissipation relation presented in SI Section V E.
Hence, we omit magnetic dipole contributions, whenever we present the response profiles
\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}(z)
, but consider them when we present the total spectra
\tilde{S}^{(2)}_{ijk}
.
In contrast to the electric dipole and electric quadrupole contributions, the magnetic dipole contribution does depend on
{\omega^{\mathrm{VIS}}}
, set to
2730\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, which is the center frequency of the VIS-field employed in the measurement of the bending contribution
Fellows et al. 2025
. The VIS frequency in the other experiments
Chiang et al. 2022
;
Yu et al. 2023
is quite similar .

IV.4
The Dielectric Profile

We define the linear response of the polarization density
{p_{i}(z,t)=D_{i}(z,t)-\varepsilon_{0}E_{i}(z,t)}
to an external field of amplitude
\mathcal{F}^{\alpha}_{i}
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}p^{(1)}_{i}(z,t)=e^{-i\omega^{\alpha}t}\tilde{s}^{(1,\mathrm{P})}_{ij}(z)\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\alpha}+c.c.\,.

(23)

The extraction of
\tilde{s}^{(1,\mathrm{P})}_{ij}(z)
from molecular dynamics simulation has been described before
Bonthuis, Gekle, and Netz 2011
;
Bonthuis, Gekle, and Netz 2012
;
Bonthuis and Netz 2013
;
Gekle and Netz 2014
;
Loche et al. 2018
;
Loche et al. 2019
;
Becker, Loche, and Netz 2022
and is reproduced in the supplementary information (SI) Section V A.
At the interface, the dielectric profile is tensorial, and the component perpendicular to the interface is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{zz}^{\mathrm{\alpha}}(z)=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{z}}{\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{z}(z)}=\frac{1}{1-\tilde{s}^{(1,\mathrm{P})}_{zz}(z)}\,,

(24)

while the component parallel to the interface is determined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}_{xx}(z)=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}(z)}{\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{x}}=1+\tilde{s}^{(1,\mathrm{P})}_{xx}(z).

(25)

Here,
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{i}(z)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\alpha}_{i}(z)
, are defined in Equations (
10
) and (
9
), respectively.
Due to the symmetry of our system we have
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}_{yy}(z)=\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}_{xx}(z)
. In bulk, the dielectric tensor reduces to the isotropic component

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(z)=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}_{xx}(z)+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}_{yy}(z)+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}_{zz}(z)}{3}\,.

(26)

IV.5
Electric Dipole SFG Contribution as a Fingerprint for Interfacial Structure

The response of the second-order electric current density to spatially constant external fields, which are
z
-polarized D-fields and
x
or
y
-polarized E-fields, is given in Equation (
7
).
As we want to relate SFG spectra to the molecular orientation distribution, we are interested in the second-order response of the electric dipole density to the average amplitude of the local E-field
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{L},\alpha}_{i}(z)
acting on the molecular centers.
We define the second-order electric dipole susceptibility
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
by

\varepsilon_{0}^{-1}j_{i}^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}(z,t)=\\
-i\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}f^{\mathrm{SFG}}_{i}(z)e^{-i\omega^{\mathrm{SFG}}t}\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{L,VIS}}_{j}(z)\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{L,IR}}_{k}(z)+c.c.\,.

(27)

The second-order electric dipole susceptibility
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
plays a central role in SFG theory as it allows to relate the macroscopic electric dipole contribution to the molecular orientation
Wei and Shen 2001
;
Morita 2018
;
Sun et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2022a
.
We can relate
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
to the second-order response function by

\displaystyle\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)=f^{\mathrm{SFG}}_{i}(z)f^{\mathrm{VIS}}_{j}(z)f^{\mathrm{IR}}_{k}(z)\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)\,,

(28)

where
f^{\alpha}_{i}(z)
are the laterally averaged local field factors, defined in Equation (
1
).
This is the formalism traditionally used in SFG theory
Shiratori and Morita 2011
;
Shiratori and Morita 2012
;
Shen 2016
;
Morita 2018
;
Chiang et al. 2022
;
Fellows et al. 2024a
;
Hirano and Morita 2024
.
Note that, we do not introduce any locality approximations in the computation of the second-order response
\tilde{s}^{(2)}_{ijk}(z)
, but rather predict
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
by dividing the nonlocal second-order response profile
\tilde{s}^{(2,\mathrm{DD})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
, by the averaged local field factors
f^{\alpha}_{i}(z)
.
We neglect the frequency dependence of
f^{\alpha}_{i}(z)
by approximating
f^{\alpha}_{i}(z)\approx f^{\mathrm{VIS}}_{i}(z)
.
We compare
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{DL})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
with
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{ORI})}_{ijk}\left(z\right)
,
which is solely based on the anisotropic orientation of the molecules.

Figure 6:
Sketch of the molecular Eckart frame for a water molecule.

The Euler angles
\theta
and
\psi
, specify the molecule’s orientation, and we employ the
z^{\prime}y^{\prime}z^{\prime}
convention
Gray and Gubbins 1984
.
We define the molecular Eckart frame
Eckart 1935
;
Herzberg 1945
;
Adler-Golden and Carney 1985
;
Rey 1998
by aligning the molecular
z^{\prime}
-axis with the permanent dipole vector. The molecular
x^{\prime}
-axis points out of the molecular plane, while the molecular
y^{\prime}
-axis is chosen to be perpendicular to both
x^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}
.The molecular Eckart frame is depicted in Figure
6
. As shown in SI Section IV, the molecular hyperpolarizability
\tilde{\beta}_{ijk}(\theta,\psi)
is determined by three orientation-dependent functions
q_{10}(\theta)
,
q_{30}(\theta)
and
q_{32}(\theta,\psi)
.
The first and third Legendre polynomials
Gray and Gubbins 1984

\displaystyle q_{10}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\cos{\theta}

(29)

\displaystyle q_{30}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[5\cos^{3}{\theta}-3\cos{\theta}\right]\,

(30)

describe the orientation of the molecular dipoles, while
q_{32}(\theta,\psi)
accounts for the rotation of the molecule around the molecular dipole moment and is only relevant if the molecule is not uniaxial.
Consequently, it is the biaxiality parameter, given by

\displaystyle q_{32}(\theta,\psi)

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)(\sin^{2}{\theta}\cos^{2}{\psi}-\sin^{2}{\theta}\sin^{2}{\psi})\,.

(31)

We obtain
\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{ORI})}_{ijk}(z)
by spatial averaging all hyperpolarizabilities

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}^{(2,\mathrm{ORI})}_{ijk}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\sum\limits_{n}^{N_{\mathrm{mol}}}\beta_{ijk}(\theta^{n},\psi^{n})

(32)

\displaystyle=\rho(z)\left[q_{10}(z)\tilde{\beta}_{ijk}^{10}+q_{30}(z)\tilde{\beta}_{ijk}^{30}+q_{32}(z)\tilde{\beta}_{ijk}^{32}\right]\,,

(33)

where
V
is the volume,
\rho(z)
is the molecular density and
q_{lm}(z)
is the z-dependent average of
q_{lm}(\theta^{n},\psi^{n})
over all molecules.
The frequency dependent coefficients
\tilde{\beta}_{ijk}^{lm}
are presented in Figure
3
J. A detailed description of the orientation analysis can be found in SI Section IV. The full information about the orientation distribution is determined by the orientation distribution function defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{ORI}}(\theta,\psi)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sin{\theta}N_{\mathrm{mol}}}\sum\limits_{n}^{N_{\mathrm{mol}}}\left\langle\delta(\theta-\theta^{n})\delta(\psi-\psi^{n})\right\rangle\,.

(34)

The prefactor ensures that
\rho_{\mathrm{ORI}}(\theta,\psi)
is properly normalized as

\int\limits_{0}^{\mathrm{2\pi}}\mathrm{d}\phi\int\limits_{0}^{\mathrm{\pi}}\sin{\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta\int\limits_{0}^{\mathrm{2\pi}}\mathrm{d}\psi\,\rho_{\mathrm{ORI}}(\theta,\psi)=1
. In an isotropic system
\rho_{\mathrm{ORI}}(\theta,\psi)
is constant.

V
Methods

All simulations are done in the NVT ensemble at room temperature
T=$298\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
with the MB-Pol force field
Babin, Leforestier, and Paesani 2013
;
Babin, Medders, and Paesani 2014
;
Medders, Babin, and Paesani 2014
. For starting configurations, 94 equally spaced initial configurations are extracted from a
20\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
long simulation using the SPC/E force field
Berendsen, Grigera, and Straatsma 1987
and the GROMACS molecular dynamics software
Abraham et al. 2015
. The SPC/E simulations are done with a time step of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}
and a velocity rescaling thermostat
Bussi, Donadio, and Parrinello 2007
,
with a relaxation time of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
.
Each of the initial configurations is equilibrated with the MB-Pol force field for
20\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
, and afterward, production runs with an average runtime of
0.86\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
are executed using the LAMMPS software
Thompson et al. 2022
.
Again, a velocity-rescaling thermostat is employed. However, for MB-Pol simulations, the relaxation time is set to
5\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
, and the time step is
0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}
.
The box size is
2\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
in the x and y dimensions and
6\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
in the z dimension. This size was chosen to ensure that the box contains more than twice as much air as water, since the water slab spans approximately
2.6\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
.
The box is filled with
352
water molecules and the two interfaces lie in the
xy
plane.
The total dipole moment of the simulation box is computed with the modified TTMF-4 model, which describes the electrostatics of the MB-Pol potential
Burnham et al. 2008
;
Babin, Leforestier, and Paesani 2013
.
The same model is used to predict the dielectric profile in the IR range, where we compute polarization density trajectories from monopole and dipole density trajectories.
Molecular polarizabilities are computed with single-molecule quantum chemistry calculations using the software Gaussian 16
Frisch et al. 2016
.
The theoretical level is CCSD(T)/aug-cc-PVTZ and B3LYP/aug-cc-PVTZ in the parameterization of
\alpha^{n,\mathrm{DD}}_{ij}(t)
and
\alpha^{n,\mathrm{QD}}_{ijk}(t)
, appearing in Equations (
19
) and (
20
), respectively. Electrostatic interactions between the induced multipoles are computed as described in SI Sections V and XII, involving the particle mesh Ewald algorithm included in OpenMM
Eastman et al. 2024
.
The simulations of the bulk systems are done in a cubic box with a length of
2.0727\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
consisting of 297 molecules.
The magnetic dipole contribution is extracted from a different simulation, as it requires a much smaller write-out frequency. The initial configurations for the simulations used to extract the dielectric constant and magnetic dipole contribution are taken from a simulation, using the SPC/E forcefield, after
20\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
and
40\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
, respectively. Here, the first
10\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
with the MB-Pol model are discarded, and
400\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
are used for the computation of the bulk dielectric constant, and
300\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
for the computation of the second-order magnetic dipole susceptibility.
The simulation parameters of the bulk simulations are the same as for the interface. We use the recommended settings of the MB-Pol potential from the GitHub repository "MBX - Version 0.7" from the Paesani group
Riera et al. 2023
. The write-out frequency is
3.2\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}
for all spectra except
0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}
for the magnetic dipole component.
The smoothing procedure is described in SI Section VIII. In all simulations, periodic boundary conditions are imposed. However, we correct for the unphysical field arising from the periodic replicas as described in SI Section XI, previously applied in the context of the dielectric profile
Stern and Feller 2003
;
Shiratori and Morita 2011
;
Loche et al. 2019
.
