A High Order IMEX Method for Generalized Korteweg de-Vries Equations Thanks: Draft version, August 24, 2026
Seth Gerberding

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a high order space-time approximation of generalized Korteweg de-Vries equations. More specifically, the method uses continuous H 1 H^{1} -conforming finite elements for the spatial approximation and implicit-explicit methods for the temporal approximation. The method is high order in both space, provably stable, and mass-conservative. The scheme is formulated, its properties are proven, and numerical simulations are provided to illustrate the proposed methodology.

high-order method, mass conservative

1
Introduction

This paper is concerned with the numerical approximation of generalized Korteweg de-Vries (gKdV) equations using
H^{1}
-conforming finite elements and implicit-explicit (IMEX) time stepping. The generalization considered here is one that generalizes the usual hyperbolic flux
\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2}
to a generic Lipschitz flux
\mathbf{f}(u)
. The objective of this work is to construct an approximation technique that is robust (conservative and stable), high order accurate in space and time, agnostic to the choice of hyperbolic flux, and stable up to a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition of the type
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h)
.

The KdV equation is a classic partial differential equation in the field of non-linear dispersive waves. It has the special property that
it admits
soliton
solutions (also known as solitary waves), which are traveling waves that maintain their shape and speed. In general, solutions of the KdV equation can be analytically decomposed into solitons and dispersive waves using the inverse scattering method (
Miura [30]
). This phenomenon was first observed numerically by
Zabusky and Kruskal [41]
.

In applications, the KdV equation is a prototypical equation for shallow water models. It was first derived as a model to explain solitary waves first observed in
Russell [32]
. Since then, it has also been used to study plasma physics (see
Hong and Lee [22]
), optics (
Horsley [23]
), and other physical phenomenon (see
Crighton [10]
). Its generalizations also have wide applications in plasma physics, traffic flow, and water waves (
Leblond and Mihalache [27]
,
Li and Liu [28]
,
Benjamin et al. [3]
). A generalization in the context of shallow water models is the Serre-Green-Naghdi equation (
Serre [35]
,
Green et al. [14]
), which is a dispersive system that admits solitons and is used to model shoaling, wave propagation, and dam breaks, for example in
Guermond et al. [18]
.

Numerically, generalized KdV equations pose several challenges. Explicit time stepping methods require severe CFL restrictions, often of the kind
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h^{3})
, as encountered in
Sanz-Serna [33, Equation 2.22]
. This restriction requires a prohibitive number of time steps even when the final time is small; see
Greigh and Morris [15]
. Implicit time stepping methods may circumvent the CFL restriction issue, for example in
Bona et al. [5]
,
Sanz-Serna and Christie [34]
,
Fu and Shu [13]
, but the nonlinear term leads to a system of nonlinear equations. Lastly, it is desirable for numerical methods to preserve discrete analogs of important properties admitted by the equation (e.g., conservation of physical quantities). Particular attention has been paid to the mass and the energy (
L^{2}
-norm) of the solution; see
Karakashian and Xing [25]
,
Sanz-Serna [33]
,
Winther [38]
.

In this paper, we propose a method which uses
H^{1}
-conforming finite elements and an IMEX time-stepping scheme. The method is conservative, high order accurate, and
\ell^{2}
stable at the discrete level. The IMEX split allows us to avoid the restrictive
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h^{3})
CFL condition by treating the third order term implicitly. We also avoid nonlinear solves by treating the nonlinear flux explicitly, which only requires a CFL condition of the kind
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h)
. We do not claim originality for the splitting approach; the reader is referred to
Holden et al. [20]
for an analysis on operator splitting for the KdV equation. To ensure robustness of the hyperbolic step (
Holden et al. [21]
), we use a method developed by
Guermond and Popov [16]
to solve hyperbolic conservation laws. We also reduce the third order derivative to second order by introducing auxiliary variables; doing so permits us to use
H^{1}
-conforming finite elements.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The model problem is outlined in section
2
. The details of the temporal and space discretizations are provided in section
3
. Section
4
describes how to make the method high order and conservative, and section
5
contains simulations that demonstrate the theory. The main results of the paper are the
\ell^{2}
stability property (Theorem
3.9
) and the properties of the high-order scheme (Theorem
4.6
).

2
The model problem

In this section, we describe the model problem. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
D=[a,b]^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the fundamental domain with length
l_{D}:=b-a
. Let
\mathbf{f}\in\text{Lip}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a Lipschitz, vector-valued function, hereafter referred to as the
hyperbolic flux
or simply
flux
. This choice is to indicate that we have in mind functions
\mathbf{f}
such that the equation

(2.1)

\partial_{t}u+\Div(\mathbf{f}(u))=0,

is a hyperbolic conservation law. Let
u_{0}(x)\in L^{\infty}(D)
,
T>0
and
\epsilon>0
. We consider the following Cauchy problem for a generalized KdV equation: Find
u(t,x):[0,T]\times D\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)
,
u
is periodic with respect to
D
, and satisfies:

(2.2)

\partial_{t}u+\Div(\mathbf{f}(u))+\epsilon\Delta(\partial_{x}u)=0~\text{ in $(0,T)\times D$}.

We call (
2.2
) a “generalized” KdV equation because the hyperbolic flux,
\mathbf{f}(u)
is generic and does not have to be the usual Burgers flux. Generalizations in the past have included changing the polynomial degree of the flux, i.e.,
u^{p}\partial_{x}u
, for example in
Bona et al. [6]
,
Karakashian and Makridakis [24]
. Other authors have also assumed a generic flux, for example in
Fu and Shu [13]
.

Let us briefly mention several properties of (
2.2
) but omit their proofs for brevity. These properties are reflected in the numerical method; see theorem
3.10
for mass conservation and theorem
3.9
for a discrete
l^{2}
stability result. If
u
is a smooth solution to (
2.2
), then we have
\partial_{t}\int_{D}u(x,t)~\mathrm{d}x=0
(mass conservation), and if
\int_{D}\Div(\mathbf{f}(u))u~\mathrm{d}x=0
, then
\partial_{t}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(D)}=0
(energy conservation). For example, many generalized KdV equations consist of a polynomial flux, i.e.,
f(u)=u^{p}
for
p\in\mathbb{N}
, and such fluxes satisfy these properties.

Concrete examples of (
2.2
) include the following:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+6u\partial_{x}u+\partial_{xxx}u

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{(KdV)}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}u+\partial_{xxx}u

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{(Linear-KdV)}

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+6u\partial_{x}u+\Delta(\partial_{x}u)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{(Zakharov–Kuznetsov (ZK) (\cite[citet]{\@@bibref{Authors Phrase1NumberPhrase2}{Wazwaz_2008}{\@@citephrase{[}}{\@@citephrase{]}}}))}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{p}u^{p}\right)+\partial_{xxx}u

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{(Polynomial gKdV)}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}u+\partial_{x}u^{p}+\partial_{xxx}u

\displaystyle=0.

We pose the model problem in multiple spatial dimensions, but in practice the problems we are interested in are one dimensional.

3
Low-Order Approximation details

In this section, we present the details for the low-order spatial and temporal approximation. It begins with the IMEX time-stepping method while remaining continuous in space. Then, it provides the spatial discretization details for the
H^{1}
-conforming finite elements. Next we provide the fully discrete explicit hyperbolic prediction and implicit dispersive update sub-steps. It ends with a stability result in the discrete
l^{2}
norm defined in (
3.14
) and a mass-conservation result.

3.1
Temporal Discretization

Let
t^{n}
be the current time with
n\in\{0:N\}
,
t^{0}=0
and
t^{N}=T
. Let
\tau^{n}>0
be the current time step with
t^{n+1}:=t^{n}+\tau^{n}
. For the remainder of this work, we remove the dependency of
\tau
on
n
to alleviate the notation, but in practice the time step depends on
n
. Let
u^{n}:D\to\mathbb{R}
be the approximation at time
t^{n}
. We construct
u^{n+1}
using an implicit-explicit method, where the hyperbolic flux is treated explicitly and the third order operator is treated implicitly. Hence,
u^{n+1}
is found by solving the following equation:

(3.1)

\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}+\Div(\mathbf{f}(u^{n}))+\epsilon\Delta(\partial_{x}u^{n+1})=0.

We introduce an intermediate stage,
w^{n+1}
, and solve (
3.1
) by rewriting it as the following system:

(3.2)

\displaystyle w^{n+1}

\displaystyle=u^{n}-\tau\Div(\mathbf{f}(u^{n})),

(3.3)

\displaystyle u^{n+1}+

\displaystyle\tau\epsilon\Delta(\partial_{x}u^{n+1})=w^{n+1}.

Equation (
3.2
) is an explicit update with the hyperbolic flux, which then becomes the source term in the implicit update (
3.3
).

3.2
Reformulation

In this section, we introduce auxiliary variables to reformulate the model and reduce (
3.3
) to second order in space. Doing so allows us to use
H^{1}
-conforming finite elements, whereas keeping (
3.3
) would require
H^{2}
-conformity. Since this analysis concerns (
3.3
), we consider the following equation:

(3.4)

\partial_{t}u+\epsilon\Delta(\partial_{x}u)=0.

The solution satisfies the energy estimate
\partial_{t}\int_{D}u^{2}~\mathrm{d}x=0
. Assuming
d=2
, our reformulation of (
3.4
) consists of auxiliary variables
z,g
which satisfy the following system:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\epsilon\partial_{xx}z

\displaystyle+\epsilon\partial_{y}\partial_{x}g=0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}u,

(3.7)

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\partial_{y}u.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(u,z,g)
be a smooth periodic solution to (
3.5
)-(
3.7
). Then the following energy conservation equation holds:

(3.8)

\partial_{t}\int_{D}u^{2}~\mathrm{d}x=0.

\square

Proof.

Multiply equation (
3.5
) by
u
, (
3.6
) by
\epsilon\partial_{x}z
, and (
3.7
) by
\epsilon\partial_{x}g
. Integrate each over
D
. Since
g,z
are periodic, we deduce
\int_{D}z\partial_{x}z~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}g\partial_{x}g~\mathrm{d}x=0
. We thus arrive at the following:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{D}\frac{u^{2}}{2}~\mathrm{d}x-\epsilon\int_{D}\partial_{x}z\partial_{x}u~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\epsilon\int_{D}\partial_{x}g\partial_{y}u~\mathrm{d}x=0

\displaystyle\epsilon\int_{D}\partial_{x}u\partial_{x}z~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\epsilon\int_{D}\partial_{y}u\partial_{x}g~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0.

Summing these equations and multiplying by
2
yields (
3.8
).

Motivated by this analysis, we replace (
3.4
) by the following system:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{u^{n+1}-w^{n+1}}{\tau}+\epsilon\partial_{xx}z^{n+1}

\displaystyle+\epsilon\partial_{y}\partial_{x}g^{n+1}=0,

(3.10)

\displaystyle z^{n+1}=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u^{n+1},

(3.11)

\displaystyle g^{n+1}=

\displaystyle\partial_{y}u^{n+1}.

We note that auxiliary variables have been used before for higher order derivatives. For example,
Yan and Shu [39]
reduced the third order problem to a system of first order equation for use in a local discontinuous Galerkin method.

3.3
Spatial Discretization

In this section, we give the spatial discretization and finite element formulation. Recall
D=[a,b]^{d}
. Let
\{{\mathcal{K}}_{h}\}_{h>0}
be a shape-regular sequence of meshes that cover
D
exactly. Now let
h>0
be given, and let
(\hat{K},\hat{P},\hat{\Sigma})
be the reference element. For each
K\in\mathcal{K}_{h}
, let
T_{K}:\hat{K}\to K
be an affine geometric mapping. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
be the set of all polynomials of degree
n
on
\hat{K}
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{V}:=\{1:I\}
be an enumeration of the degrees of freedom. Now we consider the space:

(3.12)

V_{h}:=\{v\in C^{0}(D):v\circ T_{K}\in\widehat{P}\text{ for all $K\in K_{h}$ and is periodic with respect to $D$}\}.

Note that
V_{h}\subset H^{1}(D)
. The reference shape functions are denoted by
\{\hat{\theta}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}
with
\mathcal{N}:=\{1:n_{sh}\}
. These function forms a basis of
\hat{P}
and we assume they satisfy the partition of unity property, i.e.,
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{\theta}_{i}(\hat{x})=1
for all
\hat{x}\in\hat{K}
. We denote the global shape functions by
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
. We assume that the global shape functions also have the partition of unity property. For
i\in\mathcal{V}
, we let
\mathcal{I}(i):=\{j\in\mathcal{V}:~\phi_{j}\phi_{i}\not\equiv 0\}
. The space approximation of
u
at time
t^{n}
is given by
u_{h}^{n}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}^{n}\phi_{i}\in V_{h}.
Using the basis functions, we define several quantities:

(3.13)

m_{i}:=\int_{D}\phi_{i}(x)~\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mathbf{c}_{ij}:=\int_{D}\phi_{i}(x)\nabla\phi_{j}(x)~\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mathbf{n}_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{c}_{ij}}{\|\mathbf{c}_{ij}\|_{l^{2}}}.

We assume that
m_{i}>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
. The partition of unity property implies that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{c}_{ij}=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
, and
j\in\mathcal{I}(i)
iff
i\in\mathcal{I}(j)
. We let
(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the
L^{2}
inner product and also define the following weighted
l^{2}
-norm on
V_{h}
, which we use to prove stability:

(3.14)

\|u_{h}\|_{l^{2}}^{2}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}^{2}m_{i}.

3.3.1
Explicit Hyperbolic Prediction

In this section, we outline the hyperbolic sub-step by fully discretizing (
3.2
) using a graph-viscosity technique developed in
[
16
]
. Given
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
, compute
w_{h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
by:

(3.15)

m_{i}\frac{W^{n+1}_{i}-U_{i}^{n}}{\tau}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{f}(U_{j}^{n})\cdot\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d_{ij}^{n}(U_{j}^{n}-U_{i}^{n})=0.

Here,
d_{ij}^{n}:=\max(\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{n}_{ij},U_{i}^{n},U_{j}^{n})\|\mathbf{c}_{ij}\|_{l^{2}},\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{n}_{ji},U_{j}^{n},U_{i}^{n})\|\mathbf{c}_{ji}\|_{l^{2}})
is called graph viscosity, where
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{n}_{ij},U_{i}^{n},U_{j}^{n})
is a bound on the max wave speed on the one-dimensional Riemann problem

(3.16)

\partial_{t}u+\Div(\mathbf{f}(u)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{ij})=0;\quad u_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}U_{i}^{n}&x<0\\
U_{j}^{n}&x>0\end{cases};\quad\mathbf{n}_{ij}:=\mathbf{c}_{ij}/\|\mathbf{c}_{ij}\|_{l^{2}}.

Using the graph viscosity, (
3.15
) has several key properties, which we recall in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
. Assume
\widehat{\theta}_{i}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\widehat{K}
and
i\in\mathcal{V}
. Assume the following CFL conditions holds:

(3.17)

\min_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\left(1-2\tau\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)\setminus\{i\}}d_{ij}^{n}}{m_{i}}\right)\geq 0.

Then the following statements hold true.

1.

Let
U_{i}^{m,n}:=\min_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\{U_{j}^{n}\},~U_{i}^{M,n}:=\max_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\{U_{j}^{n}\}
. Then,

W_{i}^{n+1}\in[U_{i}^{m,n},U_{i}^{M,n}]
.

2.

Let
U^{n}_{\min}:=\min_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\{U_{j}^{n}\}
and
U^{n}_{\max}:=\max_{j\in\mathcal{V}}\{U_{j}^{n}\}
. Then
w_{h}^{n+1}(x)\in

[U_{\min}^{n},U_{\max}^{n}]~\forall x\in D.

3.

Let
\eta\in C^{2}(D)
be convex. Define
\mathbf{q}(u):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying
q_{l}^{\prime}(u)=\eta^{\prime}(u)f^{\prime}_{l}(u)
, i.e.,
(\eta,\mathbf{q})
form an entropy pair for (
2.1
). Then the following discrete entropy inequality holds:

(3.18)

\displaystyle\frac{m_{i}}{\tau}\left(\eta(W_{i}^{n+1})-\eta(U_{i}^{n})\right)+\int_{D}\Div\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{q}(U_{j}^{n})\phi_{j}\right)\phi_{i}~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}d_{ij}^{n}(\eta(U_{j}^{n})-\eta(U_{i}^{n}))\leq 0.

\square

Proof.

See
Ern and Guermond [11]
.

Now we prove an
\ell^{2}
stability result for the hyperbolic prediction.

Corollary 3.3
(Hyperbolic stability).

Let
\eta(u)=\frac{u^{2}}{2}
. Assume the conditions of lemma
3.2
are met. Then, the following
\ell^{2}
bound holds:

(3.19)

\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|u_{h}^{n}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

\square

Proof.

Sum (
3.18
) over
i\in\mathcal{V}
. The partition of unity property and periodic boundary conditions imply the second term vanishes; the third term is antisymmetric, and so vanishes under the sum. By invoking the definition of
\eta
the proof is complete.

Lemma 3.4
(Hyperbolic mass conservation).

Let
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
and let
w_{h}^{n+1}
be computed via (
3.15
). Then,
\int_{D}w_{h}^{n+1}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}u_{h}^{n}~\mathrm{d}x
.

\square

Proof.

We refer the reader to remark 81.4 in
[
11
]
.

3.3.2
Implicit Dispersive Update

In this section, we outline the spatial discretization for the dispersive sub-step (
3.9
)-(
3.11
). Let
w_{h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
. The fully discrete dispersive update is given by: Find

(u_{h}^{n+1},z_{h}^{n+1},g_{h}^{n+1})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}\times V_{h}
so that, for all
(v_{h},r_{h},s_{h})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}\times V_{h}
:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{u_{h}^{n+1}-w_{h}^{n+1}}{\tau},v_{h}\right)-\epsilon(\partial_{x}z_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}v_{h})-

\displaystyle\epsilon(\partial_{x}g_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{y}v_{h})-\epsilon c(z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}v_{h})-

(3.20)

\displaystyle\epsilon c

\displaystyle(g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{y}v_{h})=0,

(3.21)

\displaystyle(z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1},

\displaystyle cr_{h}-\partial_{x}r_{h})=0,

(3.22)

\displaystyle(g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1},

\displaystyle cs_{h}-\partial_{x}s_{h})=0.

where
c>0
is a dimensional constant that scales by the inverse of the length scale.

Lemma 3.5
(Dispersive stability).

Let
w_{h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
and let
u_{h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
be computed by solving (
3.20
)-(
3.22
). Then, for all
\tau>0
, the following holds:

(3.23)

\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u_{h}^{n+1}-w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\tau\epsilon c\|z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\\
2\tau\epsilon c\|g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

\square

Proof.

We test (
3.20
) with
v_{h}=u_{h}^{n+1}
, (
3.21
) with
r_{h}=z_{h}^{n+1}
, and (
3.22
) with
s_{h}=g_{h}^{n+1}
, which yields:

(3.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\|u_{h}^{n+1}-w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}}{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{2}-\tau\epsilon(\partial_{x}z_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1})

\displaystyle-\tau\epsilon(\partial_{x}g_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1})-

\displaystyle\tau\epsilon c(z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\tau\epsilon c(g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1})=0,

(3.25)

\displaystyle c(z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1},z_{h}^{n+1})-

\displaystyle(-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}z_{h}^{n+1})=0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle c(w_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1},w_{h}^{n+1})

\displaystyle-(-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}w_{h}^{n+1})=0.

Multiplying equation (
3.25
) and (
3.26
) equations by
\tau\epsilon
and adding them to (
3.24
) then gives

\displaystyle\frac{\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\|u_{h}^{n+1}-w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}}{2}-\frac{\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{2}+

(3.27)

\displaystyle\tau\epsilon c\|z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau c\epsilon\|g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0.

Remark 3.6
(Well-posedness of the dispersive update).

We conclude the dispersive update problem (
3.20
)-(
3.22
) is well posed by using the stability result and invoking the Rank-Nullity theorem.

\square

Remark 3.7
(Lumped mass for implicit dispersive update).

In order to prove a stability result for the IMEX method, we need to approximate the time derivative in (
3.9
) using the lumped mass. The problem becomes: for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
, find
U_{i}^{n+1}
so that:

(3.28)

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{U_{i}^{n+1}-W_{i}^{n+1}}{\tau}-\epsilon(\partial_{x}z_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}\phi_{i})-\epsilon(\partial_{x}g_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{y}\phi_{i})-

\displaystyle\epsilon c(z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{x}\phi_{i})-\epsilon c(g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1},\partial_{y}\phi_{i})=0,

while (
3.21
)-(
3.22
) remain the same. The stability result is similar to lemma
3.5
, but with different norms:

(3.29)

\displaystyle\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+\|u_{h}^{n+1}-w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+2\epsilon\tau\|z_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{x}u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+

\displaystyle 2\epsilon\tau c\|g_{h}^{n+1}-\partial_{y}u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}^{2}.

The proof proceeds as follows: multiply (
3.28
) by
U_{i}^{n+1}
and sum over
i\in\mathcal{V}
. As the restrictions are the same, the proof continues as in lemma
3.5
.

\square

Remark 3.8
(Mass Conservation).

By testing (
3.20
) with
v_{h}=1
, we deduce that dispersive update is mass conservative. The result holds as well if we use the lumped mass.

\square

3.3.3
Euler-IMEX

This section contains the stability and conservation results for the complete IMEX method. These results are direct consequences of the lemmas regarding the explicit and implicit sub-problems.

Theorem 3.9
(Stability with lumped mass).

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
, and let
w_{h}^{n+1}
be computed using the explicit hyperbolic prediction (
3.15
). Let
u_{h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
be computed via the implicit dispersive update with lumped mass matrix, i.e., solving (
3.28
) and (
3.21
)-(
3.22
). Then, for
\tau\leq\tau^{*}
with

\tau^{*}:=\min_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{m_{i}}{4\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}d_{ij}^{n}},

the following inequality holds:

(3.30)

\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}\leq\|u_{h}^{n}\|_{l^{2}}.

\square

Proof.

Let
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
and
\tau\leq\tau^{\ast}
. Then, by corollary
3.3
, since
\tau\leq\tau^{\ast}
,
\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}\leq\|u_{h}^{n}\|_{l^{2}}
. The analysis in remark
3.7
implies that
\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}\leq\|w_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}
. Thus,
\|u_{h}^{n+1}\|_{l^{2}}\\
\leq\|u_{h}^{n}\|_{l^{2}}
.

This theorem shows the benefit of utilizing an IMEX method: since
m_{i}=\mathcal{O}(h)
, the method only requires a CFL condition of
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h)
for stability. Furthermore, we require no nonlinear solves, as the nonlinearity is computed explicitly.

Remark 3.10
(Mass Conservation for Euler-IMEX).

If
u_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}
, then
\int_{D}u_{h}^{n+1}~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\dots=\int_{D}u_{h}^{0}~\mathrm{d}x
. This fact follows from lemma
3.4
and the analysis done in remark
3.8
.

\square

4
High Order

This section contains the details to make the IMEX method high order in time and space. The idea is to make the hyperbolic prediction (
3.15
) high order in space by using higher degree polynomials in conjunction with high order graph viscosity and a limiting operator. The dispersive update (
3.9
)-(
3.11
) is made high order in space by using higher order polynomials in the definition of the finite element space. To make the time integration high order, we follow the work done in
Ern and Guermond [12]
and use a particular set of explicit and diagonally implicit Runge-Kutta methods (ERK and DIRK methods, respectively). These methods are constructed to be high order in time and mass conservative.

4.1
High Order Hyperbolic Prediction

Let us outline the procedure to make the hyperbolic prediction high order in space. Begin by constructing a high order graph viscosity. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{\psi_{k}^{n}\}_{k\in\mathcal{V}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers. The high order graph viscosity is given by:

(4.1)

d_{ij}^{H,n}:=d_{ij}^{n}\max(\psi_{i}^{n},\psi_{j}^{n}).

There are several ways to choose
\psi_{k}^{n}
. Examples include
smoothness based graph viscosity
, where
\psi_{k}^{n}
are chosen based upon the local smoothness of the solution,
greedy graph viscosity
, where
\psi_{k}^{n}
are based on a set of values derived from the solution and the
d_{ij}^{n}
s, and
entropy-based graph viscosity
, where the
\psi_{k}^{n}
are chosen using entropy inequalities. Further details on all of these examples can be found in
[
11
]
.

The next step is to utilize the consistent mass matrix, given by
m_{ij}:=\int_{D}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}~\mathrm{d}x
, in place of the lump-mass matrix. These two changes give rise to the following hyperbolic prediction:

(4.2)

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\frac{m_{ij}}{\tau}(W_{j}^{n+1}-U_{j}^{n})+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\left(\mathbf{f}(U_{j}^{n})\cdot\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d_{ij}^{H,n}(U_{j}^{n}-U_{i}^{n})\right)=0.

With this expression and
(
3.15
)
in mind, we define the following maps:
\mathbf{F}^{L}:\mathbb{R}^{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{I}
and
\mathbf{F}^{H}:\mathbb{R}^{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{I}
via:

(4.3)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{F}^{L}(U))_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{f}(U_{j})\cdot\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d_{ij}^{n}(U_{j}-U_{i}),

(4.4)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{F}^{H}(U))_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{f}(U_{j})\cdot\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d^{H,n}_{ij}(U_{j}-U_{i}).

The low and high order hyperbolic predictions can be written using (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) as follows:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{L}W^{L,n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}^{L}U^{n}-\tau\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n}),

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{H}W^{H,n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{M}^{H}U^{n}-\tau\mathbf{F}^{H}(U^{n}),

where
(\mathbb{M}^{L})_{i}:=\int_{D}\phi_{i}~\mathrm{d}x
denotes the lumped mass matrix and
(\mathbb{M}^{H})_{ij}=m_{ij}
denotes the consistent mass matrix.

The next step consists of using a limiting operator. Since there are many such operators in the literature, we formalize this technology via the following assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

There exists a map, called a
limiter
,
\ell:[a,b]^{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{I}\times\mathbb{R}^{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{I}
such that the following properties hold:

1.

(Maximum principle) If
\mathbb{M}^{L}U^{n}-\tau\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n})\in[a,b]^{I}
, then

\ell(U^{n},\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n}),\mathbf{F}^{H}(U^{n}))\in[a,b]^{I}
.

2.

(Mass conservation) Let
U^{n+1}:=\ell(U^{n},\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n}),\mathbf{F}^{H}(U^{n}))
. Then

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(i)}U_{i}^{n+1}m_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(i)}U_{i}^{n}m_{i}
.

\square

Examples of such a limiter include
flux corrected transport
, found in
Zalesak [42]
and
Kuzmin et al. [26]
and
convex limiting
, which relies upon quasi-concave functionals, found in
[
11
]
.

We now have all the pieces to describe the high order (in space) hyperbolic prediction: given
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
, compute
w_{h}^{L,n+1}
and
w_{h}^{H,n+1}
via (
4.5
) and (
4.6
). Then we compute
w_{h}^{n+1}
via:

(4.7)

W^{n+1}=\ell(U^{n},\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n}),\mathbf{F}^{H}(U^{n})).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let (
3.9
) hold. Let
u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
and let
w_{h}^{L,n+1}
be computed using (
4.5
),
w_{h}^{H,n+1}\in V_{h}
with (
4.6
), and
w_{h}^{n+1}\in V_{h}
by applying (
4.7
) to
w_{h}^{L,n+1}
and
w_{h}^{H,n+1}
. Then,
\int_{D}w_{h}^{n+1}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}u_{h}^{n}~\mathrm{d}x
.

\square

Proof.

By lemma
3.4

\int_{D}w_{h}^{L,n+1}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}u_{h}^{n}~\mathrm{d}x
. Then, since the limiter is conservative by assumption, we obtain
\int_{D}w_{h}^{n+1}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}w_{h}^{L,n+1}~\mathrm{d}x
. Combining these two equalities gives the result.

4.2
High Order Dispersive Update

To alleviate notation, we introduce an operator
\mathbf{G}:V_{h}\to V_{h}
defined by

(4.8)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{G}(u_{h}),v_{h})=-\epsilon(\partial_{x}z_{h}(u_{h}),\partial_{x}v_{h})-\epsilon(\partial_{x}q_{h}(u_{h}),\partial_{y}v_{h})-

\displaystyle\epsilon c(z_{h}(u_{h})-\partial_{x}u_{h},\partial_{x}v_{h})-\epsilon c(q_{h}(u_{h})-\partial_{x}u_{h},\partial_{x}v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}

where
z_{h}(u_{h}),q_{h}(u_{h})
are defined by satisfying the following system:

(4.9)

\displaystyle(z_{h}-\partial_{x}u_{h},cs_{h}-\partial_{x}s_{h})=0

\displaystyle~~~\forall s_{h}\in V_{h},

(4.10)

\displaystyle(q_{h}-\partial_{y}u_{h},cr_{h}-\partial_{x}r_{h})=0

\displaystyle~~~\forall r_{h}\in V_{h}.

Proposition 4.3
.

The map
\mathbf{G}
is well defined.

\square

Proof.

Let
u_{h}\in V_{h}
. It suffices to show that system (
4.9
)-(
4.10
) can be solved when
u_{h}
is known. Suppose
u_{h}=0
. Then
(z_{h},cs_{h}-\partial_{x}s_{h})=0
. Now we test with
s_{h}=z_{h}
. This yields
c\|z_{h}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+(z_{h},\partial_{x}z_{h})=0
. However, since
z_{h}
is periodic, this leaves us with
c\|z_{h}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0
, and thus
z_{h}=0
. The same argument applies to
q_{h}
. Thus the system is well-posed when
u_{h}
is given, and thus
\mathbf{G}
is well-defined.

Remark 4.4
(Lack of limiting on dispersive update).

Contrary to
[
12
]
, we do not limit the implicit update. There is no limiting because the dispersive step does not satisfy a maximum principle.

\square

We assume that
(c_{l})_{l\in\{1:s\}}
is nondecreasing and define the following quantity:

(4.11)

\Delta c^{\text{max}}:=\max_{2\leq l\leq s+1}(c_{l}-c_{l-1}).

Observe that
\Delta c^{\text{max}}\geq\frac{1}{s}
and
\Delta c^{\text{max}}=\frac{1}{s}
when all stages are equi-distributed, i.e.,
c_{l}=\frac{l-1}{s},~l\in\{1:s+1\}
. We further assume

(4.12)

\sum_{l\in\{1:j\}}a_{j,l}^{e}=\sum_{l\in\{1:j\}}a_{j,l}^{e}=c_{j}\quad\forall j\in\{1:s\}.

We are now prepared to explain the high order IMEX method. Given
U^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
, set
U^{n,1}:=U^{n}
, and decompose each stage
l\in\{2:s+1\}
into the following steps:

(4.13)

U^{n,l-1}\underbrace{\longrightarrow(W^{n,L,l},W^{n,H,l})\longrightarrow W^{n,l}}_{\text{Hyperbolic prediction}}\underbrace{\longrightarrow U^{n,l}}_{\text{Dispersive Update}}.

The update is given by
U^{n+1}=U^{n,s+1}
.

4.3.1
Hyperbolic Prediction

We first compute the low-order and high-order hyperbolic predictions defined by:

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{L}W^{L,l}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{L}U^{n,l-1}+\tau(c_{l}-c_{l-1})\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n,l-1}),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{H}W^{H,l}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{H}U^{n,l-1}+\tau\sum_{k\in\{1:l-1\}}(a^{e}_{l,k}-a_{l-1,k}^{e})\mathbf{F}^{H}(U^{n,k}),

(4.16)

\displaystyle W^{n,l}=

\displaystyle\ell(U^{n,l-1},\mathbf{F}^{L}(U^{n,l-1}),\mathbf{F}^{H}(U^{nl-1})).

Remark 4.5
(Incremental method).

The stages are presented in incremental form for two reasons. The first is that the limiter requires that the low and high order updates come from the same stage, namely
U^{n,l-1}
. Secondly, the time step size is maximized so that each stage uses the
\tau^{\ast}
time step so that a single IMEX step advances
\tau\times s
in time. Further commentary is provided in remark
4.7
.

\square

4.3.2
Dispersive Update

Once we compute
W^{n,l}
, we compute the parabolic update at stage
l\in\{2:s+1\}
by solving the following system:

(4.17)

\mathbb{M}^{H}U^{n,l}+\tau\mathbf{G}(U^{n,l})=\mathbb{M}^{H}W^{n,l}-\tau\sum_{k\in\{1:l-1\}}(a_{l,k}^{i}-a_{l-1,k}^{i})\mathbf{G}(U^{n,k}).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\tau^{\ast}
be same as in
3.2
and let

(4.18)

\tau\in\left(0,\frac{\tau^{\ast}}{\Delta c^{\max}}\right].

Let
U^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
. Consider the
s
-stage IMEX scheme composed of the (
4.14
) - (
4.17
) for
l\in\{2:s+1\}
. Then the scheme satisfies the following:

1.

It is well defined.

2.

It is conservative.

\square

Proof.

Let
U^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
and assume
4.18
holds true. We are going to show that
U^{n,l}
is well defined and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U^{n,l}_{i}m_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}^{n}m_{i}
. We do so by induction. Since
U^{n}=U^{n,1}
, conditions (1) and (2) are trivially satisfied. Now let
U^{n,l-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
. Since (
4.14
) -(
4.16
) are either explicit or utilize the limiter,
W^{n,l}
is well-defined. Furthermore, lemma
4.2
implies that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}W_{i}^{n,l}m_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}^{n,l-1}m_{i}
. Finally, remark
3.6
implies that (
4.17
) can always be solved for
U^{n,l}
; the fact that
(\mathbf{G}(U),\mathbf{1})=0
for all
U\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
and the analysis done in lemma
3.8
imply that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}^{n,l}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}W_{i}^{n,l}m_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}^{n,l-1}m_{i}
. This completes the proof.

Remark 4.7
(Maximally Efficient Implementation).

In the case when the
c_{j}
coefficients are equi-distributed, we modify the implementation so that the computed time step
\tau^{\ast}>0
from the low-order hyperbolic prediction may be used at each stage. To do so, note
\Delta c^{\text{max}}=c_{l}-c_{l-1}=\frac{1}{s}
for all
j\in\{2:s+1\}
. Thus, setting
\tau=\frac{\tau^{\ast}}{\Delta c^{\text{max}}}
implies
\tau=\tau^{\ast}\times s
. Looking at equations (
4.2
) and (
4.17
), we can apply
s
to the coefficients, and so when assembling the sum, we use
s(a_{l,k}-a_{l-1,k})
. This allows us to use
\tau^{\ast}
as the time step.

\square

4.4
Extensions

Let us mention a few possible extensions of this method. The first involves including a viscous term
-\nu\partial_{xx}u
in the model, which is sometimes known as the KdV-Burgers Equation (
Canosa and Gazdag [8]
). The viscous term is treated implicitly. Given the method’s agnosticism to the hyperbolic flux, it can be extended to equations of the form:

(4.19)

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(f(u))-\nu\partial_{xx}u+\alpha\partial_{xxx}u=0.

The method can also be extended to systems of KdV-like equations. One such case is found in
Bona et al. [7]
, where the authors consider systems of the form:

(4.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{xxx}u+\partial_{x}P(u,v)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\partial_{xxx}v+\partial_{x}Q(u,v)

\displaystyle=0.

The functions
P
and
Q
are assumed to produce a hyperbolic flux
\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{U}):=\\
(P(u,v),Q(u,v))^{T}
, where
\mathbf{U}:=(u,v)^{T}
. Then the low order method consists of solving

(4.22)

m_{i}\frac{\mathbf{W}_{i}^{n+1}-\mathbf{U}_{i}}{\tau}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{U}_{j}^{n})\cdot\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d_{ij}^{n}(\mathbf{U}_{j}^{n}-\mathbf{U}_{i}^{n})=0,

followed by solving the dispersive parts for
u
and
v
separately.

5.2
Zabusky-Kruskal

To demonstrate that our method is capable of solving well-known problems, we apply it to several benchmark cases. The first is the Zabusky-Kruskal experiment (
Zabusky and Kruskal [41]
). This simulation uses a KdV-type equation that takes the form:

(5.2)

\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u+\delta^{2}\partial_{xxx}u=0.

The authors use
\delta=0.022
with a final time of
T=\frac{3.6}{\pi}
. The initial condition consists of a cosine wave
u_{0}(x)=\cos(\pi x)
on a domain
D=(0,2)

\tau=\mathcal{O}(h^{3})

\mathbb{P}_{2}
elements and 1,025 degrees of freedom.

5.5
3-Soliton Interaction

The fourth simulation consists of an initial condition of three super-imposed solitons arranged in descending order of amplitude. The initial condition is given by

(5.4)

u_{0}(x)=4\sech^{2}(\sqrt{2}x)+2\sech^{2}(x-7)+\sech^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x-15)\right),

on a domain of
D=(-5,45)
and a final time of
T=5
. A similar simulation can be found in
Anco [1]
. We use
\mathbb{P}_{1}
finite elements with 2,049 degrees of freedom and IMEX(3,3;1) time stepping method. Figure
5
displays the results. The left image is a space-time plot of the solution; the right image is a contour space-time plot of the solution.

Figure 5
:
Space-time plots for the three-soliton interaction for the KdV equation (
2.3
). Left: Surface plot. Right: contour plot.

We observe three interactions. The first occurs when the tallest soliton interacts with the second tallest and the two “bump”. The three waves remain visible, but the middle soliton grows in amplitude and thereby begins to travel faster while the left soliton loses amplitude and slows. This interaction can be seen at approximately
t=1.5,~x=11
in the contour plot. The second interaction occurs when the now tallest middle soliton interacts with the smallest. The two briefly merge into a single wave before the larger passes through, maintaining its shape and speed but undergoing a phase shift, similar to the 2-soliton test. This can be seen at at approximately
t=2.5,~x=18
in the contour plot. The third interaction occurs when the now second tallest soliton reaches the smallest, and a “bump” interaction can be observed. This can be seen at approximately
t=3.5,~x=20
in the contour plot.

6
Conclusion

An IMEX scheme using
H^{1}
-conforming finite elements has been introduced for generalized KdV equations. The method is provably stable up to a CFL condition
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h)
and mass-conservative. High-order techniques have been introduced and analyzed. The numerical simulations demonstrate both the low and high order capabilities and robustness of the method. The next extension of this work is to apply the approach to dispersive systems, such as the Serre-Green-Naghdi shallow water equations (
Guermond et al. [18]
). Solving higher-order models, such as the Kawahara equation
Biswas [4]
is another direction for future research. Another avenue would be to modified the method to account for Boussinesq type models, which often include terms like
\partial_{t}\partial_{xx}u
; see
Benjamin et al. [3]
.
