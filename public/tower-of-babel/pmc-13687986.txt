Embolization of a fluoropolymer-coated left atrial appendage occlusion device to the infrarenal aorta presenting with acute neurologic symptoms: A case report
Authors: Tate Barney, Jacob Weaver, Nathaniel Dusini, Naveen Thota
Published: 2026

Introduction

Transcatheter left atrial appendage occlusion is now widely performed in the United States using 2 Food and Drug Administration-approved device platforms: the WATCHMAN series and the Amplatzer Amulet.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Although procedural success rates are high, device-related complications remain recognized risks. Pericardial effusion represents the most common serious procedural complication, whereas device embolization occurs in approximately 0.2%–0.7% of cases and is typically related to malpositioning, suboptimal sizing, or unfavorable appendage anatomy.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

The WATCHMAN FLX Pro (Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, MA), represents the newest iteration of the WATCHMAN platform and incorporates a polyvinylidene fluoride–co–hexafluoropropylene surface treatment designed to enhance hemocompatibility and reduce thrombogenicity.10, 11, 12, 13 Preclinical studies have demonstrated reduced inflammatory response and thrombus formation compared with earlier devices, and early clinical reports suggest high procedural success and favorable short-term outcomes.11, 12, 13 Nevertheless, clinical experience with the FLX Pro remains limited, and the spectrum of device-specific complications has not yet been well defined.10

Despite the low reported incidence of device embolization, migration of left atrial appendage occlusion devices remains a rare but potentially catastrophic complication and may present with atypical clinical manifestations. We report, to our knowledge, the first peer-reviewed case of WATCHMAN FLX Pro embolization to the infrarenal abdominal aorta presenting with acute neurologic deficits and systemic embolic phenomena, successfully managed with endovascular retrieval.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation status post pulsed-field ablation and left atrial appendage occlusion with a WATCHMAN FLX Pro device presented to the emergency department with acute-onset band-like low back pain radiating to both lower extremities. On presentation to the emergency department, he was taking apixaban 5 mg twice daily. Symptoms began while he was performing manual work and progressed over several hours to difficulty ambulating. His wife reported a single episode of urinary incontinence. He denied trauma, fever, malignancy history, intravenous drug use, or saddle anesthesia.

The FLX Pro device was placed 10 days before his presentation, and the initial deployment attempt utilized a 31-mm WATCHMAN FLX Pro device but was unsuccessful because the device was positioned too close to the mitral shoulder and could not be advanced deeper into the left atrial appendage. The device was recaptured and exchanged for a 27-mm WATCHMAN FLX Pro device, which was successfully deployed. Implant assessment demonstrated satisfactory position, anchor, size and seal criteria, including appropriate position, anchor engagement, device compression, and seal. The maximal left atrial appendage ostial width measured 22 mm. Residual peri-device leak was 0 mm at the time of implantation, and device stability was confirmed with standard tug testing.

The patient had additional medical history to include coronary artery disease (prior percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass graft), bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement, peripheral arterial disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus with peripheral neuropathy.

At the referring emergency department, he was hemodynamically stable. Examination demonstrated midline lumbar tenderness and cool lower extremities with difficulty obtaining dorsalis pedis pulses bilaterally (posterior tibial pulses present by Doppler) and strong popliteal pulses. Rectal examination raised concern for possible cauda equina syndrome. Initial neurologic examination showed intact sensation and full strength; however, during reassessment he developed ascending numbness from the feet to the thighs with new bilateral weakness (left greater than right). Lumbar spine magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an L4–L5 disc bulge with anterior thecal sac compression but no clear compressive lesion to explain the evolving deficits. Systemic anticoagulation was initiated, and the patient was transferred to a tertiary care center for vascular management. On arrival, he remained hemodynamically stable. Doppler signals were present in the right dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial distributions, whereas the left lower extremity was cool with diminished pedal signals. Vascular surgery confirmed embolization of the WATCHMAN device to the infrarenal aorta with preserved distal perfusion. Given the absence of overt acute limb ischemia and uncertainty regarding the relationship between device position and neurologic symptoms, the initial plan included close neurovascular monitoring and multidisciplinary evaluation before retrieval.

Neurology and neurosurgery were consulted. Lumbar imaging did not demonstrate a surgical lesion. Magnetic resonance imaging of the thoracic spine showed multilevel spondylosis without significant compression (Figure 2). Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated scattered acute and subacute infarcts in multiple vascular territories within the cerebral hemispheres and left cerebellum, suspicious for an embolic etiology (Figure 3). Stroke neurology considered these infarcts most consistent with a cardioembolic source but unlikely to fully explain the lower-extremity symptoms; spinal cord ischemia related to device migration was considered possible. Definitive management occurred 3 days after presentation with endovascular retrieval of the embolized WATCHMAN device. Under general anesthesia, the left common femoral artery was accessed under ultrasound guidance. Initial access was obtained with a 6-French catheter and serially dilated to accommodate a 22-French sheath. Aortography confirmed the embolized WATCHMAN device at the level of the renal arteries without associated thrombus. The device was successfully captured using Raptor endoscopic forceps (US endoscopy, Mentor, OH) and retrieved intact through the large-bore sheath. Completion angiography demonstrated patent superior mesenteric, renal, infrarenal aortic, and iliac vessels without dissection or vascular injury. There was no report of thrombus in or around the device upon retrieval.

Immediately following retrieval, the patient developed loss of the right posterior tibial Doppler signal. Repeat angiography demonstrated patent proximal vessels with chronic tibial disease but distal tapering and poor pedal filling concerning for vasospasm or microembolization. Intra-arterial vasodilator therapy produced partial improvement, and a thrombolysis catheter was placed with initiation of alteplase infusion. Distal Doppler signals and capillary refill subsequently returned to baseline.

On postoperative day 1, thrombolysis was discontinued and the catheter removed after improvement in distal perfusion. By postoperative day 2, vascular surgery documented stable vascular examination and cleared the patient for mobilization. The WATCHMAN device was not replaced during the index hospitalization. Anticoagulation was resumed with apixaban, and outpatient electrophysiology follow-up was arranged to consider repeat left atrial appendage occlusion. The patient’s strength improved but did not fully normalize during hospitalization, and he was discharged in stable condition to an inpatient rehabilitation facility. Following discharge, evaluation by an outside spine specialty team documented ongoing concern for anterior spinal artery infarction as the cause of the patient’s persistent neurologic deficits. However, the outside imaging studies and complete records were unavailable for independent review. Therefore, although spinal cord ischemia was considered by the treating physicians, definitive radiographic confirmation could not be established within the available medical record.

Discussion

The FLX Pro platform was developed to enhance hemocompatibility and device–tissue interaction through a fluoropolymer surface modification intended to reduce thrombogenicity and inflammatory response.10, 11, 12, 13 Early clinical experience with this device has demonstrated high procedural success and favorable short-term safety profiles.11, 12, 13 The precise mechanism of embolization in this case remains uncertain. The implanted 27-mm WATCHMAN FLX Pro device was selected for a maximal left atrial appendage ostial width of 22 mm and met standard position, anchor, size and seal criteria, including satisfactory position, anchor engagement, compression, seal, and stability on tug testing. Although an initial attempt with a 31-mm device was unsuccessful because of positioning within the appendage, the final implanted device appeared stable with no residual peri-device leak. Nevertheless, embolization occurred approximately 10 days later. Potential explanations include delayed loss of device stability, unrecognized anatomic characteristics of the left atrial appendage, or sizing considerations that were not apparent during the index procedure. This case suggests that delayed embolization may occur despite apparently satisfactory implantation parameters. The occurrence of embolization in this context highlights that, despite advances in device engineering and encouraging early data, rare but serious mechanical complications remain possible and continued post-market surveillance will be essential as real-world experience with this platform expands.

A similar case of device embolization involving the prior-generation WATCHMAN FLX platform has been reported in which the device migrated to the abdominal aorta at the level of the renal arteries with associated thrombus formation and resultant renal dysfunction, ultimately requiring open surgical retrieval.14 In contrast, the present case involved embolization of the newer-generation WATCHMAN FLX Pro device to a comparable infrarenal aortic location without associated thrombosis and was successfully managed using an endovascular approach. The absence of thrombus in our case may be consistent with the proposed thromboresistant properties of the fluoropolymer surface coating incorporated in the FLX Pro device. However, given the limited clinical experience with this platform, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding whether the observed absence of thrombosis reflects device-specific characteristics or the clinical circumstances of this individual case.

Prior studies have demonstrated that when embolization of a left atrial appendage occlusion device occurs, the aorta represents the most common site of migration for WATCHMAN devices.4, 5, 6 This pattern contrasts with other occlusion platforms in which embolization more frequently involves the cardiac chambers.4,6 Reviews of device embolization have emphasized that the approach to management depends primarily on the location of migration, the size of the device, and the patient’s hemodynamic stability.4, 5, 6 In hemodynamically stable patients without evidence of significant vascular injury or obstruction, percutaneous retrieval is generally considered the preferred initial strategy, whereas surgical retrieval may be required in cases involving device entrapment within cardiac structures, large thrombus burden, or associated end-organ compromise.4, 5, 6 The successful endovascular retrieval in this case further supports the feasibility of a minimally invasive approach when device position and clinical stability permit.

Finally, this case highlights the potential for embolized left atrial appendage occlusion devices to present with atypical neurologic symptoms that may initially mimic spinal pathology. The patient’s initial presentation raised concern for cauda equina syndrome, prompting spinal imaging before identification of the embolized device. Recognition of vascular findings on physical examination, including asymmetric distal pulses and limb temperature changes, ultimately redirected the diagnostic evaluation. Notably, the patient’s history of diabetes mellitus with peripheral neuropathy and underlying peripheral arterial disease introduced additional diagnostic complexity. Follow-up evaluation by a spine specialty team documented persistent concern for anterior spinal artery infarction as the etiology of the patient’s neurologic deficits. However, the outside imaging studies and complete records were not available for independent review. Consequently, although the clinical presentation was considered concerning for spinal cord ischemia by the treating physicians, definitive radiographic confirmation could not be established within the available medical record. Therefore, the relationship between the embolized WATCHMAN device and the patient’s neurologic symptoms remains uncertain. Although the temporal association raises the possibility that device migration contributed to spinal cord ischemia, the presence of multifocal cerebral infarcts, peripheral arterial disease, diabetes-associated neuropathy, and other vascular comorbidities precludes definitive attribution. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity that may accompany embolized left atrial appendage occlusion devices presenting with neurologic symptoms.

Conclusion

Despite advances in device design intended to improve hemocompatibility and thromboresistance, device migration remains a rare but serious complication of left atrial appendage occlusion. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for device embolization in patients presenting with new neurologic or vascular symptoms following recent left atrial appendage occlusion, as timely recognition can facilitate successful endovascular management.
