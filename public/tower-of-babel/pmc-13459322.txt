Prognostic value of C-reactive protein to albumin ratio for in-hospital mortality in acute stroke patients
Authors: Tuba Gül, Yasemin Kaya, Ahmet Kaya, Şükran Kaygısız, Fatih Akkaya, Gökce Yüce Onur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of the C-reactive protein to albumin ratio (CAR) in predicting in-hospital mortality among patients presenting with acute stroke.MethodA total of 483 adult patients (≥ 18 years) consecutively hospitalized for stroke over a one-year period (July 2022 – July 2023) were analyzed retrospectively. Exclusion criteria include malignancy, autoimmune/rheumatological disorders, chronic infections, conditions affecting inflammation or protein status, including liver failure and nephrotic syndrome, cachexia (BMI < 18 kg/m²). Baseline demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory indices were recorded upon admission. The CAR was derived by dividing the C-reactive protein (CRP) level by the serum albumin level. Comparative analysis was conducted between the mortality and non-mortality groups. The independent prognostic value of CAR was determined via binary logistic regression model.ResultsSignificant differences were observed between the mortality and non-mortality groups regarding baseline laboratory parameters. Patients in the in-hospital mortality group exhibited significantly higher levels of glucose (p = 0.001), AST (p = 0.001), white blood cell (p = 0.001), CRP (< 0.001) and CAR (< 0.001) levels were found to be significantly higher. Conversely, hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in the mortality group (p = 0.031). As a result of Binary logistic regression analysis to find independent predictors of in-hospital mortality CAR (Exp(B) 95% CI = 1.047 (1.026–1.068) p < 0.001) and glucose (Exp(B) 95% CI = 1.006 (1.000-1.011) p = 0.048) were found as independent predictors. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that a CAR cutoff value of 1.06 predicted in-hospital mortality with 74% sensitivity and 65% specificity [AUC: 0.774, 95% CI: 0.686–0.862].ConclusionThe present study found a significant association between elevated CAR levels and early in-hospital mortality from all causes in patients with acute stroke. It also demonstrated that CAR may be an independent predictor of early in-hospital mortality from all causes in all patients hospitalized with acute stroke, both in the general ward and intensive care unit.

Introduction

Cerebrovascular disease is among the primary causes of mortality and prolonged disability globally. It is the second leading cause of mortality worldwide and results in significant neurological deficits in adulthood [1, 2]. Notwithstanding progress in acute management, stroke persists as a considerable clinical and socioeconomic burden. Recent epidemiological data indicate that stroke-related mortality has stabilized and is rising in certain demographics, especially among young adults aged 35–64 [3–5].

Stroke is a multifaceted clinical disease characterized by a cascade of inflammatory responses initiated by both ischemia and hemorrhagic mechanisms. Subsequent to brain injury, there is an activation of intravascular leukocytes and infiltration of peripheral leukocytes. Pro-inflammatory cytokines produced by the endothelium and brain parenchyma facilitate the onset of the inflammatory response. Although inflammation encompasses advantageous processes that aid in the restoration of tissue homeostasis via multiple pathways, the collateral damage induced by the acute inflammatory response can considerably aggravate ischemia injury [6–10].

C-reactive protein (CRP) is a recognized acute phase reactant produced in response to inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), with the capacity for fast elevation. The fast escalation indicates the systemic inflammatory burden and has been demonstrated to correlate with poorer outcomes and disease advancement in both acute and chronic conditions [11–15]. Conversely, albumin, the principal plasma protein produced by the liver, diminishes in blood concentrations with systemic inflammation and malnutrition. It indicates both dietary and inflammatory conditions. Hypoalbuminemia, recognized as a detrimental acute phase reactant, has been correlated with poorer outcomes in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders [16–22]. The C-reactive protein/albumin ratio (CAR) has recently emerged as a composite biomarker for evaluating both systemic inflammation and nutritional status in patients. In contrast to individual indicators, CAR offers a more holistic representation of the body’s reaction to systemic stress. Despite substantial research on CAR in situations like sepsis, cancer, and heart failure [14, 16, 23–31], its prognostic significance in acute stroke is still little documented. Existing research on stroke populations and the prognosis of hospitalized stroke patients is constrained.

The literature has a limited number of papers investigating the association between acute stroke and CAR. Binyang et al. assessed the correlation between CAR and mortality at 28, 60, and 90 days in ischemic stroke patients monitored in the critical care unit, concluding that CAR is a predictor of mortality during these intervals [32]. Tatar et al. assessed the correlation between CAR, disease severity, and nutritional status in patients with acute ischemic stroke, indicating that CAR may facilitate the early identification of malnutrition risk and the forecasting of disease prognosis in this patient population [33]. Duan et al. demonstrated in their research that CAR serves as an independent predictor of stroke-associated pneumonia [34].

This study aims to investigate the prognostic effectiveness of CAR in predicting in-hospital mortality among acute stroke patients and to examine the value of this measure as an ‘early warning system’ in preliminary clinical risk management.

Method

The study protocol received approval from the Ordu University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: [2023/212, dated August 18, 2023]). This retrospective, single-center investigation was performed in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 483 individuals aged 18 and older, hospitalized and treated for stroke between July 1, 2022, and July 1, 2023, were consecutively incorporated into the analysis. The study’s inclusion criteria were: individuals aged over 18 years having a diagnosis of hemorrhagic or ischemic cerebrovascular episodes, confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography of the brain, in accordance with World Health Organization standards [12]. The exclusion criteria included malignancies, rheumatological disorders, autoimmune diseases, chronic infections, chronic liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, cachexia (BMI < 18 kg/m²). The patients’ data were examined retrospectively. Age, gender, chronic illnesses, hemogram and biochemical parameters, as well as hospital mortality status, were documented. Blood values of the patients included in the study at the time of first admission and before the start of treatment were evaluated. Data regarding the “NIHSS score (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale) and the duration from baseline to hospital admission” were inaccessible and so could not be incorporated into the assessment.

The patients were divided into 2 groups as in-hospital mortality and non-mortality. C-reactive protein albumin ratio (CAR = C-reactive protein / albumin) was calculated. In emergency department, blood samples were collected by venipuncture prior to the initiation of their medical treatment. Biochemical parameters were analyzed using an ARCHITECT c8000 clinical analyzer (Abbott, IL, USA). A CELL - DYN Ruby automated hematology analyzer (Abbott, IL, USA) was used for hemogram parameters.

Statistics

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 Statistical Package for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the data distribution. Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, the Mann Whitney U test was employed to compare the groups. The effect size was computed (r = Z/√N) and incorporated into the table.

The Chi-square test was employed for categorical variables. Due to the non-normal distribution of the data, numerical variables were represented as medians (with minimum and maximum values), whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages. Predictors of in-hospital mortality were assessed in all stroke cases and ischemic strokes using binary logistic regression analysis. Regression analysis was not conducted due of the low in-hospital mortality rate among patients with hemorrhagic stroke. Multicollinearity was assessed by the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and collinearity tolerance (VIF < 10 and collinearity tolerance > 0.10 were obtained). The model’s adequacy to the data was evidenced by the Hosmer and Lemeshow test for regression analysis and ROC curve analysis. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression model. An ROC curve study was conducted to ascertain the optimal threshold value.

Results

In the comparison of in-hospital mortality and non-mortality groups, glucose (p = 0.001), AST (p = 0.001), white blood cell (WBC) count (p = 0.001), CRP (< 0.001) and CAR (< 0.001) (Fig. 1) levels were considerably elevated. Hemoglobin levels decreased significantly (p = 0.031). No differences were seen between the groups regarding age, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, and stroke type. The proportion of women was greater in the group with positive in-hospital mortality (p = 0.011) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study concluded that CAR may be an independent biomarker for predicting all-cause in-hospital mortality in patients with acute stroke.

C-reactive protein is an acute phase reactant produced in the liver during inflammatory responses, stimulated by cytokines. It can demonstrate both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory activities; it may exacerbate inflammation by augmenting complement activation and cytokine release, while also serving an anti-inflammatory function through elevated IL-10 and suppression of interferon-gamma [12, 35, 36]. Elevated CRP levels correlate with heightened inflammatory intensity and poorer prognosis across numerous illnesses [37–40]. Albumin is a crucial plasma protein produced in the liver, contributing to osmotic equilibrium, antioxidant functions, and molecular transport [41–43]. Its concentration diminishes in instances of inflammation and malnutrition [13, 44]. Reduced albumin concentrations correlate with heightened inflammation, deteriorating clinical condition, and adverse prognosis in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders [18, 19]. Moreover, albumin has been documented to possess neuroprotective properties, with elevated levels correlating with improved stroke outcomes [22].

Rather than representing a single biological mechanism, CAR synthesizes two complementary processes: systemic inflammation and nutritional status. This dual representation may elucidate why CAR functions as an independent predictor. Recent studies have demonstrated that CAR can serve as a prognostic marker in numerous conditions, including acute coronary syndrome, inflammatory disorders, and various malignancies [14, 17, 25, 27, 29]. Moreover, research involving heart failure, critical care, and surgical patients indicates that elevated CAR values correlate with heightened mortality and morbidity [24, 31, 45, 46].These data confirm the notion that CAR may serve as a universal predictive biomarker for predicting mortality across various clinical situations, including stroke.

Inflammation plays a central role in the pathophysiology of stroke. In stroke patients, mediators released from damaged tissue and necrotic cells cause inflammation [7]. The resulting inflammation is essential for the healing of damaged tissue. Inflammation contributes to both tissue damage and repair [8]. Inflammation increases blood flow to the damaged tissue. With increased blood flow, cells are supplied with the necessary glucose and oxygen. This is one of the positive effects of inflammation and increased blood flow. At the same time, increased blood flow also increases calcium transport, exacerbating the damage. This is one of the negative effects of inflammation. Furthermore, the migration of inflammation-activated leukocytes to the damaged tissue increases [9, 10]. These leukocytes are essential for the repair of damaged tissue and the removal of necrotic tissue. At the same time, these leukocytes release cytokines to attract more inflammatory cells to the damaged area. This can increase the severity of existing inflammation, and excessive inflammation can lead to more serious consequences. Publications have reported that the inflammatory response plays a significant role in stroke and seriously affects the healing process [9, 47]. The post-ischemia inflammatory response is a complex process involving the activation and induction of various inflammatory cells and inflammatory factors [13].

Few studies involving stroke patients have indicated that elevated CAR levels correlate with worse prognosis and increased death. In a particular study, Du et al. gathered modified Rankin Scale (mRS) ratings from 630 young stroke patients at 30 and 90 days post-follow-up. The patients were categorized into four groups according to CAR quartiles, and their CAR levels were compared. They demonstrated that the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) elevated in cohorts exhibiting a high CAR. In conclusion, they asserted that a markedly elevated CAR was independently linked to a heightened risk of unfavorable outcomes in young stroke patients [13]. Binyang et al. assessed the correlation between CAR and all-cause mortality at 28, 60, and 90 days in critically sick stroke patients monitored in the intensive care unit, concluding that elevated CAR was linked to increased all-cause mortality in the short and medium term [32]. The present study found a significant association between elevated CAR levels and early in-hospital mortality from all causes in patients with acute stroke. It also demonstrated that CAR may be an independent predictor of early in-hospital mortality from all causes in all patients hospitalized with acute stroke, both in the general ward and intensive care unit.

The literature contains studies evaluating the relationship between NIHSS and various inflammatory parameters in stroke patients. While some of these studies found a relationship between NIHSS scores and inflammatory markers, others did not show any relationship. In one such study, Demirel et al. found that in acute ischemic stroke patients, the multiple inflammation index was significantly lower in patients with NIHSS scores of 1–4 compared to those with scores of 5–14, 15–20, and ≥ 21 [48]. Brooks et al. evaluated the relationship between NIHSS and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio. They found a significant relationship between the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio and mRS, but no significant relationship with NIHSS [49]. No study evaluating the relationship between CAR and NIHSS has been found in the literature. An important disadvantage of this study is the lack of established clinical severity measures, such as the NIHSS, which hindered the evaluation of whether CAR functions as an independent predictor of stroke severity at admission.

Nevertheless, the growing body of evidence emphasizes the prognostic utility of composite biomarkers that integrate inflammatory and nutritional indices in vascular diseases. The HALP score (hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocytes, and platelets), recognized by Tunca et al. as a crucial predictor of outcomes in peripheral artery disease, exhibits this tendency [50]. Eren et al., in their study on patients with acute anterior circulation ischemic stroke who underwent endovascular treatment, showed that high Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW) and Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) values at admission were independent predictors of death in these patients. They also found that RDW potentially predicts reperfusion failure [51]. In their study on patients hospitalized with acute stroke, Cáceda-Samamé et al. examined the relationship between neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-lymphocyte ratio, and mortality. They found that only NLR levels above 0.7 showed a positive prognostic performance (AUC: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.65–0.85), and after considering confounding factors, they showed that higher levels were associated with an increased risk of death (aRR: 3.66; 95% CI: 1.77–8.85). In conclusion, they suggested that the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio may have acceptable prognostic performance for mortality in patients with acute stroke and can be used to classify patients’ risk and evaluate timely alternative care and management [52]. Such findings reinforce the hypothesis that integrated biomarkers may more accurately reflect systemic physiological stress than isolated parameters. Nonetheless, it remains to be clarified whether CAR provides superior or complementary predictive value compared to these known indices in stroke populations. Additional extensive, prospective investigations are necessary to confirm these connections.

Conclusion

This study suggests that CAR can predict the risk of all-cause in-hospital mortality in acute stroke cases followed in wards and intensive care units. In this study, the number of variables included in the regression analysis was kept as limited as possible to improve model stability, and because it is a single-center study, larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to generalize the results and validate the predictive value of CAR in acute stroke patients.

Limitations

Despite its positive attributes, this study possesses several limitations that need attention. The single-center, retrospective methodology may automatically restrict the generalizability of our findings to broader, more diverse groups. Secondly, owing to the retrospective nature of the data, known clinical severity indicators—specifically the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS)—and time-dependent variables such as symptom-to-door time were inaccessible for analysis. The lack of these criteria prevented an evaluation of the independent relationship between CAR and the severity of neurological deficits. Moreover, the restricted incidence of hemorrhagic stroke patients hindered a comprehensive subgroup analysis; hence, our findings predominantly represent the prognostic framework of ischemic stroke. The study was limited to in-hospital mortality due to database constraints, necessitating future prospective trials to clarify the effects of CAR on long-term functional recovery and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores.
