Increased white blood cell in young adults with family histories of alcohol and other substance use disorders
Authors: Mustafa N Mithaiwala, Nikki S Phillips, Dylan H Nguyen, Melanie S Beehler, Harrison S Ballard, Andrea S Vincent, William R Lovallo, Peter Kochunov, L Elliot Hong, Jason C O'Connor, Steve Cole, Ashley Acheson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndividuals with a family history of alcohol or other substance use disorders (FH+) are at increased risk for developing alcohol and other substance use disorders (AUD/SUD) compared to individuals with no such family histories (FH−). FH+ young adults have blunted stress reactivity, lower cognitive performance and altered frontal white matter microstructure compared to FH− controls. We hypothesized that family history of AUD/SUD disrupts neuroendocrine regulation of the immune system in FH+ individuals, resulting in altered blood immune cell composition, inflammation and neurocognitive alterations that, ultimately, increases risk for AUD/SUD and associated psychopathology. We examined white blood cell (WBC) parameters derived from complete blood counts in FH+ (n = 37) and FH− (n = 77) young adults without AUD/SUD to test if immune system dysregulation is present in FH+ individuals. The total WBC count, number of neutrophils and number of monocytes and associated systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) were significantly increased in the FH+ group. Further, WBC, neutrophil, monocyte counts and SIRI values were all positively correlated with FH density (number of biological parents and grandparents with AUD/SUD). These novel data are the first to identify an association between family history of AUD/SUD and increased circulating leukocytes, which is likely indicative of immune dysregulation in FH+ young adults prior to onset of AUD/SUD. Additional studies are warranted to characterize the functional relevance of the observed immune cell composition in FH+ individuals, but the notion that inexpensive and widely available blood tests may help identify addiction risk could be transformative.Keywords: complete blood count, family history of alcohol or substance use disorder, HPA axis, inflammation, leukocytes, SIRI

INTRODUCTION

Young adults have some of the highest lifetime risk of developing an alcohol or other substance use disorder (AUD/SUD 1 , 2 ), and those with a family history of AUD/SUD (FH+) are much more likely to develop an AUD/SUD themselves compared to those with no such family history (FH−). 3 , 4 FH+ young adults display a behavioural phenotype comprised of increased anti‐social, depressive and anxious tendencies and increased impulsivity, 5 similar to behavioural profiles observed in individuals with AUD/SUDs. 6 , 7

We and others have identified biological and neural differences in FH+ young adults including blunted cortisol reactivity to psychosocial stressors and altered frontal white matter microstructure. 8 , 9 Similar findings are reported in individuals with AUD/SUD. 10 , 11 Furthermore, we have demonstrated that the blunted cortisol reactivity, increased externalizing and internalizing tendencies and increased discounting of delayed rewards observed in FH+ individuals are correlated with the number of biological parents and grandparents with AUD/SUD (FH density) and are partially linked to increased early life adversity exposure. 12 , 13 Collectively, this pattern of findings suggests an overarching neurobiological phenotype may be present in FH+ individuals that predisposes them to problem alcohol and other substance use.

A possible culprit for this overarching phenotype may be immune dysregulation—most often measured as ‘inflammation’. Inflammation has been independently associated with AUD/SUD 14 , 15 and with each of the clinical and neural components of the FH+ phenotype noted above. 16 , 17 , 18 Evidence for a bidirectional relationship between immune system activation and addiction disorders exists 19 , 20 ; however, it is unknown whether immune system dysfunctions are apparent in individuals at higher risk for developing AUD/SUD. Two important questions emerge: (1) is immune system dysfunction a plausible underlying mechanism in the development of AUD/SUDs? (2) Are simple laboratory measures of immunological biomarkers useful in understanding individual risk for developing AUD/SUD? This study aimed to begun to address these gaps in knowledge. A commonly used, and easy to obtain, measure of inflammation or immune dysfunction is the differential analysis of whole blood derived from complete blood counts. Additionally, ratios of white blood cell (WBC) cell types such as the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) can provide additional insights into immune system functioning and are increasingly shown to predict disease outcomes. SIRI is calculated as a ratio between the predominant circulating ‘first responder’ innate immune cells (neutrophils and monocytes) versus the cells responsible for adaptive immune responses, inducing antigen‐specific immune responses and maintaining immunological memory (lymphocytes). Indexes such as SIRI may predict inflammation‐related outcomes across a broad spectrum of diseases such as prediction of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, survival outcomes in cancer progression and recovery outcomes following a stroke. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Here, we compared WBC counts and SIRI in FH+ and FH− young adults as part of our work to determine if immune dysregulation is present in FH+ individuals.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

We enrolled 151 healthy young adults, ages 18–25, recruited using social media advertisements and posted flyers locally in the Little Rock, AR, metropolitan area. Participants were pre‐screened using online surveys and telephone interviews. Qualified participants were invited for on‐site screening session where signed informed consents were obtained that described the screening and testing procedures and confidentiality safeguards. Psychiatric history was assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5 (SCID‐5 25 ) by a trained interviewer under supervision by a licenced clinical psychologist. Participants were required to be in good physical health, have normal intelligence as indicated by a Shipley Institute of Living verbal scale score of ≥20, 26 have at least one biological parent with an AUD/SUD (for inclusion in the FH+ group) or have no biological parents or grandparents with AUD/SUD (for inclusion in the FH− group). Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, physical or developmental disabilities that could interfere with the ability to understand or complete study requirements, past 12 month DSM‐5 mood disorders, psychotic disorders, substance use disorders, anxiety disorders, obsessive‐compulsive and related disorders, feeding and eating disorders, and trauma and stressor‐related disorders, lifetime DSM‐5 psychotic disorders or moderate or severe substance use disorders or obsessive‐compulsive and related disorders, or any required medications acting on the CNS. All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences, in Little Rock, AR, and participants were compensated financially for their participation in the study.

Family history of alcohol and other substance use disorders

Family history of AUD/SUD was determined by Family History Research Diagnostic Criteria (FH−RDC) outlined previously. 27 , 28 FH+ participants had at least one biological parent who met at least two of the possible six criteria for AUD/SUD. FH− participants reported no AUD/SUD in their biological parents and grandparents. Family history density (FH density) was calculated as the total number of biological parents and grandparents with AUD/SUD, with possible values ranging from 0 (for FH−) to 6 (for FH+ who had AUD/SUD in each parent and grandparent).

Participant enrolment flow

We had 1482 potential participants who completed the online survey form. Of those, 1125 were screened out due to study exclusions including lack of knowledge about biological parents or grandparents, use of required psychoactive medications or other chronic health conditions, and 110 were unable to be contacted or declined to come to the lab for the onsite screening. Of the 247 who completed the onsite screening, 82 were screened out due to AUD or SUD or other DSM‐5 exclusions, intermediate family history of AUD/SUD or low Shipley IQ scores, and 14 declined further participation or were lost to follow up.

Blood sampling

Participants were not required to be fasting before blood sample collection but had to be abstinent from alcohol or other substances, as confirmed by a breath alcohol screening and a urine drug screen test. Approximately 24 ml of venous blood (non‐fasting) was drawn from all eligible participants by a research assistant trained in phlebotomy. Complete blood cell (CBC) counts were performed on an aliquot of whole blood by Quest Diagnostics©®, and results were sent to the study team for data analysis. A maximum of three needle sticks were attempted per participant during the study visit. Participants on whom we were not able to successfully obtain blood were invited back to the laboratory to try again. Ultimately, blood collection was unsuccessful on 34 out of 151 participants; thus, data from FH+ (n = 37) and FH− (n = 77) participants are included in the analyses. The systemic inflammatory response index SIRI was calculated ([absolute neutrophils × absolute monocyte]/absolute lymphocytes count) as previously described. 21

Statistical analysis

A log10 transformation was applied to CBC count data because values were skewed. Non‐transformed data are shown in tables and figures for easier comparisons to standard CBC presentations. Independent sample t tests were used to compare WBC measures between FH− and FH+ groups, followed by Pearson correlations with FH density and WBC measures. All statistical significance was set at 0.05 for analyses conducted in this study with Bonferroni corrections applied for multiple comparisons. All data analyses reported in this study were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0.

RESULTS

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics

The FH+ and FH− groups did not differ in age, ethnicity, race or years of education, but FH+ participants had significantly lower estimated intelligence (Shipley Mental Age) and lower childhood socioeconomic status in comparison to FH− participants (Table 1). FH+ participants also had significantly more early life adversity, higher anxiety scores on the Beck Anxiety Inventory and greater antisocial tendencies measured using the California Personality Inventory–Socialization Scale (Table 1). There were no significant differences between participants who did or did not provide blood samples (data not shown). We did not observe any sex differences on participant demographics or clinical characteristics.

Complete blood cell counts

Whole blood counts from FH+ participants had a significantly higher number of total WBCs (Table 2 and Figure 1A). Underlying this increase in WBC count was a significant increase in the absolute number of neutrophils and monocytes (Table 2 and Figure 1B,C). There were no group differences on other discrete WBC population counts (lymphocytes, eosinophils and basophils). Consistent with the increase in neutrophil and monocyte but not lymphocyte counts, SIRI was significantly higher in FH+ compared to FH− participants (Table 2 and Figure 1D). FH density was also positively correlated with each of these four immunologic measures (Table 2). There were no significant effects of family history on red blood cell counts, red blood cell‐related variables or platelet count. Secondary analysis revealed no apparent effects of sex for any variable measured (Figure 1, denoted by colour).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we compared blood cell counts in FH+ and FH− young adults without AUD/SUD and found FH+ individuals have higher overall WBC counts that were driven by increased absolute neutrophil and monocyte counts. FH+ individuals also have a higher SIRI, a potential biomarker of dysregulated haematopoietic balance. 21 , 29 Notably, these measures are all significantly correlated with FH density, a key predictor of risk for developing AUD/SUD. 5 , 9 , 13 These results parallel previous reports of increased WBC, neutrophil and monocyte counts and increased SIRI in individuals with AUD/SUD 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 and are consistent with immune system alterations being part of an overarching addiction endophenotype.

The higher neutrophil and monocyte counts in FH+ subjects without AUD/SUD suggest an altered regulation of the innate immune system and associated inflammatory process prior to development of alcohol and other substance use problems. Elevated monocytes and neutrophils relative to lymphocytes is a potential marker of inflammatory disease and endothelial dysfunction and can predict cardiovascular disease and associated mortality. 21 SIRI is a novel prognostic marker found to be elevated in patients with various diseases including autoimmune conditions, 23 , 34 mood and personality disorders 35 and AUD. 33 Notably, we derived the WBC profiles and SIRI values from CBC reports that are inexpensive and widely available. Approved biomarkers such as alcohol and other drug metabolites are used for testing recent substance use, but there are no clinically available immune biomarkers to predict risk for AUD/SUDs.

Neutrophils and monocytes are the most prominent cell types of the innate immune system. These cells are the ‘first responders’ of the immune system and express receptors required to mediate anti‐bacterial and anti‐viral responses among other functions. 36 The increased neutrophils and monocytes in FH+ individuals could contribute to chronic inflammation, a known pathogenic factor for the development of cardio/cerebrovascular and metabolic disorders. 37 However, even in the absence of increased inflammation, increased neutrophil and monocyte numbers could alter the immune response to a given challenge. Increased cell numbers could make an individual's immune system ‘primed’ to produce a hyper‐reactive or maladaptive immune response. Conversely, an increase in the absolute number of neutrophils and monocytes could reflect a compensatory haematopoietic response against diminished individual cell function or a haematopoietic response to chronic stress and neuroendocrine activity (e.g., sympathetic nervous system activation). 38 , 39 , 40 It is plausible that immune dysregulation, if present as an underlying immunologic endophenotype, could contribute to the altered frontal white matter 9 , 41 and increased antisocial, depressive and anxious tendencies and increased impulsivity observed in FH+ individuals 5 , 42 , 43 that are similar to findings in individuals with AUD/SUD. 6 , 7 , 10 However, additional investigation is necessary to resolve the underlying functional implications of our observations. Preclinical models have demonstrated that systemic immune dysfunction has the capacity to precipitate cellular, structural and molecular changes within the brain. 44 , 45 Manipulations of multiple immunological genes have been demonstrated to alter alcohol consumption in rodent models. 46 , 47 , 48 In addition, activation of immune responses in mice with a lipopolysaccharide challenge increased alcohol consumption. 49 Together, these patterns of findings indicate the propensity/predisposition to alcohol consumption is influenced by the immune system. However, the potential contribution of immune dysregulation to increased risk for AUD/SUD has yet to be explored. The complete blood count data reported in this manuscript reflecting altered blood immune cell counts suggest that a family history of AUD/SUDs may be associated with a state of altered haematopoietic programming. Whether the observed shift in WBC subpopulations results in a functional difference in immunoreactivity is the subject of ongoing work in our laboratory.

It is possible that the WBC alterations we report here could be related to altered neuroendocrine signalling in FH+ individuals. Previously, we have shown that FH+ individuals exhibit blunted cortisol and heart rate stress reactivity, 8 aligned with findings in individuals with AUD/SUD. 11 Interestingly, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and immune system are well known to exhibit bidirectional regulation 50 , 51 as are the sympathetic nervous system and the immune system. 51 For example, experimental induction of corticosteroid resistance (HPA axis blunting) in rodents exposed to chronic stress causes peripheral and central immune dysfunction, increases peripheral immune cell trafficking to the CNS and influences behaviour. 52 , 53 This relationship has been confirmed in clinical contexts with chronic stress induced neuroendocrine dysfunction leading to a ‘transcriptional fingerprint’ on immune cells linked to hyperreactivity. 39 , 52 Our current data reveal potential disruptions in the state of the innate immune system within FH+ individuals. Considered in context with the blunted stress reactivity, altered frontal white matter structure and behavioural patterns observed in FH+ individuals, innate immune system disruptions could play an important role in an overarching AUD/SUD endophenotype.

This study has several strengths and limitations. We report novel associations between family histories of AUD/SUD and WBC profiles, shedding light on how immune dysregulation may be contributing to addiction risk. However, the clinical significance of these effects remains to be determined in future research, as does their causal relationship to alcohol or other substance abuse. Ongoing studies in our laboratory are investigating whether immune response following an activation stimulus (e.g., lipopolysaccharide) are different between FH+ and FH− individuals. FH+ individuals had differences in several demographic and clinical variables including increased early life adversity, anxious and antisocial tendencies, and lower childhood socioeconomic status (SES) and estimated intelligence, consistent with prior findings, 5 but these variables were generally not correlated with WBC measures (Figures S1–S5). Given the lack of associations of these variables with WBC measures, the close associations with these measures and FH+ status (i.e., common phenotypic characteristics of FH+ individuals) and relatively modest sample size (particularly for the FH+ group), we did not statistically control for these measures in our analyses. Further, in observational studies where the covariate may differ considerably in naturally occurring groups (like FH+ and FH− individuals), it is often problematic to adjust to a ‘common’ level of the covariate, particularly if that value is not typical of either group. 54 , 55 However, we will continue to monitor for relationships with these variables as we collect more data. Our data cannot determine if WBC elevations predict development of AUD/SUD, but this could be potentially examined using existing longitudinal datasets. Importantly, WBC counts for both FH+ and FH− were clustered within the normal reference ranges. 56 Noteworthy, individuals with family histories of AUD/SUD were likely included in the datasets used to generate normal complete blood count reference ranges. Thus, the current normal reference ranges may include values that represent increased risk for addictions or other psychopathologies. In our analysis, we did not detect sex differences on WBC measures; however, we will continue to monitor for this. There is evidence that females may be more vulnerable to immune perturbations following long term alcohol use, 57 for example.

In summary, this study finds FH+ young adults without AUD/SUD have increased blood immune cell compositions that are reflected in the higher inflammatory response index (SIRI) and suggest their immune systems may be disproportionately primed for activation. These stable immune perturbations may be an early biological risk factor increasing propensity for AUD/SUD during later adulthood. Results of the current study shed light on the evolving landscape of understanding the nuances between genetic predisposition, environmental factors and immune responses within the context of addiction and addiction risk. The observed results provide a roadmap to begin constructing criteria using clinical and simple biological measures to identify a ‘pre‐addiction’ phenotype analogous to prediabetes or precancerous conditions that can inform therapeutic targeting and strategies. Elevated WBC counts alone likely will not predict AUD/SUD risk but could potentially be informative when combined with other risk factors. Additional studies are underway to characterize the functional relevance of the observed immune cell imbalance of WBCs in the FH+ group. Discoveries unravelled may hold implications for clinicians and scientists aiming to understand and identify individuals at risk for AUD/SUDs.
