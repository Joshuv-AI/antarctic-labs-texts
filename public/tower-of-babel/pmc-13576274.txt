Longitudinal associations between memory and emergent literacy from 3 to 6 years of age: a four-wave random-intercept cross-lagged panel study
Authors: Jin-long Liang, Xin-ling Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMemory and emergent literacy are foundational to children’s early learning. However, it remains unclear whether their longitudinal association reflects stable differences between children, developmental fluctuations within children, or both. The present study examined their bidirectional associations from 36 to 72 months while distinguishing between-child and within-child variation.MethodsThis secondary analysis used four waves of caregiver-reported data from the KIT longitudinal study, collected when children were approximately 36, 48, 60, and 72 months old. The analytic sample comprised 1,694 children. A traditional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) was used to estimate overall longitudinal associations, and a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) was used to separate stable between-child differences from time-specific within-child deviations.ResultsMean scores for both constructs increased with age, and memory and emergent literacy were positively correlated within and across waves. In the CLPM, memory was prospectively associated with subsequent emergent literacy across all three intervals, whereas emergent literacy was prospectively associated with subsequent memory in two of the three intervals. In the RI-CLPM, the correlation between the stable between-child components of memory and emergent literacy was 0.81. At the within-child level, deviations in each construct were positively associated with subsequent deviations in the other across all three intervals. The largest estimated emergent-literacy-to-memory coefficient was observed from 60 to 72 months.ConclusionsMemory and emergent literacy were bidirectionally associated at the within-child level from 36 to 72 months, while the prospective association from memory to emergent literacy was more consistent across the CLPM and RI-CLPM. Their longitudinal relation involved both stable between-child differences and time-specific within-child variation.

Introduction

Early childhood is an important period for the development of memory and emergent literacy. During this period, children become increasingly able to maintain, update, and retrieve information [1, 2]. Shared reading and other early literacy experiences provide opportunities to develop knowledge about books and print, recognize print, and engage in early writing [3, 4]. These skills are associated with later reading and spelling [5]. However, evidence on the direction of the longitudinal relation between memory and emergent literacy remains limited. It is also unclear whether this relation reflects stable differences between children, within-child variation over time, or both.

Memory and emergent literacy in early childhood

Memory refers to the processes through which information is encoded, retained, retrieved, and used. Rather than constituting a single system, memory encompasses related processes involving attention, temporary maintenance, long-term representation, and retrieval [6, 7]. Short-term, working, episodic, semantic, and prospective memory can be distinguished theoretically [6, 8–10], although these systems often operate together in everyday learning. Among children aged 36–74 months, working memory capacity and updating have been shown to be related but distinct and to increase with age [11].

In the present study, memory was assessed using a broad caregiver-reported measure rather than a performance-based task targeting a single memory component. The memory subscale comprises 18 caregiver-rated items covering personal and family information, semantic concepts, numerical sequences, recognition of familiar storybooks, pretend play, and symbolic representation [12]. These items involve multiple aspects of memory, retrieval, and symbolic representation [6–8, 13–16]. Accordingly, memory refers here to the caregiver-observed behaviors captured by this scale and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of any single memory system.

Emergent literacy is also multidimensional. It refers to the knowledge, skills, and dispositions related to reading and writing that develop before and around the beginning of formal literacy instruction through experiences such as language interaction, shared reading, play, early mark-making, and exposure to print [17, 18]. The construct may include oral-language and print-related skills, such as vocabulary, phonological awareness, character knowledge, concepts about print, rapid naming, and emergent writing [5, 18].

The caregiver-reported KIT Emergent Literacy Scale assesses children’s understanding of signs and pictorial symbols, knowledge of book and print conventions, understanding or recognition of simple Chinese characters, and expression through drawings, symbols, or early written forms [19]. These domains are consistent with established conceptualizations of emergent literacy and environmental print knowledge [18, 20]. It does not directly assess phonological awareness, rapid naming, vocabulary, narrative ability, or sound–symbol correspondence. Emergent literacy therefore refers here to the specific domains represented by the scale rather than to the full range of emergent literacy skills. The complete items for both measures are provided in Appendix A.

A transactional perspective on reciprocal development

Transactional models conceptualize development as an ongoing process in which children’s characteristics and their experiences mutually influence developmental change over time [21, 22]. Although the present study did not directly measure child–environment transactions, this perspective provides a conceptual rationale for examining reciprocal longitudinal associations between memory and emergent literacy.

Memory may help children retain story information, recognize familiar symbols, remember book conventions, retrieve the meanings of environmental print, and apply prior knowledge in drawing and early writing. Cross-sectional studies have reported associations between working memory or broader cognitive abilities and early literacy [23, 24], while longitudinal research has linked verbal working memory with reading development from kindergarten to Grade 4 [25]. Conversely, a review based largely on alphabetic literacy proposed that learning to read may shape verbal working memory through rehearsal and the development of phonological and orthographic representations [26]. Although these processes are more specific than the constructs assessed in the present study, they support examining whether memory and emergent literacy are reciprocally related over time.

This question also requires stable between-child differences to be distinguished from time-specific within-child deviations. A between-child association does not show whether a child’s higher- or lower-than-usual level in one domain precedes a subsequent deviation in the other [27, 28]. Examining reciprocal development therefore requires these two sources of variation to be considered separately.

Longitudinal evidence and methodological limitations

Longitudinal evidence on the relations between specific memory components and reading remains limited, and findings vary across memory components and developmental periods [26]. Cunningham et al. [29] assessed 780 children at ages 4, 5, 6, and 9. Verbal short-term memory predicted early word-level reading from ages 4 to 6, whereas reading did not predict later verbal short-term memory. In contrast, nonword repetition and reading predicted one another across the five-year period. Zhang and Joshi [25] followed children from kindergarten to Grade 4 and examined the development of verbal working memory and reading across linguistic groups. Although their growth rates were related, the strength of this relation varied across developmental phases and linguistic groups. Earlier verbal working memory had only small or nonsignificant effects on later reading, and earlier reading did not predict later verbal working memory. Together, these findings suggest that longitudinal associations between memory-related abilities and reading depend on the memory component, developmental period, and measures examined. Both studies, however, used performance-based measures of specific memory components and formal reading outcomes rather than the broader constructs assessed here.

Evidence involving related cognitive abilities is also mixed. Liu et al. [30] found that executive function and Chinese and English word reading reciprocally predicted one another during the transition from kindergarten to primary school. Mauer et al. [31] reported partial evidence of reciprocal relations between executive function and word-level reading: a composite measure of executive function and pseudoword reading predicted one another, while both word and pseudoword reading predicted later working memory. Yang et al. [32] found that rapid automatized naming predicted later Chinese word reading, whereas Chinese word reading predicted later phonological awareness. Thus, the direction of longitudinal associations differs across cognitive and literacy measures. Executive function and phonological processing are not equivalent to the broad memory construct used here, and word-level reading represents only one aspect of emergent literacy. These studies are therefore informative but do not directly address the reciprocal relation examined in the present study.

Measurement differences further limit direct comparison. Previous longitudinal studies have generally used performance-based tasks to assess verbal short-term memory, verbal working memory, phonological processing, or executive function and have focused primarily on word recognition, decoding, or formal reading outcomes [25, 29–32]. The present study instead uses caregiver reports of everyday memory-related behaviors and an emergent literacy measure covering environmental print, book conventions, familiar character recognition, and symbolic expression (see Appendix A). Existing evidence has therefore not established whether these broad memory-related behaviors and emergent literacy predict one another between 36 and 72 months.

Model choice presents a further methodological issue. The traditional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) controls for prior scores but does not separate stable between-child differences from time-specific within-child deviations. Consequently, its cross-lagged estimates may combine between- and within-child information [27, 28]. Using a large cohort followed from kindergarten into elementary school, Willoughby et al. [33] found that between-child associations between executive function and academic achievement were substantially stronger than the corresponding within-child associations; at the within-child level, earlier reading was the most consistent predictor of later executive function. Comparisons of alternative cross-lagged models likewise indicate that estimates of prospective associations can depend on model specification [34]. By separating stable between-child differences from within-child deviations, the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) can clarify whether longitudinal relations between memory and emergent literacy reflect stable differences between children, within-child processes, or both [27, 35].

The present study

The present study examined bidirectional longitudinal associations between memory and emergent literacy using four waves of data from the KIT longitudinal study, collected at 36, 48, 60, and 72 months old. Memory was defined as children’s retention, recognition, retrieval, and use of previously learned personal, semantic, sequential, and symbolic information. Emergent literacy was defined as children’s understanding of environmental signs and symbols, knowledge of book and print conventions, understanding or recognition of simple Chinese characters, and expression through drawings, symbols, or early written forms.

Both the CLPM and RI-CLPM were estimated. The CLPM described overall longitudinal associations without separating between- and within-child components, whereas the RI-CLPM distinguished stable between-child differences from time-specific within-child deviations [27, 35]. The RI-CLPM was used to test whether a child’s higher- or lower-than-usual level in one domain was prospectively associated with a subsequent deviation in the other.

Based on the transactional perspective [21, 22] and the evidence reviewed above, the following hypotheses were tested:

H1: Memory and emergent literacy would each show positive autoregressive associations across adjacent waves from 36 to 72 months.

H2: Memory and emergent literacy would be positively associated with each other for each wave.

H3: After stable between-child differences were separated, a higher-than-usual level of memory would be positively associated with a higher-than-usual level of emergent literacy at the subsequent wave.

H4: After stable between-child differences were separated, a higher-than-usual level of emergent literacy would be positively associated with a higher-than-usual level of memory at the subsequent wave.

H5: The random intercepts of memory and emergent literacy would be positively correlated, indicating that children with higher stable levels of memory would also tend to have higher stable levels of emergent literacy.

Methods

Participants

The present study involved a secondary analysis of four waves of de-identified data from the KIT longitudinal study, collected when children were approximately 36, 48, 60, and 72 months old. Children with records at each of the four selected waves were included, resulting in a final analytic sample of 1,694 children. At each wave, a parent or primary caregiver reported on the child’s everyday behaviors, including memory-related and emergent literacy behaviors. The authors neither recruited nor contacted the participants and were not involved in the original data collection.

The original data collection was approved by the relevant institutional research ethics committees (Approval Nos. 201408ES007 and 201707HS003). Informed consent was obtained from the children’s parents or legal guardians before their participation in the original study. The present secondary analysis was conducted using de-identified data in accordance with relevant guidelines, regulations, and KIT data-use requirements.

Measures

The memory and emergent literacy measures were drawn from the cognitive development and language development domains of the KIT secondary dataset. The memory subscale comprises 18 caregiver-rated items covering personal and family information, semantic concepts, numerical sequences, recognition of familiar storybooks, pretend play, and symbolic representation [12]. These items capture children’s retention, recognition, retrieval, and use of personal, semantic, sequential, and symbolic information in everyday contexts [6–8, 13–16]. Because the measure covers a broad range of observable memory-related behaviors, it should not be interpreted as a direct assessment of any single memory system.

The emergent literacy subscale comprises six caregiver-rated items covering environmental signs and pictorial symbols, book handling and print conventions, understanding or reading simple Chinese characters, and expression through drawings or symbols, including early written forms [19]. These items represent established domains of emergent literacy, including book concepts, environmental print, print conventions, early print recognition, and emergent writing [17, 18, 20]. The scale does not comprehensively assess all domains of emergent literacy, such as phonological awareness, rapid naming, vocabulary, or narrative ability.

Both scales used a four-point response format ranging from 1 (completely unable) to 4 (very proficient). At each wave, the 18 memory items were summed to produce a memory total score ranging from 18 to 72, and the six emergent literacy items were summed to produce an emergent literacy total score ranging from 6 to 24. Higher scores indicated higher levels of the corresponding memory-related or emergent literacy behaviors. No items were reverse-scored because all items were coded in the same developmental direction.

The KIT measures rely on caregiver reports designed to capture behaviors across everyday contexts. This approach provides information based on observations accumulated across settings and occasions rather than performance during a single assessment session. However, because both constructs were rated by the same informant, possible reporting bias and shared method variance should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In the present sample, Cronbach’s α coefficients for memory at 36, 48, 60, and 72 months were 0.87, 0.86, 0.84, and 0.85, respectively. The corresponding coefficients for emergent literacy were 0.72, 0.74, 0.73, and 0.77. These values indicated good internal consistency for memory and acceptable internal consistency for emergent literacy [36, 37].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlations

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations were calculated using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 31.0 to summarize the distributions of memory and emergent literacy scores and their bivariate associations across waves [38]. Item-level missingness was minimal. Across the 162,624 possible item responses (1,694 children × 24 items × four waves), 123 were missing (0.08%). For individual items, the number of missing responses at any wave ranged from two to seven. The longitudinal models were estimated using full information maximum likelihood (FIML), allowing all available repeated-measure scale scores to contribute under the missing-at-random assumption [39].

Cross-lagged panel models

A traditional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) and a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) were estimated to examine longitudinal stability, within-wave associations, and prospective cross-lagged associations between memory and emergent literacy. The CLPM estimates longitudinal associations without separating stable between-child differences from time-specific within-child deviations. By contrast, the RI-CLPM decomposes repeated measures into stable between-child components and time-varying within-child components, allowing the cross-lagged parameters to be interpreted at the within-child level [27, 28, 35].

This distinction was central to the present study because an association estimated by the CLPM may reflect persistent differences between children, within-child fluctuations over time, or both. Separating these components allows the RI-CLPM to provide a more specific test of whether deviations in one construct are associated with subsequent deviations in the other construct within the same child.

Traditional cross-lagged panel model

In the CLPM, total scores for memory and emergent literacy at 36, 48, 60, and 72 months were entered as observed variables (Fig. 1). The model included autoregressive paths within each construct, within-wave correlations between the constructs, and cross-lagged paths in both directions. The CLPM served as a benchmark for comparison with previous longitudinal studies and with the RI-CLPM.

Random-intercept cross-lagged panel model

In the RI-CLPM (Fig. 2), random intercepts for memory and emergent literacy represented stable between-child differences across the four waves. The within-child factors represented time-specific deviations from each child’s expected score after stable between-child differences and wave-specific means had been accounted for. The model estimated within-child autoregressive and cross-lagged paths, within-wave associations, and the correlation between the two random intercepts.

Model estimation and evaluation

Both models were estimated in Mplus Version 9.1 using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR) [40]. STDYX-standardized coefficients were reported, and two-sided tests used α = 0.05. Model fit was assessed jointly using the chi-square statistic, CFI, TLI, RMSEA with its 90% confidence interval, and SRMR [41]. AIC and BIC were reported as supplementary indices, with lower values indicating better relative fit. Because the models were not treated as nested, no chi-square difference test was conducted.

Autoregressive paths were used to evaluate H1, and within-wave associations were used to evaluate H2. Within-child cross-lagged paths in the RI-CLPM were used to test H3 and H4, and the correlation between the two random intercepts was used to test H5.

Discussion

The present study examined the longitudinal associations between memory and emergent literacy across four waves of early childhood, from age 3 to around school entry. The CLPM showed consistent prospective associations from memory to emergent literacy, whereas associations in the reverse direction were weaker and less consistent. After stable between-child differences were separated, the RI-CLPM identified bidirectional within-child associations across all three intervals, together with a strong positive association between the random intercepts. These findings are consistent with a transactional developmental perspective in which memory and emergent literacy are related through both enduring differences between children and variation within children over time [21, 22].

Developmental stability and concurrent associations

Memory and emergent literacy both showed significant autoregressive continuity across early childhood. Children’s earlier levels of memory-related and emergent literacy behaviors were therefore prospectively associated with their later levels in the same domains. Memory generally showed stronger autoregressive coefficients than emergent literacy, which may reflect differences in the developmental characteristics and measurement of the two constructs. The memory measure covered a relatively broad set of personal, semantic, sequential, and symbolic information that may accumulate across everyday experiences. Emergent literacy, by comparison, may be more responsive to variation in shared reading, preschool curricula, home literacy resources, and exposure to print [3, 4]. More broadly, prospective evidence has linked literacy enrichment in toddlerhood with later student engagement [42]. Its lower autoregressive coefficients may therefore reflect greater sensitivity to changing literacy experiences, although differences in scale length and reliability may also have contributed to this pattern.

The autoregressive coefficients were generally smaller in the RI-CLPM than in the CLPM. This difference is consistent with the distinction between the two models: CLPM autoregressive coefficients do not distinguish stable between-child differences from within-child continuity, whereas within-child autoregressive coefficients in the RI-CLPM represent the carry-over of deviations from a child’s own usual level [27, 28, 34, 35]. The larger coefficients in the CLPM may therefore reflect both enduring differences between children and continuity within children. Nevertheless, the significant within-child autoregressive paths in the RI-CLPM indicate that deviations from children’s usual levels in both domains carried forward across adjacent waves.

Memory and emergent literacy were also positively associated within each wave. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence linking working memory, phonological processing, and related cognitive abilities to early literacy skills [23, 24, 43]. The association is also compatible with the behavioral content of the KIT measures. Recognizing environmental signs, retrieving the meanings of familiar characters and symbols, remembering book conventions, and expressing ideas through drawings or early written forms may draw on the retention and retrieval of semantic, sequential, and symbolic information. Repeated engagement with print, books, stories, and symbolic expression may, in turn, provide children with opportunities to organize and retrieve such information.

The strong positive correlation between the random intercepts further indicated that children with higher stable levels of memory across the four waves also tended to have higher stable levels of emergent literacy. This association may reflect shared developmental conditions, including general cognitive and language abilities, family literacy resources, and preschool learning opportunities [3, 4, 23, 24]. However, the concurrent and stable between-child associations should be interpreted cautiously because both constructs were reported by caregivers. Shared rater impressions and differences in observation opportunities may have contributed to their covariance, particularly to the association between the random intercepts.Taken together, these findings supported H1, H2, and H5: both constructs showed positive autoregressive associations, were positively associated within each wave, and had positively correlated random intercepts.

Prospective bidirectional within-child associations

The most consistent cross-lagged finding was the prospective association from memory to emergent literacy. This pathway was significant across all three intervals in both the CLPM and RI-CLPM. In the RI-CLPM, higher-than-usual memory-related behavior at one wave was associated with higher-than-usual emergent literacy at the subsequent wave. This pattern indicates that the prospective association was not attributable solely to children who consistently scored higher than others in both domains.

This finding is consistent with research linking working memory and related cognitive abilities to early language, literacy, and reading development [24, 25, 44, 45]. Memory-related processes may support emergent literacy by helping children retain and retrieve the meanings of environmental print, familiar characters, book features, and symbolic representations. Understanding a story or book convention also requires children to maintain relevant information, connect current content with previous events, and retrieve knowledge accumulated through earlier literacy experiences. Although the KIT Memory Scale was not a direct assessment of working memory or any single memory system, its emphasis on the retention, recognition, retrieval, and use of previously learned information provides a plausible basis for its prospective association with emergent literacy.

The reverse association from emergent literacy to subsequent memory was weaker and less consistent in the CLPM but was significant across all three intervals in the RI-CLPM. Thus, the reverse association was more consistently observed at the within-child level. This distinction is important because CLPM estimates do not isolate within-child associations from stable between-child differences in repeated measures. Once these stable differences were separated, higher-than-usual emergent literacy was prospectively associated with higher-than-usual memory-related behavior at the next wave.

Previous research has mainly examined memory as a precursor of literacy, and direct evidence that literacy predicts subsequent memory remains limited. A previous review proposed that learning to read may shape verbal working memory through rehearsal and the development of phonological and orthographic representations [26]. Longitudinal evidence has also shown that Chinese word reading can predict later phonological awareness, although phonological awareness is related to but distinct from memory [32]. Literacy experiences repeatedly require children to encode, organize, maintain, and retrieve orthographic, semantic, event, and sequential information. Shared reading requires children to remember characters and events, connect information across a narrative, and anticipate what may occur next. Print recognition and emergent writing likewise involve retrieving symbol–meaning relations and organizing information in a meaningful sequence. Repeated participation in such activities may provide practice in the memory-related behaviors captured by the KIT scale.

The largest estimated reverse coefficient was observed from 60 to 72 months, which may reflect the increasing complexity and regularity of literacy experiences near school entry. During this period, children may encounter more demanding activities involving character recognition, story comprehension, drawing, symbolic representation, and early writing. These activities place increasing demands on the retrieval and organization of previously learned information. However, this interpretation remains tentative because the study did not directly measure changes in literacy instruction, home literacy activities, or preschool learning experiences. The larger coefficient may also have been affected by reduced score variability and possible ceiling effects at 72 months.

Taken together, the cross-lagged findings are compatible with a reciprocal developmental relation between the two constructs. Memory-related behaviors may facilitate children’s engagement with print, books, and symbols, while literacy experiences may provide repeated opportunities to exercise the retention and retrieval of meaningful information. Nevertheless, the paths represent prospective associations rather than causal effects. The RI-CLPM separates stable between-child differences in the repeated measures, but it does not eliminate the influence of time-varying factors such as changes in shared reading, attention, language stimulation, preschool instruction, or caregiver rating tendencies. The temporal ordering of the associations is therefore consistent with reciprocal development but does not establish the mechanisms responsible for that development.Within these observational limits, the RI-CLPM findings supported H3 and H4, indicating bidirectional prospective associations between memory and emergent literacy at the within-child level.

Theoretical and practical implications

Viewed through a transactional developmental perspective, this two-level pattern suggests that the relation between memory and emergent literacy involves both enduring between-child differences and within-child variation over time. The strong random-intercept association suggests that the two domains share relatively stable developmental foundations, whereas the cross-lagged paths indicate that deviations from children’s usual levels are prospectively related over time. These results align with the view that development reflects continuity within the child as well as ongoing interactions among abilities and experiences [21, 22]. However, because environmental transactions were not directly measured, the present study should be regarded as providing evidence consistent with this perspective rather than as a comprehensive test of the transactional model.

Methodologically, the comparison between the CLPM and RI-CLPM illustrates why separating between-child and within-child variation matters in longitudinal developmental research [27, 28, 34, 35, 46]. The CLPM facilitated comparison with previous cross-lagged research, but its coefficients represented a mixture of enduring differences between children and processes occurring within children. The RI-CLPM provided a more direct examination of the study’s within-child questions and revealed a more consistent reverse association from emergent literacy to memory. The two models therefore offered complementary information rather than interchangeable estimates of the same developmental process.

The findings also suggest the potential value of preschool and family literacy activities that engage memory-related processes. During shared reading, adults may invite children to recall characters, retell events in sequence, connect earlier and later parts of a story, or anticipate what may happen next. Activities involving environmental print, drawing, and emergent writing may likewise encourage children to retrieve meanings, organize ideas, and use symbolic representations. Such activities reflect the everyday overlap between the two domains measured in this study. These implications should nevertheless remain tentative because the study did not test an intervention or establish that increasing one ability would cause improvement in the other.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

The study has several strengths. It used four waves of data spanning 36 to 72 months, a large analytic sample, and both the CLPM and RI-CLPM to distinguish overall longitudinal associations from within-child dynamics. The use of caregiver reports also provided information about memory-related and emergent literacy behaviors observed across everyday settings, complementing what can be captured during a single direct assessment session.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. First, the operational definitions of memory and emergent literacy were constrained by the secondary dataset. The Memory Scale did not isolate a single memory system, and the Emergent Literacy Scale did not comprehensively assess phonological awareness, rapid naming, vocabulary, narrative ability, or sound–symbol correspondence. The findings should therefore be interpreted in relation to the specific survey-based constructs measured in this study rather than generalized to all components of memory and emergent literacy. Future studies could combine differentiated memory tasks with direct assessments of distinct emergent literacy domains.

Second, both constructs were rated by caregivers, creating the possibility of common method variance, social desirability, rater impressions, and differences in observation opportunities. Caregiver reports can capture behaviors across repeated naturalistic contexts, but they cannot replace direct assessments of children’s performance. Future research should integrate caregiver and teacher ratings with standardized memory tasks, direct emergent literacy assessments, and structured observations. Such multi-method designs would help separate developmental associations from variance attributable to a shared reporting source.

Third, both scales approached their upper limits at 72 months, particularly the memory measure. Reduced variability at the upper end may have affected autoregressive and cross-lagged estimates, especially those involving the 60-to-72-month interval. The Emergent Literacy Scale also showed moderate internal consistency across waves. Although its coefficients were acceptable for a brief six-item measure covering several related behaviors, measurement error may have attenuated some longitudinal associations. Future studies should use measures with greater difficulty and broader coverage at later ages.

Fourth, the study used observed total scores and did not directly test whether the measures functioned equivalently across the four ages. Without evidence of longitudinal measurement invariance, changes in scores or associations may partly reflect developmental changes in how the items represent the underlying constructs. Future item-level studies should evaluate longitudinal measurement invariance before comparing developmental levels and structural relations across ages.

Fifth, the RI-CLPM remains an observational model. Although it separated stable between-child differences from within-child variation, it did not identify the sources of either component or eliminate time-varying confounding. Time-invariant factors, such as parental education and family socioeconomic background, may help explain stable differences between children. Changes in home literacy activities, preschool experiences, language exposure, attention, and learning motivation may also influence the within-child associations observed over time. Future studies should incorporate theoretically relevant time-invariant and time-varying covariates and, where feasible, use experimental or intervention designs to examine possible mechanisms.

Finally, the sample was drawn from a single large-scale longitudinal cohort within one linguistic and educational setting, and the analytic sample included only children with records at all four selected waves. This restriction may have introduced selective-retention bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, because the emergent literacy measure included the recognition of simple Chinese characters, the findings may not transfer directly to contexts using alphabetic writing systems. Cross-linguistic research across different writing systems and literacy environments is needed to assess the generalizability of the observed bidirectional associations. Future studies should also distinguish specific memory components and emergent literacy subdomains to identify the processes most closely related over time.

Conclusions

Memory and emergent literacy were longitudinally associated from 36 to 72 months. Both constructs showed temporal stability and positive within-wave associations. After stable between-child differences were separated, higher-than-usual memory was consistently associated with higher-than-usual emergent literacy at the subsequent wave. Higher-than-usual emergent literacy was also associated with higher-than-usual memory at the subsequent wave across all three intervals, with the largest estimated reverse coefficient observed from 60 to 72 months. These findings indicate that the longitudinal relation between memory and emergent literacy involves both enduring differences between children and bidirectional within-child associations. They also highlight the value of distinguishing between-child differences from within-child developmental dynamics during early childhood.
