Impact of Albuminuria‐Lowering Treatments on Cardiovascular Predictive Ceramides in Diabetes: Post Hoc Analysis of the ROTATE Trials
Authors: Kristina Pigsborg, Simon Maria Kienle, Asger Wretlind, Viktor Rotbain Curovic, Martin Bæk Blond, Hiddo J. L. Heerspink, Tine Willum Hansen, Frederik Persson, Karolina Sulek, Cristina Legido‐Quigley, Peter Rossing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality in individuals with diabetes. Diabetic kidney disease, closely related to CVD risk, is prevalent in up to 40% of this population. Emerging evidence suggests ceramide lipids as accurate biomarkers for CVD. We assessed the effect of four albuminuria‐lowering drugs on CVD‐related ceramides in diabetes by post hoc analysis of the ROTATE trials.Materials and MethodsTwenty six adults with type 1 (T1D) as well as 37 with type 2 diabetes (T2D) with a urine albumin‐creatinine ratio (UACR) of 30–500 mg/g participated in a 4‐week 4‐time randomized crossover study with periods of telmisartan, empagliflozin, linagliptin and baricitinib treatment, each separated by a 4‐week washout period. Blood samples were collected at the beginning and end of each period and ceramide lipids (Cer16, Cer18, Cer20, Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1) were measured. The effect of each treatment was evaluated using linear mixed‐effect models.ResultsAt baseline, individuals with T2D had greater levels of Cer22 and Cer24 compared to the individuals with T1D. Among the treatments, linagliptin was the only drug that demonstrated a reduction of Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1 from baseline by 22.6% (95% CI: −33.58; −9.79, p = 0.001), 25.7% (95% CI: −38.94; −9.69, p = 0.003) and 19.6% (95% CI: −31.34; −5.95, p = 0.007), respectively. No changes in the ceramides were observed for the other drugs.ConclusionOur exploratory findings suggest that certain albuminuria‐lowering drugs may affect ceramide levels as a secondary effect. However, further mechanistic investigations are needed.

Introduction

Despite substantial advancements in medical therapy over the past decades, cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality among individuals with diabetes [1]. This population faces a 1.5–2‐fold higher risk of cardiovascular complications compared to adults without diabetes [2, 3]. Concurrently, diabetic kidney disease (DKD), frequently characterized by persistent albuminuria, emerges as one of the most prevalent and serious complications of diabetes, affecting up to 40% of individuals with diabetes [4, 5]. DKD not only worsens the progression of diabetes but also imposes a substantial burden on the cardiovascular system, further compounding the risk of adverse outcomes. Albuminuria is recognized as a biomarker of CVD, highlighting the interconnected nature of these complications [6].

Traditional biomarkers for CVD risk include low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol and triglycerides [7]. However, studies investigating CVD have suggested that LDL cholesterol and triglycerides may not be highly predictive, and there is emerging evidence that potentially another class of lipids, ceramides, might be a better clinical biomarker for CVD risk development [8]. Studies have shown that ceramides function as potential biomarkers of CVD independent of cholesterol [9]. Ceramides are bioactive sphingolipids linked to inflammation, insulin resistance and CVD [10]. Moreover, a recent study associated specific ceramides levels and their ratios to rapid decline in kidney function and end‐stage kidney disease in individuals with T2D, linking them to both CVD and kidney disease [11] highlighting the need to test treatment options to modulate these ceramides in individuals with T2D and DKD.

Ceramides function as risk markers of atherosclerosis and CVD in various populations [11, 12, 13] and specific ceramide ratios have been developed into a risk score that is currently used in clinics as a tool to identify non‐diabetic individuals at high risk for later cardiovascular complications [14, 15]. While the score offers to identify those at high risk of CVD, little is known about the effect of treatments on ceramide levels. As ceramides are found in and transported by circulating lipoprotein particles, such as LDL and HDL cholesterol, it is considered that ceramide levels can be lowered with cholesterol‐lowering measures [16]. Statins, PCSK9 inhibitors and fenofibrate have shown modest ceramide‐lowering effects [17, 18]. Furthermore, we have previously shown that glucagon‐like peptide receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs), such as liraglutide, were able to reduce the level of specific ceramides and phospholipids [19, 20].

Despite these insights, the impact of albuminuria‐lowering drugs commonly used in diabetes management on ceramide levels remains underexplored. This post hoc analysis, utilising data from the “Rotation for Optimal Targeting of Albuminuria and Treatment Evaluation” (ROTATE) trials [21], investigates the effects of four albuminuria‐lowering drugs on previously identified high‐risk CVD ceramides (i.e., Cer(d18:1/16:0), Cer(d18:1/18:0), Cer(d18:1/20:0), Cer(d18:1/22:0), Cer(d18:1/24:0) and Cer(d18:1/24:1), referred to as Cer16, Cer18, Cer20, Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1, respectively, throughout the paper) among individuals with T1D and T2D. Secondly, we investigated whether albuminuria‐lowering drugs were affecting ceramide levels in a reproducible manner.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This exploratory post hoc analysis is based on the two randomized, prospective, open‐label, multicentre, crossover ROTATE trials, including participants with T1D and T2D (ROTATE‐1 and ROTATE‐2, respectively), conducted between February 2017 and October 2019 at Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen (Herlev, Denmark), Ziekenhuis Groep Twente (Almelo, the Netherlands) and Bethesda Diabetes Research Center (Hoogeveen, the Netherlands). Both trials aimed to assess the individual UACR‐lowering responses of telmisartan, empagliflozin, linagliptin and baricitinib in participants with diabetes and elevated urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR). The inclusion and exclusion criteria were previously outlined in detail [21]. In short, eligible participants included adults diagnosed with either T1D or T2D, presenting with a UACR ranging from 30 to 500 mg/g, and possessing an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m2. Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals who had experienced a cardiovascular event within the preceding 6 months of enrolment or were undergoing treatment with a GLP‐1 RA. Furthermore, the use of renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) blockers, sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), or dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4i) was discontinued for at least 4 weeks upon randomization and during follow‐up.

Eligible participants received 4 weeks of treatment with an angiotensin‐receptor blocker (telmisartan 80 mg daily), an SGLT2i (empagliflozin 10 mg daily), a DPP4i (linagliptin 5 mg daily), and the anti‐inflammatory JAK‐STAT inhibitor (baricitinib 2 mg daily) in random order with 4‐week washout periods in between. As described in the primary result publication, a 4‐week treatment period was chosen based on previous studies showing that the effects of these drugs were fully present after 4 weeks [21]. At the end of the four‐way rotation schedule, participants were re‐exposed to a fifth 4‐week treatment period with the drug that induced the greatest albuminuria‐lowering decrease for each participant. Plasma samples were collected before and after each treatment period. All participants gave written informed consent, and the trials were conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. The trials were registered at clinicaltrialregister.nl (NTR5602, NTR5603) and at the European Union Trials Register (2015‐005691‐26, 2017‐004641‐25). Local ethics committees approved both trials at each participating site.

Untargeted Lipidomics Analysis of Ceramides

Untargeted lipidomics analysis was performed on plasma blood samples and was done as previously described (details in Supporting Information) [22, 23, 24, 25]. Data processing, including batch correction and normalization to exogenous internal standard, ceramide(d18:1/17:0), was done in R version 4.3.0. Outliers, defined as measures more than four standard deviations away from the median, were truncated.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous data are presented as means and SD for normally distributed data; otherwise as medians with 25th and 75th percentiles. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages of the total. Differences in baseline characteristics between the diabetes types were tested depending on data distribution. Changes in ceramide levels were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models including period and diabetes type as fixed effects. Ceramides were log‐transformed, and results presented as geometric mean percentage changes. Analyses were adjusted for clinical covariates, with Bonferroni correction for six comparisons. Data were back‐exponentiated and are presented as geometric means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and geometric mean percentage change from baseline with 95% CI. The changes within each diabetes type were also investigated with the same model, stratifying for diabetes type. The main model, which included all participants, was adjusted for: (1) sex, BMI and age; (2) LDL cholesterol and (3) UACR. A two‐sided 5% level of significance was regarded as statistically significant. Multiple comparison correction was carried out for the six ceramides, with Bonferroni correction p‐value = 0.05/6 [26]. An additional post hoc regression analysis was added during the review process with a more comprehensively adjusted model containing adjustments for age, sex, BMI, LDL, HDL, HbA1c, UACR, eGFR, CRP, diabetes duration, statins, beta blockers, metformin and insulin at baseline was additionally applied. Analysis of associations between the six ceramides and the clinical outcome were done by adding the different outcomes individually to the main model as a fixed effect, from where beta and p‐values were extracted. Both clinical outcomes and the ceramides were scaled by setting the mean to zero and dividing by the standard deviation. Linear mixed models were also used to test differences in the participant's first and second exposure to the same drug (ordinary vs. optimal treatment) over time (Week 0 vs. Week 4) with a time × treatment interaction within each drug. Amount of participants for the respective re‐exposure was dependent on the individual UACR response and thus have various participant numbers [21]. The statistical analyses and figures were done in RStudio v.4.3.0.

Results

Study Population at Baseline

The study population is characterized in Table 1. A total of 63 participants from the ROTATE trials were included in this post hoc analysis. Overall, 83% were men with a mean age of 63 ± 10 years and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 30.0 ± 4.2 kg/m2. When stratifying for diabetes type, 26 and 37 had T1D and T2D, respectively. Glycaemic status, kidney function and levels of albuminuria were the same independent of diabetes type. However, the participants with T1D were younger than the ones with T2D (60 ± 12 vs. 67 ± 8 years, p = 0.008) and had higher levels of HDL cholesterol (1.82 ± 0.53 vs. 1.27 ± 0.85 mmol/L, p < 0.001).

Ceramide Changes by Albuminuria‐Lowering Treatments

The effect of each drug on the ceramide levels is presented in Figure 1 (also see Table S2 and Figure S1). Overall, linagliptin induced the greatest reduction. Levels of the very long‐chained Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1 were reduced by 22.6% (95% CI: −33.58; −9.79, p = 0.001), 25.7% (95% CI: −38.94; −9.69, p = 0.003) and 19.6% (95% CI: −31.34; −5.95, p = 0.007), respectively, for all participants. No changes were observed for Cer16, Cer18 and Cer20 by linagliptin. The mentioned reductions were mainly driven by individuals with T2D (Cer22: −19.58, 95% CI: −32.32; −4.46, p = 0.015; Cer24: −22.36, 95% CI: −38.03; −2,72, p = 0.029; Cer24:1: −16.81, 95% CI: −28.05; −3.81, p = 0.014) but tended to decrease for individuals with T1D as well (Cer22: −25.87, 95% CI: −44.98; −0.12, p = 0.049; Cer24: −28.53, 95% CI: −50.99; 4.23, p = 0.079; Cer24:1: −21.00, 95% CI: −45.26; 14.02, p = 0.195). The reductions remained significant after adjusting for sex, age and BMI as well as LDL cholesterol and UACR (Table S3). After adjusting for multiple comparisons, ceramides are still significant. During the treatment period with baricitinib, Cer18 and Cer20 were increased by 4.5% (95% CI: 0.88; 8.16, p = 0.015) and 5.0% (95% CI: 0.64; 9.55, p = 0.025), respectively, which also remained significant after adjustments for covariates, but not after adjusting for multiple comparisons. The increases induced by baricitinib were not significant when stratifying for the type of diabetes, however, both groups showed the same direction. No effect on any of the ceramides was observed in the treatment periods with empagliflozin and telmisartan for all participants, however, an 8.9% (95% CI: 1.10; 17.19, p = 0.026) increase of Cer20 was observed after telmisartan for participants with T1D.

Ceramide Levels in Relation to Clinical Outcomes

Associations between the ceramides and the clinical outcome measures during the trial were studied to investigate the relationship (Figure 2A). All ceramides showed positive associations with traditional clinical measures of the lipid profile, such as LDL and total cholesterol levels (p < 0.0001 for all); however, HDL cholesterol only showed positive associations with long‐chained Cer16 and Cer20. HbA1c showed positive associations with Cer18, Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1. Inflammation in terms of c‐reactive protein (CRP) concentration showed a negative association with Cer24. No associations were observed for eGFR; however, UACR showed positive associations with all ceramides (p < 0.0001 for all). Whereas most associations between the ceramides and the clinical markers had a linear relationship, UACR had a non‐linear relationship to all ceramides exemplified with Cer24, Cer24:1 and Cer22 (Figures 2B and S2). The greatest relationship for Cer24 was for UACR < 100 mg/g, where after the association flattened out. Intra‐Individual Response to the Same Drug

Due to the design of the study with a fifth period chosen as a re‐exposure of one of the four drugs that performed best for an individual in relation to UACR improvements, it was possible to investigate the intra‐individual response for each drug (Table S4). The participants being re‐exposed to linagliptin (n = 11) still had a decrease in the levels of Cer22 and Cer24; however, the reductions were reduced from −23.22% (95% CI: −34.13; −10.51, p = 0.001) and −26.47% (95% CI: −39.56; −10.54, p = 0.003) for Cer22 and Cer24, respectively, to −9.35% (95% CI: −35.73; 27.86, p = 0.537) and −4.79% (95% CI: −29.23; 28.08, p = 0.718). No exposure‐time interaction was observed for the two ceramides (p = 0.346 and p = 0.153). For the participants being re‐exposed to baricitinib (n = 8), Cer18 and Cer20 also increased after the second exposure with double the effect of the first exposure, but the difference between the first and second exposure was not large enough to detect any exposure‐time interaction. Most participants were re‐exposed to telmisartan (n = 33), and here we did not observe differences between any of the ceramides from first to second exposure.

Discussion

In this exploratory post hoc analysis of the ROTATE trials including individuals with both T1D and T2D, we investigated the effect of 4 albuminuria‐lowering drugs on specific ceramides known to be potential risk markers for the development of CVD within 2–5 years. The main finding was linagliptin's ability to induce a 20% reduction of the very‐long chained ceramides Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1. Moreover, baricitinib induced a small 5% increase for Cer18 and Cer20. In the sensitivity analysis, comparing the responses for individuals with T1D and T2D, no differences were observed.

The findings from the linagliptin treatment align with previous research as DPP‐4 inhibitors are well‐known to reduce overall circulating lipid levels [27]. Interestingly, in a study from Wretlind et al. a 23% reduction in Cer24 and Cer24:1 levels was observed following a 26‐week treatment with liraglutide/GLP‐1 RA (up to 1.8 mg/day) compared to placebo with a 0.5%–3% increase [19]. However, as liraglutide supplements synthetic GLP‐1 externally, linagliptin as a DPP‐4 inhibitor slows the degradation of endogenous GLP‐1; thus, it was hypothesized that liraglutide would have a more pronounced effect on ceramide reduction. Surprisingly, our study revealed similar percentage reductions in these specific ceramides despite the different mechanisms of action between liraglutide and linagliptin. Moreover, no reduction of Cer22 was found in that study; however, they also observed a reduction of Cer16 and Cer18 with 6% and 13%, respectively, after the liraglutide treatment compared to their placebo group (3% and 5%, respectively) [19]. In addition, liraglutide has been shown to provoke a fast improvement in diabetic and CVD‐related outcomes within 2–8 weeks, whereas linagliptin reaches the steady‐state of metabolic remodelling between 24 and 48 weeks [28, 29, 30]. However, early metabolic adaptation to DPP4i treatments can be observed after 1–4 weeks. Thus, our findings likely reflect early to intermediate effects to the treatment [31]. Moreover, GLP‐1 RAs are known to exert renoprotective effects that appear only partly dependent on weight loss or glycaemic control. Across clinical trials, GLP‐1 RAs have consistently been shown to reduce albuminuria by approximately 15%–30% [31, 32, 33]. In addition, GLP‐1 RAs affect direct and indirect lipid metabolism [34] and were found to reduce neutral lipid levels in blood in people with diabetes [19, 35]. In contrast, the primary ROTATE trial analyses demonstrated only a modest effect of linagliptin on albuminuria, with a mean UACR reduction of −8.5% (95% CI: −16.9 to 0.7). This suggests that liraglutide is, overall, a better treatment option for a potential reduction of ceramides related to CVD and kidney disease risk.

Furthermore, GLP‐1 RA has also been shown to reduce the progression of kidney disease, cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in individuals with T2D and chronic kidney disease [36]. These different effects of DPP‐4 inhibitors and GLP‐1 RA on ceramide levels may also be connected to the distinct enzymatic pathways for ceramide synthesis. While very‐long chained ceramides are predominantly generated by the ceramide synthase (CerS)2 and CerS3, shorter chained ceramides are mainly generated by CerS5‐6 [37]. Thus, it seems that DPP‐4 inhibitors tend to affect the ceramide synthesis pathway regulated by CerS2 and CerS3 more specifically than GLP‐1 RA, which seems to have a more general effect on ceramides independent of the ceramide synthase involved.

Baricitinib is known as a Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor for rheumatoid arthritis treatment [38]. Due to the close link between the immune and inflammatory response, recent studies aimed to elucidate the prospective role of JAK inhibitors in the management of metabolic syndrome including DKD [38]. Even though baricitinib has shown potential improvements in albuminuria in individuals with T2D and DKD [39], in a meta‐analysis baricitinib was shown to increase LDL and HDL cholesterol levels after daily treatment with 2 mg baricitinib for 6–52 weeks [40]. A further increase in LDL and HDL cholesterol was noted after doubling the treatment dose, making the LDL cholesterol increase dose‐dependent. This could perhaps explain why in the present study a 5% increase in Cer18 and Cer20 was observed; however, this has not been investigated by others.

SGLT‐2 inhibitors have been shown to have beneficial effects on heart failure, kidneys and CVD mortality in both individuals with and without diabetes [41]. However, in this analysis there were no changes in any of the ceramides after the empagliflozin treatment period. Considering the short treatment period and the small sample size, this finding suggests that the reno‐cardioprotective effects of SGLT‐2i are most likely not mediated by changes in the studied ceramide levels, even though SGLT‐2i indirectly affect the lipid metabolism through lowering blood glucose levels and increasing fatty acid oxidation. Moreover, for the telmisartan treatment period, no changes were observed for any of the ceramides. This is in line with our expectations for a study period of 4 weeks since telmisartan targets primarily the angiotensin II receptor to modulate blood pressure. However, other studies found a small decrease in blood lipid levels, mainly for triglycerides but also for LDL cholesterol, though this was often over a longer treatment period [42]. The suggested mechanism was via partial hepatic PPARα agonist activity and increasing lipoprotein lipase expression [43]. Nevertheless, none of these effects seem to be associated with the pathways related to ceramides. The potential lack of an effect of SGLT‐2i and telmisartan on the levels of the respective ceramides could be clinically relevant because it may provide treatment opportunities in cases when ceramide levels are not dysregulated and thus should not be targeted.

Even with significant differences in baseline levels of Cer22 and Cer24 between the two types of diabetes, there was no indication of a different response for these specific ceramides. However, the overall response to all ceramides after the telmisartan treatment period appears to diverge for the two types of diabetes, with a reduction observed in individuals with T2D and an increase in those with T1D. Nevertheless, these differences were not statistically significant. Whether this discrepancy reflects an actual distinction between diabetes types or is merely due to chance cannot be determined with certainty given the size of the subgroups. The effects on CVD have not been investigated in T1D with any of the agents.

As mentioned previously, ceramides are closely linked to cholesterol transport [37]. Hence, it is not unexpected that we identified a pronounced association between all six ceramides and both total and LDL cholesterol levels. Notably, only Cer16 and Cer20 demonstrated significant associations with HDL cholesterol. Additionally, our findings support previous research indicating a positive association between HbA1c levels and ceramide concentrations. Interestingly, ceramides have been utilized in predicting the onset of T2D, as evidenced by a higher Cer18/Cer16 ratio [44].

Prior research has established associations between ceramides and kidney disease. One study found higher levels of Cer16 in individuals with DKD [45], whereas another study found increased levels of all six ceramides investigated in this paper in individuals with chronic kidney disease when compared to those without [12]. In individual with T2D lower levels of Cer24 and higher levels of Cer16/Cer24, Cer18/Cer24 and Cer24:1/Cer24 ratios associated with an increased likelihood of early rapid decline in kidney function and higher levels of Cer16/Cer24 and Cer24:1/Cer24 ratios associated with increased risk of incident in end‐stage kidney disease [11]. In line with this study and building on it, we provide insights into how these ceramides can be modulated by the albuminuria‐lowering drug linagliptin, which is able to reduce both UACR (−8.5% mean change to baseline [21]) and very long‐chained ceramides. Thus, our results support mechanistically the link between ceramides and kidney function in individuals with T2D and show for the first time how both can be modulated simultaneously. Besides this, in our previous investigations, we have observed positive associations between urinary albumin excretion rate (UAER) and all six ceramides in individuals with T2D and albuminuria, but when albuminuria was not an inclusion criterion for participation in trials, the associations were mixed [19]. In the ROTATE trials, all participants exhibited some degree of albuminuria. However, from our association analysis, it seemed that UACR and the ceramide levels were most affected for a UACR < 100 mg/g.

Contradictory to the literature, weak negative associations were observed for both age and CRP to all the ceramides in this post hoc analysis. Ceramides are often associated with age‐related diseases such as atherosclerosis [46], cancer [47] and Alzheimer's [48], and age has in several studies been linked to higher levels of ceramides; however, the relationships seem to be specific to the acyl chain length and saturation [49]. Furthermore, ceramides are also often connected with low‐grade systemic inflammation caused by obesity that promotes inflammation through the activation of toll‐like receptor 4, interleukin‐6, and interleukin‐8 receptors on immune cells [50]. Accordingly, the observed negative association of Cer24 with CRP is difficult to explain.

A unique aspect of the study design was the additional fifth period where participants were re‐exposed to the drug where they had the greatest response in UACR during the four first treatment periods. Here, 52% of the participants had telmisartan as their best‐performing drug in relation to UACR improvements. For the overall reduction of the ceramides, linagliptin was the best treatment option for ~35%, whereas telmisartan was only the best for ~12% of the participants. The ~20% reduction of Cer22, Cer24 and Cer24:1 observed after the first exposure to linagliptin was not observed after the second exposure. When re‐exposed, baricitinib's increase in Cer18 and Cer20 doubled compared to the small increase in the first exposure and was significant even though the group was very small (n = 8). However, as the fifth period was a re‐exposure to one of the four drugs, all groups were underpowered and hence the results are very explorative. Nevertheless, no interactions between the first and second treatment periods were observed for the two treatments, indicating that the intra‐individual responses are to some extent similar and that the drugs have similar effects after both the first and second exposure.

Although the ROTATE trials feature novel designs that reflect the trial‐and‐error process of identifying optimal drugs for patients, the study has its limitations. The ROTATE trials included a rather small sample size of 63 participants (26 with T1D and 37 with T2D) and the trials were focused on the impact of different types of drugs on DKD, where the cardiovascular outcomes and ceramide levels were not originally planned as an endpoint of the trials. The intervention period for each drug was restricted to 4 weeks based on previous literature, which suggested that this period was adequate to observe the full effect of the drugs on albuminuria levels [39, 51, 52, 53, 54]. However, the optimal duration required to influence ceramide levels remains unclear. Although we detected certain changes in the present study with its short follow‐up period, a potential need for a longer intervention period is indicated by another study that demonstrated reductions of Cer16 with GLP‐1 RA after 26 weeks [19]. The trials also lacked a placebo period, a notable absence that could have enhanced the study design and provided valuable insights into the individual effects of each drug, as well as the stability of ceramide levels. Additionally, while a robust lipid analysis was conducted, the measures of ceramide were not quantitative but rather relative intensities. Lastly, the sample size, particularly when considering diabetes types separately, was modest, thereby preventing definitive conclusions. Nonetheless, the present analysis underscores the importance of future studies, with a greater number of participants and longer periods assessing the effects of interventions on ceramide levels in individuals with diabetes and the ensuing complications. Such a study as for example, a randomized controlled trial with additionally more potent medication acting on the same pathway, could help to further substantiate our findings. However, our data suggest that individuals may respond differently to albuminuria‐lowering therapies, raising the possibility that treatments could be tailored to the specific needs of a certain population. To enable such an approach based on ceramide levels, routinely clinical measurements of ceramides using lipidomic methods would be required to identify the individuals most likely to benefit from a given therapy.

In conclusion, this explorative post hoc analysis showed that among individuals with diabetes and albuminuria, linagliptin reduces the levels of specific ceramides as a secondary effect in addition to its UACR lowering effect. However, further research is needed to better understand this effect before such treatments can be translated into diabetic care.
