Trace Element Changes in Goat Milk Following Experimentally Induced Intramammary Infection With Biofilm Positive and Negative Strains of Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: M Ghari, M Heidarpour, M Ghaemi, F Salmannejad, B Khoramian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTrace elements in milk provide valuable information about mammary gland health and immune response. The biofilm‐forming ability of Staphylococcus aureus is known to contribute to persistent and chronic mastitis infections.ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare changes in milk trace element concentrations following experimental intramammary infection with biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative strains of S. aureus in goats.MethodsNine healthy lactating goats (native Iranian breed, 1–5 years old) were allocated to two groups and inoculated intracisternally in one udder half with either biofilm‐positive (n = 5) or biofilm‐negative (n = 4) strains of S. aureus (103 CFU/mL). The strains were isolated from bovine mastitis and confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the nuc. Ceftiofur was administered for treatment after confirmation of infection. Ceftiofur was chosen because both strains were sensitive to this antibiotic and previous research has shown Ceftiofur to be effective against both biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative S. aureus isolates. Milk samples were collected immediately before challenge (T0), at 6 (T1), 12 (T2), 24 (T3), 36 (T4), 48 (T5) and 72 (T6) hours post‐challenge, immediately prior to treatment (T7), and at 1 (T8), 7 (T9) and 14 (T10) days post‐treatment. Concentrations of copper, calcium, selenium, zinc, magnesium, potassium, sodium and phosphorus were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES).ResultsThe milk calcium concentration at T2 and T4 was significantly higher in the biofilm‐positive group than that in the biofilm‐negative group (p < 0.05). The amount of milk selenium at T4 and T10 was significantly higher in the biofilm‐positive group than that in the biofilm‐negative group (p < 0.05). The milk potassium and phosphorous levels at T7 were significantly higher in the biofilm‐positive group than that in the biofilm‐negative group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsBiofilm‐positive strains of S. aureus induced a greater secretion of certain trace elements (particularly calcium, selenium, potassium and phosphorus) into goat milk compared with biofilm‐negative strains. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that biofilm‐forming strains cause more extensive mammary epithelial damage and barrier dysfunction. These alterations may be indicative of increased mammary epithelial permeability and may also lead to compromised local immunity, allowing for persistent mastitis development by biofilm positive strains.Keywords: antibiotic resistance, biofilm, goat, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES), intramammary infection, mastitis, milk biomarkers, Staphylococcus aureus, trace elements

Introduction

Mastitis is one of the most common and economically important diseases in dairy animals, resulting in substantial losses through reduced milk yield and quality (Cheng and Han 2020), shortened productive lifespan (F. Zigo et al. 2019; Musayeva et al. 2020), increased labour costs (Halasa et al. 2007) and compromised animal welfare due to mammary tissue damage (Zigo et al. 2020; Bandeira Junior et al. 2023). This tissue damage, induced by apoptosis or necrosis, reduces the number and activity of mammary epithelial cells, thereby contributing to decreased milk production (Zhao and Lacasse 2008). Both genotypic and phenotypic characteristics play significant roles in mastitis susceptibility and pathogenesis (Oget et al. 2019).

Practical Implications

These findings suggest that milk trace element profiling could serve as a non‐invasive biomarker for assessing mastitis severity and differentiating infections caused by biofilm‐forming versus non‐biofilm‐forming S. aureus strains, potentially guiding more targeted treatment strategies in dairy goat management.

Mastitis is primarily caused by microbial infection, with bacteria being the most common etiological agents (Zhao and Lacasse 2008; Brennecke et al. 2021). Infection typically increases milk somatic cell count; however, non‐infectious factors, such as oestrus, season, milking frequency, milk yield and stage of lactation, can also elevate somatic cell numbers. In small ruminants, bacteriological culture of milk remains a standard diagnostic tool for mastitis (Toledo and Dacey 2021).

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram‐positive bacterium recognized as a major zoonotic pathogen responsible for a wide range of clinical conditions on dairy farms. Depending on strain characteristics and infection site, this opportunistic pathogen can cause invasive infections or toxin‐mediated disease (DeLeo et al. 2009). In sheep and goats, S. aureus is the predominant cause of subclinical mastitis, accounting for 45%–48% of cases in sheep and 60%–80.7% in goats (Toledo and Dacey 2021). Recent years have highlighted diverse genetic and phenotypic virulence traits of S. aureus, including antibiotic resistance and enhanced biofilm‐forming ability, both of which contribute significantly to disease pathogenesis (Lira et al. 2016).

The role of biofilms in human and veterinary medicine is well established. Biofilms confer high‐level resistance to antibiotics and disinfectants, render bacteria less susceptible to macrophage phagocytosis due to aggregated cell mass and enable the evasion of host immune responses, frequently leading to persistent and chronic infections (Vargová et al. 2023; Monzón et al. 2001, 2002; Conlon 2014). Previous research reported that biofilm‐positive S. aureus isolates from bovine mastitis show significantly higher resistance to antibiotics, including penicillin, lincomycin, oxacillin, methicillin and vancomycin compared to biofilm‐negative isolates (Ghari et al. 2022).

Most veterinary research on biofilm formation has focused on S. aureus strains isolated from bovine mastitis (Melchior et al. 2006; Oliveira et al. 2006), with limited studies in caprine mastitis.

Trace elements play critical roles in immune function and antioxidant defence and are essential for maintaining health and preventing diseases such as mastitis. Milk trace element concentrations can vary substantially under different physiological and pathological conditions (Gera et al. 2011). During mastitis, mammary tissue damage and increased blood–milk barrier permeability alter trace element transfer into milk (Zhao and Lacasse 2008). Consequently, changes in milk trace element profiles following S. aureus infection may provide valuable insights into mammary gland health and immune status. Components of the inflammatory response, including the loss of tight junctions between mammary epithelial cells, recruitment of polymorphonuclear neutrophils, and dysregulation of metal transport proteins lead to alterations in milk mineral levels (Li et al. 2018; Singh et al. 2017).

Research Gap: To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined trace element changes in goat milk following experimental intramammary S. aureus infection nor have the influence of biofilm‐forming ability on these changes been investigated.

Alterations of milk composition due to bovine mastitis have been well documented (Mahran et al. 1992; Guha et al. 2012; Kotur et al. 2014). However, comparable data regarding goats are limited, despite the fact that they are also common dairy animals, especially in developing countries worldwide. Moreover, there have been no studies concerning biofilm positive and negative strains' influence on trace element homeostasis in any dairy species. Correlations between biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance phenotype and altered milk trace element profile are poorly understood. Therefore, our study aimed to assess changes in goat milk trace elements following experimental challenge with biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative strains of S. aureus.

We hypothesize that biofilm‐positive strains cause greater disruption of trace element homeostasis compared to biofilm‐negative strains, reflecting more severe mammary epithelial damage and barrier dysfunction, potentially due to their enhanced virulence and antibiotic resistance characteristics.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Preparation

S. aureus strains previously isolated from chronic cases of bovine mastitis. Milk samples were cultured on blood agar and MacConkey agar (Merck, Germany). Identification of S. aureus was based on haemolytic pattern, Gram staining, colonial morphology, positive catalase test, mannitol fermentation on mannitol salt agar and positive coagulase test. Confirmation was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the nuc gene using a 613‐bp primer (F; CTGGCATATGTATGGCAATTGTT, R; TATTGACCTGAATCAGCGTTGTCT) (Amiri et al. 2024).

Biofilm Formation Ability Assay

Biofilm‐forming ability was evaluated using a microtitre plate assay with modifications to the method described by Kim et al. (2022). Confirmed S. aureus strains were incubated in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Merck, Germany) for 24 h at 37°C. Cultures were then diluted 1:100 in fresh TSB, and 150 µL aliquots were added to wells of a 96‐well microtitre plate and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. Wells were emptied, washed three times with 200 µL phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and dried at 65°C for 1 h. Biofilms were fixed with 100 µL of 99% methanol for 15 min, stained with 100 µL of 2% crystal violet for 20 min and washed with water. Bound dye was solubilized with 150 µL of 33% acetic acid, and optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm.

Strains were classified into non‐biofilm‐forming, weak, moderate or strong categories based on OD values. OD values were categorized using standard criteria: non‐biofilm: OD ≤ ODc; weak: ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc; moderate: 2 × ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc; strong: OD > 4 × ODc, where ODc is the mean OD of negative controls plus three standard deviations. Isolates with OD values above three standard deviations added to the mean OD of negative controls were considered biofilm‐positive. One strong biofilm‐positive strain and one biofilm‐negative strain with similar antibiotic susceptibility patterns were chosen for further experimentation.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing and Treatment Selection

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using disc diffusion method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI 2020). The antibiotics tested included ampicillin (10 µg), enrofloxacin (5 µg), tylosin (30 µg), penicillin (10 IU), tetracycline (30 µg), lincomycin (2 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (25 µg), oxacillin (1 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), methicillin (5 µg), vancomycin (30 µg) and cefazolin (30 µg) (PadtanTeb, Iran). S. aureus ATCC 29213 was used as the control strain.

On the basis of the susceptibility results, both selected strains (biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative) showed susceptibility to Ceftiofur. Ceftiofur was selected for treatment for the following reasons:

Animals

Nine healthy female goats (native Iranian breed, average body weight 32 ± 6.2 kg, aged 1–5 years) in early‐ to mid‐lactation were used. Animals underwent veterinary clinical examination, and health certificates were issued. Udder examination revealed no signs of subclinical mastitis (pain, swelling, heat, redness, or abnormal secretion). The absence of pre‐existing mammary infection was confirmed by negative bacteriological culture of two milk samples collected 1 week apart.

Initially, 10 goats (n = 5 per group) were enrolled in the study. However, one goat in the biofilm‐negative group developed unexpectedly severe acute mastitis with systemic signs, requiring intensive supportive therapy (fluid and anti‐inflammatory treatment). In accordance with our ethical protocol and to avoid confounding effects of additional therapeutic interventions, this animal was excluded from the final analysis, resulting in unbalanced groups (biofilm‐positive: n = 5; biofilm‐negative: n = 4).

Intramammary Infection

Prior to challenge, goats were hand‐milked and teat ends disinfected with chlorhexidine (Ramyar Shimi, Iran). The left udder half of each goat was infused intracisternally via the teat canal with 1 mL of inoculum containing 103 CFU/mL of either biofilm‐positive (n = 5 goats) or biofilm‐negative (n = 4 goats) S. aureus, using a sterile blunt insulin needle.

Treatment

Animals were considered infected 48 h after challenge if they were milk culture positive with an associated increase in SCC (>500,000 cells/mL). As per antibiotic susceptibility testing and discussion above, Ceftiofur (Afrindaru, Iran) was chosen for treatment. Infected animals were injected intracisternal daily with Ceftiofur 125 mg once daily for 3 days.

Health Monitoring and Animal Welfare

Animals were monitored twice daily for clinical signs of mastitis (udder swelling, heat, pain and abnormal milk secretion), systemic illness (fever, anorexia and depression) and overall well‐being throughout the study. Rectal temperatures were recorded once daily. Clinical signs of mastitis and overall well‐being were assessed using a standardized numerical scoring system. After treatment, animals were observed for resolution of clinical signs and normalization of milk production. No animals required premature withdrawal or administration of additional supportive care.

Sampling

Milk samples were collected aseptically from the challenged udder half immediately before inoculation (T0), at 6 (T1), 12 (T2), 24 (T3), 36 (T4), 48 (T5) and 72 (T6) hours post‐challenge, immediately prior to treatment (T7), and at 1 (T8), 7 (T9) and 14 (T10) days post‐treatment.

Before collection, teats were disinfected with 20% benzalkonium chloride solution (Rooyan Darou, Iran), dried with disposable paper towels and wiped with 70% (v/v) ethanol‐soaked cotton. The first 4–5 squirts were discarded, and approximately 15 mL of milk was collected into sterile tubes. Samples were transported on ice at 4°C and processed immediately for analysis.

Measurement of Trace Elements

Milk samples were analysed for copper, calcium, selenium, zinc, magnesium, potassium, sodium and phosphorus using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES; Spectro Arcos, Germany). Briefly, 15 mL milk was mixed with 3 mL nitric acid and 2 mL hydrogen peroxide. Samples were digested in a 95°C water bath for 2 h, filtered, adjusted to 14 mL with deionized water and injected into the ICP‐OES instrument. Operating parameters are presented in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 17. The primary analysis focused on comparing the two independent groups (biofilm‐positive vs. biofilm‐negative) at each time point. For this purpose, an independent samples t‐test was used to compare the mean concentrations of each trace element between the two groups at each sampling time (T0–T10). As a secondary analysis, the Friedman test was employed to assess the overall trend of changes over time within each group, followed by the Wilcoxon test to compare each time point with the baseline (T0) within each group. A Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical Observations

Following intramammary challenge, goats in the biofilm‐positive group developed more pronounced clinical signs compared to the biofilm‐negative group. All goats developed udder swelling and heat within 24–48 h post‐challenge. However, the biofilm‐positive group showed more severe inflammation, with 3/5 goats developing detectable systemic signs (mild fever, reduced appetite) compared to 1/4 goats in the biofilm‐negative group. After Ceftiofur treatment, clinical signs resolved within 3–5 days in both groups, and milk cultures became negative by Day 7 post‐treatment. All animals recovered fully without long‐term sequelae.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that experimental intramammary infection with S. aureus induced significant changes in goat milk trace element concentrations, with biofilm‐forming ability exerting a notable influence on the magnitude and pattern of these alterations. Biofilm production is a key virulence factor in S. aureus, and previous work has shown that a high proportion of isolates from goat milk possess this capability, which has implications for both pathogenicity and public health (Lira et al. 2016). Modern analytical techniques, such as ICP‐OES and ICP‐MS, offer high sensitivity and accuracy, facilitating precise monitoring of trace elements as indicators of mammary gland health and animal welfare during mastitis (Chen et al. 2020).

The observed differences in clinical severity between biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative groups are consistent with the known virulence characteristics of biofilm‐forming strains. Biofilm formation enhances bacterial resistance to host immune defences and antimicrobial therapies causing more severe clinical infections (Vargová et al. 2023; Conlon 2014). Greater clinical scores were likely observed in the biofilm positive group due to greater tissue damage caused by inflammatory responses during biofilm formation.

Selenium is an important antioxidant in damaged tissues (Blanco‐Penedo et al. 2014), and lower blood selenium concentrations have been reported in cows with subclinical or clinical mastitis compared with healthy animals (Ranjan et al. 2005). In the current study, milk selenium concentrations were significantly higher in the biofilm‐positive group at 36 h post‐inoculation and 14 days post‐treatment (p < 0.05). Selenium supports both specific (humoural and cellular) and non‐specific immune functions (Sordillo 2013; Wang et al. 2021). The elevated secretion of selenium into milk during infection with biofilm‐positive strains may potentially reduce its availability to the host immune system; however, this hypothesis requires confirmation through the measurement of selenium levels in blood, mammary tissue, and immune cells. Without such measurements, this remains speculative. Nonetheless, this observation raises the possibility that compromised local defences may contribute to the persistence of mastitis associated with biofilm‐forming strains.

Milk calcium concentrations were significantly elevated in the biofilm‐positive group at 12 and 36 h post‐inoculation (p < 0.05). Similar increases in milk calcium (and selenium) have been observed in human mastitis (Li et al. 2018) and in cows with clinical or subclinical mastitis (Mahran et al. 1992; Kotur et al. 2014). These elevations are consistent with disrupted tight junctions and influx of polymorphonuclear neutrophils into the infected gland (Smith et al. 1997; Lönnerdal 2000). Although calcium may promote lactobacilli growth and tight junction formation in the mammary environment (Li et al. 2018), the higher concentrations observed here in the biofilm‐positive group suggest greater epithelial permeability and barrier dysfunction induced by biofilm‐forming S. aureus.

Potassium and phosphorus concentrations prior to treatment were also significantly higher in the biofilm‐positive group (p < 0.05), which is consistent with degradation of ductal secretory cells and epithelium in response to biofilm‐associated infection. In contrast, sodium and magnesium concentrations were initially higher in the biofilm‐negative group at 6 h post‐inoculation (p < 0.05), but differences disappeared thereafter due to gradual increases in the biofilm‐positive group. These patterns suggest that biofilm‐positive strains ultimately provoke greater release of sodium and magnesium into milk. Reduced blood magnesium has been documented in cows with subclinical mastitis, consistent with increased secretion into milk (Siddiqe et al. 2015). Elevated milk sodium due to S. aureus‐induced mammary cell damage correlates with somatic cell count in subclinical cases (Arney and Phillips 2005; Singh et al. 2017). Electrolyte shifts help maintain milk osmolality during mastitis and serve as useful diagnostic markers (Singh et al. 2017). The electrolyte profiles observed here are consistent with potentially more severe mammary cell damage in goats infected with biofilm‐positive S. aureus.

Previous studies have reported increased milk zinc in Gram‐positive subclinical mastitis (Guha et al. 2012), with corresponding decreases in blood zinc attributed to secretion into milk (Naresh et al. 2001). Breaches in epithelial integrity and blood–milk barrier permeability are primary drivers of elevated iron, zinc and cobalt in mastitis milk (Gera et al. 2011; Zhao and Lacasse 2008). However, no significant differences in zinc or copper concentrations were detected between biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative groups in the present study.

Why Ceftiofur was chosen for treatment should be discussed. Antibiotic susceptibility tests revealed that both strains were susceptible to Ceftiofur. Previous research has shown that biofilm‐positive S. aureus isolates from bovine mastitis exhibited different antibiotic resistance patterns, with significantly higher resistance to penicillin (77.1% vs. 58.5%), lincomycin (68.6% vs. 36.9%), oxacillin (65.7% vs. 29.2%), methicillin (65.7% vs. 36.9%) and vancomycin (62.9% vs. 38.5%) compared to biofilm‐negative isolates (Ghari et al. 2022). However, enrofloxacin and cefazolin showed the highest efficacy against both biofilm‐positive and biofilm‐negative isolates (Ghari et al. 2022). This demonstrates the importance of antibiotic susceptibility testing to ensure successful treatment of biofilm‐associated infections.

Health Impact of Goats Before and After Antibiotic Use

Prior to challenge, goats were clinically healthy with normal udder appearance, negative milk cultures and no signs of systemic illness. Following intramammary challenge with S. aureus, goats developed local inflammation characterized by udder swelling, heat and pain, with the biofilm‐positive group showing more pronounced clinical signs. The biofilm‐positive group also showed higher somatic cell counts consistent with increased inflammation.

What the results show about infection resolution in both groups validates the choice of Ceftiofur for treatment. As goats infected with the biofilm‐positive strain had more severe clinical signs and trace element alterations that were greater than what was seen in the biofilm‐negative group, infections with biofilm‐positive bacteria may require closer monitoring or extended therapy to ensure the prevention of recurrence.

Study limitations include the small and unbalanced sample size, lack of correction for multiple comparisons, the absence of blood trace element measurements and use of bovine‐derived S. aureus strains. Therefore, our results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, requiring validation in future larger‐scale studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, intramammary infection with biofilm‐positive strains of S. aureus induced greater secretion of key trace elements—particularly selenium, calcium, potassium and phosphorus—into goat milk compared with biofilm‐negative strains. These results support the idea that biofilm positive strains lead to more severe damage to mammary epithelial cells and disruption of epithelial barrier function due to biofilm formation capabilities and likely their overall increased virulence and antibiotic resistance mechanisms (Ghari et al. 2022).

These alterations may reflect increased mammary epithelial permeability and could potentially impair host immune defences by reducing local availability of essential elements, thereby contributing to the chronic and persistent nature of mastitis caused by biofilm‐forming strains. The more severe clinical signs and greater trace element alterations in the biofilm‐positive group support this interpretation.

This mechanism remains to be directly tested in future work that would assess trace element concentrations in blood, markers of oxidative stress and mammary permeability markers. Applied implications of this study include using milk trace element profiling to guide management decisions regarding non‐invasive diagnosis of biofilm‐associated mastitis and targeted therapy use within dairy goat operations. Results that biofilm positive strains exhibited different antibiotic sensitivities and induce more pronounced changes in trace elements highlight the importance of targeted mastitis treatment.
