Letter to the Editor: Commentary on “Dietary fat and risk of inflammatory bowel disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis”
Authors: Yiwen Wang, Peihong Li, Yikun Zhang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Zhang et al, which examined the association between dietary fat intake and the onset of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)[1]. Their synthesis of multiple observational cohorts provides timely quantitative evidence regarding fat-related susceptibility, and the authors are to be commended for evaluating dose–response relationships across different fatty acid subclasses. This work makes a meaningful contribution to ongoing discussions about modifiable dietary determinants in IBD prevention.

Despite these strengths, several methodological considerations merit further reflection to improve the interpretability and translational relevance of the findings.

First, modeling dietary fat intake as a single isolated exposure does not account for the multicollinearity inherent within dietary patterns. High-fat intake rarely occurs independently and often coexists with lower fiber intake, higher free sugar consumption, and increased intake of ultra-processed foods. These components interact biologically through microbiota-mediated inflammatory pathways and may represent the actual drivers of disease risk[2]. Without incorporating broader dietary structures, pooled estimates may inadvertently over-attribute risk to fat itself rather than to the composite dietary patterns in which fat is embedded. Integrating dietary pattern approaches, such as principal component analysis, Healthy Eating Index scoring, or multi-exposure modeling, would allow future analyses to more clearly distinguish fat-specific associations from overall dietary practices.

Second, the exclusive reliance on food frequency questionnaires introduces substantial measurement error that was not assessed within sensitivity or heterogeneity analyses. Food frequency questionnaires vary widely across regions in structure, item granularity, and cultural food representation, and are known to systematically underestimate fat intake[3]. Such misclassification may differ between populations and contribute to deceptively low heterogeneity estimates. Failure to account for these biases limits the precision and validity of the dose–response findings. Application of calibration equations, measurement-error-adjusted modeling, or weighting schemes acknowledging high-bias instruments would strengthen future meta-analytic frameworks.

Third, interpretation of subgroup results among Asian, male, and younger participants requires careful contextualization. These groups exhibited higher relative risks, yet the analyses did not incorporate baseline disease incidence, fat-intake distribution, or distinct dietary structures. Without accounting for these background factors, the observed associations may reflect underlying demographic or epidemiological characteristics rather than true biological susceptibility. Approaches such as marginal structural models, baseline-risk-adjusted subgroup analysis, or estimation of absolute risk differences could more accurately identify whether these variations represent effect modification or residual confounding.

In summary, the review by Zhang et al provides valuable quantitative evidence linking dietary fat intake to IBD onset. Addressing the interdependence of dietary exposures, incorporating strategies to mitigate nutritional measurement error, and refining subgroup analyses through risk-adjusted modeling would offer greater clarity regarding fat-specific effects and support more targeted nutritional recommendations for IBD prevention.
