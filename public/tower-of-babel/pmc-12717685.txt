Weekend warriors, physical activity pattern and all-cause mortality: a prospective study of 150,145 Mexican adults with and without hypertension
Authors: Weder Alves da Silva, Diego G. D. Christofaro, Pablo del Val Martín, Adilson Marques, Miguel Peralta, Leandro F. M. Rezende, Eduardo Rossato de Victo, Gerson Ferrari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is associated with higher risk of premature all-cause mortality. Regular physical activity has been associated with reduced risk of all-cause mortality. In this study, we aimed to examine the association between physical activity patterns and all-cause mortality in adults with and without hypertension.MethodsWe included 150,145 participants (31,228 with hypertension) from the Mexico City Prospective Study. Cox regression model was performed to estimate the associations of frequency (no sport or exercise, weekend warrior, and regularly active) and duration (<30; or ≥30 min/day of sport or exercise) of physical activity with all-cause mortality in adults with and without hypertension. ResultsOver 15.6 years of follow-up, 25,295 deaths occurred, with 8,957 among adults with hypertension. Compared to those engaging in no sport or exercise, both weekend warriors (HR: 0.65, 95% CI 0.48–0.87 for adults without hypertension; HR: 0.70, 95% CI 0.50–0.91 for adults with hypertension) and those regularly active (HR: 0.79, 95% CI 0.59–0.99 for adults without hypertension; HR: 0.85, 95% CI 0.73–0.97 for adults with hypertension) had lower all-cause mortality. Engaging in sport or exercise sessions lasting ≥ 30 min/day showed a stronger associationwith lower all-cause mortality both in adults with and without hypertension. Both weekend warrior and regularly active individuals exhibited lower all-cause mortality risk estimates among those without hypertension compared with those with hypertension.ConclusionsWeekend warriors and regularly active physical activity patterns were associated with lower all-cause mortality in adults without and with hypertension, particularly when session duration was ≥30 min/day.

Background

Hypertension is the leading cause of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality worldwide [1], being responsible for, approximately, 8.5 to 10.4 million deaths annually [2, 3]. It is estimated that one in three adults has been diagnosed with hypertension, with higher prevalence in low- and middle-income countries [4]. In Mexico, for instance, the prevalence of hypertension exceeds 49% of the adult population [5].

The aetiology of hypertension is multifactorial, involving genetic and age-related factors, and is heavily influenced by behavioural aspects such as a sedentary lifestyle, unhealthy diet, obesity, and stress [6–9]. Approximately, 7% of all deaths from cardiovascular diseases are attributable to physical inactivity, with a higher impact in lower-income countries [10]. Physical inactivity has been associated with a 30% higher risk of mortality compared to those who maintain regular physical activity [11]. This increased risk is particularly notable among individuals with pre-existing chronic diseases who engage in < 30 min/week of physical activity [12].

Regular physical activity is widely recognized as a key preventive and therapeutic strategy for hypertension [13]. Physiological adaptations include reduced vascular and arterial resistance, improved endothelial function, decreased oxidative stress, enhanced renal function, weight loss, greater baroreflex sensitivity, reduced sympathetic activity, increased parasympathetic activity, and lower renin–angiotensin system activity [14, 15]. Together, these adaptations contribute to blood pressure regulation, producing both hypotensive and preventive effects [14, 15]. Importantly, the benefits of regular physical activity are comparable to those achieved with pharmacological antihypertensive agents [16]. In addition, long-term engagement in physical activity can reduce the need for medication [17] and confer health benefits even at low frequencies [18].

Given these effects, the protective association of physical activity with mortality may differ between individuals with and without hypertension. Hypertension can influence cardiovascular responses to exercise, including heart rate, blood pressure regulation, and vascular function, potentially modifying the physiological benefits of physical activity [19]. Studies have shown that individuals with hypertension often exhibit exaggerated blood pressure responses during exercise compared to their without hypertension counterparts, which may affect the overall cardiovascular benefits of physical activity [20–23]. This provides a rationale for considering hypertension as a potential effect modifier in the relationship between physical activity and mortality.

Hypertension may also affect an individual’s ability to engage in physical activity. Elevated blood pressure increases cardiovascular workload, particularly when hypertension is uncontrolled or poorly managed [22]. While guidelines from the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology recommend regular physical activity for all adults with hypertension, including moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, they note that exercise prescriptions should be individualized based on blood pressure control and comorbidities [24]. This may lead to avoidance of high-intensity or strenuous activity among some individuals with hypertension, potentially altering the overall activity patterns.

Nevertheless, even irregular or infrequent physical activity may confer substantial health benefits. The weekend warrior pattern—where physical activity is concentrated on weekends or performed intermittently—has been associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with inactivity [25, 26]. Evidence also suggests that weekend warriors and regularly active individuals experience similar mortality risks when engaging in equivalent total volumes of physical activity [27]. However, studies specifically examining the impact of the weekend warrior pattern on mortality among hypertensive versus non-hypertensive populations remain limited.

To date, few large-scale, population-based investigations—and none, to our knowledge, in Latin America—have examined whether hypertension status modifies the association between physical activity patterns, including the weekend warrior pattern, and all-cause mortality. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for tailoring physical activity recommendations to high-risk groups and informing public health strategies in regions with distinct epidemiological and sociodemographic contexts, such as Mexico. In the present study, we used data from 150,145 Mexican adults (31,228 with hypertension and 118,917 without) to examine the association between physical activity patterns (no sport or exercise, weekend warrior, and regularly active) and all-cause mortality, and to evaluate whether hypertension modifies this relationship.

Methods

Study design and sample

The Mexico City Prospective Study (MCPS) was conducted in two contiguous urban districts of Mexico City: Coyoacán, located in the central area, and Iztapalapa, in the northeastern part of the city. These districts include a socioeconomically diverse population comprising both long-term residents and more recent migrants from northern and southern regions of Mexico [28]. Recruitment teams then systematically visited households, and at least one participant aged 35 years or older was recruited from 94% of eligible households. The resulting sample was deemed to be broadly representative of the population aged 35 years or older in Mexico City. Prior to recruitment, a comprehensive household record was compiled in 1995–1997 using census-style door-to-door interviews, documenting the names and ages of household members, as well as maps indicating stairs and entrances. Recruitment teams, each consisting of two to three trained nurses, systematically visited households using this record. Trained nurses collected data in the participant’s household and the baseline survey took place from 1998 to 2004. Information obtained included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviours, medical history, and medication use. During the recruitment period, residents of 112,333 eligible households were invited to participate. One or more individuals from 106,059 households provided informed consent, resulting in the enrolment of more than 150,000 middle-aged adults in the study [28].

The study was approved by the Mexican Ministry of Health, the Mexican National Council of Science and Technology, and the University of Oxford in the United Kingdom. Data used in this report were obtained through an open-access data request to the director of the MCPS. Following ethical guidelines, all participants provided written informed consent [28].

Our analysis included 150,145 of the 159,517 participants (age ≥ 35 years) from the MCPS. Participants were excluded due to missing data on physical activity patterns (frequency and duration), health conditions (including hypertension status), sociodemographic variables, or lifestyle risk factors.

Physical activity

The exposure was physical activity pattern at baseline [25]. While the terms “sport” and “exercise” may carry negative connotations in some Western contexts, in Latin America asking about participation in sport is a culturally neutral and common question, similar to asking about musical preferences [29]. Participants were asked whether, in a typical week, they exercised or played sports (yes or no). Those who answered yes were asked how many times a week they exercised (< 1, 1–2, or ≥ 3 times/week) and the duration of each physical activity session (< 30, 30–60, ≥ 30 min/day). For analysis, the duration of each physical activity session was categorized as 30 or ≥ 30 min/day. Participants who reported no to the question about whether they participated in exercised or played sports were classified into the “no sport or exercise” group [25, 30]. Participants who reported that they carried out a frequency of physical activity between once or twice a week were classified as weekend warrior. Finally, participants who reported that they carried out a frequency of physical activity greater ≥ 3 times a week were classified as “regularly active”. The questionnaire was administered at the start of the study, between April 1998 and December 2004, and responses were automatically uploaded to the study’s electronic database.

Consistent with previous large-scale epidemiological studies [25, 26, 30], our classification of weekend warriors was based on frequency and session of physical activity (30 or ≥ 30 min/day). This operational definition was used because the dataset did not allow precise estimation of total moderate-to-vgorous physical activity min/week for each individual. Consequently, our weekend warrior category may not fully overlap with the traditional definition, which combines infrequent sessions with sufficient total weekly physical activity volume.

Hypertension assessment

Participants were asked at baseline whether they had ever been diagnosed with hypertension (baseline disease history: hypertension, yes or no). Before blood pressure assessment, participants were instructed to remain seated for 5 min. Blood pressure was then measured three times, with 3-minute intervals between each measurement, while participants remained seated. A standard mercury sphygmomanometer, accurate to 1 mmHg, was used for these measurements. The mean of the second and third readings was used for the analysis, as the first measurement was considered less stable. Participants were subsequently classified according to their hypertensive status (no hypertension or hypertension).

Hypertension was defined based on a physician’s diagnosis or an average systolic blood pressure of ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 80 mmHg, according to the classification established by the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and the American Heart Association (AHA) [31]. Additionally, participants were also considered hypertensive if they reported using any of the following antihypertensive medications: alpha-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin-II receptor blockers, other venodilators, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, centrally acting agents, other diuretics, thiazide diuretics, loop diuretics, or potassium-sparing diuretics.

Mortality ascertainment

Participants were followed for cause-specific mortality through probabilistic linkage using names (including phonetic coding), age, and sex (place of birth information was not available) to the Mexican System for Epidemiologic Death Statistics (Subsistema Epidemiológico y Estadístico de Defunciones, SEED), an electronic death registry managed by the Ministry of Health in Mexico City. All-cause mortality was ascertained through this registry [32] nd monitored until January 1, 2021. Causes of death recorded on certificates were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10).

Personal identifiers, including names, dates of birth and death, and addresses, were recorded in a Ministry of Health database created for this study and in other official databases. Investigators at the Ministry of Health had secure, confidential access to this database and used these identifiers to automatically link death records to participants, usually within two to three months of death. Paper copies of death certificates were obtained for central review, particularly for deaths attributed to diabetes, ischemic heart disease, or stroke, and households were revisited when necessary to verify vital status.

Participants who emigrated abroad were likely lost to follow-up, although this was estimated to affect less than 1% of participants annually. Minor losses could also occur if participants moved outside Mexico City to areas where death certification was incomplete. Nonetheless, even in rural regions, approximately 98% of adult deaths are physician-certified and are therefore reliably captured by the registry [28].

Confounders

Trained nurses implemented the baseline questionnaire. Confounders that may influence the relationships between physical activity pattern, hypertension and mortality include sex, age, occupation, education and income level, diet quality, alcohol consumption, smoking status, body mass index, total volume of physical activity, comorbidities, and medication used [33, 34]. The selection of covariates was based on the justification of prior knowledge of potential confounders [25].

Information on sex, age (both as a continuous variable and categorized as < 65 and ≥ 65 years), occupation (categorized as workers, students, or housework/unemployed), educational level (none/primary, secondary, or professional), and income level (low, middle, or high) were assessed. Diet quality was evaluated based on the frequency of fruit and vegetable consumption, classified into four categories: never, 1–2 days/week, 3–4 days/week, and ≥ 5 days/week. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current. Participants also reported their frequency of alcohol consumption, which was categorized as never, former, current, occasional, or at least once per week. Weight (in kilograms) and height (in meters squared) were measured to calculate body mass index (BMI), classified according to World Health Organization criteria for adults: underweight/normal weight (< 25 kg/m²) and overweight/obesity (≥ 25.0 kg/m²). We also calculated the total volume of physical activity by combining information on frequency and duration. This approach overlooks important dimensions of total volume of physical activity behavior, such as frequency and duration, which are well-established factors influencing mortality risks [27, 35]. Adjusting for total volume of physical activity may allow us to understand whether, for the same amount of total volume of physical activity, high frequency and high duration of total volume of physical activity may actually offer additional health benefits compared with low frequency and low duration of total volume of physical activity. Participants also reported the primary types of medication used, if applicable, to manage and control blood pressure, blood glucose, and lipid levels, with at least one medication required for consideration. To create a comorbidities score, we used self-reported data on medical diagnoses of 16 diseases (emphysema, heart attack, angina, asthma, stroke, chronic kidney insufficiency, peptic ulcer, cirrhosis, diabetes, some types of cancer and peripheral arterial disease).

Statistical analyses

Firstly, each participant was categorized based on their physical activity frequency (no sport or exercise, weekend warrior, and regularly active) and hypertension status (without hypertension or hypertension). Subsequently, participants were further categorized by the duration of each physical activity session, weekly frequency, and hypertension status (without hypertension or hypertension): i) < 30 min/day of sport or exercise (no sport or exercise, weekend warrior, and regularly active); ii) ≥ 30 min/day of sport or exercise (no sport or exercise, weekend warrior, and regularly active). Chi-square tests were used to examine associations between categorical variables, including comparisons of individuals with and without hypertension, as well as differences in physical activity patterns between these groups.

Participants were followed from baseline (April 1998 to September 2004) until either the date of death or the end of the follow-up period, whichever occurred first. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were performed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) for the association of physical activity patterns with all-cause mortality in adults with and without hypertension [36]. The following adjustment models were built: (i) age-sex-adjusted and (ii) multivariable model adjusted for sociodemographic variables (sex, age, occupational status, education, income), fruit and vegetable consumption, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, total volume of physical activity, comorbidities score and type of medication for adults with hypertension [25, 26]. We analyzed both the additive and multiplicative interactions between the physical activity and hypertension with all- cause mortality [37, 38]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A significance level of P < 0.05 was used for all tests.

Results

Participants enrolled in our study were followed from an average 15.6 years (interquartile range: 14.3–18.3 years), during which 25,295 deaths were recorded. Table 1 presents participant characteristics based on physical activity patterns (no sport or exercise, weekend warrior, and regularly active) and hypertension status (with and without hypertension). The sample predominantly comprised women, constituting 68.7% of the total sample. Adults accounted for the majority at 82%, with half (50.8%) engaged in domestic work or unemployed. A significant proportion had attained secondary education (57.2%), and a majority reported low income (67.5%). Regarding health behaviors, 54.2% of participants consumed fruits and vegetables on ≥ 5 days per week, 49.5% were non-smokers, and 60% reported occasional alcohol consumption. Furthermore, 79% of participants had overweight/obesity and 36% had morbidity at baseline.

Table 4 displays the associations between physical activity patterns and all-cause mortality in adults with and without hypertension. Both among adults without hypertension (HR: 0.65, 95%CI: 0.48–0.87) and those with hypertenson (HR: 0.70, 95%CI: 0.50–0.91) weekend warrior pattern of physical activity was associated with lower all-cause mortality compared to those not engaging in sports or exercise. Regularly active pattern showed significant reductions in all-cause mortality risk among adults without hypertension (HR: 0.79, 95%CI: 0.59–0.99) and with hypertension (HR: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.73–0.97). Moreover, both weekend warrior and regularly active individuals exhibited lower all-cause mortality risk estimates among those without hypertension compared with those with hypertension. We observed significant additive and/or multiplicative interactions between physical activity and hypertension in relation to all-cause mortality.

The duration of physical activity sessions under 30 min/day did not yield protective benefits; mortality risks were not significantly reduced among both weekend warriors (HR: 0.73, 95%CI: 0.35–1.45 among adults without hypertension and HR: 1.70, 95%CI: 0.91–3.12 among adults with hypertension) and those regularly active (HR: 1.14, 95%CI: 0.84–1.53 among adults without hypertension and HR: 1.07, 95%CI: 0.75–1.51 among adults with hypertension). However, when physical activity sessions lasted ≥ 30 min/day, both weekend warriors and regularly active adults showed stronger association with lower all-cause mortality. Weekend warriors with sessions ≥ 30 min/day had reduced mortality in participants without hypertension (HR: 0.71, 95%CI: 0.55–0.87) and with hypertension (HR: 0.78, 95%CI: 0.59–0.98). Similarly, regularly active individuals with session durations ≥ 30 min/day had lower all-cause mortality in adults without (HR: 0.77, 95%CI: 0.60–0.93) and with hypertension (HR: 0.82, 95%CI: 0.68–0.97) with evidence of both additive and multiplicative interactions. Moreover, both weekend warrior and regularly active individuals engaging in sessions ≥ 30 min/day exhibited lower all-cause mortality risk estimates among those without hypertension compared with hypertension individuals (Table 5).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that both weekend warrior and regularly active physical activity patterns are associated with lower all-cause mortality among adults with and without hypertension. Notably, we observed significant additive and multiplicative interactions between physical activity and hypertension, suggesting that the magnitude of the protective association may differ slightly by hypertension status. Specifically, weekend warriors without hypertension had a HR of 0.65 (95%CI: 0.48–0.87), compared to 0.70 (95%CI: 0.50–0.91) in those with hypertension. Regularly active adults with hypertension had an HR of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.73–0.97), versus 0.79 (95% CI: 0.59–0.99) in those without hypertension. These results indicate that both individuals with and without hypertension benefit from physical activity, although the relative reduction in mortality appears to be slightly greater among adults without hypertension.

The findings suggest that weekend warriors can also benefit from irregular patterns of physical activity. This aligns with previous studies showing that both low and high levels of leisure-time physical activity reduce the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality compared to physical inactivity [25]. Another study further supports these benefits, demonstrating significant reductions in mortality risk among insufficiently active individuals and weekend warriors [25]. Recently, O’Donovan et al., [25] reported a 15% lower risk of all-cause mortality among weekend warriors who exercised once or twice weekly, comparable to the reduced mortality risk observed in regularly active individuals who exercised three or more times per week, which is consistent with our findings. Our study highlights the beneficial outcomes associated with both weekend warrior and regularly active physical activity patterns. Additionally, session duration influenced outcomes only when participants engaged in ≥ 30 min/day of sport or exercise.

In this context, Inoue et al., [39] reported that even sporadically meeting recommended daily step counts significantly lowers mortality risk for individuals who do not consistently reach daily activity targets. Similarly, Shiroma et al. [40] demonstrated that weekend warriors who concentrated their weekly physical activity into one or two days achieved mortality risk reductions comparable to those who exercised more frequently. These findings suggest that even low levels of physical activity may provide significant health benefits compared to no physical activity at all [41].

Biologically, hypertension is associated with altered cardiovascular responses to exercise, including elevated blood pressure, increased arterial stiffness, and impaired endothelial function [42]. These changes may influence the capacity to perform physical activity safely and effectively, potentially modifying its benefits [43]. Our results suggest that even adults with hypertension can achieve reductions in mortality risk through irregular but sufficiently long bouts of physical activity, such as the weekend warrior pattern, highlighting the flexibility of effective exercise strategies for this population.

The duration of physical activity sessions further modified these associations. Sessions lasting less than 30 min/day did not confer significant mortality reductions in adults with and without hypertension. In contrast, sessions of ≥ 30 min/day were associated with stronger protective effects in both groups. Weekend warriors with sessions ≥ 30 min/day had reduced mortality in participants without hypertension (HR: 0.71, 95%CI: 0.55–0.87) and with hypertension (HR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.59–0.98). Similarly, regularly active individuals with sessions ≥ 30 min/day had lower mortality in adults without (HR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.60–0.93) and with hypertension (HR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.68–0.97). Overall, the risk of all-cause mortality was lower among individuals without hypertension compared with those with hypertension.

These results align with previous evidence indicating that moderate-intensity physical activity is particularly effective in lowering all-cause mortality among adults with hypertension [44]. Current guidelines recommend achieving at least 30 min of physical activity on most days of the week [45], and higher volumes of activity have been linked to greater mortality reductions, independent of blood pressure [37]. Exceeding traditional activity recommendations further enhances cardiovascular protection and lowers all-cause mortality [46]. Collectively, these findings highlight that both the frequency and duration of physical activity sessions are critical for maximizing mortality benefits, with clear clinical implications for hypertensive populations.

This underscores the importance for adults with hypertension to closely adhere to physical activity guidelines concerning both duration and frequency [47, 48]. Consistent daily activity may provide greater benefits for this group, as irregular patterns could reduce health advantages [15, 49]. However, despite recommendations favoring regular activity, our study found that irregular patterns, such as the weekend warrior approach, offer benefits comparable to more systematic, regular activity. In terms of reducing adverse health risks, Gubelmann et al. [50] demonstrated that both weekend warrior and regularly active patterns significantly lower the risk of cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension (HR: 0.62, 95%CI: 0.47–0.81 and HR: 0.70, 95%CI: 0.56–0.88, respectively). Similarly, Kany et al. [51] showed that whether physical activity follows a weekend warrior pattern or is evenly distributed throughout the week, as long as it exceeds 230 min/week, it yields similar reductions in the risk of adverse health outcomes, such as hypertension. It’s worth noting that these studies [50, 51] used objective measures (accelerometers) to assess physical activity levels, which allows for precise intensity evaluation, unlike our study. Additionally, while our outcomes assessed mortality, other studies [36–38] used objective measures (accelerometers) to assess physical activity levels, allowing precise evaluation of activity intensity, unlike our study. Additionally, while our outcomes focused on mortality, other studies [50–52] assessed disease risks. Activities involving prolonged duration and large muscle groups may optimize these benefits, though our study did not specifically evaluate these factors [53, 54]. Supporting this premise, various studies have shown that irregular doses of physical activity can similarly reduce adverse health risks, akin to regular activity patterns [50, 51].

This study makes a valuable contribution to the scientific literature by providing robust, long-term evidence on the relationship between different physical activity patterns and all-cause mortality in adults stratified by hypertension status. While previous research has established the general benefits of regular physical activity [55, 56], few studies have specifically examined the weekend warrior pattern in hypertensive individuals using a large prospective cohort with extensive follow-up. This expands the current understanding of how flexible physical activity routines can still yield substantial health benefits, particularly for individuals with chronic conditions like hypertension who may face barriers to engaging in daily exercise.

Clinically and from a public health perspective, our results emphasize that adults with hypertension should be encouraged to achieve recommended weekly activity volumes, even if concentrated into fewer days, as in the weekend warrior pattern [57]. This is relevant for individuals facing barriers to daily exercise due to work, comorbidities, or lifestyle constraints. The significant interaction between hypertension and physical activity suggests that promoting structured, sufficiently long exercise sessions may yield relatively greater benefits for adults with hypertension, supporting targeted strategies for populations at elevated cardiovascular risk. Finally, our study adds nuance to existing literature by providing robust evidence on how physical activity frequency and duration interact with hypertension status to influence mortality. While both hypertensive and normotensive adults benefit from physical activity, hypertensive adults may experience slightly enhanced protective effects, particularly with the weekend warrior pattern [44, 58]. These findings reinforce the principle that some activity is better than none, and they highlight the flexibility in exercise prescriptions that can still achieve meaningful health benefits for high-risk populations.

This study has several strengths as well as some limitations. The Mexico City Prospective Study is broadly representative of the population aged ≥ 35 years, with a large sample size of over 150,000 adults and a high response rate, including at least one participant from more than 94% of eligible households [28]. However, neither the original survey nor the re-survey were stratified by demographic parameters such as age, sex, or socioeconomic status, which could affect representativeness. Physical activity data were self-reported and lacked detailed information on exercise type, intensity, or total weekly volume in minutes. Furthermore, it is not possible to confirm whether all physically active participants were able to walk independently. While questionnaires were used to assess physical activity, it is generally preferable to combine questionnaires with accelerometers in cohort studies, as each method has unique advantages: questionnaires can capture specific types of sport and exercise, whereas accelerometers are better suited for measuring total activity volume [59]. We also acknowledge the limitation of measuring physical activity at a single time point. In our study, the weekend warrior pattern was defined based on frequency (1–2 days/week), consistent with several prior population-based studies [25, 26, 30]. The traditional concept of a weekend warrior typically refers to individuals who achieve the recommended total volume of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity despite infrequent sessions. Our operationalization was limited by the available data, which precluded precise quantification of total weekly minutes. This should be considered when interpreting our results, as the weekend warrior group may include individuals who do not meet the 150 min/week threshold, as well as those who exceed it. Nevertheless, frequency-based classifications have previously demonstrated meaningful associations with health outcomes, indicating that our approach remains relevant for public health surveillance and epidemiological analyses. Moreover, such frequency-based definitions are commonly used in large-scale epidemiological studies, especially those with long-term follow-up [25]. Although physical activity behaviors may change over time, baseline activity levels still provide valuable insights into long-term health outcomes. Additional limitations include the lack of hypertension staging, which may influence exercise prescription recommendations. Finally, while we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusion

Weekend warriors’ and regularly active pattern were associated with lower all-cause mortality in adults without and with hypertension, particularly when session duration is ≥ 30 min/day. The risk of all-cause mortality was lower among individuals without hypertension compared with those with hypertension. Future studies on this topic may explore critical aspects of physical activity, such as exercise modality, intensity levels, and cumulative weekly volume.
