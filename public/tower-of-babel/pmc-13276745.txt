Myeloid cell–mediated killing of B-ALL by CD38 and CD20 IgA antibody variants is enhanced by CD47/SIRPα interference∗

ABSTRACT
Key Points•IgG1 and IgA2 antibodies against CD38 or CD20 were effective in recruiting macrophages for ADCP, but only IgA2 triggered ADCC by PMN.•Myeloid effector cell activation was enhanced by interfering with the CD47/SIRPα axis, especially when IgA2 antibodies were applied.

Introduction

Intensive chemotherapy has improved the outcome for patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) but often leads to treatment-related toxicities.1 Immunotherapy aims to reduce these toxicities while further improving outcomes.2,3 In addition to chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells,4 antibody-based therapeutics, such as blinatumomab,5 rituximab (RTX),6 and inotuzumab-ozogamicin7 (targeting CD19, CD20, and CD22, respectively), have become standard of care in adult patients with ALL. We investigated approaches to improve myeloid effector cell recruitment in antibody-based therapy against different molecular subtypes of B-ALL.

Myeloid cells, such as monocytes/macrophages, granulocytes (polymorphonuclear leukocytes [PMN]), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, are commonly found in the microenvironment of many solid tumors8,9 and have been observed in close contact with B-ALL cells in the bone marrow.10 Myeloid cell infiltrates are often associated with poor patient prognosis,8,9 but macrophages were also identified as an important effector cell population for many monoclonal antibodies.11,12 For example, the efficacy of CD20 antibodies in syngeneic mouse models is mediated by macrophages,13,14 for example, residing in the liver.15 Furthermore, studies in immunodeficient mice, which have functional myeloid but no lymphoid or natural killer cells, demonstrated therapeutic efficacy of antibodies against CD19 and CD20 against B-cell malignancies.16, 17, 18 PMN are the most abundant myeloid cells but are poorly recruited for tumor cell killing by human immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) antibodies.19,20 However, tumor cell killing by PMN is significantly enhanced when IgA2 antibodies engaging CD89 are used instead of IgG1 antibodies21, 22, 23 and when myeloid immune checkpoints are blocked.24,25

Similar to T cells, immune checkpoint molecules have also been described for myeloid cells, with CD47 on tumor cells and signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) on effector cells being the most established example.26,27 Checkpoint blockade by CD47 or SIRPα targeting molecules has been demonstrated to enhance the efficacy of therapeutic antibodies in many preclinical models,27 including T-cell ALL (T-ALL) and B-ALL.28,29 However, despite impressive early clinical results with RTX and the CD47 antibody magrolimab in B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma,30 the clinical development of magrolimab has recently been discontinued based on negative data in myeloid malignancies.31 Inhibition of glutaminyl-peptide cyclotransferase-like protein (QPCTL), the enzyme that catalyzes functionally relevant pyroglutamate (pGlu) formation on many peptides and proteins, including CD47, was shown to modify the myeloid tumor cell infiltrate32 and improve antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) and antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) against tumor cells.33, 34, 35

Here, we investigated the efficacy of CD47/SIRPα interference in combination with either human IgG1 or IgA2 antibodies against CD19, CD20, or CD38 in macrophage-mediated ADCP or PMN-mediated ADCC against B-ALL. In particular, anti-CD38 IgA2 with CD47 blockade evolved as a promising novel approach, which also proved effective in a xenograft model of human B-ALL in CD89 transgenic (tg) mice.

Materials and methods

RNA profiling of patient samples and cell lines

RNA profiling of samples from a previously described cohort of 559 adult and pediatric patients with B-ALL was performed as described.36,37 The same protocols were followed to analyze B-ALL cell lines.

Cell lines

Human B-ALL cell lines were purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ). The generation of gene-modified REH cells (luciferase-transduced REHLUC) is described in supplemental Materials and methods.

Flow cytometric analyses

Flow cytometric analyses of cell lines were performed on a Navios flow cytometer (Beckmann Coulter) using protocols as previously described.38,39 To quantify the antigen density on the cell surface, the specific antibody-binding capacity was determined using QIFIKIT (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse blood samples were measured using flow cytometry (FACS Canto II) and analyzed using FACSDiva software.

Isolation of human effector cells

PMN and peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from healthy donors as described.38 Nonpolarized (M0) macrophages were generated from monocytes stimulated with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (PeproTech) for 11 to 14 days.39

Antibody-dependent effector cell-mediated assays

ADCP was measured by live-cell imaging using the IncuCyte System (Sartorius).28 Briefly, antibodies and pHrodo-labeled (Thermo Fisher) target cells were added to M0 macrophages at an effector-to-target cell ratio of 1:1. Phagocytosis was measured every 20 minutes for hours and was quantified as red object counts per image, reflecting phagolysosomal acidification and degradation of internalized pHrodo-labeled target cells.

PMN-mediated ADCC was measured in 3-hour chromium-51 [51Cr] (Revvity) release assays or imaged with a Thunder RT widefield microscope (GE Healthcare) at an effector-to-target cell ratio of 80:1.38 For all functional assays, antibodies were used at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL (unless otherwise indicated), and the anti-CD47 IgG2σ was used at 20 μg/mL. Cetuximab and its IgA2 variant were used as isotype controls.

In vivo studies

CD89 tg SCID and NXG mice40 and their non-tg (wild-type [wt]) littermates were bred and maintained at Janvier under a customized breeding agreement with the University Medical Center (UMC). For the therapeutic study, REHLUC cells were injected into NXG mice. Treatment with anti-CD38 IgA2 and soluble SIRPα-Fc fusion protein was given as explained in supplemental files. The animal studies underwent review and were granted approval by the animal ethical committee of the UMC Utrecht (Algemene Vergunning Dierproeven [AVD] number: 115002021 15442).

Statistical analyses

Flow cytometric analyses and functional in vitro assays were performed in ≥3 independent experiments with effector cells from different donors. Depicted values are means ± standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was determined using 1- or 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction. P values <.05 were considered significant. Graphical and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.

Statistical analyses for RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data were performed in the R package 'ggpubr',41 using 1-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons of gene expression means and t test for pairwise comparison of means (leukemic vs healthy B-cell gene expression).

Ethics approval

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the ethics committees of the respective institutions approved all studies with isolated effector cells from healthy human blood donors, who gave written informed consent before analyses (University Medical Center Utrecht: 07-125/O, Christian-Albrecht University Kiel: D 563/18).

Additional methodological details are provided in supplement.

Results

Correlation between mRNA-seq data of B-ALL cell lines and patient samples from corresponding molecular subtypes

B-ALL has long been classified according to its immunophenotype,42 resembling physiological stages of B-cell differentiation.43,44 More recently, mRNA profiling emerged as a diagnostic alternative to more precisely classify B-ALL samples based on their transcriptional activity.45 The ALLCatchR tool allocates 21 gene-expression defined molecular B-ALL subtypes from RNA-seq profiles with high accuracy.36 To identify potential target antigens for immunotherapy, we analyzed mRNA expression of antigens against which approved antibody therapeutics are available across an adult cohort representing 18 of these subtypes (Figure 1A). To identify leukemia cell lines for functional studies, we generated mRNA profiles from 8 candidate cell lines representing 7 molecular entities and analyzed if their gene expression profiles matched with respective patient samples. Uniform manifold approximation and projection clustering of all batch-corrected samples according to the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-selected subtype-specific gene sets used in the ALLCatchR resulted in a clear separation of molecular subtypes (Figure 1B). Except for cell line TOM-1 (BCR::ABL1-positive), the other 7 tested B-ALL cell lines clustered with the respective patient samples of their corresponding subtypes. These results support that the selected cell lines can serve as representative models.

Next, we assessed mRNA expression levels of 3 potential target antigens for myeloid effector cell recruitment (CD19, CD20, and CD38) across the respective subtypes in more detail (Figure 1C). We found CD19 broadly expressed both within patient samples and cell lines (Figure 1C, left). Expression levels of CD20 (MS4A1) were found to be more subtype related, with particularly low levels in ETV6::RUNX1 samples and high levels in PAX5alt samples (Figure 1C, center). Among the cell lines, MHH-CALL-4 and MUTZ-5 (both BCR::ABL1-like) and TOM-1 (BCR::ABL1-positive) showed the highest CD20 mRNA expression levels, whereas others, such as NALM-6 (DUX4), SEM (KMT2A), and 697 (TCF3::PBX1), contained low levels. CD38 was broadly expressed by all subtypes, with the lowest levels in BCR::ABL1-like and BCR::ABL1-positive cell lines (Figure 1C, right). For all 3 antigens, expression varied markedly between patients within all subgroups (multicomparison ANOVA, P < 2.2 × 10−16), which was particularly large for CD20. Furthermore, we compared diagnostic flow cytometry-based protein expression with corresponding mRNA-seq values for routinely assessed markers. In samples with matched data (n = 119; supplemental Figure 1A), mRNA and protein levels correlated for CD19 (R2 = 0.22) and CD20 (R2 = 0.47), both statistically significant (P < .001). Because CD38 was not included in this diagnostic panel, we analyzed the FORALL multiomics data set of 40 B-ALL cell lines, which showed strong mRNA-protein correlations for CD19, CD20, and CD38 (R2 > 0.7; supplemental Figure 1B). Because previous studies have shown that myeloid effector cells require rather high antigen expression levels to mediate tumor cell killing,46, 47, 48 we measured target antigen expression on B-ALL samples (supplemental Figure 1C) and on the previously identified candidate B-ALL cell lines. Here, all B-ALL cell lines expressed similar levels of CD19 and CD38, whereas CD20 expression was more variable and absent on some cell lines (Figure 1D). Subsequently, CD19, CD20, and CD38 served as target antigens for ADCP by macrophages and ADCC by PMN.

Myeloid cell–mediated tumor cell killing with CD19, CD20, and CD38 antibodies of IgG1 and IgA2 isotypes

To investigate whether myeloid effector cell activation against B-ALL can be improved by IgA2 compared with IgG1 antibodies, IgA2 variants of clinically approved IgG1 antibodies against CD19, CD20, and CD38 were generated using the variable regions of tafasitamab (TAFA), obinutuzumab (OBZ), and daratumumab (DARA), respectively (Figure 2A). Antigen binding was similar for IgG1 and IgA2 variants (Figure 2B). Against CD20, a matched set of RTX variants was also analyzed (supplemental Figure 2A-B). Based on the antigen expression levels observed in Figure 1D, we tested cell lines with high expression levels for each antigen in myeloid cell–mediated killing assays. Thus, SEM (KMT2A) and 697 (TCF3::PBX1) served as target cells for CD19 and MUTZ-5 (BCR::ABL1-like) MHH-CALL-4 (BCR::ABL1-like) for CD20, and 697 and REH (ETV6::RUNX1) for CD38. Although both the IgG1 and IgA2 variants of the CD19 and CD38 antibodies were similarly effective in triggering ADCP by macrophages (Figure 2C), the glyco-engineered IgG1 antibody OBZ against CD20 performed better than its IgA2 variant (Figure 2C). On the other hand, only the IgA2 but not the IgG1 variants of CD20 and CD38 antibodies mediated ADCC with PMN as effector cells (Figure 2D). Compared with RTX IgG1, OBZ IgG1 showed enhanced ADCP of MHH-CALL-4 (but not MUTZ-5), whereas OBZ IgA2 was consistently superior to RTX IgA2 in PMN-mediated ADCC (supplemental Figure 2C). Against CD19, neither IgG1 nor IgA2 antibodies recruited PMN for ADCC, whereas significant ADCP was observed against some but not all cell lines (Figure 2C-D; supplemental Figure 2D). Thus, anti-CD38 and anti-CD20 were effective as IgG1 and IgA2 in ADCP by macrophages, but only IgA2 variants against both antigens mediated ADCC by PMN. Because IgG1-dependent leukemia cell killing is well documented,18,28,29 we focused on IgA2 in our further experiments.

CD47 expression on B-ALL cells and its role as an immune checkpoint in ADCC and ADCP in vitro

Myeloid cell–mediated tumor cell killing is regulated by the immune checkpoint molecules CD47/SIRPα.27 In the FORALL data set, CD47 showed a strong positive correlation between mRNA and protein expression (supplemental Figure 1B). Thus, we assessed CD47 mRNA expression across the molecular B-ALL subtypes of our patients’ samples and correlated its expression with our selected cell lines. As shown (Figure 3A), CD47 is broadly expressed at high mRNA levels in all B-ALL subtypes and in our cell lines, reflected by cell surface expression on cell lines (Figure 3B). An Fc-silent CD47 blocking antibody based on the variable regions of magrolimab (anti-CD47 IgG2σ28) was used to investigate the impact of CD47 blockade on ADCC and ADCP by IgA2 antibodies. Importantly, CD47 blockade alone (in the absence of a tumor-targeting antibody) did not trigger ADCP or ADCC in vitro (Figure 3C-D), supporting that activating signals on effector cells, for example, through immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif-containing FcR, are required.49,50 Importantly, both ADCP and ADCC were significantly enhanced by CD47/SIRPα interference in the presence of IgA2 variants of CD20 and CD38 antibodies (Figure 3C-D; supplemental Figure 3A). In contrast, the CD47 blocking antibody combined with anti-CD19 IgA2 did not enhance ADCC or ADCP (Figure 3C-D; supplemental Figure 3B). Representative ADCP and ADCC experiments are visualized in Figure 3E-F.

CD47 blockade improves the therapeutic efficacy of a CD38 IgA2 antibody against B-ALL in vivo

CD47 blockade has been shown to enhance the in vivo activity of IgG1 antibodies targeting several antigens,27,49,50 including CD19 in B-ALL29 and CD38 in T-ALL.28 However, the combination of CD47/SIRPα blockade and IgA antibodies has been investigated in more limited settings,24,25,38,51 so far not including ALL. Because CD38 antibodies are increasingly investigated in patients with ALL,52 we evaluated whether an IgA2 variant of DARA would be effective against B-ALL in CD89 tg NXG mice. In a pilot experiment, we compared the engraftment of REHLUC cells in CD89 tg SCID and NXG mice (supplemental Figure 4A-B).

Six groups of NXG mice (CD89 tg or wt as indicated) received REHLUC cell IV and were treated intraperitoneally with vehicle, SIRPα-Fc, anti-CD38 IgA2, or the combination of both (Figure 4A). All mice received pegylated granulocyte colony-stimulating factor subcutaneously, which is known to enhance tumor cell killing by CD89.53 Based on previous experience with IgA2 antibodies in vivo,21,22 mice were treated with anti-CD38 IgA2 at the indicated time points (Figure 4A). A high-affinity human SIRPα-Fc with a silent Fc,25 binding both mouse and human CD47, with ADCC and ADCP comparable to the previously used CD47-blocking antibody (supplemental Figure 5A-B), was used alone or combined with anti-CD38 IgA2 to block CD47-SIRPα interactions in vivo. Ex vivo analyses of blood samples showed that monocyte and granulocyte counts were similar in all treatment groups (supplemental Figure 6A). Human IgA on granulocytes was not detected at any time point (supplemental Figure 6B), whereas SIRPα-Fc on granulocytes was observed until day +58 (supplemental Figure 6C). As previously described,40 CD89 expression was found on blood neutrophils in CD89 tg but not in wt mice (supplemental Figure 6D). With respect to therapeutic efficacy, bioluminescence imaging (BLI) images showed that mice in the control groups 1 (vehicle) and 2 (anti-CD38 IgA2 in non-tg mice) were the first to develop tumors (Figure 4B-D). As expected from previous results,28,29 SIRPα-Fc alone was effective in tg and wt mice and outperformed vehicle control and IgA2 monotherapy (Figure 4B-D). The combination treatment of SIRPα-Fc and anti-CD38 IgA2 in tg mice resulted in the highest number of tumor-free mice (Figure 4C) and the greatest prolongation of survival, although the survival difference was not statistically significant compared with treatment with SIRPα-Fc alone (Figure 4D). Nevertheless, a significantly higher percentage of mice were tumor free as assessed by BLI after combination therapy in the tg compared with the wt combination group and compared with the SIRPα-Fc alone group (Figure 4B-C). Importantly, BLI demonstrated that both anti-CD38 IgA2 monotherapy and its combination with SIRPα-Fc were effective exclusively in CD89 tg mice, but not wt mice, highlighting the requirement for IgA-CD89 interactions (supplemental Figure 6E).

At the experimental end point, bone marrow samples from surviving mice were analyzed by flow cytometry (supplemental Figure 6F). Myeloid populations included PMN (mLy6G+), macrophages (mF4/80+), and a double-positive subset. In hCD89 tg mice, all mLy6G+ cells expressed hCD89, whereas only a subset of mF4/80+ cells were hCD89+ (supplemental Figure 6G-H). Bone marrow samples from mice surviving day 63 were assessed for the presence of minimal residual disease by human CD19/CD38 staining (Figure 4E-F; supplemental Figure 6I). In all 3 groups, human cells were <1%. Importantly, 6 of the 8 combination-treated tg mice showed <0.1% residual tumor cells (Figure 4E), resulting in increased minimal residual disease–low survival in group 6 vs other groups, with a trend that did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4F). Together, these in vivo results support the therapeutic efficacy of CD47 blockade in combination with anti-CD38 IgA2 in the treatment of B-ALL.

QPCTL-mediated posttranslational pGlu formation of CD47 impacts the efficacy of CD47/SIRPα interactions and can be targeted by small molecule inhibitors

The functional activity of CD47 is regulated by its enzymatic modifier QPCTL.33,34 mRNA profiling revealed that QPCTL expression is more strongly upregulated than that of CD47 in B-ALL compared with normal B cells (Figure 5A). QPCTL mRNA expression could also be confirmed across all molecular B-ALL subtypes and in our respective cell lines (Figure 5B). The Golgi-resident enzyme QPCTL catalyzes N-terminal pGlu formation,33,34 which is part of the CD47 binding pocket for SIRPα, as known from crystallographic analyses (Figure 5C).54,55 However, on a molecular level, the direct involvement of pGlu in the CD47/SIRPα interaction has not been confirmed yet. We generated human CD47 Fc-silent fusion proteins (IgG1σ-Fc)56 that contain either a native CD47 ectodomain with N-terminal pGlu (CD47-wt) or a mutated version with alanine at the N-terminus (CD47-Q1A; Figure 5D). Amino acid exchange and the posttranslational modification of glutamate to pGlu were confirmed by mass spectrometry. Here, the 2 different N-terminal fragments could be identified as separate peaks (Figure 5E). Importantly, the 2 CD47-Fc fusion proteins displayed clear differential binding to recombinant SIRPα in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay experiments, with significantly reduced binding of the mutated CD47-Q1A molecule compared with CD47-wt (Figure 5F). Consistent with the expression of SIRPα on macrophages (supplemental Figure 7A), binding of CD47-Q1A to macrophages was significantly reduced compared with CD47-wt (Figure 5G), supporting that pGlu is essential for CD47/SIRPα interactions. Incorrect folding of the mutated CD47 ectodomain is unlikely because the binding of the pGlu-independent CD47 antibody B6H12 (binding total CD47) was similar for CD47-wt and CD47-Q1A (supplemental Figure 7B, left). In contrast, the pGlu-dependent CD47 antibody CC2C633 hardly interacted with the mutated CD47-Q1A, confirming that binding of this antibody depends on the presence of pGlu (supplemental Figure 7B, right).

The QPCTL-catalyzed posttranslational pGlu formation of CD47 can be inhibited by small molecules.57 To investigate the functional impact of QPCTL in the context of CD38-targeted therapy for B-ALL, REH cells were treated with the QPCTL inhibitor SEN177. As shown in supplemental Figure 7C, soluble SIRPα binding was reduced on SEN177-treated REH cells. Consequently, QPCTL inhibition by SEN177 improved both ADCC and ADCP of REH cells by anti-CD38 IgA2 compared with the respective control cells (Figure 5H). Overall, these data support the involvement of QPCTL in regulating CD47/SIRPα interactions in B-ALL cells and suggest small-molecule QPCTL inhibitors as another therapeutic approach to interfere with this myeloid checkpoint.

Discussion

ALL has emerged as a model disease for novel immunotherapeutic approaches,1,2 with the first bispecific T-cell engager (blinatumomab58), the first CAR T-cell product (tisagenlecleucel59), and the second ADC (inotuzumab-ozogamicin7) being approved for B-ALL. Nevertheless, the development of conventional monoclonal antibodies is lagging in ALL, although many B-cell restricted antigens have been identified. Thus, we analyzed mRNA expression levels across 18 previously defined molecular B-ALL subgroups36 for potential target antigens, against which antibody-based products are clinically approved.60 We then focused on CD19, CD20, and CD38 and investigated how myeloid effector cell recruitment by antibodies against these target antigens could be improved.

Data presented here confirm that the target antigen selection critically impacts antibody efficacy in vitro.61 For example, Fc-mediated tumor cell killing was notoriously weak using unmodified wt human IgG1 antibodies against CD1916 but could be significantly improved by respective Fc engineering.39 Consequently, the clinically available CD19 antibody TAFA is engineered for improved FcR binding compared with wt IgG1 and also demonstrated preclinical activity in depleting B cells against B-ALL in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) studies18,29 and in nonhuman primate models.16 Further enhancement of ADCP in vitro and therapeutic activity in vivo was observed in combination with CD47 blockade,29 which (at least in vitro) showed limited efficacy for a nonengineered CD19 antibody containing the TAFA variable regions. Furthermore, a CD89-directed bispecific antibody62 or isotype switching of CD19 antibodies to IgA did not recruit PMN for ADCC, the latter not even in the presence of CD47 blockade. The limited efficacy of CD19 antibodies in myeloid-mediated killing may be attributed to the epitope dependency of Fc-mediated effector functions,51 the observation that CD19 is less effective than other similarly expressed B-cell antigens in recruiting myeloid cells despite adequate surface density,39 and potential inhibitory contributions of intracellular CD19 domains.62 These results with myeloid effector cells contrast with observations with T cells, where CD19-directed bispecific antibodies or CD19-directed CARs induce potent killing of leukemia and lymphoma cells.58,63 These differences may be related to the fundamentally different tumor cell killing mechanisms of T cells compared with myeloid cells; although T cells kill via FAS/FAS ligand interactions64 or induce membrane pores into tumor cells,65 macrophages kill tumor cells by phagocytosis66 and PMN by “frustrated” phagocytosis, called trogoptosis.67 Although mechanisms of sensitivity and resistance against tumor cell killing by T and natural killer cells or macrophages begin to emerge,66,68,69 less is known about molecular mechanisms affecting the efficacy of trogoptosis.70

In line with results from lymphoma studies,14,17 CD20 antibodies effectively triggered ADCP and ADCC also against CD20-expressing B-ALL cells, which was further enhanced by CD47 blockade. Effective ADCP by macrophages was observed with the glyco-engineered OBZ but also with the nonengineered RTX and with IgA2 variants of both antibodies. As expected from experiments with lymphoma cells,51 ADCC by PMN was only observed with anti-CD20 IgA2 variants but not with their clinically available IgG1 versions, irrespective of CD47 blockade. This difference between IgG1 and IgA2 isotypes in triggering ADCC by PMN is probably explained by differences in antibody binding to FcR, leading to more potent downstream signaling of CD89 compared with Fcγ receptors in PMN.23 An obvious limitation of all CD20 antibodies in the treatment of B-ALL is the restricted expression of CD20 on certain subtypes, which is less obvious from mRNA studies compared with classical immunological classifications, where early pro-B-ALL samples and respective cell lines (such as NALM-6 or SEM) are typically CD20−. Nevertheless, RTX is nowadays widely used in the treatment of adult patients with B-ALL.3 Approaches to increase the efficacy of CD20-directed antibody therapy, for example, by switching to OBZ, IgA2 variants, or combinations with CD47 blockade, require further studies.

The CD38 antibody DARA continues to change the therapeutic landscape for plasma cell dyscrasias,71 and CD38 expression is also found on many B- and T-ALL leukemia cells. Preclinical in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that CD38 antibodies, such as DARA and isatuximab, could also have therapeutic potential for the treatment of patients with ALL.52 Recently published results from a phase 2 study showed promising results for DARA in patients with T-ALL, whereas results in B-ALL were less impressive.72 Results presented here demonstrate that myeloid effector cell recruitment by DARA could be improved by switching isotypes from IgG1 to IgA2 and by combining CD38 antibodies with CD47 blockade. These effects were observed for both macrophages and PMN. In vivo results confirmed FcαRI (CD89)-dependent efficacy of anti-CD38 IgA2, which was significantly enhanced by a combination with a SIRPα-Fc fusion protein. However, contrary to the in vitro data, CD47 blockade alone was also highly effective in vivo, which has also been observed in other ALL28,29 but not in solid tumor models25 and may reflect particular sensitivity of ALL engraftment to CD47/SIRPα interactions.73 Although clinical studies with the CD47 antibody magrolimab were discontinued,31 clinical studies with SIRPα antibodies and a SIRPα-Fc fusion protein are ongoing.74 Importantly, an unbiased screening approach found combined CD47 and CD38 targeting particularly effective in killing malignant B cells,75 and clinical studies with CD47×CD19 and CD47×CD38 bispecific antibodies are ongoing in patients with lymphoma and myeloma, respectively.76,77 Depending on their toxicity and efficacy in these other indications, they can also be considered for testing in ALL therapy. An additional option to interfere with CD47/SIRPα interactions could be small molecule QPCTL inhibitors,57 which were demonstrated to enhance ADCP and ADCC33, 34, 35 and favorably modify the tumor microenvironment.32

IgA antibodies induce potent tumor cell killing against several target antigens in vitro and in vivo, but their clinical development faces several challenges.78 Nevertheless, many biotechnology companies have or had ongoing IgA programs, but the first IgA antibody still has to enter clinical trials. For the concept of myeloid checkpoint blockade in antibody-based immunotherapy, IgA antibodies appear to be an ideal combination partner because IgA effectively activates both macrophages and PMN. Potentially, ALL could become a model disease again when IgA variants of CD38 antibodies would be developed together with CD47/SIRPα interference.
