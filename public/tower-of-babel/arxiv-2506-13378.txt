Some functor calculus on semirings
Pronay Biswas, Amartya Goswami, Sujit Kumar Sardar

Abstract
. We develop a functorial framework for the ideal theory of commutative semirings using coherent frames and spectral spaces. Two central constructions—the radical ideal functor and the k k -radical ideal functor—are shown to yield coherent frames, with the latter forming a dense sublocale of the former. We define a natural transformation between these functors and analyze their categorical and topological properties. Further, we introduce a notion of support that assigns to each semiring a bounded distributive lattice whose spectrum is homeomorphic to its prime spectrum. This enables the reconstruction of the radical ideal frame via lattice-theoretic data. Applications include adjunctions between quantales and complete idealic semirings; and a comparison of prime and k k -prime spectra in semisimple r r -semirings. Our results unify various spectral constructions in semiring theory through a categorical and pointfree perspective.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

The study of semirings has emerged as a natural generalization of both ring theory and lattice theory, offering a framework flexible enough to model structures lacking additive inverses. This generalization is not merely formal; semirings arise organically in a variety of mathematical and applied contexts.

In theoretical computer science, semirings furnish a natural algebraic setting for weighted automata, where transitions are assigned values from a semiring to encode cost, probability, or other quantities. This theory is elaborated by Kuich and Salomaa
[
42
]
, who develop a general framework for formal language theory based on semiring-valued functions.

Tropical mathematics considers the semiring
(\mathds{R}\cup\{\infty\},\min,+)
, wherein addition is defined as the minimum and multiplication as the usual addition. This idempotent semiring appears in diverse applications, including enumerative geometry and optimization. The relevance of tropical semirings is detailed in
[
12
,
13
,
27
,
49
]
.

In functional analysis (see
[
45
]
) and mathematical physics, idempotent semirings such as
(\mathds{R}\cup\{-\infty\},\max,+)
serve as the foundation for idempotent integration, a theory parallel to classical measure theory but adapted to optimization and variational problems. Viro
[
57
]
(see also
[
58
]
) provides a conceptual connection between idempotent semirings and classical geometry through a process termed dequantization.

In categorical algebra, semirings emerge naturally as fibrewise algebraically cartesian closed categories is discussed in
[
20
]
. For semirings in the context of
algebraic
K
-theory, see
[
59
]
, while for topos theory, we recommend
[
34
]
. For references on further applications of semirings in specific contexts, like discrete event systems, differential equations; optimization, process algebras, automata theory, and more, we refer to
[
25
,
31
]

The omission of subtraction introduces rich ideal-theoretic behavior not seen in classical settings, and this motivates a refined algebraic and categorical study of semirings, their spectra, and their functorial invariants.
The aim of this paper is twofold. First, we investigate a family of functors that arise naturally from semirings, particularly in connection with their spectral theory, and examine the key structural properties they exhibit. Second, we undertake a detailed analysis of specific classes of semirings that support or reflect these properties, leading to a broader understanding of their categorical and topological behaviour. Our analysis of topological properties is carried out largely within a setting which provides an categorical and lattice-theoretic perspective better suited to the semiring-based structures under consideration.

The initial motivation for this work stems from Banaschewski’s foundational paper
[
3
]
, which introduced the radical ideal functor from the category of commutative rings with unity into the category of coherent frames. The study of the radical ideal functor and the associated coherent frames of radical ideals has attracted considerable attention in recent years. Notable contributions in this direction can be found in the works
[
17
,
18
,
48
]
), where various structural and categorical aspects of these constructions have been systematically explored.

Since semirings generalize rings, they admit a broader variety of ideal structures.
The theory of radical ideals in semirings, viewed as a natural analogue of semiprime ideals in ring theory, has evolved in a variety of algebraic frameworks
[
26
,
29
,
39
,
43
]
. A recurring theme in this development is the structural inadequacy of radical ideals under standard lattice operations, particularly in contexts where additive closure is not sufficiently robust. To address this, the notion of the
k
-radical—defined as the intersection of all
k
-prime ideals containing a given ideal—offers a refinement that aligns more faithfully with the machinery of locale theory and frame semantics. In many natural settings, the collection of
k
-radical ideals forms a coherent frame, thereby endowing the associated spectrum with the structure of a spectral space in the sense of Hochster
[
32
]
. This perspective is reinforced by the foundational insights of Johnstone
[
34
]
, who observed that coherent frames serve as the open-set lattices of spectral spaces, thus providing a pointfree topological interpretation of the underlying algebraic structure.
In Section
3
, we investigate two functorial constructions arising naturally from the theory of semirings: one determined by the lattice of radical ideals, and the other by the lattice of
k
-radical ideals. Both functors take values in the category of coherent frames and are linked via a natural transformation. We establish that each of these lattices carries the structure of a coherent frame, though notably, the frame of
k
-radical ideals does not embed as a subframe of the radical ideal frame. Nevertheless, we show that it determines a dense sublocale, thus preserving a strong topological relationship. Our treatment of these constructions is guided by the foundational accounts of frame theory presented in
[
34
,
50
]
.

The study of spectra across various algebraic structures, such as rings, lattices, monoids, and beyond, often yields deep insight into the structures themselves. In his seminal work
[
32
]
, Hochster established that every spectral space arises as the prime spectrum of a commutative ring with unity. Since every ring is, in particular, a semiring, it follows trivially that every spectral space can also be realized as the spectrum of a semiring. However, such realizations are far from unique: a given spectral space may arise from multiple non-isomorphic semirings. In contrast, each spectral space corresponds uniquely to the prime spectrum of a bounded distributive lattice. While semiring spectra have been studied from a topological and algebraic perspective, the categorical interactions via coherent frames and support functors remain less explored.

Motivated by this distinction, we seek to associate to each semiring
S
a bounded distributive lattice
L_{S}
such that their spectra are homeomorphic. In the case of rings, this idea underpins the notion of reticulation, as developed by Simmons
[
53
]
, and is closely related to Joyal’s notion of support
[
36
]
, later studied by Luis
[
44
]
and clarified in
[
34
, V. Lemma 3.2]
. The general philosophy, translating spectral data of an algebraically rich object to a simpler associated structure, has since been explored in numerous contexts, including bounded lattices
[
6
,
41
]
, tensor triangulated categories
[
1
,
2
,
40
]
, quantales
[
21
,
24
]
, and universal algebras
[
22
,
23
]
.

In Section
4
, we introduce the notion of support for semirings: a functorial construction assigning to each semiring a bounded distributive lattice whose spectrum is homeomorphic to that of the semiring. This is achieved via a canonical support map, from which the lattice structure can be reconstructed. We show that the frame of radical ideals of the semiring is isomorphic to the frame of ideals of the associated lattice. Furthermore, we identify a substructure that captures the spectral space of strong prime ideals via a restricted support. An important outcome of this analysis is a characterization of regularity (in the sense of von Neumann) within a class of lattice-ordered semirings. The section concludes with a categorical perspective on the various support structures arising from a semiring.

In the final Section
5
, we turn our attention to two restricted classes of semirings. The first one is the Idealic semiring which arose in Takagi’s work in schemes over
\mathds{F}_{1}
and tropical geometry; see
[
56
]
. Within this class, we focus on complete idealic semirings, which allow for a reconstruction of the
k
-prime spectrum of a semiring via the Zariski space associated to the complete idealic semiring formed from its radical
k
-ideals. In the realm of complete idealic semirings, we find that the lattice of
k
-ideals of a complete idealic semiring can be turned into a special quantale, namely the unitally bounded quantales. Each unitally bounded quantale can be realised as a complete idealic semiring. We demonstrate that the forgetful functor from the category of unitally bounded quantales to that of complete idealic semirings admits a right adjoint, given by the functor assigning to each complete idealic semiring the lattice of
k
-ideals of complete idealic semirings. The second class of semirings under consideration is that of

r
-semirings, defined as semirings in which every maximal ideal is a

k
-ideal, as studied in
[
10
]
. Within this class, we examine the images of the functors induced by prime and
k
-prime ideals. While, in general, the spectra arising from prime and

k
-prime ideals may differ significantly, we show that in the case of semisimple

r
-semirings, the
k
-prime spectrum forms a dense subspace of the prime spectrum.

We denote by

2.3.
Quantales and frames

A
quantale

(Q,\vee,\ast)
is a complete lattice with a semigroup operation
\ast\colon Q\times Q\rightarrow Q
, such that the distribution law

a\ast\bigg(\bigvee S\bigg)=\bigvee_{s\in S}\big(a\ast s\big)
holds
for all
a\in Q
and
S\subseteq Q
.
A quantale
Q
is said to be
commutative
if
a\ast b=b\ast a
for all
a,b\in Q
. A commutative unital quantale
(Q,\vee,\ast,1)
is said to be
unitally bounded
if the unit
1
is the maximum element.
A map between two quantales is said to be a
quantale homomorphism
if it preserves arbitrary joins and the semigroup operation. Our main reference for quantales is
[
51
]
.

A
frame
is a quantale when
\ast=\wedge
. Our main references for
frames and locales
are
[
34
,
50
]
.
A map
f\colon L\rightarrow M
between two frames is said to be a
frame homomorphism
if
f
preserves arbitrary joins and finite meets. Each frame homomorphism
f\colon L\rightarrow M
has a right adjoint
f_{*}\colon M\rightarrow L
defined by

f_{*}(y):=\bigvee\bigl\{x\in L\mid f(x)\leqslant y\bigr\},\qquad(y\in M).

The category of frames is denoted by
\mathds{F}\mathrm{rm}
.
An element
k
of a frame (resp. complete lattice)
L
is said to be
compact
if, for any
S\subseteq L
,
k\leqslant\bigvee S
implies that there is a finite
T\subseteq S
, such that
x\leqslant\bigvee T
. Allied to this, an element
k
of a complete lattice is said to be
finite
if
k\leqslant\bigsqcup D
for some directed set
D
implies
k\leqslant d
for some
d\in D
.

An element
x
of a frame
L
is said to be
complemented
if there exists
x^{\bot}\in L
such that
x\vee x^{\bot}=1
and
x\wedge x^{\bot}=0
. A frame
L
is called
zero-dimensional
if every element is a join of complemented elements.
A frame
L
is said to be
compact
if
1
is a compact element.
A complete lattice
L
is said to be
algebraic
if each element can be written as a join of compact elements below it.
An algebraic frame
L
is said to
coherent
if
L
is compact and satisfies the
finite intersection property
on the set of compact elements (
viz
., the meet of any two compact elements is again compact).
A
coherent frame homomorphism
is a frame homomorphism which maps compact elements to compact elements. We denote the category of coherent frames by
\mathds{C}\mathrm{oh}\mathds{F}\mathrm{rm}
.
The opposite category of frames, called
locales
, is denoted by
\mathds{L}\mathrm{oc}
.
As with coherent frames,
mutatis mutandis
, we say that a quantale
(Q,\vee,\ast)
is
coherent
if its lattice reduct
(Q,\vee,\wedge)
is compact and algebraic, and for all compact elements
c,d\in Q
, the product
c\ast d
is compact.

3.
The functors
\mathsf{RId}
and
\mathsf{RId}_{k}

In this section, we develop two key functors,
\mathsf{RId}
and
\mathsf{RId}_{k}
, which assign to each commutative semiring the lattice of radical ideals and
k
-radical ideals, respectively. We study their algebraic and lattice-theoretic structures, prove that they form coherent frames, and examine their behaviour under semiring homomorphisms. The section culminates with the construction of a natural transformation between these functors, highlighting their categorical significance.

3.1.
Structures of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}

For a semiring
S
, the set of all
k
-radical ideals, denoted by
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
, forms a poset under set inclusion. To make it a lattice, we can readily assign two operations on
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. For any
I,J\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
we define:

\bullet

I\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}J\coloneqq\sqrt[k]{I+J}
.

\bullet

I\wedge J\coloneqq I\cap J
.

Observe that under these two operations
(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S}),\bigvee_{\!\!\!k},\wedge)
is a lattice and is bounded by
(0)
and
S
. Moreover,
(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S}),\wedge)
is a complete
\wedge
-semilattice. This forces
(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S}),\bigvee_{\!\!\!k},\wedge)
to be a complete lattice. In fact, we will show that
(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S}),\bigvee_{\!\!\!k},\wedge)
is a frame.

Remark 3.1
.

Let
\mathsf{RId}(S)
be the poset of all radical ideals of
S
under inclusion. For any two radical ideals,
I
and
J
of
S
, we define
I\vee J\coloneqq\sqrt{I+J}
and
I\wedge J\coloneqq I\cap J
. The rest of the section is about the lattice-structural aspects of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
. The reader should keep in mind that, as in the case of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
(which, although not a sublattice of
\mathsf{RId}(\textit{S})
), we can show that
\mathsf{RId}(S)
is a frame and has similar properties to
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
; the proof follows similar techniques used for
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
.

We recall the following salient result regarding the
k
-radical of the product of ideals.

Lemma 3.2
.

(
[
29
, Lemma 3.9]
)
For any
I
and
J
in
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
,
\sqrt[k]{IJ}=I\wedge J
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any semiring
S
,
(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S}),\bigvee_{\!\!\!k},\wedge)
is a frame.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
I\wedge{\underset{i\in\Lambda}{\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}}}J_{i}\subseteq{\underset{i\in\Lambda}{\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}}}I\wedge J_{i}
. Due to Lemma
3.2
, this boils down to showing

\displaystyle\sqrt[k]{I\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}}\subseteq\sqrt[k]{\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}\sqrt[k]{IJ_{i}}}.

Let
\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}\sqrt[k]{IJ_{i}}\subseteq P
, where
P\in\speck
. Then
IJ_{i}\subseteq P
, for all
i\in\Lambda
. If
I\subseteq P
, then clearly
I\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}\subseteq P
. If
I\nsubseteq P
, then
J_{i}\subseteq P
, for all
i\in\Lambda
. Hence
\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}\subseteq P
, and therefore
I\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}\subseteq P
. In turn, we have

\mathcal{V}_{k}\left(\underset{i\in\Lambda}{\sum}\sqrt[k]{IJ_{i}}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{k}\left(I\underset{i\in\Lambda}{\sum}J_{i}\right),

and hence,
\sqrt[k]{I\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}}\subseteq\sqrt[k]{\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}\sqrt[k]{IJ_{i}}}
.
The opposite inclusion holds in any complete lattice.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If we turn our focus to some arbitrary semiring, then pathologies may arrive; in some cases,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
may not even be a distributive lattice. We provide one such example. Let
(S,\vee,0)
be a
\vee
-semilattice, where
S:=\left\{0,a,b,a\vee b,c,d\right\}
, subject to the relations:
a\vee c=d
,
b\vee c=d
,
0
is the identity element and
d
is
\vee
-absorbing. Observe that
(S,\vee,0)
is a commutative idempotent monoid. To make it a semiring, we give the trivial multiplication, that is
x\odot y:=0
, for all
x,y\in S
. Now observe that any submonoid of
(S,\vee,0)
is an ideal in
(S,\vee,\odot,0)
and each ideal is a radical ideal. The lattice
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
contains the following pentagon:

\textstyle{S\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\{0,a,a\vee b\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\{0,c\}}
\textstyle{\{0,a\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\{0\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Clearly
\{0,a,a\vee b\}\vee\{0,c\}=\{0,a,a\vee b,c,d\}
, and its
k
-closure is
S
. Therefore,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
is not modular (
cf.

[
14
, Theorem 4.10]
), and hence, is not distributive.

Let us now redirect our focus to the lattice-theoretic properties of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. By
[a]_{k}
, we denote a principal
k
-radical generated by
a
, and by definition
[a]_{k}=\sqrt[k]{\langle a\rangle}
. First, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
S
is a semiring. Then for any
a_{1},a_{2}\in S
,
[a_{1},a_{2}]_{k}=[a_{1}]_{k}\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}[a_{2}]_{k}
. More generally, for any finite subset
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
of
S
, we have

\displaystyle[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}]_{k}=[a_{1}]_{k}\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}[a_{2}]_{k}\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}\cdots\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}[a_{n}]_{k}.

Proof.

We prove only the first part of the claim, and the second follows by induction. Let
x\in[a_{1},a_{2}]_{k}
. Then there exists
n\in\mathds{N}
, such that

x^{n}\in\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle\subseteq\langle a_{1}\rangle+\langle a_{2}\rangle\subseteq\sqrt[k]{\langle a_{1}\rangle}+\sqrt[k]{\langle a_{2}\rangle}.

Therefore,
x\in[a_{1}]_{k}\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}[a_{2}]_{k}
, and hence
[a_{1},a_{2}]_{k}\subseteq[a_{1}]_{k}\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}[a_{2}]_{k}
. The opposite inclusion holds since
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
is a lattice, and we have
[a_{1}]_{k}\subseteq[a_{1},a_{2}]_{k}
and
[a_{2}]_{k}\subseteq[a_{1},a_{2}]_{k}
.
∎

Recall that the notions of finite and compact elements coincide in a complete lattice. Moreover, the set of all compact elements forms a
\vee
-subsemilattice (see
[
14
, Lemma 7.16]
).
The following theorem gives a complete description of compact elements of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The compact elements of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
are precisely the finitely generated
k
-radical ideals.

Proof.

First, we show that
[a]_{k}
is a compact element for all
a\in S
. Take a directed family
\{J_{i}\}_{i\in\Lambda}\subseteq\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
such that

[a]_{k}\subseteq{\underset{i\in\Lambda}{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}}J_{i}=\sqrt[k]{\underset{i\in\Lambda}{\sum}J_{i}}.

Then by
[
39
, Proposition 3.13]
,
(a)^{n}\subseteq\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}
. Also
\Sigma_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}={\bigsqcup_{i\in\Lambda}J_{i}}
. Therefore,
a\in J_{i}
for some
i\in\Lambda
and since
J_{i}\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
, we conclude that
[a]_{k}\subseteq J_{i}
. So,
[a]_{k}
is a finite,
viz
., a compact element of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. Therefore, by
[
14
, Lemma 7.16]
and Lemma
3.5
, for any finite subset
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\subseteq S
,
[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}]_{k}
is a compact element of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. Now, let
I
be any compact element of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. Clearly, for any ideal
I
,
I=\underset{a\in I}{\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}}[a]_{k}
. Then there exist
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}\in I
such that,

I=[b_{1}]_{k}\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}\cdots\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}[b_{n}]_{k}=[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]_{k}.\qed

Lemma 3.7
.

The frame
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
is algebraic.

It is well-known that coherent frames are precisely the open set lattice of spectral spaces. As a frame-theoretic characteristic of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
, we show the following.

Theorem 3.8
.

The frame
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
is coherent.

Proof.

For a semiring
S
, the lattice
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
is an algebraic frame, follows from Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
3.7
. It remains to show that the meet of finitely many compact elements is again compact.
First, we claim that
[ab]_{k}=[a]_{k}\wedge[b]_{k}
for any
a,b\in S
. To see this, let
x\in[ab]_{k}
. Then, for some
n\in\mathds{N}
,

x^{n}\in\langle ab\rangle\subseteq\langle a\rangle\cap\langle b\rangle\subseteq\sqrt[k]{\langle a\rangle}\cap\sqrt[k]{\langle b\rangle}=[a]_{k}\wedge[b]_{k}.

Therefore,
x\in[a]_{k}\wedge[b]_{k}
, and hence
[ab]_{k}\subseteq[a]_{k}\cap[b]_{k}
. The opposite inclusion is easy to show.
Now,
I=[a_{1}]_{k}\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}\cdots\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}[a_{s}]_{k}
and
J=[b_{1}]_{k}\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}\cdots\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}[b_{t}]
be two compact elements of
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. Then

\displaystyle I\wedge J

\displaystyle=\big(\overset{s}{\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{i=1}}[a_{i}]_{k}\big)\wedge\big(\overset{t}{\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{j=1}}[b_{j}]_{k}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{i,j}\big([a_{i}]_{k}\wedge[b_{j}]_{k}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{i,j}[a_{i}b_{j}]_{k},

which is compact by the fact that compact elements form a
\vee
-subsemilattice.
∎

The rest of the section is dedicated to the study of the functors
\mathsf{RId}
and
\mathsf{RId}_{k}
, from
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
to
\mathds{C}\mathrm{oh}\mathds{F}\mathrm{rm}
. It concludes with the construction of a natural transformation between them (see Proposition
3.20
).
As noticed in Remark
3.1
, the frame
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
is not a subframe of
\mathsf{RId}(S)
in general.
Recall that a map
\mathfrak{N}\colon L\rightarrow L
is said to be a
nucleus
on a frame
L
if for all
a,b\in L
the following holds:

(i)

a\leqslant\mathfrak{N}(a)
,

(ii)

\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{N(a)})=a
,

(iii)

\mathfrak{N}(a\wedge b)=\mathfrak{N}(a)\wedge\mathfrak{N}(b)
.

For a nucleus
\mathfrak{N}
on
L
, let
L_{\mathfrak{N}}\coloneqq\{a\ |\ \mathfrak{N}(a)=a\}
be the set of all fixed points of
L
with respect to
\mathfrak{N}
. Then
L_{\mathfrak{N}}
is a frame and
\mathfrak{N}
induces a frame homomorphism from
L
to
L_{\mathfrak{N}}
(see
[
34
, II Lemma 2.2]
).

The following proposition relates this in the case of
\mathsf{RId}(\textit{S})
.

Proposition 3.9
.

The map
\mathfrak{j}_{S}\colon\mathsf{RId}(S)\rightarrow\mathsf{RId}(S)
defined by

\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I):=\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}\bigl\{[a]_{k}\mid a\in I\bigr\},\qquad(I\in\mathsf{RId}(S))

is a nucleus.

Proof.

It is clear that for any
I,J\in\mathsf{RId}(S)
,
\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)
is a
k
-radical ideal, and if
I\subseteq J
, then
\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)\subseteq\mathfrak{j}_{S}(J)
.
First, observe that for any
a\in I
, we have
a\in[a]_{k}
. Therefore,
I\subseteq\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)
. Next, we take
x\in\mathfrak{j}_{S}(\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I))
. By definition,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{j}_{S}(\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I))=\mathop{\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}}\bigl\{[y]_{k}\mid y\in\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)\bigr\}.

Since
\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)
is a
k
-radical ideal,
y\in\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)
implies that
[y]_{k}\subseteq\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)
. Therefore
\mathfrak{j}_{S}(\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I))\subseteq\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)
, which forces the equality. Finally, for
I,J\in\mathsf{RId}(S)
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)\cap\mathfrak{j}_{S}(J)=\left(\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{a\in I}[a]_{k}\right)\cap\left(\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{b\in J}[b]_{k}\right)=\mathop{\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}}\limits_{a_{i}\in I,b_{j}\in J}[a_{i}b_{j}]_{k}.

Since
a_{i}b_{j}\in IJ\subseteq I\cap J
, we have
\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I)\cap\mathfrak{j}_{S}(J)\subseteq\mathfrak{j}_{S}(I\cap J)
, and the reverse inequality follows easily. Therefore,
\mathfrak{j}_{S}
is a nucleus on
\mathsf{RId}(S)
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

The map
\mathfrak{j}_{S}\colon\mathsf{RId}(S)\rightarrow\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
is a frame homomorphism.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
k
-radical ideals are the fixed points of the nuclues
\mathfrak{j}_{S}
, and then by applying
[
34
, II Lemma 2.2]
.
∎

Recall that a
sublocale
of
L
is a
regular subobject
of
L
. As a consequence of
[
34
, II Theorem 2.3]
, each sublocale corresponds to the subset of the form
L_{\mathfrak{N}}
for some nucleus
\mathfrak{N}
. A sublocale
L_{\mathfrak{N}}
is said to be
dense
if
L_{\mathfrak{N}}
contains the bottom element of
L
. Observe that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
is a sublocale of
\mathsf{RId}(S)
, and moreover, for a semiring
S
, the ideal
(0)
is a
k
-radical ideal. Given this discussion and Proposition
3.9
, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 3.11
.

For any semiring
S
,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
is a dense sublocale of
\mathsf{RId}(S)
.

Definition 3.1
.

For a morphism
f\colon S\rightarrow T
in
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
and for
I\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
, we define

\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)\colon\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})\rightarrow\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T)
by

\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I):=\sqrt[k]{\langle f(I)\rangle}.

Remark 3.12
.

Clearly,
y\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)
if and only if

y^{n}=t_{1}f(x_{1})+t_{2}f(x_{2})+\cdots+t_{n}f(x_{n}),

for some
0\neq n\in\mathds{N}
,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in T
, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in I
.

We want to show that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)\colon\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})\rightarrow\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T)
is a
coherent frame homomorphism
(a frame homomorphism preserving compact elements). without loss of generality,
\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}
and
\wedge
will both serve as operations in
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
and
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{T})
. Before proceeding, we state a lemma.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
x\in S
and
f\colon S\rightarrow T
be a semiring morphism. Then
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)([x]_{k})=[f(x)]_{k}
.

Proof.

First, we take
y\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)([x]_{k})\ \big(\coloneqq\sqrt[k]{\langle f([x]_{k})\rangle}\big)
. Then by Remark
3.12
, for some
0<n\in\mathds{N}
, we have

y^{n}=t_{1}f(x_{1})+t_{2}f(x_{2})+\cdots+t_{m}f(x_{m}),

where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in T
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in[x]_{k}
. That is,
x_{i}^{n_{i}}\in(x)
for some
0<n_{i}\in\mathds{N}
, for each
i
. Then
f(x_{i}^{n_{i}})\in f(\langle x\rangle)\subseteq\langle f(x)\rangle
. Hence by the definition of principal
k
-radical ideals,

f(x_{i})\in\sqrt[k]{\langle f(x)\rangle}=[f(x)]_{k}.

We conclude that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)([x]_{k})\subseteq[f(x)]_{k}
.
Conversely, for the reverse inclusion, observe that
x\in[x]_{k}
, then
f(x)\in\langle f([x]_{k})\rangle
. Therefore,
\langle f(x)\rangle\subseteq\langle f([x]_{k})\rangle
. Applying
\mathcal{V}_{k}
we get,
\mathcal{V}_{k}(\langle f([x]_{k})\rangle)\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{k}(\langle f(x)\rangle)
, and hence,

\displaystyle\sqrt[k]{\langle f(x)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\bigcap\mathcal{V}_{k}(\langle f(x)\rangle)

\displaystyle=\bigcap\mathcal{V}_{k}(\langle f([x]_{k})\rangle)

\displaystyle=\sqrt[k]{\langle f([x]_{k})\rangle}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)([x]_{k}).\qed

Proposition 3.14
.

A semiring homomorphism
f\colon S\rightarrow T
, induces a coherent frame homomorphism

\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)\colon\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})\rightarrow\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T).

Proof.

Let us first show that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)
is a lattice homomorphism. Since
f(0_{S})=0_{T}
and
f(1_{S})=1_{T}
, we have
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)([0_{S}])=[0_{T}]
and
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)([1_{S}])=[1_{T}]
. Now, to show that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)
preserves joins, it is enough to show that

\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}J)\subseteq\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)\bigvee\nolimits_{\!\!\!k}\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(J).

The opposite inclusion follows easily from the fact that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)
preserves order. Let us now suppose that
y\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}J)
. Then by Remark
3.12
, for some
n>0
, we have

y^{m}=t_{1}f(x_{1}^{k_{1}})+\cdots+t_{n}f(x_{n}^{k_{n}}),

for some
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in T
and
x_{1}^{k_{1}},\ldots,x_{n}^{k_{n}}\in I+J
. If we set each
x_{i}^{k_{i}}:=a_{i}+b_{i}
, for some
a_{i}\in I
and
b_{i}\in J
, then

y^{n}=\big(t_{1}f(a_{1}^{k_{1}})+\cdots+t_{n}f(a_{n}^{k_{n}})\big)+\big(t_{1}f(b_{1}^{k_{1}})+\cdots+t_{n}f(b_{n}^{k_{n}})\big),

which then implies that
y\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(J)
. Therefore,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}J)=\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)\bigvee_{\!\!\!k}\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(J)
.
The same strategy can be applied to show that

\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I\wedge J)=\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)\wedge\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(J).

We define
\varphi_{f}\colon\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T)\rightarrow\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
by
\varphi_{f}(J):=f^{-1}(J)
. Now,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)\subseteq J
in
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T)
if and only if
I\subseteq\varphi_{f}(J)
, that is,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)
and
\varphi_{f}
form a data of Galois connection. Hence, the left adjoint
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)
preserves arbitrary joins, forcing it to be a frame homomorphism.
From this discussion and Lemma
3.13
, we conclude that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)
is a coherent frame homomorphism.
∎

Proposition 3.15
.

The data
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S\xrightarrow{f}T)\coloneqq\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})\xrightarrow{\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)}\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T)
in
\mathds{C}\mathrm{oh}\mathds{F}\mathrm{rm}
, for any morphism
f\colon S\rightarrow T
in
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
, gives rise to a covariant functor from
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
to
\mathds{C}\mathrm{oh}\mathds{F}\mathrm{rm}
.

Proof.

We define the following:

\textstyle{{\mathsf{RId}_{k}:}}
\textstyle{{\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathds{C}\mathrm{oh}\mathds{F}\mathrm{rm}}}
\textstyle{{S}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)}
\textstyle{{T}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\mathsf{RId}_{k}(T)}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

First observe that, for any
I\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
, and the identity morphism
1_{S}\colon S\rightarrow S
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{RId}_{k}(1_{S})(I)=\sqrt[k]{\langle 1_{S}(I)\rangle}=I.

Therefore,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(1_{S})=1_{\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)}
.
Now, for any two composable pairs of morphisms
S\xrightarrow{f}T\xrightarrow{g}U
, we want to show that

\displaystyle\mathsf{RId}_{k}(g\circ f)=\mathsf{RId}_{k}(g)\circ\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f).

(*)

To do this, we take a radical ideal
I
from
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(\textit{S})
. Observe that

\displaystyle g\big(\langle f(I)\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\subseteq g\big(\sqrt[k]{\langle f(I)\rangle}\big)

\displaystyle\subseteq\sqrt[k]{\bigg(g\big(\sqrt[k]{\langle f(I)\rangle}\big)\bigg)}=\mathsf{RId}_{k}(g)(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I)).

Therefore,
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(g\circ f)(I)\subseteq\mathsf{RId}_{k}(g)(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I))
.
Conversely, the radical ideal
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(g\circ f)(I)
contains all elements
g(y)
, where,

y^{n}=t_{1}f(x_{1})+\cdots+t_{n}f(x_{n})\in\mathsf{RId}_{k}(f)(I),

for some
0\neq n\in\mathds{N}
;
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in T
and

U\wedge V=\text{Int}(U\cap V)
and
\vee
is considered to be the multiplication; then, for
U=(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)
and
V_{n}=\left(-\frac{1}{n},\frac{1}{n}\right)
, we have

U\vee\underset{n}{\bigwedge}V_{n}\neq\underset{n}{\bigwedge}U\vee V_{n}.

Let us denote the category of unitally bounded quantale and quantale homomorphisms by
\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}
; and the category of complete idealic semirings and semiring homomorphisms by
\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}
. Clearly, we have the forgetful functor
\mathcal{U}\colon\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}\rightarrow\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}
, that is,
\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}
is a (non-full) subcategory of
\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}
. In the opposite direction we have the functor
\mathsf{Id}_{k}(-)\colon\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}\rightarrow\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

The functor
\mathsf{Id}_{k}(-)
is the left adjoint of the functor
\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Let
\ddot{S}
be a complete, idealic semiring and
(Q,\vee,\ast,1)
be a unitally bounded quantale viewed as a semiring, and
f\colon\ddot{S}\rightarrow Q
is a semiring homomorphism. Since
\ddot{S}
is additively idempotent,
(\mathsf{Id}_{k}(\ddot{S}),\bigvee^{k},\ast)
is a unitally bounded quantale.
Define
\varphi_{f}\colon\mathsf{Id}_{k}(\ddot{S})\rightarrow Q
by

\varphi_{f}(I)\coloneqq\underset{x\in I}{\bigvee}f(x).

To show that
\varphi_{f}
is a morphism in
\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}
,
it is clear from the definition of
\varphi_{f}
, that it preserves order, that is, if
I\subseteq J
, then
\varphi_{f}(I)\leqslant\varphi_{f}(J)
. Since
Q
is unitally bounded,
\varphi_{f}(\langle x\rangle_{k})=f(x)
. Indeed if
y\leqslant xs
, for some
s\in\ddot{S}
, then
f(y)\leqslant f(x)\ast f(s)\leqslant f(x)
. Therefore,

\varphi_{f}(\ddot{S})=\varphi_{f}(\langle 1\rangle_{k})=f(1_{\ddot{S}})=1_{Q}.

Now, let
J=\underset{i\in\lambda}{\bigvee}^{k}I_{i}
. If
z\in J
, then
z\leqslant y_{1}+\cdots+y_{k}
, where
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}\subseteq\underset{i}{\bigcup}I_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle\varphi_{f}(\langle z\rangle_{k})

\displaystyle=f(z)

\displaystyle=f(y_{1})\vee\cdots\vee f(y_{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\underset{x\in J}{\bigvee}f(x)

\displaystyle=\underset{i}{\bigvee}(\underset{a\in I_{i}}{\bigvee}f(a))

\displaystyle=\underset{i}{\bigvee}\varphi_{f}(I_{i}).

The opposite inequality follows from the fact that
\varphi_{f}(I_{i})\leqslant\varphi_{f}(J)
, for all
i
. Lastly,
\varphi_{f}(IJ)=\varphi_{f}(I)\ast\varphi_{f}(J)
follows easily. Therefore,
\varphi_{f}\colon\mathsf{Id}(\ddot{S})\rightarrow Q
is a morphism in
\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}
; and by the definition of
\varphi_{f}
, becomes the unique morphism to make the following diagram commute:

\textstyle{{S}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{Id}_{k}(-)}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{{\Idk}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{!~\varphi_{f}}
\textstyle{{Q}.}

Conversely, if we have a morphism
\psi\colon\mathsf{Id}_{k}(\ddot{S})\rightarrow Q
, then define
f_{\psi}\colon\ddot{S}\rightarrow Q
by
f_{\psi}(s):=\psi(\langle s\rangle_{k})
. The fact that
f_{\psi}
is a semiring homomorphism follows from Lemma
5.3
. The definition of
f_{\psi}
makes it the unique semiring morphism for which the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{{S}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{Id}_{k}(-)}
\scriptstyle{!~f_{\psi}}
\textstyle{{\Id}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{{Q},}

From this discussion, we get hold of the data of unit
(\ddot{S}\xrightarrow{\eta_{\ddot{S}}}\mathcal{U}\circ\mathsf{Id}_{k}(\ddot{S}))_{\ddot{S}\in\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}})
and counit
(\mathsf{Id}_{k}(-)\circ\mathcal{U}(Q)\xrightarrow{\epsilon_{Q}}Q)_{Q\in\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top}}
. Hence
\mathsf{Id}_{k}(-)\dashv\mathcal{U}
.
∎

5.2.
r
-semirings

The subspace
\speck
and the whole space
\spec
can be contrasting in nature. For example, take
S=\mathds{Q}^{+}[\sqrt{2}]
with usual addition and multiplication. Let
I
be any proper ideal of
\mathds{Q}^{+}[\sqrt{2}]
. Then any non-zero element in
I
is of the form
a+b\sqrt{2}
such that
a\neq 0
and
b\neq 0
; otherwise, it will contradict the properness of
I
. Now observe that in
\mathds{Q},
either
a^{2}>2b^{2}
or
2b^{2}>a^{2}
. As a consequence, we can write either

\displaystyle\big(a+b\sqrt{2}\big)\bigg(\frac{a}{a^{2}-2b^{2}}\bigg)=\big(a+b\sqrt{2}\big)\bigg(\frac{b\sqrt{2}}{a^{2}-2b^{2}}\bigg)+1;\ \text{or},

\displaystyle\big(a+b\sqrt{2}\big)\bigg(\frac{b\sqrt{2}}{2b^{2}-a^{2}}\bigg)=\big(a+b\sqrt{2}\big)\bigg(\frac{a}{2b^{2}-a^{2}}\bigg)+1.

Therefore,
I
can never be a proper
k
-ideal. As a result,
\mathsf{Spec}_{k}(\mathds{Q}^{+}[\sqrt{2}])
contains only
(0)
. Although,
\mathsf{Spec}(\mathds{Q}^{+}[\sqrt{2}])
contains more than one point. Moreover,
\mathsf{Spec}_{k}(\mathds{Q}^{+}[\sqrt{2}])
is dense in
\mathsf{Spec}(\mathds{Q}^{+}[\sqrt{2}]
). In particular, if
(0)
is a
k
-prime ideal in a semiring
S
, then
\speck
is dense in
\spec
.

Definition 5.3
.

(
[
10
, Definition 3.3]
)
A semiring
S
is said to be an
r
-semiring
if each of its maximal ideals is a
k
-ideal

Let us denote the category of
r
-semirings and semiring homomorphisms by
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}_{r}
, which is a full subcategory of
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
. The following proposition concerns the images of the functors
\mathsf{Spec}
and
\mathsf{Spec}_{k}
within a restricted subcategory of
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}_{r}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
S
is an
r
-semiring and semisimple (that is,
\bigcap\mathrm{Max}(S)=0
), then
\speck
is dense in
\spec
.

Proof.

Since
\bigcap\mathrm{Max}(S)=0
, for every non-zero and non-unit element
a\in S
, there exists an
M\in\mathrm{Max}(S)
such that
a\notin M
. Therefore, in such a case,
D_{k}(a)\neq\emptyset
. Now let,
P\in\spec\setminus\speck
. Then, there exists a non-zero
a\in S\setminus U(S)
such that
P\in D(a)
. Suppose not; that is,
P\notin D(a)
for all non-zero
a\in S\setminus U(S)
, then
P=S\setminus U(S)
, and hence, becomes the unique maximal ideal in
S
, which contradicts our hypothesis of
\bigcap\mathrm{Max}(S)=0
.
So,
P\in D(a)
, for some non-zero and non-unit element of
S
, and we know
D(a)\cap\speck=D_{k}(a)\neq\emptyset
by our observation. Therefore, any open set containing
P
intersects
\speck
. All in all, any
P\in\spec\setminus\speck
is a limit point of
\speck
. Therefore,
\speck
is dense in
\spec
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The above class of semirings contains all positive semisimple semirings. For some semirings not in this class,
\speck
can be dense in
\spec
. For example, take the semiring
\mathds{N}
, which is not in our prescribed class. The principal ideals generated by the non-negative prime numbers and
\mathds{N}\setminus\{1\}
exhaust the list of prime ideals of
\mathds{N}
. Then
\mathsf{Spec}_{k}(\mathds{N})\setminus\mathsf{Spec}(\mathds{N})=\{\mathds{N}\setminus\{1\}\}
, and the only open set containing
\mathds{N}\setminus\{1\}
is the whole set
\mathsf{Spec}(\mathds{N})
. Therefore,
\mathsf{Spec}_{k}(\mathds{N})
is dense in
\mathsf{Spec}(\mathds{N})
.

Concluding remarks

As a next step, developing an enriched version of the functor calculus for semirings (
cf.

[
4
]
) could be an interesting problem. More precisely,
define functors from
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
to categories enriched over locales, toposes, or
\infty
-categories, and
study whether such enriched functors admit adjoint pairs, natural transformations, or monads reflecting algebraic or geometric structure. It would be interesting to see how these enriched structures capture more refined properties of semiring spectra.

Although we have discussed a few types of supports (e.g., Definition
4.3
), a systematic classification of support functors may be worth trying.
In this regard, one may take the approach to determine conditions under which two support functors are naturally isomorphic and explore further categorical properties of supports, e.g., limits, colimits, and adjunctions.
Investigating the internal logic of the support lattice
L
, e.g., whether it supports Heyting or modal operations, could also be an interesting question.

We have shown that
\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S)
is a coherent frame (Theorem
3.8
). Based on this fact, one may study the topos
\mathsf{Sh}(\mathsf{RId}_{k}(S))
of sheaves on the frame of
k
-radical ideals and analyze the role of global sections and stalks in detecting algebraic properties of
S
(
cf.

[
10
,
11
,
37
]
). Furthermore, exploring connections with tropical sheaf theory and idempotent analysis may shed some light on the internal structures of semirings.

Extending the idea of reticulation (see
[
21
,
23
,
24
]
) by constructing a universal reticulation functor
\mathsf{Ret}\colon\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}\to\mathds{D}\mathrm{ist}\mathds{L}\mathrm{at}_{0,1}
and studying whether this functor admits left or right adjoints, and determining its essential image could also be an interesting problem.
Using this functor, one can also examine semirings through coarse geometry and spectral realization.
En route
, analyse the adjunction (Theorem
5.4
)

\textstyle{\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsf{Id}_{k}}
\scriptstyle{U}
\scriptstyle{\bot}
\textstyle{\mathds{Q}\mathrm{uant}_{\top},}

and explore
the monad
\mathbb{T}=\mathcal{U}\circ\mathsf{Id}_{k}
on
\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}
, the Eilenberg-Moore category
\mathds{C}\mathrm{omp}\mathds{I}\mathrm{d}\mathds{R}\mathrm{ig}^{\mathbb{T}}
and its comparison to
\mathds{CR}\mathrm{ig}
, and extend these constructions to categorical doctrines or enriched Lawvere theories.

Another possible path of exploration is investigating the interrelations among
\mathsf{Spec}(S)
,
\mathsf{Spec}_{k}(S)
, and
\mathsf{Spec}_{\mathfrak{l}}(S)
by analysing the morphisms of spectral spaces (
cf.

[
46
]
) induced by the inclusion of ideal classes and determining topological invariants distinguishing the spaces, such as dimension, compactness, and sobriety.

Revisiting semirings arising from valuation theory (
[
38
]
), measure theory (e.g.,
[
9
]
), and logic (see
[
5
]
) from the functorial perspective may illuminate further information about structural aspects of semirings.
