Abstract
. In this note we discuss the Efimov effect emerging in a three-particle quantum system with zero-range interactions. In particular, we consider two non-interacting identical bosons plus a different lighter particle such that the interaction between a boson and the light particle is resonant. We also assume the validity of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. Under these conditions we show that the three-particle system exhibits infinitely many negative eigenvalues which accumulate at zero and satisfy the universal geometrical law characterising the Efimov effect. The result we find is a generalisation of previous results recently obtained in [ 12 , 24 ] . Keywords: Zero-Range Interactions, Three-Body Hamiltonians, Efimov Effect. MSC 2020: 81Q10; 35P20; 35Q40; 47B25.



1.
Introduction

The Efimov effect is an interesting physical phenomenon occurring in three-particle quantum systems in three dimensions
[
7
,
8
]
.
It is characterised by the appearance of an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues
E_{n}
of the three-body Hamiltonian, where
E_{n}\,\rightarrow\,0
as
n\,\rightarrow\,\infty
, under the specific condition that the two-particle subsystems have no bound states and at least two of them exhibit a zero-energy resonance or, equivalently, an infinite two-body scattering length.
For a review, see
e.g.

[
21
]
.

A remarkable feature of this effect is that the distribution of the eigenvalues satisfies the universal geometrical law

(1.1)

\frac{E_{n}}{E_{n+1}}\>\,\rightarrow\,\>e^{\frac{2\pi}{s}}\qquad\mbox{for\; $n\,\rightarrow\,\infty$}\,,

where the parameter
s>0
depends only on the mass ratios and possibly on the statistics of the particles.

For the first experimental evidence of Efimov quantum states we refer to
[
17
]
.

The mathematical analysis of the Efimov effect was initiated by Yafaev in 1974
[
28
]
.
He studied a symmetrised form of the Faddeev equations for the bound states of the three-particle Hamiltonian and proved the existence of an infinite number of negative eigenvalues.
In 1993 Sobolev
[
25
]
used a slightly different symmetrization of the equations and proved the following asymptotic behaviour

(1.2)

\lim_{z\,\rightarrow\,0^{-}}\frac{N\mspace{-1.5mu}(z)}{\lvert\log\lvert z\rvert\mspace{0.75mu}\rvert}=\frac{s}{2\pi}\,,

where
N\mspace{-1.5mu}(z)
denotes the number of eigenvalues smaller than
z<0
.
Note that (
1.2
) is consistent with the law (
1.1
).
In the same year Tamura
[
27
]
obtained the same result under more general conditions on the two-body potentials.
Other mathematical proofs of the effect were obtained by Ovchinnikov and Sigal in 1979
[
23
]
and Tamura in 1991
[
26
]
using a variational approach based on the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.
Further results on the subject have been obtained in
[
16
]
, in
[
4
]
, for the case of two identical fermions and a different particle, and in
[
15
]
, for a two-dimensional variant of the problem.
We stress that a rigorous derivation of the law (
1.1
) is lacking in the mathematical works mentioned above.

Regarding the Efimov effect, it is also worth mentioning the work of Minlos and Faddeev (
[
19
,
20
]
) on the problem of defining the Hamiltonian for a system of three bosons with zero-range interactions in three dimensions. They constructed a self-adjoint Hamiltonian by imposing suitable two-body boundary conditions on the coincidence hyperplanes,
i.e.
, where any two particle positions coincide, and a three-body boundary condition at the triple-coincidence point,
i.e.
, where all three particle positions coincide.
They also proved that such a Hamiltonian is unbounded from below due to the presence of an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues diverging to
-\infty
.
As a further interesting result, in the case of infinite two-body scattering length (corresponding to the resonant case), they proved the occurrence of the Efimov effect with a rigorous derivation of the law (
1.1
) (note that the works of Minlos and Faddeev predate those of Efimov).
Nevertheless, such a result is somewhat tainted by the fact that the Hamiltonian is unbounded from below and therefore unsatisfactory from the physical point of view.

We mention that the problem of constructing a lower bounded Hamiltonian for a three-body system with zero-range interactions has been recently approached in the literature (see,
e.g.
,
[
3
,
13
,
18
]
and, for the many particle case,
[
10
,
11
]
).
The idea is to introduce an effective three-body force acting only when the three particles are close to each other, preventing the fall-to-the-centre phenomenon.

In
[
9
]
, the role of the effective three-body force is played by a three-body hard-core repulsion.
It is shown that the Hamiltonian is self-adjoint and bounded from below; moreover, it is also proved that the Efimov effect occurs in the resonant case,
i.e.
, there exists an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues satisfying (
1.1
).

In this note, we discuss the approach to the Efimov effect in a three-particle system with zero-range interactions by using the Born-Oppenheimer approximation (for recent results in this direction, see
[
12
,
13
,
24
]
; see also
[
14
]
for a result in a model with separable potentials, and
[
5
]
for the analysis of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation in the one dimensional case).

More precisely, we consider a system made of two identical scalar bosons with mass
M
and another different spinless particle with mass
m
and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the coordinates of the bosons and the other particle, respectively.
We introduce the system of Jacobi coordinates
\boldsymbol{r}_{\!\mathrm{cm}},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined as

\boldsymbol{r}_{\!\mathrm{cm}}\vcentcolon=\frac{M(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\mspace{-1.5mu}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2})+m\,\boldsymbol{x}_{3}}{2M\mspace{-1.5mu}+m}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{y}\vcentcolon=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\mspace{-1.5mu}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\vcentcolon=\boldsymbol{x}_{3}-\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\mspace{-1.5mu}+\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{2}\,.

We study the problem in the centre-of-mass reference frame and we set
\hbar=1
.
The Hilbert space encoding the bosonic symmetry of the system is the following

(1.3)

L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{6}):=\left\{\psi\mspace{-1.5mu}\in\mspace{-1.5mu}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\>\big|\;\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\psi(\boldsymbol{x},-\boldsymbol{y})\mspace{-1.5mu}\right\}.

For simplicity, we also assume that the two identical bosons do not interact.
Then the heuristic Hamiltonian describing our three-particle system is expressed by

(1.4)

\widetilde{H}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}-\frac{1}{\nu}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}/2)+\delta(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}/2),

where

(1.5)

\mu=M,\qquad\nu=\frac{4Mm}{2M\mspace{-1.5mu}+m}\,.

The first step is to give a rigorous meaning to the above formal Hamiltonian on the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
.
By definition, the Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint extension of the symmetric operator

(1.6)

\displaystyle\dot{H}_{0}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\frac{1}{\nu}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{y}}\,,

\displaystyle D(\dot{H}_{0})=\left\{\psi\mspace{-1.5mu}\in\mspace{-1.5mu}L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\cap H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\>\big|\;\psi|_{\pi_{\pm}}\!=0\right\},

where

(1.7)

\pi_{\pm}\vcentcolon=\left\{\mspace{-1.5mu}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\mspace{-1.5mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}\;\big|\;\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{y}/2\right\}\mspace{-1.5mu}.

Using the results in
[
10
]
, we consider the bounded from below Hamiltonian
H,D(H)
in
L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
satisfying the two conditions:

(C-1)

\;H\psi=H_{0}\psi
, where
H_{0}
is the free three-body Hamiltonian, for
\psi\in D(H)
with
\psi|_{\pi_{\pm}}\!=0
;

(C-2)

\;\psi\in D(H)
satisfies the boundary condition on
\pi_{\pm}

(1.8)

\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{\xi(\pm\boldsymbol{y})}{\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\mp\boldsymbol{y}/2\rvert}+\Big(\alpha+\frac{\theta(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert}\Big)\,\xi(\pm\boldsymbol{y})+o(1)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{for}\;\;\boldsymbol{x}\,\rightarrow\,\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2

where

(1.9)

\xi(\boldsymbol{y})\mspace{-1.5mu}\vcentcolon=\!\lim\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\rightarrow\,\boldsymbol{y}/2}\;\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\mspace{-1.5mu}-\boldsymbol{y}/2\rvert\,\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),

\alpha\mspace{-1.5mu}\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\mathbb{R}
, and
\theta\,:\,\mathbb{R}^{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is smooth and satisfies the conditions

\theta(0)=1\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;\theta(r)=0\;\;\;\text{ for}\;\,\;r\geq r_{0}>0.

The parameter
\alpha
is the inverse of the two-body scattering length.
Moreover, the role of the function
\theta/\lvert\mspace{1.5mu}\cdot\mspace{1.5mu}\rvert
is to regularise the interaction when the positions of the three particles coincide, thereby preventing the fall-to-centre phenomenon (for additional technical details, we refer to
[
10
]
).
Notice that
r_{0}
can be chosen to be arbitrarily small.

Our aim is to study the eigenvalue problem

(1.10)

H\Psi=E\mspace{1.5mu}\Psi\,,\qquad E<0

and the occurrence of the Efimov effect for the Hamiltonian
H,D(H)
under the two assumptions:

-

\alpha=0
,
i.e.
, for an infinite two-body scattering length,

-

M\gg m
(equivalently,
\mu\gg\nu
).

More precisely, in this note we assume the validity of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, which means that

(1.11)

E\simeq E_{\mathrm{BO}}\,,\qquad\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\simeq\zeta(\boldsymbol{y})\mspace{1.5mu}\phi(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{y})

where, for any fixed
\boldsymbol{y}
,
\phi(\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,;\boldsymbol{y})
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)
of the formal one-body Hamiltonian with two point interactions placed at
\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2

(1.12)

\widetilde{h}(\boldsymbol{y})=-\frac{1}{\nu}\Delta+\delta(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}-\boldsymbol{y}/2)+\delta(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}+\boldsymbol{y}/2),

and
\zeta
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
E_{\mathrm{BO}}
of the one-body Hamiltonian

(1.13)

h_{\mathcal{E}}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\Delta+\mathcal{E}(\lvert\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mspace{1.5mu}\rvert).

Hence, the problem is reduced to first solving the eigenvalue problem for the fast dynamics associated with (
1.12
), and then treating the resulting eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)
as an effective potential for the slow dynamics described by (
1.13
).

It is important to stress that the one-body Hamiltonian
\widetilde{h}(\boldsymbol{y})
is obtained by freezing the slow dynamics in the three-body Hamiltonian
H
(for
\alpha=0
) and then considering the coordinate
\boldsymbol{y}
as a fixed parameter.
Hence, the rigorous counterpart
h(\boldsymbol{y}),D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))
of (
1.12
) is characterised as the self-adjoint and bounded from below operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfying the two conditions:

(c-1)

\;h(\boldsymbol{y})u=h_{0}u
, where
h_{0}=-\frac{1}{\nu}\Delta
is the free one-body Hamiltonian, for
u\in D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))
with
u(\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2)=0
;

(c-2)

\;u\in D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))
satisfies the boundary condition at
\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2

(1.14)

u(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{q}{\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\mp\boldsymbol{y}/2\rvert}+\frac{\theta(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)}{\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert}q+o(1)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{for}\;\;\boldsymbol{x}\,\rightarrow\,\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2

where

(1.15)

q\mspace{-1.5mu}\vcentcolon=\!\lim\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\,\rightarrow\,\boldsymbol{y}/2}\;\lvert\boldsymbol{x}\mspace{-1.5mu}-\boldsymbol{y}/2\rvert\,u(\boldsymbol{x}).

Such an operator is known as a Hamiltonian with non local point interactions at the points
\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2
, and its main properties are recalled in the next section.

The eigenvalues
E_{\mathrm{BO}}
of the one-body Hamiltonian
h_{\mathcal{E}}
are usually assumed to be a good approximation of the true eigenvalues
E
of the three-body Hamiltonian
H,D(H)
for
M\gg m
.
A rigorous proof of this fact is not discussed in this note, and it will be addressed in a future work.

The next theorem summarises our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

The following two statements hold:

(i)

for any fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Hamiltonian
h(\boldsymbol{y})
admits the negative eigenvalue

(1.16)

\mathcal{E}(|\boldsymbol{y}|)=-\frac{\Big(W\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(e^{\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)})-\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|\big)\Big)^{\!\!2}}{{\nu|\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}}

where
W:\big[\!-\mspace{-1.5mu}\frac{1}{e},\infty\big)\longrightarrow[-1,\infty)
denotes the principal branch of the Lambert function;

(ii)

the Hamiltonian
h_{\mathcal{E}}
admits an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues
\{E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\lim\limits_{n\,\rightarrow\,\infty}E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}=0
and

(1.17)

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,\infty}\frac{E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}}{E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n+1}}=\,e^{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}},

where
\,\beta=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\nu}W(1)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\;
and
\;W(1)\simeq 0.5671
.

Remark 1.1
.

The result expressed in Theorem
1.1
is essentially the same found in
[
12
,
24
]
. The only difference is that in these latter works our function
\theta
is replaced by the function

g(r)=e^{-r/\sqrt{2}}\Big(\sin\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}+\cos\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\,.

Such a function emerges a specific choice in the construction of the one-body Hamiltonian
h(\boldsymbol{y})
with (non-local) point interactions placed at
\boldsymbol{x}=\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2
in the context of the theory of self-adjoint extensions.
On the other hand, in this note the Hamiltonian
h(\boldsymbol{y})
is obtained as the limit for
M\,\rightarrow\,\infty
of the three-body Hamiltonian
H
.
In this sense we believe that our approach here is more natural from the physical point of view and, moreover, the result is slightly more general, since it holds for any choice of the function
\theta
satisfying the properties stated above.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will be given in the following two sections.

In Section
2
we recall definition and main properties of the Hamiltonian
h(\boldsymbol{y})
and then solve the corresponding eigenvalue problem. In particular, we compute the eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}(|\boldsymbol{y}|)
and the corresponding eigenfunction.

In Section
3
we study the eigenvalue problem for
h_{\mathcal{E}}
, showing that there is an infinite sequence of negative eigenvalues accumulating at zero and satisfying the asymptotic law (
1.17
). The corresponding eigenfunctions are also explicitly characterised.

2.
Fast Dynamics

We start this section giving the rigorous definition of the operator formally written in (
1.12
).
Let

G^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{e^{-\sqrt{\lambda\nu}\mspace{1.5mu}|\boldsymbol{x}|}}{|\boldsymbol{x}|}

for
\lambda>0
, be the solution to
\;(-\frac{1}{\nu}\Delta+\lambda)G^{\lambda}=\frac{4\pi}{\nu}\delta_{\boldsymbol{0}}\;
normalised so that it has the desired singular behaviour

G^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{x}|}-\sqrt{\lambda\nu}+o(1)

as
\boldsymbol{x}\,\rightarrow\,0.
Then (see
[
1
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
12
]
), the operator
h(\boldsymbol{y})
has domain

(2.1)

\displaystyle D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))=\bigg\{

\displaystyle u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):u=\omega^{\lambda}+q\,G^{\lambda}(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}+\boldsymbol{y}/2)+q\,G^{\lambda}(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}-\boldsymbol{y}/2),\;q\in\mathbb{C},\;\omega^{\lambda}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),

\displaystyle\omega^{\lambda}\big(\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2\big)=q\Big(\frac{\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)}{|\boldsymbol{y}|}+\sqrt{\lambda\nu}-G^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{y})\Big)\bigg\}\,.

The action of
h(\boldsymbol{y})
on
u\in D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))
is given by

(2.2)

(h(\boldsymbol{y})+\lambda)u=(h_{0}+\lambda)\omega^{\lambda}

where
h_{0}=-\frac{1}{\nu}\Delta
. Notice that
the last equality in (
2.1
) rephrases the boundary condition (
1.14
), (
1.15
) and, moreover, if
u\in D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))
and
u(\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2)=0
then (
2.2
) implies
h(\boldsymbol{y})u=h_{0}u
. Hence the two conditions (c-1), (c-2) stated in the Introduction are satisfied.

The operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
defined by (
2.1
), (
2.2
) is self-adjoint and bounded from below and it is known in the literature as a Schrödinger operator with non local point interactions at
\pm\boldsymbol{y}/2
. The non local character is due to the presence of the function
\theta
which makes the boundary condition, and therefore the strength of the interaction, dependent on the distance of the two point interactions. Notice that the form chosen for the function
\theta
is such that the boundary condition remains well defined in the limit
|\boldsymbol{y}|\,\rightarrow\,0
(whereas if
\theta=0
it loses its meaning in the limit).

The Hamiltonian
h(\boldsymbol{y})
defines a solvable model, in the sense that the spectrum and the (proper and generalised) eigenfunctions can be explicitly characterised.
In particular, it is easy to check that
h(\boldsymbol{y})
does not have any positive eigenvalues. Let’s now show the existence of a negative eigenvalue. Assume
-\lambda<0
is an eigenvalue and let
\phi=\omega^{\lambda}+q\Big(G^{\lambda}(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}+\boldsymbol{y}/2)+G^{\lambda}(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}-\boldsymbol{y}/2)\Big)\in D(h(\boldsymbol{y}))
be the corresponding eigenvector. Equation (
2.2
) immediately yields
(h_{0}+\lambda)\omega^{\lambda}=0
, and therefore
\omega^{\lambda}=0
.
Thus, by the boundary condition in (
2.1
) we conclude that
\lambda
is a solution of the equation

\frac{\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)}{|\boldsymbol{y}|}+\sqrt{\nu\lambda}-G^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{y})=0.

Introducing the new variable
s=\sqrt{\nu\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y}|+\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)
and recalling the expression of
G^{\lambda}
we rewrite the equation as

se^{s}=e^{\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)}\,.

Since
e^{\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)}>0
, the only solution to the above equation is given by
s=W(e^{\theta(|\boldsymbol{y}|)})
with
W
the principal branch of the Lambert function defined as
W=f^{-1}
with
f(s)=s\mspace{1.5mu}e^{s},s\geq-1
.
Note that
W
is smooth, non-negative, and increasing, with
W(e)=1
.
We conclude that the only eigenvalue of the operator
h(\boldsymbol{y})
is

\mathcal{E}(|\boldsymbol{y}|)=-\lambda=-\frac{\Big(W(e^{\theta(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)})-\theta(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)\Big)^{2}}{\nu|\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}.

In particular, recalling the assumption on
\theta
, we have that
\;\mathcal{E}(|\boldsymbol{y}|)=-\frac{W(1)^{2}}{\nu|\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}\;
for
\;|\boldsymbol{y}|\geq r_{0}.
Moreover, for
\;|\boldsymbol{y}|<r_{0}\;
we have that
\;\mathcal{E}(|\boldsymbol{y}|),\,
whose exact expression depends on
\;\theta,
is always continuous and bounded.
The corresponding eigenvector is

\phi(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mspace{1.5mu};\boldsymbol{y})=q\Big(G^{-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{|y|})}(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}+{\boldsymbol{y}}/{2})+G^{-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{|y|})}(\mspace{1.5mu}\boldsymbol{\cdot}-{\boldsymbol{y}}/{2})\Big)

with
q
normalising constant.

It is important to stress once again the regularising role of the function
\theta
. Indeed, in the case
\theta=0
the eigenvalue would reduce to
\;\mathcal{E}(|\boldsymbol{y}|)=-\frac{W(1)^{2}}{\nu|\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}\;
for any
\;\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and therefore the potential
\;\mathcal{E}(\lvert\boldsymbol{y}\rvert)\,
for the slow dynamics would be too singular for
\boldsymbol{y}\,\rightarrow\,0
.

3.
Slow Dynamics

Here we study the eigenvalue problem for the one-body Hamiltonian

h_{\mathcal{E}}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\Delta+\mathcal{E}(|\cdot|)\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;D(h_{\mathcal{E}})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

where
\mathcal{E}(|\cdot|)
is the continuous and bounded function given by (
1.16
). We restrict the analysis to the
s
-wave sector and therefore the problem to be solved is

(3.1)

\begin{cases}-\zeta^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{2}{r}\mspace{1.5mu}\zeta^{\prime}(r)+\mu\,\mathcal{E}(r)\mspace{1.5mu}\zeta(r)=\mu\mspace{1.5mu}E_{\mathrm{BO}}\,\zeta(r),\qquad\min\mathcal{E}\leq E_{\mathrm{BO}}<0,\,r\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\\

Recall that
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\subset C^{\mspace{1.5mu}0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

Consider the substitution

\displaystyle K_{i\beta}(z)=\sqrt{\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\pi}{2\mspace{1.5mu}z}}\,e^{-z}\!\left[1-\tfrac{4\beta^{2}+1}{8\mspace{1.5mu}z}+O(z^{-2})\right]\!,\qquad\text{for }\,z\,\rightarrow\,{\scriptstyle+}\mspace{1.5mu}\infty,

(3.5b)

\displaystyle K_{i\beta}(z)=-\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{\beta\sinh(\pi\beta)\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}\sin\!\big(\beta\log\tfrac{z}{2}-\vartheta_{\beta}\big)+O(z^{2}),\qquad\text{for }\,z\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}\mspace{-1.5mu},

with
\vartheta_{\beta}\vcentcolon=\arg\Gamma(1+i\beta)\in(-\pi,\pi]
.

Hence, the problem (
3.2
) has the following solution:

(3.6)

u_{\lambda}(r)=\begin{cases}A\,w_{\lambda}(r),\qquad&0\leq r\leq r_{0},\\
B\mspace{1.5mu}\sqrt{r\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r),\quad&r>r_{0},\end{cases}

with
A,B
proper constants such that

\lim_{r\,\rightarrow\,r_{0}^{-}}u_{\lambda}(r)=\lim_{r\,\rightarrow\,r_{0}^{+}}u_{\lambda}(r),\qquad\lim_{r\,\rightarrow\,r_{0}^{-}}u_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)=\lim_{r\,\rightarrow\,r_{0}^{+}}u_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r).

The previous matching condition is due to the embedding
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow C^{\mspace{1.5mu}1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Therefore, one must require

(3.7)

\begin{cases}w_{\lambda}(r_{0})\,A-\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})\mspace{1.5mu}B=0,\\
w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r_{0})\,A-\left[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda\,r_{0})+\lambda\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}^{\prime}(\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})\right]\!B=0.\end{cases}

In order to exclude the identically zero solution
u_{\lambda}\equiv 0
, one cannot admit
A=B=0
.
Consequently, the matrix associated with the previous linear system of equations must be singular:

-w_{\lambda}(r_{0})\left[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda\,r_{0})+\lambda\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}^{\prime}(\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})\right]+\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})\,w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r_{0})=0,

namely,

(3.8)

\left[\frac{w_{\lambda}(r_{0})}{2}-r_{0}\,w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r_{0})\right]\mspace{-1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda\,r_{0})+\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}\,w_{\lambda}(r_{0})\,K_{i\beta}^{\prime}(\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})=0.

Our goal is to prove that (
3.8
) can be solved for infinitely many energy levels accumulating at zero according to Efimov’s asymptotic geometrical law.

Combining
[
22
, eqs. 10.27.3, 10.29.1, and 10.30.2]
, one can compute the asymptotic expansion for the derivative of the Macdonald function as
z\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}

\displaystyle K_{i\beta}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\mspace{1.5mu}z}\left[\Gamma(1\mspace{-1.5mu}-i\beta)(\tfrac{1}{2}z)^{i\beta}+\Gamma(1\mspace{-1.5mu}+i\beta)(\tfrac{1}{2}z)^{-i\beta}\right]+O(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lvert\Gamma(1\mspace{-1.5mu}+i\beta)\rvert}{2\mspace{1.5mu}z}\left[e^{-i\vartheta_{\mspace{-1.5mu}\beta}}\mspace{1.5mu}e^{i\beta\ln(\frac{1}{2}z)}+e^{i\vartheta_{\mspace{-1.5mu}\beta}}\mspace{1.5mu}e^{-i\beta\ln(\frac{1}{2}z)}\right]+O(z),

where we have used
\Gamma(\bar{z})=\overline{\Gamma(z)}
. Hence, by exploiting
[
2
, eq. 5.4.3 p. 137]
for the absolute value of the Gamma function, one gets

(3.9)

z\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}^{\prime}(z)=-\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi\beta}{\sinh(\pi\beta)\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}\cos\!\big(\beta\ln\tfrac{z}{2}-\vartheta_{\beta}\big)+O(z^{2}),\qquad\text{for }\,z\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}.

Introducing the parameters

(3.10)

\displaystyle a\vcentcolon=\frac{w_{\mspace{1.5mu}0}(r_{0})}{2}-r_{0}\,w_{\mspace{1.5mu}0}^{\prime}(r_{0}),

\displaystyle b\vcentcolon=\beta\,w_{\mspace{1.5mu}0}(r_{0}),

condition (
3.8
) can be equivalently rewritten as

(3.11)

-a\sin\!\big(\vartheta_{\beta}-\beta\mspace{1.5mu}\ln\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}}{2}\big)+b\cos\!\big(\vartheta_{\beta}-\beta\mspace{1.5mu}\ln\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}}{2}\big)=F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}(\lambda),

for some continuous function
F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\lim_{\lambda\,\rightarrow\,0^{+}}\!F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}(\lambda)=0,\qquad\text{for all }\,r_{0}\mspace{-1.5mu}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

We stress that
a
and
b
cannot both be equal to zero.
Otherwise, it follows that
w_{\mspace{1.5mu}0}(r_{0})=w_{\mspace{1.5mu}0}^{\prime}(r_{0})=0
, and the initial value problem (
3.3
) for
\lambda=0
with such conditions has the identically zero function as a solution.
By uniqueness,
w_{\mspace{1.5mu}0}(r)=0
for all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
, contradicting
w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.

Let us solve (
3.11
) in the case where
F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}
is replaced by zero:

(3.12)

\displaystyle-a

\displaystyle\sin\!\big(\vartheta_{\beta}-\beta\mspace{1.5mu}\ln\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}}{2}\big)+b\cos\!\big(\vartheta_{\beta}-\beta\mspace{1.5mu}\ln\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}}{2}\big)=0

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\tan\big(\vartheta_{\beta}-\beta\mspace{1.5mu}\ln\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}}{2}\big)=\frac{b}{a},\qquad\text{provided }\,a\neq 0,

thus,

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\beta}-\beta\mspace{1.5mu}\ln\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\lambda\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}}{2}=\arctan\tfrac{b}{a}+n\mspace{1.5mu}\pi,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}

(3.13)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}^{0}=\tfrac{2}{r_{0}}\,e^{\frac{\vartheta_{\mspace{-1.5mu}\beta}-\,\alpha}{\beta}}\,e^{-n\,\frac{\pi}{\beta}},\qquad\alpha\vcentcolon=\arctan{\tfrac{b}{a}}.

Note that
\alpha=0
in the case
b=0
, while (
3.13
) remains valid for
a=0
upon choosing
\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}
.
In the following, we show that equation (
3.11
) admits infinitely many solutions
\lambda_{n}
with the same asymptotic behaviour as
\lambda_{n}^{0}
as
n\,\rightarrow\,{\scriptstyle+}\mspace{1.5mu}\infty
.

To this end, let us consider the equation

(3.14)

\sin\eta=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\!\mspace{-1.5mu}\sqrt{a^{2}\mspace{-1.5mu}+b^{2}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta}{\beta}}\big),\qquad\eta\in\big(\!-\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mspace{1.5mu}\big).

Since
\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta}{\beta}}\in\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{-\frac{\pi}{2\mspace{1.5mu}\beta}},\,\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\pi}{2\mspace{1.5mu}\beta}}\big)
and
\lim\limits_{n\,\rightarrow\,{\scriptstyle+}\mspace{1.5mu}\infty}\!F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta}{\beta}}\big)=0
, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(v,r_{0},\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\frac{\lvert F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta}{\beta}}\big)\rvert}{\sqrt{a^{2}\mspace{-1.5mu}+b^{2}\mspace{1.5mu}}}<1,\qquad\forall\,n\geq n_{0}.

Therefore, for all
n\geq n_{0}
, (
3.14
) has a unique solution
\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}\mspace{-1.5mu}\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(\!-\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mspace{1.5mu}\big)
satisfying
\lim\limits_{n\,\rightarrow\,{\scriptstyle+}\mspace{1.5mu}\infty}\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}=0
.

We now have all the elements required to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{n}^{0}\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a solution to (
3.12
).
Then, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(v,r_{0},\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}
such that if
n\geq n_{0}
, there exists a unique solution
\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}\!\in\mspace{-1.5mu}\big(\!-\mspace{-1.5mu}\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mspace{1.5mu}\big)
to (
3.14
), with
a,b
given by (
3.10
).
Moreover, equation (
3.8
) admits infinitely many solutions of the form

(3.15)

\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\,\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}/\beta},\qquad n\geq n_{0}.

Proof.

We have already discussed the existence and uniqueness of
\eta_{n}
; therefore, we focus on the proof of (
3.15
).
Equation (
3.8
) is equivalent to (
3.11
), which can, in turn, be rewritten by replacing
\lambda
with
\lambda^{0}_{n}\,e^{\frac{\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}}{\beta}}

-a\sin(\alpha+n\mspace{1.5mu}\pi-\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n})+b\cos(\alpha+n\mspace{1.5mu}\pi-\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n})=F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}}{\beta}}\big).

Hence,

-a\mspace{1.5mu}\big(\sin\alpha\mspace{1.5mu}\cos\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}\mspace{-1.5mu}-\cos\alpha\mspace{1.5mu}\sin\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}\big)+b\mspace{1.5mu}\big(\cos\alpha\mspace{1.5mu}\cos\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}\mspace{-1.5mu}+\sin\alpha\mspace{1.5mu}\sin\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}\big)=(-1)^{n}F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}}{\beta}}\big).

Since
-a\sin\alpha+b\cos\alpha=0
(see (
3.12
)), one has

(3.16)

\big(a\mspace{1.5mu}\cos\alpha+b\mspace{1.5mu}\sin\alpha\big)\sin\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}=(-1)^{n}F_{\mspace{-0.75mu}r_{0}}\big(\lambda_{n}^{0}\,e^{\frac{\eta_{\mspace{1.5mu}n}}{\beta}}\big).

Lastly, exploiting the identities

\displaystyle\cos\alpha=\frac{a}{\!\mspace{-1.5mu}\sqrt{a^{2}\mspace{-1.5mu}+b^{2}}},

\displaystyle\sin\alpha=\frac{b}{\!\mspace{-1.5mu}\sqrt{a^{2}\mspace{-1.5mu}+b^{2}}},

it is straightforward that (

) is equivalent to (
3.14
) with

; moreover, (
3.14

This concludes the proof.

∎

The preceding analysis yields the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Using the notation of Proposition
3.1
, the eigenvalue problem (
3.1
) has infinitely many negative solutions
\{E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}\}_{n\mspace{1.5mu}=\mspace{1.5mu}n_{0}}^{\infty}

\lim\limits_{n\,\rightarrow\,\infty}E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}=0
.
Moreover, for all
n\geq n_{0}

E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}=-\frac{4}{\mu\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}^{2}}\,e^{\frac{2}{\beta}\big(\vartheta_{\beta}\mspace{1.5mu}-\,\arctan\frac{b}{a}\mspace{1.5mu}+\,\eta_{n}\big)}\,e^{-\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\mspace{1.5mu}n}

u_{n}(r)=\begin{cases}A_{n}\,w_{\lambda_{n}}(r),\qquad&0\leq r\leq r_{0},\\
B_{n}\mspace{1.5mu}\sqrt{r\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}K_{i\beta}(\lambda_{n}\mspace{1.5mu}r),\qquad&r>r_{0}\end{cases}

A_{n}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}}{w_{\lambda_{n}}\mspace{-1.5mu}(r_{0})}\,K_{i\beta}(\lambda_{n}\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})\mspace{1.5mu}B_{n},\qquad&\text{if }\,w_{\lambda_{n}}\mspace{-1.5mu}(r_{0})\neq 0,\\
\frac{K_{i\beta}(\lambda_{n}\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})+2\mspace{1.5mu}\lambda_{n}\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0}\,K_{i\beta}^{\prime}(\lambda_{n}\mspace{1.5mu}r_{0})}{2\mspace{1.5mu}\sqrt{r_{0}\mspace{1.5mu}}\mspace{1.5mu}w_{\lambda_{n}}^{\prime}\!(r_{0})}\,B_{n},\qquad&\text{if }\,w_{\lambda_{n}}\mspace{-1.5mu}(r_{0})=0,\end{cases}

The result is derived from Proposition
3.1

u_{\lambda}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
(implying
w_{\lambda}(r_{0})\neq 0\lor w_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r_{0})\neq 0

∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition, one obtains the proof of the asymptotic geometrical behaviour of the energy levels in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,\infty}\frac{E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n}}{E_{\mathrm{BO};\,n\mspace{1.5mu}+1}}=\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,\infty}e^{\frac{2}{\beta}(\eta_{n}-\,\eta_{n\mspace{1.5mu}+1})}\,e^{\frac{2\pi}{\beta}}=e^{2\pi/\beta}.
