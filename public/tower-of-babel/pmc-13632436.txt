Dynamic Modeling and Model Predictive Control of an Oxygen Distribution Pipeline System
Authors: Hakeem Hamdan, Ankit Chachad, Helen H. Lou, Qiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxygen distribution pipelines are necessary infrastructure for delivering a consistent supply to a wide range of industries. However, the pipeline system also presents inherent risks associated with pure oxygen transport and the challenge of maintaining designed flow rate and pressure conditions, which must be controlled effectively. In this paper, an oxygen distribution pipeline system has been modeled and simulated with traditional proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers. To further improve its dynamic overall performance under a series of abrupt disturbances affecting control valves and pressures at key locations throughout the pipeline system, a model predictive control (MPC) strategy was also developed and virtually demonstrated. The simulation results demonstrate that MPC provides more effective and robust pressure regulation under abrupt disturbances, highlighting its capability to outperform traditional PID control and improve operator confidence in real-world oxygen pipeline applications.

Introduction

Across the U.S., 10.993 million tonnes of oxygen per year is produced at 137 production sites, which is widely employed by many economic entities including, steel manufacturing, petrochemical production, welding, glassmaking, pulp and paper bleaching, medical use, and water treatment. Pure oxygen can be produced by different methods. The dominant method for large-scale production of high-purity oxygen is to perform cryogenic distillation via air separation units (ASUs). Other commercialized methods such as pressure swing adsorption (PSA), vacuum pressure swing adsorption (VPSA), membrane separation, and water electrolysis are also widely used. − As oxygen is a powerful oxidizer that can dramatically increase fire, combustion, and explosion risks, its production, storage, and distribution need sufficient care. Typically, an oxygen producer can serve several local consumers. Thus, methods of transport can differ. If demand is low, liquefied oxygen transport via special cryogenic container trucks is the simplest and most cost-effective way, and there is also the option for gas cylinders if required supply needs are especially low. For large-scale industrial applications, oxygen is commonly supplied through pipeline networks, which enable the continuous delivery of large quantities of gaseous oxygen to processes with substantial and sustained oxygen demand. Note that industrial oxygen pipelines are relatively short compared to other gas pipelines. One of the world largest oxygen suppliers, Air Liquide Inc. has over 2000 miles of pipelines supplying oxygen to its 350 consumers. For a reference, the natural gas industry has over 3 million miles of pipelines.

Pipelines transporting oxygen present unique risks. These risks stem from both intrinsic properties of oxygen as well as pressure and flow rate variations induced by transporting process upsets or fluctuating customer demands. Under worst-case conditions, such disturbances can lead to ignition, severe fires, and rapid combustion or mechanical failures of the pipeline. Several oxygen-specific ignition mechanisms contribute to this risk. One such mechanism is particle or jet impingement, in which high-velocity oxygen streams or entrained particles strike the pipe wall, generating localized heating that can initiate the ignition. Another significant hazard is rapid adiabatic compression, where a sudden increase in pressure results in a sharp temperature rise capable of igniting combustible materials within the system. Therefore, oxygen pipeline networks must operate within carefully controlled pressure and flow rate limits, meaning abrupt changes in operating conditions should be avoided to minimize potential risks.

Note that disturbances to pressure and flow rate can occur for various reasons. Pipelines are designed to maintain specific flow rates and pressures for their customers, who may require adjustments in supply. However, external interventions can also affect the pipeline operations. Cyberattacks and security-related incidents have been increasing in frequency within the chemical process industry. Pipelines are among the most frequently targeted assets and pose significant fire- and explosion-related risks. In a database of cybersecurity-related incidents compiled by Moreno et al., pipelines accounted for 85% of cyber events with at least one fatality. Particularly, oxygen distribution systems nowadays almost exclusively depend on remote controls via supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems, which often utilize standardized control technologies with external connections, thereby providing many hidden access opportunities for technologically dangerous hackers. , Disruptions to pipelines also carry substantial sabotage potential, slowing or halting operations and affecting customers who rely on their supply for days or weeks. Simulation of pipeline performance under both normal and abnormal conditions will be instrumental in assessing potential hazards and characterizing the effectiveness or limitations of control strategies at the process level.

Various control strategies are employed for the oxygen pipeline networks. The basic one is the proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control strategy, which compares a process control variable (CV) to its setpoint (SP) and adjusts a relevant manipulated variable (MV) periodically in feedback ways under various disturbances. Based on regular PID controls, the advanced process control (APC) strategy uses sophisticated and hierarchical techniques to simultaneously manage multiple CVs and MVs to predict future process behavior and optimize the economic performance of the control system. Model predictive control (MPC) is a typical APC. There are two primary merits for MPC compared with PID controls: (i) multiple input and multiple output (MIMO) handling capability and (ii) predictive capability. MIMO allows all MVs to synergistically work together to drive all CVs toward their designated SPs within a reasonable control time horizon. This can excel over single input to single output (SISO) PID controllers, where each MV is tuned to support one CV in a control loop without synergies among multiple different control loops. Therefore, communication among all of the CVs and MVs from MPC is more effective and efficient. Taking this advantage, the predictive capability of MPC employs a certain amount of time into the future where the MPC calculates and predicts how each MV changes will affect the overall control performance, allowing us to determine the best course of control actions.

Given complications incurred during the development and implementation of MPC, hindrances that limit its industrial application should also be mentioned. One of the main concerns is its expensive cost, meaning it not only needs proficient people taking time to develop proper MPC but also needs well-trained personnel monitoring and maintaining MPC applications regularly. Another main hindrance is the potential lack of confidence under uncertainty. Standard PID controllers are straightforward and simple to understand and analyze. However, the MPC acting with the MIMO and prediction horizon could perform in a way not inherently clear to onsite operators or even engineers.

MPC systems for industrial applications have been developed in the past. A few models were found regarding the application of an MPC system for pressure and flow regulation in the gas distribution. An oxygen distribution pipeline system model by Zhu et al. was created through a series of differential equations relating flow and pressure changes between discrete node locations. In this study, several cases were performed where certain scenarios arise that require the MPC to act in controlling oxygen production sites to regulate pressure at several key node locations. Another similar model was developed by Rahimi and Fanaei, although for a natural gas system. This controller operated a compressor to control pressure to maintain customer flow rate requests along the length of the pipeline. This model similarly had designated node locations with differential equations calculating the flow and pressure. Similarly, Bu and Aalto also developed natural gas pipelines with MPC control systems using differential equations to represent pipeline dynamics and controller functionality. , Applying MPC to pipeline systems purely based on differential equations is theoretically viable, which, however, would have caveats and limitations on industrial applications. The major root cause is that pipeline system dynamics are highly nonlinear in nature due to complex pressure-flow relationships, gas thermodynamics, and many other factors. The above-discussed MPC models for gas pipeline pressure regulation provide a solid foundation and demonstrate strong application potential, although their modeling is primarily based on differential equations with relatively simplified hydraulic and thermodynamic properties. This leaves room for further development and improvement.

In this paper, we further investigated an MPC development, which employs an unconstrained dynamic matrix control (DMC) and its application for an oxygen distribution pipeline system using Aspen HYSYS v15 and the Peng–Robinson equation of state. , A new pipeline model with a DMC control strategy has been developed, implemented, and simulated with Aspen HYSYS v15. Meanwhile, four cases with various abrupt disturbances affect control valves and pressures at key locations throughout the pipeline system. These disturbance cases focus on the process-level response of the oxygen distribution system to abnormal operating conditions irrespective of their origin. The case studies demonstrate that DMC provides effective and robust pressure regulation under abrupt disturbances, highlighting its advantages and potential to outperform traditional PID control and improve operator confidence in real-world oxygen pipeline applications.

Process Modeling and DMC Development

Description of the Studied Pipeline System

The layout of this oxygen distribution pipeline is based on the structure from Zhu et al., which has been modeled as shown in Figure . Several significant modifications have been made to better mimic industrial applications including: (1) rigorous hydraulic and thermodynamic properties are employed for this system modeling and simulation through the use of Aspen HYSYS v15 using the Peng–Robinson equation of state as the flowsheet thermodynamic package; (2) additional flow and pressure regulation valves, i.e., control valves, check valves, and pressure relief valves, have been included with PID and DMC control loops to better address control and safety concerns; (3) detailed pipeline segment information, e.g., size and length, have been built in the pipeline system model; (4) ordinary operating conditions including pressure, flow rate, and temperature for each feed stream have been set; and (5) instead of fixing the flow rate for each customer withdrawal streams from Zhu et al., this paper fixes the customer-withdrawal pressures, allowing the corresponding flow controllers to dynamically adjust the withdrawal flow rates.

MPC (DMC) Control Strategy Development

Figure shows the advanced control strategy of the studied oxygen pipeline system, including the regular PID loops for monitoring and controlling their associated CVs and the MPC (DMC) controller with its CVs and MVs. Seven regular PID loops handle local control actions for customer withdrawal streams (flow rate controllers of FIC-101 through FIC-107) and the pressure controller of PIC-104 controlling the letdown valve PV4. The MPC controller coordinates multiple control loops in the system. As shown in Figure , the block MPC-100 is the implemented DMC controller. Its three CVs are outlet pressures of pipeline streams S6, S11, and S29, which are denoted as pressures P S6, P S11, and P S29, respectively. The three pressures are critical to controlling the overall operation of the entire pipeline system. The six MVs of MPC-100 include rotation speeds of compressors K1 through K3 and opening percentages of pressure control valves PV1 through PV3. Note that each of the three rotation-speed MVs from the DMC controller is actually cascaded to a local speed controller at compressors K1 through K3 (see Figure ). This means that the three outputs (MVs) from MPC-100 provide remote setpoints for local rotation-speed controllers of compressors K1 through K3, and each of these local controllers will control the rotation speed by manipulating the power load (duty) of the corresponding compressor.

DMC control strategy for the studied oxygen distribution pipeline system.

It should be noted that MPC-100 is implemented as a direct regulatory controller for some MVs in this study. In most industrial MPC applications, MPC is instead implemented as a supervisory control layer that provides setpoints to underlying regulatory PID controllers. This hierarchical MPC–PID structure and the considerations involved in control structure selection are discussed by Qin and Badgwell and Skogestad. However, direct MPC control without an underlying PID regulatory layer for all MVs has also been demonstrated in previous applications. The present work uses this MPC configuration based on rigorous process modeling to investigate its system-level performance for the oxygen pipeline network as a direct regulatory controller.

Table summarizes the detailed control strategies for each regular PID control controller and the DMC controller as shown in Figure . For the MPC-100 controller, process gains (the magnitude changing ratio of CVs and MVs between two steady-state conditions) and settling times (time duration to reach a new steady state) need to be provided to MPC-100. This model identification process is accomplished through multiple step tests, meaning to apply step changes to each CV setpoint and collect the resulting system response, particularly paying attention to settling time and oscillatory behavior. The model identification process is iteratively performed and adjusted until a satisfactory system dynamic performance is achieved. From the obtained step-response data following an initial MV change process, gains and settling times are represented by a series of incremental gains at discrete time steps over the duration of the settling time. These step-response parameters describe the dynamic behavior of the process and form the basis of the predictive model used by the MPC-100 controller in the construction of the process gain matrix A. The relationship between these step-response data, process gains, MV movements, and the construction and functionality of the MPC-100 controller is described in greater detail in Section . The final determined model parameters allow the DMC controller to capture the full dynamic mapping between the MV movements and CV responses. Thus, the obtained model parameters enable the DMC controller to predict how changes in MVs affect the CVs during operation of the pipeline system.

Because the MV limits in eq are imposed at the equipment level rather than within the DMC optimization, a calculated control action may cause an MV to reach its physical operating limit. In such cases, the applied MV remains at the corresponding upper or lower bound, and the controller calculates the subsequent control action at the next control interval on the basis of the resulting process response. Consequently, periods in which an MV remains at its operating limit represent actuator saturation rather than optimization constraints imposed within the DMC formulation.

In summary, the DMC algorithm predicts the future evolution of CVs using a linear step-response model that relates MVs changes to identify the optimal changes of MVs. This relationship is captured through a set of step-response matrices of A i , which are assembled into the gains matrix A to describe the dynamic behavior of the system over the prediction horizon. Future control actions are represented by the sequence of MV increments ΔU, which are optimized over a finite control horizon. The DMC computes the error between the predicted outputs and the reference setpoints and minimizes weighted quadratic cost functions balancing output tracking performance and control effort. This optimization computes a future sequence of control moves and continuously updates it at each time step as new process measurements become available.

PID Control Strategy Development for Comparison

In addition to the DMC control strategy development, this paper also builds a control strategy using PID only for the studied oxygen distribution pipeline system to comprehensively compare both control performances and highlight the merits of the DMC strategy. As shown in Figure , the PID-based control strategy has implemented six additional PID control loops in addition to all the regular controllers from Figure and Table . Specifically, to control the critical outlet pressure of three pipeline streams of S6, S11, and S29, two PID controllers are added for each stream: one is to manipulate the rotation speed of the relevant compressor (K1, K2, and K3), and the other is to manipulate the valve opening related to the relevant ASU inlet valve (PV1, PV2, and PV3). Again, each rotation speed control of K1 through K3 is cascaded to a local speed controller by manipulating the energy duty of each compressor.

Dynamic Performance Evaluation under Different Cases

To examine the performance of the developed DMC control strategy and its comparison with the PID-based control strategy, four upset cases have been studied, where critical disturbances were made to those concerned CVs. These disturbances can occur either due to hardware malfunctions such as valves failing to open or close or because of intended or unintended human-induced control maloperation. For any of these cases, the oxygen distribution pipeline system is assumed initially at its steady-state operating condition and thus all controller parameters such as the gains or integration times are kept the same for all studied cases. For controllers not unique to either the DMC- or PID-based control strategy, including FIC-101 through FIC-107 and PIC-104, the controller setpoints are consistent between the two control strategies and remain unchanged from their steady-state values, unless otherwise specified within each case.

The dynamic responses of the two control strategies are compared based on relevant and appreciable differences observed in pressure deviations from setpoints, the time required for pressures to reach or recover to their setpoints, and changes in the associated manipulated variables. Case 1: Letdown Valve (PV4) Suddenly Full Open

The letdown valve (PV4) is controlled by the pressure controller PIC-104, which has the primary task of controlling the inlet pressure of stream S17 to a normal setpoint pressure of 143.0 psig. The letdown valve reduces the pressure of supplied oxygen from ASU1 and ASU2 as it flows from the higher-pressure side to the lower-pressure side (see Figure ), preventing mechanical damage to process equipment of the lower-pressure side. Under the initial steady-state operating conditions, this letdown valve opens at about 64% with a pressure drop of 21.5 psig. In this case, PV4 is assumed to have an instant open at hour 1.0 from 64% to 100% due to some sudden malfunction, making the oxygen flow rate across PV4 to experience a big increment. This will also result in pressure changes on both sides quickly, that is, the pressure drops on the higher-pressure side while the pressure rises on the lower-pressure side.

Under such a sudden valve opening change, dynamic simulation results under both DMC and PID-based control strategies are shown in Figure . System performances under both control strategies are shown side-by-side for comparison in Figures a,c,e for the DMC control strategy and Figures b,d,f for the PID-based control strategy. Figures a,b compare pressure responses of three CVs (P S6, P S11, and P S29), which clearly demonstrate fewer upset impacts from the DMC control strategy than the PID-based control strategy. For the DMC control strategy, the maximum pressure error is in PS11, reaching a minimum pressure of 161.8 psig and taking 0.97 h to return to the pressure setpoint of 165.0 psig. S29, on the low-pressure side of this oxygen pipeline, decreases marginally in pressure, reaching 141.7 psig. On the low-pressure side of the PID-based strategy, S29 rises in pressure after opening PV4, reaching a maximum pressure of 143.0 psig. Figures c,d compare dynamic opening changes of four valves (PV1 through PV4). As PV4 is the introduced upset for this case, its step increase is the same at both Figures c,d. Between the two control strategies, both strategies experienced a similar final valve increment change for each of the three pressure control valves.

Case 2: Product Stream Valves of FV5 and FV7 Clogged

Case 2 investigates system dynamic responses under an unpredicted shut-off control valves FV5 and FV7 at Hour 1.0, which serve for oxygen withdrawals for customers 5 and 7. As indicated in Table , FV5 regulates flow rate of S29 to Customer 5, with a normal oxygen drawing rate of 255.8 lbmol/h; and FV7 controls oxygen flow rate of S34 to Customer 7 with a normal rate of 377.9 lbmol/h. The sudden shut off of these two valves at Hour 1.0 causes an instantaneous drop in demand of 633.7 lbmol/h of oxygen, which requires the entire system responses to handle the pressure buildup issue as it is now producing excess oxygen due to Customers 5 and 7 no longer exporting expected amounts.

When the incident is introduced, pressures near Customers 5 and 7 will increase quickly as shown by P S29 in both Figures a,b and then settle back. Obviously, the P S29 upset magnitude under DMC strategy, reaching a maximum pressure of 153.7 psig, is much less than PID-based strategy, with PS29 reaching a maximum pressure of 161.8 psig, though the settling time of the DMC strategy is longer, requiring 0.9 h after disturbance to return to the 142.0 psig setpoint. Correspondingly, the oxygen supplies under DMC strategy at the higher-pressure side will slightly reduce pressures of P S6 and P S11 to echo the pressure changes of P S29 (see Figure a). In PID-based strategy, however, such CVs’ coordination is absent (see Figure b). As shown in Figures c,d, both control strategies select to close PV3 from 51% down to 21%. This control action reduces the oxygen flow rate from ASU3, which is the closest supplier to Customers 5 and 7. The greater difference between DMC and PID strategies is through the compressor speed MVs, reflected by compressor duty (see Figure e,f). As shown, both K1 and K2 duties drop temporarily under the DMC strategy, in addition to compressor K3 having the most noticeable and permanent change in duty. Comparably, only the K3 duty drops with a significant spike under the PID-based strategy, while K1 and K2 remain constant in duty. Ultimately, this case highlights how the DMC works on coordinating pressure regulation between the higher-pressure and lower-pressure sides more preemptively in its response compared to the PID-based strategy, and utilizing all MVs in its multivariable application to reduce maximum pressure offset in P S29.

Case 3: P S11 Setpoint Change

In this case, the setpoint of one CV, P S11, is suddenly changed from 165 psig to 180 psig at Hour 1.0. As the P S11 setpoint changes, the DMC strategy makes smoother and longer control actions than PID-based strategy, which are shown in Figures a,b. For the DMC strategy, the new PS11 is reached after 1.9 h while the PID strategy requires only 1.0 h. Neither strategy causes overshoot in P S11; however, P S6 experiences less upsetting through the DMC-based strategy, reaching a maximum pressure of 167.1 psig compared to the PID maximum pressure of 173.9 psig. Correspondingly, the oxygen supply from ASU2 increases as the PV2 opening increases to raise P S11. To balance oxygen supplies of the entire pipeline system, the oxygen flow rate from ASU1 drops during the same time period, which can be seen from Figures c,d. For the DMC strategy valves shown in Figure c, PV1 will open briefly to 60% as this valve is set to have a positive gain with P S11, which at the time of raising P S11 setpoint has a greater error and therefore greater authority over PV1 than P S6. PV3 and PV4 openings have smaller changes under both control strategies, but the DMC strategy takes a longer time to gradually increase the PV3 opening than the PID-based strategy. Case 4: Dangerous Increments of P S11 Setpoint and Duty Upper Limit of K2

In extreme scenarios, the oxygen pipeline network could be vulnerable to severe cyberattacks aimed at making unauthorized and drastic changes to critical controller settings. To replicate such a scenario, the setpoint of CV PS11 is abruptly changed at Hour 1.0, from 165 psig to 275 psig, which exceeds its standard operating range by a wide margin. As shown in Figures a,b, P S11 initially climbs rapidly, resulting in an P S6 increment correspondingly. Certainly, P S11 under the DMC strategy changes slower than that under the PID-based strategy, reaching the first 215 psig plateau at 1.90 and 1.74 h, respectively. Pressure P S11 will rise until reaching approximately 215 psig because the pressure safety valve PSV2 is triggered to open at 215 psig, preventing exceeding this safety limit. A more noteworthy difference in strategy is in P S6, which reaches a maximum pressure of 191.1 psig at 1.12 h in Figure b before settling back down to the setpoint of 165 psig. Comparably, the DMC strategy P S6 only reaches a maximum pressure of 171.9 psig and holds at this pressure, due to P S11 having the greater error against its setpoint and therefore more effect on PV1 and K1 in the DMC multivariable control strategy, even when PS6 is above setpoint.

To satisfy such a huge increment of P S11, PV2 must immediately and fully open to supply more oxygen, while both PV1 and PV3 have to reduce their opening to reduce their oxygen supplies as shown in Figures b,c. Note that PV4 has to also gradually close to help buffer the rapid pressure increase at the lower-pressure side of the pipeline system. Valve responses between the two control strategies are similar, aside from PV3 of the DMC strategy closing in anticipation of increased oxygen on the high-pressure side, which causes a decrease in P S29, dropping to 138.9 psig.

At 215 psig, P S11 is far above its standard pressure of 165 psig. A critical controller setting for K2, the MV upper limit of the cascade inner controller SIC-102 (see Figures and ), can be manipulated to significantly worsen the already dangerous situation. In any controller settings, CV and MV should be collaborated with appropriate limits (ranges) in advance of an application. This means that if the MV range of a controller is changed, the MV value will be instantly scaled to the new limit value. In this case, the MV upper limit of SIC-102 is deliberately raised from 40,000 Btu/h to 1,000,000 Btu/h at Hour 3.0, causing instant power usage increment to jump the outlet pressure of K2. This change naturally shows an immediate and big spike in power consumption in both Figures c,d, as well as another big pressure increment of P S11 in both Figures a,b. Note that when P S11 reaches 262 psig, near Hour 3.90 for DMC and 3.75 for PID strategies, its downstream S12 and S7 will reach 260 psig. This triggers another severe event: the reverse backflow at the check valve of CV1, which means that the outlet pressure of CV1 (260 psig) is much higher than its inlet pressure (PS6 at 170.8 psig) at Hour 3.9, causing a catastrophic failure of the check valve. Thus, oxygen of S7 flows backward toward stream S6 near Hour 3.9.

Dynamic simulations indicate the immediate decrease of P S11 and immediate increase of P S6 right after CV1 failure as shown in Figure a,b, with P S6 reaching a maximum pressure of 211.0 and 207.7 psig under DMC and PID strategies, respectively. This actually suggests the oxygen supply at Customer 1 is fully provided by ASU2 and thus PV1 is completely closed to shut off the oxygen production from ASU1 from Figures c,d. These two figures also suggest that the PV3 takes more obvious closing-and-opening actions under the DMC control strategy than those under PID-based strategy in terms of responding to the two dangerous manipulations for controller settings. It should be pointed out that in real industrial applications, such an extreme reverse-flow pressure differential would cause a severe safety emergency and lead to catastrophic consequences. As disclosed in Figures g,h, significant temperature changing spikes occur in Streams S6 and S11 due to the two dangerous controller settings. As shown, the maximum temperature change of S6 and S11 under the DMC strategy reaches 32 °F (see Figure g), while the maximum temperature change of S6 and S11 under the PID-based strategy reaches 54 °F (see Figure h). Although the DMC strategy significantly outperforms the PID-based strategy, a sudden failure of the check valve (CV1) near Hour 3.9 still causes a rapid temperature excursion that could lead to catastrophic consequences, including pipeline rupture, leaking, or ignition.

Conclusions

An oxygen distribution pipeline system has been developed, modeled, and simulated with two control strategies in this paper: DMC strategy and traditional PID-based strategy have been compared as a means of evaluating the native model predictive controller within Aspen HYSYS. The dynamic performances of the development under various abrupt disturbances affecting control valves and pressures at key locations throughout the pipeline system have been examined and analyzed. Simulation results demonstrate how the DMC strategy effectively outperforms capabilities of conventional PID-based strategy in terms of having abilities to (1) predict systematic impacts on CVs from indirectly associated disturbances, (2) simultaneously manage and coordinate complex interactions from multiple MVs to serve multiple CVs throughout the entire pipeline network system, and (3) reduce maximum absolute pressure offset under these various scenarios. This study provides valuable insights into how to effectively control and analyze the performance of oxygen distribution pipeline systems to ensure smooth operations.
