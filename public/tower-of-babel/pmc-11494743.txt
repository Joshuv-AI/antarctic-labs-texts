Development and validation of a nomogram to predict the risk of adjacent segment disease after transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion in patients with lumbar degenerative diseases
Authors: Miao Yu, Jiawen Ye, Hui Xing, Hong Yin, Tao Jiang, Minghan Liu, Changqing Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdjacent segment disease (ASD) is a common and serious complication that can develop in the mid- to long-term after lumbar fusion surgery. However, the underlying mechanisms of ASD are not yet fully understood. This study aimed to develop and validate a risk prediction model for ASD in patients who underwent transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) for lumbar degenerative diseases.MethodsPatients with lumbar degenerative disease who underwent TLIF between January 2015 and December 2016 were included in the retrospective study. The participants were divided into two groups: ASD and non-ASD. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors influencing ASD after TLIF surgery. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate the model’s discrimination, calibration and clinical value, respectively.ResultsA total of 11.5% (59/513) of patients developed ASD. Higher BMI, lower BMD, higher disc grade, and reduced disc height were identified as independent risk factors for ASD after TLIF. The model demonstrated good discrimination in both the training and validation sets, with calibration and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests confirming accuracy, and DCA demonstrating clinical applicability.ConclusionsThe nomogram model demonstrated promise in predicting ASD in patients who underwent TLIF, aiding clinicians in selecting the most suitable surgical approach and optimizing surgical decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-024-05170-4.Keywords: Transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, Adjacent segment disease, Nomogram, Predictive model

Lumbar fusion is a commonly surgical procedure for treating lumbar degenerative diseases. Adjacent segment disease (ASD) is a mid- to long-term complication that frequently follows lumbar fusion and is a major reason for revision surgery. ASD results from the gradual progression of conditions such as intervertebral disc degeneration, spinal stenosis, segmental instability, spondylolisthesis, and posterior facet joint hypertrophy. The reported incidence of ASD varies widely, ranging from 2.8% to 30.3% across different regions and studies, with a notable increase observed over time as follow-up periods extend after lumbar fusion surgery [1–3].

Multiple factors contribute to the development of ASD, including natural degeneration, patient–specific factors, and surgery-related factors [4].Obesity, osteoporosis, age and spinal stenosis are identified as key individual risk factors for ASD [5, 6]. These factors primarily contribute to ASD through mechanisms such as decreased intervertebral disc water content, increased load, and accelerated degeneration. Surgical factors, including the type of procedure, the number of fusion segments, fixation methods, and decompression techniques, are also critical [7], as they influence the integrity and stability of the spine’s anatomical structure. Additionally, sagittal plane imbalance is considered a potential risk factor for ASD following lumbar fusion surgery [8], although systematic and comprehensive research on this topic is still lacking.

Given the unclear mechanism underlying ASD, further research is crucial. Natural degenerative factors and individual patient factors are significant in the mid- to long-term development of ASD. This study aimed to investigate the incidence and preoperative clinical and radiological risk factors for ASD in patients undergoing TLIF surgery with lumbar degenerative diseases. The primary objective was to develop and validate a risk prediction model to assist spinal surgeons in preoperative planning and reduce the incidence of ASD.

Materials and methods

Study population

Clinical records of 513 patients with lumbar degenerative disease who underwent TLIF at Xinqiao Hospital of Army Medical University between January 2015 and December 2016 were retrospectively collected. There were 277 males (54.0%) and 236 females (46.0%) with an average age of 57.0 (48.0,67.0) years. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (359, 70% of the patients) and a validation set (154, 30% of the patients). Figure 1 illustrates the overall process of model building. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with lumbar degenerative disease, including lumbar disc herniation (LDH), degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) and lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), who underwent TLIF; (2) Patient whose fusion segment is less than or equal to 2; (3) Patients with a follow-up time more than 24 months. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with lumbar fracture, infection, tumors or other spinal diseases; (2) Patients with a history of Lumbar fusion surgery; (3) Patient whose number of fusion segments is more than 2; (4) Patients with a follow-up time of less than 24 months. This study followed the TRIPOD + AI statement: updated guidance for reporting clinical prediction models that use regression or machine learning methods [8].

Data collection

The demographic data included sex (male/female), age (years), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), bone mineral density (BMD, T score), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), smoking history (yes/no). The radiological parameters included the disc height of the proximal adjacent segment (mm), the grade of proximal spinal canal stenosis (grades 0–3), the grade of proximal intervertebral disc degeneration (grades I-V), preoperative and postoperative lumbar lordosis (LL), sacral slope (SS), pelvic incidence (PI), pelvic tilt (PT), pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis (PI-LL). The surgery-related data included operative time (mins), blood loss (ml), fusion segments (levels), cage height (mm) and surgical level. All radiological parameters were assessed by two research personnel.

Criteria for ASD

Radiographical ASD was defined as a reduction of ≤ 50% in disc height on a neutral lateral radiograph, an increase of ≥ 3 mm in vertebral slip on a neutral lateral radiograph, and a decrease of ≥ 5° in the intervertebral angle on a flexion lateral radiograph [9]. Clinical ASD was defined as the presence of consistent clinical symptoms such as lower limb pain and numbness accompanied by imaging degeneration of adjacent segment. Intervertebral disc degeneration was evaluated according to Pfirrmann’s classification (grades I-V) [10], and spinal canal stenosis was evaluated according to Lee’s classification (grades 0–3) via T2-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) [11].

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent standard TLIF performed by one qualified surgeon in our hospital. Patients were given general anesthesia and placed in a prone position. In this procedure, we removed the inferior articular process and part of superior articular processes. The ligamentum flavum was then excised, the thecal sac and nerve root were exposed. Remove the herniated disc and cartilage endplates of the intervertebral space completely. Fixed with pedicle screws and rods after nerve decompression was completed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS Statistics software (version 26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the R software package (version 4.2.1). Continuous variable data with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and were analysed via independent t–tests. Data with a abnormal distribution are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were analysed via Mann–Whitney tests. The categorical variables are presented as proportions and were compared via chi–square tests, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were substituted into the multivariate logistic regression analysis and screened by the backward stepwise regression method to determine the risk factors of ASD. The R language was used to construct a visual nomogram of the model, and the predictive performance of the model was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA).

A total of 629 patients met the inclusion criteria. After excluding 116 patients with TLIF (13 patients with more than 2 fusion segments, 51 patients with spinal fractures, tumors or infections, 20 patients with a history of lumbar spine surgery and 32 patients with incomplete follow-up data), 513 patients were included in the final analysis. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between the training cohort and validation cohort in terms of demographic information, clinical characteristics, imaging data, and surgery-related data (Table 1).

Univariate and multivariate analysis

Table 2 showes statistically significant differences in the BMD, BMI, disc height and Pfirrmann grade of the proximal adjacent segment between the two groups (P<0.05 ). The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified BMD (OR, 1.596; 95% CI 1.007–2.501; P = 0.042), BMI (OR, 2.536; 95% CI 0.948–6.503; P = 0.056), disc height in the proximal adjacent segment (OR, 0.079; 95% CI 0.034–0.174; P = 0.001), and Pfirrmann grade in the proximal adjacent segment (OR, 1.599; 95% CI 1.156–2.266; P = 0.006) as independent risk factors for ASD.

Establishment of the nomogram

The training cohort was used to develop the nomogram. Univariate logistic regression analysis first identified factors associated with ASD. Variables significantly linked to ASD in the univariate analysis were then included in a multivariate logistic regression analysis. The backward stepwise process, guided by the minimum value of the Akaike information criterion (AIC), was applied to select independent prognostic factors that significantly impacted patient outcomes. These factors were then integrated to construct a nomogram for ASD. In the nomogram, each variable was assigned a score on the basis of its influence, and these scores were summed to produce a total score. This total score was then used to estimate the probability of ASD (Fig. 2).

Validation of the efficiency and clinical application of the nomogram

The AUC value of the training set was 0.841 (95% CI: 0.771–0.911), with an optimal threshold of 0.269, a specificity of 0.935, and a sensitivity of 0.622. For the validation set, the AUC value was 0.800 (95% CI: 0.690–0.909), with an optimal threshold of 0.228, a specificity of 0.902, and a sensitivity of 0.636. These results indicate that the model has good discrimination ability (Fig. 3). The calibration curves for both the training set and validation set demonstrated good model applicability. The goodness–of–fit test proposed by Hosmer–Lemeshow was used for evaluation, with χ2 values of 8.789 for the training set and 6.282 for the validation set. The corresponding P–values were 0.456 and 0.711, respectively (P > 0.05), indicating that the model was well–calibrated (Fig. 4).The clinical decision analysis (DCA) features a horizontal axis representing the threshold probability. The horizontal line shows that all samples are negative, resulting in a net benefit of 0 when no intervention occurs. The diagonal line indicates that all samples are positive, where the net benefit during full intervention corresponds to the inverse slope of negative efficiency. The DCA for both the training set and validation set indicated that the model has good clinical applicability (Fig. 5). The ROC curve for the entire dataset was plotted, revealing an AUC value higher than that of any individual influencing factor. This further confirms the model’s strong discriminative power (Fig. 6).

Discussion

There are numerous risk factors associated with the accelerated onset of lumbar ASD in patients, most scholars agree that the occurrence of ASD is influenced by individual factors such as age and weight. In elderly patients, a decrease in proteoglycans and water content within the intervertebral discs, combined with higher stress on adjacent segments in obese patients, may contribute to the higher likelihood of ASD following fusion surgery. Additionally, some research suggests a possible correlation between ASD and genetic factors. A study by Omair et al. followed patients who underwent lumbar interbody fusion for an average of 13 years and found that a single nucleotide polymorphism in the IL-18RAP gene increased the incidence of ASD by affecting disc height [12]. However, this finding has yet to be replicated, and further research is needed to confirm the connection.

Currently, the development of prediction models for ASD after spinal surgery is mainly focused on the cervical spine [13, 14], with few models addressing ASD following lumbar spine surgery. Research by Zhang et al. primarily emphasizes preoperative imaging features; however, this approach overlooks important patient-specific factors and preoperative degeneration [15].Therefore, a practical, fast, and effective personalized prediction model for ASD following TLIF surgery is crucial for both patients with lumbar degenerative disease and surgeons. Currently, the nomogram model, which incorporates various risk factors to provide visualized predictions, is widely used in clinical prediction research [16, 17]. In this study, factors such as BMI, BMD, and preoperative adjacent segment degeneration were included in the nomogram on the basis of collected clinical data (Fig. 7). A risk prediction model was successfully constructed to assess the likelihood of ASD in patients with lumbar degenerative diseases post-TLIF surgery. The results demonstrated that the nomogram model has good discrimination and calibration ability, making its predictions reliable. This model can assist surgeons in optimizing surgical strategies.

A recent study by Sehan Park et al. revealed that old age (P = 0.045), a significant number of levels fused (P = 0.047), and a narrow preoperative disc height of the proximal adjacent level (P = 0.011) were risk factors for early revision [18].

BMI plays a significant role in the development of ASD [19–21]. Duan et al. followed 190 patients and reported a significant increase in the incidence of ASD in those with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² [22]. A study by Ou et al. involving 190 patients revealed that BMI is a critical factor influencing the onset of ASD. Specifically, any increase of 1 in the mean BMI increases the adjacent segment disease rate by 67.6% [6]. Ehresman et al.‘s reported that bone quality, as measured by the VBQ score, is a significant risk factor for reoperation after lumbar fusion surgery [23]. Further research indicated that, obesity may be associated with an increased risk of surgery for ASD after TLIF in patients who have a PI-LL mismatch, but in obese patients without PI-LL mismatch, this association was not observed [24].

There is still no definitive conclusion on whether BMD influences the occurrence of ASD. Xi Z et al. measured the BMD of vertebral bodies via Hounsfield units (HU) and suggested that osteoporosis could be a biomechanical risk factor for intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) [25]. The use of anti–osteoporosis drugs, including bisphosphonates, RANKL antagonists, and parathyroid hormone (PTH), in osteoporosis treatment can increase BMD, preserve intervertebral disc height, and maintain vertebral endplate thickness, thereby helping to prevent ASD after fusion surgery [26–28]. Conversely, a biomechanical study by Zhang et al. reported that osteoporosis can significantly reduce stress on the intervertebral disc after fusion surgery, potentially lowering the incidence of ASD [29].

Multiple studies have emphasized that preoperative degeneration of adjacent segments significantly increases the risk of ASD [30]. Anandjiwala et al. conducted a 5 years follow-up of 68 patients who underwent lumbar fusion surgery, reporting that preoperative degeneration of adjacent segments was a critical factor, with 20.6% of patients developing radiographic ASD during the follow-up period [31]. Liang et al. confirmed that preoperative adjacent disc herniation and degeneration are strong predictors of symptomatic ASD, with factors such as preoperative sagittal alignment and disc height playing a significant role further suggested that preoperative adjacent disc herniation is one of the most accurate predictors of ASD [32]. Research has demonstrated that preoperative imaging, particularly MRI and CT scans, can provide valuable insights into the likelihood of ASD development. One study showed that preoperative disc degeneration observed on MRI is a significant predictor of ASD, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.783 (P = 0.003) [33]. Cheung et al. found that patients with lumbar spinal stenosis had a 3.9 times higher risk of developing ASD compared to those without stenosis. This study highlights the importance of considering preoperative conditions such as developmental spinal stenosis (DSS) as they significantly increase the risk of ASD and reoperation [34].Additionally, a three-dimensional finite element analysis confirmed that The results showed that postoperative stress on the nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus significantly increased with greater preoperative degeneration, confirming that adjacent segment degeneration is a key factor in the development of ASD [35]. In this study, we also found that preoperative degeneration of adjacent segments was a risk factor for postoperative ASD after TLIF. Therefore, the degree of adjacent segments degeneration should be taken into account to evaluate and predict the ASD more accurately after TLIF in patients with lumbar degenerative diseases.

Although clinical prediction models show significant promise, they are not yet sufficiently advanced to replace clinicians in decision-making, especially considering the ethical implications. These models should be regarded as supplementary tools that help clinicians in making informed decisions. To develop preoperative treatment strategies personalized and assess postoperative risk accurately, the prediction models must be taken each patient’s unique circumstances and medical history into account.

While this study demonstrated promising results and underscored the potential benefits of using a nomogram model to predict ASD in patients who underwent TLIF, it is important to recognize certain limitations. First, as a single center retrospective study, it is prone to patient selection bias. Second, the small clinical sample size restricts the identification of additional risk factors, and the lack of external data prevents validation of the model. Moreover, some parameters, such as the extent of superior facet invasion during surgery, were excluded because of the unavailability of precise information in the surgical records. Finally, future research should focus on prospective, large–sample, multicenter studies to validate the clinical applicability of this model.

Conclusion

In summary, this study integrates BMI, BMD and preoperative adjacent segment degeneration to propose a novel ASD risk prediction model for TLIF patients. This study highlights the potential of clinical prediction models in forecasting ASD after TLIF surgery. It provides valuable guidance for early intervention in this patient population. With advancements in prediction models and the availability of more data, we anticipate that predictive tools in spinal surgery will become increasingly accurate and practical, ultimately improving patient care and treatment outcomes.
