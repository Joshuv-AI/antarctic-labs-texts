Explainable AI molecularly imprinted polymer electrochemiluminescence biosensor for discriminative methamphetamine and amphetamine detection
Authors: Emre Dokuzparmak, Emine Sezer, İrem Nur Ceylan, Nur Ceylin Çetin, Hilal Özçelik, Sinan Akgöl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIndependent forensic quantification of methamphetamine (MA) and amphetamine (AMP) remains challenging due to their near-identical structures and overlapping electrochemical signatures. We report an end-to-end AI-integrated MIP-ECL biosensing framework employing target-specific MIP-NP/MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE platforms. The Ir(III) luminophore within an MWCNT/Nafion matrix produced markedly superior ECL emission over the [Ru(bpy)3]2+ benchmark (40 mM DBAE, pH 8.0). MIP nanoparticles conferred high selectivity against structurally related interferents, validated in saliva, urine, and blood plasma, achieving detection limits of 0.11 ng mL−1 for MA and 0.28 ng mL−1 for AMP. A structured explainable machine learning pipeline employing CatBoost with Group-K-Fold cross-validation eliminated data leakage and achieved test R2 values of 0.9864 (MA) and 0.9675 (AMP). SHAP analysis confirmed that model predictions were consistent with known electrochemical kinetics. This modular framework establishes a generalizable paradigm for intelligent MIP-ECL biosensing with direct applicability in forensic toxicology and point-of-care diagnostics.

Introduction

Modern analytical chemistry is undergoing a fundamental transformation, shifting from simple signal detection toward autonomous, data-driven diagnostic capability. While conventional sensing platforms have achieved remarkable sensitivity, they often remain limited by rigid linear calibration models that fail to account for the non-linear complexities of real-world samples. The integration of advanced machine learning (ML) frameworks represents an important step toward addressing these limitations.1 However, the way ML is deployed within these systems varies considerably and critically determines the level of analytical insight that can be achieved.2,3

Within electrochemiluminescence-based sensing, early efforts to integrate machine learning have been predominantly shaped by the parallel rise of smartphone-compatible portable platforms. By converting ECL emissions into digital intensity values, these systems made data-driven analysis possible.4 While these platforms offer visual convenience, they suffer from inherent data loss during digitization and remain highly susceptible to environmental lighting conditions. Even systems employing fixed-voltage visual monitoring5 lack the dynamic resolution required for accurate quantification in complex mixtures. The integration of molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) has further enhanced the selectivity of these platforms; notably, Zhang et al.6 developed a MIP-ECL sensing platform for furosemide detection utilizing deep learning to minimize noise in smartphone-captured images, and Zhang et al.7 reported machine learning-optimized MIP-ECL systems for the selective detection of 5-hydroxytryptamine. However, across these advances, machine learning is consistently deployed as a tool for image refinement or noise reduction rather than as a core analytical component. Beyond this methodological limitation, traditional calibration approaches continue to struggle with non-linear signal responses and matrix-induced fluctuations inherent to real-world ECL measurements,8 further constraining the diagnostic reliability of existing platforms.

This study addresses both limitations through a fundamentally different computational strategy. Rather than converting ECL emissions into visual representations, we perform direct training on high-resolution tabular signal data, treating each individual voltage-ECL data point as a discrete numerical feature. This approach preserves the full kinetic depth of the electrochemical response and captures signal dynamics that image-dependent models inherently discard. Recent evidence confirms that ML-integrated MIP platforms can effectively learn underlying non-linear signal patterns, overcoming the calibration limitations of conventional approaches.9,10 Building on this principle, we propose a generalized, end-to-end AI-integrated architecture for MIP-based ECL biosensing that systematically couples sensor fabrication, raw signal acquisition, feature engineering, and cross-validated machine learning with SHAP-based model interpretability, forming a complete pipeline from physical analyte recognition to explainable concentration prediction. To date, no such generalized framework has been established for raw electrochemical data, and this study directly addresses this gap.

To rigorously evaluate the performance and real-world applicability of this generalized framework, the discriminative detection of methamphetamine (MA) and amphetamine (AMP) was employed as a comprehensive case study. These structurally similar compounds produce intricate, overlapping electrochemical signatures in biological matrices, rendering their individual quantification from complex mixtures an analytically demanding task. Critically, MA and AMP are not merely co-occurring substances: MA undergoes hepatic conversion to AMP via CYP2D6-mediated N-demethylation, establishing a dynamic and time-dependent concentration relationship between the two analytes. The temporal evolution of this MA/AMP ratio carries direct forensic interpretive value, transforming dual-target profiling from a simple detection task into an interpretive problem that demands both analytical precision and predictive capability.

MA and AMP belong to the amphetamine-type stimulant (ATS) class, among the most widely abused synthetic drugs worldwide, acting primarily as central nervous system stimulants with potential hallucinogenic and psychotomimetic effects.11 ATS abuse is strongly associated with psychiatric and behavioral disturbances including aggression and impulsivity, and is frequently encountered in forensic toxicology and death investigations.12,13 Following intake, MA can be detected in biological fluids within 24–48 h in plasma and for several days in urine depending on physiological conditions,14,15 with an elimination half-life of 7–12 h.16,17 Given the increasing prevalence of ATS trafficking and use—particularly in regions such as Türkiye18—the demand for rapid, reliable, and portable detection methods is both urgent and growing. This forensic necessity is, however, accompanied by a significant analytical challenge: MA and AMP are structurally nearly identical, producing overlapping electrochemical signatures that confound conventional sensing platforms and render their discriminative quantification a rigorous test of any detection framework. The combination of forensic relevance and analytical difficulty makes MA and AMP a practically meaningful test case for the proposed framework.

Conventional analytical techniques for ATS detection, including gas chromatography, liquid chromatography, and mass spectrometry, offer high accuracy but are costly, time-consuming, and unsuitable for on-site analysis.19 Electrochemical and ECL-based sensors have therefore emerged as promising alternatives for rapid drug screening. Compared to conventional electrochemical systems operating at micromolar levels,20 ECL platforms provide significantly enhanced sensitivity, often reaching picomolar detection limits,21 making them particularly suitable for biological samples with low analyte concentrations.11,22,23 Among available luminophores, ruthenium-based complexes such as [Ru(bpy)3]2+ remain the most widely employed; however, their broad reactivity results in limited selectivity in complex biological matrices24—a critical shortcoming for forensic applications where structurally similar metabolites are invariably present. Iridium-based cyclometalated complexes offer a compelling alternative, combining superior emission quantum efficiency with inherently improved selectivity, properties that are particularly advantageous for the discrimination of structurally related analytes such as MA and AMP. Among the various Ir(III) luminophores reported for ECL applications, Ir(pq)2(acac) was selected in the present study based on its superior ECL performance. Previous studies have demonstrated that Ir(pq)2(acac) generates one of the highest ECL emission intensities among commonly investigated Ir(III) complexes in the presence of tri-n-propylamine (TPrA), substantially exceeding that of the conventional Ru(bpy)32+ system. In addition, its compatibility with carbon nanotube-modified electrodes has been shown to enable stable and intense ECL emission in aqueous media, making it particularly attractive for biosensing applications requiring high analytical sensitivity. Therefore, Ir(pq)2(acac) was selected as the luminophore for constructing the proposed ECL sensing platform.25,26 Their principal limitation—poor aqueous solubility—has historically restricted their deployment in biological sensing. MWCNT/Nafion composite architectures have been shown to effectively overcome this barrier by enabling stable luminophore immobilization and efficient ECL generation in aqueous media,27,28,29 and this approach is adopted in this study. Nevertheless, even with an optimized luminophore platform, selectivity against co-occurring metabolites and endogenous interferents remains a fundamental challenge that the transducer architecture alone cannot fully resolve.

Biorecognition elements have been widely explored to address matrix selectivity challenges. Antibody-based systems offer high affinity but are constrained by stability and reproducibility concerns,30,31,32 while aptamer-based approaches, despite their design flexibility, introduce added complexity in synthesis and validation.33,34 MIPs offer a robust and cost-effective alternative to biological recognition elements, providing tailored binding cavities with high chemical stability.35,36 Their successful integration into electrochemical sensing platforms across diverse analytical targets has been extensively documented.37,38 In particular, p(HEMA)-based polymeric matrices functionalized with tryptophan derivatives have been shown to facilitate strong aromatic interactions and molecular recognition pathways.39,40,41 Building on these structural configurations, target-specific MIP sub-microstructures can be precisely engineered for high-affinity template binding. Although MIP-based electrochemical sensors for MA have been reported, they are predominantly limited to single-analyte detection and narrower dynamic ranges, and none have integrated an Ir(III) luminophore within a MIP-ECL architecture for biological matrices. More critically, no existing platform couples this physical sensing strategy with a generalized, end-to-end ML framework capable of automated concentration prediction and explainable signal interpretation—the central contribution of this study.

In this study, we report an end-to-end AI-integrated framework for MIP-based ECL biosensing, implemented through the development of an Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/MIP-NP platform for the discriminative detection of MA and AMP in biological matrices. While the high ECL efficiency of the Ir(III) luminophore and the tailored molecular recognition of MIP-NPs constitute a highly sensitive and selective physical foundation, the core novelty of this work resides in its computational architecture: a structured pipeline encompassing raw signal acquisition, systematic feature engineering, group-cross-validated machine learning, and SHAP-based model interpretability. By operating directly on high-resolution tabular ECL signal data rather than image-derived proxies, the framework achieves superior predictive performance for unseen concentration levels and enables reliable MA/AMP ratio profiling with direct forensic interpretive value. Beyond this case study, the proposed architecture establishes a generalized, modular blueprint for intelligent AI-integrated MIP-ECL biosensing that is readily transferable to alternative analyte systems and sensing configurations, with broader implications for point-of-care diagnostics and on-site forensic screening.

This section presents the experimental and computational results obtained from the Ir(III)-MIP-ECL biosensing platform. The analysis is structured to validate the sequential transition from interfacial material engineering to explainable concentration prediction. Initially, the systematic optimization of the physical sensing layers is addressed, evaluating the synergistic effects of film composition, nanomaterial loading, and electrochemical behavior across varying chemical conditions. Following the establishment of baseline analytical performance, linear calibration dynamics, and cross-reactivity profiles in complex biological matrices, the discussion shifts to the computational deployment of the data-centric ML pipeline. Finally, the predictive accuracy and generalizability of the optimized tree-based models are analyzed, complemented by SHAP-based feature importance to decode the underlying potential-dependent emission kinetics and confirm the consistency of the model predictions with electrochemical principles.

Optimization of the sensing platform materials

In the study, an electrochemically activatable and ECL-active MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/TPA electrode platform was systematically investigated and comprehensively characterized to evaluate its analytical performance. The maximum ECL intensity obtained from the developed system was comparatively assessed against the MWCNT/Nafion/[Ru(bpy)3]2+/TPA configuration under identical experimental conditions. Given that [Ru(bpy)3]2+ is one of the most extensively employed luminophores in aqueous ECL systems due to its well-defined redox chemistry, high stability, and reproducible emission behavior, it was selected as a benchmark reference. This comparison enabled a rigorous and technically meaningful evaluation of luminophore efficiency and emission performance within the same interfacial architecture.

The electrochemical behavior of the MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE platform (0.20% MWCNT, 0.6% Nafion (w/v), and 1 mg Ir(III) complex) was investigated within a potential window of 0.0 to +1.40 V in 10 mM PBS containing 40 mM TPA or DBAE. Scan rates ranging from 20 to 100 mV s-1 were evaluated (Figures 3A and 3B). In both co-reactant systems, the anodic peak currents increased progressively with increasing scan rate. A linear dependence was observed for both DBAE and TPA (Figures 3C and 3D), confirming that the electrochemical oxidation of the co-reactants at the modified SPCE surface is predominantly governed by mass transport. The high correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.9955 for DBAE and R2 = 0.9934 for TPA) validate the diffusion-controlled nature of the system within the investigated scan rate interval and indicate stable interfacial charge transport without kinetic limitations.

Comparative analysis under identical concentration conditions (40 mM) revealed that DBAE consistently produced higher anodic peak currents than TPA (Figure 3A). This enhanced electrochemical response is attributed to the more favorable radical generation pathway of DBAE, which undergoes proton abstraction more readily following electrochemical oxidation, thereby facilitating efficient formation of reactive radical intermediates and improved electron-transfer dynamics.42

From an analytical standpoint, the higher current response and enhanced charge-transfer efficiency observed for DBAE indicate superior signal amplification capability, supporting its selection as the preferred co-reactant for achieving improved sensitivity and lower LoD/LoQ values in subsequent electrochemical and ECL measurements. Based on the overall electrochemical evaluation, a scan rate of 100 mV s-1 was selected as optimal, as it provided the highest stable current response without compromising linearity or diffusion-controlled behavior.

The MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE platform was prepared, and ECL emission intensities were evaluated in the presence of varying concentrations (10, 20, 40, 60, and 80 mM) of TPA and DBAE (Figures 4A and 4C). In addition, to directly compare co-reactant performance and ECL intensity under identical electrochemical conditions, ECL measurements were conducted over a potential range of 0.0 to +1.60 V at a scan rate of 100 mV s-1 in the presence of 40 mM TPA and DBAE (Figure 4E).

Among the investigated concentrations, 40 mM DBAE produced the highest ECL intensity. Based on these findings, 40 mM DBAE was selected as the optimal co-reactant type and concentration for the proposed sensing platform (Figures 4A, 4C, and 4E). The enhanced ECL response observed for DBAE can be rationalized mechanistically. As an aliphatic tertiary amine structurally analogous to TPA, DBAE is expected to follow a similar oxidative co-reactant pathway as described for the [Ru(bpy)3]2+/DBAE system.44,45 In this mechanism, DBAE undergoes initial electrochemical oxidation at approximately +0.60 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) to generate the radical cation (DBAE·+), which subsequently deprotonates to form the strongly reducing neutral radical (DBAE·). This radical species can reduce Ir(pq)2(acac), leading to the formation of the excited-state Ir(pq)2(acac)∗ complex. Radiative decay of this excited state then produces the observed ECL emission.

Consistent with the CV results, DBAE generated higher anodic currents compared to TPA (Figure 3), and correspondingly produced stronger ECL signals (Figure 4). The agreement between electrochemical and ECL data supports the proposed mechanistic interpretation and confirms that DBAE acts as a more efficient co-reactant than TPA under the investigated conditions. Literature reports further indicate that DBAE possesses strong electron-donating capability and can generate stable and intense ECL signals in aqueous media,43 in agreement with the present findings.

Interestingly, a slight decrease in ECL intensity was observed at concentrations above 40 mM for both DBAE and TPA (Figures 4C and 4D). This decline is attributed to quenching effects occurring at elevated co-reactant concentrations. Quenching can arise from either electron-transfer or energy-transfer mechanisms. One possible pathway involves Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), in which excited species interact over short intermolecular distances through dipole-dipole coupling.46 At higher concentrations, co-reactant molecules are in closer proximity, increasing the probability of non-radiative energy transfer between excited species. Such interactions reduce the effective population of reactive radical intermediates available to participate in electron transfer with the Ir(III) luminophore, thereby decreasing the overall ECL emission intensity.46 Accordingly, partial self-quenching among DBAE molecules at concentrations exceeding 40 mM likely accounts for the observed reduction in ECL signal.

Overall, the combined electrochemical (CV) and ECL analyses clearly demonstrate that DBAE not only produces higher oxidation currents but also yields significantly enhanced ECL emission compared to TPA. A co-reactant concentration of 40 mM DBAE was therefore selected for all subsequent measurements.

As shown in Figure 5A, increasing the Nafion concentration from 0% to 0.6% resulted in a significant enhancement of ECL signal intensity. This improvement can be attributed to the film-forming and ion-exchange properties of Nafion, which facilitate effective immobilization of Ir(pq)2(acac) complex and promote efficient electron-transfer interactions between the luminophore and DBAE without inducing mass transport limitations. The sulfonate groups within the Nafion matrix contribute to enhanced ionic conductivity and improved interfacial charge transfer, thereby supporting efficient redox cycling within the film.25

However, at higher Nafion concentrations (0.7–1.0%), a pronounced decrease in ECL intensity was observed. This decline is attributed to excessive film thickness at elevated Nafion content, which can hinder the diffusion of DBAE molecules into the polymer matrix. Restricted mass transport reduces the rate of redox interactions between DBAE-derived radicals and the immobilized Ir(pq)2(acac), thereby limiting excited-state generation and decreasing ECL emission efficiency. Such diffusion barriers are commonly observed in overly dense polymer films and can compromise overall sensor performance.

Based on the combined electrochemical and ECL evaluation, 0.6% (w/v) Nafion was identified as the optimal concentration for the MIP-NP/MWCNT/Ir(pq)2(acac),/Nafion/SPCE platform, as it provided maximal ECL intensity while maintaining efficient charge-transfer dynamics and minimal diffusion limitations.

Optimization of chemical and binding parameters

Ir(pq)2(acac) complex was employed as the luminophore in the designed optical sensor platform due to its high emission efficiency, photostability, and favorable excited-state properties. Cyclometalated Ir(III) complexes are widely recognized for their superior luminescence characteristics and high ECL efficiency. However, despite their advantageous emissive properties and electrochemical stability, their practical application in aqueous systems remains limited because of poor water solubility. Recent studies have demonstrated that appropriate surface modification strategies can enable Ir-based ECL systems to generate strong and stable emission signals in aqueous environments, including applications in biological fluids.25,47 In particular, carbon nanotube (CNT or MWCNT) modification has been shown to significantly enhance the aqueous ECL performance of Ir complexes by improving interfacial electron-transfer efficiency.

To promote efficient ECL generation of Ir(pq)2(acac) in aqueous media, the sensor platform was modified with MWCNTs. The influence of MWCNT concentration on ECL intensity was systematically investigated using a 1 mg mL−1 Ir and 0.6% Nafion-modified SPCE platform, in which the MWCNT content was systematically varied, in the presence of 40 mM DBAE (Figure 5B). In the absence of MWCNTs (0 mg mL−1), the ECL signal was negligible, indicating insufficient interfacial electron-transfer efficiency. Upon incorporation of 0.15 mg mL−1 MWCNT, a pronounced enhancement in ECL intensity was observed.

This improvement can be attributed to the intrinsic electrical conductivity of MWCNTs, which arises from their conjugated π-electron structure. The extended π-network facilitates rapid electron transport across the electrode surface and promotes efficient interfacial charge transfer. Furthermore, π-π interactions between the graphitic surface of MWCNTs and the aromatic Ir complex enhance immobilization and electronic coupling. As a result, the redox cycling of the Ir(pq)2(acac) luminophore is accelerated, leading to increased excited-state generation and enhanced ECL emission. The beneficial role of carbon nanotubes in accelerating redox kinetics and improving sensor performance has been widely reported in the literature.25

However, at higher MWCNT loadings (0.20 and 0.25 mg mL−1), a substantial decrease in ECL intensity was observed (Figure 5B). This decline is attributed to excessive nanotube aggregation at elevated concentrations, which can disrupt optimal interfacial organization. Increased π-π stacking among nanotubes may reduce effective interaction between the Ir complex and the conductive network, thereby limiting efficient electron transfer. Additionally, excessive MWCNT content can produce a denser composite film within the Nafion matrix, potentially leading to partial light scattering or absorption effects that further attenuate ECL emission. Similar concentration-dependent behavior of CNT-modified ECL systems has been reported previously.25

Overall, the results indicate that 0.15 mg mL−1 MWCNT provides an optimal balance between enhanced electron-transfer kinetics and controlled film morphology, enabling efficient ECL emission of the Ir(III) complex in aqueous media. An MWCNT loading of 0.15 mg mL−1 was therefore adopted for all subsequent experiments.

Effect of Ir(pq)2(acac) concentration on electrochemical and ECL signal intensity

Iridium-based luminophores are widely employed in ECL systems owing to their high emission efficiency, long excited-state lifetimes, and excellent photostability.26 These favorable photophysical properties render cyclometalated Ir(III) complexes particularly attractive for high-performance ECL sensor platforms. In polymer-immobilized systems, however, the surface density of the luminophore within the Nafion matrix represents a critical parameter that directly influences electron-transfer kinetics, excited-state generation efficiency, and overall signal output. Therefore, optimization of Ir(pq)2(acac) loading was systematically performed.

To evaluate the effect of luminophore concentration, CNT/Nafion/Ir/SPCE electrodes were fabricated using Ir(III) concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 1.2 mg mL−1, while maintaining the previously optimized MWCNT and Nafion contents. ECL measurements were conducted under identical experimental conditions (Figure 5C). As the Ir(III) concentration increased from 0.2 to 0.8 mg mL−1, a pronounced enhancement in ECL intensity was observed. This increase can be attributed to the higher surface coverage of electroactive luminophore species, which increases the probability of productive interactions between DBAE-derived radical intermediates and the immobilized Ir(pq)2(acac), thereby promoting efficient excited-state formation.

However, at higher concentrations (1.0 and 1.2 mg mL-1), a partial decline in ECL intensity was detected. This decrease is attributed to concentration-dependent aggregation and self-quenching effects within the Nafion film. At elevated surface densities, Ir(III) complexes may undergo intermolecular interactions that facilitate non-radiative deactivation pathways. In particular, excited Ir(III)∗ species can undergo energy or electron transfer to neighboring ground-state complexes, leading to self-quenching and reduced photon emission.48 Aggregation-induced quenching in luminophore-rich polymer films has been widely reported in the literature and is consistent with the trend observed in Figure 4C.

Overall, the results demonstrate that, in the presence of optimized MWCNT and Nafion content, the Ir(III) complex exhibits efficient electron-transfer cycling and strong ECL emissions in aqueous media. An optimal Ir(pq)2(acac) concentration of 0.8 mg mL−1 was identified, providing maximal ECL intensity while avoiding aggregation-induced signal attenuation. An Ir(pq)2(acac) concentration of 0.8 mg mL−1 was adopted for all subsequent measurements.

Effect of film thickness on ECL signal intensity

To achieve optimal electrochemical and ECL performance of the Ir(III)/CNT/Nafion/SPCE platform, the influence of the deposited composite film volume was systematically investigated. Under previously optimized composition conditions, the Ir(III)/CNT/Nafion suspension was drop-cast onto the SPCE surface in varying volumes (5, 10, 15, and 20 μL). ECL measurements were performed in the presence of 40 mM DBAE under the optimized conditions.

As shown in Figure 5D, increasing the deposited film volume from 5 to 10 μL resulted in a significant enhancement of ECL intensity. This improvement can be rationalized in terms of increased electroactive surface coverage and higher effective luminophore loading. According to the Randles-Ševčík relationship (Equation 1), the peak current (ip) in a diffusion-controlled system is proportional to the effective electrode area (A) and the square root of the diffusion coefficient (Dct).49 The moderate increase in film volume likely increases the effective electroactive area and the number of accessible Ir(III) redox centers, thereby enhancing charge-transfer processes and excited-state generation.

However, further increases in film volume (15 and 20 μL) led to a noticeable decline in ECL intensity. This behavior is attributed to excessive film thickness, which introduces mass transport limitations within the composite layer. A thicker polymer-nanotube matrix can hinder the diffusion of DBAE toward the immobilized Ir(III) sites, effectively reducing the apparent diffusion coefficient and slowing interfacial redox kinetics. Although the geometric coverage (A) may continue to increase with film thickness, the concurrent decrease in mass transport efficiency dominates the system behavior, resulting in reduced peak current and consequently lower excited-state formation rates.

Since ECL emission intensity is directly linked to the generation rate of excited Ir(pq)2(acac)∗ species, reduced electron-transfer efficiency at higher film thicknesses leads to diminished photon emission. Thus, an optimal balance between surface coverage and diffusion accessibility is required. Based on the experimental findings, a deposition volume of 10 μL was identified as optimal, providing maximal ECL intensity while maintaining efficient mass transport and interfacial charge-transfer dynamics. A deposition volume of 10 μL was therefore selected as optimal for all subsequent experiments.

Effect of pH on ECL signal intensity

pH is a critical parameter in ECL-based systems, as it directly influences reaction kinetics, radical generation efficiency, and interfacial charge-transfer processes. In particular, the protonation-deprotonation equilibria of co-reactants and the stability of excited-state species are strongly dependent on the solution pH.25 Therefore, the impact of pH on the ECL performance of the optimized MIP-NP/Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE platform was systematically investigated.

ECL measurements were conducted under previously optimized conditions across a pH range of 6.0–10.0 using 10 mM buffer solutions. The resulting ECL emission intensities obtained at different pH values are presented in Figure 6. The modified electrode system exhibited strong ECL responses within the pH range of 7.5–9.0 (Figure 6). The ECL intensity increased progressively up to pH 8.0, after which a partial decline was observed at higher pH values. This trend is primarily attributed to the pH-dependent deprotonation step of DBAE prior to its reaction with Ir(III) complex.

Efficient ECL generation in co-reactant systems requires the formation of a highly reducing neutral radical species following oxidation of the tertiary amine. Deprotonation of the oxidized DBAE radical cation facilitates the formation of the reactive DBAE· species, which subsequently participates in rapid redox interaction with Ir(pq)2(acac), leading to efficient excited-state generation. This mechanistic pathway is analogous to the well-established Ir(III)/TPA system.

At moderately alkaline conditions (around pH 8.0), deprotonation of DBAE is sufficiently favored, promoting efficient radical formation and enhanced electron-transfer kinetics. Consequently, the highest ECL intensity was observed at pH 8.0. However, at higher pH values, partial degradation or reduced availability of reactive species may occur, leading to diminished interaction efficiency and a corresponding decrease in ECL emission.25 Overall, pH 8.0 was identified as the optimal condition for maximizing ECL signal intensity, as it provides a favorable balance between radical generation efficiency and system stability.

Comparative evaluation of ECL intensity: Ir(pq)2(acac) vs. [Ru(bpy)3]2+

Due to their inherently low solubility in aqueous media, only a limited number of Ir(III)-based ECL systems have been reported in water. In previously published studies, the performance of aqueous Ir(III) systems has typically been benchmarked against ruthenium-based luminophores to enable objective evaluation of emission efficiency and analytical capability.25,27,46,50 Among these, [Ru(bpy)3]2+ remains one of the most extensively studied and widely accepted reference luminophores for aqueous ECL systems.

To directly compare emission performance under identical experimental conditions, a Ru-based electrode platform (Ru/CNT/Nafion/SPCE) was fabricated and evaluated alongside the optimized Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE system in the presence of 40 mM DBAE. The resulting ECL intensities were recorded and comparatively analyzed (Figure 5E).

The Ir(pq)2(acac)-based system exhibited a markedly higher ECL intensity compared to the Ru-based platform under the same conditions. This enhanced emission performance can be attributed to the superior photophysical properties of cyclometalated Ir(III) complexes, including higher emission quantum efficiency and more favorable excited-state dynamics. The stronger ECL response observed for the Ir(III) system highlights its potential advantage over conventional ruthenium luminophores in aqueous sensor configurations.

The MWCNT/Nafion interfacial architecture thus effectively overcomes the aqueous solubility limitation of Ir(III) complexes, yielding ECL intensities that surpass those of the [Ru(bpy)3]2+ benchmark.

Surface loading and optimization of MA- and AMP-Selective MIP nanoparticles

Following the characterization and optimization of the Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE platform, the synthesized and characterized MIP nanoparticles (MIP-NPs) were immobilized onto the electrode surface to construct the MIP-NP/Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE sensing system. The synthesized MA- and AMP-selective MIP nanoparticles exhibited hydrodynamic particle sizes of 671.2 nm and 964.9 nm, zeta potentials of −3.1 mV and −2.3 mV, and binding capacities of 1039 mg g−1 and 967 mg g−1, respectively; complete physicochemical characterization (FTIR, SEM, AFM) is reported in our previous work.51 Since the loading amount of MIP-NPs directly influences mass transport properties and the apparent diffusion coefficient at the electrode interface, this parameter was systematically optimized.

To evaluate the effect of MIP-NP loading on ECL performance, aqueous MIP-NP dispersions (5 mg mL−1) were drop-cast onto the modified electrode surface in varying volumes ranging from 3 to 20 μL. As shown in Figure 5F, ECL intensity remained largely preserved when 3–18 μL of MIP-NP solution was applied. However, a noticeable decrease in ECL signal was observed at 20 μL.

The reduction in ECL intensity at higher loading volumes is attributed to excessive surface coverage by the polymeric nanoparticles. Overloading of MIP-NPs likely creates a denser recognition layer, hindering the diffusion of the co-reactant DBAE toward the electrode surface and reducing the efficiency of interfacial redox reactions. In diffusion-controlled ECL systems, restricted mass transport directly limits the generation rate of excited Ir(pq)2(acac)∗ species, thereby attenuating photon emission.

At 18 μL MIP-NP loading (5 mg mL−1), the system exhibited an optimal balance between recognition site density and mass transport efficiency. Below this threshold, diffusion pathways remained sufficiently accessible, while the increasing number of imprinted binding sites enhanced the probability of analyte interaction. Beyond 18 μL, diffusion limitations became dominant, resulting in a decline in ECL intensity.

Upon binding of MA or AMP to the imprinted cavities, additional steric and diffusional constraints are introduced at the electrode interface. This further restricts DBAE transport and amplifies the ECL signal modulation mechanism. At the optimized 18 μL loading, these diffusion-mediated signal changes become more pronounced even at low analyte concentrations, thereby enhancing analytical sensitivity.

Based on the overall evaluation, 18 μL of 5 mg mL−1 MIP-NP was selected as the optimal immobilization volume. At this loading, diffusion equilibrium is maintained while maximizing the number of active imprinted sites on the electrode surface. This optimized configuration significantly contributes to the high-sensitivity detection of MA and AMP in biological fluids using the MIP-NP/Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE platform.

Incubation optimization of MIP-NPs on the Ir(III)/CNT/Nafion/SPCE surface

To achieve stable and reproducible ECL performance following MIP-NP immobilization, the incubation conditions of the MIP-NP/Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE platform were systematically optimized. After deposition of the dispersed MIP-NP solution (5 mg mL−1) onto the pre-optimized Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE surface, the electrodes were incubated for varying durations ranging from 10 to 90 min. The purpose of this investigation was to determine the optimal incubation time required to ensure stable nanoparticle attachment and consistent ECL signal generation (Figures 5G and 5H).

Under optimized compositional conditions, 18 μL of MIP-NP suspension was applied to the electrode surface, followed by incubation for the designated time intervals. After each incubation period, ECL measurements were performed to evaluate signal intensity and stability.

To assess reproducibility and signal robustness, five consecutive ECL measurements were recorded for each incubation time, and the corresponding standard deviations were calculated. This approach enabled evaluation of signal stability as a function of incubation duration and identification of the incubation time that provided the most stable and reproducible ECL response.

Similar results were also obtained for the AMP-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp)/Ir(III)/MWCNT/Nafion/SPCE sensor system.

ECL signals obtained in biological samples were compared with those obtained in PBS (taken as 100%), and the sensor response in each matrix was expressed as relative signal efficiency (%). For blood samples, analyses were performed using the plasma phase, as MA is more homogeneously distributed in plasma and this phase generally provides more consistent results for quantitative drug analysis.68,69 In addition, plasma prevents variations associated with clot formation in serum, allowing more stable and reliable ECL signals to be obtained.

Overall, the developed sensor systems demonstrated successful performance in all tested biological matrices. Blood plasma and urine samples produced stable signals without requiring any pretreatment, while comparable results in saliva were achieved after appropriate dilution. In all matrices, the sensors showed reliable responses with a maximum relative ECL signal deviation of approximately 15%, indicating high analytical precision and sensitivity. Maximum relative ECL deviations of ∼15% across all three matrices confirm the platform’s robustness in complex biological environments.

Machine learning-based concentration prediction and model generalizability

The integration of advanced machine learning architectures was a decisive step in overcoming the inherent non-linearity and matrix-induced variations typically encountered in complex ECL sensing systems. In this study, the traditional signal-to-concentration approach was replaced by an ML-based predictive model to maximize the quantification robustness of the Ir(III)-MIP-ECL platform across its entire dynamic range.

Comparative analysis and the impact of feature engineering for MA and AMP

Ultimately, the strong predictive power achieved by the optimized CatBoost model, leveraging the engineered ECL feature space, demonstrates its potential for forensic applications. The parallel quantification of both MA and AMP from complex biological matrices addresses a critical analytical challenge in forensic toxicology screening. This AI-assisted biosensor architecture provides a rapid, low-cost alternative to traditional chromatographic methods in forensic toxicology.

Compared with previously reported electrochemical and ECL-based sensing platforms, the proposed sensor exhibits a distinct signal transduction mechanism in which target binding to the MIP cavities results in a decrease in ECL intensity, rather than the signal enhancement commonly observed in conventional ECL sensors. This response provides a high initial ECL signal in the absence of the analyte, thereby improving signal-to-noise characteristics at low analyte concentrations and contributing to the low detection limits achieved in this study. Critically, none of the comparative studies incorporate machine learning for concentration prediction, and none simultaneously address the parallel quantification of both MA and AMP with biological matrix validation across multiple sample types. Furthermore, the Ir(pq)2(acac)-based ECL system demonstrated superior discriminative capacity within the amphetamine-type stimulant class, overcoming the cross-reactivity limitations reported for [Ru(bpy)3]2+-based platforms, which have been shown to produce comparable responses to structurally related interferents such as cocaine and diamorphine. Collectively, the integration of a high-affinity MIP recognition layer, a sensitive Ir(III)-based ECL transducer, and an interpretable machine learning pipeline positions the proposed platform as the first AI-augmented, multi-analyte MIP-ECL framework validated across three distinct forensic biological matrices.

General discussion and perspectives

The proposed architecture leverages the direct integration of molecular imprinting and gradient-boosted ML. By employing MIP-NPs, the platform successfully isolates the target analytes from structurally similar interferents (e.g., paracetamol, cocaine) directly at the physical interface. Following this physical recognition, the CatBoost-driven framework functions as a digital calibration layer, effectively resolving the non-linear emission dynamics of the Ir(III) system. This dual-layered approach ensures both data quality and predictive accuracy, facilitating the direct analysis of complex matrices without extensive sample pre-treatment.

The analytical performance of the developed platform, characterized by low detection limits in biological fluids, supports its potential utility in rapid drug screening. Because the predictive model is trained to autonomously process engineered multidimensional features (e.g., ECL, log-transformed ECL, and voltage), it effectively eliminates the need for manual data interpretation. Consequently, the proposed Ir(III)-MIP-ECL architecture provides a robust foundation for developing automated, on-site forensic screening devices capable of delivering rapid quantitative outputs.

At the sensor level, the Ir(pq)2(acac)-based system exhibited significantly higher ECL emission compared to the conventional [Ru(bpy)3]2+ platform, highlighting the superior photophysical properties of cyclometalated Ir(III) complexes. The integration of MWCNTs and Nafion enabled efficient charge transfer and stable luminophore immobilization, while molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticles (MIP-NPs) provided high selectivity toward structurally similar analytes.

On the computational side, rather than relying on conventional linear calibration models, the proposed approach leverages structured feature engineering to capture the non-linear relationships inherent in ECL responses. Distinct feature representations were required for each analyte, where AMP prediction was effectively achieved using fundamental signal descriptors, whereas MA quantification necessitated non-linear feature engineering, including log-transformations and second-order polynomial terms, to accurately capture its unique electrochemical signatures. This adaptive feature space design enabled the CatBoost-based models to achieve high predictive performance (test R2 = 0.9675 for AMP and 0.9864 for MA), demonstrating strong generalization capability for unseen concentrations. Furthermore, this performance also reflects the ability of the AI framework to capture and compensate for the inherent trade-off between molecular selectivity and signal efficiency arising from MIP-induced diffusion constraints and interfacial effects.

Overall, this study introduces a generalized and modular paradigm for AI-integrated MIP-ECL biosensors. The combination of high-sensitivity ECL sensing with adaptive machine learning enables precise quantification and automated interpretation of complex analytical signals. This approach offers a scalable alternative to traditional methods and holds strong potential for real-time, on-site applications in forensic toxicology, clinical diagnostics, and portable drug screening systems.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Sensor validation was performed in artificial saliva, artificial urine, and commercially sourced human blood plasma rather than in matrices collected from authentic forensic case samples; while such surrogate matrices are widely used for initial biosensor validation, they cannot fully reproduce the compositional variability of real specimens, and prospective testing on case-derived samples remains an important next step, which may require fine-tuning or recalibration of ML model to accommodate matrix-specific signal variations not captured during training on surrogate fluids. In addition, selectivity was evaluated at a single, forensically relevant interferent concentration (100 ng mL−1), at which all tested compounds produced signal deviations below 10%; extending this evaluation across a broader concentration gradient would further strengthen confidence in the sensor’s selectivity margin. Similarly, ML models were trained and validated exclusively on data acquired from the in-house μStat ECL bipotentiostat/galvanostat system, and their transferability to signals recorded on different potentiostat platforms or across independently fabricated SPCE batches has not been formally assessed. The dilution and handling protocol also differed across the three biological matrices to accommodate their distinct physical properties, and a fully standardized detection protocol applicable to saliva, urine, and plasma alike was not established. Finally, the dual linear calibration behavior observed for both MA (transition near 30 ng mL−1) and AMP (transition near 50 ng mL−1) introduces a narrow concentration window with comparatively higher predictive uncertainty, which the ML framework mitigates but does not eliminate. Addressing these points through prospective validation on authentic case samples, expanded interferent profiling, and cross-platform reproducibility testing represents an important direction for future work.

Method details

This section outlines the comprehensive experimental and computational methodology designed to realize the intelligent, AI-integrated MIP-ECL biosensing framework. The methodology is systematically structured into two core domains: the physical engineering of the high-affinity transduction interface and the development of the data-centric machine learning pipeline. Initially, the specification of analytical reagents, the synthetic preparation of target-specific molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticles (MIP-NPs), and the layer-by-layer fabrication of the modified screen-printed carbon electrode (SPCE) surfaces are detailed. Subsequently, the protocols for raw electrochemical signal acquisition and surface characterization are presented, immediately followed by the mathematical and structural deployment of the explainable AI pipeline encompassing advanced feature engineering, group-cross-validated ensemble training, and SHAP-based feature interpretability.

Reagents, materials, and electrochemical platform

L-Tryptophan (≥98%), sodium nitrite (NaNO2, ≥99%), potassium carbonate (K2CO3, ≥99%), methacryloyl chloride (≥97%), acetonitrile (ACN, 99.9%), 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA, ≥97%), ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA, ∼98%), ammonium persulfate (APS, ≥98%), sodium bisulfite (NaHSO3, ≥98%), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, ≥99.7%), methamphetamine hydrochloride (MA, ≥98%), amphetamine hydrochloride (AMP, ≥98%), (2,4-pentanedionato)bis[2-(2-quinolinyl)phenyl]iridium(III) Ir(pq)2(acac), tris(2,2′-bipyridyl)ruthenium(III) chloride, 2-(dibutylamino)ethanol (DBAE), tri-n-propylamine (TPrA), multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), and Nafion solution and Human serum ( from human male AB Plasma) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were of analytical grade. Screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs) served as the electrochemical platform for all measurements.

Synthesis of MATrp and target-specific MIP nanoparticles

The functional monomer N-methacryloyl-(L)-tryptophan methyl ester (MATrp) was synthesized according to our previously reported procedure.70

Molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticles (MIP-NPs) specific to MA and AMP, together with the corresponding non-imprinted nanoparticles (NIP-NPs), were prepared by free radical polymerization of HEMA in the presence of MATrp and EGDMA as crosslinker. Detailed synthesis procedures and physicochemical characterization are reported in our previous work.51 Since MA and AMP require distinct molecular recognition cavities, MA-specific and AMP-specific MIP-NPs were synthesized separately and used to construct two independent sensing platforms, as detailed in the Layer-by-Layer Fabrication section below.

Layer-by-layer fabrication of the modified SPCE interface

MWCNTs were first dispersed in an ethanol:methanol (1:1, v/v) mixture by ultrasonication to obtain a homogeneous suspension, which was subsequently incorporated into a composite solution containing 0.6% (w/v) Nafion and 1 mg/mL Ir(pq)2(acac). In this architecture, the Nafion matrix serves a dual function: it facilitates stable immobilization of the Ir(III) luminophore through electrostatic interactions while simultaneously promoting efficient interfacial electron transfer via the embedded MWCNT network. The resulting composite was ultrasonicated prior to deposition, and a 15 μL aliquot was drop-cast onto the working electrode area of the SPCE and allowed to dry at room temperature in the dark for 2 h, forming the primary MWCNT/Ir(pq)2(acac)/Nafion transduction layer.

For each sensing platform, the target-specific MIP-NPs (MA-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp) or AMP-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp)) were independently dispersed in 0.6% (w/v) Nafion and drop-cast onto the modified electrode surface to form the outermost molecular recognition layer. The completed platforms, MA-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp)/MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE and AMP-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp)/MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE, were each dried for a further 2 h. The step-by-step layer-by-layer assembly protocol and the subsequent signal modulation mechanism of the completed MIP-NP/CNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE platforms are schematically illustrated in Figure 1. Both platforms were stored at 4 °C in the dark to preserve luminophore stability and prevent photobleaching until use.

Electrochemical signal acquisition and sensor characterization

All electrochemical and ECL experiments were performed using a portable μStat ECL bipotentiostat/galvanostat system (Metrohm) equipped with an integrated ECL detection cell. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted over a potential window of 0.0 to +1.40 V at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1, with scan rate optimization experiments performed between 20 and 100 mV s−1 to evaluate the electrochemical behavior of the MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE platform.

ECL measurements were carried out over the same potential window using 10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 40 mM co-reactant (TPrA or DBAE). Both CV and ECL responses were recorded on the modified SPCE platform in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and analytical reliability. The obtained signals were analyzed to evaluate interfacial charge-transfer behavior, ECL emission intensity, and overall sensor performance under the investigated conditions.

The influence of pH on the ECL response of MA-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp)/MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE and AMP-imp-p(HEMA-MATrp)/MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE systems was investigated over the pH range of 6.0–10.0. Measurements were performed in buffer solutions containing 40 mM DBAE as co-reactant. PBS was used for pH 6.0–8.0, Tris buffer for pH 9.0, and carbonate/bicarbonate buffer for pH 10.0. ECL responses were recorded under optimum conditions.

The selectivity of the MA- and AMP-imprinted MIP-NP/MWCNT/Nafion/Ir(pq)2(acac)/SPCE platforms was evaluated against a panel of potential interferents selected based on structural similarity to the target analytes or co-occurrence in forensic biological samples. ECL measurements were first performed using 100 ng/mL MA with the MA-imprinted sensor and 100 ng/mL AMP with the AMP-imprinted sensor to establish reference signals. The sensors were subsequently exposed to 100 ng/mL solutions of nicotine (NIC), paracetamol (PCM), diamorphine (DIAM), cocaine (COC), and p-hydroxymethamphetamine (p-OH-MA) individually, and to a mixed solution containing MA, AMP, and all interferents simultaneously at the same concentration. Sensor selectivity was assessed by comparing relative ECL signal responses and calculating percentage signal changes with respect to the target-only reference.

To evaluate analytical performance in biologically relevant matrices, the optimized sensing platforms were further tested in saliva, urine, and blood plasma samples spiked with MA and AMP at 20, 50, and 100 ng/mL. Saliva samples were diluted 10-fold with PBS prior to analysis to reduce viscosity-related diffusion limitations that adversely affected the ECL response. The selected dilution factor was experimentally optimized to maintain a linear relationship between analyte concentration and ECL signal. In contrast, artificial urine and commercially available human blood plasma did not exhibit viscosity-related diffusion limitations under the optimized experimental conditions and were therefore analyzed without dilution. PBS was used as the reference matrix (100% relative efficiency) to provide a common baseline for evaluating sensor performance across different biological matrices. For the interference study, all interferents were introduced by the standard addition method after sample preparation, and identical final interferent concentrations were employed in all biological matrices to ensure comparable analytical conditions. Blood plasma was used in preference to whole blood or serum to ensure homogeneous analyte distribution and minimize matrix-related signal variability. ECL responses obtained in biological matrices were normalized against PBS reference signals and expressed as relative signal efficiency (%).

Explainable AI-Driven MIP-ECL biosensing framework

Realizing the full diagnostic potential of MIP-based ECL sensing requires a cohesive computational architecture that transforms raw sensor outputs into reliable, interpretable predictions. Such an architecture moves beyond traditional smartphone-based or visual monitoring constraints.4,6 The proposed framework integrates physical sensing and data analysis across six sequential stages: signal acquisition, data splitting, feature engineering, model training, evaluation/optimization, and explainability analysis, as shown in Figure 2.

Each voltage step and its corresponding ECL intensity are directly acquired via electrochemical measurements as discrete numerical data points, forming a high-resolution tabular dataset in which every (voltage, ECL) pair constitutes an independent observation (Figure 2A). Before any transformation is applied, the dataset must be partitioned into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets following a group-based strategy (Figure 2B). This approach ensures that all replicates measured at a given analyte concentration are assigned exclusively to either the training or the testing set, strictly preventing data leakage that produces artificially optimistic model performance and obscures the true generalizability of the framework. Within the training set, a 5-fold Group-K-Fold cross-validation strategy was employed to assess model performance across diverse concentration subsets, ensuring that the model evaluates truly unseen concentration profiles during validation.

Following data partitioning, a systematic feature engineering strategy was applied to both datasets to capture the non-linear characteristics of the ECL response more faithfully (Figure 2C). Starting from the directly acquired voltage (V) and ECL intensity measurements, a candidate feature pool was constructed comprising logarithmic transformations (log10ECL), second-order polynomial expansions (V2, ECL2), and interaction terms (V × ECL, ECL / V). Input features were standardized using a preprocessing scaling pipeline to ensure scale invariance across all algorithms.

Multiple supervised regression algorithms, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost, were systematically compared alongside a baseline Linear Regression model (Figure 2D), leveraging both linear and ensemble-based architectures to map complex electrochemical surface interactions and capture non-linear signal patterns.5,9 Following algorithm selection, the hyperparameters of the best-performing models were optimized using a GridSearchCV approach conducted exclusively on the training set, ensuring an unbiased final evaluation on the isolated test set (Figure 2E). Finally, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis, based on cooperative game theory, was applied to the finalized models to convert the “black-box” ensemble into a transparent, physically aligned analytical engine73 (Figure 2F). This explainability layer quantifies the marginal contribution of every input feature to individual concentration predictions, verifying that the model's predictive logic aligns closely with known electrochemical principles.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All electrochemical and electrochemiluminescence measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Model performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). Model generalizability was further assessed using Group-5-Fold cross-validation, with all reported metrics given as means across folds (see method details).
