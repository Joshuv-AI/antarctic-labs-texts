Pelagic sea cucumbers in the Galapagos: Midwater grazers that short‐circuit deep‐sea carbon flux
Authors: Bruce H Robison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDirect observations from submersible dives off the Galapagos Islands have revealed that Pelagothuria natatrix, the only known holopelagic echinoderm, actively intercepts settling particles well before they can reach the deep seafloor, thereby short‐circuiting the typical downward flux of organic carbon generated in the photic zone.Keywords: deep pelagic ecology, Galapagos Islands, particulate carbon flux, pelagic sea cucumbers

Holothurian sea cucumbers occur worldwide at depths ranging from subtidal to hadal (1 to 10,000 m). All but one of the more than 1700 described species are closely associated with the seafloor, where they feed chiefly on organic material in the sediments. The sole exception is Pelagothuria natatrix (Figure 1), the only known holopelagic echinoderm. Direct observations from submersible dives off the Galapagos Islands have revealed that Pelagothuria actively intercepts settling particles well before they can reach the deep seafloor, thereby short‐circuiting the typical downward flux of organic carbon generated in the photic zone. This strategy has been termed flux feeding by Jackson (Jackson, 1993; Stukel et al., 2019).

Pelagothuria natatrix was described by Ludwig (1894) from specimens collected by the fisheries steamer Albatross in the eastern tropical Pacific. A single species is recognized (WoRMS Editorial Board, 2025), and distributed in tropical regions of the eastern Pacific, South Atlantic, and Indian Oceans (Selig et al., 2019). Little is known about its natural history.

The Galapagos Islands are swept by a complex of strong surface currents. Below about 100 m, the cold, eastward flowing Cromwell Current (or Equatorial Undercurrent) is dominant (Kessler, 2006). Rising steeply from the deep seafloor to intercept these currents, the islands generate oases of marine production because of local upwelling and nutrient runoff (Gove et al., 2016). These conditions create a situation where weak swimmers are challenged by the currents, but strong swimmers can more readily access the halo of enhanced productivity around the islands. Evidence of this distinction was apparent in the composition of the midwater community observed during a series of submersible dives that surveyed the water column around the islands.

In situ observations, video recordings, and collections were conducted from the research submersible Johnson‐Sea Link II, during a cruise of the R/V Seward Johnson to Ecuador's Galapagos Islands. Dives (23 by the author) were made along the steep slopes of islands throughout the archipelago. Typical dive profiles consisted of water column surveys during a gradual, stepped descent to bottom depths ranging from 300 to 900 m, followed by benthic surveys working back up the slope (Appendix S1: Figure S1).

At water column depths between 250 and 650 m, Pelagothuria was a prominent member of the mesopelagic community. Estimated density within its depth range was one individual per 300 m3 (Appendix S2). Depth of occurrence and altitude above the seafloor were independent of bottom depth, except where the island slope intersected Pelagothuria's depth range (Appendix S1: Figure S1). Extended observations were made with the submersible to neutral buoyancy. In this mode, it was possible to carefully follow swimming individuals and to hold steady in the water column for long‐term observations of non‐swimming specimens.

For a holothurian, Pelagothuria has a “bizarre” configuration and it is the only echinoderm known to spend its entire life cycle off the seabed (Gebruk & Kremenetskaia, 2025; Miller & Pawson, 1990). The body is gelatinous, slim, short, and tapered, with a ring of flexible tentacles surrounding the mouth. The anterior body is dominated by 12 or more long, circumoral podia extending out radially, with a thin, transparent web between them. This podial veil is the principal food aggregator as well as the sole means of propulsion. Average total length was estimated at 17 cm and the diameter of the web was about the same. The compact body provides balance, both as a fulcrum for swimming, and as a plumb bob for feeding. Swimming forward (head first) is accomplished by sweeping the podial veil backward along the body (Miller & Pawson, 1990). To reverse direction the veil is directed forward, beyond the head, creating a graceful medusoid pulse, as in Figure 1.

When first encountered, the majority of individuals (73 of 104 observed) were in a characteristic passive feeding posture, with the body oriented vertically, the oral region directed upward, and the webbed podia spread outward like a funnel toward the surface (Figure 2). As particles settled into the conical web, the oral tentacles collected them from the veil and conveyed them to the mouth. The tentacles were also observed capturing particles while the animals were swimming, and from batches of water enclosed by the web. No individuals were observed within 2 m of the bottom, except for a dead specimen being carried by a crab.

Gut contents from the eight specimens examined included: phytoplankton debris (in all 8 individuals), fecal pellets (8), ambiguous detrital matter (8), pteropod shells (2), salp stomachs (2), fish remains (2), sand grains (2), ctene row fragments (1). These results are consistent with reports in the literature of gut contents from three other specimens (Miller & Pawson, 1990; Selig et al., 2019). The passage of food through the coiled digestive tract was clearly visible through the nearly transparent body wall (Figure 2). Material being digested formed a continuous bolus from the stomach toward the anus. Fecal pellets, characteristically lozenge‐shaped, formed in the posterior third of the intestine and in some cases extended beyond the anus in a chain.

Gentle mechanical stimulation, in situ by the submersible, or in the laboratory, yielded a bright, sustained pulse of epidermal luminescence that radiated outward from the point of contact to encompass most or all of the body. The color of the light was distinctly green, and it was by far the brightest luminescence observed at depth. Light is produced by a network of granules found just below the thin, lightly pigmented surface of the skin. However, unlike its closest relative, Enypniastes eximia (Gebruk & Kremenetskaia, 2025), which readily sheds its glowing, sticky skin to deter predators (Robison, 1992), Pelagothuria has tough skin that is not easily broken free by contact. Chromatophore expansion can change the appearance of an individual from transparency to a bright pink color.

Compared with our reference community in the Monterey Canyon (Choy et al., 2017; Robison, 2004; Robison et al., 2010), the deep pelagic community around the Galapagos shows a substantially different mix of gelatinous detritivores. Off Monterey, the principal grazing gelata in the mesopelagic are tunicates: larvaceans, salps, pyrosomes, and doliolids, along with the aberrant polychaete worm Poeobius. Among these grazers, only the salps and pyrosomes are strong enough swimmers to undertake diel vertical migrations.

Swimming ability is linked to feeding mode among gelatinous grazers. Salps utilize muscular contractions to push water through their internal feeding nets and to provide propulsion. Pyrosomes are colonial, and their propulsion results from their combined feeding currents. Doliolids also have internal mucus webs, but their feeding currents are generated by cilia, and thus their swimming capabilities are more limited. Larvaceans have external mucus filters for feeding, which are ill‐suited to locomotion. Poeobius is a flux feeder, relying on an external mucus web to collect particles. Swimming by the larvaceans and Poeobius is hampered by their external feeding structures.

In the mesopelagic depths of the Galapagos dive areas, the most abundant gelatinous grazers were Pelagothuria and salp chains. Small oikopleurid larvaceans and Poeobius were present but only in relatively small numbers. Compared with Monterey, large larvaceans and pyrosomes were rare, as were doliolids. The Galapagos midwater community also had a full complement of both hard‐bodied and gelatinous species at higher trophic levels, although small physonect and calycophoran siphonophores were uncommon.

Other differences between the Galapagos and Monterey midwater communities were evident but the examples are not as dramatic nor as readily characterized as with Pelagothuria. Remarkably, rhodalid siphonophores and cydippid ctenophores, both relatively poor swimmers, were observed clinging to the bottom while probing for food with an unanchored tentacle (Robison, 2004).

Most holothurians feed on seafloor sediments. A few, like Enypniastes and Peniagone, make brief excursions up into the benthic boundary layer after feeding, most likely to relocate or avoid predators (Barnes et al., 1976; Miller & Pawson, 1990). Pelagothuria's holopelagic feeding strategy provides essentially the same type of food, but it brings them closer to the source. An advantage of this strategy is that the organic content of particulate detritus in the mesopelagic is likely to be “richer” than that on the deep seafloor, after it has been degraded nutritionally by microbes during a long, gradual descent. Pelagothuria shunts carbon out of the detrital rain that feeds the benthos and makes it available to the mesopelagic community through its predators. This step bypasses the longer detrital food chain and transfers energy directly to higher trophic levels within the pelagic. While the literature contains no record of predation on Pelagothuria, a wide variety of nektonic species, as well as large cnidarians, feed regularly on gelatinous prey within its size range.

Kiørboe (2011) has observed that “Feeding trade‐offs may be modulated by environmental conditions.” In the present case, the prominence of Pelagothuria as a mesopelagic grazer correlates with the condition of strong prevailing currents in its environment. It appears that the relatively strong‐swimming Pelagothuria has proliferated in a niche that in other regions is typically occupied by weaker swimmers (Robison, 2004). This is further evidence that similar trophic niches exist in different regions and that localized environmental conditions can determine who fills them.

Seafloor mining of polymetallic nodules involves the discharge of sediment plumes into the water column, which poses a significant threat to midwater communities (Dowd et al., 2025). Pelagothuria's unique feeding mode and its occurrence in the targeted Clarion‐Clipperton region of the Pacific Ocean (Gebruk & Kremenetskaia, 2025; Selig et al., 2019) make it particularly vulnerable to fouling by the midwater discharge of tailings from nodule mining.
