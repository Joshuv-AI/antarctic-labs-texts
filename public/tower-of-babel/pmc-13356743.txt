Characterization of Orthopaedic Surgeon Expert Witnesses in Pediatric Orthopaedic Malpractice Litigation: A Multi-Decade Analysis
Authors: Ashleigh N. Pyle, Haad A. Arif, Devan Devkumar, Michael C. Thomas, Kevin A. Williams, Michael J. Conklin, Hassan K. Ghomrawi, Shawn R. Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExpert witness (EW) testimony is central to malpractice adjudication. Pediatric orthopaedics presents unique medicolegal considerations yet EW characteristics in this domain remain poorly defined. This study aimed to characterize orthopaedic surgeon EWs in publicly available pediatric orthopaedic medical malpractice cases.MethodsA national legal database was queried for orthopaedic EWs testifying in pediatric orthopaedic malpractice cases from 1980 to 2024. An internet search and Scopus database query was conducted for each orthopaedic EW, to collect party retention (plaintiff vs defendant), fellowship training, years in practice, practice setting, Hirsch index (h-index), and public advertisement of EW services.ResultsA total of 47 of 3,093 records screened met inclusion criteria. These cases involved 83 orthopaedic surgeon EWs providing 92 testimonies. Of these, 43 (51.8%) EWs were pediatric fellowship trained, accounting for 52/92 (56.5%) testimonies. Pediatric-trained EWs were more often academically affiliated (76.7% vs 40.0%, P < .001) and demonstrated higher mean h-index values compared with nonpediatric-trained EWs (26.3 vs 17.4, P = .079). No additional statistically significant differences were detected between the plaintiff and defense-retained or pediatric and nonpediatric-trained EWs (P > .05).ConclusionsOrthopaedic EWs involved in pediatric malpractice cases were frequently fellowship trained, academically affiliated, and demonstrated substantial scholarly activity. Pediatric fellowship-trained EWs accounted for over half of total EWs. No significant differences between plaintiff and defense-retained EWs were identified across measured characteristics.Key Concepts(1)Over half of all orthopaedic experts were pediatric fellowship trained.(2)Plaintiff and defense-retained expert witness did not differ significantly.(3)Presence of a pediatric-trained expert was not associated with case outcome.Level of EvidenceIII, Retrospective cohort study

Introduction

Medical malpractice remains common and costly in the United States (US) health care system [1]. Orthopaedic surgery consistently ranks among the specialties most frequently subjected to litigation, accounting for approximately 12%-14% of all surgical malpractice claims and indemnity payments averaging over $300,000 per claim [1,2]. These trends likely reflect orthopaedic surgery’s procedural complexity, high patient expectations, and the potential for lasting functional impairment [1,3]. Within orthopaedic surgery, pediatric orthopaedics presents distinct medicolegal vulnerabilities due to the long-term impact of delayed or inappropriate treatment during critical periods of musculoskeletal development [4]. Pediatric orthopaedic malpractice accounts for approximately 13% of orthopaedic litigation and is associated with higher rates of plaintiff-favorable outcomes and greater indemnity payments compared with adult cases [2,[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Expert witness (EW) testimony plays a central role in malpractice adjudication by providing courts with the clinical framework necessary to interpret medical decision-making, define the applicable standard of care, and assess for potential deviations [8]. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) mandates that its members who testify as EWs sign the Expert Witness Affirmation Statement, pledging to provide truthful, objective, and evidence-based opinions regarding subject matters with which they have relevant clinical experience or specific orthopaedic knowledge [8,9]. Prior studies in surgical specialties suggest that expert characteristics, including subspecialty training and academic productivity, may influence case outcomes [10,11]. However, pediatric orthopaedics remains underrepresented in this literature, with limited data available to characterize expert witnesses in this domain [8,12].

Given the complexity of pediatric orthopaedic care and the influence of expert testimony on litigation outcomes, understanding who serves in this role is essential [5,8]. The present study reviewed publicly available pediatric orthopaedic surgery malpractice cases litigated in the US since 1980 to characterize EWs in pediatric orthopaedic malpractice litigation and compare the academic, subspecialty, and professional profiles of plaintiff- and defense-retained experts.

Materials and methods

Data source and search strategy

The Westlaw legal database was queried to identify pediatric orthopaedic malpractice cases reported between January 1, 1980, and December 31, 2024, using the terms “pediatric” OR “minor” OR “children” AND “orthopaedic”. Westlaw is a subscription-based legal research platform that aggregates publicly available federal and state legal records from jurisdictions throughout the United States, including court opinions, jury verdicts, settlements, and appellate decisions. Case inclusion within the database depends on public reporting and legal documentation and therefore does not capture all malpractice claims occurring nationally, particularly privately settled, sealed, or unpublished cases. Prior orthopaedic medicolegal studies have utilized Westlaw as a practical resource for identifying litigated malpractice claims and evaluating litigation trends [7,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Accordingly, the present study was designed as a descriptive analysis of publicly reported litigated cases rather than a population-level estimate of all pediatric orthopaedic malpractice claims. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to conducting this study.

Data collection

Inclusion criteria were defined as reported cases levied against a pediatric orthopaedic surgeon due to allegations of negligent orthopaedic care of a minor with settlement or a finalized jury verdict. Cases were excluded if they (1) lacked a definitive legal outcome, (2) were unrelated to malpractice claims, or (3) involved patients 18 years of age or older. In order to retain specificity and relevance to pediatric orthopaedic surgeons, cases involving non-orthopaedic providers or orthopaedic surgeons without pediatric subspecialty training or concerning care unrelated to orthopaedic management were also excluded. Extracted variables included date and location of case filing, case ruling, any listed monetary award, and details regarding EW training, specialization, and practice type.

Each case was analyzed for orthopaedic EWs. The names of the identified orthopaedic EWs were recorded and used to collect individual-level variables including fellowship subspecialization, years in practice, academic vs community practice setting, scholarly activity as determined by Hirsch index (h-index), and any public advertisement of EW services. The Doximity medical network was reviewed to identify year of residency and fellowship graduation (when applicable), fellowship subspecialization, and any academic affiliation. The number of years in practice was calculated from year of fellowship graduation (or residency graduation if no fellowship was completed) to the year of case trial and used as a proxy for surgeon expertise. In instances when one orthopaedic surgeon served as an EW for multiple cases, the year of the most recent cases was used to calculate years in practice.

The Scopus database, in conjunction with a PubMed search, was used to determine each orthopaedic surgeon's h-index as a measure of scholarly activity at the time of initial data collection. Finally, surgeon personal websites, social media presence, and EW directories [22,23] were queried to identify the frequency of advertisement of EW services. All data reported in this study are de-identified and in aggregate.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were represented as a mean ± standard deviation while categorical variables were represented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons based on plaintiff vs defense-retention was performed using paired-samples t-tests. Characteristics of pediatric vs nonpediatric fellowship-trained EWs were compared using two-sided χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. The threshold for statistical significance was predetermined to be P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) by a trained biostatistician.

Results

A total of 3,093 cases were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After excluding 102 duplicates and 1,765 personal injury, workers compensation, and products/premises liability cases, 1,226 remained for review. Of these, 482 involved adult patients and 697 were unrelated to orthopaedic care, leaving a final cohort of 47 cases (Fig. 1). These 47 cases included testimony from 177 health care professionals. Among them, 94 (53.1%) EWs were non-orthopaedic surgeons (eg, radiologists, obstetrician-gynecologists, psychiatrists, or nonmedical professionals) and were excluded from further analysis. One orthopaedic surgeon’s name was redacted and could not be evaluated. Data on the remaining 83 orthopaedic surgeon expert witnesses are presented in this study.Figure 1Screening flow diagram.

Characterization of EWs

In total, the 83 orthopaedic surgery EWs provided 92 expert testimonies. Male EWs comprised the majority of the included cohort (n = 76, 91.6%). Nearly every orthopaedic surgery EW (n = 82, 98.8%) held a Doctor of Medicine degree with one surgeon holding a Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine degree. Fig. 2 demonstrates the geographic distribution of the 83 EWs. California was the most represented with 16 EWs (19.3%), followed by New York (n = 8, 9.6%), and Louisiana (n = 6, 7.2%). One (1.2%) EW practiced in Canada. Discussion

EW testimony is an integral component of medical malpractice litigation. In pediatric orthopaedics, where residual growth, remodeling potential, and developmental timing complicate both diagnosis and treatment, expert testimony may carry particular importance in defining the applicable standard of care and eventual case outcome [4,5,8]. To our knowledge, this study represents the most comprehensive evaluation to date of orthopaedic surgeon EWs in pediatric malpractice litigation. Compared with prior reports in adult orthopaedics and other surgical fields, pediatric orthopaedic EWs in this cohort were earlier in their careers, demonstrated greater scholarly productivity, and were frequently academically affiliated [8,10,11,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. Notably, we found no statistically significant differences between plaintiff-retained and defense-retained EWs across the measured characteristics.

The characteristics of the EWs analyzed in this study highlight the central role of subspecialty expertise and academic affiliation in pediatric orthopaedic malpractice litigation. Unlike adult malpractice cases, where general orthopaedic surgeons more commonly serve as expert witnesses, pediatric cases demonstrated a near-equal distribution between pediatric-trained (51.8%) and nonpediatric-trained (48.2%) orthopaedic experts [8,[31], [32], [33]]. Additionally, the predominance of academically affiliated experts likely reflects the workforce structure of pediatric orthopaedics, which is largely concentrated within academic and institutional settings [[31], [32], [33]]. Prior work by Park et al. suggests that higher academic representation among defense experts in adult litigation may reflect selection bias or structural differences in recruitment [8]. In pediatric orthopaedics, the relatively small pool of fellowship-trained surgeons may further limit expert availability, particularly when attorneys seek testimony against professional peers.

We also observed a greater overall mean h-index and fewer years in practice for EWs involved in pediatric orthopaedic malpractice litigation compared with other surgical fields, including adult orthopaedics (mean h-index: 10.1), otolaryngology (mean h-index: 6.3-10.0), and neurosurgery (mean h-index: 5.5-8.8), relative to a mean h-index of 22.6 in the present cohort [8,10,11,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. Orthopaedic EWs in our cohort averaged 23 years in practice, approximately a decade less than the 31-36 years reported in adult and general surgical cohorts [8]. This pattern suggests that subspecialization and academic productivity may play a more prominent role in shaping the EW pool within pediatric orthopaedics. It is important to note that participation as an expert witness is likely multifactorial and influenced by additional considerations such as available time, interest in medicolegal work, compensation, and other unmeasured factors. One strategy proposed to mitigate expert witness bias includes the use of a single neutral expert to provide testimony for both plaintiff and defense [10,34]. However, in our cohort, EWs who testified in multiple cases consistently did so on behalf of the same party, a pattern that has also been observed across other surgical specialties [10,11].

These insights offer important implications for clinical policy and the future of expert witness standards in pediatric orthopaedics. Because expert testimony is central to determining whether a breach of the standard of care has occurred, understanding who serves in this role is important to the integrity of the judicial process [30]. Despite this, considerations surrounding expert witness selection have been largely absent from recent reforms in medical malpractice law, underscoring it as an underrecognized yet important area for further discussion [35]. Future work should further evaluate how EW characteristics influence selection, testimony standards, and access to qualified experts within smaller orthopaedic subspecialties. National organizations such as the AAOS and the Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America may also consider whether additional guidance is needed to better define terms such as “relevant clinical experience” and “specific orthopaedic knowledge.” Any such definitions should account for the reality that some expert witnesses may not be actively engaged in routine pediatric clinical practice despite providing testimony in these cases [8,9]. Given the central role of expert testimony in establishing the standard of care, preserving objectivity, credibility, and neutrality remains essential to ensuring fairness within the medicolegal process [30].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis is based on a relatively small number of cases identified over a prolonged study period, which limits statistical power and the precision of subgroup comparisons. Because the Westlaw database includes only publicly available cases with finalized verdicts or settlements, privately resolved, arbitrated, sealed, or otherwise unpublished claims were not captured. As a result, the cohort may not fully represent the national landscape of pediatric orthopaedic malpractice litigation and may overrepresent cases that are more complex, more contentious, or more likely to reach public record. Although the study spans multiple decades across the US, it was not adequately powered to evaluate temporal changes in EW characteristics by year or geography, and practice patterns may have differed between states and over the duration of the study period. Future studies utilizing multiple legal databases may help further evaluate differences in EW characteristics over time and geography. Additionally, metrics such as years in practice, h-index, and academic affiliation are imperfect surrogates for expert witness qualification. Years in practice may not accurately reflect operative volume, case complexity, or medicolegal expertise, while the h-index is time-dependent, data source–specific, and influenced by both publication volume and citation frequency, which may not fully reflect clinical expertise or testimony quality [8]. These measures should therefore be interpreted as relative indicators rather than definitive metrics of qualification. Moreover, while we attempted to account for outward interest in expert witness participation by assessing the presence of online listings of EW services, we were unable to evaluate subjective factors such as availability, compensation, referral networks, or willingness to participate. Importantly, some experts contributed multiple testimonies, introducing possible clustering effects not fully addressed in the primary analysis. Importantly, this study did not assess the scientific validity or clinical accuracy of individual expert testimony. Finally, the underlying clinical and legal details of each malpractice case were beyond the scope of this analysis and are described separately [7]. Despite these limitations, this work provides a focused descriptive assessment of expert witness characteristics in publicly available pediatric orthopaedic malpractice litigation and establishes a foundation for future research and policy discussion.

Conclusion

This Westlaw database analysis found that, compared with adult literature, orthopaedic EWs in pediatric malpractice cases are earlier in their careers, more academically productive, and commonly fellowship trained and academically affiliated, with no significant differences observed between plaintiff- and defense-retained experts. These findings provide a foundation for future work on expert selection and testimony standards in pediatric orthopaedic malpractice litigation.
