Existence and Uniqueness Theorem of Continuous and Monotone Bayesian Nash Equilibrium and Stability Analysis This project is supported by a CUHK start-up grant.
Ziheng Su, Huifu Xu

Abstract
Since the seminal work by Meirowitz 12 , there has been growing attention on the existence and uniqueness of continuous Bayesian Nash equilibria. In the existing literature, existence is typically established using Schauder’s fixed-point theorem, relying on the equicontinuity of players’ best response functions. Uniqueness, on the other hand, is usually derived under additional monotonicity conditions. In this paper, we revisit the issues of existence and uniqueness, and advance the literature by establishing both simultaneously using the Banach fixed-point theorem under a set of moderate conditions. Furthermore, we analyze the stability of such equilibria with respect to perturbations in the joint probability distribution of type parameters, offering theoretical support for the application of Bayesian Nash equilibrium models in data-driven contexts.



Keywords:
Bayesian Nash equilibrium, contraction, existence and uniqueness, stability analysis

1
Introduction

In recent years, there has been a growing focus on Nash games involving private information. A common assumption in such games is that the initial distribution of all players’ types is publicly known. Each player has full knowledge of their own type, which determines their utility function, while remaining unaware of their opponents’ types. Based on the prior information, each player selects a response strategy within their type space based on the Nash conjecture, and an equilibrium
arising from this kind of game is known as a Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE). The concept of the Bayesian game was introduced by Harsanyi
7
;
8
;
9
and has attracted considerable attention, spanning from modeling to equilibrium analysis and applications in strategic decision-making under incomplete information.

Existence of pure strategy Bayesian Nash equilibria (BNEs) has been studied extensively ever since.
A majority of research on the existence of monotone BNE (MBNE) has focused on conditions related to supermodularity and single-crossing condition (SCC).
Vives
23
analyzes existence of Nash equilibria of non-cooperative games by using lattice theoretical methods, where each player’s payoffs satisfy certain supermodularity properties which are directly related to strategic complementarities. The author also establishes existence of BNE as the supermodularity is preserved by integration.
Athey
2
studied Bayesian games with one-dimensional actions and type spaces, assuming that types are drawn from an atomless joint probability distribution.
When each player’s expected utility function exhibits SCC property, i.e., each player’s optimal response is increasing with respect to the increase of its type parameter in response to rivals’ increasing strategies (for type varying), she demonstrated existence of pure strategy Nash equilibria (PSNEs) in every finite action game with each player’s optimal response strategy being increasing and step-like. Moreover, when action spaces are continuous, Athey shows existence of a sequence of increasing step-like PSNEs of finite action games that converges to an equilibrium with the continuum-action, which means that an equilibrium in continuous action spaces can be approximated by a sequence of increasing step-like PSNEs in finite action spaces.
Mcadams
11
extends Athey’s results into more general settings by considering the quasi-supermodularity (in own action) and single-crossing (in own action and type), where both action and type spaces are considered to be multidimensional and only partially ordered.
Reny
15
further relaxes the requirements on payoff functions, showing that monotone best-reply conditions can hold under weaker assumptions.
Van Zandt and Vives
22

introduce a concept called “monotone supermodularity” in Bayesian games, where, apart from strategic complementarities,
each player’s payoff also has increasing differences in their own action and the profile of types, and that each player’s posterior beliefs are increasing in type with respect to first-order stochastic dominance.
They provide an induction method to construct a greatest and a least BNE, with both equilibria being monotone in type.

Another research stream focuses on the continuity property of BNEs.
By using Schauder’s fixed-point theorem under some moderate conditions,
Meirowitz
12
establishes existence of a continuous BNE (CBNE) with each player’s equilibrium strategy being continuous. One of the main conditions is the equicontinuity of the set of optimal response strategies, which ensures that the operator mapping the set of optimal response strategies to itself is compact. Meirowitz comments that the equicontinuity condition is either likely to fail or difficult to verify in practical applications.
Ui
21
provides a sufficient condition for existence and uniqueness of a differentiable BNE by regarding it as a solution of a variational inequality problem. The author demonstrates that when the Jacobian matrix of the payoff gradient is negative definite for each state, a BNE is unique.
Guo et al.
6
take a step forward by presenting some verifiable conditions for the required equicontinuity, namely, some growth conditions of the expected utility function of each player. Moreover, under some monotonicity conditions, the authors demonstrate the uniqueness of a CBNE. The authors also develop some computational approaches for finding an approximate CBNE by using the polynomial decision rule to restrict the strategy function.

More recently, there has been some research extending the BNE model to the generalized Bayesian Nash equilibrium (GBNE) model, where the key difference between GBNE and BNE is that, in GBNE, the action space depends on the type parameter or rivals’ actions due to some constraints.
Liu et al.
10
propose the price competition model under multinomial logit demand where each firm’s marginal cost is private information, and each firm’s action space is dependent on its marginal cost.
Tao and Xu
18
consider a generalized Bayesian game where each player’s action space depends on its own type parameter and the rivals’ actions.
Under some moderate conditions, they demonstrate
existence of continuous GBNE and the uniqueness of such an equilibrium when each player’s action space is only dependent on its type.
In the case that each player’s action space is also dependent on rivals’ actions, they give a simple example to show that the uniqueness of GBNE is not guaranteed under standard monotone conditions.
In a further study, Su and Xu
17
introduce a Bayesian Nash risk equilibrium (BNRE) model, where they considered the concepts of aleatoric uncertainty and epistemic uncertainty, discussing the model from two perspectives: monotonicity of a continuous BNRE and continuity of a monotone BNRE.

In this paper, we revisit the BNE problem with two new and substantial theoretical contributions.

•

We establish the Lipschitz continuity of each player’s optimal response function with respect to both their own type parameter and the actions of their rivals, by leveraging the classical implicit function theorem rather than relying on growth conditions under the assumption that each player’s utility function is strongly monotone (Theorem
3.1
). This result enables us to simultaneously establish the existence and uniqueness of CBNE using the Banach fixed-point theorem (Theorem
3.2
).

•

Under a set of moderate conditions, we quantify the stability and sensitivity of the CBNE with respect to perturbations in the joint probability distribution of type parameters (Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
). These new results address an important gap in the literature on Bayesian Nash equilibria and provide theoretical foundations for applying BNE models to data-driven problems, where the true distribution of type parameters is often only approximately recovered from empirical data.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section

Section
3

In Section

Finally, Section

The Model

We consider a Bayesian game with
n
players.
Each player possesses a preference utility function
denoted by
u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta)
, for
i\in N:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
,
which depends on the player’s
action
a_{i}
, its rival’s actions
a_{-i}:=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},\cdots,a_{n})
, and
the player
i
’s type
\theta_{i}
.
We assume that a type
\theta_{i}
takes values from set
\Theta_{i}

and an action
a_{i}
takes values from action space
{\cal A}_{i}
,
where
\Theta_{i}
and
{\cal A}_{i}
are non-empty, compact and convex subsets
of
{\rm I\!R}^{d_{i}}
and
{\rm I\!R}^{z_{i}}
respectively.
Following the terminology of Meirowitz
12
, a profile of types is a vector
\theta:=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n})\in\Theta:=\Theta_{1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{n}
and a profile of actions is a vector
a:=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})\in{\cal A}:={\cal A}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\cal A}_{n}
.
Using the standard notation, we denote by
a_{-i}
and
\theta_{-i}

respectively the vector of actions and the types of all players except
i
.
Assume
\theta_{i}
is a random variable with a probability distribution, denoted by
\eta_{i}(\theta_{i})
, for
i\in N
.
Conditional on its type
\theta_{i}
, player
i
’s posterior belief about
\theta_{-i}
is represented by a conditional probability distribution
\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})
, which describes the probability of player
i
’s rivals taking a particular type
\theta_{-i}
.

Information on players’ types is private which means each player
only knows its own type but not other’s. Following the standard set-up in Bayesian games, we assume the
joint probability distribution of
\theta
, denoted by
\eta(\theta)
, is
public information with density function
q(\theta)
defined over
\Theta
. This information describes the probability of all players taking a particular
\theta
, which may be retrieved from empirical data.
Throughout the paper, we will use
\theta
to denote a deterministic element of
{\rm I\!R}^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}}
or a random vector
\theta(\omega):(\Omega,\mathscr{B},\eta)\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}}
depending on the context.
With
\eta(\theta)
and
\eta_{i}(\theta_{i})
, we can derive the conditional probability density function
q_{i}\left(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}\right):=\frac{q(\theta_{-i},\theta_{i})}{\int_{\Theta_{-i}}q(\theta_{-i},\theta_{i})d\theta_{-i}}
, which represents player
i
’s belief about the distribution of rivals’
type parameters
\theta_{-i}
.

In Bayesian games with pure strategy, the response (strategy) function of player
i
is represented by
f_{i}
mapping from type space
\Theta_{i}
to action space
{\cal A}_{i}
.
For
i\in N
, we denote by
{\cal F}_{i}
the set of measurable functions
f_{i}:\Theta_{i}\to{\cal A}_{i}
endowed with the infinity norm
\|f_{i}\|_{\infty}=\max_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}}\|f_{i}(\theta_{i})\|
, and
{\cal C}_{i}\subset{\cal F}_{i}

the set of continuous functions,
where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
Moreover, we use

{\cal F}_{i}^{+}\subset{\cal F}_{i}
and

{\cal C}_{i}^{+}\subset{\cal C}_{i}

to denote the sets of increasing functions and increasing continuous functions
respectively.
For the simplicity of notation,
we write

\displaystyle{\cal F}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal F}^{+}):=\prod_{i\in N}{\cal F}_{i}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal F}^{+}),\quad{\cal F}_{-i}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal F}_{-i}^{+}):=\prod_{i\in N\backslash\{i\}}{\cal F}_{i}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal F}_{i}^{+}),

(2.1a)

\displaystyle{\cal C}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal C}^{+}):=\prod_{i\in N}{\cal C}_{i}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal C}_{i}^{+}),\quad{\cal C}_{-i}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal C}_{-i}^{+}),:=\prod_{i\in N\backslash\{i\}}{\cal C}_{i}\ (\mbox{\rm{res.}}~{\cal C}_{i}^{+}).

(2.1b)

We define each player’s expected utility function given the rivals’ strategy function
f_{-i}
by

{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}):=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}\left(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}\right),\ \forall\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},

(2.2)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the conditional probability distribution
\eta_{i}\left(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}\right)
,
and
f_{-i}(\theta_{-i})
represents the rivals’ action at scenario

\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}:=\Theta_{1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{i-1}\times\Theta_{i+1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{n}
.
Assuming each player sets its optimal strategy based on their maximal expected utility function (
2.2
) under the Nash conjecture, which means each player identifies its optimal response function by holding rivals’ optimal response functions
f_{-i}
as fixed, we consider the definition of Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE) arising from it as follows.

Definition 2.1
(Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE))

A

pure strategy Bayesian Nash equilibrium

is an
n
-tuple of functions
f^{*}:=(f^{*}_{1},\cdots,f^{*}_{n})\in{\cal F}

mapping from
\Theta_{1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{n}
to

{\cal A}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\cal A}_{n}

such that

\mbox{\rm{(BNE)}}\quad f^{*}_{i}(\theta_{i})\in\mathop{\arg\max}_{a_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}}\int_{\Theta_{-i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f^{*}_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}),\ \forall\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},

(2.3)

for
i\in N
.

f^{*}
is said to be a
continuous and monotone BNE (CMBNE)
if
f_{i}^{*}
is continuous and monotone over
\Theta_{i}
for
i\in N
.

Based on (
2.2
), we can write the BNE model (
2.3
) succinctly as

\displaystyle f^{*}_{i}(\theta_{i})\in\mathop{\arg\max}_{a_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}}\ {\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f^{*}_{-i},\theta_{i}),\ \forall\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},

(2.4)

for
i\in N
.
Model (
2.4
) can be
interpreted purely from Nash equilibrium perspective,
where each player’s optimal action
is assumed to be a function of
player’s type parameter, rather than a fixed action in a Nash game.
This is an infinite-dimensional
Nash equilibrium problem where each player’s optimal action is a function.

3
Existence and uniqueness

In this section, we investigate the existence and uniqueness of a CMBNE.
In the literature of Bayesian games, there are two primary approaches to establishing the existence of a BNE, depending on the structure of the objective functions.
One is based on Schauder’s fixed-point theorem, see e.g.
6
;
12
;
18
, where the BNEs are continuous with respect to type varying.
The other is based on
the fixed-point theorems associated with single-crossing and quasi-supermodularity properties, see e.g.
2
;
11
;
15
;
22
;
23
, where the BNEs are not necessarily continuous but monotone under some moderate conditions.
Su and Xu
17
examined the existence of BNRE models from two perspectives: the monotonicity of a continuous BNRE and the continuity of a monotone BNRE, thereby unifying two strands of existing research. In this section, we revisit this issue by presenting a novel approach for establishing existence and uniqueness. Specifically, we focus on the case where each player’s objective function is strongly monotone in its action and uniformly so with respect to other parameters.

3.1
Lipschitz continuity of the optimal response function

We begin by deriving Lipschitz continuity of the optimal response function with respect to the
type parameter and the rival’s actions. To this end, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1

Consider the BNE model (
2.3
).
For
i\in N
,
(a)
u_{i}(a,\theta)
is continuously differentiable in
(a,\theta)
;
(b) for each
f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}
and
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
,
for every
\hat{a}_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}
, there exists a closed neighborhood
{\cal U}_{i}(\hat{a}_{i})\subseteq{\cal A}_{i}
and an

\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})
-integrable function

h_{\hat{a}_{i}}:\Theta_{-i}\times\Theta_{i}\to{\rm I\!R}_{+}
,
with
h_{\hat{a}_{i}}(\cdot,\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\!\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big)
,
such that

\sup_{a_{i}\in{\cal U}_{i}(\hat{a}_{i})}\big\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\big\|\;\leq\;h_{\hat{a}_{i}}(\theta_{-i},\theta_{i}),\quad\forall\,\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}.

(3.1)

Under Assumption
3.1
, for each fixed
f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}
and
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
,

{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})

is continuously differentiable on
{\cal A}_{i}
and

\displaystyle\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}).

(3.2)

The conclusion can be easily established
by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem.
A stronger but often easier-to-verify condition that implies the same conclusion is the global Lipschitz in
a_{i}
, i.e., there exists
h_{i}:\Theta_{-i}\times\Theta_{i}\to{\rm I\!R}_{+}
with
h_{i}(\cdot,\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\!\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big)
such that, for all
a_{i}^{\prime},a_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in{\cal A}_{i}
and all
\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}
,

\displaystyle\big|u_{i}(a_{i}^{\prime},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})-u_{i}(a_{i}^{\prime\prime},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\big|\;\leq\;h_{i}(\theta_{-i},\theta_{i})\|a_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i}^{\prime\prime}\|.

(3.3)

Together with Assumption
3.1
(a), this implies

\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\|\leq h_{i}(\theta_{-i},\theta_{i})
for all
a_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}
, so Assumption
3.1
(b) holds with
h_{\hat{a}_{i}}\equiv h_{i}
.
In the forthcoming discussions, we use Assumption
3.1
.

Proposition 3.1

Consider the BNE model (
2.3
).
Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds,
and for
i\in N
,
(a) for each
\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}

\Theta_{i}
with modulus
\gamma_{i}
, that is,

|q_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}^{\prime})-q_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime})|\leq\gamma_{i}\|\theta_{i}^{\prime}-\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\|,\ \forall\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta_{i}.

Then
\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})

\Theta_{i}
for all

and
f_{-i}
with modulus
\kappa_{i}

\|\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}^{\prime})-\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime})\|\leq\kappa_{i}\|\theta_{i}^{\prime}-\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\|,\ \forall\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta_{i},

where
\kappa_{i}:=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\nu_{i}(\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}^{\prime})+\max\limits_{a_{\in}{\cal A},\theta\in\Theta}\left\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a,\theta)\right\|\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\gamma_{i}\ d\theta_{-i}

Proof.

By Assumption
3.1
, we have

\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}).

For any
\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta_{i}

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}^{\prime})-\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime})\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}^{\prime})-\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime},\theta_{-i})d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\left\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{-i})-\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime},\theta_{-i})\right\|d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}^{\prime})

Consider the BNE model (

For

(3.5)

\underset{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}}{\mathrm{ess\,sup}}\ \|\Delta_{i}(\theta_{i})\|,&\mbox{\rm{for}}\;p=\infty.\end{cases}

Before providing a proof,

The main condition that we impose is the uniform strong concavity condition of

The new result will allow us to derive existence and uniqueness of a continuous BNE in one go and a completely

Proof.

In Theorem

Theorem

The condition means that
the minimal effect on each player’s marginal utility
caused by the change of its own action
(measured by

(measured by
\sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}

and

Corollary 3.1
(Existence and uniqueness of BNE)

Consider the BNE model (

Suppose that Assumptions

-
3.2
are satisfied, and for

This result is straightforward, and hence we do not give the proof here.
The following symmetric three-player Bayesian Cournot example illustrates that this dominance condition
\sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}<\sigma_{i}
can naturally hold in practice.

Example 3.1
(Symmetric three-player Bayesian Cournot game)

Consider a symmetric Bayesian Cournot game with three players.
For each player
i=1,2,3
, let

u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta)=a_{i}\,p(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})-c_{i}(a_{i},\theta_{i}),

where
p(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\alpha-\beta(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}),\ \alpha,\beta>0,
is the inverse demand function
and
c_{i}(a_{i},\theta_{i})=\theta_{i}a_{i}+\frac{c}{2}a_{i}^{2},\ c>0
, is the cost function.
The action and type spaces are

{\cal A}_{i}=[0,\overline{a}],\ \Theta_{i}=[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}],i=1,2,3,

and the type profile
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})
is drawn from a distribution
\eta
supported on
\Theta_{1}\times\Theta_{2}\times\Theta_{3}
. Assume the density function
q_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})
of the conditional distribution
\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})
is Lipschitz continuous in
\theta_{i}
with modulus
\gamma_{i}>0
.
Then the utility function of player
i
takes the form

u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta)=(\alpha-\theta_{i})a_{i}-\Big(\beta+\frac{c}{2}\Big)a_{i}^{2}-\sum_{j\neq i}\beta a_{i}a_{j}.

The gradient of
u_{i}
is

\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta)=(\alpha-\theta_{i})-(2\beta+c)a_{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\beta a_{j},

which is Lipschitz continuous in
a_{j}
with modulus
\tau_{ij}=\beta,\ j\neq i
.
And the expected utility of player
i
given rival strategy
f_{-i}
is

{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})=(\alpha-\theta_{i})a_{i}-\Big(\beta+\frac{c}{2}\Big)a_{i}^{2}-\beta a_{i}\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{2}^{T}f_{-i}(\theta_{-i})\right)q_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})\,d\theta_{-i},

where
\mathbf{1}_{2}:=(1,1)^{T}
.
It is easy to verify that
{\vartheta}_{i}
is strongly concave in
a_{i}
, since

\nabla^{2}_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})=-(2\beta+c),

which gives a strong concavity modulus
\sigma_{i}=2\beta+c
. Furthermore, the gradient

\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})=\alpha-\theta_{i}-\beta\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{2}^{T}f_{-i}(\theta_{-i})\right)q_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})\,d\theta_{-i}-(2\beta+c)a_{i}

is Lipschitz continuous in
\theta_{i}
with modulus

\kappa_{i}=1+\beta\gamma_{i}\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\mathbf{1}_{2}^{T}f_{-i}(\theta_{-i})\,d\theta_{-i}.

Since
\tau_{ij}=\beta
and
\sigma_{i}=2\beta+c
, the inequality
\sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}=2\beta<\sigma_{i}
holds. By Theorem
3.2
, it guarantees that the optimal response mapping is a contraction mapping. Therefore, the Bayesian Cournot game admits a unique equilibrium in which all players’ strategy functions are uniformly Lipschitz continuous.

3.2
Monotonicity of the optimal response function

In Section
3.1
, we discuss the case where each player’s optimal response function is Lipschitz continuous
with respect to variations of their types and rivals’ actions.
In some practical applications,
the optimal response function
{\cal A}_{i}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})

may display some monotonicity w.r.t. variation type parameter
\theta_{i}
and the rivals’ actions.
For example, in Bertrand competition, a higher marginal cost (type) pushes up the optimal price (action), and rivals’ higher prices also raise a firm’s optimal response price (strategic complements), see
1
;
23
.
This kind of research is in alignment with the literature of monotone comparative statics,
see e.g.,
3
;
13
;
20
, which uses order-theoretic tools such as quasi-supermodularity and single-crossing property to characterize how optimal decisions react to changes in parameters.
We briefly recall some relevant notions.

A
partially ordered set
(poset) is a pair
(S,\geq)
, where
\geq
is a reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric binary relation on
S
. For
x,y\in S
, we write
x>y
if
x\geq y
and
x\neq y
.
A poset
(S,\geq)
is (totally) ordered if for any
x,y\in S
, either
x\geq y
or
y\geq x
.
Given a non-empty subset
L\subset S
, an element
u\in S
is an upper bound of
L
if
u\geq a
for all
a\in L
. If there is an upper bound
u^{*}
of
L
such that
u^{*}\leq u
for every upper bound
u
of
L
, then
u^{*}
is the
least upper bound
(or supremum) of
L
, denoted
\vee L
. The
greatest lower bound
(or infimum)
\wedge L
is defined analogously.

A poset
(S,\geq)
is a
lattice
if any two elements

x,y\in S
have a least upper bound
x\vee y:=\vee\{x,y\}
and a greatest
lower bound
x\wedge y:=\wedge\{x,y\}
in
S
.
A lattice is
complete

if every non-empty subset
L\subset S
has a supremum
\vee L
and an infimum
\wedge L
in
S
.
For example,
S=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0)\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{2}
is not a lattice, since
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
have no joint upper bound in
S
.
A subset
L
of the lattice
S
is a
sublattice
of
S
if the supremum and infimum of any two elements of
L
also belong to
L
.

Given a lattice
(S,\geq,\vee,\wedge)
and an
index set
\Theta
, a function
f:S\times\Theta\to{\rm I\!R}

is
quasi-supermodular

in
x
if

f(x^{\prime},\theta)\geq(>)f(x^{\prime}\wedge x,\theta)\Rightarrow f(x^{\prime}\vee x,\theta)\geq(>)f(x,\theta)

for all
x^{\prime},x\in S
and all
\theta\in\Theta
.
The function
f
is
supermodular
in
x

if

f(x^{\prime}\vee x,\theta)+f(x^{\prime}\wedge x,\theta)\geq f(x^{\prime},\theta)+f(x,\theta),\ \forall x^{\prime},x\in S,\theta\in\Theta.

If
f
is positive, it is

log-supermodular
in
x

if

f(x^{\prime}\vee x,\theta)\times f(x^{\prime}\wedge x,\theta)\geq f(x^{\prime},\theta)\times f(x,\theta),\ \forall x^{\prime},x\in S,\theta\in\Theta.

Both properties are stronger than quasi-supermodularity.
In the case that
S={\rm I\!R}^{m}
and
f
is twice-continuously differentiable in
x
, then
f
is supermodular if and only if
\frac{\partial^{2}f(x,\theta)}{\partial x_{i}\,\partial x_{j}}\geq 0

for all
x\in S,\theta\in\Theta
and
i\neq j
,
see
(
19
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2)
.

Given a parameter poset
(T,\geq)
, a function

g:S\times T\times\Theta\to{\rm I\!R}
is said to satisfy
single-crossing property

in
(x,t)
if

g(x^{\prime},t,\theta)\geq(>)g(x,t,\theta)\Rightarrow g(x^{\prime},t^{\prime},\theta)\geq(>)g(x,t^{\prime},\theta)

for all
x^{\prime}>x\in S
,
all
t^{\prime}>t\in T
, and all

\theta\in\Theta
.
A sufficient condition of the single-crossing property is

increasing differences

in
(x,t)
, that is,

g(x^{\prime},t,\theta)-g(x,t,\theta)
is increasing in
t
for
x^{\prime}>x
, or equivalently, if

g(x,t^{\prime},\theta)-g(x,t,\theta)
is increasing in
x
for
t^{\prime}>t
.
Supermodularity is a stronger property than increasing differences: if
T
is also a lattice and if
g
is supermodular over
X\times T
, then
g
has increasing differences in
(x,t)
.
In addition, if the positive function
g
is log-supermodular over
X\times T
, it also satisfies the weak single-crossing property in
(x,t)
.

Reny
15
further introduces the concepts of
weakly quasi-supermodular
and

weak single-crossing
, where both of them release the requirements for strict inequalities, playing important roles in the following discussion.

Assumption 3.3

For each player
i\in N
, the action space
{\cal A}_{i}
is a sublattice of
{\rm I\!R}^{z_{i}}
.

In Section
2
, we only assume that
{\cal A}_{i}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{z_{i}}
is compact and convex,
which does not ensure the lattice property under the coordinatewise (natural) order.
For instance, let
{\cal A}_{i}:=\left\{(a,b)\in{\rm I\!R}^{2}:a^{2}+b^{2}\leq 1\right\}
.
Then
{\cal A}_{i}
is compact and convex, but it is not a sublattice of
{\rm I\!R}^{2}
: with
x=(1,0)
and
y=(0,1)
in
{\cal A}_{i}
, the coordinatewise join is
x\vee y=(1,1)\notin{\cal A}_{i}
.
Thus, we cannot start monotone iterations from a bottom/top action, nor guarantee that the pointwise infimum/supremum of monotone sequences of actions remain in
{\cal A}_{i}
.

By Topkis
(
20
, Theorem 2.3.1)
, a sublattice of
{\rm I\!R}^{z_{i}}
is subcomplete if and only if it is compact. Hence any non-empty compact sublattice is a complete lattice under the coordinatewise order.
A simple and widely used instance is the closed interval in
{\rm I\!R}^{z_{i}}
, that is,

\mathcal{A}_{i}:=\left\{a_{i}=(a_{i1},\dots,a_{iz_{i}})\in{\rm I\!R}^{z_{i}}\ \big|\ \underline{a}_{ij}\leq a_{ij}\leq\overline{a}_{ij},\ \mbox{\rm{for}}\;j=1,\cdots,z_{i}\right\},

Assumption
3.3
strengthens the structure to a complete lattice precisely to guarantee the presence of extremal elements (so that we can initialize from
\wedge{\cal A}_{i}
or
\vee{\cal A}_{i}
) and the closure of
{\cal A}_{i}
under arbitrary supremum and infimum.
These properties are crucial for constructing extremal equilibria via monotone optimal–response iterations and for carrying out lattice–theoretic monotone comparative statics.

Proposition 3.2
(Monotonicity of the optimal response)

Consider the BNE model (
2.3
).

Sufficient conditions for quasi-supermodularity and single-crossing in Bayesian games are well-documented such as supermodularity and increasing differences (see e.g.,

(Existence and uniqueness of

(b)
{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})

is weak single-crossing over
{\cal A}_{i}\times{\cal F}_{-i}^{+}
for all
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
.
Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

The problem has a greatest BNE and a least BNE, with all players’ equilibrium strategy functions being uniformly Lipschitz continuous.

(ii)

If, in addition,

{\vartheta}_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i})

satisfies weak single-crossing over
{\cal A}_{i}\times\Theta_{i}
for all
f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}^{+}
and
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
, then the greatest and the least CBNEs are monotone.

(iii)

Furthermore,
if
\sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}<\sigma_{i}
for all
i\in N
, then the greatest and least CMBNEs coincide, hence the equilibrium is unique.

Proof.

Part (i)
.
We only show existence of the greatest BNE
as existence of the least BNE can be proved analogously.
By Theorem
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
(i),
{\cal A}^{*}_{i}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})

is Lipschitz continuous over

{\cal F}_{-i}\times\Theta_{i}
,
and the functional mapping

{\cal A}^{*}_{i}(f_{-i},\cdot):{\cal F}_{-i}\rightarrow{\cal C}_{i}

is increasing with respect to

f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}
.
Let
\Psi(f)
be defined as in (
3.12
), and hence we can conclude from the discussions above that the operator
\Psi(\cdot)
is increasing in
f
.

Next, we show existence of the greatest CBNE.
For each
i
, let
f_{i}^{0}(\theta_{i}):=\vee{\cal A}_{i}
for all
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
, i.e., the constant function at the top element of
{\cal A}_{i}
. Let
f^{0}:=(f_{1}^{0},\dots,f_{n}^{0})\in{\cal F}
.
Let
f^{0}:=(f^{0}_{1},\dots,f^{0}_{n})\in{\cal F}^{+}
, and define the sequence of functions

f^{k}:=\Psi(f^{k-1}),\quad\mbox{\rm{for}}\;k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.

Since
f^{0}
is the profile of the greatest
response functions, it follows that

f^{1}\leq f^{0}
.
Together with the increasing property of operator
\Psi(\cdot)
,
we can deduce that

f^{2}=\Psi(f^{1})\leq\Psi(f^{0})=f^{1}
.
By induction, it follows that the sequence of optimal response functions

\{f^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing.
This implies that

\{f^{k}_{i}(\theta_{i})\}
is a decreasing sequence for all
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},i\in N
.
Since the sequence is lower bounded by
\wedge{\cal A}_{i}
,
then every decreasing sequence in
{\cal A}_{i}
converges to its infimum, denoted by
f^{\infty}_{i}(\theta_{i})
.
Thus, we can conclude that
f^{k}_{i}

converges pointwise to
f^{\infty}_{i}
.
Since
\Theta
is compact and
\{f^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a decreasing sequence of continuous functions, Dini’s theorem implies that
f^{k}
converges to
f^{\infty}
uniformly. Moreover, by Theorem
3.1
each
f^{k}
is uniformly Lipschitz with a common modulus independent of
k
, so
f^{\infty}
is also Lipschitz continuous.

Finally, we show that the limit
f^{\infty}
must be the greatest equilibrium. To see this,
let
\tilde{f}\neq f^{\infty}
be a BNE. Then
\tilde{f}\leq f^{0}
.
By applying the operator
\Psi(\cdot)
on both sides of the inequality and using the fact that

\Psi(\tilde{f})=\tilde{f}
, we
arrive at

f^{k}\geq\tilde{f}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
By driving
k
to
\infty
, we
obtain
f^{\infty}\geq\tilde{f}
.

Part (ii)
.
By Proposition
3.2
(ii) guarantee that for each
f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}^{+}
,
the optimal response
{\cal A}^{*}_{i}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})
is Lipschitz continuous and increasing over
\Theta_{i}
,
which
means that

{\cal A}^{*}_{i}(f_{-i},\cdot)\in{\cal C}_{i}^{+}
.
Therefore, the operator
\Psi:{\cal C}^{+}\to{\cal C}^{+}
.
Since
f^{0}=\left(\vee{\cal A}_{1},\dots,\vee{\cal A}_{n}\right)\in{\cal C}^{+}
, it implies that

f^{1}=\Psi(f^{0})\in{\cal C}^{+}
.
As the same induction scheme of Part (i), we can derive the greatest Lipschitz continuous equilibrium
f^{\infty}
.
For all
\theta\geq\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and

k\in\mathbb{N}
,
we have

f^{k}(\theta)\geq f^{k}(\theta^{\prime})
,
then it follows that

f^{\infty}(\theta)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f^{k}(\theta)\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}f^{k}(\theta^{\prime})=f^{\infty}(\theta^{\prime})
.
Therefore,
the greatest equilibrium

f^{\infty}
is increasing over
\Theta
.

Part (iii)
.
When

for all
i
, Theorem

(ii) shows that the optimal response operator

is a contraction on

(under the

norm), hence the CBNE is unique. In particular, the greatest and least CMBNEs constructed in Parts (i)–(ii) must coincide with this unique equilibrium.

\hfill\square

4
Stability of CBNE

In this section, we investigate the stability of continuous Bayesian Nash equilibrium against the perturbation of
\mu
, the joint distribution of
\theta
.
The main motivation which underlies this part of research is that in data-driven problems, the true probability distribution
\eta
may be unknown and has to be recovered from empirical data. Moreover, the empirical data may be potentially contaminated. This raises a question as to how to obtain an approximate CBNE based on empirical data and whether such an approximate equilibrium is stable against the data perturbation.

Let
\mathscr{P}(\Theta)
denote the set of all probability distributions over
\Theta
and let
\eta\in\mathscr{P}(\Theta)
be the true probability distribution of
\theta

as discussed in Section
3
.
For
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\Theta)
, denote

\lambda(\theta):=\frac{d\mu}{d\eta}(\theta),\qquad\lambda_{i}(\theta_{i}):=\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\eta_{i}}(\theta_{i}),

whenever the Radon-Nikodym derivatives exist.
We define the admissible class of perturbation distributions as

{\cal M}(\Theta):=\left\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\Theta)\;:\;\begin{array}[]{l}\mu\sim\eta,\ \text{i.e., }\mu\ll\eta\text{ and }\eta\ll\mu,\\[3.00003pt]
\text{and for each }i\in N\text{ there exists }C_{i}\in(0,\infty)\text{ such that}\\[3.00003pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda(\theta)}{\lambda_{i}(\theta_{i})}\Bigr)^{2}\,d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})\leq C_{i},\text{ for $\eta_{i}$-a.e.\ }\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}\end{array}\right\}.

(4.1)

Since
\mu\sim\eta
, each marginal
\mu_{i}
is equivalent to
\eta_{i}
,
so
\lambda_{i}=d\mu_{i}/d\eta_{i}
is well-defined and strictly positive

\eta_{i}
-a.e. on
\Theta_{i}
.
Then, for each
i\in N
, the conditional
distributions satisfy

d\mu(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})=\frac{\lambda(\theta)}{\lambda_{i}(\theta_{i})}\,d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}).

By Assumption
3.1
, for each
\hat{a}_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}
there exist a neighborhood

{\cal U}_{i}(\hat{a}_{i})
and an integrable function
h_{\hat{a}_{i}}(\cdot,\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\!\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big)
, such that

\sup_{a_{i}\in{\cal U}_{i}(\hat{a}_{i})}\big\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\big\|\;\leq\;h_{\hat{a}_{i}}(\theta_{-i},\theta_{i}),\quad\forall\,\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}.

Hence, for any
a_{i}\in{\cal U}_{i}(\hat{a}_{i})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\big\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\big\|\,d\mu(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\big\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\big\|\frac{\lambda(\theta)}{\lambda_{i}(\theta_{i})}\,d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\big\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\big\|^{2}\,d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})\right)^{\!1/2}\left(\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\left(\frac{\lambda(\theta)}{\lambda_{i}(\theta_{i})}\right)^{2}\,d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})\right)^{\!1/2}<\infty,

where the second term is finite for any
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
by
(
4.1
).
Therefore, the interchangeability condition in Assumption
3.1

remains valid for all perturbation distributions
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
.

Let
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
be a perturbation.
For
i\in N
,
define

{\vartheta}_{i,\eta}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}):=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta)d\eta_{i}\left(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}\right),\ \forall\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}

(4.2)

and

{\vartheta}_{i,\mu}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}):=\int_{\Theta_{-i}}u_{i}(a_{i},f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta)d\mu_{i}\left(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i}\right),\ \forall\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}.

(4.3)

Let

{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}):=\mathop{\arg\max}_{a_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}}\ {\vartheta}_{i,\eta}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}),\ {\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}):=\mathop{\arg\max}_{a_{i}\in{\cal A}_{i}}\ {\vartheta}_{i,\mu}(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}).

(4.4)

Assuming that
{\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})
and
{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})
are singleton, we quantify
the difference between the corresponding
CBNEs in terms of the Kantorovich distance between the probability distributions

\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})
and
\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})
for
i\in N
.
We need the following technical assumption.

Assumption 4.1

Consider the BNE model (
2.3
).
For
i\in N
,

\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})
is Lipschitz continuous in
\theta_{-i}
uniformly with respect to

a
and
\theta_{i}
with constant modulus
\varrho_{i}
, i.e.,

\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime})-\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}(a_{i},a_{-i},\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime})\|\leq\varrho_{i}\|\theta_{-i}^{\prime}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|,\;\;\forall\theta_{-i}^{\prime},\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta_{-i}.

(4.5)

The condition requires each player’s marginal utility function to be Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. change of rivals’ type parameters. The condition is not demanding in that type parameters of rivals don’t directly affect a player’s utility function or its marginal in practice.

Theorem 4.1
(Stability of CBNE)

Suppose: (a) Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
4.1

hold;
(b) all the conditions in Proposition
3.1
are satisfied;
(c)
\sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}<\sigma_{i}
, for
i\in N
.
Let
f^{*}_{\eta}
be the true equilibrium, and
f^{*}_{\mu}
be the unique CBNE under perturbation
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
. Then

\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{i\in N}\max_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}}\frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}(1-\alpha)}\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big),

(4.6)

where
\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}
denotes the Kantorovich (Wasserstein–1) distance and
\beta:=\left(\max\limits_{i\in N}\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)
.

Proof.

We use Lemma
A.1
to prove the result.
Under Assumption
4.1
and the conditions in Theorem
3.2
,
we assert that
\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i}\left(a_{i},f_{-i},\theta_{i}\right)

is strongly monotone in
a_{i}
uniformly with respect to

f_{-i},\theta_{i}

and conditional probability distribution

\eta_{i}(\cdot|\theta_{i})
.
We consider
f\in\Psi({\cal F})
,
by fixing up
f_{-i},\theta_{i}
and treating

\eta_{i}(\cdot|\theta_{i})
as a varying parameter, we obtain by virtue of Lemma
A.1
that for any
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
,

\displaystyle\|{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})-{\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\|\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i,\eta}({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i},\theta_{i})-\nabla_{a_{i}}{\vartheta}_{i,\mu}({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\left\|\int_{\Theta_{-i}}\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta\big)\big(d\eta_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})-d\mu_{i}(\theta_{-i}|\theta_{i})\big)\right\|.

(4.7)

Observe that
\forall\theta_{-i}^{\prime},\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime}\big)-\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\big)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime}\big)-\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime}\big)\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime}\big)-\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\big)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{ij}\|f_{j}(\theta_{j}^{\prime})-f_{j}(\theta_{j}^{\prime\prime})\|+\varrho_{i}\|\theta^{\prime}_{-i}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:lipschitz-2} and \eqref{eq:lipschitz-3})}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{ij}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\sum_{i\neq j}\|f_{j}(\theta_{j}^{\prime})-f_{j}(\theta_{j}^{\prime\prime})\|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varrho_{i}\|\theta^{\prime}_{-i}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|\quad\text{(by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)}

\displaystyle=\tau_{i}\|f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime})-f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime})\|+\varrho_{i}\|\theta^{\prime}_{-i}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{i}\left(\max_{i\in N}\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\|\theta_{-i}^{\prime}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|+\varrho_{i}\|\theta^{\prime}_{-i}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|\quad\text{(by virtue of \eqref{eq:Lip-opt-res-in-theta_i})}

\displaystyle=(\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i})\|\theta^{\prime}_{-i}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|,

which shows that

\nabla_{a_{i}}u_{i}\big({\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}),f_{-i}(\theta_{-i}),\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}\big)

is Lipschitz continuous over
\Theta_{-i}
with modulus
(\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i})
, where
\tau_{i}:=\big(\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{ij}^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\beta:=\big(\max\limits_{i\in N}\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\big)
.
For the metric space
(\Theta_{-i},\|\cdot\|)
, the Kantorovich (Wasserstein–1) distance between two probability distributions
\eta,\eta^{\prime}
on
\Theta_{-i}
admits the dual representation

\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}(\eta,\eta^{\prime}):=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{1}(\Theta_{-i})}\left|\int_{\Theta_{-i}}g(\theta_{-i})\,d\eta(\theta_{-i})-\int_{\Theta_{-i}}g(\theta_{-i})\,d\eta^{\prime}(\theta_{-i})\right|,

where

\mathcal{G}_{1}(\Theta_{-i}):=\Big\{g:\Theta_{-i}\to{\rm I\!R}:\ |g(\theta_{-i}^{\prime})-g(\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime})|\leq\|\theta_{-i}^{\prime}-\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\|,\ \forall\theta_{-i}^{\prime},\theta_{-i}^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta_{-i}\Big\}

is the set of all
1
-Lipschitz functions.
Therefore, it follows
by (
4
)

\|{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})-{\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|\leq\frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big).

(4.8)

We are now ready to quantify the difference between the CBNEs
f^{*}_{\eta}
and
f^{*}_{\mu}
. We need the mapping
\Psi
defined in (
3.12
).
Note that since the function
{\vartheta}_{i}
defined in (
2.2
) depends on the underlying distribution
\eta
, then
\Psi
also depends on the distribution.
Thus we write
\Psi_{\mu}
and
\Psi_{\eta}
respectively when the joint distribution of
\theta
is perturbed from
\eta
to
\mu
.

\displaystyle\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\Psi_{\eta}(f^{*}_{\eta})-\Psi_{\mu}(f^{*}_{\mu})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi_{\eta}(f^{*}_{\eta})-\Psi_{\eta}(f^{*}_{\mu})\|_{\infty}+\|\Psi_{\eta}(f^{*}_{\mu})-\Psi_{\mu}(f^{*}_{\mu})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}+\max_{i\in N}\|{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i,\mu}^{*},\cdot)-{\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i,\mu}^{*},\cdot)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty}+\max_{i\in N}\max_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}}\frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big),

(4.9)

where
\alpha
is defined in
the proof of Theorem
3.2
(ii), see (
3.14
).
A reorganization of
the inequality above gives rise to
(
4.6
)

\square

Inequality (
4.6
) provides
a bound on the difference between the CBNEs before and after perturbation of the probability distribution of
\theta
under the infinity norm.
When the range of the support set
\Theta
is large, the error bound may be large. This raises
a question as to whether we can quantify the difference by
{\cal L}_{p}
-norm. To address the question, we need an additional condition on the marginal distributions of the type parameters.

Assumption 4.2
(Equal own-type marginals)

Let
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
. We assume that, for each player
i\in N
, the
marginal distribution of the own type
\theta_{i}
under
\mu
coincides with

\eta
, that is,

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(\theta_{i})=\mu_{i}(\theta_{i})\quad\text{for all }\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}.

(4.10)

Assumption
4.2
ensures that the joint distributions
\eta
and
\mu
share the same marginal, which means that a player’s belief about its own type
is not affected by the perturbation, while its beliefs about the
opponents’ types and the dependence structure between
\theta_{i}
and
\theta_{-i}
may change.
Thus, the optimal response gap
\Delta_{i}(f_{-i},\theta_{i}):={\cal A}^{*}_{i,\eta}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})-{\cal A}^{*}_{i,\mu}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})
can be measured in the
\mathcal{L}^{p}(\eta_{i})
for each fixed
f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}
,

\|\Delta_{i}(f_{-i})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}(\eta_{i})}:=\begin{cases}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Theta_{i}}\|\Delta_{i}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|^{p}d\eta_{i}(\theta_{i})\right)^{1/p},&1\leq p<\infty,\\[6.00006pt]
\underset{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}}{\mathrm{ess\,sup}}\ \|\Delta_{i}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|,&p=\infty,\end{cases}

which averages type–wise deviations according to its own type distribution.
By the pointwise sensitivity bound in (
4.8
),

\|\Delta_{i}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|\ \leq\ \frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}\left(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\right).

Taking the
\mathcal{L}^{p}(\eta_{i})
–norm yields, for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
f_{-i}\in{\cal F}_{-i}
,

\|\Delta_{i}(f_{-i})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{p}(\eta_{i})}\leq\frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\left(\int_{\Theta_{i}}\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}^{p}\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big)d\eta_{i}(\theta_{i})\right)^{1/p}.

(4.11)

When
p=1
,
the expectation on the right-hand side coincides with the conditional Wasserstein-1 (Kantorovich) distance, as studied by Chemseddine et al.
4
. In their work, they establish a relationship between the Kantorovich distance of two joint distributions and the distance between their corresponding conditional distributions as follows:

\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}(\eta,\mu)\leq\left(\int_{\Theta_{i}}\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big)d\eta_{i}(\theta_{i})\right).

(4.12)

Unfortunately, we cannot use
inequality (
4.12
)
as we want to estimate

\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}\big(\eta_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i}),\mu_{i}(\cdot\mid\theta_{i})\big)

in terms of
\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{K}(\eta,\mu)

in (
4.6
).
This motivates use to adopt the relationship
between kantorovich metric and
KL-divergence semi-distance bridged by the total variation metric when
\Theta
is bounded, as established in
5
.

Theorem 4.2
(Stability and sensitivity of CBNE in
\eta
)

Assume the setting and conditions of Theorem
4.1
.
For
i\in N
, let

\mbox{\rm{Diam}}(\Theta_{-i})
be the diameter
of set
\Theta_{-i}
.
Then the following assertions hold.

(i)

For any perturbation distribution
\mu\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
,

\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{L}^{2}(\eta)}\leq\max_{i\in N}\left(\frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}(1-\alpha)}\mbox{\rm{Diam}}(\Theta_{-i})\right)\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{KL}(\eta\ \|\ \mu)}{2}},\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{KL}(\mu\ \|\ \eta)}{2}}\right\},

(4.13)

where

\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{L}^{2}(\eta)}:=\max_{i\in N}\|{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i,\eta}^{*},\cdot)-{\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i,\mu}^{*},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\eta_{i})},

and
\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{KL}
denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence.

(ii)

Let
\hat{\eta}\in{\cal M}(\Theta)
and
\mu:=(1-\epsilon)\eta+\epsilon\hat{\eta}
. If, in addition, Assumption
4.2
is satisfied, then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\|f^{*}_{\eta}-f^{*}_{\mu}\|_{\infty,\mathcal{L}^{2}}}{\epsilon}\leq\max_{i\in N}\left(\frac{\beta\tau_{i}+\varrho_{i}}{\sigma_{i}(1-\alpha)}\mbox{\rm{Diam}}(\Theta_{-i})\right)\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{KL}(\eta\ \|\ \hat{\eta})}{2}},\sqrt{\frac{\mathsf{d\kern-0.70007ptl}_{KL}(\hat{\eta}\ \|\ \eta)}{2}}\right\}.

(4.14)

Proof.

Part (i)
. For fixed
f_{-i},\theta_{i}
, we have by inequality (
4.8

\displaystyle\|{\cal A}_{i,\eta}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})-{\cal A}_{i,\mu}^{*}(f_{-i},\theta_{i})\|^{2}

Consider a symmetric Bayesian Cournot game with two players.

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we explore a new way to derive existence and uniqueness of continuous Bayesian Nash equilibrium and we have managed to do so by deriving Lipschitz continuity of each player’s optimal response function w.r.t. variation of its type parameter and the rivals’ optimal response functions uniformly w.r.t. other parameters. We derive a contraction condition, we enables us to derive the existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium by virtue of Banach fixed-point theorem which significantly simply the proofs in the literature
6
;
21
.
We also make the first attempt to quantify impact of
perturbation of the joint probability distribution of type parameters on the CBNE by deriving an explicit error bound. The new results will have potential
effect on deriving new computational methods for solving
an approximate CBNE. It remains an open question as to whether similar results can be established where the space of type parameters is unbounded.

Lemma A.1

Let
{\cal X}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{m}

x

y

\|x^{*}(y_{1})-x^{*}(y_{2})\|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\Phi(x^{*}(y_{2}),y_{1})-\Phi(x^{*}(y_{2}),y_{2})\|,\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in{\cal Y},

(A.1)

where

(A.2)

Proof.

Existence.

Fix
y
and write
F(\cdot):=\Phi(\cdot,y)
. For any
\tau>0

T(x):=\Pi_{{\cal X}}\big(x-\tau F(x)\big),\qquad x\in{\cal X},

where

is the Euclidean projection onto
{\cal X}
. Since
F
is continuous on compact

and

\langle F(x^{*}),\,x-x^{*}\rangle\ \geq\ 0,\ \ \forall x\in{\cal X},

, so a solution exists.

Uniqueness.

\langle\Phi(x_{1},y),\,x_{2}-x_{1}\rangle\ \geq\ 0,\ \langle\Phi(x_{2},y),\,x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle\ \geq\ 0.

Lipschitz continuity

y
.

\Phi(x,y_{1})+{\cal N}_{{\cal X}}(x)
in
x

A.2
Schauder’s fixed point theorem and Arzelá-Ascoli theorem

Let
Z
be a Banach space and
T:Z\rightarrow Z
be an operator. A set in
Z
is called
relatively compact
if its closure is compact.
The operator
T
is said to be
compact
if it is continuous and maps bounded sets into relatively compact sets.

Lemma A.3
(Schauder’s fixed-point theorem
[
24
, Chapter 3]
)

If
{\cal Z}
is a non-empty, closed, bounded, convex subset of a Banach space
Z
and
T:{\cal Z}\rightarrow{\cal Z}
is a compact operator, then
T
has a fixed-point.

It is well-known that the relative compactness may be characterized by the uniform boundedness and equicontinuity. The next lemma states this.

Lemma A.4
(Arzelà–Ascoli theorem
[
16
, Theorem A5]
)

A set
{\cal Z}\subset Z
is relatively compact if
and only if
{\cal Z}
satisfies the following two conditions:
(a) uniform boundedness, i.e.,
\sup_{f\in{\cal Z}}\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
, and
(b) equicontinuity, i.e., for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that
\sup_{f\in{\cal Z}}\|f(\theta)-f(\theta^{\prime})\|<\epsilon,\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}
with
\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|<\delta
.
