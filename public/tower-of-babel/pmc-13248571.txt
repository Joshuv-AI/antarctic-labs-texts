Male Hypogonadism and Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms: Testicular Volume as an Independent Predictor
Authors: Il Woo Park, Taeyoung Park, Kyung Jin Chung, Kwang Taek Kim, Chang Hee Kim, Tae Beom Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax) is the most clinically relevant uroflowmetry parameter and is strongly associated with the International Prostate Symptom Score. However, traditional predictors such as age, prostate volume, and voided volume (VV) only partially explain the wide variability in Qmax among men with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Given the recognized role of male hypogonadism in LUTS progression and the potential of testicular volume as a surrogate marker of long-term androgenic status, this study investigated whether testicular volume could serve as a novel predictor of Qmax. MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 170 men with LUTS evaluated between April 2012 and March 2017. All patients underwent uroflowmetry, transrectal ultrasonography, and testicular volume assessment using a Prader orchidometer. Serum prostate-specific antigen and testosterone levels were measured. Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore associations between clinical variables and Qmax, followed by multivariate linear regression to identify independent predictors.ResultsThe mean age was 69.3 years, mean total prostate volume (TPV) 66.1 mL, and mean total testicular volume (TTV) 32.2 mL. In univariate analysis, age, height, TPV, TTV, VV, and residual urine (RU) were significantly correlated with Qmax. Multivariate regression demonstrated that TTV (β=0.268, P=0.001), VV (β=0.370, P<0.001), and RU (β=-0.193, P=0.009) were independent predictors, whereas prostate volume, age, and serum testosterone were not.ConclusionsThe TTV is a novel independent predictor of Qmax in men with LUTS. Smaller testicular volume was associated with a lower rate of urinary flow, potentially reflecting the cumulative endocrine decline associated with male hypogonadism. As a simple and noninvasive measurement, TTV can serve as a valuable clinical marker to complement existing assessments, helping to identify patients at higher risk for poor voiding function and guiding further comprehensive evaluations.Keywords: Testicular volume, Maximum urinary flow rate, Lower urinary tract symptoms, Uroflowmetry, Predictor, Male hypogonadism

INTRODUCTION

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) are highly prevalent in aging men, with up to 85% of those aged >50 years experiencing at least one symptom [1]. These symptoms can significantly impair quality of life by affecting daily activities, sleep, and overall well-being [2, 3]. Therefore, an accurate and objective assessment of a patient’s voiding status is the cornerstone of urological practice for establishing appropriate treatment strategies [4].

This evaluation begins by quantifying the severity of subjective symptoms using validated questionnaires, such as the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) [3]. However, alongside the patient’s subjective discomfort, an objective measure reflecting the physical state of voiding function is essential [4]. At this juncture, uroflowmetry and its key parameter, maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax), play a decisive role. As a physical quantification of the force of the urinary stream, Qmax demonstrates a strong correlation with subjective IPSS scores, making it the most critical indicator of objective symptom severity [5].

Although research to identify the factors influencing Qmax is ongoing, with age, prostate volume, and voided volume (VV) recognized as traditional predictors [6], these factors alone fail to fully account for the wide variation in Qmax observed among patients. Furthermore, study findings are often inconsistent. This suggests that a significant gap remains in our understanding of male voiding dynamics, and underscores the need for a broader investigation beyond conventional urological parameters to discover novel predictive factors.

Age-related male hypogonadism leads to a progressive decline in testosterone production, which has been implicated in the development and progression of LUTS and BPH [7, 8]. Intriguingly, recent reports have also suggested a potential link between LUTS and systemic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [9, 10]. Our group’s previous research has established significant associations between testicular volume and pulmonary function, and subsequently between pulmonary function and Qmax [11, 12], with testicular volume recognized as a stable indicator of male endocrine status in this context. A reduction in testicular volume can therefore be understood as a reflection of diminished androgenic capacity resulting from hypogonadism, suggesting a potential triangular relationship among testicular, pulmonary, and voiding functions, and providing a biological rationale for investigating the association between testicular volume and Qmax.

Therefore, this study was designed to overcome the limitations of existing predictors by building on the possibilities raised by our prior findings. We aimed to identify independent predictors of Qmax by comprehensively analyzing demographic, anatomical, and functional variables along with endocrine markers, including testicular volume, in male patients with LUTS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population and Data Collection

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Gachon University Gil Medical Center (IRB No. GBIRB2025-289) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of 170 male patients of Korean ethnicity who presented with LUTS at our institution between April 2012 and March 2017. Patients were included if they had undergone a complete evaluation including uroflowmetry, transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS), and testicular volume measurement. We excluded patients with a history of prostate cancer, urethral stricture, urinary tract infection, or neurogenic bladder dysfunction. Additionally, patients with a history of previous prostate surgery or those currently taking medications that could affect voiding function (e.g., 5-alpha reductase inhibitors or anticholinergics) were excluded from the analysis.

All participants underwent uroflowmetry, TRUS, testicular volume measurements, and serum biochemical evaluations. The collected variables included basic anthropometric data such as age, height, and weight. The total prostate volume (TPV) and transitional zone volume (TZV) were measured using TRUS, and serum levels of prostate-specific antigen (PSA), total testosterone, and free testosterone were assessed. Testicular volume was measured using a Prader orchidometer, and the total testicular volume (TTV) was calculated as the sum of both testes. Uroflowmetry was performed to record the Qmax, VV, and residual urine (RU).

Testicular Volume

Testicular size was measured using a standardized Prader orchidometer. The volumes of the right and left testes were assessed for each subject and the TTV was calculated as their sum.

Prostate Volume

Prostate volumes including TPV and TZV were measured using TRUS. The uroflowmetry-recorded parameters included the Qmax, VV, and RU. PSA density was calculated by dividing serum PSA levels by TPV. Uroflowmetry was performed by using a Flow-814 gravimetric uroflowmeter (Nidhi Meditech Systems, Ahmedabad, India) installed in a private restroom to ensure patient privacy. Recording equipment was placed outside the restroom. Participants were instructed to arrive with a full bladder and void when they felt a normal urge to urinate. The device was regularly calibrated using an internal self-calibration program to ensure accuracy.

Statistical Analysis

All variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation, with ranges also provided. First, a Pearson correlation analysis (univariate analysis) was performed to assess individual associations between each clinical variable and Qmax. Subsequently, to identify independent predictors of Qmax, multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted, which included all variables used in the univariate analysis.

The correlations between key variables were visualized using scatter plots, and the results of the multivariate analysis were presented using forest plots. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Co., USA), and R ver. 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used to create graphs.

Sample size was calculated to ensure the study was sufficiently powered to detect a clinically significant correlation between TTV and Qmax, our primary variables of interest. Based on preliminary data and related studies, we anticipated a small to medium correlation. To detect a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of 0.25 with a power (1-β) of 0.80 and a 2-tailed significance level (α) of 0.05, a minimum sample size of 123 patients was required. Therefore, our study cohort of 170 patients was determined to be sufficiently large to identify significant correlations and independent predictors.

During the preparation of this manuscript, an artificial intelligence-generated content tool was used to assist with English language proofreading and editing to improve clarity and readability.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

A total of 170 male patients with LUTS were included in this study. The mean age of the patients was 69.3±7.0 (range, 54– 90) years. The mean TTV was 32.2±9.3 (range, 9.0–54.0) mL, and the mean TPV was 66.1±32.3 (range, 18.0–204.3) mL. The mean Qmax was 8.6±3.9 (range, 1.0–19.8) mL/sec. Correlation and Regression Analyses

In the univariate correlation analysis, age (r=-0.156, P=0.042), height (r=0.183, P=0.017), TTV (r=0.315, P<0.001), TPV (r=-0.176, P=0.022), VV (r=0.471, P<0.001), and RU (r=-0.203, P=0.008) showed significant correlations with Qmax (Table 2). Among these variables, TTV and VV positively correlated with Qmax, whereas age, TPV, and RU negatively correlated. The positive correlation between TTV and Qmax is clearly visualized in a scatter plot (Fig. 1). Weight and PSA and testosterone (T) levels were not significantly correlated with Qmax.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the predictors of Qmax in men with LUTS and demonstrated that TTV (β=0.268, P=0.001) is a significant independent predictor, alongside VV (β=0.370, P<0.001) and RU (β=-0.193, P=0.009).

Testicular Volume as a Superior Predictor to Serum Testosterone

From a pathophysiological standpoint, the most intriguing finding of this study was that testicular volume, rather than serum testosterone level, emerged as an independent predictor of Qmax. The testes are the primary source of testosterone [13], which plays a crucial role in maintaining smooth muscle and neural integrity of the lower urinary tract [14, 15]. Previous studies have suggested that testosterone deficiency is associated with LUTS onset or exacerbation [16, 17]. However, in our analysis, serum testosterone was not an independent predictor (β=-0.028, P=0.707), whereas testicular volume showed sig-nificant predictive power. This paradoxical finding can be interpreted from several perspectives.

First, in our elderly LUTS cohort (mean age, 69.3 years), various age-related factors that affect voiding function, such as benign prostatic hyperplasia and bladder dysfunction, are already intertwined in a complex manner [18]. Therefore, the isolated influence of highly variable serum testosterone levels may be minor. It has been noted that serum testosterone is subject to significant diurnal and daily fluctuations and the timing of blood collection, making a single measurement an unreliable reflection of the true physiological androgen status [19]. In contrast, despite interpersonal variations, the testicular volume is considered a relatively stable and reproducible surrogate marker [20].

Second, testicular volume may serve as a stable surrogate marker reflecting long-term cumulative testicular health [13, 21]. In the broader context of male hypogonadism, age-related decline in testicular function and testosterone production may manifest anatomically as a reduction in testicular volume. Given that androgens play a supportive role in maintaining smooth muscle tone and neurological integrity of the lower urinary tract, a prolonged hypogonadal state could theoretically contribute to voiding dysfunction [7, 8]. Our finding that smaller testicular volume is associated with lower Qmax is consistent with this theoretical framework, suggesting that testicular volume may reflect chronic endocrine changes that cannot be fully captured by a single serum testosterone measurement [19].

However, we acknowledge that this represents a moderate statistical association and should be interpreted with caution. The structural reduction in testicular volume may be intertwined with general aging or overall decline in systemic health, rather than acting solely through direct hormonal pathways. Therefore, while testicular volume offers a novel perspective as a noninvasive marker of hypogonadism-related change, prospective mechanistic validation is needed to avoid overestimation of its independent prognostic value.

The Triangular Relationship and Systemic Health

This perspective allows us to connect our findings with the “triangular relationship” hypothesis proposed in the introduction. Our previous studies showed that a larger testicular volume is independently associated with better pulmonary function, which is an independent predictor of Qmax [11, 12]. By confirming a direct and independent association between the testicular volume and Qmax, we completed the final link in this triangle. This supports an integrated pathophysiology in which testicular volume reflects systemic health status, represented by pulmonary function, which in turn influences voiding function.

Revisiting Traditional Predictors and Clinical Implications

While the predictive power of traditional functional indicators, such as VV and RU, was confirmed alongside the novel systemic marker of TTV, it is interesting to note that other important variables did not demonstrate an independent influence. Notably, TPV (r=-0.176, P=0.022) and age (r=-0.156, P=0.042), which were significant in the univariate analysis, lost their independent significance in the multivariate analysis (TPV: β=-0.039, P=0.651; age: β=0.004, P=0.964). However, this does not imply that these variables are irrelevant to Qmax. Rather, this suggests that their effects were statistically adjusted or diluted by more powerful predictors such as VV, RU, and TTV in the multivariate model. In other words, the influence of anatomical factors such as prostate volume may have had their explanatory power superseded by functional indicators [22].

This study provides one of the first pieces of evidence that testicular volume is independently associated with Qmax in patients with LUTS. This novel finding not only deepens the current understanding of lower urinary tract dysfunction but also highlights the new clinical value of testicular volume in the assessment of male LUTS. Clinically, assessing testicular volume using a simple, noninvasive method such as an orchidometer can provide valuable additional information for predicting Qmax and evaluating the severity of voiding dysfunction [20]. It could be particularly useful for identifying patients with a smaller testicular volume as a high-risk group for poor urinary flow, thereby helping to tailor their management more effectively.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design demonstrated an association but could not prove causality. Second, testicular volume was measured using an orchidometer, which may be less precise than ultrasound [23]. Third, the study population comprised elderly patients at a tertiary referral center, which may have limited the generalizability of our findings to all patients with LUTS. Fourth, the moderate correlation observed between TTV and Qmax (r=0.315) represents a statistical association and does not imply causality. Potential confounding factors, including age and overall systemic health status, cannot be fully excluded despite multivariate adjustment. Furthermore, the mechanistic interpretation linking testicular volume to voiding function through androgen-mediated pathways remains speculative at this stage. Prospective studies with more rigorous confounder control and mechanistic investigation are warranted to validate and extend the current findings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that TTV is an independent predictor of Qmax in men with LUTS. Smaller testicular volume was associated with lower Qmax, irrespective of prostate volume or serum testosterone levels, potentially reflecting the cumulative androgenic decline associated with male hypogonadism. These findings suggest that testicular volume may offer a novel perspective in the assessment of male voiding function, complementing existing indicators such as prostate volume, VV, and RU. However, given the moderate magnitude of the observed association and the cross-sectional study design, these results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. The clinical utility of testicular volume as a routine assessment marker requires confirmation through future prospective multicenter studies, along with mechanistic investigation to elucidate the underlying pathways.
