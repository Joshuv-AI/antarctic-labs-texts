Abstract
. We use Zagier’s one-sentence proof approach to show that a prime number p p admits a form p = a 2 + a ​ b + b 2 p=a^{2}+ab+b^{2} for some integers, a a and b b , if and only if p = 3 p=3 or p ≡ 1 ( mod 3 ) p\equiv 1\pmod{3} .



1.
Introduction

In
[
2
]
, Zagier gave a one-sentence proof for the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Fermat)
.

Let
p
be a prime number. There exist integers
x
and
y
such that
p=x^{2}+4y^{2}
if and only if
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

The main tool was the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
q
be a prime number, and let
G
be a finite
q
-group acting on a finite set
S
. Then
|S|\equiv|S^{G}|\pmod{q}
, where
S^{G}
is the set of the elements of
S
fixed by the
G
-action.

Zagier considered two different involutive transformations
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
on the set

\displaystyle S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}:x^{2}+4yz=p\}.

The transformation
g_{1}
sends
(x,y,z)\in S
to
g_{1}(x,y,z)=(x,z,y)
. The transformation
g_{2}
is defined as a piece-wise linear transformation on
S
. These transformations generate two groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
of two elements acting on
S
. It turns out that the set
S^{G_{2}}
has odd number of elements, thus, by Lemma 2, the set
S^{G_{1}}
is non-empty, proving Theorem 1. We use a similar idea to prove the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
p
be a prime number. There exist integers
a
and
b
such that
p=a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
if and only if
p=3
or
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

The following theorem will be used.

Theorem 3

(
[
1
]
)
.

A prime number
p
satisfies
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
if and only if there exist unique positive integers
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p=x^{2}+3y^{2}
.

2.
Proof of Theorem A

In this section, we prove Theorem A.
The only if part is easily checked by exhausting all possibilities of
a
and
b
modulo 3. Let us prove the converse. The case
p=3
is trivial as
p=1^{2}+1\cdot 1+1^{2}
. Now, assume
p>3
and
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.
Define

\displaystyle S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}:3x^{2}+yz=p\}.

Define a transformation
g_{0}
on
S
as
g_{0}(x,y,z)=(x,z,y)
. Note that
G_{0}=\langle g_{0}\rangle_{2}
has two elements and acts on
S
. Then, Theorem 3 says that the set
S^{G_{0}}
has exactly one element.

We want to construct another involutive action on
S
. Partition
S
into the subsets

\displaystyle S_{1}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:6x\leq y-3z\},

\displaystyle S_{2}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:5(y-3z)<30x\leq 6(y-2z)\},

\displaystyle S_{3}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:12(y-2z)<60x\leq 5(3x-4z)\},

\displaystyle S_{4}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:7(3y-4z)<84x\leq 24(y-z)\},

\displaystyle S_{5}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:24(y-z)<84x\leq 7(4y-3z)\},

\displaystyle S_{6}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:5(4y-3z)<60x\leq 12(2y-z)\},

\displaystyle S_{7}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:6(2y-z)<30x\leq 5(3y-z)\},

\displaystyle S_{8}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:3y-z<6x\leq 6y\},

\displaystyle S_{9}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:y<x\},

\displaystyle S_{10}=\{(x,y,z)\in S:y=x\}.

Define

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}(x+z,z,-6x+y-3z),&S_{1}\\
(-5x+y-2z,-12x+3y-4z,6x-y+3z),&S_{2}\\
(5x-y+2z,6x-y+3z,-12x+3y-4z),&S_{3}\\
(-7x+2-2z,-12x+4y-3z,12x-3y+4z),&S_{4}\\
(7x-2y+2z,12x-3y+4z,-12x+4y-3z),&S_{5}\\
(-5x+2y-z,-6x+3y-z,12x-4y+3z),&S_{6}\\
(5x-2y+z,12x-4y+3z,-6x+3y-z),&S_{7}\\
(-x+y,y,6x-3y-z),&S_{8}\\
(x-y,6x-3y+z,y),&S_{9}\\
(x,y,z),&S_{10}\end{array}\right.

Then
g
is a well defined transformation on
S
. For example, if
(x,y,z)\in S_{1}
, then we have
3x^{2}+yz=p
and
6x\leq y-3z
. Then for
(a,b,c)=g(x,y,z)=(x+z,z,-6x+y-3z)
, we have

3a^{2}+bc=3(x+z)^{2}+z(-6x+y-3z)=3x^{2}+yz=p
and
(x+z,z,-6x+y-3z)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
. The other cases can be checked in a similar way.

If we denote

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\
0&1&0\\
6&-3&1\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad A=BX=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&0&1\\
-6&1&-3\end{pmatrix}

the transformation
g
coincides with
T_{1}=A
,
T_{2}=-XA^{2}
,
T_{3}=-A^{2}
,
T_{4}=XA^{3}
,
T_{5}=A^{3}
,
T_{6}=-XA^{4}
,
T_{7}=-A^{4}
,
T_{8}=XA^{5}
,
T_{9}=A^{5}
, and
T_{10}=A^{6}=I_{3}
when restricted on
S_{1}
,
\dots
,
S_{9}
, and
S_{10}
, respectively. In other words, for all
1\leq i\leq 10
, we have
g|_{S_{i}}=T_{i}
. It is easily checked that

\displaystyle T_{1}T_{9}=T_{3}T_{7}=T_{2}^{2}=T_{4}^{2}=T_{5}^{2}=T_{6}^{2}=T_{8}^{2}=T_{10}=I_{3}.

These identities prove that
g
is an involution. For example, if
(x,y,z)\in S_{9}
then
g(x,y,z)=(x-y,6x-3y+z,y)
is in
S_{1}
since
6(x-y)\leq(6x-3y+z)-3y
, and
g(g(x,y,z))=T_{1}T_{9}(x,y,z)=(x,y,z)
. The other cases are handled similarly. Thus,
G=\langle g\rangle_{2}
acts on
S
.

From Lemma 2 and Theorem 3, we know the set
S^{G}
has odd number of elements. Let us look more into the equation

(2.1)

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)=(x,y,z)\text{ on }S.

Suppose
(x,y,z)\in S
is a solution to
(2.1)
.

(1)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{1}
, then
x=x+z>x
, which is impossible.

(2)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{2}
, then
y=6x+2z
and

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+(6x+2z)z\equiv 2z^{2}\not\equiv 1\pmod{3},

which is impossible.

(3)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{3}
, then
z=(-12x+3y)/5
and

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+y(-12x+3y)/5\equiv 0\pmod{3},

which is impossible.

(4)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{4}
, then
y=4x+z
and

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+(4x+z)z=(x+z)(3x+z),

which is impossible.

(5)

We skip the case
(x,y,z)\in S_{5}
intentionally.

(6)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{6}
, then
y=3x+z/2
and

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+(3x+z_{0})2z_{0}\equiv 2z_{0}^{2}\not\equiv 1\pmod{3},

which is impossible.

(7)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{8}
, then
y=2x
and

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+2xz=x(3x+2z),

so
(x,y,z)=(1,2,\frac{p-3}{2})
.

(8)

If
(x,y,z)\in S_{10}
, then
y=x
and

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+xz=x(3x+z),

so
(x,y,z)=(1,1,{p-3})
.

So far, we have exactly 2 solutions. Thus there are odd number of solutions in the set
S_{5}
. In particular, there exists an element
(x,y,z)\in S_{5}\cap S^{G}
which satisfies

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+yz\quad\text{and}\quad(x,y,z)=(7x-2y+2z,12x-3y+4z,-12x+4y-3z).

It follows that
y=3x+z
and

(2.2)

\displaystyle p=3x^{2}+yz=3x^{2}+(3x+z)z=3x^{2}+3xz+z^{2}=x^{2}+x(x+z)+(x+z)^{2}

Now, it is enough to choose
a=x
and
b=x+z
to have
p=a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
.
