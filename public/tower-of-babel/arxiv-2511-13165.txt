An inverse design method for generalized zero-étendue sources and two targets Journal: opticajournal Publication type: Research Article
P. A. Braam, 1,* J. H. M. ten Thije Boonkkamp, 1 M. J. H. Anthonissen, 1 K. Mitra, 1 L. Kusch, 1 and W. L. IJzerman 1,2

Abstract
We present an inverse method to compute freeform optical surfaces that transform a light distribution, parameterized by two source planes, into two separate target distributions. The surfaces can be reflectors or lenses, and control both the spatial and directional source and target coordinates of light rays. From energy conservation we derive Jacobian equations for optical mappings, and the optical path length provides generating functions for the optical surfaces. A three-stage least-squares algorithm numerically solves the resulting equations. We present examples with complex source and target distributions.

Introduction

In non-imaging optics, optical surfaces are used to transform light from a given source emittance to a desired target irradiance distribution. While these surfaces may exhibit geometric symmetries in certain cases, their shapes are in general non-symmetric, often referred to as
freeform
. The goal in freeform optical design is to determine the shapes and positions of these optical surfaces, which can be either reflector (mirror) or lens surfaces.

Freeform optical design methods can be broadly categorized as
forward
or
inverse

[

,

,
32
]
.

2
Mathematical model

In this section, we first formulate a mathematical model for the reflector and lens system by deriving generating functions. Next, we use energy conservation to find generated Jacobian equations for the optical mappings, which are used in combination with the generating functions to determine the shapes of the optical surfaces. We end this section by discussing how the second target can be replaced by a far-field target.

\underline{\hat{\bm{s}}}

|\cdot|

\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x}))>0

\displaystyle\det(\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})),

\displaystyle F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle:=\frac{f_{2}(\bm{x})}{g_{1}(\bm{m}(\bm{x}))},

(17a)

which we will use for computing the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
. Similarly, by imposing energy conservation between
\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}
with the assumption
\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x}))>0
, and energy conservation between
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
with the assumption
\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y}))>0
, we find the Jacobian equations

\displaystyle\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x}))=F_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})),

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle:=\frac{f_{2}(\bm{x})}{f_{1}(\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x}))},

(17b)

\displaystyle\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y}))=F_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y},\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})),

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y},\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y}))

\displaystyle:=\frac{g_{1}(\bm{y})}{g_{2}(\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y}))},

(17c)

which we will use for computing
\bm{w}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})

[
51
]
.

In addition to the energy balances, we impose boundary conditions. Since all light originating from
\mathcal{S}_{1}
should reach
\mathcal{S}_{2}
,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, we require
\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{S}_{2})=\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
\bm{m}(\mathcal{S}_{2})=\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathcal{T}_{1})=\mathcal{T}_{2}
. To transform these into boundary conditions, we use the edge-ray principle
[
41
,
54
]
, which states that these conditions are equivalent to the transport boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\partial\mathcal{S}_{2})

\displaystyle=\partial\mathcal{S}_{1},

(18a)

\displaystyle\bm{m}(\partial\mathcal{S}_{2})

\displaystyle=\partial\mathcal{T}_{1},

(18b)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{1})

\displaystyle=\partial\mathcal{T}_{2},

(18c)

see
[
1
, p. 65-66]
.

2.4
Optical path length V

Under the assumption that the optical mappings
\bm{w}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})
are known, a light ray’s optical momentum at
\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})=\frac{\bm{x}-\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})}{\sqrt{|\bm{x}-\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})|^{2}+L_{0}^{2}}},

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{y})=\frac{\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})-\bm{y}}{\sqrt{|\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})-\bm{y}|^{2}+(L_{2}-L_{1})^{2}}}.

(19)

We can calculate the optical path length
V
from
\mathcal{S}_{2}
to
\mathcal{T}_{1}
by using these expressions in combination with the equations

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}V=-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{y}}V=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{y}),

(20)

which can be derived using Hamilton’s characteristic functions
[
8
, p. 142]
. We are only interested in
V(\bm{x},\bm{y})
for
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
. Therefore, we define
\widetilde{V}=\widetilde{V}(\bm{x})=V(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))=V(x_{1},x_{2},m_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}),m_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}))
with gradient

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widetilde{V}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{1}}\frac{\partial m_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{2}}\frac{\partial m_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}\vskip 2.0pt\\
\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{2}}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{1}}\frac{\partial m_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{2}}\frac{\partial m_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{2}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial m_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial m_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial m_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}&\frac{\partial m_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{2}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}}V+(\text{D}\bm{m})^{\text{T}}\nabla_{\bm{y}}V=-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})+(\text{D}\bm{m})^{\text{T}}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{m}(\bm{x})).

(21)

We will use this equation to determine
\widetilde{V}
.

2.5
H
-convex analysis

Generating functions
G
and
H
for the reflector system (
G_{\text{R}}
and
H_{\text{R}}
) and lens system (

and

u_{1}(\bm{x})=G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y});V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}),V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
. There are many pairs
(u_{1}(\bm{x}),u_{2}(\bm{y}))
satisfying these equations. Since we can verify that
H_{w}<0
for Eqs. (

), a possible solution is given by the
G-
convex
H-

\displaystyle\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\;\;\;u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y}),V(\bm{x},\bm{y})),

(22a)

\displaystyle\forall\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}\;\;\;u_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}),V(\bm{x},\bm{y})).

(22b)

The value
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
that maximizes
G
in Eq. (

) defines
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
, implying that
\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
has a stationary point at
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
. Thus,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\;

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}),V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))

\displaystyle+H_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}),V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))\nabla u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle+H_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x}),V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))\nabla_{\bm{x}}V(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\bm{0}.

(23)

Provided
\nabla u_{1}
and
\nabla_{\bm{x}}V
are known, the implicit function theorem implies that this equation provides a mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
under the condition that the mixed Hessian matrix

\displaystyle\bm{C}=\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))=\text{D}_{\bm{xy}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{\partial x_{i}\,\partial y_{j}}\right),

(24)

is invertible for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
, which we verified for our numerical simulations. After substituting
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
in Eq. (
23
) and taking the derivative with respect to
\bm{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\bm{xx}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))+\text{D}_{\bm{xy}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x})=\bm{O},

(25)

which we rewrite to

\displaystyle\bm{C}\text{D}\bm{m}=\bm{P},

\displaystyle\bm{P}=\bm{P}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{x},\bm{y})):=-\text{D}_{\bm{xx}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y}),

(26)

where
\text{D}_{\bm{xx}}\widetilde{H}
denotes the Hessian matrix of
\widetilde{H}
with respect to
\bm{x}
. A sufficient condition for the maximum in Eq. (

) is for
\bm{P}
to be symmetric positive definite (SPD), satisfying

\displaystyle\text{det}(\bm{P})=F(\cdot,\bm{m})\text{det}(\bm{C}),

(27)

where

is given in Eq. (
17a
)
[

, p. 124]

2.6
A far-field target with stereographic projection

Although both the reflector and lens systems have two targets, target
\mathcal{T}_{2}
can be replaced by a far-field target that specifies the target direction of the light rays. To this end, consider a fixed target vector
\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}
which originates from point
(\bm{y},L_{1})
on the first target
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and is directed towards the point
(\bm{z},L_{2})
on the second target
\mathcal{T}_{2}
. A
generalized gnomonic projection
is the relation between
\Delta\bm{y}:=\bm{z}-\bm{y}
and
\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}
, and can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{y}=\frac{L_{2}-L_{1}}{t_{3}}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}},

(28)

which is undefined for
t_{3}=0
. Alternatively, a
stereographic projection
from the south pole projects target vector
\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}
, represented by a point on the unit sphere, onto the plane
z=0
, defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{ster}}[\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}]=\frac{1}{1+t_{3}}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}},

(29)

which is undefined for
t_{3}=-1
. Generalized gnomonic projections (
28
) can be expressed in stereographic projections by the formula

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{y}=\frac{2(L_{2}-L_{1})\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{ster}}[\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}]}{1-|\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\text{ster}}[\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}]|^{2}}.

(30)

Thus, we can replace target
\mathcal{T}_{2}
by a far-field target using stereographic coordinates to find the target direction
\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}
of light rays and then convert them back to positional generalized gnomonic coordinates on target
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.

3
The least-squares algorithm

To compute the optical surfaces in the reflector and lens systems, we apply the three-stage least-squares algorithm illustrated in Fig.
4
. We will first discuss Stage 3 in detail. Stages 1 and 2 are simpler cases, as we will show at the end of this section.

Stage 3:
The final stage of the algorithm iteratively computes the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
, the optical path length
V=V(\bm{x},\bm{y})
and the reflector surfaces. To compute
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
, it starts from an initial guess
\bm{m}^{0}
, which maps the smallest bounding box enclosing
\mathcal{S}_{2}
to the smallest bounding box enclosing
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. Subsequently, the algorithm minimizes the functionals

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}||\bm{C}\text{D}\bm{m}-\bm{P}||_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(31a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m},\bm{b}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{S}_{2}}|\bm{m}-\bm{b}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s,

(31b)

\displaystyle J[\bm{m},\bm{P},\bm{b}]

\displaystyle=\alpha J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]+(1-\alpha)J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m},\bm{b}],

(31c)

\displaystyle K[\bm{m},V]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}|\nabla_{\bm{x}}V(\bm{x},\bm{m})+\bm{p}_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{x})-(\mathrm{D}\bm{m})^{T}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{m})|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(31d)

\displaystyle I[\bm{m},u_{1},V]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\Big|\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{m},u_{1},V)+H_{V}(\bm{x},\bm{m},u_{1},V)\nabla_{\bm{x}}V(\bm{x},\bm{m})}{H_{w}(\bm{x},\bm{m},u_{1},V)}+\nabla u_{1}\Big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(31e)

where
||\cdot||_{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm, by computing

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m}^{k},\bm{P}],

(31f)

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m}^{k},\bm{b}],

(31g)

\displaystyle\bm{m}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{m}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J[\bm{m},\bm{P}^{k+1},\bm{b}^{k+1}],

(31h)

\displaystyle V^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{V\in C^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{2})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;K[\bm{m}^{k+1},V],

(31i)

\displaystyle u_{1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{u_{1}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{2})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;I[\bm{m}^{k+1},u_{1},V^{k+1}],

(31j)

with function spaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{P}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{S}_{2})^{2\times 2}\mid\bm{P}\text{ is SPD, }\text{det}(\bm{P})=F(\cdot,\bm{m}\cdot)\text{det}(\bm{C})\},

(31k)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{b}\in C^{1}(\partial\mathcal{S}_{2})^{2}\mid\bm{b}(\bm{x})\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{1}\}.

(31l)

In the first two steps of this scheme, the functionals
J_{\mathrm{I}}
and
J_{\mathrm{B}}
are minimized for
\bm{P}
and
\bm{b}
to satisfy Eq. (
26
) under the constraint (
27
) and the transport boundary condition (
18b
). The third step of the scheme then minimizes a linear combination
J
with weighing factor
\alpha\in(0,1)
of both functionals
J_{\mathrm{I}}
and
J_{\mathrm{B}}
to update the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
. The fourth step of the scheme minimizes the functional
K
to update
V
and satisfy Eq. (
21
). Finally, the fifth step minimizes the functional
I
to update
u_{1}
and satisfy Eq. (
23
). At the end of each iteration, we update
u_{2}(\bm{y})=H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
and matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
using Eqs. (
24
) and (
26
). These steps are then repeated for
k=0,1,2,...
. Finally, we use Eqs. (
3
) or (
9
) to calculate the shapes of the reflectors or lenses.

An in-depth analysis for the minimization of the functionals
J_{\mathrm{I}}
,
J_{\mathrm{B}}
and
J
using finite volume methods can be found in Romijn
et al.

[
46
]
. A discussion on the minimization of functionals
K
and
I
can be found in Braam
et al.

[
12
]
, which converts the minimization problem to a boundary value problem. We enforce unique solutions by setting the values
V
and
u_{1}
of the center rays equal to
V_{0},u_{1,0}\in\mathbb{R}
, respectively.

Notably, in
[
12
]
, we considered a specific case of the reflector system where the source was assumed to be parallel. The first of Eqs. (
20
) then implies that the optical path length is independent of the source coordinates, so that
V=V(\bm{y})
. As a result, its calculation can be done in a separate stage in the least-squares algorithm. For the general sources discussed in this article, the optical path length is a function of both
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
and its calculation can therefore not be decoupled from the final stage of the algorithm.

Stages 1 and 2:
The computation of
\bm{w}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})
resembles the procedure discussed in Stage 3. In this case, however, there are no optical surfaces between the source planes and target planes and therefore, we choose the generating functions

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}+w,

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}+w,

(32)

with inverse generating functions

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=-\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}+w,

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=-\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}+w,

(33)

corresponding to a quadratic cost function, for which a derivation can be found in
[
46
, p. 71]
. To compute both
\bm{w}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{y})
, we again use the iteration scheme (
31
) subject to the following modifications:

•

In each of the expressions in the iteration scheme,
\bm{m}
is replaced by
\bm{m}_{\mathcal{S}}
or
\bm{m}_{\mathcal{T}}
,
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is replaced by
\mathcal{S}_{1}
or
\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is replaced by
\mathcal{S}_{2}
or
\mathcal{T}_{2}
,
H(\bm{x},\bm{y},w;V)
is replaced by
H_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)

5
Conclusion

This paper presents an inverse method to compute freeform optical surfaces that transform a general light source, parameterized by two planes, to two light distributions at different targets using either two freeform reflectors or two freeform lens surfaces. We used the optical path length to construct generating functions and used energy conservation to derive generated Jacobian equations. A three-stage least-squares algorithm was presented to compute the reflectors and lenses for complicated sources and targets, which we applied in two examples and which we verified by ray-tracing. These examples showed that the systems can handle point sources and far-field targets. Overall, the results advance our understanding of how the optical path length can be used to control both the position and the direction of a light ray at the target using an inverse method. In future work, we will include non-planar targets
[
48
]
and physical phenomena such as scattering effects
[
35
]
. Moreover, since two target planes can serve as two new source planes of a subsequent identical system, we will also explore concatenation of optical systems.
