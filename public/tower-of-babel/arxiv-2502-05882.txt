1.
Introduction

1.1.
{\rm BMO}
and the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function

The space of functions of bounded mean oscillation,
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, was introduced by John and Nirenberg
[
10
]
. This space is the collection of locally integrable functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(1.1)

\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}=\sup_{B}\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|<\infty,

where
f_{B}
denotes the mean of
f
over a ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the supremum is taken over all the balls
B
. This is not properly a norm, since any function which is constant almost everywhere has zero oscillation. However, modulo constant,
{\rm BMO}
becomes a Banach space with respect to the norm (
1.1
). The space
{\rm BMO}
plays an important role in the harmonic analysis and in the study of partial differential equations. One can check that

L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, but there exist unbounded
{\rm BMO}
functions. However, the fundamental inequality of John-Nirenberg
[
10
]
states that
{\rm BMO}
functions have exponential oscillations over the balls. Namely, for any ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

(1.2)

|\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-f_{B}|>\lambda\}|\leq c_{1}|B|\exp(-c_{2}\lambda/\|f\|_{\rm BMO}),\quad\lambda>0,

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are positive absolute constants. A remarkable results about
{\rm BMO}
are the duality relationship between
{\rm BMO}
and the Hardy space
H^{1}
due to Ch. Fefferman
[
9
]
and a deep connection between the Carleson measures and the
{\rm BMO}
-functions established by Ch. Fefferman and E. Stein
[
8
]
.
There are different characterizations and generalizations of spaces
{\rm BMO}
. Coifman and Rochberg in
[
5
]
have introduced the space
{\rm BLO}
of functions of bounded lower oscillation. This space is defined analogously to
{\rm BMO}
except that in (
1.1
) one subtracts from
f
the essential infimum of
f
on
B
instead of the average
f_{B}
. One can easily check that
L^{\infty}\subset{\rm BLO}\subset{\rm BMO}
and moreover,

\|f\|_{\rm BMO}\leq 2\|f\|_{\rm BLO},\quad\|f\|_{\rm BLO}\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}.

It was shown in
[
5
]
that every
{\rm BMO}
-function is representable as a difference of two
{\rm BLO}
-functions.

It is of interest the problem of boundedness of certain operators in harmonic analysis on spaces
{\rm BMO}
and
{\rm BLO}
. The fact that ordinary Calderón–Zygmund operators map
L^{\infty}
boundedly into
{\rm BMO}
were independently obtained by Peetre
[
18
]
, Spanne
[
20
]
and Stein
[
21
]
. Peetre
[
18
]
also observed that the translation-invariant Calderón–Zygmund operators actually map
{\rm BMO}
to itself. We refer also papers
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
4
]
for extensions and
alternative proofs of these results, including consideration of some operators on spaces
{\rm BLO}
.

In the present paper we are mostly interested in boundedness of maximal operators on spaces
{\rm BMO}
and
{\rm BLO}
. A classical case of such an operator is the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function, which for a locally integrable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by

(1.3)

Mf(x)=\sup_{B\ni x}\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}|f|,

where the supremum is taken over all the balls
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, containing the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A classical result of Bennett-DeVore-Sharpley
[
1
]
states that the operator
M
is bounded on
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Bennett
[
2
]
extended this result, proving that the maximal function maps boundedly
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into
{\rm BLO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Further works in this direction can be found in
[
6
,
4
,
17
,
19
]
.

In this paper we consider maximal operators of general type. Let
\phi(x)\geq 0
be a bounded integrable spherical function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, decreasing with respect to
|x|
. Consider the convolution type maximal operator

(1.4)

\mathcal{M}_{\phi}f(x)=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t)|\phi\left(\frac{x-t}{r}\right)dt.

As a corollary of the main theorem of the paper (Theorem
1.2
), we particularly obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\phi
be a bounded spherical function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, decreasing with respect to
|x|
and satisfying the bound

(1.5)

I(\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\log(2+|x|)dx<\infty.

If
f\in{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{M}(f)
is not identically infinite, then
\mathcal{M}_{\phi}(f)\in{\rm BLO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

(1.6)

\|\mathcal{M}_{\phi}(f)\|_{{\rm BLO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c_{d}I(\phi)\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where
c_{d}
is a constant depending only on the dimension
d
.

In addition to the function
\phi
being constant on the unit ball
B=\{|x|\leq 1\}
, we may require the same bound (
1.6
) for the non-tangential maximal function

(1.7)

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\phi}f(x)=\sup_{r>0,\,|u|<r}\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t)|\phi\left(\frac{x+u-t}{r}\right)dt.

Observe that choosing
\phi=\textbf{1}_{\{|x|<1\}}
, the operators
M_{\phi}
and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\phi}
coincide with the centered and the uncentered maximal operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
respectively. Other corollaries of the main result will be considered in Section
6
.

1.2.
Definition of ball-bases

We will work on abstract measure spaces equipped with a ball-basis. The concept of ball-basis was introduced in
[
12
,
13
]
. Its definition is a selection of basic properties of classical balls (or cubes) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are crucial in the study of singular operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The same papers have also introduced a class of bounded oscillation (BO) operators, unifying many operators in harmonic analysis (such as Calderón-Zygmund operators, the maximal function homogeneous and non-homogeneous martingale transforms, Carleson operators, etc.). BO operators were studied in these papers in the context of weighted estimates, sparse domination, good-
\lambda
and exponential decay inequalities. We would like to refer also
[
3
]
for a multilinear generalization of BO operators and those properties.

Working with general ball-bases enables us to unify different maximal operators (centered, uncentered, dyadic or martingale) in one, rather than consider each operator separately. Note that there is no equivalence principles between different maximal operators (such as centered, uncentered, dyadic) on spaces
{\rm BMO}
or
{\rm BLO}
, since those spaces are not Banach latices. Even the boundedness of the uncentered maximal operator on
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
does not imply that of for the centered one. Moreover, the classical approach provided in
[
1
]
is applicable only for the uncentered maximal operator.

Now recall the definition of ball-basis.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{M}
and a measure
\mu
. A family of measurable sets
\mathscr{B}
is said to be a ball-basis if it satisfies the following conditions:

B1)

0<\mu(B)<\infty
for any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
.

B2)

For any points
x,y\in X
there exists a ball
B\ni x,y
.

B3)

If
E\in\mathfrak{M}
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a (finite or infinite) sequence of balls
B_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, such that
\mu(E\bigtriangleup\cup_{k}B_{k})<\varepsilon
.

B4)

For any
B\in\mathscr{B}
there is a ball
B^{*}\in\mathscr{B}
(called hull-ball of
B
), satisfying the conditions

(1.8)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:\,\mu(A)\leq 2\mu(B),\,A\cap B\neq\varnothing}A\subset B^{*},

(1.9)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mu\left(B^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{K}\mu(B),

where
\mathcal{K}
is a positive constant independent of
B
.

Ball-bases can additionally have the following properties.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
is doubling if there is a constant
\eta>2
such that for any ball
B
with
B^{*}\neq X
one can find a ball
B^{\prime}\supset B
such that

(1.10)

\displaystyle 2\mu(B)\leq\mu(B^{\prime})\leq\eta\cdot\mu(B).

Definition 1.3
.

A ball basis
\mathscr{B}
is said to be regular if there is a constant
0<\theta<1
such that for any two balls
B
and
A
, satisfying
\mu(B)\leq\mu(A)
,
B\cap A\neq\varnothing
we have
\mu(B^{*}\cap A)\geq\theta\mu(B^{*})
.

Let us state some basic examples of ball-bases:

(1)

The family of Euclidean balls (or cubes) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a doubling ball-basis.

(2)

Dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
form a doubling ball-basis.

(3)

Let
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi
be the unit circle. The family of arcs in
\mathbb{T}
is a doubling ball-basis.

(4)

The families of metric balls in measure spaces of homogeneous type form a ball-basis with the doubling condition (see
[
12
]
, sec. 7, for the proof)

(5)

Let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space and
\{\mathscr{B}_{n}:\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
be collections of measurable sets (called filtration) such that

•

each
\mathscr{B}_{n}
forms a finite or countable partition of
X
,

•

each
A\in\mathscr{B}_{n}
is a union of some sets of
\mathscr{B}_{n+1}
,

•

the collection
\mathscr{B}=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{B}_{n}
generates the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{M}
,

•

for any points
x,y\in X
there is a set
A\in X
such that
x,y\in A
.

For any
A\in\mathscr{B}_{n}
we denote by
\mathrm{pr}(A)
the parent-ball of
A
, that is the unique element of
\mathscr{B}_{n-1}
, containing
A
. One can easily check that
\mathscr{B}
satisfies the ball-basis conditions B1)-B4), where for
A\in\mathscr{B}
the hull-ball
A^{*}
is the maximal element in
\mathscr{B}
, satisfying
\mu(A^{*})\leq 2\mu(A)
. Observe that
A^{*}
can coincide with
A
and it occurs when
\mu(\mathrm{pr}(A))>2\mu(A)
.
Besides, a martingale ball-basis is doubling if and only if
\mu(\mathrm{pr}(A))\leq c\mu(A)
for any
A\in\mathscr{B}
and for some constant
c>0
.

We also note that all these ball-bases are regular (see Definition
1.3
)

Definition 1.4
.

A family of non-negative real valued functions

(1.11)

\{\phi_{B}:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\}\subset L^{1}(X)\cap L^{\infty}(X)

is said to be kernel-structure if there are positive constants
c_{0}
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
such that

K1)

For every ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
we have

(1.12)

\int_{X}\phi_{B}=1.

K2)

For any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
we have the inequalities

(1.13)

c_{1}\cdot\frac{\textbf{1}_{B}(x)}{\mu(B)}\leq\phi_{B}(x)\leq c_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\omega\left(\frac{d(x,B)}{\mu(B)}\right),\quad x\in X(\hbox{ see }\eqref{x48}).

where
\omega:[1,\infty)\to[0,1]
is a modulus of continuity, i.e. it is a non-increasing function such that
\omega(1)=1
and
\omega(2x)\leq c_{0}\cdot\omega(x)
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let for any
x\in X
we are given a families of balls
\mathscr{B}(x)\subset\mathscr{B}
.
The collection

(1.14)

\{\mathscr{B}(x):\,x\in X\}

is said to be a basis-structure if

G1)

x\in B
for any
B\in\mathscr{B}(x)
,

G2)

for any ball
B\ni x
(not necessarily from
\mathscr{B}(x)
) there is a ball
\bar{B}\in\mathscr{B}(x)
with
\bar{B}\supset B,\quad\mu(\bar{B})\leq\eta\mu(B)
,
where
\eta\geq 1
is a constant,

Examples of kernel and basis structures will be considered in Section
6
. Given a kernel-structure and a basis-structure define a kernel-basis couple (KB-couple)

(1.15)

{\mathscr{G}\,}=\big\{\{\phi_{B}:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\},\{\mathscr{B}(x):\,x\in X\}\big\}.

Any KB-couple
{\mathscr{G}\,}
defines a maximal operator (see (
1.29
)), which will be the main object of study of the present paper.

1.3.
Notations

Denote by
L_{loc}(X)
the space of functions (called locally integrable), which have finite integrals on any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
, and let
L^{0}(X)
be the space of almost everywhere finite measurable functions on
X
. We set

(1.16)

\displaystyle f_{B}=\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}f,

(1.17)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{B}=\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}|f|,

(1.18)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{B}^{*}=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:A\supset B}\langle f\rangle_{A},

(1.19)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}=\langle f-f_{B}\rangle_{B}=\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|,

(1.20)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}^{*}=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:A\supset B}\langle f\rangle_{\#,A},

(1.21)

\displaystyle{\rm SUP}_{E}(f)={\rm esssup\,}_{x\in E}|f(x)|,

(1.22)

\displaystyle{\rm INF}_{E}(f)={\rm essinf\,}_{x\in E}|f(x)|,

(1.23)

\displaystyle{\rm OSC}_{E}(f)={\rm esssup\,}_{x,x^{\prime}\in E}|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|={\rm SUP}_{E}(f)-{\rm INF}_{E}(f),

(1.24)

\displaystyle d(x,B)=\inf_{A\in\mathscr{B}:\,A\supset B\cup\{x\}}\mu(A).

(1.25)

\displaystyle{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)=\inf_{E\subset B:\,\mu(E)>\alpha\mu(B)}{\rm OSC}_{E}(f),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(1.26)

\displaystyle{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)=\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle E\subset B:\,\mu(E)>\alpha\mu(B)}}{{{\rm INF}_{E}(f)={\rm INF}_{B}(f)}}}{\rm OSC}_{E}(f),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(1.27)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\phi_{B}}=\int_{X}|f|\phi_{B},

where
f\in L_{loc}(X)
,
A,B\in\mathscr{B}
and
E\subset X
is a measurable set.
The
\alpha
-oscillation functional
{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)
can be equivalently defined as follows: that is
{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)=\inf_{a<b}(b-a)
, where the infimum is taken over all the numbers
a<b
satisfying
\mu\{x\in B:\,f(x)\in[a,b]\}>\alpha\mu(B)
. Considering only intervals
[a,b]
with
a={\rm INF}_{B}(f)
, we similarly get the ”lower”
\alpha
-oscillation
{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)
. Clearly we have

(1.28)

{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)\leq{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}(f).

In the sequel positive constants depending only our ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and the KB-couple
{\mathscr{G}\,}
will be called admissible constants. The relation
a\lesssim b
will stand for
a\leq c\cdot b
, where
c>0
is admissible. We write
a\sim b
if the relations
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
hold simultaneously. The notation
\log a
will stand for
\log_{2}a
.

1.4.
Main results

Let
(X,\mu)
be a measure space equipped with a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and and suppose that
{\mathscr{G}\,}
is a KB-couple. For any function
f\in L_{loc}(X)
(locally integrable on
X
) consider the maximal operator

(1.29)

\mathcal{M}_{{\mathscr{G}\,}}f(x)=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}(x)}\langle f\rangle_{\phi_{B}}.

In general,
\mathcal{M}_{{\mathscr{G}\,}}(f)
need not to be finite for all locally integrable functions
f
, as well as it can also be non-measurable. We just note that in the above notations (in particular in
{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}
and
{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}
) one can allow to consider a non-measurable function
f
. Moreover, in some places below we will also need to integrate non-measurable functions and work with non-measurable function. For this purpose, in Section
3
we will give definitions of the outer measure and
L^{p}
-spaces of non-measurable functions.

Let us now state the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a doubling ball-basis and let
\omega:[1,\infty)\to[0,1]
be a modulus of continuity, satisfying

(1.30)

I(\omega)=1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\omega(t)\log(1+t)dt<\infty.

If
f\in L_{loc}(X)
and the maximal function
\mathcal{M}(f)
is not identically infinite, then for any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
with
\langle f\rangle^{*}_{\#,B}<\infty
, we have

(1.31)

{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{{\mathscr{G}\,}}(f))\lesssim I(\omega)(1-\alpha)^{-1}\langle f\rangle^{*}_{\#,B}

for any
0<\alpha<1
.

Remark 1.1
.

An inequality like (
1.31
) for the standard maximal function

(1.32)

\mathcal{M}f(x)=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}:\,x\in B}\langle f\rangle_{B}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}:\,x\in B}\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}|f|

was proved in
[
13
]
, where instead of the lower oscillation
{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(f))
the standard oscillation
{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(f))
was considered.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a doubling ball-basis and suppose that
\omega:[1,\infty)\to[0,1]
is a modulus of continuity, satisfying (
1.30
). If
f\in{\rm BMO}(X)
, and
\mathcal{M}(f)
is not identically infinite, then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{G}\,}(f)\in{\rm BLO}(X)
and

(1.33)

\|\mathcal{M}_{{\mathscr{G}\,}}(f)\|_{{\rm BLO}(X)}\lesssim I(\omega)\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}(X)}.

Moreover, if
\mu(X)<\infty
, then we can say
\mathcal{M}_{\mathscr{G}\,}
boundedly maps
{\rm BMO}(X)
into
{\rm BLO}(X)
.

Remark 1.2
.

It is an open question whether condition (
1.30
) in the statements either of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
or Corollary
1.1
can be replaced by
\int_{1}^{\infty}\omega(t)dt<\infty
.

Corollary
1.1
is obtained by a combination of Theorem
1.2
and the following John-Nirenberg type theorems. For those statements, first recall the definitions of spaces
{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}
,
{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}
,
{\rm BMO}
and
{\rm BLO}
, which are the families of functions
f\in L^{0}(X)
with the norms

(1.34)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)<\infty,

(1.35)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}{\rm LOSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)<\infty,

(1.36)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\langle f-f_{B}\rangle_{B}<\infty,

(1.37)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\langle f-{\rm INF}_{B}(f)\rangle_{B}<\infty,

respectively. The following relations between these norms can be easily checked:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}\leq\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}},\quad\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}\leq 2\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}}

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}\leq 2(1-\alpha)^{-1}\|f\|_{\rm BMO},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}}\leq 2(1-\alpha)^{-1}\|f\|_{\rm BLO},

whereas the followings are more delicate properties of these norms.

Theorem 1.3
.

For an admissible constant
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
we have
{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}(X)={\rm BLO}(X)
. Moreover, for any
f\in{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}(X)
,

(1.38)

\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}}\sim\|f\|_{{\rm BLO}_{\alpha}}.

Theorem 1.4
.

There is an admissible constant
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
such that
{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}(X)={\rm BMO}(X)
. Moreover, for any
f\in{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}(X)
we have
\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}\sim\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Theorem
1.4
is an extension of the analogous result on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
proved in
[
11
,
22
]
. As for Theorem
1.3
, to the best of our knowledge it is new even in the classical situations. The proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
are based on Proposition

(see Section

), which is itself interesting. In particular, from Proposition
5.1
it follows that:
if
f\in{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then for any ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.39)

\displaystyle|\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-f_{B}|>

\displaystyle c(n+1)\|f\|_{\rm BMO}\}|

(1.40)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot|\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-f_{B}|>cn\|f\|_{\rm BMO}\}|

where

is an absolute constant. Clearly, this immediately implies the classical John-Nirenberg inequality (

,

and
{\rm BLO}

was recently proved by D. Wang et al. in
[

]
.

Remark 1.4
.

G. Dafni et al. in
[
6
]

2.
Outer measure and
L^{p}
-norms of non-measurable functions

It is well known for classical examples of ball-bases that the union of any collections of balls is measurable and such a property is important to ensure measurability of certain maximal operators. For general ball-bases non-countable union of balls need not to be measurable. So in some cases we will need to use outer measure generated by the basic measure
\mu
. Hence, let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space. Define the outer measure of a set
E\subset X
by

\mu^{*}(E)=\inf_{F\in\mathfrak{M}:\,F\supset E}\mu(F).

For any function
f\in L^{0}(X)
we denote

\displaystyle G_{f}(t)=\{x\in X:\,|f(x)|>t\},\quad t\geq 0,

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(t)=\mu^{*}\left(G_{f}(t)\right).

Observe that
\lambda_{f}(t)
is a distribution function, i.e. it is right-continuous.
For arbitrary
f\in L^{0}(X)
we define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}}=\left(p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\lambda_{f}(t)dt\right)^{1/p},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p,\infty}}=\sup_{t>0}t(\lambda_{f}(t))^{1/p}.

One can easily check that the standard triangle and Hölder inequalities as well as the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem hold in such setting of
L^{p}
norms. We will use below weak-
L^{1}
bound of the standard maximal function (
1.32
).

Theorem 2.1

(
[
12
]
, Theorem 4.1)
.

The maximal function (
1.32
) satisfies weak-
L^{1}
inequality. Namely,

(2.3)

\mu^{*}\{x\in X:\,\mathcal{M}f(x)>\lambda\}|\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{1}(X)}/\lambda,\quad\lambda>0.

3.
Preliminary properties of ball bases

Some of lemmas proved in this section are versions of similar statements from
[
12
,
13
]
. We find necessary to state those proof completely. Let
\mathscr{B}
be a ball-basis in the measure space
(X,\mu)
. From B4) condition it follows that if balls
A,B
satisfy
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(A)\leq 2\mu(B)
, then
A\subset B^{*}
. This property will be called two balls relation. We say a set
E\subset X
is bounded if
E\subset B
for a ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
(X,\mu)
is a measure space equipped with a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and
\mu(X)<\infty
, then
X\in\mathscr{B}
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in X
and let
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathscr{B}
be the family of balls containing
x_{0}
. By B2) condition we can write
X=\cup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}A
. Then there exists a
B\in\mathscr{A}
such that
\mu(B)>\frac{1}{2}\sup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}\mu(A)
and using B4) condition we obtain

X=\cup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}A\subset B^{*}.

Thus
X=B^{*}\in\mathscr{B}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a measure space equipped with a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and
G\in\mathscr{B}
. Then there exists a sequence of balls
G=G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
(finite or infinite) such that

(3.1)

X=\cup_{k}G_{k},\quad G_{k}^{*}\subset G_{k+1}.

Moreover for any
B\in\mathscr{B}
there is a ball
G_{n}\supset B
. If the ball-basis is doubling, then we can additionally claim

(3.2)

2\mu(G_{k})\leq\mu(G_{k+1})\leq\beta\mu(G_{k}),

with an admissible constant
\beta>2
.

Proof.

Choose an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in G
and let
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathscr{B}
be the family of balls containing the point
x_{0}
. Choose a sequence
\eta_{n}\nearrow\eta=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}\mu(A)
, such that
\eta_{1}<\mu(G)
, where
\eta
can also be infinity. Let us see by induction that there is an increasing sequence of balls
G_{n}\in\mathscr{A}
such that
G_{1}=G
,
\mu(G_{n})>\eta_{n}
and
G_{n}^{*}\subset G_{n+1}
. The base of induction is trivial. Suppose we have already chosen the first balls
G_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,l
. There is a ball
A\in\mathscr{A}
so that
\mu(A)>\eta_{l+1}
. Let
C
be the biggest in measure among two balls
A
,
G_{l}^{*}
and denote
G_{l+1}=C^{*}
. By property B4) we get
A\cup G_{l}^{*}\subset C^{*}=G_{l+1}
. This implies
\mu(G_{l+1})\geq\mu(A)>\eta_{l+1}
and
G_{l+1}\supset G_{l}^{*}
, completing the induction.
Let us see that
G_{n}
is our desired sequence of balls. Indeed, let
B
be an arbitrary ball. By B2) property there is a ball
A
containing
x_{0}
, such that
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
. Then by property B4) we may find a ball
C\supset A\cup B
. For some
n
we will have
\mu(C)\leq 2\mu(G_{n})
and so once again using B4), we get
B\subset C\subset G_{n}^{*}\subset G_{n+1}
.

The second part of the lemma can be proved by a similar argument. Using the doubling condition (Definition
1.2
) one can easily find a sequence of balls
G=G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
such that

(3.3)

G_{k}^{*}\subset G_{k+1},\quad 2\mu(G_{k}^{*})\leq\mu(G_{k+1})\leq\eta\mu(G_{k}^{*})\leq\eta\mathcal{K}\mu(G_{k}).

If
\mu(X)<\infty
, then clearly the ball sequence will be finite and its last term say
G_{n}
will coincide with
X
. So the proof follows. If
\mu(X)=\infty
, then our ball sequence is infinite and
\mu(G_{n})\to\infty
. As in the first part of the proof, for an arbitrary ball
B
we can find a ball
C\supset B
such that
C\cap G\neq\varnothing
. There is a ball
G_{n}
such that
\mu(G_{n})>\mu(C)
and we get
B\subset C\subset G_{n}^{*}\subset G_{n+1}
. This completes the proof of lemma.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

If
\mu(X)<\infty
, then the sequence
G_{n}
in Lemma
3.2
is finite and its last-most term coincides with
X
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a measure space with a ball bases
\mathscr{B}
. If
E\subset X
is bounded and a family of balls
{\mathscr{G}\,}
is a covering of
E
, i.e.
E\subset\bigcup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}G
, then there exists a finite or infinite sequence of pairwise disjoint balls
G_{k}\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
such that
E\subset\bigcup_{k}G_{k}^{*}
.

Proof.

The boundedness of
E
implies
E\subset B
for some
B\in\mathscr{B}
. If there is a ball
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
so that
G\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(G)>\mu(B)
, then by two balls relation we will have
E\subset B\subset G^{*}
. Thus our desired sequence can be formed by a single element
G
. Hence we can suppose that every
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
satisfies
G\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(G)\leq\mu(B)
and again by two balls relation
G\subset B^{*}
. Therefore,
\bigcup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}G\subset B^{*}
.
Choose
G_{1}\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
, satisfying
\mu(G_{1})>\frac{1}{2}\sup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}\mu(G)
. Then, suppose by induction we have already chosen elements
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
from
{\mathscr{G}\,}
. Choose
G_{k+1}\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
disjoint with the balls
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
such that

(3.4)

\mu(G_{k+1})>\frac{1}{2}\sup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}:\,G\cap G_{j}=\varnothing,\,j=1,\ldots,k}\mu(G).

If for some
n
we will not be able to determine
G_{n+1}
the process will stop and we will get a finite sequence
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
. Otherwise our sequence will be infinite. We shall consider the infinite case of the sequence (the finite case can be done similarly). Since the balls
G_{n}
are pairwise disjoint and
G_{n}\subset B^{*}
, we have
\mu(G_{n})\to 0
. Choose an arbitrary
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
with
G\neq G_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
and let
m
be the smallest integer satisfying

\mu(G)\geq 2\mu(G_{m+1})
.
So we have
G\cap G_{j}\neq\varnothing

for some
1\leq j\leq m
, since otherwise by (
4.30
),
G
had to be chosen instead of
G_{m+1}
. Besides, we have
\mu(G)<2\mu(G_{j})
because of the minimality property of
m
, and so by two balls relation
G\subset G_{j}^{*}
. Since
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
was chosen arbitrarily, we get
E\subset\bigcup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}G\subset\bigcup_{k}G_{k}^{*}
.
∎

Definition 3.1
.

For a measurable set
E\subset X
a point
x\in E
is said to be density point if for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a ball
B\ni x
such that

\mu(B\cap E)>(1-\varepsilon)\mu(B).

We say a ball basis satisfies the density property if for any measurable set
E
almost all points
x\in E
are density points.

Lemma 3.4
.

Every ball basis
\mathscr{B}
satisfies the density condition.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
3.2
one can check that it is enough to establish the density property for the bounded measurable sets. Suppose to the contrary there exist a bounded measurable set
E\subset X
together its subset
F\subset E
(maybe non-measurable) with an outer measure
\mu^{*}(F)>0

such that

(3.5)

\mu(B\setminus E)>\alpha\mu(B)\text{ whenever }B\in\mathscr{B},\,B\cap F\neq\varnothing,

where
0<\alpha<1
. According to the definition of the outer measure one can find a measurable set
\bar{F}
such that

(3.6)

F\subset\bar{F}\subset E,\quad\mu(\bar{F})=\mu^{*}(F).

By B3)-condition there is a sequence of balls
B_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, such that

(3.7)

\mu\left(\bar{F}\bigtriangleup(\cup_{k}B_{k})\right)<\varepsilon.

We discuss two kind of balls
B_{k}
, satisfying either
B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing
or
B_{k}\cap F\neq\varnothing
. For the first collection we have

\mu^{*}(F)\leq\mu\left(\bar{F}\setminus\bigcup_{k:\,B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}B_{k}\right)\leq\mu(\bar{F})=\mu^{*}(F).

This implies

\mu\left(\bar{F}\bigcap\left(\bigcup_{k:\,B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}B_{k}\right)\right)=0

and therefore, combining also (
3.7
), we obtain

(3.8)

\mu\left(\bigcup_{k:\,B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}B_{k}\right)<\varepsilon.

Now consider the balls, satisfying
B_{k}\cap F\neq\varnothing
. It follows from (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) that

(3.9)

\mu(B_{k}\setminus\bar{F})\geq\mu(B_{k}\setminus E)>\alpha\mu(B_{k}),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.

Since
E
and so
\bar{F}
are bounded, applying Lemma
3.3
and (
3.7
), one can find a subsequence of pairwise disjoint balls
\tilde{B}_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, such that

\mu\left(\bar{F}\setminus\cup_{k}\tilde{B}_{k}^{*}\right)<\varepsilon.

Thus, from B4)-condition, (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(F)<\mu(\bar{F})

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\cup_{k}\tilde{B}_{k}^{*}\right)+\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{K}\sum_{k}\mu(\tilde{B}_{k})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\mu\left(\bigcup_{k:\,\tilde{B}_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}\tilde{B}_{k}\right)+\mathcal{K}\sum_{k:\,\tilde{B}_{k}\cap F\neq\varnothing}\mu(\tilde{B}_{k})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle<\mathcal{K}\varepsilon+\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\alpha}\sum_{k}\mu(\tilde{B}_{k}\setminus\bar{F})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{K}+1)\varepsilon+\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\alpha}\mu\left(\bar{F}\bigtriangleup(\cup_{k}B_{k})\right)<\varepsilon\left(2\mathcal{K}+1+\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\alpha}\right).

Since
\varepsilon
can be arbitrarily small, we get
\mu^{*}(F)=0
and so a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 4.1
.

If
A\subset B
are balls and
x\notin B^{*}
, then
d(x,A)\geq\mu(B)
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
d(x,A)<\mu(B)
, which means there is a ball
A^{\prime}\supset A
such that
x\in A^{\prime}
and
\mu(A^{\prime})<\mu(B)
. Then we get
A^{\prime}\subset B^{*}
, which is in contradiction with
x\notin B^{*}
.
∎

The following two lemmas are standard and well-known in the classical situations.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any function
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(X)
and balls
A,B
with
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(A)\leq\mu(B)
we have

(4.1)

|f_{A}-f_{B}|\lesssim\frac{\mu(B)}{\mu(A)}\cdot\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*}.

Proof.

First suppose that
A\subset B
. Then we get

(4.2)

\displaystyle\left|f_{A}-f_{B}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\int_{A}|f-f_{B}|

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu(B)}{\mu(A)}\cdot\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|\leq\frac{\mu(B)}{\mu(A)}\cdot\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}.

In the general case, by the two ball relation we can write
A\subset B^{*}
and so applying (
4.3
) we obtain

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left|f_{A}-f_{B}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|f_{A}-f_{B^{*}}\right|+\left|f_{B}-f_{B^{*}}\right|

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\mu(B^{*})}{\mu(A)}+\frac{\mu(B^{*})}{\mu(B)}\right)\langle f\rangle_{\#,B^{*}}\lesssim\frac{\mu(B)}{\mu(A)}\cdot\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*}.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a doubling ball-basis on
X
. Then for any

and balls

it holds the inequality

(4.6)

\langle f-f_{A}\rangle_{B}\lesssim(1+\log(\mu(B)/\mu(A)))\cdot\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*}.

Applying the second part of Lemma
3.2
, we find a sequence of balls

, satisfying (

). We have

\mu(G_{n})<\mu(B)\leq\mu(G_{n+1})
for some integer

and we can write

.
By Lemma

,

(4.7)

\displaystyle|f_{G_{k}}-f_{G_{k+1}}|\lesssim\langle f\rangle_{G_{k},\#}^{*}\lesssim\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*}\hbox{ (see }\eqref{x5}),

(4.8)

\displaystyle|f_{G_{n}}-f_{B}|\lesssim\langle f\rangle_{G_{n},\#}^{*}\lesssim\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*},

so we obtain

\displaystyle\langle f-f_{A}\rangle_{B}

\displaystyle\leq\langle f-f_{B}\rangle_{B}+|f_{A}-f_{B}|

\displaystyle\leq\langle f-f_{B}\rangle_{B}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|f_{G_{k}}-f_{G_{k+1}}|+|f_{G_{n}}-f_{B}|

\displaystyle\lesssim n\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*}\lesssim(1+\log(\mu(B)/\mu(A)))\langle f\rangle_{\#,A}^{*}.

Lemma 4.6

). The left hand side bound in (
1.13
) is clear. It remain to show for
I=I_{n}(x)
that

(6.18)

\phi_{I}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{n-j}\frac{\textbf{1}_{I_{j}(x)}(y)}{|I_{j}(x)|}\leq\frac{1}{|I|}\cdot\omega\left(\frac{d(y,I)}{|I|}\right),\quad y\in[0,1).

First, suppose that
y\not\in I=I_{n}(x)
. Then we will have
y\in I_{k}(x)\setminus I_{k+1}(x)
for some
0\leq k\leq n-1
and therefore

\phi_{I}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\alpha_{n-j}\cdot 2^{j}.

We also have
d(y,I)=2^{n-k}|I|
. Hence the right hand side of (
6.18
) is equal

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|I|}\cdot\omega\left(2^{n-k}\right)=2^{n}\sum_{j\geq n-k}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{2^{j}}\geq\sum_{j=n-k}^{n}\alpha_{j}\cdot 2^{n-j}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\alpha_{n-j}\cdot 2^{j}.

Thus (
6.18
) follows if
y\not\in I
. In the case
x\in I
(
6.18
) holds trivially.
Thus we conclude that the collection of kernels
\{\phi_{I}\}
satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.1
. This completes the proof of corollary.
∎
