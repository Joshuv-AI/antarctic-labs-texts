Metastability for the Curie–Weiss–Potts model with unbounded random interactions
Johan L. A. Dubbeldam, Vicente Lenz Burnier, Elena Pulvirenti, Martin Slowik

Abstract
. We analyse the metastable behaviour of the disordered Curie–Weiss–Potts (DCWP) model subject to a Glauber dynamics. The model is a randomly disordered version of the mean-field q q -spin Potts model (CWP), where the interaction coefficients between spins are general independent random variables. These random variables are chosen to have fixed mean (for simplicity taken to be 1 1 ) and well defined cumulant generating function, with a fixed distribution not depending on the number of particles. The system evolves as a discrete-time Markov chain with single spin flip Metropolis dynamics at finite inverse temperature β \beta . We provide a comparison of the metastable behaviour of the CWP and DCWP models, when N → ∞ N\to\infty . First, we establish the metastability of the CWP model and, using this result, prove metastability for the DCWP model (with high probability). We then determine the ratio between the metastable transition time for the DCWP model and the corresponding time for the CWP model. Specifically, we derive the asymptotic tail behavior and moments of this ratio. Our proof combines the potential-theoretic approach to metastability with concentration of measure techniques, the latter adapted to our specific context.

1.
Introduction

Over the past 50 years, the mathematical study of statical and dynamical aspects of disordered mean-field spin systems has attracted considerable interest. In this paper, we continue the analysis of metastable behaviour of these systems, as initiated in
[
7
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
5
]
, by examining the disordered Curie–Weiss–Potts (DCWP) model with unbounded interactions. This model generalises the disordered mean-field Ising model to
q\geq 2
spins. Here, “disordered” refers to the fact that spin interactions are independent and identically distributed random variables. These random variables are chosen to have a fixed mean and a well-defined cumulant generating function, with a distribution independent of the number of spins. In particular, this model also encompasses the Potts model on homogeneous dense random graphs. Specific examples that fit within our framework include the Potts model on Erdős–Rényi random graphs, the Potts model on multi-edge random graphs, and the Potts model with Gaussian noise.

As a first result, we prove metastability in the sense of
[
22
]
for the Curie–Weiss–Potts (CWP) model at fixed temperature in large volumes. Further, we show that metastability of the CWP model implies metastability of the DCWP model with respect to the same metastable sets, for almost all realisations of the random interactions. After identifying specific metastable sets for both models, we derive estimates for the ratio of mean metastable transition times in the DCWP and CWP models in the regime of large-volumes and fixed temperatures. These estimates are of two types: the first one provides insight into the tail behaviour, showing that, asymptotically in
N
, this ratio behaves like a random variable of order an exponential of a sub-Gaussian random variable. Moreover, we derive moment estimates for this random ratio, again in large volumes and at fixed temperatures. Our strategy is based on techniques developed in
[
5
]
and
[
22
]
. Where adaptations are necessary, references are provided to the specific results in these publications.

Our methods are based on the potential-theoretic approach to metastability. This method was initiated in
[
6
]
and allows us to express mean metastable exit times in terms of capacities and weighted sums of the equilibrium potential, the latter frequently referred to as harmonic sums (for a general overview of this method, we refer to
[
3
]
). Estimates on the former can be obtained with the help of well-known variational principles, while estimates on the latter are generally more involved and, in this manuscript, rely on a new definition of metastability given by
[
22
]
. This definition differs slightly from the standard one given in
[
3
]
, yet it provides crucial insights, particularly regarding the localisation of the harmonic sums around the initial metastable set. Additionally, our proof offers a strategy for verifying metastability in other similar mean-field models. Similar to previous works on metastability in disordered models, the use of concentration of measure is pivotal in the comparison of the disordered and mean-field model. However, in contrast to these studies, we allow for potentially unbounded random interactions. To handle this, we develop concentration inequalities using Chernoff-type bounds tailored to our setting, inspired by results from
[
15
]
.
Furthermore, the presence of multiple critical temperatures, unlike the single critical temperature of the Curie–Weiss model, necessitates a careful analysis of the free energy landscape and its phase transition structure (we refer to
[
19
]
for a complete description of the free energy landscape for the CWP model). This in turn means that different temperature regimes are linked to different properties of the critical points of the free energy landscape and therefore need to be treated in different manners.

Another class of disordered models is the random-field Curie–Weiss–Potts (RFCWP) model, in which the magnetic field is given by i.i.d. random variables. To our knowledge, the metastability of the RFCWP model has only been studied in
[
23
]
. The techniques developed in this paper are also applicable to the study of the metastable behaviour of the disordered Curie–Weiss–Potts model with an additional random field, under a suitable assumption on the structure of the free-energy landscape of the underlying RFCWP model.

1.1.
The model

The
disordered Curie–Weiss–Potts (DCWP) model
is a generalisation of the disordered mean-field Ising model to
q
components. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider an enumeration of the vertex set consisting of
N
elements. To each vertex
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
we associate a spin variable
\sigma_{i}
taking values in
\{1,\ldots,q\}
,
q\geq 2
, the so-called set of colours. We write
\mathcal{S}_{N}=\{1,\ldots,q\}^{N}
to denote the corresponding state space. Elements of
\mathcal{S}_{N}
are denoted by Greek letters
\sigma,\eta
, and will be called
configurations
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
be an abstract probability space and let
\mean
and
\var
denote expectation and variance with respect to
\prob
. Let
J\equiv(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq\infty}
be a triangular array of real random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
whose law satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

For some
v\in(0,\infty)
assume that the triangular array
(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq\infty}
consists of i.i.d. random variables with

(i)

\mean\bigl[J_{12}\bigr]=1
and
\var[J_{12}]=v
,

(ii)

the set
\mathscr{D}=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\colon\mean\bigl[\exp(\lambda J_{12})\bigr]<\infty\right\}
has non-empty interior containing
0
.

Given a realisation of
J
and
N\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, we consider the following
random
Hamiltonian,
H_{N}\colon\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle H_{N}(\sigma)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}J_{ij}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}\}}.

The corresponding random Gibbs measure,
\mu_{N}
, at inverse temperature
\beta\geq 0
is defined by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{N}(\sigma)\;\equiv\;\mu_{N,\beta}(\sigma)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H_{N}(\sigma)}}{Z_{N}},

where
Z_{N}\equiv Z_{N,\beta}
denotes the partition function. In view of Assumption
1.1
-(ii), the expected value of the partition function is finite, for all values of
\beta
and provided that
N
is chosen large enough. Notice that for
q=2
, the model becomes the disordered Curie–Weiss model.

The spin configuration evolves as a discrete-time Markov chain
\Sigma^{N}\equiv(\Sigma^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, with state space
\mathcal{S}_{N}
and Glauber-Metropolis transition probabilities given for any
\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\pi_{N}(\sigma,\eta)\;\equiv\;\pi_{N,\beta}(\sigma,\eta)\;=\;\begin{cases}\,(Nq)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\left[H_{N}(\eta)-H_{N}(\sigma)\right]_{+}},&\text{if }d_{H}(\sigma,\eta)=1,\\
1-\sum_{\eta\neq\sigma}\pi_{N}(\sigma,\eta)&\text{when }\sigma=\eta,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
[a]_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\{0,a\}
and
d_{H}(\sigma,\eta)
denotes the Hamming distance between configurations
\sigma
and
\eta
. To lighten notation we will also write
\sigma\sim\eta
, if
d_{H}(\sigma,\eta)=1
. The Markov chain,
\Sigma^{N}
, defined by
\pi_{N}
is irreducible and reversible with respect to the Gibbs measure
\mu_{N}
. The associated (discrete) generator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
acts on bounded functions
f\colon\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(1.4)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{N}f\bigr)(\sigma)\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\pi_{N}(\sigma,\eta)\bigl(f(\eta)-f(\sigma)\bigr).

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\Prob_{\!\nu}^{N}
to denote the law of
\Sigma^{N}
starting from an initial distribution
\nu
in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, and
\Mean_{\nu}^{N}
to denote the corresponding expectation. Furthermore, for
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}
, we define the
first return time

\tau_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}
to be the following

(1.5)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}\;\equiv\;\tau_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}\bigl(\Sigma^{N}\bigr)\;\coloneqq\;\inf\bigl\{t>0\colon\Sigma^{N}(t)\in\mathcal{A}\bigr\}.

The goal of the present paper is to compare the metastable behaviour of the DCWP model with that of the standard mean-field Curie–Weiss–Potts (CWP) model. The latter is the model with Hamiltonian

(1.6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma)\;\coloneqq\;-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}\}}\;=\;\mean\bigl[H_{N}(\sigma)\bigr].

Quantities such as
\widetilde{Z}_{N},(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0},\widetilde{\pi}_{N},\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}
and any other one with the
\sim
superscript are defined analogously, taking
\widetilde{H}_{N}
instead of
H_{N}
. With an abuse of terminology and in accordance with the literature (see e.g.
[
7
]
and
[
5
]
), we sometimes refer to the models defined in terms of
H_{N}
and
\widetilde{H}_{N}
as the
quenched
and the
annealed
model, respectively.

A particular feature of the CWP model is that its Hamiltonian can be expressed in terms of the
empirical measure
,
L_{N}
, encoding the relative frequencies of the different colours. For this purpose, define
L_{N}\colon\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathcal{P}_{N}
by

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sigma\longmapsto\bigl(L_{N}(\sigma)[\{1\}],\dots,L_{N}(\sigma)[\{q\}]\bigr)\quad\text{with}\quad L_{N}(\sigma)[\{k\}]\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma_{i}=k\}},

where
\mathcal{P}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x\in[0,1]^{q}:\sum_{k=1}^{q}x_{k}=1\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{N}_{0}^{q}\cap\mathcal{P}
. Then, the Hamiltonian of the CWP model can be rewritten as

(1.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma)\;=\;-\frac{N}{2}\lVert L_{N}(\sigma)\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

Since the transition probabilities
\widetilde{\pi}_{N}
depend only on the energy difference of two adjacent configurations, we have that the process is lumpable, that is,
\bigl(L_{N}(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{N}(t))\bigr)_{t\geq 0}
is also a reversible, discrete-time Markov process, see e.g.
[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Our choice of the quenched model is very general and we now illustrate it with three specific examples. The first two pertain to the Potts model on two different types of random graphs, while the third example outlines a Potts model incorporating Gaussian noise.

Example 1.2

(Potts model on the Erdős–Rényi random graph)
.

By choosing
J_{12}
distributed as
p^{-1}\operatorname{Ber}(p)
with
p\in(0,1]
,
H_{N}
in (
1.1
) becomes the Hamiltonian of the CWP model on the Erdős–Rényi random graph in which edges are present with probability
p
.

Example 1.3

(Potts model on the multi-edge random graph)
.

Let
K\sim\text{Pois}(p\binom{N}{2})
and let
(I_{k}^{N})_{k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. uniform random variables in
\{\{i,j\}:1\leq i<j\leq N\}
. These define the so-called multi-edge random graph with edge set
E
, also known as Norros–Reittu model (see
[
20
]
). The CWP model on the multi-edge random graph is therefore defined by the Hamiltonian

(1.9)

\displaystyle H_{N}(\sigma)\;=\;-\frac{1}{Np}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}\}},

that is, we sum over the edges present in the random graph and set the interaction identically equal to
1
. We obtain the same model by defining the random variable

(1.10)

\displaystyle J_{ij}\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\{I^{N}_{k}=\{i,j\}\}}

and replacing it in the Hamiltonian (
1.1
). This is the same as choosing
J_{12}
distributed as
p^{-1}\operatorname{Pois}(p)
with
p\in(0,1]
in (
1.1
). Notice that these random variables are not sub-Gaussian.

Example 1.4

(Potts model with Gaussian noise)
.

By letting
J_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(1,v)
results in an only
partially
ferromagnetic model, as the random variables are allowed negative values. However, our results show that, for fixed
v
and for
N
going to infinity, it behaves as the ferromagnetic mean-field model. In addition, the form of the cumulant generating function simplifies the expression of some results, for instance dropping the error term from lemma
3.2
and it’s consequences.

1.2.
Main results

Our main objective is to compare the metastable transition times of the CWP model with the ones of the DCWP model. For this purpose, let us first recall the definition of metastable Markov chains and metastable sets following
[
22
, Definition 1.1]
.

Definition 1.5

(
\rho_{N}
-Metastability)
.

For
\rho_{N}>0
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\bigl\{\mathcal{M}_{1,N},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{K,N}\bigr\}
be a collection of disjoint subsets of
S_{N}
and set
\mathcal{M}_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}\mathcal{M}_{i,N}
. The Markov chain
(\Sigma^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is
\rho_{N}
-metastable with respect to
\bigl\{\mathcal{M}_{1,N},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{K,N}\bigr\}
when

(1.11)

\displaystyle K\frac{\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\Prob^{N}_{\mu_{N}\rvert\mathcal{M}_{j,N}}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{M}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{j,N}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{M}_{j,N}}\bigr]}{\min_{\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{N}}\Prob^{N}_{\mu_{N}\rvert\mathcal{X}}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigr]}\;\leq\;\rho_{N}\;\ll\;1,

where, for a non-empty set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}
,
\mu_{N}\rvert\mathcal{X}
denotes the invariant measure
\mu_{N}
conditioned on the set
\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 1.6
.

This definition of metastability covers both
metastable transitions
and
tunneling transitions
. In the former, the system evolves towards states of lower energy, whereas in the latter the system moves between states with the same energy. Due to the symmetry of the CWP model, we see both types of states.

In general, identifying suitable candidates for metastable sets can be a challenging task that is highly dependent on the specific model being considered. However, for mean-field spin systems, it is well established, cf.
[
3

21
]
, that metastable sets correspond to the local minima of the free energy landscape. Specifically, in the context of the Curie–Weiss–Potts model, it is known (see, for example,
[
13
]
) that for any
\beta\in(0,\infty)
, the limiting free energy
\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(1.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{\beta N}\ln\widetilde{Z}_{N}\;=\;\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{P}}\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{x}),

where

(1.13)

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{x})\;=\;-\frac{1}{2}\,\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\ln\boldsymbol{x}_{i}.

Our first result says that the Curie–Weiss–Potts model exhibits metastable behaviour in the sense of Definition

Theorem 1.7

(Metastability of the CWP model)
.

For every
\beta>\beta_{1}(q)
there exists
k_{1}\equiv k_{1}(\beta,q)
such that, for all
k<k_{1}
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with the property that for every
N>N_{0}
the Markov chain
(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is
\mathrm{e}^{-kN}
-metastable with respect to the metastable sets
\{\mathcal{M}_{i,N}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\}
defined in (
1.15
).

Remark 1.8
.

Although the above result is primarily used in the proof of the next theorem, that addresses the metastability of the disordered Curie–Weiss–Potts model, it is a novel development in its own right. The parameter
k_{1}
, whose explicit value is given in the proof of Theorem

, encodes the strength of metastability with respect to the chosen sets
\{\mathcal{M}_{i,N}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\}
and, consequently, the separation of time scales induced by the underlying free energy landscape. For a discussion of the optimality of
k_{1}
, see Remark

.

The next theorem states that the disordered Curie–Weiss–Potts model is also
\rho

Theorem 1.9

(Metastability of the DCWP model)
.

For every
\beta>\beta_{1}(q)
and any
k\in(0,k_{1})
, where

is defined as in Theorem

, the event

(1.16)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\textnormal{meta}}(N)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\Bigl\{({\Sigma}^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}\text{ is $\mathrm{e}^{-kN}$-metastable}\text{ w.r.t.}\,\{\mathcal{M}_{i,N}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\}\Bigr\}

satisfies

(1.17)

\displaystyle\prob\biggl[\liminf_{N\to\infty}\Omega_{\textnormal{meta}}(N)\biggr]\;=\;1.

Remark 1.10
.

Note that,
\prob
-almost surely, the additional disorder induced by the random variables
(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
does not affect the strength of metastability of the Markov chain
\Sigma^{N}
with respect to

. Actually, as we argue in Remark

is optimal (on exponential scale): for every

there exists a random variable

such that,

the Markov chain
\Sigma^{N}
is not
\mathrm{e}^{-kN}
- metastable.

In our second set of results, we compare the mean transition times between specific disjoint subsets of the metastable sets of the Markov chain
(\Sigma^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
with those of the corresponding Markov chain
(\widetilde{\Sigma}^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. For this purpose, we distinguish between metastable and tunnelling transitions. In the former, we examine the mean hitting times of the metastable set,
\mathcal{B}_{N}
, associated with the global minima of
\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}
(stable states) when the corresponding Markov chain starts in the metastable set,
\mathcal{A}_{N}
, linked to local minima of
\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}
(metastable states). In contrast, the latter pertains to transitions between stable states.

Definition 1.11
.

For metastable transitions we consider the following metastable and stable sets:

(i)

\mathcal{A}_{N}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}\mathcal{M}_{i,N}
and
\mathcal{B}_{N}=\mathcal{M}_{0,N}
if
\beta_{1}(q)<\beta\leq\beta_{2}(q)
,

(ii)

\mathcal{A}_{N}=\mathcal{M}_{0,N}
and
\mathcal{B}_{N}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{q}\mathcal{M}_{i,N}
if
\beta_{2}(q)<\beta<q
.

For tunnelling transitions we consider the following stable sets:

(iii)

\mathcal{A}_{N}=\mathcal{M}_{1,N}
and
\mathcal{B}_{N}=\bigcup_{i=2}^{q}\mathcal{M}_{i,N}
if
\beta_{2}(q)<\beta
.

Remark 1.12
.

Notice that in case (iii) it is possible to define
\mathcal{A}_{N}
as any of the sets
\mathcal{M}_{i,N}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,q\}
, and
\mathcal{B}_{N}
as the union of the remaining ones.

Moreover, we define, for non-empty disjoint sets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}
, the so-called
last-exit biased distribution
on
\mathcal{A}
for the transition from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
by

(1.18)

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(\sigma)\;\equiv\;\nu_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}^{N}(\sigma)\;=\;\frac{\mu_{N}(\sigma)\Prob_{\!\sigma}^{N}\bigl[\tau_{\mathcal{B}}^{N}<\tau_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}\bigr]}{\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\Prob_{\!\sigma}^{N}\bigl[\tau_{\mathcal{B}}^{N}<\tau_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}\bigr]},\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{A}.

1.3.
Methods and outline

Our proofs crucially rely on the
potential theoretic
approach to metastability, which links the probabilistic objects describing the metastable behaviour of the system to the solutions of certain boundary value problems. This approach was initiated by the paper
[
6
]
and leads to precise asymptotics of the metastable transition time (for a general overview of this method we refer to
[
3
]
). Furthermore, we establish Chernoff-type concentration inequalities for arbitrary Lipschitz functions of the edge weights, which can be achieved under a condition pertaining to the existence of the cumulant generating function. This approach was inspired by
[
15
]
, where the concept of subgaussian random variables is generalised to encompass arbitrary metric probability spaces.

1.3.1.
Key notions of potential theory

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
and non-empty, disjoint subsets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}
, the
equilibrium potential

h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{S}_{N}\to[0,1]
is the unique solution of the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\big(\mathcal{L}_{N}f\big)(\sigma)&\mspace{-5mu}=\mspace{-5mu}&0,&\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}\setminus(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}),\\[2.15277pt]
f(\sigma)&\mspace{-5mu}=\mspace{-5mu}&\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma),&\sigma\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}.\end{array}\right.

Notice that
h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
has the following probabilistic interpretation: for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}\setminus(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B})
, we have
h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(\sigma)=\Prob^{N}_{\!\sigma}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{A}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{B}}\bigr]
. Another pivotal quantity in potential theory is the
capacity
of the pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
that is defined by

(1.23)

\displaystyle\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\,\Prob^{N}_{\!\sigma}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{B}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bigr]\;=\;\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\bigl(-\mathcal{L}_{N}h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\bigr)(\sigma).

Recalling that we write
\mu_{N}\rvert\mathcal{A}
to denote the Gibbs measure
\mu_{N}
conditioned on the set
\mathcal{A}
, we clearly have that

(1.24)

\displaystyle\Prob^{N}_{\mu_{N}\rvert\mathcal{A}}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{B}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{A}}\bigr]\;=\;\frac{\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}{\mu_{N}[\mathcal{A}]}.

Furthermore, since
h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(\sigma)+h^{N}_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}}(\sigma)=1
, for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{N}
applied to a constant function vanishes, the definition of metastability also implies
\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})=\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
. Moreover, for arbitrary sets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}
with
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
,

\displaystyle\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\,\Prob^{N}_{\!\sigma}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{A}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{C}}\bigr]

(1.25)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\,\Prob^{N}_{\!\sigma}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{B}}<\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{C}}\bigr]\;=\;\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}).

By using potential theory, see e.g.
[
3
, Corollary 7.11]
, one obtains an expression for the mean hitting time between two sets, albeit with respect to the last-exit biased distribution defined in (
1.18
)

(1.26)

\displaystyle\Mean^{N}_{\nu_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}}\bigl[\tau^{N}_{\mathcal{B}}\bigr]\;=\;\frac{1}{\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})}\,\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\,h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(\sigma)\;=\;\frac{\|h^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\|_{\mu_{N}}}{\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mu_{N}}
denotes the
\ell_{1}(\mu_{N})
-norm. The presence of the last-exit biased distribution in the formula is an artefact of the method. However, if the starting set is atomic it reduces to a Dirac measure. From Equations (
1.24
) and (
1.26
) we see that, via the potential-theoretic approach, the definitions and quantities relevant to metastability admit both probabilistic and analytical representations. One difficulty in this route is estimating the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the harmonic function
h_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}^{N}
, for which we rely on results from
[
22
]
. In order to estimate capacities effectively, we employ two dual variational principles. The
Dirichlet principle
states that

(1.27)

\displaystyle\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\;=\;\inf\bigl\{\mathcal{E}_{N}(f)\,:\,f\in\mathcal{H}^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\bigr\},

where
\mathcal{H}^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bigl\{h\colon\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}:0\leq h\leq 1,\,h\rvert_{\mathcal{A}}=1,\,h\rvert_{\mathcal{B}}=0\bigr\}

and

(1.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{N}(f)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\,\pi_{N}(\sigma,\eta)\,\bigl(f(\sigma)-f(\eta)\bigr)^{2}

is the Dirichlet form. Analogously, the
Thomson principle
states that

(1.29)

\displaystyle\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\;=\;\sup\biggl\{\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}_{N}(\varphi)}:\varphi\in\mathcal{U}^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\biggr\}\;=\;\Bigl(\inf\bigl\{\mathcal{D}_{N}(\varphi):\varphi\in\mathcal{U}^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\bigr\}\Bigr)^{-1},

where
\mathcal{U}^{N}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
denotes the space of all unit anti-symmetric
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
-flows, while

(1.30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N}(\varphi)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\frac{1}{\mu_{N}(\sigma)\pi_{N}(\sigma,\eta)}\varphi(\sigma,\eta)^{2}.

These dual variational principles yield matching upper and lower bounds on the capacity when suitable test functions and flows are chosen. A common corollary of these principles is the Rayleigh shortcut method (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
). Let
\gamma\colon\{0,\dots,K\}\to\mathcal{S}_{N}
be a cycle free path from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{B}
with
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
,
\gamma_{K}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\pi_{N}(\gamma_{n},\gamma_{n+1})>0
for all
n\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
. From either variational principle we obtain

(1.31)

\displaystyle\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\;\geq\;\Biggl(\sum_{n=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{N}(\gamma_{n})\pi_{N}(\gamma_{n},\gamma_{n+1})}\Biggr)^{\!\!-1}=\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\;\capacity_{\gamma,N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})

We will denote by
\mathcal{E}_{N},\mathcal{D}_{N},\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
the forms defined for the specific cases of the quenched and annealed models respectively.
Sections
4
and
5
are devoted to controlling the denominator (the capacity) and the numerator (the harmonic sum), respectively.

(2.15)

\displaystyle\capacitym_{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{y}_{N})\overset{\eqref{mesocap}}{\;\leq\;}\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\boldsymbol{x},N}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\notin\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\boldsymbol{x},N}}\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})\,\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}).

and

\displaystyle\bigl|\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})-c(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\bigr|\;\leq\;\bigl|\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})-\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{z})\bigr|+\bigl|\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{z})-\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}})\bigr|\overset{\eqref{eq:Lcont+unif}}{\;\leq\;}\frac{\sqrt{2}K_{\varepsilon,1}+K_{\varepsilon,2}}{N}.

(2.16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})\;\leq\;c_{\beta,\varepsilon}\,\frac{\exp\bigl(-\beta Nc(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\bigr)}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}_{N}},\qquad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\in\partial\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\boldsymbol{x},N}\text{ with }\boldsymbol{z}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}.

(2.19)

\displaystyle\capacitym_{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{y}_{N})\overset{\eqref{eq:rayleigh_def}}{\;\geq\;}\Biggl(\sum_{i=0}^{K_{N}-1}\frac{1}{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i})\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i+1})}\Biggr)^{\!\!-1}.

Set
\bar{N}\equiv\bar{N}(\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\{1/(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}-\varepsilon),2\sqrt{q}/\varepsilon\}
. For

fix some
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}
and let
\boldsymbol{M}_{\!N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i,N}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\}
be the set of nearest lattice approximation of the different local minima of
\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}
. Again, the proof of a lower bound on
\capacitym_{N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{M}_{\!N})
uses Rayleigh’s shortcut method. However, due to the divergence of
\nabla\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}
at the boundary of
\mathcal{P}
, extra care is required when constructing a vertex-disjoint lattice path which starts at
\boldsymbol{z}
and ends in
\boldsymbol{M}_{N}
. Therefore, we treat the cases
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}

For
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
let
\Gamma\colon[0,1]\to\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{P})
be the time-reparametrised solution of the initial-value problem
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(x(t))

\Gamma(1)\in\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\}

and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}_{\ell}

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\;=\;-\boldsymbol{x}_{l}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q\beta}\ln\biggl(\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{l})^{q}}{\prod_{i=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;-\frac{1}{q\beta}\ln\biggl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-q+1\biggr)+\frac{1}{q}-\varepsilon\;\leq\;-\varepsilon\;<\;0,

for all

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K_{N}}^{N})

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N}=\boldsymbol{z}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K_{N}}^{N}\in\boldsymbol{M}_{\!N}

and

, and
\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N})>0
for every

. Then, by applying Rayleigh’s shortcut method,

\displaystyle\frac{\capacitym_{N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{M}_{N})}{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})}\overset{\eqref{eq:rayleigh_def}}{\;\geq\;}\Biggl(\sum_{i=0}^{K_{N}-1}\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i})\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i+1})}\Biggr)^{\!\!-1}.

By construction,
\Gamma(t)\in\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
for every
t\in[0,1]
and, for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,K_{N}\}
, we can find
t_{i}\in[0,1]
so that
\min_{t\in[0,1]}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N}-\Gamma(t)\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N}-\Gamma(t_{i})\|_{2}
. This implies that, for any

, the lattice path
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}
lies in
\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon/2}
. Therefore, by applying (

\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{z})=\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\Gamma(t_{0}))\geq\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\Gamma(t_{i}))
for any

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})-\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{z})-\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\Gamma(t_{i}))-\bigl|\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\Gamma(t_{i}))-\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})\bigr|

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}-\bigl|\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})-\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{z})\bigr|-\bigl|\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})-\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})\bigr|

(2.22)

\displaystyle\frac{\capacitym_{N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{M}_{N})}{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})}\;\geq\;\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\sqrt{q}K_{\varepsilon,1}+2K_{\varepsilon,2})}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}{q}\,N^{-q},

where we again used that
\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N})\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}/(qN)
and
K_{N}\leq N^{q-1}
.

It remains to consider the case that
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
. We first construct explicitly a vertex-disjoint lattice path
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k_{N}}^{N})
from
\boldsymbol{z}
to some

along which

is non-increasing, see Figure

(Right). The remaining lattice path
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k_{N}+1}^{N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{K_{N}}^{N})
from
\boldsymbol{z}^{\ast}
to
\boldsymbol{M}_{N}
is then obtained, as above, as a vertex-disjoint lattice approximation of the time-reparametrised solution
\Gamma\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
of the initial-value problem
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla\widetilde{F}_{\beta,q}(x(t))
with
x(0)=\boldsymbol{z}^{\ast}
, chosen so that
\Gamma(1)\in\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}\}
. We are now going to construct
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k_{N}}^{N})
inductively. Set
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\boldsymbol{z}
, and suppose that for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
the vertex-disjoint path
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})
has already been constructed such that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
for all

,
\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}^{N},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j+1}^{N})>0
and
\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j}^{N})\geq\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{j+1}^{N})
for every
j\in\{0,\ldots,i-1\}
. By the pigeonhole principle and the choice
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, it follows that, for any
N\geq\bar{N}
, there exist
k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
such that
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})_{k}<\varepsilon
and
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})_{\ell}\geq\varepsilon+1/N
. Set
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N}+\hat{e}_{k}-\hat{e}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}
. By construction, the extended lattice path
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}^{N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N})
remains vertex-disjoint and, in view of (

), we have
\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N})>0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})-\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N})\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\,\Bigl(f\bigl((\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})_{k}+1/N\bigr)-f\bigl((\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})_{\ell}\bigr)\Bigr),

where

is the function

. Since

attains its global minimum at
\beta^{-1}
and for any
N\geq\bar{N}
one has
\varepsilon+1/N\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}<\beta^{-1}
, using also that
\bar{N}\geq 1/(\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}-\varepsilon)
, it follows that

\displaystyle f\bigl((\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})_{k}+1/N\bigr)\;\geq\;f\bigl(\varepsilon+1/N\bigr)\;=\;\max_{x\in[\varepsilon+1/N,1]}f(x)\;\geq\;f\bigl((\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})_{\ell}\bigr).

Hence,
\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}^{N})\geq\widetilde{F}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N})
. If
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N}\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
, repeat the construction. Otherwise, set
k_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=i+1
and
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i+1}^{N}
.

Consequently, by using that

for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,K_{N}\}
, it follows from Rayleigh’s shortcut method,

(2.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\capacitym_{N}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{M}_{N})}{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})}\;\geq\;\Biggl(\sum_{i=0}^{K_{N}-1}\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z})}{\widetilde{Q}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i})\widetilde{p}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{N}_{i+1})}\Biggr)^{\!\!-1}\;\geq\;\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\sqrt{q}K_{\varepsilon,1}+2K_{\varepsilon,2})}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}{q}\,N^{-q}.

This completes the proof of (
2.12
).
∎

Next, we establish a lower bound for the denominator in (

∎

Metastability for the DCWP model

In this section we analyse the metastable behaviour of the DCWP model by comparison with the CWP model studied in Section
2
. In particular, our aim is to prove Theorem
1.9
. Its proof depends on preliminary comparisons of several quantities of interest in the two models; these comparisons appear as Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
in Section
3.1
, while the proof of Theorem
1.9
is presented in Section
3.2
.

3.1.
Preliminary comparison between the CWP and DCWP models

In order to simplify notation, define the following random variable

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;H_{N}(\sigma)-\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma).

Further, let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
be the log-moment generating function of the centred random variable
J_{12}-1
, that is,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\phi(t)\;=\;\ln\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{t(J_{ij}-1)}\bigr].

The next lemma gives an expression of the annealed Gibbs density in terms of
\phi
. The strategy is inspired by the arguments given by the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\beta\geq 0
and recall that
v=\mathbb{V}[J_{12}]
.

(i)

For any
2\leq N\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\beta/N\in\mathscr{D}
, with
\mathscr{D}
as in Assumption
1.1
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta\Delta_{N}(\sigma)}\Bigr]\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{-N\phi(\pm\beta/N)\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma)}\qquad\forall\,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}.

(ii)

For
N\to\infty
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\beta^{2}v}{4q}(1+o(1))}\;\leq\;\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta_{N}(\sigma)}\Bigr]\;\leq\;\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta_{N}(\sigma)}\Bigr]\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\beta^{2}v}{4}(1+o(1))}.

(iii)

For
N\to\infty
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}\\
\sigma\sim\eta\end{subarray}}\frac{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta(H_{N}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}(\eta))}\bigr]}{\mathrm{e}^{\pm\beta(\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma)\vee\widetilde{H}_{N}(\eta))}}\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\beta^{2}v}{4}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

Proof.

Throughout the proof set
\widetilde{M}(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq N}\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}}=-N\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma)
for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
.

(i)
Since the random variables
(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta_{N}(\sigma)}\Bigr]\;=\;\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\beta(J_{ij}-1)}{N}}\Bigr]^{\widetilde{M}(\sigma)}\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{\widetilde{M}(\sigma)\phi(\beta/N)},

where
\phi
is the log–moment generating function defined in (
3.2
).

(ii)
By a Taylor expansion, we have that
\phi(x)=vx^{2}/2+o(x^{2})
as
x\to 0
. Since, for any
N>q
, there are at most
N^{2}/2
unordered pairs and, by the pigeonhole principle, at least
N(N-1)/(2q)
pairs of equal spin, if follows that
N(N-1)/(2q)\leq\widetilde{M}(\sigma)\leq N^{2}/2
for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
. Hence, the assertion follows from (
3.6
).

(iii)
Since the expectation of the minimum of two random variables is upper bounded by the minimum of the expectations, we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(H_{N}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}(\eta))}\Bigr]\;\leq\;\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H_{N}(\sigma)}\Bigr]\wedge\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\beta H_{N}(\eta)}\Bigr].

Thus, in view of (ii), we obtain the desired upper bound (
3.5
).

We now treat the the other sign. For this, since
\sigma
and
\eta
differ at most in one spin we can find
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
\ell\in\{1,\dots,q\}
such that
\eta=\sigma^{k,\ell}
, where

\displaystyle\sigma^{k,\ell}_{i}\;=\;\begin{cases}\sigma_{i},&\quad k\neq i\\
\ell,&\quad k=i.\end{cases}

We further introduce the decomposition

\displaystyle S^{k}(\sigma)\;=\;\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i,j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{N}J_{ij}\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}},\qquad D^{k}(\sigma)\;=\;\frac{-1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{N}J_{kj}\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{j}}.

One can observe that
H_{N}=S^{k}+D^{k}
,
S^{k}(\sigma)=S^{k}(\sigma^{k,\ell})
, and that
S^{k}(\sigma)
is independent of
D^{k}(\eta)
for any
\sigma,\eta
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta(H_{N}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}(\eta))}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta H_{N}(\sigma)}\vee\mathrm{e}^{\beta H_{N}(\eta)}\Bigr]\;=\;\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta S^{k}(\sigma)}\Bigr]\,\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\sigma)}\vee\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\sigma^{k,\ell})}\Bigr].

Also note that for any positive random variables
X,Y
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mean\bigl[X\vee Y\bigr]\;\leq\;\Bigl(\mean[X]\vee\mean[Y]\Bigr)\biggl(1+\biggl(\frac{\sqrt{\var[X]}}{\mean[X]}+\frac{\sqrt{\var[Y]}}{\mean[Y]}\biggr)\biggr).

This leads to the following estimate

\displaystyle\frac{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta(H_{N}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}(\eta))}\bigr]}{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta H_{N}(\sigma)}\bigr]\vee\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta H_{N}(\sigma^{k,\ell})}\bigr]}\;\leq\;1+\frac{\sqrt{\var\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\sigma)}\bigr]}}{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\sigma)}\bigr]}+\frac{\sqrt{\var\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\sigma^{k,\ell})}\bigr]}}{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\sigma^{k,\ell})}\bigr]}

In order to control the right hand side, we first observe that

\displaystyle\mean\Bigl[(\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\eta)})^{2}\Bigr]\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-2\beta M(\eta)}{N}+M(\eta)\phi\left(\frac{-2\beta}{N}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\eta)}\Bigr]^{2}\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-2\beta M(\eta)}{N}+2M(\eta)\phi\left(\frac{-\beta}{N}\right)},

where
M(\eta)=\sum_{j\neq k}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\eta_{k}=\eta_{j}}
with
\eta\in\{\sigma,\sigma^{k,\ell}\}
, and
\phi
is defined in (
3.2
). Therefore, using the convexity of
\phi
and bounding
M
by
N
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\var\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\eta)}\bigr]}{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\beta D^{k}(\eta)}\bigr]^{2}}\;=\;\mathrm{e}^{M(\eta)\left(\phi\left(\frac{-2\beta}{N}\right)-2\phi\left(\frac{-\beta}{N}\right)\right)}-1\;\leq\;\frac{v\beta^{2}}{N}(1+o(1)).

Since
H_{N}(\sigma)=\widetilde{H}_{N}(\sigma)+\Delta_{N}(\sigma)
, an application of (
3.4
) yields the desired bound.
∎

In the next lemma we obtain both estimates for
\Delta_{N}(\sigma)
in the form of concentration inequalities and estimates for the probability of the following event

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Xi_{N}(a)\;=\;\biggl\{\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\lvert\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\rvert\leq a\sqrt{N}\biggr\},\qquad a>0.

In particular,
\Xi_{N}(a)^{c}
turns out to be negligible in the limit as
N\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.2
.

(i)

Let
(t_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\infty)
be a sequence such that
t_{N}/N\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Then, for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[\lvert\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\rvert\geq t_{N}\bigr]\;\leq\;2\exp\biggl(-\frac{t_{N}^{2}}{v}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\biggr).

(ii)

For any
a>0

(3.11)

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[\Xi_{N}(a)^{c}\bigr]\;\leq\;2\exp\biggl(N\ln q-\frac{a^{2}N}{v}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\biggr).

Proof.

(i)
First note that
\prob\bigl[\lvert\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\rvert\geq t\bigr]=\prob\bigl[\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\geq t\bigr]+\prob\bigl[\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\leq-t\bigr]
, so by symmetry it suffices to bound the first term. Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and choose
N
large enough that
-\lambda/N
lies in the domain of
\phi
defined in (
3.2
). Then, an application of Markov’s inequality and Lemma
3.1
give

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\geq t\bigr]\;\leq\;\frac{\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\Delta_{N}(\sigma)}\bigr]}{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t_{N}}}\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\lambda^{2}v}{4}(1+o(1))-\lambda t_{N}}.

Optimising the exponent by taking
\lambda=2t_{N}/v(1+o(1))
yields the claimed bound.

(ii)
A union bound yields

\displaystyle\prob\biggl[\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\lvert\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\rvert>a\sqrt{N}\biggr]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\prob\Bigl[\lvert\Delta_{N}(\sigma)\rvert>a\sqrt{N}\Bigr]\;\leq\;2\,\mathrm{e}^{N\ln(q)-Na^{2}/v(1+o(1))},

where the final inequality uses (
3.10
) and
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{N}\rvert=q^{N}
.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.9

In the previous section we provided a comparison of the quenched Hamiltonian
H_{N}
and the annealed Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{N}
. In the following proof we will proceed by comparing the quadratic forms of the quenched and annealed models, introduced in Section

. This proof follows along the lines of
[

with minor modifications. However, we present it here for the convenience of the reader.

Proof of Theorem

.

First note that for any two adjacent configurations

,

\displaystyle Z_{N}\mu_{N}(\sigma)\pi_{N}(\sigma,\eta)\;=\;\frac{1}{Nq}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(H_{N}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}(\eta))}.

Hence, the quadratic form in (

\displaystyle Z_{N}\mathcal{E}_{N}(f)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}\\
\sigma\sim\eta\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{Nq}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(H_{N}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}(\eta))}\bigl(f(\sigma)-f(\eta)\bigr)^{2}.

Let
a>0
and recall the definition of
\Xi_{N}(a)
given in (
3.9
). Then, on the event

,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\beta a\sqrt{N}}\,\widetilde{Z}_{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{N}(f)\;\leq\;Z_{N}\mathcal{E}_{N}(f)\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{\beta a\sqrt{N}}\,\widetilde{Z}_{N}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{N}(f)

for any

. Hence, the Dirichlet principle (

,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\beta a\sqrt{N}}\,\widetilde{Z}_{N}\widetilde{\capacity}_{N}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\;\leq\;Z_{N}\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{\beta a\sqrt{N}}\,\widetilde{Z}_{N}\widetilde{\capacity}_{N}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

Likewise, on the event
\Xi_{N}(a)
, we have that, for any
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}

4.
Capacity estimates for the DCWP model

In this section, we derive bounds for the capacity of the quenched model in relation to that of the annealed model. These estimates are regarded as general, as they do not require any assumptions regarding metastability and are applicable to arbitrary subsets of the configuration space. To establish bounds on the capacity, we adopt the same strategy as developed in
[
5
]
. However, the adaptation is not straightforward due to the particularities of the DCWP model and the selection of (potentially) unbounded random variables.

4.1.
Concentration inequalities

We begin with establishing a concentration inequality for functionals of independent random variables. By employing Chernoff-type bounds, this approach generalise McDiarmid’s concentration inequality by replacing the bounded-difference assumption with a Lipschitz condition together with mild regularity on the law of the involved random variables. Furthermore, this method yields tighter estimates in the case of vanishing variance, that is, as
v\to 0
, in comparison to
[
7
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
be a probability space and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider a vector
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
of independent,
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
such that, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the symmetrised cumulant generating function

(4.1)

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(\lambda)\;\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\;\ln\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\lambda X_{i}}\bigr]+\ln\mean\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda X_{i}}\bigr]

have domains
\mathcal{D}_{i}
containing an open neighbourhood of 0. Further, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function and suppose that there exists
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in[0,\infty)
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\bigl|f(x)-f(y)\bigr|\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,\bigl|x_{i}-y_{i}\bigr|.

Then, for
\lambda\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{i}\cap[0,\infty)
and
t>0
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[f(X)-\mean\bigl[f(X)\bigr]>t\bigr]\;\leq\;\exp\biggl(-\lambda t+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(\lambda c_{i})\biggr).

If, additionally, the random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are identically distributioned then, for any
t>0
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[f(X)-\mean\bigl[f(X)\bigr]>t\bigr]\;\leq\;\exp\bigl(-n\varphi_{1}^{\ast}\bigl(t/(Cn)\bigr)\bigr),

where
C\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
and
\varphi_{1}^{\ast}
denotes the Legendre tranform of
\varphi
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the method of martingale differences. For this purpose, set
\mathcal{F}_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
and, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma(X_{j}:j\leq i)
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{i}
. Further, define
Z_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mean\bigl[f(X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}\bigr]
for any
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
. Then, the stochastic process
(Z_{i})_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}
is a martingale, and

(4.5)

\displaystyle f(X)-\mean[F(X)]\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}).

For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
define the random vector
X^{(i)}
by
X_{j}^{(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=X_{j}
for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
j\neq i
and
X_{i}^{(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=X_{i}^{\prime}
, where
X_{i}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
X_{i}
. Using the independence of the random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, we obtain that,
\prob
-a.s.,

\displaystyle\mean\bigl[f(X^{(i)})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}\bigr]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-i+1}}f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1},y_{i},\ldots,y_{n})\,\prod_{j=i}^{n}\mathrm{d}\bigl(\prob\circ X_{j}^{-1}\bigr)(y_{j})=\mean\bigl[f(X)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]

for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. In particular, for any
\lambda\in\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{j}\cap[0,\infty)
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have that
\exp(\lambda(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1}))\in L^{1}(\prob)
and,
\prob
-a.s.,

\displaystyle\mean\bigl[\exp\bigl(\lambda(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1})\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mean\bigl[\exp\bigl(\lambda\mean\bigl[f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}\bigr]\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mean\bigl[\exp\bigl(\lambda(f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]

(4.6)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mean\bigl[\cosh\bigl(\lambda(f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr],

where in the final equality we used that
\prob\circ(X_{i},X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{n})^{-1}=\prob\circ(X_{i}^{\prime},X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{n})^{-1}
. Since
\cosh(t)\leq\cosh(s)
for any
s>|t|
, if follows from (
4.2
) that,
\prob
-a.s.,

\displaystyle\mean\bigl[\cosh\bigl(\lambda(f(X)-f(X^{(i)})\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mean\bigl[\cosh\bigl(\lambda c_{i}|X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime}|\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\mean\bigl[\cosh\bigl(\lambda c_{i}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime})\bigr)\bigr]

(4.7)

\displaystyle\;=\;\mean\bigl[\exp(\lambda c_{i}X_{i})\bigr]\mean\bigl[\exp(-\lambda c_{i}X_{i})\bigr]\;=\;\exp\bigl(\varphi_{i}(\lambda c_{i})\bigr).

Thus, by combining the estimates (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), it follows that, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mean\bigl[\exp\bigl(\lambda(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1})\bigr)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigr]\;=\;\exp\bigl(\varphi_{i}(\lambda c_{i})\bigr)\qquad\prob\text{-a.s.}

In particular, by applying (
4.8
) iteratively, we get

\displaystyle\mean\Biggl[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1})}\Biggr]\;=\;\mean\Biggl[\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1})}\mean\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda(Z_{n}-Z_{n-1})}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\Bigr]\Biggr]\overset{\eqref{eq:exponential:martingale:difference}}{\;=\;}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{\varphi_{i}(\lambda c_{i})}.

Thus, by applying the exponential Markov inequality, we obtain for any
t>0

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[f(X)-\mean[f(X)]>t\bigr]

\displaystyle\;=\;\prob\Biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1})>t\Biggr]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda}\mean\Biggl[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda(Z_{i}-Z_{i-1})}\Biggr]\;=\;\exp\biggl(-t\lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(\lambda c_{i})\biggr),

which conclude the proof of (
4.3
).

If, additionally, the random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are identically distributed, setting
C\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max\{c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\}
, the right-hand side of (
4.3
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[f(X)-\mean[f(X)]>t\bigr]\;\leq\;\exp\bigl(-\lambda t+n\varphi_{1}(\lambda C)\bigr).

Since
\varphi_{1}
is an even function and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\leq 0}\bigl(t\lambda-\varphi_{1}(\lambda)\bigr)\;=\;\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\bigl(-t\lambda-\varphi_{1}(-\lambda)\bigr)\;\leq\;\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\bigl(t\lambda-\varphi_{1}(\lambda)\bigr),

the assertion follows by optimising over all
\lambda>0
.
∎

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be i.i.d. random variables taking values in
\mathbb{R}
with cumulant generating function defined in an open interval containing

. Let

) with

. Then, for any sequence

(4.9)

\displaystyle\prob\bigl[f(X)-\mean[f(X)]>t_{n}\bigr]\;\leq\;\exp\biggl(-\frac{t_{n}^{2}}{4\var[X_{1}]\,c^{2}n}(1+o(1))\biggr).

Proof.

First observe that
\varphi_{1}
as defined in (

) is the cumulant-generating function of

minus an independent copy of itself. Consequently, its Legendre transform inherits some useful properties. As
\varphi_{1}
is smooth, strictly convex and satisfies

and
\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(0)=2\var[X_{1}]

\varphi_{1}^{\ast}(0)={\varphi_{1}^{\ast}}^{\prime}(0)=0
and
{\varphi_{1}^{\ast}}^{\prime\prime}(0)=1/(2\var[X_{1}])

(4.10)

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{\ast}(t)\;=\;\frac{t^{2}}{4\var[X_{1}]}+o(t^{2}),

∎

4.2.
Concentration of the capacity

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\beta>0
and
(t_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\infty)
be a sequence such that
t_{N}/N\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Then, for any two non-empty, disjoint subsets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{S}_{N}
,

(4.11)

\displaystyle\prob\Bigl[\Bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\bigr)-\mean\bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\capacity_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\bigr)\bigr]\Bigr|>t_{N}\Bigr]\;\leq\;2\exp\biggl(-\frac{t_{N}^{2}}{2\beta^{2}v}(1+o(1))\biggr).

Proof.

To emphasise the dependence on the random array
J=(J_{ij})_{1\leq j<j\leq N}
, we henceforth write
H_{N}^{J}
,
Z^{J}_{N}
,
\mathcal{E}^{J}_{N}
,
\capacity^{J}_{N}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. Further, let
J^{\prime}=(J_{ij}^{\prime})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
be the triangular array of random variables which coincides with
J
except that
J_{kl}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
J_{kl}
for some fixed pair
(k,l)
. To apply of Lemma
4.1
we first show that, for every
N
, the map
J\mapsto F_{N}(J)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\ln(Z_{N}^{J}\capacity_{N}^{J}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}))
satisfies the bounded difference condition (
4.2
). By linearity,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\bigl|H_{N}^{J}(\sigma)-H_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\sigma)\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{|J_{kl}-J^{\prime}_{kl}|}{N}.

In particular, for every
\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{S}_{N}

\displaystyle H_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\eta)-\frac{|J_{kl}-J^{\prime}_{kl}|}{N}\;\leq\;H_{N}^{J}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}^{J}(\eta)\;\leq\;H_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\sigma)\vee H_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\eta)+\frac{|J_{kl}-J^{\prime}_{kl}|}{N}.

Hence, for any test function

, it follows that

\displaystyle\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(h)\bigr)-\frac{\beta}{N}|J_{kl}-J_{kl}^{\prime}|\;\leq\;\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{J}(h)\bigr)\;\leq\;\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J^{\prime}}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(h)\bigr)+\frac{\beta}{N}|J_{kl}-J_{kl}^{\prime}|.

In view of the Dirichlet principle (
1.27

\displaystyle\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J}\capacity_{N}^{J}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\bigr)

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J}\mathcal{E}_{N}^{J}(h_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}^{J^{\prime}})\bigr)\;\leq\;\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J^{\prime}}\capacity_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\bigr)+\frac{\beta}{N}|J_{kl}-J_{kl}^{\prime}|.

By repeating the computation above with
J
and
J^{\prime}
interchanged, we obtain

(4.13)

\displaystyle\bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J}\capacity_{N}^{J}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\bigr)-\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J^{\prime}}\capacity_{N}^{J^{\prime}}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})\bigr)\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{\beta}{N}|J_{kl}-J_{kl}^{\prime}|

Finally, to verify that
F_{N}
satisfies the condition (
4.2
), let
J=(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
and
J^{\prime}=(J_{ij}^{\prime})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
be two independent triangular arrays. By interpolating between
J
and
J^{\prime}

and

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose that
\beta>\beta_{1}

Proof.

Fix some arbitrary

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{\beta}}\widetilde{\Prob}_{\!\sigma}^{N}\bigl[\widetilde{\Sigma}^{N}(\tilde{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}^{N})\in\mathcal{M}_{j,N}\bigr]

Consequently, for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{M}_{i,N}
we have
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,N}}^{N}(\sigma)\geq 1/|\mathcal{I}_{\beta}|
. On the other hand, using the properties of the equilibrium potientials
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,N}}^{N}
and
\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}}^{N}
, it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\;=\;\langle\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,N}}^{N},-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}\widetilde{h}_{\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}}^{N}\rangle_{\tilde{\mu}_{N}}

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\beta}|}\,\widetilde{\capacity}_{N}\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\bigr)-\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{M}_{i,N}}\mspace{-6mu}\tilde{\mu}_{N}(\sigma)\,\widetilde{\Prob}_{\sigma}^{N}\bigl[\tilde{\tau}_{\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{M}_{i,N}}^{N}<\tilde{\tau}_{\mathcal{M}_{N}}^{N}\bigr]

(5.5)

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\beta}|}\,\widetilde{\capacity}_{N}\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\bigr)-\tilde{\mu}_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{M}_{i,N}\bigr].

Thus, we are left with bounding
\widetilde{\capacity}_{N}\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\bigr)
from below. Repeating the argument of (
2.28
), estimating
|\boldsymbol{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i,N}\}|\leq N^{g}

-(ii), we deduce that for any

\displaystyle\widetilde{\capacity}_{N}\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{M}_{i,N},\mathcal{M}_{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{mesocap}}{\;=\;}\capacitym_{N}\bigl(\boldsymbol{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i,N}\},\boldsymbol{M}_{N}\bigr)

(5.6)

\displaystyle\;\geq\;N^{-\ell_{3}-q}\,\widetilde{Q}_{N}\bigl[\boldsymbol{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\{\boldsymbol{m}_{i,N}\}\bigr]\;=\;N^{-\ell_{3}-q}\,\tilde{\mu}_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{i,N}\!\setminus\!\mathcal{M}_{i,N}\bigr]

Thus, by combining (

with an analogous lower bound.

, we obtain that for any sufficiently large

\displaystyle\biggl|\mean\biggl[\ln\biggl(\frac{\lVert h_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}^{N}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}}{\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A},N}\bigr]}\biggr)\mathbbm{1}_{\Xi_{N}(a)^{c}}\biggr]\biggr|

(5.25)

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq\;\biggl(\beta^{2}v\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)+N\biggl(\frac{\beta}{2}+2\ln q-\frac{a^{2}}{v}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\biggr)\biggr)\,\mathrm{e}^{-N(a^{2}/v(1+o(1))-\ln q)}.

Thus, by combining (

5.2.
Concentration estimates

We are now ready to state some concentration inequalities for the logarithm of the harmonic sum.

Proposition 5.6
.

For any
\beta>\beta_{1}
let
\mathcal{A}_{N}
and
\mathcal{B}_{N}
be chosen as in Definition
1.11
, and let
C_{2}
be as in Lemma
5.5
. Then, there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
t>0
, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\prob\Bigl[\bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)-\mean\bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)\bigr]\bigr|>t\Bigr]

(5.26)

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq\;2\exp\biggl(-\frac{(t-c\,\mathrm{e}^{-C_{2}N})^{2}}{2\beta^{2}v}(1+o(1))\biggr)+2\exp\bigl(-C_{2}N\bigr).

Proof.

Let
a>\sqrt{v(C_{2}+\ln q)}
. By Proposition
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
there exists a
c\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
N
sufficiently large, on the event
\Xi_{N}(a)
,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)-\mean\Bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr|

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq\;\Bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}_{N}}\bigr]\bigr)-\mean\Bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A},N}\bigr]\bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr|+c\,\mathrm{e}^{-C_{2}N}.

Hence, for any
t>0
and
N
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\prob\Bigl[\bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)-\mean\bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)\bigr]\bigr|>t\Bigr]

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq\;\prob\Bigl[\bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)-\mean\bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\lVert h^{N}_{\mathcal{A}_{N},\mathcal{B}_{N}}\rVert_{\mu_{N}}\bigr)\bigr]\bigr|>t,\,\Xi_{N}(a)\Bigr]+\prob\bigl[\Xi_{N}(a)^{c}\bigr]

(5.27)

\displaystyle\mspace{36mu}\leq\;\prob\Bigl[\bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}_{N}}\bigr]\bigr)-\mean\bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}_{N}}\bigr]\bigr)\bigr]\bigr|>t-c\,\mathrm{e}^{-C_{2}N}\Bigr]+\prob\bigl[\Xi_{N}(a)^{c}\bigr].

By Lemma
3.2
-(ii) and our choice of
a
, the second term on the right-hand side of (
5.27
) is, for all sufficiently large
N
, bounded by
\mathrm{e}^{-C_{2}N}
. Hence, it remains to bound the first term. To that end we employ an argument analogous to the one used in the proof of Lemma
4.2
. For any integer
N\geq 2
, consider the map

\displaystyle(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\;\longmapsto\;\ln\bigl(Z_{N}^{J}\,\mu_{N}^{J}[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A},N}]\bigr),

and, as before, write
H_{N}^{J}
,
Z_{N}^{J}
and
\mu_{N}^{J}
to emphasise the dependence on the random array
J=(J_{ij})_{1\leq i<j\leq N}
. Since
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A},N}
is defined in terms of the CWP model and is therefore independent of
J
, (
4.12
) immediately implies that

\displaystyle\bigl|Z_{N}^{J}\mu_{N}^{J}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}.N}\bigr]-Z_{N}^{J^{\prime}}\mu_{N}^{J^{\prime}}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}.N}\bigr]\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\bigl|J_{ij}-J_{ij}^{\prime}\bigr|.

Hence, by applying Corollary
4.1
with
n=N(N-1)/2
, we get for any
t>0
,

(5.28)

\displaystyle\prob\Bigl[\bigl|\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}_{N}}\bigr]\bigr)-\mean\bigl[\ln\bigl(Z_{N}\mu_{N}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{A}_{N}}\bigr]\bigr)\bigr]\bigr|>t\Bigr]\;\leq\;2\,\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/(2\beta^{2}v)(1+o(1))}.

By combining (
5.27
) and (
5.28
), we assertion follows.
∎
