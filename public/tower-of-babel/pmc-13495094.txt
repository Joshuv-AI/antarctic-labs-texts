Somatic DICER1-mutated Sertoli–Leydig cell tumor in a postmenopausal woman: eight years of peritoneal relapse managed with cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC – a case report
Authors: Emily Frances Brown, Katrina Tang, Ruwanthi Wijayawardana, Vivek Arora, Winston Liauw, Lawrence Kasherman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSertoli–Leydig cell tumors are rare ovarian sex cord–stromal tumors that usually arise in young women and are often hormonally active. Intraperitoneal dissemination and relapse are uncommon, and recurrence patterns in postmenopausal women are poorly characterized. This report describes a 55-year-old postmenopausal woman with a poorly differentiated, estrogen receptor-negative, non-functional Sertoli–Leydig cell tumor. Despite adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy, she relapsed within one year. Over eight years her disease followed a pattern of recurrent intraperitoneal dissemination, managed with multiple cytoreductive procedures, including three with heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy. Somatic molecular profiling identified a DICER1 mutation without a germline alteration. Systemic therapies, including platinum-based chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibition, produced only transient disease control, and repeated surgery, rather than systemic therapy, was the mainstay of management. The case illustrates an aggressive, somatic DICER1-mutated tumor arising outside the usual age range, and suggests that such tumors can behave aggressively in older women, in whom systemic therapy has limited efficacy and management is necessarily multidisciplinary.Keywords: Sertoli–Leydig cell tumor, DICER1, Sex cord–stromal tumor, Cytoreductive surgery, HIPEC, Postmenopausal

Introduction

Sertoli–Leydig cell tumors (SLCTs) are rare ovarian sex cord–stromal tumors, accounting for less than 0.5% of ovarian tumors [1]. They usually arise in women aged 10 to 30 years and are frequently hormonally active. Most present at stage I and, when well-differentiated and confined to the ovary, carry a favorable prognosis after surgery [1], [2].

A minority behave aggressively. Poor differentiation, intraoperative rupture and heterologous elements are recognized risk factors for relapse [3]. Alterations in the microRNA-processing gene DICER1 are characteristic of moderately and poorly differentiated tumors. They are reported most often in younger patients and are frequently germline [4], and their significance in older women remains uncertain.

When SLCTs recur, relapse is usually early and confined to the abdomen and pelvis [2]. Evidence to guide treatment of intraperitoneal recurrence is sparse, drawn from small series and case reports [3], and there is no established role for repeat CRS or HIPEC. This report describes a postmenopausal woman with a DICER1-mutated SLCT and recurrent intraperitoneal disease over eight years. Her clinical course is summarized in Fig. 1.

Case Presentation

Era 1: Initial Diagnosis and Adjuvant Therapy (Age 55)

A 55-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with several weeks of abdominal cramping, bloating and distension. She had no virilizing or feminizing symptoms and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0. Her history included obesity managed with a gastric band (placed the previous year, with 20 kg subsequent weight loss) and a hysterectomy at age 40 for menorrhagia. There was no family history of a hereditary cancer syndrome. Imaging identified a large (approximately 20 cm) pelvic mass, and serum CA-125 was 60 kU/L, with normal testosterone, inhibin and AFP.

She underwent primary cytoreductive surgery, comprising bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy and appendectomy. The operation was complicated by intraoperative tumor rupture and hemoperitoneum, and disease was staged FIGO IC2. Histopathology revealed a hypercellular spindle-cell tumor with focal sertoliform tubules and inconspicuous Leydig cells, with a high mitotic index of up to 20 per 10 high-power fields. Tumor cells were positive for inhibin, calretinin, CD56 and broad-spectrum cytokeratins, and negative for epithelial membrane antigen, PLAP, synaptophysin, HMB-45, SOX10, CD34 and lymphoid markers, excluding epithelial, germ cell, neuroendocrine, melanocytic and lymphoid neoplasms. The appearances were those of a poorly differentiated, estrogen receptor–negative, non-functional SLCT (Fig. 2). Given the poor differentiation and rupture, she received six cycles of carboplatin and paclitaxel; paclitaxel was dose-reduced and then omitted for grade 3 peripheral neuropathy. Performance status fell to ECOG 2 by the end of treatment, recovering to ECOG 0 within three months; restaging imaging confirmed no residual disease. She was subsequently monitored with interval cross-sectional imaging and clinical review.

Era 2: Early Recurrent Disease and Chemotherapy Resistance (Ages 56–57)

Within one year she developed recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms. Imaging revealed a 28 × 21 × 14 cm cystic pelvic mass without distant disease. She underwent a second laparotomy with resection of abdominopelvic disease, gastric band removal and right ureterolysis. The predominantly cystic mass ruptured intraoperatively, and residual disease under 5 mm remained. Bleomycin, etoposide and cisplatin (BEP) was commenced but stopped after two cycles for toxicity (deconditioning, falls and a decline to ECOG 3). Subsequent imaging demonstrated no residual disease, and after inpatient rehabilitation she returned to ECOG 0.

Era 3: Locoregional Surgical Control with HIPEC (Ages 57–61)

The following year, restaging identified a 25 × 19.8 cm complex pelvic mass with loculated ascites and enhancing solid components. She underwent a third debulking laparotomy, including a 15 cm terminal ileal (small-bowel) resection, with no macroscopic residual disease at completion. Histopathology again confirmed poorly differentiated SLCT. Molecular profiling identified a somatic DICER1 mutation, low tumor mutational burden and no targetable alterations. A further relapse was treated on a trial of ipilimumab and nivolumab, with progression after four cycles.

With disease confined to the peritoneum and few systemic options, she underwent her first CRS with HIPEC. This involved multivisceral resection (right hemicolectomy, sigmoid colectomy, small-bowel resection and pelvic peritonectomy), achieving complete macroscopic cytoreduction (CC-0), followed by cisplatin HIPEC. Before the next operation, recurrent retroperitoneal disease caused right ureteric obstruction, managed first with a ureteric stent and then a nephrostomy. A second CRS with HIPEC was then undertaken, with right nephrectomy and retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (carboplatin HIPEC, AUC 5) and CC-0 cytoreduction. A third CRS with HIPEC (carboplatin AUC 6) followed, complicated by disease involving major pelvic vessels, and entailed internal iliac artery ligation and partial psoas resection with nerve preservation. Disease stayed within the abdomen and pelvis and performance status generally recovered between procedures, although intervals between recurrences progressively shortened.

Era 4: Advanced Recurrent Disease and Palliative Phase (Ages 62–63)

She subsequently developed locally advanced pelvic recurrence with neurovascular involvement and was managed with radical surgery. This comprised en bloc resection of the right common and external iliac vein, partial psoas resection, a Hartmann's procedure with formation of an end colostomy, and intraoperative cryoablation and brachytherapy to residual disease (Fig. 3). Histopathology again showed SLCT. Molecular profiling identified the DICER1 mutation together with a CTCF splice-site alteration, with no targets eligible for a clinical trial. Multifocal progression after an interval of under six months was then considered inoperable. Weekly paclitaxel produced a partial radiological response but required dose modification for cognitive toxicity. The disease progressed further; bevacizumab was declined, and carboplatin was stopped after an anaphylactic reaction. She was referred for palliative care and received radiotherapy to symptomatic parastomal, pelvic, presacral and vaginal vault disease. Across the eight-year course her performance status fluctuated with treatment but recovered to ECOG 0 between interventions. At last follow-up she was alive with progressive disease and receiving palliative care, more than eight years from diagnosis.

Discussion

Three features make this case unusual: the patient's postmenopausal age, the somatic DICER1 mutation, and a prolonged peritoneal-confined course refractory to systemic therapy.

SLCTs typically arise in adolescents and young adults [1], and this tumor was additionally non-functional and estrogen receptor-negative. Despite stage I disease, she relapsed within a year. A registry cohort of 191 patients reported recurrence in 18% at a median of 14 months [5]; although her time to first recurrence was similar, she fell within the minority who relapse and then, atypically, recurred repeatedly over eight years. Reported five-year survival is 100%, 96% and 33% for well, moderately and poorly differentiated tumors [1], so survival beyond eight years with poorly differentiated disease is uncommon.

DICER1 alterations are a recognized feature of moderately and poorly differentiated SLCTs. They are usually reported in younger patients and are often germline [6]. More recent data suggest that DICER1-associated moderately and poorly differentiated tumors appear biologically distinct from well-differentiated tumors [7], and aggressive, treatment-refractory courses have been described [8]. In this patient, germline testing was negative while somatic profiling confirmed a DICER1 mutation, consistent with a sporadic tumor. No therapies directed against DICER1 are available in clinical practice, and whether somatic DICER1 alterations independently influence behavior in older patients is not yet established.

Sustained recurrence confined to the peritoneal and retroperitoneal compartments, without distant metastasis, is rarely described, and raises the question of whether locoregional approaches used in epithelial ovarian cancer can be extrapolated to SLCT. Guidelines provide limited direction. ESGO and NCCN recommendations for ovarian sex cord–stromal tumors mainly address primary surgery and adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy for high-risk or advanced disease. In recurrent disease, recommendations are extrapolated from small series, with no defined standard and no established role for CRS or HIPEC. As this tumor was non-functional and lacked a reliable serum marker, surveillance relied on periodic imaging and clinical review.

In this patient, repeated CRS achieved complete macroscopic cytoreduction on each occasion, with intervening periods of radiological control while disease remained intraperitoneal. The cumulative burden was considerable: repeated laparotomies, nephrectomy, vascular resection, stoma formation and deconditioning. These individualized, multidisciplinary decisions should therefore not be read as endorsing routine repeated surgery in recurrent SLCT. Supporting literature is scarce: a small series reported two SLCT patients treated with repeat CRS and HIPEC, both achieving CC-0 without major morbidity [9], and a single further case has been described [10]. Aggressive, DICER1-associated SLCT with treatment resistance has been reported in younger patients [8], but a postmenopausal, peritoneal-confined course of this length appears unusual.

Systemic options for advanced SLCT are sparse [11]. Platinum-based chemotherapy is used most often, but the supporting evidence is drawn largely from broader sex cord–stromal populations [12]. Anti-angiogenic therapy has shown modest activity [13], and immunotherapy has shown only limited efficacy in small cohorts [14]. Early platinum relapse, absent actionable alterations and cumulative toxicity constrained treatment, and checkpoint inhibition produced no durable benefit. Radiotherapy, including intraoperative brachytherapy, contributed to symptom control. Most of these data derive from cohorts dominated by granulosa cell tumors, with limited generalizability to SLCT (Table 1); dedicated trials in rare sex cord–stromal histologies are needed.

Conclusion

This case shows that a somatic DICER1-mutated SLCT can arise in a postmenopausal woman and pursue an aggressive, peritoneal-confined course that resists systemic therapy. With systemic options exhausted, repeated cytoreductive surgery became the mainstay of management, although its effect on the overall course cannot be determined from a single case. The case argues for early molecular profiling in older women with SLCT and for including these rare tumors in clinical trials.
