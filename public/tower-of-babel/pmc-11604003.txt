Neck circumference, waist-to-height ratio, Chinese visceral adiposity index and incident heart failure
Authors: Zhijun Wu, Zhe Huang, Liang Sun, Yuang Fu, Shuohua Chen, Shouling Wu, Xiang Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe indicators of abdominal obesity have shown to be associated with a high risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), even adjusted for body mass index (BMI). We aimed to investigate the association between neck circumference, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI) and incident heart failure (HF) in Chinese adults.MethodsThe current study included 86,546 participants from two independent cohorts: the Kailuan I study established in 2006 and the Kailuan study II established in 2008. Participants aged 18–80 years who were free of CVDs or cancer were recruited at baseline. The values of neck circumference, WHtR and CVAI were available in 2014. The trajectory analysis was conducted using repeated measures of WHtR and CVAI in the 2008, 2010, 2012 and 2014 surveys. The incident HF cases were identified via reviewing medical records by cardiologists.ResultsDuring a mean follow-up of 5.62 years, 724 incident HF cases were documented. The highest quartiles of neck circumference, WHtR and CVAI respectively, were significantly associated with a high risk of HF compared to the lowest quartiles of adiposity measures (neck circumference: HR:1.30, 95%CI: 1.03–1.65; WHtR: HR:1.49, 95%CI: 1.16–1.92; and CVAI: HR:1.98, 95%CI: 1.48–2.65). The combination of adiposity measures with BMI or metabolic abnormalities jointly predicted incident HF. Presence of hypertension and diabetes appeared to be the major mediators, accounting for ∼ 8.11–24.7% of the associations between three indicators of abdominal obesity and HF risk.ConclusionThe new indices of abdominal adiposity could help to identify those who were at a high risk of HF, especially in the presence of high BMI or metabolic abnormalities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-024-01048-7.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a major cause of mortality, hospitalizations, and poor prognosis of other cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and has raised serious public health concerns with heavy economic burden globally [1]. The prevalence of HF has increased in recent decades and was reported as 1.3% of the Chinese population aged ≥ 35 years in the China Hypertension survey from 2015 to 2017 [2]. Adiposity has been highly prevalent worldwide and led to an increase in obesity-related morbidity such as HF [3, 4]. The efforts to reduce the growing trend of adiposity have been proposed. In 2012, the global non-communicable disease targets regarded adiposity as a risk factor with a target of halting rise by 2025 [5]. Body mass index (BMI) has been popularly used as a metric to evaluated obesity but it is less sensitive to reflect the proportional changes of weight derived from body fat, muscle mass and body fluid [6]. Individuals with excessive visceral fat still have a high mortality risk even they have a normal BMI [7]. Using BMI alone in obesity-related studies would result in misclassification of individuals at a high CVD risk [8].

Abdominal obesity, a surrogate of excess visceral fat accumulation, has shown to be associated with a high risk of mortality and CVDs, independent of BMI [7]. Direct assessment of regional fat distribution by magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography is a gold standard but not cost-effective, which limits its application in clinical practice. New anthropometric parameters such as neck circumference, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI) can be easily and economically assessed and have been validated to have high predictive capability for visceral obesity and clinical consequences of obesity such as CVDs [9–11]. Neck circumference is an index of upper body subcutaneous adipose distribution and has been reported as a strong indicator for atherosclerotic diseases [12] and metabolic syndrome [13]. Neck circumference significantly correlated with BMI and waist circumference and exhibited a stronger association with cardiometabolic factors than waist circumference [14]. High neck circumference was associated with composite cardiovascular events and the predictive rates of neck circumference with BMI or waist circumference were similar [15].

Waist circumference commonly reflects the changes in the body size caused by nutrition status and lifestyle habits but probably over- and under-estimates risk for tall and short people with similar waist circumferences. The formula of WHtR corrects the waist circumference for the height, which considers the body sizes more comprehensively than a single anthropometric parameter. Therefore, WHtR has been proposed as an improved proxy for abdominal fat accumulation [16]. In the Nurses’ Health Study, WHtR was shown to be a predictor of coronary artery diseases, comparable or superior to waist circumference and BMI [17, 18]. CVAI is a novel indicator of visceral fat mass according to the characteristics of body adiposity in Chinese adults [19]. CVAI takes sex, age, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride, BMI and waist circumference into account and has shown a better predictive ability of metabolic diseases [20] and CVDs [21] than other traditional adiposity indices in Chinese adults. Previous studies found that CVAI was significantly associated with subclinical atherosclerosis [22], stroke [23] and coronary artery diseases [24]. However, few studies have investigated the predictive values of these 3 adiposity measures for HF. In the current study, we prospectively examined the association of these adiposity measures with risk of developing HF among ∼ 86,000 Chinese adults who underwent repeated adiposity measurements during an eight-year follow up period. The incremental gain in HF prediction was quantified with these adiposity measures, singly and in combination. Considering obesity status may deteriorate over time, we further investigated whether the longitudinal patterns of adiposity measures were associated with a high risk of HF.

Methods

Study populations

The current study involved individuals from two independent cohorts: the Kailuan I study established in 2006 and the Kailuan study II established in 2008, as detailed elsewhere. Participants aged 18–80 years who had lived in the Kailuan community (Tangshan city, China) were recruited and underwent a standardized questionnaire, a physical examination, and a laboratory test. Information on socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education level, occupation, income and family history of CVD), lifestyle behaviors (e.g., physical activity, snoring, cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption), the history of medications and chronic diseases were collected at baseline and in the follow-up surveys every 2 years [25]. Body weight, height and waist circumference were assessed in each survey. Neck circumference was initially measured in the 2014 survey and we thus treated 2014 as the baseline in our analysis. Participants were not considered if they (1) died before 2014 (n = 6,549); (2) had previous CVDs or cancer diagnoses before 2014 (n = 12,848); (3) had incomplete measures of height, weight, waist circumference or neck circumference (n = 37,081). A total of 86,546 alive participants were included for the analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1). We also used multiple imputation to handle missing data of adiposity measures [26].

The Kailuan study received the approval by ethics committee of Kailuan General Hospital (No.2006-05) and followed the guidelines of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consents were obtained from all participants.

Anthropometric measurements

Neck circumference, weight, height, waist circumference and blood pressure were measured following a standardized procedure by trained staff [27]. Participants were asked to stand upright with face directing forward. Neck circumference was measured at the upper margin of the laryngeal prominence horizontally using an inelastic tape. Waist circumference was measured halfway between the bottom of ribs and the top of hipbone. Height was measured in the standing position with heels, buttocks and upper back against a stadiometer and weight was measured using a scale platform. All anthropometric measurements were performed twice and the average values were used in the current analysis. BMI was calculated as dividing weight (kg) by height in meters squared (m2) and WHtR was calculated by waist (cm)/height (cm). CVAI was calculated according to the following formula [28]: For men, CVAI = − 267.93 + 0.68 * age + 0.03 *BMI + 4.00 *waist circumference + 22.00 *Log10 triglyceride − 16.32 *HDL-C; For women, CVAI = − 187.32 + 1.71 * age + 4.23 *BMI + 1.12 *waist circumference + 39.76 *Log10triglyceride − 11.66 *HDL-C.

Assessment of covariates

Heart rate was measured on a 12-lead electrocardiogram at the time of the first survey. Physical activity was estimated based on the Guidelines for Data Processing and Analysis of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire using a validated questionnaire [29]. Blood pressure was measured when individuals were quietly seated for at least five minutes using a mercury sphygmomanometer.

Fasting blood glucose, lipid profiles, creatinine and high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were measured enzymatically based on standard protocol using an auto-analyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi), as previously described [30, 31]. The urine protein concentration was assessed using a dry chemistry method by a urine analyzer (N-600; Changchun Dirui Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). Non-HDL-C was calculated as total cholesterol minus HDL-C. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation was used to calculated estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) taking creatinine concentration, sex, and age into account [31]. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90mmHg, or self-reported use of blood pressure lowering drugs. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting blood glucose concentration of ≥ 126 mg/dL, non-fasting glucose concentration of ≥ 200 mg/dL, or self-reported use of glucose lowering drugs [32]. We considered non-HDL-C, rather than total cholesterol, as the metric of blood lipids because measures of non-HDL-C reflects the amount of atherogenic lipoprotein fractions [33]. Dyslipidemia was defined as non-HDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L or self-reported use of lipid lowering drugs [34].

Ascertainment of incident HF cases

The first occurrence of nonfatal and fatal HF cases was regarded as the primary endpoint in the current study. All suspected HF events were identified by 3 cardiologists of the Kailuan endpoint adjudication committee via searching clinical charts in the 11 Kailuan Hospitals’ Discharge Register. Participants with typical clinical symptoms (e.g., difficulty in breathing, fluid retention or diagnosed with the New York Heart Association cardiac function class ≥ II or Killip cardiac function class ≥ II) were diagnosed as HF if they satisfied at least 1 objective criterion: (1) increased concentrations of N-terminal pro-B type natriuretic peptide; (2) left ventricular ejection fraction ≤ 50% determined by the doppler echocardiography [35]. Deaths attributed to HF between 2014 and 2020 were ascertained through data linkage to the local death registration centers or phoning to the participants’ family numbers [25].

Statistical analysis

Follow-up began the finishing date of the 2014 survey and participants were censored at the date of HF occurrence, death, or the end of follow-up (December 31st, 2020). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of neck circumference and WHtR were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models after adjusting for age, sex, education, occupation, monthly salary, physical activity, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, fasting glucose, hs-CRP, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, non-HDL-C, eGFR, family history of myocardial infarction and stroke, proteinuria, and heart rate. Because the formula of CVAI included HDL-C and triglyceride, only LDL-C, rather than non-HDL-C, was adjusted in the COX model. Because neck circumference was closely associated with obstructive sleep apnea, a risk factor of HF. We adjusted snoring, a symptom of obstructive sleep apnea in the model. The categorical covariates were based on the information collected in the 2014 survey through questionnaire and the continuous variables were adjusted using the cumulative mean based on available repeated measures before the end of follow-up (e.g., onset of HF). HRs were calculated with quartiles and clinically defined categories of adiposity measures in combination, stratified by education, incomes, lifestyle behaviors and obesity-related diseases. The linear trend in risk across ordered groups was calculated by imputing the median value for each group and computing these values as continuous variables. We excluded coalminers, the users of aspirin or HF medication (e.g., Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors, Angiotensin Receptor Blockers, β-blockers or diuretics), participants with frequent snoring, or those who developed HF within the first 2 year of follow-up or suffered atherosclerotic CVDs during the follow-up period to avoid any confounding effect when assessing the association between adiposity measures and the risk of HF. We further examined the interactions between anthropometric measures and age (< 65 versus ≥ 65 years), sex (men versus women), education level (high school diploma and below versus university degree), monthly salary (< 3000 versus ≥ 3000¥), obesity (normal weight versus overweight/obesity), metabolic abnormalities (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension or dyslipidemia) (yes versus no), lifestyle behaviors (e.g., alcohol consumption or physical activity) (yes or no) and chronic kidney diseases (yes or no). The test of the interaction was conducted by comparing models with and without the cross-product term using the likelihood ratio test. Stratum-specific analyzes were conducted by sex, age, socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, metabolic abnormalities and chronic kidney diseases, even the interaction terms were not significant. Participants were divided into 4 categories based on the values of BMI: (1) underweight, < 18.5 kg/m2; (2) normal weight, 18.5–23.9 kg/m2; (3) overweight, 24–27.9 kg/m2; (4) obesity, ≥ 28 kg/m2 [36]. The WHtR cut-off value of 0.5 [37] and the median of neck circumference (39 cm) and CVAI (100.6) were also applied in the current study. Participants were regarded as metabolic unhealthy if they had hypertension, diabetes mellitus or dyslipidemia. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were assessed to determine the correction of these three adiposity measures with waist circumference. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the concordance statistic were used to assess the ability of adiposity measures to predict HF incidence.

To explore whether the effect of abdominal obesity on HF was mediated by metabolic diseases or other comorbidities, we conducted a mediate analysis to divide the total effect of adiposity indicators on incident HF into an indirect effect and a direct effect. The indirect effect may be interpreted as the difference in HF incidence across adiposity indicators quartiles that could be explained by the difference in diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, hs-CRP or chronic kidney diseases (also known as mediators). In contrast, the direct effect is the adiposity measures-HF association that remained after controlling for the mediators [38].

We conducted trajectory analysis using repeated measures of WHtR and CVAI in the 2008, 2010, 2012 and 2014 surveys. The latent mixture model fit was assessed by Bayesian information criterion and finally the model with 3 patterns showed the best fit [39].

All analyses were conducted with STATA software version 12.0 (STATA Institute) and SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A 2-sided p value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors and health status differed by abdominal obesity status. People with high values of adiposity measures were more likely to be men, to have a lower level of education, to smoke or drink more and to have higher levels of urine protein, fasting blood glucose, blood pressure, low density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglyceride, hs-CRP, a lower level of HDL-C and lower proportion of white collars (Table 1).

During a mean follow-up period of 5.62 years, a total of 724 HF cases were documented. Participants with highest quartile of adiposity measures had a higher risk of HF than those with lowest quartile of adiposity measures (neck circumference: HR:1.30, 95%CI: 1.03–1.65; WHtR: HR:1.49, 95%CI: 1.16–1.92; and CVAI: HR:1.98, 95%CI: 1.48–2.65) in the fully adjusted models. Excluding HF events occurring during the first 2 years, individuals with incident atherosclerotic CVDs, the users of aspirin or other HF medication, or coalminers did not materially alter the results. The association between neck circumference and incident HF was weakened after excluding participates with frequent snoring (HR for Quartile 4 versus Quartile 1: 1.29, 95%CI: 1.01–1.66, Table 2). There was non-significant difference between HRs in the imputation model and in the complete data (data not shown).

Discussion

In the current study, we found that neck circumference, WHtR and CVAI, whether assessed singly or in combination, could be used to capture the risk of HF in Chinese adults. We further used two methods to assess the long-term dynamic alterations of adiposity measures in relation to HF. Both cumulative average and trajectories of WHtR and CVAI were consistently associated with increased risk of HF. Participants with stable high values of anthropometric parameters had a higher risk of HF than those with long-term low values of adiposity measures.

Considering atherosclerotic CVD is closely related to obesity and is a common cause of HF, we excluded participants with atherosclerotic CVDs during the follow-up period and found the results did not materially change, indicating a direct effect of visceral obesity on cardiomyocyte injury, rather than mediated by atherosclerotic CVDs. Visceral obesity, implying excessive fat accumulation in pericardium/epicardium, would lead to increased cardiac wall stress, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, myocardial injury and fibrosis, low cardiorespiratory fitness and activation of inflammatory pathways and ultimately the occurrence of diastolic and systolic cardiac failure [40].

The amount of ectopic fat storage may account for the difference in predictive abilities of metabolic abnormalities and CVD risk of adiposity measures [41]. The adipose tissue in the subcutaneous neck region secrets leptin, adiponectin, and interleukin 6 that could active inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress [42]. There is more free fatty acid release in upper-body adipose tissue than in lower-body fat [43]. Elevated free fatty acid levels contribute to insulin resistance and oxidative stress [42]. In addition, neck circumference conferred the indicators of obstructive sleep apnea (e.g., snoring) [44] and insulin resistance such as fasting blood glucose, serum fasting insulin level, homeostasis model assessment-estimated insulin resistance and glycated hemoglobin [45], which contributed to the pathophysiology of HF [46, 47]. In our study, snoring attenuated the association between neck circumference and HF but did not influence the predictive ability of WHtR and CVAI, suggesting that obstructive sleep apnea particularly contributed to the relationship between neck circumference and HF. However, when we fitted neck circumference with waist circumference and other adiposity measures (e.g., WHtR, CVAI) in a single model, the association between neck circumference and incident HF was no longer significant. This result suggested that the waist circumference-related adiposity measures may be more robust and sensitive for prediction of HF incidence than neck circumference.

WHtR is a ratio metric both taking waist circumference and height into account and has a better description on the overall body shape and muscle mass distribution than waist circumference. WHtR was reported to be associated with a reduction in cardiorespiratory fitness [48] and post-heart failure mortality [49]. CVAI is a China-specific body fat index and has proved to better predict subclinical atherosclerosis [22, 50] and atherosclerotic diseases [51, 52] than BMI or waist circumference. High CVAI represents excessive abdominal adipose tissue and would result in insulin resistance, oxidative stress damage and endothelial dysfunction that would affect cardiac function [53–55]. These results invoke a need to explore the potential mechanisms of regional adiposity-related cardiac dysfunction through future research.

We found that the combination of high BMI and high values of other adiposity measures significantly improved HF prediction, probably because this combination strongly reflected an excess fat depots but less muscle in the trunk mass [56]. Previous studies also found that the combination use of BMI and waist circumference more accurately predicted all-cause mortality [57]. In the Health Survey for England, participants with normal weight/overweight (as determined by BMI) and WHtR ≥ 0.5 had higher cardiometabolic risk factors as compared to those with high BMI but WHtR < 0.5 [58]. Both World Health Organization [56] and the US National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute [59] advocated additional assessment of indicators of abdominal obesity in those with overweight/obesity defined by BMI.

The association between adiposity measures and HF was affected by the status of metabolic abnormalities. Anthropometric indices showed a strong predictive performance in people without metabolic diseases. In a collaborative analysis of 58 cohorts including 221,924 participants, the Emerging Risk Factor Collaboration found that BMI and waist circumference failed to improve the prediction of CVD risk when considering metabolic characteristics (e.g., blood pressure, history of diabetes mellitus and lipid profiles) [41], suggesting metabolic abnormalities provided substantive risk information for HF beyond adiposity measures. Interestingly, we found that 16.5–22.1% effect of adiposity measures on incident HF was mediated by hypertension, suggesting abdominal obesity may exert direct and indirect effects (e.g., worsening hypertension) on the occurrence of HF.

Several limitations should be addressed. First, neck circumference was measured at a single point in time (2014) and we could not study the incident HF in relation to long-term changes in neck circumference. Second, the participants of the Kailuan study were Chinese residents, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups should be further studied. Third, residual confounding is of concern although we adjusted for a wide range of covariates. For example, we did not assess sarcopenia status, which conferred the efficacy of strategies for preventing HF by reshaping the body and reducing abdominal obesity. Fourth, the formula of CVAI considers age and sex and HF incidence differed by sex and age. We could not exclude the probability that the association between CVAI and HF incidence was influenced by sex ang age. Fifth, we did not find that the predictive accuracy of these three adiposity measures were superior to the traditional obesity indictors because the C- index and AUC of neck circumference, WHtR and CVAI were similar to waist circumference. Nevertheless, our study suggested the novel indicators of abdominal obesity can be regarded as alternative predictors of non-atherosclerotic CVDs.

Conclusions

Neck circumference, WHtR and CVAI, alone or in combination with BMI and metabolic abnormalities status, can be used to improve the predictive ability of individuals’ HF risk. Adiposity measures might provide additional HF risk for the individuals with overall obesity or at metabolic abnormalities status. These indices of abdominal adiposity should be considered when assessing obesity in clinical practice.
