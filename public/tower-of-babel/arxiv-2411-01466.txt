Dispersive decay for the energy-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation
Matthew Kowalski

Abstract
. We prove pointwise-in-time dispersive decay for solutions to the energy-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation in spatial dimensions d = 3 , 4 d=3,4 for both the initial-value and final-state problems.



1.
Introduction

This paper studies the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the
energy-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation
:

(NLS)

\begin{cases}iu_{t}+\Delta u\pm|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u=0,\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\end{cases}

where
u(t,x)
is a complex-valued function on spacetime
\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\mathbb{R}_{x}^{d}
. With this convention,
+
represents the focusing equation and
-
represents the defocusing equation.

This equation is
energy-critical
: the scaling symmetry of (
NLS
),

u(t,x)\mapsto\lambda^{\frac{d-2}{2}}u(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)\quad\text{for}\quad\lambda>0,

preserves the (conserved) energy

E(u)=\int\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla u(t,x)|^{2}\mp\tfrac{d-2}{2d}|u(t,x)|^{\frac{2d}{d-2}}dx.

We discuss (
NLS
) for initial data in the Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{1}
and the Besov space
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}
, both of which are scaling-critical.

In the seminal work
[
3
]
, Colliander, Keel, Staffilani, Takaoka, and Tao proved global well-posedness of (
NLS
) in the defocusing case in spatial dimension
d=3
. Their argument was generalized in
[
32
,
36
]
to show global well-posedness for spatial dimensions
d\geq 4
, and simplified proofs were presented in the subsequent works
[
20
,
37
]
.

For the focusing case, global well-posedness is conjectured to hold for initial data
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}
which satisfies
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
and
E(u_{0})<E(W)
, where
W
is a stationary solution to (
NLS
) given by

W(x)=\big(1+\tfrac{1}{d(d+2)}|x|^{2}\big)^{\frac{2-d}{2}}.

This conjecture was resolved for radial initial data in spatial dimension
d=3
, see
[
18
]
, and for general initial data in spatial dimensions
d\geq 4
, see
[
4
,
19
]
.

In the following theorem
[
3
,
4
,
18
,
32
,
36
]
, we summarize the well-posedness results that are needed:

Theorem 1.1

(Well-posedness)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
and let
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In the focusing case, assume that
u_{0}
satisfies
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}<\|W\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}
and
E(u_{0})<E(W)
.
In the
d=3
focusing case, further assume that
u_{0}
is radial. Then there exists a unique global solution
u\in C_{t}\dot{H}_{x}^{1}
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
which satisfies

(1.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(t,x)|^{\frac{2(d+2)}{d-2}}dxdt\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\big).

Moreover, there exist scattering states
u_{\pm}\in\dot{H}^{1}
such that

(1.2)

\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\|u(t)-e^{it\Delta}u_{\pm}\|_{\dot{H}_{x}^{1}}=0.

The property (
1.2
) is widely known as
scattering
. It indicates that solutions to the nonlinear equation (
NLS
) asymptotically resemble solutions to the linear Schrödinger equation. This then begs the question of which properties of the linear Schrödinger flow are exhibited by solutions to (
NLS
). In particular, one characteristic of the linear Schrödinger equation is
dispersive decay
:

(1.3)

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\lesssim_{p,d}|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}},

for
2\leq p\leq\infty
. This is derived by interpolating between the conservation of mass,

(1.4)

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L^{2}}=\|f\|_{L^{2}},

which follows from the Plancherel theorem, and the dispersive estimate,

(1.5)

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-d/2}\|f\|_{L^{1}},

which follows from the fundamental solution to the linear Schrödinger equation.

In light of scattering (
1.2
) and the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
), it is natural to wonder whether solutions to (
NLS
) also exhibit dispersive decay. We answer this for spatial dimensions
d=3,4
in the following theorem.
We focus on dimensions
d=3,4
as these lead to polynomial nonlinearities which are the most physical. In addition, complications arise for dimensions
d\geq 5
due to the low power of the nonlinearity; see (
3.2
) and the following note.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
d\in\{3,4\}
and
p
such that

\begin{cases}2<p\leq\infty,&\text{ if }d=3\\
2<p<\infty,&\text{ if }d=4.\end{cases}

Given
u_{0}\in L^{p^{\prime}}\cap\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, let
u(t)
denote the unique global solution to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
. Then

(1.6)

\|u(t)\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},d,p\big)|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}

uniformly for
t\neq 0
.

We believe that the preceding theorem is the optimal nonlinear analogue of dispersive decay (
1.3
). Notably, it only requires that initial data lie in the scaling-critical space
\dot{H}^{1}
, it recovers a linear dependence on the initial data, and it has constants which depend only on the size of the initial data.

Absent from Theorem
1.2
is
L^{\infty}
-decay for spatial dimension
d=4
. The methods presented here fail for that case; see the discussion preceding Lemma
3.4
and the progression from (
3.22
) to (
3.23
). In general, none of our methods appear able to show decay which is
|t|^{-1-s_{c}}
or faster for initial data in a critical Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{s_{c}}
. This restriction also appears in
[
5
]
, which studies the mass-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation.

In spite of this, if we restrict our initial data to the Besov space
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}
, we can show full dispersive decay for spatial dimension
d=4
. This Besov space is scaling-critical, but is strictly stronger than the Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{1}
. In particular,
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}\hookrightarrow\dot{H}^{1}
. As an immediate corollary of this Besov result, we gain full dispersive decay for initial data in
\dot{H}^{\alpha}\cap\dot{H}^{\beta}
for
\alpha<1<\beta
; see Corollary
5.1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Given
u_{0}\in L^{1}\cap\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, let
u(t)
denote the unique global solution to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
. Then

(1.7)

\|u(t)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}},p\big)|t|^{-2}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}

uniformly for
t\neq 0
.

With scattering (
1.2
) established, it is natural to investigate the final-state problem, where a scattering state
u_{\pm}
is given and
u
is sought. Indeed, this is reminiscent of particle experiments where particles are made to interact and the resulting states are measured. Standard arguments then imply that this final-state problem is globally well-posed and scatters for initial data which satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
.

For solutions to the final-state problem, we then establish full dispersive estimates, analogous to Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. Remarkably, though the solution may reach a singularity in
L^{p}
at the interaction time
t=0
, dispersive decay persists through this singularity.

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix
d\in\{3,4\}
and
p
such that

\begin{cases}2<p\leq\infty,&\text{ if }d=3\\
2<p<\infty,&\text{ if }d=4.\end{cases}

Given
u_{\pm}\in L^{p^{\prime}}\cap\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, let
u(t)
denote the unique global solution to (
NLS
) with final-state
u_{\pm}
. Then

\|u(t)\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\big(\|u_{\pm}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},d,p\big)|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u_{\pm}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}

uniformly for
t\neq 0
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Given
u_{\pm}\in L^{1}\cap\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, let
u(t)
denote the unique global solution to (
NLS
) with final-state
u_{\pm}
. Then

\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\big(\|u_{\pm}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}},p\big)|t|^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|u_{\pm}\|_{L^{1}}

uniformly for
t\neq 0
.

The arguments for the final-state problem are nearly identical to the arguments for the initial-value problem in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. As such, we do not restate the entire proof; instead, in Section
6
we present an example of the necessary changes.

Prior work

Prior to the development of Strichartz estimates, dispersive decay of the form (
1.3
) was the primary tool for understanding long-time behavior of solutions.
As such, these estimates were closely tied to the study of well-posedness and scattering and required smooth initial data; see

[
12
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
33
]
for examples.

Given recent successes in scaling-critical well-posedness and scattering (such as Theorem
1.1
), subsequent work has significantly lowered the regularity required for dispersive decay.
However, until
[
5
]
, these results have required strictly more regularity than well-posedness or scattering and have been unable to demonstrate a linear dependence on the initial data in the sense of (
1.3
).

In
[
5
]
, Fan, Killip, Vişan, and Zhao demonstrated dispersive decay for the mass-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation for initial data in the scaling-critical space
L^{2}
.
In the same sense as Theorem
1.2
, the result of
[
5
]
is the optimal nonlinear analogue of (
1.3
) for the mass-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation.
In Section
3.1
, we adapt the methods of
[
5
]
to prove Theorem
1.2
for
2<p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
.
For
p\geq\frac{2d}{d-2}
, the methods of
[
5
]
no longer apply to (
NLS
) and so we present a more nuanced argument which exploits the energy-criticality of our model.

For our model (
NLS
) in particular, dispersive decay was shown for initial data with
H^{1+}
regularity in
[
27
]
.
We note that
[
27
]
worked primarily on the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and then remarked on the extension to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
This improved on previous results which required initial data with
H^{3}
regularity, see
[
7
,
11
]
.
In this paper, we lower the needed regularity to the scaling-critical spaces
\dot{H}^{1}
and
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}
.

The study of dispersive decay is extensive, with many contributing authors and variations. As an overview of the topic, we direct the reader to the following sources and references therein. For work on a variety of nonlinear Schrödinger equations, see
[
6
,
8
,
12
,
23
,
25
]
.
For work on various wave equations, see
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
26
,
28
,
33
]
.
For recent work on the generalized Korteweg–de Vries and Zakharov-Kuznetsov equations, see
[
34
]
.
Finally, for work on a variety of completely integrable models, see
[
2
,
13
,
16
,
17
]
.

In the note
[
24
]
, we adapt the methods presented here to the energy-critical nonlinear wave equation.

Notation

We use the standard notation
A\lesssim B
to indicate that
A\leq CB
for some universal constant
C>0
that will change from line to line. If both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
then we use the notation
A\sim B
. When the implied constant fails to be universal, the relevant dependencies will be indicated within the text or included as subscripts on the symbol.

We abbreviate the maximum and minimum of two numbers
a
and
b
as
a\vee b=\max(a,b)
and
a\wedge b=\min(a,b)
respectively.

When working with embeddings, we will always use
\hookrightarrow
to mean a continuous embedding, i.e.
X\hookrightarrow Y
if the inclusion map
X\to Y
satisfies
\|f\|_{Y}\lesssim\|f\|_{X}
.

Our conventions for the Fourier transform are

\widehat{f}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-i\xi x}f(x)dx\quad\text{so}\quad f(x)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{i\xi x}\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi.

This Fourier transform is unitary on
L^{2}
and yields the standard Plancherel identities.
When a function
f(t,x)
depends on both time and space, we let
\widehat{f}(t,\xi)
denote the Fourier transform of
f
in only the spatial variable.

For
s\geq 0
, we define the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{s}
as the completion of the Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to the norm

\|f\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\int|\xi|^{2s}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi.

With this definition of the Fourier transform, we define the
Littlewood–Paley projections
as follows: Let
\varphi
denote a smooth bump function supported on
\{|\xi|\leq 2\}
such that
\varphi(\xi)=1
for
|\xi|\leq 1
. For dyadic numbers
N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we then define
P_{\leq N}
,
P_{>N}
, and
P_{N}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{P_{\leq N}f}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\xi/N)\widehat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle\widehat{P_{>N}f}

\displaystyle=[1-\varphi(\xi/N)]\widehat{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle\widehat{P_{N}f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=[\varphi(\xi/N)-\varphi(2\xi/N)]\widehat{f}(\xi).

We will often denote
P_{N}f=f_{N}
,
P_{\leq N}f=f_{\leq N}
and
P_{>N}f=f_{>N}
. As above, when a function
f(t,x)
depends on both time and space, we let
f_{N}(t,x)
denote the Littlewood–Paley projection of
f
in only the spatial variable
x
.

As Fourier multipliers, the Littlewood–Paley projections commute with derivative operators and the free Schrödinger propagator. Moreover, they are bounded on
L^{p}
for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
and on
\dot{H}^{s}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. For
1<p<\infty
, we have that

\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}P_{N}f\to f\quad\text{in $L^{p}$}.

In addition,
P_{N}
,
P_{\leq N}
, and
P_{>N}
are bounded pointwise by a constant multiple of the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function,

|P_{N}f|+|P_{\leq N}f|\lesssim Mf.

Associated to the Littlewood–Paley projections are the
Bernstein inequalities
, which state

(1.8)

\begin{split}\||\nabla|^{s}P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}}&\sim N^{s}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}}\\
\||\nabla|^{s}P_{\leq N}f\|_{L^{p}}&\lesssim N^{s}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}}\\
\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{p}},\|P_{\leq N}f\|_{L^{p}}&\lesssim N^{d(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p})}\|P_{N}f\|_{L^{q}}\end{split}

for all
s\geq 0
and
1\leq q\leq p\leq\infty
.
Additionally, we have the Littlewood–Paley square function estimate which states

\big\|\|f_{N}(x)\|_{\ell_{N}^{2}}\big\|_{L^{p}}\sim\|f\|_{L^{p}},

for
1<p<\infty
.

Following
[
3
]
, we use
\text{\O}(X)
to denote a term that is schematically like
X
. That is, a finite linear combination of terms that look like
X
but potentially with some terms replaced by their absolute value, complex conjugate, or a Littlewood–Paley projection.
For examples, see (
5.7
) and Lemma
4.2
where we will write

(1.9)

|v+w|^{2}(v+w)=\sum_{j=0}^{3}\text{\O}(v^{j}w^{3-j})\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\leq N/8}\cdot g_{N_{1}}\cdot h=\text{\O}(fg_{N_{1}}h).

We use
L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(T\times X)
to denote the mixed Lebesgue spacetime norm

\|f\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(T\times X)}=\big\|\|f(t,x)\|_{L^{q}(X,dx)}\big\|_{L^{p}(T,dt)}=\bigg[\int_{T}\bigg(\int_{X}|f(t,x)|^{q}dx\bigg)^{p/q}dt\bigg]^{1/p}.

When
p=q
, we let
L^{p}_{t,x}=L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{p}
. When
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(T)=L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(T\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is generalized to mixed Lorentz spacetime norms
L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}
in the obvious way; see Definition
2.1
.

2.
Lorentz theory

Throughout our analysis, it will be crucial to employ Lorentz refinements of standard inequalities and methods. Here we recall the definition and properties of Lorentz spaces which are needed. For a textbook treatment of Lorentz spaces, we direct the reader to
[
9
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Lorentz space)
.

Fix
d\geq 1
,
1\leq p<\infty
, and
0<q\leq\infty
. The Lorentz space
L^{p,q}
is the space of measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
which have finite quasinorm

(2.1)

\|f\|_{L^{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=p^{1/q}\Big\|\lambda\big|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|f(x)|>\lambda\}\big|^{1/p}\Big\|_{L^{q}((0,\infty),\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda})},

where
|*|
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

It follows that
L^{p,q}
is a quasi-Banach space for any
1\leq p<\infty
and
0<q\leq\infty
. The inclusion of
0<q<1
will be necessary for the proof of Lemma
3.3
; see (
3.13
) and Lemma
2.10
.

In the case of
1<p<\infty
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we find that

\|f\|_{L^{p,q}}\sim_{p,q}\sup_{\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}}=1}\bigg|\int f(x)\overline{g(x)}dx\bigg|

where
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
are the respective Hölder conjugates. Therefore for all
1<p<\infty
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
, it follows that
L^{p,q}
is normable. In the case of
p=q
,
L^{p,p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
coincides with the standard Lebesgue space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We then use the convention that
L^{\infty,\infty}=L^{\infty}
and leave
L^{\infty,q}
undefined for
q<\infty
.

From direct calculation with (
2.1
), we find that

(2.2)

\big\||x|^{-d/p}\big\|_{L^{p,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\sim_{d}1,

and hence
|x|^{-d/p}\in L^{p,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
p\geq 1
. This is the extent to which we will use the exact form of (
2.1
).

In the same manner as the sequence spaces
\ell^{q}
, the Lorentz spaces
L^{p,q}
satisfy a nesting property in the second index
q
. In particular, we have the continuous embedding
L^{p,q_{1}}\hookrightarrow L^{p,q_{2}}
, i.e.

\|*\|_{L^{p,q_{2}}}\lesssim_{p,q_{1},q_{2}}\|*\|_{L^{p,q_{1}}},

for all
0<q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\infty
.

Lorentz spaces arise most naturally as real interpolation spaces between the usual
L^{p}
spaces. This is achieved through the Hunt interpolation inequality, otherwise known as the off-diagonal Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem; see
[
14
,
15
]
. We recall a specific case of the theorem here:

Lemma 2.2

(Hunt interpolation)
.

Fix
1\leq p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\leq\infty
such that
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
and
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
. Let
T
be a sublinear operator which satisfies

\|Tf\|_{L^{p_{i}}}\lesssim_{p_{i},q_{i}}\|f\|_{L^{q_{i}}}

for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then for all
\theta\in(0,1)
and all
0<r\leq\infty
,

\|Tf\|_{L^{p_{\theta},r}}\lesssim_{p_{\theta},q_{\theta},r}\|f\|_{L^{q_{\theta},r}}

where
\frac{1}{p_{\theta}}=\frac{\theta}{p_{1}}+\frac{1-\theta}{p_{2}}
and
\frac{1}{q_{\theta}}=\frac{\theta}{q_{1}}+\frac{1-\theta}{q_{2}}
.

Lorentz spaces enjoy many of the standard estimates used in the Lebesgue spaces
L^{p}
. In particular, Hölder’s inequality carries over in the following form:

Lemma 2.3

(Hölder’s inequality)
.

Given
1\leq p,p_{1},p_{2}\leq\infty
and
0<q,q_{1},q_{2}\leq\infty
such that
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}
and
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}
,

\|fg\|_{L^{p,q}}\lesssim_{d,p_{i},q_{i}}\|f\|_{L^{p_{1},q_{1}}}\|g\|_{L^{p_{2},q_{2}}}.

In addition, Lorentz spaces satisfy the Young–O’Neil convolutional inequality, see
[
1
,
30
,
31
]
, of which the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality is a special case:

Lemma 2.4

(Young–O’Neil convolutional inequality)
.

Given
1<p,p_{1},p_{2}<\infty
and
0<q,q_{1},q_{2}\leq\infty
such that

\frac{1}{p}+1=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}
and
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}
,

\|f*g\|_{L^{p,q}}\lesssim_{d,p_{i},q_{i}}\|f\|_{L^{p_{1},q_{1}}}\|g\|_{L^{p_{2},q_{2}}}.

From Hunt interpolation and the usual Sobolev embedding theorems, we also find an analog of Sobolev embedding in Lorentz spaces,

Lemma 2.5

(Sobolev embedding)
.

Fix
1<p<\infty
,
s\geq 0
, and
0<\theta\leq\infty
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{s}{d}=\frac{1}{q}
. Then

\|f\|_{L^{p,\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim_{p,s,\theta}\big\||\nabla|^{s}f\big\|_{L^{q,\theta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Finally, we may show a basic Leibniz rule in Lorentz spaces. We recall that the Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are dense in
L^{p,q}
for
q\neq\infty
, see
[
9
]
. By the classical Leibniz rule and extending by density, we then find the following lemma:

Lemma 2.6

(Leibniz rule)
.

Given
1<p,p_{i}<\infty
and
0<q,q_{i}<\infty
for
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
such that
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}=\frac{1}{p_{3}}+\frac{1}{p_{4}}
and
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}=\frac{1}{q_{3}}+\frac{1}{q_{4}}
,

\|\nabla[fg]\|_{L^{p,q}}\lesssim_{d,p,q,p_{i},q_{i}}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p_{1},q_{1}}}\|g\|_{L^{p_{2},q_{2}}}+\|f\|_{L^{p_{3},q_{3}}}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{p_{4},q_{4}}}.

2.1.
Lorentz–Strichartz estimates

As observed in
[
30
]
, the standard proof of Strichartz estimates for (
NLS
) admits a Lorentz extension using the Young–O’Neil convolutional inequality in place of Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev. Here we allow both time and space to be placed in a Lorentz space, which presents a necessary strengthening of the inequalities used in
[
5
,
30
]
.

Definition 2.7

(Schrödinger-admissible)
.

Fix a spatial dimension
d\geq 3
. We say that a pair
2\leq p,q\leq\infty
is
Schrödinger-admissible
if

\frac{2}{p}+\frac{d}{q}=\frac{d}{2}.

We say that
(p,q)
is a
non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pair
if
2<p,q<\infty
. Finally, we say that
(p,q)
is
Schrödinger-admissible with
s
spatial derivatives
if

\frac{2}{p}+d\bigg(\frac{1}{q}+\frac{s}{d}\bigg)=\frac{d}{2}.

Proposition 2.8

(Lorentz–Strichartz estimates)
.

Suppose that
2<p,q<\infty
is a Schrödinger-admissible pair.
Then for all
f\in L^{2}
and any spacetime slab
J\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the linear evolution satisfies

(2.3)

\displaystyle\big\|e^{it\Delta}f\big\|_{L_{t}^{p,2}L_{x}^{q,2}(J)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,q}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Moreover, for all
0<\theta\leq\infty
;
1\leq\phi\leq\infty
; and any time-dependent interval
I(t)\subset J
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{I(t)}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(s,x)ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}(J)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,q,\theta,\phi}\|F\|_{L_{t}^{p^{\prime},\theta}L_{x}^{q^{\prime},\phi}(J)}.

Proof.

We argue akin to the usual proof of Strichartz estimates and begin with linear dispersive decay. Applying the Hunt interpolation inequality to (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), we find that for
2<q<\infty
and
0<\phi\leq\infty
,

(2.5)

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{x}^{q,\phi}}\lesssim_{q,\phi}|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})}\|f\|_{L_{x}^{q^{\prime},\phi}}.

We now prove (
2.4
). For
1<p<\infty
and
1\leq\phi\leq\infty
, we recall that
L_{x}^{p,\phi}
is normable. Then for any time-dependent interval
I(t)\subset J
and any spacetime slab
J\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the triangle inequality and (
2.5
) imply

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{I(t)}\hskip-1.0pte^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(s,x)ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}(J)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q,\phi}\bigg\|\int_{J}\big\|e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(s,x)\big\|_{L_{x}^{q,\phi}}ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}(J)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q,\phi}\bigg\|\int_{J}|t-s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})}\|F(s,x)\|_{L_{x}^{q^{\prime},\phi}}ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}(J)}.

For all
0<\theta\leq\infty
, the Young-O’Neil convolutional inequality and (
2.2
) then imply

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{I(t)}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(s,x)ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}(J)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,q,\theta,\phi}\Big\||t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})}\Big\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{2q}{d(q-2)},\infty}}\|F\|_{L_{t}^{p^{\prime},\theta}L_{x}^{q^{\prime},\phi}(J)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d}\|F\|_{L_{t}^{p^{\prime},\theta}L_{x}^{q^{\prime},\phi}(J)},

which concludes the proof of (
2.4
).

To prove (
2.3
), we proceed with a
TT^{*}
argument. Define
T:L_{x}^{2}\to L_{t}^{p,2}L_{x}^{q,2}
by

[Tf](t,x)=[e^{it\Delta}f](x).

Then

TT^{*}:L_{t}^{p^{\prime},2}L_{x}^{q^{\prime},2}\to L_{t}^{p,2}L_{x}^{q,2}
is given by

[TT^{*}F](t,x)=\int e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(s,x)ds.

Applying (
2.4
) with
I(t)=\mathbb{R}
and
\phi=\theta=2
to
TT^{*}
, we then find that
TT^{*}
is bounded
L_{t}^{p^{\prime},2}L_{x}^{q^{\prime},2}\to L_{t}^{p,2}L_{x}^{q,2}
. This implies that
T
is bounded
L_{x}^{2}\to L_{t}^{p,2}L_{x}^{q,2}(J)
and hence concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

2.2.
Lorentz spacetime bounds

We may now prove global bounds in mixed Lorentz spacetime norms for solutions to (
NLS
). We present the proof for all spatial dimensions
d\geq 3
and all non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pairs.

Proposition 2.9

(Spacetime bounds)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
and
\phi,\theta\geq 2
. Suppose that
2<p,q<\infty
is a Schrödinger-admissible pair and suppose that
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
. Then the corresponding global solution
u(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
satisfies

\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}).

The same estimate holds for the final-state problem with
u_{0}
replaced by
u_{\pm}
.

Proof.

We focus on the initial-value problem first before remarking on the needed changes for the final-state problem.

It is well-known that Strichartz estimates and (
1.1
) imply spacetime bounds for all Schrödinger-admissible pairs, see e.g.
[
3
,
32
]
. Thus for all Schrödinger-admissible pairs
(p,q)
,

(2.6)

\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}}\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\big).

We then turn our attention to the Lorentz case and recall the Duhamel formula:

(2.7)

u(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}\mp i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds.

For all
\theta,\phi\geq 2
, Proposition
2.8
then implies

\begin{split}\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}}&\lesssim\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}+\big\|\nabla|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big\|_{L_{t}^{p^{\prime},\theta}L_{x}^{q^{\prime},\phi}}.\end{split}

Consider an arbitrary non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pair
(p,q)
. As
p,q>2
, it follows that
p^{\prime}<\theta
and
q^{\prime}<\phi
. By the nesting of Lorentz spaces, we may then estimate

\begin{split}\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}}&\lesssim\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}+\big\|\nabla|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big\|_{L_{t}^{p^{\prime}}L_{x}^{q^{\prime}}}\\
&\lesssim\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}+\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}}\big\|u\big\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{4p}{(d-2)(p-2)}}L_{x}^{\frac{4q}{(d-2)(q-2)}}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}.\end{split}

A quick calculation shows that
\big(\frac{4p}{(d-2)(p-2)},\frac{4q}{(d-2)(q-2)}\big)
is a non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pair with one spatial derivative. With (
2.6
), this concludes the proof of the proposition for the initial-value problem.

To adapt the preceding proof to the final-state problem, the only modifications needed are to adjust the interval of integration in (
2.7
) to
(-\infty,t)
for
u_{-}
and to
(t,\infty)
with an added negative sign for
u_{+}
. All other changes are only in notation.
∎

An unfortunate weakness of Proposition
2.9
is the inability to control Lorentz exponents below
2
. This will be necessary in the proof of Lemma
3.3
; see (
3.13
). Though this level of control appears inaccessible for the linear evolution, with (
2.4
) we can gain additional control over the nonlinear correction.

Corollary 2.10

(Nonlinear correction bounds)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
,
\theta\geq\frac{2(d-2)}{d+2}
, and
\phi\geq\max\big(\frac{2(d-2)}{d+2},1\big)
. Suppose that
2<p,q<\infty
is a Schrödinger-admissible pair, and suppose that
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
. Then the corresponding global solution
u(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
satisfies

\bigg\|\nabla\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}).

The same estimate holds for the final-state problem with the interval of integration changed to
(-\infty,t)
(resp.
(t,\infty)
) and
u_{0}
replaced by
u_{-}
(resp.
u_{+}
).

Proof.

We focus on the initial-value problem first before remarking on the needed changes for the final-state problem.

Applying the Strichartz estimate (
2.4
) and the nesting of Lorentz spaces, we find

(2.8)

\begin{split}\bigg\|\nabla\int_{0}^{t}&e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{t}^{p,\theta}L_{x}^{q,\phi}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\lesssim\Big\|\nabla|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\Big\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{p}{p-1},\theta}L_{x}^{\frac{q}{q-1},\phi}}\\
&\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p,\frac{\theta(d+2)}{d-2}}L_{x}^{q,\frac{\phi(d+2)}{d-2}}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{4p}{(d-2)(p-2)},\frac{\theta(d+2)}{d-2}}L_{x}^{\frac{4q}{(d-2)(q-2)},\frac{\phi(d+2)}{d-2}}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\\
&\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{L_{t}^{p,2}L_{x}^{q,2}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\frac{4p}{(d-2)(p-2)},2}L_{x}^{\frac{4q}{(d-2)(q-2)},2}}^{\frac{4}{d-2}}.\end{split}

A quick calculation shows that
\big(\frac{4p}{(d-2)(p-2)},\frac{4q}{(d-2)(q-2)}\big)
is a non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pair with one spatial derivative. Proposition
2.9
then concludes the proof of the proposition for the initial-value problem.

To adapt the preceding proof to the final-state problem, the only modification needed is to change the interval of integration in (
2.8
) from
(0,t)
to
(-\infty,t)
for
u_{-}
and from
(0,t)
to
(t,\infty)
for
u_{+}
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We decompose the proof of Theorem
1.2
into the cases
2<p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
and
\frac{2d}{d-2}\leq p
. For
2<p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
, we find that the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) is integrable near
t=0
. We therefore call
2<p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
the
integrable case
of Theorem
1.2
. This integrability leads to a simplified argument which parallels the proof in
[
5
]
for the mass-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation. We present this proof in Section
3.1
.

For
p\geq\frac{2d}{d-2}
, the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) is no longer integrable near
t=0
and so a more nuanced argument is needed. This will be completed in Section
3.2
.

3.1.
Integrable case

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
in the case where
2<p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
.
This proof forms a template for the remaining cases of Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.4
, and
1.5
. In addition, the result in this section will be used in the proofs of Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
.

This section focuses only on the initial-value problem (
NLS
). The final-state problem is discussed in Section
6
where we present the proof of Theorem
1.4
for
d=3
,
p=3
specifically. As such, for an example of (essentially) the following proof with explicit numbers, we direct the reader to Section
6
.

Proof of the integrable case of Theorem
1.2
.

It suffices to work with
t>0
as
t<0
will follow from time-reversal symmetry. By the density of Schwartz functions in
\dot{H}^{1}\cap L^{p^{\prime}}
, it suffices to consider Schwartz solutions of (
NLS
).

For
0<T\leq\infty
, we define the norm

\|u\|_{X(T)}=\sup_{t\in[0,T)}|t|^{d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u(t)\|_{L_{x}^{p}}.

It then suffices to show

(3.1)

\|u\|_{X(\infty)}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}},

for which we proceed with a bootstrap argument.

Let
\eta>0
denote a small parameter to be chosen later, depending only on universal constants. A quick calculation shows that

(q,r)=\big(\tfrac{8p}{(d-2)(2d-(d-2)p)},\tfrac{4p}{(p-2)(d-2)}\big)

is a non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pair with one spatial derivative. Proposition
2.9
then implies that we may decompose
[0,\infty)
into
J=J(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},\eta)
many intervals
I_{j}=[T_{j-1},T_{j})
on which

(3.2)

\|u\|_{L_{s}^{q,\frac{4}{d-2}}L_{x}^{r}(I_{j})}<\eta.

We note that this relies on
q=\frac{8p}{(d-2)(2d-(d-2)p)}<\infty
, which requires
p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
, and on
\frac{4}{d-2}\geq 2
, which requires
d\in\{3,4\}
.

We aim to show that for all
j=1,\dots,J
,

(3.3)

\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}+C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H^{1}}})\|u\|_{X(T_{j-1})}+\eta^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}.

Choosing
\eta>0
sufficiently small based on the constants in (
3.3
), we could then iterate over
j=1,\dots,J(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})
to yield (
3.1
) and conclude the proof of the integrable case of Theorem
1.2
.

We therefore focus on (
3.3
). Fix
t\in[0,T_{j})
and recall the Duhamel formula:

u(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}\mp i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds.

By the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
), the contribution of the linear term to
\|u(t)\|_{X(T_{j})}
is immediately seen to be acceptable:

(3.4)

\big\|e^{it\Delta}u_{0}\big\|_{X(T_{j})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}.

We thus focus on the nonlinear correction.

By the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) and Hölder’s inequality, we may estimate

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}|t-s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u(s)\|_{L^{p}}\big\|u(s)\big\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r}}ds.

By definition,
|s|^{d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u(s)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|u\|_{X(s)}
. Then

(3.5)

\begin{split}\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}&\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\\
&\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}|t-s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}|s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r}}ds.\end{split}

We decompose
[0,t)
into
[0,t/2)
and
[t/2,t)
. For
s\in[0,t/2)
, we note that
|t-s|\sim|t|
, and for
s\in[t/2,t)
, we note that
|s|\sim|t|
. Doing so, we find

(3.6)

\begin{split}\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}&u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\\
&\lesssim|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\int_{0}^{t/2}|s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r}}ds\\
&\hskip 11.0pt+|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\int_{t/2}^{t}|t-s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r}}ds.\end{split}

Because
2<p<\frac{2d}{d-2}
, it follows that
|s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}
and
|t-s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}
both lie in
L_{s}^{\frac{2p}{d(p-2)},\infty}
, see (
2.2
). Hölder’s inequality then implies

\displaystyle\bigg\|

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\lesssim|t|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\Big\|\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r}}\Big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{2p}{(2-d)p+2d},1}\left([0,t)\right)}.

The preceding calculation relies on both that (
1.3
) is integrable near
t=0
and on the Lorentz improvements in Proposition
2.8
.

For
t\in[0,T_{j})
, we now decompose
[0,t)
into
[0,t)\cap[0,T_{j-1})
and
[0,t)\cap I_{j}
. Doing so, (
3.2
) and Proposition
2.9
then imply

\displaystyle\Big\|\|u\|_{X(s)}

\displaystyle\|u(s)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r}}\Big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{2p}{(2-d)p+2d},1}\left([0,t)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{X(T_{j-1})}\|u\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{s}^{q,\frac{4}{d-2}}L_{x}^{r}([0,T_{j-1}))}+\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}\|u\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{s}^{q,\frac{4}{d-2}}L_{x}^{r}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u\|_{X(T_{j-1})}+\eta^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}.

Combining the two preceding estimates and taking the supremum over
t\in[0,T_{j})
, we find that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{X(T_{j})}\lesssim C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u\|_{X(T_{j-1})}+\eta^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}.

Along with the linear term (
3.4
), this yields the bootstrap statement (
3.3
) and concludes the proof of the integrable case of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

3.2.
Non-integrable case

We now complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
by considering
\frac{2d}{d-2}\leq p
. We follow the structure of the proof of the integrable case, with care now taken to avoid the non-integrability of the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) near
t=0
.

As in the integrable case, for each
t>0
we will decompose the integral over
[0,t)
into an early-time interval
[0,t/2)
and a late-time interval
[t/2,t)
. Unlike the integrable case, these intervals must now be treated separately.

We begin with the early-time interval,
[0,t/2)
. On this interval, we carefully apply the integrable case of Theorem
1.2
to produce a factor of
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}
. In doing so, we avoid the bootstrap norm which would produce a non-integrable term
|s|^{-d(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}
; see (
3.5
). As this argument is independent of the bootstrap structure, we present this estimate in Lemma
3.1
for
d=3
and in Lemma
3.3
for
d=4
.

We focus first on
d=3
as it is the simpler argument.

Lemma 3.1

(Early-time interval,
d=3
)
.

Fix
6\leq p\leq\infty
. Suppose that
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{1}\cap L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
. Then the corresponding solution
u(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
satisfies

\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t/2}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})|t|^{-3(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}.

Proof.

As before, we note that
|t-s|\sim|t|
for
s\in[0,t/2)
. Then by the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) and Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t/2}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t/2}|t-s|^{-3(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\big\|\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}ds

\displaystyle\lesssim|t|^{-3(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\int_{0}^{t/2}\|u(s)\|_{L_{x}^{3}}^{\frac{5p-6}{3p}}\big\|u(s)\big\|^{\frac{10p+6}{3p}}_{L_{x}^{\frac{6(5p+3)}{4p-3}}}ds

By the integrable case of Theorem
1.2
, Hölder’s inequality and Sobolev embedding imply that

\displaystyle\|u(s)\|_{L_{x}^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})|s|^{-1/2}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}^{\frac{3p}{5p-6}}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{6}}^{\frac{2p-6}{5p-6}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})|s|^{-1/2}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}^{\frac{3p}{5p-6}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t/2}

\displaystyle e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})|t|^{-3(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}\int_{0}^{t/2}|s|^{-\frac{5p-6}{6p}}\big\|u(s)\big\|^{\frac{10p+6}{3p}}_{L_{x}^{\frac{6(5p+3)}{4p-3}}}ds

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})|t|^{-3(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}\big\|u\big\|^{\frac{10p+6}{3p}}_{L_{s}^{\frac{4(5p+3)}{p+6},\frac{10p+6}{3p}}L_{x}^{\frac{6(5p+3)}{4p-3}}}.

Because
\big(\frac{4(5p+3)}{p+6},\frac{6(5p+3)}{4p-3}\big)
is a non-endpoint Schrödinger-admissible pair with one spatial derivative and
\frac{10p+6}{3p}\geq 2
, Proposition
2.9
then concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We now turn our attention to the early-time interval
[0,t/2)
in spatial dimension
d=4
. Here, an additional challenge arises from the limited copies of
u
present in the nonlinearity. This prevents the use of Proposition
2.9
due to the requirement that the Lorentz exponent be above
2
; see (
3.13
) and the follow note.

To handle this, we decompose
u
into its linear term and nonlinear correction:

(3.7)

u(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}\mp i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{2}u\big](s)ds=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}+u_{nl}.

In light of Corollary
2.10
, we have spacetime bounds for
u_{nl}
down to Lorentz exponent
2/3
. This will allow us to adapt the proof of Lemma
3.1
for the nonlinear correction
u_{nl}
.

We start with
e^{it\Delta}u_{0}
, which we address in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
1\leq q\leq 2
and suppose that
f\in L^{q}\cap\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Then

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{t}^{3}L_{x}^{3q}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{q}}^{1/3}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2/3}.

Proof.

Consider a Littlewood-Paley piece
f_{N}
for some
N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Because
(3,3)
is a Schrödinger-admissible pair, Strichartz estimates and the Berntein inequalities (
1.8
) imply that

\displaystyle\|e^{it\Delta}f_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{3}L_{x}^{3q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{4q-4}{3q}}\|e^{it\Delta}f_{N}\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}}\lesssim N^{\frac{4q-4}{3q}}\|f_{N}\|_{L^{2}}.

Using the Bernstein inequalities (
1.8
) again, we may estimate
e^{it\Delta}f_{N}
in two ways:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|e^{it\Delta}f_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{3}L_{x}^{3q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{q-4}{3q}}\|\nabla f_{N}\|_{L^{2}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|e^{it\Delta}f_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{3}L_{x}^{3q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{\frac{2(4-q)}{3q}}\|f_{N}\|_{L^{q}}.

We now decompose
f
into high and low frequencies based on a cutoff
M\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
to be chosen later. We apply (
3.8
) to the high frequencies and (
3.9
) to the low frequencies. Countable subadditivity then implies that

\displaystyle\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{t}^{3}L_{x}^{3q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{N>M}N^{\frac{q-4}{3q}}\|f_{N}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}+\sum_{N\leq M}N^{\frac{2(4-q)}{3q}}\|f_{N}\|_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim M^{\frac{-(4-q)}{3q}}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}+M^{\frac{2(4-q)}{3q}}\|f\|_{L^{q}}.

Choosing
M^{\frac{4-q}{q}}\sim\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}/\|f\|_{L^{q}}
then concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Early-time interval,

For

, we note that
|t-s|\sim|t|
. The linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) then implies that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t/2}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{2}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

We now decompose

, we may estimate

(3.10)

\begin{split}&\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t/2}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{2}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}\\

We consider the integral term

and proceed as in the proof of Lemma

. For ease of notation, we define

(3.11)

\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{5/3}}^{\beta}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{4}}^{\frac{(8p-20)\beta}{15p-20}}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}.

By Hölder’s inequality, we estimate

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg\|\big\|e^{is\Delta}u_{0}\big\|_{L_{x}^{5/2}}^{\alpha\vee\beta}\big\|u_{nl}(s)\big\|^{\beta-\alpha\vee\beta}_{L_{x}^{5/2}}\Big\|(e^{is\Delta}u_{0})^{\alpha-\alpha\vee\beta}u_{nl}^{3-\alpha\wedge\beta}(s)\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\frac{7p}{p+1}}}\bigg\|_{L_{s}^{1}}.

) and the integrable case of Theorem
1.2
to

(3.12)

\|u_{nl}(s)\|_{L_{x}^{5/2}}\leq\|e^{is\Delta}u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{5/2}}+\|u(s)\|_{L_{x}^{5/2}}\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\big)|s|^{-2/5}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{5/3}}.

Combined with the linear dispersive decay (

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

As

; see (

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}\big\|(e^{is\Delta}u_{0})^{\alpha-\alpha\vee\beta}u_{nl}^{3-\alpha\wedge\beta}(s)\big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{7p}{p+8},1}L_{x}^{\frac{7p}{p+1}}}.

We note that

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}\big\|e^{is\Delta}u_{0}\big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{7p(3-\beta)}{p+8},\infty}L_{x}^{\frac{7p(3-\beta)}{p+1}}}^{\alpha-\alpha\vee\beta}\big\|u_{nl}\big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{7p(3-\beta)}{p+8},3-\alpha\wedge\beta}L_{x}^{\frac{7p(3-\beta)}{p+1}}}^{3-\alpha\wedge\beta}

\displaystyle=C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}\big\|e^{is\Delta}u_{0}\big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{6p+20}{p+8},\infty}L_{x}^{\frac{6p+20}{p+1}}}^{\alpha-\alpha\vee\beta}\big\|u_{nl}\big\|_{L_{s}^{\frac{6p+20}{p+8},3-\alpha\wedge\beta}L_{x}^{\frac{6p+20}{p+1}}}^{3-\alpha\wedge\beta}.

A quick calculation verifies that

is Schrödinger-admissible with one spatial derivative and that

(3.13)

3-\alpha\wedge\beta\geq 3-\beta=\tfrac{6p+20}{7p}\geq\tfrac{2}{3}

for all

. We note here that the case

requires the additional Lorentz control from Corollary
2.10
.

Corollary

I_{\alpha}\leq C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}},

for all

∎

We now turn our attention to the late-time interval

In the excluded case

In order to consider the case

when

, we must address the failure of endpoint Sobolev embedding. To circumvent this, we establish the following lemma, which acts as a combination of Sobolev embedding and linear dispersive decay (

Lemma 3.4
.

For
f\in L^{1}\cap\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-1/2}\||\nabla|f\|_{L_{x}^{3/2,1}}.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon>0
, we take a Gaussian approximation to the identity,

\widehat{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(\xi)=e^{-\varepsilon|\xi|^{2}}.

The linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) then implies that

(3.14)

\begin{split}\|e^{it\Delta}f\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim\|e^{it\Delta}[\psi_{\varepsilon}*f]\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}+|t|^{-3/2}\|\psi_{\varepsilon}*f-f\|_{L_{x}^{1}}.\end{split}

Because
f\in L^{1}
, the second term will vanish as
\varepsilon\to 0
. It thus suffices to consider the first term.

For the first term, we set up an oscillatory integral. Applying the Young–O’Neil convolutional inequality, we find that

(3.15)

\begin{split}\|e^{it\Delta}[\psi_{\varepsilon}*f]\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}&\sim\|e^{it\Delta}|\nabla|^{-1}\psi_{\varepsilon}*[|\nabla|f]\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\\
&\lesssim\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-it|\xi|^{2}+ix\cdot\xi-\varepsilon|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|^{-1}d\xi\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{3,\infty}}\||\nabla|f\|_{L_{x}^{3/2,1}}.\end{split}

Consider the integral term. Converting to spherical coordinates with
x
taken as the zenith direction, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-it|\xi|^{2}+ix\cdot\xi-\varepsilon|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|^{-1}d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\rho^{2}+i|x|\rho\cos\phi-\varepsilon\rho^{2}}\rho\;d\rho\;d\cos\phi\;d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{i|x|}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\Big(e^{-it\left[\rho^{2}+\frac{|x|}{t}\rho\right]}-e^{-it\left[\rho^{2}-\frac{|x|}{t}\rho\right]}\Big)e^{-\varepsilon\rho^{2}}d\rho.

Here the limit representation is justified because the integrand is in
L^{1}
.

Consider the phase factors
\rho^{2}\pm\frac{|x|}{t}\rho
. For all
\rho,t,
and
|x|
, we find

\bigg|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}\Big(\rho^{2}\pm\frac{|x|}{t}\rho\Big)\bigg|\geq 1.

Applying Van der Corput’s lemma, see
[
9
, Cor. 2.6.8]
, we may then estimate

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{R}\Big(e^{-it\left[\rho^{2}+\frac{|x|}{t}\rho\right]}-e^{-it\left[\rho^{2}-\frac{|x|}{t}\rho\right]}\Big)e^{-\varepsilon\rho^{2}}d\rho\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim|t|^{-1/2}\Big(e^{-\varepsilon R^{2}}+\big\|\partial_{\rho}e^{-\varepsilon\rho^{2}}\big\|_{L_{\rho}^{1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim|t|^{-1/2},

uniformly for
R>0
and for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Taking the limit as
R\to\infty
, we then find that

(3.16)

\begin{split}\bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-it|\xi|^{2}+ix\xi-\varepsilon|\xi|^{2}}|\xi|^{-1}d\xi\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{3,\infty}}\lesssim\big\||x|^{-1}|t|^{-1/2}\big\|_{L_{x}^{3,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\lesssim|t|^{-1/2},\end{split}

uniformly for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. Combining the estimates (
3.14
), (
3.15
), and (
3.16
), we may then take
\varepsilon\to 0
to conclude proof of the lemma.
∎

We now complete the proof of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

It remains to consider

.

It suffices to work with

It then suffices to show

Fix a small parameter

\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}\lesssim C(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\Big(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}}+\|u\|_{X(T_{j-1})}+\eta^{\frac{4}{d-2}}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}\Big).

Consider first

\begin{split}\bigg\|\int_{t/2}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[&|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}\\

For

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{t/2}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{t/2}^{t}|t-s|^{\frac{d+2p-pd}{2p}}\|u(s)\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\|\nabla u(s)\|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}_{L_{x}^{r,2}}ds.

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{t/2}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}

\displaystyle\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L^{p}}

(3.22)

4.
Besov theory

For the proof of Theorem
1.3
, it will be necessary to decompose our solutions into Littlewood-Paley pieces and employ the use of Besov spaces. Here we recall the definition of homogeneous Besov spaces and briefly develop some Besov theory for (
NLS
). For a more general treatment of nonlinear Schrödinger equations in Besov spaces, see
[
29
]
and references therein. For textbook treatments of Besov spaces, we direct the interested reader to
[
10
,
35
]
, though we caution that many conventions exist for the notation.

Definition 4.1
.

Fix
d\geq 1
,
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
, and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. The homogeneous Besov space
\dot{B}^{s}_{p,q}
is the completion of the Schwartz functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to the norm

\|f\|_{\dot{B}^{s}_{p,q}}=\big\|N^{s}\|f_{N}(x)\|_{L^{p}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big\|_{\ell_{N}^{q}(2^{\mathbb{Z}})}.

We note that Bernstein’s inequality (
1.8
) implies that the factor
N^{s}
acts as
|\nabla|^{s}
.

Throughout our analysis, it will be crucial to employ a simple paraproduct decomposition for the cubic nonlinearity of (
NLS
) in
d=4
. This will allow us to restrict attention to terms which feature a high frequency. We recall such a decomposition in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2

(Paraproduct decomposition)
.

Suppose that
f^{1},f^{2},f^{3}\in L^{2}
. Then
P_{N}(f^{1}f^{2}f^{3})
can be expressed as

\begin{split}P_{N}(f^{1}f^{2}f^{3})&=P_{N}\bigg(f^{1}_{\geq N/8}f^{2}f^{3}+f^{1}_{<N/8}f^{2}_{\geq N/8}f^{3}+f^{1}_{<N/8}f^{2}_{<N/8}f^{3}_{\geq N/8}\bigg)\\
&=P_{N}\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}\sum_{(i,j,k)}\text{\O}(f^{i}_{N_{1}}f^{j}f^{k}),\end{split}

where
(i,j,k)
is summed over all permutations of
(1,2,3)
and
Ø
is defined in (
1.9
).
In particular,

\begin{split}|P_{N}(f^{1}f^{2}f^{3})|&\lesssim\sum_{(i,j,k)}\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}M\big[f^{i}_{N_{1}}\cdot Mf^{j}\cdot Mf^{k}\big],\end{split}

uniformly for
N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}
where
M
is the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function.

In a similar vein to Proposition
2.9
, we may establish mixed Besov-type spacetime bounds for solutions to (
NLS
) with a initial data in
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}
.

Proposition 4.3

(Besov spacetime bounds)
.

Let
(p,q)
be a Schrödinger-admissible pair for
d=4
. Suppose that
u_{0}\in\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})\subset\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
. Then the corresponding global solution
u(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
satisfies

\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}}\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\big).

The same estimates hold for the final-state problem with
u_{0}
replaced by
u_{\pm}
.

Proof.

We focus on the initial-value problem first before remarking on the needed changes for the final-state problem. We first consider
p=q=3
before generalizing to all Schrödinger-admissible pairs
p,q
.

As
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}\hookrightarrow H^{2}
, it suffices to consider
u_{0}\in H^{2}
so that persistence of regularity (see
[
4
,
32
]
) implies that all mixed Besov norms considered are finite.

We proceed via a bootstrap argument and introduce a small parameter
\eta>0
to be chosen later based on absolute constants. By Theorem
2.9
, we may decompose
\mathbb{R}
into
J=J(\eta,\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}})
many intervals
I_{j}
on which

(4.1)

\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{6}(I_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{4})}<\eta.

By the Duhamel formula and Strichartz estimates, for each spacetime slab
I_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}
and any
t_{j}\in I_{j}
, we may estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L^{3}_{t,x}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\big\|(|u|^{2}u)_{N}\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}(I_{j})}.

For the second term, we apply the paraproduct decomposition, Lemma
4.2
. Hölder’s inequality and the boundedness of the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function then imply that

\displaystyle\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L^{3}_{t,x}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}N\|u_{N_{1}}(Mu)^{2}\|_{L^{3/2}_{t,x}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\|u\|^{2}_{L^{6}_{t,x}(I_{j})}\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}N\|u_{N_{1}}\|_{L^{3}_{t,x}(I_{j})}.

Summing over
N
first, (
4.1
) then implies that

(4.2)

\begin{split}\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L^{3}_{t,x}(I_{j})}&\lesssim\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\eta^{2}\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\|u_{N_{1}}\|_{L^{3}_{t,x}(I_{j})}.\end{split}

Choosing
\eta
sufficiently small relative to the constants in (
4.2
), a standard bootstrap argument yields

\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}(I_{j})}\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\big)\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}.

By the Duhamel formula and Strichartz estimates, for any Schrödinger admissible pair
(p,q)
we find similarly that

\displaystyle\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\|u\|^{2}_{L^{6}_{t,x}}\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\|u_{N_{1}}\|_{L^{3}_{t,x}}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\big)\|u(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}.

By the
p=\infty,q=2
estimate, an iterative argument with appropriately chosen
t_{j}
then implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}N\|u_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}},j\big).

Summing over
j=1,\dots,J(\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}})
, this concludes the proof of the proposition for the initial-value problem.

To adapt the preceding proof to the final-state problem, it suffices to change every instance of
u_{0}
to
u_{\pm}
and one instance of
u(t_{j})
to
u_{\pm}
.
∎

In addition to spacetime bounds, we will require a stability result for initial data in
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. This proof parallels the usual proof of stability in
\dot{H}^{1}
, only with a paraproduct decomposition used to understand the nonlinearity of (

).

Proposition 4.4

(
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}
stability)
.

Fix
d=4
and let
p,q
be a Schrödinger-admissible pair. Suppose that
u_{0},v_{0}
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
and obey the bound
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}},\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R
. Let
u(t),v(t)
be the corresponding solutions to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}
respectively. Then

\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}\|\nabla(u_{N}-v_{N})\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}}\leq C(R)\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}.

The same estimates hold for the final-state problem with
u_{0},v_{0}
replaced by
u_{\pm},v_{\pm}
.

Proof.

We focus on the initial-value problem first before remarking on the needed changes for the final-state problem. We first consider
p=q=3
before generalizing to all Schrödinger-admissible pairs
p,q
.

We proceed via a bootstrap argument and introduce a small parameter
\eta>0
to be chosen later based on absolute constants and
R
. By Proposition
4.3
, we may decompose
\mathbb{R}
into
J=J(R,\eta)
many intervals
I_{j}
on which

(4.3)

\big\||u|\wedge|v|\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{6}(I_{j})}\leq\|u\|_{L_{t,x}^{6}(I_{j})}+\|v\|_{L_{t,x}^{6}(I_{j})}<\eta.

By the Duhamel formula and Strichartz estimates, for each spacetime slab
I_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}
and any
t_{j}\in I_{j}
, we may estimate

(4.4)

\begin{split}\sum_{N}N\|P_{N}(u-v)\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}}&\lesssim\|u(t_{j})-v(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\sum_{N}N\Big\|P_{N}\big(|u|^{2}u-|v|^{2}v\big)\Big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}\\
&=\|u(t_{j})-v(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+\textup{II}.\end{split}

We focus on the second term and decompose the nonlinearity as

\displaystyle|u|^{2}u-|v|^{2}v

\displaystyle=u^{2}\overline{(u-v)}+\overline{v}u(u-v)+|v|^{2}(u-v).

Then

II

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{N}N\bigg\{\Big\|P_{N}\big[u^{2}\overline{(u-v)}\big]\Big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}+\Big\|P_{N}\big[\overline{v}u(u-v)\big]\Big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}+\Big\|P_{N}\big[|v|^{2}(u-v)\big]\Big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}\bigg\}.

Applying the paraproduct decomposition, Lemma (
4.2
), we schematically have three terms, each term corresponding to the case where one of
(u-v),u,v
lies at the high frequency
N_{1}
. Expanding the sum and bounding
u,v
by
|u|\wedge|v|
for convenience, we then find that

\displaystyle\textup{II}\lesssim

\displaystyle\;\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}N\bigg[\big\|u_{N_{1}}M(|u|\wedge|v|)M(u-v)\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}+\big\|v_{N_{1}}M\big(|u|\wedge|v|\big)M(u-v)\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}N\big\|(u-v)_{N_{1}}\big[M\big(|u|\wedge|v|\big)\big]^{2}\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}.

Summing in
N
first and then applying Hölder’s inequality, we may estimate

\begin{split}\textup{II}\lesssim&\;\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\bigg[\big\|u_{N_{1}}M\big(|u|\wedge|v|\big)M(u-v)\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}+\big\|v_{N_{1}}M\big(|u|\wedge|v|\big)M(u-v)\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}\bigg]\\
&+\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\big\|(u-v)_{N_{1}}\big[M\big(|u|\wedge|v|\big)\big]^{2}\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{3/2}}\\
\lesssim&\;\big\||u|\wedge|v|\big\|_{L_{t,x}^{6}}\|u-v\|_{L_{t}^{3}L_{x}^{12}}\bigg(\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\|u_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t}^{6}L_{x}^{12/5}}+\sum_{N_{1}}{N_{1}}\|v_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t}^{6}L_{x}^{12/5}}\bigg)\\
&+\big\||u|\wedge|v|\big\|^{2}_{L_{t,x}^{6}}\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\|(u-v)_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}}.\end{split}

Proposition
4.3
and (
4.3
) then imply that

(4.5)

\begin{split}\textup{II}\lesssim C(R)\eta\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\|(u-v)_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}}.\end{split}

Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), we then find that for each spacetime slab
I_{j}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}
,

(4.6)

\sum_{N}N\|P_{N}(u-v)\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}}\lesssim\|u(t_{j})-v(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}+C(R)\eta\sum_{N_{1}}N_{1}\|(u-v)_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}}.

Taking
\eta
sufficiently small based only on the constants in (
4.6
) and
R
, a bootstrap argument then implies that for all
j=1,\dots,J(R)
,

(4.7)

\sum_{N}N\|(u-v)_{N}\|_{L_{t,x}^{3}(I_{J})}\leq C(R)\|u(t_{j})-v(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}.

For any Schrödinger-admissible pair, the Duhamel formula and Strichartz inequalities similarly imply that

\displaystyle\sum_{N}N\|(u-v)_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq C(R)\|u(t_{j})-v(t_{j})\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}.

By the
p=\infty,q=2
estimate, an iterative argument with appropriately chosen
t_{j}
then implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{N}N\|(u-v)_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq C(R,j)\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}.

Summing over
j=1,\dots,J(R)
, this concludes the proof of the proposition for the initial-value problem.

To adapt the preceding proof to the final-state problem, it is sufficient to change every instance of
u_{0},v_{0}
to
u_{\pm},v_{\pm}
respectively and one instance of
u(t_{j}),v(t_{j})
to
u_{\pm},v_{\pm}
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
. Though we again use a bootstrap argument, we can no longer close the bootstrap by decomposing

into small intervals as was done in (

) and (

; see (

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

For
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define the norm

\|f(t,x)\|_{X}=\sup_{t\neq 0}|t|^{2}\|f(t)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}.

By the density of Schwartz functions in
\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}\cap L^{p^{\prime}}
, it suffices to consider Schwartz solutions of (
NLS
).

We proceed via induction on
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}
. As Theorem
1.3
holds trivially for
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}=0
, it suffices to show the inductive step.

Suppose for the sake of induction that there exists some
R_{0}\geq 0
and
C(R_{0})
such that for all
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}
, the corresponding solution
u(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
satisfies

(5.1)

\|u\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0})\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}.

By induction, it then suffices to show that (
5.1
) extends to all
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+1
, perhaps with a new constant
C(R_{0})
.

We show this incrementally. Fix some
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small to be chosen later based only on
R_{0}
and absolute constants.
Suppose for the sake of iteration that there exists
k
with
R_{0}+(k+1)\varepsilon\leq R_{0}+1
such that for all
\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+k\varepsilon
, the corresponding solution
v(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
v_{0}
satisfies

(5.2)

\|v(t)\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0},k,\varepsilon)\|v_{0}\|_{L^{1}}.

We then aim to show that for all
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+(k+1)\varepsilon
, the corresponding solution
u(t)
to (
NLS
) with initial data
u_{0}
satisfies

(5.3)

\|u(t)\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0},k,\varepsilon)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}.

Provided that
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(R_{0})
is chosen based only on
R_{0}
, iterating over
k=0,\dots,\varepsilon^{-1}-1
will then extend (
5.1
) to all
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+1
, potentially with a new constant
C(R_{0})
.

We therefore focus on (
5.3
) and proceed via a bootstrap argument. Suppose that
u_{0}
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
with
\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+(k+1)\varepsilon
. To make use of the iterative assumption (
5.2
), we decompose

as

u_{0}=\big(\tfrac{R_{0}+k\varepsilon}{R_{0}+(k+1)\varepsilon}\big)u_{0}+\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{R_{0}+(k+1)\varepsilon}\big)u_{0}=v_{0}+w_{0}.

Then
\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+k\varepsilon
;
\|w_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq\varepsilon
; and
\|v_{0}\|_{L^{1}},\|w_{0}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}
.

Let
v(t)
be the solution to (
NLS
) with initial data
v_{0}
and let
w(t)=u(t)-v(t)
. Note that Proposition
4.4
then implies that

(5.4)

\sum_{N}N\|w_{N}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}L_{x}^{2}}\leq C(R_{0})\varepsilon,

because
R_{0}+(k+1)\varepsilon\leq R_{0}+1
.

As
\|v_{0}\|_{\dot{B}^{1}_{2,1}}\leq R_{0}+k\varepsilon
, the iterative assumption (
5.2
) implies that

(5.5)

\|u\|_{X}\leq\|v\|_{X}+\|w\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0},k,\varepsilon)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}+\|w\|_{X}.

To prove (
5.3
), we then aim to show that
w
satisfies the bootstrap statement

(5.6)

\|w\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0},k,\varepsilon)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}+C(R_{0})\varepsilon\|w\|_{X}.

Taking
\varepsilon\leq 1/2C(R_{0})
would then imply

\|w\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0},k,\varepsilon)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}.

Along with (

) and conclude the proof of the theorem.

We thus focus our attention on (
5.6
). By definition,

\displaystyle iw_{t}+\Delta w\pm(|u|^{2}u-|v|^{2}v)=0,

Decomposing into early- and late-time intervals, the Duhamel formula for

(5.7)

\begin{split}w(t)&=e^{it\Delta}w_{0}\mp i\int_{0}^{t/2}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{2}u-|v|^{2}v\big](s)ds\\
&\hskip 11.0pt+\int_{t/2}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[\text{\O}(w^{3})+\text{\O}(w^{2}v)+\text{\O}(wv^{2})\big](s)ds\\
&=\textup{I}+\textup{II}+\textup{III}(w^{3})+\textup{III}(w^{2}v)+\textup{III}(wv^{2}).\end{split}

For term

\|\textup{I}\|_{X}\lesssim\|w_{0}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}

which is acceptable for the bootstrap statement (
5.6
). For term
II
in (
5.7
), because

and

both satisfy (

implies that

(5.9)

\begin{split}\|\textup{II}\|_{X}&=\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t/2}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{2}u-|v|^{2}v\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{X}\leq C(R_{0})\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}},\end{split}

which is similarly acceptable for (
5.6
).

It then remains to estimate the terms
\textup{III}(w^{3})
,
\textup{III}(w^{2}v)
, and
\textup{III}(wv^{2})
in (
5.7
).
We first consider general terms of the form
\textup{III}(*)
before specializing.
To align with the notation of Lemma
4.2
, we then consider terms of the form

(5.10)

\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}\big\|\textup{III}\big(P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh]\big)\big\|_{X}=\sum_{N_{1}\gtrsim N}\bigg\|\int_{t/2}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh](s)ds\bigg\|_{X},

for arbitrary functions

N_{1}\gtrsim N
, we first consider individual terms
\textup{III}\big(P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh]\big)
.

We decompose
g
into Littlewood-Paley pieces and then introduce an integration cutoff

as

\displaystyle\big\|\textup{III}\big(P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh]\big)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{N_{2}}\bigg\|\int_{t/2}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}g_{N_{2}}h](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N_{2}}\int_{t/2}^{(t-B)\wedge t/2}\big\|e^{i(t-s)\Delta}P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}g_{N_{2}}h](s)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}ds

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt+\sum_{N_{2}}\int_{(t-B)\wedge t/2}^{t}\big\|e^{i(t-s)\Delta}P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}g_{N_{2}}h](s)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}ds.

In doing so, we have isolated the singularity at

into the interval
((t-B)\wedge t/2,t]
.

On the interval
(t/2,(t-B)\wedge t/2]

) directly. On the interval

, we apply Bernstein’s inequality (
1.8
) and then the conservation of mass (
1.4
). Because
P_{N}
is bounded on

\displaystyle\big\|\textup{III}\big(P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh]\big)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{N_{2}}\int_{t/2}^{(t-B)\wedge t/2}|t-s|^{-2}\|[f_{N_{1}}g_{N_{2}}h](s)\|_{L_{x}^{1}}ds

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt+\sum_{N_{2}}\int_{(t-B)\wedge t/2}^{t}N^{2}\big\|[f_{N_{1}}g_{N_{2}}h](s)\big\|_{L_{x}^{2}}ds.

For each term, we place
h
into the bootstrap norm and note that

for

. Doing so, we find that

\displaystyle\big\|\textup{III}\big(P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh]\big)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\big\|\textup{III}(P_{N}

\displaystyle[f_{N_{1}}gh])\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|t|^{-2}\|h\|_{X}\sum_{N_{2}}\bigg[B^{-1}\|f_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}L_{x}^{2}}\|g_{N_{2}}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}L_{x}^{2}}+BN^{2}\|f_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{t}^{\infty}L_{x}^{2}}\|g_{N_{2}}\|_{L_{t,x}^{\infty}}\bigg]

, we may then estimate

\big\|\textup{III}\big(P_{N}[f_{N_{1}}gh]\big)\big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-2}N\|f_{N_{1}}\|_{L_{s}^{\infty}L_{x}^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{N_{2}}N_{2}\|g_{N_{2}}\|_{L_{s}^{\infty}L_{x}^{2}}\bigg)\|h\|_{X}.

In this section, we prove dispersive decay for the final-state problem, Theorems

given:

(6.1)

u(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{+}\pm i\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{\frac{4}{d-2}}u\big](s)ds.

The proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
follow nearly identical arguments to the proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
for the initial-value problem (
NLS
). The only significant change arises from the decomposition
[0,t)=[0,t/2)\cup[t/2,t)
; see (
3.6
) for its use in the initial-value problem. This decomposition allowed us to estimate
|t-s|\gtrsim|t|
for
s\in[0,t/2)
and
|s|\gtrsim|t|
for
s\in[t/2,t)
, but the exact form of the decomposition played no other part in the proof. To prove Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
, it then suffices to find a similar decomposition of
[t,\infty)
which allows for the same estimates.

For the case of
t<0
, we now make the decomposition
[t,\infty)=[t,t/2)\cup[t/2,\infty)
. Then
|t-s|\gtrsim|t|
for
s\in[t/2,\infty)
and
|s|\gtrsim|t|
for
s\in[t,t/2)
. With this decomposition, the proofs presented for Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
can be adapted with only minor changes in notation.

It then remains to consider
t>0
. In this case, we must modify the bootstrap argument in addition to the notation and decomposition. As these modifications will be consistent across all cases, we only present the proof in the case of
p=3,d=3
. This doubly serves to provide an example of explicit numbers for the proof of Theorem
1.2
in the integrable case, see Section
3.1
.

Proof of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

As noted, we consider only the case of
t>0
for
u_{+}
and we fix
p=3
,
d=3
for concreteness. By the density of Schwartz functions in
\dot{H}^{1}\cap L^{3/2}
, it suffices to consider Schwartz solutions of (
NLS
).

For
0\leq T<\infty
, we define the norm

\|u\|_{X(T)}=\sup_{t\geq T}|t|^{1/2}\|u(t)\|_{L_{x}^{3}}.

It then suffices to show

(6.2)

\|u\|_{X(0)}\leq C(\|u_{+}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u_{+}\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}},

for which we proceed with a bootstrap argument.

Let
\eta>0
denote a small parameter to be chosen later, depending only on universal constants. Proposition
2.9
then implies that we may decompose
[0,\infty)
into
J=J(\|u_{+}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}},\eta)
many intervals
I_{j}=[T_{j},T_{j+1})
on which

(6.3)

\|u\|_{L_{s}^{8,4}L_{x}^{12}(I_{j})}<\eta.

We aim to show that for all
j=1,\dots,J
,

(6.4)

\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{L^{3/2}}+C(\|u_{+}\|_{\dot{H^{1}}})\|u\|_{X(T_{j+1})}+\eta^{4}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}.

Choosing
\eta=\eta(R_{0})>0
sufficiently small based on the constants in (
6.4
), we could then iterate over
j=1,\dots,J
to yield (
6.2
) and conclude the proof of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

We therefore focus on (
6.4
). Fix
t\in[T_{j},\infty)
and recall the Duhamel formula (
6.1
). By the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
), the contribution of the linear term to
\|u(t)\|_{X(T_{j})}
is immediately seen to be acceptable:

(6.5)

\big\|e^{it\Delta}u_{+}\big\|_{X(T_{j})}\lesssim\|u_{+}\|_{L_{x}^{3/2}}.

We thus focus on the nonlinear correction.

By the linear dispersive decay (
1.3
) and Hölder’s inequality, we may estimate

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{3}}\lesssim\int_{t}^{\infty}|t-s|^{-1/2}\|u(s)\|_{L^{3}}\big\|u(s)\big\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{12}}ds.

By definition,
|s|^{1/2}\|u(s)\|_{L^{3}}\leq\|u\|_{X(s)}
. Then

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}

\displaystyle\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\lesssim\int_{t}^{\infty}|t-s|^{-1/2}|s|^{-1/2}\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{12}}ds.

We decompose
[t,\infty)
into
[t,2t)
and
[2t,\infty)
. For
s\in[t,2t)
we note that
|s|\sim|t|
, and for
s\in[2t,\infty)
, we note that
|t-s|\gtrsim|t|
. Then

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|t|^{-1/2}\int_{t}^{2t}|t-s|^{-1/2}\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{12}}ds

\displaystyle\hskip 11.0pt+|t|^{-1/2}\int_{2t}^{\infty}|s|^{-1/2}\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{12}}ds.

As
|t-s|^{-1/2},|s|^{-1/2}\in L_{s}^{2,\infty}
, see (
2.2
), this decomposition and Hölder’s inequality then imply

\displaystyle\bigg\|

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\lesssim|t|^{-1/2}\Big\|\|u\|_{X(s)}\|u(s)\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{12}}\Big\|_{L_{s}^{2,1}\left([t,\infty)\right)}.

For
t\in[T_{j},\infty)
, we decompose
[t,\infty)
into
[t,\infty)\cap[T_{j+1},\infty)
and
[t,\infty)\cap I_{j}
. Doing so, (
6.3
) and Proposition
2.9
then imply

\displaystyle\Big\|\|u\|_{X(s)}

\displaystyle\|u(s)\|^{4}_{L_{x}^{12}}\Big\|_{L_{s}^{2,1}\left([t,\infty)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{X(T_{j+1})}\|u\|^{4}_{L_{s}^{8,4}L_{x}^{12}([T_{j+1},\infty))}+\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}\|u\|^{4}_{L_{s}^{8,4}L_{x}^{12}(I_{j})}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|u_{+}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\big)\|u\|_{X(T_{j+1})}+\eta^{4}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}.

Combining these estimates, we find that

\displaystyle\bigg\|\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{i(t-s)\Delta}\big[|u|^{4}u\big](s)ds\bigg\|_{X(T_{j})}\leq C(\|u_{+}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}})\|u\|_{X(T_{j+1})}+\eta^{4}\|u\|_{X(T_{j})}.

Along with (
6.5
), this yields the bootstrap statement (
6.4
) and concludes the proof of Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
.

∎
