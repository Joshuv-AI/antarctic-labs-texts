Abstract
. We show the vanishing of the first direct image of the structure sheaf of a normal scheme X X which is mapped properly and birationally over an regular scheme of any dimension. On the other hand, for any dimension greater than two, we show examples of a proper birational morphism from a normal and Cohen-Macaulay scheme to a regular scheme such that the second direct image does not vanish and has an isolated support.



1.
Introduction

Let
\varphi:X\to Y:={\mathrm{Spec\ }}R
be a projective birational morphism of schemes.
It is well known that if
X
and
Y
are regular schemes of finite type over a field of characteristic
0

the following holds:

R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0\ \mbox{ for\ every}\ i>0

by
[
6
, pp.144-145]
.
This is generalized by
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
for excellent regular schemes
X,Y
.

To illustrate some history, we temporarily assume that
Y
is a variety over a field
k
of characteristic
0
with singularities.
Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a resolution of the singularities of
Y
.
Then
R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
(
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
) play important roles in measuring how far the singularities of
Y
are from
regularity since these are zero if
Y
is regular.
The closest singularities to a regular point in this viewpoint are rational singularities defined as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Assuming
{\operatorname{char}}k=0
, we say that
Y
has
rational singularities
if for every resolution

\varphi:X\to Y
of the singularities of
Y
, the following holds:

(1)

R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0\ \mbox{for\ every\ }i>0.

Here, note that once the vanishings above hold for a resolution
\varphi
, then the vanishings also hold for every resolution of the
singularities of
Y
.
It is also notable that a rational singularity automatically has the Cohen-Macaulay (CM for short) property,
which is considered an essential condition for a singularity to be “good”.
On the other hand, in positive characteristic, the vanishing (
1
) does not automatically imply CM property.
Based on this and the present situation, the existence of resolutions of singularities in positive characteristic
is not yet proved,
there is a challenge reasonably defining a rational singularity without using a resolution of the singularities as follows:

Definition 1.2

(
[
10
, Definition 1.3]
)
.

A scheme
Y
is said to have rational singularities, if

(i)

Y is an excellent normal Cohen-Macaulay scheme that admits a dualizing complex, and

(ii)

for each excellent normal Cohen-Macaulay scheme
X
, and each
\varphi:X\to Y
locally projective
birational morphism, the following vanishing holds:

R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0\ \mbox{for\ every\ }i>0.

From now on, we will distinguish between rational singularities in
{\rm char}k=0
and those in the definition
1.2
by denoting them as rational singularities and “rational singularities”, respectively.
Definition
1.2
asserts that
\varphi:X\to Y
plays the role of a resolution of the singularities to
characterize “rational singularities”.
If the definition of “rational singularity” is reasonable, in principle, a regular point should be “rational” (cf.
[
10
, Theorem 9.12]
).
However,
Linquan Ma shows an example of a regular point that does not satisfy this definition of “rational singularity”.
More concretely,
there is a projective birational morphism

\varphi:X\to{\mathbb{A}}^{3}_{\mathbb{C}},
from normal and CM variety
X
, such that
R^{2}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\neq 0

(see Example
3.2
).
It means that
\varphi:X\to Y
in Definition
1.2
does not play the role of a resolution of the singularities of
Y
to characterize
rational singularities.

Here, note that in this example, the following vanishing still holds:

(2)

\ R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0\ \mbox{ holds\ for\ every\ }0<i<N-1,

where
N=\dim Y
.
Therefore, one may still expect that

\varphi:X\to Y
with normal CM variety
X
would play the role of a resolution of the singularities to
characterize an isolated CM singularity.
Here, remember that if
\varphi:X\to Y
is a resolution of
N
-dimensional isolated singularity
(Y,0)
(therefore
R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}|_{Y\setminus\{0\}}=0

(i>0)
),
then,
the singularity
(Y,0)
is CM if and only if

\ R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0\ \mbox{ holds\ for\ every\ }0<i<N-1.

Question.

For a CM closed point
0\in Y
on an
N
-dimensional scheme (
N\geq 3
) and a proper birational
morphism
\varphi:X\to Y
from a normal CM scheme
X
,
if
R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}|_{Y\setminus\{0\}}=0

(i>0)
holds, then does
the following vanishings hold?

(3)

\ R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0\ \mbox{ holds\ for\ every\ }0<i<N-1.

In this paper, we demonstrate that this question is only positively answered in a specific case.
Concretely,
if
i=1
, the vanishing holds in a stronger form (Theorem
1.3
), and as its application we obtain the vanishing

\ R^{1}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0

for regular
Y
(Corollary
1.4
).
Here, we do not need CM for
X
.
If
i=2
, the answer to the question is “NO” even for smooth
Y
and normal CM variety
X
of any dimension
N\geq 3
.
We summarize our results in the following.
A local ring
(R,{\mathfrak{m}})
is called
equidimensional

if
\dim R/\mathfrak{p}=\dim R
holds
for every minimal prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
R
.
Let
\widehat{R}
denote the
{\mathfrak{m}}
-adic completion of
R
.
The ring
R
is called
quasi-unmixed

if
\widehat{R}
is equidimensional.
Therefore, if
\widehat{R}
is an integral domain, the local ring
R
is
quasi-unmixed.
For example, a regular local ring
R
is quasi-unmixed,
because it is well known that
the completion
\widehat{R}
is also
a regular local ring (see, eg.,
[
15
, Theorem 23.7]
),
in particular
\widehat{R}
is an integral domain.

A Noetherian ring
R
is called
locally quasi-unmixed

if every
\mathfrak{p}\in{\mathrm{Spec\ }}R
,
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
is a quasi-unmixed local ring.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
Y
be an Noetherian scheme of dimension
N\geq 1
.
Assume
Y
is locally quasi-unmixed and has pseudo-rational singularities in codimension two and the Serre’s condition
S_{3}
.
Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a proper birational morphism of finite type with normal
X
.
Then, the following vanishing holds,

(4)

\ R^{1}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0.

The following corollary follows from Theorem
1.3
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
Y
be a regular scheme of dimension
N\geq 1
.
Let
\varphi:X\to Y
be a proper birational morphism of finite type
with normal
X
.
Then

R^{1}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}=0.

Here, note that we do not assume CM on
X
.

Remark 1.5
.

The paper
[
14
, Theorem 1]
proves a result similar to that in Theorem
1.3
.
Our condition on
Y
for vanishing is slightly less restrictive than that of
[
14
]
.
Specifically, the key difference between the conditions of these theorems is that we assume
Y
is quasi-unmixed, whereas
[
14
]
assumes
Y
is quasi-excellent.

Example 1.6
.

Let
(R,{\mathfrak{m}})
be a regular local ring of dimension
N\geq 3
essentially of finite type over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
There exists a projective birational morphism

\varphi:X\to Y:={\mathrm{Spec\ }}R
with a normal and CM variety

such that

R^{2}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\neq 0,\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ R^{i}\varphi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X}|_{Y\setminus\{{\mathfrak{m}}\}}=0\ \ (i>0)

This paper is organized as follows: in the second section we prove the theorem and the third section
we prove the example.

Lemma 2.1

Let

E_{2}^{p,q}=R^{p}\varphi_{*}(R^{q}\psi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{\prime}})\Rightarrow E^{p+q}=R^{p+q}(\varphi\circ\psi)_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{\prime}}

0\to R^{1}\varphi_{*}(\psi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{\prime}})\to R^{1}(\varphi\circ\psi)_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{\prime}}\to\varphi_{*}(R^{1}\psi_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{\prime}})\to.

As the middle term is zero, the first term becomes zero too, which implies

∎

We will interpret our vanishing problem into the depth of the Rees algebra in the following

Lemma 2.2

Let

0pt_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{R}(I^{n})\geq 3

Proof.

Since

[

So we may assume that

from

Fix an integer

0pt_{{\mathcal{M}}}G(I^{n})\geq 2
.
If

0pt_{{\mathcal{M}}}\mathcal{R}(I^{n})\geq 0pt_{{\mathcal{M}}}G(I^{n})\geq 3

0pt_{{\mathcal{M}}}G(I^{n}){=2<0ptR}

It follows from

0pt_{{\mathcal{M}}}\mathcal{R}(I^{n})=0pt_{{\mathcal{M}}}G(I^{n})+1=2+1=3.

∎

is isomorphic outside a curve in
{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
.
As
X^{(3)}
is projective birational over
{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
, there exists an ideal
I\subset{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]

such that

X^{(3)}={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}(I).

Corollary 1.4 in
[
1
]
guarantees the existence of a curve
C\subset Z^{(3)}
whose blowup gives
X^{(3)}
.
However, it is not clear if the curve is in the fiber
{f^{(3)}}^{-1}(0)
of the origin
0\in{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
or not.

So, the following two cases can happen, where we note that
f^{(3)}
is isomorphic outside the
origin
0\in{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
and

(a)

The ideal
I
is a primary ideal associated to
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, in case
C\subset{f^{(3)}}^{-1}(0)
, and

(b)

The ideal
I
has height
2
associated primes which define a smooth (possibly non-irreducible) curve on
{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
, in case
C\not\subset{f^{(3)}}^{-1}(0)
.

Here, about (a), we note that an ideal
I
whose blowup gives an isomorphism outside a point is locally principal there
in general.
However, as we are working on UFD
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
we can replace
I
by a maximal primary ideal.

Starting with the three-dimensional model
\varphi^{(3)}:X^{(3)}={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}(I)\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
,
we construct
N
-dimensional model
\varphi^{(N)}:X^{(N)}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
by induction on the dimension
N
.
Then, we will show that
X^{(N)}
is normal and CM, and
H^{2}(X^{(N)},{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N)}})\neq 0
and also

R^{i}\varphi^{(N)}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N)}}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\setminus\{0\}}=0\ \ (i>0)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S_{N}:={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
be the polynomial ring of
N
-variables.
In the connection between
S_{N}
and
S_{N+1}
, we denote the
N+1
-th variable
x_{N+1}
by
z
.
Then,
S_{N+1}=S_{N}[z]
and
S_{N}
is a direct summand of
S_{N+1}
.
Let
I\subset S_{N}
be a non-zero ideal and consider the Rees algebra of
(I,z)\subset S_{N+1}

over
S_{N+1}
.
Then, we obtain the following:

(i)

{\mathcal{R}}(I)
is a direct summand of
{\mathcal{R}}((I,z))
.

(ii)

If
{\mathcal{R}}(I)
is normal, then
{\mathcal{R}}((I,z))
is also normal.

(iii)

If
X^{(N)}:={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}(I)
is normal, then for
n\gg 0
,

Z^{(N+1)}:={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}((I^{n},z))
is normal.

Proof.

Every element of
{\mathcal{R}}((I,z))
is uniquely expressed as the sum of an element without
z
and that with
z
.
The element without
z
is contained in
{\mathcal{R}}(I)
, this yields the proof of (i).

For the proof of (ii), it is enough to prove that
(I,z)^{n}
is integrally closed by an induction on
n\geq 1
.

Now suppose
f(z)=f_{0}+f_{1}z+\cdots+f_{\ell}z^{\ell}\in\overline{(I,z)^{n}}
, where
f_{i}\in R
.
We first show
f_{0}\in\overline{I^{n}}=I^{n}
.
By definition, there exist
N\geq 1
and
a_{i}(z)\in(I,z)^{ni}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
such that

(7)

f(z)^{N}+a_{1}(z)f(z)^{N-1}+\cdots+a_{N}(z)=0,

where
a_{i}(z)=a_{i0}+a_{i1}z+\cdots+a_{i\ell_{i}}z^{\ell_{i}}\in(I,z)^{ni}
for each
i
.
In the degree
0
term in Eq(
7
), we have

f_{0}^{N}+a_{10}f_{0}^{N-1}+\cdots+a_{N0}=0

For each
i
,
a_{i}(z)\in(I,z)^{ni}
implies
a_{i0}\in I^{ni}
.
Hence the above equation gives an integral equation of
f_{0}\in\overline{I^{n}}=I^{n}
, as required.

In particular, when
n=1
, this means
f=f_{0}+z(f_{1}+f_{2}z+\cdots+f_{\ell}z^{\ell-1})\in(I,z)
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
and
\overline{(I,z)^{k}}=(I,z)^{k}
for every
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
.
Then we must show
\overline{(I,z)^{n}}=(I,z)^{n}
.
Now suppose
f(z)\in\overline{(I,z)^{n}}
.
By arguing as above, we obtain
f_{0}\in I^{n}
.
So we may assume
f_{0}=0
.
Then we can write
f(z)=z\cdot g(z)
.
Notice that
g(z)\in\overline{(I,z)^{n}}\colon z=\overline{(I,z)^{n-1}}
.
By assumption, we get
f(z)=z\cdot g(z)\in z(I,z)^{n-1}\subset(I,z)^{n}
.

(iii) By the assumption of (iii) and also by
0ptI>0
, we see that

{\mathcal{R}}(I^{n})
is normal for
n\gg 0
(
[
9
, Proposition 3.6]
)
Then, by (ii)
{\mathcal{R}}((I^{n},z))
is also normal for
n\gg 0
,
a fortiori
Z^{(N+1)}:={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}((I^{n},z))
is normal.
∎

Once we have
\varphi^{(N)}:X^{(N)}={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}(I)\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
for
I\subset S_{N}

such that
X^{(N)}
is normal and CM with
H^{2}(X^{(N)},{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N)}})\neq 0

then
we obtain the
(N+1)
-dimensional model

f^{(N+1)}:Z^{(N+1)}={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}((I^{n},z))\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N+1}

for
n\gg 0
such that

H^{2}(Z^{(N+1)},{\mathcal{O}}_{Z^{(N+1)}})\neq 0,

by (i) in Lemma
3.3
.
As
X^{(N)}
is normal, so is
Z^{(N+1)}
by (iii) of Lemma
3.3
.
However even if
X^{(N)}
is CM, the scheme
Z^{(N+1)}
is not necessarily CM.
We will see that
Z^{(N+1)}
has at most one non-CM point
so that we can apply Brodmann’s Macaulayfication to get
X^{(N+1)}
which will be normal and CM
satisfying

H^{2}(X^{(N+1)},{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N+1)}})\neq 0.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
I\subset S_{N}:={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
be an ideal either of the following types:

(a)

a maximal primary associated to
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\subset S_{N}
, or

(b)

\surd\overline{I}
defines a curve which is smooth (possibly non-irreducible) on
{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
.

Assume
X^{(N)}:={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}(I)
is normal and CM such that the restriction of
\varphi^{(N)}:X^{(N)}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
onto
{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
is either isomorphic (in case (a)) or
the blowup at the smooth curve (in case (b)).
Then, replacing
I
by
I^{n}
for
n\gg 0
, we obtain the following:

(i)

Z^{(N+1)}:={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}((I,z))
is normal and has at most one non-CM point,

(ii)

There is a projective birational morphism
g^{(N+1)}:X^{(N+1)}\to Z^{(N+1)}
such that

X^{(N+1)}
is normal and CM, and
Rg^{(N+1)}_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N+1)}}={\mathcal{O}}_{Z^{(N+1)}}
holds.

Proof.

Let
I
be generated by
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in S_{N}={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
, then

(I,z)\subset S_{N+1}={\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},z]
is generated by
z,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
.
By using these generators, we have an open affine covering
X^{(N)}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}U_{i}
, where

U_{i}={\mathrm{Spec\ }}S_{N}\left[\frac{g_{1}}{g_{i}},\ldots,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\vee}},\ldots\frac{g_{r}}{g_{i}}\right].

In the same way, we have an open affine covering
Z^{(N+1)}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{r}\widetilde{U}_{i}
, where

\widetilde{U}_{0}={\mathrm{Spec\ }}S_{N}\left[z,\frac{g_{1}}{z},\ldots\ldots\frac{g_{r}}{z}\right]\ \ \mbox{and}

\begin{array}[]{lll}\widetilde{U}_{i}&={\mathrm{Spec\ }}S_{N}\left[z,\frac{z}{g_{i}},\frac{g_{1}}{g_{i}},\ldots,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\vee}},\ldots\frac{g_{r}}{g_{i}}\right]&\\
&={\mathrm{Spec\ }}S_{N}\left[\frac{z}{g_{i}},\frac{g_{1}}{g_{i}},\ldots,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\vee}},\ldots\frac{g_{r}}{g_{i}}\right]&=U_{i}\times{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\ \ (i=1,\ldots,r)\\
\end{array}

Then, for
i=1,\ldots,r
,
\widetilde{U}_{i}
is CM since
U_{i}
is CM.
Hence, the non-CM locus on
Z^{(N+1)}
is on the closed subscheme

\widetilde{U}_{0}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\widetilde{U}_{i}=Z\left(\frac{g_{1}}{z},\ldots\ldots,\frac{g_{r}}{z}\right)=:\mathcal{Z},

which is isomorphic to

{\mathrm{Spec\ }}{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N},z]/(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=({\mathrm{Spec\ }}{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]/I)\times{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}.

Here, denote
\mathcal{Z}_{0}:={\mathrm{Spec\ }}{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]/I
, then
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
is mapped isomorphically
to the center of the
blow up
\varphi^{(N)}:X^{(N)}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.
Let

P:=(0,0)\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}\times{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\subset\widetilde{U}_{0},

where the first coordinate
0\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
corresponds to the origin
0\in{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
by
\varphi^{(N)}
,
and the second coordinate
0\in{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
is the point
z=0
in
{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}={\mathrm{Spec\ }}{\mathbb{C}}[z]
.
Then,
P\in\widetilde{U}_{0}
is the unique possible non-CM point in
Z^{(N+1)}
.
Indeed, every point

Q\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}\times({\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\setminus\{0\})

is a smooth point in
\widetilde{U}_{0}
, because
z(Q)\neq 0
,
which implies
Q
is not in the exceptional divisor that is defined by
z=0
on
\widetilde{U}_{0}
.

In case (a), the support of
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
is one point.
Therefore, in this case, smoothness of
\widetilde{U}_{0}
at
any point in
\mathcal{Z}_{0}\times({\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\setminus\{0\})
implies that

P\in Z^{(N+1)}
is the unique possible non-CM point.

In case (b),

\widetilde{U}_{0}
is smooth at
every point
Q\in(\mathcal{Z}_{0}\times\{0\})\setminus\{P\}
, because
Z^{(N+1)}
is smooth on

(\psi^{(N+1)})^{-1}\left(({\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\right)
by the assumption on
I
and
Q
is in this
inverse image.
This completes the proof of (i).

Next we will find a center of the blowup to get a normal Macaulayfication as in
[
2
, Corollary 6.12]
.

In case (a), let
f:=z
and

{\mathfrak{q}}:=(z,\frac{g_{1}}{z},\ldots,\frac{g_{r}}{z})\subset{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{\widetilde{U}_{0},P}.

Then

and the maximal primary ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}
satisfy the condition of
[
2
, Lemma 6.7]
,
and therefore they produce a generic complete intersection
L=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
, where
N=\dim Z^{(N+1)}-1

and the locus of
L
contains a non-CM point
P\in\widetilde{U}_{0}
.
(The ideal generated by the sequence obtained in
[
2
, Lemma 6.7]
is called a generic complete intersection and
the generators become a generic

sequence, see
[
2
, Remark 6.9]
.)
By
[
2
, Corollary 6.12]
, the blow-up by
\surd\overline{L}^{h}
gives a normal and arithmetically
Cohen-Macaulay for
h\gg 0
.

In case (b), there is a coordinate function, say
x_{1}
, of
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}]
such that
x_{1}\not\in\surd\overline{I}
.
Indeed, otherwise
I\subset S_{N}
would be maximal primary at
0
.
Let
f:=z
and

{\mathfrak{q}}:=(z,x_{1},\frac{g_{1}}{z},\ldots,\frac{g_{r}}{z})\subset{\mathfrak{m}}_{P}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{\widetilde{U}_{0},P}.

Then
f\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\widetilde{U}_{0},P}
and the maximal primary ideal
{\mathfrak{q}}
satisfy the condition of
[
2
, Lemma 6.7]
,
and therefore we also obtain a generic complete intersection
L=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
.
In the same way as in (a), the blow-up by
\surd\overline{L}^{h}
gives a normal and arithmetically
Cohen-Macaulay for

.

Now in both cases (a) and (b), take the blowup
g^{(N+1)}:X^{(N+1)}\to Z^{(N+1)}
by the ideal
\surd\overline{L}^{h}
, then

X^{(N+1)}
is normal and arithmetically CM.
Here, by
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
, it follows
R\varphi^{(N+1)}_{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N+1)}}={\mathcal{O}}_{Z^{(N+1})}
.
∎

By Lemma
3.3
and
3.4
,

H^{2}(X^{(N+1)},{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N+1)}})\neq 0
holds if

.
We know
H^{2}(X^{(3)},{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(3)}})\neq 0
.
Then, by induction on the dimension
N
, we obtain for every
N\geq 3

\varphi^{(N)}:X^{(N)}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}

such that
X^{(N)}
is normal and CM, and satisfies

R^{i}\varphi_{*}^{(N)}{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N)}}|_{{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\setminus\{0\}}=0,\ \ (i>0),\ \mbox{and}\ H^{2}(X^{(N)},{\mathcal{O}}_{X^{(N)}})\neq 0.

Remark 3.5
.

Let
k
a field of characteristic not
3
and
put
R=k[x,y,z]_{(x,y,z)}
and
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x,y,z)
.
Then Huckaba and Huneke
[
7
, Theorem 3.11]
gave an

{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary normal ideal
I\subset R
with
H^{2}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})\neq 0
,
where
X={\mathrm{Proj\ }}{\mathcal{R}}(I)
.

X
is known to be non-CM, however the dimension of non-CM locus is not known.
If
k
is an algebraically closed field and the non-CM locus of
X
consists of finite closed points,
then by starting from this, one can obtain a similar example as Example 1.5.
