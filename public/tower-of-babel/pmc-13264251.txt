Transformer-based cardiac substructure segmentation from contrast and non-contrast computed tomography for radiotherapy planning
Authors: Aneesh Rangnekar, Nikhil Mankuzhy, Jonas Willmann, Chloe Min Seo Choi, Abraham Wu, Maria Thor, Andreas Rimner, Harini Veeraraghavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Radiotherapy, Cardiac substructures, Segmentation, Computed tomography, Heart chambers, Deep learning

Introduction

Radiation exposure to cardiac substructures from radiotherapy is associated with the development of acute and late cardiac adverse effects and subsequent shorter survival in patients with lung [1], [2], [3] and breast cancers [4]. Current treatment planning approaches typically consider the heart as a single organ, which is insufficient to capture differences in substructure sensitivity [5]. Accurate automated segmentation could reduce manual delineation burden and support substructure-specific dose assessment.

Models with performance that is robust to common clinical variations, such as CT contrast, patient anatomy, and scan position, are essential for reliable automated radiotherapy workflows. nnU-Net [6], a commonly used framework for biomedical image segmentation, uses data-specific automated configuration of architecture and customized preprocessing and has achieved high accuracy for segmentation of thoracic organs [7], [8] and cardiac substructures [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. However, data-specific configuration may lead to “dataset fingerprinting”, which in turn can reduce generalization when test data differ from the training distribution, unless such variation is represented during training; this is often difficult as multi-institutional datasets are hard to acquire [15], [16]. A dataset-agnostic architecture with transfer learning capabilities could preserve a consistent pipeline across deployments while maintaining acceptable accuracy.

Self-supervised learning (SSL) of vision transformers has shown that pretrained representations can be fine-tuned with fewer labeled data while maintaining fixed architectures [16], [17], [18]. However, whether such fixed architectures can achieve clinically acceptable accuracy remains unclear for cardiac substructures, which exhibit anatomic and imaging-based variability.

This study tested whether a hybrid transformer-convolutional network with a self-supervised pretrained encoder, the Self-distilled Masked Image Transformer (SMIT), can enable data-efficient cardiac substructure segmentation while preserving accuracy under common clinical variations. We compared SMIT configurations trained with all available data versus balanced contrast/non-contrast training, evaluated sensitivity to imaging and patient variations, and benchmarked against self-reconfiguring nnU-Net to assess cross-cohort generalization.

Materials and methods

Patient cohorts

Two retrospectively collected institutional patient cohorts were analyzed following approval of the local Institutional Review Board. Cohort I, used for network training and validation, consisted of 240 CT scans from patients with locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer treated with conventionally fractionated radiotherapy between 2004 and 2014 [19], [20], [21]. Patients received 6 MV photons using five-field intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), typically delivered over 30 fractions at 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction. Treatments were planned with an in-house treatment planning system, with dose recalculated using the Eclipse TPS (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) [22] with inhomogeneity correction applied. All patients were positioned head-first supine, and one 4DCT and one free-breathing CT were acquired at the time of treatment planning. The whole heart volume was dose-constrained during optimization. Cohort I was split into training (N = 180) and validation (N = 60) sets, stratified by use of contrast agent.

Cohort II, used as an external test set, comprised 65 patients with stage I-III breast cancer who received radiotherapy [23]. Patients were treated with either 3D conformal RT (N = 58) or static/rotational IMRT (N = 7), typically at 2.65 Gy over 16 fractions between 2007 and 2021. Treatments were planned with an in-house TPS prior to 2014 and Eclipse TPS (v15.0; Varian Medical Systems) thereafter. Deep-inspiration breath hold was used for left-sided tumors and typically also for right-sided tumors. The whole heart was dose-constrained during optimization.

Publicly available CT data from the TotalSegmentator dataset [24] consisting of 1,204 CT scans with publicly provided segmentations were used to train SMIT and nnU-Net for assessing public-to-institutional generalization.

Imaging characteristics

Cohort I included contrast-enhanced CT (CECT, N = 80) and non-contrast CT (NCCT, N = 160) scans acquired across GE and Siemens scanners, with an average voxel spacing of 1 x 1 x 3 mm3. All patients were scanned in the supine position. Cohort II consisted of NCCT scans acquired in either supine (N = 45) or prone (N = 20) positions, with an average voxel spacing of approximately 1 x 1 x 3 mm3, matching Cohort I. TotalSegmentator scans [24] were provided pre-resampled to 1.5 mm isotropic resolution.

Delineation guidelines

Eight cardiac substructures were manually delineated on all scans from Cohorts I and II: the aorta (AO or AA), pulmonary artery (PA), superior vena cava (SVC), inferior vena cava (IVC), right atrium (RA), right ventricle (RV), left atrium (LA), and left ventricle (LV). Contouring followed institutional guidelines based on Feng et al. [25], and all delineations were verified and refined as necessary by an expert radiation oncologist for quality assurance.

Deep learning method

Our hybrid model (Fig. S1) used publicly available SMIT checkpoint (Supplementary Material A) with a convolutional U-Net decoder [26] that was randomly initialized, with skip connections linking transformer backbone features for spatial detail preservation.

Two primary training configurations were evaluated: SMIT-Balanced, using 32 CECT and 32 NCCT scans, and SMIT-Oracle, using all 180 training scans as an achievable performance ceiling. Ablation testing of SMIT-Balanced included frozen-encoder fine-tuning, CECT-only training (N = 56), and NCCT-only training (N = 56). The CECT/NCCT-only models were limited by the number of available CECT scans; this training-size difference was within the performance plateau identified in Fig. S2. To evaluate training-set size, SMIT was trained using balanced subsets of 8 + 8, 16 + 16, 32 + 32, and 48 + 48 CECT plus NCCT scans. Performance was averaged across five-fold cross-validation at each training-set size, and consecutive training sizes were compared using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

For public-to-institutional generalization, SMIT was trained without modification on the public TotalSegmentator dataset and evaluated on both study cohorts.

All models were trained on randomly sampled 128 x 128 x 128 voxel patches using equally weighted soft Dice and cross-entropy loss, optimized with Adam over 32,000 iterations (Supplementary Material B). Inference used full-scan 3D sliding-window evaluation with 50% overlap. Experiments were implemented in MONAI (v0.8.1) [27] and PyTorch (v2.0.1) [28]. Code for model training, inference, and analysis is available at https://github.com/The-Veeraraghavan-Lab/SMIT-HeartSubSeg.

nnU-Net comparison models

SMIT-Balanced was also benchmarked against nnU-Net [6] trained using the exact same data, with same training/validation splits and evaluation metrics. nnU-Net followed its standard automatic configuration protocol with architecture and preprocessing, resulting in an input size of 192 x 192 x 64 voxels at 1 x 1 x 3 mm3 spacing, and was trained for 250,000 iterations (Supplementary Material C).

We additionally evaluated the publicly available nnU-Net–based TotalSegmentator model [24], using the heartchambers_highres variant [29] for the aorta, pulmonary artery, and cardiac chambers, and the standard variant for remaining substructures. For Cohort II, a subset of scans was stored with non-canonical axis orderings; nnU-Net and TotalSegmentator produced unstable inference on these volumes and required explicit reorientation to RAS alignment as a preprocessing step. SMIT processed the same volumes in stored orientation, which we attribute to its isotropic 128 x 128 x 128 patch geometry being less sensitive to axis ordering than the anisotropic 192 x 192 x 64 patches used by nnU-Net. Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis

Segmentation accuracy was evaluated using the Hausdorff distance at the 95th percentile (HD95) as the primary metric, with Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), surface DSC (sDSC, 1 mm tolerance), volume ratio (VR), and dose metrics as secondary metrics. When a model produced an empty prediction for a structure, overlap metrics (DSC, sDSC) were set to 0 by definition, and bounded sentinel values were assigned for HD95 (50 mm, exceeding the largest observed substructure diameter in either cohort) and volume ratio (10), to retain such cases in the primary end-to-end analysis and avoid selective reporting bias from failure-mode exclusion.

Cases were excluded only when all evaluated models failed to detect a structure. Because HD95 and volume ratio are not physically meaningful when one mask is empty, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis in which HD95 and volume-ratio summaries were calculated only among successful segmentations, with segmentation failure counts reported separately (Supplementary Material E).

Cross-cohort HD95 degradation was defined as the change in from Cohort I to Cohort II, reported both as an absolute difference in millimeters and as a relative percentage change: (HD95Cohort II − HD95Cohort I) / HD95Cohort I x 100.

Statistical comparisons between paired models used two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction. Independent-group comparisons (CECT versus NCCT, supine versus prone positioning, biological sex) used two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests. Dose-based manual-versus-AI comparisons also used paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Associations between segmentation accuracy, measured by HD95, and patient factors including age and body mass index were evaluated using Spearman rank correlations. Significance was defined at p < 0.050 after correction. Segmentation metrics and statistical analyses were performed using SciPy (v1.11.1) and pandas (v2.2.2).

Dose-volume metrics were computed by overlaying manual delineations and AI segmentations on the same dose distribution. Maximum dose (DMax) and mean dose (DMean) were computed for all structures, and PA was evaluated using V40Gy [30]. Mean-dose agreement between manual and AI segmentations was assessed using Pearson correlation across substructures. DVH error was summarized as the median absolute percentage-point difference between manual-contour and AI-contour DVH curves.

Results

Mean HD95 was similar between SMIT-Balanced and nnU-Net (Cohort I: 6.6 versus 5.5 mm; Cohort II: 10.0 versus 9.4 mm). Under paired Wilcoxon, nnU-Net showed statistically lower HD95 for several substructures in both cohorts, but median paired Δ HD95 was within 1.0 mm in Cohort I and within 0.8 mm in Cohort II, indicating small directional differences within voxel spacing (Fig. 1). nnU-Net showed higher DSC across all 8 substructures in both cohorts, with median paired Δ DSC of 0.01 to 0.03 in Cohort I and 0.02 to 0.03 in Cohort II; below typical inter-observer variability for cardiac substructure delineation. Relative HD95 degradation from Cohort I to Cohort II was 62% for nnU-Net versus 50% for SMIT-Balanced (absolute: 4.5 versus 3.4 mm).

Segmentation performance quantified with Hausdorff Distance at 95th percentile (HD95) for (a) Cohort I and (b) Cohort II.

TotalSegmentator showed higher mean HD95 than SMIT-Balanced in Cohort I (14.8 ± 6.3 mm, p < 0.001) and Cohort II (31.6 ± 19.3 mm), corresponding to a 114% cross-cohort HD95 increase. TotalSegmentator also failed to generate segmentations for SVC and IVC in nearly all scans, with only 1 out of 60 in Cohort I segmented for SVC (Fig. 2). In the sensitivity analysis, segmentation failures were concentrated in TotalSegmentator SVC and IVC outputs, while successful-segmentation HD95 summaries supported the same qualitative interpretation (Supplementary Material E).

Representative auto-segmentations across CECT and NCCT scans from Cohorts I and II. Columns: (a) SMIT-Balanced, (b) SMIT-Oracle, (c) nnU-Net, and (d) TotalSegmentator.

SMIT-Balanced also approached SMIT-Oracle accuracy despite using 64% fewer training scans, with median paired Δ HD95 within 1.0 mm and median Δ DSC within 0.03. Mean HD95 was 6.6 versus 5.4 mm in Cohort I and 10.0 versus 9.4 mm in Cohort II. SMIT-Oracle showed small but statistically detectable advantages for HD95 and DSC in several substructures, while volume ratio differed primarily in Cohort II (Figs. S3-S5). The relative accuracy degradation from Cohort I to Cohort II was lower for SMIT-Balanced (50%) than SMIT-Oracle (65%).

Training-set size analysis showed that performance improved from 16 + 16 to 32 + 32 scans (mean HD95 reduction: 0.7 mm), whereas further increases yielded marginal, non-significant gains (<0.3 mm per step; p > 0.050), consistent with a plateau after 32 + 32 scans (Fig. S2).

Robustness analyses showed SMIT-Balanced had minimal correlation between segmentation accuracy (HD95) and patient age, with Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ) ranging from −0.19 for AO to 0.12 for RA (Fig. 3). For body mass index (BMI), a moderate negative correlation (ρ = -0.33) was observed only for PA, while other substructures remained weakly correlated (−0.11 to 0.09). No significant differences were observed across biological sex for any substructure (all p > 0.050).

SMIT-Oracle showed weak correlations with age (ρ: −0.24 to + 0.09); nnU-Net showed weak correlations with age (ρ: −0.15 to + 0.13). For BMI, only PA showed a moderate negative correlation in either model (ρ = -0.27 for SMIT-Oracle, ρ = -0.30 for nnU-Net); other substructures remained weakly correlated (Figs. S6 and S7).

SMIT-Balanced showed differences between CECT and NCCT only for AO (p = 0.026), SVC (p = 0.015), and RA (p = 0.029), while the remaining five structures showed no differences (Fig. S8). nnU-Net showed differences for AO (p = 0.014) and SVC (p = 0.004), and SMIT-Oracle differed only for IVC (p = 0.026). Fine-tuning with a frozen backbone produced similar HD95 to full fine-tuning for most substructures (Fig. S9), with significant differences only for AO (p = 0.015), SVC (p = 0.012), and RA (p = 0.006). Training with NCCT-only reduced performance when evaluated on CECT across all substructures, while CECT-only training reduced performance on NCCT scans for AO (p = 0.005).

The accuracy of all models was affected by patient positioning (Fig. S10). For SMIT-Balanced, prone positioning increased mean HD95 relative to supine for PA (7.3 to 8.9 mm), RA (6.3 to 10.4 mm), RV (8.4 to 13.4 mm), LA (6.2 to 9.9 mm), and LV (7.6 to 14.2 mm), while IVC showed the reverse direction (19.7 to 10.8 mm; all p < 0.005). SMIT-Oracle showed positioning-related differences in 7 of 8 structures, and nnU-Net showed differences in 6 of 8 structures. The consistent pattern across all three architectures supports an anatomical rather than architectural origin for prone-positioning effects.

In public-to-institutional testing, TotalSegmentator-trained SMIT showed lower HD95 than TotalSegmentator-trained nnU-Net for AA, IVC, LA, LV, and RV in Cohort I, with the largest differences for IVC (8.4 versus 50.0 mm) and LV (6.7 versus 15.8 mm; Supplementary Table D4). On Cohort II, SMIT showed lower HD95 for AA, SVC, IVC, LA, and LV, with the largest differences for SVC (8.9 versus 50.0 mm) and LV (11.2 versus 18.2 mm; Supplementary Table D5). TotalSegmentator-trained nnU-Net showed lower HD95 for PA and RA in Cohort I and PA, RA, and RV in Cohort II (Supplementary Tables D4 and D5).

Dose-volume analysis showed strong mean-dose agreement between manual and AI segmentations across substructures (r2 > 0.95). Quantitative DVH analysis demonstrated a median absolute difference of 1.73 percentage points (IQR: 1.18 to 2.27) for Cohort I and 0.04 percentage points (IQR: 0.01 to 0.20) for Cohort II (Fig. 4), indicative of excellent agreement between AI-segmentations and manual delineations (Fig. 5, Fig. S11). Mean dose differences were below 1 Gy for all cardiac chambers, and volume metrics showed no statistically significant differences (all p > 0.050, Tables S1 and S2). SMIT-Oracle and nnU-Net achieved similar DVH performance (Fig. S12).

Discussion

This study developed and validated a hybrid pretrained transformer-convolutional network (SMIT) for cardiac substructure segmentation from CT scans across common imaging variations encountered in radiotherapy planning. Several findings were observed. First, SMIT-Balanced achieved accuracy comparable to SMIT-Oracle despite using 64% fewer labeled training scans, demonstrating data efficiency within the SMIT architecture. Second, under matched training data, SMIT-Balanced approached nnU-Net with small median paired differences in HD95 and DSC, with smaller cross-cohort HD95 degradation of 50% versus 62%, respectively. Positioning differences affected 6 of 8 structures for SMIT-Balanced, 7 of 8 for SMIT-Oracle, and 6 of 8 for nnU-Net, while contrast differences affected 3 of 8 structures for SMIT-Balanced, 2 of 8 for nnU-Net, and 1 of 8 for SMIT-Oracle.

SMIT additionally showed improved performance on AA, IVC, LA, and LV when trained on the public TotalSegmentator dataset and tested on institutional data, relative to the nnU-Net-based TotalSegmentator model [24], [29]. Both nnU-Net configurations required manual reorientation on Cohort II, whereas SMIT did not, a practical consideration for batched and automated clinical workflows. In summary, whereas dataset-specific reconfiguration was associated with strong nnU-Net performance on Cohort I held-out testing, the same advantage was not observed in either cross-cohort or public-to-institutional experiments. This result is consistent with prior work that also showed performance degradation of dataset-specific reconfiguration with nnU-Net [15], [16].

Prior cardiac substructure segmentation studies have used atlas-based and deep learning approaches. Unlike atlas-based methods [13], [14], [31], which often require prior lung and/or heart segmentation, both SMIT and nnU-Net avoid serialized preprocessing steps that can propagate upstream errors. Previous work using 2D DeepLab [9] focused only on supine lung cancer cohorts and did not evaluate variability across contrast-enhanced and non-contrast CT or other patient factors. Larger clinical validations have shown that deep learning can reduce segmentation time while maintaining expert-level accuracy and robustness to common challenges such as imaging artifacts and non-contrast breast radiotherapy CTs [32], [33].

The strengths of our study include evaluation of pretraining-based transformer as well as convolutional methods across multiple clinically relevant imaging variations and assessment of model transferability across patient populations without retraining. Ablation analysis demonstrated that frozen-encoder training performed similarly to full fine-tuning, indicating that task-relevant anatomical representations were largely captured during pretraining.

Our study has limitations. First, the evaluation was restricted to a single institution, and multi-institutional validation is needed to establish broader generalizability. Cross-cohort generalization was evaluated only for cardiac substructure segmentation, and generalization of SMIT or other methods to different tasks remains future work. Clinician contouring time was not evaluated, as the study focused on segmentation accuracy and robustness across imaging and patient variations. Finally, coronary arteries and cardiac valves were not included, and single-expert delineations precluded assessment of inter-observer variability.

In conclusion, this work showed that pretrained transformers can achieve robust performance for cardiac substructure segmentation under common patient and imaging variations as well as cross-cohort generalization. The accuracy gains in Cohort I held-out testing were comparable to off-the-shelf nnU-Net, but higher robustness was noted in a single cross-cohort analysis. Large-scale multi-institutional evaluation is required to further assess robustness and generality of this approach.
