A design of experiments approach to production of medicinal bioactives in Mitragyna speciosa (Korth.) Havil
Authors: Ryland T. Giebelhaus, Paula N. Brown, Mariam Ali, Amy M. Dodds, Lauren A.E Erland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextMitragyna speciosa (Korth.) commonly known as ‘kratom’ is a tropical tree native to southeast Asia, that produces psychoactive monoterpenoid indole alkaloids including mitragynine. Kratom products are growing in popularity for increasing energy, improving mood and alleviating minor pain, but availability of commercial plant material is limited in North America. Propagation relies primarily on shoot cuttings rooted in soils with high levels of humus and is relatively inefficient.ObjectiveThis study aimed to optimize in vitro shoot induction system for kratom using a Design of Experiments (DOE) approach and compare the metabolite profiles of in vitro and greenhouse-grown plants using metabolomics.Materials and methodsA DOE approach was used to minimize the number of treatments necessary to optimize in vitro culture media to induce shoot and root morphogenesis in kratom explants. A clean mother stock of in vitro cultures was established and evaluated for medicinal bioactives via targeted and untargeted analyses by ultra-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS).ResultsWe found no significant difference in the quantified levels of mitragynine, melatonin, and some related metabolites, between in vitro-grown and greenhouse-grown plants within the experimental conditions of this study.Discussion and conclusionsThe presence of similar specialized metabolite profiles in greenhouse and in vitro-grown kratom plants paves the way for further studies on biotechnological engineering and production of these valuable compounds. In vitro propagation is a viable approach to produce bioactive compounds in kratom plants.

Introduction

Mitragyna speciosa (Korth.) is a tropical tree native to Indonesia, Malaysia, Myanmar, Papua New Guinea, and Thailand (Figure 1). Documented traditional uses of the plant include treatment of rashes, fatigue, anemia, wasting diseases, appetite loss, gastrointestinal disorders, liver and kidney illnesses, drug addiction and malaria (reviewed in Brown et al. 2017). Commonly called kratom, the leaves were either chewed or prepared as teas and decoctions for consumption by males in rural regions as a restorative and to manage minor aches and pains during the workday (Suwanlert, 1975; Babu et al., 2008; Vicknasingam et al., 2010; Adkins et al., 2011; Tanguay, 2011; Barceloux, 2012; Raffa 2014). Today, kratom leaves are consumed for much the same reasons; to combat fatigue, improve mood and alleviate minor aches and pains associated with physical exertion (Saingam et al., 2013; Singh et al., 2014; Swogger, 2015; Singh et al., 2016; Garcia-Romeu et al., 2020; Grundmann et al., 2023).

Reports suggest kratom was first imported into the United States in the late 1900s with broader commercial marketing increasing through the early 2000s (Henningfield et al., 2018; Veltri and Grundmann, 2019). A 2016 survey estimated that there were approximately 10,000 vendors of kratom products in the United States and consumers were able to access products through the Internet, herbal stores, tobacco/smoke shops and ‘head’ shops where it is primarily marketed as an herbal supplement to address a variety of ailments (Henningfield et al. 2018; Veltri and Grundmann 2019; Grundmann et al, 2021). The number of kratom consumers in Western countries remains vague, however, a 2020 survey reported 6.1% (approximately 9 million adults) of the US adult population had consumed kratom within their lifetime (Covvey et al., 2020). More recently, lifetime prevalence was reported at 9.1%, considerably higher than previous estimates (Grundmann et al., 2025). These reports demonstrate that present day kratom consumption is not rare and is growing outside of Southeast Asia (Henningfield et al., 2019; Veltri and Grundmann 2019; Covvey et al. 2020; Schimmel et al., 2021).

Health Canada classifies kratom as a natural health product (NHP) under Schedule 1 item 1 (plant or plant material) of the Natural Health Product Regulations. It is illegal to sell any health products in Canada without authorization from Health Canada; however, there are provisions for importation for personal use (Health Canada 2018, 2024). According to the Health Product Border Compliance Program, individuals may import a single course of treatment, established as 50 g of dried leaves or powdered leaves or a 90-day supply as 1170 g of dried leaves or powdered leaves. In the United States kratom qualifies as a dietary ingredient under section 201(ff)(1) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (the Act) [21 U.S.C. 321(ff) (1)]. In 2014, FDA issued Import Alert 54-15 which clarified that when marketed as a dietary ingredient, FDA considers kratom to be a new dietary ingredient under section 413(d) of the Act [21 U.S.C. 350b(d)] for which notification must be made prior to market entry. FDA updated this Import alert in 2021 and stated kratom “appears to be a dietary supplement or contains a dietary ingredient that is a new dietary ingredient for which there is inadequate information to provide reasonable assurance that such ingredient does not present a significant or unreasonable risk of illness or injury.” Despite the FDA import alert, according to Indonesia’s Central Statistics Agency (BPS), the United States imported 4,694 tons of kratom from Indonesia in 2023 (BPS-Statistics Indonesia, 2024, n.d.).

Developing kratom through tissue culture and greenhouse cultivation could establish a reliable domestic supply chain with improved genetic consistency, contaminant control, and year-round production capacity. In vitro mass propagation methods historically use a completely randomized block design that requires testing of plant growth regulators alone and in combination across a range of concentrations (Niedz and Evens 2016a). Each variable is tested individually termed a ‘one factor at a time’ (OFAT) approach (Weissman and Anderson 2015; Niedz and Evens 2016a). Using this approach, Wungsintaweekul et al., 2012 reported establishment of seedling cultures of kratom in vitro. Hairy root cultures of kratom have also been described (Phongprueksapattana et al., 2008), but none of the methods have described de novo regeneration. A different approach was required to optimize media for mass plant propagation in the species. A Design of Experiments (DOE) methodology uses fractional factorial designs, a machine learning and multivariate statistical analysis approach to predict the interactions between multiple variables in a single experiment (Mandenius and Brundin 2008; Cao et al. 2018). DOE experiments are emerging as a powerful tool for optimizing culture media, explants and growth conditions for plant regeneration but have not previously been attempted with kratom.

The need for production of medicinal bioactives and metabolites, used as precursors and lead compounds for pharmaceuticals, drives the demand for mass propagation and bioreactor production systems for kratom (Valluri 2009; Nguyen et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2020). While de novo production of mitragynine and speciogynine in Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been achieved using genetic engineering, there is still considerable demand for kratom leaf material for use in traditional preparations (Holtz et al. 2024). Kratom produces monoterpene indole alkaloids (MIAs), including the psychoactive compound mitragynine (Houghton et al. 1991; Mudge and Brown 2017). Mitragynine (Figure 3(A)) is derived from tryptamine, a derivative of the amino acid tryptophan, and the monoterpene secologanin to form the intermediate strictosidine, which eventually gets transformed into mitragynine (Charoonratana et al. 2013; Kim et al. 2023; Schotte et al. 2023; Garza-Garcia and Qu 2024; Holtz et al. 2024). Tryptamine is also the precursor for several plant growth regulators, such as the classical phytohormone auxin (indole-3-acetic acid) and the indoleamine neurohormones melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) and its biosynthetic precursor serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine). Serotonin undergoes covalent modifications to form a number of conjugates such as N-coumaroyl serotonin, and N-feruloyl serotonin, as well as melatonin (Figure 3(B)) through the intermediate N-acetyl serotonin (NAS) (Murch et al. 2000; Ramakrishna et al. 2011; Erland and Saxena 2019). These tryptamine metabolites play an essential role in the regulation of plant growth and stress responses (Yu et al. 2018; Forsyth et al. 2020; Giebelhaus et al. 2023), mediating physiological processes such as morphogenesis, reproductive development, and responses to abiotic and biotic stress (Arnao and Hernández-Ruiz 2006; Erland et al. 2016; Forsyth et al. 2020).

Our overall objective was to develop an in vitro propagation system for shoot induction in kratom suitable for production of plant tissues for studies of medicinal bioactives including MIAs and indoleamines. We achieved this by (1) using a DOE approach to optimize media composition for de novo regeneration and in vitro tissue culture growth, (2) developing and validating a robust method for quantification of mitragynine, (3) assessing in vitro-grown kratom as a novel and sustainable source of mitragynine, (4) using untargeted metabolomics and hormonomics tools to discover the impacts of in vitro growth systems on the phytochemistry of kratom and (5) determine if kratom produces the ancient phytohormone melatonin. Successful development of an in vitro shoot induction system requires verification that cultured tissues retain both relevant bioactive metabolites and a similar metabolic profile. Tissue culture conditions can alter primary and secondary metabolism through changes in nutrient availability, exogenous plant growth regulators, and environmental conditions (Jones and Saxena 2013; Manam et al. 2026). Therefore, targeted quantification of mitragynine was deployed to ensure the production of mitragynine was not diminished in vitro. Untargeted metabolomic profiling was used to determine whether tissue culture substantially altered the metabolome relative to greenhouse-grown plants. We were also interested in determining if melatonin is produced in kratom. Melatonin is an ancient phytohormone found in many medicinal plants (Arnao and Hernández-Ruiz 2019; Erland et al. 2019) responsible for mediating physiological responses, however it is also an important neuro hormone in humans (Erland and Saxena 2017; Murch and Erland 2021). These methods establish in vitro-grown kratom as a reliable source of mitragynine and melatonin for phytopharmaceuticals.

Materials and methods

Culture establishment

Nodal cuttings (∼ 5 cm) were taken from greenhouse-grown kratom “Pink Rifat” plants (maintained in the University of British Columbia plant growth facility (Kelowna, British Columbia, Canada)) and collected into 40 mL of water containing 10 mL of 1% zinc pyrithione shampoo (Head and Shoulders™, Procter & Gamble, Cincinnati, OH, USA) and immediately transported to the laboratory. Cuttings were surface sterilized in a solution of 20% commercial bleach (7.4% sodium hypochlorite; Clorox) and 3 mL/L Head & Shoulders™ in water for 5 min, then rinsed twice with sterile distilled water. Preliminary experiments tested several antibiotic treatments for control of bacteria and fungi on the tissues. Microbial growth was controlled with rifampicin (13 µg/mL, Millipore Sigma, Oakville, ON) added to the media prior to autoclaving and plant preservative mixture (PPM, Plant Cell Technology, Washington, DC, USA) at 10 µL per Petri dish pipetted over the top of the solidified media. When necessary to control microbial growth, individual cultures with infections were dipped in 70% ethanol followed by 20% bleach for 15 min and rinsed three times with sterile distilled water before being placed in clean medium.

All media were prepared with 0.25% phytagel (Sigma, Mississauga, ON) and 8 mg/L of zinc pyrithione (Sigma) to mitigate fungal growth. pH was adjusted to 5.75 using 1 N HCl and NaOH and media were autoclaved (Steris, Mentor, OH, USA) at 121 °C and 18 psi for 20 min. 10 mL of media was poured across 30 mL glass test tubes (22 × 120 mm). Cultures were incubated in a controlled-environment growth room at 27 °C with a 16-hour photoperiod (SunblasterTM full spectrum daylight fluorescent bulbs; 40 μmol/m2/s).

Botanical vouchers of kratom from the plant used in this study have been submitted to Beaty Biodiversity Museum and are pending review. Kratom plants were putatively identified as M. speciosa in the field by Dr. Susan J. Murch (University of British Columbia).

Design of experiments

The DOE approach used Ellistat software v5.30 (Ellistat, Chavanod, France) with a definitive screening design (Jones 3 levels) with 11 runs (Table 1). Three basal salts were compared: Murashige and Skoog medium (MS, PhytoTech Labs, Lenexa, KS, USA; (Murashige and Skoog 1962)), Lloyd and McCown woody plant medium (WPM, PhytoTech Labs; (Lloyd and McCown 1980)), and Cape Sundew/Venus Flytrap Pretransplant Basal Medium (CPM, PhytoTech Labs). Data were collected after 24 days including: the number of new shoots and leaves, number of roots, presence of callus, tissue reddening, and media browning. The number of new roots and shoots were used as response factors in the DOE (N = 1 for all).

Effects of benzylaminopurine (BAP)

To examine the potential to further enhance shoot production, as DOE analyses did not identify an optimal BAP concentration, a full factorial experiment was performed using the media developed for kratom above (1.11 g/L MSO salts, 7.5 g/L sucrose, 8 mg/L zinc pyrithione, 2.5 g/L Phytagel) supplemented with 1, 2, 3, and 4 mg/L 6-BAP. Plantlets with single nodes generated from previous experiments were transferred onto these media and allowed to grow for twenty-four days before observations were collected.

Establishment of clean mother stock

Nodal cuttings were taken from tissue-cultured plants established and maintained on either optimized shooting media or ½ liquid MSO. Optimized shooting media (DOE media) consisting of 1.11 g/L MS salts, 7.5 g/L sucrose, 8 mg/L zinc pyrithione, 2.5 g/L Phytagel, and 4 mg/L BAP, was prepared as described above and poured across Magenta boxes (PhytoTech Labs). The ½ liquid MSO media consists of 2.22 g/L MS salts (PhytoTech Labs) and 30 g/L sucrose, prepared as described above. 2 mL of sterile media was added to sterile glass culture tubes (PhytoTech Labs). All in vitro cultures used in this work were maintained in a controlled-environment growth room at 27 °C with a 16-hour photoperiod (SunblasterTM full-spectrum daylight fluorescent bulbs; 40 μmol/m2/s). Kratom cuttings were allowed to grow for 21 days before new leaves were harvested for phytochemical analysis.

Targeted metabolomics

Quantification of mitragynine

The extraction method was previously reported in Giebelhaus et al. (2022) with minor modifications. In brief, samples of plant tissue were harvested directly into liquid nitrogen (whole leaf) and ∼30 mg of each tissue was accurately weighed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube (VWR). Extraction buffer (80% methanol in 0.5 N trichloroacetic acid; Optima LC/MS grade, Fisher) was added to each sample 3:1 (volume:weight) and the sample was homogenized in the tube using a disposable tissue grinder (Kontes Pellet Pestle; Fisher). Samples were then vortexed (Vortex Genie 2; Scientific Industries, Bohemia, USA) for 30 s. The resulting homogenate was transferred to a 400 µL microcentrifuge filter (0.22 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane; Millipore, Toronto, ON, Canada) and centrifuged (mySPIN 12; Thermo Scientific, Mississauga, ON, Canada) for 5 min at 10,000 RPM. The filtrate was then diluted 1:10 with water and then further diluted with serial dilutions to achieve a dilution factor of 1:500 to obtain a mitragynine concentration within the established linear range of the method. The sample was then aliquoted into an amber glass autosampler vial (Waters) with a 200 µL conical glass insert (Canadian Life Science, Peterborough, ON, Canada) and capped with a preslit Teflon cap. Samples were placed into the autosampler (Acquity I Class Sample Manager, Waters) at 4 °C. 4.0 µL of sample was injected onto an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm × 1.7 µm) heated to 30 °C with a gradient of 0.1% formic acid (Sigma): acetonitrile (Optima LC/MS grade, Fisher Chemical, Mississauga, ON, Canada), (initial, 90:10% (v/v); 0.5–3.5 min, 90:10% (v/v); 3.5–4.2 min, 40:60% (v/v); 4.2–6.5 min, 5:95% (v/v); 6.5-7 min, 5:95% (v/v); 7 min, 90:10% (v/v)) with a curve of 6. The analytes were eluted at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min over 7 min. The needle wash solvent and purge solvent were both 10:90% water: acetonitrile (v/v).

Mitragynine was detected and quantified using electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive ion mode with optimized MRM settings (Table 2). The capillary voltage was 3 kV; desolvation gas rate of 500 L/hr; cone gas at 150 L/hr; desolvation temperature at 400 °C; source temperature at 150 °C; nebulizer pressure 7.0 bar. Quantification was done by comparison to a dilution series of authentic mitragynine standard (Sigma) (0, 0.31, 0.63, 1.25, 2.5, 5.0, 10, 20, 40 ng/mL) in the homogenizing solution.

Quantification of melatonin

Melatonin was extracted, detected, and quantified as previously described in (Erland et al. 2017; Giebelhaus et al. 2023). In brief, samples of greenhouse-grown plant tissue (N = 8) were harvested directly into liquid nitrogen (whole leaf) and ∼150 mg of each tissue was accurately weighed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube (VWR). Samples were homogenized, and then extraction solvent consisting of 50% methanol and 4% acetic acid (glacial, LC-MS Grade, Fisher Scientific, Canada) in water was added to each sample in a 3:1 (volume: weight) ratio. Samples were vortexed for 30 s then spun down (3 min at 13,000 RPM) and the supernatant was removed. Supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 µm centrifuge filter (PVDF, Sigma-Aldrich) and the filtrate was diluted 5 times with water. The sample was then aliquoted into an amber glass autosampler vial (Waters) with a 200 µL conical glass insert (Canadian Life Science, Peterborough, ON, Canada) and capped with a preslit Teflon cap. Samples were placed into the autosampler (Acquity I Class BSM, Waters) at 4 °C.

Separation was performed over a Waters Acquity BEH column (2.1 × 50 mm × 1.7 µm) on an Acquity I-Class BSM UPLC (Waters) with a gradient of 10 mM ammonium acetate pH 9, adjusted with ammonium hydroxide (Eluent A; Millipore Sigma) and 100% MeOH (Eluent B; LC-MS grade, Fisher Chemical) with initial conditions of 95% A and 5% B increased to 5% A and 95% B over 5.5 min using a curve of 8. Column temperature was 40 °C and the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min.

Melatonin was detected and quantified with a tandem mass spectrometer (Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, Waters) in ESI positive mode using optimized settings as follows: capillary voltage at 0.8 kV; desolvation gas rate at 800 L/hr; cone gas at 150 L/hr; desolvation temperature at 500 °C; source temperature at 150 °C; nebulizer pressure 7.0 bar. Melatonin was detected in selected ion recording (SIR) mode by monitoring the 233 m/z ion with a cone voltage of 15 V. Quantification was done by comparison to a dilution series of authentic melatonin standard (Sigma) (0, 1.56, 3.13, 6.25, 12.50, 25.00, 50.00, 100.00 ng/mL) in the extraction buffer. The lowest concentration standard, 1.56 ng/mL, was taken as the LOQ.

Untargeted metabolomics

Sample collection and preparation

The same extraction protocol used for mitragynine was utilized for extraction prior to untargeted analysis, with slight modifications. Sample homogenization occurred in the dark to prevent the degradation of any phytohormones, the filtrate was not diluted, and 5.0 µL was directly injected.

Liquid chromatography – mass spectrometry

Standards and blanks were injected between blocks of samples (Giebelhaus et al. 2022). Separation was performed on an Acquity BEH C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm × 1.7 µm) heated to 30 °C with a gradient of 0.25% formic acid (Sigma): acetonitrile (Optima LC/MS grade, Fisher Chemical), (initial, 95:5% (v/v); 0.0–10.0 min, 95:5-5:95% (v/v); 10.0–15.0 min, 5:95 (v/v); 15.0–20.0 min, 5:95-95:5 (v/v); 20.0–25.0 min, 95:5 (v/v)) with a curve of 6. The analytes were eluted at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min over a 25-minute gradient. The needle wash solvent and purge solvent were both 10:90% water: acetonitrile (v/v).

Analytes were ionized by ESI in positive ion mode and detected by a Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters) using the following optimized settings: Mass Range: 75.0–1100.0 m/z; capillary: 3.5 kV; cone voltage: 30.0 V; Source offset: 60.0 V; Source temperature: 150 °C; desolvation temperature: 400 °C; cone gas flow: 150 L/hr; desolvation gas flow: 550 L/hr; collision gas flow: 0.00 mL/min; nebulizer gas flow: 7.00 Bar.

Data were collected with MassLynx v4.1 and exported to a .csv via MarkerLynx. The following parameters in MarkerLynx were used: marker time was 0.1–25.0 min, marker mass range 75–1100 amu, mass tolerance 0.02 amu, noise elimination level 100.0, smoothing disabled, deisotoping data. Data were processed in ExcelTM to remove features that were common between all blanks and all samples and align retention times as described in (Brown et al. 2012b, 2012a; Turi and Murch 2013).

Multivariate analysis

To visually represent the phytochemical differences between the growth conditions, the untargeted dataset was submitted to MetaboAnalyst (v5.0) and then normalized using total useful peak area (Nam et al. 2020, 2024) scaled using auto scaling (Chong et al. 2019). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed.

Putative identification

Using previously described methods (Turi et al. 2015; Erland et al. 2020; Giebelhaus et al. 2022), putative identification of phytohormones in their native state was performed by searching for the M + H adduct across the untargeted dataset, matching on both m/z and RT. A mass query tolerance of ± 0.2 Da was used. The entire untargeted metabolomics dataset was subjected to putative identification to Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level III (Sumner et al. 2007).

Experimental design, statistical analysis & method performance characteristics

For growth comparisons, young and mature leaves were harvested from greenhouse-cultivated kratom plants. Young leaves were defined as leaves from the top node of the plant and were approximately <10 cm in length, old leaves were harvested 3 or more nodes from the top and were approximately >10 cm in length.

Statistical analysis of the tissue culture data was processed using Ellistat v5.30. The percent contribution of variance for each factor was determined by using an analysis of variance (ANOVA). The optimal parameters for the media were also determined by using the Analyze Y function in the Ellistat Software. The number of new shoots produced on each media was used as the dependent variable in the optimization analysis. LC-MS/MS data was acquired and processed in MassLynx v4.1 data analysis system (Waters). Mitragynine levels between tissues (n = 3) were compared using a single factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) in Microsoft ExcelTM with α = 0.05.

The limit of detection was taken as the concentration of the standard with a signal to noise ratio > 3, and the limit of quantification was taken as the concentration of the standard where the signal to noise ratio was > 10. Calibration curves were visually evaluated and the R2 was calculated through linear regression to measure the linearity of the methods. For quantification, R2 values of 0.99 or higher were considered acceptable. Inter-day retention time (RT) shift was determined by calculating the percent relative standard deviation of the RT for all standards in the linear range injected over three days.

Percent recovery was used to determine analytical accuracy. A matrix of St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum (wild type)) was spiked with a known concentration of standard at two levels, 2.5 and 10 ng/mL of mitragynine following extraction. H. perforatum was selected as the matrix since it does not produce mitragynine. The unspiked matrix was screened for its concentration of the analytes of interest prior to spike recovery analysis.

Melatonin quantification

All eight of the kratom leaf tissues assayed had detectable levels of melatonin (S/N > 3). Four of the samples had quantifiable levels of melatonin, with the highest reported level at 66.60 ng/g of fresh weight. The four tissues had an average concentration of 24.42 ± 12.40 ng/g.

Untargeted metabolomics

Metabolomics data and statistical analysis

Discussion

Biotechnological approaches, such as micropropagation and tissue culture, allow for the production of large volumes of tissue from minimal starting material making it a potential tool for conservation of native and/or recalcitrant species (Fay 1992; Sudhersan et al. 2003). However, development and optimization of tissue culture protocols often requires large amounts of starting materials for optimization, which presents a significant challenge for researchers working with rare or endangered species. The increasing use of kratom as a medicinal plant, sold as kratom, for recreational use, has led to increasing interest in the large-scale production and engineering of bioactive compounds produced by the plant (Brown et al. 2017). Since kratom is an evergreen tropical tree, having an in vitro protocol to propagate kratom allows for biomass generation for production of bioactive compounds and easier study and manipulation of the bioactive compounds produced by the plant. As understanding of mitragynine biosynthesis improves, in vitro culture systems capable of producing bioactive compounds provides platforms for investigating pathway regulation, metabolic engineering, and understanding differences between chemotypes or genotypes (Flores-Bocanegra et al. 2020; Garza-Garcia and Qu 2024).

Melatonin is a hormone that serves to control circadian rhythms in humans, and has been found to be broadly present in plants where it plays a dual function in stress defense and morphogenesis. In plants, melatonin is derived from tryptamine, rather than 5-hydroxytryptophan, leading to the possibility of interactions between mitragynine and melatonin (Erland and Saxena 2017; Murch et al. 2000; Murch and Erland 2021; Giebelhaus et al. 2023). We therefore wanted to explore whether melatonin was present in kratom, with potential downstream implications for both plant physiology, and human biology e.g., in contribution to biological activity of kratom. Here we present an in vitro protocol for propagation and shoot induction of kratom, guided by DOE. We also present the first report of melatonin in kratom tissues.

The DOE approach enabled efficient optimization of the kratom shoot induction, while minimizing the amount of starting material required. Using this experimental design optimization was achieved in only 11 runs, compared with the 243 runs that would have been required using a full factorial OFAT design. We explored 5 variables: salt type, salt concentration, sugar concentration, BAP concentration, and NAA concentration. Our results found that the most variability came from the concentration of BAP. This was expected as BAP is a synthetic cytokinin which is used to induce shoot formation in many plant species (Skoog and Miller 1957; Sim et al. 1989; Malik and Saxena 1992). BAP has also been reported to be effective in inducing shoot growth in other species of Mitragyna. Roy et al. (1988) found that Mitragyna parvifolia exhibited the best shoot growth on MS medium with 4.44 µM BAP (Roy et al. 1988). This medium is very similar to the formulation that our DOE determined to be optimal, which consisted of quarter strength MS media with 1 mg/L BAP (4.44 µM). As rooting was only observed in one media, the statistics applied to the DOE data from the shooting could not be applied, therefore further experimentation will be needed to determine an ideal formulation to induce rooting in kratom.

In addition to their potential as a source of plantlets for commercial cultivation, in vitro-grown tissues may provide a platform for production and study of mitragynine. We found that in vitro-grown plants produced levels of mitragynine that did not significantly differ from greenhouse-grown plants. The quantified mitragynine concentrations were within the range reported in other studies that performed similar alkaloid analyses (Mudge and Brown 2017; Fowble and Musah 2019). Kratom is known to produce multiple MIAs, given this, it may be a good model species to study MIA metabolism (Brose et al. 2021; Schotte et al. 2023). A similar DOE approach to the one outlined herein could be employed using the concentration of mitragynine as a response factor, thereby allowing for the effects of media composition on mitragynine production to be studied.

Although an independent validation experiment using a new set of explants was not performed, due to limited plant material, the DOE-selected medium was used to establish and maintain the in vitro culture used throughout the chemical analyses. The successful propagation and maintenance of these cultures shows that the DOE-selected medium supports sustained shoot growth under the study conditions. Nonetheless, future work should include replicated validation using independent biological material sourced from different populations to quantitatively confirm that the DOE medium is optimal for shoot generation.

Untargeted LC-MS metabolomics was applied here largely for comparative profiling (Figure 6), as our MS system is low resolution. Along PC1 in Figure 6, we see separation of greenhouse-grown tissues from in vitro cultures. This is unexpected as culturing plants on media will alter their metabolism, specifically by providing an alternative carbon source through sugar supplementation. PC2 discriminates ½ MSO tissues from the DOE tissues, suggesting the different media compositions differentially alter metabolism in kratom. Additionally, secologanin, strictosidine, and mitragynine were all putatively identified in the untargeted dataset (MSI Level III, (Sumner et al. 2007)). The terpenoid, secologanin, is believed to be a precursor in mitragynine biosynthesis in kratom (Charoonratana et al. 2013). The relative abundances of mitragynine in DOE and greenhouse-grown kratom are similar, which supports our evidence that in vitro grown kratom is a good source of mitragynine. The hypothesized precursors, secologanin, and strictosidine were both identified, however, the hypothesized amine source, tryptamine, was not putatively identified in any of the tissues. 7-hydroxy mitragynine was not putatively identified in any samples. Future work should aim to quantify other alkaloids in in vitro-grown kratom tissue, such as speciociliatine, paynantheine, speciogynine, and corynantheidine to examine how the DOE medium impacts the production of minor MIAs in kratom.

We detected and quantified melatonin in greenhouse-grown kratom tissues. Melatonin is related to mitragynine as they both share an indolamine backbone, derived from tryptophan (Murch et al. 2000; Charoonratana et al. 2013). Melatonin has been detected and quantified in other medicinal plants including St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum L.) and Scutellaria spp. (Murch et al. 2000; Forsyth et al. 2020). Provided that melatonin was detected in kratom, it may play an important role in the psychoactive activity of kratom products (Erland and Saxena 2017). Given their shared precursor, tryptamine, there is a possibility for crosstalk between the MIA and indoleamine pathways. The in vitro propagation protocol and analytical methods employed in this study could be used to further investigate the relationship between these two metabolites. Further studies should also explore whether melatonin is present in dried kratom leaf and explore if different postharvest treatments impact melatonin levels.

Here we describe an in vitro shoot propagation method for shoot induction in M. speciosa and demonstrate that under the conditions examined in this study, in vitro propagated M. speciosa produced levels of mitragynine and metabolite profiles that were broadly comparable to greenhouse-grown tissues. Further work is still needed to determine an optimized method for root production as only one of the plants in this study rooted in vitro. The DOE methodology demonstrated in this work can be utilized in cases where there is a limited supply of plant tissue, such as following field collections or during conservation efforts. This study is also the first reported instance of melatonin being detected in M. speciosa. Production of mitragynine by in vitro-grown plants provides an opportunity for both commercial production as well as a scientific model for study of MIA metabolism. This work has implications for the further study of mitragynine biosynthesis and the relationship between the mitragynine and melatonin biosynthetic pathways.
