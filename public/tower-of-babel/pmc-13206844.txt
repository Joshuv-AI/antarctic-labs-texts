YTHDF1-Mediated m6A Modification of NREP Promotes Corneal Fibrosis via TGF-β–Smad Signaling
Authors: Yi Guan, Yapeng Jing, Shumei Yang, Xinlin Yan, Xuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCorneal fibrosis ranks among the foremost drivers of global vision loss and blindness, yet therapeutic options remain scarce. This study investigated neuronal regeneration-related protein (NREP), which is recognized by the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification reader protein YTH domain family member 1 (YTHDF1), in promoting corneal fibrosis in keratocytes.MethodsIn vivo, a standardized alkali burn model was created in C57BL/6 mice with corneas harvested on days 7, 14, and 21. Fibrosis, NREP, and m6A modifications were estimated using slit-lamp, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and western blotting. In vitro, isolated keratocytes were treated with TGF-β1. Small interfering RNA (si-RNA) was used to downregulate NREP and YTHDF1 expression in keratocytes. SKLB-Y13 was used to specifically inhibit YTHDF1. Cell proliferation, migration, and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression were assessed using CCK-8 assays, scratch-wound assays, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. Following keratocyte fibrosis induction using 10% fetal bovine serum, the indicators were evaluated.ResultsCorneal fibrosis peaked on day 14 post-injury, coinciding with increased NREP protein levels versus controls. NREP knockdown reduced keratocyte proliferation and migration and abolished TGF-β1–induced α-SMA expression. YTHDF1 depletion phenocopied these effects and lowered NREP protein levels, suggesting translational control. Sequence-based RNA adenosine methylation site predictor (SRAMP) revealed enriched m6A modifications in NREP mRNA. YTHDF1 deficiency reduced NREP translation and impaired TGF-β–Smad signaling, leading to decreased keratocyte proliferation and motility and impaired fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation.ConclusionsYTHDF1 promotes corneal fibrosis through m6A-dependent enhancement of NREP translation, potentiating TGF-β–Smad and myofibroblast transdifferentiation, and thus may serve as a therapeutic target for fibrotic corneal disorders.

Corneal fibrosis is recognized as the second most prevalent cause of corneal impairment worldwide, affecting over 19 million individuals.1 The pathogenesis of corneal fibrosis is complex and involves the interplay of multiple signaling pathways and cellular processes.2 Human keratocytes play an essential role in maintaining the stromal extracellular matrix (ECM), which is critical for corneal transparency.3 Upon corneal injury, various cytokines such as IL-1 and TGF-β are released, prompting quiescent keratocytes to undergo activation and differentiate into proliferative fibroblasts. A subset of these fibroblasts further differentiates into myofibroblasts, characterized by the de novo expression of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and contractile properties. Although this phenotypic conversion is essential for wound contraction and initial repair, the persistent activation and accumulation of myofibroblasts are central to the pathogenesis of corneal fibrosis, driving excessive ECM deposition and scar formation.4 This sustained myofibroblast activity leads to progressive corneal opacity and scarring, hallmark features of fibrotic disease.5–9 Keratocytes are distinct from fibroblasts in several ways. They vary in their appearance, gene expression, transparency, capacity to modify the surrounding matrix, and functions related to nerves.10 It is important to know how keratocytes change into fibroblasts to understand eye diseases and how wounds heal. This knowledge is valuable for using cells in therapy and building tissues.

Although antifibrotic medications have demonstrated limited effectiveness in clinical studies,11,12 corneal transplantation is the best way to help people see again if they have serious corneal problems.13,14 Nonetheless, several things make long-term success difficult. These include short graft life, rejection of donor grafts, the need for drugs to suppress the immune system, high costs, and a global lack of donor material.15 Consequently, there is a need for more efficient strategies to either prevent or enhance the healing of excessive corneal fibrosis.

Neuronal regeneration-related protein (NREP or P311), is a protein that helps with nerve cell repair. NREP, found on human chromosome 5 and mouse chromosome 18, binds to and stabilizes mRNAs encoding proteins involved in cytoskeletal remodeling and repair signaling, such as eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha 1 (eEF1A1)/eEF1A2 and TGF-β isoforms, to enhance cell motility and tissue regeneration. It possesses multiple functions and exhibits similar characteristics across diverse species. It encodes an intracellular protein with a molecular weight of 8 kDa, which was first identified in the brain tissue of late embryonic mice in 1993.16 NREP is involved in the regeneration of nerves and lungs,17,18 glial histiocytoma invasion,19 blood pressure homeostasis,20 myofibroblast differentiation, and amoeboid migration.21 These tissue-specific functional differences suggest that they may be epigenetically regulated by the microenvironment. NREP facilitates the incorporation of these mRNAs into the translational machinery to enhance their translation.22 NREP is the first regulatory molecule identified to influence all three types of TGF-β and is the sole protein known to enhance TGF-β translation.20 Nonetheless, the function of NREP in corneal fibrosis remains poorly understood.

Recent findings suggest that RNA modifications can serve as potent mechanisms for the dynamic control of gene expression.23 N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is one of the most prevalent post-transcriptional RNA modifications.24 Similar to DNA methylation, the m6A modification is reversible, a process facilitated by a unequivocal enzymes group. The m6A modification itself is carried out by a stable core catalytic complex.24,25 The enzymes fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) and AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) are responsible for reversing methylation.26,27 Consequently, m6A nucleotides serve as “readable markers” for mRNA molecules.28 So far, three main “reader” proteins have been found: YTH domain family member 1 (YTHDF1), YTHDF2, and YTHDF3. These proteins recognize m6A nucleotides using their YTH structural domains.29 YTHDF1 facilitates the initiation of protein synthesis from m6A-modified transcripts,30,31 YTHDF2 helps break down transcripts through bringing in the carbon catabolite repression 4 (CCR4)–negative on TATA-less (NOT) complex,32 and YTHDF3 primarily functions to cooperatively regulate the translation and degradation of methylated transcripts.33 Despite the clinical relevance of m6A modification and its fibrogenic effects, they have not been studied in detail. YTHDF1 is a key part of the m6A reader proteins. It helps target mRNAs more effectively by recognizing the GGACU sequence.34 YTHDF1 enhances the translation of Yin Yang 1 (YY1) and mouse double minute 2 (MDM2), leading to a transformed cellular phenotype.35 In liver fibrosis, YTHDF1 promotes stellate cell activation by regulating lysyl oxidase-like 2 (LOXL2) mRNA expression. However, its function in hypoxic microenvironment of avascular tissues, such as the cornea, has not been explored. Bioinformatics analysis predicted m6A modification sites in NREP mRNA. We think that YTHDF1 may increase NREP levels, which could play a role in corneal fibrosis.

We investigated the potential role of NREP in the pathogenesis of corneal fibrosis. Specifically, this study sought to elucidate how NREP, regulated by YTHDF1, promotes fibrotic progression in keratocytes. Elucidating the function of NREP in this context may offer novel insights into the mechanisms driving corneal fibrosis.

Methods

Alkali-Induced Corneal Burn

The research involved male C57BL/6 mice 6 to 8 weeks old, obtained from Weitonghua Company (Tianjin, China). All animal procedures complied with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The Nankai Center's Animal Management Organization in Tianjin approved the experiments (approval no. NKYY-DWLL-2023-051). The mice were anesthetized using 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 40 to 50 mg/kg. Proparacaine eye drops were administered to anesthetize the ocular surface. To induce corneal injury, a 2-mm-diameter filter paper soaked in 1-N NaOH was applied to the mouse cornea for 20 seconds, then removed. The eyes were then rinsed with saline solution (∼25 mL). After the injury, tobramycin eye ointment was applied. The ocular surface was examined using a specialized microscope on days 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21 post-injury, with photographs and observations recorded. Mice were euthanized on days 7, 14, and 21 after injury, and their corneas were collected for further analysis. Uninjured eyes served as negative controls.

Evaluation of Corneal Opacity

Following anesthesia, corneal opacity induced by alkali burns in mice was assessed using a slit-lamp microscope according to standard protocols. A standardized grading scale from 0 to 4 was employed, where 0 corresponded to a completely clear cornea, 1 indicated slight haze with clearly visible iris and pupil structures, 2 represented mild opacity with discernible iris and pupil details, 3 denoted significant opacity with only barely visible pupils, and 4 represented complete corneal opacification with no visible pupil. To reduce subjectivity, three independent investigators evaluated each cornea, and the final opacity score was determined by averaging their individual assessments.

Cell Culture and Treatment

Human keratocytes were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (6520; Carlsbad, CA, USA). We used fibroblast medium (2301; ScienCell Research Laboratories) for culturing keratocytes in vitro. The cells were maintained at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Keratocytes between passages 3 and 5 were utilized for all experiments. Keratocytes were treated with TGF-β1 at a concentration of 10 ng/mL. Keratocytes were stimulated with 2.5-µm SKLB-Y13 (HY-176848; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), a selective YTHDF1 inhibitor, for 24 hours.36

Transfection of Cells

To knock down NREP and YTHDF1, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting these genes were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). This collection was comprised of four different siRNAs for each gene (NREP siRNA1–4 and YTHDF1 siRNA1–4), as well as negative control (si-NC) and positive control (si-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase [GAPDH]) siRNAs. Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (L3000015; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Real-Time Quantitative Reverse-Transcription PCR

The corneas were cut out at the limbus, and any tissue attached to the corneal endothelium was carefully removed. Total RNA was isolated using a commercial RNA purification kit (DP430; Tiangen Biotech, Tianjin, China). The RNA concentration and purity were measured using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with acceptable quality defined by OD260/OD280 optical density ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 and OD260/OD230 ratios above 2.0. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized through reverse transcription using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (M1701; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Real-time quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A; Takara Bio, Beijing, China). All primer sequences are provided in Table 2. Gene expression levels were normalized against GAPDH.

Western Blotting

Following lysis in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) with protease inhibitors, corneal tissue and keratocyte samples were prepared for protein analysis. We quantified total protein using a Lowry Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Samples containing 30 µg of protein each were loaded onto 6% to 15% gels and electrophoresed using Tris/glycine/sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) buffer. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) using a Tris/glycine/methanol buffer. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk before being incubated with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used (all obtained from Abcam, Cambridge, UK) included rabbit anti-α-SMA (1:10,000; ab124964), anti-COL3A1 (1:1000; ab7778), anti-Smad2/3 (1:1000; ab40855), anti-p-Smad2/3 (1:1000; ab280888), anti-β actin (1:1000; ab179467), anti-fibronectin (1:1000; ab268020), anti-YTHDF1 (1:1000; ab252346), anti-TGF-β1 (1:1000; ab215715), and anti-GAPDH (1:1000; ab128915). An additional antibody against NREP (rabbit anti-NREP; 1:1000; TA367517S; OriGene Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA) was also used. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies while being gently shaken at 4°C for 12 hours. Following this, they were treated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (diluted 1:10,000; ab205718; Abcam) for 1 hour at 37°C. Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China) and analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The grayscale values of target proteins were normalized to those of GAPDH, β-actin, or Smad2/3.

Immunofluorescent Staining

For immunohistochemical analysis, cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 30 min. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting either NREP (rabbit anti-NREP, 1:100; Abcam) or α-SMA (rabbit anti-α-SMA, 1:1000; ab124964; Abcam). The next day, the slides were treated for 1 hour with an Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat anti-rabbit Fab fragment secondary antibody (1:200; 111-547-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) for 5 minutes. Finally, images were captured using a Nikon C1 laser scanning confocal microscope with EZ-C1 software (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry

Protein concentrations were determined using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Aliquots containing 150 µg of protein were prepared and diluted to a final volume of 100 µL with 50-mM ammonium bicarbonate (A6141; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Disulfide bonds were reduced with 2 µL of 200-mM dithiothreitol (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) at 37°C for 2.5 hours, followed by alkylation with 12 µL of 500-mM iodoacetamide (Aladdin) in the dark for 40 minutes. The alkylation reaction was quenched by adding 1 µL of 500-mM dithiothreitol. The mixture was then combined with 400 µL of 8-M urea (Aladdin) and transferred to a 10-kDa ultrafiltration tube for centrifugation. Sequential washes were performed with 200 µL of 8-M urea buffer (three times) and 200 µL of 50-mM ammonium bicarbonate (twice), with centrifugation at 12,000g for 20 minutes each. The retained proteins were resuspended in 100 µL of 50-mM ammonium bicarbonate and digested with trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:100 (w/w) for 16 to 18 hours at 37°C. Digestion was terminated by acidification with trifluoroacetic acid (Aladdin) to a final concentration of 0.4% (v/v). The resulting peptide samples were stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Peptide mixtures were analyzed using an EASY-nLC 1200 nano-liquid chromatography system coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap mass spectrometer via a nanoelectrospray ion source (all Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were acquired in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. Raw DIA data were processed using Spectronaut 18 (Biognosys, Schlieren, Switzerland). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed using R 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Apoptosis Assay by Flow Cytometry

The culture medium was transferred into a centrifuge tube, and the cells were washed with ice-cold PBS (4°C) before being collected again. The cells were then detached using 500 µL of trypsin without ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and collected by centrifuging at 1000 rpm for 3 minutes. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of Annexin V binding buffer. Next, 5 µL each of Annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and DAPI were added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 minutes. Blank controls and single-stained controls were prepared for comparison purposes. Before analysis, 400 µL of Annexin V binding buffer was added to each sample and gently mixed. Fluorescence analysis was performed using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) with a 488-nm laser. FITC fluorescence was detected at 530/30 nm (FL1 channel), and DAPI fluorescence was measured at 610/20 nm (FL3 channel). Data were analyzed with FlowJo software to determine the proportions of viable, early apoptotic, and late apoptotic or dead cells. Results are expressed as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

Prediction of m6A Sites

We used the sequence-based RNA adenosine methylation site predictor (SRAMP) to identify potential m6A modification sites on NREP mRNA. Using partial mRNA fragment sequences from NREP as input, the tool identified putative m6A sites along with their corresponding confidence scores.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell growth was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) after keratocytes were transfected with either NREP/YTHDF1 siRNA or a si-NC control. The cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 to 2 × 103 cells per well. Cell viability was measured at 0, 12, and 48 hours post-transfection. Then, the culture medium was replaced with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) containing 10% CCK-8 reagent, and the plates were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere before absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell Migration Assay Test

Keratocytes were plated at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well of the six-well plate. When the cells reached approximately 100% confluence, two perpendicular scratches were made in the cell layer using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip to create uniform areas for analysis. After the original medium was removed, the cells were gently rinsed three times with PBS before being cultured in serum-free medium. The wound width at the reference intersection was immediately recorded under a microscope, and cell migration was later assessed by measuring the change in wound area using ImageJ.

Statistical Analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± SD based on a minimum of three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were conducted with Prism 9 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Group means were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with differences deemed statistically significant for P < 0.05.

Results

Corneal Fibrosis After Alkali Injury

To assess the pathological changes caused by alkali burns, we analyzed the mouse corneas with a slit-lamp microscope and noted the formation of corneal opacity after the injury. Corneal fluorescein staining revealed a substantial epithelial defect immediately post-injury (day 0), which was largely resolved by day 7 (Fig. 1A). Following alkali burns, the corneal opacity score increased significantly at day 0, peaked at day 14, and showed a slight decrease by day 21 (Fig. 1B). To understand the molecular processes involved in corneal fibrosis post-alkali burns, we examined the level of crucial markers linked to myofibroblast transdifferentiation including collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1), α-SMA, and fibronectin (FN1). We examined the corneas on days 7, 14, and 21 following alkali burns. Over time, the levels of key markers linked to fibroblast activation significantly rose after alkali injury (Figs. 1C–E). Measurements of both protein and mRNA, assessed through western blot and RT-qPCR analyses, consistently showed that the expression of α-SMA, fibronectin, and COL3A1 gradually increased following the injury, reached their highest point on day 14, and then declined (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that the fibrotic response in mouse corneas following alkali injury is most pronounced between days 14 and 21, peaking on day 14.

NREP Upregulation Is Associated With Corneal Fibrosis

To examine changes in NREP expression during corneal fibrosis, we measured its levels in total corneal RNA at 7, 14, and 21 days after alkali injury. The RT-qPCR findings indicated a significant rise in Nrep levels starting on day 14 post-alkali burns when compared to undamaged corneas, with the highest level observed on day 14 (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis revealed that NREP levels were significantly upregulated following alkali burn. Although no significant increase was observed at day 7 compared to the normal group, expression peaked markedly at day 14 and remained significantly elevated at day 21 (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that NREP expression is elevated during corneal fibrosis.

Deficiencies in NREP Result in Diminished Proliferation and Motility of Keratocytes

The transformation of corneal stromal fibroblasts into myofibroblasts is a key indicator of corneal fibrosis. This research examined how the absence of NREP affects fibroblast growth and movement. We created siRNAs that were designed to specifically target NREP. Among these siRNAs, the NREP siRNA MIX, which had the highest silencing efficiency, was selected for further analysis (Fig. 3A). We assessed how reducing NREP levels impacts keratocyte proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. Cell viability was evaluated by measuring absorbance at 450 nm (Fig. 3B). The CCK-8 assay showed that knockdown of NREP significantly reduced cell viability compared to the siRNA control group at 48 and 72 hours. Similarly, the cell scratch assay (Fig. 3C) showed that fibroblasts with NREP knockdown displayed reduced migration relative to the control cells. Collectively, these findings indicate that a deficiency in NREP leads to reduced fibroblast proliferation and migration.

The apoptosis assay performed using flow cytometry (Fig. 3D) showed that cell apoptosis increased after NREP was knocked down, indicating that NREP plays a role in promoting cell proliferation. Proteomic analysis of keratocytes with silenced NREP demonstrated a widespread disruption in fibrogenic networks, affecting signaling, metabolic, and trafficking pathways (Figs. 3E, 3F). KEGG analysis indicated suppression of TGF-β–associated pathways, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Ras, as well as energy metabolism, specifically oxidative phosphorylation (P < 0.001).37 This was accompanied by defects in the mitochondrial outer membrane components, a phenotype reminiscent of the YTHDF1 knockdown models of hepatic fibrosis.38,39 GO enrichment further identified impairments in intracellular transport, particularly endoplasmic reticulum-to-Golgi vesicle trafficking (P < 0.001) and ribosomal biogenesis, notably rRNA processing (P < 0.001), which are processes typically regulated by m6A-mediated translation.40,41

Loss of NREP Abrogates the Process of Fibroblast-to-Myofibroblast Differentiation

Based on the significant increase in TGF-β1 levels in the mouse cornea after alkali injury, we further investigated the changes in NREP under the influence of TGF-β1. After 24 hours of stimulation with TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL), keratocytes exhibited a marked increase in the expression of fibrosis-related marker genes. The results showed that knocking down NREP reduced the mRNA levels of α-SMA, FN1, and COL3A1 in keratocytes stimulated with TGF-β1 (Fig. 4A). α-SMA serves as an indicator of myofibroblasts. As demonstrated by cellular immunofluorescence findings (Fig. 4B), fibroblasts with NREP knockdown exhibited a notably diminished ability to differentiate into myofibroblasts compared to fibroblasts stimulated with TGF-β1, thereby attenuating fibrotic progression. The observed protein expression patterns were consistent with the corresponding mRNA levels (Fig. 4C). These results show that reducing NREP lowered the transformation of corneal fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. These findings further suggest that NREP contributes significantly to corneal fibrosis, potentially through mechanisms involving TGF-β–related signaling pathways.

Silencing of YTHDF1 in Keratocytes Results in Reduced NREP Translation and YTHDF1 Is Positively Correlated With NREP in Corneal Fibrosis

We employed SRAMP to analyze m6A sites within the NREP gene sequence. Results indicate that NREP RNA (NM_001142474.2) harbors 19 possible m6A sites, with four of these being identified as high-confidence sites (Fig. 5A). YTHDF1 initiates protein synthesis by attaching m6A-marked mRNAs to ribosomes.35 This finding suggests that NREP is a plausible substrate for YTHDF1. Considering the role of YTHDF1 in enhancing the translation of profibrotic factors, the data indicate that NREP functions as a critical m6A-regulated effector, linking epitranscriptomic regulation to stromal remodeling.42,43 To investigate the functional interaction between YTHDF1 and NREP, four distinct siRNAs targeting YTHDF1 were designed. YTHDF1 expression was significantly reduced in cells treated with si-YTHDF1 (1–4) relative to the si-NC group. Among these, si-YTHDF1 demonstrated the greatest knockdown efficiency, so all subsequent experiments utilized si-YTHDF1 (Fig. 5B). At the same time, validation was performed at the protein level using western blot, and it was found that the knockdown efficiency was approximately 69% (Fig. 5C). After the downregulation of YTHDF1 expression, no significant changes in NREP RNA expression were observed (Fig. 5D). Western blot revealed that NREP protein levels were markedly decreased after YTHDF1 was knocked down. This result indicated that YTHDF1 mainly affected the protein translation process of NREP, with little impact on its transcription (Fig. 5E). To understand how YTHDF1 affects keratocytes, we looked at its role in cell death and growth under normal conditions. The flow cytometry apoptosis tests (Fig. 5F) showed that reducing YTHDF1 led to more cell death. This increase was greater than the increase seen when NREP was reduced.

YTHDF1 plays a greater role in promoting cell growth than NREP. It also acts as an upstream regulator of NREP. After silencing of YTHDF1, the proliferation of keratocytes was significantly decreased (Fig. 5G). To investigate the possible involvement of YTHDF1 in the healing process of damaged corneal keratocytes, we measured its expression levels at 7, 14, and 21 days after injury. The results showed YTHDF1 was elevated on days 14 and 21 after trauma, with the most significant increase observed on day 14. The changes in YTHDF1 expression were not statistically significant on day 21 (Fig. 5H). Based on the above studies, we hypothesized that the inhibition of YTHDF1 might inhibit corneal fibrosis by suppressing NREP translation. Thus, we examined how knocking down YTHDF1 influences NREP when stimulated with TGF-β1. To better understand the specific function of YTHDF1 in corneal fibrosis, cells were exposed to 10-ng/mL TGF-β1 for 24 hours after YTHDF1 was silenced. The findings revealed that reducing YTHDF1 expression caused a notable decrease in the mRNA levels of the fibrosis markers α-SMA and NREP (Figs. 5I, 5J). Similarly, we validated these findings at the protein level, and the results were consistent with the mRNA data. After stimulation with TGF-β1, silencing YTHDF1 significantly reduced the expression levels of fibronectin, COL3A1, and α-SMA (Fig. 5K). Meanwhile, following selective inhibition of YTHDF1 with SKLB-Y13 for 24 hours, the protein levels of α-SMA and NREP were decreased upon TGF-β1 stimulation (Fig. 5L). Our research revealed that YTHDF1 knockdown decreased the proliferative ability of keratocytes following TGF-β1 stimulation.

YTHDF1 Depletion Disrupts the NREP–TGF-β–Smad Signaling Axis in Fibroblasts

The TGF-β–Smad cascade dictates fibroblast-to-myofibro-blast conversion, collagen deposition, and contractile activity.44 Because the TGF-β–Smad axis is firmly established as the master regulator of fibroblast-to-myofibroblast conversion,45 we next examined whether NREP participates in this transition. To identify a suitable fetal bovine serum (FBS) concentration for inducing the differentiation of corneal keratocytes into myofibroblasts, we cultured the cells in graded FBS concentrations (1%, 5%, 10%, and 20%) to evaluate the impact.46,47 We then compared these results with TGF-β1 stimulation to evaluate the transdifferentiation capacity of the keratocytes. The results in Figure 6 showed that stimulation with 10% FBS produced levels of NREP and fibrosis marker upregulation in keratocytes similar to those induced by TGF-β1, with no statistically significant differences (Fig. 67A). Therefore, we chose a 10% FBS concentration as the stimulation condition. We then silenced NREP in keratocytes cultured either with or without 10% FBS and measured TGF-β1, α-SMA, and Smad2/3 abundances. Compared with the control group, NREP knockdown produced modest, non-significant reductions in all three proteins. Relative to control fibroblasts, si-NREP cells exhibited pronounced, statistically significant reductions in TGF-β1, α-SMA, and Smad2/3 under 10% FBS stimulation (Fig. 6B). Proteomic analysis was performed after knocking down NREP under 10% FBS stimulation. The results indicated that fibrosis-related biological processes, such as actin filament severing, wound healing, and actin cytoskeleton, were overrepresented among the proteins differentially expressed upon NREP knockdown (Figs. 6C, 6D). Finally, we knocked down YTHDF1 to determine whether it affects the NREP–TGF-β–Smad pathway. Western blot analysis showed that stimulating with 10% FBS led to a decrease in NREP expression after YTHDF1 was knocked down. This reduction was in line with the observed changes in the fibrosis markers α-SMA and Smad2/3. All of these alterations were statistically significant (Fig. 6E). Similarly, SKLB-Y13 exerted the same effect, reducing the protein levels of NREP, TGF-β1, α-SMA, and p-Smad2/3 regardless of the presence or absence of 10% FBS stimulation (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NREP depletion blunts TGF-β–Smad signaling in fibroblasts.

Discussion

When severe injuries result in excessive fibrotic healing, it can cause lasting cloudiness in the cornea.8 This research explored how NREP, identified by the m6A modification reader protein YTHDF1, contributes to the development of corneal fibrosis in keratocytes. Using an alkali-burn mouse model, we found that corneal fibrosis peaked on day 14 post-injury, coinciding with an increase in NREP protein levels. NREP knockdown reduces keratocyte proliferation, migration, and TGF-β1–induced α-SMA expression. YTHDF1 depletion had similar effects, specifically lowering NREP protein levels without altering mRNA stability, thus implicating translational control. SRAMP revealed enriched m6A modifications within NREP mRNA. Silencing both NREP and YTHDF1 depletion attenuated TGF-β1–driven fibronectin accumulation and derailed the TGF-β–Smad cascade in fibroblasts. The study concludes that YTHDF1 promotes corneal fibrosis through m6A-dependent enhancement of NREP translation, which potentiates TGF-β–Smad signaling and myofibroblast transdifferentiation, revealing a novel therapeutic target for fibrotic corneal disorders. Our experimental findings revealed for the first time, to our knowledge, the profibrotic function of NREP in corneal fibrosis while also elucidating the YTHDF1-dependent m6A epitranscriptomic regulatory axis.

Corneal alkali burn pathology progresses through three overlapping but distinct phases: acute, inflammation/repair, and remodeling/scar. The acute phase involves tissue necrosis and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and heat shock proteins, triggering inflammatory infiltration, stromal activation, matrix turbidity, and scarring. To capture these dynamics, we analyzed corneas at 7 days (acute), 14 days (peak fibrosis/subacute), and 21 days (more chronic) after injury. The sustained elevation of fibrosis markers and NREP expression observed at day 21 (as demonstrated in Figs. 1F, 2B) indicates that our model facilitates investigation into the transition toward a more persistent fibrotic state, extending beyond the immediate acute injury. NREP exhibits a highly synchronized spatiotemporal expression pattern with classic myofibroblast markers during corneal fibrosis: On day 7 after alkali burn, keratocytes had not yet fully transformed into α-SMA–positive myofibroblasts, but the transcriptional level of NREP had already risen significantly, suggesting that it can serve as an “early-activated molecular timer.” By day 14, NREP protein peaked simultaneously with α-SMA. By day 21, although transcriptional levels continued to rise, protein expression plateaued, aligning with the slowed rate of ECM deposition. Compared with fibrotic tissues rich in blood vessels, such as skin, lungs, and kidneys,34,48,49 this study demonstrated that NREP is also expressed in keratocytes and regulates their fibrotic response, a function that may be precisely modulated by the unique mechanical and hypoxic gradients within the corneal microenvironment. These results, for the first time, to our knowledge, identify NREP as a key driver of corneal fibrosis and provide a time-window rationale for its use as an early intervention target.

In this study, the functional consequences of modulating the YTHDF1–NREP axis were consistently validated across multiple experimental approaches. The functional consequences, including inhibition of proliferation, migration, and α-SMA expression, were consistently observed with three independent siRNA sequences targeting YTHDF1 and NREP separately, as well as the YTHDF1-specific inhibitor SKLB-Y13. Such reproducibility strongly argues against off-target effects being the primary driver of the phenotypes, linking them specifically to the loss of YTHDF1 or NREP function. Our data construct a logical, multilayered regulatory cascade: Bioinformatic analysis (SRAMP) predicts the presence of consensus m6A motifs within NREP mRNA, and knockdown of YTHDF1, a well-established m6A reader, specifically reduces NREP protein abundance. This pattern is a hallmark of m6A reader-mediated regulation of translational efficiency. Knockdown of either YTHDF1 or its putative target NREP produces convergent phenotypic outcomes—namely, the suppression of key fibrogenic cellular behaviors.

The translational amplification effect of the YTHDF1–m6A–NREP axis in the cornea is novel and may be broadly applicable to other organs. Previous studies have confirmed that in cardiac and renal fibrosis YTHDF1 promotes collagen deposition by enhancing ribosomal occupancy of target mRNAs,50–52 whereas this study extended these findings to the cornea, a “low-vascular, low-immune” microenvironment. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses showed that the interaction of YTHDF1 with the m6A-modified NREP transcript accelerates translational elongation. Upon YTHDF1 knockdown by si-YTHDF1 or pharmacological inhibition by SKLB-Y13, downregulation of NREP protein expression was observed, concomitant with the suppression of cell migration and collagen secretion. This observation dovetails with the established function of YTHDF1: It acts as a translational amplifier for m6A-tagged mRNAs that drive metabolic rewiring and cytoskeletal remodeling.38 The regulatory effect of YTHDF1 on NREP expression and fibrogenic phenotypes is further potentiated in the presence of TGF-β1 or 10% FBS, suggesting that m6A modifications may function as a “switch” in corneal fibrosis. Consistently, NREP knockdown downregulates YTHDF1-associated pathways such as energy metabolism and vesicle trafficking, reinforcing the notion that YTHDF1 sustains fibrosis progression by recognizing m6A-modified NREP. The observed decreases in energy metabolism, vesicle trafficking, and TGF-β signaling mirror the consequences of YTHDF1 depletion in established fibrosis models.43 Thus, the YTHDF1–m6A–NREP axis not only supplements the pro-fibrotic epitranscriptomic framework shared across organs but also provides a translational molecular basis for the local delivery of m6A inhibitors in the cornea.

This study has three main limitations. First, although the mouse alkali burn model can rapidly reproduce corneal scarring, it lacks the microenvironment of chronic ocular surface inflammation and repeated mechanical stimulation commonly observed in clinical settings, which may lead to an overestimation of NREP expression. Second, keratocytes cultured in two dimensions cannot replicate the three-dimensional mechanics and hypoxic gradient of the stromal collagen layer; therefore, the results regarding migration and ECM deposition need to be validated using three-dimensional matrix gels or corneal stroma explants. Meanwhile, the 21-day alkali burn model mimics chronic scar formation in clinical settings; however, a single-injury model may not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of chronic clinical fibrotic disorders arising from diverse etiologies. Future studies employing complementary models—such as suture-induced fibrosis, corneal micropocket assays, three-dimensional organoid cultures, or corneal tissue explants—would be valuable to confirm and extend our mechanistic findings to a broader pathological context. Third, the m6A site has only been indirectly confirmed through bioinformatics prediction and knocking YTHDF1 experiments. Additional research, including methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)–qPCR or RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) sequencing experiments, is necessary to elucidate the role of adenosine and to eliminate the possibility of compensatory effects from YTHDF2/3. Furthermore, the long-term safety and impact of localized siRNA delivery on corneal re-epithelialization must be rigorously evaluated in large-animal models. Despite identifying the YTHDF1–NREP axis as a promising therapeutic target for corneal fibrosis, its clinical translation necessitates addressing several interconnected challenges, including developing efficient localized delivery systems to target stromal keratocytes, optimizing intervention timing within the dynamic fibrotic window, and overcoming potential functional compensation by other m6A modification-related proteins that may require strategic combination therapies. Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings support the following working model (Fig. 7): the m6A reader protein YTHDF1 binds to m6A-modified NREP transcripts and promotes their translation; the resulting increase in NREP protein then activates the canonical TGF‑β–Smad signaling axis, leading to enhanced keratocyte proliferation, migration, myofibroblast differentiation, and ultimately corneal fibrosis after injury.

Conclusions

Our study identified NREP as a novel protein upregulated during corneal fibrosis in a mouse alkali burn model. Using an in vitro model of human keratocyte activation, we provide mechanistic evidence that the m6A reader YTHDF1 promotes key fibrogenic responses, including proliferation, migration, and myofibroblast differentiation, likely through enhancing the translation of NREP mRNA. Although our in vivo data established a correlation between NREP expression and fibrotic progression, the functional interplay between YTHDF1 and NREP was characterized primarily in cultured cells. Therefore, our work suggests that the YTHDF1–NREP axis may represent a potential contributory pathway in corneal fibrogenesis and highlights it as a candidate target worthy of further investigation in more complex in vivo systems.
