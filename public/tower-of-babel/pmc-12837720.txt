Why will increasing medical insurance reimbursement levels not lead to over-medicalization? Evidence from China’s medical insurance payment reform
Authors: Mu Yingtan, Zuo Yaxuan, Hu Qiuming
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgainst the backdrop of ongoing reforms in medical insurance payment methods, this study utilizes sample data from China employee medical insurance settlements in a provincial capital to examine the impact of adjustments to the pooled hospitalization reimbursement ratio. We use a fuzzy regression-discontinuity design to estimate the local causal effect of changes in the pooled hospitalization reimbursement ratio on total inpatient expenditures and their components.The empirical results reveal three main findings. First, an increased reimbursement ratio optimized the cost structure, leading to a 5% reduction in total per capita medical expenditures. Second, mechanistic analysis indicates that changes in insurance payment standards incentivized patients to prioritize cost-effective services within the insurance catalog and prompted medical institutions to restructure clinical pathways. Third, the higher reimbursement ratio encouraged patients to select higher-quality healthcare resources, thereby reducing the risk of readmission. Heterogeneity analysis further shows that cost reductions were most pronounced in tertiary hospitals and among patients with severe comorbidities, highlighting the allocative efficiency of high-quality medical resources. This study provides evidence of a dose–response relationship in the precise adjustment of medical insurance benefit policies and supports the implementation of differentiated reimbursement strategies, with higher tiers for tertiary hospitals and complex cases to reinforce positive incentives.Keywords: Medical insurance benefit level, Excessive medical treatment, Medical expenses

Introduction

As China’s basic medical insurance system undergoes a strategic transition from broad coverage to high-quality services, policy interventions—such as increased reimbursement ratio, higher payment ceilings, and expanded drug formularies—have substantially improved access to healthcare. However, this progress has generated debate over whether enhanced coverage inevitably leads to excessive utilization of medical resources [1, 2].

One perspective, grounded in Arrow’s[1] analysis of the unique characteristics of the healthcare market, emphasizes a moral hazard–dominant framework. This view argues that improvements in insurance benefits reduce patients’ out-of-pocket costs, potentially inducing moral hazard and overutilization of medical services. Empirical evidence from the RAND Health Insurance Experiment supports this mechanism: Aron-Dine et al.[3] found that higher out-of-pocket cost sharing significantly reduced both outpatient and inpatient service utilization, thereby lowering total healthcare expenditures. In China, Xiang et al. [4] reported that increasing the overall coordination level of insurance schemes significantly raised hospitalization costs by elevating reimbursement ratio. Subsequent studies further delineate this mechanism. On the demand side, patients’ price sensitivity diminishes as reimbursement ratio increase, resulting in more active utilization of medical resources [5]. Conversely, when out-of-pocket expenses rise, patients substantially reduce medical consumption [6], with high-deductible plans shown to reduce expenditures by 11.8%–13.8% [7]. On the supply side, induced demand emerges as medical institutions respond to economic incentives embedded in insurance plans, increasing hospitalization and emergency service rates, as well as the use of medications and basic preventive care, thereby driving up overall medical costs [8].

A contrasting perspective challenges the linear assumption that increased benefits inevitably cause overmedicalization. Under scientifically designed institutional frameworks, enhanced benefits are not merely drivers of cost inflation but serve as instruments to promote structural transformation in healthcare delivery. Saha et al.[2], demonstrate that improved insurance benefits not only expand equitable access and enhance population health but also contribute to economic stability. Chinese evidence reveals nonlinear effects of benefit improvements. Zhao et al.[9], found that when hospitalization reimbursement ratio were below 60%, a 10-percentage-point increase raised hospitalization utilization by 12.3%; however, the effect declined to 3.2% when the rate exceeded 75%, illustrating diminishing marginal utility. Furthermore, service substitution effects have been observed: Wang et al.[10], reported that policies lifting outpatient caps increased outpatient spending by 18.6%, while reductions in unnecessary hospitalizations saved 24.9%, yielding a net 6.3% decrease in insurance fund expenditures.

A third perspective emphasizes heterogeneity in the relationship between insurance benefits and resource utilization. this study organizes existing evidence within a three-dimensional analytical framework encompassing population characteristics, disease type, and service level. First, population characteristics shape marginal effects of insurance benefits through income gradients and health disparities [11]. Low-income groups exhibit higher sensitivity to medical service prices.Shigeoka [12], leveraging a natural experiment in Japan, found that reduced out-of-pocket rates for 70-year-olds disproportionately increased utilization among low-income individuals. Chinese data corroborate these findings:Huang and Gan [13], reported that low- and middle-income individuals were 2.3 times more responsive to changes in outpatient prices than inpatient prices, indicating that relaxed insurance benefits have a greater impact on lower-income populations. Second, disease type modulates responsiveness to insurance adjustments. Clinical characteristics and treatment rigidity determine price elasticity. For example, Feng et al.[14] found that patients with high-cost, long-term conditions, such as cancer, exhibit greater behavioral responses to reimbursement changes than those with common illnesses. Third, service-level differentiation shapes demand and supply responses [15]. On the demand side, Huang and Gan [13] observed that outpatient services are more price-elastic than inpatient services, while preventive care exhibits near-zero elasticity, contrasting with therapeutic services. On the supply side, medical institutions respond heterogeneously: Wang et al.[16] found that high-cost procedures at tertiary hospitals are roughly 1.8 times more sensitive to reimbursement changes than similar services at primary facilities, reflecting differences in profit margins and resource allocation capacity.

Building on these insights, this study proposes that improving insurance benefits does not necessarily lead to overmedicalization. The critical factor is whether institutional design effectively promotes healthy behavior and optimizes resource use. Specifically, we test the following hypotheses: Hypothesis 1: Given the generally low baseline benefits of basic medical insurance, increasing the reimbursement ratio exerts a “cost-suppressing effect,” reducing patients’ total medical expenditures. Hypothesis 2: Constrained by demand price elasticity, the costs of non-essential services rise with the reimbursement ratio, while expenditures on essential services decline. Hypothesis 3: Institutional and patient-level heterogeneity results in significant cost reductions in tertiary hospitals with increased reimbursement ratios, whereas costs at secondary hospitals may increase. Hypothesis 4: Patients with severe complications, due to more rigid medical needs, experience greater cost reductions from increased reimbursement ratios than those with moderate or no complications. Hypothesis 5: Higher reimbursement ratios indirectly reduce medical expenses and hospitalizations by promoting timely care and improving patient compliance, generating a cumulative health effect that buffers costs.

To elucidate the mechanisms through which insurance benefit improvements influence behavior, we analyze urban employee basic medical insurance settlement data from a provincial capital using a regression discontinuity design. Results indicate that higher reimbursement ratios lead patients to increase utilization of non-essential services, decrease reliance on essential services, and achieve overall reductions in medical expenditures. Notably, cost reductions are more pronounced among patients at tertiary hospitals and among those without complications or with severe comorbidities. Mechanistically, these effects operate through: (i) direct effects, where higher reimbursement shifts patient behavior toward covered services and prompts medical institutions to optimize diagnosis and treatment processes, achieving cost control; and (ii) indirect effects, whereby patients preferentially utilize high-quality medical resources, reducing readmissions or extending intervals between readmissions, producing cumulative health benefits. We further propose an “incentive compatibility threshold”—a moderate reimbursement ratio that simultaneously maximizes patient welfare and rationalizes medical costs.

The study’s marginal contributions are twofold: This study systematically examines the relationship between medical insurance benefit levels and patients’ utilization of healthcare resources, offering empirical evidence from the Chinese context. Moreover, it confirms the positive impact of enhanced insurance generosity on patients’ health outcomes, thereby broadening the analytical framework for evaluating medical insurance performance.

Methods and data

Data source

Based on China urban employee basic medical insurance benefits policy in a given province, reimbursement ratio are designed to increase with patient age, with distinct starting rates corresponding to different levels of medical institutions. Specifically, for patients under 50 years old, the reimbursement rate is defined as 80% plus 0.2% for each additional year of age. For patients aged 50 and above, the baseline rate gradually rises, reaching 84% at age 60, 86% at age 70, and 88% at age 80, with all rates increasing by 0.2% per additional year. This age-dependent reimbursement mechanism creates natural breakpoints suitable for quasi-experimental analysis. Since insured individuals are automatically eligible for the adjusted rates upon reaching the specified ages—without the need for reapplication or additional medical examination—these changes can be treated as exogenous shocks. Leveraging this feature, the study employs a regression discontinuity design (RDD) design to estimate the causal effect of medical insurance reimbursement ratio on patient utilization of medical resources. While the provincial-level hospitalization reimbursement ratio exhibit jumps at ages 50, 60, 70, and 80, the primary analysis focuses on the 60-year threshold, with robustness checks conducted at the other breakpoints. Data source

This study used sample data from a province’s urban employee basic medical insurance settlements from April 2021 to December 2022. The data primarily included personal information, disease diagnosis and treatment information, hospital information, and cost information. Personal information included a serial number (the patient’s unique personal identification code; the patient’s personal information has been standardized, making it impossible to identify the patient from the data], gender, and age. Treatment information included the disease diagnosis, ICD-10 code, admission and discharge date, DRG code, length of stay, and number of surgical procedures. Hospital information included the name and level of the medical institution. Cost information included total hospitalization costs and out-of-pocket expenses from the medical insurance settlement data, as well as comprehensive medical service fees, drug costs, and consumables costs from the medical record homepage.

At age 50, mean total medical expenses show a modest “jump” following a previously gradual upward trend, after which the growth rate becomes steeper. Benchmark regression results

The impact of the medical insurance reimbursement ratio on patients’ medical resource use is demonstrated in Tables 3, 4 and Fig. 3. This study employed a triangular kernel function combined with the CCT method to precisely determine the optimal bandwidth for a regression discontinuity analysis. The results showed that the estimated local average treatment effect (LATE) was negative at a 1% significance level, indicating that, when patients reached age 60, their medical expenses decreased by approximately 5% on average as the medical insurance reimbursement ratio increased. The signs and significance of the regression coefficients remained unchanged, confirming the robustness and reliability of our empirical results.

Heterogeneity analysis

According to the aforementioned theoretical model, the optimal reimbursement ratio is related to the price elasticity of demand, b. Therefore, this study comprehensively analyzes the impact of the reimbursement ratio on patient medical resource utilization by considering factors that influence the price elasticity of demand, b, such as different ADRG disease groups, hospital grades, and comorbidities and complications.

Considering the differences in price elasticity and demand behavior between basic and non-basic medical services, this study conducted a heterogeneity analysis and found that increased medical insurance reimbursement ratio have a positive incentive effect on demand for non-basic medical services, while for basic medical services, they have the effect of inhibiting overutilization and promoting standardized allocation. Therefore, this study conducted heterogeneity analysis on the non-basic medical service rehabilitation group (XR2) and the basic medical service cerebral hemorrhage group (BR2) (Fig. 5). Both groups employed a triangular kernel function combined with the CCT method to determine the optimal bandwidth for regression discontinuity analysis. Table 6 shows that the estimated local average treatment effect (LATE) values were all significant at the 1% level, but the coefficient for XR2 was positive for the non-basic medical service rehabilitation group and negative for BR2 for the basic medical service cerebral hemorrhage group. Several factors may explain these observed patterns. First, for non-basic medical services, patients face a broader set of options and often seek higher-quality, higher-value services. Enhanced medical insurance benefits further stimulate this demand. In contrast, for basic medical services, patients generally select standardized, simple, and cost-effective treatments, which can reduce unnecessary utilization as insurance coverage improves.Second, the pricing mechanism for non-basic services is relatively flexible, and physicians—motivated by income incentives—may be more likely to recommend higher-value interventions. By comparison, basic services have standardized pricing and well-defined payment structures, with insurance policies providing stricter coverage rules and oversight, thereby limiting potentially inappropriate recommendations by medical institutions.Finally, as part of ongoing reforms in medical insurance payment systems, basic medical services have become a focal point of regulatory oversight. Through standardized care pathways, fee caps, and performance-based assessments, their cost structures and service delivery are carefully managed. Consequently, improvements in insurance benefits have not led to overutilization but have instead promoted a more rational allocation of medical resources.

Given that the tier of medical institutions may influence the effectiveness of medical insurance benefit adjustments through demand structure and service characteristics, this study further analyzes the heterogeneity of tiered hospitals. The results show that the increased medical insurance reimbursement ratio has a more significant impact on patients in tertiary hospitals, highlighting the key role of medical insurance policies in guiding tiered medical care and optimizing resource allocation. A discontinuity regression analysis using a triangular kernel function combined with the CCT method to determine the optimal bandwidth shows (Table 7) that the estimated local average treatment effect (LATE) for tertiary hospitals is significant at 1%, while the effects for secondary and primary hospitals are not significant. As shown in Fig. 6, medical expenses for tertiary hospitals decrease at the breakpoint, while those for secondary hospitals increase at the breakpoint. These patterns can be attributed to two key factors. First, on the demand side, patients exhibit a substitution effect across different hospital tiers. As medical insurance reimbursement ratios increase, out-of-pocket expenses decline, reducing marginal medical costs while expanding patients’ choice between hospital levels. For patients with stable chronic conditions and prolonged treatment cycles, secondary hospitals offer advantages in geographic accessibility, shorter waiting times, and rehabilitation services, whereas tertiary hospitals primarily treat highly complex or critically ill patients. Consequently, some patients who initially sought care at tertiary hospitals shift to secondary hospitals for rehabilitation or follow-up care, resulting in higher overall costs for secondary hospitals and a relative decline in demand for high-intensity services at tertiary institutions.Second, on the supply side, hospitals respond differently to reimbursement incentives. Tertiary hospitals, operating under stricter regulatory oversight, tend to focus on efficiency improvements by optimizing clinical pathways, reducing average lengths of stay, and minimizing unnecessary services. In contrast, secondary hospitals, facing comparatively looser regulation, are more likely to expand service volumes—including rehabilitation, physical examinations, and extended care programs—to increase their medical insurance surplus, which contributes to higher total medical expenditures.

Considering that the severity of a patient’s illness may influence their responsiveness to changes in medical insurance benefits, this study conducted further heterogeneity analysis. The results showed that the incentive effect of increased medical insurance reimbursement ratio was more pronounced for patients with severe complications than for those without complications, highlighting that medical insurance policies not only fulfill their universal role of “guaranteeing basic needs” but also demonstrate a strong precision in providing protection for high - cost, high-risk populations. Higher reimbursement ratios increase their sensitivity to medical costs, encouraging cost-conscious behavior in diagnosis and treatment decisions and prompting proactive reductions in unnecessary tests and procedures. Patients with minor complications occupy an intermediate position: their medical needs exhibit some flexibility, making them moderately responsive to reimbursement adjustments and less sensitive than high-severity patients but more responsive than those without complications.

Analysis of the mechanism of action

Increases in medical insurance reimbursement ratio not only directly influence patients’ healthcare-seeking behavior and the clinical processes of medical institutions—thereby facilitating cost control—but also indirectly encourage patients to utilize higher-quality medical resources, improve health outcomes, and reduce readmission rates or extend the intervals between readmissions.

Direct effects: price incentives and supply and demand behavior

According to the health demand model [19], higher reimbursement ratio reduce patients’ effective out-of-pocket costs, potentially increasing short-term utilization of medical services. However, under payment constraint mechanisms such as DRG or DIP, medical institutions’ revenues are capped, and their behavioral responses are shaped by incentive structures. When reimbursement ratio rise, patients’ willingness to pay increases, but hospitals experience a decline in marginal reimbursement revenue, prompting a strategic shift toward greater efficiency in diagnosis and treatment and optimized resource allocation.From the supply side, enhanced medical insurance benefits incentivize hospitals to preserve profitability by standardizing care pathways, reducing redundant testing, and strengthening cost-control measures. From the demand side, higher reimbursement ratio encourage patients to follow medical advice and seek timely care, lowering out-of-pocket expenses and preventing the accumulation of avoidable costs. Together, these demand- and supply-side mechanisms constitute a “direct effect” that reduces total medical expenditures.

On the one hand, this study further examines the impact of increased medical insurance reimbursement ratio on different expense categories, focusing on the structure of medical expenses. The paper finds that the effect is more pronounced for medical treatment expenses, reflecting structural differences in hospital behavioral responses within the context of payment system reform. this study divides total medical expenses into medical treatment expenses, drug expenses, and consumables expenses. Using a triangular kernel function combined with the CCT method to determine the optimal bandwidth, this study conducts discontinuity regression to explore the impact of the medical insurance reimbursement rate on each component of medical expenses. The results in Table 9 show that the estimated local average treatment effect (LATE) for medical treatment expenses is significant at 1%, while the effects for drug expenses and consumables expenses are not significant (Fig. 8). Several factors explain this discrepancy. First, since the implementation of China’s new medical reform in 2009, and particularly following the national centralized bulk drug procurement program (the “4 + 7” pilot) in 2019, prices for drugs and high-value consumables have been tightly controlled. According to the Opinions of the General Office of the State Council on Promoting Centralized Drug Procurement, hospitals now purchase drugs and consumables at a unified winning bid price, effectively eliminating the profit opportunities previously available under the “drug-to-medical” model. This institutional change removes financial incentives for physicians to overprescribe medications or add consumables, thereby neutralizing their revenue-generating potential [20]. Furthermore, oversight of medical insurance payments has intensified, with the Medical Insurance Bureau increasingly focusing on clinical practices and service structures rather than drug costs. Following the elimination of drug markups, reimbursement for drugs and consumables often adopts total budget caps or fixed per-item payments, significantly reducing their price responsiveness to changes in insurance benefits. In contrast, medical treatment costs retain some flexibility, particularly under disease-specific fee structures such as DRG or DIP. Hospitals and physicians can optimize resource allocation across services and treatment frequency to maximize revenue, which explains why medical treatment costs respond more strongly to increases in reimbursement ratio.

Indirect effects: health capital accumulation and readmission behavior adjustment

In Grossman's [19], health capital model, health is conceptualized as a form of capital that can be accumulated through medical investments. Higher medical insurance reimbursement ratio reduce the marginal cost of healthcare, incentivizing patients to increase their investment in health and thereby accumulate and maintain health capital. Over the long term, this reduces disease recurrence and acute exacerbations, prolonging intervals between hospitalizations and manifesting as lower readmission rates. Concurrently, reforms in medical insurance payment systems have intensified quality controls and performance assessments for hospitals, compelling medical institutions to emphasize treatment efficacy and relapse prevention. The interaction between hospitals’ “quality efforts” and patients’ “health investments” thus establishes a theoretical link between enhanced medical insurance benefits and a reduced risk of readmission.

On the one hand, this study uses the triangular kernel function and the CCT method to determine the optimal bandwidth for discontinuity regression to explore the impact of the medical insurance reimbursement ratio on the number of hospitalizations . The study found that the increase in the medical insurance reimbursement ratio not only has a significant impact on patients’ medical behavior, but also promotes the improvement of medical service quality and the efficiency of resource allocation by optimizing the incentive mechanism on both the supply and demand sides. The results in Table 10 (1) show that the estimated value of the local average treatment effect (LATE) of the number of hospitalizations is significantly negative at 1%, indicating that as the medical insurance reimbursement ratio increases, the number of hospitalizations of patients decreases (Fig. 10). On the other hand, this study only retains the patient sample with a second hospitalization, uses the triangular kernel function and the CCT method to determine the optimal bandwidth for discontinuity regression to explore the impact of the medical insurance reimbursement ratio on the number of days between readmissions of patients. The results in Table 10 (3) show that the estimated value of the local average treatment effect (LATE) of the number of hospitalizations is significantly positive at 5%, indicating that as the medical insurance reimbursement ratio increases, the number of days between readmissions increases (Fig. 11). With rising medical insurance reimbursement ratio, the financial burden on patients has been effectively alleviated, while healthcare providers face heightened quality constraints and performance assessment pressures. This dual influence prompts medical institutions to improve service efficiency and quality while maintaining reasonable profitability. Consequently, enhanced insurance benefits affect not only patients’ medical behavior but also, more fundamentally, the incentive structures and quality orientation of healthcare providers.Higher reimbursement ratio have been associated with more timely care-seeking and greater patient compliance, facilitating early detection and treatment of diseases, reducing exacerbations and complications, and ultimately improving health outcomes such as lower readmission rates and extended intervals between readmissions. These improvements reflect not only enhanced medical service quality but also the ability of higher insurance benefits to balance efficiency and equity by incentivizing “high-value care” rather than “high-frequency utilization.”

However, these results may be influenced by differences in disease severity and unobserved heterogeneity in patient health status. Patients with milder conditions are more likely to fall into groups with higher reimbursement ratio, potentially generating a “spurious decline” in readmission rates. In other words, some observed improvements may reflect sample composition rather than a causal effect of reimbursement ratio. To address this, the study conducted subgroup analyses based on complication severity, examining the impact of reimbursement ratio on the interval between readmissions across different patient groups. As shown in Table 11, for patients with general complications, higher reimbursement ratio were significantly positively associated with longer intervals between readmissions (p < 0.05), indicating that improved benefits enable patients to access adequate medical care and rehabilitation resources, thereby delaying readmission. This evidence further supports the hypothesis that enhanced medical insurance benefits indirectly reduce readmission risk through improvements in both health capital and service quality.

In summary, higher levels of medical insurance benefits promote medical quality through three primary mechanisms: (1) encouraging medical institutions to prioritize efficacy and adherence to service standards, (2) improving patient health awareness and compliance with medical advice, and (3) facilitating rational allocation and optimization of medical resources through institutional incentives and regulatory oversight.

Main conclusions and policy recommendations

This study systematically examines the nonlinear relationship between enhanced medical insurance benefits and medical resource utilization using regression discontinuity analysis. First, we explored the concept of an optimal medical price, balancing the interests of patients, insurers, and healthcare providers, and analyzed the effect of reimbursement ratio on overall social welfare. Using sample data from urban employee medical insurance settlements in a provincial capital, we further investigated variations in hospitalization reimbursement ratio and their impact on patient medical resource utilization. The main findings are as follows:

First, increasing medical insurance reimbursement ratio does not necessarily lead to overutilization of medical services. The ultimate effect depends on the integrated institutional design encompassing payment reforms, behavioral incentives, and quality monitoring. Within an appropriate range of hospitalization reimbursement ratio, patient welfare can be maximized while promoting cost rationalization.Mechanisms of action are twofold. The direct effect primarily reflects changes in patient medical behavior: higher reimbursement ratio encourage patients to select insurance-covered services, reducing reliance on non-essential or out-of-pocket services. At the same time, under the dual pressure of insurance payments and regulatory oversight, medical institutions optimize diagnostic and treatment processes and standardize care pathways, enhancing service efficiency and reducing operating costs. The indirect effect captures the long-term impact on patient health behavior and outcomes: higher reimbursement ratio incentivize the use of high-quality, standardized medical resources, improving health and reducing the likelihood of repeat hospitalizations. Our analysis further shows that increased reimbursement ratio significantly reduce hospitalization frequency and extend the interval between readmissions, demonstrating that improved benefits alleviate financial burdens while enhancing health outcomes and overall medical service efficiency.

Second, a three-dimensional analytical framework—categorizing patients by service type, disease characteristics, and institutional level—reveals significant heterogeneity in the effects of medical insurance benefits. Regarding service type, utilization of non-essential services increased, whereas essential services exhibited relatively rigid demand. Higher reimbursement ratio encouraged patients to prioritize high-value rehabilitation services, improving quality of life. Regarding institutional level, patient costs decreased more at tertiary hospitals than at primary or secondary hospitals, primarily because higher-tier hospitals can convert technological advantages into efficiency gains through the pathway: high reimbursement → improved case mix index (CMI) → standardized treatment pathways → reduced per-visit costs. Regarding disease severity, reductions in medical costs were more pronounced among patients with severe complications, as these patients require more resources and complex care, making their treatment decisions and cost burdens highly sensitive to reimbursement adjustments.

Policy recommendations to achieve a dynamic balance between enhanced benefits and medical resource utilization include:

Establish a dynamic adjustment mechanism for an “appropriate protection range.” reimbursement ratio should be treated as adjustable parameters informed by simulation estimates and empirical data, calibrated by age, disease type, and hospital level, and coordinated with DRG/DIP payment systems to ensure incentive-compatible cost control and service quality.

Optimize payment structures and catalog boundaries to guide rational healthcare-seeking behavior, prioritize high-value services, and limit coverage for non-essential or inefficient services, thereby improving resource efficiency without increasing overall payment pressure.

Strengthen incentives for tiered diagnosis and treatment. Policy design should consider functional roles and behavioral responses of hospitals at different levels, linking CMI to cost control and reinforcing efficiency constraints at high-tier institutions while enhancing primary care capabilities for chronic disease management and rehabilitation.

Target high-cost and high-risk groups. Implement tiered reimbursement based on disease complexity and comorbidity levels to improve protection for high-need populations without increasing fiscal pressure, guiding hospitals toward optimized treatment pathways and better health outcomes.

Limitations of this study include: first, data coverage is limited to a single province, which may reflect specific regional policy implementation, healthcare resource distribution, and population health status. Second, the data are cross-sectional, capturing only static policy effects and unable to evaluate the long-term dynamic impacts of benefit adjustments on patient behavior and health outcomes.
