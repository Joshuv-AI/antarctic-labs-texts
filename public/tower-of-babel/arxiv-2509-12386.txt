Amulet : a Library for Assessing Interactions Among ML Defenses and Risks
Asim Waheed, Vasisht Duddu, Rui Zhang, Sebastian Szyller, N. Asokan

Abstract
ML models are susceptible to risks to security, privacy, and fairness. Several defenses are designed to protect against their intended risks, but can inadvertently affect susceptibility to other unrelated risks, known as unintended interactions . Several jurisdictions are preparing ML regulatory frameworks that require ML practitioners to assess the susceptibility of ML models to different risks. A library for evaluating unintended interactions that can be used by (a) practitioners to evaluate unintended interactions at scale prior to model deployment and (b) researchers to design defenses which do not suffer from an unintended increase in unrelated risks. Ideally, such a library should be i) comprehensive by including representative attacks, defenses and metrics for different risks ii) extensible to new modules due to its modular design iii) consistent with a user-friendly API template for inputs and outputs iv) applicable to evaluate previously unexplored unintended interactions. We present Amulet , a Python library that covers risks to security, privacy, and fairness, which satisfies all these requirements. Amulet can be used to evaluate unexplored unintended interactions, compare effectiveness between defenses or attacks, and include new attacks and defenses.

2.1
Risks against Machine Learning

Following the systematization in previous work
[
11
]
, we summarize the different risks in ML to security, privacy, and fairness.
We start by summarizing different security risks (
S1
,
S2
, and
S3
):

S1

Evasion
involves a class of attacks that attempt to fool a model
\mathcal{M}
to misclassify inputs.
The most common way to force misclassification is by adding a small amount of noise to data records to obtain adversarial examples
[
32
]
.
The adversarial noise added to the data records is computed by identifying the direction of higher loss for
\mathcal{M}

[
32
]
.
Such attacks are widely used as a benchmark for model robustness.

S2

Data Poisoning
modifies
\mathcal{M}
’s decision boundary by adding malicious data records to the training data set.
This forces
\mathcal{M}
to misclassify inputs, leading to a loss of utility.
Backdoor attacks
[
16
]
, a specific case of data poisoning, inject a backdoor (or
trigger
) into
\mathcal{M}
such that any input with that trigger is misclassified.
They remain a central challenge because even a small fraction of poisoned data can significantly degrade model utility.

S3

Unauthorized model ownership
refers to attacks where an adversary derives a surrogate model that mimics the functionality of
\mathcal{M}
.
This may be an identical copy of
\mathcal{M}
or derived using model extraction attacks (training a local model using the output of
\mathcal{M}

[
38
]
.

We now present risks to privacy (
P1
,
P2
,
P3
, and
P4
):

P1

Membership Inference
attacks aim to infer whether a specific data record was part of
\mathcal{M}
’s training dataset (
\mathcal{D}_{tr}
).
They exploit the difference in
\mathcal{M}
’s predictions on data records sampled from
\mathcal{D}_{tr}
and data records sampled from elsewhere
[
7
]
.
For example, training
\mathcal{M}
on cancer patient data could allow an adversary to infer whether a patient has cancer, even if this information is not explicitly included in training or the output of
\mathcal{M}
.

P2

Attribute Inference
where the goal is to infer the value of an attribute that is not included in the input or
\mathcal{D}_{tr}

[
1
]
.
When this attribute is sensitive (e.g., race or gender), it may be a privacy violation in the case where the data owner does not want to share this information.
The inferred attributes can often be socially or legally sensitive.

P3

Data Reconstruction
recovers the average data record of a particular class in
\mathcal{D}_{tr}
using the outputs of
\mathcal{M}

[
14
]
.
Some attacks may also recover individual data points
[
8
]
.
In the case of generative models, the adversary can force the model to generate exact training data records
[
36
]
.

P4

Distribution Inference
infers properties of the distribution (e.g., ratio of men to women) from which training data were sampled from
[
42
]
.
The adversary exploits the difference in
\mathcal{M}
’s behavior using shadow models trained on datasets with different distributions.

Finally, we present the risks to fairness (
F
):

F

Discriminatory Behavior
occurs when
\mathcal{M}
behaves differently between different demographic subgroups
[
13
]
.
This could be due to inherent bias in
\mathcal{D}_{tr}
or due to bias introduced through training optimizations.

2.2
Machine Learning Defenses

For each of the risks, we present a corresponding defense.
We first present defenses against security risks (
SD1
,
SD2
,
SD3
, and
SD4
):

SD1

Adversarial Training
reduces the susceptibility against
S1
(evasion) by training
\mathcal{M}
with adversarial examples
[
32
]
. We minimize the maximum loss from the worst-case adversarial examples.

SD2

Outlier Detection
works as a defense against
S2
(data poisoning).
This works either by suppressing the effect of poisoned data points while training
[
25
]
, by detecting and removing them from the training dataset
[
47
]
, or by pruning
\mathcal{M}
after training
[
49
]
.

SD3

Fingerprinting
is a post-hoc defense against
S3
(model ownership verification) that extracts a unique fingerprint from the victim model and the suspect model to differentiate between them
[
33
]
.
Fingerprints can be extracted from various model characteristics (for example, transferable adversarial examples
[
31
]
, decision boundary information
[
33
]
).

SD4

Watermarking
is another defense against
S3
that adds watermarks (marked data records) to the training dataset that can be later extracted to verify model ownership
[
3
]
.
The watermarks are typically out-of-distribution samples which are memorized by
\mathcal{M}
, and can be easily extracted during verification
[
3
]
.

To defend against risks to privacy (
PD1
):

PD1

Differential privacy
(DP), specifically differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DPSGD)
[
2
]
, is a defense against
P1
(Membership Inference) and
P3
(Data Reconstruction).
DPSGD clips the gradients and adds an appropriate amount of noise to them during training s.t. model observables are indistinguishable with and without a single data record in the training dataset.

Currently, there are no defenses with theoretical guarantees against
P2
(Attribute Inference) and
P4
(Distribution Inference).

Finally, we present adversarial debiasing (
FD1
) as a defense for fairness risks:

FD1

Adversarial Debiasing
ensures model behavior is similar across different demographic subgroups.
The most common approach for this is to use a GAN-style network where the the adversary acts as the discriminator to try and predict the demographic subgroup based on the differences in model behavior
[
52
,
30
]
.

2.3
Interactions among Defenses and Risks

Defenses are designed to protect against specific risks (as discussed in Section
2.2
).
However, a deployed model could be susceptible to various risks simultaneously.
Hence, a practitioner must understand (a) the conflicts in combining defenses to protect against multiple risks
[
43
]
, and (b) the interactions of the defense with other unrelated risks
[
11
]
.

Conflicts among Defenses.
Defenses can be combined to protect against multiple risks simultaneously
[
12
]
. However, defenses may conflict with each other and reduce the effectiveness of defenses or model utility after combination
[
43
]
. These can be viewed as unintended interactions among defenses.

Unintended Interactions Among Defenses and Risks.
When a defense designed to protect against a specific risk is effective, it may increase or decrease susceptibility to other unrelated risks (aka “unintended interactions”)
[
11
,
43
]
.
For example, using adversarial training increases the susceptibility to other risks to privacy
[
41
]
and fairness
[
5
]
.
Duddu et al.
[
11
]
systematically identified interactions among defenses and risks. They showed that overfitting and memorization are the underlying causes for unintended interactions.
When a defense is effective, it affects overfitting and memorization, thus influencing the susceptibility to other risks that exploit overfitting and memorization.
In addition, the factors influencing overfitting and memorization (e.g. size of the model and training dataset, distance to the decision boundary, etc.) also influence unintended interactions.
However, they did not present a comprehensive evaluation of various unintended interactions.

In this work, we focus on unintended interactions between defenses and risks.

2.4
Other Libraries

ML-Doctor
[
28
]
is the closest related work that consolidates different privacy and security attacks such as model extraction, model inversion (data reconstruction), membership inference, and attribute inference attacks.
Their goal is to evaluate the attacks against some benchmarks to help understand their relationship.
In addition, they evaluate the impact of overfitting and data complexity on different privacy risks.
Similarly,
[
35
]
present the ML Privacy Meter.
However, it is limited to membership inference attacks and does not consider other interactions.
Lastly, there are libraries for implementing and benchmarking different variants of DP in ML
[
51
,
48
]

There are separate frameworks that focus on the security of ML models.
They implement methods to evaluate robustness threats against ML models by evaluating models against several evasion and poisoning attacks.
These frameworks also implement adversarial training to help defend against these risks
[
40
,
10
,
37
,
26
]
.
The adversarial robustness toolbox also includes some risks to privacy
[
37
]
.
TROJANZOO
[
39
]
is another security-focused library that primarily implements backdoor attacks.

Finally, for discriminatory behavior, there are libraries that implement methods for model explanations (e.g. Captum
[
23
]
) and algorithmic fairness
[
4
,
6
]
.

All these works design risks and defenses separately which do not allow us to easily evaluate their interactions.

3
Problem Statement

Our goal is to design a library to evaluate unintended and intended interactions among ML defenses and risks.
However, there are many challenges that make this evaluation difficult.
Different risks may use different datasets, threat models, and implementations.
Prior work shows even evaluation strategies are often flawed and inconsistent
[
7
]
.
Thus, there is a need to standardize these differences to allow for a thorough evaluation.

Desiderata.
Ideally, a library must be:

D1

comprehensive
by covering state-of-the-art attacks, defenses, and metrics for different risks;

D2

consistent
by offering a user-friendly API;

D3

extensible
by allowing easy addition of new modules;

D4

applicable
to evaluate both intended and unintended interactions among ML defenses and risks.

4
Design of
Amulet

We design
Amulet
to be modular, and target the following users:

i)

researchers
who explore previously unexplored interactions, study how variants of attacks, defenses, metrics, and other factors influence the interactions, and design effective defenses to mitigate unintended and intended interactions;

ii)

practitioners
who analyze the trade-offs in deploying ML defenses to specific to their domain, and regulatory environment.

We design
Amulet
to satisfy all four requirements discussed in Section
3
.

Design Rationale.
The core module in
Amulet
is a
risk
.
For each risk, we implement

i)

attacks which exploit the risk

ii)

defenses which protect against the risk

iii)

metrics to evaluate the susceptibility to the risk.

This modular structure is depicted in Figure
2
.

We now discuss how we design
Amulet
to be comprehensive, extensible, and consistent.

]
.

CIFAR10
consists of
60,000
images of
32\times 32
color images belonging to one of ten classes, with
6,000
images per class. There are
50,000
training images and
10,000
test images.

CENSUS
contains
46{,}447
records with
42
demographic and socioeconomic attributes from the 1994 US Census
[
22
]
. Sensitive fields include gender and race, while others describe education, occupation, and work hours. The classification task is to predict whether annual income exceeds $50k, with
23{,}223
training and
23{,}224
test records.

Labeled Faces in the Wild
(LFW) was originally developed for facial recognition. It includes
13{,}233
images (original size
62\times 47
) of public figures, combined with attributes such as gender, race, and age from the Columbia Imaging and Vision Laboratory (CAVE)
8
8

8

www.cs.columbia.edu/CAVE/databases/pubfig/download/lfw_attributes.txt
. These attributes convert LFW into a property inference task
[
34
]
, with each image flattened to a vector of length
8{,}742
.

CelebFaces Attributes Dataset
(CelebA) is an image dataset containing
202,599
images and
40
attributes for each image
[
29
]
.
It is often used as a computer vision benchmarking tool for face attribute recognition (e.g. is the person in the image smiling).

Amulet
uses CelebA for a binary classification task with the default attribute "Smiling".
Users using
Amulet
can easily change this attribute by changing a parameter.

Model Configuration.

Amulet
also provides some basic models for quick testing, with configurable model sizes, input and output dimensions, and the ability to include or remove batch normalization where applicable:

•

VGG
[
27
]
: includes VGG11, VGG13, VGG16, and VGG19.

•

ResNet
[
17
]
: includes ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50, and ResNet101

•

LinearNet: a simple fully connected neural network with configurable layer sizes, similar to SciKit-Learn
9
9

9

https://scikit-learn.org/stable/api/sklearn.neural_network.html
.

Pipeline Modules.
We implement the following algorithms in
Amulet
:

•

S1
(Evasion) &
SD1
(Adversarial Training): We use the PGD algorithm from the CleverHans library
10
10

10

https://github.com/cleverhans-lab/cleverhans

[
32
]
to generate adversarial examples for both evasion attacks and adversarial training.

•

S2
(Poisoning): We adopt BadNets from its original implementation
11
11

11

https://github.com/Billy1900/BadNet

[
16
]
, which injects a simple trigger at the edge of every input to induce misclassification.

•

S3
(Unauthorized Model Ownership): We implement model extraction using ML-Doctor
[
28
]
, which trains a surrogate model to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between its output logits and those of the target model.

•

P1
(Membership Inference): We use LiRA
[
7
]
, which estimates membership by comparing the model’s behavior on an input to that of shadow models trained with and without the input, using a likelihood ratio test.

•

P2
(Attribute Inference): We implement the attack described in
[
1
]
, which trains an MLP classifier on the target model’s outputs to infer sensitive attributes of the input data.

•

P3
(Data Reconstruction): We use the implementation from ML-Doctor
[
28
]
, which applies gradient descent to reconstruct an input that maximizes the target model’s confidence for a given class.

•

SD2
(Outlier Removal): We implement outlier removal by deleting records with the highest Shapley values, following the data valuation approach in
[
20
]
.

•

SD3
(Fingerprinting): We implement the dataset inference method from
[
33
]
12
12

12

https://github.com/cleverhans-lab/dataset-inference/tree/main
, which determines whether a specific dataset was used to train a model by comparing adversarial vulnerability fingerprints between models.

•

SD4
(Watermarking): We use the backdoor-based neural network watermarking method from
[
3
]
13
13

13

https://github.com/adiyoss/WatermarkNN
, which embeds a unique pattern into the model’s behavior via a backdoor trigger.

•

PD1
(Differential Privacy): We incorporate differentially private stochastic gradient descent using Opacus
[
51
]
14
14

14

https://opacus.ai/api/privacy_engine.html
, which ensures formal privacy guarantees during model training.

We have shown that
Amulet
satisfies
D1
,
D3
,
D2
, and now demonstrate the
D4
applicability of
Amulet
.
First, we show the baseline results for the risks implemented in
Amulet
for the CelebA dataset.
These baselines allow us to compare the difference in metrics before and after a defense is implemented and may be used as a reference.
We use these baselines to tune the default hyperparameters in the library.
We chose the CelebA dataset for our baselines, as it is an image-based dataset that also contains sensitive attributes.
Then, we show three examples of unexplored unintended interactions.

6.1
Baseline Results

Using the setup in Section
5
, we run each experiment ten times and report the mean and standard error.
We train VGG models for 100 epochs with cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer, and evaluate each algorithm using
Amulet
’s metrics, comparing to the original paper when possible.
Baseline results are shown in Table
4
.
Overall, our reproductions closely match prior work, with exceptions noted below.

S1
(Evasion).

Prior work evaluates the PGD algorithm on CIFAR10 using an epsilon of 8 (with the data range being
0-255
).
They report that the robust accuracy (
Acc_{rob}
) is between
0.8-3.5\%
for a ResNet model (Figure 6 in Appendix C)
[
32
]
.
We reproduced the results using
Amulet
with a comparable model (ResNet34) and the same perturbation budget (
\epsilon=0.03
), obtaining a robust accuracy (
\text{Acc}_{\text{rob}}
) of
3.8\%
.
Our results using CelebA show a robust accuracy between
~8.6\%
and
~10\%
, which makes it less effective than the original work.
We suspect this is due to the CelebA dataset being much larger in size (
60,000
images vs.
\approx 202,000
images), as larger datasets result in a more robust model.

S2
(Poisoning).

The original work only tested the attack on the MNIST and traffic signs datasets
[
16
]
.
Although MNIST is no longer commonly used for algorithm evaluation — since achieving
99\%
accuracy is relatively straightforward — we reproduced the results in MNIST using a simple two-layer CNN, obtaining a poisoning accuracy (
Acc_{pois}
) of
99\%
.
Our results show that using CelebA, the poisoned model achieves a
97\%

Acc_{pois}
, while the clean model (
\mathcal{M}_{std}
) only achieves a
8\%

Acc_{pois}
.
The low accuracy of the poisoned dataset on
\mathcal{M}_{std}
suggests that the dataset is correctly poisoned.
The extremely high accuracy of the poisoned dataset on
\mathcal{M}_{def}
further suggests that the model learned the backdoor.
Given the high
Acc_{pois}
on
\mathcal{M}_{pois}
, the attack is effective.

S3
(Unauthorized Model Ownership).

Previous work evaluates model extraction using CelebA and VGG19, using an experimental setup similar to ours
[
28
]
.
However, they combine three attributes to form an 8-class classification problem instead of the binary classification problem we use.
The best fidelity they achieve between
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
and
\mathcal{M}_{std}
using VGG19 and CelebA is
~\approx 81\%
.
The model extraction attack in
Amulet
outperforms this by training a surrogate model with a fidelity of
\approx 94\%
for CelebA.
We suspect that this is because the target models in the prior work are more overfit than ours (Table 2 in
[
28
]
).
In addition, we also report the correct fidelity (
Fid_{corr}
) between both models.

Fid_{corr}
is fidelity conditioned on correctness, that is, when models agree, how often are they also correct?
Although this metric has not been reported in previous work, we include it in
Amulet
for a more comprehensive evaluation suite.

P1
(Membership Inference).

Amulet
implements the true positive rate (TPR) at a fixed false positive rate (FPR) of
1\%
, as recommended in prior work
[
7
]
.
Furthermore, we also include the
balanced accuracy
metric which finds the best accuracy given the thresholds from the ROC curve.
We also report the AUC score.
Prior work trained a ResNet model on CIFAR10 as the target model.
For the attack, they used 256 shadow models and achieved a TPR of
2.2\%
at
0.001\%
FPR and
8.4\%
TPR at
0.1\%
FPR, and a balanced accuracy of
63.8\%

[
7
]
.
To achieve results close to the original work, we had to use a ResNet-18 model and force the model to overfit (as noted by the train and test accuracies in Table
4
).
A well-trained generalized model using the entire CelebA dataset is not susceptible to this membership-inference attack.

P2
(Attribute Inference).

Recall that the goal of attribute inference is to infer the value of an attribute that is not otherwise available.
This is measured using the attack accuracy (
Acc_{att}
) and the
AUC
score.
Although prior work did not evaluate the algorithm on the CelebA dataset, we can look at the range of
Acc_{te}
across their results.
In particular, the maximum and minimum
Acc_{att}
the authors achieved was
62\%
on the Compass dataset and
53\%

Acc_{att}
on the MEPS dataset
[
1
]
, the minimum.
We achieve
\approx 57\%
accuracy for the CelebA dataset (Table
4
).
Furthermore, during the preliminary tests we achieved an accuracy of
\approx 65\%
(Race) and
\approx 59\%
(Sex) on the Census dataset, which is in line with the accuracy of the original work of
\approx 56\%
(Race) and
\approx 58\%
(Sex) on the same dataset.
Given the prior results and our preliminary testing, we believe these results to be reasonable.

P3
(Data Reconstruction).

Prior work applied the same algorithm as
Amulet
using a similar model architecture on the CelebA dataset (with 8 classes instead of 2 as mentioned under
S3
results).
However, they achieved a lower MSE (
\approx 0.1
vs.
\approx 0.2
) than our reproduction
[
28
]
.
Since the attack finds an
average
data point for a particular class, having more granular classes could lead to a better attack and a lower MSE.

SD1
(Adversarial Training) and
S3
(Unauthorized Model Ownership).

We evaluate how adversarial training (
SD1
) impacts the performance of model extraction attacks (
S3
), summarized in Table
7
.
In this setting, the adversary extracts
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
from a robustly trained model
\mathcal{M}_{def}
.
We report results across four datasets and observe that the interaction between the two defenses varies by domain and robustness level.

On
CENSUS
and
FMNIST
, adversarial training has minimal a effect on extractability.
Across increasing robustness levels (
\epsilon_{rob}
from
0.01
to
0.10
), the surrogate model
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
maintains high fidelity (
Fid
) to the robust model
\mathcal{M}_{def}
, achieving up to
\approx 98\%
and
\approx 96\%
, respectively.
Correct fidelity (
Fid_{cor}
) remains stable, around
84\%
for
CENSUS
, and decreases from
\approx 88\%
to
\approx 82\%
from
FMNIST
.
Since
\mathcal{M}_{def}
retains high test accuracy (
Acc_{te}
),
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
effectively approximates both its behavior and correctness.

In contrast, on more complex datasets like
LFW
and
CIFAR
, we observe a different pattern.
Fidelity (
Fid
) continues to increase with stronger adversarial training, showing that
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
is more closely tracking
\mathcal{M}_{def}
’s predictions.
However,
Fid_{cor}
drops substantially, which is expected because
\mathcal{M}_{def}
itself becomes less accurate at higher
\epsilon_{rob}
values.
For instance, on
CIFAR
, at
\epsilon_{rob}=0.10
,
\mathcal{M}_{def}
has
Acc_{te}\approx 62\%
and
Acc_{rob}\approx 19\%
, while
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
achieves only
\approx 61\%
test accuracy and a
Fid
of
\approx 87\%
(the highest on
CIFAR
).
This indicates that
\mathcal{M}_{stol}
faithfully replicates a model that is increasingly incorrect.

Taken together, these findings suggest that adversarial training can, in fact,
increase
susceptibility to model extraction, as it leads to more consistent and predictable decision boundaries that are easier to imitate.
While adversarial training reduces test accuracy and increases robustness of the model itself, it appears to also enhance the extractability of the model’s behavior.
More robust training may thus inadvertently aid an attacker in constructing high-fidelity surrogates
However, this requires more research with better-tuned adversarial training to confirm this trend more conclusively.

and

).

7
Discussion

Extending
Amulet
to modern architectures.

Most modules in
Amulet
assume simple model architectures that are now obsolete in favor of transformer-based models or diffusion models.
We did this because most ML security research up until recently has used these old architectures to evaluate algorithms.
Since
Amulet
uses implementations from popular ML security research papers, it is not trivial to adapt every algorithm to work with more modern architectures.
Although it is easy to plug these modern architectures into
Amulet
, it is questionable whether the same algorithm would perform as expected.
Thus, more research would be required to understand how applicable older algorithms are to more modern architectures.

Adding more attacks and defenses.

As explained in Section
4
, while
Amulet
covers a wide range of risks, the majority of the modules in
Amulet
are not state-of-the-art attacks or defenses for a particular risk.
While selecting the initial algorithms to include in
Amulet
, we prioritized algorithms with open-source implementations that have been analyzed by multiple peer-reviewed articles.
Furthermore, each algorithm that we implemented required rigorous testing, as our goal was to use the algorithm with a variety of datasets and models.
Thus, it would be helpful if the open source community could add more attacks and defenses to
Amulet
to make it more useful to study unintended interactions.

Studying conflicting defenses.

While
Amulet
is good at studying the effect of one defense on another unrelated risk, it is still difficult to study how two defenses interact with each other.
For example, both adversarial training and DP-SGD are applied during training, such case is not implemented by
Amulet
.
This is important to study for real-world deployments where a user may need to defend their models against multiple risks at the same time
[
43
]
.

For example, the effect of
SD1
on fingerprinting (
SD3
) is probably worth studying.
Depending on the techniques used, many fingerprinting techniques exploit decision boundaries
[
33
,
44
]
and transferrable adversarial examples
[
31
,
43
]
in some way to verify model ownership.
Since
SD1
affects the decision boundary and adversarial examples of the model, it is questionable whether
SD3
will work alongside
SD1
.
