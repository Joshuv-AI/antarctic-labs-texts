EBV (LMP1) enhances cisplatin resistance by enhancing degradation of interferon regulatory factor 3
Authors: Yueshuo Li, Na Liu, Chenxing Yang, Shan He, Cheng Luo, Lin Wang, Min Tang, Xiangjian Luo, Ya Cao, Li Shang, Feng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEBV employs multiple strategies to subvert host innate immunity, including suppression of type I IFN signaling. However, the precise mechanism by which its latent oncoprotein LMP1 modulates this pathway in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) remains unclear. Here, we demonstrated that LMP1 enhances the interaction between interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and E3 ubiquitin ligase Ro52, which leads to the degradation of IRF3 protein. Low IRF3 expression correlates with poor prognosis in NPC patients. Notably, IRF3 may inhibit cell G1/S transition by directly inducing CDKN2C transcription, which suggests the direct effect as a moonlighting protein in regulating cell cycle. Cisplatin is an effective DNA-damaging anti-tumor agent which may activate type I IFNs-mediated anti-tumor immunity by stimulating the release of dsDNA. EBV (LMP1) inhibits cisplatin-activated type I IFN signaling by reducing IRF3 expression. The activation of IRF3 enhances the sensitivity of EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC cells to cisplatin in vitro and in vivo. These findings establish the LMP1-Ro52-IRF3 axis as a critical immune evasion mechanism that drives cisplatin resistance and suggest that targeting this axis represents a viable strategy to enhance chemotherapy efficacy in EBV-positive NPC.Keywords: Epstein-Barr virus, Interferon regulatory factor 3, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Ubiquitination, Cisplatin resistance

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a significant subtype of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma prevalent in southern China and Hong Kong. The pathogenesis of NPC is influenced by viral, genetic, and environmental factors [1, 2]. Currently, the main treatment for NPC is intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) combined with chemotherapy [3, 4]. Cisplatin-based chemotherapy treatments, including induction chemotherapy and concurrent chemotherapy, significantly improved distant metastases and prolonged survival in patients with NPC [5, 6]. Despite this, more than 30% of patients with NPC still have recurrence and metastasis [7]. Therefore, a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms that regulate treatment resistance in NPC will help distinguish patients at different risks and develop effective treatment strategies.

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection is the leading cause of NPC, which infects more than 95% of adults worldwide. EBV employs multiple effective strategies to evade innate immune sensing and block the subsequent antiviral response, thereby enabling persistent infection [8–11]. Latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1), which is the key oncogenic protein encoded by EBV. It drives tumor formation by regulating diverse processes such as survival, immune evasion, metastasis, and metabolism in a context-dependent manner [8, 12].

Type I interferon (IFN) response is a vital antiviral defense mechanism in host cells. EBV has been reported to suppress the type I IFN signaling pathway by decreasing the expression or activity of ISGs [13–15]. Besides, EBV disturbs mitochondrial dynamics and stimulates mitophagy to dampen type I IFNs induction [16]. Interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) such as IRF3, IRF5, IRF7, and IRF8 act as positive regulators of type I IFN gene induction downstream of viral RNA/DNA-sensing pattern recognition receptors [17]. EBV-LMP1 plays multifaceted roles in regulating innate immunity to achieve long-term viral persistence and pathogenesis: it activates the negative regulator IRF4 to inhibit type I IFN responses [18]. Besides, LMP1 induces IRF7 expression, and differentially modulates IRF7 ubiquitination—promoting it through TRAF6 while inhibiting it via the deubiquitinase A20—thereby exploiting both ubiquitination and deubiquitination machinery to subvert host antiviral pathways [19, 20]. However, the exact roles of LMP1 in regulating IRFs in NPC remain unclear.

EBV is well known to manipulate the host ubiquitin machinery to facilitate its persistence and oncogenesis [8]. Studies have found that EBV can induce the ubiquitination and degradation of STING, and thereby interfere with the activation of STING-IRF3-IFNs signaling transduction through the cellular E3 ligase TRIM29 [21–23]. Additionally, EBV-encoded deubiquitinase BPLF1 decreases the K63 ubiquitination of STING and TBK1, which suppresses IFN-β production and innate immune response [24].

Our previous work has reported that EBV inhibits deubiquitinase CYLD expression to facilitate host cell cycle G1/S transition for viral replication and cancer cell proliferation [25]. We also found EBV (LMP1) promotes K63-linked polyubiquitination of RIPK1 through up-regulating the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF2 to resist host cell necroptosis [26]. Further research is still necessary to clarify the regulatory mechanism of EBV on the ubiquitin system.

In this work, we will investigate the mechanism of EBV (LMP1)- regulated IRF3 signaling. And further explore the role of IRF3 in EBV (LMP1)-induced cisplatin-resistance of NPC. Better understanding of the molecular mechanisms will provide an experimental basis for effectively triggering tumor-specific immune responses and enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy in NPC.

Materials and methods

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR

Total mRNA was isolated by using the NucleoZOL reagent (Cat: 740404, Macherey Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany). The RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat: K16325, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for reverse transcription. Quantitative PCR analysis was performed in triplicate using the SYBRTM Green Master Mix (Cat: A25742, Invitrogen) and the ABI7500 Real-Time System (Applied Biosystems). All primers used in this study were listed in the supplemental table.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was conducted as previously described [25]. Visualization was performed using the ChemiDoc XRS system and Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Western blots were derived from the same experiment and processed in parallel.

Ubiquitination assay

Ubiquitination assays were performed as described [25]. The specific steps are as follows. Cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 48 h followed by treatment with MG132 (10 µM) or Bortezomib (100 nM) for for 6 h. Cell were disrupted in 1% SDS lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors for 50 min, then cell lysates were heated at 95 °C for 10 min and diluted 10 times in non-SDS buffer and samples were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with rotation. Then the samples were washed and prepared for immunoblot analysis as described previously.

Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry/ mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

Clarified lysates from 107 cells was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of specific primary antibodies or an isotype-matched negative control IgG. Subsequently, the samples were incubated for 1 h with 200 µl of magnetic beads conjugated with protein G (Invitrogen catalog no. 10004D) and then washed three times with IP wash buffer. They were then subjected to liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis (Bioprofile, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescence analysis was conducted as previously described [29]. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Interacting proteins were detected by the DuoLink® In Situ Red Starter Kit Mouse/Rabbit (DUO92101, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany). Cells were seeded in 8-well chamber slides (Millicell EZ SLIDE, Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and cultured overnight. Experimental steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol and conducted as described previously [26]. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was examined using the CCK-8 solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The plate was read at 450 nm wavelength on a microplate reader (Biotek EL × 800, USA).

Chromatin immuno-precipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were performed using Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Kits (Catalog # 17-10085, Millipore). Cells were lysed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell lysates were sonicated to shear DNA to 200–1000 bp and insoluble material was removed. Subsequent steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Finally, DNA was purified with spin columns and eluted with 20 ml of ddH2O and subjected to quantitative PCR analyses.

Luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase activities were detected using Dual-Luciferase Reporter Kit (E1910, Promega) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Cells were transfected as firefly luciferase reporter gene plasmid: sea kidney luciferase reporter gene plasmid = 50:1. After 48 h, cell were cleaved by Passive Lysis Buffer (PLB) 100 µl. 15–30 min, collecting the pyrolysis liquid. First measurement: add PLB 20 µl into the plate with light transmission at the bottom. Add Luciferase Assay Buffer II 100 µl to avoid light. The first fluorescence value is measured by chemiluminescence method. Second fluorescence value measurement: add 100 µl Stop&Glo reagent for the second fluorescence value measurement. Result analysis: Ratio= (first fluorescence value background fluorescence value)/ (second fluorescence value background fluorescence value).

Clinical specimens

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. 201803134). Nasopharyngitis and NPC tissues were collected from the department of pathology at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, Changsha, China. All clinical data were obtained from the hospital pathologic records. The NPC tissue array (n = 140) was purchased from Outdo Biotech (NPC1401, Shang Hai, China).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted as described previously [30] The staining score of each protein was analyzed by two pathologists independently. The immunohistochemistry score depended on the degree of staining and the rate of positive cells. Slides were scanned by Pannoramic MIDI digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH). The patients were divided into low and high expression groups according to the median scores. IHC images were acquired by a BioTek Lionheart FX automated microscope (Agilent).

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence of NPC tissue array was detected using Multiplex immunofluorescence staining assay kit (AFIHC025, AiFang biological, China) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The tyramide signal amplification was TSA (520-TSA, 620-TSA). Multispectral images were analyzed, and positive cells were quantified at a single-cell level by inForm 2.6.0 and Phenochart 1.0.9 image analysis software.

Tumor xenograft studies

All animal studies were performed in compliance with ethical guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Central South University (No. 2020sydw0277). Female BALB/c-nude mice (5–6-week-old) were purchased from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Changsha, China). All mice were subcutaneously inoculated with HONE1-EBV cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) in the right armpit. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm3 (day 9 after injection), the mice were divided randomly into 4 groups (n = 6 each; saline- treated (control), KIN1148-treated, cisplatin -treated, cisplatin and KIN1148-treated). Single-drug treatment with KIN1148 (25 mg/kg), or cisplatin (4 mg/kg) was initiated by i.p. injection. The vehicle control was administered in 0.9% saline. Tumor volume and B.W. were recorded every 2 days. The tumor size was calculated as follows: tumor size = ab2/ 2, where a and b are the larger and smaller diameters, respectively. After 10 days of treatment, the mice were euthanized, and tumors were removed and weighed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were performed with the GraphPad Prism 9 software. The experimental data are presented as the mean value ± SD. The statistical significance of the data was analyzed using ANOVA or a standard Student’s t test. Overall and disease-free survival were determined by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using a log-rank test. P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

EBV (LMP1) inhibits the induction of type I IFNs by reducing IRF3 expression

IRF3 is the essential transcription factor for inducing type I IFN and ISGs. We found the protein expression of IRF3 was significantly down-regulated in EBV-LMP1-positive NPC cells (Fig. 1A). Upon activation of innate immune signaling cascades, such as cGAS-STING pathway, IRF3 goes through diverse processes, including phosphorylation, dimerization and translocation to the nucleus, which initiates the transcription of type I interferons (type I IFN) [31, 32]. To further explore the role of EBV (LMP1) in type I IFN signaling, Interferon stimulatory DNAs (ISD, a cytosolic DNA mimic) were used to trigger IRF3 activation. The results showed that upon ISD stimulation, IRF3 was significantly phosphorylated and dimerized in EBV (LMP1)-negative NPC cells, whereas no notable IRF3 activation was detected in EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC cells (Figure S1). The expression of IRF3-responsive type I IFN genes (IFNA4 and IFNB) induced by ISD was measured by qPCR (quantitative PCR) and ELISA. Compared with that in EBV (LMP1)-negative NPC cells, the mRNA and protein expression of IFN-α4 and IFN-β were drastically abolished in EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC cells in response to ISD treatment (Fig. 1C-F). These results remind us that EBV (LMP1) may disrupt type I IFN signaling. Then, IRF3 was exogenous-expressed in EBV-LMP1 positive cells (Fig. 1B). Compared with those in empty vector control, the induction of IFN-α4 and IFN-β was drastically recovered in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells with IRF3-exogenous expression in response to ISD treatment (Fig. 1K-N). These results indicated that EBV (LMP1) may disturb type I IFN signaling by down-regulating IRF3 expression.

EBV (LMP1) induces the K48-linked ubiquitination and protein degradation of IRF3

Since EBV-LMP1 did not obviously affect the IRF3 mRNA level (Figure S2), the protein level of IRF3 was analyzed following the treatment with proteasome inhibitors MG132, lysosomal inhibitors Baf-A1, and pan-caspase inhibitors Z-VAD. The results showed that MG132 significantly increased the protein level of IRF3 (Figure S3). The downregulation of IRF3 in HONE1-EBV cells could be recovered by the proteasome inhibitor, MG132, in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2A-B). Since MG132 could significantly decrease HNE2-LMP1 cell viability, another proteasome inhibitor, Bortezomib, was used in HNE2-LMP1, and similar results were obtained (Fig. 2C-D). These data suggested that LMP1 downregulates the protein level of IRF3 by promoting its proteasomal degradation. Then, cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit protein biosynthesis, and protein extracts obtained at indicated time points were analyzed. We found that the half-life of IRF3 protein was significantly decreased in EBV-positive NPC cells (Fig. 2E-F). To further understand the underlying mechanism, we measured the level of polyubiquitylation of IRF3. The results showed that IRF3 polyubiquitylation was evaluated in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Fig. 2G). K48-linked polyubiquitylation is mainly responsible for protein degradation. As shown in Fig. 3H, K48-linked polyubiquitylation of IRF3 was significantly increased in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Fig. 2H). These results suggest that EBV (LMP1) may promote the degradation of IRF3 protein through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

EBV-LMP1 enhances IRF3 degradation by increasing the interaction with Ro52

E3 ubiquitin ligases determine the specific recognition of target proteins in ubiquitin-proteasome degradation signaling. To search for the E3 ligase of IRF3, LC-MS/MS was used to identify IRF3-associated proteins. The E3 ligase Ro52, the 52 members of the TRIM family, was present in the purified IRF3 complexes (Table S2). To confirm the interaction between Ro52 and IRF3, the proximity ligation assay (PLA) was used. The assay will generate a positive signal only when the distance between the two proteins is less than 40 nm and is highly suited for directly detecting protein interactions. In this approach, fluorescent intensity signals were observed in HONE1-EBV cells (Fig. 3A), suggesting that Ro52 may directly interact with IRF3. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed the colocalization of LMP1, Ro52, and IRF3 (Fig. 3B-C). Notably, Ro52 significantly down-regulated IRF3 protein level with LMP1 co-expression, while Ro52 over-expression alone did not influence the level (Fig. 3D). Then, we detected the direct interaction between Ro52 and IRF3 with or without LMP1 expression. As shown in Fig. 3E-F, fluorescent positive signals were significantly increased in LMP1-expression HONE1 cells. These results indicate that LMP1 may be located in the same complex as Ro52 and IRF3, and it plays a crucial role in the interaction.

Next, we investigated whether LMP1 regulates the E3 ligase activity of Ro52. It has been showed that Ro52 could form oligomers under high ROS state, which results in reduced E3 ligase activity [33]. Our previous study demonstrated that EBV induces high levels of oxidative stress in NPC cells [34]. Therefore, Ro52 oligomer was detected by non-reduced SDS page, and the result showed that LMP1 expression and the antioxidant NAC treatment inhibits the oligomer formation of Ro52 (Fig. 3G). This suggested that LMP1 may maintain the E3 ligase activity of Ro52 by protecting it from oxidation. It has been reported that neddylation of Ro52 enhanced its E3 ligase activity and induced the interaction to target protein [35]. Thus, we investigated whether LMP1 influences the neddylation of Ro52. In LMP1-overexpression HONE1 cells, IRF3, NEDD8 and LMP1 were detected in the immunoblots of endogenous Ro52 protein immunoprecipitates. Treatment with the neddylation inhibitor, MLN4924, markedly reduced the intensity of NEDD8. Conversely, MLN4924 significantly restored both the total protein level of IRF3 and the Ro52-bound IRF3 (Fig. 3H). Ubiquitylation assays showed that LMP1 significantly increased the wild type (WT) and K48-linked polyubiquitination of IRF3 (Fig. 3I). These results indicated that EBV-LMP1 promotes the interaction between Ro52 and IRF3, which leads to the enhanced degradation of IRF3. To identify the interaction domain of LMP1 responsible for Ro52 binding, we constructed plasmids encoding truncated LMP1 variants (Fig. 3J). Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that only LMP1 constructs retaining the CTAR3 domain were capable of binding to Ro52 (Fig. 3K), indicating that the CTAR3 region is essential for the LMP1-Ro52 interaction.

Low expression of IRF3 is positively associated with poor prognosis of NPC patients

The expression of IRF3 and LMP1 was analyzed in pathological sections of nasopharyngitis (NP) and NPC patients. Results revealed that IRF3 expression was down-regulated in NPC tissues, and was negatively associated with EBV-LMP1 expression (Fig. 4A-C; Table 1). In addition, immunofluorescent staining in NPC tissue microarray showed low IRF3 protein expression was detected in NPC tissues, whereas high expression was present in adjacent noncancerous tissues (Fig. 4D-E). The negative relationship of IRF3 and LMP1 expression was also determined in these samples (Fig. 4F; Table 2) Next, we dichotomized the 126 NPC samples in the tissue microarray into a high IRF3 expression group and a low IRF3 expression group for Kaplan–Meier analysis, which revealed that the patients with low IRF3 expression had significantly shorter locoregional recurrence-free survival (median survival time: 97 months vs. 110 months) and overall survival times (median survival time: 85 months vs. 102 months) (Fig. 4G-H). Also, low IRF3 expression had a significant relationship with patients’ recurrence and metastasis (Table 3). These results demonstrated that low IRF3 expression indicates a poor survival prognosis, as well as a high likelihood of recurrence and metastasis in NPC patients. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis showed that IRF3 expression, lymph node metastasis, and advanced clinical stage were significantly correlated with poorer overall survival and recurrence-free survival (Tables 4 and 5).

IRF3 inhibits cell G1/S transition by inducing CDKN2C expression

According to the clinicopathologic parameters analysis, low IRF3 expression was significantly associated with a high Ki67 positive rate (Table 3). This indicates that IRF3 may directly regulate NPC cell proliferation. To investigate the effect of IRF3 on EBV (LMP1) mediated tumorigenesis, we conducted cell proliferation assays. The results revealed that IRF3 over-expression inhibited the cell viability and colony formation of EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Fig. 5A-B). Further, flow cytometry analysis showed that IRF3 over-expression significantly decreased the cell cycle G1/S transition (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that IRF3 inhibits cell proliferation by delaying G1/S transition. Since IRF3 mainly works as a transcriptional factor, we further investigate whether IRF3 regulates transcription of cell cycle-related genes. Online database (Database of Human Transcription Factor Targets, hTFtarget) was used to predicate the potential transcriptional target genes of IRF3. Then the target genes were intersected with the down-regulated differential genes in EBV-positive NPC cells. We found the presence of CDKN2C in the potential target genes (Fig. 5D). CDKN2C encodes Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs) p18, which inhibits cell G1/S phase transition. Real-time PCR and Immunoblot analysis showed that CDKN2C (p18) was down-regulated in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Fig. 5E-F and I).

To verify whether IRF3 indeed transcriptionally regulated CDKN2C expression, we transfected cells with IRF3 plasmid, which resulted in significant increase of CDKN2C (p18) expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5G-H and J). We analyzed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data of 113 NPC patients (GSE102349). Based on the expression level of IRF3 and CDKN2C genes, patients were divided into high expression and low expression groups. IRF3 and CDKN2C were significantly positively correlated (Fig. 5K; Table 6). Promoter analysis identified eight putative binding sites of IRF3 in the promoter region of CDKN2C gene (Fig. 5L). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) PCR analysis revealed that IRF3 was indeed recruited to #1, #3, and #4 sites (Fig. 5M). Single expression of these sites can also enhance the CDKN2C promoter activity, suggesting that IRF3 regulates CDKN2C expression through binding to these sites (Fig. 5N). To determine whether p18 is required for IRF3 function, we examined the effect of p18 knockdown on IRF3-mediated cell proliferation. As shown in Figure S4A-B, the reduced proliferative capacity of IRF3-overexpressing cells was significantly rescued by p18 knockdown. Our results identified that IRF3 inhibits cell G1/S transition by inducing CDKN2C (p18) expression.

EBV (LMP1) decreases the production of type I IFNs induced by cisplatin

EBV infection is the leading risk factor in cisplatin resistance to nasopharyngeal carcinoma [36, 37]. LMP1 plays a central role in promoting therapeutic resistance in related tumors. Our previous studies have found that excessive oxidative stress and attenuated DNA damage response induced by EBV-LMP1 could result in the radioresistance of NPC [34, 38]. In addition, EBV-LMP1 may increase mitochondrial fission and decrease mitochondrial membrane permeability, which contributes to the chemoresistance of NPC [39, 40]. In this work, we also found that the IC50 of cisplatin in EBV (LMP1)-negative cells was dramatically lower than that in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Fig. 6A-C, Figure S5A). This result indicated that EBV-encoded oncoprotein LMP1 promotes cisplatin resistance in NPC cells. Cisplatin treatment could lead to the release of cytoplasmic DNA as a consequence of nuclear DNA damage [41]. Research has reported that DNA fragments induced by chemotherapy can be recognized in tumor cells, which further initiates IRF3 signaling cascade, induces type I IFN production, enhances the anti-tumor immune response, and kills NPC cells [42]. Since we identified that EBV (LMP1) inhibited the IRF3-IFNs pathways. We investigated whether EBV-LMP1 is involved in cisplatin-induced activation of type I IFN innate immune signaling. To confirm this hypothesis, we first revealed that cytosolic dsDNA was significantly elevated after cisplatin treatment in NPC cells (Fig. 6D-E). Further, the production of type I IFNs (IFN-α4 and IFN-β) induced by cisplatin treatment was measured by ELISA (Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay). Upon cisplatin treatment, the levels of IFN-α4 and IFN-β in EBV (LMP1)-negative NPC cells were significantly increased, while the levels in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells were not changed significantly (Fig. 6F-G). Consistent with this observation, LMP1 overexpression significantly abrogated the cisplatin- induced upregulation of phosphorylated IRF3 (Figure S5B). These results suggested that EBV (LMP1) may disturb cisplatin-activated type I IFN signaling in NPC cells.

Activated IRF3 enhances the sensitivity of EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC cells to cisplatin in vitro and in vivo

As we mentioned above, IRF3 plays a key role in both cisplatin-induced type I IFN signaling and cell G1/S transition. Given that IFN signaling and cell cycle progression are two mechanisms associated with cancer treatment resistance in malignancies [43, 44]. Therefore, we wondered whether the activation of IRF3 could alleviate EBV-induced cisplatin resistance in NPC. We found that IRF3 over-expression significantly decreased the IC50 of cisplatin in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Figure S6A-B). The treatment of IRF3 agonist KIN1148 significantly decreased cell viability in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells (Fig. 7A-B). In addition, KIN1148 combined with cisplatin significantly reduced cell viability compared to cisplatin or KIN1148 alone (Fig. 7A-B, S6C). Further, a xenograft mouse model was used to determine whether activated IRF3 sensitizes NPC cells to cisplatin in vivo (Fig. 7C). As shown in Fig. 7D-F, the tumor volume and weight were significantly decreased in KIN1148 treatment group (Fig. 7D-F). Moreover, there is a significant reduction in tumor volume (Fig. 7D-E) and tumor weight (Fig. 7F) in the KIN1148 and cisplatin combination group compared to the other groups. Additionally, the immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that p18 expression was significantly increased in the KIN1148 and KIN1148 and cisplatin combination treatment groups. Moreover, the number of TUNEL-positive cells was higher in the KIN1148 and cisplatin combination group (Fig. 7G-I). These results indicated activation of IRF3 enhances the sensitivity of EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC to cisplatin (Fig. 8).

Discussion

This work explored the role of IRF3 in EBV (LMP1)-induced cisplatin-resistance. We found that EBV-LMP1 activated the E3 ubiquitinase Ro52, which promoted the degradation of the key transcription factor IRF3. Loss expression of IRF3 disturbed cisplatin-activated type I IFN signaling, and also accelerated the G1/S process in EBV (LMP1)-positive cells, which enhanced cisplatin-resistance and tumor cell viability. Based on these, IRF3 agonist can effectively enhance the cytotoxicity effect of cisplatin in EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC (Fig. 8).

The EBV-encoded oncoprotein LMP1 has been extensively described in ubiquitination-relative functions [45, 46]. LMP1 recruits TRAF1/2/6 to activate MAPK and NF-κB pathways [47, 48, suppresses necroptosis via TRAF-mediated RIPK1/RIPK3 ubiquitination [49, induces p53 stability through MDM2 and TRAF2 [50], and promotes malignant phenotypes via Ubc9 interaction or UCH-L1/USP2a induction [51–53]. In NPC, Ro52 promotes VDAC2 degradation via K48 ubiquitination, reducing radiation-induced anti-tumor immunity [54]. Ro52, a TRIM family RING E3 ligase, regulates immune responses by targeting IRFs for degradation. Ro52 mediates IRF3 ubiquitination to negatively regulate IFN-β expression [55–57, though this regulation is context-dependent [58, 59]. However, Ro52’s role in EBV infection remains unclear. In this work, we showed that LMP1 positively regulates E3 ligase Ro52 activity and promotes the degradation of its target protein IRF3.

It is worth noticing that Ro52 induces IRF3 K48-linked ubiquitination only in the presence of EBV-LMP1 which strengthens the interaction of Ro52 and IRF3. Our data suggest that LMP1 may regulate the enzyme activity of Ro52. We previously found that EBV triggers “Redox Resetting” in NPC cells—a state marked by both high ROS and enhanced antioxidant capacity via NOX2 upregulation and Nrf2 activation [34]. Redox dysregulation drives disease pathogenesis through two primary mechanisms: direct ROS-induced oxidative damage to biomolecules or aberrant redox signaling [60]. As second messengers, ROS induce direct oxidative modification of active cysteine residues, resulting in functional changes of the modified proteins [61]. Ro52 forms oligomers under high ROS state, which reduces its E3 ligase activity [33]. In this work, we found that EBV-LMP1 interacts with Ro52 and inhibits the formation of oligomers, suggesting that EBV-LMP1 may protect Ro52 from oxidation by the interaction. Further, the neddylation of RING E3 ligases is important for the enzyme activity. The neddylation of Ro52 is necessary for the binding to its target proteins [35]. Here we found that EBV-LMP1 could also induce the neddylation of Ro52. The neddylation inhibitor decreased the neddylation level of Ro52 and recovered IRF3 expression.

Thus far, most IRF3-centric studies have focused on its function in innate immune cells. It has been proven that IRF3 exerts cancer-promoting functions in some cases. IRF3 is ubiquitously expressed in cells with distinct origins. In adipocytes, IRF3 inhibits glycolysis via transcribing ISG15 [62]. The role of IRF3 in tumor cells remains controversial. Studies have reported that IRF3 negatively regulates Wnt signaling by inhibiting the nuclear translocation of β-catenin in colorectal cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma patients [63]. In this work, we identified that IRF3 inhibits cell proliferation by transcribing cell cycle inhibitor CDKN2C (p18), which delays cell G1-S transition. Besides, we confirmed that patients with low IRF3 expression suggested poor overall survival and recurrence-free survival.

Manipulation of the host cell cycle is a shared feature of gamma-herpesviruses (GHVs). By controlling the host cyclin-CKI axis, GHVs promote its infection and the pathogenesis, including acute lung infection, viral persistence, and reactivation from latency [64]. CDKN2C (p18), a member of INK4 family, mainly inhibits cell G1-S transition, which restricts GHVs infection and reactivation. Some GHVs, including KSHV, GHV68, and herpesvirus saimiri, are able to bypass host cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs) by encoding a conserved viral cyclin (v-cyclin) which is homologous to host cyclin D [65]. EBV genome does not encode a v-cyclin, while it can up-regulate host cyclin D expression and down-regulate CKIs expression. Our previous study has shown that EBV could enhance p18 degradation by inhibiting its deubiquitinase [25]. In this study, we identified that IRF3 works as a transcriptional activator of CDKN2C (p18), and EBV-LMP1 decreases CDKN2C (p18) expression by accelerating IRF3 degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Our findings characterize a novel strategy to intervene host CKI p18 with EBV-LMP1.

Cisplatin is a DNA-damaging anti-tumor agent that is highly effective in treating NPC. It predominantly induces a transient S-phase arrest and a robust G2/M-arrest, which allows DNA repair and prevents DNA replication or mitosis in the presence of genomic damage [66, 67]. When repair fails, DNA damage leads to apoptosis [68]. Therefore, cell cycle arrest and cell fate are intimately linked under cisplatin treatment. Specifically, slowly proliferating cells within tumors showed a higher probability of death than highly proliferative cells, which may be acutely vulnerable to chemotherapy [69]. In this study, we discovered a promising role of IRF3 in regulating the CDKN2C expression, EBV(LMP1) promoted cell cycle G1-S phase transition by suppressing IRF3. This allows DNA-damaged cells to repair their DNA before proceeding with DNA synthesis. KIN1148 (IRF3 agonist) arrested the cell cycle in the G1 phase, which prevented cells from entering the S phase and failing to repair cisplatin-induced DNA damage. This interrelationship has the potential to enhance cisplatin sensitivity in NPC.

Although type I IFNs were initially described based on their anti-viral properties, it was quickly realized that these cytokines had anti-cancer activities [44, 70]. DNA-damaging chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin, may stimulate the release of dsDNA (Double-stranded DNA) into the cytoplasm and activate DNA recognition receptors, which plays a major role in type I IFNs-mediated anti-tumor immunity [41].

In this work, we found that cisplatin failed to induced type I IFN production in EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC cells. Further, it was identified that EBV-LMP1 mediates IRF3 protein degradation by regulating Ro52 activity, promoting cell cycle progression from G1 to S and preventing IRF3-type I IFNs signal. Treatment with IRF3 agonist KIN1148 effectively enhanced the chemotherapy sensitivity of EBV (LMP1)-positive NPC cells to cisplatin in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 8). Notably, patients might be benefit from the combined therapy of IRF3 agonists and cisplatin, thereby providing novel perspectives for the precision therapy of NPC.
