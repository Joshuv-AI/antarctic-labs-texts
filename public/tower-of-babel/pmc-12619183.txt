Protocol to examine the feasibility and acceptability of a randomized controlled trial of a chaplain-delivered compassion intervention to improve psychological safety among interprofessional healthcare teams
Authors: Nicholas A Giordano, Deanna M Kaplan, Caroline Peacock, Ishani Vyas, Nicole Pozzo, Maureen Shelton, Cam Escoffery, Shaheen Rana, Charles L Raison, George H Grant, Jennifer S Mascaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare provider burnout is pervasive, imparts harm to providers and patients, increases healthcare and workforce disparities, and exacerbates physician and nursing shortages. Although burnout is primarily caused by organizational stressors, supportive and cooperative interdisciplinary teams foster psychological safety and are protective against burnout. To address the critical and unmet need for evidence-based, acceptable, and scalable interventions to improve burnout, this study will evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the Compassion-Centered Spiritual Health Team Intervention (CCSH-TI), a novel team-based intervention delivered by healthcare chaplains embedded within the interprofessional team. Using CCSH-TI, chaplains teach mindfulness and compassion-based approaches designed to bolster compassion for self and others and to improve psychological safety and team civility.MethodsThis study will utilize a phase 1 clustered randomized, “standard of care” (SOC) controlled, and mixed-method design to examine the feasibility and acceptability of CCSH-TI. Employees (n = 80; nurses, advanced practice providers (APPs), physicians, staff) working at a National Cancer Institute-Designated Comprehensive Cancer Center will be randomized by team to CCSH-TI or to SOC. We will evaluate CCSH-TI feasibility (accrual, retention, CCSH-TI attendance) and acceptability (satisfaction, credibility, perceived benefit). Focus groups will identify contextual determinants of feasibility, acceptability, and implementation success. Prior to and after the intervention, as well as at 12-week follow-up, we will conduct 3-day ambulatory assessments: (1) ecological momentary assessments (EMA) of social connection, incivility, and burnout and (2) electronically activated recorder (EAR), a naturalistic observation method that samples ambient sound from participants’ momentary environments used widely outside healthcare settings. We will develop and validate EAR behavioral codebooks tailored to the healthcare environment to quantify behavioral indices of psychological safety, incivility, and interprofessional teamwork.DiscussionThis study will provide data on the acceptability and feasibility of a chaplain-delivered team intervention for interprofessional healthcare teams working in oncology. The findings will inform the design of a future study to examine this innovative and scalable approach to increase access, equity, and inclusion of burnout prevention and mitigation among all healthcare providers. Background

Healthcare provider burnout—characterized by feelings of emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced personal accomplishment—is pervasive, costly, and undermines both provider well-being and the quality of patient care [1–12]. In addition to reducing patient safety and exacerbating the impending physician and nursing workforce shortages, burnout increases providers’ risk for depression, sleep dysfunction, suicidal ideation, substance abuse, and chronic disease [13–23]. Although provider burnout is largely caused by organizational stressors [24], supportive and cooperative interdisciplinary teams are protective against burnout, depression, and medical error [25–32]. Interprofessional support and collaboration are especially critical in oncology, where comprehensive cancer treatment requires efficient teamwork. However, compared to their healthcare peers, physicians and nurses in oncology demonstrate a higher risk for burnout due to the frequency of patient death, stressful workload, and requirement to manage patients with high levels of pain and treatment plans that cause adverse side effects [33–35]. The National Academy of Medicine (NAM) and the US surgeon general have made it a national priority to develop acceptable, effective, and scalable interventions that support the interprofessional collaboration of healthcare teams to improve the well-being of healthcare providers and to address the needs of the twenty-first century healthcare workforce [36].

Current interventions to reduce provider burnout focus on individual resiliency and are only minimally effective [37]. Though there have been very few robust evaluations of team-based interventions, the evidence base indicates that improving teamwork and fostering a culture of professional civility and safety are among the most effective approaches to reducing burnout [38]. Psychological safety, the shared belief within a team that it is safe to take interpersonal risks such as asking questions, sharing ideas, and reporting mistakes or problems [39], is vital for interprofessional healthcare teams [40] and is consistently found to be protective of burnout [41, 42]. Several studies have identified psychological safety as the mediating link between improved healthcare environment and reduced burnout [41–44]. In contrast, workplace incivility and mistreatment are consistently associated with burnout across licensure and professional status [23, 45–48]. Taken together, these findings support the development and testing of team-based interventions designed to improve the psychological safety of interprofessional oncology teams. Despite the urgent clinical need for interventions to bolster interprofessional teamwork and reduce burnout, barriers to accessing burnout interventions arise at individual and healthcare-system levels [49]. To overcome the barriers to achieving success in burnout prevention interventions, the Compassion-Centered Spiritual Health Team Intervention (CCSH-TI) was developed (Fig. 1).

Scientific rationale for the intervention and study protocol

CCSH-TI is a teams-based approach that incorporates elements that are ongoing (as opposed to “one-off”) [50], embedded within the clinic setting [51], and prioritizes organization-directed approaches that address team dynamics and working environment (as opposed to only focusing on individual resilience) [49, 52, 53]. CCSH-TI leverages a well-established and highly acceptable clinical service that successfully overcomes provider- and system-level barriers commonly encountered when developing new approaches to address provider well-being [54, 55]. Chaplains are present in half to two-thirds of all hospitals in the USA, and they are trained to recognize and respond to emotional, psychosocial, spiritual, and moral distress of both patients and healthcare providers [56, 57]. Previous research indicates that CCSH-TI was a feasible, credible, acceptable, and effective intervention to augment individual resilience among team-based oncology clinical research coordinators [54]. Prior to evaluating the effectiveness of CCSH-TI (or any interventions to reduce burnout and improve psychological safety), research is necessary to improve upon the methodological rigor of prior approaches. Previous interventional research aimed at improving clinician well-being is limited by a focus on individual-level rather than multi-level factors and depends upon an overreliance on retrospective self-report measures. Such methodological approaches are burdensome and fraught with common-method variance [58], as well as evaluative and recall biases [59–61].

The overreliance on self-report psychometrics is problematic for at least four reasons. First, self-report measures focus on individual-level factors that miss dyadic and dynamic inter-relational factors that are crucial to understanding the causal relationship between interprofessional teamwork, well-being, and patient safety [62]. Second, self-report measures are time-consuming and burdensome, which is particularly problematic for already overburdened populations like healthcare providers. While limiting questions can reduce responder burden, it also significantly impedes research rigor (e.g., limiting sensitivity to measure the effectiveness of policy or intervention changes) [63–66]. Third, research is limited by imprecision and a lack of consensus on measurements of burnout and closely related constructs such as depression and stress [67–69]. Relatedly, most self-report measures of psychological safety were not developed in a healthcare setting and thus do not capture the complexity of interprofessional behaviors uniquely tapping into the construct of psychological safety [70]. Fourth, relying on self-report is a concern for diversity, equity, and inclusion in the context of burnout prevention and mitigation. Studies consistently find that survey response rates are biased based on demographic categories and hospital role, including age, gender, socio-economic status, physician vs. non-physician status, and length of employment [63–65]. A recent scoping review of burnout interventions finds that although there are a multitude of small studies investigating a number of interventions, the evidence base remains extremely low due to the poor quality of data [71]. Evaluating the feasibility, acceptability, effectiveness, and mechanisms of action of burnout prevention strategies among the employees most vulnerable to burnout requires rigorous, low-burden, objective, and minimally invasive measurement.

Interventions and policies to improve interprofessional connection and psychological safety are paramount but must be pragmatic, scalable, and rigorously evaluated. The randomized controlled trial outlined in this protocol will evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of a chaplain-delivered compassion intervention to improve psychological safety among interprofessional healthcare teams. The aims of this research study are twofold. First, we will evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of CCSH-TI delivered to mixed-role oncology teams. We will use the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [72], focusing on the inner setting and process to detect contextual determinants of intervention feasibility and acceptability and to identify modifiable factors that improve the implementation of CCSH-TI. Second, we will develop and establish the feasibility, acceptability, and validity of a novel, low-burden, multi-method ambulatory assessment (AA) toolkit comprised of ecological momentary assessments (EMA) [73, 74] and the electronically activated recorder (EAR) [75–78], a naturalistic observation method that samples ambient sound from participants’ momentary environments used widely outside healthcare settings.

Methods/design

Overview of approach

We will conduct a phase 1 clustered randomized, controlled trial of CCSH-TI delivered to teams working across disease-specific units (8–12 employees/group) (Fig. 2). To execute the first aim, we will use a sequential mixed-methods approach (survey, focus groups) to determine the feasibility (recruitment, retention, and attendance) and the acceptability (satisfaction, credibility, and perceived benefit) of CCSH-TI. Our second aim will use self-report psychometrics and ambulatory measures (EMA and EAR). During the study, we will tailor data collection and analysis procedures to optimize acceptability and adherence for this population. We will also develop and evaluate a set of novel codebooks to identify indices of psychological safety, incivility, and teamwork from EAR data. All participants will provide written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study, and all data collected in the study will be assigned an anonymous identification number and stored on password-protected computers, only accessible to the study team.

Study population

We will recruit (N= 80) healthcare employees (physicians, nurses, APPs, pharmacists, and staff) using emails to team listservs followed by online study presentations. Inclusion criteria are healthcare employees who (1) work full time (> 20 h/week) in disease-specific teams within a National Cancer Institute (NCI)-Designated Comprehensive Cancer Center, (2) are over 18 years of age, and (3) speak English (because CCSH-TI is delivered in English). There are no exclusion criteria. All participants will provide written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study. Based on previous (unpublished) enrollment in CCSH-TI groups, approximately 73% of participants will be female, and approximately 70% will be non-White employees. We aim to enroll 70% women and at least 40% non-White employees into the study. This distribution of women and non-White participants recruited from our urban and highly diverse cancer center is higher than is seen in the larger oncology workforce and will allow us to better examine race/ethnicity differences in this study [79]. Although our study aims are not predicated on gender or ethnic/racial differences, given that previous studies have found that both gender and ethnicity/race are associated with differential risk for burnout, psychological safety, and for the experience of workplace incivility/mistreatment, we will explore sex/gender and race/ethnicity differences in this study.

Doing so will enable our team to evaluate the effect of race and gender on recruitment and retention outcomes. Employees will be randomized by team (using a random number generator in Microsoft Excel) to receive CCSH-TI or to “standard of care” (SOC) group that will have access to all wellbeing resources available to employees.

Compassion-Centered Spiritual Health Team Intervention (CCSH-TI)

CCSH-TI is composed of four sessions delivered every other week and lasting 60 min each. Sessions teach participants to attune to their interpersonal relationships, acknowledge and allow difficult emotions, and access compassion. It also provides psychoeducation about skillful coping strategies. Each session consists of didactic material about team norms and safety, a feeling check-in, facilitated group discussions about social connection and professional team building, and mind–body practices to promote mindfulness, cultivate a feeling of being nurtured, and access compassion for self and others. CCSH-TI is designed to be offered within clinic workflow and does not include any home practice.

CCSH-TI is delivered to healthcare teams by spiritual health clinicians (SHC, aka: healthcare chaplains) proficient in group facilitation and in the CCSH model of spiritual intervention and care [80, 81]. Training in the CCSH model begins with a 4-week intensive course in cognitively based compassion training (CBCT) [82], followed by learning and applying the CCSH four-stage care model: (1) the chaplain prepares by calming their mind and setting a compassionate intention, (2) attunes to the care-seeker’s emotional state to guide the encounter, (3) accesses compassion to offer practices that foster connection and resilience, and (4) entrusts the care-seeker(s), affirming their capacity for ongoing resilience and self-care. After certification in the CCSH model, chaplains receive additional training in the team intervention by first observing and then co-facilitating sessions. This prepares them to lead groups with proficiency, fostering a safe, supportive space where teams facing stress or burnout can strengthen resilience and build community.

Feasibility and acceptability assessments

We will use a sequential explanatory mixed-method design [83] to evaluate two vital aspects of the feasibility of CCSH-TI: (1) Demand (i.e., the extent to which CCSH-TI is likely to be used by employees) and (2) acceptability (i.e., individual recipients’ perception that CCSH-TI is appropriate, credible, and beneficial) [84]. To evaluate demand, we will quantify (1) the proportion of eligible employees (all and according to licensure, sex/gender, race/ethnicity) who enroll and are willing to be randomized, (2) CCSH-TI attendance and completion rates, and (3) retention rates at three assessment timepoints. To identify factors influencing demand, feasibility, and acceptability among employees who choose not to attend CCSH-TI, we will administer a brief survey to team members who choose not to or are unable to attend to identify barriers to participation [85]. To evaluate acceptability, we will administer pre-intervention interest and post-intervention surveys of satisfaction [86], perceived intervention credibility and expectation [87], and perceived benefit using the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure [88, 89].

In addition, we will conduct six focus groups with key stakeholders (n = 30: nurses, APPs, oncologists, staff, high-level leadership in the NCI-Designated Comprehensive Cancer Center) to elicit conversations among stakeholders, explore areas of consensus and disagreement, help elaborate on the quantitative results, and so that nonverbal communications and group interactions can be observed. Two focus groups will be mixed-role, and the other four will be single-role groups to mitigate concerns that lower-status/power roles will feel unsafe in disclosing their thoughts. Focus groups will mix stakeholders across clinics and ensure that direct supervisors are not in a focus group with supervisees. We will limit the sample size for each group to 4–6 participants to facilitate group discussion by all participants.

We will use the CFIR [72] framework to guide the development of the focus group guide, with a focus on the inner setting and process (planning, engaging, executing, reflecting/evaluating). Mixed- and single-role guides will be identical. Interviews will explore facilitators and barriers (by CFIR inner and outer setting levels) to delivering CCSH-TI in more depth, such as difficulties incorporating interventional approaches into the hospital workflow. Two members of the research team, serving in the roles of moderator and observer, will conduct each focus group. The observer will record observations of participants’ nonverbal interactions and behaviors. Focus groups will last approximately 30 min and be digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Feasibility and acceptability analyses

For surveys, we will calculate Cronbach’s alpha for all quantitative measures to assess scale reliability. We will quantify accrual, attendance, and retention rates for the entire group and by role, gender, and race/ethnicity to evaluate whether accrual, retention, or acceptability covary with sociodemographic characteristics. Intervention completion rates will be characterized with a completion benchmark set at three or more sessions attended. We will also quantify treatment-specific (i.e., CCSH-TI vs. SOC) retention rates. Because burnout is not required for enrollment, we will examine the relationship between burnout and enrollment, attendance, and retention to determine whether CCSH-TI is more or less relevant for individuals reporting burnout. We will also characterize barriers endorsed by employees who did not regularly join the CCSH sessions. We will conduct all quantitative analyses in IBM SPSS version 29.

For focus group data, we will use a thematic analysis approach to analyze all focus groups and identify common themes within and across groups (i.e., physicians, nurses). This will involve a step-by-step process of deconstructing text, identifying salient themes, conceptualizing relationships among salient themes, and synthesizing these results [90]. Two primary coders will independently code all focus group transcripts. The entire research team will discuss emergent codes to ensure concordance and reliability. We will use MAXQDA software to manage and analyze all qualitative data. This program can manage and store large amounts of textual data and can link qualitative data with quantitative data stored in Excel or various statistical databases for a mixed-methods research approach. To improve the reflexivity of thematic analysis, we will use a rigorous reflexive thematic analysis reporting checklist to improve the transparency and clarity of any team perspectives, values, and assumptions that may influence the generation, analysis, and interpretation of focus group data [91]. Upon completion of this qualitative assessment, we will engage focus group participants in a process known as “member checking,” a validation technique where we will ask whether our conclusions accurately reflect stakeholder viewpoints [92]. We will also use the CFIR [72] to deductively identify adoption and implementation barriers to integrating CCSH-TI into the workflow of oncology interprofessional teams. Collectively, by utilizing these approaches, we will ensure the rigor and reproducibility of research findings. Priority for this mixed-method sequential explanatory design will be for the quantitative data, given the goal of evaluating feasibility benchmarks to make go/no-go decisions for a future definitive trial (benchmarks detailed in Table 1). Focus group data will be used to inform the quantitative data, with some preliminary integration occurring prior to the focus groups to inform the focus group guide and with full integration occurring during the interpretation of the outcomes for the entire study. Particular emphasis will be on the use of focus group data to inform future implementation strategies and to enhance accrual and retention rates by identifying specific clinic- and role-based barriers to enrollment (Fig. 3).

Overview of how study procedures will lead to expected outcomes for Aims 1 and 2

For focus groups, we expect to reach saturation with a focus group size (n= 30, 6 focus groups), based on recent meta-analyses indicating that saturation is reached by 4–8 groups and another indicating that 90% of themes are discoverable with 3–6 focus groups [93, 94]. Overview of ambulatory assessment (AA) toolkit development

Employees enrolled in the longitudinal, randomized, and SOC-controlled pilot study of CCSH-TI will complete psychosocial assessments at three time points: before CCSH-TI (T1), immediately after completion of CCSH-TI (T2), and 12 weeks after completion (LT). All study participants will complete assessments at these intervals, including employees randomized to the SOC group and employees who are randomized to CCSH-TI but do not attend sessions. The three assessment periods will include (1) validated self-report psychometric surveys administered online that can be compared with ambulatory assessment measures and (2) 3-day ambulatory assessment periods that will include EMA and EAR to measure constructs relevant to testing the effectiveness and mechanisms of action in burnout prevention. Research staff collecting outcome assessments will be blinded/masked.

Because future research will require differentiation of burnout from other clinically relevant and often comorbid symptoms, we will measure depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] [96–98]), anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-Short Form [STAI] [99, 100]), sleep impairment (PROMIS Sleep-Related Impairment—Short Form 4a [101, 102]), and substance use (PROMIS Alcohol Use—Short Form 7a [101, 102]) using validated Likert scale measures demonstrating sensitivity to change. So that we can include social determinants of health (SDOH) as covariates, we will administer the NIH BRICS SDOH measure [103] and collect data on environmental drivers of burnout (e.g., patient acuity levels, deaths, staffing ratios). Measures will be administered virtually via a secure Qualtrics link and platform (116 items total, 30 min).

Ecological momentary assessments (EMA)

During each 3-day EMA assessment period, participants will receive notifications via their own smartphone using Fabla, a mixed-method EMA platform, to complete four assessments/day. Assessment alerts will be signal contingent, occurring at quasi-random times (delivered randomly during 120-min periods) throughout the workday. At each alert, participants will use a Likert scale (0 “not at all”—4 “extremely”) to rate their current experiences of the following items, all of which, except the incivility item, are adapted from the Professional Fulfillment Index: [95] (1) connection with colleagues (“I feel connected with my colleagues”), (2) connection with patients (“I feel connected with my patients”), (3) team support (“I feel supported by my colleagues”), (4) professional fulfillment (“I feel happy, worthwhile, and satisfied with work”), (5) burnout (“I feel exhausted, unenthusiastic, and hopeless with work”), and (6) incivility experienced (“Since the last assessment, I have experienced incivility from (a) a patient or their family or (b) a colleague”). In addition, each participant will receive a once-daily prompt via Fabla that asks them: “For the next 3–5 min, we’d like for you to tell us about your day.” Once prompted, participants will have 30 min to provide their ratings. Each six-question survey takes 1 min to complete; the time burden of EMA assessments will be approximately 5 min per day. After the T1, T2, and LT assessments, participants will complete an EMA feasibility and acceptability questionnaire that asks participants whether the EMA assessments impeded daily activities or changed their behaviors, rated on a Likert scale (1 “not at all”—5 “a great deal”).

Electronically activated recorder (EAR)

The EAR is a naturalistic observation method that uses a mobile audio recording app (used with an Android device provided to participants) to silently and unobtrusively record short samples of ambient sounds while participants go about their workday. For the present study, we will use a sampling rate of 30 s every 10 min (yields audio for 5% of the recording interval), keeping 95% of participants’ workdays unrecorded and private. Participants will wear the EAR for three workdays, consecutively or as close to consecutive as possible. Although a previous study found that a sampling rate of 30 s every 12 min over 2 days provides good temporal stability for a wide range of social and behavioral variables [78], we will use a slightly more frequent sampling rate and include one additional day because (a) we anticipate settings and conversation partners to change more rapidly in the healthcare environment and (b) we expect some key behavioral events (e.g., instances of incivility) to be relatively rare.

Legally, this study will be conducted in a one-party consent state. Therefore, there is no legal infringement of privacy rights of individuals who interact and converse with the participant while the EAR is recording. Ethically, guaranteeing anonymity to conversation partners and bystanders by proactively minimizing their chances of being captured and by retroactively erasing potentially identifying information from the sound files minimizes the risk of loss of privacy and eliminates the risk of loss of confidentiality for individuals who do not provide their informed consent.

In addition to measuring compliance rates and data completion rates to evaluate EAR feasibility, we will measure the acceptability and appropriateness of EAR methods via the following procedures. We will measure EAR acceptability after the T1, T2, and LT assessments using the EAR Experiential Questionnaire [77], a self-report measure that assesses the acceptability of wearing the EAR. Questions focus on participants’ comfort with wearing the EAR, whether the EAR impeded daily activities, whether the EAR changed their behaviors or others’ behaviors, and how typical the days were that the EAR was worn. Each question is rated on a Likert scale (1 “not at all” — 5 “a great deal”).

Following established procedures for coding EAR data [75, 105], we will create two codebooks: (1) a molar (gestalt) codebook that will generate a global rating of participants’ (a) social support received, (b) social support given, and (c) psychological safety and (2) a behavioral codebook that will use binary coding (presence/absence) for a suite of behaviors of interest recognizable by audio (e.g., incivility received and given, microaggressions received and given, social support received and given, help-seeking, expressions of gratitude). The molar codebook will be developed using existing validated self-report and observational measures of psychological safety [70] and incivility [106]. The behavioral codebook will be inclusive of all previously validated categories in the Social Environment for Coding of Sound Inventory (SECSI), many of which have direct relevance to the present study (e.g., conflict, gratitude, emotional disclosure, providing and receiving social support, among others) [75, 105]. The SECSI will additionally be expanded to include other behavioral categories unique to the healthcare environment (e.g., locations, activities, clinical vs. interprofessional interaction partner). After creating drafts of the molar and behavioral codebooks, we will use “member-checking” feedback sessions in which at least four participants from each stakeholder group (physicians, nurses, APPs, staff) will review the codebooks and provide feedback on the behavioral categories, definitions, and rules for coding.

Participants will also be compensated US $10/day in which they responded to all four assessment alerts during the day (US $2 per EMA, US $4 for EMA + daily diary response) and compensated with a US $100 bonus for an overall > 80% response rate for EMA and > 80% wear rate for EAR (US $190 total). A previous experimental study found that monetary incentives increase EMA compliance rates and willingness to participate [107]. Although previous studies have found that participants only delete approximately 1–2% of EAR audio files [108, 109], we may discover that healthcare employees delete a larger percentage of active files given the sensitivity of their interactions, that participants wear the EAR less than expected or press the “privacy” button more than expected, or that participants respond to < 80% of EMA assessment alerts/day. We will conduct a planned review of EAR acceptability and EAR and EMA data completion after the first two cohorts (n = 16–20) complete their T1 assessments to identify and address obstacles in future T1 as well as all T2 and LT assessments. If EAR or EMA data completion is ≤ 50%, we will conduct a follow-up focus group with participants to identify obstacles, amend protocol, and develop a participant training tool to facilitate usability prior to enrolling subsequent cohorts.

Ambulatory assessment data analysis and statistical methods

Missing items in the psychometric scales will be estimated with expectation maximization [110] using other items within the scale as predictor variables (only if missing items do not account for more than 5% of total data and if all items are missing at random as determined by null Little’s Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) tests). All data will be evaluated for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and appropriate procedures will be performed in cases where normality is violated. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviation, and standard error for continuous; frequency and % for categorical) will be used to characterize baseline demographics and responses to all three assessments. We will calculate Cronbach’s alpha for all quantitative measures to assess scale reliability and assess concurrent validity of all measures. We will also examine whether data completeness or scores covary with sociodemographic characteristics.

To assess EMA feasibility, we will report dropout and compliance (% of items completed), and we will also assess measurement reactivity (the extent to which the act of measurement affects study participants [111], analyzed here by characterizing linear change in the EMA reports over time in the SOC control group), all of which are underreported in EMA research and will be vital for using this methodology in the healthcare environment [112]. We will also evaluate participants’ self-reported EMA feasibility and acceptability. Because meta-analytic studies indicate that gender and age are associated with EMA compliance [112], we will also characterize feasibility metrics by gender, age, and role. To evaluate the criterion validity of concurrent measures (i.e., the relationship between EMA-assessed variables and validated psychometric measures), we will compare 3-day average scores on each EMA item with scores on validated self-report psychometric measures of burnout, psychological safety, and team support using correlation analyses.

To assess EAR acceptability and appropriateness, we will (a) evaluate the validity and reliability of developed codebooks to determine the appropriateness of ambulatory linguistic behaviors as a measure of intervention effectiveness and to evaluate mechanisms of action in future studies, (b) evaluate participants’ self-reported acceptability of EAR data collection, and (c) characterize data completion using the following detailed procedures.

To analyze the appropriateness of codebooks, four EAR coders trained using established training procedures will transcribe and code data from 10 participants to refine the codebooks by identifying ambiguities and difficult categories, merging/splitting categories, and clarifying definitions and rules [75]. The entire research team will discuss problem areas to ensure concordance and reliability. Trained EAR coders will code data from an additional 10 participants to determine inter-coder reliability estimates, computed as intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). If ICC scores for each category are > 0.70, we will finalize the coding system. If items score < 0.70, the team will discuss and remove, merge, and/or clarify the item(s) following the established iterative process for coding system refinement [75]. In addition, we will characterize the frequency of occurrences of incivility, microaggressions, social support, help-seeking, and expressions of gratitude, and we will quantify the variability and distribution for each behavioral category. To explore the predictive validity of the EAR, we will use correlation analyses to examine whether the frequency of these behavioral occurrences is associated with scores on both validated psychometric and EMA measures of burnout, psychological safety, and team support.

To evaluate the acceptability of EAR data collection, EAR coders will transcribe and code the entire dataset (n = 80) using the final codebooks, such that two research assistants will code every audio file. For each audio file, coders will denote whether participants wore the EAR, did not wear the EAR, or were wearing the EAR but cannot be coded because of bad sound quality. Then, we will calculate frequency scores to indicate the percentage of waking hours that participants wore the EAR. To evaluate the acceptability of EAR data collection, we will calculate average scores for the EAR Experiential Questionnaire. To evaluate the appropriateness of the EAR method for evaluating burnout and psychological safety, we will generate ICC scores for the molar codebook global ratings and for each category for the behavioral codebook.

To quantify the feasibility of EAR data collection, we will (a) characterize the number of participants providing at least 70% of the data at each of the three assessment time points (1.8 h/assessment × 70% = 76 min/assessment), (b) characterize the number of times participants pushed the privacy button, and (c) characterize the number of deleted files (previous studies have found that participants delete approximately 1–2% of files [108, 109]).

We did not conduct a formal power analysis for the second aim, as it was not designed to evaluate efficacy. Moreover, it is difficult to conduct a power analysis for EAR coding, as we do not know the base rates for the behaviors under investigation. Because the goal of Aim 2 is to determine the feasibility and acceptability of EAR and EMA, we estimated the sample size necessary to determine the variability in base rates for relatively infrequently occurring events. Because we do not know the base rates for the behaviors under investigation and quantified using EAR and EMA, we use a conservative estimate (n= 80) for the sample size necessary to estimate the standard deviation for continuous outcomes [113]. Moreover, the sample size here is in line with other studies that have successfully used EAR and EMA to examine relevant constructs among healthcare employees. Previous EMA studies among physicians have used sample sizes of N = 61 [114] and N= 88 [73]. A recent meta-analysis of studies using EMA to measure momentary mood and stress found sample size ranges from N= 20–87. The only other study to date that used ambulatory audio in a healthcare environment used a sample size of 53 resident physicians [104]. In determining the EMA sampling rate and duration of EMA data collection, we have chosen to balance the goal of adequate sampling with the need to minimize the burden of data collection during the workday [115]. Our sampling frequency matches studies that have effectively used EMA methodology in the healthcare context using an average of 9–13 total EMA prompts [73, 114]. For these reasons, we believe the sample size and sampling rate will be powered to estimate outcome variability, to evaluate data completion rates, and ultimately to evaluate the appropriateness and acceptability of coding methods.

Discussion

Acceptable, effective, and scalable interventions to support the interprofessional teamwork and psychological safety of healthcare teams are imperative to improve the well-being of healthcare providers, to achieve the “quadruple aim,” and to address the needs of the twenty-first century healthcare workforce [116]. However, intervention components found to be most effective — interventions that are ongoing, embedded within the clinical setting and workflow, and team based — are also challenging to implement. For this reason, successful implementation of evidence-based interventions to improve well-being requires feasibility research. Therefore, this study will evaluate whether CCSH-TI can be successfully used among interprofessional, mixed-role teams, based on perceived feasibility and acceptability from clinician participants [88]. Rigorously assessing feasibility will be particularly critical to establish healthcare community partnerships and to allow for sensitivity to the unique considerations of the healthcare environment and employee populations [84]. However, there has been very little pre-implementation or implementation science to identify contextual factors that influence the successful implementation of burnout interventions [71].

In addition, rigorously evaluating the feasibility and acceptability across diverse participant samples is critical to address — and to refrain from perpetuating — disparities in workforce and workforce well-being. Previous research points to disparities in burnout and psychological safety [117, 118], highlighting the importance of using a health equity framework [119] in evaluating the demand for and acceptability of CCSH-TI. This is imperative, as interventions targeting burnout and psychological safety risk exacerbating inequity if employees who are already vulnerable to burnout and lacking psychological safety are less likely or able to engage with the intervention. The new information generated with this aim will be essential to evaluate unforeseen barriers to interprofessional team interventions and optimize the implementation of CCSH-TI into the clinical workflow.

The rigor of research evaluating interventions and policies to improve burnout and psychological safety also requires the development of alternatives to self-report and retrospective psychometric tools to effectively measure interprofessional teamwork and provider well-being [40]. However, developing objective-embedded measures requires tailoring coupled with feasibility and acceptability testing, especially given the unique demands and privacy concerns associated with working in a healthcare environment. As such, this study will tailor established ambulatory assessment procedures to minimize assessment burden among this population, and we will evaluate adherence, acceptability, and feasibility of these procedures.

We expect interprofessional team members to find CCSH-TI acceptable, credible, and appropriate, as well as well attended. The main objective regarding stakeholder data is that they will provide us with valuable information for ways to sustainably and feasibly establish reach, retain participants, and incorporate CCSH-TI into the clinical workflow. This new information will form the basis for what needs to be adapted in the intervention regarding workflow and system-level considerations of delivering the intervention. We will pay special attention to barriers and facilitators to implementation and recommended strategies to overcome these barriers, especially for groups vulnerable to burnout and lacking psychological safety.

This feasibility and acceptability-focused work will also advance the utility of EMA as a means for more timely assessments of burnout and clinician well-being. Also, in line with previous research, we expect participants to provide EMA ratings on > 75% of scheduled assessments [112], and we expect participants to wear the EAR for at least 70% of their waking hours [120]. Although some previous EMA studies have found that the overall number of assessment days affects compliance rates more than the number of assessments per day, a recent meta-analysis of 496 study samples found that assessment days and number of assessments affect compliance equally [112]. Moreover, we have prioritized a shorter questionnaire length given previous research indicating that questionnaire length has a more detrimental effect on perceived burden and compliance than sampling frequency [121]. Based on these studies, we have opted to collect more frequent ratings using a shorter questionnaire over a shorter time span to coincide with the EAR data collection time period. Based on previous research, we expect participants to delete fewer than 2% of audio files and to report high levels of acceptability to wearing the EAR. Although it is possible that study participants will modify their behavior with the knowledge that they are being observed, previous studies show that participants report that the EAR has minimal effects on their behavior, and that they acclimate to wearing it after a few hours [78, 108, 122]. We expect to develop two codebooks that can be used in future research to evaluate psychological safety and social support outcomes using ambulatory audio data and tailored to a healthcare environment. Codebooks will be uploaded onto the Open Science Framework (OSF) EAR Repository, a publicly accessible collaborative repository of EAR.

The study outlined in this protocol will utilize a state-of-the-art approach with high rigor as reflected by the following: (1) The focus on the interprofessional healthcare team rather than atomized individuals, (2) the use of ambulatory methods to obtain objective and low-burden assessments that mitigate the problems inherent in relying on self-report, (3) the use of multi-level “member-checking” procedures to evaluate the appropriateness of multi-method ambulatory assessment methods, (4) assessment of multiple potential confounders of constructs relevant to burnout (depression, anxiety), and (5) the emphasis on the collection and integration of several units of analysis (self-report, momentary self-report, behavior).

Although there are numerous strengths of this protocol, we acknowledge some limitations and potential obstacles. With respect to our first aim of evaluating the feasibility and acceptability of CCSH-TI, it is possible that employees in a specific role or sociodemographic groups are less likely to enroll or complete the study or intervention. However, we do not anticipate encountering insurmountable barriers, as the accrual and completion rates of our previous pilot study and of CCSH-TI groups run without formal recruitment indicate that we will be able to recruit and retain the proposed sample. Moreover, our pilot study indicated that participants did not find conflict between CCSH-TI and their religious or spiritual beliefs, mitigating concerns that some employees may be dissuaded by religious or spiritual connotations. We will use the CFIR model, which provides a framework to resolve discrepancies between an implementation plan and its operationalization.

Because Aim 2 is designed to develop and optimize a new method of data collection in the healthcare environment, we have identified several possible obstacles and their solutions. First, we may discover that ambulatory methods are not feasible for all healthcare employees given the sensitivity of their interactions. We will disseminate all feasibility findings as well as protocol modifications to maximize what can be learned from this study. Another limitation of the proposed ambulatory methods is that we may miss important behavioral indicators of burnout by focusing on EAR data acquired during working hours. For example, previous research indicates that a key factor associated with burnout — and with gender disparities in burnout — is conflict between home and work responsibilities [123]. We have chosen to prioritize measurement in the workplace as interactions in this context are most germane to the intervention and because these methods have already been successfully used outside of healthcare and can more readily be incorporated in future efficacy studies. Moreover, EMA is not immune from critiques of other self-report psychometric assessments. Although this embedded approach helps overcome recall biases and inaccuracies of other self-report measures, evaluative biases may remain. Pairing EMA outcomes with EAR data may help reveal these biases as well as new ways to overcome them.

In sum, we envision that the knowledge learned using this protocol will set the foundation for a future RCT that could have application toward yielding a highly scalable evidence-based team intervention. Furthermore, we propose that the findings of the ambulatory methodology component of this study would have broad applicability to other research on burnout and associated workplace outcomes such as psychological safety and incivility. The primary route to disseminating knowledge will be via abstract presentations at conference meetings and the publication of scientific journals. We also aim to reach a broader audience in the healthcare community and beyond by engaging with other methods of communication such as podcasts and verbal media interviews.

Conclusion

Burnout has harmful effects on patient safety and quality, imparts great cost to healthcare systems, harms employee health, and exacerbates healthcare disparities. Interventions to improve interprofessional connection and psychological safety are paramount but must be feasible, acceptable, scalable, and rigorously evaluated to effectively mitigate burnout and optimize clinician well-being. By evaluating the feasibility and acceptability of CCSH-TI, the data generated by the clinical trial outlined in this protocol will inform future studies testing the effectiveness of a comprehensive compassion-based intervention delivered by spiritual health clinicians. Additionally, this study will develop and establish the feasibility, acceptability, and validity of a novel, low-burden, multi-method AA toolkit comprised of EMA and the EAR, which will help strengthen the psychometrics of timely, repeated assessments of burnout and well-being. By centering the mind, cultivating self-compassion, and bolstering an inclusive and more accurate understanding of others on the interprofessional team, CCSH-TI has the potential to reduce burnout and its concomitant risks by increasing psychological safety and reducing team incivility. The data generated from this study will inform future research aimed at implementing interventions to bolster team cohesion to reduce burnout while simultaneously optimizing low-burden assessment approaches, aligning with national research initiatives to bolster a strong and resilient healthcareworkforce [124–127].
