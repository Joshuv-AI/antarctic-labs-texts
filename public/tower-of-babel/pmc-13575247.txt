Bidirectional associations between loneliness and physical multimorbidity and the mediating role of social inactivity
Authors: Yaguan Zhou, Siqi Zhu, Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Mika Kivimäki, Xiaolin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical multimorbidity and loneliness are increasingly recognised as public health concerns, but the nature of their potential relationships remains unclear. Here we report bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness in middle-aged and older adults and the mediating role of social inactivity. Longitudinal data from adults aged ≥45 years are analysed across 19 countries from five prospective cohorts: US Health and Retirement Study; English Longitudinal Study on Ageing; Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe; China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study; and Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging. Cox regression models suggest longitudinal associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness in both directions (from physical multimorbidity to loneliness: adjusted hazard ratio (HR) = 1.25, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.20 to 1.30; from loneliness to physical multimorbidity: adjusted HR = 1.10, 95% CI = 1.05 to 1.17). Random-intercept cross-lagged panel models confirm significant association between T1 physical multimorbidity and T3 loneliness, with T2 social inactivity serving as a mediator. These findings suggest that physical multimorbidity and loneliness may create a vicious cycle where each condition exacerbates the other, with social inactivity mediating the direction from physical multimorbidity and loneliness.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Psychiatric disorders, Risk factors

Introduction

Loneliness, a negative subjective experience resulting from a perceived gap between expected and achieved levels of social connection1, is a growing public health issue2,3. In older adults, the global prevalence of loneliness ranges between 4.2% and 24.2%, with a lower prevalence (2.4% and 18.0%) in the middle-aged population3,4. Loneliness is a risk factor for many chronic physical conditions, including coronary heart disease, stroke, and diabetes5–7. Population ageing, greater life expectancy and lifestyle alteration have contributed to the accumulation of chronic diseases, resulting in the increase of physical multimorbidity, defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic physical diseases8. Most people aged 65 years and older are affected by physical multimorbidity9, which is associated with reduced quality of life and premature mortality, posing an ongoing challenge to global healthcare systems10,11.

Although research interest in loneliness and physical multimorbidity has expanded significantly over the past two decades, few studies to date have focused on the reciprocal relationships between them. A German cross-sectional study of 7604 adults aged 40 and over reported an association between multimorbidity and increased loneliness12, a trend supported by another study from Canada and Australia13. Using data from middle-aged and older adults in Denmark, loneliness was linked to increased odds of physical multimorbidity14. This finding aligned with a previously published study in 17 European countries, suggesting loneliness was an important associated factor of multimorbidity15. Longitudinal studies in Germany also suggested that increased loneliness was associated with multimorbidity16, especially in individuals aged 65 years and older17. In Thailand, a significant association between loneliness and incident multimorbidity was observed in individuals aged 45 and over18. Using data from 4018 middle-aged and older adults in the US, Freilich et al found that baseline loneliness predicted increasing counts of chronic physical conditions19. This finding aligns with a previously published paper, where researchers found that the perception of loneliness was associated with multimorbidity, no matter whether multimorbidity was defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions or three or more body systems affected by chronic conditions20. In addition, most studies have focused on populations in a single country with different study periods and analytical strategies, precluding the comparison and generalisation of their findings across regions and countries.

Social activity, e.g., participation in social groups and attending educational or training courses, is linked to both physical and psychological well-being21–24. It may be hypothesised as a mediator in the bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. On one hand, individuals with chronic physical conditions often face limitations that restrict their ability to engage in social activities25,26. This reduced participation can negatively impact psychological health and well-being, contributing to feelings of loneliness27. In this context, social inactivity would mediate the association between existing physical multimorbidity and an increased risk of loneliness. On the other hand, previous studies suggested that loneliness may elevate the risk of developing chronic diseases by fostering a tendency to withdraw from social activities that support physical and mental health28,29. Since having one chronic disease increases the likelihood of developing additional conditions, reduced participation in social activities may also mediate the relationship between loneliness and subsequent physical multimorbidity.

Since both loneliness and physical multimorbidity are becoming global concerns, it is important to evaluate the potential bidirectional associations between loneliness and physical multimorbidity as well as key factors underlying mediators. To elucidate these associations, we analysed longitudinal data from adults aged ≥45 years, including information on physical multimorbidity, social inactivity, and loneliness from 19 countries in North America, Europe, and Asia. Our findings revealed that physical multimorbidity and loneliness are bidirectionally associated, with significant indirect effects of social inactivity on the direction from physical multimorbidity and loneliness.

Results

Physical multimorbidity as a risk factor for loneliness

A total of 111,332 participants provided information on loneliness and physical multimorbidity at baseline. We defined physical multimorbidity as the co-occurrence of two or more conditions, including cancer, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, chronic lung disease, and arthritis. Participants already with loneliness at baseline (n = 14,056), with no information on loneliness during follow-up (n = 14,845) or with missing covariates (n = 12,255) were excluded, yielding 70,176 individuals entering the physical multimorbidity-loneliness analyses (Fig. 1). Of these, 13,511 (19.3%) were from the US, 2945 (4.2%) from China, 5975 (8.5%) from South Korea, 5082 (7.2%) from England and 42,663 (60.8%) from 15 other European countries (see Supplementary Table 1). The mean age of participants was 64.8 (s.d. 9.1) years, 38,794 (55.3%) were females, and 27,230 (38.8%) reported having physical multimorbidity at baseline. Participants with physical multimorbidity were more likely to be older and females, have lower socioeconomic status, do less physical activity, drink less frequently, not smoke currently, and have a higher body mass index (BMI) and depressive symptoms, compared to those without baseline physical multimorbidity (Table 1).

At follow-up, 12,779 (18.2%) reported being lonely (for the characteristics of participants according to loneliness, see Supplementary Table 2). To analyse the association between physical multimorbidity and the risk of loneliness in the survival analyses, we assessed the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld’s residuals method and found no violations. After adjusting for demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, BMI and depressive symptoms, individuals with physical multimorbidity had 1.25 [95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.20–1.30] times the risk of loneliness compared to those without physical multimorbidity at baseline (see Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, the number of physical conditions was dose-dependently associated with loneliness, with each additional condition at baseline linked to a 13% (95% CI: 11–15%) higher hazard of loneliness.

We conducted a series of supplementary analyses to validate the robustness of this association. First, we observed a statistically significant association with loneliness in relation to every physical condition, the corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.17 (95% CI = 1.09–1.27) for cancer to 1.51 (95% CI = 1.38–1.65) for stroke. Second, there were no statistically significant interacting effects of age, sex, educational level and total household wealth with physical multimorbidity on loneliness, and the strength of the physical multimorbidity-loneliness association was broadly similar in all subgroups (see Supplementary Fig. 1a). Third, in a meta-analysis summarising study-specific estimates, the pooled HR for the physical multimorbidity-loneliness association was 1.26 (95% CI = 1.19–1.33), corresponding to the findings from the main analysis, and with no statistically significant heterogeneity in cohort-specific estimates (I2 = 29.6%, p = 0.224) (Fig. 3). Fourth, the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale was used as an alternative to measure loneliness in individuals from the US and England, and similar patterns of results were observed, although the incidence rate of loneliness varied across different cut-off values [total score ≥6, total score ≥7, and a score of at least one domain =3 (‘often’), see Supplementary Tables 4–7]. In other sensitivity analyses, including applying initial cut-off values of the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-8) and 10-item CESD (CESD-10), conducting Cox regression models with mixed effects to account for cluster effect across studies, and treating death before loneliness as a competing event, similar results were obtained (see Supplementary Tables 8–10). Imputing missing values in covariates yielded a substantially greater sample size (N = 82,431) and broadly similar results of the physical multimorbidity-loneliness association to those in the main analyses (see Supplementary Table 11). For the change in the loneliness score, the fully adjusted linear mixed-effect model showed an increased loneliness score in participants with physical multimorbidity (β = 0.046, 95% CI = 0.038–0.054, p < 0.001) compared with those without physical multimorbidity. As the number of physical conditions increased, the loneliness score also increased over time (βper condition = 0.023, 95% CI = 0.020–0.026, see Supplementary Table 12).

In addition, to test both directions of the physical multimorbidity-loneliness association within the same baseline population, we repeated the main analyses among a population with no physical multimorbidity or loneliness at baseline. As shown in Fig. 1, after excluding 49,751 participants with physical multimorbidity and loneliness at baseline, a total of 61,581 participants entered the second wave. Of these, 32,200 participants reporting loneliness at Wave 2, missing information on follow-up loneliness, and with missing information on covariates were excluded, yielding 29,381 participants for the physical multimorbidity-loneliness analyses. The mean age of participants was 64.4 (s.d. 8.3), the proportion of females was 54.7%, and 3163 (10.8%) reported having physical multimorbidity at Wave 2 (see Supplementary Table 13). After adjusting for the same sets of covariates, the HR for incident loneliness was 1.17 (95% CI = 1.05–1.29) in those with physical multimorbidity, although the associations were not statistically significant for several physical conditions (Table 2, Supplementary Table 14).

Loneliness as a risk factor for physical multimorbidity

In the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses, we excluded participants with physical multimorbidity at baseline (n = 43,180), with no information on physical multimorbidity during follow-up (n = 8270), and with missing covariates (n = 11,639), leaving 48,243 participants in the final analyses (Fig. 1). Of these, 7131 (14.8%) were from the US, 2767 (5.7%) were from China, 4696 (9.7%) were from South Korea, 3646 (7.6%) were from England and 30,003 (62.2%) were from 15 other European countries (see Supplementary Table 1). The mean age of participants was 62.6 (s.d. 8.7) years, 26,715 (55.4%) were women, and 4,169 (8.6%) reported being lonely at baseline (Table 3). Participants with loneliness were more likely to be older and females, have lower socioeconomic status, do less physical activity, drink alcohol less frequently, smoke currently, and have a BMI of <18.5 or ≥30.0 kg/m2 and depressive symptoms.

A total of 16,781 (34.8%) participants developed physical multimorbidity during the follow-up (for the characteristics of participants according to incident physical multimorbidity, see Supplementary Table 15). In an adjusted Cox regression model, individuals reporting loneliness at baseline had 1.10 (95% CI = 1.05–1.17) times the risk of developing physical multimorbidity, compared to those not reporting loneliness, with no violated proportional hazards assumption (see Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 16). Results from the quasi-Poisson regression model showed that baseline loneliness was associated with an increased number of physical conditions during the follow-up. The relative number of physical conditions was 5% higher for participants with loneliness (adjusted relative risk=1.05, 95% CI = 1.01–1.09), compared to those without loneliness. Reporting loneliness was associated with an increased risk of single physical conditions during follow-up, with the highest HR for developing arthritis (1.12, 95% CI = 1.06–1.18) and chronic lung diseases (1.12, 95% CI = 1.01–1.25). There were no statistically significant interacting effects of age, sex, educational level or total household wealth with loneliness on physical multimorbidity (see Supplementary Fig. 1b). In our supplementary analyses, the pooled HR for the loneliness-physical multimorbidity association from a meta-analysis of the included studies was 1.10 (95% CI = 1.05–1.17), with no statistically significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.613) (Fig. 3). Analyses using the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale to measure loneliness in individuals from the US and England still yielded significant associations across difference cut-off values [total score ≥6, total score ≥7, and a score of at least one domain =3 (‘often’), see Supplementary Tables 17–20]. Further sensitivity analyses applying initial cut-off values of CESD-8 and CESD-10, and conducting Cox regression models with mixed effects, showed similar results (see Supplementary Tables 21 and 22). When treating the loneliness score as a continuous variable, we found that a higher loneliness score was associated with an elevated risk of physical multimorbidity (HR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.02–1.05). Using Fine–Grey subdistribution hazard models to treat death as a competing event, the associations of loneliness with hypertension, heart diseases and chronic lung diseases attenuated and did not reach statistical significance (see Supplementary Table 23). Imputing missing values in covariates yielded a substantially greater sample size (N = 58,998) and broadly similar results of the loneliness-physical multimorbidity association to those in the main analyses (see Supplementary Table 24).

In the population with no physical multimorbidity or loneliness at baseline, we further excluded 30,855 participants reporting physical multimorbidity at Wave 2, with missing information on follow-up physical multimorbidity, and with missing information on covariates, leaving 30,726 participants for the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses. The mean age of participants was 64.1 (s.d. 8.3), the proportion of females was 55.2% and 1897 (6.2%) reported being lonely at Wave 2 (see Supplementary Table 25). In the fully adjusted model, individuals with loneliness at Wave 2 had 1.17 (95% CI = 1.07–1.27) times the risk of physical multimorbidity compared to those reporting no loneliness (Table 2, Supplementary Table 26).

The mediating role of social inactivity

We conducted the random-intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPMs) to examine the mediating effects of social inactivity, using repeated measures of physical multimorbidity, social inactivity and loneliness at 3-time points. By modelling the stable between-person variations as random intercepts, RI-CLPMs can generate unbiased causal relationships between variables30,31. A total of 80,643 participants with information on physical multimorbidity, loneliness and social inactivity at baseline and during follow-up were included in the RI-CLPMs (Fig. 1). The characteristics of included participants are presented in Supplementary Table 27, with 39,189 (48.6%) reporting to be socially active at baseline. The constrained model with time-invariant cross-lagged associations fit better than other models and did not show a statistically significant difference from the unconstrained model (Supplementary Table 28).

Figure 4 shows the standardised coefficients for significant paths across physical multimorbidity, loneliness and social inactivity, adjusted for all the above-mentioned covariates. Physical multimorbidity, social inactivity and loneliness were cross-sectionally associated at T2 (two waves after the baseline) and T3 (last follow-up) (βPhysical multimorbidity-social inactivity = 0.022–0.031, p < 0.001; βPhysical multimorbidity-loneliness = 0.029, p < 0.001; βSocial inactivity-loneliness = 0.041–0.058, p < 0.001). Across all time points, the presence of physical multimorbidity at T predicted social inactivity (β = 0.022–0.023, p = 0.016) and loneliness at T + 1 (β = 0.030–0.028, p = 0.001). The association between physical multimorbidity at T and loneliness at T + 2 was statistically significantly mediated through social inactivity at T + 1 (β = 0.0006, 95% CI = 0.0001–0.0012, p = 0.037, Table 4). On the reverse direction, the presence of loneliness at T predicted social inactivity (β = 0.015–0.016, p < 0.001–=0.015), but not physical multimorbidity at T + 1. There was no statistically significant mediating effect of social inactivity at T + 1 on the association between loneliness at T and physical multimorbidity at T + 2 (Table 4). Furthermore, the RI-CLPM identified significant between-person correlations among the random intercepts, with the latent trait correlations of 0.131 (p < 0.001) between physical multimorbidity and loneliness, 0.139 (p < 0.001) between physical multimorbidity and social inactivity and 0.095 (p < 0.001) between social inactivity and loneliness.

Discussion

This large multi-cohort study of middle-aged and older adults from 19 countries has three key findings: First, baseline physical conditions and multimorbidity increased the risk of loneliness, with a dose-response relationship between the number of physical conditions and loneliness. Second, baseline loneliness was associated with a higher risk of physical multimorbidity, although this association was weaker and the separate associations with several physical conditions did not reach statistical significance. The RI-CLPMs showed a significant association between physical multimorbidity and subsequent loneliness, but not in the reverse direction. Third, social inactivity significantly mediated the associations between physical multimorbidity and subsequent loneliness, but the indirect effect was small.

Given the high and increasing prevalence of physical multimorbidity and loneliness globally, understanding their reciprocal relationships is vital to identify integrated solutions for prevention. Our study provides longitudinal evidence on the bidirectional associations between loneliness and physical multimorbidity in a large multi-national sample. Although previous studies have reported associations between loneliness and physical multimorbidity, most of them came from high-income countries only12–14,16,20,32–34. In addition, the number and the range of selected physical conditions have varied across studies, ranging from 833 to 2520 conditions, preventing direct comparisons between studies. By using a multi-national sample and the same seven common physical conditions across analyses of temporal directions, our findings confirmed the longitudinal link from physical conditions and multimorbidity to loneliness incidence, which was independent of lifestyle factors, BMI and depressive symptoms, and additionally followed a dose-response relationship with a higher risk of loneliness seen in individuals with a greater number of comorbid physical conditions. The association between physical multimorbidity and an increased risk of loneliness was highly generalisable. This association was observed among individuals of different sexes, educational levels and household wealth. In addition to Cox regression models, results from the RI-CLPM also confirmed the predictive associations of physical multimorbidity with loneliness. By constraining the RI-CLPM with time-invariant cross-lagged associations, we were allowed to explicitly observe the general cross-lagged effects across physical multimorbidity, social inactivity and loneliness over time, covering the same time window of Cox regression models.

These findings suggest that physical multimorbidity may signal an increased risk of loneliness and a need to consider interventions aimed at protecting the physical and mental health of affected middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, individuals with a higher number of physical conditions should be prioritised in clinical practice and self-management programmes to prevent further loneliness or other mental health outcomes. In addition to the above-mentioned individual-level efforts, reinforced societal-level actions could potentially bring further prevention benefits. According to Frieden’s Health Impact Pyramid35, modifying the environmental context—such as constructing age-friendly communities and enhancing social connectedness—could help mitigate the adverse impacts of accumulating physical conditions and broadly protect older adults with physical multimorbidity from psychological decline.

The underlying mechanisms for the longitudinal association between physical multimorbidity and loneliness are complex, and one possible explanation is that physical multimorbidity is associated with chronic pain and functional limitations36,37, which, in turn, reduces the opportunity to participate in social activity, gradually isolating from social groups and inducing loneliness38. In addition, participants with physical multimorbidity might suffer from self-stigma and fear of negative reactions from society, thereby experiencing a restriction in social participation39,40. Such social disconnections would further lead to loneliness. Loneliness and social inactivity were quite different in concept, with loneliness defined as a negative subjective experience of social disconnection, while social inactivity emphasising the behaviours of people to interact with others. According to the Social Production Function theory, people have needs for affection, behavioural confirmation, and status, whereas participating in social activities is a basis to fulfil their social needs. When people fail to fulfil these social needs, they may perceive negative feelings, including loneliness41. This supported that social inactivity can mediate the pathway from physical multimorbidity to loneliness, but not the reverse direction. However, the RI-CLPM showed that the indirect effects of social inactivity are small, suggesting that promoting social activity of middle-aged and older adults with physical multimorbidity has only mild impacts on preventing loneliness. Moreover, other social factors, including social relationships and social network (e.g., marital status), can also contribute to the bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. As suggested by published reviews, satisfying social relationships and network are critical for mental and physical well-being, whereas impaired social relationship and network can lead to loneliness42–44. Using longitudinal data from older people in the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam, Domènech-Abella and colleagues also found that social network size predicted a higher level of loneliness45. In our analysis, the information on the quality of social relationships was unavailable in the studies included, and marital status was adjusted in Cox regression models and RI-CLPM. Future studies including more social factors would help disentangle the paths from physical multimorbidity and subsequent loneliness. In addition, the categorisation of social inactivity in our analysis was coarse and varied slightly between studies, which potentially increased heterogeneity and contributed to an underestimation of the associations, calling for more evidence with standardised assessment methods. A further potential explanation for the longitudinal association between physical multimorbidity and loneliness comes from sociology. Individuals with physical multimorbidity often have complex health needs, while the medical specialties and a lack of care coordination between health professionals may contribute to poor doctor-patient communications46,47. This could exacerbate the individual’s subjective feeling of loneliness.

After conducting study-specific models, we observed that the indirect effects of social inactivity were only significant in SHARE. This discrepancy might be due to the variations in loneliness measurement. SHARE used the UCLA 3-item Loneliness Scale to assess loneliness, including lacking companionship, feeling left out and feeling isolated48. These items were more relevant to individuals’ unmet needs for social interaction. In other studies, the direct, single-item measure from CESD lowered the breadth and complexity in measuring the concept of loneliness, which is especially suitable for clinical settings49. Although previous research widely acknowledged good correlations between single-item measures and multi-item scales49, we cannot rule out the possibility that different measurements of loneliness influenced our findings. In addition, limited socioeconomic status had been found to contribute to lower social participation and loneliness50–52. A previous study indicated that older individuals in southern and central European countries were more likely to face financial and health problems than those in northern and western European countries and have a higher rate of reporting loneliness53. This finding can give partial evidence for the significant indirect effects of social inactivity between physical multimorbidity and incident loneliness in SHARE but not in ELSA. In addition, cultural context may also matter, and some countries weigh more heavily on social network and social participation53,54. For example, Italian society might attach great stigma to those living alone or having lower social participation53. Therefore, future work is warranted to consider people’s normative orientations and vulnerabilities in the cultural context of countries.

The impact of mental health on physical multimorbidity has been well documented55,56, but only a couple of studies have paid attention to the longitudinal loneliness-physical multimorbidity association18,19. Our results of Cox regression models confirmed the relationship between loneliness and subsequent physical multimorbidity, although this association was weaker than the one between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. Furthermore, the significance of this association disappeared when considering single physical conditions. Loneliness is closely related to depression. In our analyses, multivariable adjustments included the depressive symptoms of individuals. In spite of this, the loneliness-physical multimorbidity association still existed, suggesting an independent effect and emphasising the importance of social factors in public health2. The nature of the association between loneliness and incident physical multimorbidity is likely to be complex. Results from RI-CLPM indicated that the presence of loneliness failed to predict physical multimorbidity at later time points, with no statistically significant indirect effects of social inactivity. Despite the differences in results from Cox regression models and the RI-CLPM, we concluded that participants reporting being lonely should also be monitored closely, in order to prevent physical health outcomes over a longer period of time.

Notably, according to the study-specific results of RI-CLPMs, we observed the associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness were significant in both directions in CHARLS. China was the only upper-middle-income country in our analyses, where larger health inequalities may have existed57,58. Future studies involving low- and middle-income countries are warranted to validate and extend the evidence from the present study, which was conducted mostly in high-income countries.

The strengths of this study include the use of a large population sample from five ageing cohorts across 19 countries and a prospective study design on the bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. This study also has several limitations. First, our harmonised measure of physical multimorbidity captured malignancies and diseases affecting the arterial, cardiac, cerebrovascular, metabolic, respiratory and musculoskeletal systems. A broader definition that could have provided a more comprehensive assessment of physical multimorbidity. However, it would also have introduced greater heterogeneity among cases, potentially complicating the interpretation of the findings. Information on physical conditions was self-reported, which is subject to recall bias, although previous studies have demonstrated the good credibility of self-reported information on chronic diseases59–61. Second, the included participants were from high-income countries or upper-middle-income countries. Whether the bidirectional associations also exist among populations in lower-income countries warrants further verification. Third, the harmonised measurement of loneliness was relatively crude, relying on a dichotomous classification. Previous studies have shown marked differences between the scales when their scores were dichotomised, although we obtained consistent results from a sensitivity analysis using the more granular UCLA Loneliness Scale to measure loneliness in two cohorts. To minimise bias from arbitrarily defined cut-off values for loneliness across cohorts, we applied linear mixed-effects models examining changes in the continuous loneliness score as the outcome (physical multimorbidity-loneliness analysis) and Cox regression models using the continuous loneliness score as a risk factor (loneliness-physical multimorbidity analysis), both replicating the main findings. These analyses were based on pooled data from three cohorts due to data unavailability in the other two cohorts. Fourth, in the main analyses, loneliness was measured using one item from CESD-8 or CED-10 except SHARE, which might introduce bias due to the varied wording and response format compared to validated loneliness scales. However, a longitudinal study in France reported a significant correlation (Spearman test r = 0.696; p < 0.001) and concordance (Kappa coefficient = 0.88) between the loneliness item of the CESD scale and the components of the UCLA Loneliness Scale62. In a validation study, researchers found that the single-item measure of loneliness was highly correlated with the multi-item measures, including the Rasch-Type Loneliness Scale (r = 0.66), the 20-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (r = 0.63) and the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (r = 0.66). In addition, the single-item frequency measure has a reliability that can be considered adequate (r = 0.77)63. Therefore, it seems possible to reliably capture loneliness in adults using single-item measures, especially when financial or time-related constraints do not allow researchers to include multi-item measures of loneliness in large epidemiological studies63. However, we acknowledge that short measures of loneliness cannot capture a construct in all its complexity and should not be used in settings where a very nuanced perspective is required. Therefore, consistently using a validated measurement tool of loneliness is recommended across population-level studies to confirm our results and move a step forward in studying the reciprocal relationships between physical multimorbidity and loneliness in older adults from a diverse context. Fifth, the information on symptoms, severity and subtypes of physical conditions was unavailable, which could influence observed associations. Sixth, the categorisation of social inactivity varied slightly between studies, potentially increasing heterogeneity and contributing to an underestimation of the associations. Future research using studies with standardised assessment methods and protocols could provide more precise estimates of the role of social inactivity in the bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. Seventh, the baseline population in the two directions of analyses was not the same, reducing the comparability of results due to variations in sample characteristics. To minimise differences between these samples, we additionally performed analysis among a common subpopulation free of both physical multimorbidity and loneliness at baseline, examining the direction of the association between physical multimorbidity and loneliness from the second wave onwards. By drawing samples from the same baseline population, participant characteristics in the two sets of analyses were closely aligned, enhancing the comparability of results from the bidirectional analyses. Eighth, the information on death was unavailable in the ageing study from England; therefore, the Fine–Grey subdistribution hazard models could only account for the competing risk of death in the other four studies. Ninth, loneliness is a dynamic emotional state that can fluctuate over time; therefore, we may miss the information on the change in loneliness status after they reported for the first time being lonely. Although the RI-CLPMs have been additionally performed, the time interval was too long to capture the timely fluctuation of loneliness. Tenth, previous theories and models suggested that other social factors (e.g., the quality and quantity of social relationships) also linked the association between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. However, these were not examined in this analysis due to data availability, and future studies are needed to elucidate this matter. Finally, although we conducted a series of models to adjust for potential covariates, the possibility of residual confounding by other factors (e.g., genetics) could not be ruled out.

In conclusion, our research suggests that physical multimorbidity is statistically associated with loneliness, while the association from loneliness to physical multimorbidity is much weaker. Social inactivity slightly mediated the longitudinal association between physical multimorbidity and the incidence of loneliness. A holistic and integrated prevention approach targeting both physical multimorbidity and loneliness, with the elements of disease prevention and social environment, may have the potential to improve the health status of middle-aged and older populations and mitigate the interaction of these conditions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective multi-cohort study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting criteria. Data were harmonised across 19 countries from five sister cohort studies from the Global Aging, Health and Policy program64: the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS); the English Longitudinal Study on Ageing (ELSA); the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE); the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS); and the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA). These studies shared the same methodologies and conducted surveys every two or three years, allowing cross-study comparisons65–69. All studies were granted relevant local ethics approval, with written informed consent given at each interview.

Data from the latest five waves were captured in each study, including 2012–2021 in HRS, 2012–2021 in ELSA, 2014–2023 in SHARE, 2011–2021 in CHARLS, and 2012–2021 in KLoSA (see Supplementary Tables 30–32). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. We included participants if they (1) were aged ≥45 years at the fifth to last wave (baseline); (2) reported complete information on physical multimorbidity and loneliness at baseline and follow-up; and (3) had full information on covariates. Participants aged >85 years at baseline were excluded to minimise survival bias.

Physical conditions and multimorbidity

In the harmonised data, seven common age-related physical conditions with public health importance were available based on participants’ self-reports: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke and arthritis. In SHARE, medication records for hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, and chronic lung diseases were additionally used to determine the presence of these four physical conditions. The aforementioned seven physical conditions were summed, with physical multimorbidity defined as the co-occurrence of two or more of these conditions.

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured and dichotomised using specific questions across studies (see Supplementary Table 31). In HRS and ELSA, loneliness was measured by an item in the CESD-8: “Much of the time during the past week, have you felt lonely?”, with those answering “Yes” recorded as lonely70. In CHARLS and KLoSA, loneliness was measured by a similar item in CESD-10, with participants reporting feeling lonely for three or more days last week as lonely7. In SHARE, the 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale71 was used to measure loneliness: “feeling lack of companionship”, “feeling left out”, and “feeling isolated from others”. Each item was scored on a 3-point Likert scale: 1 (hardly ever or never), 2 (some of the time) and 3 (often). We defined those who scored ≥6 as lonely7,72. The 3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale has been used extensively in studies of older adults, with good internal reliability70,73. Previous research also reported a significant correlation (Spearman test r = 0.696; p < 0.001) and concordance (Kappa coefficient = 0.88) between the loneliness item of the CESD scale and the components of the UCLA Loneliness Scale62.

Social inactivity and covariates

Participants’ involvement in social activities was self-reported in all studies. In HRS, SHARE, CHALRS and KLoSA, participants reported whether they participated in activities at least once a month, including volunteer or charity work, educational or training courses, sports or social clubs, etc., and were classified into socially active and socially inactive. In ELSA, participants were asked whether they were members of any organisations, clubs, or societies; those who responded affirmatively were classified as socially active. Detailed descriptions of the assessment of social inactivity in each study are provided in Supplementary Table 32.

Self-reported baseline covariates included demographic characteristics (age, sex, and study), socioeconomic status (marital status, educational level, and total household wealth), lifestyle factors (physical activity, current smoking, alcohol consumption), body mass index (BMI) and depressive symptoms. The variable of study referred to the cohort participants came from, including five categories: HRS, ELSA, SHARE, CHARLS and KLoSA. In HRS and ELSA, information on race was additionally available for study-specific analyses. Marital status was categorised as married or partnered, separated or divorced, widowed, and never married. Educational level was harmonised into three levels: primary, secondary and tertiary, according to study-specific categories. Total household wealth was defined as the sum of all wealth, including homes, businesses, vehicles, and savings accounts, excluding other debts or loans, and grouped by order of magnitude. We used quartiles of total household wealth in each study, ranging from the lowest (quartile 1) to the highest (quartile 4). Participants taking part in vigorous/moderate physical activity once a month or more were regarded as physically active, otherwise physically inactive. Smoking status was determined by asking participants whether they smoked at present, with answers of “yes” (current smoking) or “no” (never smoking or quitting smoking). Self-reported alcohol consumption on ≥1 day per week was regarded as weekly or more frequent drinking, and less than weekly drinking. BMI was derived by dividing weight (kg) by height (m2), which was categorised into four groups: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2). Depressive symptoms were measured using CESD-10 in CHARLS and KLoSA, and CESD-8 in HRS and ELSA, by calculating the sum of the remaining items excluding the item on loneliness74,75. The cut-off score for defining depressive symptoms was determined according to previous studies76, and we used the modified cut-off scores to define depressive symptoms, excluding the item of loneliness. For SHARE, the EURO-D Scale was used to measure depressive symptoms of participants.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of participants were described according to their baseline physical multimorbidity and loneliness status, respectively. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages), and χ2 tests were used to compare the between-group differences.

Two sets of separate analyses, the physical multimorbidity-loneliness analyses among participants with no baseline loneliness, and the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses among those with no baseline physical multimorbidity were conducted to explore their bidirectional associations. Cox regression models were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for the bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness. Participants were censored at the interview month of outcomes or death, or end of follow-up of each cohort, whichever came first. Individuals reporting for the first time being lonely at follow-up were recorded as incident loneliness cases in the physical multimorbidity-loneliness analyses, and those first reporting having two or more physical conditions at follow-up were recorded as incident physical multimorbidity cases in the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses. Five models involving adjustment for different covariates were conducted: Model 1 was a crude model; Model 2 adjusted for demographic characteristics (age, sex, and study); Model 3 was as Model 2 additionally adjusted for socioeconomic status (marital status, educational level, and total household wealth); Model 4 was as Model 3 and additionally adjusted for demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking status, and alcohol consumption) and BMI; and Model 5 was as Model 4 and additionally adjusted for depressive symptoms.

We further estimated the bidirectional associations between each physical condition and loneliness using the same set of models. In the physical multimorbidity-loneliness analyses, the dose-response relationship between the number of physical conditions and loneliness was further examined by two approaches. First, we treated the number of physical conditions as a continuous variable and examined the HR and 95% CI for loneliness events as the number of physical conditions increased. Second, the linear mixed-effects model was performed using harmonised data from HRS, ELSA and SHARE, to estimate the longitudinal change in loneliness score during the follow-up. The association between baseline physical conditions and changes in loneliness score was examined using interaction terms between physical conditions or multimorbidity × data wave, adjusting for the same sets of covariates as in Models 1–5 described above. In the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses, the quasi-Poisson regression model was used to estimate the impact of baseline loneliness on the number of physical conditions during follow-up, adjusting for all Model 5 covariates described above. The quasi-Poisson regression model can be used to model counts where the variance exceeds the mean, accounting for overdispersion and generating robust standard errors for valid inference77,78. In the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses, the loneliness score was also treated as a continuous variable to assess its dose-response relationship with physical multimorbidity. Subgroup analyses were performed to compare these associations by age, sex, educational level, total household wealth and study. In HRS and ELSA-specific analyses, race was additionally adjusted in the Cox regression models. Random-effects meta-analyses were then performed using the R package ‘metafor’ to pool study-specific HRs and 95% CIs, with the between-study heterogeneity tested using the I2-test. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the main results by (1) using the UCLA Loneliness Scale in ELSA and HRS to measure loneliness with different cut-off values according to a psychometric comparison study, including total score ≥6, total score ≥7, and a score of at least one domain =3 (‘often’); (2) using the initial cut-off values of CESD-8 and CESD-10 to define depressive symptoms in HRS, ELSA, CHARLS, and KLoSA; (3) repeating Cox regression models with mixed effects with the gamma and the log-normal distributions to account for cluster effect across studies79; (4) using Fine–Grey subdistribution hazard models, wherein death before outcomes was set as a competing event, and repeating the main analyses; and (5) applying multiple imputation via chained equations to impute missing values in covariates. The missingness was assumed at random and was imputed five times, with the model estimates pooled to generate final results. To further confirm the comparability of results from the physical multimorbidity-loneliness analyses and the loneliness-physical multimorbidity analyses, we used a subsample free of both physical multimorbidity and loneliness at baseline and repeated the Cox regression models. In this way, the bidirectional associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness were evaluated from the second wave onwards, based on analytic samples drawn from the same baseline (Wave 1) population. In addition, we examined the bidirectional associations between each physical condition and loneliness using the baseline population.

We used the RI-CLPM of structural equation modelling to examine the mediated effects of social inactivity in the bidirectional relationships between physical multimorbidity and loneliness measured repeatedly at 3-time points (T1: baseline, T2: two waves after baseline, and T3: the last wave). Compared to the traditional CLPM, RI-CLPM decomposes the observed variance into stable between-person differences and temporal within-person dynamics30, which can disentangle the temporal ordering and generate unbiased cross-lagged effects between variables31. The between-person component of the RI-CLPM includes the latent random intercept factors for physical multimorbidity, social inactivity and loneliness across time points (RIs in Fig. 4). The within-person component for the RI-CLPM is presented by latent variables based on observed variables80. All factor loadings were constrained to one, and the residual measurement variances were constrained to zero. Three sets of paths were specified: (1) autoregressive paths, representing the associations between a given variable at baseline and the same variable at follow-up; (2) concurrent paths, representing the cross-sectional associations between variables; and (3) cross-lagged paths, representing the prospective associations between physical multimorbidity and loneliness, and between loneliness and physical multimorbidity. Four RI-CLPMs were conducted, including individuals with information on physical multimorbidity, loneliness and social inactivity at baseline and during follow-up. The first model was unconstrained, the second model was constrained with time-invariant autoregressive and cross-lagged associations, the third model was constrained with time-invariant cross-lagged associations, and the fourth model was constrained with time-invariant autoregressive associations. Comparison between the models was performed via the following fit indices: root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA), standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The criteria for a good fit are RMSEA ≤ 0.06, SRMR ≤ 0.06, CFI and TLI ≥ 0.9581. Models with lower BIC indicate a better model fit with greater parsimony82. The χ2 difference test was used to compare the model fit of these four models83. A more parsimonious model was chosen when the model fit of constrained models did not show a significant difference from the unconstrained model84.

We combined the longitudinal mediation analysis with the RI-CLPMs to examine the mediating role of social inactivity for both the physical multimorbidity-loneliness path and the loneliness-physical multimorbidity path84. These models confirm that the exogenous variable (e.g., physical multimorbidity) precedes the mediator variable (e.g., social inactivity) and the outcome variable (e.g., loneliness). Therefore, the longitudinal mediation model accounts for the time-lags and the temporal order that is necessary for testing causal inference85. The bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples method was applied to determine the indirect effects and 95% CIs of social inactivity85. Specifically, we longitudinally examined the mediating role of social inactivity (T2) in the association between physical multimorbidity (T1) and loneliness (T3) by the product of βT1 physical multimorbidity-T2 social inactivity and βT2 social inactivity-T3 loneliness. Similarly, we longitudinally examined the mediating role of social inactivity (T2) in the association between loneliness (T1) and physical multimorbidity (T3) by the product of βT1 loneliness-T2 social inactivity and βT2 social inactivity-T3 physical multimorbidity. We further applied RI-CLPMs to generate study-specific results.

All tests in this study were two-sided with a significance level of P < 0.05. The random-effects meta-analyses were performed in R statistical software (version 4.4.1), the cross-lagged model and mediation analyses were performed in Mplus (version 8.3), and all other statistical analyses were performed in SAS (version 9.4).
