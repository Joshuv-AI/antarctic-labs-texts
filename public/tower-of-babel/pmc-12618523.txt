Revealing new associations between lncRNAs and diseases through cross attention mechanism and multiple level feature fusion
Authors: Xueming Luo, Xiaoling Li, Zongzheng Bai, Yan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Revealing new lncRNA-disease associations (LDAs) is necessary to decipher pathological mechanisms and find new clues of diagnosis and therapy for complex diseases. However, experimental methods for LDA identification need a significant amount of time and cost. Here, we introduce a novel deep learning-based method, LDA-CAMF, to infer LDA candidates. LDA-CAMF first designs a cross-attention mechanism to dynamically decode high-order interdependencies between lncRNAs and diseases, presents a multi-level feature fusion strategy to aggregate hierarchical node representations learned from different layers, and then fuse the original features and the optimized representations to enhance the model expressive ability, finally captures novel LDAs using XGBoost. In comparison with six state-of-the-art methods (SDLDA, LDNFSGB, IPCARF, LDASR, LDA-VGHB, and GEnDDn), LDA-CAMF computed the highest AUCs of 0.9632 and 0.9759, and the best AUPRs of 0.9369 and 0.9783 on lncRNADisease and MNDR under 5-fold cross validation, respectively. Under “cold-start” scenarios for lncRNAs and diseases, LDA-CAMF outperformed the above six baselines under most conditions. Five ablation studies further validated better predictive performance of LDA-CAMF. Visualization of LDP feature distributions also demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed LDP feature learning strategy. Case studies elucidated that HNF1A-AS1 and BCYRN1 associated with prostate cancer, and HAR1A linked with diabetes. We forecast that LDA-CAMF assists in biomarker identification and mechanism investigation of complex diseases.

Introduction

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are usually regarded as a class of non-coding-protein RNAs containing more than 200 nucleotides1. lncRNAs severely affect gene expression regulation at transcription, post-transcription, and epigenetics2,3. They also link with the occurrence and development of human diseases, particularly with cancers4–6.

The development of translation and proteomics technologies enables multiple lncRNAs to decipher micropeptides or small peptides7. Peptides originated from lncRNAs exhibit distinct biological functions and can serve as tumor suppressors or oncogenic drivers8. Consequently, inference of lncRNA-disease associations (LDAs) has been a hotspot. But conventional biological experiments usually face limitations in huge time consumption, expensive cost and intensive labor9,10. Hence, high-efficient computational techniques are needed to uncover hidden LDAs for complementing biological experiments.

Recently, various computational methods have been developed to predict LDAs, which can be broadly categorized into three types: network-based methods, matrix decomposition-based methods, and machine learning-based methods11. Network-based methods construct heterogeneous networks to discover new associations, such as HyperGeometric distribution12, flow propagation13, bipartite networks14, network consistency projection15, and multi-layer networks16. Although network-based methods are effective in LDA prediction, they often struggle to capture complex and high-order relationships among lncRNAs and diseases, which may limit their predictive performance when dealing with heterogeneous biological data.

Matrix decomposition methods employ matrix factorization models for prediction. For example, matrix factorization17, inductive matrix completion18, geometric matrix completion19, singular value decomposition20, and neighborhood-regularized matrix factorization21 inferred novel LDAs based on the reconstructions of low-rank matrices. However, they may lose critical information and can’t capture complex relationships.

Machine learning methods pay close attention to select multiple features and train classifiers based on known associations22–25. Especially for RNA-disease association inference, various high-efficient machine learning algorithms have been devised, for instance, rotation forest26, random forest27,28, cluster correlation29, robust multi-label learning30, and heterogeneous Newton boosting31.

Meanwhile, deep learning has obtained wide concentration when inferring LDAs, for instance, dual-net neural architecture32, graph attention network33, and multimodule fusion34. Particularly, contrastive learning exhibits a powerful advantage for sparse data or high-cost annotation35. Combining contrastive learning with graph-based algorithms, graph contrastive learning has been also applied to LDA inference due to its advantages in learning abundant representations from LDA network while not relying on known associations36. For example, multi-view contrastive learning37, multi-task graph contrastive learning38, and hypergraph contrastive learning39, boosted inference accuracy based on complex intermolecular structures and associations. However, graph contrastive learning has a difficulty in exploiting an optimal data augmentation strategy to conserve graph integrity while producing meaningful positive and negative pairs40. But existing augmentation strategies urgently need more sophisticated techniques for generating high-quality samples.

To solve these limitations, we present a multi-level feature fusion framework with the cross-attention mechanism, LDA-CAMF, for LDA prediction. To capture new LDAs, this framework integrates lncRNA-related features with disease-related information. Experimental outcomes demonstrate that LDA-CAMF outperformed other methods in various evaluation metrics and exhibited significant potentials in capturing LDAs, which may contribute to the development of novel targeted therapeutic strategies. This study has three main contributions:

A cross-attention module is presented to dynamically decode high-order interdependencies between lncRNAs and diseases.A multi-level feature fusion strategy is designed to preserve low-level structural features while aggregating high-level interaction-aware information for each lncRNA-disease pair (LDP).The original features are further incorporated with the optimized features through concatenation operation for enhancing the model expressive ability.

Method overview

We introduce a deep learning model, LDA-CAMF, to discern potential LDAs. As depicted in Fig. 1, LDA-CAMF comprises three primary steps: (a) Similarity calculation. lncRNA similarity and disease similarity networks are constructed through biological and association information; (b) Feature extraction. LDP features are extracted based on cross-attention and multi-level feature fusion; (c) Prediction. LDP association scores are computed by XGBoost.

Similarity calculation

To enrich features for lncRNAs and diseases, we first need measure their similarity. Here, similar to existing works31,32, disease similarity is estimated through their semantic information and Gaussian association profile (GAP) kernels (GAPKs). lncRNA similarity is estimated through their functional information and GAPKs.

The cross-attention module

After constructing lncRNA and disease similarity networks, LDA-CAMF introduces a cross-attention module to effectively model the relationships between lncRNAs and diseases, thereby extracting enhanced representations for their relationships and providing more reliable feature representations for LDA prediction. As shown in Fig. 1b, by conducting two-way information relationship between key and value of disease attention and ones of lncRNA attention, LDA-CAMF constructs a cross-attention module to realize their information exchange and relationship, thus capturing their interaction features. During this phage, disease features can model corresponding expression based on attention weights of lncRNA features, which is applicable to lncRNA features. Through association and adjustment mechanism, the cross-attention module can advance information flow during feature mapping, high-efficient integrate lncRNA and disease features, and more comprehensively decipher LDP feature representations.

Multi-level feature fusion

Based on the cross-attention module, we learn lncRNA features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{l}$$\end{document} and disease features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{d}$$\end{document}. Subsequently, we develop a multi-level feature fusion strategy to fuse features of lncRNAs and diseases. In the first-stage feature fusion, to ensure dimensional consistency with the embedding dimension of the cross-attention mechanism, the original lncRNA and disease similarity features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_l$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_d$$\end{document} are transformed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_l$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_d$$\end{document} based on linear transformation (i.e, two separate fully connected layers with unshared weights). After that, the features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{l}$$\end{document} obtained from the cross-attention mechanism are combined with the linearly transformed features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{l}$$\end{document} through element-wise addition to obtain the first-stage fused representations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{zl}$$\end{document} for lncRNAs by Eq. (16). Similarly, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z_{d}$$\end{document} are combined with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{d}$$\end{document} to obtain the first stage fused representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{zl}$$\end{document} for diseases by Eq. (17). Thus, we can preserve low-level structural features while aggregating high-level interaction-aware information based on the above hierarchical fusion process:

16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{zl}= & Z_l + T_l&\end{aligned}$$\end{document} 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{zd}= & Z_d + T_d&\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Next, the lncRNA and disease features are downsampled to generate one-dimensional lncRNA feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_l$$\end{document} and disease feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_d$$\end{document} through global average pooling by Eqs. (18, 19):

18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X_l= & \text {Avgpooling}(F_{zl}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document} 19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X_d= & \text {Avgpooling}(F_{zd}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Through the first-stage feature fusion, lncRNA features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_l$$\end{document} and disease features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_d$$\end{document} are computed. To optimize these features, we compute the predicted LDP association score matrix U by Eq. (20):

20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} U = X_l^\top X_d \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{ij}$$\end{document} indicates the greater relationship probability between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_j$$\end{document}.

During training, the binary cross-entropy is adopted to train the cross-attention mechanism and the multi-level feature fusion module. Specifically, it is adopted to estimate the difference between the predicted matrix U and known adjacency matrix Y. By minimizing the loss function on LDA training sets, the feature representations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_l$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_d$$\end{document} could be further optimized for forecasting novel LDAs.

After training, the feature representations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_l$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_d$$\end{document} of lncRNAs and diseases are optimized. And the optimized features are concatenated to construct descriptor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ij}^{'}$$\end{document} for each LDP \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_i$$\end{document}-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_j$$\end{document} by Eq. (21):

21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{ij}^{'} = [X_l(i) ; X_d(j)] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

To preserve the original representations while enhancing the model’s expressive power, we perform the second feature fusion by incorporating the raw input features O into the learned embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{'}$$\end{document} by Eq. (22):

22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M_{ij} = [F^{`}_{ij} ; O_{ij}] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_{ij} = [S_{l_i}; S_{d_j}]$$\end{document}.

LDA prediction

eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a superior classification algorithm. It aggregates multiple weak classifiers to advance classification performance while alleviating model complexity through gradient boosting24,41. It optimizes the regularized objective function and encourages parallel computation, thereby exhibiting superior precision in many classification tasks. Here, we train XGBoost on LDP descriptors extracted from the second feature fusion to predict novel LDAs.

Experiments

Datasets

To evaluate the precision and robustness of predictions, dependable and extensive LDA datasets are essential. Here, we adopted two newer and dependable LDA datasets: lncRNADisease42 and MNDR43. lncRNADisease42 systematically integrates LDAs and provides insights into deciphering potential molecular mechanisms and lncRNA-based biomarkers for complex diseases. lncRNADisease contains 157 diseases, 82 lncRNAs, and 605 known associations. MNDR43 is a well-rounded database. It arranges diverse ncRNA-disease associations from multiple different species. It contains 190 diseases, 89 lncRNAs and 1529 known associations.

Experimental settings

We employed 20 time 5-fold cross validation (CV) to evaluate the model performance. During each CV, all LDPs were divided into 5 subsets at random, in which each subset serves as test samples in turn and the remaining were taken as training samples. During dividing training and test sets, we computed the similarities of lncRNAs and diseases solely on the training set to ensure that the training samples did not contain association information from the test samples, thereby avoiding data leakage. All methods computed a prediction score for each LDP, which was then sorted in descending order. The mode performance was estimated based on multiple metrics, including area under the ROC curve (AUC), area under the precision-recall (PR) curve (AUPR), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The above metrics provide a comprehensive viewpoint for assessing the discriminative ability of models.

To ensure reproducibility, we specified the hyperparameter settings used in our experiments. For the deep learning components, the key parameters were set as follows: embedding dimension = 8, attention heads = 8, learning rate = 0.04, and weight decay = 1e–3. For the XGBoost classifier, the key parameters were set as follows: n_estimators = 125, max_depth = 6, and learning_rate = 0.1.

To build a relatively robust LDA prediction model, we conducted 5-fold CV. During each fold, known linked LDPs were taken as positive samples, and an equal number of unconfirmed LDPs were randomly selected as negative samples. Consequently, we constructed training set and test set. Table 1 show the predictive performance of LDA-CAMF and the above six baselines on lncRNADisease and MNDR.

Cold-start study

Although computational LDA prediction has made significant progress, many current models severely depend on well-characterized elements and may exist performance decline when facing unobserved nodes, which are usually regarded as the “cold-start” issue. To estimate the robustness and generalization ability of LDA-CAMF in this scenario, we performed experiments to simulate the scenario with new lncRNAs or rare diseases without prior associations. Particularly, under training across one 5-fold CV, 20% of lncRNAs or diseases were selected at random, and all corresponding heterogeneous associations were removed. The remaining was used to estimate the model’s ability to fit previously unseen associations.

Table 2 summarizes the performance under lncRNA cold-start scenario. The outcomes elucidated that LDA-CAMF gained the best-perform predictions under all metrics on lncRNADisease and MNDR. In comparison with the second-best method LDA-VGHB, LDA-CAMF improved AUC by 2.76% and 0.80%, and AUPR by 2.34% and 0.54% on lncRNADisease and MNDR, respectively. Additionally, LDA-CAMF computed higher recall and implemented more stable predictions with lower standard deviations.

Influence of different classifiers

We also tested the influences of different classification algorithms on predictions. That is, we compared XGBoost with four widely-used models: random forest, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), decision tree, and Support Vector Machine (SVM). To fairly compare their performance, each model was trained on the same feature vector after feature concatenation.

Figure 3 depict their performance. The outcomes across six metrics elucidated that XGBoost consistently outperformed other four classifiers in most cases, suggesting its superior power in balancing inference accuracy and generalization ability. Particularly, it calculated the best AUC scores of 0.9362 and 0.9759 on two datasets, respectively, indicating strong discriminative performance in differentiating positive from negative LDPs. The relatively lower predictive precision computed by decision tree and SVM stand out the importance of gradient boosting-based algorithms in solving complex feature interactions when capturing novel LDAs.

Ablation study

To estimate the effects of individual modules in LDA-CAMF, we built several variant models by excluding individual modules or input representations. One variant removed GAPK similarity for both diseases and lncRNAs (Without GAPK ). Another excluded lncRNA functional similarity and disease semantic similarity (Without Fun and Sem). Moreover, we tested LDA-CAMF without cross-attention (Without cross-attention), and LDA-CAMF without multi-level feature fusion (Without feature fusion). Additionally, we also investigated a variant where the final embeddings were not concatenated with the original features (Without concatenation). These ablated modules enabled us to estimate their contributions to the overall performance. Figure 4 give the results.

The outcomes suggested that the full model LDA-CAMF, integrating all designed modules, performed better in capturing associations. Compared to these variants without individual modules, the complete LDA-CAMF framework had superior effectiveness in LDA prediction, protruding the active effects of multiple data source integration, the cross-attention mechanism, multi-level feature fusion, and feature concatenation.

Discussion and conclusion

Uncovering LDA candidates using computational methods is necessary to investigate the roles of lncRNAs in deciphering pathological mechanisms, diagnosis and therapy of complex diseases. Here, we presented a novel computational model, LDA-CAMF, for identifying LDA candidates by incorporating a cross-attention mechanism and multi-level feature fusion strategy. First, semantic information, functional information, and GAPK were integrated to construct disease and lncRNA similarity networks. Next, a cross-attention module was presented to dynamically decipher high-order interdependencies between lncRNAs and diseases. Subsequently, a multi-level feature fusion strategy was devised to incorporate hierarchical node representations learned from different layers. Finally, the learned representations of lncRNAs and diseases were concatenated and fed into XGBoost to perform association prediction.

To comprehensively estimate the LDA-CAMF performance, we conducted multiple extensive experiments on lncRNADisease and MNDR. First, to test the robustness of LDA-CAMF, we compared it with six baselines, i.e., SDLDA, LDNFSGB, IPCARF, LDASR, and LDA-VGHB. The results from 5-fold CV elucidated that LDA-CAMF had superior LDA inference ability from unknown pairs. Next, to estimate the robustness and generalization ability of LDA-CAMF under the “cold-start” issue, we simulated the scenario with new lncRNAs or rare diseases. Under the scenarios, LDA-CAMF gained the best-perform predictions. Additionally, to evaluate the influence of classifiers on predictions, we calculated the accuracy of six classifiers, XGBoost, random forest, MLP, decision tree, SVM. LDA-CAMF demonstrated potential power in balancing LDA predictive accuracy and generalization ability. Subsequently, to validate the contributions of individual modules on predictions, we considered five variant models by excluding individual modules. LDA-CAMF with all designed modules performed better. Finally, we used LDA-CAMF to uncover potential lncRNAs for prostate cancer and diabetes. The results demonstrated that HNF1A-AS1 and BCYRN1 had associations with prostate cancer, and HAR1A had an association with diabetes.

LDA-CAMF performed better than other algorithms due to its three advantages. First, the cross-attention mechanism enabled LDA-CAMF to effectively model complex and heterogeneous interactions between lncRNA and disease features. Second, The multi-level feature fusion strategy effectively preserved low-level structural information while incorporating high-level interaction-aware features. Finally, the original features were integrated to the optimized features and enhanced the model expressive ability.

LDA-CAMF accurately performed predictions for novel LDAs. However, abundant biological information assists in LDA prediction. In the future, we will aggregate more biological features to improve LDA inference. Moreover, well-characterized classifiers can advance LDA prediction. Thus, a high-efficient classification model, for example, graph representation learning52–54, contrastive learning refinements55,56 or deep neural network57,58, will be necessary to accurate LDA prediction. We hope that LDA-CAMF contributes to biomarker identification and mechanism investigation of complex diseases.
