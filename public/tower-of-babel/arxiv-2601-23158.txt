Some series representing the zeta function for ℜ ⁡ s > 1 \Re s>1
Jean-François Burnol

Abstract
. We present series converging to the Riemann zeta function in its half-plane of convergence, and possessing remainders whose sizes decrease geometrically. They are easy to implement numerically, using only polynomial and power functions, and are efficient for obtaining dozens or hundreds of digits (when the imaginary part is not too large). They may prove less suited to very high precision (tens of thousands of digits), due to a linear cost for each new term. One can express the coefficients as linear combinations of Bernoulli numbers, but this is not advantageous numerically. The method is a development of tools introduced by the author for the evaluation of harmonic series with restricted digits in a given radix.

Main result

In this section, we specialize to
b=2
the Theorem
2
which
concludes this paper and applies to all integers
b>1

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}
,
\Re s>1
. Define coefficients
u_{m}^{*}(s)
via a linear recurrence:

\displaystyle u_{0}^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-2},

(1)

\displaystyle m\geq 1

\displaystyle\implies u_{m}^{*}(s)=\frac{1}{2^{s+m}-2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(s+m)\dots(s+m-j+1)}{j!}u_{m-j}^{*}(s).

Let
\ell
be an integer at least
2

(2)

\zeta(s)=\sum_{0<n<2^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}+\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-2}\sum_{2^{\ell-1}\leq n<2^{\ell}}\frac{1}{n^{s}}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{s\,u_{m}^{*}(s)}{s+m}(\sum_{2^{\ell-1}\leq n<2^{\ell}}\frac{1}{n^{s+m}}).

The series with alternating signs at the end of (

convergent. For

Explicitly, with

\zeta(s)=1+\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-2}\bigl(\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{3^{s}}\bigr)\\
+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{s\,u_{m}^{*}(s)}{s+m}\bigl(\frac{1}{2^{s+m}}+\frac{1}{3^{s+m}}\bigr).

With

\begin{split}\zeta(s)&=1+2^{-s}+3^{-s}+\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-2}\bigl(4^{-s}+5^{-s}+6^{-s}+7^{-s}\bigr)\\
&+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{s\,u_{m}^{*}(s)}{s+m}\bigl(4^{-s-m}+5^{-s-m}+6^{-s-m}+7^{-s-m}\bigr).\end{split}

A priori bounds allow to estimate how many terms will be enough for a given

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle>1:\frac{1}{2^{s}-2}<u_{m}^{*}(s)\leq\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-2},

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\sigma+it:\limsup_{m\to\infty}|u_{m}^{*}(s)|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\sigma+1)}{|\Gamma(s+1)|}\frac{2^{\sigma}}{2^{\sigma}-2}.

Defining

u_{m}(s)=\frac{1}{m+1}\frac{2^{s}}{2^{s}-2}-\frac{2^{s+1}}{2(2^{s+1}-2)}+\sum_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor}\frac{m!}{(m-2k+1)!}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{2^{s+2k}}{2^{s+2k}-2}\;.

2.
Measures on words, and moments

Let
b>1
an integer. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the space of words (also called finite
strings) on the alphabet
\mathsf{D}=[\![0,b-1]\!]
of the
b
-ary digits. The
empty word is noted
\epsilon
. The length of a word
w
is noted
\ell(w)
.
The power notation
w^{j}
means contatenation (
w^{2}=ww
, etc…). And

w^{0}=\epsilon
.

Definition 1
.

For
w=d_{1}\dots d_{l}\in\mathcal{W}
we say that
w
“represents” the integer

n(w)=d_{1}b^{l-1}+\dots+d_{l}\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
n(\epsilon)=0
. And we
define
x(w)\in[0,1\rparen
by the formula
x(w)=n(w)/b^{\ell(w)}
. In other
terms,
x(w)
is obtained in
b
-radix representation from positioning the
word
w
immediately to the right of the radix separator.

Let
A
be some subset of
\mathsf{D}
. Its elements will be called the
“admissible” digits, and words using only them are said to be
“admissible”. The admissible integers are those whose minimal

b
-representation is an admissible word. So
0
is always an admissible
integer, but is not necessarily an admissible digit.

Throughout this section it is assumed that the set
A
of admissible digits is
neither empty nor reduced to the singleton
\{0\}
. So the set
x(\mathcal{W})

contains positive elements. Contrarily to
[
4
]
it is now
allowed for
A
to be the full alphabet of digits. We let
N
be the
cardinality of
A
and
N_{1}
the one of
A\setminus\{0\}
. So
0<N_{1}\leq N\leq b
.

Definition 2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We define a positive measure
\mu_{A,s}
on
\mathcal{W}
.

•

If the word
w
is admissible,
\mu_{A,s}(\{w\})=b^{-s\ell(w)}
.

•

Else,
\mu_{A,s}(\{w\})=0
.

For
s
complex non real, such that the positive measure
\mu_{A,\Re s}
is
finite (see next), we use the same formulas. So
\mu_{A,s}
is a complex
measure and its measure of variations is
\mu_{A,\Re s}
.

Observe that
\mu_{A,s}(\{\epsilon\})=1
, whenever
\mu_{A,s}
is defined,
and that
\mu_{A,s+2\pi i/\log(b)}=\mu_{A,s}
if
\mu_{A,s}
is a complex measure.

We compute the total mass for real
s
:

\mu_{A,s}(\mathcal{W})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}b^{-sl}N^{l}=\begin{cases}\frac{b^{s}}{b^{s}-N}&(s>\log_{b}(N))\\
+\infty&(s\leq\log_{b}(N))\end{cases}

For
s
complex, the series converges if and only if it converges absolutely
if and only if
|b^{s}|>N
. Thus,
\mu_{A,s}
is well-defined as a complex
measure if and only if
\Re s>\log_{b}(N)
. Let us record this formula for
the total “mass” (but this term is a bit mis-leading if
s
is non-real) for

s
in the open half-plane of convergence:

(4)

\mu_{A,s}(\mathcal{W})=\frac{b^{s}}{b^{s}-N}.

Except for the sole case with
A=\mathsf{D}
, one has
N<b
,
\log_{b}(N)<1
, so then
s=1

is in the open half-plane of those complex numbers to which we have associated
a complex measure on the space of words
\mathcal{W}
.

We record the following observation (which is not
used elsewhere) for real
s
:

Lemma 1
.

For any non-negative bounded function
\phi
on
\mathcal{W}
, such that there is at
least one admissible non-empty word
w
with
\phi(w)>0
, the function

g(\sigma)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\phi(w)d\mu_{A,\sigma}(w)
is positive, strictly decreasing,
and log-convex on
\lparen\log_{b}(N),\infty\rparen
.

Proof.

The quantity
g(s)
is, up to the additive constant
\phi(\epsilon)
, a
positive combination of strictly decreasing log-convex functions
\sigma\mapsto b^{-l\sigma}
, for some indices
l\geq 1
at least.
∎

Definition 3
.

The “moments”
u_{A,s}(m)
of the complex measure
\mu_{A,s}
are:

u_{A,s}(m)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}x(w)^{m}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(w).

Note that the moments are invariant under
s\mapsto s+2\pi i/\log(b)
.
Observe further, using that
A
is neither empty nor reduced to
\{0\}
, the strict
inequality:

\Im s\notin\frac{2\pi\mathbb{Z}}{\log b}\implies|u_{A,s}(m)|<u_{A,\Re s}(m).

(if
a\neq 0
is any admissible digit it contributes in particular

a^{m}b^{-m-s}+(ba+a)^{m}b^{-2m-2s}
and the modulus of this can be equal to the
sum of the moduli of the two summands only if their ratio is a positive real
number, i.e. if and only if
b^{s}>0
). As we have assumed that
A
is
neither empty nor reduced to the sole digit
0
, the measure is not supported
only on those
w
’s such that
x(w)=0
. Consequently, if
s
is real (and

>\log_{b}N
), the sequence of moments is strictly decreasing. And for every
complex
s
with
\Re(s)>\log_{b}N
, the moments converge to zero as

m\to\infty
, from dominated convergence (or elementary arguments).

Proposition 1
.

The moments of
\mu_{A,s}
obey the following recurrence relation:

(5)

m\geq 1\implies(b^{m+s}-N)u_{A,s}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(\sum_{a\in A}a^{j})u_{A,s}(m-j).

Proof.

We decompose non-empty words as a one-digit prefix
a
and a suffix of
length one less. The “weight” of
aw
is
b^{-s}
times the weight of

w
. In the next equations
\delta_{0}
is the Direc delta function. Let

m\geq 0
:

\displaystyle u_{A,s}(m)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(m)+\sum_{a\in A}\int_{\mathcal{W}}x(aw)^{m}b^{-s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(w),

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(m)+\sum_{a\in A}\int_{\mathcal{W}}b^{-m}\bigl(a+x(w)\bigr)^{m}b^{-s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(w),

\displaystyle u_{A,s}(m)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(m)+b^{-m-s}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(\sum_{a\in A}a^{j})u_{A,s}(m-j).

For
m=0
we recover
u_{A,s}(0)=\frac{b^{s}}{b^{s}-N}
. For
m>0
, we obtain
(
5
).
∎

Let us record here:

(6)

\displaystyle u_{A,s}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{s}\sum_{a\in A}a}{(b^{s}-N)(b^{s+1}-N)},

(7)

\displaystyle u_{A,s}(2)

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{s}\left(2(\sum_{a\in A}a)^{2}+(b^{s+1}-N)\sum_{a\in A}a^{2}\right)}{(b^{s}-N)(b^{s+1}-N)(b^{s+2}-N)}.

Consider now the following «restricted» zeta value, where the
′
sign indicates
that only those integers are kept, which are
A
-admissible:

(8)

K_{b,A,s}=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{n>0}\frac{1}{n^{s}}.

We have included
b
into the subscript, it should arguably have been used
also with the measure and the moments. We choose a
level

\ell\geq 1
,
and consider the contributions from those admissible integers having at least

\ell
digits. We gather them according to the
admissible
word
w
of
their first
\ell
digits, so that the minimal representation of
n
is
wz
with
some word
z
, possibly empty.

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=b^{|z|}\bigl(b^{|w|}x(w)+x(z)\bigr)

(9)

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{n\text{ starts with }w}\frac{1}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle=\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\bigl(n(w)+x(z)\bigr)^{-s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(z)

Caution that here we do not allow
w=\epsilon
, else, we would need to
restrict the integral to those
z
’s not starting with a
0
(i.e. such that

x(z)\geq b^{-1}
), which would then indeed give a formula for the full

K_{b,A,s}
, but we do not use it here. In the process leading to the formula
(
9
) with
s\in\mathbb{R}
arbitrary, everything is non-negative
and the re-writing of the Dirichlet series as an integral against a positive
measure
\mu_{A,s}
is valid for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. As any function
x\mapsto(a+x)^{-s}
on
[0,1\rparen
with
a>0
has a positive minimum and is bounded, its
integral against a positive measure is finite if and only if that measure is
finite. So the restricted harmonic series (
8
) converges if and only
if the push-forward of
\mu_{A,s}
under the map
x:\mathcal{W}\to[0,1\rparen
is a finite
measure, i.e. if and only if
\mu_{A,s}(\mathcal{W})<\infty
, i.e. if and only if

s>\log_{b}N
. See
[
8
,
10
,
1
]
for
prior occurrences of this abscissa of convergence in the literature.

For
\Re(s)>\log_{b}(N)
the partial series are thus absolutely convergent and
the formula (
9
) is valid. Throughout the paper from now
on, we assume
\Re(s)>\log_{b}(N)
.

We appeal to the binomial series and intervert series and integral:

(10)

\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{(s)_{m}}{m!}\frac{x(z)^{m}}{n(w)^{m+s}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{(s)_{m}}{m!}\frac{u_{A,s}(m)}{n(w)^{m+s}}

Here,
(s)_{m}=s(s+1)\dots(s+m-1)
is the ascending factorial with
m
terms
(Pochhammer symbol). We have assumed
n(w)>1
so that the series depending on

x(z)
is uniformly and absolutely convergent on
\mathcal{W}
and termwise
integration is justified. It is asserted in the next proposition that this
interversion is valid also for
n(w)=1
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
s
be a complex number
such that
\Re s>\log_{b}(N)
. The series

L_{b,A}(s)\coloneq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\frac{(s)_{m}}{m!}u_{A,s}(m)

converges and is equal to
\int_{\mathcal{W}}\bigl(1+x(w)\bigr)^{-s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(w)
.
Let

\lambda=\frac{\max A}{b-1}\in\lparen 0,1]
.

•

The remainders are
o(\lambda^{m})
. If
\Re s\geq 1
, they are more precisely

O(\lambda^{m}/m)
.

•

If
\lambda=1
and
s
is real, the series
is only semi-convergent.

Multiplying each term with an additional factor
n^{-m-s}
for
n
some
integer
>1
(or any real number
>1
), we thus obtain a geometrically
convergent series. For
s
real, its partial sums are upper and lower
bounds depending on parity of number of terms.

Proof.

In this proof, we appeal to estimates which will be established only later.
Let us start first with a Taylor expansion with integral remainder for the
function
(n+x)^{-s}
where
n>0
is for now some arbitrary positive real
number,
s
is a complex number, and
x\geq 0
. It reads:

(n+x)^{-s}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!n^{s}}\frac{x^{k}}{n^{k}}=(-1)^{m}\frac{(s)_{m}}{(m-1)!}\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)^{m-1}(n+t)^{-s-m}\mathrm{d}t.

We observe in passing that, if
s
is a positive real number, the sign of the
left hand side has thus to be
(-1)^{m}
, except of course for
x=0
. Let now
s

again be some arbitrary complex number, with real part
\sigma=\Re s
. We get:

\left|(n+x)^{-s}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!n^{s}}\frac{x^{k}}{n^{k}}\right|\leq\frac{|(s)_{m}|}{(m-1)!n^{\sigma+m}}\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)^{m-1}(1+n^{-1}t)^{-\sigma-m}\mathrm{d}t.

Suppose now that
\sigma+m\geq 0
. Then (and this is surely a standard calculus exercise):

\left|(n+x)^{-s}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!n^{s}}\frac{x^{k}}{n^{k}}\right|\leq\frac{|(s)_{m}|x^{m}}{m!n^{\sigma+m}}

We assume from now on that
\sigma=\Re s>\log_{b}N
. So in particular the
hypothesis
\sigma+m\geq 0
is definitely true. We will apply the above upper
bound after replacing
x
by the function
x(w)
on
\mathcal{W}
. For now, we still
do not suppose
n
to be an integer, only
n>0
.

\displaystyle\delta_{m}(s,n)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left|\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\bigl(n+x(z)\bigr)^{-s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(z)-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!n^{s+k}}u_{A,s}(k)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\biggl(\bigl(n+x(z)\bigr)^{-s}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!n^{s+k}}x(z)^{k}\biggr)\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\left|\bigl(n+x(z)\bigr)^{-s}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(s)_{k}}{k!n^{s+k}}x(z)^{k}\right|\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,\sigma}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\frac{|(s)_{m}|x(z)^{m}}{m!n^{\sigma+m}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,\sigma}(z)=\frac{|(s)_{m}|}{m!n^{\sigma+m}}u_{A,\sigma}(m).

Recall
(s)_{m}/m!\sim_{m\to\infty}\Gamma(s)^{-1}m^{s-1}
, so
|(s)_{m}|\sim m!|\Gamma(s)|^{-1}m^{\sigma-1}
. Further, we know
u_{A,\sigma}(m)\leq u_{A,\sigma}(0)
(and even that it decreases to zero). We can thus say for

n>1
, that
\delta_{m}(s,n)=o(c^{m})
for any
c\in\lparen n^{-1},1\rparen
. This
provides, for the particular situation with

an admissible integer

>1
, a more detailed explanation for our earlier discussion of equations
(
9
) and (
10
).

Let us now assume
n=1
(we do not require it to be admissible). So

\delta_{m}(s,1)
gives an upper bound to the distance from the
m
th partial
sum of
L_{b,A}(s)
to
\int_{z\in\mathcal{W}}\bigl(1+x(z)\bigr)^{-s}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(z)

(the latter integral is obviously absolutely convergent because the integrand
is bounded). Suppose first
\sigma\leq 1
. From
|(s)_{m}|\sim m!|\Gamma(s)|^{-1}m^{\sigma-1}
, we deduce that

\delta_{m}(s,1)=O(u_{A,\sigma}(m))
, hence, it is
o(1)
. More precisely, from
next Lemma
2
, it is
o(\lambda^{m})
with
\lambda=\max A/(b-1)
.
This establishes the convergence and the value of
L_{b,A}(s)
.

Take now
\sigma>1
. We have

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{|(s)_{m}/m!|}{(\sigma)_{m}/m!}=\frac{\Gamma(\sigma)}{|\Gamma(s)|},

and thus, there is some positive constant
C(s)
such that
\delta_{m}(s,1)\leq C(s)u_{A,\sigma}(m)\frac{(\sigma)_{m}}{m!}
. Hence, from Proposition

4
, we conclude that for
\Re s>1
(even
\geq 1
) the quantity

\delta_{m}(s,1)
is
O(\lambda^{m}m^{-1})
and conquently is
o(\lambda^{m})
.

Finally, assuming
s=\sigma
is real, we have from Proposition

3
the lower bound

\frac{(\sigma)_{m}}{m!}u_{A,\sigma}(m)>\frac{\sigma}{\sigma+m}\lambda^{m}/(b^{\sigma}-N)
.
Hence, if
\lambda=1
the series defining
L_{b,A}(\sigma)
is only
semi-convergent.
∎

We shall now establish the various estimates used in the
previous proof. We use the notation
f=\max A
. So
f\leq b-1
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
s
with
\Re s>\log_{b}(N)
,
the sequence
(\Bigl(\frac{b-1}{f}\Bigr)^{m}u_{A,s}(m))_{m\geq 0}
has limit zero.

Proof.

Due to
|u_{A,s}(m)|\leq u_{A,\Re s}(m)
, we can assume
s
to be real. Now
define for each word
w
the rescaled function
y(w)=\frac{b-1}{f}x(w)
.
Thus
0\leq y(w)<1
for every
admissible

w\in\mathcal{W}
. The
non-admissible
w
may have
y(w)\geq 1
but they live in a set of zero
measure. By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, the integral against

\mu_{A,s}
of the powers of the function

go to zero (if
s
is real,
we can even say that the rescaled moments decrease to zero).
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
m\geq 0
.
For
s>\log_{b}(N)
there holds (with
f=\max A
):

u_{A,s}(m)>\frac{s}{m+s}\frac{m!}{(s)_{m}}\frac{(f/(b-1))^{m}}{b^{s}-N}.

Proof.

Let us temporarily set
v_{A,s}(m)=u_{A,s}(m)/u_{A,s}(0)
. They obey the
same recurrence (
5
) as the
u_{A,s}(m)
:

(b^{m+s}-N)v_{A,s}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(\sum_{a\in A}a^{j})v_{A,s}(m-j).

We can only decrease these quantities if we replace in this recurrence

b^{m+s}-N
by its upper bound
b^{m+s}-1
in the left hand side, and

\sum_{a\in A}a^{j}
by its lower bound
f^{j}
, where
f=\max A
. So

v_{A,s}(m)\geq w_{A,s}(m)
where
w_{A,s}(0)=1
and for
m\geq 1
:

(b^{m+s}-1)w_{A,s}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}f^{j}w_{A,s}(m-j).

But this means that
w_{A,s}(m)=u_{A^{\prime},s}(m)/u_{A^{\prime},s}(0)
where
A^{\prime}
is the
singleton
\{f\}
. For the
A^{\prime}
-admissible words
f^{j}
, one has
x(f^{j})=(f/(b-1))(1-b^{-j})
. So:

u_{A^{\prime},s}(m)=f^{m}(b-1)^{-m}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b^{-sj}(1-b^{-j})^{m}.

Now, consider the function
t\mapsto g(t)=(1-t^{s^{-1}})^{m}
on
[0,1]
,
and assume
m>0
. This function is strictly decreasing. Considering the
evaluation points
\xi_{j}=b^{-sj}
,
j\geq 1
, we obtain:

\int_{0}^{1}(1-t^{s^{-1}})^{m}\mathrm{d}t<g(\xi_{1})(1-\xi_{1})+g(\xi_{2})(\xi_{1}-\xi_{2})+\dots=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-b^{-j})^{m}b^{-sj}(b^{s}-1).

So, putting everything together, we obtain, for
s>\log_{b}(N)\geq 0
,
m\geq 1
:

u_{A,s}(m)>\frac{b^{s}}{b^{s}-N}\frac{b^{s}-1}{b^{s}}\frac{f^{m}}{(b-1)^{m}}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}(1-t^{s^{-1}})^{m}\mathrm{d}t}{b^{s}-1}=\frac{s}{m+s}\frac{m!}{(s)_{m}}\frac{(f/(b-1))^{m}}{b^{s}-N}

We used the well-known formula for the Beta-integral. This final result holds
also for
m=0
and completes the proof of the lower bound.
∎

In view of the previous inequality we now define, for
m\geq 0
:

(11)

u_{A,s}^{*}(m)=\frac{(s+1)_{m}}{m!}u_{A,s}(m)=\frac{s+m}{s}\frac{(s)_{m}}{m!}u_{A,s}(m),

where again
(s+1)_{m}
is an
ascending
partial factorial.
With these quantities, (
5
) becomes

(12)

(b^{m+s}-N)u_{A,s}^{*}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(s+m)\dots(s+m-j+1)}{j!}(\sum_{a\in A}a^{j})u_{A,s}^{*}(m-j),

For the upper bound we will need the following lemma from calculus:

Lemma 3
.

Let
s\geq 1
be a real number,
m
a non-negative integer, and
a
a
non-negative real number. Then

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(s+m)\dots(s+m-j+1)}{j!}a^{j}\leq(1+a)^{s+m}-1-a^{s+m}.

Proof.

Of course the left-hand side is defined to be zero if
m=0
. The proof can
be done by induction on
m
, or, one can apply the Taylor
formula with integral remainder for the expansion of
(1+a)^{s+m}
in
ascending powers of
a
to
m
th order. This expansion gives
1
plus the
left-hand side above plus

\int_{0}^{a}\frac{(a-t)^{m}}{m!}(s+m)\dots(s+1)s(1+t)^{s-1}\mathrm{d}t.

As
s\geq 1
, we can use
(1+t)^{s-1}\geq t^{s-1}
in this integral. Then we
conclude via a Beta integral formula or we recognize that we have now the
remainder integral for the evaluation of
(0+a)^{s+m}
in ascending powers
of
a
up to order
m
. So this lower bound gives
a^{s+m}
and moving

a^{s+m}
and
1
to the other other side of the inequality we obtain the
result.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let
s\geq 1
(
s>1
if
N=b
). Let
f=\max A
. There holds, for every
m\geq 0
:

\frac{s+m}{s}\frac{(s)_{m}}{m!}u_{A,s}(m)=u_{A,s}^{*}(m)\leq\frac{f^{m}}{(b-1)^{m}}\frac{b^{s}}{b^{s}-N}.

Proof.

Let
c=u_{A,s}^{*}(0)
. Let
m\geq 1
and suppose by induction hypothesis

u_{A,s}^{*}(m-j)\leq(f/(b-1))^{m-j}c
for
1\leq j\leq m
. The power sums

\sum_{a\in A}a^{j}
with

are maximal when the
N
admissible
digits are
f
,
f-1
, …,
f-N+1
. In fact, we can stop with

,
because if

, this means that

contains the digit

, and the latter does
not contribute to the power sums anyhow. So, from (

):

\displaystyle(b^{m+s}-N)u_{A,s}^{*}(m)

\displaystyle\leq c\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(s+m)\dots(s+m-j+1)}{j!}\Bigl(\frac{f}{b-1}\Bigr)^{m-j}\sum_{f-N_{1}<a\leq f}a^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\frac{f}{b-1}\Bigr)^{m}c\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(s+m)\dots(s+m-j+1)}{j!}\sum_{f-N_{1}<a\leq f}\Bigl(\frac{(b-1)a}{f}\Bigr)^{j}.

By hypothesis

, so, using Lemma

(13)

(b^{m+s}-N)u_{A,s}^{*}(m)\leq\Bigl(\frac{f}{b-1}\Bigr)^{m}c\sum_{f-N_{1}<a\leq f}\Bigl((\frac{b-1}{f}a+1)^{s+m}-1-\bigl(\frac{b-1}{f}a\bigr)^{s+m}\Bigr).

As the

\frac{b-1}{f}a\geq\frac{b-1}{f}(a-1)+1\geq 1.

We can thus, as

-(\frac{b-1}{f}(f-N_{1})+1)^{s+m}

\displaystyle(b^{m+s}-N)u_{A,s}^{*}(m)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\frac{f}{b-1}\Bigr)^{m}\bigl(b^{s+m}-N_{1}-1\bigr)c

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\frac{f}{b-1}\Bigr)^{m}\bigl(b^{s+m}-N\bigr)c.

Hence the validity for

of the induction hypothesis is

∎

3.
Moment generating function

Let again

\mathsf{D}=[\![0,b-1]\!]

\mathcal{W}
the set of words on
the alphabet
\mathsf{D}
and

,

,

,
\Re s>\log_{b}(N)
. Recall from Definition

the map

Definition 4
.

The (exponential) moment generating function
E_{A,s}
is the entire
function defined by the formula:

E_{A,s}(t)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{e}^{tx(w)}\mathrm{d}\mu_{A,s}(w).

Proposition 5
.

Let
\alpha_{A}(t)=\sum_{a\in A}e^{at}
.
The following holds:

(15)

\forall t\in\mathbb{C}\hskip 10.00002ptE_{A,s}(t)=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}b^{-js}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\alpha_{A}(b^{-i}t).

There is a functional equation:

(16)

E_{A,s}(t)=1+b^{-s}\alpha_{A}(b^{-1}t)E_{A,s}(b^{-1}t).

Proof.

Indeed for
j\geq 1
,

\prod_{i=1}^{j}\alpha_{A}(b^{-i}t)=\sum_{a_{1},\dots,a_{j}\in A}\mathsf{e}^{(a_{1}b^{-1}+\dots+a_{j}b^{-j})t}=\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}\limits_{\ell(w)=j}\mathsf{e}^{x(w)t}.

This gives (
15
). And the functional equation (
16
) follows.
∎

The convergence is guaranteed by the original definition, but if one is a bit
dubious, one only needs to reformulate equation (
15
) to read:

(17)

E_{A,s}(t)=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{(b^{s}/N)^{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\frac{\alpha_{A}(b^{-i}t)}{N}.

Indeed, as
\alpha_{A}(z)/N=1+O_{z\to 0}(z)
, the infinite product

\beta_{A}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{A}(b^{-i}t)}{N}
is convergent,
everywhere in the complex plane (it vanishes at the
b^{j}z_{0}
,
j\geq 1
,

\alpha_{A}(z_{0})=0
, which exist except if
A
is a singleton). So, either the
series (
17
) has only finitely many non-zero terms (if
t
is a zero
of
\beta_{A}
) or its terms are equivalent up to some multiplicative factor
to those of the geometric series of ratio
N/b^{s}
. And we are assuming

\Re(s)>\log_{b}(N)
so this latter series converges.

Suppose (provisorily) that
0\in A
, then for

\Re t<0
the infinite product
\gamma_{A}(t)=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{A}(b^{i}t)
is convergent. Expanding in powers of

q=\mathsf{e}^{t}
,
|q|<1
, we recognize the series
\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}_{n\geq 0}}{\sum}q^{n}
where the exponents are the
admissible
integers.

One checks that the product
G_{A}(t)=\beta_{A}(t)\gamma_{A}(t)
(defined for
\Re t<0
) verifies the functional equation
G_{A}(bt)=N^{-1}G_{A}(t)
. However, we
prefer to not pursue that discussion, in favour of another direction, very
much alike in terms of tools, which does not make the assumption that
0
is
an admissible digit and is more focused on properties of
E(t)
for

t\to+\infty
.

For this, let
f=\max A
,
B=f-A
so that it contains the zero digit, and
let us write
\sum_{a\in A}\mathsf{e}^{at}
as
\mathsf{e}^{ft}\alpha_{B}(-t)=\mathsf{e}^{ft}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in B}\mathsf{e}^{-a^{\prime}t}
. The formula for the moment generating
function becomes:

(18)

E_{A,s}(t)=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}b^{-js}\mathsf{e}^{f(b-1)^{-1}(1-b^{-j})t}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\alpha_{B}(-b^{-i}t)

We observe that for
\Re t>0
, defining

\gamma_{B}(-t)\coloneq\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{B}(-b^{i}t)
, we obtain a
convergent infinite product, which in the variable
q=\mathsf{e}^{-t}
is
the subseries of
\sum q^{n}
where the exponents are restricted to have their
radix
b
digits only from the set
B=f-A
(in particular

is there).
Let
\lambda=f/(b-1)\in\lparen 0,1]
. We observe that we can reformulate
(

\Re t>0
, as:

(19)

t^{s}\mathsf{e}^{-\lambda t}\gamma_{B}(-t)E_{A,s}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigl(b^{-j}t\bigr)^{s}\mathsf{e}^{-\lambda b^{-j}t}\gamma_{B}(-b^{-j}t).

We realize that negative
j
’s would give a very quickly convergent series.
So we define for
\Re t>0
:

(20)

F_{A,s}(t)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(b^{j}t\bigr)^{s}\mathsf{e}^{-\lambda b^{j}t}\gamma_{B}(-b^{j}t).

The “corrections” (now associated with positive
j
’s as we replaced
-j
by

j
) go to zero exponentially fast when
t\to+\infty
. The limit function
being invariant under
t\mapsto bt
, it is susceptible to be expanded, say for

, into a Fourier series in the variable
\log t
(or better, a Laurent
series in the variable

, but we do not go into this here).

Proposition 6
.

Let
f=\max A
,
\lambda=\frac{f}{b-1}
,
B

and
\mathcal{B}
the

B

\lim_{k\to+\infty}(b^{k}t)^{s}\mathsf{e}^{-b^{k}\lambda t}E_{A,s}(b^{k}t),

and its value is, using the notation

:

t^{s}\mathsf{e}^{-\lambda t}\phi_{B}(q)E_{A,s}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigl(b^{j}t\bigr)^{s}\mathsf{e}^{-\lambda b^{j}t}\phi_{B}(q^{b^{j}}).

This limit
F_{A,s}(t)
is an analytic function of
t

\Re t>0
and it is invariant under

. Restricting to real positive

\int_{0}^{1}F_{A,s}(b^{u})e^{-2\pi iku}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\Gamma(s-2\pi i\frac{k}{\log b})}{\log b}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{(n+\lambda)^{s-2\pi i\frac{k}{\log b}}}.

Proof.

In equation (
20
), the non-positive indices contribute

, as shown in equation (
19
).
Replacing in (

gives the existence and value of the limit from the proposition.

We compute the

There are no problems of convergence (everything converges absolutely):
