Closing the loop on tourniquet safety in distal radius fracture fixation surgery: a two-cycle quality improvement project
Authors: Mahmoud Mersal, Samer Sarofeen, Osama Embaby, Sara Shammout, Ciara O’Sullivan, Chloe Lawrence, Sitaram Giri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe application of intraoperative tourniquets is a fundamental component of distal radius fracture surgery, yet it carries a risk of significant complications. The British Orthopaedic Association (BOA) has published safety guidelines (BOA Standards for Trauma (BOAST)) to standardise practice, but adherence is often suboptimal. This quality improvement project aimed to evaluate and improve compliance with these guidelines in a real-world clinical setting.MethodsA two-cycle retrospective audit was conducted at a major trauma centre in the UK. The first cycle (January to April 2023, n=34) established baseline compliance with seven key BOAST documentation standards. Following this, a multifaceted intervention was implemented, including staff education, the introduction of a standardised checklist and the placement of visual reminders in operating theatres. A re-audit was then conducted (May to June 2024, n=63) to measure the impact of these interventions.ResultsBaseline documentation compliance was extremely low, with only tourniquet pressure recorded in 20.5% (7/34) and tourniquet time documented in 55.9% (19/34). Following the interventions, documentation of tourniquet pressure increased to 59.0% (37/63) and tourniquet site, padding, compressive exsanguination, skin condition, tourniquet size and tourniquet time showed modest improvements. Overall compliance, however, remained below optimal levels.ConclusionsA targeted quality improvement initiative combining education and practical tools can lead to meaningful improvements in adherence to tourniquet safety guidelines. Despite these gains, persistent documentation gaps highlight the need for more robust, system-level solutions, such as integration into electronic health records, to ensure sustained compliance and enhance patient safety.Keywords: Quality improvement, Clinical Audit, Patient safety

Introduction

The use of a pneumatic tourniquet to create a bloodless surgical field is a near-ubiquitous practice in limb surgery, particularly for open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of distal radius fractures.1 While indispensable for improving visualisation and surgical efficiency, tourniquets are not benign devices. Their application is associated with a spectrum of potential complications, ranging from minor issues like pain and transient nerve palsy to severe, irreversible consequences such as permanent nerve injury, compartment syndrome, skin necrosis and thromboembolic events.2 3 The risk of these adverse outcomes is directly related to the pressure applied and the duration of ischaemia, necessitating strict adherence to evidence-based safety protocols.

Tourniquet-related nerve injuries remain the most prevalent complication, with recent evidence demonstrating that high pressure levels and pressure gradients are the primary causative factors.4 5 Studies using intraoperative neuromonitoring have shown that lower tourniquet pressures significantly reduce the risk of nerve injury, emphasising the importance of appropriate pressure selection.6 Furthermore, prolonged tourniquet application in emergency and elective settings may lead to post-tourniquet syndrome, characterised by limb weakness, stiffness and pallor, as well as an increased risk of thromboembolic events.5

To standardise practice and mitigate these risks, the British Orthopaedic Association (BOA) published its Standards for Trauma and Orthopaedics (BOAST) guideline on ‘The Safe Use of Intraoperative Tourniquets’ in October 2021.7 This guideline provides clear, evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice, including the use of a timer alarm, appropriate pressure settings based on age and systolic blood pressure, documentation of skin condition pre-application and post-application, the use of protective padding and contraindications for compressive exsanguination. These standards serve as a benchmark against which clinical practice can be audited and improved.

Despite the availability of these guidelines, studies consistently reveal significant variability in practice and suboptimal compliance with safety standards across institutions.8,10 A common theme identified in the literature is the failure of adequate documentation, which not only represents a medicolegal risk but also hinders the ability to monitor practice, identify trends in complications and implement effective quality improvement measures. Recent systematic reviews have synthesised best evidence for safe tourniquet use, highlighting 34 key recommendations spanning indications, contraindications, preoperative assessment, cuff selection, pressure settings, monitoring and documentation.11 This gap between evidence-based guidelines and real-world clinical practice represents a significant patient safety concern.

This quality improvement project was conducted at a major trauma centre in the UK that manages a high volume of orthopaedic trauma, including distal radius fractures. An initial internal review suggested that, despite the publication of the BOAST guidelines, there was no standardised protocol for tourniquet application or documentation in the operative record. This led to concerns about variable practice and a lack of auditable data to ensure patient safety.

Therefore, the primary aim of this project was to evaluate and improve adherence to the 2021 BOAST guidelines for tourniquet use in patients undergoing ORIF for distal radius fractures. We sought to quantify baseline compliance, implement a multifaceted quality improvement intervention and re-audit our practice to assess the impact of these changes and identify persistent barriers to implementation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This quality improvement project was structured as a retrospective, two-cycle audit conducted at a major trauma centre in the UK. The project focused on patients undergoing ORIF for distal radius fractures where an intraoperative tourniquet was used. The study was designed and is reported in accordance with the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE 2.0) guidelines.12

Participants and data collection

All patients who underwent ORIF for a distal radius fracture with the use of an intraoperative tourniquet during the two audit periods were included. Data were retrospectively extracted from the electronic operative record system, Prescribing Information and Communication System (PICS). No patient identifiers were collected, and all data were anonymised at the point of collection. We included all consecutive eligible distal radius fracture ORIF cases during each audit period. Cases were identified from theatre lists and operative records and cross-checked against the electronic record. We excluded cases where an upper-limb tourniquet was not used and cases with missing operative documentation. Clinical outcomes (eg, fracture healing or complications) were not assessed; the project evaluated documentation adherence to BOAST tourniquet standards.

Practice was audited against the standards set forth in the 2021 BOAST guideline, ‘The Safe Use of Intraoperative Tourniquets’.7 The following seven key documentation parameters were assessed:

Outcome measures and analysis

The primary outcome was the percentage of cases with documented compliance for each of the seven BOAST audit standards. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics. The absolute percentage change in compliance between cycle 1 and cycle 2 was calculated to measure the impact of the interventions. Due to the nature of this quality improvement project, formal inferential statistical testing was not performed. The primary outcome was documentation compliance. We did not analyse fracture healing, functional outcome or complication rates as part of this quality improvement project.

Results

Compliance with BOAST parameters

The implementation of the quality improvement interventions resulted in a marked improvement in the documentation of tourniquet safety parameters. At baseline, compliance was exceptionally poor, with five of the seven audited standards having a 0% documentation rate. Following the interventions, improvements were observed in all but one parameter.

The most significant improvements were seen in the documentation of tourniquet pressure, which increased from 20.5% in cycle 1 to 59.0% in cycle 2 (an absolute improvement of 38.5%), and tourniquet site, which rose from 0% to 32.0%. Modest but important gains were also made in the documentation of padding use (0% to 14.0%), skin condition (0% to 11.0%), correct tourniquet size (0% to 10.0%) and use of compressive exsanguination (0% to 13.0%). Documentation of tourniquet time remained stable, with 55.9% compliance at baseline and 56.0% in the second cycle.

Checklist completion was encouraged as part of the intervention; however, retrospective operative records did not consistently capture whether the checklist was completed for each case. This is acknowledged as a limitation, and future cycles will prospectively measure checklist uptake (eg, mandatory attachment/sticker or electronic tick-box).

Patient demographics

The mean age was similar across both cycles (52.4 years in cycle 1 vs 53.0 years in cycle 2), with a wide age range reflecting the diverse patient population presenting with distal radius fractures. In cycle 1, the majority of patients were female (76.5%), consistent with the known epidemiology of distal radius fractures. Approximately half of the fractures in cycle 1 were intra-articular (52.9%), indicating a cohort with moderate to high complexity injuries requiring surgical intervention.

Discussion

This two-cycle quality improvement project demonstrates that a targeted, multifaceted intervention can lead to significant improvements in the documentation of tourniquet safety measures. Starting from a baseline of near-zero compliance for most BOAST parameters, our interventions of education, a standardised checklist and visual reminders led to notable gains, particularly in the recording of tourniquet pressure and application site. However, the results also underscore the persistent challenges in achieving high-level compliance, as documentation rates for most parameters remained below 50% even after the interventions. This highlights that while educational and awareness campaigns are crucial first steps, they are often insufficient on their own to embed new practices fully.

Our primary finding is that documentation of tourniquet pressure saw the greatest improvement, increasing by 38.5 percentage points. This is a critical patient safety parameter, as excessive pressure is a key risk factor for nerve and soft tissue injury.2 9 Recent evidence using intraoperative neuromonitoring has confirmed that lower tourniquet pressures significantly reduce the impact on nerve innervation, with affected nerves recovering more quickly after deflation.6 The introduction of a checklist with a dedicated field for pressure likely prompted this change. The stability in the documentation of tourniquet time, which was already the most frequently recorded parameter at baseline, suggests that this metric was already considered a de facto standard of care, likely due to its direct link to ischaemic time and reperfusion injury risk.3 The modest improvements in other areas, such as skin condition and padding, suggest that these are still not consistently perceived as essential documentation points, despite their importance in preventing direct mechanical and chemical injury to the skin. Despite improvement, absolute documentation rates for tourniquet pressure (59.0%) and tourniquet time (56.0%) remained modest. Missing documentation does not necessarily indicate uncontrolled intraoperative practice; however, it prevents verification against safe standards, limits learning from any tourniquet-related complications and represents a patient-safety and medicolegal risk. Sustained high compliance is likely to require system-level change such as mandatory structured fields within the electronic operative note.

The barriers to consistent and complete documentation are multifactorial. The findings from our departmental discussions align with those in the literature, which cite time pressures in the operating theatre, a perceived increase in administrative burden and a lack of immediate negative feedback for non-compliance as significant obstacles.8,10 A recent comprehensive review of tourniquet use in trauma and orthopaedics emphasised that proper documentation is essential not only for medicolegal purposes but also for monitoring practice patterns and identifying complications.13 While our checklist was intended to streamline documentation, its adoption was not universal, indicating that passive tools are not always sufficient to change established habits. The slight dip in some metrics during intermediate phases of the second cycle (as seen in the raw data) may reflect the waning of initial enthusiasm and highlights the need for ongoing reinforcement.

Recent quality improvement initiatives in other institutions have demonstrated similar challenges and successes. Kafagi et al reported a three-cycle audit showing progressive improvements in tourniquet documentation following the implementation of electronic patient records (EPR), achieving higher compliance rates than our study.8 Similarly, Garner et al introduced a new proforma and achieved significant improvements across all aspects of tourniquet documentation, with electronic proformas proving particularly effective.9 These findings suggest that digital transformation and integration of structured documentation into EPR systems may be the most effective long-term solution to ensure sustained compliance.

The importance of comprehensive tourniquet safety extends beyond documentation. A recent scoping review of tourniquet-related complications identified nerve palsy as the most prevalent complication, with prolonged application leading to severe outcomes including post-tourniquet syndrome and thromboembolic events.5 The authors emphasised that improved training is essential to help providers accurately assess bleeding severity, apply appropriate interventions and reduce complications. This aligns with our findings that education alone, while beneficial, must be coupled with system-level changes to achieve optimal outcomes. Furthermore, evidence synthesis from multiple guidelines and systematic reviews has produced 34 best practice recommendations covering the entire spectrum of tourniquet use, from preoperative assessment to equipment safety and staff training.11 These comprehensive recommendations underscore the complexity of safe tourniquet use and the need for structured, multifaceted approaches to quality improvement.

This project has several limitations. Its retrospective design means we were reliant on the accuracy and completeness of the electronic operative notes; the actual practice of tourniquet use may have been better than the documentation suggests. However, from a medicolegal and quality assurance perspective, care that is not documented is considered not done. The study was conducted at a single major trauma centre, which may limit the generalisability of our findings, although the identified barriers are common to many healthcare systems. Furthermore, we did not measure the clinical impact of these documentation changes, and we cannot determine if the improved documentation led to a reduction in tourniquet-related complications. Future studies should incorporate clinical outcome measures, such as rates of nerve injury, skin complications and post-tourniquet syndrome, to assess whether improved documentation translates into improved patient safety. Additional limitations include potential confounders between cycles such as surgeon grade/experience, rotating theatre staff and workload/operational pressures, which may influence documentation behaviour. We also did not reliably capture checklist completion as a formal process measure during retrospective data collection.

Despite these limitations, the project provides valuable insights for clinical practice. It confirms that a structured quality improvement approach can be effective but also reveals the necessity of moving beyond education and reminders towards more robust, system-level solutions. To build on the progress made, our next steps will focus on integrating mandatory, structured data entry fields for tourniquet use within our electronic operative record system. This would make the correct documentation the default and easiest option, a strategy that has proven effective in other areas of quality improvement.8 14 Continuous monitoring of compliance and periodic re-audits will be essential to sustain and further improve our practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this two-cycle audit demonstrates that a focused quality improvement initiative, combining education with the introduction of a standardised checklist and visual aids, can significantly improve the documentation of tourniquet safety practices in orthopaedic surgery. While substantial progress was made from a very low baseline, achieving full and sustained compliance with national guidelines requires more than passive interventions. System-level changes, such as the integration of mandatory fields into electronic health records, are likely necessary to overcome persistent barriers and ensure that best practices are followed for every patient. We plan to continue this quality improvement journey with further audit cycles to assess the impact of such system-level changes.
