MedDRA adoption and adverse event reporting quality in gastrointestinal and abdominal surgery randomised controlled trials: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Nicholas C Camasso, Kohl Kirby, Noah Calvert, John Stroup, Ryan Langerman, Matthew Vassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to establish the frequency of Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) utilisation in gastrointestinal and abdominal surgical trials, identify predictors of its adoption and quantify the association between MedDRA use and adverse event (AE) reporting quality as measured by Completeness scores, registry–publication Concordance and overall Transparency indices.DesignCross-sectional analysis of matched randomised controlled trial registry–publication pairs.Participants116 gastrointestinal and abdominal surgery randomised controlled trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with results posted between September 2009 and December 2024 and an associated peer-reviewed publication.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were differences in AE reporting quality between MedDRA-documenting and non-documenting trials, measured using Harms Reporting Completeness Score (0–8), Concordance Score (0–7) and Harms Transparency Index (0–15). Secondary outcomes included prevalence of MedDRA adoption and predictors of MedDRA documentation via univariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 116 included trials, only 22 (18.8%) explicitly documented MedDRA use. Industry-funded trials (OR=29.32, 95% CI 8.94 to 118.50, p<0.001) and those with at least one US site (OR=4.59, 95% CI 1.22 to 30.02, p=0.050) demonstrated significantly higher rates of MedDRA adoption. Trials documenting MedDRA use demonstrated significantly improved reporting across all three score parameters: Completeness score (p<0.001), Concordance score (p=0.002) and Transparency Index (p<0.001). MedDRA use was also associated with lower rates of registry–publication discordance across key safety metrics: serious AE participant count registry–publication discordance was 59.1% in MedDRA-documenting trials and 85.1% in non-MedDRA trials; mortality reporting discordance was 60.0% in MedDRA trials and 82.1% in non-MedDRA trials.ConclusionDespite strong association with greater AE reporting completeness and registry–publication concordance, MedDRA adoption in gastrointestinal and abdominal surgical trials remains below 20%, concentrated among industry-funded studies. The predominance of unstandardised terminology and free-text strategies contributes to reporting inadequacies that complicate evidence synthesis and undermine evidence-based surgical practice. Journals, funding agencies, academic institutions and researchers should prioritise the adoption of standardised AE terminology to enhance transparency and improve surgical research.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251081191.Keywords: SURGERY, Adverse events, Patient-Centered Care, Research Design, Clinical Trial

Introduction

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) form the foundation of evidence-based surgical practice and inform clinical practice guidelines (CPG) such as those published by the American College of Surgeons, European Association for the Study of the Liver and American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons.1–3 However, RCTs are only as valuable as their adverse event (AE) reporting. Despite Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Harms guidance,4 substantial heterogeneity persists across surgical and medical trials, whether evaluating procedures, nerve blocks or perioperative strategies.5

Discordance between ClinicalTrials.gov registries and their associated peer-reviewed publication has been demonstrated across a variety of fields,6–15 representing a critical concern within the broader field of medical research. If physicians are basing standards of care on published research, such research must provide transparent AE reporting. Furthermore, the terminology used to report the same AEs differs between, and sometimes even within, studies,16 complicating the interpretation of AE information. Such discrepancies may undermine confidence in results and alter meta-analyses, ultimately affecting surgical decision making and patient outcomes. The Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) is a standardised and hierarchical terminology for AE classification.17 Although MedDRA is well established in regulated pharmaceutical trials, its uptake in academic and surgical research has received little attention and its association with AE reporting quality in surgical RCTs remains largely uninvestigated.18–23

This study aimed to characterise the adoption of standardised AE terminology in gastrointestinal and abdominal surgery RCTs and to describe the association of its adoption with reporting quality. By analysing matched registry–publication pairs, we sought to establish the prevalence of MedDRA documentation, identify the trial characteristics associated with its adoption and quantify the association between MedDRA documentation and AE reporting completeness and registry–publication concordance. In doing so, we aimed to characterise the current AE reporting landscape in this field and to evaluate whether standardised terminology represents an observable and actionable marker of reporting quality.

Methods

Study framework and protocol registration

This investigation constitutes a planned secondary analysis of a larger cross-specialty project evaluating AE reporting in clinical trials. The overarching study received PROSPERO pre-registration (ID:CRD420251081191).24 Comprehensive protocol documentation, including predetermined condition classifications, was uploaded to the Open Science Framework (OSF) before data extraction began.25 The current analysis targets trials related to gastrointestinal and abdominal surgery.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Search methodology

On 28 June 2025, the parent investigation executed a systematic ClinicalTrials.gov search, capturing interventional phase 2–4 and ‘Not Applicable’ trials with results posted from 27 September 2009 through 31 December 2024, consistent with Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act Final Rule timelines.26 We used a manually constructed Boolean search strategy containing surgical terms, identified synonyms and conceptually related terms to avoid the registry’s automated term expansion functionality. The full search strategy is available on OSF and in online supplemental file 1.

For each identified registered trial meeting inclusion criteria, the associated peer-reviewed publication was identified through the following sequence. First, the publication linked within the ClinicalTrials.gov record was retrieved where present. Where no linked publication was available, two reviewers independently searched PubMed and Google Scholar using, in order, (1) the trial’s ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT) registration number and (2) the exact registered trial title. Where these searches did not yield a match, a combination of the registered intervention and condition terms, together with the principal investigator name as recorded in the registry, was used to identify trial-associated publications. No date, language or publication-type filters were applied at the search stage; eligibility restrictions were applied subsequently during screening. The peer-reviewed publication whose primary outcomes matched those documented in the registry was retained. This retrieval process was performed in duplicate, with discrepancies resolved by consensus discussion. Obtained articles were deduplicated after article comparison. Other registries, such as the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, and unpublished literature were not consulted. ClinicalTrials.gov served as the exclusive registry given its unique structure and numerically defined AE fields enabling cross-platform comparison.

Inclusion and exclusion standards

We included trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov that evaluated at least one primary intervention or perioperative strategy related to gastrointestinal or abdominal surgery and had publicly posted results and an associated peer-reviewed publication. Eligible interventions encompassed procedural approaches (eg, bariatric surgery, colectomy, cholecystectomy, hernia repair) or perioperative strategies (eg, analgesic techniques, surgical devices, fluid management protocols). Trials were excluded if they lacked a corresponding peer-reviewed publication, were phase 1 or observational studies, reported only a trial protocol, were published in a non-English language, lacked surgical AE data or focused on non-gastrointestinal or abdominal surgery. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) diagram of the parent investigation is publicly accessible on OSF.25

Trial selection and surgery classification

Two independent reviewers screened all registry entries and publications according to predetermined eligibility standards. Inclusion of studies involved dual independent review with disagreement resolution through consensus discussion or third reviewer arbitration. This secondary analysis required no additional screening or data collection beyond the original investigation.

Data collection protocol

The parent database originated from dual independent extraction using a standardised, pilot-tested Google Form. Extractors completed structured training sessions and calibration exercises before commencing full-scale extraction. Variables captured encompassed trial features such as sponsorship, intervention classification, participant numbers and trial phase as well as comprehensive AE-related elements from both ClinicalTrials.gov and matched publications. AE language was documented inclusively, encompassing variant equivalent terminology such as ‘complications’ or ‘undesirable effects’, consistent with 42 CFR §11.10(a) regulatory definitions.26 Presentation ambiguities were documented and resolved during extraction. Data for the current analysis were obtained directly from this parent dataset.

Primary exposure: medical dictionary for regulatory activities documentation

The principal exposure involved explicit citation of MedDRA use within either the ClinicalTrials.gov results or the publication. Direct textual mention was mandatory; presumed or implicit usage was not recognised. Trials were stratified as registry-only, publication-only, both sources or absent MedDRA documentation from both the registry and publication. For regression modelling, MedDRA documentation was dichotomised as present versus absent.

Study outcomes: harms reporting completeness score

The principal outcome comprised an eight-component Harms Reporting Completeness Score developed from CONSORT Harms guidelines and the extraction framework structure.27 Publications earned one point for documenting each element: serious AE (SAE) participant counts, SAE event counts, other AE (OAE) participant counts, OAE event counts, explicit OAE frequency threshold, any AE results format (descriptive or tabular), AE-related treatment discontinuations and all-cause mortality. Total scores spanned 0–8.

Registry–publication agreement: concordance Score

Agreement between ClinicalTrials.gov and publications was evaluated across the seven AE dimensions: SAE participant counts, SAE event counts, OAE participant counts, OAE event count, AE-related discontinuations, mortality and OAE threshold (similar to the Harms Reporting Completeness Score minus the ‘any AE results format’). Dimensions achieved concordance when numerically equivalent across platforms. Trials lacking data in both sources for each specific dimension were omitted from that dimension’s denominator and therefore did not contribute to percentage calculations. Total Concordance scores ranged 0–7.

Combined harms transparency index

An integrated index combined the Completeness Score (0–8) and Concordance Score (0–7), producing a Harms Transparency Index spanning 0–15.

Scoring instrument development and reliability

The Harms Reporting Completeness Score, Concordance Score and Harms Transparency Index represent novel composite instruments developed specifically for this investigation and have not been formally validated in prior literature. Each domain included in the Completeness Score was selected based on its direct correspondence to a reporting element specified in the CONSORT Harms 2022 guidance and its extractability from both ClinicalTrials.gov structured results fields and peer-reviewed publications. Domains were selected to capture the core dimensions of harms transparency identified in the CONSORT Harms framework: whether events occurred, how many patients were affected, how many events occurred, under what reporting threshold and whether events led to treatment discontinuation or death. Equal weighting was applied across all domains on the basis that each individual element represents a discrete and meaningful dimension of reporting completeness; the absence of any single element constitutes a distinct reporting gap regardless of whether other elements are present.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), computed separately for registry and publication data. Registry extraction demonstrated near-perfect agreement (median κ=0.916; range: 0.000–1.000). Several variables with low kappa values, including SAE participant counts (κ=0.000) and SAE and OAE event counts (κ=0.284 and 0.301, respectively), reflected systematic divergence in not-reported classification rather than numeric disagreement; ICCs for these variables ranged from 0.991 to 1.000. Publication extraction demonstrated moderate to substantial agreement (median κ=0.657; range: −0.025 to 0.800), consistent with genuine ambiguity in how AE data, particularly the distinction between participant counts and event counts, are presented in clinical manuscripts. Variables with near-zero kappa values in the publication panel were similarly attributable to marginal prevalence constraints rather than rater error. For this reason, ICC values for the publication extraction remained within the range of 0.681–1.000 with a median ICC of 0.884. Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion.

Predictor analysis of medical dictionary for regulatory activities documentation

Univariable logistic regression examined predictors of MedDRA documentation (yes vs no). Predictor variables, selected prior to data extraction, included trial phase (eg, phase 2), sponsorship (eg, non-industry), AE burden (high vs low, determined by median total AE count from ClinicalTrials.gov), log10-transformed enrolment and US site involvement. Complete case analysis proceeded without imputation.

Analytical approach

Descriptive statistics characterised trial features and outcome distributions. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests assessed differences in Completeness, Concordance and Harms Transparency Index scores between MedDRA-documenting and non-documenting trials. Concordance percentages reflected only evaluable trials. All analyses used R (V.4.3 or later).

Bias mitigation and protocol adherence

Since the goal centred on evaluating documentation practices rather than clinical endpoints, conventional risk-of-bias instruments were not employed. Bias mitigation strategies included duplicate screening, duplicate extraction, reviewer training and predetermined analytic methods. Parent protocol deviations are documented on OSF; no deviations occurred for this surgery MedDRA analysis.25

Transparency and resource availability

All data, code, scoring rubrics, complete protocol documentation, search strategy and the PRISMA diagram of the parent investigation are publicly accessible through OSF.25 This investigation follows best practices for transparency, reproducibility and open meta-research standards.

Results

General characteristics of included trials

Online supplemental table 2 describes the general characteristics of included trials, including trial phase, allocation and enrolment, location, funding source, intervention type, MedDRA use and AE reporting frequency. Notably, of the 116 included studies, 18 (15.5%) reported the use of MedDRA solely in ClinicalTrials.gov, 1 (0.9%) reported the use of MedDRA strictly in the publication, 3 (2.6%) studies reported MedDRA use in both the registry and publication and 94 (81%) did not report MedDRA use in either data source.

Registry–publication concordance in adverse event reporting

Registry–publication concordance and discordance in AE reporting across the seven AE dimensions compromising the concordance score (SAE participant counts, SAE event counts, OAE participant counts, OAE event count, AE-related discontinuations, mortality and OAE threshold) is available in table 1. Most commonly, discordance was noted for each domain, with only AE-related discontinuations more commonly not reported in either source. The greatest discordance was found in the OAE reporting threshold, with 111 (95.7%) trials being discordant in this reported value. OAE participant counts also showed high discordance, with 105 (90.5%) studies reporting differing information between their registry and publication. The most commonly reported concordant statistic was SAE participant counts, where 23 (19.8%) trials reported identical information in both sources.

Temporal trends In medical dictionary for regulatory activities adoption

A Cochran-Armitage trend test evaluating MedDRA adoption by year of registry results posting identified no statistically significant temporal trend over the study period (p=0.085). Given the small number of MedDRA-documenting trials distributed across 13 years, annual adoption rates were highly variable and should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

This study’s analysis reveals strikingly low MedDRA adoption in gastrointestinal and abdominal surgery RCTs; fewer than 20% of trials explicitly documented its use in either the registry or associated peer-reviewed publication, despite growing calls for transparent and standardised AE reporting.28 This pattern is not unique to surgery: a recent review of AE data across public sources for approved pharmaceuticals similarly found standardised MedDRA queries were inconsistently applied, indicating that unstandardised AE reporting is a cross-disciplinary concern.28 Our findings demonstrate that industry funding and US site involvement are the dominant drivers of MedDRA utilisation, with industry funding demonstrating more than 29 times greater likelihood of adoption. Those that did document MedDRA use, however, showed significantly greater AE reporting scores. Completeness scores (5.0 vs 2.0), Concordance scores (1.0 vs 0.0) and Transparency Index scores (7.0 vs 2.0) were all significantly greater, on average, compared with their non-MedDRA-using counterparts.

As a cross-sectional analysis, this study establishes association and cannot support causal inference; documented MedDRA use may reflect, rather than produce, the more complete reporting observed. It is equally plausible that MedDRA documentation is simply a marker of trials conducted with greater methodological rigour, better-resourced data management and stronger reporting practices overall. This distinction, however, does not diminish the practical implications of our findings. Whether standardised terminology directly enhances reporting or simply identifies trials already predisposed to report well, it constitutes a disclosed, observable signal associated with substantially more complete and concordant AE reporting. In a field such as abdominal and gastrointestinal surgery, where AE reporting is demonstrably poor, the value of this marker does not depend on resolving the question of causation.

These marked differences in AE reporting carry direct implications for clinical practice. AE rates contribute to the development of CPG, inform patient counselling on procedural risks and contribute to accurate risk-benefit assessments for the practising surgeon. Regardless of MedDRA association, the magnitude of registry–publication discordance observed in this study contributes to a growing population of literature highlighting AE reporting failures.29–33 Trials using MedDRA were significantly more likely to report critical elements: SAE counts that distinguish minor perioperative AEs from life-threatening issues, explicit frequency thresholds clarifying reporting criteria and treatment discontinuation rates that signal concern.

MedDRA’s five-level hierarchy offers advantages beyond regulatory compliance. Unlike free-text descriptions, it provides precise event classification, minimising issues with synonymy and inconsistent categorisation. Currently, trialists may classify the same AE under differing high-level categories. For example, wound dehiscence was reported under ‘injury, poisoning, and procedural complications’ in one trial, and ‘infections and infestations’ in another.34 35 Trials may also vary in their description of the AE. While some may take the more descriptive approach, ‘Non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction’, others provide broader terminology, like ‘myocardial infarction’.35 36 Meta-analysts face impossible decisions in determining event equivalence, risking the introduction of subjectivity to the literature, and may therefore be forced to limit analysis to broader classifications by organ system grouping.

Standardised systems, such as MedDRA, have the potential to limit the inherent ambiguity present in current free-text descriptions, where identical AEs are described using different terms and categories across trials. Its hierarchical structure may simplify meta-analysis by promoting greater statistical summation and more consistent AE data for use in evidence-based CPG, patient education and risk-benefit analysis. Standardised classifications also have the potential to enhance registry–publication concordance by simplifying consistent reporting across platforms. These practices fulfil the ethical obligation to trial participants and physicians and ultimately to the patients, who rely on reproducible, accessible evidence that advances surgical care.

Industry funding emerged as the dominant predictor of MedDRA adoption, with industry-sponsored trials showing 29-fold greater odds of use compared with non-industry trials. This disparity likely reflects multiple mechanisms: pharmaceutical and device companies face regulatory requirements for standardised AE reporting to agencies like the FDA, possess the financial resources to licence MedDRA and house dedicated data management infrastructure with trained personnel. Academic investigators, conversely, may lack familiarity with MedDRA despite its widespread use within pharmaceutical trials, receive little training in standardised terminology systems and operate without dedicated safety reporting staff. These findings suggest MedDRA adoption barriers are structural and financial, not intellectual. Mandating use without addressing licensing costs, training infrastructure and data management support risks creating compliance burdens without enabling implementation.

Addressing the disparity in AE reporting requires coordinated action on the part of researchers, journals, meta-analysts, funding agencies and clinical practitioners. Journals should mandate standardised AE terminology as a requirement for publication, or prioritise manuscripts documenting standardised terminology as a quality marker analogous to trial registration; funding agencies should require the use of standardised AE terminology as a condition with provisions for MedDRA licensing and training, and academic medical centres should incorporate terminology training into their research fellowship curriculum. While MedDRA utilisation alone cannot ensure comprehensive AE reporting, it represents a critical component of transparency infrastructure that researchers, journals, academic medical institutions and funding agencies should prioritise in surgical research. The question, ultimately, is not whether MedDRA use proves methodological rigour, but whether a field with this magnitude of reporting discordance can justify continuing to forgo a tool consistently associated with better reporting.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration in interpreting the findings of this study. This analysis focused exclusively on gastrointestinal and abdominal surgery. Its generalisability to other surgical subspecialties and medical RCTs remains uncertain, though widespread reporting challenges, outside of MedDRA use, have been documented previously. This focus was deliberate: the present analysis constitutes one component of a larger, multi-specialty investigation of AE reporting. This investigation required explicit MedDRA documentation. Trials using MedDRA internally without disclosure would be misclassified as non-users. The direction of any resulting bias is uncertain, though given the observed association between MedDRA documentation and higher reporting quality, misclassified trials would most plausibly, though not absolutely, align with documented MedDRA user’s reporting quality, suggesting the true association may be stronger than estimated. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that some undisclosed MedDRA-using trials reported poorly, which could complicate this directional interpretation. The magnitude of misclassification remains unquantifiable without access to internal trial data management records. Additionally, undocumented MedDRA use itself represents a transparency failure; readers and meta-analysts cannot verify standardised coding practices without explicit disclosure.

Furthermore, our concordance assessment examined numerical agreement without evaluating the clinical impact of these discrepancies; those with 1% discrepancy and those with 20% discrepancy were both documented as discordant, though the clinical implication of the discordance is vastly different. Future work incorporating clinical significance thresholds to distinguish minor numerical variation from meaningful reporting failures would further enhance the interpretability of registry–publication concordance analyses in surgical research. Additionally, the predictor analysis was restricted to univariable logistic regression; with only 22 MedDRA-documenting trials, the 10 events-per-variable rule precluded reliable multivariable modelling. The association between industry funding and MedDRA adoption may therefore partially reflect confounding by correlated trial characteristics, and these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Equal weighting was applied across all domains of the Harms Reporting Completeness Score; alternative weighting schemes, such as those prioritising SAE reporting, may represent a direction for future methodological work.

The Harms Reporting Completeness Score, Concordance Score and Harms Transparency Index are investigator-developed instruments created for this study and have not undergone external validation. Each component was derived directly from a discrete reporting element specified in the CONSORT Harms 2022 guidance, conferring content validity by construction, and inter-rater reliability for their application is reported in the Methods. Nonetheless, these indices should be interpreted as pragmatic, transparent measures of reporting completeness and concordance rather than as formally validated instruments. External validation of these scores represents an important direction for future work.

This study did not examine alternative factors associated with more complete reporting; thus, MedDRA adoption may be an indicator of unmeasured factors such as the institutional research culture and data-management infrastructure present at trial sites. This study relied exclusively on ClinicalTrials.gov, excluding other major registries such as the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, the European Union (EU) Clinical Trials Register, the International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) registry, the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry, the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials and the Chinese Clinical Trial Register. This restriction may introduce selection bias and limits generalisability to trials registered outside the USA. Such trials may differ significantly in MedDRA adoption rates, reporting infrastructure and regulatory requirements; the prevalence and predictors of MedDRA adoption in those populations remain unknown.

Conclusion

The utilisation of MedDRA with its explicit documentation in gastrointestinal and abdominal surgical trials is strongly associated with greater AE reporting completeness, concordance and overall transparency; yet adoption of MedDRA in this field falls below 20% of trials. Strong indicators of MedDRA use include industry-funded trials and those with at least one US site. As a whole, RCTs in this field report AEs with insufficient rigour to support evidence-based clinical decision making and reliable evidence synthesis. Current AEs reporting inconsistencies undermine patient counselling, guideline development and evidence-based care, problems that standardised terminology may help address. If journals prioritise MedDRA utilisation, it encourages funding agencies to mandate and budget for its implementation and prompts academic institutions to incorporate AEs reporting training into their research curriculum. Without standardised AE reporting, we risk building the future of evidence-based surgery on a foundation of incomparable and unreliable data.
