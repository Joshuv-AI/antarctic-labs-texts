Biomimetic photodynamic nanoparticles exert anti-tumor therapy by inducing ferroptosis in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Ziyan Hua, Naijian Zou, Bangyun Liu, Yuxin Niu, Liang Kong, Yang Yu, Juan Zang, Ruibo Guo, Yingqiang Lu, Xuetao Li, Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) has a high incidence and mortality. Conventional therapeutics suffer from poor targeting and severe side effects, making it still a great challenge to provide safe and effective treatment strategies. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a treatment option that is minimally invasive to patients and can be used repeatedly, and it is widely applied in clinical practice. However, PDT alone is limited by insufficient efficacy, tumor hypoxia, short circulation time in vivo, and low targeted delivery efficiency. The nanodelivery system provides important support for combination therapy and efficient drug delivery. In this study, biomimetic self-oxygenating nanoparticles (M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips) were constructed. Using 20(S)-Ginsenoside Rh2 (Rh2) as the membrane material, the system achieves stable co-loading of the chlorin e6 (Ce6) and catalase (CAT), and is surface-modified with an M1 macrophage membrane. In this system, ginsenoside Rh2 serves as a key anti-tumor component that targets SLC7A11, GPX4, and the ferroptosis pathway; it directly inhibits the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant axis, reduces GSH synthesis and GPX4 activity, promotes iron overload and lipid peroxidation, and synergizes with PDT to induce ferroptosis, while stabilizing the liposomal membrane structure. CAT decomposed hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to relieve tumor hypoxia, and the M1 macrophage membrane coating enabled active targeting and prolonged circulation. The nanosystem triggered rapidly accumulates through reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reduces the levels of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), glutathione (GSH) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), triggering ferroptosis to exert its effect.thereby realizing synergistic antitumor activity between Rh2 and PDT, and effectively suppressing the progression of NSCLC. This strategy shows promising application prospects in tumor biomimetic nanotherapy.Keywords: Biomimetic nanoparticles, Non-small cell lung cancer, Macrophage membrane, Photodynamic therapy, Hypoxia relief, Targeted delivery

Introduction

Lung cancer is a malignant tumor with a high number of new cases worldwide and the highest mortality rate [1]. NSCLC is the most common among the three main types of lung cancer. NSCLC is characterized by considerable therapeutic challenges and an unfavorable prognosis [2].Currently, multiple therapeutic strategies are available for NSCLC, ranging from chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Nevertheless, conventional treatments suffer from several limitations, such as severe trauma, prominent side effects, and a high risk of tumor recurrence [3]. Therefore, research new approaches to minimize trauma, alleviate adverse reactions, and suppress tumor recurrence is of great clinical significance for NSCLC patients.

As a promising tumor therapeutic modality, PDT has garnered considerable interest among researchers by virtue of its minimal invasiveness and superior therapeutic selectivity, and has been widely approved and utilized in clinical settings [4]. The antitumor activity of PDT is dependent on the synergistic action of photosensitizers, molecular oxygen (O2), and laser light with a specific wavelength [5]. Upon laser irradiation, energy is delivered from the photosensitizer to nearby oxygen molecules, leading to the production of abundant ROS. Excessive ROS dramatically increases intracellular oxidative stress and induces lipid peroxide accumulation, thereby exerting powerful antitumor effects [6]. Moreover, Ce6, a representative second-generation photosensitizer [7], possesses distinct advantages including a suitable absorption wavelength, strong tissue penetration, high singlet oxygen yield, and low toxicity, making it a highly promising candidate for PDT [8,9]. However, its poor aqueous solubility has severely hampered its clinical translation.

For solid tumors, the therapeutic efficacy of PDT is significantly limited by the hypoxic milieu of solid tumors. During PDT, oxygen is rapidly depleted by the continuous generation of ROS, which further aggravates local hypoxia and markedly impairs therapeutic efficacy [10,11].To overcome the hypoxia-related bottleneck, numerous methods have been developed to alleviate the hypoxic condition within tumors and thus enhance the therapeutic efficacy of PDT [12].Owing to metabolic disturbances and impaired antioxidant systems, tumor tissues exhibit massive accumulation of endogenous H2O2, at concentrations remarkably higher than those in normal tissues [13,14]. CAT is capable of catalyzing endogenous H2O2 decomposition into oxygen within the tumor microenvironment, thereby providing a feasible strategy to mitigate hypoxia and potentiate the therapeutic outcome of PDT [15].

Rh2 is a rare protopanaxadiol-type triterpenoid saponin derived from red ginseng. As a core bioactive constituent of ginseng, it has garnered extensive attention as a promising candidate for antitumor and adjuvant tumor therapy owing to its potent antitumor activity [16]. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that 20(S)-ginsenoside Rh2 exerts anti-non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) activity mainly by targeting the core ferroptosis-related SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 signaling axis. Mechanistically, Rh2 directly downregulates the mRNA and protein levels of SLC7A11 (xCT), a critical cystine/glutamate antiporter. Suppression of SLC7A11 markedly reduces cellular cystine uptake and subsequently blocks the biosynthesis of reduced glutathione (GSH). The resultant GSH depletion further leads to the loss of GPX4 enzymatic activity and the downregulation of GPX4 protein expression, thereby disrupting the homeostasis of the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 axis. The breakdown of this axis further causes uncontrolled lipid peroxidation, intracellular iron overload and mitochondrial damage, and eventually initiates ferroptosis in NSCLC cells [17]. In addition, Rh2 exhibits a chemical structure highly similar to cholesterol and can act as a lipid membrane component during nanocarrier preparation, which stabilizes the lipid bilayer and enhances drug encapsulation efficiency [18]. Therefore, Rh2 can be designed as a bifunctional molecule combining both membrane material functionality and antitumor activity, which not only facilitates efficient synergistic therapy with PDT but also provides a potentially effective regimen for targeted drug delivery.

As a crucial modality of regulated cellular demise, ferroptosis is elicited by the lethal buildup of a large amount of lipid peroxides within the cells and the dysfunction of antioxidant systems [19,20].It is mainly regulated via the GSH-GPX4 axis and is closely associated with iron metabolism and lipid metabolism. Intracellular free ferrous ions (Fe2+) generates hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction, which target and attack membrane-localized polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), thereby inducing a cascade of lipid peroxidation [21,22].Concurrently, GSH exhaustion and loss of GPX4 activity compromise the cellular capacity to scavenge lipid peroxides, ultimately leading to structural collapse of the plasma membrane due to oxidative imbalance [23,24]. Furthermore, mitochondria contribute to and accelerate ferroptosis by generating ROS and modulating iron metabolism [22,25,26]. Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis can potentiate the therapeutic potency of PDT, holding considerable promise for cancer treatment.

With the advancement of biomedical nanotechnology, nanoscale drug delivery systems exhibit numerous advantages in drug delivery and enhancement of drug solubility [27,28]. Co-encapsulation of Ce6 and CAT using nanocarriers cannot only effectively improve the solubility of the photosensitizer itself, but also achieve efficient delivery of CAT, providing an ideal platform for high-performance antitumor therapy [29].However, conventional nanodelivery systems may be removed by the host mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), causing a remarkable decrease in tumor accumulation efficiency [30].To tackle these challenges, the development of biomimetic cell membrane-coated nanoplatforms for reinforced nanomedicine delivery holds great promise in biomedicine and may open new avenues for precise tumor therapy [31,32]. Biomimetic nanosystems are fabricated by fusing synthetic nanoparticles with natural cell membranes, which combine the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials and the biological functions of cell membranes, thus enabling immune escape, prolonged blood circulation, and targeted accumulation in tumor tissues. To date, various cell sources have been utilized to construct biomimetic delivery systems, including red blood cell membranes [33], macrophage membranes [34], platelet membranes [35], mesenchymal stem cell membranes [36], and cancer cell membranes [37]. Among these, immune cell membranes have attracted substantial attention owing to their inherent tumor-homing potential and favorable long-circulation behavior in vivo. Macrophages, critical immune cells derived from bone marrow stem cells and differentiated from circulating monocytes [38,39], are closely involved in tumor progression and metastasis [40]. They are generally categorized into two major phenotypes, classically activated M1 and alternatively activated M2 macrophages. M1 macrophages mediate antitumor effects by phagocytosing and eliminating neoplastic cells; conversely, M2 macrophages contribute to tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [41]. Accordingly, coating nanoparticles with M1 macrophage membranes can not only extend blood circulation time but also achieve tumor-targeted accumulation. Mechanistically, M1 macrophages specifically recognize and adhere to tumor tissues through the interaction between integrin α4β1 on the cell membrane and VCAM-1 overexpressed on tumor cells and tumor vascular endothelium [42,43]. Meanwhile, the M1 membrane coating enables immune evasion from mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) clearance, further improving tumor-targeted delivery [44,45]. Thus, M1 macrophage membrane coating represents a highly promising biomimetic platform for precise tumor therapy.

In this study, a novel biomimetic self-oxygenating nanoparticle (M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips) was designed for precise tumor targeting and tumor eradication through hypoxia relief and drug-synergized photodynamic therapy. Specifically, the natural antitumor drug Rh2 was used instead of cholesterol as the membrane material of nanoparticles. Meanwhile, the photosensitizer Ce6 and CAT were encapsulated, and the surface was camouflaged with a biomimetic M1 macrophage membrane to form M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. After intravenous injection, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips achieved tumor-targeted accumulation through the interaction between surface membrane proteins (α4-integrin, β1-integrin) and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) expressed on cancer cell membranes [42].After the drug reached the tumor site, the photosensitizer Ce6 generated ROS under laser irradiation to exert PDT effects; CAT catalyzed intracellular H2O2 to generate O2, alleviating hypoxia and potentiating PDT. Furthermore, Rh2 attenuated the antioxidant capacity by inhibiting the SLC7A11-GPX4-GSH axis, promoted lipid peroxidation and induced ferroptosis [46], and synergized with PDT to amplify oxidative stress and tumor killing. The nanoparticles constructed in this study are expected to promote the development of hypoxia-enhanced PDT and enrich the design ideas of biomimetic nanocarriers and the theory of synergistic therapy.

Preparation of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips

Preparation of CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips

Nanoparticles were developed by means of the film dispersion approach. Overall, 22 mg of EPC, 5 mg of Rh2, and 0.5 mg of Ce6 were accurately weighed and solubilized in methanol, then rotary evaporated in a round-bottom flask to establish a lipid membrane film. Hydration of the film was performed using 5 mL of PBS incorporating 1 mg of CAT. Probe sonication was applied for 10 min, followed by filtration twice through a 0.22 μm membrane to obtain CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. Free CAT was removed by dialysis (molecular weight cutoff: 500 kDa, sample-to-PBS volume ratio: 1:100). Rh2 Lips and Ce6-Rh2 Lips were prepared using the same procedure.

Isolation of M1 macrophage membranes

RAW264.7 cells were co-treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (20 ng/mL) for 48 h to induce M1-type macrophages. Cell membranes were lysed using a membrane protein extraction kit combined with repeated freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen (5 cycles, 8 s each time). Membrane pellets were then isolated via two-step centrifugation (4 °C, 2500 rpm for 10 min; followed by 12000 rpm for 30 min). According to the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, the total membrane protein yield was 0.27 ± 0.035 mg per 1 × 108 M1 macrophages. The obtained membrane materials were stored at −80 °C until further use.

Preparation of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips

M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips were fabricated by coating CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips with M1 macrophage membranes via the extrusion method. Briefly, the isolated macrophage membranes were dispersed in an appropriate volume of PBS and sonicated for 3 min (45 W, 2 s on/2 s off) to form membrane vesicles. Subsequently, the macrophage membranes were thoroughly mixed with liposomes at the optimized phospholipid-to-macrophage membrane protein mass ratio. The mixture was sequentially extruded 10 times through 400 nm, 200 nm, and 100 nm polycarbonate membranes to yield uniform emulsion-like M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips.

In vitro release and stability studies

To investigate the in vitro release behavior, the formulations were dialyzed against PBS (pH 7.4) containing 10% FBS as the release medium to study their in vitro release kinetics. Briefly, 1 mL of each formulation was placed into a dialysis bag and incubated in the dark at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. At 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, 0.5 mL of the release medium was sampled, and an equal volume of fresh medium was replenished simultaneously. The Ce6 concentration was determined by HPLC, and the cumulative release was calculated accordingly.

The stability of the formulations was evaluated in terms of storage stability and serum stability. For storage stability, the formulations were stored at 4 °C for 30 days, and samples were collected at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days to determine the particle size and EE. For serum stability, the formulations were incubated in PBS containing 10% FBS at 37 °C, and samples were collected at the same time points to measure indicators including particle size and encapsulation efficiency.

In vitro targeting ability of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips

Based on the fluorescence property of Ce6, cellular uptake of the preparation was evaluated by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. Flow cytometry: 6-well plates were inoculated with LLC cells at a seeding density of 3 × 105 cells per well, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. The cells were then incubated with Ce6 solution, CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips (Ce6 concentration = 1 μg/mL), respectively. Untreated cells served as the negative control. After incubation for the designated time, cells were rinsed with PBS, digested, centrifuged, and resuspended. The fluorescence intensity of Ce6 was monitored using the FL3-H channel of a flow cytometer.

Fluorescence microscopy: LLC cells (1.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 48-well plates and cultured for 24 h, followed by the same drug administration as above and incubation for the designated time. Following PBS rinsing, cell fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature over 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 (λ = 352/461 nm) in the dark for 15 min. Cells were observed and imaged, and cellular uptake was evaluated according to the blue fluorescence (Hoechst, nucleus) and red fluorescence (Ce6, λ = 406/645 nm).

In addition, the cellular uptake capacity of BEAS-2B cells was evaluated in parallel using the same experimental methods described above.

Drug synergy and cytotoxicity analysis

Cytotoxicity of Ce6 and Rh2 in LLC cells

Growing LLC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were then exposed to various concentrations of Ce6 or Rh2, with 5 replicate wells per group. Following 4 h of incubationn, the cells were divided into a non-illuminated group (kept in the dark throughout) and an illuminated group (exposed to a 660 nm laser at 100 mW/cm2 for 5 min), and then incubated for a further 24 h under dark conditions. The culture medium was discarded, and the cells were fixed with trichloroacetic acid at 4 °C, stained with 0.4% Sulforhodamine B (SRB) staining solution, washed with 1% acetic acid for 5 times, and shaken in Tris-base solution for 30 min. Synergistic effect analysis

The procedure was performed as described in Section 2.9.1. LLC cells were seeded and allowed to adhere. Concentration gradients of Ce6 (0, 0.3125, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 μg/mL) and Rh2 (0, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200 μg/mL) were combined using the checkerboard method, with culture medium as the blank control. Each group included 5 replicate wells. Four hours after treatment, cells were irradiated with a 660 nm laser (100 mW/cm2) for 5 min. After 24 h incubation, cytotoxicity was evaluated by SRB assay, and drug synergy or antagonism was analyzed using SynergyFinder 2.0 [47,48].

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was determined by SRB assay according to Section 2.9.1. LLC cells were treated with Free Ce6, Rh2 Lips, Ce6-Rh2 Lips, CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. After 4 h incubation, cells were either kept in the dark (non-irradiated group) or irradiated with a 660 nm laser (100 mW/cm2) for 5 min (irradiated group). Following 24 h culture, cytotoxicity was evaluated by SRB assay. Cell viability curves were plotted and IC50 values calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2.

Hypoxia improvement assay

Hypoxia assessment was conducted in LLC cells. Cells were seeded into 48-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated under hypoxic conditions (1% O2) for 6 h. Thereafter, cells were treated with different formulations and exposed to light irradiation, followed by further incubation under hypoxia for 12 h. The expression level of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) in various treatment groups was detected by Western blot and immunofluorescence staining.

Western blot

Cell lysis was performed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing 1% PMSF (Beyotime). After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were harvested and mixed with 5× loading buffer at a volume ratio of 4:1, followed by denaturation in a boiling water bath for 10 min. Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated with HIF-1α primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), and then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. β-actin served as an internal control. Protein signals were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA), and quantitative gray analysis was performed with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence experiment

After removing the culture medium, cells in each group were fixed, washed with PBS, permeabilized, and rinsed with PBS again. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature, the cells were incubated with HIF-1α primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The cells were then incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Following PBS washing, the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 for 15 min in the dark. After a final rinse with PBS, nuclear morphology was observed and imaged under an inverted fluorescence microscope.

In vitro antitumor efficacy evaluation of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips

Colony formation assay

To evaluate the in vitro antitumor efficacy of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, a colony formation assay was performed. LLC cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 48-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. After cell adhesion, the cells were treated with different formulations and exposed to light irradiation. Following incubation for 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the cells were cultured for another 7-14 days. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to paraformaldehyde fixation followed by crystal violet staining. Long-term cell proliferation in each group was assessed by quantifying the colony number.

EDU cell proliferation assay

LLC cells were seeded into 48-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well. Upon reaching 70% confluence, cells were treated with different formulations and exposed to light irradiation, followed by further incubation for 24 h. Apoptosis assay

The pro-apoptotic effects of various formulations were analyzed by Calcein acetoxymethyl ester/propidium iodide (Calcein AM/PI) staining and flow cytometry.

For Calcein AM/PI staining: LLC cells were seeded in 48-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well. At 80% confluence, the cells were grouped, administered with different formulations, and subjected to laser treatment. After 12 h of incubation, Calcein AM/PI staining solution was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope. Live cells showed green fluorescence of Calcein AM, while dead cells showed red fluorescence of PI. Three random fields were selected, and the numbers of cells were counted using ImageJ.

For flow cytometry: Logarithmically growing LLC cells (approximately 3 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with different formulations according to the same experimental grouping as described above, and subjected to light irradiation 4 h after administration, followed by further incubation for 12 h. After trypsin digestion and centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended. Then, 10 μL of Annexin V-FITC was added and incubated for 30 min in the dark, followed by 50 μL of PI for another 10 min in the dark. After centrifugation, the cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The apoptosis rate was quantified using ImageJ.

Investigation of In vitro antitumor mechanisms

Detection of intracellular ROS, singlet oxygen (1O2), and superoxide anion (O2−·) levels

The intracellular total ROS levels in LLC cells after treatment with various formulations were detected using the 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe combined with flow cytometry. The SOSG singlet oxygen detection kit and dihydroethidium (DHE) superoxide anion detection kit were used to determine the content of 1O2 and O2−·, individually. All assays were performed in accordance with the corresponding manufacturer's instructions. Additionally, intracellular singlet oxygen (1O2) production was detected using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) fluorescent probe. After treatment with different nanoparticles and laser irradiation, cells were stained with DPBF probe, and then subjected to fluorescence staining observation and flow cytometric analysis to quantitatively determine the relative yield of 1O2.

Observation of mitochondrial structure

LLC cells treated with PBS, Rh2 Lips, Ce6-Rh2 Lips, CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, and Fer-1 + M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips were fixed and prepared into ultrathin sections applying the same method as described above. The mitochondrial morphology was observed and photographed using TEM.

Detection of SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels

The expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 in each treatment group were analyzed by immunofluorescence assay and Western blot analysis. The experimental procedures were the same as those described in Section 2.10.

Detection assays of GSH, Fe2+ content, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP)

The content of reduced GSH was determined using a GSH assay kit. Intracellular Fe2+ levels were detected by the FerroOrange fluorescence method. Lipid peroxidation was evaluated using 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) immunofluorescence staining, MDA measurement, and a lipid peroxidation assay kit (BODIPY 581/591C11). Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured with a mitochondrial red fluorescent staining kit and a JC-1 kit. The opening degree of MPTP in LLC cells was detected using an MPTP assay kit. All methods were implemented as directed in the instructions.

Long-circulating property and In vivo targeting ability of the preparations

Results and discussion

Characterization of the formulation

To achieve efficient therapy for NSCLC, biomimetic self-oxygenating nanoparticles were prepared. The preparation process is shown in Fig. 1. After a series of formulations were successfully prepared via the thin-film hydration method, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips were fabricated by extrusion (Fig. 2A). The appearance characterization of various formulations is shown in Fig. S1. All formulations remained uniformly dispersed without obvious stratification, demonstrating excellent colloidal stability and meeting the requirements for intravenous administration. To verify the loading of Ce6, UV-Vis absorption spectra were recorded as shown in Fig. 2B. Free Ce6 exhibited obvious characteristic absorption peaks at 400 nm and 660 nm, whereas Rh2 Lips showed no significant absorption at these wavelengths. In contrast, CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips displayed similar characteristic absorption peaks near 400 nm and 660 nm to free Ce6, demonstrating that Ce6 was successfully loaded into the biomimetic liposomes [49]. These results collectively confirm the successful preparation of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. FTIR spectroscopy was employed to verify the successful encapsulation of Ce6 into Rh2 Lips (Fig. 2C). Free Ce6 showed characteristic absorption peaks at approximately 1708.14 cm−1 (C=O stretching vibration of the carboxyl group), 1596.29 cm−1 (C=C skeleton vibration of the chlorin ring), and 1233.46 cm−1 (C-O stretching vibration). Blank Rh2 Lips exhibited typical peaks at 1029.54 cm−1 (P-O-C stretching vibration), 2929.62 cm−1 (C-H stretching vibration), and 3419.31 cm−1 (O-H/N-H stretching vibration). The spectrum of Ce6-Rh2 Lips retained all characteristic peaks of blank Rh2 Lips, confirming the integrity of the lipid membrane. Importantly, key Ce6-derived peaks at ∼1711.55 cm−1 and ∼1584.70 cm−1 were also observed in Ce6-Rh2 Lips, with slight shifts relative to free Ce6. No new absorption peaks appeared, indicating that Ce6 was encapsulated mainly through physical interactions, and the encapsulation process did not alter the chemical structures of Ce6 or the liposome vector. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) results demonstrated that the hydrodynamic diameter of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips increased from 109.64 nm to 139.53 nm compared with uncoated CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, which was attributed to the successful coating of the M1 macrophage membrane (Fig. 2D and E). The PDI values of all formulations were below 0.3, suggesting that membrane coating did not compromise the homogeneity and high dispersibility of the liposomes (Fig. 2F). Zeta potential measurements revealed that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exhibited a more negative surface potential than CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips (Fig. 2G), which was close to that of the native M1 macrophage membrane. These results further verified the successful coating of the M1 macrophage membrane on the surface of CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. In addition, the EE of Ce6 in all formulations exceeded 90%. Specifically, the EE of Ce6 in M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips was 95.25% ± 1.36% (Fig. 2H), and the encapsulation efficiency of CAT was 63.09% ± 7.9%. The drug loading contents of Ce6, CAT and Rh2 were calculated to be 1.67%, 2.21% and 17.54%, respectively. As shown in the TEM images (Fig. 2I), in contrast to CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exhibited well-defined spherical-like morphology with a distinct core-shell structure and good monodispersity, further confirming the successful coating of the M1 macrophage membrane and the favorable physicochemical characteristics of the biomimetic liposomes.

To evaluate the fluorescence imaging performance of this nanoplatform and investigate the photophysical properties of Ce6 encapsulation, we characterized the fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of free Ce6 and various lipid-based formulations. As shown in Fig. S2A, no characteristic fluorescence signal was detected in the Rh2 Lips group within the detection range of 550-750 nm, confirming the absence of background fluorescence interference from the carrier. Free Ce6 exhibited a characteristic fluorescence emission peak at 663.5 nm, while all Ce6-loaded liposomes displayed characteristic peaks in the 665-670 nm region, indicating that the formulation preparation process, drug loading capacity, and M1 cell membrane coating did not alter the intrinsic photophysical properties of Ce6. After liposome encapsulation, the maximum emission wavelength of Ce6 shifted red to 672.2-674.2 nm, accompanied by enhanced fluorescence intensity. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to changes in the polarity of the lipid bilayer microenvironment, which reduced non-radiative decay pathways and mitigated the self-quenching effect of Ce6 in aqueous solutions. Notably, the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 liposome exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity among all Ce6-loaded samples, demonstrating that the M1 cell membrane coating did not quench Ce6 fluorescence but instead preserved or even enhanced its fluorescence signal. The excitation spectrum results (Fig. S2B) further corroborate the characteristic optical properties of Ce6. All Ce6-containing samples exhibited a strong excitation peak around 400 nm, consistent with the excitation wavelength used in the emission spectrum analysis. These findings demonstrate that the constructed nanoplatform effectively preserves and optimizes the fluorescence characteristics of Ce6, making it suitable as a tracer probe and providing a reliable foundation for subsequent in vitro cell imaging, in vivo bioavailability studies, and tumor-targeted imaging research.

Surface membrane protein characterization of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips

SDS-PAGE was employed to analyze membrane-associated protein composition of the biomimetic liposomes [31,50]. The results demonstrated that M1 macrophage membranes and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips displayed similar protein bands, whereas no obvious protein signal was observed in CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips (Fig. 2J). This suggests that the protein components of the M1 macrophage membrane were well retained in M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, further verifying that liposomes were successfully coated with cell membranes. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that macrophages can target malignant cells via the interaction between integrin α4β1 and VCAM-1. Western blot analysis (Fig. 2K) further verified this targeting mechanism. The results showed that both M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1 macrophage membranes expressed integrin α4 and integrin β1, confirming the successful coating of M1 macrophage membranes onto CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. The retention of integrin α4β1 is responsible for the specific binding to VCAM-1 on tumor cells, thereby mediating active tumor targeting and enhancing cellular internalization [42]. These properties endow the biomimetic liposomes with immune evasion capacity and efficient tumor-targeted delivery.

Stability and In vitro release profiles

The stability results of various formulations (Fig. 2L–O) demonstrated that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exhibited superior stability both at 4 °C and in serum. In addition, the in vitro cumulative release of Ce6 was investigated in PBS containing 10% FBS, as presented in Fig. 2P. The percentage of Ce6 released from M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips within 48 h was 52.48 ± 3.99%, which was lower than those from Ce6-Rh2 Lips (65.6 ± 3.45%) and CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips (66.4 ± 3.49%). These results demonstrate that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exhibited favorable sustained-release properties, which could be ascribed to the macrophage membrane coating that enhanced formulation stability and retarded Ce6 release.

Furthermore, the release of different components from this liposomal formulation is regulated by distinct yet complementary mechanisms, which collectively ensure the optimal synergistic antitumor effect. Ce6 is embedded in the lipid bilayer composed of EPC and Rh2, and its release is mainly driven by the concentration gradient across the liposomal membrane, representing a typical diffusion-controlled release process. CAT is encapsulated in the aqueous core of the liposomes, and its release depends on the integrity of the lipid membrane, belonging to membrane barrier-controlled release. Notably, Rh2, as a structural component of the lipid bilayer, is released synchronously with the degradation of the lipid membrane. Upon coating with M1 macrophage membranes, the liposomes form a dense core-shell structure, which increases the diffusion resistance of all components and effectively prevents premature drug leakage during blood circulation. This mechanism is consistent with the in vitro release assay results, where the amount of Ce6 released from M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips within 48 h was significantly lower than that from uncoated liposomes. Once the nanoparticles accumulate in tumor tissues, the acidic conditions, high levels of ROS, and abundant proteases in the tumor microenvironment promote the gradual shedding and degradation of the M1 membrane, which in turn leads to the destabilization of the lipid bilayer structure and triggers the on-demand targeted release of Ce6, CAT, and Rh2 at the tumor site. This dual characteristic of long-acting sustained release in blood circulation and tumor microenvironment-responsive release can maintain stable and durable effective drug concentrations in tumor tissues, reduce systemic toxic side effects, and maximize the synergistic antitumor efficacy of photodynamic therapy and ferroptosis.

CAT enzymatic activity and resistance to enzymatic degradation of various formulations

To evaluate the catalytic activity of liposome-encapsulated CAT, a Goth assay was performed. As shown in Fig. S3A, the CAT catalytic activity in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group was comparable to that in the CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group. Notably, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips retained 89.07 ± 5.15% of the catalytic activity of free CAT at an equivalent concentration. These results confirm that CAT encapsulated in liposomes maintained high enzymatic activity. Owing to the abundance of proteases in vivo, free CAT is prone to protease-mediated degradation and rapid inactivation during blood circulation. To verify the resistance of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips to enzymatic degradation, the residual CAT activity was quantitatively determined at different time intervals using the Goth method in the presence of proteinase K. As shown in Fig. 2Q, after co-incubation with proteinase K at 37 °C for 6 h, CAT encapsulated in CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips retained 75.42 ± 7.76% and 78.29 ± 6.24% of its original catalytic activity, respectively. In contrast, free CAT retained only 17.56 ± 6.68% of its initial activity after proteinase K digestion. The intuitive anti-enzymatic degradation images (Fig. S3B) further revealed that free CAT could rapidly catalyze H2O2 to generate oxygen in the absence of proteinase K, whereas no obvious oxygen bubbles were observed after treatment with proteinase K, suggesting that free CAT is readily degraded by proteases. Meanwhile, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exhibited similar oxygen bubble generation regardless of proteinase K treatment for 6 h, confirming that the liposomal carrier not only delivers CAT but also protects it from inactivation, with M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips showing a more pronounced protective effect than CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. These results lay a solid foundation for the intratumoral oxygen-generation function of the liposomes.

In vitro hemolysis assay

Following intravenous administration, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips enter the bloodstream and circulate systemically, where they may interact with blood cells and immune-related cells. Thus, evaluating the blood compatibility of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips is critical for its potential clinical translation. In vitro hemolysis assays were performed accordingly. As shown in Fig. 2R, in contrast to the bright red color found in the positive control group, the hemolysis rates of all formulation groups were below the 5% critical threshold. These results demonstrate that all formulations possess favorable hemocompatibility and are suitable for intravenous.

In vitro targeting evaluation

The cellular internalization efficiency of nanoparticles in tumor cells is a key factor affecting the efficacy of antitumor therapy. To investigate whether M1 macrophage membrane modification could enhance the cellular uptake of the formulation by LLC cells, and to further evaluate its tumor targeting specificity and biological safety, LLC tumor cells and human normal bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells were selected as research models. Flow cytometry (FCM) and laser scanning confocal microscopy were used to systematically investigate the endocytosis behavior of the preparations.

In LLC cells, FCM results (Fig. 3A and C) revealed that the fluorescence intensity of each formulation gradually increased with prolonged incubation time, whereas no obvious fluorescence was detected in the blank control group. The rank order of fluorescence intensity among the experimental groups was as follows: M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips > CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips > Free Ce6.Fluorescence microscopy observations (Fig. 3B and D) demonstrated that the M1 macrophage membrane-modified formulation exhibited the strongest red fluorescence intensity and the highest cellular internalization, which was significantly higher than that of the other groups (P < 0.05) [49]. These results indicated that the low cellular uptake of free Ce6 might be attributed to its poor water solubility. In contrast, M1 macrophage membrane modification effectively facilitated the internalization of liposomal formulations by LLC cells and markedly improved the tumor targeting ability.

In contrast, at all incubation time points, the cellular uptake level of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips in normal BEAS-2B cells was significantly lower than that in LLC tumor cells (Fig. S4A–D). After 4 h of incubation, the mean fluorescence intensity of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips in BEAS-2B cells was remarkably decreased compared with that in LLC cells (p < 0.01), verifying that the nanoplatform preferentially accumulated in tumor cells rather than normal cells.

Therefore, it is highly significant to assess the antitumor efficacy of the liposomes fabricated in this study against LLC cells. The dose-response curves were established via the SRB assay to evaluate the biological effects of Ce6 and Rh2 on LLC cells in the dark and under light irradiation, respectively (Fig. 4A and B). The results demonstrated that Ce6 exhibited negligible cytotoxicity toward LLC cells in the dark, but exerted significant inhibitory effects under light irradiation. Furthermore, light irradiation exerted no obvious influence on the therapeutic efficacy of Rh2. The IC50 values of Ce6 and Rh2 are presented in Fig. 4C and D.

In addition, the phototoxic effect of light irradiation on LLC cells was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4E, light irradiation alone exerted no significant effect on the viability of LLC cells. Subsequently, ZIP model analysis was performed for the two agents at a series of concentration gradients. The ZIP model results revealed that the combination of Ce6 and Rh2 not only achieved a high comprehensive sensitivity score (CSS = 84.3065, Fig. 4H), but also exhibited a significant synergistic effect with a ZIP synergy score of 8.55 (Fig. 4I), confirming a strong synergistic interaction rather than an additive or antagonistic effect. These findings demonstrated that the combined administration of Ce6 and Rh2 exerted a synergistic antitumor effect. Furthermore, the heat maps and three-dimensional surface plots (Fig. 4J) further verified that the most potent synergism occurred within the low-to-medium concentration range, thus determining the optimal combined concentration ratios for Ce6-Rh2 Lips, CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips. Subsequent evaluation of the cytotoxic effects of different formulations on LLC cells under dark and light conditions demonstrated that all formulations exerted concentration-dependent antiproliferative effects following light irradiation (Fig. 4K and L). The corresponding IC50 values were calculated and are presented in Fig. 4M. Under dark conditions, Rh2 Lips exhibited the highest IC50 value, whereas M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips showed a relatively lower IC50, suggesting that targeted delivery and a multi-drug co-delivery system could enhance cellular inhibitory effects to a certain extent. After light irradiation, the IC50 values of all Ce6-containing formulations were markedly decreased, with M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips displaying the lowest IC50. These results indicated that light irradiation triggered the photodynamic effect of Ce6, and the incorporation of targeting modifications together with CAT further synergistically augmented the antitumor activity. After light irradiation, the IC50 values of all Ce6-containing formulations were markedly decreased, with M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips displaying the lowest IC50 (**P < 0.0001). After statistical analysis, under laser irradiation, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exhibited significantly enhanced anti-proliferative activity compared with CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and Ce6-Rh2 Lips. These results confirmed that M1 macrophage membrane modification and CAT-mediated hypoxia relief significantly enhanced the synergistic antitumor effect of Ce6 and Rh2.

Evaluation of hypoxia alleviation effect

HIF-1α is a core transcriptional regulator in cells under hypoxic conditions, which is widely used to monitor and reflect cellular hypoxia. Western blot analysis (Fig. 4N) and the corresponding quantitative data (Fig. 4O) revealed that, compared with the blank control group, the Ce6-Rh2 Lips treatment group slightly upregulated HIF-1α protein expression. In contrast, the CAT-containing formulations (CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, both under light irradiation) significantly downregulated HIF-1α expression, with the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips roup showing the lowest relative expression level of HIF-1α.Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4P) and fluorescence intensity quantification (Fig. 4Q) were consistent with the Western blot results. Distinct nuclear green fluorescence of HIF-1α was observed in the blank control, Rh2 Lips, and Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups, with a slight increase in fluorescence intensity in the Ce6-Rh2 Lips group. In contrast, green fluorescence was markedly diminished in the CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips treatment groups (both under light irradiation), which was further verified by the quantitative fluorescence intensity data. These results suggested that monotherapy with Ce6-Rh2 Lips may aggravate tumor hypoxia, whereas CAT could alleviate hypoxia by catalyzing oxygen generation and thereby downregulate HIF-1α expression. Moreover, M1-mediated targeting modification facilitated drug accumulation at the tumor site, and the light-activated photodynamic effect of Ce6 further synergistically boosted hypoxia alleviation.

in vitro by colony formation assay and EDU staining. Exploration of the underlying antitumor mechanism

To investigate the association between the antitumor mechanism of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and ferroptosis, ferroptosis-related assays were performed. Results from ROS generation assays (Fig. 6A, B and 6C, 6D), 1O2 detection (Fig. 6E and F), and O2−· detection (Fig. 6G and H) revealed almost no target fluorescence in the blank control group. The Rh2 Lips group exhibited slightly enhanced but weak fluorescence, while fluorescence intensity was increased in the Ce6-Rh2 Lips group and further elevated in the CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups. Compared with the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group, the Fer-1-treated M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group showed significantly weakened fluorescence. These results demonstrate that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips significantly promote the production of ROS, 1O2, and O2−•, whereas pretreatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 markedly reverses these effects. Additionally, the results of intracellular singlet oxygen (1O2) generation detected using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) fluorescent probe combined with fluorescence staining and flow cytometry (Fig. S5A–D) were consistent with the above findings. M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips significantly enhanced intracellular 1O2 production, and this effect was further strengthened by CAT-mediated hypoxia relief. Furthermore, cellular immunofluorescence (Fig. 6I and J) and western blotting (Fig. 6K and L) analyses of SLC7A11 expression revealed that, compared with the blank control group, the Rh2 Lips group significantly downregulated SLC7A11 expression, and the Ce6-Rh2 Lips, CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups exhibited similar inhibitory effects. Pretreatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 markedly reversed these effects. Taken together, these results support that Rh2 may suppress SLC7A11 activity via the SLC7A11 pathway [51]. Collectively, these results suggest that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips can effectively trigger the production of ROS (including 1O2 and O2−•) and downregulate SLC7A11 expression, thereby laying a molecular foundation for ferroptosis.

To further explore the molecular mechanism underlying ferroptosis induced by M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, a comprehensive multidimensional analysis was performed, including mitochondrial ultrastructure, antioxidant systems, lipid peroxidation, iron overload, and mitochondrial function.

Transmission electron microscopy observations (Fig. 7A) showed that mitochondria in the blank control group exhibited intact structures with clear cristae. In contrast, mitochondria in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group displayed marked swelling, fractured cristae and vacuolization, which are typical ultrastructural features of ferroptosis. Analysis of the antioxidant system revealed that the GSH content was significantly decreased in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group (Fig. 7B). Meanwhile, the protein expression and fluorescence intensity of GPX4 were also markedly downregulated (Fig. 7C–F), indicating a remarkable impairment of the cellular antioxidant capacity. Lipid peroxidation analysis demonstrated that the MDA content was significantly elevated in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group (Fig. 7G). The immunofluorescence intensity of 4-HNE, a major lipid peroxidation product, was markedly increased (Fig. 7H and I). Meanwhile, C11-BODIPY staining revealed a significantly higher proportion of oxidized lipids (green fluorescence) (Fig. 7J and K), indicating the occurrence of severe intracellular lipid peroxidation. Iron overload detection revealed that intracellular free Fe2+ levels were significantly elevated in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group (Fig. 7L and M), which may further accelerate ROS generation and lipid peroxidation through the Fenton reaction. Mitochondrial function assays demonstrated that the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was markedly reduced (Fig. 7N–Q) and the opening of MPTP was significantly enhanced (Fig. 7R and S) in this group, indicating severe mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, all these alterations were significantly reversed by Fer-1 pretreatment. In summary, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips can effectively induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including singlet oxygen (1O2) and superoxide anion (O2−•), and downregulate SLC7A11 expression at both transcriptional and translational levels. Inhibition of SLC7A11 reduces cellular cystine uptake and thereby suppresses the synthesis of glutathione (GSH). Intracellular GSH depletion further inactivates GPX4, the core antioxidant enzyme that scavenges lipid peroxides. Ultimately, the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 antioxidant defense system is thoroughly disrupted, leading to massive lipid peroxide accumulation, iron overload, and mitochondrial dysfunction, and eventually triggering ferroptosis in LLC cells [52,53].

To evaluate the blood circulation profiles of different formulations, blood samples were collected at predetermined time points, and the dynamic changes in drug fluorescence intensity within 96 h post-administration were monitored using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system. As shown in Fig. 8A and B, no obvious fluorescence signal was observed in the blank control and free Ce6 groups. By contrast, the blood fluorescence intensity in the CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups declined gradually over time. To investigate the tumor-targeting capacity, in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence imaging were performed to assess the biodistribution of different formulations in tumor-bearing mice. As shown in Fig. 8C, fluorescence signals in the free Ce6 group were mainly concentrated in metabolic organs such as the liver, with only weak and rapidly fading fluorescence detected in the tumor region. In contrast, the CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group, and particularly the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group, exhibited intense fluorescence at the tumor site shortly after administration, which peaked at 6–24 h and persisted in the tumor for up to 96 h. Fig. 8D displays ex vivo fluorescence images of tumor tissues harvested from tumor-bearing mice at 96 h post-injection. The tumor fluorescence signal in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group was the strongest, which was consistent with the in vivo imaging results. These data confirm that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips demonstrate excellent tumor-targeting ability.

Antitumor effect evaluation in ectopic tumor-bearing mice

To verify the antitumor efficacy of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips against NSCLC, a widely used, easily operated and highly reproducible mouse ectopic tumor model was established to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effect. The experimental timeline is presented in Fig. 9A. Based on the macroscopic images of excised tumors (Fig. 9B), continuous monitoring of tumor volume growth (Fig. 9C), and quantitative analysis of the tumor inhibition rate (Fig. 9D), the tumor volume in the blank control group increased rapidly over time. Treatment with Rh2 Lips alone showed a weak inhibitory effect on tumor growth. By contrast, the Ce6-Rh2 Lips and CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups displayed moderate tumor growth suppression. Importantly, the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group exhibited the most significant inhibition of tumor growth, with a therapeutic efficacy distinctly superior to that of the other groups.

To further explore the underlying antitumor mechanism, a series of staining assays were performed on tumor tissue samples. Immunofluorescence staining for Ki-67 in tumor sections (Fig. 9E and F) showed that cellular proliferative activity was remarkably suppressed in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group. Meanwhile, TUNEL staining (Fig. 9G and H) showed a markedly increased proportion of apoptotic cells. Furthermore, H&E staining (Fig. 9I) demonstrated the most extensive tumor necrosis in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group. Collectively, these results confirm that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips can effectively suppress tumor growth and trigger tumor cell apoptosis. Meanwhile, immunofluorescence staining for HIF-1α on frozen tumor sections (Fig. 9J and K) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 9L and M) revealed that HIF-1α expression was significantly downregulated in the CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips and M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups compared with the Ce6-Rh2 Lips group, indicating that CAT can effectively alleviate tumor hypoxia. These results demonstrate that Ce6-mediated PDT exhibits a remarkable antitumor effect. Moreover, the introduction of CAT further enhances the antitumor efficacy by ameliorating tumor hypoxia, while M1 membrane-based targeting modification greatly improves tumor accumulation of the formulation and potentiates its PDT efficacy.

Mechanism underlying ferroptosis induced by M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips in tumor-bearing mice

To investigate the underlying antitumor mechanism of M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, we systematically analyzed ferroptosis-associated parameters in tumor tissues. First, ROS generation induced by PDT was assessed. Fluorescence staining and quantitative analysis of SOSG and DHE (Fig. 10A–D) revealed that the fluorescence intensity in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group was significantly higher than those in the blank control, Rh2 Lips, Ce6-Rh2 Lips, and CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips groups. These findings demonstrate that M1-targeted modification combined with CAT-mediated hypoxia alleviation synergistically enhances the PDT efficacy of Ce6, thereby triggering robust oxidative stress. As a pivotal transporter protein responsible for regulating ferroptosis, downregulation of SLC7A11 suppresses the biosynthesis of GSH, the major intracellular antioxidant, thereby facilitating ferroptosis. Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis targeting SLC7A11 (Fig. 10E, F, 10I, 10J) revealed that SLC7A11 expression was markedly downregulated in the Rh2 Lips group compared with the blank control group, and a similar inhibitory effect was observed in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group. Collectively, these results indicate that Rh2 exerts its anti-tumor effects, at least in part, via downregulating SLC7A11 expression. Furthermore, GPX4 acts as a pivotal antioxidant enzyme that negatively regulates ferroptosis. Immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis of GPX4 (Fig. 10G, H, 10I, 10K) revealed that both the protein expression level and fluorescence intensity of GPX4 in tumor tissues were significantly reduced in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group. Consistently, quantitative detection of GSH confirmed that GSH content declined to the lowest level in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group (Fig. 10L).Collectively, these results demonstrate that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips potentiate PDT-induced oxidative stress via M1-mediated targeting and hypoxia alleviation, and synergistically suppress the GSH-GPX4 axis—the core antioxidant pathway governing ferroptosis—through Rh2-dependent downregulation of SLC7A11.

Meanwhile, 4-HNE fluorescence staining (Fig. 10M and N) and MDA quantitative analysis (Fig. 10O) revealed that lipid peroxidation product levels in the M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips group were significantly higher than those in all other groups. In addition, Prussian blue staining for iron ions in tumor tissues (Fig. 10P) visually demonstrated markedly enhanced iron deposition in this group. Quantitative analysis of Fe2+ levels (Fig. 10Q) further confirmed the establishment of iron overload. These results indicate that the collapse of the ferroptosis defense system directly drives the massive accumulation of lipid peroxides and the occurrence of iron overload.

In summary, M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips effectively trigger ferroptosis and exert remarkable antitumor efficacy in tumor tissues by synergistically modulating oxidative stress, iron metabolism, and the antioxidant defense system. Specifically, this nanodelivery system achieves targeted accumulation through M1 macrophage-mediated targeting and alleviates tumor hypoxia via CAT, which markedly enhances Ce6-mediated PDT, generates abundant ROS, and induces robust oxidative stress; meanwhile, Rh2-mediated downregulation of SLC7A11 suppresses cystine uptake and GSH biosynthesis, further reduces GPX4 expression, and collectively disrupts the SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4 ferroptosis defense axis. These synergistic effects ultimately lead to iron overload and excessive accumulation of lipid peroxidation products (4-HNE and MDA), thereby initiating ferroptosis in tumor cells. M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips exerts significant synergistic antitumor efficacy through the integration of M1-mediated active targeting, CAT-enhanced oxygen supply, Ce6-based photodynamic therapy, and Rh2-triggered ferroptosis, rather than simple combination of each component.

Evaluation of the In vivo safety profile of the formulation

During formulation development, the safety profile of the preparation serves as a critical criterion for assessing the feasibility of a therapeutic strategy. In this study, we evaluated the in vivo safety of various formulations by monitoring body weight changes in treated mice, performing blood routine and biochemical analyses, and conducting histopathological examinations of major organs. As body weight fluctuation is a typical indicator of systemic toxicity induced by toxic agents, mouse body weight monitoring results (Fig. S7A) demonstrated that the body weight changes in all formulation - treated groups remained within a normal range. The blood test results (Fig. S7B–F) revealed that all hematological parameters, including WBC, LYM, MON, GRA, and MCV, remained within normal reference ranges compared with the control group. Furthermore, H&E staining results (Fig. S7G) demonstrated no obvious pathological lesions in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney of mice. Collectively, these findings confirmed that the as-prepared liposomes exhibited no significant systemic toxicity in mice, indicating favorable biosafety.

Conclusion

Overall, this study successfully fabricated a novel biomimetic self-oxygenating nanoparticle (M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips), which exhibited potent antitumor efficacy against NSCLC and effectively triggered ferroptosis in tumor cells. In this formulation, cholesterol was replaced by Rh2 as the lipid membrane component, and the nanoparticles were further camouflaged with M1 macrophage membranes. By virtue of the cancer cell-targeting property of the M1 membrane coating, the nanoparticles achieve active tumor targeting through the specific interaction between integrin α4β1 and VCAM-1 overexpressed on tumor cells. Meanwhile, the M1 membrane coating enables immune escape and prolonged blood circulation, which further boosts tumor accumulation [54]. This system significantly prolongs circulation half-life and enhances tumor targeting efficiency. The formulation-loaded photosensitizer Ce6 generates Ros upon 660 nm laser illumination to achieve photodynamic effects. Meanwhile, CAT catalyzes endogenous H2O2 to produce O2, thereby alleviating tumor hypoxia and potentiating Ce6-mediated photodynamic therapy. Rh2 acts as a multi-target anti-tumor ingredient that mainly targets SLC7A11 and GPX4 to inhibit the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 axis, induces ferroptosis by promoting GSH depletion, lipid peroxidation, and iron overload, and synergizes with PDT to exert potent anti-NSCLC efficacy. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that this formulation can substantially suppress NSCLC tumor growth, lower tumor recurrence rate, and exhibit no obvious systemic toxicity. Notably, the combination of Rh2 and PDT exhibited a strong synergistic effect with a ZIP synergy score of 8.55. Collectively, this study demonstrates that M1@CAT-Ce6-Rh2 Lips, as a promising drug delivery platform, enable efficient therapy against NSCLC. This strategy enriches the design rationale of nanoscale drug delivery systems and provides novel insights and experimental support for the clinical treatment of NSCLC.
