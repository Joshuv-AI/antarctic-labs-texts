A glycan-based adjuvant expands the breadth and duration of protection of mRNA-based vaccines

ABSTRACT
The activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) orchestrates inflammation and regulates adaptive immunity. To test whether tuning inflammation through PRR stimulation enhanced the efficacy of mRNA vaccines, we combined an mRNA-based vaccine generated against the ancestral spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 with mannadjuvant, a formulation of fungal mannan and aluminum hydroxide targeting the PRR dectin-2. In mice and non-human primates, mannadjuvant increased the magnitude and durability of the response elicited by the mRNA-based vaccine, and it also led to the induction of neutralizing antibodies directed against variants of concern with a high escape capacity, overcoming antigenic imprinting. Mechanistically, prolonged type I interferon (IFN) production and potentiated interleukin-1 (IL-1) signaling locally within the draining lymph node in mice or in human cells were necessary and sufficient to exert the effect of mannadjuvant. Our data indicate that antifungal PRRs can be harnessed to create more potent and durable mRNA-based vaccines.

Main

Mammals recognize pathogens and instruct a rapid immune response that protects the host, also generating long-term memory. The immune response is largely driven by the activation of PRRs that recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)1,2. PRR stimulation initiates inflammation, allowing the activation and migration of phagocytes into the draining lymph node (dLN) to promote adaptive immune cell activation and memory formation3–8. However, the molecular programs initiated by inflammation to control adaptive responses require better understanding.

To investigate how inflammation forges adaptive immunity, we used mRNA-based vaccines, which comprise a lipid nanoparticle (LNP) shell and an mRNA encoding for an antigen of interest and whose efficacy has been demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic9–11. Although several immune mediators have been involved in the response to mRNA-based vaccines12–16, the immune pathways that control their effectiveness remain debated17,18, and it is largely overlooked whether the efficacy of mRNA-based vaccines can be ameliorated by tuning inflammation with the use of adjuvants. Although carbohydrates are relatively understudied as adjuvants19,20, mannan from Candida albicans was shown to activate the C-type lectin receptor (CLR) dectin-2, enhancing activation of adaptive lymphocytes21. Even more, a spontaneous reaction between C. albicans mannan and aluminum hydroxide (hereafter ‘alum’) resulted in a new formulation, referred to as ‘mannadjuvant’ (MA), that potentiates antigen protein-based vaccines directed against SARS-CoV-2 or influenza A virus (IAV)21.

In the present study, we determined whether MA could be used to potentiate the activity of the original monovalent mRNA-based vaccine BNT162b2 Comirnaty (hereafter ‘WA1mRNA’), which encodes the spike protein of the ancestral WA1 SARS-CoV-2 strain. We found that MA expanded the magnitude and duration of protection induced by WA1mRNA in mice and non-human primates (NHPs) and instructs distinct inflammatory programs in human myeloid cells, extending the breadth of protection to SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern (VOCs) and overcoming antigenic imprinting.

Results

MA enhances and expands WA1mRNA activity

We first assessed whether intradermal (i.d.) injection of MA potentiated the dLN response to WA1mRNA, as reported for immunization with protein-based vaccines21. Mice were simultaneously immunized i.d. with MA and intramuscularly (i.m.) with WA1mRNA to target the same dLN at day 0 (prime) and day 14 (boost) (Extended Data Fig. 1a), whereas mice that received i.m. injections with WA1mRNA alone or i.d. pre-fusion stabilized WA1 SARS-CoV-2 spike protein trimer21 (hereafter ‘spike’)+MA at day 0 and day 14 were used as controls (Extended Data Fig. 1a). At day 28, but not at day 14, after immunization, spike+MA led to a significant increase in spike-specific antibodies in the serum compared to mice immunized with WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA and MA (Extended Data Fig. 1b). No differences in spike-specific total IgG or IgG1 were detected in mice immunized with WA1mRNA compared to WA1mRNA and MA, whereas spike-specific IgG2c was more efficiently induced in mice immunized with WA1mRNA, regardless of presence or absence of MA, compared to spike+MA (Extended Data Fig. 1b) at day 14 or day 28. Next, we measured the induction of neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), which is predictive of immune protection22. The serum of mice immunized with WA1mRNA and MA showed increased neutralization against WA1 SARS-CoV-2 compared to WA1mRNA or spike+MA (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Thus, drainage of MA to the same dLN targeted by WA1mRNA increased the induction of nAbs.

To test whether i.m. co-injection of WA1mRNA and MA further enhanced the cooperation between MA and WA1mRNA in inducing nAbs, we first assessed whether MA injected i.m. increased the magnitude of the antibody response compared to C. albicans mannan21. Mice injected i.m. or i.d. with spike+MA had increased spike-specific IgG in the serum compared to mice injected i.m. or i.d. with spike+mannan at day 14 and/or day 28 after priming (Extended Data Fig. 1d), with higher responses induced by i.d. vaccination compared to i.m. vaccination, suggesting tissue-specific differences. To test whether MA and LNPs or WA1mRNA could be admixed without disrupting either component, we performed dynamic light scattering (DLS) on MA, LNP, alum or WA1mRNA alone, and LNP+MA or WA1mRNA admixed to MA (hereafter ‘WA1mRNA+MA’), at different ratios, and we found no alteration in the size of any combinations up to 24 hours (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). Measurement of mRNA leakage from WA1mRNA with or without MA, using 0.5% w/v Triton to lyse LNPs as a positive control, found that less than 3% of the mRNA content was released after 24 hours, independently of MA (Extended Data Fig. 1g), indicating that MA did not alter WA1mRNA stability. To test whether admixing MA and WA1mRNA modulated their activity, mice were immunized i.m. with WA1mRNA+MA (Fig. 1a). Total anti-spike IgG, IgG1 and IgG2 were significantly increased in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA mice at day 14 and/or day 28 after immunization (Fig. 1b). Because SARS-CoV-2 is a rapidly evolving virus23,24, we tested one of the most divergent VOCs available at the time of our investigation: Omicron B.1.1.529 (hereafter ‘BA.1’). At day 28, WA1mRNA mice had fewer anti-BA.1 nAbs compared to anti-WA1 nAbs in the serum (Fig. 1c), confirming reduced effectiveness of WA1mRNA against BA.1 SARS-CoV-2 (ref. 25), whereas WA1mRNA+MA mice had significantly higher amounts of anti-BA.1 nAbs compared to WA1mRNA mice, although still less compared to anti-WA1 nAbs (Fig. 1c). At day 35 after immunization, the amount of anti-WA1 spike IgG was significantly higher compared to day 28 in WA1mRNA+MA and WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 1d), but WA1mRNA+MA mice and not WA1mRNA mice, had similarly high anti-BA.1 and anti-WA1 nAbs (Fig. 1e). Body temperature and systemic inflammatory cytokines were similar in WA1mRNA+MA and WA1mRNA mice during the first 48 hours after immunization (Fig. 1f,g and Extended Data Fig. 1h), indicating unaltered reactogenicity.

We next tested whether MA extended the duration of the response elicited by WA1mRNA. Mice were i.m. immunized at day 0 and day 14 with WA1mRNA, WA1mRNA+MA or WA1mRNA+alum (Fig. 1h). Anti-WA1 spike total IgG, IgG1 and IgG2c were significantly higher in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+alum mice up to 500 days after immunization (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 1i), indicating that the mannan, rather than the alum, contained in MA was required to extend the activity of WA1mRNA. We also tested whether persistence of anti-WA1 spike IgGs in WA1mRNA+MA mice was due to increased numbers of bone marrow-resident plasma cells. The percentage of anti-spike antibody secreting cells (ASCs) measured via ELISpot or of CD19−CD38+CD138+ long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs) assessed by cytofluorimetry in the bone marrow of WA1mRNA+MA mice, but not of WA1mRNA+alum mice, was significantly higher compared to WA1mRNA mice at day 54, day 84 and/or day 500 (Fig. 1j and Extended Data Fig. 2). The neutralization of WA1 SARS-CoV2 or of a pseudovirus expressing the WA1 spike bearing the D614G mutation (hereafter ‘WA1 spike’) was significantly extended up to day 84 or day 500, respectively, in WA1mRNA+MA mice, but not in WA1mRNA+alum, mice compared to WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 1k,l). Thus, the production of WA1 anti-spike antibodies and nAbs against WA1 or BA.1 SARS-CoV-2 could be increased quantitatively and extended over time by admixing MA, but not alum, with WA1mRNA.

MA expands germinal centers and fosters anti-VOC nAbs

To assess whether BGC cells acquired the capacity to recognize the spike of Omicron sublineages such as BA.4, BA.5 and BA.5.2 or XBB.1.5, which, due to extensive mutations, have acquired increased evasion properties29, we measured absolute numbers and ratio of VOC spike-specific CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells (Extended Data Fig. 3f). A higher number and ratio of CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells that recognized WA1 spike was detected in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA and WA1mRNA+alum mice up to day 500 after immunization (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a). WA1mRNA+MA, but not WA1mRNA+alum, significantly increased the number and ratio of CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells that expressed a B cell receptor (BCR) that recognized the spike of BA.4/BA.5 and XBB.1.5 (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a), whereas the number of CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells that recognized the spike of BA.4/BA.5 or XBB.1.5 in WA1mRNA mice was significantly lower compared to the ones that recognized the WA1 spike (Extended Data Fig. 4b), consistent with the limited capacity of WA1mRNA to protect against these Omicron sublineages29. The absolute number of memory B (BM) cells identified as CD38+IgD−IgM− BM cells30 or as IgG1+CD80+PD-L2+ BM cells31 (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d) that recognized spike of WA1, BA.4/BA.5 and XBB.1.5 was significantly increased in WA1mRNA+MA compared to WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+alum mice at day 56 (Fig. 2e,f). Serum isolated from WA1mRNA+MA mice, but not WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+alum mice, neutralized pseudoviruses expressing the spike of BA.5 or XBB.1.5 up to day 500 after immunization (Fig. 2g), as well as BA.1 and BA.5 SARS-CoV-2 viruses at day 35 and day 56 (Fig. 2h), at levels similar to neutralization of WA1 virus.

To assess whether other adjuvants used in FDA-approved vaccines exerted a similar activity as MA, we used the alum-based adjuvant AS04, which incorporates 3-O-desacyl-4’-monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL). At day 35 and day 56, anti-spike total IgG or IgG1 was significantly upregulated in WA1mRNA+MA or WA1mRNA+AS04 mice, whereas only WA1mRNA+MA mice significantly increased IgG2c, compared to WA1mRNA mice (Extended Data Fig. 5a). WA1mRNA+MA or WA1mRNA+AS04 both increased the number of TFH and BGC cells compared to WA1mRNA or MA alone, but WA1mRNA+MA outperformed WA1mRNA+AS04 (Extended Data Fig. 5b). The number of spike-specific BGC cells, as well as serum neutralization of pseudoviruses expressing WA1, BA.5 or XBB.1.5 spike, was significantly increased by WA1mRNA+MA compared to WA1mRNA and/or WA1mRNA+AS04 at day 56 (Extended Data Fig. 5c,d). Thus, MA expanded the capacity of WA1mRNA to drive TFH and anti-spike BGC as well as nAbs directed against Omicron sublineages characterized by high escape capacity.

MA boosts somatic hypermutation and B cell diversity

To get mechanistic insights into how WA1mRNA+MA broadened the capacity of B cells to recognize distinct VOCs, we sorted CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells specific for WA1 or XBB.1.5 spikes (Extended Data Fig. 5e) and performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) coupled with single-cell B cell receptor sequencing (scBCR-seq) in C57BL/6 mice injected on day 0 and on day 14 i.m. with WA1mRNA (from which we isolated anti-WA1 spike BGC cells), WA1mRNA+MA (from which we isolated anti-WA1 and anti-XBB.1.5 spike BGC cells) or monovalent Comirnaty developed against XBB.1.5 spike (hereafter ‘XBB.1.5mRNA’), from which we isolated anti-XBB.1.5 spike BGC cells. Initial clustering identified 16 clusters, 13 of which included Cd19+ or Jchain+ B cell populations, whereas three clusters expressed markers of T cells (Cd3d/Cd3e, cluster C12) and myeloid cells (Cd14/Cd68, clusters C14 and C15) (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). Reclustering after exclusion of T and myeloid cells generated 14 subclusters (Fig. 3a) characterized by distinct driving genes (Fig. 3b). scRNA-seq revealed that WA1 spike-specific B cells from WA1mRNA+MA mice showed expansion of light zone B cells (cluster C9) and proliferating dark zone B cells (cluster C10) compared to WA1 spike-specific B cells from WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 3b–d). Among WA1-specific plasma cell and plasmablast lineages, we found expansion of a population of memory-derived ASCs (cluster C13), post-GC proliferating plasmablasts (cluster C11) as well as non-proliferative plasma cells, which had higher expression of maturity markers such as Jchain and Sdc1 (CD138) (cluster C0) relative to plasma cells (cluster C5) in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 3b–d). Among WA1 spike-specific plasmablast lineages (clusters C2 and C3), cells in cluster C3 (showing increased expression of Jchain and Tnfrsf17 and representing more mature cells transitioning toward plasma cells) were expanded in WA1mRNA+MA compared to WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 3b–d). Finally, whereas the frequency of WA1 spike-specific recently stimulated/effector-prone BM cells (cluster C8) was decreased, the proportion of BM lineage with high expression of the memory maturation marker Klf2 (cluster C1) was increased in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 3b–d). Analyses of XBB1.5 spike-specific B cells showed reduced fraction differences across distinct clusters between WA1mRNA+MA and XBB.1.5mRNA mice (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). scBCR-seq analyses of heavy chain CDR3 length distributions did not reveal major differences in mean length or frequency between WA1 spike-specific B cell clones from WA1mRNA+MA and WA1mRNA mice (Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). Minor differences in CDR3 length distribution were observed between XBB.1.5 spike-specific B cell clones from XBB1.5mRNA mice compared to WA1mRNA+MA mice (Extended Data Fig. 6e,g), whereas differences in heavy chain V gene usage were detected between WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA and XBB.1.5mRNA mice among WA1-specific and XBB.1.5-specific B cell clones, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 6h,i). Plotted rank abundance curves for WA1 spike-specific B cell clones identified 6,455 heavy chain sequences, including 2,871 from WA1mRNA mice and 3,584 from WA1mRNA+MA mice. The rank abundance curve for WA1mRNA mice dropped sharply at the highest-ranked clone (top clone approximately 4.39% of total sequences), consistent with oligoclonal dominance, whereas the curve from WA1mRNA+MA mice was flatter, indicating reduced dominance and greater clonal evenness (Fig. 3e). A similar, although less pronounced, pattern was observed among XBB.1.5 spike-specific B cell clones in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to XBB.1.5mRNA mice (Fig. 3f), indicating that MA reduced dominant clonal expansions and promoted a more even and diverse antigen-specific BCR repertoire.

Clonal diversity was assessed by quantifying both clone richness and evenness using Hill diversity indices across q values from 0 to 4, after rarefaction to equalize sample sizes and enable fair comparison. Rarefaction curves were flatter in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA or XBB.1.5mRNA mice (Fig. 3g,h) as a reflection of the observations that, among WA1 spike-specific B cell clones, WA1mRNA+MA mice exhibited higher overall diversity than WA1mRNA mice (P ≤ 0.0446). Clone richness (q = 0) increased by 37.85 effective clones (P = 0.0280); diversity weighted toward evenness (q = 2) increased by 20.61 (P = 0.0012); and Shannon entropy (q ≈ 1) was modestly higher in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA mice (5.456 versus 5.348). Across all q values, diversity was significantly higher (P = 0.00001 for all q); richness (q = 0) increased by 85.33 effective clones; and evenness-weighted diversity (q = 2) increased by 28.60, with corresponding increases in entropy (4.937 versus 4.394) in XBB.1.5 spike-specific B cell clones in WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to XBB.1.5mRNA mice. These data indicated reduced clonal dominance and increased repertoire evenness. Analyses of non-rarefied datasets showed similar trends but exaggerated low-q differences (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k), confirming the importance of rarefaction for minimizing sample size bias.

We also assessed somatic hypermutation (SHM) in heavy chain (total and IgG) and light chain across all B cell clones. WA1 spike-specific clones from WA1mRNA+MA mice exhibited significantly higher SHM in both heavy and light chains compared to those from WA1mRNA mice (Fig. 3i). Analysis of individual chain contributions in XBB.1.5 spike-specific clones indicated that WA1mRNA+MA mice preferentially enhanced light chain SHM, whereas XBB.1.5mRNA mice displayed relatively higher heavy chain SHM (Extended Data Fig. 6l), consistent with antigen-driven effects rather than adjuvant-mediated changes. Analyses of antibody isotype usage did not detect statistically significant differences between WA1 or XBB.1.5 spike-specific clones from WA1mRNA, WA1mRNA+MA or XBB.1.5mRNA mice (Extended Data Fig. 6m).

To directly test whether WA1mRNA+MA expanded B cell clones that could recognize distinct epitopes, we performed VirScan32. Compared to WA1mRNA mice, serum antibodies from WA1mRNA+MA mice recognized a greater number of distinct epitopes within WA1 spike and showed significantly increased recognition of epitopes within the spike of OC43 coronavirus and a trend toward increased epitope recognition within the spike of Middle East respiratory syndrome-related coronavirus (MERS-CoV) (Fig. 3j) but no significant increase in epitope recognition for spike of HKU1 or 229E coronaviruses (Extended Data Fig. 6n). A self-antigen protein array33 found no evidence that WA1mRNA+MA disrupted tolerance to self-antigens compared to sera from mice with systemic lupus erythematosus that served as positive control (Extended Data Fig. 6o). Thus, MA expanded BGC cell clones able to recognize distinct coronaviruses without breaking tolerance to self-antigens and enhanced SHM and clonal diversity, leading to increased breadth and balance of antibodies.

MA overcomes antigenic imprinting

Antigenic imprinting limits the efficacy of mRNA vaccines against new VOCs34–37. To test whether MA could bypass antigenic imprinting due to previous immunizations, we i.m. immunized C57BL/6 mice at day 0 (prime) and at day 14 (boost) with WA1mRNA, followed by a second boost with WA1mRNA (hereafter ‘WA1mRNA+WA1mRNA’), XBB.1.5mRNA (hereafter ‘WA1mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA’) or XBB.1.5mRNA+MA (hereafter ‘WA1mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA+MA’) at day 56 after prime (Fig. 4a). Alternatively, mice received prime boost and second boost with XBB.1.5mRNA (hereafter ‘XBB.1.5mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA’). Mice injected with MA alone were used as negative controls. At day 84 after prime, WA1mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA+MA mice had significantly higher WA1 spike-specific IgGs compared to all other groups (Fig. 4b). Serum from XBB.1.5mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA mice showed efficient neutralization of a pseudovirus expressing XBB.1.5 spike, which was significantly higher compared to WA1mRNA+WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA mice (Fig. 4c), indicating antigenic imprinting after prime and boost with WA1mRNA. WA1mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA+MA mice neutralized XBB.1.5 spike-expressing pseudovirus with the same efficacy as XBB.1.5mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA mice (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, WA1mRNA+XBB.1.5mRNA+MA mice showed a significant increase in neutralizing activity directed against a pseudovirus expressing BA.5 or WA1 spikes (Fig. 4c), in the number of spike-specific CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells, CD19+CD80+PDL2+IgG1+IgM−IgD− BM cells (Fig. 4d,e) and CD4+CXCR5+CD278+ TFH cells (Fig. 4f) as well as in IFNγ secreted by splenocytes restimulated with WA1 spike peptides and percent of bone marrow cells secreting anti-WA1 spike IgGs measured by ELISpot (Fig. 4f) compared to all other groups. MA rewires WA1mRNA-driven inflammation in the dLN

To gain a better understanding of the programs that were regulated in WA1mRNA+MA mice, we performed bulk RNA-seq on whole dLNs isolated at days 1, 3 and 7 from mice injected i.m. with WA1mRNA, WA1mRNA+alum, WA1mRNA+MA or saline. Principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the transcriptional programs activated in the dLN of WA1mRNA and WA1mRNA+alum mice overlapped at all timepoints, whereas WA1mRNA+MA mice occupied a discrete space (Fig. 5a). Compared to day 1, the transcriptional programs activated at day 3 in all groups regressed toward the transcriptional landscape of saline mice (Fig. 5a), suggesting a decrease at day 3 in transcriptional reprogramming associated with the inflammatory burst characterizing day 1. PCA showed a profound change at day 7 compared to days 0, 1 or 3 for all immunized groups (Fig. 5a), suggesting that the activation of adaptive immunity profoundly impacted overall gene transcription. Principal component-driving genes for both components were higher at day 1 compared to day 3, whereas a completely different set of genes was driving the two principal components at day 7 for all immunized groups (Extended Data Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 1). Next, we inhibited IFN signaling by co-injecting IFNAR1 and IFNγ-specific antibodies or isotype control intravenously in mice immunized i.m. with WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+MA at day 0 and day 14 (Fig. 6f). WA1mRNA+MA mice receiving blocking antibodies compared to isotype control abrogated the increase in anti-spike total IgG and IgG1 (while, as expected, IgG2c induction was blocked in all groups) (Fig. 6g and Extended Data Fig. 8c), in the number of CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC and CD4+CXCR5+CD278+ TFH cells (Fig. 6h), in the neutralization of pseudoviruses expressing the spike of WA1, BA.5 or XBB.1.5 (Fig. 6i) and in the number of BGC cells expressing BCRs recognizing the spike of WA1, BA.5 or XBB.1.5 (Fig. 6j) and also reduced the percentage of CD19−CD38+CD138+ LLPCs (Fig. 6k) while only minimally affecting these readouts in WA1mRNA mice. Thus, IFN signaling was necessary for the activity of WA1mRNA+MA without being involved in the response to WA1mRNA.

To test whether IL-1 and IFN signaling were linked to each other in WA1mRNA+MA mice, we assessed the induction of IFN proteins and IFN-induced CXCL10 in total dLN lysates on day 1 after immunization in WA1mRNA and WA1mRNA+MA mice treated or not with anakinra as above. Whereas WA1mRNA+MA increased the expression of IFNs and CXCL10 compared to WA1mRNA mice, these effects were abrogated in anakinra-treated WA1mRNA+MA mice (Extended Data Fig. 8d). Inhibition of IFN with blocking antibodies as above also abrogated the increase in the amount of IL-1β protein in total lysates of dLN of WA1mRNA+MA mice compared to WA1mRNA mice (Extended Data Fig. 8e), indicating that MA initiated a positive feedback loop between IL-1 and IFN signaling that sustained each other and that was necessary to potentiate the response to WA1mRNA.

Finally, we immunized BALB/c Il1rn−/− mice, which model a rare human autoinflammatory disease caused by excessive IL-1 signaling due to lack of IL1RN39, with WA1mRNA, along with wild-type BALB/c mice immunized with WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+MA, to test whether excessive IL-1 signaling mirrored the activity of MA. IL-1β, IFNα and CXCL10 proteins in dLN either before immunization or at day 1 after immunization were increased in WA1mRNA Il1rn−/− mice compared to WA1mRNA wild-type mice but were similar compared to WA1mRNA+MA wild-type mice (Extended Data Fig. 8f). Spike-specific IgGs (Fig. 6l and Extended Data Fig. 8g), CD19+CD95+GL7+ BGC cells recognizing WA1, BA.4/BA.5 or XBB.1.5 spike proteins, as well as neutralization of pseudoviruses expressing the spike protein of WA1, BA.5 or XBB.1.5 (Fig. 6m,n), were similar in WA1mRNA Il1rn−/−mice and WA1mRNA+MA wild-type mice at day 56. Thus, MA initiated a positive feedback loop between IFN and IL-1 signaling that was necessary and sufficient to potentiate the activity of WA1mRNA.

MA contains a 1:1 ratio of soluble and particulate mannan21. We performed a dose response to assess whether different ratios of mannan to alum affected the immune response in human cells and found that admixing 20 µg of mannan with 10 µg of alum significantly increased the release of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF as well as the amount of intracellular pro-IL-1β in GM-CSF-treated PBMCs compared to other mannan:alum ratios tested (Fig. 8c–e and Extended Data Fig. 10b). Soluble mannan induced the phosphorylation of the transcription factor STAT-1 and upregulated the protein levels of viperin (which is induced by IFNs) in GM-CSF-treated PBMCs (Fig. 8f), suggesting that soluble mannan drives IFNs in human cells, similar to mouse cells21. Nevertheless, MA further increased phosphorylation of STAT-1 and expression of viperin (Fig. 8f), indicating increased signaling when mannan was formulated with alum. Thus, MA activated human cells.

Discussion

Here we showed that the activity of mRNA vaccines against the ancestral WA1 SARS-CoV-2 strain as well as that of updated XBB.1.5-Comirnaty mRNA vaccines was expanded by admixing MA, a formulation comprising C. albicans mannan and alum. The mannan component of MA, but not alum, was needed to extend the durability of serum antibodies and to induce nAbs that recognized new VOCs, without increasing reactogenicity or breaking self-tolerance in mice and NHPs. Use of WA1mRNA+MA was associated with an extension of the amplitude and duration of T cell responses, antibody levels and epitopes recognized by distinct immunoglobulins and of nAbs.

Our transcriptional and functional analyses demonstrated that IFN and IL-1β signaling were necessary and sufficient to drive the activity of MA. IFN signaling in immune and non-immune cells modulates a variety of functions, including chemokine expression and vascular permeability42,43, TFH cell formation44 and BM identity45, but how MA-induced IFNs affect the response to WA1mRNA remains to be elucidated. Although the dose and LNP shell of the mRNA vaccine formulation influence the ability of mRNA vaccines to induce IFNs in the dLN13, we found that responses to mice immunized with WA1mRNA alone were not altered by blockade of IFN signaling, whereas potentiation of the response after MA administration was completely dependent on IFNs. Regulation of IL-1β production has also been proposed to control the early response to mRNA vaccines12,13, and we confirmed these data, but we also found that IL-1β signaling was absolutely necessary to initiate a positive feedback loop with type I IFNs to sustain the activity of MA.

The inability to protect against multiple variants or sublineages of a given virus is a major limitation of the commercially available mRNA vaccines. Expansion of GC correlates with a broader and more robust response in mice and humans46. During prolonged GC reaction, high-affinity antibodies masking the immunodominant epitopes of an antigen allow GC entry of B cell clones with lower affinity and a more diverse repertoire of antibodies47. Consistent with this observation, we found that MA promoted extensive GC reactions and a more diverse and balanced repertoire of B cell clones. Follow-up studies are needed to link these activities of MA to distinct molecular and cellular processes governed by IFN and/or IL-1 signaling.

We also found that human PBMCs secreted IL-1β in response to MA. Release of IL-1β is typically associated with inflammasome activation48, and IL-1β and caspase-1 had an essential role in MA-induced activity in mice in vivo, further indicating involvement of inflammasome activation downstream of MA. How exactly MA controls inflammasome activation remains to be determined, especially because, in our hands, alum alone did not induce IL-1β secretion in human PBMCs. Both mouse cells21 and human cells also activated the IFN pathway in response to soluble mannan, but MA further potentiated this activity, whereas cells produced proinflammatory cytokines only in response to MA, not mannan. How activation of the IFN pathway differs from the induction of canonical proinflammatory cytokines also needs to be further investigated.

Overall, our findings stress the importance of innate immune activation in controlling adaptive immune responses and demonstrate that fungal glycans that target dectin-2 can be used in conjugation with mRNA-based vaccines to potentiate the protection against multiple variants of a virus.

Methods

Mice

Six-to-eight-week-old female C57BL/6J mice (no. 00664), BALB/C mice (no. 000651) and K18-hACE2 mice (no. 034860) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. B6.129S7-Il1r1tm1Imx/J (Il1rn−/−) mice were a gift from the Nigrovic laboratory (Boston Children’s Hospital) and were originally generated by the Iwakura laboratory (University of Tokyo). Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at Boston Children’s Hospital, and all procedures were approved under the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and operated under the supervision of the Department of Animal Resources at Children’s Hospital.

NHP studies

The study protocol was approved by the IACUC (approval no. XJTUAE2025-2666). All procedures involving M. fascicularis were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, 2011). Ten male cynomolgus monkeys (M. fascicularis), aged 3–5 years and confirmed to be seronegative for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies prior to the study, were obtained from Guangxi Spring Biological Technology Development Co., Ltd. Each animal was individually housed in cages within a temperature-controlled and humidity-controlled animal room. Environmental conditions were maintained at a target temperature of 22–26 °C and a relative humidity of 40–70%. A 12-hour light/dark cycle was implemented. Monkeys were fed a maintenance diet twice daily, supplemented with fruits and vegetables once daily. Water was available ad libitum. After a 14-day acclimation period, animals were randomized into two groups (n = 5 per group) to receive either WA1mRNA or WA1mRNA+MA. All formulations were administered via i.m. injection into the left quadriceps muscle in a final volume of 0.5 ml. Immunization was performed on day 0 (prime) and on day 21 (boost) using WA1mRNA (30 µg per monkey) alone or WA1mRNA+MA (Alhydrogel (1 mg per monkey) + mannan (5 mg per monkey)). Body weight and rectal temperature were measured at each point. Blood samples were collected from a forelimb or hindlimb vein on day −1 (pre-immune) and on days 20, 42, 60, 90, 120 and 180 after prime. After an overnight fast, serum was separated by standard methods and stored in a −80 °C freezer until analysis.

Cell culture

Vero E6 cells (American Type Culture Collection, CRL-1586) and 293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin−streptomycin (Lonza). 293T cells stably overexpressing ACE2 and TMSPPR2 (Sino Biological, OEC003) and 293FT cells stably overexpressing ACE2 and TMSPPR2 (kindly provided by M. Johnson, University of Missouri School of Medicine) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin−streptomycin and 1% sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Viruses

Samples of SARS-CoV-2 (WA1) were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention after isolation from a patient in Washington State (Biodefense and Emerging Infections Research Resources Repository (BEI Resources), isolate USA-WA1/2020, NR-52281; referred to as ‘WA1’ throughout this paper). SARS-CoV-2 variants are B.1.1.529/BA.1 (a gift from M. Suthar (Emory)), BA.4 (BEI NR-56806), BA.5 (BEI NR-58620) and XBB.1.5 (BEI NR-59104). All viruses were grown up on Vero E6 expressing human TMPRSS2 (VeroT) cells, originally made by M. Takeda at the National Institute of Infectious Diseases in Tokyo, Japan. Virus was stocked at high-titer concentrations for dilution in future experiments. All viruses have been sequenced confirmed for fidelity.

Adjuvant and vaccine formulation

Mannan was isolated from C. albicans SC5314 and quality controlled by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy as described previously21,49,50. The formulation of alum (Alhydrogel adjuvant 2%; Invivogen) and mannan (referred to as ‘MA’) was obtained by mixing alum (100 µg per mouse) and mannan (500 µg per mouse), and total volume was adjusted with saline (total volume for one injection: 50 µl per mouse).

To formulate the MA with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein trimer or the WA1mRNA vaccine (Pfizer), the volume of saline was reduced to account for the additional volume. WA1mRNA was used at 1 µg per injection. SARS-CoV-2 spike protein trimer (referred to as ‘spike’ throughout this paper) was used at 1 µg per injection and was kindly provided by the Precision Vaccine Program at Boston Children’s Hospital. WA1mRNA (1 µg per mouse) or SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (1 µg per mouse) alone or formulated with alum (100 µg per mouse), mannan alone (500 µg per mouse) or saline were used as controls.

Further information on this formulation can be found in the NIH/National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) Vaccine Adjuvant Compendium (https://vac.niaid.nih.gov).

AS04 or PHAD-504 (synthetic monophosphoryl lipid A-504, 50 mg per mouse) (Avanti Polar Lipids, 699810P) was prepared by a simple admixture with alum (100 µg per mouse) in neutral pH.

Immunization of mice

Mice were immunized by injecting compounds i.m. in the thigh muscle or i.d. in the forelimb on day 0 (prime) and on day 14 (boost).

Quantification of anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike antibody titers

Blood samples were collected by retroorbital bleeding on days 14 (pre-boost), 28, 35, 56, 84, 180, 300 or 500, and serum was isolated by centrifugation of whole blood samples twice at 1,500g for 10 minutes. SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific total IgG, IgG1 or IgG2c antibody levels were quantified in serum samples by ELISA according to a modified, previously described protocol51. In brief, high-binding flat-bottom 96-well plates were coated with 0.5 µg ml−1 SARS-CoV-2 spike trimer in PBS, incubated overnight at 4 °C, washed once with 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS (PBS-T) and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Serum samples were added at an initial dilution of 1:100 in 1% BSA in PBS. To generate 11-point curves, 1:4 serial dilutions were performed. Samples were incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. Plates were washed three times with PBS-T and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Southern Biotech, 1036-05), IgG1 (Southern Biotech, 1071-05) or IgG2c (Southern Biotech, 1078-05) antibodies and, for NHP experiments, HRP-conjugated anti-monkey IgG (Southern Biotech, 4700-05) antibody. Finally, plates were washed five times with PBS-T and developed with tetramethylbenzidine (BD OptEIA Substrate Solution for spike; BD Biosciences) for 5 minutes and then stopped with 2 N H2SO4. Optical densities were read at 450 nm with a SpectraMax iD3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices), and endpoint titers were calculated using, as cutoff, three times the optical density of the background. Value less than 100 were reported as 80.

LN dissociation

The dLNs (brachial or inguinal) were collected and processed for subsequent analyses at indicated timepoints after injection as previously described21. LN weight was assessed on an analytical scale prior to further processing. To generate a single-cell suspension, LNs were subjected to a total of three cycles of incubation at 37 °C for 20–30 minutes (first cycle) or 10 minutes (second and third cycle) in 400 µl (first cycle) or 200 µl (second and third cycle) of digestion mix (1% penicillin−streptomycin, 2% FBS, 100 mg ml−1 collagenase from Clostridium histolyticum (MilliporeSigma), 100 mg ml−1 Dispase II (MilliporeSigma) and 10 mg ml−1 DNase I from bovine pancreas (MilliporeSigma) in Iscove’s Modified Dubecco’s Medium (IMDM)). After each incubation, LNs were homogenized by pipetting with a 1,000-µl tip, and supernatants were transferred to new tubes and kept at 4 °C while digestion mix was added to the remaining larger pieces and incubated at 37 °C. Then, pooled supernatants of individual LNs were divided into two aliquots: one for flow cytometry analysis, and another one was centrifuged at 300g for 5 minutes and the cell pellet resuspended in 800 µl of TRI Reagent (Zymo Research) for subsequent RNA isolation.

Preparation of fixed-dose combinations comprising WA1mRNA or LNP and MA complex

MA complex was prepared by incubating alum with mannan (ratio of alum:mannan = 1:5) in 0.9% saline for 30 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, WA1mRNA or LNP (1 mg ml−1 mRNA) was added to the MA complex and incubated at 4 °C for 1 hour or 24 hours. The ratio between WA1mRNA or LNP and MA in these fixed-dose combination (FDC) vaccines ranged from 1:1 to 1:100. DLS (ZetaPlus Zeta Potential Analyzer; Brookhaven Instruments) was performed to determine the stability of each active pharmaceutical ingredient in the FDC solution.

Determination of non-specific mRNA release from WA1mRNA in the FDCs

Three FDC vaccines with varying WA1mRNA:MA ratios were prepared using the abovementioned protocol. After approximately 24 hours of incubation, these FDC vaccines were briefly centrifuged using a benchtop centrifuge to remove the MA complex from the solutions. The resulting solutions were then transferred to a Nunc MicroWell 96-well optical bottom plate, and total ‘free’ mRNA concentration in each FDC was quantified by the Quant-it RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence measurement was performed using a Synergy HT Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments).

RNA isolation and sequencing

RNA was isolated from TRI Reagent (Zymo Research) samples using Direct-zol RNA Microprep and Miniprep kits (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA library preparation with poly(A) selection and Illumina sequencing were conducted at Azenta Life Sciences as follows. RNA samples were quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies), and RNA integrity was checked using an Agilent TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were prepared by using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina following the manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs). In brief, the enriched RNAs were fragmented for 8 minutes at 94 °C. First-strand and second-strand cDNA were subsequently synthesized. The second strand of cDNA was marked by incorporating dUTP during the synthesis. cDNA fragments were adenylated at 3′ ends, and indexed adapter was ligated to cDNA fragments. Limited cycle polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used for library enrichment. The incorporated dUTP in second-strand cDNA quenched the amplification of the second strand, which helped to preserve the strand specificity. The sequencing library was validated on the Agilent TapeStation and quantified by using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer as well as by quantitative PCR (qPCR) (KAPA Biosystems).

The sequencing libraries were clustered on the flowcell. After clustering, the flowcell was loaded on the Illumina instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were sequenced using a 2 × 150-bp paired-end configuration. Image analysis and base calling were conducted by the control software. Raw sequence data (.bcl files) generated by the sequencer were converted into FASTQ files and demultiplexed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq 2.20 software. One mismatch was allowed for index sequence identification.

FastQC quality-controlled reads were mapped to the mouse transcriptome (GRCm39) based on Ensembl annotations using Kallisto version 0.50.1. Transcript counts were imported and aggregated to gene counts using tximport. Only genes passing a threshold of six or more counts in four or more samples were considered as expressed and further analyzed using the R package DESeq2 (ref. 52). Differential expression tests were set up with an alternate hypothesis of an absolute log2 fold change greater than 0.5. Heatmaps were produced with the R package ComplexHeatmap, using z-scored rlog-normalized counts. Volcano plots were produced using the R package EnhancedVolcano, with a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted threshold of 0.01 and a log2 fold change threshold of 1. Pathway analysis was performed with the R package fgsea, using the entire Broad Institute’s Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Hallmark gene set collection and select signatures from Reactome, Gene Ontology Biological Process or publications, as indicated in the Results section. Bubble plots were generated using the R package ggplot2, showing pathways passing a threshold of adjusted P value of 0.05.

Splenocyte restimulation assay

To extract splenocytes, spleens were passed through a 70-µm cell strainer, rinsed with PBS and treated with 2 ml of ammonium–chloride–potassium (ACK) lysis buffer for 2 minutes at room temperature to lyse red blood cells (RBCs). After one more washing step with PBS, splenocytes were distributed into flat-bottom 96-well plates, with a density of 2 × 106 cells per well. SARS-CoV-2 spike peptides (PepTivator SARS-CoV-2 Prot_S; Miltenyi Biotec) were added to each well at a concentration of 0.6 nmol ml−1 in a total volume of 200 µl per well. Supernatants were collected after 96 hours for the quantification of IFNγ levels using the ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Mouse IFNγ (BioLegend, 430804), as per the manufacturerʼs instructions.

SARS-CoV-2 neutralization titer determination

All serum samples were heat inactivated at 56 °C for 30 minutes to deactivate complement and allowed to equilibrate to room temperature prior to processing for neutralization titer. Samples were diluted in duplicate to an initial dilution of 1:5 or 1:10, followed by 1:2 serial dilutions, resulting in a 12-dilution series with each well containing 100 μl. All dilutions were performed in DMEM (Quality Biological), supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (heat inactivated; MilliporeSigma), 1% (v/v) penicillin−streptomycin (GeminiBio) and 1% (v/v) L-glutamine (2 mM final concentration; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Dilution plates were then transported into the Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory, and 100 μl of diluted SARS-CoV-2 (WA1, B.1.1.529, BA.4/BA.5 or XBB.1.5 (BEI Resources)) inoculum to result in a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 was added to each well. A non-treated, virus-only control and a mock infection control were included on every plate. The sample/virus mixture was then incubated at 37 °C (5.0% CO2) for 1 hour before transferring to 96-well flat-bottom plates with confluent Vero-TMPRSS2 cells.

Twenty-four hours (48 hours for B.1.1.529) after infection, cells were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich) for at least 1 hour at 4 °C as per BSL-3 standard operating procedures and immunofluorescence stained for the SARS-CoV-2 N protein. Formaldehyde was quenched by incubating cells with 50 mM ammonium chloride in PBS for 15 minutes, prior to permeabilizing the cells with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.2% (w/v) BSA in PBS (blocking buffer) for 10 minutes. Cells were blocked for 10 minutes with blocking buffer and then incubated with anti-N antibody (Sino Biological, 40143-R004) for 1 hour (diluted 1:10,000 in blocking buffer). Primary antibody was removed by washing 3 × 5 minutes with blocking buffer, and cells were then incubated with goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor-488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A11008) and diluted 1:2,000 in blocking buffer for 45 minutes. Cells were washed a further 3 × 5 minutes with PBS and incubated with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted 1:2,000 in PBS for 10 minutes. Cells were washed a final 2 × 5 minutes with PBS and then imaged with a Celigo high-content imager (Nexcelom). Inhibition data were normalized according to the following formula based on cell-only and virus-only controls: %Neutralization = (1 − ((treatment − cell only) / (virus only − cell only))) × 100%. Nonlinear regression analysis was performed on the normalized inhibition data, and half-maximal inhibitory dilution (ID50) values were calculated from fitted curves (log (agonist) versus response − variable slope (four parameters)) using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). ID50 values extrapolated outside the dilution range tested were reported as greater than the highest concentration tested or less than the lowest concentration tested.

SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus neutralization assay

SARS-Cov2 pseudovirus neutralization assay was performed as described previously53. In brief, 293T cells were co-transfected with a pHDM vector expressing SARS-CoV-2 spike protein variants, pLenti CMV Puro LUC (w168-1) (a gift from E. Campeau and P. Kaufman52; Addgene plasmid no. 17477) and psPAX2 (a gift from D. Trono; Addgene, plasmid no. 12260) with Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 48 hours, supernatant containing pseudoviruses was collected. For NHP experiments, pseudoviruses were purchased from Sino Biological (SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV) spike pseudovirus (cat. no. PSV001), BA.4/BA.5/BA.5.2 (Omicron) spike pseudovirus (cat. no. PSV022) and XBB.1.5 (Omicron) spike pseudovirus (cat. no. PSV030)). In brief, 293FT cells stably expressing human ACE2 and TMRPSS2 were infected with pseudoviruses diluted in DMEM containing 10% FBS to test titers. For neutralization assays, 2 × 104 293FT cells expressing human ACE2 and TMRPSS2 were plated per well of a 96-well flat-bottom plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C. On the next day, serum samples collected from immunized mice were serially diluted and incubated with pseudovirus at 37 °C for 1 hour, and the mixture was added in duplicates to the cells. After 48 hours, cells were lysed using ONE-Glo luciferase reagent (Promega), and the luminescence signal was recorded on a BioTek Synergy H1 instrument (Agilent Technologies). Serum titers were determined by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 10 of log-transformed luciferase signal and represented as 50% neutralizing titer (NT50) (the titer to cause 50% inhibition of infection). The NT50 was set to the limit of detection (LOD) if the initial dilution of serum (1:30) did not reach a neutralization of 50% or if the regression fit was poor (<0.7).

ELISpot assay

Ninety-six-well multiscreen filter plates (MilliporeSigma, MSIPS4510) were pre-wetted with 20 μl of 35% ethanol in water (v/v) for 1 minute and washed five times with PBS followed by coating with 100 µl of anti-mouse IgG (15 μg ml−1; SouthernBiotech, 1030-01) overnight at 4 °C. On the next day, plates were washed five times with PBS and blocked in 200 μl of RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 61870127) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin−streptomycin and 1% L-glutamine for 2 hours at 37 °C. Freshly isolated bone marrow cells were seeded at 2 × 105 cells per well after RBC lysis and incubated for 48 hours at 37 °C. Medium-containing wells without cells were used as blank. After incubation, medium was decanted, and plates were washed five times with PBS followed by addition of 100 µl of biotinylated anti-mouse total IgG (Mabtech, 3825-6) or biotinylated spike protein (Sino Biological, 40589-V27B-B) in 0.5% BSA in PBS for 2 hours at room temperature. Plates were washed six times with PBS and subsequently incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with 100 µl of a 1:1,000 dilution of Streptavidin-ALP enzyme conjugate (Mabtech, 3310-10) in 0.5% BSA in PBS. After washing with PBS for five times, 100 µl of filtered SIGMAFAST BCIP/NBT (Sigma-Aldrich, B5655) solution was added, and plates were incubated at room temperature until distinct spots became visible. Reaction was stopped by washing the plate with dH2O and allowed to dry completely. Finally, the plate was read on an ImmunoSpot microanalyzer plate reader (Cellular Technology Ltd.). No background subtraction was carried out, and spike+ spot numbers were normalized to numbers of total IgG spot numbers.

Multiplexed cytokine analysis (LEGENDplex)

Cytokine levels from immunized mice serum samples or dLNs were analyzed using the LEGENDplex Mouse Anti-Virus Response Panel (13-plex) with a V-bottom plate kit (BioLegend, 740622). The protein standard was diluted in lysis buffer. All other procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Single-cell library preparation and sequencing

Single-cell suspensions of FACS-sorted GC B cells from four biological samples were processed using the Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kit v3 (10x Genomics, 1000699) and the Single Cell 5′ Gel Bead Kit v3 (10x Genomics, 1000700). Cells (27,000–35,000 per channel) were loaded onto a Chromium X Series instrument (10x Genomics) to generate single-cell gel beads-in-emulsion (GEMs), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The recovery rate was approximately 45–55%. Captured cells were lysed within individual GEMs, and released RNA was barcoded during reverse transcription. 5′ gene expression (GEX) libraries, single-cell V(D)J libraries (10x Genomics, 1000255) and cell surface protein libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Library quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2200 TapeStation, and final libraries were quantified by qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms. Libraries were diluted to 2 nM and pooled into two separate batches, and pooled libraries at 650 pM were mixed with 5% PhiX control library prior to sequencing. Sequencing was performed on two independent runs using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform with the P4 XLEAP-SBS Reagent Kit (200 cycles), with paired-end reads configured as follows: 28 cycles for read 1, 10 cycles for the i7 index, 10 cycles for the i5 index and 90 cycles for read 2. Sequencing achieved a median depth of approximately 32,000 reads per cell for GEX, 55,000 reads per cell for BCR and 5,000 reads per cell for hashtag (antibody-derived tag (ADT)) libraries.

Single-cell data processing and GEX analysis

Raw binary base call (BCL) files generated from the Illumina NextSeq 2000 were demultiplexed into FASTQ files using Cell Ranger mkfastq (version 8.0.1)54. GEX and ADT libraries were processed simultaneously using the Cell Ranger count pipeline (version 8.0.1) with feature barcode analysis to generate multimodal matrices containing both GEX and surface protein counts. V(D)J libraries were processed using Cell Ranger vdj (version 8.0.1) to generate contig FASTA sequences and annotations.

Downstream analyses were performed using the Seurat package (version 5.0.1)55 in RStudio. Hashtag demultiplexing was performed using a custom classification workflow rather than the default Seurat hashtag oligo (HTO) pipeline. The Seurat implementation uses an automated thresholding approach that can be overly conservative in high-dimensional or pre-filtered datasets and may misclassify a substantial fraction of cells as doublets. To provide controlled and dataset-specific handling of HTO signals, we implemented a custom-written CLR normalization and clustering strategy.

For normalization, CLR normalization with margin = 1 was applied because the datasets contained a small number of hashtags, and this approach provides straightforward scaling that avoids inflating doublet probabilities when only two or three tags are present. After normalization, k-medoids clustering (k = 2) was applied to each HTO to separate high-expression and low-expression populations. k-medoids was chosen for its robustness to outliers and stability with skewed, zero-inflated HTO distributions. The minimum value of the high-expression cluster was used as the cutoff for each HTO. Cells were classified as singlets, doublets or negatives based on the number of HTOs exceeding their respective cutoffs, and singlets were retained for downstream analysis.

After demultiplexing, Seurat objects were renamed for consistent sample identification, and metadata fields were standardized across datasets. All processed datasets were subsequently merged into a single Seurat object to allow integrated downstream analysis, including dimensionality reduction and clustering. Merging ensures consistent normalization, facilitates cross-sample comparisons and enables joint visualization of cellular populations across all experimental conditions.

After merging the samples into a single Seurat object, GEX counts were log normalized and scaled. Variable features were identified using FindVariableFeatures, selecting the top 3,000 highly variable genes, a commonly used setting in large scRNA-seq datasets. BCR genes, including immunoglobulin heavy chain and light chain variable regions (Igh, Igk and Igl), were excluded from the variable feature set to prevent clonotype-driven clustering, as recommended in standard B cell scRNA-seq workflows.

PCA was performed on the scaled GEX matrix using the selected variable features. The top principal components (determined by elbow plot inspection) were used to construct a shared nearest neighbor graph (FindNeighbors), and clusters were identified using Louvain modularity optimization via FindClusters. Clustering resolution was tested across a range of values, and the resolution yielding the best separation of known biological populations was selected. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and visualization.

Cluster-level differential expression was first assessed using the Seurat function FindAllMarkers to identify unbiased marker genes for each cluster. These markers were then interpreted using predefined canonical B cell marker sets to assign biological identities. GC cells were defined by high expression of Bcl6, Aicda, Mki67, Top2a, Cdk1, Pcna, Ccnb1 and Cdc20. Selection-associated clusters were annotated based on Nr4a1, Cd86 and Cd83. Memory precursor states were identified using Zbtb32, Bcl2, Klf2, Fcrl5 and Ccr6, and plasmablast and plasma cell precursor populations were defined by expression of Prdm1, Xbp1, Sdc1, Jchain, Tnfrsf17 and Mzb1. Additional lineage markers, including Cd19, Cd27, Ccr7, Clec9a and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) genes, were used to support initial lineage assignment and exclude non-B cell populations. Expression of these marker sets across clusters was visualized using DoHeatmap. Clusters corresponding to the target B cell populations (GC subsets, plasmablasts and memory precursors) were then subsetted and reclustered for focused downstream comparative analyses.

BCR repertoire analysis and clonal assignment

BCR repertoire analyses were performed using the Immcantation framework (version 4.5.0)56. Starting from Cell Ranger vdj contig FASTA and annotation files, germline V, D and J gene segments were assigned using the MakeDb and AssignGenes tools from Change-O (ref. 56) against the international ImMunoGeneTics (IMGT) reference database57. Only productive heavy chain (IGH) sequences from cells expressing exactly one IGH contig and 0−2 light chain contigs (IGK or IGL) were retained to minimize artifacts from cell doublets or incomplete assemblies.

In each BCR library, clonal groups were defined using the hierarchicalClones function from the ‘scoper’ package58. The function was executed with only_heavy = TRUE to calculate distances using heavy chain sequences only and split_light = TRUE to refine the inferred clones by splitting based on light chain sequences. Clustering was performed within partitions sharing the same V gene, J gene and junction length, applying a normalized Hamming distance threshold of 0.2 (equivalent to ≥80% amino acid sequence identity in the junction region) to accommodate somatic hypermutations within lineages.

Heavy chain CDR3 amino acid lengths were calculated as the junction amino acid length minus 2 (excluding the conserved cysteine and tryptophan/phenylalanine residues at the boundaries). V gene usage was computed as the proportion of sequences assigned to each heavy chain V gene using the ‘alakazam’ package56. Clonal abundance was visualized using rank abundance curves generated with ‘alakazam’ (10,000 bootstrap replicates). Diversity of the heavy chain clonal repertoire was quantified using Hill-based diversity indices across a range of q values (0–4) with 10,000 bootstrap iterations and rarefaction to the smaller sample size for fair pairwise comparison, also implemented in ‘alakazam’. Finally, SHM frequencies and isotype annotations were extracted from the curated Adaptive Immune Receptor Repertoire (AIRR)-formatted tables59 or downstream characterization.

CDR3 length statistical analysis

Heavy chain CDR3 length distributions were analyzed in GraphPad Prism version 10.6.1. Normality was assessed using Kolmogorov−Smirnov tests and quantile−quantile plots. Differences across groups were evaluated using the Kruskal−Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test (α = 0.05). Diversity differences were assessed using non-parametric bootstrap tests implemented in ‘alakazam’.

Anakinra, VX765 and anti-IFN treatments

Mice were treated intraperitoneally during prime and boost with anakinra (Raleukin; MedChemExpress, HY-108841) at a dose of 50mg kg−1 or caspase-1 inhibitor VX765 (InvivoGen, inh-vx765i-5) at a dose of 25mg kg−1 on day −1 (before prime), day 0 (prime) and day +1 (1 day after prime). For blocking IFN experiments, mice were treated intravenously with anti-IFNAR1 blocking antibody (BioLegend, 127324, clone: MAR1-5A3, 500 μg per mouse) and anti-IFNγ (BioLegend, 505848, clone: XMG1.2, 200 μg per mouse) on day −1 (before prime), day 0 (prime) and day +3 (3 days after prime). The same treatment was administered during boost. Control mice were treated with PBS and respective isotype controls.

Mouse infections

Female 6–8-week-old K18-hACE2 mice were immunized as above. After immunization, mice were transferred and housed in the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC)-accredited animal facilities at the University of Maryland, Baltimore, until 56 days after immunization. All mouse infections were carried out in an animal BSL-3 facility in accordance with approved practices. On day 0, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine and xylazine raised to a volume of 50 µl total in sterile PBS. While anesthetized, mice were intranasally inoculated with either 50 µl of sterile PBS or 1 × 105 plaque-forming units (PFU) of SARS-CoV-2. Mice were monitored daily for weight loss and signs of morbidity. On day 4 after infection, mice were euthanized, and lung tissue was collected for further analysis.

Lung viral titration by plaque assay

Vero T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS, 1% (v/v) penicillin−streptomycin and 1% (v/v) L-glutamine (2 mM final concentration) (Vero media). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2. SARS-CoV-2 lung titers were quantified by homogenizing mouse lungs in 1 ml of PBS using 1.0-mm glass beads and a Bead Ruptor. Vero T cells were plated in 12-well plates with 1.5 × 105 cells per well. SARS-CoV-2 virus titer in PFU was determined by plaque assay. In the plaque assay, 25 µl of the lung homogenate was added to 225 µl of PBS and diluted 10-fold across a six-point dilution curve with 200 µl of diluent added to each well. After 1 hour, a 2-ml semi-solid agar overlay containing DMEM was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 2 days at 37 °C (5% CO2) before plaques were counted manually by the investigator.

Histology

Lung sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for a minimum of 48 hours, after which they were sent to the Histology Core at the University of Maryland, Baltimore, for paraffin embedding and sectioning. Five-micrometer sections were prepared and used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining by the Histology Core Services. Sections were imaged at ×10 magnification, and representative images were chosen. Figures were put together using Adobe Photoshop and Illustrator software.

Histology scoring

Slides were prepared as 5-μm sections and stained with H&E. The scorer was blinded to information identifying the treatment groups, and fields were examined by light microscopy and analyzed. The severity of peribronchiolar, interstitial and perivascular inflammation was each evaluated and converted to a score of 0–5, with 0 being no inflammation and 5 being most severe. The degree of severity was evaluated by the number of inflammatory cells present and overall percent of the involvement of the section. When scoring was complete, scores for each group were averaged for an overall severity score, and the standard deviation for the scoring was computed.

Autoantigen microarray profiling

Autoantigen microarrays were manufactured in the Microarray & Immune Phenotyping Core Facility of The University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center. A selection of several autoantigens was made based on published literature—prior known autoantibodies in various immune-related disease, cancer, allergic disease, etc. The positive controls were also imprinted on the arrays as positive controls. There are 16 identical arrays on each slide. Each typical array contains autoantigens and internal controls. For sample processing, each slide was processed with 15 samples and a PBS as negative control.

Mouse serum samples were first treated with DNase I to remove free DNA and then applied onto autoantigen arrays with 1:50 dilution. The samples were incubated with autoantigen array, and the autoantibodies binding to the antigens on the array were detected with laser wavelengths 532 nm (Cy3-labeled anti-IgG) and 635 nm (Cy5-labeled anti-IgM), and TIFF images were generated. GenePix Pro 7 software was used to analyze the images and generate the GenePix report (GPR) files (Molecular Devices). The net fluorescence intensity (NFI) of each antigen was generated by subtracting the local background and negative control (PBS or simplified as PBS) signal. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR = (foreground median − background median) / standard deviation (background)) was also generated for each antigen. SNR is used as a quantitative measure of the ability to resolve true signal from background noise. A higher SNR indicates higher signal over background noise. The autoantibodies whose SNR value was less than 3 in more than 90% of the samples were considered negative and excluded from further analysis. NFI was normalized by a robust linear model using positive controls with different dilutions.

Preparation of C. albicans β-glucan particles

β-glucan was isolated from C. albicans SC5314 as previously described60. In brief, glucan was isolated from C. albicans using a base/acid extraction approach, which provides water-insoluble glucan particles that are more than 95% pure. The structure and purity of the glucan was determined by 1H-NMR in DMSO-d6 (ref. 60). Prior to use, the β-glucan particles are depyrogenated and sterilized.

Isolation, differentiation and stimulation of human phagocytes

Human PBMCs were isolated or differentiated from collars of blood from healthy donors received from the Boston Children’s Hospital blood donation center. Blood was diluted 1:2 in PBS, and PBMCs were isolated using Histopaque (MilliporeSigma, 1077-1) gradient centrifugation. Monocytes were positively selected from PBMCs using CD14 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-050-201) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PBMCs and CD14+ monocytes were further differentiated in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 61870127) supplemented with 10% FBS in the presence of 10 ng ml−1 GM-CSF (BioLegend, 572903) (referred to as ‘GM-CSF PBMC’) for 16 hours or 20 ng ml−1 GM-CSF (BioLegend, 572903) and 20 ng ml−1 IL-4 (BioLegend, 574004) (referred to as ‘moDCs’ (monocyte-derived dendritic cells)) for 7 days. PBMCs without GM-CSF treatment isolated at the same time were used as controls (referred to as ‘PBMCs w/o GM-CSF’). Prior to stimulation, cultured cells were detached with 2 mM EDTA in PBS and re-plated in flat-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 105 cells per well in 200 µl of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine and 1% penicillin−streptomycin and stimulated for 20–24 hours. If not otherwise indicated, cells were stimulated with 1 µg ml−1 LPS from Escherichia coli, serotype O55:B5 (TLRGRADE) (ready-to-use) (LPS; Enzo Life Sciences, ALX-581-013-L001), β-glucan (10 µg ml−1), alum (4 µg ml−1), mannan (10 µg ml−1), MA (10 µg ml−1 mannan together with 4 µg ml−1 alum) or saline as a vehicle control. Cell culture supernatants were collected to assess cytokine levels. To measure intracellular pro-IL-1β levels, cells were lysed in PBS by freezing/thawing, and the lysate was analyzed by ELISA.

ELISA for cytokines

Cytokine production was quantified from cell culture supernatants or lysates harvested after stimulation using the following kits according to the manufacturer’s protocol: ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Human IL-1β (BioLegend, 437004), ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Human IL-6 (BioLegend, 430504) and ELISA MAX Deluxe Set Human TNF-α (BioLegend, 430204).

Western blot

Western blot was performed using standard molecular biology techniques. Blots were probed using antibodies against p-STAT1 (Tyr701) (Cell Signaling Technology, 9167, clone 58D6), viperin (Cell Signaling Technology, 13996, clone D5T2X) and β-actin (Proteintech, 20536-1-AP, polyclonal, lot no. 00105345).

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality using the D’Agostino−Pearson test. If normality was not rejected at 0.05 significance level, data were analyzed using parametric tests. If normality was rejected and/or variances were different across groups, log transformation was performed to meet the criteria of parametric tests. Statistical differences between two or more groups in datasets with one categorical variable were evaluated by one-way ANOVA with correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical differences between groups in datasets with two categorical variables were evaluated by two-way ANOVA with correction for multiple comparisons. * or §, ** or §§, *** or §§§ and **** or §§§§ indicate P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001 and P < 0.0001, respectively.
