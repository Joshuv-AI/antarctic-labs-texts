Sums of the floor function related to class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields Thanks: Research supported in part by the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, Grant # 145628481
Marc Chamberland, Karl Dilcher

Abstract
. A curious identity of Bunyakovsky (1882), made more widely known by Pólya and Szegő in their “Problems and Theorems in Analysis”, gives an evaluation of a sum of the floor function of square roots involving primes p ≡ 1 ( mod 4 ) p\equiv 1\pmod{4} . We evaluate this sum also in the case p ≡ 3 ( mod 4 ) p\equiv 3\pmod{4} , obtaining an identity in terms of the class number of the imaginary quadratic field ℚ ⁡ ( − p ) {\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p}) . We also consider certain cases where the prime p p is replaced by a composite integer. Class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields are again involved in some cases.

1.
Introduction

The
floor function
, also known as the
greatest integer function
,
of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
is the integer
\lfloor{x}\rfloor
defined by

\lfloor{x}\rfloor\leq x<\lfloor{x}\rfloor+1
.
Sums involving the floor function have a long history and have been extensively
studied. One of the best known sums of this type is

(1.1)

\lfloor{x}\rfloor+\left\lfloor{x+\tfrac{1}{n}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor{x+\tfrac{2}{n}}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor{x+\tfrac{n-1}{n}}\right\rfloor=\lfloor{nx}\rfloor,

where
n
is a positive integer. The identity (
1.1
) is
due to Hermite
[
7
, p. 315]
; see also
[
6
, p. 85]
or

[
11
, Part 8, Problem 9]
.

A different type of such a sum, involving square roots, is

(1.2)

1+\lfloor{\sqrt{2}}\rfloor+\lfloor{\sqrt{3}}\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor{\sqrt{n-1}}\rfloor=na-\tfrac{1}{3}a(a+\tfrac{1}{2})(a+1),

where
a=\lfloor{\sqrt{n}}\rfloor
; see
[
6
, p. 87]
. A somewhat related
identity can be found in the famous two-volume “Problems and Theorems in
Analysis” by Pólya and Szegő
[
11
]
as Problem 20 in Part 8: if

p
is a prime of the form
4n+1
, then

(1.3)

\left\lfloor{\sqrt{p}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor{\sqrt{2p}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor{\sqrt{3p}}\right\rfloor+\cdots+\left\lfloor{\sqrt{\tfrac{p-1}{4}p}}\right\rfloor=\frac{p^{2}-1}{12}.

Pólya and Szegő attribute this identity to Bouniakowski
[
3
]

(better known as V. Ya. Bunyakovsky), and
in
[
11
]
it is the last of several exercises that use the technique of
counting lattice points. A different method was used by Shirali in
[
12
]
,
which has been the main inspiration for the current paper.

The identity (
1.3
) leads us to consider the arithmetic function

(1.4)

f(n):=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor{n/4}\rfloor}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor-\frac{n^{2}-1}{12}\qquad(n\in{\mathbb{N}}).

We can therefore rewrite (
1.3
) as follows.

Proposition 1.1

(Bouniakowski, 1882)
.

For any prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
we have

(1.5)

f(p)=0.

The function
f(n)
is the main object of study in this paper, and (
1.5
)
leads to a first obvious question: what can we say about
f(p)
for primes

p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
?

To explore this question, we list
f(p)

p<100

It is now quite clear how columns 3, 4 and 7, 8 relate to each other. In fact,
we can state the following result.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
p\geq 7
be a prime with
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Then

(1.6)

f(p)=\frac{1}{4}\big(1-p-2h(-p)\big),

where
h(-p)
is the class number of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p})
.

It is the purpose of this paper to prove Proposition
1.2
and other
identities of this type. One main tool is the connection between
f(n)

and quadratic residues that was established by Shirali
[
12
]
in a special
case. We generalize this in Section
2
to arbitrary
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

and obtain some initial elementary results in Section
3
.

A second important tool is a class number formula of Dirichlet. We introduce
it in Section
4
, where we also establish or quote specific
versions used in this paper. All this is then applied in Section
5

to obtain identities for
f(2p)
and
f(4p)
, where
p
is an odd prime, and
in Section
6
to arbitrary powers of all primes. We finish with some

.

2.
Connections with quadratic residues

In the process of providing an alternative proof of Bouniakowski’s result
(
1.5
), Shirali
[
12
]
established a connection with quadratic residues.
We extend this approach and set the stage for a connection with class
number formulas. Following
[
12
]
, we set

(2.1)

F(n):=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor{n/4}\rfloor}\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor\qquad(n\in{\mathbb{N}}),

and for integers
a\geq 0
,
b\geq 1
we denote by Rem
(a\div b)
the smallest
nonnegative remainder as
a
is divided by
b
. We are now ready to state and
prove the main connection between
F(n)
and quadratic residues.
In what follows, we always assume that
n
is a positive integer, and we write

(2.2)

n=4\nu+r,\quad 0\leq r\leq 3,\quad\hbox{so that}\quad\textstyle{\nu=\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor.}

We will also use the fact that any nonzero integer has a unique representation
as a product of a squarefree integer and a perfect square; see, e.g.,

[
9
, p. 29]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

(a)
With
n
as in (
2.2
), let
1\leq r\leq 3
and
n=P_{n}\cdot Q_{n}^{2}
,
with
P_{n}
squarefree. Then

(2.3)

F(n)=2\nu^{2}-\frac{\nu}{3n}\cdot\left(8\nu^{2}+6\nu+1\right)+\frac{1}{2}(Q_{n}-1)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n).

(b)
If
n=4\nu
, let
\nu=\overline{P}_{n}\cdot\overline{Q}_{n}^{2}
, with

\overline{P}_{n}
squarefree. Then

(2.4)

F(4\nu)=\tfrac{4}{3}\nu^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nu-\tfrac{1}{12}+\overline{Q}_{n}+\frac{1}{4\nu}\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4\nu).

Proposition
2.1
(b) simplifies if we consider the function
f(n)

defined by (
1.4
). The following can be obtained by straightforward
manipulation.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
be a positive integer. Then

(2.5)

f(n)=-\frac{n}{8}+\overline{Q}_{n}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n),

where
\frac{n}{4}=\overline{P}_{n}\cdot\overline{Q}_{n}^{2}
, with
\overline{P}_{n}

squarefree.

To prove Proposition
2.1
, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer, and denote

(2.6)

A(n):=\left|\left\{k:1\leq k\leq\textstyle{2\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor-1},n\mid k^{2}\right\}\right|.

Then the following is true:

(a)

If
4\nmid n
and
n=P\cdot Q^{2}
, where
P
is squarefree, then

A(n)=\frac{1}{2}(Q-1)
.

(b)

If
n=4\cdot P\cdot Q^{2}
, where
P
is squarefree, then
A(n)=Q-1
.

Proof.

(a) The condition
n\mid k^{2}
is equivalent to
k^{2}=mPQ^{2}
for some integer

m\geq 1
. Since
P
is squarefree, the fact that
P
divides
k^{2}
implies

P\mid k
, and thus
P\mid m
as well. We therefore get

(2.7)

\left(\frac{k}{P}\right)^{2}=\frac{m}{P}\cdot Q^{2},\quad\hbox{or}\quad\widetilde{k}^{2}=\widetilde{m}\cdot Q^{2},

with
\widetilde{k}:=k/P
and
\widetilde{m}:=m/P
. Furthermore, the second
identity in (
2.7
) implies that
\widetilde{m}
is also a square, and
thus
\widetilde{k}
is a multiple of
Q
. Hence

(2.8)

k\in\left\{PQ,2PQ,\ldots,\tfrac{Q-1}{2}\cdot PQ\right\},

where we claim that the last term in (
2.8
) is the largest one that
satisfies
k\leq 2\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor-1
. To see this, we note that

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor=\frac{PQ^{2}-r}{4},\qquad\hbox{and thus}\qquad 2\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor-1=\frac{PQ^{2}-r-2}{2},

where
1\leq r\leq 3
. Now we have

\tfrac{Q-1}{2}\cdot PQ\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(PQ^{2}-r-2\right)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad PQ\geq r+2,

and it is easy to check that the right-hand inequality holds for all
n\geq 5
.
Finally, it is obvious that
\frac{1}{2}(Q+1)PQ>2\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor-1
,
and by definition we have
A(n)=0
for
n=1,2,3
; this completes the proof
of part (a).

(b) This time we rewrite
n\mid k^{2}
as
k^{2}=4mPQ^{2}
, with some integer

m\geq 1
, and we proceed as before. In analogy to (
2.7
) we get

(2.9)

\left(\frac{k}{2P}\right)^{2}=\frac{m}{P}\cdot Q^{2},\quad\hbox{or}\quad\widetilde{k}^{2}=\widetilde{m}\cdot Q^{2},

with
\widetilde{k}:=k/(2P)
and
\widetilde{m}:=m/P
. The second identity in
(
2.9
) means that
\widetilde{m}
is also a square, and thus

\widetilde{k}
is a multiple of
Q
. So, in analogy to (
2.8
) we have

(2.10)

k\in\left\{2PQ,4PQ,\ldots,2(Q-1)PQ\right\},

where we claim that the last term in (
2.10
) is the largest one that
satisfies
k\leq 2\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor-1
. This time we note that

2\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\rfloor-1=2PQ^{2}-1
, and clearly

2(Q-1)PQ<2PQ^{2}-1<2Q\cdot PQ,

which proves the claim. Therefore, by (
2.10
),
A(n)=Q-1
, as desired.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We begin by dealing jointly with both cases. Adapting the main ideas in

[
12
]
, we fix an integer
k
and define
N_{n}(k)
to be the cardinality

(2.11)

N_{n}(k):=\left|\left\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}:1\leq j\leq\lfloor n/4\rfloor,\lfloor\sqrt{jn}\rfloor=k\right\}\right|.

With the goal of summing over all relevant
k
, we note
that
j\leq\nu
is equivalent to
k^{2}\leq n\nu
. Hence with (
2.2
),

(2.12)

k^{2}\leq n\nu=(4\nu+r)\nu<4\nu^{2}+4\nu<(2\nu+1)^{2},

so that
k\leq 2\nu
. Therefore (
2.1
) and (
2.11
) imply

(2.13)

F(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}k\cdot N_{n}(k).

First we evaluate
N_{n}(2\nu)
. By (
2.12
) we have

\lfloor\sqrt{\nu n}\rfloor=2\nu
, while

(\nu-1)n=(\nu-1)(4\nu+r)=4\nu^{2}-(4-r)\nu-r<4\nu^{2}

since
0\leq r\leq 3
, and thus
\lfloor\sqrt{(\nu-1)n}\rfloor=2\nu-1
.
Therefore

(2.14)

N_{n}(2\nu)=1.

We can now restrict our attention to
k\leq 2\nu-1
and note that

\left\lfloor{\sqrt{jn}}\right\rfloor=k
if and only if

(2.15)

k^{2}\leq jn<(k+1)^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{k^{2}}{n}\leq j<\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{n}.

From the right-hand relation in (
2.15
) we see that

(2.16)

N_{n}(k)=\left\lfloor{\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor+\delta_{n}(k),

where

\delta_{n}(k)=\begin{cases}-1&\hbox{when}\;n\mid(k+1)^{2},\\
0&\hbox{when}\;n\nmid k^{2}\;\hbox{and}\;n\nmid(k+1)^{2},\\
1&\hbox{when}\;n\mid k^{2}.\end{cases}

This, together with (
2.13
) and (
2.14
), gives

(2.17)

F(n)=2\nu+\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu-1}k\left(\left\lfloor{\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu-1}k\cdot\delta_{n}(k).

Let
S_{1}(n)
and
S_{2}(n)
be the first, resp. the second, sum on the right of
(
2.17
). Then we have

(2.18)

\displaystyle S_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu-1}\left(k\left\lfloor{\frac{(k+1)^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor-(k-1)\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor\ -\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=(2\nu-1)\left\lfloor{\frac{(2\nu)^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor-\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu-1}\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=2\nu\left\lfloor{\frac{(2\nu)^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor-\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor,

where we have used telescoping. Next we rewrite

(2.19)

S_{2}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
n\mid k^{2}\end{subarray}}^{2\nu-1}k-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
n\mid(k+1)^{2}\end{subarray}}^{2\nu-1}k.

To simplify this expression, we first note that whenever
k
occurs in the
first sum, then
k-1
occurs in the second sum. Conversely, the final term

k=2\nu-1
in the second sum in (
2.19
) may not correspond to a term in
the first sum. This happens exactly when
n\mid(2\nu)^{2}
, and since

n=4\nu+r
,
0\leq r\leq 3
, this is the case if and only if
r=0
. Hence with
(
2.19
) we have

(2.20)

\displaystyle S_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
n\mid k^{2}\end{subarray}}^{2\nu-1}1-\begin{cases}2\nu-1&\hbox{when}\;r=0,\\
0&\hbox{when}\;1\leq r\leq 3,\end{cases}

\displaystyle=A(n)-\begin{cases}2\nu-1&\hbox{when}\;r=0,\\
0&\hbox{when}\;1\leq r\leq 3,\end{cases}

where we have used (
2.6
).

To put everything together, we begin with
r=0
, noting that

\lfloor(2\nu)^{2}/n\rfloor=\nu
. Then (
2.18
), (
2.20
), and
Lemma
2.3
(b), combined with (
2.17
), give

(2.21)

F(4\nu)=2\nu+2\nu^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor+\overline{Q}_{n}-1-(2\nu-1).

When
1\leq r\leq 3
, then it is easy to see with (
2.2
) that

\lfloor(2\nu)^{2}/n\rfloor=\nu-1
. In this case, (
2.18
), (
2.20
),
and Lemma
2.3
(a), combined with (
2.17
), give

(2.22)

F(n)=2\nu+2\nu(\nu-1)-\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor+\frac{Q_{n}-1}{2}.

By division with remainder we have

(2.23)

k^{2}=\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor\cdot n+{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n),

and thus

(2.24)

\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\left\lfloor{\frac{k^{2}}{n}}\right\rfloor=\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\frac{k^{2}}{n}-\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\frac{{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n)}{n}.

Using the well-known identity for sums of consecutive squares, we get

(2.25)

\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}\frac{k^{2}}{n}=\frac{1}{6n}\cdot 2\nu(2\nu+1)(4\nu+1)=\nu\cdot\frac{8\nu^{2}+6\nu+1}{3n}.

Substituting this last identity into (
2.21
) and (
2.22
), we obtain
after some straightforward manipulations the desired identities (
2.4
)
and (
2.3
), respectively.
∎

3.
First consequences of Proposition
2.1

As a special case of Proposition
2.1
(a) we consider
n=p
, where
p

is an odd prime. We first recall a few basic facts about quadratic residues.
For a prime
p>2
and an integer
a
with

p\nmid a
, the number
a
is said to be a
quadratic residue
modulo
p

if there is an integer
k
such that
k^{2}\equiv a\pmod{p}
; in this case we
write
a\in QR(p)
. When
p\nmid a
, the
Legendre symbol
is defined by

(3.1)

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{when}\;\;a\in QR(p),\\
-1&\hbox{when}\;\;a\not\in QR(p);\end{cases}

this is supplemented by
(\frac{a}{p})=0
when
p|a
.
We can now prove the following connection between (
2.3
) and the Legendre
symbol.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any prime
p\geq 3
we have

(3.2)

\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)=\frac{p(p-1)}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right).

Proof.

By definition of the Legendre symbol we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in QR(p)}j-\sum_{j\not\in QR(p)}j=2\sum_{j\in QR(p)}j-\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)-\frac{p(p-1)}{2},

and (
3.2
) follows immediately.
∎

We can now obtain a proof of Proposition
1.1
. We consider
Lemma
3.1
for
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and use the well-known fact that

(3.3)

\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)=0\qquad(p\equiv 1\pmod{4}).

The identity (
3.3
) can be shown, for instance, by noting that in this
case we have
(\frac{p-j}{p})=(\frac{j}{p})
, and by changing the direction
of summation we get

(3.4)

\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}(p-j)\left(\frac{p-j}{p}\right)=p\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right).

The first sum on the right is 0 since there are as many quadratic residues as
nonresidues, and then (
3.4
) implies (
3.3
).

Now (
3.3
), together with (
3.2
) and (
2.3
) with
Q_{p}=1
,
leads to
F(p)=(p^{2}-1)/12
. This is, basically, the idea behind
Shirali’s proof in
[
12
]
of Proposition
1.1
.

Since (
3.3
) does not hold for
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, Lemma
3.1

will be different in this case; we will deal with this in the next section.

Quite surprisingly, Proposition
1.1
remains true when we replace the
prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with a product of distinct primes of this form. We
will see that this, and a related identity, are consequences of a result by
Shirali. We begin with a lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any odd integer
n\geq 3
we have

(3.5)

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2n)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n).

Proof.

We claim that of the smallest nonnegative remainders of
k^{2}
and
(n-k)^{2}

modulo
2n
, one is
<n
, while the other is
>n
.
Indeed, suppose that some
k
with
1\leq k\leq n-1
is such that

k^{2}=2ns+a
,
1\leq a\leq n-1
, for some integer
s
. Then

(n-k)^{2}\equiv n^{2}+k^{2}\equiv n^{2}+a=2n\cdot\tfrac{n-1}{2}+n+a\equiv n+a\pmod{2n},

which proves the claim. So there are as many remainders modulo
2n
that are

>n
as are
<n
, namely
\frac{n-1}{2}
, and the excess in the sum is

\frac{n-1}{2}\cdot n
, while obviously
(n-k)^{2}\equiv k^{2}\pmod{n}
. This proves
the identity (
3.5
).
∎

We now state Shirali’s result as another lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(Shirali
[
12
]
)
.

Let
n
be a product of primes that are congruent to
1\pmod{4}
, not
necessarily distinct, and write
n=P_{n}\cdot Q_{n}^{2}
, with
P_{n}
squarefree. Then

(3.6)

\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div P)=\frac{n(n-Q_{n})}{4}.

Proposition 3.4
.

With
n
as in Lemma
3.3
, we have

(3.7)

f(n)=\frac{1}{4}\left(Q_{n}-1\right)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad f(2n)=\frac{1}{4}\left(Q_{n}-1-n\right).

In particular, if
n
is squarefree, then

(3.8)

f(n)=0\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad f(2n)=-\frac{n}{4}.

Proof.

To obtain the first identity in (
3.7
), we use (
2.3
) and note that

\nu=(n-1)/4
. Then with (
1.1
) we get after some easy manipulations,

f(n)=\frac{1}{2}Q_{n}-\frac{n+1}{4}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n),

and with (
3.6
) we get the first identity in (
3.7
).

Next we replace
n
by
2n
in (
2.3
) and note that
\nu=(n-1)/2
. Then
with (
1.1
) we get

(3.9)

f(2n)=\frac{1}{2}Q_{n}-\frac{3n}{4}+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2n).

Now, with (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2n)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n)=n\left(n-\frac{Q_{n}+1}{2}\right);

this, combined with (
3.9
), gives the second identity in (
3.7
).
Finally, when
n
is squarefree then
Q_{n}=1
, and (
3.8
) follows
immediately from (
3.7
).
∎

4.
Class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields

To set the stage, we recall a few basic facts from elementary and algebraic
number theory; for further details we refer the reader to any introduction to
algebraic number theory, for instance
[
1
]
. A
quadratic field
is a
field extension of the rationals of the form
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{n})
, where

n\neq 0
is a squarefree integer. When
n>0
, the field is called
real
,
otherwise
imaginary
. The
discriminant

d
of

{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{n})
is given by

(4.1)

d=\begin{cases}n&\hbox{when}\;\;n\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
4n&\hbox{when}\;\;n\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

When
d<0
, the number of units
w(d)
in the ring of integers of

{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{n})
is 2 when
d<-4
, while
w(-3)=6
and
w(-4)=4
.

Next we recall two extensions of the Legendre symbol. First,
the
Jacobi symbol
extends (
3.1
) as follows: if

m=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}
, where
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}
are odd primes,
then

\left(\frac{a}{m}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(\frac{a}{p_{j}}\right)^{\alpha_{j}}.

The
Kronecker symbol
extends this further by

\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{2}{a}\right)&a\;\;\hbox{odd},\\
0&a\;\;\hbox{even};\end{cases}\qquad\left(\frac{a}{-1}\right)=\begin{cases}1&a>0,\\
-1&a<0;\end{cases}\qquad

see, e.g.,
[
4
, p. 36]
. Now we can state Dirichlet’s class number
formula for imaginary quadratic number fields, as given in
[
1
, p. 322]
.
See also
[
2
, p. 342ff.]
, where a proof can be found.

Theorem 4.1

(Dirichlet, 1839)
.

Let
K
be a quadratic number field with discriminant
d<0
. Then the class
number of
K
is

(4.2)

h(K)=\frac{-w(d)}{2|d|}\sum_{j=1}^{|d|-1}j\left(\frac{d}{j}\right),

where
(\frac{d}{j})
is the Kronecker symbol.

In this paper we are mainly interested in quadratic fields of the form

{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p})
, where
p
is an odd prime. Due to the discriminant
identity (
4.1
), it is convenient to distinguish between two cases.
In what follows we use the notation
h(-p)=h(K)
when

K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p})
.
It is quite likely that the identities in Corollary
4.2

can be found in the literature.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
we have

(4.3)

h(-p)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

and equivalently

(4.4)

h(-p)=\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

where
(\frac{j}{p})
is the Legendre symbol defined by (
3.1
).

Proof.

When
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then by (
4.1
) we have
d=-4p
. Using
properties of the Kronecker and Jacobi symbols, not all listed above (such as
quadratic reciprocity), we have
(\frac{-4p}{j})=0
when
j
is even, while

\left(\frac{-4p}{j}\right)=\left(\frac{-4}{j}\right)\left(\frac{p}{j}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{j}\right)\left(\frac{p}{j}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)\qquad(j\;\hbox{odd}).

Since
w(d)=2
, (
4.2
) now gives

(4.5)

\displaystyle h(-p)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{4p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{4p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{4p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}\left((-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)+(-1)^{\frac{2p+j-1}{2}}(2p+j)\left(\frac{2p+j}{p}\right)\right).

We note that

(-1)^{\frac{2p+j-1}{2}}=-(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\left(\frac{2p+j}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

so (
4.5
) simplifies to

h(-p)=\frac{-1}{4p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}(-2p)\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

which immediately gives (
4.3
).

To obtain (
4.4
), we sum the last term in (
4.5
) differently, namely

(4.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}

\displaystyle(-1)^{\frac{2p+j-1}{2}}(2p+j)\left(\frac{2p+j}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}(2p-j)\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{2p-j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{2p-j}{p}\right),

where we have reversed the order of summation in the last sum. Now

\left(\frac{2p-j}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-j}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

by the first complementary law of quadratic reciprocity, since

p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Furthermore,

(-1)^{\frac{2p-j-1}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}\quad\hbox{since}\quad\frac{2p-j-1}{2}-\frac{j-1}{2}=p-j\equiv 0\pmod{2}

because both
p
and
j
are odd. Hence with (
4.6
),

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{2p+j-1}{2}}(2p+j)\left(\frac{2p+j}{p}\right)=-3\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

and combining this with (
4.5
), we get (
4.4
).
∎

The next corollary is well known and is attributed to Jacobi; see, e.g.,

[
5
, Ch. 6]
. For the sake of completeness we show how it follows from
Dirichlet’s formula.

Corollary 4.3
.

For any prime
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
,
p\neq 3
, we have

(4.7)

h(-p)=-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

where
(\frac{j}{p})
is the Legendre symbol defined by (
3.1
).

Proof.

When
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then by (
4.1
) we have
d=-p
, and when

p\neq 3
, then
w(d)=2
. Furthermore, using Proposition 2.2.6 in

[
4
, p. 36]
, we have the Kronecker symbol identities

\left(\frac{d}{j}\right)=\left(\frac{-p}{j}\right)=\left(\frac{j}{-p}\right)=\left(\frac{j}{-1}\right)\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{j}{p}\right).

This, together with (
4.2
), yields (
4.7
).
∎

As a first application of Corollary
4.3
we derive the identity
(
1.6
).

Proof of Proposition
1.2
.

We use (
2.3
) with
n=p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, so that
\nu=\frac{p-3}{4}
. Then

(4.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)-{\rm Rem}((\tfrac{p-1}{2})^{2}\div p)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(p-1)}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)-\frac{p+1}{4},

where we have used Lemma
3.1
and the fact that

(4.9)

\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{p+1}{4}=p\cdot\frac{p-3}{4}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Hence with the class number formula (
4.7
), the identity (
4.8
)
becomes

\sum_{k=1}^{2\nu}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n)=\frac{p^{2}-2p-1}{4}-\frac{p}{2}\cdot h(-p).

Finally, we substitute this into (
2.3
) and recall that

f(p)=F(p)-(p^{2}-1)/12
; then we get the desired identity (
1.6
) after some
straightforward manipulations, using again the fact that
Q_{p}=1
.
∎

5.
Evaluating
f(2p)
and
f(4p)

Before proceeding to more general arguments of the function
f(n)
, we consider

n=2p
and
n=4p
, where
p
is an odd prime. The case
n=2p
turns out to be
quite straightforward and is based on Lemma
3.2
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any prime
p\geq 3
we have

(5.1)

f(2p)=-\frac{p}{4}+\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right),

and in particular, for
p\geq 5
,

(5.2)

f(2p)=\begin{cases}-\frac{p}{4}&\hbox{when}\;\;p\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
-\frac{p}{4}-\frac{1}{2}h(-p)&\hbox{when}\;\;p\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We use (
2.3
) with
n=2p
and note that
Q_{2p}=1
. Then

\nu=\frac{p-1}{2}
, and with (
3.5
) for
n=p
we get after some easy
manipulations,

f(2p)=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{p}{2}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p).

This, with Lemma
3.1
, gives (
5.1
).

When
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, we already saw in (
3.3
) that the sum on the
right of (
5.1
) vanishes, which leads to the first part of (
5.2
). For

p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the class number formula (
4.7
) immediately gives the
second part of (
5.2
).
∎

The first part of (
5.2
) is actually a special case of (
3.8
).
We now turn to the more challenging determination of
f(4p)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For any prime
p\geq 5
we have

(5.3)

f(4p)=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{p}{2}-\delta(p)h(-p),

where

\delta(p)=\begin{cases}1/2,&p\equiv 1\pmod{4},\\
2,&p\equiv 3\pmod{8},\\
1,&p\equiv 7\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

We prove this through a sequence of lemmas which may be of interest in their
own rights.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, let

(5.4)

S_{1}(p):=\sum_{j\in A_{1}(p)}j,\qquad S_{3}(p):=\sum_{j\in A_{3}(p)}j,

where
A_{r}(p):=\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid j\leq 2p-1,j\equiv r\pmod{4},(\frac{j}{p})=1\}
. Then

(5.5)

h(-p)=\frac{1}{p}\left(S_{1}(p)-S_{3}(p)\right).

Proof.

Using the definition of the Legendre symbol, we can rewrite

S_{1}(p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\equiv 1(4)\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}\frac{j}{2}\left(\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)+1\right)-\frac{p}{2},\qquad S_{3}(p)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=3\\
j\equiv 3(4)\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}\frac{j}{2}\left(\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)+1\right),

where in the case of
S_{1}(p)
we needed to subtract
p/2
since

(\frac{j}{p})=0
for
j=p
. These two identities then combine to give

(5.6)

\frac{S_{1}(p)-S_{3}(p)}{p}=\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j.

The first sum on the right is the right-hand side of (
4.4
), while the
second sum can be evaluated by splitting it into two:

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}j+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{2p-j+1}{2}}(2p-j)=\frac{2p}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\,odd\end{subarray}}^{2p-1}(-1)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}=p.

This and (
4.4
) substituted into (
5.6
) then gives (
5.5
).
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
be a prime. Then

(5.7)

h(-p)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\,odd\end{subarray}}^{p-1}\left(2p-{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)\right).

Proof.

We will show that the summands on the right of (
5.7
) are exactly those of

S_{1}(p)
and
S_{3}(p)
in the previous lemma. For greater ease of notation we
set
a_{k}:={\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)
. By definition,
k^{2}\equiv a_{k}\pmod{4p}
, which
implies
k^{2}\equiv a_{k}\pmod{p}
, and so
a_{k}\in QR(p)
. If we set
r_{k}:=2p-a_{k}
,
then

\left(\frac{r_{k}}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{2p-a_{k}}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{a_{k}}{p}\right)=1,

since
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
; therefore
r_{k}\in QR(p)
as well. Next, by
definition, we have
a_{k}=k^{2}+4pm\equiv 1\pmod{4}
for odd
k
and some

m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Since
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, this implies

r_{k}=2p-a_{k}\equiv 2-1=1\pmod{4}
.

Furthermore, we claim that the summands
r_{k}
are distinct for

k=1,3,5,\ldots,p-2
. Indeed, if
r_{j}\equiv r_{k}\pmod{4p}
, then

j^{2}\equiv k^{2}\pmod{4p}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 4p\mid(j-k)(j+k).

Now
j+k
is even,
j+k\in\{2,4,6,\ldots,2p-4\}
, and thus
p\nmid j+k
.
Hence
p\mid j-k
. But
1\leq j,k\leq p-2
, and therefore
j=k
, as claimed.

We have thus shown that the summands
r_{k}
consist of
\frac{p-1}{2}
odd
integers between
-2p+1
and
2p-1
, all are congruent to 1 (mod 4), and they
are all quadratic residues modulo
p
. But these are exactly the elements

j\in S_{1}(p)
and
-k
, where
k\in S_{3}(p)
. Hence (
5.5
) implies
(
5.7
).
∎

In the next lemma we remove the restriction “
k
odd” from the summation.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
be a prime. Then

(5.8)

h(-p)=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\left(2p-{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)\right).

Proof.

Comparing (
5.8
) with (
5.7
), it remains to be shown that the sum over
all even
k
in (
5.8
) vanishes. Setting
k=2j
in this sum and dividing
both sides by 4, we see that this is equivalent to

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\frac{p}{2}-{\rm Rem}(j^{2}\div p)\right)=0,\quad\hbox{or}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(j^{2}\div p)=\frac{p(p-1)}{4}.

But this last identity follows from (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
be a prime,
p\neq 3
. Then

(5.9)

\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)=p-\frac{3}{2}+\big(\varepsilon(p)-2\big)h(-p),

where
\varepsilon(p)=0
when
p\equiv 3\pmod{8}
and
\varepsilon(p)=1

when
p\equiv 7\pmod{8}
.

Proof.

Combining (
3.2
) with (
4.7
) and using symmetry on the left of
(
3.2
), we get

(5.10)

\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)=\frac{p(p-1)}{2}-p\cdot h(-p)\qquad(p\equiv 3\pmod{4}),

where
p>3
is a prime. Hence we are done if we can evaluate the expression

(5.11)

S(p):=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)-4\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p).

To do so, we first note that Rem
((2k)^{2}\div 4p)=4\cdot{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)
,
so that

(5.12)

\displaystyle S(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}((2k-1)^{2}\div 4p)-4\sum_{k=\frac{p+1}{2}}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}((p-2k)^{2}\div 4p)-4\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p),

where in both sums we have reversed the order of summation.
Next, since
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we have
p^{2}\equiv 3p\pmod{4p}
, and thus

(5.13)

(p-2k)^{2}=p^{2}-4pk+4k^{2}\equiv 3p+4k^{2}\pmod{4p}.

When Rem
(4k^{2}\div 4p)<p
, which is equivalent to Rem
(k^{2}\div p)<\frac{p}{4}
,
then (
5.13
) gives

(5.14)

{\rm Rem}((p-2k)^{2}\div 4p)-4\cdot{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)=3p.

On the other hand, when
p<{\rm Rem}(4k^{2}\div 4p)<4p
, then we have

(p-2k)^{2}={\rm Rem}(4k^{2}\div 4p)-p
, and thus

(5.15)

{\rm Rem}((p-2k)^{2}\div 4p)-4\cdot{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)=-p.

Now, the number of
k
,
1\leq k\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
, for which
Rem
(k^{2}\div p)<\frac{p}{4}
, is exactly the number
N
of quadratic residues
between 1 and
p/4
, that is,

N=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
(\frac{j}{p})=1\end{subarray}}^{\lfloor p/4\rfloor}1=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-3}{4}}\left(\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)+1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-3}{4}}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)+\frac{p-3}{8}.

The sum of the Legendre symbols on the right is known to be

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{p-3}{4}}\left(\frac{j}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}0,&p\equiv 3\pmod{8},\\
h(-p),&p\equiv 7\pmod{8};\end{cases}

see, for instance, the particularly well-organized tables in
[
8
]
. Hence

(5.16)

N=\frac{p-3}{8}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(p)h(-p),

with
\varepsilon(p)
as defined after (
5.9
). Using the definition of

N
together with (
5.12
), (
5.14
) and (
5.15
), we get

(5.17)

S(p)=3pN-p\left(\frac{p-1}{2}-N\right)=4pN-\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\ =-p+2p\varepsilon(p)h(-p),

where we have used (
5.16
). Finally, combining (
5.11
) with
(
5.17
) and (
5.10
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)

\displaystyle=-p+2p\varepsilon(p)h(-p)+4\left(\frac{p(p-1)}{2}-ph(-p)\right)

\displaystyle=2p^{2}-3p+2p\big(\varepsilon(p)-2)h(-p),

and upon dividing everything by
2p
we get (
5.9
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

By (
1.4
) and (
2.4
) with
\nu=p
, and noting that

\overline{Q}_{p}=1
, we have

(5.18)

f(4p)=1-\frac{p}{2}+\frac{1}{4p}\sum_{k=1}^{2p}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p).

Clearly,
{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)=0
for
k=2p
. Furthermore, if

p\equiv r\pmod{4}
, then

p^{2}=4p\frac{p-r}{4}+rp\equiv p\pmod{4p}
, and we have Rem
(p^{2}\div 4p)=rp
. We
also have
(2p-k)^{2}\equiv k^{2}\pmod{4p}
, and thus we can rewrite (
5.18
) as

(5.19)

f(4p)=1+\frac{r}{4}-\frac{p}{2}+\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p).

Finally, we rewrite (
5.8
) as

\frac{1}{2p}\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4p)=p-1-\frac{1}{2}h(-p),\qquad(p\equiv 1\pmod{4}).

Then this and (
5.9
), substituted into (
5.19
), gives the desired
identity (
5.3
).
∎

We observe that for a fixed prime
p
, the sequence

\{f(p^{\alpha})\}_{\alpha\geq 1}
exhibits very different behaviour, depending
on whether
p=2
,
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, or
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.
An explanation is provided by the following main result of this section.

Proposition 6.1
.

(a)
For an integer
\beta\geq 1
we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle f(2^{2\beta})

\displaystyle=2^{2\beta-3}-2^{\beta-2}+\frac{3}{4},

(6.2)

\displaystyle f(2^{2\beta+1})

\displaystyle=2^{2\beta-2}-2^{\beta-1}+\frac{3}{4}.

(b)
When
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
\beta\geq 1
, then

(6.3)

f(p^{2\beta})=f(p^{2\beta+1})=\frac{1}{4}\left(p^{\beta}-1\right).

(c)
When
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
\beta\geq 1
, then

(6.4)

\displaystyle f(p^{2\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(p^{\beta}-1\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{p-1}h^{*}(-p)\right),

(6.5)

\displaystyle f(p^{2\beta+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{4}\left(p^{\beta+1}-1\right)\left(p^{\beta}+\frac{2}{p-1}h^{*}(-p)\right),

where
h^{*}(-p)=h(-p)
when
p\geq 7
, and
h^{*}(-3)=1/3
.

The second part of (
6.3
) and the identity (
6.5
) also hold for

\beta=0
; in this case we recover Propositions
1.1

and
1.2
, respectively.

In view of Proposition
2.1
it is clear that the main ingredient
in the proof of Proposition
6.1
would be the evaluation of the
respective sums of remainders on the right of (
2.4
) or (
2.6
).
In fact, the following identities hold.

Lemma 6.2
.

(a)
For any integer
\beta\geq 1
we have

(6.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2\beta-1}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div 2^{2\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2\beta-2}\left(2^{2\beta}-3\cdot 2^{\beta}+3\right),

(6.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2\beta}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2\beta-1}\left(2^{2\beta+1}-4\cdot 2^{\beta}+3\right).

(b)
If
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
is a prime, then

(6.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p^{2\beta}-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{2\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}p^{3\beta}\left(p^{\beta}-1\right)\qquad(\beta\geq 1),

(6.9)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p^{2\beta+1}-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{2\beta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}p^{3\beta+1}\left(p^{\beta+1}-1\right)\qquad(\beta\geq 0).

(c)
If
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
is a prime, then for
\beta\geq 1
, resp.
\beta\geq 0
,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p^{2\beta}-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{2\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}p^{2\beta}\left(p^{\beta}-1\right)\left(p^{\beta}-\frac{2}{p-1}h^{*}(-p)\right),

(6.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p^{2\beta+1}-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{2\beta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}p^{2\beta+1}\left(p^{\beta+1}-1\right)\left(p^{\beta}-\frac{2}{p-1}h^{*}(-p)\right).

With this lemma it is now straightforward, though tedious, to obtain

Proof of Proposition

.

(a) By definition of
\overline{Q}_{n}
in Corollary

we have

\overline{Q}_{2^{2\beta}}=\overline{Q}_{2^{2\beta+1}}=2^{\beta-1}.

We substitute this and (

), resp. (
6.7
), into (

) and
obtain (
6.1
), resp. (
6.2
).

(b) When
n=p^{2\beta}
, resp.

, we have

\nu=(p^{2\beta}-1)/4
, resp.
\nu=(p^{2\beta+1}-1)/4
, and

in both cases. Then (
2.3
) and (
1.4
), together with (
6.8
),
resp. (
6.9
), give both parts of (

).

(c) This is very similar to part (b), except that for

we have

\nu=(p^{2\beta+1}-3)/4

(6.12)

For the proof of Lemma
6.2
we require some auxiliary results,
collected in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
p
be a prime.
(a)
For any integers
k\geq 1
and
\alpha\geq 1
we have

(6.13)

{\rm Rem}\left((kp)^{2}\div p^{\alpha+2}\right)=p^{2}\cdot{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{\alpha}\right).

(b)
For fixed integers
r
,
1\leq r\leq p-1
, and
\alpha\geq 0
, the
following remainders are distinct:

(6.14)

{\rm Rem}\left((jp+r)^{2}\div p^{\alpha+2}\right),\qquad 0\leq j\leq p^{\alpha+1}-1.

(c)
For
\alpha\geq 2
we have

(6.15)

{\rm Rem}((2^{\alpha-1}-k)^{2}\div 2^{\alpha})={\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2^{\alpha}),\quad 0\leq k\leq 2^{\alpha-1}.

(d)
For any integers
\ell\geq 0
and
\alpha\geq 1
we have

(6.16)

{\rm Rem}((2\ell+1)^{2}\div 2^{\alpha+2})\equiv 1\pmod{8}.

Proof.

(a) By division with remainder we have

{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p^{\alpha})=k^{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{p^{\alpha}}\right\rfloor\cdot 2^{\alpha}

for any integer
k
. Using this twice, we get

\displaystyle{\rm Rem}((kp)^{2}\div p^{\alpha+2})

\displaystyle=(kp)^{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{(kp)^{2}}{p^{\alpha+2}}\right\rfloor\cdot 2^{\alpha+2}

\displaystyle=p^{2}\cdot\left(k^{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{k^{2}}{p^{\alpha}}\right\rfloor\cdot 2^{\alpha}\right)=p^{2}\cdot{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p^{\alpha}),

as claimed.

(b) Suppose that two of the terms in (
6.14
) are identical. Then

p^{\alpha+2}
divides

(jp+r)^{2}-(kp+r)^{2}=((j+k)p+2r)\cdot(j-k)\cdot p,

which means that

p^{\alpha+1}\left|((j+k)p+2r)\cdot(j-k).\right.

Since
1\leq r\leq p-1
, we have gcd
((j+k)p+2r,p)=1
, and so

p^{\alpha+1}\mid j-k
. This, by the condition in (
6.14
), can only happen
when
j=k
, which completes the proof of (b).

(c) We expand
\left(2^{\alpha-1}-k\right)^{2}=2^{2\alpha-2}-2^{\alpha}\cdot k+k^{2}\equiv k^{2}\pmod{2^{\alpha}}
,
where the congruence holds when
2\alpha-2\geq 2
, which is equivalent to

\alpha\geq 2
. This proves part (c).

(d) We use the fact that
(2\ell+1)^{2}=4\ell(\ell+1)+1\equiv 1\pmod{8}
. But
also, division with remainder gives

(2\ell+1)^{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{(2\ell+1)^{2}}{2^{\alpha+2}}\right\rfloor\cdot 2^{\alpha+2}+{\rm Rem}((2\ell+1)^{2}\div 2^{\alpha+2}).

Since
\alpha\geq 1
, this implies the congruence (
6.16
).
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

(a) We prove (

. When
\beta=1
, then

\sum_{k=1}^{2}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 4)=1+0
, while on the right of (

; this is the induction beginning.

For the induction step, we use symmetry (Lemma

(c)) to obtain

(6.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2\beta+1}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2})=2\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2\beta}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2})

\displaystyle\qquad=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{2^{2\beta-1}}{\rm Rem}((2\ell)^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2})+2\sum_{\ell=0}^{2^{2\beta-1}-1}{\rm Rem}((2\ell+1)^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2}).

We deal with the sums in the second row of (
6.17
) separately. First, by
(
6.13
) we have

(6.18)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{2^{2\beta-1}}{\rm Rem}((2\ell)^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2})=4\sum_{\ell=1}^{2^{2\beta-1}}{\rm Rem}(\ell^{2}\div 2^{2\beta})=2^{2\beta}\left(2^{2\beta}-3\cdot 2^{\beta}+3\right),

where we have used (
6.6
) as induction hypothesis.

To deal with the second sum on the right of (
6.17
), we note that by parts

the

summands are distinct

. Since

, these summands are not reduced

, and we have

(6.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{2^{2\beta-1}-1}{\rm Rem}((2\ell+1)^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2^{2\beta-1}-1}(8j+1)

\displaystyle=2^{2\beta-1}+8\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(2^{2\beta-1}-1\right)2^{2\beta-1}

\displaystyle=2^{2\beta-1}\left(2^{2\beta+1}-3\right).

Finally, substituting (
6.18
) and (
6.19
) into (

), we get
after some straightforward manipulations,

\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2\beta+1}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div 2^{2\beta+2})=2^{2\beta}\left(2^{2\beta+2}-3\cdot 2^{\beta+1}+3\right).

This completes the proof of (
6.6
) by induction. The proof of (
6.7

is analogous; we leave the details to the reader.

(b) We begin with (
6.9
), using again induction on
\beta
. For
\beta=0
,

) reduces to

(6.20)

\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div p)=\frac{1}{4}p(p-1),

which is true by (

) and (

). We now assume that (
6.9
)
holds for some
\beta\geq 0
. Using the fact that for any integer

we have the symmetry relation

\left(p^{\alpha}-k\right)^{2}=p^{2\alpha}-2kp^{\alpha}+k^{2}\equiv k^{2}\pmod{p^{\alpha}},

we can write

(6.21)

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{p^{2\beta+3}-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{2\beta+3}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p^{2\beta+3}-1}{\rm Rem}\left(k^{2}\div p^{2\beta+3}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{p-1}\sum_{j=0}^{p^{2\beta+2}-1}{\rm Rem}\left((jp+r)^{2}\div p^{2\beta+3}\right).

We will now evaluate the inner sum on the right of (
6.21
). When
r=0
,
then (
6.13
) gives

(6.22)

\sum_{j=0}^{p^{2\beta+2}-1}{\rm Rem}\left((jp)^{2}\div p^{2\beta+3}\right)=p^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{p^{2\beta+2}-1}{\rm Rem}\left(j^{2}\div p^{2\beta+1}\right).

We split

as

, with

and

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p^{2\beta+2}-1}{\rm Rem}\left(j^{2}\div p^{2\beta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}\sum_{s=0}^{p^{2\beta+1}-1}{\rm Rem}\left(s^{2}\div p^{2\beta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2p\sum_{s=0}^{\frac{p^{2\beta+1}-1}{2}}{\rm Rem}\left(s^{2}\div p^{2\beta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2p\cdot\frac{1}{4}p^{3\beta+1}\left(p^{\beta+1}-1\right),

where we have used symmetry and then (

7.
Some conjectures and remarks

The results in this paper so far give rise to the question: What can we say
about
f(n)
when
n
has two or more distinct prime factors, at least one of
which is of the form
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
? With Propositions
3.4

and
6.1
in mind, the next step would be the case

n=p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}
, where one or both of
p
and
q
are
\equiv 3\pmod{4}

and
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
.

Extensive computations led us to formulate the following conjectures. For the
sake of simplicity and in view of Proposition
2.1
, we state some
conjectured identities only for the sum

S_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\rm Rem}(k^{2}\div n).

As we did in Proposition
6.1
, we set
h^{*}(-p)=h(-p)
when

p\geq 7
, and
h^{*}(-3)=1/3
.

Conjecture 7.1
.

Let
n=p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}

both primes and
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
. Then

(7.1)

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor}q^{\lfloor\frac{\beta}{2}\rfloor}}{2}-\frac{q^{\lfloor\frac{\beta+1}{2}\rfloor}-1}{q-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\left(\frac{p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha+2}{2}\rfloor}-1}{p-1}-\frac{p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor}-1}{p-1}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\right)h^{*}(-q)-\frac{p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\rfloor}-1}{p-1}h^{*}(-pq)\right).

Conjecture 7.2
.

Let
n=p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}
with
p\equiv q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
distinct primes and
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
. Then

(7.2)

\displaystyle S_{n}=\frac{n-p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor}q^{\lfloor\frac{\beta}{2}\rfloor}}{2}

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{q^{\lfloor\frac{\beta}{2}\rfloor}-1}{q-1}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)+\frac{q^{\lfloor\frac{\beta}{2}\rfloor+1}-1}{q-1}\right)\cdot\frac{p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\rfloor}-1}{p-1}h^{*}(-p)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor}-1}{p-1}\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)+\frac{p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor+1}-1}{p-1}\right)\cdot\frac{q^{\lfloor\frac{\beta+1}{2}\rfloor}-1}{q-1}h^{*}(-q).

In the special case
\alpha=\beta=1
, (
7.2
) becomes

(7.3)

S_{pq}=\frac{pq-1}{2}-h^{*}(-p)-h^{*}(-q)\qquad(p\equiv q\equiv 3\pmod{4}\quad\hbox{distinct}),

and Proposition
2.1
and (
1.4
) give, after some effort,

(7.4)

f(pq)=-\frac{h^{*}(-p)+h^{*}(-q)}{2}\qquad(p\equiv q\equiv 3\pmod{4}\quad\hbox{distinct}).

The next conjecture concerns all squarefree odd positive integers and can be
seen as the opposite extreme of Proposition
6.1
.

Conjecture 7.3
.

Let
n
be an odd squarefree positive integer.

(a)
If
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then

(7.5)

f(n)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d\equiv 3(\rm{mod}\,4)\end{subarray}}h^{*}(-d).

(b)
If
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then

(7.6)

f(n)=\frac{1-n}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d\equiv 3(\rm{mod}\,4)\end{subarray}}h^{*}(-d).

Special cases of this conjecture include Proposition
1.1
, the first
identity of (
3.8
), and (
7.4
).
The two identities (
7.5
) and (
7.6
) can be written jointly as

(7.7)

\frac{1-n}{4}\cdot\delta(n)-f(n)=\sum_{d|n}h^{*}(-d)\cdot\frac{\delta(d)}{2},

where
n
is a squarefree odd positive integer and

(7.8)

\delta(n)=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{if}\;n\equiv 3\pmod{4},\\
0&\hbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We now extend the conjectured identity (
7.7
) to all positive integers

n
by defining
\mathcal{F}(n)
to be the left-hand side of (
7.7
) when

n
is squarefree and odd, and to be the right-hand side of (
7.7
)
otherwise. Using the Möbius inversion formula, we then get

(7.9)

h^{*}(-n)\cdot\frac{\delta(n)}{2}=\sum_{d|n}\mu(\tfrac{n}{d})\mathcal{F}(d).

When
n
is squarefree and odd, then so are all divisors
d
of
n

and for
\mathcal{F}(d)
we can
use the left-hand side of (
7.7
). The identity (
7.9
) therefore
shows that the truth of Conjecture
7.3
implies that the following
is also true.

Conjecture 7.4
.

Let
n
be an odd squarefree positive integer. Then

(7.10)

h^{*}(-n)\cdot\delta(n)=\sum_{d|n}\mu(\tfrac{n}{d})\left(\frac{1-d}{2}\cdot\delta(d)-2f(d)\right),

where
\delta(n)
is as defined in (
7.8
).

This means that, conjecturally, all class numbers
h^{*}(-n)
for odd squarefree
integers
n>0
can be written in terms of the sum (
2.1
).
Proposition
1.2
is a special case.
