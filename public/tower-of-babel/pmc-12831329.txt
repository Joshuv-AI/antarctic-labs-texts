Efficacy and safety of stem cell therapy in patients with chronic lower extremity ulcers: an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Authors: Hong-Fei Wang, Zheng Lin, Chen-Xi Ma, Shan Liu, Yi Cao, Qiu-Shuang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic lower extremity ulcers (CLEUs) remain a major clinical challenge due to their prolonged healing process and risk of amputation. Stem cell therapy (SCT) has emerged as a promising regenerative strategy, with various cell types being explored for their efficacy in treating CLEUs. This umbrella review aims to consolidate the existing evidence on stem cell interventions for CLEUs, providing a comprehensive overview of the current research landscape.MethodsThis umbrella review was conducted following the PRIOR and PRISMA guidelines. We searched across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases for systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses (MAs) that included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on SCT for CLEUs. The methodological quality and evidence quality of the SRs/MAs were assessed by AMSTAR 2 and GRADE. A quantitative synthesis of all RCTs included in the SRs/MAs to obtain objective and updated conclusions.ResultsA total of 28 SRs/MAs involving 72 RCTs were included. Our updated meta-analysis reinforces that SCT offers potential therapeutic benefits for CLEUs, including improved healing rates, amelioration of tissue perfusion and pain-related indicators, and more favorable prognostic outcomes. No significant difference in all-cause mortality was observed between the SCT and control groups.ConclusionSCT represents a promising adjunctive therapy for CLEUs, with many studies demonstrating its safety and potential benefits. Since current evidence is limited by methodological flaws and study heterogeneity, high-quality RCTs in the future are crucial to prove the benefits of SCT for CLEUs truly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04853-x.

Introduction

Chronic lower extremity ulcers (CLEUs), encompassing leg and foot ulcers, are a common clinical problem, affecting approximately 1 to 2% of adults in the United States [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 70% of leg ulcers originate from venous disease, while nearly 20% result from arterial insufficiency or mixed vascular diseases. Moreover, around 85% of foot ulcers are associated with peripheral neuropathy, commonly accompanied by arterial pathology [2]. CLEUs are commonly classified into four major types: venous, arterial, neuropathic diabetic, and pressure ulcers [3]. These ulcers typically exhibit a prolonged disease course, posing challenges to effective wound management [4]. Failure to achieve timely healing may result in serious complications, including infection, necrosis, and even sepsis, ultimately resulting in amputation or mortality [5, 6]. Notably, nearly 70% of amputations have been attributed to non-healing wounds [7]. This situation imposes a considerable burden not only on patients’ quality of life [8] but also on healthcare systems, with annual costs associated with venous leg ulcers (VLUs) and diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) estimated at $14.9 billion and $9–13 billion, respectively [9, 10].

Driven by population aging and the surging prevalence of chronic metabolic disorders including diabetes mellitus (DM) and obesity, the escalating global burden of chronic ulcers has garnered heightened clinical attention in recent years [11, 12]. Current general principles of chronic ulcer management include necrotic tissue debridement, infection control, off-loading of localized pressure, dressing applications, and specific therapies based on the type of ulcer [3, 11]. However, these conventional approaches often suboptimal ulcer closure rates and a high risk of recurrence [13]. Therefore, developing novel therapeutic approaches for chronic or refractory lower extremity ulcers is urgently required. In this context, therapeutic strategies like the local application of growth factors, autologous platelet-rich gel and bioengineered skin substitutes have shown potential benefits in promoting ulcer healing [14]. Nonetheless, the current body of evidence remains inconclusive, as most available studies lack robust data from large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and reported outcomes have shown inconsistent efficacy [13, 15].

CLEUs are characterized by complex pathophysiological mechanisms, including ischemia-reperfusion injury, local tissue hypoxia, and persistent inflammation, all of which impair normal wound healing [16]. On this basis, stem cell therapy (SCT) has emerged as a promising alternative for treating refractory ulcers. Stem cells possess unique characteristics, including self-renewal, long-term viability, and the capacity to differentiate into specialized cell types [17, 18]. SCT can modulates multiple pathophysiological processes involved in ulcer repair, including controlling of inflammation, activating resident reparative cells, stimulating growth factors release, and promoting angiogenesis in ischemic tissues [19–21]. A growing body of evidence from preclinical animal studies [22–25] and early-phase clinical trials [26, 27] has supported the therapeutic potential of SCT in facilitating tissue repair and regeneration. Various types of stem cells have been explored, including adipose-, bone marrow-, and peripheral blood-derived stem cells. Nevertheless, the optimal stem cell source for treating CLEUs remains to be fully established.

Several systematic reviews have synthesized clinical evidence, but most have focused on specific stem cell types (e.g., mesenchymal stem cells (MSC) [28, 29] or bone marrow-derived cells [30, 31]), or specific ulcer etiologies [29, 32, 33]. Considerable heterogeneity exists across these reviews in terms of study outcomes, methodologies, and evidence quality. To address these limitations and provide high-level, comprehensive evidence to inform clinical decision-making, umbrella reviews serve as a valuable tool by synthesizing findings from multiple systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses (MAs) on a specific topic [34, 35]. Accordingly, this umbrella review aims to integrate existing evidence regarding stem cell-based interventions for the healing of chronic, refractory lower extremity ulcers, in order to better guide clinical practice.

Methods

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) guidelines for reporting the study [36, 37]. Our research has registered on PROSPERO (CRD420250650857).

Eligibility criteria

In this umbrella review, SRs/MAs of RCTs that assessed the efficacy and safety of SCT in patients with CLEUs were included. Eligible patients were those with CLEUs of any etiology, including but not limited to ulcers caused by DM or peripheral vascular diseases (venous or arterial ulcers). No restrictions were applied regarding participants’ age, sex, or ethnicity. Eligible interventions consisted of SCT derived from various origins, such as adipose tissue, bone marrow (BM), peripheral blood (PB), and umbilical cord blood, as well as other types of stem cells or their derivatives. The control group could receive the placebo or sham interventions, conventional treatments for CLEUs, or other active comparators. The primary outcomes assessed were healing rate and amputation rate. Secondary outcomes included were as follows: (1) other indicators related to ulcer healing, including ulcer size reduction and complete healing time; (2) amputation and survival outcomes, including amputation-free survival (AFS), and all-cause mortality; (3) perfusion indexes and angiogenesis, including transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2), ankle-brachial index (ABI), and new angiogenesis rate; (4) symptomatic pain outcomes, including rest pain score (RPS) and pain-free walking distance (PFWD). No restrictions on publication year or language were applied. Narrative reviews, conference abstracts, case series, case reports, animal studies, SRs/MAs without quantitative synthesis, and network MAs were excluded.

Literature search and screening

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across four databases, including Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, and the Cochrane Library, from inception to 3 March 2025. A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free keywords related to “stem cell”, “chronic wound”, “ulcer”, “systematic review” and “meta-analyses” were used. In addition, reference lists of the included studies were manually screened, and expert recommendations were considered to ensure the completeness of the literature search. Detailed search strategies were provided in Table S1. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, two independent reviewers conducted an initial screening of titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review. Any disagreements were adjudicated through discussion with a third reviewer to ensure consensus.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers, with subsequent cross-checking to ensure accuracy. The extracted items included: first author, year of publication, databases searched and corresponding search periods, number of included studies, year and range of included studies, types of population and stem cells, bias assessment tools and results for included RCTs, noted publication biases, key findings, outcomes of concern, and their effect sizes.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included SRs/MAs was independently evaluated by two investigators using A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews version 2 (AMSTAR 2) [38]. AMSTAR 2 contains 16 criteria, 7 out of 16 criteria were considered as ‘critical domains’ (Table S2).

The quality of evidence for our outcomes was assessed/reported using the Gradings of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation system (GRADE) [39], with five aspects: study limitations, imprecision (random error), inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. The GRADE system rates the quality of evidence as follows: very low, low, moderate, high.

Managing overlapping systematic reviews

With the increasing number of published SRs/MAs [40], RCTs are frequently included in more than one SRs/MAs. Direct inclusion of review outcomes with such overlap may result in multiple inclusion of the same primary studies, potentially leading to biased pooled results and effect estimates [41]. To quantify the extent of overlap and assess its potential impact, the corrected covered area (CCA) proposed by Pieper et al. [42] was utilized. The CCA thresholds were categorized as follows: <5% (slight overlap), 5–10% (moderate overlap), 10–15% (high overlap), and >15% (very high overlap).

Data synthesis

A meta-level narrative synthesis was conducted for all included SRs/MAs. When identical outcomes appeared across different reviews, the review including the largest number of studies, published most recently, and exhibiting the highest methodological quality was prioritized. Risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for dichotomous variables, while standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs were applied for continuous outcomes. Both random-effect and fixed-effect models will be considered where appropriate [43]. Heterogeneity was measured via the I2 statistic and p-value [44]. Subgroup analyses were performed based on stem cell source, cell dose (total cell count), route of administration, ulcer etiology, and follow-up duration. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially omitting individual studies. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots and Egger’s tests when ten or more studies were available [45]. If there existed significant publication bias, the trim-and-fill method was used to find the possible “missing studies” and further explore their impact on the pooled effect estimate subsequently [46, 47]. All statistical analyses were undertaken using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, LLC, College Station, TX, authorized by The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University).

Results

Literature search

A total of 1,413 records were identified from four databases: PubMed (n = 306), Embase (n = 436), Web of Science (n = 526), and Cochrane Library (n = 145). After removing 443 duplicate records, we screened 970 records. Based on the predefined eligibility criteria, 918 records were excluded. Full texts were retrieved for the remaining 52 records, of which 28 records were ultimately included (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Among the included 28 SRs/MAs published between 2011 and 2025, nearly 90% were published within the last ten years, indicating the growing research interest in SCT for CLEUs. Most reviews conducted comprehensive literature searches using major databases, including PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. SRs/MAs included mainly focused on patients with DFU or critical limb ischemia (CLI). Among these, 13 reviews focused on DFU, 10 targeted CLI, which results from severe peripheral arterial disease (PAD), and 5 addressed both DFU and CLI. The number of RCTs included in these SRs/MAs ranged from 4 to 27, indicating a large body of available evidence. The majority of interventions centered on SCT derived from BM, followed by PB. Controls in these studies were primarily placebo or standard care. The outcomes primarily monitored were healing of the ulcer and amputation rate, alongside surrogate endpoints of limb perfusion (ABI and TcPO2). Assessment of methodological quality mainly relied on ROB 1, ROB 2, and the Jadad scale. The GRADE approach was applied in some studies to evaluate the quality of evidence. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots or Egger’s test commonly, with most reviews reporting no significant publication bias. Key findings across these reviews consistently suggested that SCT was effective and safe in promoting wound healing, clinical prognosis, and vascular function in patients with CLEUs (Table 1). The CCA value for all outcomes exceeded 15% (except for the healing rate, which was 13.31%), indicating a substantial degree of overlap across the included SRs/MAs (Table S3).

Results of SRs/MAs quality assessment

Among the 28 included SRs/MAs, 15 were rated as critically low quality and 13 as low quality. All of them failed to meet at least one critical item, mainly including the absence of study protocol registrations (Item 2), omission of a list of excluded studies with corresponding reasons (Item 7), and failure to consider the potential impact of risk of bias when interpreting the results (Item 15).

140 SCT-related outcomes for CLEUs were extracted from the 28 SRs/MAs. According to the GRADE assessment (Table 2), the majority (70%) were considered to be low or very low-quality. For our primary outcome of healing rate, the evidence was rated as high in 4 (14.29%), moderate in 10 (35.71%), low in 12 (42.86%), and very low in 2 (7.14%) out of 28 outcomes. For the amputation rate, the evidence was rated as high in 2 (8%), moderate in 13 (52%), low in 9 (36%), and very low in 1 (4%) out of 25 outcomes. The evidence was primarily downgraded by imprecision (due to small sample size or wide confidence intervals) and study limitations (risk of bias).

Effects of SCT on the healing of CLEUs

The pooled results indicated that SCT greatly increased the healing rate of ulcers (RR 1.78, 95%CI 1.64 to 1.93, I2 = 34.0%, P < 0.05, Fig. 2). We further explored whether stem cells derived from different sources were consistently associated with a higher healing rate (Table 3). Subgroup analyses revealed that PB-derived stem cells exhibited the highest efficacy (RR 2.62, 95%CI 1.95 to 3.51), followed by BM-derived (RR 1.80 95%CI 1.54 to 2.11), adipose-derived (RR 1.57, 95%CI 1.36 to 1.81), umbilical cord-derived (RR 1.76, 95%CI 1.46 to 2.13), and stem cells from other sources (RR 1.65, 95%CI 1.37 to 1.99) (Fig. S1). Moreover, SCT was equally effective in promoting ulcer healing in ulcers caused by DM (RR 1.82, 95%CI 1.66 to 2.00) and CLI (RR 1.59, 95%CI 1.29 to 1.95) (Fig. S2). Since chronic ulcers are defined as those lasting over 3 months after appropriate treatment [48], we categorized the follow-up durations into short-term (< 3 months), medium-term (3-12 months), and long-term (≥ 1 year). Higher healing rates were observed in short-term subgroup (RR 1.82, 95% CI 1.63 to 2.03) than medium-term (RR 1.69, 95% CI 1.48 to1.94) and long-term subgroups (RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.84) slightly (Fig. S3). Stem cells delivered via intramuscular (IM) injection benefited the complete wound healing rate (RR 1.98, 95% CI 1.74 to 2.25) compared to intra-arterial (IA) injection (RR 1.30, 95% CI 0.82 to 2.08) and dressing (RR 1.62, 95% CI 1.38 to 1.89) (Fig. S4). We also found that the 107 dose group (RR 3.46, 95% CI 2.17 to 5.54) was superior to the other groups for ulcer healing (Fig. S5). Analysis of dosage by cell source revealed that adipose-derived cells were commonly administered at lower doses (≤ 106), while BM- and PB-derived cells were delivered at higher concentrations (107-109). Further analyses in both the BM- and PB-derived group also found the superior efficacy of the 107 dose (Fig. S6-7). Two studies employed umbilical cord-derived stem cells at a dosage of 10⁷ for treating diabetic ulcers. Regardless of delivery routes and ulcer types, the 10⁷ dose likewise exhibited enhanced effectiveness (Fig. S8-10). Other delivery routes, such as topical applications (e.g., dressings), typically employed lower cell doses (≤ 106). Additionally, SCT could effectively enhance ulcer size reduction (SMD − 0.46, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.20, I2 = 29.9%, P < 0.05, Fig. 3) and accelerate the time required for complete wound healing (SMD − 2.21, 95% CI −3.30 to −1.11, I2 = 96.8%, P < 0.05, Fig. 4).

Effects of SCT on the perfusion indexes and angiogenesis

ABI and TcPO2 were commonly used noninvasive methods to effectively evaluate tissue perfusion and reflect the metabolic state of lower extremities [49–51]. These parameters have been widely applied in clinical management of DFUs and CLI, particularly crucial in predicting ulcer healing potential, determining amputation level, and guiding revascularization procedure [52, 53]. Our pooled results indicated that the SCT group exhibited greater increases in ABI (SMD 0.85, 95%CI 0.64 to 1.07, I2 = 73.3%, P < 0.05, Fig. 5) and TcPO2 (SMD 1.89, 95%CI 1.28 to 2.51, I2 = 92.0%, P < 0.05, Fig. 6). This implied that enhanced tissue perfusion may meaningfully validate the observed improvements in ulcer healing rate and amputation rates. Analysis by digital subtraction angiography revealed that there was a higher rate of new angiogenesis (RR 6.26, 95%CI 3.93 to 9.97; I2 = 36.4%, P < 0.05, Fig. 7) in the SCT group. Similarly, Xie et al. [54] reported that SCT could increase the blood flow of the 10 toes, supporting the role of SCT in improving distal limb perfusion.

Effects of SCT on alleviation of symptomatic pain

The results showed that patients receiving SCT exhibited a longer PFWD (SMD 1.20, 95%CI 0.57 to 1.83, I2 = 89.0%, P < 0.05, Fig. 8) and a lower RPS (SMD − 1.83, 95%CI −2.66 to −0.99, I2 = 95.8%, P < 0.05, Fig. 9). These findings suggest that SCT may contribute to pain alleviation, thereby apparently enhance patients’ quality of life. Nevertheless, both PFWD and RPS are subjective patient-reported outcomes and may fluctuate heavily. Besides, pain indicators are susceptible to multiple factors, especially psychological factors, complicating the interpretation of pain-related outcomes.

Effects of SCT on amputation and survival outcomes

Major limb amputation, often involving removal through or above the ankle joint [55], was frequently regarded as an inevitable outcome in the end stage of DFUs or ischemic lower-extremity ulcers. Early and appropriate intervention for the ulcers was crucial for preventing major amputation. Our pooled analysis indicated that SCT could reduce major amputation rate by 40% (RR 0.60, 95%CI 0.48 to 0.75, I2 = 14.6%, P < 0.05, Fig. 10) and total amputation rate (including both major amputation and minor amputation) by 47% (RR 0.53, 95%CI 0.43 to 0.64, I2 = 0.0%, P < 0.05, Fig. 11). Subgroup analyses (Table 3) further revealed that PB derived stem cells showed more favorable outcomes (RR 0.33, 95%CI 0.21 to 0.51), followed by BM derived stem cells (RR 0.62, 95%CI 0.49 to 0.78) (Fig. S11). Moreover, patients receiving IM stem cell administration exhibited a lower risk of amputation (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.57) than those treated via IA injection (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.60 to 1.28) (Fig. S12). The protective effects of SCT against amputation were consistently observed across different follow-up durations (Fig. S13). The doses of ≤ 106, 107,108, and 109 cells could reduce the amputation rate by 43%, 48%, 28%, and 73%, respectively (Fig. S14). Analysis of dosage by cell source further revealed that both the BM- and PB-derived groups showed superior efficacy with the 109 doses (Fig. S15-16). Improved outcomes were consistently observed with the 109 doses, irrespective of the delivery route or ulcer type (Fig. S17-19). Besides, SCT effectively improved AFS (RR 1.26, 95%CI 1.12 to 1.41, I2 = 50.0%, P < 0.05, Fig. 12). Since incomplete reporting of adverse events in most studies, all-cause mortality was used as a proxy measure for safety evaluations. The pooled results showed no statistical difference in mortality between the SCT and control groups (RR 0.69, 95%CI 0.47 to 1.01, I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.059, Fig. 13).

Sensitivity analyses and publication bias

Although significant heterogeneity was observed in ABI, TcPO₂, PFWD, RPS, AFS, and complete healing time, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the results (Fig. S20-30). Our reconducted meta-analyses found potential publication bias in healing rate, amputation rate, ABI, RPS, AFS, and new angiogenesis rate (Table S4, Fig. S31-39). However, it did not affect the overall findings according to the trim-and-fill analysis (Fig. S40-43).

Discussion

This umbrella review integrates evidence from existing SRs/MAs to evaluate the efficacy and safety of SCT for CLEUs. Although advances in medical management, the treatment of CLEUs remains a significant clinical challenge. Recently, SCT has shown considerable promise in regenerative medicine [56], particularly for the treatment of chronic skin ulcers secondary to DM or peripheral vascular diseases.

Although the high CCA indicated consistency and accuracy in literature retrieval, it also highlighted substantial overlap among the included SRs/MAs. To minimize bias arising from directly merging overlapping results, we re-quantified and synthesized relevant outcome measures from the RCTs incorporated within the SRs/MAs, followed by subgroup analyses. Our re-analysis reinforced the therapeutic benefits of SCT for CLEUs, especially in improving healing rates, shortening the time to complete healing, and reducing ulcer size. Moreover, SCT appeared to ameliorate surrogate indicators of tissue perfusion, pain, and functional capacity. Importantly, SCT contributed to superior prognostic outcomes by lowering the major amputation rate and increasing AFS, while showing no significant impact on all-cause mortality compared to controls. Adverse events reported across studies mainly involved infections, procedural pain, and inguinal hematomas. Most were mild and transient, primarily related to the local implantation or infusion, with no clear evidence supporting a direct causal relationship with SCT. Analysis of all-cause mortality further supported the safety of SCT.

Currently, the management of severe DFUs and ischemic lower-extremity ulcers primarily relies on conventional wound care, pharmacotherapy, and surgical revascularization. Nevertheless, many patients are not suitable candidates for revascularization therapy, and their prognosis remains poor [57, 58]. Compared to conventional therapies, SCT offers distinct advantages. Multiple types of stem cells have demonstrated efficacy in promoting ulcer healing. Among the studies included in our review, BM was the most frequently investigated source. Stem cell selection tends to vary depending on the underlying etiology of the ulcer. For CLI caused by severe PAD, the most commonly studied cell types were bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMNCs) and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF)-mobilized peripheral blood mononuclear cells (m-PBSCs). Studies on DFUs involved a broader range of cell types, primarily including bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs), BMMNCs, and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs).

MSCs are the most commonly used stem cell type due to their abundance, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness [21]. Excessive inflammation is widely recognized as a key impediment to chronic ulcer healing [59]. MSCs contribute to wound resolution by secreting immunomodulatory factors that modulate host immune responses and help terminate the chronic inflammatory state [60]. Another critical barrier is tissue ischemia at the ulcer site, which emphasizes the importance of revascularization [21]. Through paracrine mechanisms, MSCs secrete a broad range of regulatory factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor, epidermal growth factor and angiopoietin-1. These collectively enhance the recruitment of immune cells, fibroblasts, keratinocytes, and endothelial cells to the wound bed. MSCs also possess the ability to transdifferentiate into various skin cell types. The mechanisms mentioned above work synergistically to facilitate angiogenesis and tissue repair [61]. Additionally, MSCs could help reduce fibrosis, ultimately lowering the likelihood of scar formation and ulcer recurrence [60]. BM has long been considered the predominant source possibly owing to its established protocols and mature expansion techniques. BM-MSCs exhibit angiogenic and immunomodulatory properties [62]. However, their clinical application requires careful monitoring due to the risk of tumorigenicity and immunogenicity [63]. ADSCs are another frequently used MSC type. ADSCs have several advantages over BM-MSCs, such as ease of harvest, abundant supply, stronger immunosuppressive activity (approximately threefold greater than BM-MSCs), enhanced proliferative capacity, and fewer ethical concerns [64–66]. Nonetheless, ADSCs tend to undergo senescence and chemokine depletion after repeated in vitro expansion [63]. In addition to BM and adipose tissues, alternative sources such as human umbilical cord MSCs and placenta-derived stem cells have gained attention for their ease of collection, the ability to isolate diverse cell populations, and low immunogenicity, suggesting considerable promise for clinical applications [21, 67].

Apart from MSCs, mononuclear cells (MNCs) are also widely studied, with two main sources: BM and PB. BMMNCs, containing a small number of progenitor cells, are easy to collect, process, and handle, making them a practical choice for clinical research. PB is commonly used due to its high availability, diverse progenitor populations, ease of collection, reduced need for general anesthesia, and faster hematopoietic engraftment [68, 69]. Our findings further indicated that m-PBSCs may be a more preferable source. In clinical practice, the selection of stem cell types should consider cell availability, accessibility, scalability under good manufacturing practices, and in vitro expansion potential. Autologous stem cells are generally preferred, as they eliminate the risk of immunogenic rejection, enhance cell survival within ischemic wound environments, and circumvent ethical concerns [70]. However, in specific scenarios, allogeneic stem cells may represent a viable alternative, especially when autologous cells are unavailable or insufficient under hyperglycemic conditions [71]. Recent studies have indicated that allogeneic MSCs can address unmet therapeutic needs and may be developed into ready-to-use, off-the-shelf products [72]. Therefore, future large-scale studies should actively investigate the safety, immunogenicity, and long-term efficacy of allogeneic MSCs across diverse patient populations.

Beyond the selection of cell type, the route of administration also plays a critical role in determining the therapeutic efficacy of SCT. IM injection remains the most common route of SCT delivery, while IA infusion has also been explored as a potential alternative [73, 74]. Our findings indicated that IM injection resulted in superior therapeutic outcomes compared with IA injection. Previous animal researches have consistently demonstrated that local IM injection around the wound area is safer and more effective than systemic administration [75, 76]. Given the frequent vascular impairment in CLEUs, IA injection often fails to adequately target ischemic areas, thereby limiting its expected therapeutic benefits [77]. Therefore, we suggest that IM delivery of stem cells could be a preferable and beneficial approach in clinical practice.

The dosage of implanted cells is assumed as another important factor affecting therapeutic efficacy. Our study found that a cell dose of 107 significantly improved the healing rate, while a dose of 109 showed greater potential in further reducing the risk of amputation. This discrepancy may be related to the distinct effects of different cell doses on the various physiological processes involved in tissue repair and perfusion maintenance. Besides, the therapeutic effect of each dose is influenced by multiple factors. While factors such as injection method, cell source, and ulcer type are important, the composition of functional cell subtypes is particularly crucial. For instance, the angiogenic potential of CD34+ cells has been well demonstrated in preclinical research [78]. A prior review found that higher doses of implanted CD34+ cells (total number >107 cells or >106/kg) achieved better outcomes than lower doses [79]. Other cell types like CD133+ or c-kit+/CD45– progenitor cells might also be considered as possible effector fractions in PB or BM [80, 81]. The therapeutic efficacy of cells with different immunophenotypes remains to be determined. Consequently, identifying the optimal dose and the true efficacy of functional cellular composition requires validation through well-designed and large-scale clinical trials in the future. These will be essential to balance clinical benefits against practical considerations such as resource consumption and preparation time.

Moreover, clinical efficacy varied across different follow-up durations. Our findings indicated that the benefits of SCT were observed within short- and medium-term follow-up periods (mainly within 6 months), whereas long-term benefits were less evident, possibly due to the gradual loss of therapeutic potency over time, resulting in incomplete wound healing. The protective effect against amputation appeared to persist with longer follow-up durations. Regardless, alternative treatment options should be explored in cases of unsatisfactory long-term wound healing.

Taken together, SCT holds great potential as an innovative treatment strategy for CLEUs. Specifically, combining SCT with other therapies may further enhance clinical outcomes. This is supported by a recent network meta-analysis, which reported that the combination of SCT and platelet-rich plasma achieved the highest complete wound healing rate (83.56%) [82]. Beyond clinical efficacy and safety, economic feasibility remains an important consideration in treatment selection. Despite the relatively high cost of SCT may raise concerns, its potential to facilitate earlier return to work and reduce the incidence of osteomyelitis and subsequent amputation may offset the economic burden [83].

Strengths and limitations

This umbrella review incorporated a substantial number of SRs/MAs and RCTs, providing valuable insights into the efficacy and safety of SCT for CLEUs. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, as an umbrella review, our analyses are inherently limited by the time lag in capturing newly published RCTs not yet included in any systematic review. Secondly, considerable heterogeneity was observed for several variables, primarily due to clinical variability. For instance, varied ulcer characteristics, such as depth, and size, Fontaine classification, could directly influence the ulcer microenvironment and its intrinsic healing potential [84, 85]. The donor, culture conditions, and processing methods of stem cells could affect the quantity, viability, and functional status of the input cells [86]. Technical aspects of the procedure, including injection technique [87], delivery route [88], and injection site [89] also vary considerably. Furthermore, the patient’s overall condition should be considered, as conditions such as poorly controlled blood glucose can diminish treatment efficacy [90]. Thirdly, we should acknowledge that our dose-response analysis is limited by the lack of standardized dosing reporting in the included RCTs, which mainly reported the total administered cell counts. This may influence the interpretation of the optimal stem cell dose and its clinical applicability. Future RCTs are needed to present standardized dosing parameters, preferably ulcer area-normalized (cells/cm²) or weight-adjusted (cells/kg), so that dosing recommendations derived from meta-analyses can be interpreted reliably. Fourthly, the methodological quality of the included SRs/MAs was generally rated as low or critically low. A fundamental limitation was the consistent absence of a list of excluded studies across all SRs/MAs. This omission risks the erroneous exclusion of relevant research, thereby compromising the transparency and accuracy of the review process. Furthermore, some studies lacked a pre-registered protocol, which may undermine the rigor and standardization of the methodology and increase the potential for selective reporting bias. Fifthly, only 7 of 140 outcomes (5%) were supported by high-quality evidence, and 35 (25%) by moderate-quality evidence according to the GRADE assessment. These higher-grade outcomes predominantly pertained to primary outcomes, including healing rate and amputation rate. The remaining outcomes were largely classified as low or very low quality, raising concerns about their applicability to clinical practice. The evidence was primarily downgraded due to study limitations and imprecision. In the primary RCTs, the absence of placebo controls and the lack of blinding of researchers or participants might cause a high risk of performance bias, thereby weakening the certainty of the combined effect. Furthermore, many studies had small sample sizes that were underpowered to demonstrate the superiority of SCT. This, along with frequently wide confidence intervals, also contributed to the lower rating of imprecision. Notably, higher-quality articles are more likely to report less favorable efficacy results, possibly due to stricter methodological designs [91]. Therefore, while our analyses suggest associations between SCT treatment efficacy and factors such as cell dosage and delivery methods, these findings require further validation through rigorously designed studies. Future investigations should prioritize large-scale, high-quality RCTs with diverse patient populations and longer follow-up periods. Comparative studies evaluating different stem cell types, dosages, and delivery strategies, along with long-term safety and efficacy data, are warranted to support the clinical translation of SCT.

Conclusion

SCT represents a promising advancement in treating CLEUs, with potential benefits in improving ulcer healing state, limb perfusion, symptomatic pain, and reducing amputation risk. Although safety is suggested, the low-quality evidence suggests that this conclusion needs to be treated with caution. Optimal cell sources, dosages, and delivery methods need to be determined, and the long-term effects of SCT on clinical outcomes need further exploration. Future studies must emphasize high-quality SRs&MAs involving powered RCTs to verify the therapeutic benefits of SCT in CLEUs, thereby better developing optimized strategies and personalized treatment.
