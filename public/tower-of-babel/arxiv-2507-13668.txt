Singular minimal surfaces with constant curvature
Rafael López

Abstract
. We prove that singular minimal surfaces with constant Gauss curvature are planes, spheres and cylindrical surfaces. We also classify all singular minimal surfaces with a constant principal curvature and singular minimal surfaces with constant mean curvature.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
53A04, 53B25

1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Let
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a unit vector in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\Sigma
be an oriented surface and
\Phi\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an isometric immersion of
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The surface
\Sigma
is said to be an
\alpha
-singular minimal surface
if its mean curvature
H
satisfies

H=\alpha\frac{\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle}{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle},

(1.1)

where
N
is the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
. We are implicitly assuming that
\Phi(\Sigma)
is included in one of the two halfspaces determined by the vector plane
\Pi=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle p,\vec{a}\rangle=0\}
. Singular minimal surfaces are critical points of the energy functional

\Sigma\mapsto\int_{\Sigma}\langle\Phi(p),\vec{a}\rangle^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma,

where
d\Sigma
is the area element of
\Sigma
. If
\alpha=0
, this energy is simply the area functional and the critical points are minimal surfaces. From now, we discard the case
\alpha=0
. The case
\alpha=1
extends in dimension two the notion of catenary: see
[
3
,
8
,
15
]
. The study of the singular minimal surface equation can be viewed in
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
12
,
13
]
. For geometric aspects of singular minimal surfaces, see
[
7
,
9
,
11
,
14
]
.

Among the examples of
\alpha
-singular minimal surfaces, we focus on isoparametric surfaces, that is, planes, spheres and circular cylinders.

(1)

Planes parallel to
\vec{a}
are
\alpha
-singular minimal surfaces for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

Spheres are
\alpha
-singular minimal surfaces only if
\alpha=-2
. In such a case, the center of the sphere lies in the vector plane
\Pi
. These surfaces also represent minimal surfaces in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
when
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is viewed in the upper halfspace model
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\langle p,\vec{a}\rangle>0\}
.

(3)

Circular cylinders are
\alpha
-singular minimal surfaces only if
\alpha=-1
. In such a case, the rotational axis lies in the vector plane
\Pi
.

Other examples of
\alpha
-singular minimal surfaces can be found in the family of cylindrical surfaces, that is, ruled surfaces whose rulings are parallel straight-lines. If the cylindrical surface is parametrized by
\Psi(s,t)=\gamma(s)+t\vec{v}
,
|\vec{v}|=1
, and if
\Sigma
satisfies (
1.1
), then either
\Sigma
is a plane parallel to
\vec{a}
or
\langle\vec{v},\vec{a}\rangle=0
. This implies that the generating curve
\gamma
lies contained in a plane parallel to
\vec{a}
. In such a case, Eq. (
1.1
) writes in terms of the curvature
\kappa
of
\gamma
as

\kappa=\alpha\frac{\langle{\bf n},\vec{a}\rangle}{\langle\gamma,\vec{a}\rangle},

(1.2)

where
{\bf n}
is the unit normal of
\gamma
. These surfaces have zero Gauss curvature. Cylindrical singular minimal surfaces have been classified in
[
9
,
11
]
. If
\alpha=-1
, cylindrical singular minimal surfaces are circular cylinders. In this paper, we will refer these surfaces simply by cylindrical singular minimal surfaces.

Planes parallel to
\vec{a}
, spheres centered at
\Pi
and the cylindrical singular minimal surfaces have constant Gauss curvature
K
. The purpose of this paper is to prove the converse and characterize these surfaces are the only singular minimal surfaces with constant Gauss curvature.

Theorem 1.1
.

Planes parallel to
\vec{a}
, spheres centered at
\Pi
and the cylindrical singular minimal surfaces are the only
\alpha
-singular minimal surfaces with constant Gauss curvature.

This result will proved in Sect.
3
. We also study those singular minimal surfaces with a constant principal curvature. In Sect.
4
, we prove the following classification.

Theorem 1.2
.

Planes parallel to
\vec{a}
, spheres centered at
\Pi
and cylindrical singular minimal surfaces are the only singular minimal surfaces with a constant principal curvature.

Finally we consider singular minimal surfaces with constant mean curvature.

Theorem 1.3
.

Planes parallel to
\vec{a}
and spheres centered at
\Pi
are the only singular minimal surfaces with constant mean curvature.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we describe parametrizations of a surface by lines of curvature. This will be done in open sets of a surface free of umbilic points. Let
\Sigma
be an orientable surface immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Denote by
\nabla
and
\overline{\nabla}
be the Levi-Civita connections of
\Sigma
and

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively. Denote by
N
the unit normal vector field of
\Sigma
. Let
\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
be the space of tangent vector fields of
\Sigma
. The Gauss and Weingarten formulas are, respectively,

\begin{split}\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y&=\nabla_{X}Y+h(X,Y),\\
\overline{\nabla}_{X}N&=-SX\end{split}

(2.1)

for all
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, where
h\colon T\Sigma\times T\Sigma\to T^{\perp}\Sigma
and
S\colon T\Sigma\to T\Sigma
are the second fundamental form and the shape
operator, respectively. The Gauss
equation and Codazzi equations are

\begin{split}\langle R(X,Y)W,Z\rangle&=\langle h(Y,W),h(X,Z)\rangle-\langle h(X,W),h(Y,Z)\rangle\\
(\nabla_{X}S)Y&=(\nabla_{Y}S)X,\end{split}

where
R
is the curvature tensor of
\nabla
. The mean curvature
H
and the Gauss curvature
K
of
\Sigma
are defined by

H=\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}\quad K=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2},

where
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
. In an open set
\Omega\subset\Sigma
of non-umbilic points, consider a local
orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
given by coordinates of lines of curvature. The expression of the shape operator
S
is

Se_{1}=\kappa_{1}e_{1},\qquad Se_{2}=\kappa_{2}e_{2}.

(2.2)

Since
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is an orthonormal frame, if
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
then

\nabla_{X}e_{1}=\omega(X)e_{2}\quad\nabla_{X}e_{2}=-\omega(X)e_{1},

where
\omega(X)=\langle\nabla_{X}e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=-\langle\nabla_{X}e_{2},e_{1}\rangle
. Then we have

\left\{\begin{split}&\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}=\omega(e_{1})e_{2},\quad\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}=-\omega(e_{1})e_{1}\\
&\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}=\omega(e_{2})e_{2},\quad\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{2}=-\omega(e_{2})e_{1}.\end{split}\right.

(2.3)

Using the Codazzi equations, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{2}(\kappa_{1})=(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\omega(e_{1})\\
e_{1}(\kappa_{2})=(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\omega(e_{2}),\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

where, as usually,
e_{i}(f)
denotes the derivative of a smooth function
f
along the vector
e_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Notice that
\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}\not=0
in
\Omega
.
Thanks to (
2.4
), in the set
\Omega
, the Gauss equation gives the following expression of the Gauss curvature
K
,

K=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}=-e_{1}\left(\frac{e_{1}(\kappa_{2})}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)+e_{2}\left(\frac{e_{2}(\kappa_{1})}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)-\frac{e_{1}(\kappa_{2})^{2}+e_{2}(\kappa_{1})^{2}}{(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}}.

(2.5)

Denote by
\Phi\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
the immersion of a singular minimal surface. Let decompose the vector
\vec{a}
in its tangent part
\vec{a}^{\top}
and normal part
\vec{a}^{\perp}
with respect to
\Sigma
,

\vec{a}^{\top}=\gamma e_{1}+\mu e_{2},

for some smooth functions
\gamma
and
\mu
. The functions
\gamma
and
\mu
are given by

\gamma=\langle\vec{a},e_{1}\rangle,\quad\mu=\langle\vec{a},e_{2}\rangle.

The normal part
\vec{a}^{\perp}
is
\langle N,\vec{a}\rangle N
, which by (
1.1
) is

\vec{a}^{\perp}=\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}N.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a subset of non-umbilic points of an
\alpha
-singular minimal surface. Then the functions
\gamma
and
\mu
satisfy the following equations

\left\{\begin{split}0=&e_{1}(\gamma)-\mu\omega(e_{1})-\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}\kappa_{1}\\
0=&e_{2}(\gamma)-\mu\omega(e_{2})\\
0=&e_{1}(\mu)+\gamma\omega(e_{1})\\
0=&e_{2}(\mu)+\gamma\omega(e_{2})-\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}\kappa_{2}\\
0=&e_{1}(\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})+\gamma\kappa_{1}\\
0=&e_{2}(\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})+\mu\kappa_{2}.\end{split}\right.

(2.6)

Proof.

If
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, then
\overline{\nabla}_{X}\vec{a}=0
because
\vec{a}
is constant in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then the Gauss and
Weingarten formulas imply

\begin{split}0&=\overline{\nabla}_{X}\vec{a}=\overline{\nabla}_{X}(\vec{a}^{\top}+\vec{a}^{\perp})=\overline{\nabla}_{X}\vec{a}^{\top}+\overline{\nabla}_{X}\vec{a}^{\perp}\\
&={\nabla}_{X}\vec{a}^{\top}+h(X,\vec{a}^{\top})+X(H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})N-H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}SX\end{split}

for all
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
.
Taking the tangent and the normal part in both sides of the above expression, we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\nabla}_{X}\vec{a}^{\top}-H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}SX=0\\
h(X,\vec{a}^{\top})+X(H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})N=0.\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

Equations (
2.6
) are obtained by substituting in the two previous identities
X
by
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
and writing in coordinates with respect to
\{e_{1},e_{2},N\}
. Indeed, if
X=e_{1}
, and using (
2.3
), the first equation in (
2.7
) is

\begin{array}[]{ll}0&={\nabla}_{e_{1}}(\gamma e_{1}+\mu e_{2})-H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}\kappa_{1}e_{1}\\
&=e_{1}(\gamma)e_{1}+e_{1}(\mu)e_{2}+\gamma\omega(e_{1})e_{2}-\mu\omega(e_{1})e_{1}-H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}\kappa_{1}e_{1}.\end{array}

Similarly, the second equation in (
2.7
) is

0=h(e_{1},\gamma e_{1}+\mu e_{2})+e_{1}(H\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})N=\left(\gamma\kappa_{1}+e_{1}\left(\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha}\right)\right)N.

This gives the first, third and fifth equations in (
2.6
). The other equations of (
2.6
) are obtained by putting
X=e_{2}
in (
2.7
).

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We know that planes parallel to
\vec{a}
, spheres centered at
\Pi
and cylindrical singular minimal surfaces have constant Gauss curvature. We prove the converse.

The case
K=0
is studied separately. If
K=0
, the surface is also called developable. It is known that these surfaces can be parametrized locally as ruled surfaces
[
10
]
. On the other hand, ruled singular minimal surfaces were classified in
[
1
]
, proving that these surfaces must be cylindrical surfaces. This proves Thm.
1.1
when
K=0
.

Suppose now that
K\not=0
. Let
\Sigma
be an
\alpha
-singular minimal surface with non-zero constant Gauss curvature
K
,
K=c
. If a principal curvature is constant, and because
K\not=0
, then the other principal curvature is constant. This proves that
\Sigma
is an isoparametric surface. Since
K\not=0
, then
\Sigma
is a sphere. This proves the result in this situation.

Suppose now that the two principal curvatures are not constant and we will arrive to a contradiction. We write the principal curvature
\kappa_{2}
and the mean curvature
H
in terms of
K
and the principal curvature
\kappa_{1}
. So, we have

\kappa_{2}=\frac{c}{\kappa_{1}},\qquad H=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\kappa_{1}},\qquad\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}{\kappa_{1}}.

(3.1)

We now write Eq. (
2.5
). Using that
K=c
is constant, and taking into account (
3.1
), we have

\begin{split}c\frac{(\kappa_{1}^{2}-c)^{2}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}&=\frac{c(\kappa_{1}^{2}-c)}{\kappa_{1}^{3}}e_{11}(\kappa_{1})+\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}{\kappa_{1}}e_{22}(\kappa_{1})\\
&-\frac{3c}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}e_{1}(\kappa_{1})^{2}-\frac{2\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}e_{2}(\kappa_{1})^{2}.\end{split}

(3.2)

By using the expressions of
H
and
\kappa_{2}
in (
3.1
), we have

\begin{split}e_{1}(\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})&=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}^{2}}\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle e_{1}(\kappa_{1})+\gamma\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}},\\
e_{2}(\frac{H\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle}{\alpha})&=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}^{2}}\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle e_{2}(\kappa_{1})+\mu\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}}.\\
\end{split}

(3.3)

Substituting (
3.3
) into the last two equations of (
2.6
), we obtain

\begin{split}0&=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}^{2}}\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle e_{1}(\kappa_{1})+\left(\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}}+\kappa_{1}\right)\gamma,\\
0&=\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}^{2}}\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle e_{2}(\kappa_{1})+\left(\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\alpha\kappa_{1}}+\frac{c}{\kappa_{1}}\right)\mu.\\
\end{split}

From these equations, we get expressions of
\gamma
and
\mu

\gamma=\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle(c-\kappa_{1}^{2})}{\kappa_{1}(c+(1+\alpha)\kappa_{1}^{2})}e_{1}(\kappa_{1}),\quad\mu=\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle(c-\kappa_{1}^{2})}{\kappa_{1}(\kappa_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)c)}e_{2}(\kappa_{1}).

(3.4)

Since Thm.
1.1
is local, we can assume that the denominators in (
3.4
) are not zero in some open set of
\Omega
because, otherwise,
\kappa_{1}
would be a constant function. This also holds in the next computations. We are also using in (

\alpha
is arbitrary in the denominator of
\gamma

Mathematica

[
16

We differentiate
\gamma
and
\mu
in (
3.4
) with respect to
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
and next, we compare with the corresponding derivatives given in (
2.6
). Notice that
e_{1}(\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle)=\gamma
and

. Then the derivatives of the functions (

) are

\begin{split}e_{1}(\gamma)&=\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle(c-\kappa_{1}^{2})}{\kappa_{1}(c+(1+\alpha)\kappa_{1}^{2})}e_{11}(\kappa_{1})+\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle(2+\alpha)(\kappa_{1}^{2}-3c)}{(c+(1+\alpha)\kappa_{1}^{2})^{2}}e_{1}(\kappa_{1})^{2}\\
e_{1}(\mu)&=\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle(c-\kappa_{1}^{2})}{\kappa_{1}(\kappa_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)c)}e_{12}(\kappa_{1})+\frac{\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle(2+\alpha)(c+\kappa_{1}^{2})(\kappa_{1}^{2}-(3+\alpha)c)}{(\kappa_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)c)^{2}(c+(1+\alpha)\kappa_{1}^{2})}e_{1}(\kappa_{1})e_{2}(\kappa_{1})\\

We work with equations (
2.6

\omega(e_{1})=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}e_{2}(\kappa_{1}),\quad\omega(e_{2})=-\frac{c}{\kappa_{1}(\kappa_{1}^{2}-c)}e_{1}(\kappa_{1}).\\

Inserting this in (

\left\{\begin{split}e_{1}(\gamma)&=\frac{\mu\kappa_{1}}{\kappa_{1}^{2}-c}e_{2}(\kappa_{1})+\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2}+c}{\alpha}\langle\Phi,\vec{a}\rangle\\

(3.6)

From (

(3.7)

We distinguish the cases that

(1)

Case

(3.8)

Differentiating with respect to

Simplifying by

On the other hand, using (

\begin{split}e_{22}(\kappa_{1})&=\frac{-2\kappa_{1}^{4}+(\alpha+6)\kappa_{1}^{2}c+\alpha c^{2}}{\kappa_{1}\left(c-\kappa_{1}^{2}\right)\left(\kappa_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)c\right)}e_{2}(\kappa_{1})^{2}-\frac{c\left(\kappa_{1}^{2}+c\right)\left(\kappa_{1}^{2}+(1+\alpha)c\right)}{\alpha\kappa_{1}\left(\kappa_{1}^{2}-c\right)}\\

5.
