Nanoscale synthesis and mechanical behaviour of polymeric Cu–Ni nanocomposites for antimicrobial coating
Authors: Rucha N Thaker, Suranjana V Mayani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe manuscript illustrates the advancement of polymeric Cu–Ni nanocomposite thin-film coating with enhanced mechanical and antimicrobial properties. In our work, Cu–Ni bimetallic nanoparticles were synthesized and uniformly embedded in a butyl acrylate-methyl methacrylate polymer matrix, forming a strong yet functional soft coating material. Physicochemical characterizations have been carried out through SEM-EDS, TEM, PXRD and TGA confirming the morphology, composition and thermal stability of the nanoparticles. The resulting thin-film coating was further examined for surface morphology and elemental dispersion (SEM–EDS), average coating thickness of ± 0.513 mm, cross-sectional area of 5.561 mm² and nanoscale mechanical performance, including average tensile strength, peak load respectively as 1.183 MPa, 1.3 N and maximum Young’s modulus at 43.474 MPa. Surface wettability was assessed through contact angle measurements along with chemical resistance tests to determine durability of the coating. Moreover, antimicrobial assay has been investigated for the nano particles and coating material embedded with nanoparticle, resulting in highly effective activity. In conclusion, polymeric Cu–Ni nanocomposites show an excellent mechanical strength and operative antimicrobial performance, making them suitable for strong antimicrobial coating.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Polymeric Cu–Ni nanocomposite, Antimicrobial, Coating, Durability, Mechanical properties

Introduction

The existence of pathogenic microbes in the environment can lead to various human, economic and social problems [1]. The spread of infectious diseases among mankind is, in fact, strongly influenced by the various material surfaces that are contaminated by microbes [2]. Antimicrobial coatings have become a cutting-edge approach to prevent surface contamination and microbial growth in a variety of settings, such as homes, food processing facilities and healthcare facilities. To stop bacteria, fungi and viruses from growing on treated surfaces, the mechanism of coatings works by incorporating active agents like nanoparticles or nanostructured polymers that interfere with microbial membranes, DNA, or metabolic processes [3]. Metals and their metal oxides (like silver, zinc and copper), and carbon-based materials are among the wide range of nanomaterials that are examined [4]. These substances are potentially studied due to the great antibacterial qualities that would help them fight off a diversity of microorganisms such as fungi, bacteria and viruses [5]. As a result, nanoparticles (NPs) are being used more and more as a promising substitute for antibiotics and antifungal agents in order to target the pathogens [6]. These monometallic nanoparticles (MNPs), also known as metal-based NPs, have favourable physicochemical characteristics that enhance their antibacterial capacity. Because of their high surface area to volume ratio, leading surface atoms to control the reaction to load, NPs have mechanical behaviours that are significantly different from those of their bulk counterparts [7]. A material composed of two metals, bimetallic nanoparticles (BNPs), exhibits unique properties as a result of their synergistic effects [8]. Even though the two-component synthesis is a bit similar to that of their monometallic counterparts, they cooperate to display a range of novel features and uses [9]. The researchers have recently focused a lot of attention on BNPs because of their catalytic, optical, electrical and magnetic capabilities [10]. Unlike traditional monometallic systems, the use of bimetallic nanoparticles by combining two different metals within a polymeric matrix takes advantage of synergistic interactions to enhance antibacterial effectiveness. By dispersing nanoparticles throughout the matrix, forming a nanocomposite, a high-performance composite material that incorporates nanotechnology and combines several properties to produce improved mechanical strength, thermal conductivity, and electrical properties for a variety of applications [11]. These composites can break down microbial membranes and generate reactive oxygen species, offering a broader spectrum of physicochemical properties and antimicrobial mechanisms that inactivate various harmful bacteria [12]. Chen and other researchers have demonstrated that germs such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus and Listeria monocytogenes can persist on kitchen appliance surfaces for weeks, facilitating the risk of cross-contamination [13]. The use of nanoparticles to give surface coatings inherent antibacterial activity, a continuous, non-leaching barrier against bacteria, fungi and viruses is growing [14]. Nanoparticles included in polymeric matrices to further improve their durability and processability, enabling the production of flexible films, sprayable paints, or dip-coated components that maintain their antimicrobial efficacy across numerous use cycles [15].

The easy processability of polymer matrices and the physicochemical characteristics that result from two distinct metals coexisting at the nanoscale are incorporated in polymeric bimetallic nanocomposites [16]. While the polymer network offers mechanical strength and is tailored for a particular form, which is thin-films, embedding these BNPs in polymeric scaffolds also stabilizes the particles against oxidation and aggregation [17]. Polymer nanocomposites (PNCs) combine the mechanical and conductivity benefits of nanoparticles, which are distributed as spheroids, fibers, or platelets, with the lightweight, flexible properties of polymers. Because of the increased load transfer and interfacial interactions caused by the nanoparticle’s large surface area, nanocomposites have emerged as a revolutionary class of materials in a variety of industries, including electronics and biomedical [18].

This study presents a novel polymeric Cu-Ni bimetallic nanocomposite coating based on a butyl acrylate-methyl methacrylate (BA-MMA) copolymer matrix with an average coating thickness of ± 0.513 mm. Unlike previously reported Cu-Ni antimicrobial systems, the present work combines Cu-Ni bimetallic nanoparticles with a BA-MMA copolymer to achieve a unique combination of antimicrobial, mechanical, and surface-wetting properties. The coating exhibits excellent antibacterial and antifungal activity, along with a hydrophilic surface nature that can be beneficial for specific hygienic and functional applications. The embedded Cu-Ni nanoparticles form a structured reinforcing network that enhances mechanical performance, while the BA-MMA matrix provides ductility and crack resistance. Notably, the nanocomposite demonstrates an elongation break of 445.961%, indicating exceptional flexibility while maintaining coating integrity. Furthermore, the coating shows excellent chemical resistance, and good processability, making it suitable for thin-film protective coatings on appliances and high-touch surfaces. The synergistic combination of high flexibility, antimicrobial efficacy, hydrophilicity, and durability distinguishes the present work from previously reported Cu-Ni nanocomposite coatings. This work provides a specific advancement by demonstrating that controlled polydispersity allows smaller Cu–Ni nanoparticles to perfectly nest within the interstitial voids of the polymer matrix, optimizing structural packing density to simultaneously maximize both the coating’s tensile strength and its sustained, dual-ion antimicrobial performance.

Experimental Methods

Materials

Reagents used are copper (II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2.2H2O) (Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, 98%), nickel (II) chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2.6H2O) (Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, 98%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, 97%), ethylene glycol (C2H6O2) (Sigma Aldrich, 99.8%) and hydrazine hydrate (N2H4.H2O) (Sigma Aldrich), sodium lauryl Sulphate (Needle shaped) (Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai), potassium persulphate (KPS) (Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, 98%), butyl acrylate (BA) (Sigma Aldrich, 99%) and methyl methacrylate (MMA) (Sigma Aldrich, 99%).

Synthesis of Cu-Ni bimetallic nanoparticles

The BNPs were synthesized by dissolving 3 g of copper chloride and nickel chloride salts in 50 mL of ionised water to form a homogeneous solution. The solution was then heated to 50 °C under constant stirring using a magnetic stirrer. Added 1.5 mL of ethylene glycol to the solution, followed by the same amount of dropwise addition of hydrazine hydrate as a reducing agent. The mixture was stirred for 10–15 min to ensure the complete reduction of metal ions. Sodium hydroxide (1 N) was then added to adjust the pH to 12, resulting in the formation of black precipitate. The mixture was then again stirred for an additional 3 h to ensure completion of the reaction. The resulting black precipitate was filtered and washed with acetone thoroughly. The obtained nanoparticles were dried overnight at room temperature. (yield-1.14 g)

Preparation of polymeric pre-emulsion solution

A polymeric pre-emulsion was prepared by dissolving 0.5 g of sodium lauryl sulphate as an emulsifier and 0.057 g potassium persulphate (KPS) as an initiator in 20 mL of distilled water. The mixture was stirred until complete dissolution of the components. A monomer mixture consisting of 1 mL methyl methacrylate (MMA) and 1 mL butyl acrylate (BA) was then added dropwise to the aqueous solution under constant stirring, acting as a hardening agent. The mixture was stirred for 2.5 h to facilitate emulsion polymerization.

2.4 Incorporation of Cu-Ni nanoparticles into polymeric matrix to prepare the coating material.

2.5 g of Cu-Ni bimetallic nanoparticles and 0.5 g sodium lauryl sulphate were dispersed in approximately 40 mL of distilled water and sonicated for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. A monomer mixture consisting of 1 mL methyl methacrylate (MMA) and 1mL of butyl acrylate (BA) was then added and the mixture was stirred for 30 min at 70 °C. Then 0.057 g of potassium persulfate (KPS) was subsequently added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min at 75 °C. A polymeric pre-emulsion solution of approximately 25–30 mL was then added dropwise over a period of one hour and the reaction mixture was stirred for 2 h at 80 °C. The resulting nanocomposite coating was centrifuged for 60 min and then poured it in a mould. The synthesis method was found to be reproducible, producing nanoparticles with consistent characteristics across repeated experiments. Characterization techniques

The synthesized polymeric Cu–Ni nanocomposite was analysed via a range of spectroscopic techniques. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) of a JEOL 6390LA (Japan) was applied to know morphology and elemental composition. X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker AXS, Germany) was used to identify phases and analyse the samples crystallographically. The morphological characteristics of the synthesized nanoparticles were examined using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) performed on a JEM-2100 microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan). Thermal stability and decomposition behaviour of the samples were investigated using Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) performed on a PerkinElmer STA 6000 simultaneous thermal analyser (PerkinElmer Inc., USA). Mechanical testing was performed using a Universal Testing Machine (Dak System Inc., Series 7200, 5 Ton capacity). The tests were conducted under controlled loading conditions to evaluate the mechanical properties of the samples. Contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate the surface wettability characteristics of the samples using a Kyowa Interface Measurement and Analysis System.

TEM

TEM analysis revealed well-dispersed nanoparticles with nearly spherical shaped nanoparticles. High-resolution images showed clear lattice fringes, confirming good crystallinity. TEM analysis revealed that the Cu–Ni bimetallic nanoparticles (Fig. 4) are nearly spherical and uniformly dispersed, with minimal agglomeration. The particles exhibit a narrow size distribution in the nanometre range ~ 5–9 nm confirming effective nucleation and growth during synthesis. The contrast variation observed in the TEM images suggests the formation of bimetallic Cu–Ni nanostructures rather than isolated monometallic particles. Overall, the TEM results validate the successful synthesis of well-defined Cu–Ni bimetallic nanoparticles [20]. The TEM of nanocomposite coating (Fig. 4) shows that the mixtures of nano particles pack in organized way perfectly inside the polymer coating material. The image reveals a nanocomposite coating composed of interconnected, irregularly shaped nanosheets with localized agglomeration, forming a porous network-like morphology. It demonstrated a relatively narrow nanoscale distribution, indicating uniform incorporation of nanoparticles within the nanocomposite coating despite partial aggregation.

Cu-Ni BNPs and the nanocomposite coating’s XRD patterns are displayed in Fig. 5. Using Bragg’s law, the lattice constant was derived from the associated Miller indices (hkl) and peak angular positions (2θ).

where θ is the peak angle for each reflection (hkl) and λ is the XRD wavelength [19]. The 2θ reflections at 43.6°, 50.79°, and 70.37° are indexed to the face cubic crystal structure and are identified as the (111), (200), and (220) planes of Cu-Ni nanoparticles and nanocomposite coating in comparison to the literature. In contrast, the nanocomposite coating exhibits a broad amorphous diffraction at approximately, extending roughly from 15° to 25°, which is characteristic of the polymer matrix and indicates its predominantly amorphous nature. The average crystallite size calculated using the scherrer equation was approximately 19 nm for the Cu–Ni BNPs. Based on the observed peak broadening in the coating pattern, the crystallite size of the nanocomposite coating was estimated to be in the range of 22 nm. These results confirm the effective synthesis of highly crystalline Cu–Ni bimetallic nanoparticles and their successful incorporation into the polymer matrix, leading to the formation of a stable nanocomposite coating possessing both crystalline metallic and amorphous polymeric characteristics.

TGA

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the Cu–Ni bimetallic nanoparticles show (Fig. 6) a gradual weight loss with increasing temperature, indicating good thermal stability. An initial minor weight loss below ~ 100 °C is ascribed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture. The continuous weight loss up to ~ 250 °C may be associated with the disintegration of residual organic species from the synthesis process. Beyond this temperature, the weight stabilizes with a slight increase at higher temperatures, which can be attributed to surface oxidation of Cu–Ni nanoparticles. Overall, the limited weight loss (~ 6–7%) demonstrates the enhanced thermal stability of the Cu–Ni bimetallic nanoparticles. On the other hand, in Cu-Ni polymeric composite, thermo-gravimetric curves show a significant weight losses of about 39.4% occurred from 28 to 900 °C weight loss can be mainly attributed to the decomposition of polymer (Fig. 7).

Mechanical properties

The Dak System Inc., Series 7200, 5 Ton capacity equipment was used to conduct the universal testing machine (UTM) test in order to determine the mechanical stability of the thin-film coating and to learn more about the material’s tensile characteristics. The manufactured film’s maximum tensile strength, as determined by measuring its response to tension, was 1.278 MPa, indicating significant stability for applications involving the preparation of analytical samples. While the maximum cross-sectional area, peak load and Young’s modulus determined for the film are 6.337 mm2, 8.1 N and 43.474 MPa, respectively. While the elongation break (%) shows a maximum of 445.961%, indicating an exceptionally excellent flexible thin-film coating, it demonstrates rubber-like behaviour. The value on comparison with the literature suggests that it far exceeds typical rigid coatings (< 5–10%) and aligns with elastomeric or highly plasticized polymer films (300–900% range for materials like polyethylene or TPU) [21]. The value of elongation break indicates that it can be widely used in appliances, electronics, stretchable packaging and biomedical applications where crack resistance under deformation is critical. Chemical resistance

The chemical resistance of the polymeric Cu–Ni nanocomposite coating was rigorously evaluated by immersing the coated samples in various corrosive media, including concentrated strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, and HNO₃), weak acid (acetic acid), aqua regia, strong bases (NaOH and KOH), and NaCl solution (Table 4). The coating remained intact and exhibited excellent resistance against all tested chemicals, showing no visible signs of degradation, cracking, or dissolution during the 48-h exposure period (Fig. 8). The only observable change was a slight variation in surface colour. To further assess the effect of aggressive chemical environments, SEM and EDS analyses were performed on samples immersed in H₂SO₄, aqua regia, and NaOH solutions. SEM micrographs revealed that the surface morphology remained largely unchanged after exposure, while EDS spectra confirmed the retention of the characteristic elemental composition, indicating negligible material loss or compositional changes (Fig. 9; Table 5). These complementary microscopic and compositional analyses provide qualitative evidence for the coating’s chemical stability. Notably, exposure to acidic media and aqua regia at elevated temperatures (40–45 °C) resulted in noticeable degradation after 24 h, indicating that prolonged exposure to highly aggressive acidic environments under thermal stress can compromise the coating integrity.

Contact Angle Analysis

The interaction of water with the surface of the fabricated coating was examined via contact angle measurement of the surface at different locations on the same membrane. The obtained contact angles are 35.4°, 37.7°, and 31.4° with 34.8° ± 3.2° resultant as show in fig- 8. This indicates the hydrophilic nature of the coating with good surface uniformity, as the variation is very low. Due to the excellent water attraction property, the spreading of water droplets will be more sufficient, which is also helpful to provide proper contact between microorganisms present on the contaminated surface. The hydrophilicity and antibacterial efficacy of nanocomposite coating are strongly related. As hydrophilic surfaces minimize hydrophobic interactions between microbial cell membranes and the coated surface, they often prevent effective bacterial adhesion and biofilm development. Additionally, the enhanced wettability increases the contact between bacteria and the antimicrobial Cu–Ni nanoparticles dispersed throughout the coating, encouraging the release of antimicrobial ions and boosting the effectiveness of microbial inactivation [22].

Antimicrobial property

The antibacterial activity of the nanocomposite was evaluated using broth microdilution method against Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus. The inhibitory concentration recorded for Escherichia coli was 2.23 µg/mL, 2.85 µg/mL for Pseudomonas aeruginosa species, 3.15 µg/mL for Staphylococcus aureus and 2.98 µg/mL for Bacillus cereus in a 10 µg/mL concentration (Fig-9). For control, polymer exhibited MIC values of 10, 23, 12, 16, and 30 µg/mL against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, B. cereus, and C. albicans, respectively, while the Cu- Ni BNPs control showed MIC values of 30, 59, 18, 10, and 110 µg/mL. The results demonstrate that composite coating is more effective against the gram-negative bacteria E. coli compared to P. aeruginosa, which is known for its intrinsic resistance due to its strong outer membrane structure. While among gram-positive bacteria, similar strong inhibitory activity was observed against both Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus, suggesting that the coating effectively disrupts bacterial cell wall integrity. The MIC of Candida albicans when treated with the coating film was noted as 1.84 µg/mL (Fig. 10), indicating that only a minor amount of the material is required to suppress fungal growth completely. A lower MIC value suggests high potency, confirming that the nanocomposite possess excellent antifungal activity. The observed antimicrobial activity may be attributed to Cu-Ni nanoparticle-mediated metal ion release and ROS generation, which have been reported to disrupt microbial membranes. Although the Cu–Ni nanocomposite coating demonstrated markedly improved antimicrobial activity compared with the polymer and Cu- Ni BNPs. Therefore, the desired MIC values highlight the potential of the synthesized Cu-Ni polymeric nanocomposite as a highly effective antimicrobial coating material suitable for biomedical and surface protection application.

Growth curve analysis

The effect of nanocomposite concentration and exposure time on microbial growth was evaluated using growth curve analysis. The size, structure, layers, purity, and internal arrangement all affect the microbial inhibition; different sets of results are obtained, and these results are correlated with the properties of the nanocomposite. In the presence of the nanocomposite coating (10 µg/mL), all tested microorganisms exhibited suppressed growth throughout the 10 h incubation period, as evidenced by the reduced OD600 values compared with the untreated controls. The inhibition effect became more pronounced after 4 h of incubation, indicating that the nanocomposite effectively restricted microbial proliferation during the exponential growth phase. At 10 µg/mL of concentration, however, the S. aureus strain showed superior inhibition over the E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and B. cereus strains among the strains utilized. This suggests that Cu-Ni nanocomposite coating is more effective against gram-positive S. aureus than against gram-negative bacteria E. coli and P. aeruginosa, as well as B. cereus. The activity is shown in Fig. 11. The growth curve analysis result supports the antifungal results, where significant inhibition was observed in C. albicans strain using the nanocomposite coating, as demonstrated in Fig. 12. This suggests that Cu-Ni polymeric composite exerts good antifungal inhibition. When correlated with the MIC value, it confirms that the material is highly effective against this fungus. Overall, the coating demonstrates strong antifungal activity against C. albicans, potentially even better than its activity against some of the bacterial strains tested. The observed inhibition may be attributed to interactions between the Cu–Ni nanoparticles and microbial cells, leading to membrane damage, disruption of cellular metabolism, and inhibition of cell proliferation. Overall, the growth kinetic study demonstrates the sustained antimicrobial activity of the Cu–Ni nanocomposite coating against both bacterial and fungal pathogens Figs 13, 14.

Conclusion

The synthesis of polymeric Cu-Ni nanocomposites yielded a promising material for antimicrobial coatings, exhibiting enhanced mechanical properties and potential bioactivity. The synthesized nanocomposites exhibited an average tensile strength of 1.183 MPa, a maximum Young’s modulus of 43.474 MPa, and a maximum peak load of 8.1 N, indicating improved mechanical strength and stiffness. While considering its elongation break property, it shows excellent flexibility and rubber like behaviour of the nanocomposite coating. The nature of the thin-film coating material is hydrophilic with good surface uniformity. The coating surface and microorganisms will provide perfect exposure eventually leading to kills or inhibits microbial growth. The incorporation of Cu-Ni nanoparticles not only enhanced the mechanical properties but also imparted antimicrobial efficacy against Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus, with a significant reduction in bacterial growth. While the fungus Candida albicans shows better results on comparison with bacteria. This suggests that polymeric Cu-Ni nanocomposites are a viable option for developing durable, antimicrobial coatings for various industries. The coatings potential applications include medical devices, hospital surfaces, and food storage surfaces, where reduced microbial contamination is crucial. Further studies are needed to optimize the nanoparticles loading, explore the coating’s long-term stability, and assess its toxicity and environmental impact.
