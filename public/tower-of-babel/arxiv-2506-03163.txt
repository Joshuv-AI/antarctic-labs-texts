Causal Discovery in Dynamic Fading Wireless Networks
Oluwaseyi Giwa

Abstract
Dynamic causal discovery in wireless networks is essential due to evolving interference, fading, and mobility, which complicate traditional static causal models. This paper addresses causal inference challenges in dynamic fading wireless environments by proposing a sequential regression-based algorithm with a novel application of the NOTEARS acyclicity constraint, enabling efficient online updates. We derive theoretical lower and upper bounds on the detection delay required to identify structural changes, explicitly quantifying their dependence on network size, noise variance, and fading severity. Monte Carlo simulations validate these theoretical results, demonstrating linear increases in detection delay with network size, quadratic growth with noise variance, and inverse-square dependence on the magnitude of structural changes. Our findings provide rigorous theoretical insights and practical guidelines for designing robust online causal inference mechanisms to maintain network reliability under nonstationary wireless conditions.

I
Introduction

Wireless communication networks have become increasingly complex due to rapidly growing traffic demands, dynamic channel conditions, and heterogeneous interference patterns. Identifying and understanding causal relationships among network components, such as transmitters, receivers, and interference sources, are crucial for optimizing resource allocation, improving spectral efficiency, and ensuring robust communication performance
(
pearl2009
;
shimizu2006
;
spirtes1993
)
. Traditional static causal inference methods assume fixed causal structures and stationary environments, which fail to capture the inherent dynamics of modern wireless networks. In reality, factors such as fading channels, user mobility, and fluctuating traffic loads cause causal structures within the network to evolve continuously, necessitating dynamic causal discovery approaches
(
zhang2017
)
.

The structural causal model (SCM) paradigm provides a formal foundation for representing cause-effect relationships in complex systems and has been extended to handle temporal dynamics
(
dibbendu2025
)
. Unlike static causal graphs, dynamic causal discovery methods allow the underlying directed acyclic graph (DAG) to evolve over time, capturing nonstationary dependencies. For instance, Song et al. introduced time-varying dynamic Bayesian networks (TV-DBNs) to model “structurally varying directed dependency structures” in changing environments
(
song2009
)
. Such frameworks address the challenge of inferring causality under shifting conditions by leveraging temporal regularities; some approaches impose smoothness constraints on the evolution of causal parameters to enable reliable estimation of time-varying causal models
(
zhang2017b
)
. These foundational works in causal inference provide algorithms and theory for learning and tracking evolving causal structures (e.g., via kernel-reweighted regression or online updates), laying the groundwork for applications in dynamic domains.

Wireless communication networks exemplify a nonstationary domain where fading channels, mobility, and interference continually alter the causal structure of signal interactions. Recent research has begun to fuse causal discovery techniques with wireless network modeling to better capture and adapt to these changes. For example,
Laghate and Cabric
employ an asymmetric Granger causality test on transmission timelines to infer directed links in a time-multiplexed network, effectively learning which transmitters cause interference at which receivers
(
mihir2018
)
. In cellular systems, unsupervised causal learning has been used to identify performance drivers: Sousa
et al.
apply conditional independence tests to LTE data and find that uplink throughput degradations were causally linked to resource allocation factors (e.g., reserved control channel elements)
(
marco2023
)
. Causal modeling is also being integrated into wireless optimization frameworks—Zhang
et al.
develop a resource allocation scheme that embeds an SCM of time-varying channel conditions into a Q-learning algorithm for adaptive interference management
(
yuzhu2024
)
. More broadly, the importance of causal reasoning for next-generation networks has been highlighted in recent vision papers: adopting causal discovery and inference is seen as key to building explainable, resilient 6G systems capable of dynamic adaptability and intelligent interference management
(
christo2024
)
.

However, tracking these evolving causal structures presents significant theoretical and practical challenges. Fading-induced variability in wireless channels, temporal nonstationarity of network interactions, and partial observability due to limited measurement capability complicate causal inference processes
(
henk2009
)
. This paper addresses these issues by proposing a theoretical framework specifically designed for causal discovery in dynamic fading environments. Our key contributions include establishing novel identifiability criteria for dynamic causal graphs, deriving theoretical bounds on the minimum observation window and sampling rates necessary for detecting structural changes, and quantifying how network size, fading severity, and noise levels influence these detection bounds.

II
Problem Formulation

We model the dynamic causal structure of a wireless network as a time-indexed Structural Causal Model (SCM). Let

V_{t}=\{V_{t,1},\dots,V_{t,n}\}

(1)

denote the set of observed random variables at time
t
, representing quantities such as channel-state information (CSI), interference levels, throughput, and transmit power. The evolution of each variable is governed by the structural equations

V_{t,i}\;=\;f_{t,i}\bigl(\mathrm{pa}_{t,i},\,U_{t,i}\bigr),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(2)

where
\mathrm{pa}_{t,i}\subseteq V_{t}
is the set of direct causal parents of
V_{t,i}
at time
t
, and
U_{t,i}
are independent exogenous noise variables capturing measurement noise and unobserved influences.

To capture the slowly-varying nature of the wireless environment, we impose a bounded drift condition on the structural functions:

\bigl\|f_{t+1,i}-f_{t,i}\bigr\|\;\leq\;\delta,\quad\forall\,t,\;i,

(3)

where
\delta>0
is a small constant quantifying the maximum change in causal mechanisms per time step.

II-A

Assumptions

•

Smoothness
: The functions
\{f_{t,i}\}
vary continuously in
t
, with infrequent abrupt changes in the underlying DAG.

•

Noise
: The exogenous terms
U_{t,i}
are i.i.d. with
\mathbb{E}[U_{t,i}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(U_{t,i})=\sigma^{2}<\infty
.

•

Causal Sufficiency
: All relevant confounders are included in
V_{t}
, so that no hidden common causes remain.

II-B

Key Question

Let
G_{t}=(V_{t},E_{t})
be the causal graph at time
t
, with adjacency matrix
A_{t}
. We wish to detect structural changes, i.e. edge additions or deletions, by testing at each time
t
:

\begin{cases}H_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A_{t}=A_{t-1},\\
H_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A_{t}\neq A_{t-1}.\end{cases}

Define the detection delay
\tau
as the minimum number of observations after a change at time
t^{*}
required to reject
H_{0}
with high probability. Our objective is to derive explicit lower and upper bounds on
\tau
as functions of

n,\;\delta,\;\sigma^{2},\;\text{and channel-variability parameters.}

III
Theoretical Framework

III-A

Causal Graph Dynamics

We represent the evolving causal structure of the wireless network as a sequence of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)
\{G_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
, where each

G_{t}=(V,E_{t}),\quad V=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{n}\},

(4)

and
E_{t}\subseteq V\times V
may change over time. Let
A_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix of
G_{t}
, with

[A_{t}]_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }V_{i}\to V_{j}\in E_{t},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5)

Define the structural change at time
t
by

\Delta A_{t}=A_{t}-A_{t-1},

(6)

so that
\Delta A_{t}[i,j]=1
indicates an edge addition, and
\Delta A_{t}[i,j]=-1
an edge deletion
(
grzegorczyk2008
;
song2009
)
.

III-B

Metrics of Detection

Let
t^{*}
be the (unknown) time of a true structural change. We perform sequential hypothesis tests:

\begin{cases}H_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A_{t}=A_{t-1},\\
H_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A_{t}\neq A_{t-1}.\end{cases}

Define the
detection delay

\tau
as

\tau=\inf\{\,t-t^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}t>t^{*},\ \text{reject }H_{0}\},

(7)

and two error metrics:

False Alarm Rate (FAR)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\text{reject }H_0 \mid H_0\text{ true}),

(8)

\displaystyle\text{Missed Detection Rate (MDR)}(T)

\displaystyle=\Pr(\tau> T \mid H_1\text{ true}).

(9)

Our goal is to design tests that minimize
\mathbb{E}[\tau]
, subject to prescribed bounds on FAR and MDR
(
tartakovsky2014
)
.

III-C

Identifiability Conditions

We adapt standard causal identifiability criteria to the dynamic setting:

•

Faithfulness over Intervals:
For each stationary interval (no changes), the joint distribution
P(V_{t})
is faithful to
G_{t}
, i.e.,

(V_{i}\perp V_{j}\mid S)_{P(V_{t})}\iff\text{$V_{i}$ and $V_{j}$ are d-separated by $S$ in $G_{t}$.}

•

Minimal Change Principle:
Structural changes are sparse:

\|\Delta A_{t}\|_{0}\leq k,\quad k\ll n^{2}.

•

Stationarity between Change Points:
Conditional distributions remain fixed within intervals:

P(V_{t,i}\mid\mathrm{pa}_{t,i})=P(V_{t^{\prime},i}\mid\mathrm{pa}_{t^{\prime},i}),\quad\forall\,t,t^{\prime}\text{ in same interval.}

These conditions ensure that dynamic causal discovery algorithms can distinguish true structural modifications from random fluctuations
(
pearl2009
;
peters2017
)
.

IV
Detection Algorithms

IV-A

Proposed Methodology

We learn the time-varying adjacency matrix
W(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by solving at each time
t
the constrained optimization
(
bond2019
;
hallac2017
;
zheng2018
)

\displaystyle W(t)=\arg\min_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|X(t)-X(t)\,W\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\;+\;\lambda_{1}\|W\|_{1}

(10)

\displaystyle+\;\tfrac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\bigl\|W-W(t-1)\bigr\|_{F}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle h\bigl(W\bigr)=\mathrm{tr}\!\bigl(e^{W\circ W}\bigr)-d=0\,,

where:

•

X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
is the (possibly mini-batch) data matrix at time
t
.

•

\lambda_{1}\|W\|_{1}
induces sparsity in the graph.

•

\lambda_{2}\|W-W(t-1)\|_{F}^{2}
enforces smooth temporal evolution.

•

h(W)=0
is the differentiable
NOTEARS
acyclicity constraint
(
zheng2018
)
.

We solve (
10
) via an augmented-Lagrangian method, warm-started at
W(t-1)
, yielding efficient online updates.

IV-B

Statistical Framework

To decide whether an edge
j\!\to\!i
has appeared or disappeared, we employ classical hypothesis tests on the estimated coefficients
\hat{W}_{ji}(t)

(
han2024
;
bardet2012
)
:

Edge Appearance (
0\to 1
).

H_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,W_{ji}(t)=0\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,W_{ji}(t)\neq 0.

Compute the
t
-statistic

T_{ji}(t)=\frac{\hat{W}_{ji}(t)}{\mathrm{SE}\bigl(\hat{W}_{ji}(t)\bigr)},

and reject
H_{0}
if
\lvert T_{ji}(t)\rvert>t_{1-\alpha_{\mathrm{add}}/2,\nu}
, indicating a new edge at confidence
1-\alpha_{\mathrm{add}}
(
peters2017
)
.

Edge Disappearance (
1\to 0
).

We compare the full model to one with
W_{ji}(t)=0
via an
F
-test:

F=\frac{\bigl(\mathrm{RSS}_{\!\text{no }j}-\mathrm{RSS}_{\!\text{with }j}\bigr)/1}{\mathrm{RSS}_{\!\text{with }j}/(N-p-1)},

and remove the edge if
F<F_{1,\;N-p-1;\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rem}}}
(
tartakovsky2014
)
.

CUSUM Change-Point Test.

We track

S(t)=\max\bigl\{0,\,S(t-1)+\ell(r(t))-c\bigr\},

with
\ell(r)=\|r\|^{2}
and threshold
\eta
. A rise
S(t)>\eta
signals a structural shift, triggering more aggressive updates.

Together, the above ensure that only statistically significant coefficient changes result in graph modifications, controlling false alarms and missed detections.

V
Theoretical Analysis

V-A

Lower Bound on Detection Delay

Under the Gaussian noise assumption with variance
\sigma^{2}
, and denoting by
\Delta
the minimum detectable change in edge–weight magnitude, classical sequential detection theory
(
tartakovsky2014
)
yields

\tau_{\mathrm{LB}}\;\geq\;\frac{2\,\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\;\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\bigr),

(11)

where
\alpha
is the prescribed false-alarm probability and
\tau
is the number of post-change samples required to achieve detection with confidence
1-\beta
.

V-B

Upper Bound on Detection Delay

For the CUSUM-based residual test with threshold
\eta
, letting

\mu=\mathbb{E}[\|r(t)\|^{2}\mid H_{1}]
and

c=\mathbb{E}[\|r(t)\|^{2}\mid H_{0}]
,
we obtain the upper bound

\tau_{\mathrm{UB}}\;\leq\;\frac{\eta}{\mu-c}\,.

(12)

Tightness of this bound follows when
\eta
and
c
are optimally chosen to match the pre- and post-change residual distributions.

V-C

Impact of Noise and Network Size

If the minimal change scales as
\Delta\propto 1/\sqrt{n}
in an
n
–node network, then

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{LB}}

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}\,\frac{\sigma^{2}\,n}{\delta^{2}}\;\ln\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\bigr),

(13)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{UB}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\,\frac{\eta\,n}{\delta-c^{\prime}},

(14)

where
\delta
quantifies the typical edge-weight jump, and
C_{1},C_{2}
are constants depending on fading statistics and chosen thresholds. Both bounds grow roughly linearly in network size
n
and noise variance
\sigma^{2}
, highlighting the trade-off between detection latency and network scale.

VII
Discussion and Conclusion

This study developed a rigorous theoretical and algorithmic framework for causal discovery in dynamic fading wireless networks. By extending structural causal models (SCMs) to explicitly handle evolving network topologies, we derived both lower and upper theoretical bounds on the detection delay required to accurately identify causal structure changes. Our sequential regression-based detection algorithm leverages sparsity, temporal smoothness, and statistical hypothesis testing to reliably detect structural shifts in real-time.

Simulation results confirm our theoretical predictions, illustrating that detection delays increase linearly with network size and significantly with noise variance, while stronger structural changes reduce detection latency dramatically. These insights emphasize a critical trade-off between detection accuracy and speed under varying operational conditions, highlighting the importance of choosing appropriate thresholds and regularization parameters.

Practically, our results inform wireless network designers about the necessary sampling rates and observation windows to achieve desired detection reliability. While the current framework assumes causal sufficiency and moderate smoothness in structure evolution, future research could address partial observability, latent confounders, and more complex dynamic scenarios. Extending this framework to nonlinear or non-Gaussian settings could further enhance applicability in realistic wireless environments.
