Cementoplasty as an effective surgical strategy for reverse shoulder arthroplasty with contained tuberosity bone loss: clinical midterm outcomes
Authors: Francesco Franceschi, Antonio Caldaria, Matteo La Verde, Giorgio Carrozzi, Gian Mauro De Angelis D'Ossat, Luca La Verde, Alessio Palumbo, Edoardo Franze’, Luca Saccone, Angelo Baldari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProximal humeral bone loss represents a major challenge in both revisions reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) and primary arthroplasty for acute complex fractures. When anatomical reconstruction of the tuberosities is not feasible, metaphyseal deficiency compromises implant stability, deltoid wrapping, and functional outcomes. Cementoplasty technique has emerged as a practical and cost-effective method to restore proximal humeral anatomy without the need for structural allografts or custom implants. This study evaluates the functional outcomes of a cementoplasty technique in patients undergoing revision rTSA or primary arthroplasty for acute complex fractures with proximal humeral bone loss ≤5 cm.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients treated with cementoplasty between January 2017 and August 2025. Inclusion criteria were isolated proximal humeral bone loss ≤5 cm, intact deltoid function, and ≥6 months of follow-up. All procedures were performed by a single surgeon using a standardized reverse shoulder implant. Clinical outcomes, functional scores, radiographic findings, complications, and implant survivorship were assessed. Statistical analysis was performed using R, RStudio, and Rstatix. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and group comparisons used t-tests or chi-square/Fisher exact tests. Significance was set at P< .05.ResultsThirty-eight shoulders (24 revision rTSA; 14 acute multifragmentary proximal humeral fractures) were included (mean age 74.6 years; mean follow-up 27.3 months). Both groups demonstrated satisfactory recovery with no significant differences in pain, motion, or functional scores. Forward elevation exceeded 110° in both groups, and Constant and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons scores were comparable. No stem subsidence, loosening, or migration was observed. Mild nonprogressive radiolucent lines occurred in 10.5% of patients. Three complications (7.9%) were recorded. Implant survivorship was 94.7%.ConclusionPersonalized cementoplasty effectively restores proximal humeral anatomy and provides stable midterm fixation with favorable outcomes and low complication rates. It represents a practical alternative to allografts, metallic augments, or custom implants. Longer-term evaluation is warranted.Keywords: Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Cementoplasty, Proximal humeral fractures, Metaphyseal bone loss, Revision reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Deltoid wrapping, Proximal humeral volume restoration

With the increasing utilization of shoulder arthroplasty, the number of revisions reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) procedures has continued to rise. One of the most challenging aspects of revision surgery and of primary arthroplasty for acute complex fractures is the presence of proximal humeral bone loss. In cases of multifragmentary proximal humeral fractures where anatomic reconstruction and fixation of the tuberosities is not feasible, metaphyseal bone loss compromises implant stability, soft-tissue tensioning, and, ultimately, functional outcomes.4

Restoration of proximal humeral anatomy and optimization of deltoid wrapping are key principles in reverse shoulder biomechanics.11 Several reconstructive options have been described to address bone loss in this setting.10,16,17 Among them, metallic augments have been developed to address lateral proximal humeral bone defects, offering a modular reconstructive strategy that may represent a less complex and more cost-effective alternative to custom-made proximal humeral implants.

Cementoplasty reconstruction, also referred to as the "cement collar" technique, has also emerged as a practical and cost-effective method for managing contained epiphyseal defects ≤5 cm in height, without the need for structural allografts or custom implants.2,8

This technique involves the application of a circumferential cement mantle around the humeral stem, re-establishing the proximal humeral contour and enabling appropriate soft-tissue tensioning (Fig. 1). While originally developed for use in revision arthroplasty, recent reports have described its use in primary rTSA for comminuted fractures in which tuberosity healing is unlikely. Baldari et al2 demonstrated its utility in cases involving bone loss between 5% and 15% of total humeral length, allowing controlled intraoperative restoration of shoulder mechanics. Similarly, Boileau et al3 reported satisfactory implant stability and clinical outcomes when cementoplasty was used to manage metaphyseal defects of less than 5 cm.

Despite its expanding clinical application, there remains a paucity of data regarding the long-term outcomes, implant survivorship, and complication rates associated with cementoplasty in both revision and fracture settings.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the clinical outcomes of cementoplasty reconstruction in revision rTSA performed for either revision indications or acute proximal humeral fractures with multifragmentary nonreconstructable tuberosities and up to 5 cm of metaphyseal bone loss. Specifically, we assessed implant survivorship, functional outcomes, and post-operative complications.

Material and methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at San Pietro Fatebenefratelli Hospital in Rome (Italy), and included patients treated between January 2017 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee UniCamillus–Saint Camillus International University of Health Sciences, Rome, Italy (IRB #E00710-2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. All surgeries were performed by the same senior orthopedic surgeon (F.F.), specialized in shoulder arthroplasty. We initially reviewed all patients who underwent revision rTSA and primary reverse arthroplasty for fracture during the study period and subsequently selected those meeting predefined inclusion criteria.

These included patients who underwent a proximal humeral reconstruction using a circumferential cement collar, presence of isolated epiphyseal bone loss measuring ≤5 cm, intact deltoid function, and a minimum of 6 months of clinical and radiographic follow-up.

Exclusion criteria were patients treating with structural allografts or custom-made implants, patients without proximal humeral bone loss, presence of significant glenoid bone loss, incomplete follow-up, and patients with neurological disorders.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (R Core Team, 2020), RStudio (Rstudio Team, 2020), and the Rstatix package (Kassambara, 2023). Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (mean ± standard deviation) or ranges, as appropriate. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between the revision and fracture groups were conducted using the independent samples t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. A P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed with the patient in the beach-chair position, with the operative arm secured in a limb positioner (TRIMANO, Arthrex Inc., Naples, FL, USA), under general anesthesia combined with an interscalene nerve block. A standard deltopectoral approach was used for surgical exposure. In revision cases, the previous surgical incision was reused to minimize additional soft-tissue trauma and preserve vascular supply. All procedures were performed utilizing the same implant construct: Equinoxe Reverse 145° with fracture humeral stems (Exactech, Inc; Gainesville, FL).

In revision cases, previously implanted prosthetic components were carefully explanted. Residual cement, fibrotic tissue, and any osteolytic or granulation tissue were meticulously debrided. The humeral canal was then prepared according to standard technique as outlined in the manufacturers' guidelines.

Humeral stem height was determined based on a combination of anatomical and surgical landmarks, depending on the clinical scenario. When feasible, the contralateral humerus was used as a reference to achieve humeral lengthening between 2.0 and 2.5 cm relative to the unaffected side. In cases in which the contralateral humerus was unavailable or unreliable due to prior trauma or prior arthroplasty, stem height was established using the superior border of the pectoralis major tendon insertion on the humerus. As previously described in the literature, the average distance between this landmark and the top of a well-positioned humeral stem typically measures approximately 4 cm for onlay reverse shoulder stems and around 5 cm for inlay designs.6,7 This measurement was restored intraoperatively using a sterile ruler or a trial stem. A trial humeral stem was temporarily positioned at the intended height by securing it within the canal using sterile gauze. Implant stability was assessed at this stage, and once deemed adequate, the definitive stem was implanted. In all cases, trial components were used to restore appropriate height, version, and soft-tissue tension. Once optimal positioning was confirmed, the definitive humeral stem was implanted and cemented at the predetermined height and orientation, with the stem inserted at 20° of retroversion according to the standard surgical technique. Cement restrictors were placed within the humeral canal, followed by the application of a medium-viscosity, radiopaque bone cement impregnated with gentamicin.

Following stem fixation, a circumferential cement mantle was manually constructed around the proximal humeral metaphysis using the same cement previously applied within the canal. (Fig. 2).

Circumferential cement–mantle application.

The volume of cement was adjusted intraoperatively to restore the appropriate tuberosity height and lateral offset, using the contralateral humerus on preoperative radiographs as a reference.

Results

A total of 38 patients (38 shoulders) were included in the final analysis. The cohort consisted of 24 females and 14 males, with a mean age of 74.6 years (range, 65-87). The average follow-up duration was 27.3 months (range, 12-60 months).

Twenty-four patients (63.2%) underwent revision rTSA, while 14 (36.8%) were treated for acute multifragmentary proximal humeral fractures in which anatomical reconstruction of the tuberosities was not feasible.

At final follow-up, both groups demonstrated satisfactory functional recovery. The mean visual analog scale score for pain was 1.5 ± 1.0 in the fracture group and 1.6 ± 1.2 in the revision group (P= .81). Mean active forward elevation was 112° ± 15° in the fracture group and 111° ± 17° in the revision group (P= .78). Active abduction averaged 109° ± 14° for fractures and 107° ± 16° for revisions (P= .71). External rotation at 0° abduction measured 29° ± 10° in the fracture group and 27° ± 11° in the revision group (P= .63), while external rotation at 90° of abduction was 32° ± 9° and 30° ± 10°, respectively (P= .67). Internal rotation corresponded to a mean vertebral level of L3 in both groups, with no significant difference in distribution (P= .77). Functional scores were similarly favorable across both indications. The mean Constant score was 66.3 ± 10.8 in the fracture group and 65.3 ± 11.7 in the revision group (P= .68), while the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score averaged 79.2 ± 9.9 and 78.1 ± 11.1, respectively (P= .74).

Radiographic evaluation at last follow-up revealed no evidence of humeral stem subsidence, loosening, or migration. Mild metaphyseal radiolucent lines (<2 mm) were noted in 4 patients (10.5%), but these were nonprogressive and not associated with clinical symptoms. The cement mantles remained intact in all cases. Glenoid components appeared stable with no signs of loosening or tilt. Post-operative complications were observed in 3 cases (7.9%). One patient experienced a hematoma requiring surgical drainage, 1 developed a deep infection managed successfully with oral antibiotics, and 1 had a post-traumatic dislocation treated with closed reduction and bracing. Post-operative complications occurred in 3 patients (7.9%). One patient developed a hematoma requiring surgical evacuation without further sequelae. One patient presented with a deep periprosthetic infection, and another experienced a post-traumatic anterior dislocation; both cases were managed surgically with revision of the prosthetic components.

At the final follow-up, implant survivorship, defined as the absence of prosthetic revision due to mechanical failure, loosening, or persistent infection, was 94.7%, with 36 out of 38 shoulders remaining intact. Considering all causes, the overall reoperation rate was 7.9%, and the major revision rate was 5.3%.

Discussion

The reconstruction of proximal humeral bone loss remains one of the key technical challenges in both revision rTSA and in primary rTSA performed for acute comminuted fractures.12 In this setting, the cement collar technique, consisting of a circumferential polymethylmethacrylate mantle constructed around the proximal humeral metaphysis, has proven to be a reliable, simple, and cost-effective solution for bone defects measuring ≤5 cm, effectively avoiding the need for structural allografts or customized implants.

Our findings support cementoplasty as an effective technique for restoring the proximal humeral contour, optimizing deltoid wrapping, and preserving soft-tissue tension, which are 3 critical factors in achieving functional stability and favorable biomechanical outcomes following reverse shoulder arthroplasty. By reconstituting the metaphyseal volume, the cement collar facilitates a more anatomical positioning of the deltoid muscle, improving its moment arm and enabling effective shoulder elevation and abduction, even in the absence of tuberosity healing.1,5,11,13

From a biomechanical standpoint, the cement collar serves not merely as a passive filler but as an active interface that contributes to restoring the shoulder's center of rotation and lateral offset.

This has a direct impact on the functional recovery of the patient. In our cohort, both fracture and revision cases demonstrated a mean forward elevation and abduction above 110°. These results confirm that the cement collar can offer comparable functional outcomes while greatly simplifying the surgical procedure. Proximal humeral bone loss significantly compromises rotational motion, especially external rotation, due to the loss of tendon insertions of the infraspinatus and teres minor.9,18 Although the cement collar does not restore the anatomical insertions of the rotator cuff, it contributes indirectly to functional recovery in rotation as well. By reconstructing the proximal humeral contour, the cement mantle facilitates a more anatomical and lateralized stem position, which improves residual muscle tensioning and supports a more effective, albeit limited, rotational motion. Nevertheless, compared to forward elevation and abduction, the benefit of cementoplasty on rotational recovery remains more limited. In younger patients or those with higher functional demands, additional strategies such as selective tendon transfers may be warranted to further enhance active external rotation.

Unlike other types of reconstruction, which require either allograft shaping and fixation or the use of custom-made implants,14,15,19 the cement collar can be adapted intraoperatively to the patient's specific bone loss pattern using standard surgical materials. This makes the technique highly reproducible and accessible across a variety of health care settings. Moreover, the reduction in surgical time and technical complexity may be particularly advantageous in elderly or comorbid patients, in whom minimizing operative stress is a priority.

Baldari et al2 and Boileau et al3 previously proposed the use of cementoplasty as a reconstructive option for selected types of proximal humeral bone defects, particularly in revision settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the functional outcomes of the cement collar technique and to demonstrate its clinical effectiveness in both revision rTSA and primary rTSA for acute multifragmentary fractures. In terms of implant survivorship, our midterm results are reassuring. We observed no cases of stem loosening, subsidence, or cement mantle failure. This suggests that a properly contoured and well-integrated cement collar can provide not only immediate stability but also durable metaphyseal support, even in osteoporotic bone. It must be acknowledged, however, that cementoplasty is not without limitations. Excessive cement volume or suboptimal contouring may interfere with soft-tissue tracking and potentially contribute to impingement or limited range of motion. Furthermore, the long-term behavior of the cement mantle beyond five years remains to be fully elucidated, particularly with regard to potential degradation, radiolucent lines, or delayed cement–bone interface failure. These aspects will require dedicated follow-up and future prospective investigations.

Our study presents some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The retrospective nature of the analysis introduces a potential risk of selection bias and limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Although all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon, which ensures consistency in surgical technique and follow-up, this may also reduce the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings or less experienced operators.

The relatively small sample size, especially when comparing fracture and revision subgroups, may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences in clinical outcomes or complication rates. Moreover, the absence of a control group treated with alternative reconstructive options such as structural allografts, long cementless stems, or custom implants limits our ability to make direct comparisons and prevents us from determining whether the cement collar technique is superior to other methods.

Conclusion

The cement collar is not just a method for filling bone voids; it also plays an active role in restoring biomechanical balance in reverse shoulder arthroplasty. It offers a reproducible, efficient, and clinically effective solution for metaphyseal bone loss up to 5 cm, including in cases in which tuberosity reconstruction is not possible. Given its simplicity and adaptability, cementoplasty should be considered a valuable tool in the armamentarium of shoulder surgeons, particularly in complex or high-risk cases. Further research, including long-term studies and comparisons with biological reconstruction techniques, will help refine its indications and confirm its long-term efficacy.
