A new function of offset response in the primate auditory cortex: marker of temporal integration
Authors: Peirun Song, Haoxuan Xu, Hangting Ye, Xinyu Du, Yuying Zhai, Xuehui Bao, Ishrat Mehmood, Hisashi Tanigawa, Wanqiu Niu, Zhiyi Tu, Pei Chen, Tingting Zhang, Xuan Zhao, Xiongjie Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Offset responses are traditionally viewed as indicators of sound cessation. Here, we investigate offset responses to auditory click trains, examining how they are modulated by inter-click intervals (ICIs) and train duration. Using extracellular recordings and electrocorticography (ECoG) in non-human primates, alongside electroencephalography (EEG) in humans, we show that offset responses are significantly influenced by both ICI and train length, thereby establishing them as markers of temporal integration. We introduce the concept of the ‘Neuronal Integrative Window’ (NIW), defined as the temporal span during which neurons integrate stimuli to produce or modulate the temporal integration signal. Our data reveal that on the neuronal level, the auditory cortex (AC) exhibits a more expansive NIW than the medial geniculate body (MGB), integrating stimuli over longer durations and showing a preference for larger ICIs. Furthermore, our results indicate that offset responses could serve as potential biomarkers for neurological and psychiatric conditions, highlighted by their sensitivity to pharmacological modulation with ketamine. This study advances our understanding of auditory temporal processing and proposes a novel approach for assessing and monitoring brain health.

Introduction

Sound inherently carries temporal information, making time a fundamental dimension in auditory perception. Temporal integration, a critical component of auditory processing, operates across various timescales1. Notably, studies have unveiled distinct neural responses linked to syllables, phrases, and sentences in the context of spoken language processing2. Furthermore, various brain regions exhibit sensitivity to words, sentences, and paragraphs, hinting at a hierarchical organization of temporal scales3,4. This perspective suggests that temporal processing scales could serve as a functional characteristic, offering a potential overarching principle for understanding the organization of the cerebral cortex3,5,6. However, it is important to note that most investigations of temporal integration have been conducted in humans at the macroscale level, limiting our exploration of neuronal mechanisms. Additionally, there is a lack of electrophysiological signals directly tied to temporal integration, particularly at the neuronal microscale level, which further hinders research in this area.

Previous electrophysiological studies, ranging from extracellular recording7–12, electrocorticography (ECoG)13–15, electroencephalography (EEG)16–18, to even functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)19–21, have emphasized that offset responses commonly signify the conclusion of the sound and play a crucial role in encoding sound duration. However, recent research has introduced intriguing possibilities that challenge the conventional view of offset responses as mere indicators of a sound’s end from a neuronal microscopic perspective. For instance, studies by Chong et al. 22. propose that the offset response might be influenced by the frequency trajectory of a sound. Their research demonstrated that late spiking after the end of a sound stimulus was more often sensitive to the sound’s frequency variation compared with spiking during the sound. This suggests that the offset responses in AC neurons can encode fine-grained frequency information over time, extracting and integrating information from previous auditory events, not just marking the end of a sound. Further extending this notion, studies in the auditory cortex of mice conducted by Lee and Rothschild23 suggest that offset responses could be encoding sequences of sounds that occurred prior to the offset, indicating a significant role in processing the temporal structure of auditory stimuli. In conclusion, a growing body of evidence suggests that the offset response in the auditory cortex is intricately linked to the temporal context of the sound. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that offset responses linked to the temporal context may also exist at macroscopic levels, such as EEG and ECoG signals, and may serve as markers of temporal integration across both macroscopic and microscopic levels.

To test this hypothesis, we need to construct sound stimuli that are conducive to temporal integration. In the realm of auditory stimuli, click trains offer a unique opportunity to examine temporal processing within the auditory system. While these sequences of identical pulses with varying temporal characteristics have been widely used in auditory research24–27, they have not been thoroughly examined from the perspective of temporal integration. Notably, when the inter-click interval (ICI) is less than 33 ms, click trains can be perceived as having a pitch-like quality rather than as a series of individual pulses28. This phenomenon, stemming from temporal integration, shifts the perception from discrete events to a more continuous auditory experience. As a matter of fact, previous studies primarily focused on the encoding of individual clicks, investigating temporal and rate encoding across different click rates24,25. The signal of temporal integration within the click train has been illusive for decades. Meanwhile, although offset responses to click trains have been found in both microscopic29,30 and macroscopic levels31,32, their functional significance has seldom been explored. Offset responses may provide insights into the comprehensive perception of the sound as a whole. Consequently, considering the click train as an auditory object through temporal integration, can offset responses to click trains serve as reliable markers of this temporal integration?

Our current research, using click trains as stimuli with ECoG and extracellular recording in macaque monkeys and EEG recording in humans, confirms that offset responses to these click trains represent a form of temporal integration. Building upon this, we introduce the concept of the Neuronal Integrative Window (NIW) on the neuronal level. The NIW is defined as the temporal span during which neurons integrate auditory stimuli to produce or modulate the temporal integration signal. This window reflects the temporal processing capabilities of neurons, highlighting their ability to accumulate auditory information over time before generating an offset response. This concept allows us to explore how the transformation of the NIW occurs along the auditory pathway and to investigate the neuronal mechanisms underlying temporal integration.

Results

Offset responses as a marker of temporal integration in ECoG signals

We performed 64-channel electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings covering the surface of the monkey primary auditory cortex (A1) (Fig. 1a) to analyze its responses to repetitive click stimuli. Figures 1b, c show the ECoG responses of an example channel from monkey X asterisked in Fig. 1a. In the top row of Fig. 1b, an auditory response is visible for each 0.2-ms pulse delivered at 512-ms intervals, including the final three clicks, which are indicated with red dashed lines. When the ICI was reduced to 32 ms, the first pulse elicited a significant response in the ECoG signal, subsequently leading to an oscillatory pattern, as illustrated in the middle row of Fig. 1b. Notably, for the penultimate and third-to-last clicks, no distinct responses were observed, apart from a subtle response following the final click of the train. Upon further reducing the ICI to 4 ms, only the onset response was evident, without any subsequent reactions (bottom row of Fig. 1b) within the sound delivering period. However, a pronounced response became apparent following the termination of the click train. This particular response was identified as an offset response, synchronizing with the termination of the train, as evidenced from the comparison of the responses elicited by 1024-ms and 512-ms click trains with a 4-ms ICI (Supplementary Fig. 1a). A similar pattern for the offset response was noted when comparing responses to 1024-ms and 512-ms click trains with an 8-ms ICI (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the offset response amplitude of the example channel peaked at an 8-ms ICI and systematically decreased as the ICI increased from 8 ms to 64 ms (p = 0.016, r = −0.97, Pearson correlation, Fig. 1c). To investigate the population data across all 64 electrodes, the offset response index (OFRI), defined as the normalized root mean square (RMS) of the offset response in a 300-ms window (as detailed in the “Methods”), was computed for each channel. The average OFRI across all channels showed a similar trend with a peak at an 8-ms ICI (p < 0.001, t-test for both 8 vs 4 and 8 vs 16) and a decreasing trend from 8 ms to 64 ms (p = 0.03, r = −0.94, Pearson correlation, Fig. 1d). For the computation of latency, we quantified the global field power (GFP) across all channels under each stimulus condition (Fig. 1e) and selected the peak moments of the GFP curves as the latency. The latency of the offset responses showed an increasing trend with an increasing ICI from 4 ms to 64 ms (p = 0.026, r = 0.95, Pearson correlation, Fig. 1f). We posited that the emergence of the offset response might depend on the temporal summation of click stimuli, a hypothesis derived from the presence of offset responses only in scenarios with minimal ICIs, as shown in Fig. 1b. To explore this hypothesis, we varied the number of clicks in our experiments, maintaining a constant ICI of 4 ms and exploring a range from 1 to 256 clicks (depicted from bottom to top in Fig. 2a). We noted that responses to lower click counts, such as 1, 4, and 16, showed a decrease in the onset response (p < 0.001, t-test, 1 vs. 16), with a similar response pattern featuring a single cluster of responses, and exhibited an absence of an offset response. However, a significant change was observed when the click count increased to 64. At this juncture, a distinct offset response became apparent following the termination of the click train, as indicated by the red dashed line, and this response was further accentuated with 256 clicks. This finding was corroborated by comparing the responses to 16 and 256 clicks across all 64 recorded channels in Monkey X (Fig. 2b). Only the onset response was noticeable at 16 clicks, while both onset and offset responses were evident at 256 clicks. Similar results were also confirmed in Monkey C (Supplementary Fig. 3). Offset responses as a marker of temporal integration in single neurons

Following the establishment of the offset response’s link with temporal integration in ECoG signals, our investigation progressed to the neuronal level, using 16-channel and 32-channel linear arrays for AC and MGB extracellular recording separately. In AC, our recordings mainly focused on A1. We maintained a consistent click train duration of 4 s while varying ICI from 1 ms to 128 ms (Fig. 3a). Across this range, we observed that no obvious offset responses were detectable for ICIs of 64 ms and 128 ms in an example neuron in AC (p > 0.05, t-test, Fig. 3b). However, ICIs below 32 ms triggered a burst of responses following the cessation of the click train (p < 0.001, t-test, Fig. 3b), which were confirmed as offset responses through comparison with the 1024-ms and 512-ms click trains (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Focusing on a 200-ms window aligned with the end of the click train, we observed that both the magnitude and the latency of the response were dependent on the ICI, as shown by post-stimulus time histogram (PSTH) analyses for the spiking of the example neuron (Fig. 3c). As the ICI increased, the firing rate of the offset response monotonically decreased (p = 0.003, r = −1, Spearman’s rank correlation, Fig. 3d), and its latency increased (p = 0.017, r = 0.94 for latency, Spearman’s rank correlation, Fig. 3e). Nevertheless, 29 neurons deviated from this monotonic trend. For instance, Fig. 3f–i showcase a neuron that exhibited an enhanced offset response at 8-ms and 16-ms ICIs, particularly in contrast to shorter ICIs like 1 ms and 2 ms (Fig. 3f). In cases of larger ICIs, such as 128 ms, no offset response was discernible (p = 0.46, t-test). The offset response of the neuron was confirmed through comparison with the 1024-ms and 512-ms click trains (Supplementary Fig. 4b). When aligning the termination of the click train with a 200-ms window, heightened response magnitudes were observed at 8-ms and 16-ms ICIs, as supported by the PSTHs (Fig. 3g). The function illustrating the firing rate of the offset response relative to the ICI depicted a distinct peak around the 16-ms intervals, indicating non-linearity (p = 0.18, r = 0.66, Spearman’s rank correlation, Fig. 3h), diverging from the linear relationship observed in Fig. 3d. However, the latency maintained a monotonic relationship as the ICI increased (p = 0.017, r = 0.94, Spearman’s rank correlation, Fig. 3i).

We identified two key factors characterizing neuronal offset responses to the click trains. The first factor was the preferred ICI, which elicited the strongest offset response. The distribution of preferred ICI among 93 offset-responsive neurons in AC is shown in Fig. 3j. Interestingly, the distribution peaks at an 8-ms ICI, although there is no significant difference (p = 0.16, Chi-square test, 8 ms vs 1 ms) in the percentage of neurons preferring an 8-ms ICI (25/93) compared to a 1-ms ICI (17/93). The second factor was the maximal ICI capable of eliciting a significant offset response. The distribution of maximal ICIs for these 93 offset neurons is illustrated in Fig. 3k. Among them (n = 93), 29 neurons showed a maximal ICI of 16 ms, 32 had a maximal ICI of 32 ms, and only 1 had a maximal ICI of 64 ms. Notably, no neurons exhibited an offset response at the 128-ms ICI. These findings suggest that there might be an upper limit to temporal integration for the offset response, with 128 ms potentially surpassing the NIW.

After studying the influence of ICI length on the offset response in temporal integration, our attention shifted to the role of the number of clicks in this process. Specifically, we analyzed neuronal responses to varying numbers of clicks, as outlined in Fig. 4a, set at ICIs of 4 ms and 16 ms. The raster plots and PSTHs in Fig. 4b display the neuronal activity of an example neuron with the click count increasing from 1 to 256 at a 4-ms ICI. For the example neuron, initial observations revealed that responses to 1, 2, and 4 clicks were strikingly similar, suggesting a lack of offset responses in these conditions (Fig. 4b). However, a notable shift occurred as the click count rose to and surpassed 16, with an offset response becoming evident, as depicted in both raster and PSHT plots in Fig. 4b. Interestingly, an overlapping of onset and offset responses for 8 clicks was identified, suggesting a blending of these responses into a single firing cluster. To substantiate the existence of an offset response in an 8-click sequence, we carefully analyzed the first 300-ms window, aligned with the train’s onset (Supplementary Fig. 5a). We divided this window into 25 ms bins for detailed examination. Our hypothesis was that if an offset response existed for a specific click count, it would manifest in a bin where the response magnitude surpassed those of both smaller and larger click counts. This expectation is based on the premise that onset responses generally remain consistent, while offset responses vary temporally, phase locking to the termination of the click train across different conditions. The data supported this hypothesis, as responses between 50 ms and 75 ms for 8 clicks were more pronounced than those for 4 and 16 clicks (Supplementary Fig. 5a). We also closely examined the responses of the same neuron to click trains with an ICI of 16 ms, where the click count varied from 1 to 64 (Fig. 4c). Notably, the onset and offset responses for 2 clicks appeared to converge into a single firing cluster in the raster plot. To ascertain the presence of a 2-click offset response, we investigated the initial 300-ms window, aligned with the train’s onset (Supplementary Fig. 5c), following the similar approach as in Supplementary Fig. 5a. The results showed that responses between 50 ms and 75 ms for 2 clicks were more pronounced than those for 1 and 4 clicks (Supplementary Fig. 5c), a pattern that was also reflected in the PSTH plots (Supplementary Fig. 5d). This analysis confirmed that the response to 2 clicks indeed encompassed an offset response with 16-ms ICI.

In our comprehensive analysis, we systematically collated data from 33 AC neurons to determine the minimum number of clicks required to elicit offset responses at ICIs of 4 ms and 16 ms, as displayed in the top and bottom rows of Fig. 4d respectively. Our findings revealed that at a 4-ms ICI, the minimum click count necessary to generate an offset response could be considerably high, ranging up to 64 or even 128 clicks. This was exemplified by a neuron showcasing a minimum of 64 clicks, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Furthering our exploration into the nuances of temporal integration, we analyzed the distribution of the shortest click train durations that were sufficient to induce offset responses, as depicted in Fig. 4e. Intriguingly, at a 4-ms ICI, the minimum number of clicks required was significantly greater than that at a 16-ms ICI (t-test, p = 0.032, Fig. 4d). However, when comparing the minimal durations needed to evoke offset responses between these two ICI conditions, the minimum duration at a 16-ms ICI was observed to be longer than that at a 4-ms ICI (t-test, p = 0.054, Fig. 4e). Additionally, a pairwise comparison among the 33 neurons demonstrating offset responses to both conditions indicated that most data points fell below the unity line (Paired t-test, p = 0.01, Supplementary Fig. 7a), implying that neurons required fewer clicks to elicit an offset response at a longer ICI compared to a shorter one. Conversely, the distribution of these data points above the unity line in terms of the duration necessary to elicit offset responses was significant (Paired t-test, p = 0.025, Supplementary Fig. 7b). Altogether, these findings highlight that both the number and the duration play crucial roles in eliciting offset responses.

Probing the effective temporal window in offset responses

Our research emphasized the pivotal role of temporal integration in shaping the offset response to click trains, influenced by variables such as the ICI and the total click count. To further understand the influence of the temporal window on the offset response, we designed various click train configurations (Fig. 5a). Our baseline was a regular click train with a consistent 4-ms ICI (“Reg”, depicted in the top row of Fig. 5a). In contrast, the experimental click trains were bifurcated: the latter segment replicated the regular train (Reg), while the initial part consisted of random ICIs averaging 4 ms (ranging from 1 to 8 ms, labeled as “Irreg”). The counts of intervals in the regular part varied from 4 to 32, categorizing each block as Irreg-Reg(n), where “n” indicates the number of intervals in the regular segment (Fig. 5a). Figure 5b showcases the offset responses of an example neuron, presented in both raster (top panel) and PSTH (bottom panel) formats. Compared to the control condition, this neuron displayed a significant difference in the 16-interval condition (p < 0.01, t-test), but not in the 32-interval scenario (p = 0.72, t-test). This suggests that a stimulus encompassing 16 intervals (or 64 ms) could modulate the offset response, while a longer stimulus spanning 32 intervals (or 128 ms) may not have the same effect, at least for the click trains tested. We identified the longest interval that showed a notable deviation from the control for each neuron, defining this interval as the “effective temporal window”, which helps qualify the possible size of NIW with this method. Notably, 17 out of 44 analyzed neurons demonstrated an effective temporal window extending up to 128 ms (Fig. 5c). Considering this was the maximum window examined, it raises a compelling possibility that some neurons might possess an even wider effective temporal window.

To elucidate whether the offset response in the AC may inherit from its preceding brain station, we conducted recordings in the medial geniculate body (MGB). In total, 504 thalamic neurons were recorded and 18.06% neurons presented offset responses, this ratio is significantly smaller (p < 0.001, Chi-squared test) than that of AC (41.9%). Our assessments spanned ICIs ranging from 1 ms to 128 ms. The rasters of a representative MGB neuron distinctly showed offset responses for ICIs of less than 4 ms (Fig. 6a, p < 0.05, t-test). Focusing on a 400-ms window aligned with the click train’s offset, we noted pronounced response magnitudes at a 1-ms ICI (p < 0.05, t-test, 1 ms vs 2 ms), as confirmed by the PSTH diagrams (Fig. 6b). The function illustrating the firing rate of the offset response against the ICI indicated a decrease trend as the ICI increased (p = 0.02, r = −0.81, Spearman’s rank correlation, Fig. 6c). Additionally, we analyzed the distributions of both the preferred and maximum ICI within the MGB (Fig. 6d, e). A comparison between the AC and MGB revealed that the AC had a longer preferred ICI than the MGB (p = 0.046, t-test, Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, the maximal ICI in the AC was also more extended compared to the MGB (p = 0.024, t-test, Supplementary Table 1). These insights suggest that AC neurons may integrate a longer ICI to initiate an offset response, and the AC population demonstrates a preference for larger ICIs. Pharmacologic manipulation of offset responses

Temporal integration is a fundamental neural mechanism that allows the brain to accumulate information over time. An increasing body of evidence indicates that disruptions in temporal integration could be foundational to several brain disorders33–37, underscoring its potential as a diagnostic metric for neurologic and psychiatric diseases. For instance, major depressive disorder (MDD) is associated with N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor malfunction38,39, and NMDA receptors have been linked to both offset responses40,41 and temporal integration42. Venturing further into this potential clinical application, we assessed the impact of Ketamine, an anesthetic agent and an antagonist of NMDA receptors, on the offset response. Our study involved continuous recordings in monkeys across four conditions: awake, followed by incremental Ketamine doses of 0.5 mg/kg, 2 mg/kg, and 8 mg/kg (Fig. 7a). We observed a systematic attenuation of the offset response to the click train with increasing Ketamine dosage (Fig. 7b, c), a trend that remained consistent across three sessions on different days (Fig. 7d, e). Offset responses as a marker of temporal integration in EEG signals

Furthering our research into potential clinical applications, we conducted an electroencephalography (EEG) study involving healthy humans. We evaluated train durations (click numbers) from 4 ms to 1024 ms for a 4-ms ICI click train. Notably, a pronounced auditory response to the 1024-ms click train was observed in 64-channel EEG recordings from both the temporal and occipital lobes (Fig. 8a), consistent with previous research findings43,44. The amplitude of the grand-average response increased as the duration extended from 256 ms to 1024 ms (Fig. 8b, n = 19), with the most potent offset response observed at the 1024-ms duration. These findings, corroborated for the 16-ms ICI click train (Supplementary Fig. 8), confirm that human EEG can effectively capture the offset response, which is contingent on temporal integration. This opens avenues for the offset response to be used as a diagnostic measure in human neurological and psychiatric conditions. Additionally, our study expanded to evaluate the ICI range from 4 ms to 256 ms for a click train with a duration of 1024 ms. In this scenario, we observed that the amplitude of the offset response in human EEG decreased as the ICI increased from 8 to 256 ms (p < 0.001, r = −0.99, Pearson correlation, Fig. 8c, d, n = 21). Discussion

In this study, we thoroughly investigated temporal integration in AC using ECoG signals in macaque monkeys. We discovered that the AC exhibited offset responses to repetitive click stimuli, with these responses being influenced by both ICI length (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2) and the total number of clicks (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). At the neuronal level, this offset response was clearly linked to the temporal summation of click stimuli (Figs. 3–4). Remarkably, a significant number of neurons demonstrated an “effective temporal window” that extended up to 128 ms (Fig. 5). Comparative analysis with MGB revealed distinct response patterns between the two regions (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 1), with the AC requiring more clicks and preferring a longer interval to initiate offset responses compared to the MGB (Supplementary Table 1). These insights provide a deeper understanding of auditory processing during temporal integration, potentially paving the way for new developments in neurological diagnostics (Figs. 7–8).

Our study has revealed the presence of offset responses across multiple dimensions: neuronal (Figs. 3–6), ECoG (Figs. 1–2 and 7), and EEG (Fig. 8), emphasizing the crucial role of temporal integration. This is evident from two key observations:

Firstly, the offset response is contingent on the ICI length, which must not exceed a specific threshold. This phenomenon is observed across diverse levels (Figs. 1, 3, 6 and 8), highlighting the offset response capacity to integrate click trains with shorter ICIs effectively. In macaque monkeys, the sound-evoked ECoG signal typically spans around 300 ms (Fig. 1c) and remains robust for pulses with ICIs over 128 ms (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Consequently, the offset response might merge with the onset response of the last pulse, complicating the identification of the maximal ICI for triggering offset responses in the ECoG signal. Similarly, in human EEG recordings, the sound-evoked signal, spanning about 300 ms (Fig. 8b), shows diminishing responses to each pulse when the ICI surpasses 128 ms (Supplementary Fig. 9b). However, a noticeable offset response follows the termination of click train, suggesting that the human AC can integrate click trains with ICIs as long as 128 ms (Supplementary Fig. 9b). In neuronal AC recordings, the average maximum ICI was observed to be around 18.5 ms, with only a singular instance of a 64-ms ICI (Fig. 3k), and no neurons responded to ICIs beyond 128 ms. This discrepancy may point to broader spatial summation in EEG during temporal integration or to species-specific variations, which merits further exploration. The offset response thus serves as an insightful metric for probing temporal integration capabilities across various species. Moreover, we found that the relationship between the response magnitude and the increasing ICI is not linear, showing a peak at an 8-ms ICI in both ECoG (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2a) and neuronal observations (Figs. 3j and 6d). This pattern is also reflected in human EEG data, where an 8-ms ICI train induces a stronger offset response than a 4-ms ICI (Fig. 8d), indicating that the offset response is finely tuned to temporal intervals and not solely dependent on sound energy.

Secondly, eliciting an offset response requires integrating a specific number of clicks, with this number varying across different analysis levels. At the ECoG level, 32 clicks might suffice to evoke an offset response (Fig. 2a). In contrast, neuronal data suggest a lower threshold, with an average of 28.6 clicks needed for a 4-ms ICI, and potentially as few as 8 clicks for certain AC neurons (Fig. 4d). This discrepancy between ECoG and neuronal recordings is likely to be due to their temporal resolutions. ECoG signals, with a broader temporal resolution, may lack the precision to accurately identify the minimum number of clicks needed for a response. This resolution issue is also evident in EEG data, where at least 64 clicks (corresponding to a 256-ms span) are necessary for a noticeable offset response (Fig. 8b). Interestingly, for a 4-ms ICI, a higher click count is required compared to a 16-ms ICI (Fig. 4d). However, when comparing the minimum duration required to elicit offset responses, the differences between these ICI conditions reverse (Fig. 4e), highlighting that the offset response depends on both duration and click count (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 7).

In conclusion, Our current research provides direct evidence that offset responses serve as indicators of temporal integration from mesoscopic to macroscopic levels, reinforcing the notion that the neuronal offset response is intricately linked to the temporal context of sound22,23,45 and significantly enriching our understanding of auditory processing in the temporal dimension.

The temporal dimension in auditory processing cannot be overlooked, as it is central to how we perceive rhythm, timing, and complex sound patterns46. This temporal dimension plays a foundational role in crucial aspects of speech and music perception, as well as in distinguishing environmental sounds47. Contemporary neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques have advanced our comprehension of temporal integration mechanisms in spoken language, revealing a hierarchical temporal integration structure in the human brain3,4. Yet, a knowledge gap remains concerning temporal integration in non-human animals, primarily due to the lack of a distinct neuronal signature for temporal integration, which hinders research at the neuronal level and in animal studies. The offset response, serving as a marker of temporal integration, allows us to monitor how stimuli are integrated across a temporal window. Thus, we introduce the concept of NIW, representing the temporal span that influences neuronal temporal integration. In our current research, we examine the NIW or the related offset response across three distinct temporal scales in a comparative analysis of AC and MGB.

Firstly, at the scale of milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, the offset response can be explored through the length of ICI. The offset response exhibits ICI dependence in two aspects: maximum ICI and preferred ICI. In AC, the average maximum ICI is 18.5 ms (Fig. 3k), which is 39.7% larger than in MGB (Fig. 6e), indicating an enhanced ability in AC to integrate successive clicks over time. This finding is reinforced by a significant increase in the preferred ICI in AC (Figs. 3j, 6d), suggesting an elevated capacity for temporal integration in AC. Secondly, at the scale of tens of milliseconds, the NIW is assessed through the minimum duration required to elicit an offset response at specific ICIs (e.g., 4 ms, as shown in Figs. 4, 6f–h). AC requires more than two times the duration needed by MGB to integrate a click train and evoke an offset response, favoring longer trains for temporal integration (Supplementary Table 1). Thirdly, at the scale of hundreds of milliseconds, the NIW is represented by the effective temporal window that influences the offset response (Figs. 5, 6i–k). A higher proportion of neurons in AC demonstrate a longer effective temporal window compared to the MGB (Supplementary Table 1), suggesting a broader scope of temporal integration in AC. In summary, the NIW can be explored at various temporal scales, with AC demonstrating a more expanded temporal structure than MGB across these scales. This observation is further supported by the relatively smaller proportion of offset responses in the MGB (Supplementary Table 1), indicating a limited number of offset-responsive neurons and suggesting a more extensive participation of neurons in temporal integration from the thalamus to the cortex. Additionally, substantial literature indicates that the subdivisions of the MGB exhibit distinct temporal response profiles48–50. Since we did not partition the MGB into subnuclei in our study, future research should address this aspect. Meanwhile, Previous MRI research has shown that the temporal receptive window expands along the cortical pathway3,4,51. Our findings, using the offset response as an indicator, suggest that temporal integration can be effectively investigated at the neuronal level and in several mammalian models. Additionally, our results indicate that the temporal integrative window expands from the thalamus to the cortex in the auditory pathway.

Since the offset response is not always linearly related to the ICI (Fig. 3f–i) or the number of clicks (Supplementary Fig. 10), this suggests it is not merely an accumulation of temporal history. The regular-irregular results (Fig. 5) further support this notion. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that there is a combination of excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms underlying the NIW. In fact, inhibition has been shown to play an important role in generating offset responses in the literature52,53. In our research, we observed that offset responses are usually accompanied by inhibition during the ongoing train of stimuli (Fig. 3b, f). We investigated the relationship between suppression during the stimulus train and offset responses by analyzing neuronal firing rates from 500 ms to 3500 ms before the end of the click train and correlating these rates with the magnitude of each neuron’s offset response using Pearson’s correlation. Our analysis showed that 16 out of 93 neurons had a significant negative correlation (p < 0.05, r = −0.8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\pm \,$$\end{document}±0.02 (SE), Pearson correlation), suggesting that prior inhibition may contribute to the generation of offset responses and then affect the temporal structure of the NIW. In addition to inhibition, NMDA receptors may also play an important role in generating offset responses and shaping the NIW, as demonstrated in experiments with ketamine, an antagonist of NMDA channels. NMDA receptors have been linked to both offset response40,41 and temporal integration42 in the auditory system. The combination of excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms underlying the NIW may provide a framework for neurons to accumulate stimuli history, leading to preferred ICI, nonlinearity of ICI dependence, and the regular-irregular effect. Interestingly, at the population level, the preferred ICI in the AC centered around 8 ms, echoing the results from gap detection54. Research has shown that the AC is particularly important for detecting brief gaps through temporal integration54,55. Specifically, studies have demonstrated that cortical lesions or optogenetic suppression impair the detection of brief gaps, with the effect of AC lesions peaking around 8 ms54. This suggests that the neuronal circuitry of the AC favors an 8 ms interval for temporal integration.

The combination of excitatory and inhibitory mechanisms, NMDA receptors, and inhibition in shaping the NIW, as well as the underlying neuronal circuitry, need further investigation.

Beyond its role as a neurophysiological phenomenon, the offset response emerges as a crucial marker, observable in both animal models and human subjects. This adaptability positions it as a potential tool for clinical diagnostic applications, providing a new perspective for understanding and addressing various brain disorders. Our study explored this potential by manipulating brain states using Ketamine, an anesthetic drug, which also serves as a treatment for depression56,57. We controlled the anesthetic level by varying the dosage of Ketamine (Fig. 7), and found that the offset response systematically decreased as the dosages increased. This trend was consistent across multiple recording sessions, suggesting a direct relationship between the offset response and the anesthetic level. This finding highlights the potential of using the offset response as a marker for monitoring anesthetic levels during surgical procedures, where accurate and reliable anesthesia monitoring is crucial for patient safety and optimal surgical outcomes. However, the current standard practice of using EEG for monitoring anesthesia levels faces challenges in terms of accuracy and reliability58. The observed offset response to the click train provides an innovative pathway for refining anesthesia monitoring techniques. Further extending the clinical applicability of our research, we propose the use of offset response for the assessment of psychiatric conditions. Temporal integration, a central component of brain functionality59,60, has been found to be compromised in conditions like schizophrenia37, autism spectrum disorders35,36, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder34, and Parkinson’s Disease33. Given these findings, the signal of temporal integration might be poised to emerge as a pivotal biomarker for broader clinical diagnostics. Our EEG data (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. 8) underscore this potential, particularly through two specific measure indices: the effect of duration on the offset response (Fig. 8b) and the influence of ICI on the offset response (Fig. 8c, d). These measurements provide distinct insights into the auditory brain’s capacity for temporal integration, paving the way for innovative diagnostic strategies in the realm of brain disorders.

Methods

Subjects and surgical procedures

Our study involved four male rhesus monkeys: C, X, M and Z, aged between 7 and 8 years and weighing between 5.5 and 8 kg. We conducted electrocorticography (ECoG) recording experiments with monkeys X and C and extracellular recording experiments with monkeys M and Z. The experimental protocols adhered strictly to the guidelines established by the State Council of the People’s Republic of China (GB 14925-2010) and were approved by the Bioethics Committee of Zhejiang University (ZJU20200148). These measures ensured the animals received comprehensive care and ethical treatment throughout the study.Our dedicated team of researchers and animal care staff performed daily monitoring of the monkeys to ensure their optimal health and well-being. To encourage exploratory behaviors and enhance living conditions, we enriched their 0.74 m³ home cages by providing toys and preferred food items.

For the implantation of the headpost and ECoG array, we employed aseptic surgical techniques. The premedication involved the administration of ketamine (50 mg/kg) and medetomidine (0.03 mg/kg). Subsequently, the monkeys were intubated and connected to an artificial respirator (A.D.S.1000, Engler Engineering Corp., FL) to maintain a stable body temperature of 37 °C using an electric heating mat. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, including oxygen saturation, heart rate, and end-tidal CO2 (Surgi Vet, Smiths Medical PM Inc., London, UK), allowed for precise adjustments in anesthesia levels.

The implantation process required securing the skull within a stereotactic frame (Narishige, Tokyo, Japan). After administering lidocaine, we carefully removed skin and muscles in the target area. A titanium headpost (Gray Matter Research, MT) was securely affixed to the skull using resin and bone screws. Craniotomy and durotomy procedures were performed with the assistance of a microscope (Ophthalmo-Stativ S22, Carl Zeiss Inc., Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a CMOS color camera (TS-CA-130MIII, MeCan Imaging Inc., Saitama, Japan). Following the implantation of the ECoG array, we sealed the surgical site with the dura mater, bone flap, and skin, and covered the exposed skull area with resin. Postoperatively, the monkeys received ketoprofen for three days and antibiotics for one week to ensure their well-being and minimize the risk of infection.

In our experiments involving anesthesia, we initially recorded sessions with the monkeys awake, comfortably seated in a chair. This initial phase was followed by a gradual increase in intravenous Ketamine doses across sessions (0.5 mg/kg, 2 mg/kg, 8 mg/kg). Throughout the anesthesia process, we meticulously recorded continuous physiological parameters, including the electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure. After each experimental session, the monkeys were given a full week’s rest before proceeding to any subsequent anesthesia recording.

In the human portion of our study, we recruited 26 volunteers, 21 participated in the experiment with varying ICI, and 19 took part in the experiment with varying train duration, all aged between 20 and 30 years, with normal hearing. This research adhered rigorously to ethical guidelines under the approval from Ethics committee of Zhejiang University (IRB-20230131-R). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Before commencing the experiment, we obtained informed written consent from each participant.

ECoG recording

We implanted a 64-channel electrode array (E64-2500-50-200, NeuroNexus Technologies Inc., Michigan, USA) subdurally over the auditory cortex (AC) of the monkeys, as shown in Fig. 1a. These electrodes had a diameter of 0.2 mm, with gold surfaces exposed on a 2 × 2 cm polyimide substrate. The electrode impedances, measured at 1 kHz, ranged from 20 to 50 kohms, and the inter-electrode distance was maintained at 2.5 mm. To ensure precise placement, we utilized preoperative MRI scans to determine target locations and determine appropriate craniotomy sizes. Additionally, we positioned a gold reference electrode near the ECoG array within the subdural space, oriented towards the dura. Lead wires originating from both the ECoG array and reference electrodes were connected to micro connectors (ZIF-Clip 64, Tucker-Davis Technologies, TDT, Alachua, FL). These connectors were housed within a titanium chamber affixed securely to the skull using resin. To capture neural signals, we amplified and filtered the ECoG signals with a Butterworth filter in the range of 0.5–300 Hz using a differential amplifier (RZ5, TDT). The signals were then digitally sampled at a rate of 1 kHz and stored on hard-disk drives for subsequent analysis.

Extracellular recording

In each session, we utilized multi-site linear electrodes (S-probe, Plexon, USA): 16 contacts spacing 150 micrometers for the auditory cortex (AC) and 32 contacts spacing 200 micrometers for the medial geniculate body (MGB). These electrodes were inserted into the brain based on MRI structural guidance (Supplementary Fig. 11) through 26-gauge transdural guide tubes and advanced using a remote-controlled microdrive (FHC). Neural activity was amplified 20,000-fold and filtered between 300 Hz and 3000 Hz. Neuronal spike trains were analyzed using offline spike sorting with the Kilosort algorithm, followed by manual curation in Phy61.

For our recordings within the AC, we based the partitioning on the tonotopic organization of characteristic frequency (CF)62–64. In total, we recorded from 391 neurons in the primary auditory area (A1), 4 neurons in the rostral (R) area, 24 neurons in the mediolateral (ML) area and 1 neuron in the caudolateral (CL) area using the transitional train stimulus. All recorded neurons were included in the analysis. Additionally, to facilitate the partitioning of the AC, we recorded from 43 extra neurons using only pure tones.

As for the recordings with MGB, we recorded from 504 neurons within the MGB. Our recordings did not target a specific subregion of the MGB; instead, we captured a coronal cross-section extending from the lateral to the medial aspects of the MGB. We used a 32-channel linear electrode with a site spacing of 200 micrometers, which allowed us to cover approximately 6 millimeters in depth. This setup enabled us to record across the entire extent of the MGB vertically, ensuring a comprehensive sampling of its neuronal activity. The tonotopic distributions of CF in the AC and MGB, along with the distribution of maximum ICI and preferred ICI, are depicted in Supplementary Fig. 11.

EEG recording

Throughout the experimental sessions, participants were comfortably seated with head support in a controlled environment, facing a keyboard and speaker. To ensure the subjects maintain focus, we gave them specific instructions: they were to press the right key if they detected a gap in the sound and the left key if they perceived the sound as continuous. After the conclusion of each click train, a cue was presented after an 800 ms interval, providing a response window of 700 ms for participants to register their input.

Continuous EEG data were recorded using a 64-lead cap, with electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 electrode placement standard. (NeusenW, Neuracle Inc.), at a sampling rate of 1 kHz. Five electrodes recording ECG, Horizontal Electrooculogram (HEO) and Vertical Electrooculogram (VEO) were excluded during analysis. The reference electrode was positioned at the center of the head near Cz and the ground electrode was placed on the forehead.

Sound stimulation

Experiments were conducted within a sound-proof room. Acoustic stimuli were digitally generated with a computer-controlled Auditory Workstation (RZ6, TDT) at a sampling rate of 100 KHz. These stimuli were delivered through a speaker (LS50, KEF, UK). To ensure accuracy, we calibrated sound pressure levels using a ¼-inch condenser microphone (Brüel & Kjær 4954, Nærum, Denmark) and a PHOTON/RT analyzer (Brüel & Kjær, Nærum, Denmark). All click trains used in the experiments consisted of pulses with a width of 0.2 ms.

To assess the neuronal responses, we conducted two primary measurements: the frequency response area (FRA) and the CF. To determine FRA and CF, we presented a series of tones with a duration of 100 ms and a rise-fall time of 5 ms. These tones covered a wide range of frequencies from 0.2 kHz to 19.1 kHz in 26 logarithmic steps. Additionally, we varied the intensity of the tones from 0 to 70 dB SPL in 10 dB steps. Each frequency and intensity combination was repeated five times with 500 ms interstimulus intervals.

In the monkey ECoG and extracellular recoding experiments, we employed three types of click trains to explore the Neuronal Integrative Window (NIW):

Firstly, we introduced a series of click trains with a constant duration (4 s), systematically varying the ICI ranging from 1 ms to 128 ms.

Secondly, we maintained the ICI of click trains at 4 ms and systematically altered the click number ranging from 1 to 256. To compare the importance of click number and train duration, an ICI of 16 ms was used in an additional control experiment.

Finally, to investigate the effective temporal window, we utilized five click trains, each lasting 512 ms. The control was a standard click train with a consistent ICI of 4 ms (regular). The composition of the four experimental stimuli was similar: they began with ICIs averaging at 4 ms, varying randomly from 1 ms to 8 ms (irregular), and concluded with a steady ICI of 4 ms (regular), with the number of intervals randomly selected from 4, 8, 16, 32, which aligned with the durations of the regular part being 16, 32, 64, and 128 ms, respectively.

In the monkey anesthesia experiments, we employed transitional click trains, a novel stimulus we originally developed for a separate project. These transitional click trains consist of two concatenated regular click trains, each characterized by distinct ICIs (ICI1 and ICI2), and are designated as RegICI1-ICI2. Although initially devised for another research endeavor, we utilized the dataset from these trains to investigate the offset response to the click train in our current study. We employed three ICI combinations: 4 - 4.06 ms (Reg4-4.06), 8 - 8.12 ms (Reg8-8.12), and 4 - 5 ms (Reg4-5).

In the human electroencephalography (EEG) experiment, we investigated the effects of train duration and ICI length on offset response. For train duration, we kept the ICI as 4 ms and 16 ms in two sessions, respectively, and manipulated the duration of click trains ranging from 4 ms to 1024 ms. In respect of ICI length, we designed a series of regular click trains, each lasting 1024 ms. These click trains were characterized by seven different ICI values, ranging from 4 ms to 256 ms, following a geometric progression. We generated the click sounds using Matlab R2021a (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) on a personal computer equipped with a sound card (Creative, AE-7). The click sounds were produced at a high sampling rate of 384 kHz and 32-bit precision. These click trains were presented through a stereo speaker (Golden Field M23).

Data analysis

All data analysis procedures were done with MATLAB R2021b (MathWorks) and Fieldtrip toolbox. The recorded ECoG signals were processed as follows: They were segmented to encompass a duration of 3 s before the onset and 3 s after the completion of each click train. Subsequently, these signals underwent band-pass filtering within the range of 0.5–300 Hz and were then downsampled to a rate of 600 Hz. To reduce interference from non-physiological sources, we applied independent component analysis (ICA) using the FieldTrip toolbox65.

The Offset Response Index (OFRI) was defined to quantify the magnitude of the offset response. Here, RMSOffset and RMSControl refers to the root mean square (RMS) calculated from 0 to 300 ms and −300 to 0 ms windows relative to the offset of the train stimulation, respectively.

To calculate the latency for ECoG recording, we first measured the global field power (GFP) across all channels under each stimulus condition to effectively mitigate the influence of different polarity components in the offset responses on latency calculations66,67. We then selected the peak moments of the GFP curves as the global latency for each stimulus condition.

For spike data, Peristimulus Time Histograms (PSTHs) were generated using a bin width of 15 ms and a step size of 1 ms. The strength of offset response was determined by subtracting the firing rate during the ICI length before the end of the click train from the firing rate in the 0 to 150 ms period following the end of the click train. Generally, a neuron was deemed to exhibit an offset response if, in the 150 ms window following the sound’s end, it showed activity that was significantly higher (t-test, p < 0.05) than in the 500 to 1000 ms window after the termination of the sound. The ICI that elicited the maximum offset response was defined as the preferred ICI, whereas the maximal ICI was characterized as the upper limit of ICI capable of inducing an offset response. The minimal number and minimal duration were respectively defined as the least number of clicks and the shortest click train duration required to elicit an offset response. To determine whether a neuron produces an offset response while calculating the minimal number, we adjusted our approach of detecting a significant offset response: we segmented the spike train post click train onset into segments of 25 ms each. The identification of an offset response for a specific click count was determined in a bin where the response’s magnitude was higher compared to time-aligned bins with both fewer and more clicks. A double check was conducted to confirm the minimal number. As for the latency of offset responses in spikes, we calculated the Poisson cumulative distribution function from the baseline to assess the likelihood that the observed spike counts at each time point could occur randomly68. We then determined the latency as the time point of the first spike where the Poisson probability falls below the specified threshold (1e-3).

The recorded EEG signals were processed as follows: They were segmented to encompass a duration of 1 s before the onset and 1 s after the completion of each click train. Subsequently, these signals underwent band-pass filtering within the range of 0.5–40 Hz. ICA was applied to remove artifacts, specifically electrooculogram, electrocardiogram, and motion artifacts. Mirroring the methodology used in ECoG data analysis, the OFRI was calculated using the same equation, except that the calculation windows for RMSOffset and RMSControl were changed to 0 to 500 ms and −500 to 0 ms windows relative to the termination of the train stimulation, respectively.

Statistics and reproducibility

All the statistical analyses were performed in Matlab. In the part of ECoG data, to confirm an offset response was elicited with 1024-ms duration, we compared the RMS of the signals in 0 to 300 ms and −300 to 0 ms windows relative to the sound cessation trial by trial, employing paired t-test with a significance level set at 0.001. Moreover, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied for multiple comparisons across channels.

As for spike data, the Chi-squared test was utilized for comparing the proportion of neurons that showed an offset response between the AC and MGB (Supplementary Table 1). To assess the significance of these offset responses, we compared the responses in the 150 ms window immediately following the sound offset to those in the 500 to 1000 ms window post-offset, utilizing t-tests with a significance level set at 0.05. This analysis also included comparing the preferred and maximal ICI between the AC and MGB, as well as evaluating the minimal number and duration for ICIs of 4 ms and 16 ms within the AC. Additionally, paired t-tests were applied at the neuronal level to compare the minimal number and duration of responses. For assessing the minimal number and duration between the AC and MGB, since the number of neurons exhibiting offset responses was less than 30, we employed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

The sample size and the number of replications are also reported for all experiments included in the text and “Methods”.
