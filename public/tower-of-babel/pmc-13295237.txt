Chromium tolerance mechanisms in Cosmos sulphureus: antioxidant defense and potential role of pectin chelation-based detoxification
Authors: Yu Xiaofang, Xu Fan, Yang Xuan, Luo Fuwen, Wang Xiantong, Ning Xinjing, Kang Qingqing, Xue Hanyue, Huang Yuwei, Zhou Yongxin, Wang Zishan, Li Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil chromium (Cr) contamination is escalating, and phytoremediation is optimal for its abatement. Cosmos sulphureus, an annual Asteraceae herb widely used in landscape greening and ecological restoration, has strong tolerance to heavy metals, but its Cr accumulation traits, stress response and molecular mechanisms remain unclear, limiting its application. Based on physiological measurements under four Cr concentration gradients (100–400 µM), combined with transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis at the critical stress concentration of 100 µM, this study systematically elucidated the physiological stress responses, tolerance mechanisms and early molecular expression profiles of C. sulphureus under varying Cr stress.ResultsRoots are the main Cr accumulation organ. 100 µM is the physiological tolerance threshold with high tolerance, low translocation and activated antioxidant system; Cell wall (CW) pectin serves as the main Cr immobilization site, and its Cr enrichment correlates strongly with PME-mediated demethylation, implying that low-esterified pectin might contribute to Cr detoxification through increased chelation sites; Transcriptome identified 26,505 upregulated and 14,791 downregulated genes: defense genes related to Cr-induced pectin modification and enzyme activity (PME29, PME2, GALE2, etc.) were induced, while growth-related genes involved in CW development and photosynthetic regulation (PMEU1, WAK1, LHCA3, etc.) were repressed. Metabolomics showed elevated defensive metabolites including organic acids, amino acids, flavonoids and sugars (GSH, Pro, AsA, etc.) and also promoted the enrichment of metabolites associated with the synthesis and modification of CW pectin and cellulose (L-arabinose, raffinose, etc.). ConclusionThese findings clarify the Cr accumulation characteristics of C. sulphureus roots and its remediation potential under Cr concentration ≤ 100 µM, confirming that the antioxidant system and pectin chelation detoxification are core tolerance mechanisms. Overall, C. sulphureus adopts a molecular response pattern of growth-to-defense transition. This study provides a basis for Cr remediation landscape plant breeding and plant Cr tolerance mechanism exploration.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09039-y.

Introduction

Heavy metal (HM) contamination, particularly chromium (Cr) pollution, is a growing concern in urban green spaces, as a form of severe soil contamination [1]. Cr enters soils through atmospheric deposition, wastewater irrigation, industrial effluent, and landfill leachate, resulting in elevated soil concentrations [2]. Owing to its high mobility, bioaccumulation potential, and carcinogenicity, Cr poses considerable risks to ecological safety and human health [3]. The strong redox activity of Cr allows it to exist in various oxidation states, with Cr(VI) being the most common and harmful form [4]. Cr(VI) is highly soluble, enabling it to easily enter plant tissues and induce oxidative stress, a key mechanism of Cr toxicity [5]. Cr accumulation disrupts plant growth, photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, and other metabolic processes, causing stunted growth, reduced biomass, and even plant death [6–9]. Therefore, the remediation of Cr pollution is imperative.

Phytoremediation uses plants to remove or neutralize pollutants like HM in situ, offering a cost-effective, sustainable, and visually appealing alternative to traditional physicochemical methods for long-term pollution control [10]. However, research on phytoremediation to achieve Cr pollution control remains relatively scarce compared to control of other HM pollutants. Although prior studies have examined the Cr absorption and accumulation characteristics of certain plants and their Cr remediation efficacy, these have predominantly focused on aquatic species such as Canna indica [11], Leersia hexandra [8], and Cyperus alternifolius [12]. Research on terrestrial plants, especially Cr-tolerant ornamental species suitable for landscaping applications, remains limited. Identifying such plant materials and elucidating their Cr tolerance mechanisms are pivotal for enhancing the efficiency of phytoremediation in Cr-contaminated soils.

Plant responses to Cr stress involve multiple regulatory mechanisms encompassing tolerance, detoxification, and avoidance [13]. At the physiological level, HM stress triggers adaptive responses that mitigate cellular oxidative damage and maintain growth. This is largely achieved through extensive activation of enzymatic reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging systems [14], synthesis of pivotal small-molecule metabolites [13], and sustained photosynthesis to supply energy and precursors for stress-resistance compounds [15]. At the structural level, the cell wall (CW) serves as the primary physical barrier against external hazards [16]. Through spatial exclusion and chemical binding by its polysaccharide and protein components, it effectively immobilizes HM ions, reducing their transmembrane entry into the cell interior [17, 18]. In particular, pectin within CW polysaccharides—rich in active groups, e.g., carboxyl and hydroxyl groups—strongly adsorbs metal cations [19, 20]. Previous research indicates that the regulation of polysaccharide modification by plants enables the CW not only to serve as the primary site for HM immobilization but also to dynamically adjust its components in response to stress [21].

Asteraceae plants are widely recognized as ideal candidates for the remediation of polymetallic contamination due to their strong tolerance to HM [22–24]. Within the genus Cosmos, both C. sulphureus and C. bipinnatus are annual herbs characterized by rapid growth, wide adaptability, and ornamental flowers, possessing dual values in landscape beautification and ecological restoration [25]. Previous studies have verified that C. bipinnatus exhibits great Cr remediation potential and serves as an efficient Cr phytoextractor [18, 26, 27], whereas relevant molecular response mechanisms remain unclarified. By comparison, C. sulphureus also shows promising potential for Cr remediation, while available researches are extremely scarce [28], and systematic understanding of its molecular regulatory mechanisms such as gene expression under Cr stress is still lacking. Therefore, this study integrates physiological, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to elucidate C. sulphureus’ Cr-tolerant mechanisms, filling the gap in its molecular adaptation to Cr stress and providing new insights into plant Cr tolerance and Cr phytoremediation potential.

Transcriptomics and metabolomics represent core analytical tools in contemporary molecular biology, with the potential to systematically reveal gene expression dynamics and metabolite shifts within organisms. These approaches have been extensively employed to investigate Cr response mechanisms in various plant species, including rice [29, 30], Leersia hexandra [8], Canna indica [11] and Helianthus annuus [31]. Integrating these two methodologies enables comprehensive elucidation of the molecular regulatory networks and metabolic pathways of the C. sulphureus response to Cr stress [32]. In brief, the present study was undertaken to systematically investigate, through hydroponic experiments combined with physiological, transcriptional, and metabolic multi-omics analyses (1) the physiological response characteristics and resistance mechanisms of C. sulphureus under varying Cr stress concentrations; (2) its chelation detoxification mechanisms and the absorption and fixation pathways of Cr via root CWs; and (3) identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), key metabolites, and core metabolic pathways under Cr stress. This work elucidates the Cr accumulation potential and adaptation mechanisms of C. sulphureus, providing empirical support for its application in Cr-contaminated soil remediation.

Materials and methods

Material treatment and experimental design

The Cosmos sulphureus seeds used in this study were purchased from Jiangsu Dafeng Huafeng Seed Industry Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, China). Plump seeds were surface-sterilized by soaking in a 2% NaClO solution for 20 min and then rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. They were germinated and grown on vermiculite at a constant temperature of 25 °C for one week. Healthy and uniformly growing seedlings were selected and transferred to containers of identical size for two weeks of hydroponic pre-culture in ½-strength Hoagland nutrient solution (pH 6.4–6.5). Each container held 24 seedlings, with a total of 15 containers used.

To investigate the dose-dependent response of C. sulphureus to Cr stress, we established four concentration gradient treatment groups based on previous research (100, 200, 300, and 400 µM, labeled as T1–T4, respectively), with no Cr treatment serving as the control [33]. After three weeks of normal cultivation in full-strength Hoagland nutrient solution, the treatment groups were exposed to Cr(VI) stress by adding K₂Cr₂O₇ solution to the nutrient medium, while the control group remained in Cr-free nutrient solution.

Plants were harvested after 7 days of Cr exposure for physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses, with three biological replicates for physiological and transcriptomic assays and six for metabolomic profiling. Prior to harvest, surface-adsorbed HMs were chelated using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and rinsed off with deionized water. The root samples were then immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. Throughout the entire experiment, the nutrient solution was replaced every 7 days. The plant growth environment was maintained under the following conditions: temperature 25 °C, relative humidity 70%, photosynthetic photon flux density 500 µmol m⁻²s⁻¹, and a 12-h photoperiod.

Plant phenotypic characterization and Cr content determination

Fresh plant samples were collected for phenotypic measurements. Shoot and root lengths were measured using a ruler, while root parameters, including total root surface area, number of root tips, and number of forks, were determined using a root scanner (CI-600; CID Bio-Science, USA). For the measurement of shoot and root dry weight and Cr content, approximately 0.1 g of dried, homogenized plant material was placed into a 50-mL conical flask. Five milliliters of nitric acid (HNO₃) were added, and the samples were left to digest overnight. The following day, 1 mL of perchloric acid (HClO₄) was added (resulting in an HNO₃:HClO₄ ratio of 5:1, v:v). The samples were then digested on a graphite heating plate at 260 °C until the solution became clear and transparent. The digested solution was diluted to a final volume of 25 mL and filtered, and the Cr concentration in the final solution was determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; model 7900, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The Cr translocation factor and tolerance index were calculated according to the method described by Dai et al. [34].

Determination of physiological and biochemical parameters

Malondialdehyde determination, enzyme activity measurement and photosynthetic pigment analysis

Malondialdehyde content determination

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) heating colorimetric method [35]. Briefly, 0.1 g of leaf tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 4000 xg for 10 min. A 2-mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of 0.6% TBA. The mixture was then heated in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 10 min, rapidly cooled, and centrifuged again. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 450, 532, and 600 nm.

Determination of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase antioxidant enzyme activities

To prepare the enzyme supernatant for assaying enzyme activity levels, 0.5 g of fresh sample tissue was weighed and homogenized in 10 mL of pre-chilled 50 mmol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 1 mmol/L EDTA, 1 mmol/L dithiothreitol, and 2% polyvinyl pyrrolidone. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the crude enzyme extract.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined according to a widely used method with slight modifications [36]. The 3-mL reaction mixture for each tube consisted of 0.3 mL of 130 mM methionine (MET), 0.3 mL of 0.75 mM nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), 0.3 mL of 0.1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na), 1.5 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.3 mL of 20 µM riboflavin solution, and 0.1 mL of the enzyme extract. The reaction mixture was incubated under light at 25 °C for 20 min, and the absorbance at 560 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer (Genesys 180, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For the control tube, an equal volume of PBS was used instead of the enzyme extract, and it was kept in darkness. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to induce 50% inhibition of the photochemical reduction of NBT.

Catalase (CAT) activity was measured based on the method of Aebi with modifications [37]. The 1.25-mL reaction mixture contained 100 mM H₂O₂ and 100 mM PBS (pH 7.0) at a ratio of 1:4 (v:v). After adding 0.5 mL of the enzyme extract, the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was immediately recorded for 4 min, and the enzyme activity was calculated.

Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined using the guaiacol method with modifications [38]. To 2 mL of the reaction solution (containing 10 mM guaiacol, 6 mM H₂O₂, and 100 mM phosphate buffer, pH 6.0), 1 mL of the enzyme extract was immediately added to initiate the reaction. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm was recorded for 3 min to calculate the enzyme activity (µmol·mg⁻¹·min⁻¹).

Photosynthetic pigment analysis

Photosynthetic pigment content was determined by extracting 0.2 g of leaf tissue in 10 mL of 95% ethanol for 48 h in the dark. A 10-µL aliquot of the extract (with an equal volume of 95% ethanol used as a blank) was transferred to a microplate reader (SpectraMax, Molecular Devices). The absorbances at 649, 665, and 470 nm were measured to calculate the contents of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids, respectively. (For the detailed calculation formulas, please refer to Table S1.).

Determination of glutathione, non-protein thiol, plant chelating peptides, proline, and ascorbate contents

Glutathione (GSH) content was determined by the 5,5-dithio-bis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) method. A 0.25-g sample was ground with 3% TCA containing 5 mM EDTA. The sample was then centrifuged, and 2 mL of the resulting supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of 1 mol/L NaOH and 0.1 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) were added. After adding 2 mL of DTNB reagent, the reaction was conducted at room temperature for 5 min, after which the absorbance at 412 nm was measured. The GSH content was calculated using a standard curve.

A 0.25-g sample was taken and centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 10 min) with 6 mL of 0.5% sulfosalicylic acid; 1 mL of the supernatant was collected, and 2 mL of 0.25 mol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 1 mL of 10 mM DTNB were added. The reaction was conducted at room temperature for 20 min. The absorbance at 412 nm was measured, and the non-protein thiol (NPT) content was calculated using a GSH standard curve. The content of plant chelating peptides (PCs) was calculated as follows by the difference method: PCs = NPT – GSH.

A 0.5-g fresh sample was collected, and to it, 5 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid was added. The mixture was extracted in a boiling water bath for 10 min and then centrifuged (4,000 ×g, 10 min). From the centrifuged samples, 2 mL of the supernatant was collected, and 2 mL of glacial acetic acid was added, followed by the addition of 2 mL of acidic ninhydrin reagent. The reaction was conducted in a boiling water bath for 30 min. After cooling, 4 mL of toluene was added, and the mixture was extracted and centrifuged. The absorbance at 520 nm was measured. The concentration of proline (Pro) was calculated using a standard curve. The Pro content (µg/g) was calculated using the formula Pro = [X × 5 / 2] / g, where g represents the fresh weight of sample). X is the mass of proline (µg) determined from the standard curve for the 2 mL aliquot of supernatant used in the colorimetric measurement.

A 0.2-g fresh sample was ground with 4 mL of 5% TCA in an ice bath. The homogenate was centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 10 min), and 1 mL of the supernatant was collected. Then, 1 mL of 5% TCA, 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol, 0.5 mL of 0.4% phosphoric acid in ethanol, 1 mL of 5 g/L BP in ethanol, and 0.5 mL of 0.3 g/L FeCl₃ in ethanol were added in sequence. The mixture was thoroughly mixed, and its absorbance at 525 nm (A525) was measured. The ascorbate (AsA) content (µg/g) was calculated using the following formula: AsA content = (A525 – 0.1679) × 4 / (0.0158 × 0.2).

Physiological determination of root CW components

CW polysaccharide fraction and Cr and UA content determination utilized root CWs isolated according to the method described by Zhu et al. [39]. Subsequently, pectin, hemicellulose 1, and hemicellulose 2 fractions were sequentially extracted following the procedure described by Yang et al. [40]. The contents of these polysaccharide fractions were determined using the phenol–sulfuric acid method with glucose as the standard, by measuring the sample absorbance at 490 nm [21].

Cellulose was extracted and quantified as described by Yu et al. [18]. To determine the Cr content in each fraction, a 0.6-mL aliquot of the extracted polysaccharide fraction was digested overnight with 2 mL of nitric acid, followed by the addition of 0.5 mL perchloric acid. The mixture was heated on a graphite plate at 320 °C until the solution became clear. After cooling, the digest was diluted to 25 mL and filtered, and the Cr concentration was determined by ICP-MS.

Uronic acid content was determined using the m-hydroxydiphenyl colorimetric method with galacturonic acid as the standard, by measuring the absorbance at 352 nm. All colorimetric determinations were performed utilizing corresponding standard curves.

PME activity and Cr accumulation were determined in both high- and low-esterified pectin. Briefly, 5 mg of CW material was placed in a 2-mL centrifuge tube with 0.5 mL of 1 mol/L NaCl solution, oscillated in an ice bath for 1 h, and then centrifuged at 15,000 xg for 15 min at 4 °C. A 50-µL aliquot of the supernatant was diluted to 1 mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of this dilution was mixed with 200 µL of 0.2 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) containing 0.64 g/L pectin and 10 µL ethanol oxidase. The mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 10 min. Then, 400 µL of 5 g/L Purpald solution was added, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 550 nm. A standard curve was prepared using methanol solutions to calculate PME activity.

The extraction of high-methyl-esterified and low-methyl-esterified pectin fractions followed the method described by Astier et al. [41]. A 5-mg CW sample was mixed with 3 mL of distilled water, heated in a boiling water bath for 20 min, cooled, and centrifuged at 3,000 xg for 5 min; the supernatant represented the high-methyl-esterified pectin fraction. The remaining pellet was then extracted with 3 mL of 0.5% ammonium oxalate buffer at 85 °C for 2 h and centrifuged (4,000 xg, 5 min); the resulting supernatant represented the low-methyl-esterified pectin fraction. The contents of both pectin fractions were determined by the sulfuric acid–carbazole colorimetric method. For Cr content determination in these fractions, 1 mL of the high- or low-methyl-esterified pectin fraction was digested with a mixture of HNO₃ and HClO₄ (4:1, v:v) on a graphite plate at 320 °C until the solution became clear and transparent. After filtration and dilution to 25 mL, the Cr concentration was measured by ICP-MS.

Transcriptome sequencing

Three biological replicates were selected from the control group (i.e., untreated samples) and the Cr100 treatment group, respectively, for transcriptome analysis. Following total RNA extraction and purification, RNA quality was assessed using a Qsep400 Bio-Analyzer (New Taipei City, China). High-quality RNA was used to construct cDNA libraries. These libraries were sequenced on an Illumina high-throughput sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) utilizing sequencing by synthesis technology. Raw sequencing reads were processed and filtered using fastp software [42]. The resulting clean reads were de novo assembled into transcripts using Trinity [43], and the longest transcript clusters (unigenes) were selected for subsequent analysis. Unigenes were annotated by comparing them to transcripts in several databases, including KEGG, NR, Swiss-Prot, GO, COG/KOG, and TrEMBL, using HMMER software [44]. Differential expression analysis between sample groups was performed using DESeq2 [45, 46]. The Benjamini–Hochberg method was used to correct for multiple hypothesis testing based on the hypothesis test probabilities (P-values), thereby avoiding false positives and obtaining a set false discovery rate (FDR). We first filtered out low‑abundance and potentially misassembled transcripts by retaining only genes with FPKM > 1 in at least three of the six samples (three controls and three Cr‑treated samples). Then, DEGs were identified based on |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. These DEGs were subsequently subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses. Gene expression levels were quantified and normalized using the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) metric. The transcriptome sequencing was performed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) (See appendix for additional detailed steps).

Metabolome determination

Root samples from the control and Cr100 treatment groups, with six biological replicates each, were selected for metabolome analysis. The freeze-dried samples were ground into a fine powder using a mixer mill (MM 400, Retsch, Haan, Germany). Subsequently, 30 mg of the powdered material was weighed and extracted with 1500 µL of a pre-cooled (-20 °C) mixture of 70% methanol aqueous solution containing internal standard extracts. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 xg for 3 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected, filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22-µm pore size), and subjected to UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Pooled QC samples were used to monitor instrument stability, and internal standards were employed for normalization. The raw data were processed using Progenesis QI software for baseline filtering, peak identification, and alignment. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the `prcomp` function in R software (www.r-project.org), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the `OPLSR.Anal` function. Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) score ≥ 1 from the OPLS-DA model and a threshold of fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was then performed on the identified DAMs. The metabolic analysis was performed by Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

qRT-PCR validation

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to validate the expression levels of eighteen selected DEGs. Total RNA was extracted using the SteadyPure Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). The concentration and purity of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop™One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription PreMix Kit. The qPCR amplification was conducted using a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premixed qPCR Kit. All kits were supplied by Accurate Biology Co., Ltd. The relative gene expression levels were calculated and analyzed using the 2–ΔΔ CT method. The specific primer sequences for the target and reference genes are listed in Supplementary Table S2-S3.

Statistical analysis of data

The sample size for each independent experiment met the requirement of n ≥ 3. All experimental data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way ANOVA was used to determine significance of treatment effects and ultimately differences between groups. As sample sizes were equal across groups and Levene’s test indicated homogeneous variances (P > 0.05) for the corresponding datasets, the least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc test was used to detect pairwise differences between groups. For datasets showing significant variance heterogeneity by Levene’s test (P < 0.05), standard ANOVA was replaced with Welch’s ANOVA or non-parametric tests for subsequent statistical comparisons. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s method. Linear regression was fitted to derive the coefficient of determination (R²) and its associated P-value. All figures were generated using Origin (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA, Adobe Photoshop 2022 (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA), GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA), and an online platform (https://cloud.metware.cn).

Results

Phenotypic responses and Cr accumulation in C. sulphureus

After one week of exposure to various concentrations of Cr (0, 100, 200, 300, 400 µM), the growth phenotype of C. sulphureus exhibited significant changes (Fig. 1A). As the Cr concentration increased, plant growth was markedly inhibited, manifesting as shortened shoots and roots, fewer root branches and root tips, and reduced total root surface area, though root diameter remained relatively stable (Table S4). Under 100 µM Cr treatment, although biomass decreased, no obvious damage was observed in leaves or roots, and the plants maintained their basic physiological functions. However, when the Cr concentration reached 200 µM or higher, typical toxicity symptoms, such as leaf yellowing, wilting, and severe root atrophy, appeared.

Further analysis of Cr accumulation and tolerance in C. sulphureus (Fig. 1B–C) revealed that both roots and leaves could accumulate some Cr, with roots being the primary accumulation site, providing direct evidence for the strong Cr immobilization capacity of C. sulphureus. In terms of tolerance, the plants maintained a relatively high tolerance level under 100 µM Cr stress. However, as the Cr concentration continued to rise, tolerance gradually declined. Concurrently, the transfer coefficient of Cr from roots to shoots showed an increasing trend, indicating that C. sulphureus tends to translocate Cr to the shoots under high Cr stress, which may be one of its important strategies for physiologically adapting to HM stress.

Root physiological and biochemical responses

Cr stress induced the generation of ROS, leading to a significant increase in cellular lipid peroxidation. The level of its major product MDA rose markedly, with the degree of lipid peroxidation in root cell membranes being proportional to the Cr concentration (Fig. 2A). Analysis of antioxidant enzyme activities showed that under 100 µM Cr treatment, the activities of superoxide SOD, CAT, and POD were significantly enhanced compared to the control, with increases of 12.8%, 89.7%, and 33.7%, respectively (Fig. 2B–D). Starting from 200 µM upwards, the activities of these three enzymes generally exhibited a declining trend. However, CAT activity remained higher than that of the control throughout, whereas SOD and POD activities significantly decreased under high-concentration treatments (T3, T4), with the maximum reduction in SOD activity reaching 81.9%. These results indicated that CAT is a key enzyme in the response to Cr stress, while SOD and POD demonstrate higher sensitivity to Cr stress.

Photosynthetic pigments form the material basis for energy conversion and carbon assimilation in plants. In the present study, Cr stress significantly inhibited the synthesis of leaf photosynthetic pigments (Fig. 2E–G). The contents of both chlorophyll a and b were negatively correlated with changes in Cr concentration, showing a clear dose-dependent inhibitory effect. It is possible that this is a primary cause of the observed plant growth inhibition.

Small-molecule metabolism is a key physiological indicator of plant responses to abiotic stress. Under the same treatment conditions, the PC content was consistently higher than GSH content. Additionally, the trends in PC and NPT levels were highly consistent, indicating that PCs are key metabolites involved in the response of C. sulphureus to Cr stress. Under different Cr concentrations, the contents of NPTs, GSH, and PCs remained relatively stable without substantial variability (Fig. 2H–J). This suggests that these thiol-containing small-molecule metabolites may continuously perform detoxification and antioxidant functions under Cr stress, thereby contributing to the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. This may represent one of the long-term strategies through which C. sulphureus tolerates Cr stress. In contrast, osmoregulatory substances responded more sensitively to Cr stress. For example, the content of ascorbic acid (AsA) initially increased and then decreased with escalating stress but remained higher than the control throughout, with a maximum increase of 131.7%. Pro content peaked under the T1 treatment, showing a 19.5% increase compared to the control (Fig. 2K–M). Thus, C. sulphureus primarily responded to Cr stress by dynamically regulating osmolytes, such as AsA, although its regulatory capacity was weakened under high-concentration Cr stress, a pattern similar to the response of antioxidant enzymes.

Therefore, overall, Cr stress significantly activated the antioxidant mechanisms and osmoregulatory system in C. sulphureus. The steady accumulation of NPTs, GSH, and PCs provided a relatively stable foundation for basic Cr detoxification. Meanwhile, the antioxidant enzyme activity system and osmolytes jointly mediated a more rapid stress response process, with CAT and AsA playing particularly important roles.

Root CW composition changes and Cr accumulation

Cr stress significantly affected the content of CW polysaccharide components. Under low Cr concentration conditions, the contents of all CW polysaccharides increased markedly (Fig. 3A–D). At T1, the Cr content in each polysaccharide fraction reached its peak, and subsequently decreased at increasingly higher Cr concentrations (Fig. 3E–H). Under 100 µM Cr treatment, the Cr content in pectin reached 5 mg/g, accounting for 49.93% of the total Cr content in the CW. Hemicellulose ranked second in terms of Cr content, while cellulose bound the least Cr content.

Correlation analysis (Fig. S1) revealed that the contents of pectin and cellulose in root CW material were significantly positively correlated with the amount of Cr immobilized in these polysaccharides. Notably, pectin showed a strong correlation (R2 = 0.7897, P = 0.0001). Uronic acids, which are sugar derivatives linked to polysaccharides via glycosidic bonds, can immobilize heavy metals through electrostatic adsorption or complexation via their carboxyl (–COOH) groups. The uronic acid content of polysaccharides was also affected by Cr stress (Fig. 3I–L), and correlation analysis indicated a significant positive correlation between uronic acid content and Cr content in each polysaccharide fraction, with pectin showing the highest correlation (R2 = 0.7554, P = 0.0002; Fig. S2).

Collectively, these results suggest that pectin is the predominant polysaccharide responsible for CW immobilization of Cr, whereas hemicellulose and cellulose contribute to a lesser extent. Furthermore, the uronic acid component of polysaccharides plays a critical role in mediating Cr fixation.

Analysis of pectin esterification and enzyme activity under Cr stress revealed that Cr stress significantly increased pectin methylesterase (PME) activity, while the degree of pectin esterification decreased with increasing Cr concentration (Fig. 3M–P). Moreover, under the same stress conditions, the Cr content in low-esterified pectin was higher than that in high-esterified pectin (Fig. 3Q–R). Correlation analysis (Fig. S3) showed that PME activity was significantly positively correlated with both the Cr content in pectin and the content of low-esterified pectin, with correlation coefficients of R2 = 0.7444 (P = 0.0003) and R2 = 0.8618 (P < 0.0001), respectively.

Transcriptome results and analysis

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that Cr stress significantly affected the gene expression profile of C. sulphureus. Based on the detailed RNA-Seq assembly, a total of 75,849 gene sequences were obtained (for detailed transcriptome assembly statistics see Table S5), of which at least 67,211 sequences (88.61%) were recorded in at least one database (e.g., KEGG, NR, Swiss-Prot, TrEMBL, KOG, or GO). More than half of the genes were recorded across all databases. The error rate of the samples was 0.01%, with greater than 99% and 97% of reads meeting Q20 and Q30 thresholds, respectively. The GC content of the samples ranged from 43.83% to 44.61%. Relevant assembly quality statistics (N50 = 1,369 bp, N90 = 530 bp, average length = 1,072 bp) confirmed the reliability of the transcriptomic data (Table S6). We excluded samples with low abundance, resulting in 69,282 qualified sequences.The volcano plot indicated that, compared to control conditions, a total of 41,296 genes were significantly regulated under Cr stress, with 26,505 up-regulated and 14,791 down-regulated (Fig. 4A). We used Busco software to assess the integrity of the assembled transcripts, and the results met the quality control criteria (Fig. S4). Furthermore, PCA demonstrated clear separation between treatment and control group samples along PC1, with a contribution rate of 75.06% and non-overlapping confidence intervals (Fig. S5).

Discussion

Comparative mechanisms and ecological advantages of Cr tolerance in C. sulphureus root

Cr itself does not participate in any natural metabolic reactions, but its toxicity impedes normal biological growth, triggering various stress injuries and physiological responses in plants [9] The present study observed that Cr stress induced typical growth inhibition in C. sulphureus, including dwarfing, reduced biomass, and hindered root development, consistent with reports in other species including cowpea [47], tall fescue, and alfalfa [48]. Different concentrations of Cr stress underlie variation in the degree of plant toxicity and the corresponding response mechanisms. For example, C. bipinnatus exhibited metal rejection at Cr concentrations of 0.0–0.5 mM, while it shows metal accumulation at Cr concentrations of 1.0–2.0 mM [26]. Based on the physiological and biochemical data obtained from preliminary experiments in which different Cr concentrations were applied to C. sulphureus, we found that a Cr concentration of 100 µM represents a critical tolerance threshold. Under 100 µM Cr treatment, no obvious toxic symptoms appeared in roots or leaves, and the plant phenotype remained largely stable, while higher concentrations of stress induced visible damage, such as leaf yellowing and necrosis. Therefore, 100 µM likely corresponds to a key physiological threshold for C. sulphureus. At this stress level, plant defense mechanisms are activated, but plant function remains unaffected. Accordingly, this is an excellent condition for further investigation of molecular mechanisms and for screening genes related to tolerance. In C. sulphureus, Cr stress reduced root length, tip number, and surface area, thereby potentially compromising uptake efficiency, as observed in tomato [49] and Arabidopsis thaliana [33]. This root plasticity, potentially owing to Cr-inhibited cytokinin synthesis and CW elongation, may serve as an adaptive strategy; while some key physical dimensions of roots were reduced, a stable root diameter likely helped maintain structural integrity and basic physiological function under stress.

Regarding Cr accumulation and distribution, this study found that, as observed in most plants, the roots of C. sulphureus were the primary Cr accumulation site, with limited translocation to the shoots [50]. As the Cr stress concentration was increased, root damage intensified, and the translocation factor also increased correspondingly. The plants exhibited the strongest tolerance and the lowest translocation factor under 100 µM Cr. This aligns with previous findings in Arabidopsis thaliana [33], in which root growth was significantly inhibited above this concentration. When Cr concentrations rose beyond this point, C. sulphureus tended to translocate more Cr to its shoots. This may represent an adaptive strategy employed by the plant to mitigate localized toxicity under severe stress, which could also explain the observed chlorosis and severe damage to aboveground leaves observed under high Cr concentrations.

Morphophysiological regulation, activation of antioxidant systems, and expression of Cr-tolerance proteins are widely present in crops such as rice and wheat in response to Cr stress [51]. However, feedback systems dependent on reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation and enzymatic reactions exhibit inconsistent long-term versus short-term effects and are susceptible to physiological variations among different varieties or genotypes [52, 53]. Although CW-mediated mitigation of Cr stress has also been reported in rice [54], the Cr immobilization capacity of crop CW is limited [55]; when external Cr exceeds the root accumulation capacity, Cr(VI) tends to be translocated from roots to shoots [56]. In contrast, Cosmos species can accumulate substantial amounts of Cr, exhibiting a lower translocation factor and stronger tolerance [18] (Fig. 1). This is attributed to their detoxification mechanism based primarily on CW immobilization, which differs from the easily perturbed enzyme systems in crops. Ecologically, this strategy reduces Cr translocation to aboveground parts, thereby protecting photosynthesis, and makes Cosmos more suitable as a long-term phytoremediation plant for contaminated soils.

Physiological response mechanisms in roots under Cr stress

HM ions can stimulate the formation of ROS both through direct electron transfer and by inhibiting metabolic reactions [57]. The antioxidant enzyme system precisely regulates ROS metabolism via cascades involving multiple enzymes [58]. Cr stress induced ROS accumulation in C. sulphureus, triggering oxidative stress and membrane damage (as indicated by elevated MDA). The activities of SOD and POD increased initially but declined at higher Cr concentrations, likely owing to ROS-induced enzyme denaturation or impaired synthesis [59]. In contrast, CAT activity remained high under severe stress, attributable to its peroxisomal localization and H₂O₂ specificity. The concurrent MDA content surge and widespread enzyme decline are consistent with a positive feedback cycle of oxidative damage and antioxidant collapse [60].

Photosynthetic pigments often show a biphasic pattern under stress: an adaptive increase under mild conditions for photoprotection, followed by a decline under severe stress caused by irreversible damage [61]. In the present study, leaf chlorophyll content declined under Cr stress, consistent with inhibited biosynthesis and a constrained energy supply, likely owing to impaired electron transport and chloroplast damage [62, 63]. The inhibitory effect of Cr on plant photosynthetic pigments is quite common, as also reported to occur in Brassica napus [64], Miscanthus sinensis [65], and Canna indica [66]. The findings of the present study further corroborate the general inhibitory effect of Cr stress on plant energy metabolism.

Under Cr stress, small-molecule metabolites in C. sulphureus exhibited distinct response patterns, indicating that they possess a multi-level response mechanism. Although both GSH and PCs are non-protein thiol compounds, there are fundamental differences between them in terms of their synthesis pathways, functional roles, and responses to HM stress. GSH serves as an important antioxidant and redox buffer in plants, while also acting as a precursor for the synthesis of PCs. PCs, on the other hand, are plant-specific HM-chelating peptides that directly participate in the separation and detoxification of HM. Previous research has shown that the accumulation of NPTs is primarily owing to the induced synthesis of PCs [67]. This indicates that, under Cr stress, GSH functions more effectively as a precursor for PC synthesis, thereby helping plants to counteract HM toxicity. Overall, however, the levels of NPTs remain relatively stable under various Cr stress conditions, suggesting that C. sulphureus has a strong ability to maintain thiol metabolism balance. This capability enables the plant to continue performing its chelating and redox buffering functions even under prolonged Cr exposure, allowing it to adapt to harsh environmental conditions. In contrast, AsA and Pro showed dynamic responses. The early increase in AsA indicated its direct involvement in Cr(VI) reduction and ROS scavenging, while its subsequent decline under high stress may have resulted from limited synthesis or excessive consumption—a mechanism also observed in tomato roots [49]. Pro accumulation under mild stress can help maintain osmotic balance and protect macromolecular structures, but its content did not increase further under higher Cr concentrations, possibly owing to inhibition of its biosynthetic pathway or diversion of carbon and nitrogen resources to other stress-resistance metabolites. Cr stress not only affects the metabolic levels of AsA and Pro [68, 69], but also interferes with the overall Pro metabolism pathway and the function of the AsA–GSH antioxidant cycle system in plants [70]. Therefore, these dynamic changes collectively indicate that under Cr stress, C. sulphureus may preferentially mobilize rapidly responsive osmolytes and antioxidants, such as AsA and Pro, to counteract acute oxidative damage, while simultaneously maintaining a stable chelation-based detoxification mechanism. These findings are consistent with the existence of a potential metabolic defense network that could contribute to both short-term adaptation and long-term tolerance to stress.

CW pectin modification as a putative mechanism alleviating Cr stress in Cosmos sulphureus

The CW serves as the primary site for Cr storage and sequestration in plant roots [71, 72]. Cosmos sulphureus can adapt to Cr stress by modulating the composition and structure of CW polysaccharides (Fig. 3A–D). This study showed that, under all Cr stress conditions, the Cr accumulation was consistently highest in pectin, followed by hemicellulose and cellulose (Fig. 3E–H). CW polysaccharides mainly immobilize HM through chelation, a process dependent on active substances. Uronic acids are carbohydrate derivatives formed by the oxidation of the primary hydroxyl group (C6 position) of monosaccharides to a carboxyl group (–COOH) [73]. Their content directly influences the HM adsorption capacity of polysaccharides (Fig. S2). Notably, although both pectin and cellulose contain high levels of uronic acids under Cr stress, the overall contribution of cellulose to Cr immobilization is much lower due to its smaller proportion in the CW. Therefore, pectin serves as the primary component responsible for Cr fixation in the CW (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the Cr content in pectin was significantly positively correlated with the Cr content bound to pectin uronic acids (Fig. S2A), consistent with findings in C. bipinnatus [18]. These results indicate that uronic acids are likely the key Cr-binding sites in pectin, and that pectin’s ability to immobilize Cr is largely attributable to its uronic acid residues.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that the chelating capacity of pectin is closely correlated with its degree of esterification, with de-esterification serving as the primary mechanism for regulating pectin esterification [18]. This process is directly controlled by pectin methylesterase (PME) activity [54, 74]. Pectins with lower esterification degrees exhibit stronger chelating abilities [75] (Fig. 3Q and R), primarily attributed to the increased number of carboxyl groups exposed upon removal of ester groups, which show higher affinity for Cr ions [76]. In this study, Cr stress significantly induced PME activity and increased the proportion of low-esterified pectin, with changes trending consistently with pectin-bound Cr content. Furthermore, PME activity exhibited a significant positive correlation with low-esterified pectin content (Fig. S3), indicating that enhanced PME activity is a key driver of pectin de-esterification. This process facilitates Cr sequestration in the CW, reduces its intracellular transport, and thereby alleviates Cr toxicity. These findings are consistent with those reported by Li et al. [77]. In summary, we hypothesize that under Cr stress, C. sulphureus upregulates PME activity to promote pectin de-esterification, generating more low-esterified pectin and thereby increasing carboxyl binding sites for Cr ion chelation, which constitutes one of the core mechanisms of Cr detoxification mediated by CW pectin. However, it should be noted that these conclusions are primarily based on correlational evidence and lack direct experimental validation. The observed correlations may also result from synergistic regulation of multiple CW-modifying enzymes under Cr stress, or from indirect factors such as altered pectin biosynthesis or calcium-mediated cross-linking [78]. Therefore, our proposed working hypothesis remains to be directly tested through functional experiments. Future studies employing CRISPR/Cas9 to knock out or suppress PME gene expression, or using specific PME inhibitors, are needed to determine whether alterations in PME activity causally affect Cr accumulation and plant tolerance.

CW polysaccharides other than pectin also showed specific responses to Cr stress. Although the content and Cr-binding capacity of most polysaccharides decreased with increasing Cr concentration, the barrier and immobilization functions of the CW remained stable even under high Cr stress. This stability was attributed to the mechanical support from cellulose and hemicellulose, as well as to cross-linking between pectin and other polysaccharides. These findings are consistent with Yu et al. [18]. However, the core mechanisms of Cr immobilization by other polysaccharides and their synergistic cross-linking with pectin require further study. Compared with the antioxidant system, the CW sequestration mechanism exhibits greater stability due to its physicochemical immobilization strategy.

Cr tolerance strategy involving growth-to-defense transition revealed by DEGs

Based on the systematic analysis of DEGs, we posit that the adaptive strategy of reallocating energy and resources in response to stress is a core mechanism by which plants enhance their own tolerance. The Cr tolerance mechanism of C. sulphureus is specifically manifested by a shift in energy allocation from “promoting growth” under normal conditions to “enhancing defense” under stress, and this is accompanied by coordinated gene expression changes. Notably, Cr-induced DEGs accounted for more than half of the total transcriptome, indicating that Cr triggered extensive transcriptional reprogramming. Similar phenomena have been reported in studies on Miscanthus sinensis [65] and Canna indica [11], suggesting that this is related to the high toxicity of Cr. Additionally, transcriptional reprogramming is a common mechanism through which plants respond to environmental stresses. For example, under lead stress, the expression levels of 51,281 genes were altered in Pogonatherum crinitum [79]. In contrast, when treated with both salicylic acid and cadmium, the expression levels of 37,438 genes were altered in the roots, while expression levels of 11,679 genes were altered in the stems of the hyperaccumulator Sedum alfredii [80]. It is therefore reasonable to assume that HM stress leads to changes in the transcription levels of thousands to tens of thousands of genes.

In the present study, the down-regulation of genes related to photosystem light-harvesting proteins (e.g., CB11, CB12, LHCA3) under Cr stress confirmed the inhibition of photosynthesis. Concurrently, energy was preferentially redirected towards defense-related metabolism. For example, the up-regulation of 4CL, a key gene encoding an enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, facilitates the synthesis of secondary defense products, such as flavonoids and lignin; the up-regulation of the trehalose synthesis gene TPS11 contributes to stabilizing cellular structures and scavenging ROS.

ABC transporters constitute a large family of membrane proteins that utilize ATP hydrolysis to power the transmembrane transport of various substrates, playing a crucial role in plant HM stress responses and HM detoxification [81]. Furthermore, many ABC family members are localized to the vacuole membrane, where they promote detoxification by isolating various HM chelates within the vacuole, thereby enhancing plant tolerance. In the present study, the expression of ABC family genes (such as AB1G, AB1A, AB16G, AB21G, AB3F) was significantly upregulated, indicating that under Cr stress, plant Cr accumulation is closely related to the regulation of cellular transmembrane transport responses. Additionally, this process requires a substantial amount of energy, consistent with the existence of an energy redistribution mechanism. Previous studies have confirmed that the ABC transporter family plays a specific key role in the detoxification and compartmentalization of HMs such as cadmium and arsenic [82, 83]. Our present study, in agreement with previous research [8], suggests that members of this family may also be involved in the Cr stress response of plants. This indicates that ABC transporters may function within a conserved network that plays a synergistic role in various HM detoxification mechanisms, meriting their further investigation in future studies.

Finally, in the present study, the expression patterns of CW-related genes clearly demonstrate the functional remodeling of the C. sulphureus CW under Cr stress. On the one hand, genes promoting cell expansion (e.g., EXP10, LRX4, PGLR4) and cellulose synthesis genes (e.g., CESA2, CESA8) were down-regulated, reducing energy expenditure on structural extension. On the other hand, coordinated changes in the expression of pectin modification-related genes, such as the up-regulation of PME2 and down-regulation of PMEU1, led to an increased degree of pectin de-esterification, thereby exposing more carboxyl groups for efficient fixation of Cr ions. This expression pattern is consistent with the physiological observation of increased low-methylated pectin content (Fig. 3M-R). Furthermore, the up-regulation of some CW protein-related genes (e.g., TLP, PR1A, CHSY) may further reinforce the physical barrier functionality. Unfortunately, although various omics-related data, including physiological and transcriptional data, suggested a potential positive role of CW pectin modification in Cr fixation, there remains a lack of direct evidence. To ultimately establish a direct causal relationship between pectin methylation and Cr detoxification, genetic approaches will be necessary in future research. This will include experiments involving overexpression or silencing of key PME genes, combined with subcellular localization techniques for functional validation. Our research lays a theoretical foundation for further exploration of this mechanism and provides key candidate genes for such research.

Cr tolerance mechanism involving activated antioxidant defense and CW chelation revealed by DAMs

Low-molecular-weight organic acids function as key oxygen-containing metal-chelating ligands, participating in maintaining internal metal homeostasis, various metabolic processes, and plant responses to HM stress [84]. Under Cr stress, multiple organic acids can chelate Cr ions, thus reducing Cr toxicity and availability, while also modulating rhizosphere pH such that HM speciation and bioavailability are influenced, thereby enhancing plant Cr tolerance and detoxification capacity [85]. Cr treatment altered organic acid metabolism, upregulating phenylpyruvic acid and cis-aconitic acid—key nodes in the shikimate pathway. The former is a precursor for phenolics (e.g., flavonoids, lignin) via PAL catalysis, while the latter is an upstream input of aromatic amino acid and secondary metabolite biosynthesis [86]. These findings suggest Cr stress induces metabolic fluxes in organic acids, particularly by promoting the accumulation of key intermediates in the shikimate pathway, thereby driving the synthesis of phenylpropanoids and aromatic amino acid-derived metabolites. This is likely closely associated with enhanced plant adaptation mechanisms involving antioxidation and chelation detoxification.

Amino acids, as organic acids with amino groups and other diverse functional groups, can chelate HMs, regulate cellular osmosis, and alleviate oxidative stress [87]. Glutamate (Glu), cysteine (Cys), and glycine (Gly) are crucial precursors of GSH synthesis, while Pro and alanine (Ala) participate in osmoregulation. Under Cr stress, peptides and amino acids, including Gly-Pro-Gly, Glu-Pro-Lys, Cys-Cys, and l-Pro, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5D), consistent with the physiological results (Fig. 2H–L). This indicates C. sulphureus enhances its Cr tolerance through amino acid mentalism modulation. Notably, AsA, an acidic vitamin, increased under Cr stress, thereby directly scavenging ROS and often acting synergistically with Pro and GSH [86], effectively revealing the comprehensive resistance mechanism relying on small molecule metabolites. Flavonoids, known for their antioxidant properties, are widely involved in plant stress responses. Previous studies have indicated that flavonoids act as defensive antioxidants, efficiently scavenging ROS while chelating HM ions, thus mitigating oxidative damage [88]. In the present study, representative flavonoids, including prunin and amentoflavone, were upregulated. It can thus be inferred that Cr-induced increases in flavonoid metabolites enhance the antioxidant capacity of C. sulphureus.

Previous research suggests that plants faced with stress can enhance their HM tolerance by inducing CW metabolism and structural remodeling, thus reducing ion entry into the protoplast and translocation to shoots [89]. This primarily involves increasing root pectin and cellulose content and thickening the CW to collectively regulate HM accumulation and translocation, thereby mitigating HM toxicity [90]. Pectin content influences plant Cr fixation capacity. Various sugars are the basic units of pectin, and enzymes play key roles in pectin synthesis and modification. In the present study, sugars related to CW polysaccharide (especially pectin and cellulose) synthesis and modification, including l-arabinose, raffinose, galactitol, and d-galactaro-1,5-lactone, were significantly upregulated under Cr stress. Beyond polysaccharides, lignin is critical for HM chelation and structural homeostasis in the CW. It cross-links with pectin and cellulose to immobilize metals, while its deposition strengthens the CW, thereby reducing ion penetration. The present study found that Cr stress induced the upregulation of lignin-related benzenoids and amino acids (e.g., phenylpyruvic acid, l-tryptophan) in C. sulphureus. These compounds support CW synthesis and modification, thus enhancing chelation capacity and Cr tolerance. Future targeted metabolomics could identify key metabolites involved in this response.

Ascorbate and galactose metabolism mediate antioxidant defense and CW remodeling under Cr stress

Through a joint correlation analysis of the DEGs and DAMs (Fig. S11), we identified two core pathways involved in the Cr stress response, along with the key genes and metabolites associated with the chromium stress response within these pathways. AsA is a key non-enzymatic antioxidant that mitigates oxidative stress caused by abiotic stimuli [91, 92]. Its synthesis and recycling involve two primary pathways: direct oxidation of l-galactono-1,4-lactone, and regeneration of oxidized AsA (l-dehydroascorbate) via GSH-dependent dehydroascorbate reductase, coupled with GSH metabolism in the AsA–GSH cycle for sustained antioxidant capacity. The present study observed a significant increase in AsA content under Cr stress (T1 treatment, Fig. 2K), alongside upregulated expression of genes encoding GR and Acyl-CoA oxidase (Fig. 7A), indicating Cr stress induced AsA synthesis and cycling, thus enhancing stress resistance. Biosynthetically, starting from UDP-d-glucose via the glucuronate pathway to d-glucuronate and subsequently through galactose metabolism to l-galactono-1,4-lactone, the l-galactose pathway is crucial for AsA production. Key enzymes like UGDH, GDP-l-galactose phosphorylase, and l-galactonolactone dehydrogenase were upregulated under Cr stress (Table S8), promoting the conversion of l-galactono-1,4-lactone into AsA, thereby enhancing the AsA synthesis rate. Thus, the ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathway represents a vital mechanism for the plant antioxidant response to HM stress.

Pectin, a crucial CW component, is essential for chelation and structural stability. UDP-GalA serves as the basic building block of pectin polysaccharide chains, which are elongated by glycosyltransferases. Newly synthesized pectin is highly methyl-esterified and requires PME-catalyzed de-esterification to expose its negatively charged carboxyl groups, thus forming low-esterified pectin that readily gels and chelates metal ions. The present study found increased pectin content and PME activity under Cr stress (Fig. 3). Genes encoding glycosyltransferases and PME were upregulated, while those encoding PMEU and pectin-degrading enzymes (e.g., PL, releasing galactose) were downregulated (Fig. 7B). This expression pattern collectively promoted the synthesis and accumulation of low-esterified pectin, thus enhancing CW chelation and immobilization of Cr ions. Furthermore, the galactose metabolism network, which is activated by enzymes like hexokinase, UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, phosphoglucomutase (Table S9), and galactosyltransferases, facilitates the interconversion and dynamic supply of key metabolites (e.g., galactose, UDP-galactose, UDP-glucuronic acid). This regulation supplies pectin precursors while generating stores of energy (e.g., fructose, sucrose) available for CW remodeling. Thus, under Cr stress, precise galactose metabolism prioritizes pectin synthesis and CW restructuring to enhance metal tolerance.

It should be noted that transcriptomics and metabolomics were performed only at 100 µM Cr. Given the progressive deterioration of plant phenotypes at higher Cr concentrations, dose-dependent omics studies are needed in the future to fully elucidate the response mechanisms under different stress intensities.

Conclusion

Multi-omics analysis indicated that C. sulphureus employs a restriction–detoxification strategy to cope with Cr stress. From a physiological perspective, the study indicate that Cr is primarily stored in the root CW, with pectin polysaccharides serving as the main deposition sites and that the ability of pectin to sequester Cr is closely related to the presence of low-esterified pectin. Additionally, the active antioxidant system, including hydrogen peroxide and AsA, plays a crucial role in Cr detoxification. At the transcriptome level, changes in the expression of genes associated with secondary metabolism, ABC transporters, and CW biosynthesis were observed. Genes involved in the oxidative stress response, energy metabolism, and CW synthesis, such as GALE2, 4CLLA, TPS11, PME2, and CHSY, were upregulated. In contrast, genes related to photosynthetic pigment synthesis, CW elongation, and pectin degradation, such as CB12, PMEU1, CESA2, and PGLR4, were downregulated. These changes reflect energy reallocation in C. sulphureus in response to Cr stress, in which defense mechanisms are prioritized over growth and cellular maintenance to mitigate stress-induced damage. In terms of metabolic changes, the accumulation of organic acids, amino acids, and sugars supports antioxidant responses and CW biosynthesis. These synergistic processes, particularly those involving AsA and galactose metabolism, promote the synthesis of l-AsA, d-galactose, and pectin, allowing C. sulphureus plants to efficiently detoxify Cr.

Studies have shown that C. sulphureus maintains stable growth traits under 100 µM Cr stress, exhibiting excellent adaptability and tolerance to Cr. Its root system possesses a high Cr accumulation capacity with low Cr translocation efficiency to above-ground tissues, and multiple tolerance mechanisms function synergistically in response to Cr stress. Accordingly, C. sulphureus is suitable for long-term landscape remediation in lightly Cr-contaminated areas, integrating ecological remediation and landscape beautification functions. Furthermore, this study provides novel insights into the Cr tolerance mechanisms of C. sulphureus, and reveals the potential role of pectin esterification modification in forming CW-based Cr chelation barriers. It not only enriches the theoretical framework regarding CW modification-mediated HM accumulation in plants, but also offers potential genetic resources for improving phytoremediation efficiency toward Cr via molecular breeding strategies.
