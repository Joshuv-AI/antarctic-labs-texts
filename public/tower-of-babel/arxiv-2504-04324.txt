Learning Flatness-Preserving Residuals for Pure-Feedback Systems
Fengjun Yang, Jake Welde, Nikolai Matni

Abstract
We study residual dynamics learning for differentially flat systems, where a nominal model is augmented with a learned correction term from data. A key challenge is that generic residual parameterizations may destroy flatness, limiting the applicability of flatness-based planning and control methods. To address this, we propose a framework for learning flatness-preserving residual dynamics in systems whose nominal model admits a pure-feedback form. We show that residuals with a lower-triangular structure preserve both the flatness of the system and the original flat outputs. Moreover, we provide a constructive procedure to recover the flatness diffeomorphism of the augmented system from that of the nominal model. Building on these insights, we introduce a parameterization of flatness-preserving residuals using smooth function approximators, making them learnable from trajectory data with conventional algorithms. Our approach is validated in simulation on a 2D quadrotor subject to unmodeled aerodynamic effects. We demonstrate that the resulting learned flat model achieves a tracking error 5 × 5\times lower than the nominal flat model, while being 20 × 20\times faster over a structure-agnostic alternative.



1
Introduction

Differential flatness enables the use of simple and efficient linear techniques for trajectory planning and feedback design in nonlinear systems
(
Fliess et al., 1995
)
. For systems such as quadrotors, the existence of a flatness diffeomorphism allows the system to be transformed into a chain of integrators, greatly simplifying control
(
Mellinger and Kumar, 2011
;
Greeff and Schoellig, 2018
)
. However, flatness is typically established for idealized or nominal models
(
Welde et al., 2023
)
, which often fail to capture real-world disturbances and unmodeled dynamics that arise in practice. In certain examples, known perturbations or unmodeled components can be incorporated into an augmented flat model
(
Faessler et al., 2017
)
, but there is currently no general methodology for doing so, or for assessing whether these perturbations will preserve flatness.

Motivated by this shortcoming, we propose augmenting a differentially flat nominal model by learning to approximate the unmodeled (i.e.,
residual
) dynamics from trajectory data. However, existing residual learning algorithms
(
Shi et al., 2019
;
Carron et al., 2019
;
Torrente et al., 2021
;
Chee et al., 2022
)
typically use generic functional forms for the residual, which can lead to augmented dynamics that no longer enjoy the flatness property. Furthermore, even if flatness is preserved, it is often unclear how to construct the flatness diffeomorphism for the resulting system, rendering flatness-based algorithms inapplicable. To overcome this, we restrict our attention to a class of differentially flat nominal dynamics that admit a pure-feedback form and derive a sufficient condition under which the residual preserves flatness. We show that when the residual is lower-triangular—a property that preserves the pure-feedback structure of the nominal dynamics—the flatness diffeomorphisms of the augmented system can be constructed using those of the nominal model. We validate our approach on planar quadrotor dynamics, achieving significantly lower tracking error than without learning while requiring an order of magnitude less computation time than a structure-agnostic approach that incorporated the learned residual via nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to address learning flatness-preserving residual dynamics for differentially flat systems.

1.1
Related Work

1.1.1
Learning Residual Dynamics

Leveraging learned residual dynamics in the control loop has been shown to improve tracking performance on various robotic systems, including manipulators
(
Carron et al., 2019
)
and quadrotors
(
Shi et al., 2019
;
Torrente et al., 2021
;
Chee et al., 2022
)
. Prior work incorporated residual dynamics by designing system-specific controllers or applying general techniques such as NMPC. In
Shi et al. (2019)
, the authors designed a fix-point iteration-based algorithm tailored to quadrotors to compute the control input. In
Carron et al. (2019)
;
Torrente et al. (2021)
;
Chee et al. (2022)
, the authors compute the control input with NMPC, which is drastically more computationally expensive than flatness-based control methods for quadrotors
(
Sun et al., 2022
)
. To address these challenges, we restrict our nominal dynamics to a class of differentially flat pure-feedback systems and seek to learn flatness-preserving residuals, thereby retaining the computational benefits of flatness-based approaches while eliminating the need for case-by-case controller design.

1.1.2
Learning to Linearize Nonlinear Systems

Learning-based techniques have been applied to differentially flat systems to derive linearizing coordinate transformations from data. Representative examples include seeking to identify flatness diffeomorphisms from input-output data
(
Sferrazza et al., 2016
;
Ma et al., 2022
)
, and directly learning a feedback-linearizing control law
(
Umlauft et al., 2017
;
Westenbroek et al., 2019
;
Greeff and Schoellig, 2020
)
.
For pure-feedback systems, adaptive control methods have also been proposed to deal with parametric uncertainty in the dynamics, including model reference adaptive control
(
Nam and Araposthathis, 1988
)
and neural control methods that leverage backstepping
(
Ge and Wang, 2002
;
Ge and Wang, 2004
)
. In a related vein, model-free control techniques exist for flat systems to estimate and reject more general disturbances online
(
Fliess and Join, 2013
;
Join et al., 2024
)
. In contrast to these approaches, which are complementary to our work, we focus on learning residual dynamics from data while ensuring that the resulting augmented model preserves flatness.

1.2
Contributions

Our contributions are three-fold:

•

Flatness-preserving residuals:
We identify a broad class of residual dynamics that preserve the differential flatness of pure-feedback form systems (Theorem
2
) and show how to systematically construct the flatness diffeomorphisms for the augmented model from those of the nominal model (Theorem
3
).

•

Learning algorithm:
We develop a parameterization of the residual dynamics that enforces this structure, enabling flatness-preserving residual models to be learned directly from trajectory data.

•

Empirical validation:
We demonstrate in simulation that our method significantly reduces tracking error relative to the nominal controller while being an order-of-magnitude more computationally efficient than an NMPC-based structure-agnostic approach.

Preliminaries and Problem Formulation

Consider the nonlinear system

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

(1)

with state
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and control input
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We recall the definition of a differentially flat system, as originally introduced by
Fliess et al. (1995)
, but follow the formulation given in
Hagenmeyer and Delaleau (2003)

Definition 1

(See
Hagenmeyer and Delaleau (2003)
)
.

The nonlinear system (
1
) is
(differentially) flat
if a
flat output

\mathbf{y}=\Lambda(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\dot{\mathbf{u}},\ddot{\mathbf{u}},\cdots,\mathbf{u}^{(\alpha)})

and a finite number of its time derivatives can be used to reconstruct the state
\mathbf{x}
and control input
\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\Phi(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},\ddot{\mathbf{y}},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{(\beta)}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\Psi(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},\ddot{\mathbf{y}},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{(\beta)}).

Here,
\Lambda
,
\Phi
, and
\Psi
are smooth functions, with
(\Phi,\Psi)
referred to as the

. The quantities
\alpha

\mathbf{z}
. We say that this system is

(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*})
if the equalities above hold in a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*})
.

As outlined in the introduction, differential flatness enables efficient planning and control by allowing for the nonlinear system (
1
) to be rewritten as an equivalent linear one (see,
e.g.
,
Fliess et al. (1995)
;
Murray et al. (1995)
;
Agrawal et al. (2021)
;
Greeff and Schoellig (2018)
). However, this procedure often requires exact knowledge of the dynamics
f
, whereas it is often the case that, in practice, only a nominal model
\bar{f}
is known. This motivates the decomposition

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})=\bar{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+\Delta(x,u),

(2)

of the dynamics (
1
) into a

component, defined by
\bar{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
, and a

component

\Delta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}):=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})-\bar{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}).

(3)

For example, in the context of quadrotor control, the known nominal model might capture the rigid body dynamics, whereas the unknown residual dynamics might capture harder-to-model aerodynamic effects such as rotor drag
(
Kai et al., 2017
;
Faessler et al., 2017
;
Shi et al., 2019
;
Chee et al., 2022
)
or another quadrotor’s downwash
(
Jain et al., 2019
;
Shi et al., 2020
)
.

When these unknown residual dynamics are significant enough to affect control performance, they need to be approximated so that the controller can compensate for their effects. One common approach is
residual learning
, which leverages data collected from the true system (
2
) to learn an estimate
\hat{\Delta}
of the residual dynamics (
3
), resulting in an
augmented model

\hat{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})=\bar{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+\hat{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})

(4)

that can be used for improved planning and control.

However, augmenting a differentially flat nominal model
\bar{f}
with a learned residual term
\hat{\Delta}
will in general not result in a differentially flat augmented model
\hat{f}=\bar{f}+\hat{\Delta}
.
We therefore seek to identify general parameterizations of residual dynamics
\hat{\Delta}
such that the augmented nominal model
\hat{f}

is differentially flat if the nominal model
\bar{f}
is differentially flat.
Towards that end, we restrict our attention to nominal dynamics that can be expressed in pure-feedback form.

Assumption 1

(Pure-Feedback Form Nominal Dynamics)
.

The nominal dynamics
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\bar{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
can be put into a multi-input multi-output nonlinear
pure-feedback
form

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}),

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}),

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{r},\mathbf{u}),

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are (vector) sub-states that decompose the joint state
\mathbf{x}
as
{\mathbf{x}:=[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{r}^{\top}]^{\top}}
and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the control inputs. The sub-state nominal dynamics
\bar{f}_{i}
are respectively of dimensions
\bar{f}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{(i+1)m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
1
implicitly assumes that the
m
sub-states
\mathbf{x}_{i,1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{i,m}
have the same relative degree
r
, and hence all
\mathbf{x}_{i}
share the same dimension with
\mathbf{u}
. We choose this formulation for ease of notation and exposition, but our analysis can be adapted to the case where the sub-states have different relative degrees via dynamic extension .

Given the above, we next impose a regularity condition which ensures that pure-feedback form nominal dynamics are flat. We then define a parameterization of residual dynamics models ensuring that the resulting augmented model is flat if the nominal model is flat. We also show how to construct flatness diffeomorphisms for the augmented model from those
of the nominal model. Finally, we present our flatness-preserving residual learning algorithm and illustrate it via numerical experiments on a 2D quadrotor system.

Flatness-Preserving Perturbations

We show that flat nominal dynamics satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1

We first define a class of
lower-triangular
residual dynamics that are compatible with the pure-feedback form of the nominal dynamics (

Definition 2

(Lower-Triangular Residual Dynamics)
.

Residual dynamics
\Delta(\mathbf{x})

\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\Delta_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\Delta_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{r})\end{bmatrix},

(8)

for continuously differentiable
\Delta_{i}

i=1,\dots,r

.

While this structure may seem overly restrictive, particular instances satisfying these assumptions have already been identified in the literature. We highlight one such example in the context of unmodeled aerodynamics for aerial vehicles at the end of this section.

The next theorem shows that restricting ourselves to lower-triangular residual dynamics, which preserve the pure-feedback structure and regularity of the nominal dynamics (
5

Theorem 2
.

Let the nominal model
\bar{f}
satisfy Assumptions
1
and
2

be lower-triangular. Then

are locally differentially flat around
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*})
with flat output
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{1}

Proof.

Due to the lower-triangular structure of the residual, the perturbed dynamics

are still in pure-feedback form, and share relevant Jacobians with the nominal dynamics:

D_{\mathbf{x}_{i+1}}\Big(\bar{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{i+1})+\hat{\Delta}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{i})\Big)\\
=D_{\mathbf{x}_{i+1}}\bar{f}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{i+1}),

as
\hat{\Delta}_{i}

\mathbf{x}_{i+1}

1

∎

Note that this class of residual dynamics also preserves the flat outputs of the nominal system. This is desirable, as the flat outputs often carry semantic meaning that benefits downstream planning and control tasks.

5
Learning Flatness-Preserving Residuals

The results in Section
4
identify a rich class of residual dynamics (Def.
2
) that can be added to a flat nominal system in pure-feedback form so as to define augmented systems that are also differentially flat (Thm.
2
), and for which the corresponding flatness diffeomorphisms can be easily constructed given those of the nominal system (Thm.
3
). We leverage these insights to propose an algorithm for learning flatness-preserving residuals from data.

In particular, we assume access to a set of trajectories
{\boldsymbol{\tau}=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}
. Each trajectory
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}
consists of a sequence of state and control input pairs

\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}=\Big(\big(\mathbf{x}^{i}(t_{1}),\mathbf{u}^{i}(t_{1})\big),,\ldots,\big(\mathbf{x}^{i}(t_{T}),\mathbf{u}^{i}(t_{T})\big)\Big)

sampled at times
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{T}\}
generated by the
true dynamics
(
2
). We aim to learn a flatness-preserving residual dynamics
\hat{\Delta}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
, defined by learnable parameters
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, that best augment the nominal model to match the trajectories
\boldsymbol{\tau}
generated by the true system.

So as to leverage Theorems
2
and
3
, we restrict the learnable residual dynamics model
\hat{\Delta}_{\Theta}
to be lower-triangular:

\hat{\Delta}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\Delta}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1};\theta_{1})\\
\hat{\Delta}_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2};\theta_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\hat{\Delta}_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{r};\theta_{r})\end{bmatrix},

where
\Theta:=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r})
are parameters of suitable dimensions
and the
\hat{\Delta}_{i}
are continuously differentiable in each
\mathbf{x}_{j}
. By Theorem
2
, the augmented system
\bar{f}+\hat{\Delta}_{\Theta}
is differentially flat, and its flatness diffeomorphisms can be constructed according to equation (
10
).
A practically appealing and standard choice is to parameterize the
\hat{\Delta}_{i}
as neural networks with continuously differentiable activation functions.

To find the
\hat{\Delta}_{\Theta}
that best approximates the data, we consider the loss function that sums the squared error between the true and learned state derivatives along each trajectory:

L(\Theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{T-1}\left\lVert\Delta(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}_{k})-\hat{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}_{k};\Theta)\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

(11)

where the true residual
\Delta
is approximated via a one-step finite difference as

\Delta(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}_{k}):=\frac{\mathbf{x}^{i}(t_{k+1})-\mathbf{x}^{i}(t_{k})}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}-\bar{f}(\mathbf{x}^{i}(t_{k}),\mathbf{u}^{i}(t_{k})).

The weights
\Theta
can then be optimized via backpropagation using readily available deep-learning libraries. After the disturbance is learned, the flatness diffeomorphism can be constructed offline using the formula described in (
10
), where we again leverage the deep learning libraries to compute the relevant Jacobians via automatic differentiation.

6
Numerical Experiments

We validate our algorithm via simulation experiments on the 2D quadrotor (
7
) under unmodeled air resistance. First, we test whether augmenting the nominal model with learned residual dynamics can generate more dynamically consistent trajectories in an open-loop rollout (Section
6.2
). Then, we design a flatness-based tracking controller using the learned model and compare its tracking performance and computation time against an NMPC baseline (Section
6.3
). We show that the flatness-based controller achieves tracking performance similar to that of the NMPC, while requiring an order of magnitude less time to compute the control action. This computational efficiency will allow it to be run at a higher frequency and makes it more suitable for computationally constrained hardware. Code to reproduce our experiments is available at
https://github.com/FJYang96/flat-residual/
.

Setup

We consider the 2D quadrotor dynamics (
7

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{3})+\Delta_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

(12)

\displaystyle=\bar{f}_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{3})-\underbrace{C_{r}\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{\Delta^{r}(\mathbf{x}_{2})}-\underbrace{C_{p}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\rVert\mathbf{x}_{2}}_{\Delta^{p}(\mathbf{x}_{2})},

\Delta^{p}

Faessler et al., 2017

Chee et al., 2022
)

We collect
3000

\mathbf{p}\in[-1,1]^{2}

B

0.5
seconds and sampled at
100Hz
. We parameterize
\hat{\Delta}_{\Theta}(x)
as a feedforward neural network (1 hidden layer of 32 neurons) with GeLU activations to ensure smoothness. Training for 20 epochs with ADAM on an Nvidia A5000 GPU takes under 1 minute.
To reduce the effect of randomness in the trajectory data samples and network initialization, we run the following experiments for
30

In what follows, we couple the learned model with common flatness-based techniques for planning and control. We evaluate the performance on a circular trajectory of the form

with
a=1,b=0.6

6.3
Closed-Loop Tracking Control

We now use the learned model to construct a tracking controller for the 2D quadrotor that leverages the flatness of the learned augmented dynamics. As is standard, we design a linear feedback control law for the linear flat dynamics (sometimes called the “Brunovsky canonical form”
(
Fliess et al., 1995
)
)

\mathbf{y}^{(4)}=\boldsymbol{\nu},

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}
should be interpreted as a virtual control input. For a reference trajectory
\mathbf{y}_{r}
, we choose the virtual control input
\boldsymbol{\nu}
according to the control law

\boldsymbol{\nu}=\mathbf{y}_{r}^{(4)}-k_{0}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{r})-\ldots-k_{3}(\mathbf{y}^{(3)}-\mathbf{y}_{r}^{(3)}).

(13)

We assume that the controller can observe the state
\mathbf{x}
and use a Luenberger observer to estimate the higher-order derivatives of the flat output. We then compute the input as

\mathbf{u}=\hat{\Psi}\left(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},\ddot{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}^{(3)},\boldsymbol{\nu}\right).

We compare this flatness-based approach against a nonlinear model predictive controller (NMPC) that leverages the same learned model. Given a measured state
\xi
, the NMPC controller solves the nonlinear optimal control problem

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\int_{t=0}^{H}\Big((\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{r})^{\top}Q(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}_{r})+\mathbf{u}^{\top}R\mathbf{u}\Big)dt

\displaystyle+(\mathbf{y}(H)-\mathbf{y}_{r}(H))^{\top}Q_{T}(\mathbf{y}(H)-\mathbf{y}_{r}(H))

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\bar{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})+\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{1},\qquad\mathbf{x}(0)=\xi.

The controller then applies the control action
\mathbf{u}(0)
until the next sampling step. In practice, we solve this problem using the direct method. We consider a prediction horizon of
{H=1}
second and discretize the dynamics into
100
time steps using RK4 integration. We use the cost matrices
Q=Q_{T}=I
and
R=0.001I
. We warmstart the optimization problem from the previous solution and solve it in Python using Casadi
(
Andersson et al., 2019
)
until convergence. We also assume that the controller observes the full state at each time step.

7
Conclusion

We showed that residual learning can be made compatible with differential flatness-based control methods. In particular, we showed that augmenting a nominal differentially flat system in pure-feedback form with a lower-triangular residual dynamics term preserves differential flatness of the overall system. We further showed how to construct the flat maps for the augmented system using those of the nominal system. Leveraging these results, we proposed an algorithm for learning flatness-preserving residual dynamics for pure-feedback form systems. We operationalized these insights using standard deep learning libraries, and demonstrated the promise of the approach through simulation experiments on 2D quadrotors. This provides a first step towards more principled methods for learning residual models for differentially flat systems. Future work includes relaxing the structural assumptions, extending the algorithm to allow online adaptation to slowly varying residual dynamics, and deploying the algorithm on quadrotors in physical experiments.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1

We first prove a helpful lemma before formalizing the proof of Theorem
1
. In what follows, we abuse notation and set
\mathbf{x}_{r+1}:=\mathbf{u}
to simplify the presentation.

Lemma 4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1
and
for each
k=1,\ldots,r
, there exists an open set
{\mathcal{N}_{k}\ni(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*})}
and a unique, continuously differentiable function
h_{k}
such that for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
,

\displaystyle h_{k}\left(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k},\bar{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\right)=\mathbf{x}_{k+1}.

(14)

Proof.

Consider the function
{F_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{(k+2)m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
given by

F_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k+1},\mathbf{c}):=\bar{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-\mathbf{c}.

Clearly, when
\mathbf{c}^{*}=\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{*}_{k}:=\bar{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k+1})
,

F_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k},\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k+1},\mathbf{c}^{*})=0.

By the regularity of
\bar{f}_{k}
,

\left\lvert D_{\mathbf{x}_{k+1}}F_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k+1},\mathbf{c})\right\rvert=\left\lvert D_{\mathbf{x}_{k+1}}\bar{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k+1})\right\rvert\neq 0

for all
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Thus, by the implicit function theorem, there exists an open neighborhood
V_{k}\ni(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{*}_{k},\mathbf{c}^{*})
and a unique, continuously differentiable function
{h_{k}:V_{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
on which the desired equality (
14
) holds. Defining

\mathcal{N}_{k}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m(r+1)}|\big(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k},\bar{f}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\big)\in V_{k}\},

we obtain the desired result.
∎

With this lemma in hand, we now formalize the proof.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We prove the claim for a neighborhood of
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{*})
of the form

\mathcal{N}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{N}_{k}
, on which (
14
) thus holds for all
k=1,\ldots,r
.
Lemma
4
thus implies that in this neighborhood, the values of
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}
and
\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
uniquely determine
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
via the dynamic feasibility constraints. To show that the system (
5
) is differentially flat, we leverage this fact and explicitly construct the diffeomorphism
{(\Phi,\Psi)}
.

In particular, we prove the claim by induction on
k
. For the base case
k=1
, note that
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\Phi_{1}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y}
for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{N}
. For the purposes of induction, assume that for all integers
k^{\prime}<k
, there exists a function
\Phi_{k^{\prime}}
such that

\mathbf{x}_{k^{\prime}}=\Phi_{k^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(k^{\prime}-1)}),

for
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{N}
. Then, by Lemma
4
, we can also express
\mathbf{x}_{k}
in terms of the flat outputs as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=h_{k-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1})

\displaystyle=h_{k-1}\left(\Phi_{1}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,\Phi_{k-1}(\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(k-2)}),\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{k-1}(\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(k-2)})\right)

\displaystyle=:\Phi_{k}(\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(k-1)}),

where
{\dot{\Phi}_{k-1}(\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(k-2)})}
can be computed via

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{k-1}(\mathbf{y},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(k-2)}))=D\Phi_{k-1}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{y}}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}^{(k-1)}\end{bmatrix}.

The overall flatness diffeomorphism is thus given by

\Phi=[\Phi_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\Phi_{r}^{\top}]^{\top},\qquad\Psi=\Phi_{r+1}.\qed
