Background

Glomerulonephritis (GN), characterized primarily by glomerular injury mediated by diverse immunological mechanisms [1], exhibits heightened prevalence and severity among Asian American, Hispanic, Australian, Canadian, and broader Asian populations [2–4]. Patients with GN face an elevated infection risk, driven in part by underlying autoimmune pathophysiology and the use of immunosuppressive therapies. For instance, Feldman et al. reported an infection incidence rate of 23.9 per 100 person-years in the lupus nephritis subcohort [5]. Standard GN therapies—including glucocorticoids and conventional immunosuppressants—further exacerbate this risk by suppressing host immunity [5]. Rituximab (RTX), a CD20-targeted monoclonal antibody that depletes B lymphocytes via surface CD20 protein binding [6], has emerged as a first-line therapy for several glomerular diseases (e.g., membranous nephropathy) over the past decade, owing to its superior efficacy and favorable safety profile compared to traditional immunosuppressants [7]. Despite these advantages, RTX-associated infections remain a critical clinical concern.

Evidence from other immune-mediated conditions underscores this risk: RTX-treated ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) patients experience infection rates of 6.6–16.6 per 100 person-years [8–10], while RTX use in multiple sclerosis is associated with the highest severe infection rate (19.7 per 1000 person-years) among disease-modifying therapies, with infections correlating with hospitalization, mortality, and poor prognosis [11, 12]. A meta-analysis of lupus nephritis further highlighted disparities in infection-related mortality, with Asian populations disproportionately affected [11]. Collectively, infections have surpassed primary disease mortality as the leading cause of death in these patients, necessitating targeted preventive strategies [13].

Emerging data suggest cotrimoxazole may mitigate RTX-associated infections. A retrospective case–control study reported reduced infection rates with cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in RTX-treated AAV patients [14], and analysis of the RAVE trial cohort (n = 197) demonstrated that low-dose cotrimoxazole lowered severe infection risk in AAV patients receiving either RTX or cyclophosphamide [15]. These findings imply cotrimoxazole’s potential as an effective prophylactic agent. However, no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have specifically evaluated cotrimoxazole’s safety and efficacy in preventing infections among GN patients undergoing RTX therapy. Our study thus aimed to address this evidence gap through a rigorous RCT design.

Methods

Study design

This RCT evaluated the safety and efficacy of cotrimoxazole in preventing infections among patients with GN receiving RTX therapy. The study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration no.: ChiCTR2200063564; title: A single-center randomized controlled study of the efficacy and safety of cotrimoxazole for the prophylaxis of infections in glomerulonephritis patients treated with rituximab) and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Air Force Military Medical University (ethics approval no.: KY20222140-F-1). The full study protocol is available in Additional file 2.

Participants

This single-center, open-label RCT was conducted in Xijing Hospital between October 2022 and December 2023.

Randomization

Patients were randomized to either the cotrimoxazole prophylaxis group or the control group (no cotrimoxazole prophylaxis) using a centralized Internet-based randomization system. Random number tables were generated with SPSS version 26.0 software, and sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing group assignment codes were prepared based on these tables. Upon patient enrollment in sequential order, the corresponding envelope was opened to determine group allocation.

Intervention

All patients received 1000 mg of rituximab (RTX) intravenously on days 1 and 14. CD19 + B-cell counts were monitored monthly for 6-month post-initial infusion; if CD19 + B cells remained detectable at the 6-month assessment, an additional 1000-mg dose of RTX was administered.

Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis protocol

In the intervention group, patients initiated oral cotrimoxazole (400-mg sulfamethoxazole/80-mg trimethoprim per tablet) on day 1 of RTX infusion, with one tablet daily for seven consecutive days. Prophylaxis was repeated for 7 days following each subsequent RTX infusion, resulting in a total of 14 days of prophylaxis over the study period. This 7-day dosing schedule was selected given the exploratory nature of this pilot study.

Non-cotrimoxazole group

No cotrimoxazole was administered.

Follow-up

At baseline, participants underwent a comprehensive physical examination and provided a detailed medical history (smoking status, diabetes mellitus, and urinary tract infections). Scheduled follow-up visits were conducted at 15-, 30-, 60-, 90-, 120-, 150-, and 180-day post-baseline to assess adverse events and treatment adherence.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the cumulative incidence of infection during the 180-day follow-up period. Secondary endpoints included the incidence of severe infections and the incidence of adverse events attributable to cotrimoxazole prophylaxis.

Evaluation and clinical management of infections

Patients were instructed to present to the hospital for any suspected illness, where they underwent a comprehensive evaluation for clinical signs of infection, including detailed medical history-taking and diagnostic testing. Those with a reported fever history or documented axillary temperature ≥ 37.5 °C received standardized infection assessments: urinalysis (to detect urinary tract infection signs) and chest radiography (if cough, sputum production, or dyspnea were present). Complete definitions of infection events are detailed in Table 1. All infection-related endpoints were independently adjudicated by a blinded review panel.

The definition of infection in our study

Serious infections were defined per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [16], with Grade 3 or higher qualifying as a serious infection. Infection sites were categorized as respiratory, gastrointestinal, urinary, or other.

Safety evaluation of cotrimoxazole prophylaxis

The safety of cotrimoxazole prophylaxis was evaluated in a two-stage process. First, all adverse events (AEs) observed during the prophylactic phase were documented in the follow-up records. Second, one investigator (Yang Zha) evaluated the causal relationship of each AE using standardized criteria; AEs with probable or definite causality were categorized as cotrimoxazole-associated adverse drug reactions [17]. Adverse reaction severity was graded using the CTCAE, version 5.0 [16], with Grade 3 or higher designated as serious AEs.

Statistical analysis

Based on published literature, the infection incidence following RTX administration is approximately 25%, while the infection rate with cotrimoxazole prophylaxis is 7%. Using a RCT design to compare infection rates between the two groups, with a one-sided α of 0.025, and a one-sided β of 0.1, sample size calculation was performed using PASS 2021 software. The estimated sample size per group was approximately 70 cases. Adjusting for a 5% dropout rate, the final required sample size was 75 participants per group. Continuous variables will be presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed or median (interquartile range [IQR]) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables will be reported as counts (percentages). Infection incidence rates (per 100 person-years) were calculated separately for each group. The Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank test compared time to first infection. Cox proportional hazards regression estimated the hazard ratios (HRs) for infection and severe infection, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Infections

Incidence of clinically defined infections

During follow-up, 19 infectious episodes occurred in 16 patients in the non-cotrimoxazole group, and 8 infectious episodes occurred in 7 patients in the cotrimoxazole group. The annualized infection incidence density was 54.83 (95% CI43.09–66.57) in the non-cotrimoxazole group versus 20.80 (95%CI11.36–30.24) in the cotrimoxazole group (Table 3). Cumulative infection incidence was 9.86% in the cotrimoxazole group versus 23.19% in the non-cotrimoxazole group, with an HR of 0.39 (95% CI0.17–0.88; log rank P = 0.029; Fig. 2). Infections were most frequently observed within the first 3 months following the initial RTX infusion.

Incidence of severe infections

The annualized incidence density of severe infections was 17.13 (95% CI 8.26–26.04) per 100 person-years in the non-cotrimoxazole group versus 2.97 (95% CI 0.98–6.92) per 100 person-years in the cotrimoxazole group. Serious infections occurred in five patients in the non-cotrimoxazole group and one patient in the cotrimoxazole group, with a HR of 0.17 (95% CI 0.02–1.48; log rank P = 0.11; Fig. 4). Four patients in the non-cotrimoxazole group died of severe pneumonia (infection mortality rate: 21.05%), whereas no fatal infections were observed in the cotrimoxazole group, indicating a higher infection-related mortality in the non-cotrimoxazole cohort (Fig. 4). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cultures from these four deceased patients tested positive for Pneumocystis jirovecii, with infections occurring on follow-up days 29, 53, 64, and 119. An additional non-cotrimoxazole group patient had a mycoplasma infection, while the causative pathogen of the severe infection in the cotrimoxazole group was identified as Staphylococcus aureus.

Infection events in primary GN

Given that our study included patients with secondary GN subtypes (e.g., lupus nephritis, IgG4-related nephropathy), we performed a subgroup analysis focusing on those with primary GN. At follow-up completion, 15 of 66 primary GN patients in the non-cotrimoxazole group experienced infections, corresponding to a cumulative infection incidence of 22.73%. In the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, 17 patients in the non-cotrimoxazole group and 8 in the cotrimoxazole group experienced infections, with cumulative infection incidences of 22.67% (non-cotrimoxazole) and 10.67% (cotrimoxazole), respectively. Adverse effects of prophylactic cotrimoxazole

Following cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, AEs were observed as follows: a total of 10 patients reported adverse effects, including mild elevations in liver enzyme levels (n = 2), a mild arm rash (n = 1), occasional nausea during prophylaxis (n = 5), and neutropenia (n = 2). All AEs were graded as Grade 1. These abnormalities resolved spontaneously after completion of the 7-day prophylactic regimen. No major AES (Grade ≥ 3) associated with prophylactic cotrimoxazole were observed during follow-up. In the non-cotrimoxazole group, two patients reported AEs: one patient developed Grade 2 liver enzyme elevation in month 3 and another in month 4 (Table 4). These elevations normalized after 20–30 days of treatment with oral glycyrrhizic acid preparations. Notably, although the incidence of AEs was higher in the cotrimoxazole group than in the non-cotrimoxazole group (14.08% vs. 2.90%, P < 0.05), all AEs in the cotrimoxazole group were Grade 1, and no serious AEs occurred in either group.

Discussion

In this single-center RCT, we demonstrated that short-term, low-dose cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (one tablet once daily for 14 days in the first month) significantly enhanced infection control following RTX administration, particularly within the first month of treatment. Notably, this regimen reduced both infection risk and infection-related mortality without increasing serious AEs.

RTX, a CD20-targeted monoclonal antibody that depletes B lymphocytes by binding to the CD20 protein on their surface [6], was first approved for non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma in 1997 [18]. Since then, it has become a cornerstone therapy for various autoimmune diseases, including GN [19]. Patients with GN, due to inherent abnormal autoimmune regulation and renal loss of immunoglobulins, face an elevated infection risk [20]. RTX administration further exacerbates this vulnerability [21], as evidenced by our observation that the non-prophylactic group had a high infection incidence of 54.83 per 100 person-years and an infection-related mortality rate of 21.05% (all deaths attributed to severe pneumonia). In contrast, the prophylactic group exhibited a markedly lower infection rate (20.80 per 100 person-years) and no infection-related deaths.

Infection-related fatalities predominantly occurred within the first 3 months of RTX initiation, aligning with prior evidence that RTX-associated infections cluster in the early post-treatment period [8, 10]. Notably, there was no significant difference in respiratory or urinary tract infection incidence between groups, suggesting prophylaxis did not selectively target specific sites. While systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients treated with RTX have infection rates of 6.6–16.6 per 100 person-years [8, 10], our study reported higher rates (20.80 vs. 54.83 per 100 person-years in cotrimoxazole vs. non-cotrimoxazole groups). We attribute this to three factors:

Concomitant immunosuppression: Only approximately one-third of patients in both groups received RTX monotherapy at enrollment; the remaining received concurrent glucocorticoids or tacrolimus. Prior studies have consistently shown that combined immunosuppressive therapy amplifies infection risk [21]. For example, in lupus nephritis patients on RTX plus glucocorticoids or mycophenolate mofetil, the 78-week cumulative infection rate reached 85%, with a severe infection incidence of 16.6 per 100 person-years [9].

Cotrimoxazole, initially developed for P. jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) prophylaxis in acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) patients [22], has been increasingly used to prevent non-AIDS-associated PCP [23, 24]. Retrospective studies support its efficacy in preventing a spectrum of infections in immunocompromised patients [14, 15]. Conversely, Faraji et al. noted that cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in pemphigus incurs costs without clear benefit [25]. To date, no RCTs have evaluated cotrimoxazole for RTX-associated infections in GN.

Our study revealed that over 180 days of follow-up, the cumulative infection incidence in the prophylactic group was 0.39 times that of the non-prophylactic group (95% CI 0.17–0.88, log rank P = 0.029). Additionally, both the rate of severe infections and infection-related mortality were reduced in the prophylactic group, suggesting that cotrimoxazole may serve as an effective preventive measure against infections following RTX administration. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale RCT of cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in RTX-treated GN patients. PCP prophylaxis with cotrimoxazole involves daily dosing (1–2 tablets) for 2–9 months [25–27]—longer than our 14-day regimen. Prior research provides context for our findings: Park et al. noted that cotrimoxazole prophylaxis lasting < 140 days was associated with suboptimal infection prevention [25]. A cohort study of 228 lupus patients on mycophenolate mofetil demonstrated that cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (regimens: 2 tablets daily, 2 tablets every other day, or 2 tablets thrice weekly) with a duration ≥ 1 year significantly reduced all-cause infection incidence [28]. In 2023, Odler B. et al. reported that low-dose cotrimoxazole (single- or double-strength daily) for 6 months reduced severe infection risk in AAV patients treated with RTX or cyclophosphamide/azathioprine [15]. Similarly, a retrospective study of RTX-treated AAV patients confirmed that a mean 14.67-month cotrimoxazole course significantly lowered severe infection incidence (HR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.23–0.88, P = 0.02) [14]. Notably, while the prophylactic group in our study exhibited a lower severe infection rate (1.41% vs. 7.23%), this difference did not reach statistical significance. This may be attributed to the shorter duration of our prophylactic regimen compared to the longer courses (≥ 1 year) associated with efficacy in prior studies. A modified TESTING study protocol for IgA nephropathy, which incorporated cotrimoxazole (one tablet once daily for 12 weeks), was associated with reduced infection risk [29]. A RCT involving 172 rheumatic disease patients demonstrated better adherence with a 0.5-tablet daily regimen versus 1-tablet daily, with no significant differences in efficacy or adverse effects between groups [30]. Consistently, Park J. W. et al. noted that the 1-year incidence of PCP was significantly lower in patients receiving cotrimoxazole prophylaxis for > 20 weeks [31].

Guided by prior research and safety considerations, our RCT employed a daily cotrimoxazole regimen (one tablet orally administered on the same day as RTX infusion). The selection of a 7-day prophylactic course (repeated after each RTX infusion, totaling 14 days) was based on the following rationale: first, targeting the peak infection risk window: during the early post-infusion period (1–2 weeks), a transient surge in pro-inflammatory cytokines and rapid decline in B-cell counts create a critical window of heightened susceptibility to acute infections [32]. Short-term prophylaxis delivered within this period directly addresses this peak risk. Second, balancing efficacy and adherence: while long-term prophylaxis (≥ 6 months) might more comprehensively mitigate the prolonged immunomodulatory effects of RTX, it raises concerns including antimicrobial resistance, cumulative toxicity, and suboptimal patient adherence—issues particularly relevant for patients with chronic kidney disease who often manage multiple concomitant medications. Our preliminary data showed that adherence to the 7-day regimen exceeded 95%, a level critical to ensuring consistent and effective prophylaxis.

Our study demonstrated that short-term, low-dose cotrimoxazole prophylaxis reduced infection incidence within 180 days of RTX administration. Although the long-term preventive effect beyond 180 days requires further investigation, RTX-induced immunosuppression persists for nearly 6 months. Based on our data and prior retrospective studies, continuous cotrimoxazole administration for 3–6 months may yield better preventive outcomes. Further trials are warranted to determine the optimal dose and duration.

Most infections occurred within the first 3-month post-RTX infusion, consistent with prior findings in patients with SLE and AAV [33, 34]. These studies consistently report that the highest infection risk following RTX therapy occurs within the first 6 months, with a distinct peak in the first 3-month post-treatment. Our data further demonstrated that 7-day cotrimoxazole administration (14 days within the first month) post-RTX infusion effectively prevented posttreatment infections. We hypothesize this efficacy may stem from the timing of infection risk in our cohort: since most infections occurred within the first 3 months, the short course of cotrimoxazole likely targeted this critical early high-risk period, reducing infections during this window and contributing to the overall lower infection rates observed over the study period. Additionally, cotrimoxazole’s activity against common opportunistic pathogens prevalent in the early post-RTX phase may further explain its sustained protective effect.

A subgroup analysis of 66 primary GN patients in the non-cotrimoxazole group and 67 in the cotrimoxazole group further confirmed higher infection incidence in the non-prophylactic cohort (22.73% vs. 8.96%; log rank P = 0.03).

Following cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, 10 individuals in the prophylactic group experienced AEs, all graded as Grade 1. Nausea was the most common adverse event, resolving spontaneously after completion of the prophylactic regimen. No serious AEs (Grade ≥ 3) were observed during follow-up, likely due to the short duration of prophylactic administration; however, the safety of long-term cotrimoxazole use requires further investigation.

Key strengths of this study include its randomized controlled design, adequate sample size, high follow-up retention rate, application of standardized criteria for infection identification, and classification. Our study has several limitations. First, we used a standard prophylactic dose without comparing alternative dosing regimens; additional trials are warranted to clarify the optimal dose (our results suggest one tablet once daily is effective). Second, we did not compare different prophylaxis durations. Relative to prior studies, our prophylactic course was shorter, which may explain the absence of a statistically significant difference in severe infection rates between groups. Larger-scale RCTs are needed to evaluate differences across varying prophylaxis durations. Lastly, follow-up was restricted to 6 months, leaving the long-term prognostic implications of cotrimoxazole in this population unclear.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that cotrimoxazole prophylaxis for 1-week post-RTX infusion effectively prevents post-RTX infectious complications, reduces infection-related mortality, and does not elevate the risk of serious AEs. However, it should be noted that this study was a single-center investigation without a placebo control, and these limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings.
