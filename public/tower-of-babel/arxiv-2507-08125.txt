Block Designs that Provide Optimal Power in the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel Test
David Azriel, Adam Kapelner, Abba M. Krieger

Abstract
We consider the asymptotic power performance under local alternatives of the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test. Our setting is non-traditional: we investigate randomized experiments that assign subjects via Fisher’s blocking design. We show that blocking designs that satisfy a certain balance condition are asymptotically optimal. When the potential outcomes can be ordered, the balance condition is met for all blocking designs with number of blocks going to infinity. More generally, we prove that the pairwise matching design of Greevy et al. (2004) satisfies the balance condition under mild assumptions. In smaller sample sizes, we show a second order effect becomes operational thereby making blocking designs with a smaller number optimal. In practical settings with many covariates, we recommend pairwise matching for its ability to approximate the balance condition.



1
Background

We consider a classic problem: an experiment with
2n

subjects
(
individuals
,
participants
or
units
). The experiment has two
arms
(
treatments
,
manipulations
or
groups
), which we will call treatment and control. Subjects have
p
observed subject-specific
covariates
(
measurements
,
features
or
characteristics
) denoted
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for the
i
th subject. We consider one incidence (zero or one)
outcome
(
response
or
endpoint
) of interest for the
2n
subjects denoted
\boldsymbol{y}=\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{2n}\right]^{\top}
. The investigator then wishes to test if the true odds ratio across the two arms is one (or the log odds ratio is zero).

The setting we investigate is where all
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
’s are known beforehand and considered fixed. This setting is common in industry and is also found in “many phase I studies [that] use ‘banks’ of healthy volunteers … [and] … in most cluster randomised trials, the clusters are identified before treatment is started”
(
Senn, 2013
, page 1440)
.

Formally defined, the
randomization
(
allocation
or an
assignment
) is a vector
\boldsymbol{w}=\left[w_{1},\ldots,w_{2n}\right]^{\top}
whose entries indicate whether the subject is placed into the treatment arm (coded numerically as +1) or placed into the control arm (coded numerically as -1). After the sample is provided, the only control the experimenter has is the choice of the entries in
\boldsymbol{w}
. The process whereby the experimenter summons this assignment vector is termed an experimental
design
(
strategy
,
algorithm
,
method
or
procedure
). Randomized experimental design is thus a generalized multivariate Bernoulli random variable that realizes
\boldsymbol{w}
’s.

One typical design is the balanced complete randomization design (BCRD,
Wu, 1981
, p. 1171
) which has also been called the “gold-standard”. This design has
\binom{2n}{n}
allocations each that satisfy the equal-arm-allocation constraint that
\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}=0
and each of these allocations is then chosen with equal probability. However, there are many unlucky
\boldsymbol{w}
’s from BCRD that result in large differences in the distribution of observed covariates between the two arms. The amount of covariate value heterogeneity between groups we term
observed imbalance
. In the classic setup of a linear model with continuous response, this observed imbalance creates estimation bias from the perspective of
\boldsymbol{w}
. Reducing this observed imbalance involves removing
\boldsymbol{w}
’s from the full support of the distribution which yield these observed imbalances. Hence such designs are termed “restricted designs”.

Restricted designs have a long literature once again starting with
Fisher, 1925
who wrote “it is still possible to eliminate much of the …[observed imbalance], and so increase the accuracy of our [estimator], by laying restrictions on the order in which the strips are arranged”. Here, he introduced the
blocking design
, a restricted design still popular today. Taking this design to the extreme are
n
blocks of two subjects each (i.e., pairs of subjects) where one is randomly allocated to the treatment arm and the other to the control arm. This is termed the pairwise matching (PM) design
(
Greevy et al., 2004
)
.

In our previous work that investigated blocking designs for general response type
(
Azriel et al., 2024
)
, we considered estimation of the sample average treatment effect defined as
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{1}_{2n}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{y}_{T}-\boldsymbol{y}_{C})
. Although this estimand is a natural target of inference for most outcome types, in the incidence setting this estimand (called the “mean probability difference” or the
risk difference
) is awkward; it averages large differences near 50% and small differences near 0 and 1. In this work, we consider the odds ratio estimate. We then study the asymptotic power of a standard test, the
Cochran, 1954
-
Mantel and Haenszel, 1959
Test (CMHT).

The CMHT is usually employed in an observational study for testing the response difference between the two levels of a binary variable of interest where the data is further stratified based on other variables which are not being tested explicitly. The assumed null hypothesis is: there is no difference in outcomes among subjects in the different levels of the binary variable of interest. The utility of the CMHT is that it can both provide higher power (by averaging estimates within the levels of the other variables) and can also help to avoid Simpson’s paradox (when there is confounding on the other variables).

Our perspective change herein (which we believe to be novel) is that we optimize the CMHT in randomized experiments (not in observational studies). During experimentation, one has control over allocating
\boldsymbol{w}
and can thus directly control for the strata (which are fixed by an observed variable in an observational study) as individual blocks. In other words, the binary variable of interest is
\boldsymbol{w}
and the other variables are rendered into the strata of the nominal block category.

We show herein that asymptotically optimal power for the CMHT is PM when considering the space of block designs (as the unconditional space is intractable). We define our setting formally in Section
2
, discuss our theoretical contribution in Section
3
, provide simulation evidence of our results in Section
4
and conclude in Section
5
.

2
Problem Setup

Consider the potential outcome framework of
Rubin, 2005
and let
\boldsymbol{p}_{T}\in[0,1]^{2n}
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{C}\in[0,1]^{2n}
be the unobserved probabilities of positive response under the treatment and control respectively such that for all
i=1,\ldots,2n
,

\displaystyle Y_{T,i}~{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{ind}}}~{\rm Bernoulli}(p_{T,i})~~~~\text{and}~~~~Y_{C,i}~{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{ind}}}~{\rm Bernoulli}(p_{C,i}).

(1)

In vector form, the
2n
random responses
\boldsymbol{Y}
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\boldsymbol{Y}_{T}+\boldsymbol{Y}_{C}+\text{diag}\left[\boldsymbol{W}\right](\boldsymbol{Y}_{T}-\boldsymbol{Y}_{C})\Big)

(2)

under the Stable Unit Treatment Values Assumption. We then assume that the design
\boldsymbol{W}
is independent of the pair
(\boldsymbol{Y}_{T},\boldsymbol{Y}_{C})
.

As discussed in the introduction: we limit our scope to blocking designs. Specifically, we consider homogeneous balanced-allocation blocks, denote
B
as the number of blocks,
n_{B}:=2n/B
as the number of subjects per block (which is assumed to be a whole number) and
\boldsymbol{W}_{B}
as the block design with
B
blocks. The experimental data by block
b
will then be denoted as follows:

Treatment (
w_{i}=+1
)

Control (
w_{i}=-1
)

Total

Affected (
y_{i}=1)

n_{T,1_{b}}

n_{C,1_{b}}

n_{1_{b}}

Unaffected (
y_{i}=0
)

n_{T,0_{b}}

n_{C,0_{b}}

n_{0_{b}}

Total

n_{B}/2

n_{B}/2

n_{B}

The CMHT statistic in our blocking setting can then be expressed as

MH:=\frac{\left(\,\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(n_{T,1_{b}}-\frac{n_{1_{b}}}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{4(n_{B}-1)}\sum_{b=1}^{B}n_{1_{b}}n_{0_{b}}}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y})^{2}}{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}

(3)

where the simplification on the right hand side follows from algebraic calculations found in Appendix
A.1
. The term
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}:=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{W}_{B}\right]
is the
2n\times 2n
variance-covariance matrix of the experimental design. Since we consider blocking designs, the form of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}
is block diagonal consisting of
B

n_{B}\times n_{B}
where each block consists of entries with 1 on the diagonal and
-1/(n_{B}-1)
on the off-diagonal.

This

statistic is known to have an asymptotic chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom under the null
(
Agresti, 2002
, Section 6.3.2)
. For the tractability of results, we consider the following related quantity which is asymptotically distributed as a standard normal under the null:

\displaystyle\widetilde{MH}:=\pm\sqrt{MH}=\frac{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}}~{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}~\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,1\right).

3
Main result

We wish to optimize the power of the CMHT over all block designs
\boldsymbol{W}_{B}
with B blocks. We now define the following useful quantities:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{p_{T,1}-p_{C,1}}{2}~~\frac{p_{T,2}-p_{C,2}}{2}~~\ldots~~\frac{p_{T,2n}-p_{C,2n}}{2}\right]^{\top},

\displaystyle~~\bar{\tau}_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\tau_{i},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{p_{T,1}+p_{C,1}}{2}~~\frac{p_{T,2}+p_{C,2}}{2}~~\ldots~~\frac{p_{T,2n}+p_{C,2n}}{2}\right]^{\top}~~~~\text{and}~~

(4)

\displaystyle\eta_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{T}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{p}_{T})+\boldsymbol{p}_{C}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{p}_{C})}{2}\right).

(5)

It can be shown that
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]=\bar{\tau}_{n}
and
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]=\frac{1}{2n}\eta_{n}

(see
Kapelner et al., 2023
, Appendix A.1 and Appendix A.2 respectively)
. For block
b
, define

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{b}:=\frac{n_{b}}{n_{b}-1}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}(v_{i}-\bar{v}_{b})^{2}~~\text{and}~~\bar{v}_{b}:=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}v_{i}

where
I_{b}
is the set of indexes of block
b
. This notation allows us to express the critical quadratic form term
\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}
in Equation
5
equivalently as
\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sigma_{b}^{2}
. We also define

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{min}:=\min\{\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{B}^{2}\}~~\text{and}~~\sigma^{2}_{max}:=\max\{\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{B}^{2}\}.

We allow the number both the block size (
n_{B}
) and the number of blocks (
B
) to vary with
n
and consider two types of asymptotics:

(i)

Small blocks asymptotic
, in which
n_{B}
is of smaller order than
B
, i.e.,
\frac{n_{B}}{B}\to 0
, or equivalently,
\frac{\sqrt{n}}{B}\to 0
.

(ii)

Large blocks asymptotic
, in which
n_{B}
is of larger order than
B
, i.e.,
\frac{B}{n_{B}}\to 0
, or equivalently,
\frac{B}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0
. In this regime we need an additional assumption, namely that
\sigma^{2}_{min}/\sigma^{2}_{max}
is bounded.

In the literature of the CMHT two types of asymptotics are usually considered: fixed
B

(
Zelen, 1971
, e.g.,)
, or fixed
n_{B}
(e.g.,
Breslow, 1981
and
Agresti, 2002
, Section 6.3.4
). We allow a more flexible regime in which both
n_{B}
and
B
go to infinity. Notice, however, that the case where
n_{B}
and
B
are of the same order is not covered by our asymptotics. For large blocks asymptotics we need an additional assumption, that
\sigma^{2}_{min}/\sigma^{2}_{max}
is bounded. It is satisfied when the variances of the
v_{i}
’s among different blocks are comparable. It trivially holds when
B
is fixed and
\boldsymbol{v}
is nondegenerate. Our main result is now given.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the model and assumptions in Section
2
. Consider a sequence of local alternative hypotheses where
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{2n}\bar{\tau}_{n}=c
. Assume
\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{T}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{p}_{T})+\boldsymbol{p}_{C}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{p}_{C})}{2n}
is bounded away from zero and assume
{\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}}\frac{1}{2n}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\eta_{n}=\eta_{\infty}>0
.
Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{MH}~{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}~\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{c}{\sqrt{\eta_{\infty}}},\,1\right)

(6)

in either the (i) small blocks asymptotic regime or the (ii) large blocks asymptotic regime.

The proof of the theorem is given in Appendix
A.2
and it involves non-standard asymptotics for two reasons. First, as mentioned previously we allow both
n_{B}
and
B
to go to infinity and second,
\boldsymbol{W}_{B}
has dependent entries due to the design. To overcome these difficulties we use Berry-Essen-type bounds and results from Stein’s approximation
(
Chen et al., 2010
)
.

Higher power means that the value of the asymptotic mean of
\widetilde{MH}
should be made as large as possible; hence the value of
\eta_{\infty}
should be made as small as possible over all considered designs. Recall the definition of
\eta_{n}
(Equation
5
), which is a sum of two terms. The second term does not depend on
\boldsymbol{W}_{B}
. It follows that blocking designs that send
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}
to zero in turn maximize the asymptotic power. To interpret this condition notice that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}(v_{i}-\bar{v}_{b})^{2}.

(7)

This expression is the average variance of the
v_{i}
’s within each block.
If the order of the
v_{i}
’s is known (see the discussion in Section
4.1
) then the blocking procedure that minimizes
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}
is to have the smallest
n_{B}

v_{i}
’s in the first block, the second smallest
n_{B}

v_{i}
’s in the second block etc; see Section
4.1
. When the blocking is done in this way, it is easy to see via Equation
7
that
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\to 0
for any design with
B\to\infty
. More generally, we showed in
Kapelner et al., 2025
that
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\to 0
holds for the optimal nonbipartite pairwise matching algorithm when the covariates are bounded and
v_{i}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, where
f
is Lipschitz continuous.

However, there is finite sample behavior that is relevant to optimal design. In Appendix
A.2
, we prove the following results:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}

\displaystyle~{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightarrow}\limits^{\mathbb{L}^{2}}}}~

\displaystyle\eta_{\infty},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}v_{i}(1-v_{i})+\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)\right)

and an easy calculation shows that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\in I_{B};i_{1}\neq i_{2}}\tau_{i_{1}}\tau_{i_{2}}.

(8)

The quantity on the left hand side of Equation
8
is positive if
\tau_{i}
has the same sign for all
i
’s in the same block. If this is the case,
\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)
is large when
n_{B}
is small. This term vanishes asymptotically, but for small
n
it penalizes designs with small block size by shifting the mean in Equation
6
towards zero. This finding is demonstrated in Section
4.1
. We summarize the discussion of this section in our flagship result below.

Remark 3.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, every design that satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}=0

maximizes the asymptotic power.
When the order of the
v_{i}
’s is known, the condition is met for blocking design that sends
B\to\infty
. If the order is not known, the nonbipartite pairwise matching algorithm satisfies the condition (under mild assumptions). For small
n
, there is a second order effect that penalizes designs with small block size.

4
Simulations

4.1
One Covariate

We first provide simulation evidence for our flagship result (Remark
3.1
) when the ordering of the entries of
\boldsymbol{v}
is known prior to experimental allocation. We assume the typical generalized linear model with additive treatment effect where the probability that
y=1
is log-odds-linear, i.e.,
\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{i}+\beta_{T}w_{i}
. As log odds is one-to-one with the average probability
\boldsymbol{v}
of Definition
4
, the order of
v_{i}
’s is the same as the order of the
x_{i}
’s. Here, blocking into
B
strata involves sorting
x
and slicing by sample quantiles
1/B,\ldots(B-1)/B
and PM’s optimal pair structure is the indices order corresponding to the pairs
<x_{(1)},x_{(2)}>,\ldots,<x_{(2n-1)},x_{(2n)}>
where
x_{(i)}
denotes the
i
th smallest
x
value.

As the CMHT is asymptotic and our results are asymptotic, we wish to understand performance across a range of sample sizes realistic in clinical trials and we consider all numbers of blocks of even size (which we require for the simulation in Section
4.2
) with maximum of
B=n
for PM and where
B
divides
2n
. These constraints result in 74 cells over all values of
2n\in\left\{48,96,192,288,384\right\}
, values specifically selected as they have many even factors.

For each value of
2n
, the covariates are drawn once from an iid standard normal. We then set
\beta_{0}=0
and
\beta_{1}=1
and
\beta_{T}\in\left\{0,0.7\right\}
resulting in 74
\times
2 = 148 cells. The setting of
\beta_{T}=0
allows us to gauge if the CMHT is properly sized over all sample size / block size cells and the setting of
\beta_{T}=0.7
was selected in order to induce separation in power measurements over all sample size / block size cells. As the
x_{i}
’s are drawn for each value of
2n
, for each value of
\beta_{T}
we compute the static potential probabilities of outcomes
p_{T_{i}}
and
p_{C_{i}}
for all subjects.

For all 148 cells, we simulate
N_{Y}:=1,000,000
different assignments
\boldsymbol{w}
drawn from their appropriate block design. Each of these
\boldsymbol{w}
’s selects an alternating spliced subset of the two potential probabilities of outcomes resulting in the
2n
probabilities of
y_{i}=1
. We then draw the response values of using these probabilities from independent heterogeneous Bernoullis. That is, for each of the
N_{Y}
draws, we have the realized responses
\boldsymbol{y}
, the assignment
\boldsymbol{w}
and the strata via the block structure. Thus, we can calculate the MH statistic (of Equation
3
) and then we can decide the null at
\alpha=5\%
(i.e, if
MH>3.84
, the critical
\chi^{2}_{1}
threshold, the null is rejected).

The results to gauge size of the CMHT (Bonferroni-corrected for all simulation cells described here plus those in Section
4.2
) are found in Figure
1
. For
2n=48
, most cells are inappropriately-sized, which is to be expected due to low sample size; for
2n=96
, fewer cells are inappropriately-sized; and for
2n\geq 192
, most cells are appropriately sized. Although we find statistically significant violations of size
\neq\alpha
, the estimated sizes themselves are approximately
\alpha
with the only serious departure for
B=1
(BCRD) across all sample sizes. However, BCRD is not what the CMHT was designed for (as it lacks strata).

4.2
Multiple Covariates

We now wish to explore the more realistic scenario of more than one covariate by simulating for
p\in\left\{2,5\right\}
. Here, the blocking structure becomes imperfect as it can no longer splice subjects along the ordered
\boldsymbol{v}
(see Definition
4
). For
p=2
, we first sort along the first covariate, then we create
B/2
strata at the sample quantiles. Then within each of these strata, we sort along the second covariate and divide into 2 strata. (This is the reason we required all
B
to be even in the previous section to conveniently have the same cells across for all
p
). For
p=5
, we use the same procedure ignoring the third, fourth and fifth covariates. We view this as realistic as in practice; blocking on more than two covariates in small sample sizes is known to be difficult. And for convenience, we retain the same blocking procedures for the larger simulated sample sizes.

The previous paragraph discusses how we block for
B<n
. Although this procedure theoretically allows for PM, in standard practice PM is handled categorically differently as we explain now. One first calculates the proportional between-subjects Mahalanobis distance of observed covariates
(
Stuart, 2010
, Section 2.2)
via
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}-\boldsymbol{x}_{m})^{\top}\hat{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}-\boldsymbol{x}_{m})
where
\hat{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1}
is the
p\times p
sample variance covariance matrix of the
2n
subjects’ covariate vectors for all the
\binom{2n}{n}
pairs of subjects with indices
\ell,m
. This results in a symmetric distance matrix of size
2n\times 2n
. Then, one runs the optimal nonbipartite matching algorithm that executes in polynomial time
(
Lu et al., 2011
)
which runs on the distance matrix returning the best-matched set of pairs of indices. This procedure provided a match structure that
approximates
the optimal match structure corresponding to the subject indicies of
<v_{(1)},v_{(2)}>,\ldots,<v_{(2n-1)},v_{(2n)}>
. We employ this procedure for
B=n
and thus our simulation results in this subsection will be categorically different for PM when compared to
B<n
, but not in Section
4.1
(as this procedure would be the same as sorting on the one, sole covariate).

To draw the covariate values, we use the same procedure as in Section
4.1
, i.e., iid standard normal realizations. The linear model is now expanded for
p
covariates and we set
\beta_{j}=1
for all covariates. The value of
\beta_{0}
and the value of
\beta_{T}
we keep the same as in Section
4.1
. We use the same sampling procedure for
N_{Y}=1,000,000
draws in each of the
74\times 2\times 2=296
new cells.

The Bonferroni-corrected size results are found in Figure

in Section B of the Supporting Information and the results are similar to
p=1
with a bit more violations when
p=5
. The power results are shown in Figure

) as PM maximizes the asymptotic power even when the blocking is not perfect.

5
Discussion

We examined power in the two-arm blocking design with a binary response. The
B
blocks can be thought of as different strata and our data is then arranged as a
2\times 2\times B
array of counts. We consider the CMHT with the null hypothesis of equal odds ratio of positive outcome across both arms. We then analyze its power function over local alternatives with designs of differing number of blocks. We find that under perfect blocking (i.e., knowledge of the order of potential outcomes’ probabilities) the asymptotic power is maximized for all blocking design with
B\to\infty
. For non-perfect blocking we show that PM maximizes the asymptotic power under mild assumptions.

In small sample sizes, there is a second-order drag term that becomes significant and thus the optimal design is a large number of blocks, but not as many as PM. Our simulations with realistic sample sizes show the CMHT under block designs (with more than one block) is mostly appropriately-sized which means the asymptotics for the CMHT engage relatively quickly. Simulated power estimates support our theoretical results for one covariate. For more than one covariate, we cannot optimally block (nor pairwise match) based on the order of the known probabilities of the potential outcomes’ probabilities. Here, PM is the clear best performer because it maximizes the asymptotic power and it
achieves the closest approximation of matches on the potential outcomes’ probabilities. For this practical point, it is our design recommendation.

There are many further avenues to explore. We only examined the power of the CMHT; we did not explore mean squared error of the estimator nor confidence interval coverage. Our intuition is that the resultant optimal error and optimal coverage design will likely be the same. However, it is theoretically difficult to have tractability on estimators for the odds ratio and log odds ratio. Although one typically uses logistic regression for estimation and testing in our experimental setting, it assumes a linear relationship which is not assumed here. The logistic regression estimator is also difficult to analyze as the Fisher Information depends on the unknown linear coefficients. Also, we analyzed the non-sequential (or “offline”) setting that was originally studied by
Fisher, 1925
when assigning treatments to agricultural plots. The more common
sequential
(or “online”) experimental setting where subjects arrive one-by-one and must be assigned a
w_{i}
on the spot (hence all
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
’s are
not
seen beforehand) is an interesting setting to explore.

A.1
CMHT Statistic Calculation

We wish to show that

MH:=\frac{\left(\,\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(n_{T,1_{b}}-\frac{n_{1_{b}}}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{4(n_{B}-1)}\sum_{b=1}^{B}n_{1_{b}}n_{0_{b}}}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{W}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y})^{2}}{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}.

Let
I_{b}
be the indices of subjects in the
b
-th block.
Then,

n_{T,1_{b}}=\sum_{i\in I_{b}}I(W_{B,i}=1)Y_{i},~n_{1_{b}}=\sum_{i\in I_{b}}I(W_{B,i}=1)Y_{i}.

Therefore,

n_{T,1_{b}}-\frac{n_{1_{b}}}{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{b}}[I(W_{B,i}=1)Y_{i}-Y_{i}/2].

Notice that if
W_{B,i}=1
, then
I(W_{B,i}=1)Y_{i}-Y_{i}/2=Y_{i}/2
and if
W_{B,i}=-1
, then
I(W_{B,i}=1)Y_{i}-Y_{i}/2=-Y_{i}/2
. Thus,
I(W_{B,i}=1)Y_{i}-Y_{i}/2=W_{B,i}Y_{i}/2
. Hence,

n_{T,1_{b}}-\frac{n_{1_{b}}}{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{k}}W_{B,i}Y_{i}/2.

Therefore, the numerator of the MH test statistics in Equation
3
is
\frac{1}{4}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y})^{2}
. Consider now the denominator of MH in Equation
3
.
We have that

n_{1_{b}}n_{0_{b}}=\left(\sum_{i\in I_{b}}y_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i\in I_{b}}(1-y_{i})\right)=n_{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}Y_{i}-\left(\sum_{i\in I_{b}}Y_{i}\right)^{2},

where in the last equality we used the relation
Y_{i}=Y_{i}^{2}
, which is true because
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. Furthermore,

n_{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}y_{i}-\left(\sum_{i\in I_{b}}y_{i}\right)^{2}=n_{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}_{b}\right)^{2},

where
\bar{Y}_{b}=\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}Y_{i}
. It follows that denominator of MH in Equation
3
is

\frac{1}{4(n_{B}-1)}\sum_{b=1}^{B}n_{1_{b}}n_{0_{b}}=\frac{n_{B}}{4(n_{B}-1)}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}_{b}\right)^{2}.

In our previous paper’s Supporting Information
(
Kapelner et al., 2023
, Appendix A.2, Equation 18)
we showed that
\frac{n_{B}}{n_{B}-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(Y_{i}-\bar{Y}_{b}\right)^{2}=\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}
. Hence,

MH=\frac{(\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y})^{2}}{\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}.

Proof of Main Result

By Lemma
A.0.1

\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}/2n}}
converges in distribution to
N(0,1)
under
small blocks asymptotics
and Lemma
A.0.2
implies that the same convergence holds under
large blocks asymptotics
. Notice that we assumed that
\tilde{v}_{0}
is bounded from below. By Lemma
A.0.4
,
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]
converges in
L_{2}
, and hence in probability, to zero. Also, by Lemma
A.0.4
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]=\frac{1}{2n}\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}v_{i}(1-v_{i})+\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)\right].

The maximal eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}
is bounded by 2 (which is the maximal eigenvalue for pairwise matching, i.e., when
B=n
). Hence,
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\leq\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}
. We assumed that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}=0
and therefore,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)=0.

It follows that
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]-\eta_{n}\to 0
. Hence,

\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}
converges in probability to
\eta_{\infty}
.

We have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{MH}=\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}/\sqrt{2n}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}/\sqrt{2n}}+\frac{\bar{\tau}_{n}\sqrt{2n}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}}.

(9)

We showed that
\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}/2n}}
converges in distribution to
N(0,1)
and that
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}
converges in probability to
\eta_{\infty}
. By Slutsky’s theorem,
\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}}/\sqrt{2n}}
converges in distribution to
N(0,1)
. Since

\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}
converges in probability to
\eta_{\infty}
, the left term in (
9
) converges in probability to
c/\eta_{\infty}
. The result of the theorem follows.

Lemma A.0.1
.

Consider the model of Section
2
and assume that
\eta_{n}\to\eta_{\infty}>0
, we have for a constant
C
that

\sup_{t}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}/2n}}\leq t\right)-\Phi(t)\right|\leq C\sqrt{n}/B.

Proof of Lemma
A.0.1
.
We can write

\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}).

For each
b
,
\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)
has mean zero and variance
\frac{\eta_{b}}{n_{B}}
where

\eta_{b}:=\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}(v_{i}-\bar{v}_{b})^{2}+\frac{1}{2n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left\{p_{T,i}(1-p_{T,i})+p_{C,i}(1-p_{C,i})\right\}.

The third moment of
\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)
can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)\right|^{3}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)\right]^{2}=2\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)\right]=\frac{2\eta_{b}}{n_{B}},

(10)

where the inequality is true because
|W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}|\leq 2
for all
i
.

We now invoke the Berry–Esseen inequality using the notation of
Chen et al., 2010
. Recall the definition of
\eta_{n}
given in Equation
5
. It follows that

\eta_{n}=\frac{n_{B}}{2n}\sum_{b}\eta_{b}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\eta_{b}
. Let
\xi_{b}:=\frac{\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}B/n_{B}}}
. Then
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{B}
are independent with mean zero and

\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\xi_{b}\right]=\frac{\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\eta_{B}}{B\eta_{n}/n_{B}}=\frac{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\eta_{B}}{\eta_{n}}=1.

Furthermore,

\sum_{b=1}^{B}\xi_{B}=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{Bn_{B}}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}/2n}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{W}_{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}-\bar{\tau}_{n}}{\sqrt{\eta_{n}/2n}}.

By Theorem 3.6 of
Chen et al., 2010
,

\sup_{t}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{B}\xi_{B}\leq t\right)-\Phi(t)\right|\leq C\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{b}|^{3}\right].

(11)

We assumed that
\eta_{n}\to\eta_{\infty}>0
and therefore,
\eta_{n}
is bounded from below; also, by Equation
10
, the third moment of
\frac{1}{n_{B}}\sum_{i\in I_{b}}\left(W_{i}Y_{i}-\tau_{i}\right)
can be bounded by
2\eta_{B}/n_{B}\leq C/n_{B}
. It follows that
\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{b}|^{3}\right]\leq C\frac{1/n_{B}}{\left(B/n_{B}\right)^{3/2}}
.
Therefore,

\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{b}|^{3}\right]\leq C\frac{B/n_{B}}{\left(B/n_{B}\right)^{3/2}}=C\sqrt{n_{B}/B}=\sqrt{2}C\sqrt{n}/B.

Hence, Equation
11
implies the reult.
∎

A.0.5
). Also,

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\,|\,W_{B,i}\right]\right]=v_{i}.

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]=\frac{1}{2n}\left[\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}v_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{2}}+\sum_{i}v_{i}-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\left(\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}\tau_{i_{1}}\tau_{i_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{2}})\right)\right]\\
=\frac{1}{2n}\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}v_{i}(1-v_{i})+\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}\left(\left|\left|\boldsymbol{\tau}\right|\right|^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)\right].

This shows the expectation part of the lemma.

We next show that
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]\leq C/n
.
We have that
\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}
is an average of of
B=2n/n_{B}
independent blocks, where each block has bounded variance (since
Y_{i}
’s are bounded). Therefore,
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]\leq C/n
when
n_{B}
is bounded.

Consider now the case that
n_{B}\to\infty
. We have that

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\,|\,\boldsymbol{w}_{B}\right]\right]+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\,|\,\boldsymbol{W}_{B}\right]\right].

Given
\boldsymbol{W}_{B}
, the
Y_{i}
’s are independent. In our previous work
(
Azriel et al., 2024
)
we have calculated
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Z}\right]
where
\boldsymbol{Z}
has independent entries but not identically distributed (with mean zero). Based on these computations (see Eq. (32) in
Azriel et al., 2024
) it can be shown that
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}|\boldsymbol{w}\right]\leq C/n
for a constant
C
when the moments of the
Y_{i}
’s and
\frac{1}{n}\text{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}^{2}\right]
are bounded, which is the case in our setting since
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

Consider now
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\,|\,\boldsymbol{W}_{B}\right]\right]
. We have that

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2n}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}\,|\,\boldsymbol{W}_{B}\right]\right]\\
=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}}\Big\{\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[(v_{i_{1}}+W_{B,i_{1}}\tau_{i_{1}})(v_{i_{2}}+W_{B,i_{2}}\tau_{i_{2}}),\,(v_{i_{3}}+W_{B,i_{3}}\tau_{i_{3}})(v_{i_{4}}+W_{B,i_{4}}\tau_{i_{4}})\right]\\
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{3},i_{4}}\Big\}.

(15)

If
i_{1},i_{2}
are not in the same block, then
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{2}}=0
; likewise
i_{3},i_{4}
. If
i_{1},i_{2}
are not in the same block as
i_{3},i_{4}
, then the covariance term in Equation
15
is zero. Therefore,
it is enough to consider the case that
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}
all belong to the same block. We consider different cases:

•

Suppose that
i_{1}=i_{2}=i_{3}=i_{4}
. There are order of
n
such cases and therefore,
the sum of the covariances of this case is bounded by
C/n
.

•

Suppose that
i_{1}=i_{2}=i_{3}
(or symmetrically
i_{1}=i_{3}=i_{4}
,
i_{1}=i_{2}=i_{4}
,
i_{2}=i_{3}=i_{4}
). There are order of
\frac{n}{n_{B}}n_{B}^{2}=nn_{B}
such cases. In this case
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{1}}=1
and
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{3},i_{4}}=-\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)}
. Therefore,
the sum of the covariances of this case is bounded by a contant times
nn_{B}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)}\leq\frac{2}{n}
.

•

If
i_{1}=i_{2}
(or symmetrically
i_{3}=i_{4}
) then

\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[W_{B,i_{1}},\,W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}\right]=0,

by Lemma
A.0.5
.
Also

\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}^{2},\,W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}\right]=\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[1,\,W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}\right]=0.

Therefore, in this case the covariance is zero.

•

If
i_{1}=i_{3}
(or symmetrically
i_{1}=i_{4}
,
i_{2}=i_{3}
,
i_{2}=i_{4}
) then

\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}},\,W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]\\
=-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}-\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}.

In this case
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{3},i_{4}}=\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}
.
There are order of
\frac{n}{n_{B}}n_{B}^{3}=nn_{B}^{2}
such cases. Hence the sum of the covariances of this case is bounded by a constant times

\frac{nn_{B}^{2}}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)^{3}}\leq\frac{4}{n}.

•

Suppose that
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}
are all different. Then,

\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}},\,W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]

By Lemma
A.0.5

we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]=\frac{3}{(n_{B}-3)(n_{B}-1)}.

Recall that
\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]=-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}
. Therefore we get that

\mathbb{C}\text{ov}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}},\,W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]=\frac{2n_{B}}{(n_{B}-3)(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{n_{B}}

for
n_{B}\geq 4
.
In this case
(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{1},i_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{B}})_{i_{3},i_{4}}=\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}
.

There are order of
\frac{n}{n_{B}}n_{B}^{4}=nn_{B}^{3}
such cases. Hence the sum of the covariances of this case is bounded by a constant times

\frac{nn_{B}^{3}}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{(n_{B}-1)^{2}}\frac{1}{n_{B}}\leq\frac{2}{n}.

The proof of the lemma is thus concluded. ∎

Lemma A.0.5
.

(i)

Suppose that
i_{1},i_{2}
are different indexes and belong to the same block, then
\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}\right]=-\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}
.

(ii)

Suppose that
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}
are different indexes and belong to the same block, then

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}\right]=0
.

(iii)

Suppose that
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}
are different indexes and belong to the same block, then

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i_{1}}W_{B,i_{2}}W_{B,i_{3}}W_{B,i_{4}}\right]=\frac{3}{(n_{B}-3)(n_{B}-1)}
.

Proof of Lemma
A.0.5
.

Part (i).
Consider the first block and the indexes
1,2
with out loss of generality. Let
1,\ldots,n_{B}
denote the indexes of the block. We have that
\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}W_{B,i}=0
. Therefore,

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i}W_{B,1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}^{2}\right]+(n-1)\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}\right]=1+(n-1)\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}\right],

where the first equality is due to symmetry and the second is true because
W_{B,1}^{2}=1
. The result follows.

Part (ii)
. Consider the indexes
1,2,3
without loss of generality. Similar to the first part we have that

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i}W_{B,1}W_{B,2}\right]=2\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}^{2}W_{B,2}\right]+(n-2)\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}\right]\\
=0+(n-2)\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}\right].

It follows that
\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}\right]=0
.

Part (iii)
. Consider the indexes
1,2,3,4
without loss of generality. Similar to the previous parts we have that

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{B}}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,i}W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}\right]=3\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}^{2}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}\right]+(n-3)\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}W_{B,4}\right]\\
=-3\frac{1}{n_{B}-1}+(n-3)\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}W_{B,4}\right].

It follows that
\mathbb{E}\left[W_{B,1}W_{B,2}W_{B,3}W_{B,4}\right]=\frac{3}{(n_{B}-3)(n_{B}-1)}
. ∎
