Hybrid biphoton spectrometer for time-resolved quantum spectroscopy across visible and near-infrared regions
Ozora Iso, Koya Onoda, Nicola J. Fairbairn, Masahiro Yabuno, Hirotaka Terai, Shigehito Miki, Ryosuke Shimizu

Abstract
Joint spectral measurements are a powerful tool for characterising biphoton spectral correlation, which is crucial for quantum information and communication technologies. In these applications, highly pure biphoton states are essential in any time- and frequency-mode, often obviating the need for time-resolved measurements. Conversely, spectroscopy utilising entangled photon pairs is gaining significant attention for its ability to unveil molecular dynamics, a field that critically demands time-resolved capabilities. Here, we introduce a novel methodology for capturing a biphoton spectrum that comprises visible and near-infrared photons, resulting in a three-fold non-degenerate joint spectrum. Our system employs two non-scanning spectrographs: a fibre spectrograph for near-infrared photons and a delay-line-anode single-photon imager for visible photons. We successfully measure the joint spectral intensity by leveraging a time-tagging acquisition strategy. Furthermore, our approach uniquely enables time-resolved joint spectral measurements with a temporal resolution of approximately 150 ps. This methodology bridges the gap between the requirements for pure biphoton states and the need for dynamic insights in quantum spectroscopy.

Introduction

The exploitation of quantum entanglement in photon pairs has opened up new frontiers in various photonic research areas, from quantum information processing to emerging applications in biophotonics and chemical physics. In quantum technology, entangled photons are the core resource for developing quantum computers
Ladd et al. 2010
, secure communication networks
Gisin and Thew 2007
, and metrology tools
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
. Concurrently, a new paradigm is emerging in chemical and biophotonics, where entangled photons are used as a non-classical light source to probe molecular dynamics
Li et al. 2023
. This approach offers unique advantages over traditional classical spectroscopy, particularly through the use of time- and frequency-mode correlations of biphotons
Álvarez Mendoza et al. 2025
.
For this emerging field to mature, advanced spectroscopic techniques capable of capturing the subtle, fast dynamics of molecular systems are required. While the joint spectral intensity (JSI) has become a standard method
Zielnicki et al. 2018
for characterising the frequency correlations of entangled photon pairs, its static nature fundamentally limits its application in time-sensitive studies. Current JSI measurement techniques - which include scanning dispersive optics
Baek and Kim 2009
, stimulated emission tomography
Fang et al. 2014
, and single-photon cameras
Lubin et al. 2021
- have made significant strides in efficiency. Yet, none can provide the picosecond temporal resolution necessary to track fast processes. This gap highlights a critical need for a new class of instrumentation: a time-resolved biphoton spectrometer.

We address this need by presenting the design, construction, and validation of a novel picosecond time-resolved hybrid biphoton spectroscopic system. The system achieves simultaneous time and frequency resolution by combining two distinct, highly sensitive single-photon spectrographs: a fibre-based spectrograph
Avenhaus et al. 2009
employing a superconducting nanowire single-photon detector (SNSPD) for near-infrared photons, and a delay-line-anode single-photon imager
Jeltes et al. 2007
for visible photons. This hybrid, non-degenerate detection scheme overcomes the spectral limitations of existing single-photon imagers while leveraging the high efficiency of SNSPDs. We first validate our system by generating and measuring the three-fold non-degenerate photon pairs from a lithium triborate (LBO) crystal. We then present a comprehensive analysis, including a theoretical model for the JSI, a comparison of the simulated JSI with experimentally reconstructed data, and the first demonstration of a JSI resolved on a picosecond timescale. Our results establish a foundational capability for observing quantum-correlated dynamics in real-time, with broad implications for future research in quantum-enhanced sensing and spectroscopy.

(b) shows a time-tagging strategy of the hybrid spectrometer associated with the obtained one-dimensional histograms. The TDC flexibly changes to process a stream of time-tagging data from all detectors. The following acquisition is set by the dedicated software distributed by the manufacturer. The first five channels receive electrical signals from the DLD, while the sixth and seventh channels register the signal from the SNSPD and laser, respectively. The synchronisation signals appear every 833 ns, which is the reciprocal of the repetition rate of the divided signals, triggering signals for the MCP and SNSPD. The time difference between MCP and the synchronisation signal reflects the instrument response function (IRF) of MCP. The DLD X1 and DLD X2, corresponding to the end of the X1 and X2 wires in (a), are used to locate the spectrum of signal photons when triggered by MCP signals. The idler photons, dispersed by the fibre, are measured by the start signals of the MCP and the laser synchronisation. It should be noted that SNSPD signals arrive after microseconds, reflected in the unit of SNSPD time-tagging graph, because the idler photons propagate through a 1.5 km fibre.

(a) depicts the optical schematics of time-resolved JSI measurement of three-fold non-degenerate photon pairs with the hybrid biphoton spectrometer. The details are described in the Method section, and a comprehensive setup is described in the Supplementary Information. The second-harmonic light is generated via the LBO crystal excited by the mode-locked Ti:S laser. The prism separates the fundamental laser light from the second-harmonic, and the separated laser impinges onto the photodiode as a synchronisation signal. The second-harmonic source is further incident on another LBO crystal, inducing spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) to generate non-degenerate photon pairs. The top inset shows the geometry of the phase-matching angle before and after the excitation of the LBO crystal. Two daughter photons are separated by the dichroic mirror (DM), which reflects the signal photons (~515 nm) and transmits the idler photons (~1550 nm), and then coupled by the single-mode fibres. The idler photons are dispersed through a fibre spectrometer, followed by the SNSPD, whereas the signal photons are imaged at the output of the grating spectrometer with the DLD. Both spectra are combined into a JSI, and it is resolved based on the synchronisation signals as depicted in the inserted figures.
