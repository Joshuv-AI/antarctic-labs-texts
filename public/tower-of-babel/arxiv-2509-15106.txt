Geometric optimization for quantum communication
Chengkai Zhu, Hongyu Mao, Kun Fang, Xin Wang

Abstract
Determining the ultimate limits of quantum communication, such as the quantum capacity of a channel and the distillable entanglement of a shared state, remains a central challenge in quantum information theory, primarily due to the phenomenon of superadditivity. This work develops Riemannian optimization methods to establish significantly tighter, computable two-sided bounds on these fundamental quantities. For upper bounds, our method systematically searches for state and channel extensions that minimize known information-theoretic bounds. We achieve this by parameterizing the space of all possible extensions as a Stiefel manifold, enabling a universal search that overcomes the limitations of ad-hoc constructions. Combined with an improved upper bound on the one-way distillable entanglement based on a refined continuity bound on quantum conditional entropy, our approach yields new state-of-the-art upper bounds on the quantum capacity of the qubit depolarizing channel for large values of the depolarizing parameter, strictly improving the previously best-known bounds. For lower bounds, we introduce Riemannian optimization methods to compute multi-shot coherent information. We establish lower bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement by parameterizing quantum instruments on the unitary manifold, and on the quantum capacity by parameterizing code states with a product of unitary manifolds. Numerical results for noisy entangled states and different channels demonstrate that our methods successfully unlock superadditive gains, improving previous results. Together, these findings establish Riemannian optimization as a principled and powerful tool for navigating the complex landscape of quantum communication limits. Furthermore, we prove that amortization does not enhance the channel coherent information, thereby closing a potential avenue for improving capacity lower bounds in general. This result can be of independent interest.

Introduction

Since its inception, a central goal of quantum information theory has been to determine the ultimate physical limits on processing and transmitting information encoded in quantum systems. This pursuit extends Shannon’s classical theory to the quantum realm
[
Wil11
,
Wil13
]
, where the principles of superposition and entanglement introduce fundamentally new challenges and opportunities. Entanglement, in particular, has been identified as the key resource in quantum information processing protocols
[
HHHH09
]
, e.g., quantum teleportation
[
BBC+93
]
, superdense coding
[
BW92
]
, and quantum key distribution
[
Eke91
,
Ren05
]
; and it underpins the potential advantages of quantum communication over classical methods
[
BSST02
]
. Consequently, two of the most vital problems in the field are quantifying the usable entanglement in a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
and determining the ultimate capacity of a quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
to transmit it.

The operational measures for these tasks are the
distillable entanglement

D(\rho_{AB})

[
BBP+96
,
BBPS96
]
, and the
quantum capacity

Q({\cal N}_{A\to B})

[
Smi10
]
, respectively. The former quantifies the ultimate rate at which maximally entangled states can be extracted from independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) copies of
\rho_{AB}
through local operations and classical communication (LOCC). When only one-way communication from Alice to Bob is allowed, we have the so-called
one-way distillable entanglement

D_{\to}(\cdot)
. The latter quantifies the ultimate rate for reliable quantum communication through a channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
. These quantities are inextricably linked, as the quantum capacity could also be understood as the rate at which entanglement can be generated between a sender and receiver by using the channel.

Despite their fundamental importance, computing
D_{\to}(\cdot)
and
Q(\cdot)
for general states and channels remains a notoriously difficult problem. Both quantities are proved to be given via a regularization over many copies of the resource, i.e.,

D_{\to}(\rho_{AB})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}),\quad\text{and}\quad Q({\cal N}_{A\to B})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}Q^{(1)}({\cal N}_{A\to B}^{\otimes n}),

(1)

where
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\cdot)
and
Q^{(1)}(\cdot)
are
single-shot
quantities based on the
coherent information
. The regularization can be removed, making
D_{\to}(\cdot)
and
Q(\cdot)
solvable, for classes of
degradable states

[
LDS18
]
and
degradable channels

[
DS05
]
whose
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\cdot)
and
Q^{(1)}(\cdot)
are shown to be additive. However, it is necessary in general because of the phenomenon of
superadditivity
, where the coherent information of a joint state or channel can be strictly greater than the sum of its parts
[
LLS18
,
LL18
,
LTAL19
,
NPJ20
,
SG21
,
LLS+23
,
WZM25
]
. This non-additivity makes the direct computation of the limits intractable. The computation of
D_{\to}(\cdot)
and
Q(\cdot)
, even for qubit isotropic states and depolarizing channels, still remains a long-standing open problem in quantum information theory. Thereby, research has bifurcated into two threads:

•

deriving upper bounds that prove fundamental limits on performance;

•

establishing lower bounds by constructing explicit coding schemes.

For upper bounds, a prominent one on the distillable entanglement is the Rains bound
[
Rai01
]
, which has inspired related information-theoretic upper bounds on the quantum capacity, including the Rains information
[
TWW17
]
, max-Rains information
[
WFD19
]
, and geometric-Rains information
[
FF21
]
. Another major line of research leverages the concept of
degradability
. Since the quantum capacity of a degradable channel (and correspondingly, one-way distillable entanglement of a degradable state) is known, which is a single-letter coherent information, continuity bounds based on
approximate degradability
have been established for both one-way distillable entanglement and quantum capacity
[
SSWR17
,
LDS18
,
JD24
]
. A related approach, which has also yielded competitive upper bounds, is to decompose a state or channel into a convex combination of a degradable and an antidegradable part
[
LDS18
,
ZZW24
]
.

Moreover, another intuitive and powerful strategy for deriving upper bounds is based on the construction of state or channel
extensions
. This approach relies on the principle that for any extension
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}
of a state
\rho_{AB}
, or
\widehat{{\cal N}}_{A\to BF}
of a channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
, the monotonicity

D_{\to}(\rho_{AB})\leq D_{\to}(\hat{\rho}_{ABF}),\quad\text{and}\quad Q({\cal N}_{A\to B})\leq Q(\widehat{{\cal N}}_{A\to BF})

(2)

must hold. This follows directly from the data processing inequality, as the receiver can freely discard the auxiliary system
F
. The challenge thus shifts to finding an
optimal extension
that minimizes
D_{\to}(\cdot)
or
Q(\cdot)
of the extended object. A leading technique for this purpose is the so-called
flag extension
method
[
LDS18
,
Wan21
,
FKG20
,
KFG22
]
, which has proven highly effective for structured channels, e.g., Pauli channels
[
KFG22
]
. Notably, the state-of-the-art (SOTA) upper bounds on the quantum capacity of depolarizing channels are given by the flag extension method in
[
KFG22
]
. However, the flag extension method faces a fundamental optimization bottleneck. The construction is tied to a specific convex decomposition of the state or channel (e.g., a Kraus representation), and the subsequent optimization is performed over a chosen ansatz for the ‘flag’ states. The efficacy of the method is therefore highly dependent on these initial choices, for which no systematic
a priori
guidelines exist. It has been unclear how to unlock the full potential of the extension-based approach in a general, systematic manner.

For lower bounds, on the other hand, the most well-known ones on
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\cdot)
and
Q^{(1)}(\cdot)
are given by the
coherent information
of a state
[
DW05
]
or a channel
[
BDSW96
]
, known as the
hashing bounds
. Particularly, a positive lower bound on the quantum capacity is a constructive proof that quantum communication is possible, i.e., there exists a
quantum error correction code
for the noise modeled by that channel. Due to the superadditivity, one needs to calculate the
n
-shot coherent information to pursue the ultimate performance for a blocklength of
n
, where the optimization is over all possible input
code states
. Historically, techniques in quantum error correction have been used to design highly structured codes
[
SS96
,
DSS98
,
SS07
,
FW08
]
. For certain channels, e.g., Pauli channels, exploiting the symmetries of these codes can make the coherent information calculation tractable
[
BL21
]
. More recently, some permutation-invariant code states have been proposed to achieve a large blocklength evaluation of the coherent information and an improved capacity threshold
[
BL25
]
. However, these methods are often tailored to specific symmetries and code families. Other approaches have also been explored using neural network states as an ansatz
[
BL20
]
to find code states.

In this work, we directly tackle the challenges in computing
D_{\to}(\cdot)
and
Q(\cdot)
by introducing a comprehensive framework based on
Riemannian optimization
. We develop distinct but philosophically aligned geometric approaches to obtain computable upper and lower bounds on these quantities. An overview of our results is illustrated in Figure

below.

1.1
Main results

For upper bounds, we reframe the search for optimal state and channel extensions as a tractable Riemannian optimization problem. By characterizing the space of all valid extensions as a complex Stiefel manifold, our method systematically discovers the tightest possible bounds, moving beyond the limitations of previous ad-hoc flag constructions. We further enhance this framework by incorporating a recently improved continuity bound for conditional entropy
[
ABD+24
,
BLT25
]
to derive a tighter bound on one-way distillable entanglement in terms of a state’s approximate degradability (cf. Theorem
1
), a result analogous to the upper bound on quantum capacity in
[
BLT25
, Proposition 10]
. We analyze the continuity property of the target objective function in our optimization, and use a finite-difference scheme to implement the
Riemannian gradient descent
(RGD) algorithm to optimize our bounds (cf. Section
3.2.1
).

The synergy between our optimization framework and the continuity bounds yields a powerful methodology for computing the tightest known upper bounds on
D_{\to}(\cdot)
and
Q(\cdot)
. For the qubit isotropic state, our method produces an upper bound on one-way distillable entanglement that is strictly tighter than the best previously known result reported in
[
KFG22
]
, thereby establishing an SOTA upper bound for the quantum capacity of the qubit depolarizing channel (cf. Section
3.4
). We demonstrate the versatility of our approach on other noisy states and channels, consistently achieving tighter estimates than existing methods.

For lower bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement, we formulate the computation of
D^{(1)}_{\to}(\cdot)
as an optimization problem over the complex unitary manifold (cf. Section
4.1
). Then, we develop an RGD algorithm to estimate
D^{(1)}_{\to}(\cdot)
for a multi-copy of a target state to obtain tighter lower bounds on
D_{\to}(\cdot)
than the hashing bound. Our numerical results demonstrate the efficacy of our algorithm, which clearly shows the superadditivity of
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\cdot)
for various bipartite states.

For lower bounds on the quantum capacity, we propose a scalable parameterization of the input code states using an interleaved local unitary ansatz. This transforms the optimization problem from a search over an exponentially large sphere to a tractable optimization over a product of low-dimensional unitary manifolds (cf. Section
4.2
). We develop an RGD algorithm to optimize the channel coherent information (cf. Corollary
13
), which allows for the efficient exploration of entangled input states. Numerical results show that our methods can reveal stronger superadditivity of the channel coherent information compared to previous code state constructions. Finally, we prove that amortization of the underlying channel coherent information does not enhance the original channel coherent information (cf. Theorem
2
), closing a potential pathway for improving single-letter lower bounds.

All implementations of Riemannian algorithms in this work are based on a MATLAB toolbox
Manopt

[
Inc22
,
BMAS14
]
, together with the software
CVX

[
GB14
]
and
QETLAB

[
Joh16
]
. The numerical experiments are performed on a 2.4 GHz AMD EPYC 7532 32-core Processor with 256 GB RAM under MATLAB R2024a. All MATLAB codes used to obtain numerical results, as well as all data, are publicly available at
[
Zhu25
]
.

Preliminaries

A quantum system, denoted by a capital letter such as
A
, is modeled by a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{A}
. The dimension of this space is denoted by
|A|
. The sets of linear operators and positive semidefinite operators on
{\cal H}_{A}
are denoted by
{\cal L}(A)
and
{\cal P}(A)
, respectively. The set of
quantum states
on system
A
is denoted as
\mathscr{D}(A)\coloneqq\{\rho\in{\cal P}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1\}
. A sub-normalized state is a positive semidefinite operator with trace no greater than one. The identity operator on system
A
is denoted by
{\mathds{1}}_{A}
. The trace norm of
X
is given by
\|X\|_{1}\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
, and its Frobenius norm is
\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}X^{\dagger}X}
. The operator norm
\|X\|_{\infty}
is defined as the maximum eigenvalue of
\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
. The support of a Hermitian operator
X
is denoted by
{\operatorname{supp}}(X)
.

The von Neumann entropy of a quantum state is defined as
H(\rho)\coloneqq-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\rho)
where all logarithms in this work are taken in base two. The coherent information of a bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
is defined by
I(A\rangle B)_{\rho}\coloneqq H(\rho_{B})-H(\rho_{AB})
and the condition entropy by
H(A|B)_{\rho}\coloneqq-I(A\rangle B)_{\rho}
. For
p\in[0,1]
, the binary entropy is defined by
h(p)\coloneqq-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
and the bosonic entropy is defined by
g(p)\coloneqq\left(1+p\right)h\left(p/(1+p)\right)
.

A
quantum channel

{\cal N}_{A\to B}
is a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map from
{\cal L}(A)
to
{\cal L}(B)
. A
subchannel

{\cal M}_{A\to B}
is a completely positive and trace non-increasing linear map from
{\cal L}(A)
to
{\cal L}(B)
. We denote by
\text{\rm CP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B)
the set of completely positive (CP) maps from
A
to
B
, and by
\text{\rm CPTP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B)
the set of all CPTP maps. The
diamond norm
of a linear map is defined by
\|{\cal E}\|_{\diamond}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{\rho\in\mathscr{D}(RA)}\|{\cal E}_{A\to B}(\rho_{RA})\|_{1}
. We will drop the identity map
{\cal I}
and identity operator
{\mathds{1}}
if they are clear from the context. The
Choi matrix
of a linear map
{\cal N}_{A^{\prime}\to B}
is defined as
J_{{\cal N}}=({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal N}_{A^{\prime}\to B})(|\Gamma\rangle\!\langle\Gamma|_{A^{\prime}A})
, where
|\Gamma\rangle_{A^{\prime}A}=\sum_{i}|i\rangle_{A^{\prime}}|i\rangle_{A}
is the unnormalized maximally entangled state. For every quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
, there exists an isometry
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\cal H}_{A}\to{\cal H}_{B}\otimes{\cal H}_{E}
such that
{\cal N}(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}_{E}(V\rho V^{\dagger})
where
{\cal H}_{E}
is an environment space and
V
is called a
Stinespring isometry
. The
complementary channel
of
{\cal N}
is denoted by
{\cal N}^{c}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E)
that acts as
{\cal N}^{c}(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}(V\rho V^{\dagger})
. The action of a quantum channel also has a Kraus representation, i.e.,
{\cal N}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}K_{i}(\cdot)K_{i}^{\dagger}
with
\sum_{i}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}={\mathds{1}}
.

3
Upper bounds on quantum communication

In this section, we develop Riemannian optimization methods for estimating upper bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement and the quantum capacity. First, we introduce our methods for constructing states and channel extensions.

3.1
Extensions of quantum states and channels

A common strategy for constructing extensions of states or channels is referred to as the
flag extension
method, which relies on a convex decomposition of the state or channel
[
LDS18
,
Wan21
,
FKG20
,
KFG22
]
. For a given bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
, one first decomposes it into
\rho_{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\omega_{AB}^{(i)}
where
\{\omega_{AB}^{(i)}\}_{i}
are normalized states. An extension is then constructed by appending an auxiliary
flag
state
\tau_{F}^{(i)}
to each
\omega_{AB}^{(i)}
, i.e.,

\hat{\rho}_{ABF}\coloneqq\sum_{i}p_{i}\omega_{AB}^{(i)}\otimes\tau_{F}^{(i)}.

(3)

By construction, tracing out the flag system
F
recovers the original state. A similar procedure applies to a quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
. Using a Kraus representation
{\cal N}_{A\to B}(\cdot)=\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{i}(\cdot)K_{i}^{\dagger}
, one can define an extended channel where the action of each Kraus operator is correlated with a corresponding flag state, i.e.,

\widehat{{\cal N}}_{A\to BF}(\cdot)\coloneqq\sum_{i}p_{i}K_{i}(\cdot)K_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\tau_{F}^{(i)}.

(4)

It is clear that
\operatorname{Tr}_{F}\widehat{{\cal N}}_{A\to BF}(\rho_{A})={\cal N}_{A\to B}(\rho_{A})
for any input state
\rho_{A}
. The central challenge of this method is that the subsequent optimization over the flag state ensemble
\{\tau_{F}^{(i)}\}_{i}
is fundamentally tied to the initial choice of decomposition. To establish a more general framework that avoids this dependency, we begin by formally defining state and channel extensions.

Definition 1

(State extension)
.

For any given bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
, a quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}\in\mathscr{D}(ABF)
is called an extended state of
\rho_{AB}
if
\operatorname{Tr}_{F}\hat{\rho}_{ABF}=\rho_{AB}
.

Definition 2

(Channel extension)
.

For any given quantum channel
{\cal N}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B)
, a quantum channel
\widehat{{\cal N}}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}BF)
is called an extended channel of
{\cal N}
if

{\cal N}(\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}_{F}\widehat{{\cal N}}(\rho),~\forall\rho\in\mathscr{D}(A).

(5)

After direct observation, we can gather the following facts about any state or channel extension.

Fact 1
.

For any bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
,
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}
is an extended state of
\rho_{AB}
if and only if there is an environment system
R
and an isometry
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\cal H}_{E}\to{\cal H}_{R}\otimes{\cal H}_{F}
such that
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}=\operatorname{Tr}_{R}(V|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|V^{\dagger})
where
|\phi\rangle_{ABE}
is a purification of
\rho_{AB}
.

Proof.

For the if-part, assume
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}=\operatorname{Tr}_{R}(V|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|V^{\dagger})
where
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\cal H}_{E}\to{\cal H}_{R}\otimes{\cal H}_{F}
. We can check that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{F}\hat{\rho}_{ABF}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{RF}\big[({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes V)|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes V^{\dagger})\big]

(6)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{E}\big[({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes V^{\dagger}V)|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|\big]=\operatorname{Tr}_{E}|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|=\rho_{AB}.

For the only if-part, assume
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}
is an extension of
\rho_{AB}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Tr}_{F}\hat{\rho}_{ABF}=\rho_{AB}
. Let
|\phi\rangle_{ABE}
and
|\psi\rangle_{ABFR}
be purifications of
\rho_{AB}
and
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}
, respectively. The fact that
\operatorname{Tr}_{FR}|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{ABFR}=\rho_{AB}
means that
|\psi\rangle_{ABFR}
is also a purification of
\rho_{AB}
. Then there must exist an isometry
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\cal H}_{E}\to{\cal H}_{F}\otimes{\cal H}_{R}
such that
|\psi\rangle_{ABFR}=({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes V)|\phi\rangle_{ABE}
. Hence, it is obvious to express
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}
as
\hat{\rho}_{ABF}=\operatorname{Tr}_{R}(V|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|V^{\dagger})
which completes the proof.

3.1.1
Riemannian optimization for extensions

Based on the two facts above, the key insight of our work is that the space of all valid extensions for a given state or channel can be endowed with a
Riemannian manifold
structure. Consequently, the problem of optimizing a chosen figure of merit over this space can be cast as a Riemannian optimization problem, allowing for the direct application of its powerful numerical tools; see Ref.
[
AMS08
]
for an overview.

Taking the state extension as an example, for a given bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
, suppose the figure of merit we are interested in is to minimize a function
f(\rho_{ABF})
where
\rho_{ABF}
is any extension of
\rho_{AB}
. Then using Fact
1
, for any fixed
|F|,|R|\in{{\mathbb{N}}}_{+}
, taking
\rho_{ABF}=\operatorname{Tr}_{R}(V|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|V^{\dagger})
into the objective, the problem

\min_{V\in\mathrm{St}(|FR|,|E|)}f(V)

(8)

reduces to a constrained optimization problem over the isometries. The feasible region of these isometries is the
complex Stiefel manifold

\mathrm{St}(|FR|,|E|)\coloneqq\big\{V\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|FR|\times|E|}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V^{\dagger}V={\mathds{1}}\big\},

(9)

a well-studied Riemannian manifold
[
AT60
]
. This naturally leads us to the framework of
Riemannian optimization
, which leverages the geometric structure of the manifold to generate iterates that remain within the feasible set
[
AMS08
]
. Optimization problems on Riemannian manifolds have appeared in various areas of quantum information sciences (see, e.g., Refs.
[
LRFO21
,
COT24
,
KBHM24
,
HKY+24
,
ZZAZ25
,
LSM25
,
ZPGW25
,
LHZ+25
]
as a very incomplete list).

A central element of many Riemannian optimization methods, e.g., Riemannian gradient descent (RGD)
[
Bon13
]
, is the
Riemannian gradient
. This gradient provides the direction of steepest ascent in the
tangent space
at a given point on the manifold, guiding the optimization process. However, a key challenge in many problems we would encounter is that the objective function
f(V)
is inherently non-smooth, meaning its gradient may not be defined at all points. This necessitates the use of optimization techniques suited for non-smooth problems, such as subgradient or gradient-free methods. We will further see details in Section
3.2.1
.

3.2
Upper bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement

In this section, we first establish an improved continuity bound on the one-way distillable entanglement based on the recently improved continuity bound on the quantum conditional entropy as shown in Lemma
2
. Then, we apply the Riemannian optimization methods for searching extensions of a given state to give tighter upper bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement, combining with the continuity bound.

For the task of entanglement distillation from
n
i.i.d. copies of a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
, the objective is to transform the initial state
\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}
into
m
copies of a Bell state
\Phi
via LOCC, such that the fidelity with the target state
\Phi^{\otimes m}
approaches one in the asymptotic limit (
n\to\infty
). When the classical communication is restricted from Alice to Bob, the protocol class is known as
one-way LOCC
. The corresponding optimal rate of distillation is the
one-way distillable entanglement
, denoted
D_{\to}(\rho_{A|B})
, where the notation
A|B
emphasizes that the restricted communication direction (only from
A
to
B
is possible). A seminal work by Devetak and Winter
[
DW05
]
provides a characterization of this quantity via a regularized formula

\displaystyle D_{\to}(\rho_{A|B})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{A|B}^{\otimes n}),

(10)

where
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{A|B})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{A|B})=\max_{{\cal T}}I(A^{\prime}\rangle BM)_{{\cal T}(\rho_{AB})}.

(11)

Here
{\cal T}
is a quantum instrument, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\cal T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\to A^{\prime}M,\quad{\cal T}(\rho_{A})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}T_{j}(\rho_{A})\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|,

(12)

where for each
j,~T_{j}\in\text{\rm CP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\prime})
and
\sum_{j}T_{j}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{\prime})
, and
M
is a classical system to store measurement results with
|M|=m
. Equivalently, Eq. (
11
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{A|B})=\max_{{\cal T}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}I(A^{\prime}\rangle B)_{\rho_{j}},

(13)

where
\lambda_{j}
is equal to the probability of outcome
j
, i.e.,
\lambda_{j}=\operatorname{Tr}[T_{j}(\rho_{AB})]
,
\rho_{j}=1/\lambda_{j}T_{j}(\rho_{AB})
is the state after measurement with outcome
j
.

A bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
is termed
degradable
if there exists a quantum channel
{\cal M}_{B\to E}
such that
{\cal M}_{B\to E}(\rho_{AB})=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|_{ABE}
, where
|\phi\rangle_{ABE}
is a purification of
\rho_{AB}

D_{\to}(\rho_{AB})=I(A\rangle B)_{\rho}

[
LDS18
]
. In the same work, the approximate degradability was introduced as follows.

Definition 3

(Degradability parameter of bipartite states
[
LDS18
]
)

Let
\rho\in\mathscr{D}(AB)
be a bipartite quantum state with purification
|\rho\rangle_{ABE}
, where

denotes the environment system. We say that
\rho
is
\varepsilon
-degradable if there exists a quantum channel
{\cal M}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E^{\prime})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big\|\rho_{AE}-{\cal M}_{B\to E^{\prime}}(\rho_{AB})\big\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon.

(14)

The channel

is referred to as an
\varepsilon

\rho_{AB}
. The degradability parameter of
\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle\operatorname{dg}(\rho)\coloneqq\min_{{\cal M}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E^{\prime})}\frac{1}{2}\big\|\rho_{AE}-{\cal M}_{B\to E^{\prime}}(\rho_{AB})\big\|_{1}.

(15)

Notably, the degradability parameter of a given state can be efficiently computed via semidefinite programming (SDP)

[

[

[

Theorem 1

(Improved upper bound on the one-way distillable entanglement)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathscr{D}(AB)
be an
\varepsilon
-degradable quantum state with
\varepsilon
-degrading channel
{\cal M}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E^{\prime})
. Let
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\cal H}_{B}\to{\cal H}_{E^{\prime}}\otimes{\cal H}_{G}
be a Stinespring isometry of
{\cal M}
with
E^{\prime}\cong E
. It satisfies that

\displaystyle D_{\to}(\rho_{A|B})

\displaystyle\leq U_{{\cal M}}(\rho_{A|B})+\varepsilon\log(|E|^{2}-1)+h(\varepsilon).

(19)

Proof.

The argument here closely follows the proof of
[
LDS18
, Theorem 2.12]
, leveraging Lemma
2
. Let
|\rho\rangle_{ABE}
be a purification of
\rho_{AB}
. Consider
n
copies of the state
\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}
and let
{\cal T}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{n}\to A^{\prime}M
be an instrument with isometric extension

T_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A^{n}\to A^{\prime}MN,\quad T_{n}=\sum_{m}K_{j}\otimes|m\rangle_{M}\otimes|m\rangle_{N},

(20)

where
\{K_{j}\}
are Kraus operators. For
t=1,\ldots,n
, define the pure states

\displaystyle|\psi^{t}\rangle_{A^{n}B_{t+1}\ldots B_{n}E_{1}^{\prime}\ldots E_{t}^{\prime}G_{1}\ldots G_{t}E_{1}\ldots E_{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V_{B_{1}\to E_{1}^{\prime}G_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{B_{t}\to E_{t}^{\prime}G_{t}}|\rho\rangle_{ABE}^{\otimes n},

(21)

\displaystyle|\theta^{t}\rangle_{A^{\prime}MNB_{t+1}\ldots B_{n}E_{1}^{\prime}\ldots E_{t}^{\prime}G_{1}\ldots G_{t}E_{1}\ldots E_{n}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=T_{n}|\psi^{t}\rangle,

(22)

where
V_{B_{i}\to E_{i}^{\prime}G_{i}}
are Stinespring isometries for the
\varepsilon
-degrading channels.

It was shown in
[
LDS18
, Eq. (2.27)]
that

I(A^{\prime}\rangle MB^{n})_{T_{n}\rho^{\otimes n}T_{n}^{\dagger}}=H(G^{n}|ME^{\prime n})_{\theta^{n}}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[H(E^{\prime}_{t}|ME^{\prime}_{<t}E_{>t})_{\theta^{t}}-H(E_{t}|ME^{\prime}_{<t}E_{>t})_{\theta^{t}}\right],

(23)

where
X_{<t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=X_{1}\cdots X_{t-1}
(with
X_{<1}
the trivial system) and
X_{>t}
is defined analogously. In
[
LDS18
]
, it is shown that the trace distance between
\theta^{t}_{ME^{\prime}_{1}\ldots E^{\prime}_{t-1}E_{t}\ldots E_{n}}
and
\theta^{t}_{ME^{\prime}_{1}\ldots E^{\prime}_{t}E_{t+1}\ldots E_{n}}
is bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big\|\theta^{t}_{ME^{\prime}_{1}\ldots E^{\prime}_{t-1}E_{t}\ldots E_{n}}-\theta^{t}_{ME^{\prime}_{1}\ldots E^{\prime}_{t}E_{t+1}\ldots E_{n}}\Big\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon.

(24)

Notice that

\operatorname{Tr}_{E^{\prime}_{t}}\theta^{t}_{ME^{\prime}_{1}\ldots E^{\prime}_{t}E_{t+1}\ldots E_{n}}=\operatorname{Tr}_{E_{t}}\theta^{t}_{ME^{\prime}_{1}\ldots E^{\prime}_{t-1}E_{t+1}}
, i.e., the marginals on

coincide. Thus, by applying Lemma
2
, the second term in Eq. (

) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[H(E^{\prime}_{t}|ME^{\prime}_{<t}E_{>t})_{\theta^{t}}-H(E_{t}|ME^{\prime}_{<t}E_{>t})_{\theta^{t}}\right]\leq n\left(\varepsilon\log(|E|^{2}-1)+h(\varepsilon)\right).

(25)

It follows that

\displaystyle I(A^{\prime}\rangle MB^{n})_{T_{n}\rho^{\otimes n}T_{n}^{\dagger}}

\displaystyle\leq H(G^{n}|ME^{\prime n})_{\theta^{n}}+n\left(\varepsilon\log(|E|^{2}-1)+h(\varepsilon)\right)

(26)

\displaystyle\leq H(G^{n}|E^{\prime n})_{\psi^{n}}+n\left(\varepsilon\log(|E|^{2}-1)+h(\varepsilon)\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=nH(G|E^{\prime})_{\psi}+n\left(\varepsilon\log(|E|^{2}-1)+h(\varepsilon)\right),

(28)

where the second inequality uses the data-processing inequality for conditional entropy under partial trace, and the last equality uses the additivity of conditional entropy for tensor product states as in Eq. (
21
). Since the above holds for any instrument
{\cal T}_{n}
, optimizing over all such instruments and dividing both sides by
n
, then taking the limit
n\to\infty
completes the proof.

\mathrm{St}(|FR|,|E|)

3.2.1
Numerical gradient approximation on the manifold

Since our primary goal is to establish a method that can be practically implemented to compute upper bounds for a wide range of quantum states and channels, we prioritize a pragmatic approach to optimization. We utilize an RGD algorithm but replace the intractable analytical gradient with a numerical approximation via a finite-difference scheme, a well-adopted approach in derivative-free optimization
[
Miz73
,
CK00
,
Kel11
,
NS17
,
SXON21
]
, and has been shown effective in some bilevel problems
[
SJL22
]
. To apply gradient-based optimization to the objective function in Eq. (
31
) (or Eq. (
29
)), we will show that the following objective function is actually
locally Lipschitz continuous
.

\displaystyle f(V)=I(A\rangle BF)_{\rho(V)}+2\operatorname{dg}(V)\log(\eta^{2}-1)+2h(\operatorname{dg}(V)).

(32)

For the first term
I(A\rangle B)_{\rho}
, we note that the von Neumann entropy
H(\rho)
is continuously differentiable with respect to
\rho
at any point where
\rho
is full-rank, i.e.,
\nabla H(\rho)=-(\log\rho+{\mathds{1}})
. Therefore,
I(A\rangle B)_{\rho}
is also continuously differentiable on the full rank density matrices, which means that
\rho\mapsto I(A\rangle B)_{\rho}
is locally Lipschitz continuous. The binary entropy term
h(\cdot)
is also locally Lipschitz. For the second term, the degradability parameter, we have the following results.

Lemma 4
.

The degradability parameter
\operatorname{dg}(\cdot)
is a Hölder continuous function on the set of bipartite quantum states
\mathscr{D}(AB)
with respect to the trace distance. Specifically, for any two states
\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB}\in\mathscr{D}(AB)
such that
\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\leq 1
, it holds that

|\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})-\operatorname{dg}(\sigma_{AB})|\leq\sqrt{2\varepsilon}+\varepsilon.

(33)

Proof.

Let
\rho_{AB}
and
\sigma_{AB}
be two bipartite states on
{\cal H}_{A}\otimes{\cal H}_{B}
. Choose
|\phi^{\rho}\rangle_{ABE}
and
|\phi^{\sigma}\rangle_{ABE}
to be purifications of
\rho_{AB}
and
\sigma_{AB}
on a common system
{\cal H}_{E}
with
|E|\geq\dim({\cal H}_{A})\dim({\cal H}_{B})
, such that they saturate the fidelity bound in Uhlmann’s theorem, i.e.,

F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})=|\langle\phi^{\rho}|\phi^{\sigma}\rangle|^{2},

(34)

where
F(\rho,\sigma)\coloneqq\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}^{2}
is the fidelity between
\rho
and
\sigma
. The complementary states are given by
\rho_{AE}=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}|\phi^{\rho}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{\rho}|
and
\sigma_{AE}=\operatorname{Tr}_{B}|\phi^{\sigma}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{\sigma}|
. Let us define the function
f_{{\cal M}}(\rho_{AB})
as

f_{{\cal M}}(\rho_{AB})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big\|\rho_{AE}-({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M}_{B\to E})(\rho_{AB})\big\|_{1}.

(35)

Consider that

\displaystyle\big|f_{{\cal M}}(\rho_{AB})-f_{{\cal M}}(\sigma_{AB})\big|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big|\big\|\rho_{AE}-({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M})(\rho_{AB})\big\|_{1}-\big\|\sigma_{AE}-({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M})(\sigma_{AB})\big\|_{1}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\big\|\rho_{AE}-({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M})(\rho_{AB})-\sigma_{AE}+({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M})(\sigma_{AB})\big\|_{1}

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\rho_{AE}-\sigma_{AE}\|_{1}+\big\|({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M})(\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB})\big\|_{1}\Big)

(37)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\|\rho_{AE}-\sigma_{AE}\|_{1}+\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}\Big).

(38)

Here, in Eq. (
36
), we used the reverse triangle inequality for the trace norm, i.e.,
\|X\|_{1}-\|Y\|_{1}\leq\|X-Y\|_{1}
. We used
\|X-Y\|_{1}\leq\|X\|_{1}+\|Y\|_{1}
in Eq. (
37
) and used
\|({\cal I}_{A}\otimes{\cal M}_{B\to E})(\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB})\|_{1}\leq\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}
in Eq. (
38
). Recall the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequalities

1-\sqrt{F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{1-F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})}.

(39)

We have that

\displaystyle\|\rho_{AE}-\sigma_{AE}\|_{1}\leq\||\phi^{\rho}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{\rho}|-|\phi^{\sigma}\rangle\!\langle\phi^{\sigma}|\|_{1}=2\sqrt{1-F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})},

(40)

where the first inequality is due to the monotonicity of the trace distance under partial trace, the equality is thanks to Eq. (
34
) and the fact that any pair of pure states saturates the upper bound in Eq. (
39
). Notice that

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(1+\sqrt{F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})})(1-\sqrt{F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})})}

(41)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2(1-\sqrt{F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}},

where the first inequality is because
F(\rho_{AB},\sigma_{AB})\leq 1
and the second inequality is followed from Eq. (
39
). Plugging into Eq. (
40
), we get

\|\rho_{AE}-\sigma_{AE}\|_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}}.

(42)

Now, let
{\cal M}_{\rho}
be a CPTP map that achieves the minimum for
\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})
, and let
{\cal M}_{\sigma}
be one that achieves the minimum for
\operatorname{dg}(\sigma_{AB})
. By definition, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})=f_{{\cal M}_{\rho}}(\rho_{AB}),~~\operatorname{dg}(\sigma_{AB})=f_{{\cal M}_{\sigma}}(\sigma_{AB}).

Since
{\cal M}_{\rho}
is optimal for
\rho_{AB}
, we have
\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})\leq f_{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{AB})
for any map
\mathcal{D}
, including
{\cal M}_{\sigma}
. This leads to

\displaystyle\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})-\operatorname{dg}(\sigma_{AB})

\displaystyle\leq f_{{\cal M}_{\sigma}}(\rho_{AB})-f_{{\cal M}_{\sigma}}(\sigma_{AB})

(43)

\displaystyle\leq|f_{{\cal M}_{\sigma}}(\rho_{AB})-f_{{\cal M}_{\sigma}}(\sigma_{AB})|

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2\sqrt{\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}}+\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}\right),

(45)

where we used Eq. (
38
) and Eq. (
42
) for the last inequality. By swapping the roles of
\rho_{AB}
and
\sigma_{AB}
, the same argument yields

\operatorname{dg}(\sigma_{AB})-\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2\sqrt{\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}}+\|\sigma_{AB}-\rho_{AB}\|_{1}\right).

(46)

Combining these two inequalities gives

|\operatorname{dg}(\rho_{AB})-\operatorname{dg}(\sigma_{AB})|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(2\sqrt{\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}}+\|\rho_{AB}-\sigma_{AB}\|_{1}\right).

\rho_{AB}

|\phi\rangle_{ABE}

Riemannian gradient descent.

Our RGD algorithm, shown in Algorithm

[

for a comprehensive treatment.
The Riemannian gradient, denoted
\operatorname{grad}f(V)
, is a tangent vector in the tangent space
\tangent_{V}\mathscr{M}
at a point
V
on the manifold
\mathscr{M}
. It is defined by the relation

\big\langle\operatorname{grad}f(V),\dot{V}\big\rangle_{V}=\mathrm{D}f(V)[\dot{V}],~~\forall\dot{V}\in\tangent_{V}\mathscr{M},

(54)

where
\dot{V}
is any tangent vector,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}
is the Riemannian metric on the
tangent space
, and
\mathrm{D}f(V)[\dot{V}]
is the directional derivative of
f
at
V
along
\dot{V}
.

Specifically, for the
complex Stiefel manifold

\mathrm{St}(n,p)\coloneqq\{V\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{n\times p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V^{\dagger}V={\mathds{1}}\}
, its tangent space is given by

\tangent_{V}\mathrm{St}(n,p)=\big\{VA\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{n\times n},~A^{\dagger}+A=0\big\}.

(55)

The Riemannian metric on
\tangent_{V}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
we use is given by

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tangent_{V}\mathrm{St}(n,p)\times\tangent_{V}\mathrm{St}(n,p)\to{{\mathbb{R}}},\qquad\langle X,Y\rangle_{V}=\operatorname{Tr}(X^{\dagger}Y).

(56)

The projection onto the tangent space at any point
V\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
reads

\mathrm{Proj}_{V}(X)=({\mathds{1}}-VV^{\dagger})X+V\frac{V^{\dagger}X-X^{\dagger}V}{2}.

(57)

The Riemannian gradient is then given by

\operatorname{grad}f(V)=\mathrm{Proj}_{V}\big(\nabla\bar{f}(V)\big),

(58)

where
\nabla\bar{f}(V)
is the gradient of an extension
\bar{f}
of
f
on Euclidean space. When doing an optimization on a manifold, in order to move in the direction of a tangent vector while staying on the manifold, we also need a retraction mapping
[
AMS08
, Section 4]
. There are different retractions for the Stiefel manifold, a standard one, and the one we used in Section
4.2
is the retraction based on the QR decomposition, i.e.,

\mathrm{R}_{V}(X)\coloneqq\mathrm{qf}(V+X),

(59)

where
\mathrm{qf}(X)
denotes the
Q
factor of the decomposition of
X
as
X=QR
where
Q\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
and
R
is an
p\times p
upper triangular with nonnegative diagonal entries.

To compute the full gradient vector

3.3
Upper bounds on the quantum capacity

In this section, we apply the Riemannian optimization methods for searching extensions of a target channel to obtain upper bounds on the quantum capacity. The Lloyd–Shor–Devetak theorem shows that the quantum capacity of a channel
{\cal N}
is equal to the regularized channel coherent information
[
SN96
,
Llo97
,
BKN00
,
Sho02
,
Dev05
]
,

\displaystyle Q({\cal N})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}Q^{(1)}({\cal N}^{\otimes n}),

(63)

where the
channel coherent information
is defined as
Q^{(1)}({\cal N})\coloneqq\max_{\rho\in\mathscr{D}}I_{c}(\rho,{\cal N})
(also denoted as
I_{c}({\cal N})
) with

I_{c}(\rho,{\cal N})\coloneqq H({\cal N}(\rho))-H({\cal N}^{c}(\rho)).

(64)

A quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
is said to be
degradable
if there exists a channel
{\cal M}\in\text{\rm CPTP}(B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}E)
such that
{\cal N}^{c}={\cal M}\circ{\cal N}
, where
{\cal N}^{c}
is the complementary channel of
{\cal N}
. The primary utility of this property is that the coherent information of a degradable channel is additive. Consequently, the regularization in Eq. (
63
) becomes unnecessary, leading to
Q({\cal N})=Q^{(1)}({\cal N})
. However, non-degradable channels generally exhibit non-additive coherent information
[
DSS98
,
LLS18
]
, making the regularization essential. This non-additivity can be so pronounced that an unbounded number of channel uses may be required to detect capacity
[
CEM+15
]
.

This sharp dichotomy between degradable and non-degradable channels motivates a more nuanced approach for channels that, while not strictly degradable, are
close
to being so. To formalize this, Sutter
et al.
introduced the
\varepsilon
-degradable channel

[
SSWR17
]
, defined as follows.

Definition 5
.

Let

be a quantum channel with complementary channel

, where

denotes the environment system. We say that

is

(65)

where

4
Lower bounds on quantum communication

In this section, we develop Riemannian optimization methods for estimating lower bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement and the quantum capacity.

4.1
Lower bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement

Recall that the regularized formula of the one-way distillable entanglement is

\displaystyle D_{\to}(\rho_{AB})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}D^{(1)}_{\to}(\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\max_{{\cal T}}I(A^{\prime}\rangle B^{n}M)_{{\cal T}(\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})},

(80)

where the maximization is over all quantum instruments
{\cal T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\to A^{\prime}M
on Alice’s system. It is defined by
{\cal T}(\cdot)=\sum_{j}T_{j}(\cdot)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|
, where
\{|j\rangle\}_{j=0}^{m-1}
is an orthonormal basis for the classical register, each
T_{j}
is a completely positive map such that
\sum_{j}T_{j}
is a CPTP. Note that it suffices to consider instrument
T
where each map
T_{j}
consists of only one Kraus operator
[
KW24
, Section 13.2.5]
, i.e.,

{\cal T}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}K_{j}(\cdot)K_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|.

(81)

The one-way distillable entanglement is an operational quantity of great interest in entanglement theory. Due to the superadditivity of
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\cdot)
, to obtain a lower bound on
D_{\to}(\rho_{AB})
, a direct approach is to tackle the calculation of
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})
for as large
n
as possible. To this end, we formulate the problem as a Riemannian optimization over the
unitary manifold
as follows. For any fixed
|M|=m
and any
j=0,1,...,m-1
, construct

K_{j}\coloneqq({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\langle j|_{M})U_{AM}({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes|0\rangle_{M}),

(82)

where
U_{AM}
is a unitary operator. It lies in
{\cal U}(|AM|)=\mathrm{St}(|AM|,|AM|)
, the unitary manifold, a special case of the Stiefel manifold. This parameterization is nothing but the Stinespring dilation theorem. One can check that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\langle 0|)U_{AM}^{\dagger}({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|)U_{AM}({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes|0\rangle)

\displaystyle=({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\langle 0|_{M})U_{AM}^{\dagger}U_{AM}({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes|0\rangle_{M})={\mathds{1}}_{A},

which shows
\{K_{j}\}
is a set of Kraus operators. Taking Eq. (
82
) into Eq. (
81
), for any fixed
n,m\in{{\mathbb{N}}}_{+}
, For a quantum state
\rho_{A|B}
, let us define the
coherent information cost function
\rho_{A|B}
as

\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}}(U_{AM})=-I(A^{\prime}\rangle BM)_{\sigma},

(83)

where

\sigma_{A|BM}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes\langle j|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}_{AM})({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes|j\rangle_{M})\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M}.

(84)

Lemma 9
.

For a bipartite quantum state
\rho_{AB}
and a system
M
with
|M|=m\in{{\mathbb{N}}}_{+}
, the Euclidean gradient of
\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{AB}}(U_{AM})
with respect to
U_{AM}
is given by

\nabla\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{AB}}=2\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big(\big({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}-\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\big)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M}\Big)({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})\bigg],

(85)

where
\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}=({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes\langle j|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}_{AM})({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes|j\rangle_{M})
and
\sigma_{B}^{(j)}=\operatorname{Tr}_{A}\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}

Proof.

For any unitary
U_{AM}
, let’s construct
K_{j}=({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\langle j|_{M})U_{AM}({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes|0\rangle_{M})
as that in Eq. (
82

\rho^{U}_{ABM}=({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}_{AM})

(86)

and

\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}=({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes\langle j|_{M})\rho^{U}_{ABM}({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes|j\rangle_{M}).

(87)

Then the output state
{\cal T}(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}K_{j}\rho_{AB}K_{j}^{{\dagger}}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M}
and its marginal can be written as

\sigma_{ABM}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M}~~\text{and}~~\sigma_{BM}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M},

(88)

respectively. Now, we express the coherent information cost function
\rho_{A|B}
as

\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}}(U_{AM})=\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma_{BM}\log\sigma_{BM}\big)-\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma_{ABM}\log\sigma_{ABM}\big),

(89)

and will calculate the gradient of it with respect to
U_{AM}

d\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}}=\mathrm{Re}\big(\operatorname{Tr}[(\nabla_{U}\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}})^{\dagger}dU]\big)

\log\sigma_{ABM}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big(\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\Big)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M},~~\log\sigma_{BM}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big(\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\Big)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M}.

(90)

It follows that

\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma_{ABM}\log\sigma_{ABM}\big)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\Big),~~\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma_{BM}\log\sigma_{BM}\big)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\Big).

(91)

and

\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}}(U_{AM})=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\Big)-\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\Big).

Using the fact that
d\operatorname{Tr}(X\log X)=\operatorname{Tr}[(\log X+{\mathds{1}})dX]

d\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}=({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes\langle j|_{M})d\rho^{U}_{ABM}({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes|j\rangle_{M})

\displaystyle d\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}d\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\Big)-d\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\Big)

(93)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\big(\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}+{\mathds{1}}_{B}\big)d\sigma_{B}^{(j)}\Big]-\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\big(\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}+{\mathds{1}}_{AB}\big)d\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\big({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}-\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\big)({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes\langle j|_{M})d\rho^{U}_{ABM}({\mathds{1}}_{AB}\otimes|j\rangle_{M})\Big]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\Big(Y_{ABM}d\rho^{U}_{ABM}\Big),

where in the last equality we defined

Y_{ABM}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\big({\mathds{1}}_{A}\otimes\log\sigma_{B}^{(j)}-\log\sigma_{AB}^{(j)}\big)\otimes|j\rangle\!\langle j|_{M}.

(94)

Note the differential of
\rho^{U}_{ABM}
is given by

d\rho^{U}_{ABM}=({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes dU_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}_{AM})+({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes dU^{{\dagger}}_{AM}).

(95)

We can plug it into Eq. (
93

\displaystyle d\mathrm{coh}_{\rho_{A|B}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\Big[Y_{ABM}({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes dU_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}_{AM})\Big]

(96)

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Tr}\Big[Y_{ABM}({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes dU^{{\dagger}}_{AM})\Big]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\Big((\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U^{{\dagger}}_{AM})Y_{ABM}\Big)dU_{AM}\Big]

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\operatorname{Tr}_{B}\Big(Y_{ABM}({\mathds{1}}_{B}\otimes U_{AM})(\rho_{AB}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{M})\Big)dU^{{\dagger}}_{AM}\Big].

4.2
Lower bounds on the quantum capacity

A key challenge in determining a channel’s quantum capacity,
Q({\cal N})
, is the superadditivity of its underlying coherent information, i.e., entangled input state across multiple channel uses
{\cal N}^{\otimes n}
can strictly improve the coherent information compared with strategies that use the channels independently. Consequently, the standard approach to establishing lower bounds is to compute
\frac{1}{n}Q^{(1)}({\cal N}^{\otimes n})
for large
n
, a quantity that becomes progressively tighter as the blocklength
n
increases. Recall that the
n
-shot channel coherent information is given by

Q^{(1)}({\cal N}^{\otimes n})\coloneqq\max_{\psi_{RA^{n}}}I(R\rangle B)_{{\cal N}^{\otimes n}_{A\to B}(\psi_{RA^{n}})},

(101)

where the optimization is with respect to all
code states

|\psi\rangle_{RA^{n}}
and we abbreviate
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
as
\psi
. One key strategy to obtaining a tight lower bound is to restrict the search to code state families with high symmetry
[
BL21
,
BL25
]
. This approach can render the entropy computation manageable even for large blocklengths, enabling a more accurate assessment of superadditive gains. A notable recent success in this method is the use of permutation-invariant codes
[
BL25
]
, which have led to improved capacity thresholds for several important channel models. Another strategy moves away from rigid symmetries and instead utilizes highly expressive, general-purpose ansatz. A key example is to utilize a neural network state ansatz to estimate lower bounds on quantum capacity
[
BL20
]
.

In fact, it is straightforward to see that Eq. (
101
) is an optimization on the
complex unit sphere
of the corresponding Hilbert space, a canonical example of a Riemannian manifold. Thereby, we can apply Riemannian optimization algorithms to compute Eq. (
101
). The tangent space of a complex unit sphere
\mathrm{S}^{|A|}
is given by
\tangent_{|\psi\rangle}\mathrm{S}^{|A|}=\{|\dot{\psi}\rangle\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|A|}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\rangle=0\}
, the projection onto which is

\mathrm{Proj}_{|\psi\rangle}(|v\rangle)=({\mathds{1}}-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|v\rangle,~\forall|v\rangle\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|A|}.

(102)

101
) once we can obtain the Riemannian gradient. To this end, we first calculate the Euclidean gradient of the objective function as follows.

|\psi\rangle_{RA^{3}}=(U^{(5)}_{RA_{1}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{A_{2}A_{3}})(U^{(4)}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{RA_{3}})(U^{(3)}_{A_{2}A_{3}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{RA_{1}})(U^{(2)}_{RA_{2}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{A_{1}A_{3}})(U^{(1)}_{RA_{1}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{A_{2}A_{3}})|\phi\rangle_{RA^{3}},

(111)

where
|\phi\rangle_{RA^{3}}
is a fixed initial state, e.g.,
|0\rangle^{\otimes|R||A|^{3}}
, and each
U^{(i)}
is a unitary operator acting on a specific subsystem, creating an interleaved structure as depicted in Figure
8
. We call such a circuit an
interleaved local unitary ansatz
. Similar ideas have been adopted to optimize quantum circuits for Hamiltonian simulation, e.g., using a brick wall circuit ansatz and then applying Riemannian optimization
[
KBHM24
,
LSM25
]
. Then the optimization is over a product of unitary manifolds

{\cal U}(|RA|)\times{\cal U}(|A|^{2})\times\cdots\times{\cal U}(|A|^{2})\times{\cal U}(|RA|).

(112)

The key insight of this method is to interleave local unitaries to avoid the curse of dimensionality while maintaining expressivity of the parameterized input state. If
|R|
is a fixed constant, then each constituent manifold has a fixed dimension that does not scale with
n
, ensuring the problem remains manageable even for large blocklengths. Even if we want to make
|R|
increase dependent on
n
, e.g.,
|R|=|A|^{n}
, we can alternatively divide
R
into local subsystems and construct a similar manageable local structure.

\frac{1}{3}Q^{(1)}({\cal N}_{A\to B}^{\otimes 3})
using a product of unitary manifolds. Five local unitaries are interleaved to parameterize an input code state
|\psi\rangle_{RA_{1}A_{2}A_{3}}
for 3 copies of the channel.

Formally, for
n
-copy uses of a quantum channel
{\cal N}
, let us define the
coherent information cost function of
n
-shot
{\cal N}
as

\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U}_{n})=-I(R\rangle B)_{{\cal N}^{\otimes n}_{A\to B}(\psi(\mathbf{U}_{n}))},

(113)

where

\mathbf{U}_{n}=(U^{(1)},U^{(2)},\cdots,U^{(2n-1)})\in\underbrace{{\cal U}(|RA|)\times{\cal U}(|A|^{2})\times\cdots\times{\cal U}(|RA|)}_{2n-1},

(114)

and

|\psi(\mathbf{U})\rangle_{RA^{n}}={\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{k=n+1}^{2n-1}\Big(U^{(k)}_{S_{2n-1-k}S_{2n-k}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{S^{(k)}_{c}}\Big){\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{k=1}^{n}\Big(U^{(k)}_{S_{k-1}S_{k}}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{S^{(k)}_{c}}\Big)|0\rangle_{RA^{n}}.

(115)

Here, the notation
\overleftarrow{\prod}
denotes the ordered product of operators, arranged from left to right in descending order of the index. We set
S_{0}=R,S_{j}=A_{j}
and denote the corresponding complementary subsystem by

S_{c}^{(k)}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{aligned} &S_{1}S_{2}\cdots S_{n}\setminus S_{k-1}S_{k},\quad&&\text{for}~1\leq k\leq n,\\
&S_{1}S_{2}\cdots S_{n}\setminus S_{2n-1-k}S_{2n-k},\quad&&\text{for}~n<k\leq 2n-1.\end{aligned}\right.

(116)

The tangent space of the product manifold is given by

\tangent_{U^{(1)}}{\cal U}(|RA|)\times\tangent_{U^{(2)}}{\cal U}(|A|^{2})\times\cdots\tangent_{U^{(2n-1)}}{\cal U}(|RA|),

(117)

where each local tangent space is defined in Eq. (
55
). The Riemannian gradient is given by

\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U})=\Big(\mathrm{Proj}_{U^{(1)}}\big(\partial_{U^{(1)}}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathbf{U})\big),\cdots,\mathrm{Proj}_{U^{(2n-1)}}\big(\partial_{U^{(2n-1)}}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mathrm{coh}}(\mathbf{U})\big)\Big),

(118)

where
\mathrm{Proj}_{U^{k}}(\cdot)
is the projection onto the tangent space as defined in Eq. (
57
) and
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mathrm{coh}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|RA|\times|RA|}\times{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|A|^{2}\times|A|^{2}}\times\cdots{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|RA|\times|RA|}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is an extension function of
\mathrm{coh}
that coincide with the later on the product of unitary manifolds. The retraction map is given in Eq. (
59
).

Since such an ansatz (cf. Eq. (
114
) and Figure
8
) is not guaranteed to express all possible input states, we have the relation

Q^{(1)}({\cal N}^{\otimes n})\geq-\min_{\mathbf{U}_{n}}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U}_{n}),

(119)

to give a valid lower bound on
Q^{(1)}({\cal N}^{\otimes n})
, thus a lower bound on
Q({\cal N})
. Then Eq. (
119
) is the main optimization we are concerned with, which we utilize the RGD algorithm to solve. The Riemannian gradient can be calculated as follows.

Lemma 12
.

For a quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
and its
n
-shot coherent information cost function
\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U})
, denote
V^{(k)}=U^{(k)}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{S_{c}^{(k)}},|\phi_{k}\rangle={\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=1}^{k}V^{(j)}|0\rangle_{RA^{n}}
, and
|\psi\rangle_{RA^{n}}=|\phi_{2n-1}\rangle
. Then the partial derivative of
\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U})

at
U^{(k)}
is given by

\partial_{U^{(k)}}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}=\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{c}^{(k)}}\Big[\big(V^{(2n-1)}V^{(2n-2)}\cdots V^{(k+1)}\big)^{\dagger}|G_{\psi}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{k-1}|\Big],

(120)

for
k=1,2,\cdots,2n-1
, where
|G_{\psi}\rangle=\partial_{|\psi\rangle}f_{{\cal N}}
is given in Lemma
11
.

Proof.

Based on Lemma
11
, we can use the chain rule to obtain the desired partial derivative. Denote the gradient vector with respect to a state
|\psi\rangle_{RA^{n}}
as
|G_{\psi}\rangle
. We have

df_{{\cal N}}=\mathrm{Re}\big(\langle G_{\psi}|d|\psi\rangle\big),~~d\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}=\mathrm{Re}\Big(\operatorname{Tr}\big[(\nabla_{\mathbf{U}}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n})^{\dagger}d\mathbf{U}\big]\Big).

(121)

We will calculate the gradient with respect to a single, arbitrary unitary
U^{(k)}
. To this end, we consider the change
d\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}
that results only from an infinitesimal change
dU^{(k)}
, while all other
U^{(j)}
for
j\neq k
are held constant. This is equivalent to taking a partial derivative. In this case,
dV^{(j)}=0
for all
j\neq k
. Using the fact that

d|\psi\rangle|_{U^{(k)}}=V^{(2n-1)}V^{(2n-2)}\cdots V^{(k+1)}dV^{(k)}|\phi_{k-1}\rangle,

(122)

we can deduce

\displaystyle d\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\Big(\operatorname{Tr}\Big[|\phi_{k-1}\rangle\!\langle G_{\psi}|V^{(2n-1)}V^{(2n-2)}\cdots V^{(k+1)}dV^{(k)}\Big]\Big)

(123)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\Big(\operatorname{Tr}\Big[|\phi_{k-1}\rangle\!\langle G_{\psi}|V^{(2n-1)}V^{(2n-2)}\cdots V^{(k+1)}(dU^{(k)}\otimes{\mathds{1}}_{S_{k}})\Big]\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\Big(\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{k}}\big(|\phi_{k-1}\rangle\!\langle G_{\psi}|V^{(2n-1)}V^{(2n-2)}\cdots V^{(k+1)}\big)dU^{(k)}\Big]\Big).

Compared with Eq. (
121
), we find that

\nabla_{U^{(k)}}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}=\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{k}}\Big[\big(V^{(2n-1)}V^{(2n-2)}\cdots V^{(k+1)}\big)^{\dagger}|G_{\psi}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{k-1}|\Big].

(124)

\sqcup

The Riemannian gradient is then given as follows.

Corollary 13
.

For a quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to B}
and a unitary ensemble
\{U^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{2n-1}
, the Riemannian gradient of the
n
-shot coherent information cost function at
\mathbf{U}=(U^{(1)},U^{(2)},\cdots,U^{(2n-1)})
is given by

\big[\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U})\big]_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{c}^{(k)}}\bigg[\Big({\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=k+1}^{2n-1}V^{(j)}\Big)^{\dagger}|G_{\psi}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{k-1}|-|\phi_{k}\rangle\!\langle G_{\psi}|{\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=k}^{2n-1}V^{(j)}\bigg]

(125)

for
k=1,2,\cdots,2n-1
.

Proof.

For the unitary manifold, according to Eq. (
57
), the projection onto the tangent space is given by

\mathrm{Proj}_{V}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(X-VX^{\dagger}V).

(126)

Therefore, by Lemma
12
and Eq. (
118
), we have that

\displaystyle\big[\operatorname{grad}\mathrm{coh}_{{\cal N},n}(\mathbf{U})\big]_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{c}^{(k)}}\bigg[\Big({\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=k+1}^{2n-1}V^{(j)}\Big)^{\dagger}|G_{\psi}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{k-1}|\bigg]

\displaystyle-\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{c}^{(k)}}\bigg[U^{(k)}|\phi_{k-1}\rangle\!\langle G_{\psi}|\Big({\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=k+1}^{2n-1}V^{(j)}\Big)U^{(k)}\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}_{S_{c}^{(k)}}\bigg[\Big({\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=k+1}^{2n-1}V^{(j)}\Big)^{\dagger}|G_{\psi}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{k-1}|-|\phi_{k}\rangle\!\langle G_{\psi}|{\overleftarrow{\prod}}_{j=k}^{2n-1}V^{(j)}\bigg].

\sqcup

3
to optimize a code state for a given channel
{\cal N}
and a positive integer
n
, thereby establishing a lower bound on the quantum capacity.

4.3
Superadditivity in one-way entanglement distillation

To demonstrate the efficacy of our method on computing lower bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement, we apply Algorithm
2
to a class of noisy bipartite states.

Generalized amplitude damping channel.

Similar to Eq. (
75
), we consider a pair of Bell state
|\Phi_{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)
shared between Alice and Bob is affected by a local
generalized amplitude damping channel
(GADC)
[
KSW20
]
channel. As a generalization of the AD channel, it is defined by

{\cal A}_{\gamma,N}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}A_{i}(\cdot)A_{i}^{\dagger},

(127)

where the Kraus operators
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{4}
are given by

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-N}\big(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|+\sqrt{1-\gamma}|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\big)

(128)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma(1-N)}|0\rangle\!\langle 1|

(129)

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}\big(\sqrt{1-\gamma}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|+|1\rangle\!\langle 1|\big)

(130)

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma N}|1\rangle\!\langle 0|,

(131)

with
\gamma,N\in[0,1]
. When
N=0
, it reduces to the AD channel. Thereby, we are interested in the Choi state of the GADC, i.e.,
\rho_{AB^{\prime}}=({\cal I}\otimes{\cal A}_{\gamma,N})(\Phi_{+})
. For a fixed number of copies
n=2,4
, we compute lower bounds on
D_{\to}^{(1)}(\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})
by optimizing a feasible one-way LOCC protocol using Algorithm
2
, with a fixed dimension of the classical register to
|M|=2
. The optimization is terminated when the norm of the Riemannian gradient falls below
10^{-7}
. To mitigate convergence to local optima, each data point is the result of 200 independent optimization runs with random initial unitaries.

The results of our numerical experiments are presented in Figure
9
. The green solid line represents the coherent information as a lower bound. The blue and red dashed lines show our improved multi-copy bounds for
n=2,4
, respectively, which are given by the quantity

\frac{1}{n}I(A^{\prime}\rangle B^{n}M)_{{\cal T}^{*}(\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n})}.

(132)

These bounds were calculated using the instrument
{\cal T}^{*}
, which was optimized via Algorithm
2
through the parameterization of the output unitary
U^{*}
. It demonstrates that for all tested noise parameters, the calculated lower bounds for multi-copy cases are strictly greater than the single-shot coherent information, which confirms that multi-copy protocols unlock additional distillable entanglement. The fluctuations observed in the dashed lines are attributed to the nature of the numerical optimization Algorithm
2
. As the channel parameter is varied, the algorithm may settle into different local solutions, causing small variations in the resulting lower bound. Because we set
|M|=2
for all experiments, and the issue of local minima, there may be cases where a 4-copy average rate is smaller than a 2-copy average rate in Figure
9(b)
. We can adjust the dimension of the classical system
M
to further optimize the lower bounds. Overall, the results highlight the effectiveness of our Riemannian optimization framework in discovering the non-trivial quantum instruments required to reveal these superadditive effects, even with a small measurement dimension.

4.5
Amortization does not enhance channel coherent information

It is known that the channel coherent information, and thus the quantum channel capacity, can be expressed in terms of a quantum channel divergence and its regularization
[
FGW25
, Section 5]
. One natural idea for obtaining potentially tighter lower bounds is to consider the amortized channel divergence
[
WBHK20
]
, where different input states are allowed for the two channels being distinguished. We introduce the notion of
amortized channel coherent information
and show that it is sandwiched between the channel coherent information and the regularized channel coherent information, suggesting a possible improvement over the single-letter lower bound by the channel coherent information. However, we subsequently prove that the amortized channel coherent information always coincides with the channel coherent information, thereby ruling out the potential enhancement of the quantum capacity lower bound via amortization.

Definition 6

5
Concluding remarks

In this work, we have developed a comprehensive framework based on Riemannian optimization to derive improved, computable two-sided bounds on the one-way distillable entanglement and the quantum capacity.

Our main technical contributions are twofold. First, for upper bounds, we transformed the search for state and channel extensions that can minimize information-theoretic bounds into a tractable optimization over the Stiefel manifold. This method, when combined with a refined continuity bound for conditional entropy, delivers strictly tighter upper bounds than previously known records for large values of the depolarizing parameter
[
KFG22
]
. We can also obtain improved upper bounds for different states and channels of interest. Second, for lower bounds, we transform the problem to optimizations over unitary manifolds. Our algorithm for states and channels can be flexibly used to explore the superadditivity in the one-way entanglement distillation rate and the channel coherent information. We demonstrate improved lower bounds on both quantities for various states and channels of interest, compared to previous results. Our methods may also serve as a guide for the design of entanglement distillation protocols and quantum error correction codes. Furthermore, we proved that amortization of the underlying channel divergence does not enhance the channel coherent information. This result excludes the amortization as a pathway to obtain a tighter lower bound on the quantum capacity, and can be of independent interest.

The methods presented here open several avenues for future research. For the upper-bound framework, it would be of great interest to see if the structure of the optimal isometries found via optimization can inspire new analytical insights or closed-form bounds for specific channel families. The computational cost of the finite-difference gradient approximation could be reduced by exploring more advanced non-smooth or bilevel optimization techniques tailored to the Stiefel manifold
[
SJL22
,
DCS24
,
HMJT25
]
. For the lower-bound approach, the key bottleneck of computing the entropy function remains unsolved in our methods, which limits the scalability of our algorithms. It would be interesting to explore new manifolds with a symmetry structure that can aid in the calculation of the entropy function, combined with the idea of permutation-invariant codes
[
BL25
]
. It would also be interesting and potentially impactful to explore geometric optimization for other quantum resource theories
[
CG19
,
LRWW23
,
KDWW19
,
GA15
,
WWS19
,
WWS20
,

,

,

,

,

]
and quantum information–processing tasks
[
DTW16
,
DMHB13
,

,

,

,

]
, both to establish fundamental limits and to discover better protocols or lower bounds of achievable rates.
