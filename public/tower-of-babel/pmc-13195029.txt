Developing and validating an artificial intelligence-based electronic triage model for predicting clinical outcomes among cardiac-suspected patients in the emergency department
Authors: Ahmad Bavali-Gazik, Elahe Allahyari, Amir Sezavar, Seyed Ali Moezi-Bady, Seyyed Abolfazl Vagharseyyedin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsEmergency department overcrowding, especially in cardiac units, delays care and raises mortality. Conventional triage is error-prone. We developed an AI-based model integrating routine data and automated ECGs to improve early risk classification.Methods and resultsThis retrospective cross-sectional study involved 600 medical records of patients presenting with suspected cardiac symptoms. Model development was conducted in three phases: Designing a triage model using routine triage data, designing a triage model based on ECG images, and combining the ECG-based model and triage data. Model performance was evaluated regarding standard clinical outcomes within the first 24 h and compared against the Emergency Severity Index. The best-performing model based on triage data alone (i.e. multilayer perceptron neural network) yielded an accuracy of 89.42%, F-score of 84.51, and area under the curve between 0.815 and 0.858. The best-performing model based on ECG interpretation alone (i.e. convolutional neural network) yielded an accuracy of 93.83%, F-score of 91.08, and area under the curve ranging from 0.852 to 0.914. The fusion model demonstrated superior performance, with an accuracy of 97.22%, F-score of 94.60, and area under the curve between 0.881 and 0.938—significantly outperforming the conventional Emergency Severity Index. In the fusion model, the key predictive variables included ECG interpretation, heart rate, and mode of entry to emergency department.ConclusionGiven its advantages over models using only routine data, ECG, or conventional triage, the fused AI-based triage model may effectively prioritize and predict cardiac emergency outcomes, providing a foundation for developing reliable, intelligent support systems in acute care.

Introduction

The emergency department (ED) is a vital link between hospitals and pre-hospital emergency services, increasingly acting as the primary access point to the healthcare system.1,2 ED overcrowding constitutes a significant global challenge for hospitals and is widely acknowledged as a critical public health concern.3,4 This congestion results in considerable negative consequences, notably heightened mortality rates.5 The urgency is especially significant for cardiac patients, as timely access to medical services directly affects patient morbidity and survival rates.6,7 Thus, quick patient assessment and the establishment of treatment priority upon arrival at the ED are essential.8,9

Triage was established as the first step in identifying critically ill patients.10 This long-standing process is employed to prioritize patients based on the severity of their injury or illness.11,12 Triage has evolved in clinical settings and serves as a crucial element in contemporary emergency management.13,14 Among various triage systems, the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) system has gained global recognition and is being implemented in (COUNTRY).15–17

Major challenges in triage arise from its reliance on human judgment and the heightened risk of mis-triage during periods of high volume.18–20 The potential for error poses significant consequences, as mis-triage may lead to serious patient outcomes.21 As a result, innovative triage methods, including e-triage, remote triage, and clinical decision support systems, are currently under investigation and development to improve the precision and efficiency of triage processes.22–24

Technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) are significantly progressing within the medical field, particularly in the area of triage.25 AI algorithms enhance diagnostic precision, predict risks, and minimize medical errors.26–28 Advancements in machine learning—a subfield of AI—and deep learning—a specialized subset of machine learning—enable AI technology to analyse complex clinical information, thereby assisting physicians and nurses in making more accurate decisions.29–31

In triage, AI-based models are incorporated to assess patient status using data such as vital signs, chief complaints, and medical history.32–34 Commonly employed as a standard and cost-effective method for detecting cardiac diseases, the electrocardiogram35 serves as a fundamental diagnostic tool in this domain.36–40 While human clinicians exhibit inaccuracies in ECG interpretation,40,41 AI-powered models show high precision in ECG analysis.37,42,43

In response to these challenges, this study has developed a set of AI-based electronic triage (e-triage) models. The models function in three phases using standard ESI triage data, automated ECG interpretation, and the integration of these two data types. Rather than proposing a novel algorithmic architecture, this study focuses on integrating a PTB-XL–pretrained AI-ECG interpretation model with routine ESI triage data and evaluating its incremental value over conventional triage in a real-world emergency department. This approach seeks to improve the differentiation of disease severity and enhance the prediction of clinical outcomes for patients. This study represents, as far as the literature indicates, the first integration of neural network-assisted automated ECG interpretation with routine triage data to develop a novel triage system, which is subsequently validated against the conventional ESI system through real-world patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design, setting, and sample

This is a retrospective, cross-sectional study involving predictive model development using machine-learning techniques and comparative evaluation against the conventional ESI triage system based on clinical outcomes in cardiac-suspected patients admitted to the ED.

The study population included the medical records of all patients aged 14 years and older visiting Razi Hospital Cardiac ED in (CITY), (COUNTRY), from early 2023 to early 2025. Patients presented with one or more symptoms suggestive of cardiovascular disorders, such as chest pain, angina, palpitations, myocarditis, hyper- or hypotension, arrhythmia, acute heart failure, and dyspnoea. The exclusion criteria comprised individuals with established psychological disorders, those with uncertain disease outcomes during the first 24 h, patients who died before reaching the hospital, cases with incomplete medical records, and instances of outliers, such as systolic blood pressure exceeding 300 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure exceeding 200 mmHg. Data were collected from the medical records department of the hospital upon the acquisition of ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Birjand University of Medical Sciences (ethics code: IR.BUMS.REC.1402.462). : …..).

Sample size and sampling method

The sample size for this study was determined according to the requirements of supervised machine-learning models. The development of a high-performing model necessitated careful consideration of the sample size, factoring in the model’s complexity, the number of input features, and the imperative to prevent overfitting. Data quality and diversity are essential for the convergence of the cost function and enhancing predictive precision in machine-learning models; therefore, the data volume was incrementally increased in stages.44 Once the statistical adequacy was confirmed and preliminary evaluations of model performance were performed, analyses were conducted on data from 600 patients, and the final model was developed.

Given the study’s purpose and the need for machine-learning models to have balanced data across multiple outcome classes, stratified purposive sampling was employed. This procedure ensured that samples were carefully selected from the entire population of eligible patients presenting to the cardiac ED with symptoms suggestive of cardiac disorders. The selection process sought to establish a roughly equal distribution of the three main clinical outcomes within the resulting dataset: mortality/intensive care unit admission, general ward admission, and discharge, which intentionally over-represents severe outcomes compared with their natural prevalence in the ED population so that the model does not become dominated by the majority (discharge) class.

This approach was adopted to address the critically low proportion of patients with severe outcomes (e.g. mortality, ICU admission) in typical sampling techniques. The observed disproportion adversely affects model precision and exacerbates the issue of class imbalance.

To improve model precision during the training phase, data augmentation techniques were used. ECG images were augmented with techniques such as partial rotation, contrast adjustment, and noise injection. Similarly, for the triage datasets, noise injection and the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique were employed using the default settings of the software library to oversample minority outcome classes and obtain a more balanced class distribution. These strategies were used to improve the model’s generalization capacity. Additionally, the PTB-XL database was applied to train the model for interpreting the ECGs of the patients in this study and given its imbalanced multi-label nature, the ECG interpretation performance was summarized using macro-averaged precision and F1-score, computed as the unweighted mean of the per-label metrics so that rare and frequent ECG statements contributed equally to the overall score.

Predictor variables

We employed routinely available information from ED triage and data derived from the initial ECG scans of patients presenting to the cardiac ED as predictors for the final machine-learning models.

Routine triage information: Such information included age, gender, time of arrival, pregnancy status, mode of arrival (ambulatory, wheelchair, stretcher), means of referral (private vehicle, ambulance), hospital visit history within the past 24 h, History of food and drug allergies, chief complaint, level of consciousness assessed using the Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive (AVPU) scale, medical history categorized according to International Classification of Diseases-11th Revision (ICD-11), systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, and oxygen saturation percentage (SpO2).

Outcomes

Patient outcomes were evaluated within 24 h of hospital admission and classified into three categories: (i) major adverse events, defined as in-hospital mortality, direct intensive care unit (ICU/CCU) admission, or emergent interventions [mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drug administration, or major invasive procedures (e.g. cardiac catheterization)]; (ii) general ward admission; and (iii) discharge.

Model development and training

The model was designed and trained in three phases.

Phase 1: designing a triage model using routine triage data (triage-based model)

This phase focused on creating a predictive model using routinely collected data from the ED triage process. Extracted from hospital health information systems, these data comprised initial patient information upon arrival, including age, gender, shift of admission, pregnancy status, mode of arrival (ambulatory, wheelchair, stretcher), means of referral (private vehicle, ambulance), hospital visit history within the past 24 h, chief complaint, level of consciousness (assessed via AVPU scale), documented food and drug allergies, medical history (coded according to ICD-11), systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, and SpO2.

The first step in this phase involved preprocessing the data. This included examining data quality, removing outliers, and completing any missing entries. Once the data were cleaned, modelling was carried out using various machine-learning algorithms: Decision Tree, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbours (K-NN), K-means clustering, Random Forest, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP).

Phase 2: designing a triage model based on ECG images (ECG-based model)

Initially, a model for analysing scanned ECG images was developed. Once the ECGs were scanned, multiple preprocessing steps were executed on the scanned images to facilitate signal extraction. The steps involved greyscale conversion, contrast enhancement, noise reduction, grid removal from the ECG paper, deskewing, contrast enhancement and denoising, and ultimately canny edge and signal line detection for improved extraction precision. Subsequent to these preparations, the signals were extracted from the images. The method used for this stage involved CNNs. This technique, a significant aspect of deep learning, is particularly suited for the analysis of multidimensional data such as images and has seen extensive application in medical image processing in recent years.45

The publicly accessible PTB-XL database was employed for model training. This reliable dataset comprises 21,799 12-lead clinical ECG recordings from 18 869 patients, with a gender distribution of 52% male and 48% female, and an age range of 0 to 95 years. Each ECG was recorded for 10 s and independently annotated by two cardiologists. Diagnostic labelling adhered to the Standard Communications Protocol for Computer-assisted electrocardiography (SCP-ECG), which includes 71 unique ECG interpretations categorized into three groups: rhythm (n = 12), form (n = 19), and diagnosis (n = 44). Four interpretations were prevalent in both the form and diagnosis categories. Data collection for this database took place between 1989 and 1996, followed by an open-access release in 2019. Moreover, the data can be processed with standard software.46

To develop and internally validate the CNN, PTB-XL recordings were randomly divided at the patient level into training (80%), validation (10%), and internal test (10%) subsets, ensuring that all ECGs from a given patient were assigned to a single subset to avoid data leakage.

Fifty ECGs were randomly selected from the available dataset that were not used for training the CNN model and interpreted by the designed model to assess its performance. Two cardiologists independently analysed the ECGs, and in instances of disagreement, a consensus was achieved through discussion of the discrepancies. The model results were subsequently compared with expert interpretations to evaluate the degree of agreement and the precision of the model’s performance.

To assess the concordance between our model interpretation and cardiologists’ opinions, Cohen’s kappa coefficients were calculated for 50 ECGs for each diagnostic label individually, with the average of these coefficients serving as a comprehensive index of agreement. At this point, Python was used for all calculations. It is important to acknowledge that certain labels in the reference dataset (PTB-XL) were absent in the samples collected for this study and were therefore excluded from the final calculation. The agreement coefficient between the model and the experts’ final interpretation of ECGs was 0.79, indicating substantial agreement according to Cohen's Kappa classification.

Next, the signals derived from the scanned images were analysed by the developed model using the PTB-XL database. In the development of the model, several machine-learning algorithms were applied, including Decision Tree, SVM, k-Nearest Neighbuors, K-means Clustering, Random Forest, and CNN, for ECG-based triage and prediction of clinical outcomes.

The models were evaluated using metrics including precision and F-score, resulting in the identification of the optimal model. The AUC was computed for the optimal model.

Phase 3: combining the ECG-based model and triage data (model fusion)

In the third phase, to enhance prediction precision and clinical comprehensiveness, the models developed in the preceding two phases (the triage data-based model and the ECG interpretation-based model) were integrated. The integration of the outputs from these two models resulted in a final feature vector that encapsulated the patient's information from both physiological and clinical viewpoints.

The models’ performance was assessed using standard criteria, specifically precision and F-score, and comparisons were made among them. Ultimately, the AUC was computed for the optimal model.

Data processing

Data were processed using the Python programming language, Spyder, and Google Colab. To evaluate model performance and reduce the risk of overfitting, the dataset was randomly divided at the patient level into training (80%), validation (10%), and testing (10%) subsets. Because the development cohort was intentionally constructed with approximately equal numbers of patients in each of the three outcome classes, this random split produced training, validation, and test sets with similar class proportions, without any additional rebalancing of the test set. The hybrid model was first trained using the training set, its performance was monitored and tuned with the validation set, and final evaluation was performed on the independent test set.

Models performance

The models’ performance was evaluated in each of the three phases using standard criteria, specifically precision and F-score. The best-performing model in each phase was identified, and subsequently, the AUC was calculated for these optimal models to enable comparison across different phases.

Comparison with ESI triage

A reliable metric was required to enable a comparative analysis between AI-driven e-triage (fusion model) and conventional ESI triage and to evaluate the precision of triage levels. Due to the lack of an objective gold standard for triage levels, direct validation of triage precision is inherently impossible; therefore, an outcome-based validation approach was implemented. The primary aim of triage is to prioritize patients according to the severity of their clinical condition.47 In alignment with previous research and expert consensus, a three-level triage classification was developed for both methodologies. Outcome 1, which includes mortality, intensive care unit admission, and emergency interventions, corresponds to ESI Triage Levels 1 and 2. Outcome 2, representing general ward admission and indicating a relatively stable condition requiring hospital evaluation and treatment, aligns with ESI Triage Level 3. Outcome 3, pertaining to discharge, is classified as equivalent to ESI Triage Levels 4 and 5.11,48

Finally, the precision of the best AI-based e-triage model was compared with conventional triage.

Feature importance analysis

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was employed to assess the importance of variables in predicting clinical outcomes in the optimized model. SHAP, which is based on Shapley game theory, improves model interpretability by attributing a contribution value to each feature’s impact on model prediction. The analysis integrated routine triage data with the output from a CNN model, which included ECG interpretations across three dimensions: rhythm, form, and diagnosis.

Models’ performance

ECG interpretation model performance

The CNN used for ECG interpretation in this study was pre-trained on the PTB-XL dataset. We did not perform a full re-benchmarking of our implementation on the PTB-XL test set; instead, we relied on the published benchmarking results for PTB-XL. Strodthoff et al. (2020) reported that modern CNN architectures (resnet-/inception-based) achieve high discriminative performance on PTB-XL, with macro-AUCs of approximately 0.96 for rhythm statements, 0.93 for diagnostic statements, and 0.89 for form-related statements.49 These results support the use of a PTB-XL–pretrained ECG model as a feature extractor in our triage framework.

In this phase, the MLP model exhibited the highest precision compared with the other algorithms assessed in this study. The model attained a precision of 89.42% and an F-score of 84.51% in the triage of patients and the prediction of clinical outcomes. Discussion

EDs, particularly cardiac emergencies, encounter issues such as overcrowding and delays in care delivery, resulting in considerable adverse clinical outcomes.4,5,7 The process of prioritizing patients, known as triage, is critical in managing these conditions. Current triage systems, including the ESI, primarily rely on human judgment and require enhancements due to their susceptibility to errors.18,19,50 AI has the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy through the analysis of triage data and patient ECGs.51,52 This study focused on the design and evaluation of an AI-based e-triage model that predicts disease severity and clinical outcomes of patients using ESI triage data and automated ECG interpretation, and it compares the results with the conventional ESI triage.

Statistical analysis results indicated that the model employing only triage data accurately predicted clinical outcomes with a precision of 89.42% and AUC values ranging from 0.815 to 0.858. The ECG-based model demonstrated a precision of 93.83% and AUC values ranging from 0.852 to 0.914. The integration of ECG interpretation with ESI triage data resulted in a precision of 97.33% and AUC values ranging from 0.881 to 0.938, representing a significant enhancement compared with each method alone in each phase.

Prior research has also developed several models for predicting clinical outcomes and triaging patients.

Levin et al. (2018) conducted a study to evaluate an e-triage system using machine-learning decision tree models. This system analysed routine ESI triage data to predict the likelihood of acute outcomes, thereby enhancing patient differentiation. The findings of this study indicated that the AUC for the developed model varied between 0.73 and 0.92. In comparison to ESI triage, this model demonstrated a significant enhancement in the identification of clinical outcomes and the differentiation of patients.11 The findings of the current study correspond with those observed in the model phase employing ESI triage data (Phase 1). However, the incorporation of ECG interpretation data into the ESI triage data (Phase 3) markedly enhanced model performance. Furthermore, alongside common outcomes, we incorporated patient discharge prediction into the model, thereby strengthening its practical application for clinical triage.

Raita et al. (2019) developed four machine-learning models—Lasso regression, random forest, gradient boosted decision tree, and deep neural network—to predict intensive care unit admission and hospitalization, using routine ESI triage data from the ED. All designed models outperformed ESI triage, with the deep neural network demonstrating the highest predictive capability for intensive care admission and hospitalization. Its AUC values were 0.86 for intensive care and 0.82 for hospitalization.48 However, the model developed by Raita et al. demonstrates lower efficacy compared with our integrated model in predicting both outcomes: intensive care admission and hospitalization. The difference between the model developed by Raita et al. and the integrated model in this study is primarily due to the incorporation of ECG interpretation data alongside ESI triage data in the current research. The ability to predict discharge in the developed models represents an additional advantage of the current study over the research conducted by Raita et al.

Kwon et al. (2018) used primary data from ED patients to develop deep learning models for a Deep-learning-based Triage and Acuity Score (DTAS). The methodology entailed the evaluation of three specific outcomes: in-hospital mortality, admission to the intensive care unit, and overall hospital admission. The DTAS models were compared with established systems and algorithms, including the Korean Triage and Acuity System, the Modified Early Warning Score, Random Forest (RF), and Logistic Regression. Using a much larger sample size (over one million patients), this model achieved an AUC value of 0.93.9 This shows their model’s superior performance than the current study model when only triage data (Phase 1) were used (0.815 to 0.858); this difference can be attributed mainly to the much larger sample size, the limitation of predicted outcomes to more severe and simpler cases, and the lack of more complex classes such as discharge in the model of Kwon et al. However, the results of this study suggest that even simpler models, such as MLP, can have acceptable performance in classifying disease severity and predicting clinical outcomes of cardiac patients in the ED if properly designed and effective features are selected, especially in resource-constrained settings. Notably, the performance of the hybrid model in the present study, which was designed by adding ECG interpretation to ESI triage data, could outperform the DTAS model designed by Kwon et al.

Jiang et al. (2021) evaluated four machine-learning models—multinomial logistic regression, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, RF, and gradient-boosted decision tree—using routine triage data for the triage of patients with suspected cardiovascular diseases. The eXtreme Gradient Boosting model exhibited superior performance, attaining an AUC value of 0.937.53 Jiang et al. used the nurse-assigned triage level as the outcome measure in their study. The current study employs objective clinical outcomes, such as mortality, hospitalization, and discharge, to evaluate patient acuity. Given the established high incidence of triage errors, especially in overcrowded ED environments,54–56 the reliability of nurse-assigned triage levels as a primary modelling criterion may be undermined.

This study identified the multilayer perceptron network (MLP) as the most effective model for triage data and the CNN for ECG signal data. Similarly, research by Raita et al. and Goto et al. has demonstrated the superior performance of deep learning models over traditional algorithms.48,57 The MLP architecture effectively identifies nonlinear relationships in structured triage data, whereas the CNN is more adept at processing ECG signals due to its capacity to extract local and hierarchical features from temporal data.

Our study found significant variables that influence the model’s performance in predicting clinical outcomes and its capacity to differentiate the severity of patients’ conditions. The findings indicated that ECG interpretation, heart rate, mode of admission, chief complaint, systolic blood pressure, and mode of referral were, respectively, the most significant contributors to the final model performance.

Notably, this study highlights the significance of ECG interpretation in enhancing outcome prediction and differentiation, a topic that has received limited attention in prior research and is evaluated here for the first time alongside routine triage data. Simultaneously, other significant variables in our model lined up with the results of prior research, including those conducted by Raita et al. (2018), Goto et al. (2019), and Jiang et al. (2021).48,57 These studies identify variables, including mode of entry and vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation), as predictive factors.

Lastly, the findings of this study indicate that the integration of routine triage data and ECG signals offers a reliable and comprehensive resource for enhancing clinical decision-making and optimizing the triage process in the ED. Due to their high precision and capacity to predict various clinical outcomes, these models have the potential to enhance or substitute current triage systems, thereby improving the efficiency and accuracy of decision-making in emergency contexts.

Limitations

This study presents promising results and an innovative approach; however, several important limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Limited volume of actual hospital data: Despite using secondary data (PTB-XL), the number of actual samples from Razi Hospital was limited, potentially affecting the model's performance on real-world data.

Limited reference standard for triage validation: Given the absence of an independent gold standard for assessing triage accuracy, the performance of both the models developed in this study and conventional triage was evaluated using patient outcomes. Outpatient Follow-up Limitation: Due to the retrospective nature of the study, follow-up for patients triaged at levels 4 and 5—mostly outpatients not admitted to the hospital—was limited. Only patients who returned to the same hospital were reviewed, while others may have sought care elsewhere, which was not captured in our data. This limitation may affect the accuracy of the model and ESI triage, so results for these levels should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

This study’s findings demonstrate that the integration of routine ESI triage data with ECG interpretation via AI algorithms can significantly increase the precision of predicting disease severity and triage in cardiac-suspected patients, relative to models based solely on routine ESI triage data or those based only on ECG interpretation. Furthermore, our proposed model demonstrated superior performance in identifying high-risk patients compared with the ESI triage system, which depends on subjective assessment, and achieved a more precise classification of clinical severity upon arrival at the ED.

The prominent role of automated ECG interpretation alongside routine ESI triage data highlights its potential for improved clinical decision-making, particularly in high-demand emergency environments where rapid and accurate assessments are essential. The integration of routine ESI triage data with ECG interpretation yields a model that serves as a valuable adjunct to conventional triage systems. This model aids in minimizing errors, optimizing resource allocation, enhancing decision-making processes, and improving service quality.

This study represents a significant advancement in the digital transformation of emergency services and serves as a foundational basis for the development of reliable smart tools in hospital triage.
