Mechanisms underlying retardation of aging by dietary energy restriction
Authors: Isao Shimokawa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractModerate restriction of dietary energy intake, referred to here as dietary restriction (DR), delays aging and extends lifespan in experimental animals compared with a diet of ad libitum feeding (AL) control animals. Basic knowledge of the mechanisms underlying the effects of DR could be applicable to extending the healthspan in humans. This review highlights the importance of forkhead box O (FoxO) transcription factors downstream of the growth hormone‐insulin‐like growth factor 1 signaling in the effects of DR. Our lifespan studies in mice with heterozygous Foxo1 or Foxo3 gene knockout indicated differential roles of FoxO1 and FoxO3 in the tumor‐inhibiting and life‐extending effects of DR. Subsequent studies suggested a critical role of FoxO3 in metabolic and mitochondrial bioenergetic adaptation to DR. Our studies also verified hypothalamic neuropeptide Y (Npy) as a vital neuropeptide showing pleiotropic and sexually dimorphic effects for extending the healthspan in the context of nutritional availability. Npy was necessary for DR to exert its effects in male and female mice; meanwhile, under AL conditions, the loss of Npy prevented obesity and insulin resistance only in female mice. Overnutrition disrupts FoxO‐ and Npy‐associated metabolic and mitochondrial bioenergetic adaptive processes, causing the acceleration of aging and related diseases.Keywords: aging, dietary restriction, forkhead box O transcription factor, longevity, neuropeptide Y

INTRODUCTION

As humans age, the body's functions deteriorate, leading to diseases such as cancers, cardiovascular diseases, and dementia, which ultimately result in death. Because the age‐related degradative changes in the body, called aging or senescence, are universal in animals, there must be a common biological mechanism inherent in the aging process. Consequently, understanding the primary aging mechanism can clarify how aging and diseases are delayed in humans and thus extend the healthspan.

Research on the biology of aging has been conducted from various perspectives, such as genomic instability, stem cell exhaustion, cellular senescence, chronic inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. 1 However, the mechanisms of aging are connected. For example, cellular senescence studies in vitro have revealed a link among genomic damage, cell cycle arrest, and cellular oncogenesis. 2 Those studies have extended to the presence of senescent cells in tissues and discovery of the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype, that is, activation of the innate immunity of surrounding cells through secretion of cytokines from senescent cells. The cellular level of low‐grade inflammation could be further amplified, leading to cellular dysfunction, damage, and death, resulting in tissue aging. Findings on the mechanism involved have culminated in the concept of “inflammaging.” 3 “Senolysis,” involving the experimental removal of senescent cells in tissues, is currently a topic of interest in research on therapeutic approaches to degenerative diseases in the elderly 4 because it can ameliorate aging‐related disorders.

Studies using longevity models, which began with dietary energy restriction (DR), 5 have identified longevity genes and the evolutionary biological signals that regulate aging and lifespan. 1 , 6 Studies on DR have extended to elucidate its effects on nonhuman primates and humans. Drugs or compounds that mimic DR's effects without restricting food intake have also been a focus in recent aging research. 7

In this review, the author describes how DR retards aging and related diseases by highlighting the forkhead box O (FoxO) transcription factors and neuropeptide Y (Npy).

LONGEVITY MODELS FOR AGING RESEARCH

Dietary energy restriction

Mice and rats in laboratories are usually kept in cages that allow them to drink and eat chow freely, called ad libitum feeding (AL). In contrast, restricting food intake by 30% to 40% of the average intake of AL groups with essential nutrients from or around sexual maturity, referred to as DR here, significantly extends the lifespan. 8 The incidence of age‐related diseases also decreases in DR animals. Since McCay et al. 5 reported DR's effects in rats, it has been used in aging research as a simple and reproducible model for longevity. Many experimental trials using a variety of dietary compositions have concluded that the restriction of dietary energy or calories, but not the restriction of single nutrients, is a primary factor for the retardation of aging. 9

The life‐extending effect of DR has been clarified in various experimental animals, including nematodes, flies, and rodents. A key issue in this context is whether DR can extend the human lifespan. To predict DR's possible effect on humans, two research groups in the United States have been conducting lifespan studies using non‐human primates, rhesus monkeys, since the mid‐1980s. 9 An interim report indicated that most DR‐related biomarkers in rodents changed in the same direction in DR monkeys. 10 Finally, a cohort of monkeys kept at the University of Wisconsin and the National Primate Research Center (UWNPR) showed an extension of lifespan upon 30% DR. 11 DR also inhibited the aging‐related reduction of gray matter volume in the monkeys' brains. 12 By contrast, a National Institute on Aging (NIA) group reported that DR did not prolong the lifespan in male and female monkeys; however, the incidence of cancers was lowered in the DR group. 13

Subsequent comparison of the experimental conditions between the two lifespan studies criticized the feeding regimens. 11 , 14 In the UWNPR study, the control group of monkeys became obese because they received a semisynthetic diet with high sucrose contents during the experiment. By contrast, the control monkeys in the NIA study were given a diet of more natural foods and their daily intakes were more regulated. As a result, the NIA's control group was not obese and thought to have been kept under partially restricted conditions. 15 As expected, the lifespan in the NIA control group was longer than that in the UWNPR study. Therefore, it should be emphasized that life expectancy could be significantly extended by DR of only a few percent and an absence of refined foods, when compared with that of monkeys with AL access to semi‐synthetic diets.

Two‐year‐long, well‐controlled DR studies in non‐obese, healthy people have also indicated that 10%–20% DR can be safely performed and expected to change DR biomarkers as in experimental animals and reduce risk factors for cardiovascular diseases. 16 , 17 Therefore, modest DR could be effective for extending the healthspan even in humans. However, it may not have a great effect on the average or maximum lifespan as in lower organisms.

Longevity genes and their relationship with dietary restriction's effects

Even under AL conditions, mutations in single genes have been reported to increase the lifespan of animals. The first report, by Johnson et al., was on a gene named age‐1 in long‐lived Caenorhabditis elegans. 18 It was later found that this gene encoded a phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K) component, essential for growth factor signaling such as insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1) and insulin. 19 In addition, Kenyon et al. reported that mutations in the daf‐2 gene, encoding insulin‐like receptor, increased the lifespan of C. elegans. 20 The daf‐2‐mutated conditions were also shown to necessitate the expression of the daf‐16 gene encoding mammalian FoxO transcription factors. In other words, in C. elegans, when insulin‐like signaling is attenuated, Daf‐16 is activated, its target genes are expressed, and lifespan is prolonged. To date, over 400 genes involved in an increase in the lifespan of C. elegans have been identified. 21

In mice, over 40 genes have been reported to extend the lifespan compared with that of wild‐type mice. 22 Many of these longevity genes are involved in sensing nutritional status and adapting metabolism and redox regulation. 23 Among these, mutations of genes that suppress growth hormone (GH)‐IGF‐1 signaling consistently extend mouse lifespan. 23

The findings above indicate that IGF‐1 signaling is crucial in regulating the aging process in animals. Because DR reduces the plasma concentrations of GH and IGF‐1 in rodents, 24 DR is predicted to retard aging and extend the lifespan by suppressing GH‐IGF‐1 signaling and activating FoxO transcription factors in mammals.

FOXO TRANSCRIPTION FACTORS

Isoform‐specific differential regulation of tumor and lifespan by FoxO1 and FoxO3 in dietary restriction condition

Mammalian orthologs of Daf‐16 are a family of FoxO transcription factors, including FoxO1, FoxO3, FoxO4, and FoxO6. 25 Mammalian FoxO transcription factors are phosphorylated by protein kinase B/thymoma viral proto‐oncogene downstream of growth factors such as insulin and IGF‐1 and translocated to the cytoplasm for degradation. 25 When insulin or IGF‐1 signaling is attenuated by fasting or DR, FoxO transcription factors are more likely to be activated in response to oxidative stress and regulate the expression of genes related to modulators of aging such as cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, apoptosis, autophagy or mitophagy, metabolism, and mitochondrial functions. 26 Deacetylation of FoxOs by sirtuins is also vital for activating FoxOs. 27 Therefore, we hypothesized that FoxOs are critically involved in DR's effects in mammals.

To test this, we performed lifespan studies using mice with heterozygous Foxo1 or Foxo3 gene knockout (+/–). 28 , 29 In multiple tissues of Foxo1 +/– or Foxo3 +/– mice, the respective protein or mRNA levels were reduced by up to 50% compared with those in wild‐type control mice. 28 , 29 The lifespan studies produced contrasting results. In Foxo1 +/– mice, 30% DR extended the lifespan to the same extent as in wild‐type control (Ctrl) mice. 28 However, autopsies of spontaneously dying mice revealed that the tumor‐inhibiting effect of DR was significantly attenuated in Foxo1 +/– mice compared with that in Ctrl mice. Meanwhile, in Foxo3 +/– mice, the lifespan‐extending effect of DR was abolished, but the tumor‐inhibiting effect was retained. 29 Under AL conditions, the mouse lifespan and frequency of tumors did not differ between Ctrl and Foxo1 +/– or Foxo3 +/– mouse groups. These findings suggest the FoxO‐isoform‐specific, differential regulation of tumors and aging under DR conditions (Figure 1). Our results contrasted with previously reported results indicating the dispensability of FoxO1, 3, and 4 in inhibiting tumors and regulating lifespan in mice. 30 In AL feeding conditions, only triple knockout of three Foxo genes significantly increased tumor incidence and shortened the lifespan in mice. The isoform‐specific roles of FoxOs in regulating tumors and lifespan could be apparent only under DR conditions.

Differential regulation of cancer and aging by dietary restriction via Nrf2, FoxO1, and FoxO3. Dietary restriction attenuates the inhibitory effects of insulin, IGF‐1, and other growth factors on FoxO1 and FoxO3 and promotes the activation of FoxOs in response to cellular stresses. AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and sirtuins also promote FoxO activity. The mechanism by which dietary restriction (DR) enhances the activation of Nrf2 in response to stresses remains to be elucidated. IGF, insulin‐like growth factor 1.

The isoform specificity for extending lifespan has also been reported in C. elegans, in which three or four splicing variants of Daf‐16 have been identified. Compared with the other variants, one variant, Daf‐16a 31 or Daf‐16d/f, 32 was shown to contribute more to lifespan extension through mutation of the daf‐2 gene in C. elegans. Genomic studies on human longevity have also indicated a strong relationship between FOXO3 gene polymorphisms and longevity in multiple cohorts. 33 , 34 FOXO1 gene polymorphism is associated with longevity only in the Han Chinese population. 34 Therefore, the FOXO3/Foxo3 gene could be a critical regulator of aging and lifespan in mammals.

Most subjects of genomic studies of human longevity were not under DR conditions. However, in a genome‐wide meta‐analysis of 21 studies of people of European ancestry, those with the FOXO3 longevity gene polymorphism had lower blood IGF‐1 levels, a trait of long‐lived animals. 35 However, the mechanism by which blood IGF‐1 is regulated by FOXO3 gene polymorphism remains unclear.

FoxO1 and Nrf2 mediate the tumor‐inhibiting effect of dietary restriction

The tumor‐inhibiting effect of DR was also diminished in mice with knockout of the nuclear factor, erythroid derived 2, like 2 (Nrf2) gene 36 ; however, the lifespan of Nrf2 −/− mice was extended by DR, as in Ctrl mice. Nrf2 is a well‐investigated transcription factor that triggers defense mechanisms against oxidative stress. 37 To evaluate the tumor‐inhibiting effect, Pearson et al. 36 used a two‐step skin carcinogenesis model using 7,12‐dimethylbenz[a]anthracene as an initiator and 12‐O‐tetradecanoyl phorbol‐13‐acetate as a promoter of skin tumors. Our analysis of the tumor‐inhibiting effect of DR was based on spontaneously occurring neoplasms, mostly malignant lymphoma, in a lifespan study in mice. 28 The necessity of the transcription factor for the inhibition of tumors by DR may depend on the tumor type. Therefore, we also performed the same experiment in Foxo1 +/– mice following the protocol of Pearson et al., with results comparable to those in Nrf2 –/– mice being obtained. 26 In this experiment, some Nrf2 target genes, such as heme oxygenasae (decycling) 1 (Hmox‐1), glutamate‐cysteine ligase, and catalytic subunit (Gclc) genes, were also downregulated in Foxo1 +/–‐DR mice. Therefore, a set of genes commonly regulated by Nrf2 and FoxO1 are required for tumor inhibition by DR.

In C. elegans, Daf‐16 and Skn‐1 are required for the life‐extending effect of DR. 38 In mammals, FoxO1 and Nrf2 are not critical regulators of the life‐extending effect of DR, although both transcription factors are needed for the inhibition of cancer by DR (Figure 1).

Metabolic and mitochondrial adaptation to dietary restriction

The effect of DR could be derived from metabolic and mitochondrial biogenetic or bioenergetic adaptation to long‐term, reduced dietary intake. DR mice finish a food allotment for one day approximately 6 h after feeding and are kept without food for 18 h until the next feeding. During the first 6 h after feeding, DR mice use carbohydrates as their primary energy source, while synthesizing fat and proteins. 39 After that, they gradually shift their energy sources from carbohydrates to fatty acids and amino acids. 39 , 40 , 41

Dietary restriction‐induced alterations of energy metabolism could affect mitochondrial respiration, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and redox regulation. In our study of male Wistar rats subjected to feeding every other day with 30% less food, hepatic mitochondrial ROS generation decreased after feeding (the first day), while it increased during fasting (the second day). 42 This implied that when glucose was the primary energy source, ROS levels were low; when fasting, fatty acid oxidation and amino acid catabolism supplied intermediate metabolites to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, respiratory chain, and gluconeogenesis, and ROS levels were as high as those in the AL group. 42 Correspondingly, the ratio of the oxidized form of glutathione (GSSG) to its reduced form (GSH) increased during fasting because of higher GSSG levels. Thus, we speculated that ROS levels and redox status in mitochondria in the DR group fluctuate during the feeding cycle under the DR regimen, even if feeding is performed every day. Excessive elevation of ROS causes cell death and tissue dysfunction, whereas periodic alterations of ROS within a modest range may enhance defense mechanisms against oxidative stress. This hypothesis is consistent with preconditioning, hormesis, or mitohormesis. 43

Regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis and bioenergetics by dietary restriction

Dietary restriction is known to increase mitochondrial biogenesis, the process through which cells increase their mitochondrial mass in response to metabolic or nutritional stress. 44 , 45 Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor, gamma, coactivator‐1 alpha (Pgc1α), an essential cofactor for mitochondrial biogenesis, respiration, and gluconeogenesis, is significantly upregulated by DR in multiple tissues. 46 , 47

Proteomic analysis of rat liver mitochondria showed that several mitochondrial proteins varied significantly with aging or DR. 48 We focused on cytochrome c oxidase, subunit 6B1 (Cox6b1), an assembly factor of respiratory chain complex IV (Complex IV). Cox6b1 protein levels increased by approximately 20% in DR rats compared with those in AL rats. 48 Cox6b1 is involved in forming Complex IV dimers. 49 , 50 Mutations in the COX6B1 gene have been reported in severe encephalomyopathy in human neonates. 51 , 52 These reports indicate that Cox6b1 is involved in the functional regulation of Complex IV.

Under the current understanding of respiratory chain complexes, it is indicated not only that these complexes are present individually in the mitochondrial inner membrane but also that Complexes I, III, and IV form supercomplexes (SCs). 53 Most SCs consist of one complex I, two complexes III, and one, two, or four complexes IV (I1III2IVn). The formation of these SCs could be essential for regulating respiratory chain activity, efficiency of electron transfer and ATP production, suppression of ROS production, stabilization of respiratory chain complexes, and suppression of protein aggregation in the mitochondrial inner membrane. 54

We confirmed that DR facilitates the formation of SCs in mitochondria isolated from 12‐month‐old mouse liver. 55 We also speculated that Cox6b1 is involved in SC formation in the respiratory chain, as shown in the crystal structure of bovine heart Complex IV. 49 , 50 In our experiment, overexpression of the Cox6b1 gene in a cultured cell system promoted the assembly of SCs. 55 The Cox6b1‐overexpressing cells showed increments of oxygen consumption, Complex IV activity, and ATP production. Simultaneously, ROS levels were increased, while the cells were resistant to oxidative stress. By contrast, knockdown of the Cox6b1 gene showed the opposite results. These results suggest that elevated Cox6b1 expression promotes SC formation, increases respiratory capacity, and enhances cellular resistance to oxidative stress under DR conditions. 55 A subsequent experiment demonstrated that, in Cox6b1‐overexpressing cells, Nrf2 translocated into the nucleus in response to oxidative stress induced by tert‐butylhydroquinone and increased the expression of target genes and proteins associated with antioxidative functions, such as Hmox‐1 and Gclc. Our studies suggest a link among SC formation, increased respiratory capacity, and stress tolerance.

Moderate ischemia in the heart reduces the size of subsequent myocardial infarction and promotes the recovery of cardiac functions (i.e., a hormetic response). In a mouse model of the hormesis of cardiac ischemia, Cox6b1 was upregulated with the promotion of SC formation. 56 Protective effects of Cox6b1 on the myocardium have also been reported in an in vitro ischemia and reperfusion model in which the Cox6b1 gene was overexpressed in rat myocardium; in this experiment, the formation of SCs was not investigated. 57 Moreover, in an in vitro ischemia/reperfusion model of hippocampal neurons, the overexpression of Coxo6b1 suppressed an increase of Ca2+ concentration in the cytoplasm and then inhibited apoptosis, resulting in enhanced neuronal survival. 58

These Cox6b1 overexpression models 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 and our in vivo evidence 55 suggest that DR promotes the assembly of SCs by Cox6b1‐mediated dimerization or tetramerization of Complex IV. SC formation elevates respiratory functions and may increase mitochondrial ROS. However, SC formation could enhance oxidative stress tolerance. These findings support the hypothesis that mitohormesis is an underlying mechanism of DR.

A potential role of FoxO3 in metabolic adaptation to dietary restriction and regulation of mitochondrial bioenergetics

The liver is key to metabolic adaptation to DR because it produces glucose and ketone bodies as energy sources for other tissues in the fasting period, while synthesizing glycogen and lipids in the feeding phase. In the fasting liver, fatty acids from adipose tissue and amino acids from muscle are mobilized to activate the anaplerotic processes, which facilitate the influx of intermediate metabolites (e.g., reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide [NADH], reduced form of flavin adenine dinucleotide [FADH2], and acetyl CoA) into the TCA cycle, respiratory chain, gluconeogenesis, and ketogenesis (Figure 2). 41 , 59 Amino acid catabolism should coincide with activation of the urea cycle for the detoxification of ammonia. By contrast, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and glycolysis in the liver are inactivated. In the fed phase, PPP produces the reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), which has the reducing power for the biosynthesis of fatty acids and cholesterol. The conversion of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) to NADPH in the PPP is coupled with conversion of GSSG to GSH (Figure 2). Thus, the PPP also plays a role in the defense mechanisms against oxidative stress. 59 By contrast, NADPH may activate NADPH oxidase and generate ROS. Therefore, the PPP should be properly regulated according to nutritional availability.

At the same time, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), purine metabolism, glycolysis, and fatty acid synthesis are inactivated in hepatocytes (gray rectangle). The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and respiratory chain should be regulated in a manner dependent on the energy demand in hepatocytes (blue rectangle). Anaplerotic processes, represented by amino acid catabolism and lipolysis, are also active to supply intermediate metabolites for the TCA cycle and β‐oxidation. The urea cycle is active to detoxify ammonia derived from amino acid catabolism (orange rectangles). Our metabolomic analysis suggests that FoxO3 inhibits the PPP, purine metabolism, and the TCA cycle in the fasting phase and activates the urea cycle. In Foxo3 +/–‐DR liver, aberrant activation of the PPP may affect nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and reduced form of glutathione (GSH) metabolism, and thus redox regulation (dotted blue rectangle), leading to acceleration of aging. To investigate the potential role of FoxO3 in metabolic adaptation to long‐term DR, we performed metabolomic analysis on liver tissues of middle‐aged mice in the DR fasting phase (Komatsu T, 2023, unpublished data: Text S1). The metabolome in Foxo3 +/–‐DR liver displayed a tendency for the elevation of metabolites in the PPP, purine metabolism, glycogenesis, TCA cycle, and glutathione metabolism, in comparison with those in Ctrl‐DR liver (Figure 2). By contrast, metabolites in arginine biosynthesis and arginine and proline metabolism, which are associated with the urea cycle, were decreased in Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice. The hepatic metabolome in Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice resembled that in Ctrl‐AL mice, indicating a critical role of FoxO3 in the metabolic adaptation of the liver to DR.

Although not specific to FoxO3, triple knockout of Foxo1, Foxo3, and Foxo4 genes in mice was reported to disrupt the inhibitory effect on lipogenesis under fasting conditions. 60 It was also indicated that the gene expression profile of fasted triple‐knockout mice was strikingly similar to that of Ctrl mice under fed conditions. It was also reported that deletion of the hepatic Foxo3 gene in mice elevates the expression of lipogenic enzymes in the liver. 61 By contrast, a recent study demonstrated that overexpression of Foxo3 in the liver aggravates hepatic steatosis by promoting the transcriptional activity of sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor 1. 62 Therefore, although the function of FoxO3 on hepatic lipogenesis is debatable and may depend on the experimental setting, our metabolomic analysis supports the inhibitory role of FoxO3 in the influx of metabolites into the PPP, part of the lipogenic pathway in the fasting phase of DR (Figure 2). Notably, the blood concentrations of glucose, free fatty acids, and cholesterol and the adipose tissue mass did not differ between Ctrl‐DR and Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice in middle age. Therefore, the deregulation of hepatic metabolism in Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice was overcome by compensatory mechanisms. However, the metabolic stress may affect the redox state in the liver (Figure 2, dotted blue line rectangle). Indeed, GSSG levels and the pool of total glutathione were increased in Foxo3 +/–‐DR compared with the levels in Ctrl‐DR mice, although the GSSG:GSH ratio was not significantly elevated (Figure S1). These findings suggest an increment of oxidative stress and GSH synthesis in Foxo3 +/–‐DR in middle age, although the redox balance has yet to be disrupted.

Mitochondrial bioenergetics regulated by dietary restriction depends on FoxO3

Metabolic deregulation could affect mitochondrial bioenergetics. Using liver tissues and mitochondria isolated from liver tissues of Ctrl and Foxo3 +/– mice, we investigated the role of FoxO3 in the regulation of mitochondrial bioenergetics by DR (Kim SC, 2023, unpublished data; Komatsu T, 2023, unpublished data). Our analyses revealed the following when compared with the findings in Ctrl‐DR mice: (1) mitochondrial SC formation in the liver was reduced in Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice; (2) tolerance to mitochondrially derived oxidative stress was diminished in Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice; and (3) a preventive effect of excessive elevation in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in the respiratory state 4o was minimized in Foxo3 +/–‐DR mice. Because the level of ROS steeply increases when the MMP exceeds a certain threshold, 63 the regulation of MMP is assumed to be important as an aging‐retardation mechanism of DR related to FoxO3 (Figure 3).

Hypothetical mechanisms by which dietary restriction (DR) inhibits the excessive elevation of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) via FoxO3. When ATP is elevated, cAMP‐dependent protein kinase A (PKA) phosphorylates subunit I of Complex IV. This allosteric ATP inhibition acts on the dimeric Complex IV (IV n , n = 2 or 4), part of the respiratory supercomplex (SC) but not on the Complex IV monomer. Subsequently, the elevation of MMP is inhibited. The elevation of MMP could also be lowered by proton leakage mediated by adenine nucleotide translocators (Ant) or uncoupler proteins (Ucp). The supercomplex formation and upregulation of Ucp4 (see the main text) by DR, which depend on FoxO3, reduce ROS and retard aging.

Subsequent analyses demonstrated that DR upregulated mRNA expression levels for mitochondrial uncouplers, such as uncoupling protein 4 (Ucp4), adenine nucleotide translocator (Ant1), and Ant2. These proteins in the mitochondrial inner membrane could promote proton leakage to moderate excessive elevation of MMT under DR conditions (Figure 3). Among these uncouplers, Ucp4‐mRNA levels were lower in Foxo3 +/–‐DR than in Ctrl‐DR mice. Ucp4, abundantly expressed in the brain, is upregulated by DR. 64 Ucp4 is also reported to protect neurons against oxidative stress by decreasing MMT. Because Daf‐16 regulates the Ucp4 promoter in C. elegans, 65 Ucp4 may be an essential protein for DR‐specific regulation of MMT via FoxO3 in mice. However, further studies will be needed to clarify the role of Ucp4 because the expression level of Ucp4‐mRNA is low in the liver compared with that in the nervous system. 66

Supercomplexes might be involved in the regulation of MMP. ATP produced in Complex V inhibits Complex IV allosterically to maintain a low MMT of isolated rat liver mitochondria. 63 , 67 This “relaxed state” is achieved by phosphorylation of the cytosolic domain of subunit I of Complex IV by cAMP‐dependent protein kinase A (PKA: Figure 3). 68 This mechanism is active in dimeric Complex IV but not in monomeric Complex IV. As described above, Complex IV is assembled into SCs with Complexes I and III. Complex IV in I1III2IV1 could also be dimerized at the dimeric binding site with subunit VIIa‐RP (SR‐related CTD‐associated factor 1, Scaf1). 69 Cox6b1 also participates in the dimerization of Complex IV. 70 Under stress conditions, an increase in cytosolic Ca2+ activates a Ca2+‐dependent protein phosphatase, which dephosphorylates Complex IV. 68 , 71 The dephosphorylated Complex IV becomes the monomeric form and escapes from the allosteric ATP inhibition, leading to high activity of Complex IV and elevation of MMT, that is, an “active state.” 68 , 71

The prevention of excessive elevation of MMP could be a central mechanism by which DR retards aging. Our studies suggested two potential mechanisms for regulating MMP by DR (Figure 3): (1) promotion of proton leakage via uncoupler proteins and (2) allosteric ATP inhibition of Complex IV by the assembly of SCs. However, the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Tissue‐specific role of FoxO3

FoxO3 in a specific group of cells or a tissue may play a vital role in the lifespan extension and retardation of whole‐body aging by DR. A series of experiments using mutants of C. elegans emphasized that the nervous system and the intestinal tract are critical for the extension of lifespan by DR or by attenuation of IGF‐1 signaling. 72 , 73 , 74 In C. elegans, the intestinal cells act as mammalian adipocytes for fat storage. 75 Therefore, in mammals, FoxO3 in the nervous system and the adipose tissue may be crucial for the regulation of aging and lifespan by DR.

Among the conditional Foxo3‐knockout mice that we produced using the cre‐loxP system, fatty acid binding protein 4 (Fabp4)‐cre mice may give us an excellent model to analyze a potential role for FoxO3 in the nervous system and adipose tissue for the extension of lifespan by DR. The conditional knockout mice were produced by crossing Foxo3 flox/flox and Fabp4‐cre mice (Text S1). Fabp4‐cre mice have been used to delete a target gene in white and brown adipose tissues because the Fabp4 gene is expressed exclusively in adipocytes of adult mice. 76 However, Fabp4‐cre is also expressed in the central nervous system, glandular tissue, axial bone, and testis during late gestation. 77 Thus, the Foxo3 gene could be deleted to some extent in those tissues. We therefore analyzed the Foxo3‐mRNA expression level in multiple tissues of the conditional Foxo3‐knockdown (cFoxo3‐KD) mice. The Foxo3‐mRNA expression levels were significantly reduced to one‐third in white adipose tissue, hypothalamus, and lung but not in kidney, liver, and spleen, in cFoxo3‐KD mice (Komatsu T, 2023, unpublished data; Figure S2). As such, cFoxo3‐KD mice were considered an appropriate mouse model to test the role of FoxO3 in the nervous system and adipose tissue in the context of the DR mechanism. The lifespan study demonstrated that DR extended the lifespan in cFoxo3‐KD mice, as in Ctrl mice (Figure 4; Komatsu T, 2023, unpublished data). Our findings implied that FoxO3 in the hypothalamus and adipose tissue is not critical to the life‐extending effect of DR.

When limited to the AL group, lifespan was reduced by 27.3% at the 25th percentile survival point in cFoxo3‐KD mice compared with that in Ctrl mice (Figure 4). Notably, brain weight was increased by 20% in the cFoxo3‐KD‐AL group compared with that in the Ctrl‐AL group in middle age; the body weight and organ weights, including white adipose tissue, did not differ significantly between the two groups. The finding of increased brain weight accords with previous reports on Foxo3 –/– mice 78 and conditional brain‐specific Foxo1/3/4 triple‐knockout mice. 79 Those mice were kept under AL conditions. It was emphasized that FoxO3 in the brain prevents the excess proliferation and premature differentiation of neural stem cells (NSCs) and thus premature depletion of the NSC pool. By contrast, preserving NSCs counteracts the deteriorative processes in the aging brain. It was also reported that Foxo3‐deleted NSCs in cell culture showed dysregulated glutamine and glucose metabolism and exacerbated oxidative stress, causing a reduction of the neurogenic potential of NSCs. 80 Brain FoxO3 may be linked to aging and lifespan when limited to AL conditions.

Adult neurogenesis persists even at a lower rate in the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the hippocampus, the ventricular and subventricular zone of the lateral ventricle, along with the third ventricle and mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH). 81 , 82 NSCs in the SGZ could be vital for maintaining cognitive brain functions in old age; recent experimental studies have suggested that NSCs in the MBH regulate aging and lifespan in mice. 83 , 84 , 85

Our lifespan study in mice with heterozygous and homozygous whole‐body Foxo3 gene knockout (Foxo3 +/– and Foxo3 –/–) indicated that Foxo3 –/–‐AL mice showed 10% and 14% shorter lifespan at the 25th percentile survival point compared with Foxo3 +/–‐AL and Ctrl‐AL mice, respectively. 29 The brain weight at death in the longevity study also showed a 22% increase in Foxo3 –/–‐AL mice compared with that in Ctrl‐AL mice. Therefore, FoxO3 may prevent the excess proliferation and development of NSCs in the MBH, leading to a decrease in the premature death rate in mice with AL feeding.

NEUROPEPTIDE Y

The neuroendocrine hypothesis of the effects of dietary restriction

The effects of DR have been attributed to mechanisms of adaptation to starvation in animals. 86 In the wild, dietary energy sources are seasonal or can suddenly become scarce. To survive periods of food scarcity, physiological mechanisms are needed to redirect consumed energy to essential functions for survival and inhibit growth and reproduction.

The hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARH) is around the third ventricle and above the median eminence (Figure 5). Because the median eminence lacks the blood–brain barrier, groups of neurons in the arcuate nucleus can sense changes in blood hormones and metabolites, which reflect the nutritional intake and energy storage of the body and facilitate neuroendocrine adaptation to conserve stored energy. 88

Longevity network modulated by dietary restriction (DR). Reductions of food intake and fat as an energy store affect the concentrations of hormones and metabolites in circulating blood (orange rectangle). These changes are sensed by the two groups of neurons in the arcuate nucleus of the mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH, green rectangle), leading to neuroendocrine adaptive processes such as inhibition of the neurons expressing growth hormone‐releasing hormone (Ghrh) and gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH). The changes in blood hormones, including decreases in insulin and IGF‐1, promote activation of FoxO1 and FoxO3 in peripheral cells (yellow rectangle). Sirtuins (Sirt) also activate FoxOs by deacetylation. Nrf2 is also active in response to cellular stresses. FoxOs and Nrf2 regulate target genes (see Figure 1) and attenuate cellular‐level inflammation through inhibition of nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) as a canonical signal of “inflammaging.” Attenuating the mechanistic target of rapamycin (Mtor) is also a mechanism of DR (see reviews elsewhere). 87 DR elevates mitochondrial biogenesis (brown rectangle). The assembly of supercomplexes of the respiratory complexes and uncoupling mechanism could be critical for the effect of DR because these inhibit excessive elevation of mitochondrial membrane potential and reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) (MMP, see Figure 3). These regulatory mechanisms of cellular stress responses and mitochondrial bioenergetics lead to the concept of “mitohormesis.” In the hypothalamic neurons, FoxOs and Mtor regulate the expression of Npy. Npy acts not only in the MBH but also as a competitor for the β3‐adrenergic signal to inhibit excessive lipolysis in adipose tissues under DR conditions (gray rectangle). This trait is essential for DR‐induced longevity. The DR‐associated components highlighted by the rectangle are mutually interactive, leading to retardation of whole‐body aging. Agrp, agouti‐related protein; Cart, cocaine‐amphetamine‐related transcripts; Crh, corticotropin releasing hormone; Pomc, proopiomelanocortin; Trh, thyrotropin releasing hormone.

We focused on Npy neurons in the ARH because the activation of Npy signaling is reported to be involved in suppressing GH secretion under fasting conditions 89 and enhances neuronal stress tolerance. 90 Overexpression of the Npy gene in rats improves heart functions and tends to extend lifespan. 91 Npy is also transcriptionally regulated by FoxOs. 92 The suppression of GH signaling, stress tolerance, and lifespan extension are characteristics of DR. We confirmed an elevation in the Npy‐mRNA levels in the ARH of DR rats. 93 Therefore, we predicted that the effects of DR, including the GH‐IGF‐1 signal, would be compromised in Npy gene‐deleted mice, leading to attenuation of the effects of DR.

Pleiotropic and sexually dimorphic effects of Npy on the aging processes, depending on nutritional status

The lifespan study of Npy‐gene‐knockout (Npy –/–) mice demonstrated that deletion of the Npy gene minimized the life‐extending and tumor‐suppressing effects of DR. 40 The oxidative stress resistance was also attenuated. Gene expression analysis in the liver suggested that Npy is required for the anti‐tumor, detoxification, stress response, and anti‐inflammatory effects of DR. 40 Previous studies highlighted reduced blood insulin and IGF‐1 and increased adiponectin and corticosterone levels as mechanisms of DR. 23 However, the blood hormone concentrations in Npy –/– mice were changed by DR in the same direction as in the wild‐type Ctrl mice. These results suggested that Npy regulates aging and tumors by a mechanism different from the initially predicted neuroendocrine regulation by Npy.

Subsequent studies demonstrated that Npy antagonized β3‐adrenergic signaling, which activates hormone‐sensitive lipase in white adipose tissue. 94 This mechanism inhibits excessive lipolysis and thermogenesis under DR conditions. Notably, approximately 40% of male Npy –/–‐DR mice died within 9 months after the initiation of 30% DR at 12 weeks of age 40 ; even male survivors in the Npy –/–‐DR group at 72 weeks of age showed only a slight increase in lifespan compared with Npy –/–‐AL mice but not as much as in Ctrl‐DR mice. The premature death in male mice, not seen in female Npy −/− mice, was prevented by the administration of acipimox, an inhibitor of lipolysis. 94 Our findings accord with those reported using 41 recombinant inbred strains of mice. 95 In that study, the lifespan did not increase or was even shortened in approximately half of the strains in response to 40% DR. Strains with less fat loss in response to the 40% DR displayed an increase in lifespan. This study also highlights the necessity of the mechanism of preventing excess loss of fat for lifespan extension by DR (Figure 5).

By contrast, in an outbred MF1 strain, female mice fed AL with a high metabolic rate lived longer than those with a low one. 96 Mitochondria from skeletal muscle in the high‐metabolic‐rate mice showed increased proton leakage across the inner mitochondrial membrane via ANT, compared with mitochondria from the low‐metabolic‐rate mice. The data support the uncoupling for survival hypothesis, implying that modest mitochondrial uncoupling lowers ROS generation, retards aging, and extends the lifespan. 97

In a lifespan study, female Npy –/–‐AL mice showed lower body weights than female Ctrl‐AL mice, although food intake was approximately 10% greater in Npy –/– mice. 40 A subsequent study indicated that Npy deficiency protected female mice from aging‐related obesity and proinflammatory gene expression in the white adipose tissue, which caused insulin resistance. 98 Npy deficiency induced browning in the white adipose tissue only in female mice, corroborated by the increased expression of UCP1 and elevated body temperature preferentially in female mice. 99 In menopausal mice, Npy in the hypothalamus may inhibit the gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH)‐luteinizing hormone (LH)/follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) axis and thus reduce blood estradiol. Our study suggested that Npy deficiency recovered the aging‐related reduction of GnRH‐LH signaling, increased blood estradiol, induced browning in the WAT, and finally prevented obesity in old female mice. 99

Our studies in female Npy –/– mice suggested the beneficial effect of the loss of Npy on aging‐related obesity and insulin resistance caused by mitochondrial uncoupling, although the lifespan was not significantly extended in Npy –/–‐AL mice compared with that in Ctrl‐AL mice, unlike in female MF1 mice. 96

CONCLUSION

Biological studies of aging, particularly using the DR experimental paradigm, have demonstrated the GH‐IGF‐1‐FoxO axis as the evolutionarily conserved mechanism that regulates aging and lifespan in animals (Figure 5). FoxOs regulate multiple physiological functions, including cellular metabolism, stress response, and low‐grade inflammation. Many of these regulatory mechanisms are derived from processes by which animals have adapted to overcome starvation. In modern societies, humans and pets ironically suffer from overnutrition‐induced diseases caused by the mechanisms for longevity. Therefore, it is critically important to obtain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of aging and to develop tools to optimize nutritional intake to extend the healthspan in humans.
