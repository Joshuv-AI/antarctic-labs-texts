Er:YAG laser irradiation versus conventional surface treatments: a pull-out bond strength study on glass fiber posts
Authors: Burçin Arıcan, Furkan Evrendilek, Ayşe Tuba Özalp Koca, Yemliha Senemli, Taha Özyürek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis in vitro study aimed to investigate the influence of Er:YAG laser irradiation on the retention of glass fiber posts by comparing its effect on pull-out bond strength with conventional chemical and mechanical surface treatment methods. One hundred extracted human maxillary central incisors were endodontically treated and prepared to receive glass fiber posts. The specimens were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 20) according to the post surface treatment protocol: no treatment (control), aluminum oxide sandblasting, Er:YAG laser irradiation, hydrofluoric acid etching, and silane application. All posts were cemented using a dual-cure resin cement. Pull-out bond strength was measured using a universal testing machine, and the obtained data were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey post hoc tests (α = 0.05). Pairwise comparisons revealed that both hydrofluoric acid etching and sandblasting resulted in significantly higher bond strength values compared with the control, laser, and silane groups (p < 0.001 for all relevant comparisons). Additionally, the laser group demonstrated significantly lower bond strength values than both the acid and sandblasting groups (p < 0.001), while no significant difference was observed between the laser and silane groups (p > 0.05). Within the limitations of this in vitro study, Er:YAG laser irradiation did not enhance the pull-out bond strength of glass fiber posts. In contrast, hydrofluoric acid etching and aluminum oxide sandblasting demonstrated superior bonding performance and resulted in significantly higher retention values. These findings suggest that, under the tested parameters, Er:YAG laser irradiation did not demonstrate a significant improvement in the pull-out bond strength of glass fiber posts. Introduction

Preservation of sound dentin is a key determinant of long-term success in endodontically treated teeth. When extensive coronal structure is lost, post-and-core systems are frequently required to ensure adequate retention of the final restoration and to restore functional integrity [1]. In such clinical situations, intraradicular posts contribute primarily to retention rather than reinforcement of the remaining tooth structure. Various post systems have been introduced, including metallic, zirconia-based, composite, and fiber-reinforced posts composed of glass, quartz, carbon, or silica fibers [2].

Among these options, glass fiber posts have gained widespread acceptance due to their elastic modulus being closer to that of dentin, which promotes more favorable stress distribution along the root and reduces the risk of catastrophic root fractures [3]. Structurally, glass fiber posts consist of glass fibers embedded within an epoxy or methacrylate-based resin matrix, a composition that provides adequate tensile strength, flexibility, and resistance to corrosion and biodegradation [4]. Despite these advantages, clinical failures related to debonding remain a major concern.

The retention of glass fiber posts depends on the integrity of adhesion at two interfaces: between the resin cement and intraradicular dentin, and between the cement and the post surface [5]. Debonding has been reported to occur predominantly at the post–cement interface, largely due to the limited chemical reactivity of the epoxy resin matrix covering the post surface [6, 7]. Because this polymer matrix does not readily interact with resin cement monomers, various surface conditioning strategies have been proposed to improve micromechanical retention and chemical bonding [1, 2, 8–11].

Surface treatment methods such as silane application, acid etching, hydrogen peroxide conditioning, and airborne-particle abrasion with aluminum oxide have been investigated with varying outcomes [1, 2, 8–11]. These approaches aim to modify the superficial resin layer, expose glass fibers, or increase surface roughness to enhance adhesion. In parallel, the use of laser technology for surface modification has attracted increasing interest in restorative dentistry.

Er:YAG laser irradiation has been suggested as a method capable of altering surface topography through controlled ablation while simultaneously cleaning the post surface [8, 12]. Operating at a wavelength of 2940 nm, Er:YAG laser energy is highly absorbed by water and organic components, allowing surface modification without excessive thermal damage when appropriate parameters are used. However, the literature presents conflicting evidence regarding its effectiveness. While some studies have reported improved bond strength following Er:YAG laser application [12], others have demonstrated surface degradation and compromised adhesion depending on irradiation settings [8].

To the best of our knowledge, studies directly comparing Er:YAG laser irradiation with commonly used chemical and mechanical conditioning methods within a standardized pull-out testing model remain limited. Although numerous studies have evaluated individual surface treatment protocols, direct comparison of Er:YAG laser irradiation with commonly used chemical and mechanical methods—such as aluminum oxide sandblasting, hydrofluoric acid etching, and silane application—remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the influence of Er:YAG laser irradiation with these conventional surface treatments on the pull-out bond strength of glass fiber posts. In addition, a pull-out testing model was selected because it allows direct assessment of post retention under axial dislodgement forces and may better reflect the clinical mode of fiber post debonding than push-out testing. The null hypothesis was that no significant difference would be observed among the tested surface conditioning methods.

Method

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on data from a previously published study [5], an effect size of 1.252193 was selected with a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 90%. According to this calculation, a minimum of 20 specimens per group was required.

Specimen selection

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (decision no: 2024/447; date: 27.06.2024). A total of 100 human maxillary central incisors extracted for orthodontic or periodontal reasons were included. Residual soft and hard tissue remnants were removed using hand scalers, and the teeth were stored in 0.1% thymol solution at room temperature until experimentation

Radiographic evaluation was performed in both buccolingual and mesiodistal directions to assess canal anatomy. Only teeth with a single straight canal exhibiting a curvature of less than 10°, and presenting a buccolingual dimension at least twice that of the mesiodistal dimension, were included [13]. Teeth with multiple canals, root curvature exceeding 10°, previous endodontic treatment, or apical diameters greater than 0.20 mm were excluded. All specimens were further inspected under a dental operating microscope (Zumax Medical Co. Ltd., Suzhou, China) at 20× magnification to detect cracks, fractures, caries, or resorptive defects; specimens exhibiting any of these features were discarded.

Root canal preparation and obturation

The crowns were removed under water cooling to standardize root length at 13 mm (Fig. 1A) Apical patency was verified using a size #10 K-file (Dentsply/Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), and the working length was established 1 mm short of the apical foramen.

Sequential preparation of the experimental samples. A The crowns were sectioned, and standardized root lengths were established. B Root canals were instrumented using the T-endo MUST file system. C Obturation of the root canals was performed employing the single cone technique. D Post spaces were prepared using dedicated drills. E Surface treatments were applied to the post surfaces to enhance bonding. F Glass fiber posts (GFPs) were cemented into the prepared spaces. G A pull-out test was conducted to determine the force required for specimen debonding

Canal instrumentation was carried out using the T-Endo Must file system (Dentac, Istanbul, Turkey) powered by an AI Black motor (Guilin Woodpecker, Guangxi, China) in reciprocating motion (CCW 160°, CW 40°) at 400 rpm and 4.5 Ncm torque (Fig. 1B). Sequential preparation was performed using TG, M25, and M40 files with a gentle pecking motion until an apical diameter of 0.40 mm was achieved.

Irrigation was performed with 2 mL of 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) after every three pecking movements using a 30-gauge irrigation needle (Irriflex, Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland) positioned 2 mm short of the working length. Final irrigation consisted of 5 mL 5.25% NaOCl, followed by 5 mL 17% EDTA, an additional 5 mL 5.25% NaOCl, and a final rinse with 5 mL distilled water.

Root canals were obturated using a single-cone technique with 40/0.04 gutta-percha cones (Dentac, Istanbul, Turkey) and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA) (Fig. 1C). The access cavities were temporarily sealed with Cavit (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA), and specimens were stored at 37 °C under 100% humidity for two weeks to allow complete sealer setting. The root canal preparations and obturations were performed by an experienced endodontist (B.A).

Post space preparation and cementation

After the storage period, temporary restorations were removed, and coronal gutta-percha was eliminated using #2 and #3 Gates-Glidden drills (Itena, France). Post spaces were prepared with cylindroconical drills of increasing diameters (1.00 mm, 1.20 mm, 1.30 mm, and 1.40 mm; Itena, France), leaving a 5-mm apical seal intact (Fig. 1D) Glass fiber posts of corresponding diameters (Itena, France) were selected and verified for passive fit within the canal. Before cementation, prepared root canals were irrigated with distilled water and dried using absorbent paper points to a moist surface.

Surface treatment protocols

The Specimens were randomly assigned (https://www.randomizer.org) to five experimental groups (n = 20) according to the surface treatment applied to the fiber posts using a computer-generated randomization sequence (Fig. 1E):

Group 1 (Control): No surface modification was performed.

Group 1 (Control): No surface modification was performed.

Group 2 (Aluminum oxide sandblasting): Posts were subjected to airborne-particle abrasion using 50 μm aluminum oxide particles for 20 s at a pressure of 2.5 bar from a distance of 10 mm (Aquacare, Medivance Instruments Ltd., London, UK), followed by air drying.

Group 2 (Aluminum oxide sandblasting): Posts were subjected to airborne-particle abrasion using 50 μm aluminum oxide particles for 20 s at a pressure of 2.5 bar from a distance of 10 mm (Aquacare, Medivance Instruments Ltd., London, UK), followed by air drying.

Group 3 (Er:YAG laser): Surface irradiation was performed using an Er:YAG laser system (Fotona, Ljubljana, Slovenia) operating at 150 mJ, 10 Hz, and 1.5 W with a wavelength of 2940 nm. A 400-µm optical tip was positioned at a 45° angle and 1 mm from the post surface for 60 s under continuous water cooling.

Group 3 (Er:YAG laser): Surface irradiation was performed using an Er:YAG laser system (Fotona, Ljubljana, Slovenia) operating at 150 mJ, 10 Hz, and 1.5 W with a wavelength of 2940 nm. A 400-µm optical tip was positioned at a 45° angle and 1 mm from the post surface for 60 s under continuous water cooling.

Group 4 (Hydrofluoric acid): Posts were etched with 9% hydrofluoric acid (Ultradent Porcelain Etch; Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) for 60 s, rinsed with water for 60 s, and air dried.

Group 4 (Hydrofluoric acid): Posts were etched with 9% hydrofluoric acid (Ultradent Porcelain Etch; Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) for 60 s, rinsed with water for 60 s, and air dried.

Group 5 (Silane): A silane coupling agent (Ultradent Silane; Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) was applied evenly to the post surface for 60 s using a brush and gently air dried for 5 s.

Group 5 (Silane): A silane coupling agent (Ultradent Silane; Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA) was applied evenly to the post surface for 60 s using a brush and gently air dried for 5 s.

Post cementation procedure

Post spaces were etched with 37% phosphoric acid (DentoEtch, Itena, France) for 15 s, rinsed, and gently air dried. A bonding agent (Iperbond Ultra, Itena, Paris, France) was applied for 20 s, air thinned for 5 s, and light cured using an LED curing unit (Valo X, Ultradent Products, South Jordan, UT, USA).

Dual-cure resin cement (Dentocore, Itena, France) was introduced into the canal, and the treated fiber posts (Dentoclic, Itena, France) were seated. Light polymerization was performed from all directions for 40 s. Cemented specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h prior to testing (Fig. 1F). Post surface treatment and cementation protocols were done by a single experienced operator (A.T.O.K).

Pull-out bond strength test

A circumferential notch was created on the external root surface using a diamond bur. The apical portion of each specimen was embedded in acrylic resin blocks and secured in the fixed component of a universal testing machine (Mod Dental, Ankara, Turkey), while the coronal end of the fiber post was embedded in acrylic resin and attached to the movable component. A pull-out load was applied at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min until debonding occurred. The maximum force required for post dislodgement was recorded in Newton (N) (Fig. 1G). All the pull-out test was performed by one single experienced operator who was blind the experimental groups (F.E).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test prior to ANOVA. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey post hoc comparisons was performed using SPSS software (version 21.0; IBM-SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

One-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant difference among the experimental groups (F = 26.13, p < 0.001). Levene’s test confirmed homogeneity of variance among the groups (p = 0.068). A graphical representation of the data distribution is shown in Fig. 2. Hydrofluoric acid etching and aluminum oxide sandblasting yielded the highest bond strength values and did not differ significantly from each other (p > 0.05). In contrast, the Er:YAG laser, control, and silane groups showed significantly lower values and formed a statistically similar subset (p > 0.05).

Pairwise comparisons revealed that both hydrofluoric acid etching and sandblasting resulted in significantly higher bond strength values compared with the control, laser, and silane groups (p < 0.001 for all relevant comparisons). Additionally, the laser group demonstrated significantly lower bond strength values than both the acid and sandblasting groups (p < 0.001), while no significant difference was observed between the laser and silane groups (p > 0.05).

The distribution of the data, as illustrated in Fig. 2, further supports these findings. The acid and sandblasting groups showed higher median values and wider variability, whereas the control, laser, and silane groups exhibited lower and relatively overlapping distributions. Grouping based on post hoc analysis (indicated by different letters) confirmed that the acid and sandblasting groups formed a statistically distinct subset with superior bond strength values.

Discussion

Surface modification strategies aim to enhance the interaction between fiber posts and resin cements by promoting micromechanical interlocking and chemical adhesion [8]. In the present study, the influence of different surface conditioning protocols on the pull-out bond strength of glass fiber posts was evaluated. The findings demonstrated that hydrofluoric acid etching and aluminum oxide sandblasting significantly improved post retention, whereas Er:YAG laser irradiation and silane application did not yield superior bonding outcomes when compared with untreated posts. Accordingly, the null hypothesis of no overall difference among the tested surface treatment protocols was rejected.

Airborne-particle abrasion with aluminum oxide resulted in increased pull-out bond strength values, which can be attributed to surface roughening and enlargement of the bonding area. This mechanical alteration may facilitate resin cement penetration and contribute to improved interfacial retention [14]. Previous scanning electron microscopy evaluations have shown that aluminum oxide abrasion removes the superficial resin layer of fiber posts and exposes the underlying glass fibers, thereby promoting micromechanical and chemical bonding [1, 9]. Consistent with the present findings, Arslan et al. reported improved pull-out bond strength following aluminum oxide sandblasting [2], and Prado et al. observed similar enhancement in push-out bond strength using comparable surface conditioning protocols [15, 16].

Hydrofluoric acid etching also produced significantly higher retention values. This outcome may be associated with partial modification of the resin matrix surrounding the glass fibers, which could increase the potential for micromechanical interlocking with resin cement [17]. Several studies have reported increased bond strength following hydrofluoric acid application to fiber post surfaces [1, 5, 6, 17]. Nevertheless, contradictory findings have also been documented [8, 18]. Schmage et al. reported reduced retention after hydrofluoric acid treatment, which was attributed to surface smoothing observed in scanning electron microscopy analyses [18]. Akın et al. reported that there was no statistically significant difference between the bond strength of the fiber post treated HF and the fiber post without any surface treatment [19]. Variations in acid concentration and application time may explain these inconsistencies. While lower concentrations or shorter exposure durations may be insufficient to produce a retentive surface, prolonged etching with higher concentrations has been shown to induce more pronounced surface alterations [17, 20]. In the present study, a 9% hydrofluoric acid solution applied for 60 s was selected based on these protocols reported to enhance bonding effectiveness.

The influence of silane application on fiber post retention remains controversial. Although silane coupling agents have been reported to improve bonding by chemically linking inorganic glass fibers to resin matrices [11], their effectiveness depends on prior exposure of the glass fibers. Without mechanical or chemical pretreatment, the epoxy resin matrix remains largely intact, limiting silane interaction with the fiber surface [21]. In the current study, silane application alone did not significantly increase pull-out bond strength, which is consistent with findings reported by Choi et al. [22] and Jana et al. [5], who emphasized the necessity of combining silane with surface roughening techniques to achieve measurable improvements in adhesion.

Laser-assisted surface modification has been proposed as an alternative conditioning method due to its ability to alter surface topography while avoiding chemical agents. Er:YAG laser irradiation, operating at a wavelength of 2940 nm, has been investigated for fiber post conditioning with conflicting outcomes reported in the literature [8, 12]. While certain studies have demonstrated improved bond strength following Er:YAG laser application [12], others have reported surface damage and compromised adhesion depending on irradiation parameters [8]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that laser treatment does not consistently enhance fiber post bond strength and may adversely affect surface integrity under specific conditions [23]. The results of the present study align with this conclusion, as Er:YAG laser irradiation did not improve pull-out bond strength when compared with untreated posts.

The absence of a positive effect following Er:YAG laser irradiation may be explained by the interaction between laser parameters and the structural composition of glass fiber posts [24]. Previous studies have shown that the effect of Er:YAG laser irradiation on fiber post bonding is highly dependent on irradiation settings, while the polymeric resin matrix and embedded glass fibers may respond differently to laser-induced ablation [24, 25]. Excessive or non-optimized irradiation may alter the superficial resin matrix and fiber architecture, which could adversely affect the bonding interface rather than creating a uniformly retentive surface for resin cement infiltration. Accordingly, although Er:YAG laser irradiation has theoretical potential for surface modification, its effectiveness appears to be parameter-sensitive and material-dependent, requiring further optimization before predictable clinical use can be suggested.

Discrepancies among studies may be attributed to differences in application context and laser parameters, including pulse duration, energy output, and irradiation time, as well as variations in fiber post composition [2, 26–28]. Longer exposure durations or inappropriate pulse settings may induce microstructural damage to the fiber post surface, counteracting potential benefits of surface roughening [23, 29]. For example, Er:YAG laser–activated irrigation with self-etching adhesive systems has been shown to significantly enhance push-out bond strength compared with conventional irrigation methods [30]. Furthermore, Elalfy and Elbasty reported favorable outcomes for Er:YAG laser treatment in terms of push-out bond strength; however, these results were obtained under specific experimental conditions, including laser use in combination with silane and a limited grouping design, which may account for the observed differences compared with the present findings [31]. Ayrancı et al. also reported that neither the preparation of the fiber post space nor surface conditioning with Er:YAG laser significantly influences bond strength, whereas the type of sealer has a determinative effect [32]. It should be kept in mind that these three studies’ methodology is based in push-out tests. In contrast, broader meta-analytic data suggest that Erbium laser pretreatments do not consistently increase bond strength across studies, highlighting the importance of standardizing protocols and optimizing irradiation settings [22]. In the present study, the selected Er:YAG laser parameters were identical to those reported by Arslan et al., who similarly observed no improvement in pull-out bond strength following laser irradiation [2].

Er:YAG lasers have been utilized at different stages of fiber post treatment, including post space preparation [32], canal irrigation [30], and post surface conditioning [25, 31]. The integration of these approaches may exert a synergistic influence on the bonding interface. In this context, Deng et al. demonstrated that combined irradiation of both the root canal space and the post surface significantly enhanced the push-out bond strength of fiber posts compared to the use of each modality alone [33]. Therefore, future studies evaluating combined surface treatment protocols using pull-out testing may help clarify whether similar synergistic effects can be achieved under conditions that more closely resemble clinical post retention.

Various testing methods have been employed to assess fiber post retention, including push-out, microtensile, and pull-out tests [4, 5, 23]. The pull-out test was selected in the present study because it applies an axial force that closely resembles clinical debonding conditions and minimizes specimen manipulation during preparation [34–36]. This method allows for efficient evaluation of multiple samples without altering the adhesive interface.

A major strength of the present study is the use of a standardized experimental model that closely simulates clinical conditions for fiber post debonding. Axial dislodgement forces were applied using the pull-out test, which reflects the predominant direction of functional stresses acting on adhesively cemented fiber posts in vivo. In addition, strict specimen selection criteria, standardized canal preparation protocols, and uniform post cementation procedures were implemented to minimize anatomical and procedural variability. The study design was further strengthened by adequate sample size determination based on a priori power analysis, supporting the reliability and reproducibility of the observed outcomes.

The present study was designed to evaluate the independent effects of commonly used surface treatment protocols on the retention of glass fiber posts under standardized experimental conditions. By isolating each treatment modality, the study aimed to provide a clearer understanding of their individual contributions to bond strength. Although combined surface treatment strategies (e.g., laser irradiation in conjunction with silane application) may offer additional insights, the current approach allows for a more controlled comparison and reduces potential confounding interactions between variables. Therefore, the findings contribute to the existing literature by providing a direct and systematic comparison of conventional and laser-based surface conditioning methods using a pull-out testing model.

Despite the standardized methodology, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the absence of thermocycling or mechanical aging procedures limits the ability to extrapolate the findings to long-term clinical performance, as only immediate bond strength was evaluated. Aging protocols may significantly influence the durability of the adhesive interface and therefore the long-term clinical performance of fiber post restorations. Second, surface morphology was not assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which restricts the interpretation of micromechanical changes induced by the different surface treatments. Third, failure mode analysis was not performed, preventing further insight into the nature of the bonding interface and the mechanisms of debonding. In addition, only a single type of glass fiber post and resin cement system was tested, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Future studies incorporating aging simulations, SEM evaluation, and failure mode analysis are warranted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the bonding behavior of fiber posts. In addition, only individual surface treatment protocols were evaluated. Combined approaches, such as laser irradiation followed by silanization or other multimodal conditioning strategies, may produce different bonding outcomes and could provide additional clinical insight. Future studies incorporating SEM and surface characterization techniques may provide further insight into the mechanisms responsible for the observed differences in bond strength.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, Er:YAG laser irradiation did not demonstrate a significant improvement in the pull-out bond strength of glass fiber posts. Conventional chemical and mechanical surface treatment methods, particularly hydrofluoric acid etching and aluminum oxide sandblasting, showed more favorable bonding performance under the tested conditions. However, further studies incorporating different laser parameters, combined treatment protocols, and long-term aging simulations are required before definitive clinical recommendations can be established.
