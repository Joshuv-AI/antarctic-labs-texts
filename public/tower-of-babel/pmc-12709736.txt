Preliminary study for the development of enhanced recovery after surgery program for free flap reconstruction in older population with advanced oral cancer: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Fumitaka Obayashi, Koichi Koizumi, Nanako Ito, Mirai Higaki, Atsuko Hamada, Sachiko Yamasaki, Yumio Fujioka, Shogo Nagamatsu, Isao Koshima, Souichi Yanamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) program is a combined perioperative bundle designed to reduce perioperative stress. However, whether these programs are safe and effective for older patients who have undergone free-flap reconstruction for locally advanced oral cancers remains unclear. This study retrospectively evaluated the risk factors associated with delayed postoperative recovery and prolonged hospital length of stay (LOS) in older patients with locally advanced oral cancer who underwent resection and free-flap reconstruction from an ERAS perspective.MethodsSixty-one patients who underwent resection and free-flap reconstruction for locally advanced oral cancer were included. LOS, complications, nutritional intake, and mobilization were evaluated.ResultsThe older group exhibited a longer LOS, delayed oral feeding, and more aspiration pneumonia than the non-older group. Analysis of older adults only revealed delayed oral feeding, not chronological age, as a risk factor for prolonged LOS.ConclusionsOlder patients were safely managed using ERAS; however, dysphagia may have interfered with the enhanced recovery.

Background

The number of elderly patients with oral cancer in Japan is increasing. High-quality medical care aimed at early recovery and return to their daily lives is required for systemically fragile patients with several underlying diseases or complex backgrounds without social support. Appropriate physical, mental, and social support must be provided to achieve this goal, while ensuring treatment safety. In 2000, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), an enhanced perioperative recovery program [1, 2], was proposed for early postoperative recovery. Since then, several approaches have been adopted to improve perioperative safety, decrease complications, shorten the length of stay (LOS) at the hospital, and reduce medical costs. In 2017, an ERAS protocol for cancer-free head and neck flap reconstruction was reported [3]. However, the literature on ERAS protocols for the head and neck remains limited, and few studies have focused on patients with oral cancer.

Older patients likely experience a decline in the physical and cognitive function due to long-term hospitalization; therefore, early recovery and discharge from the hospital are desirable. However, the readmission rate has reportedly increased with the adoption of ERAS [4]. Thus, whether the ERAS program should be uniformly applied to older adults remains questionable. In this study, we retrospectively reviewed the perioperative management of 61 patients with locally advanced oral cancer who underwent resection and reconstruction and evaluated the risk factors associated with delayed postoperative recovery and prolonged hospital stay in elderly patients from an ERAS perspective. We also discussed the adaptation of resection and reconstruction of locally advanced oral cancer in older adults and measures for enhanced recovery. In general, positive outcomes of cancer treatment include survival and cure rates. However, the positive outcomes expected of older adults, who have a lower life expectancy than younger patients, expectantly vary from those of younger patients. Particularly, the elderly want to be provided with food and assistance with mobility without inconvenience during their remaining lifespan. We focused on functional outcomes, such as oral eating and mobilization, in addition to the general outcomes of early recovery in older patients, including safety, complications, and hospital LOS.

The patients were divided into two groups based on the definition of older populations by the World Health Organization (WHO): those aged ≥ 65 years and older and those < 65 years.

Surgery and perioperative management

Preoperative chemotherapy was administered only in cases where the period until surgery was prolonged. Oral cancer resection and reconstructive surgery using a free flap were performed on the same day. The flap collection and vascular anastomosis were performed under the supervision of an experienced plastic surgeon. Tracheostomies were performed based on the need of the case. Patients underwent closed drainage of the wound and were admitted to the intensive care unit for one to several days. In cases of unilateral neck dissection (ND), two to three closed drains were placed on one side of the neck, and in cases of bilateral ND, three to four closed drains were placed on both sides of the neck. The day after surgery was the target date for transferring patients from the intensive care unit to the general ward. The perioperative management protocols are listed in Table 1. Gaseous anesthetics were primarily used for general anesthesia. Perioperative antimicrobials cefmetazole 1gor ampicillin/sulbactam (ABPC/SBT)1.5 g were administered intermittently every 8 h for 48 h postoperatively. In cases of aspiration pneumonia or wound infection, ABPC/SBT 3 g was administered every 8 h as the treatment dose unless the patient had renal insufficiency or was intolerant to antibiotics. Enteral nutrition was initiated on the day after surgery, and urinary catheter removal and mobilization were targeted for 2 days after surgery. Pain management was designed for multimodal analgesia with regular acetaminophen administration, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or Cox2-selective NSAIDs were administered as required. Data collection and variable

Data were collected through a retrospective review of medical records. A modified 17-item perioperative ERAS protocol recommended by Dort et al. [3] was used to evaluate the short-term goals of early recovery, including age, sex, performance status (PS), body mass index (BMI), clinical T-category, preoperative chemotherapy status, medical history, regular oral medication, comorbidity index, G8 screening, duration of intensive care unit stay, hospital LOS, oral intake status (a state in which the necessary energy is largely satisfied by oral intake alone), readmission rate, and complications. LOS was defined as the number of postoperative hospitalization days or the number of days from surgery to the start of postoperative radiation therapy. If the LOS was extended, the end date of the LOS was defined as the start date of the subsequent treatment, radiation therapy, after the patient's overall condition stabilized. PS scores were calculated using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group PS. Wound infection, aspiration pneumonia, and postoperative delirium, which commonly occur in older populations, were investigated as potential complications. Polypharmacy does not merely indicate the use of many types of drugs; in this study, polypharmacy was defined as the use of five or more drugs, which is the most commonly used definition. Overall survival was calculated from the date of surgery.

The criteria for discharge were as follows: the patient must be able to receive the necessary amount of nutrition, there must be no evidence of infection, there must be no wound problems or systemic abnormalities, pain control must be effective, the patient must be able to move independently before surgery, and the attending physician must give his/her permission.

Statistical analysis

All data were subjected to Welch T test, Fisher exact probability test, χ square test and logistic simple regression analysis for univariate analysis with the older or non-older groups as the target variable. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used for correlation analysis between chronological age and LOS. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to calculate the cut-off value for LOS delay.

Multivariable analysis was conducted to investigate the association between oral intake in the elderly and prolonged hospital stays. Variables showing p < 0.05 in univariable analysis and CCI, T-Category, G8, Pneumonia suggested to be associated with prolonged LOS for the older group were incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression model to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests were used for the overall survival analysis. All statistical analysis was performed at a 5% α risk of 95% confidence interval (CI) by JMP Ver18. In a few cases, such as when oral intake was difficult until discharge from the hospital, missing values were found, and it was determined that they should not be substituted. Outliers were eliminated using the interquartile deviation method.

Here, as multivariable analysis, we conducted logistic regression analysis to clarify the influence of potential confounding factors on the relationship between prolonged hospitalization duration and oral intake status in the elderly. Potential confounding factors selected from Tables 2, 3 and 4 that could affect LOS prolongation included age, CCI, G8, pneumonia, and T-category. The logistic regression model was statistically significant: χ2 = 4.52, p = 0.0012. Difficulty with oral intake three weeks postoperatively increased the risk of LOS prolongation by 14-fold (adjusted OR;14.3, 95% CI [1.73, 362]). Age, CCI, G8, presence of pneumonia, and T category were not associated with LOS prolongation.

Finally, we investigated the relationship between the length of hospitalization and overall survival rate (Fig. 2). The 3-year survival rate for long-term hospitalization cases was 66,7% (SE = 1.85%, 95% CI [0.44,0.81]), while the three-year survival rate for the short-term hospitalization group was 97.1% (SE = 0.11, 95% CI [72.1,97.9]). A log-rank test was performed between these two groups, and the results indicated that longer hospitalization duration was associated with poorer prognosis (χ2 = 4.25, p = 0.039). Discussion

There is no clear medical or biological definition for older adults; however, many healthcare systems, such as the WHO and other countries, regard those aged ≥ 65 as older adults. In recent years, the aging process has been slow and individual differences are great, limiting the determination of a uniform treatment based on chronological age. The NCCN guidelines recommend a geriatric assessment (GA) of five items: physical function, cognitive function, social support, mental status, and nutritional status [5]; however, the introduction of this method into clinical practice remains challenging because it requires a great deal of time to assess all items. Therefore, a simple and accurate disease-specific assessment is warranted. We are often faced with difficulties in understanding the medical condition of older patients because of their cognitive decline and the fact that their family members who live alone or accompany them for care are not fully aware of their medical condition, which requires adequate interviewing and patient education. However, reports on postoperative complications in older patients with squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck are lacking. Some reports have demonstrated a correlation between age and complications [6], whereas others have reported no particular difference between older and younger groups [7]. Mitchell et al. reported that patients with head and neck cancer aged > 80 years who underwent free flap reconstruction had a longer hospital LOS and higher postoperative medical complications, but no difference in surgical complications [8]. In this study, no mortality was noted, only one case of flap complication, and no effect of chronological age, similar to the findings of previous reports [9–12]. Age did not affect the readmission or reoperation rates. With the exception of one case, all patients were discharged after being deemed capable of living independently at home, which was considered to be a factor in the overall prolongation of LOS for older patients. The current management system did not seem to cause major problems from the viewpoint of safety in treating older patients. Surgical site infection rates were 51% and 45% in the older and non-older groups, respectively; however, these were not different from the data reported in previous studies [13]. Delirium, and postoperative cognitive dysfunction caused by invasive surgery, sudden environmental changes, and pain are common problems in the elderly. In our study, patients at high risk of delirium were identified through intervention by the palliative care team. Lenbornexants have also been used preoperatively to prevent delirium in the presence of insomnia. Opioids, NSAIDs, and H2 blockers may also increase the risk of delirium, and regular doses of acetaminophen should be administered in sufficient quantities. No significant difference was observed in the older group with regard to postoperative delirium. However, a significant difference was observed between the older and non-older groups with respect to pneumonia, which is similar to the findings of a previous report [14].

A correlation was observed between hospital LOS and chronological age in all patients. This differs significantly from previous studies [7]. However, chronological age was not a risk factor for prolonged hospital LOS in patients aged > 65 years. Two weeks after surgery, a significant difference in oral feeding was observed between the non-older and older groups. This study demonstrated that deterioration of swallowing function was a significant factor contributing to prolonged hospitalization. Because older patients are more likely to exhibit residual postoperative swelling [15] and swallowing dysfunction due to decreased pharyngeal sensation [16], preoperative swallowing screening may be an important GA criterion for older patients with oral cancer. It may also be important to provide swallowing rehabilitation at an early stage to restore function [17]. Furthermore, older adults demonstrated a longer period between the start of oral feeding and discharge, which was presumably attributed to the long time required to establish a postdischarge life, such as building a family support system and waiting for an eligibility assessment for nursing care. Social workers must cooperate to provide a smooth discharge support. The G8 screening tool included items related to physical function, medication, nutrition, and cognitive mood [18]. In that report, the cutoff value for fitting free flap reconstruction for head and neck cancer was ≤ 15. However, this screening system is presumably inadequate in terms of cognitive function, comorbidities, social skills, and geriatric syndrome. In this study, G8 screening was performed in patients aged > 65 years old. Oral feeding and weight loss exhibited large ratios in the G8 score for patients with oral cancer, which seemed to depend on pain control and food forms that were easy to consume. Therefore, adequate dietary education and pain management are essential factors [19]. Therefore, it may be necessary to develop specific GA standards for oral cancer treatment. In our study, the CCI was higher in the older group than in the younger group; hence, it may be used as a GA. However, the overall survival rates did not vary significantly between the age groups. Parsemain et al. [7] reported that unless the comorbidity index was high, it may not be significantly affected. Although we did not investigate cognitive aspects, the frequency of mild dementia in the elderly has been reported to be more than one-quarter [20]. Screening for dementia using the Mini-Cog Test and Mini-Mental State Examination may be necessary for older patients.

One of the major positive aspects of introducing the ERAS protocol is that it is possible to establish common goals not only among primary care physicians, but also among all healthcare workers and patients, thereby unifying the overall objectives. This is particularly true in the elderly population. Patient engagement in treatment is essential for early recovery. The study population included many healthy patients with low PS scores and access to standard medical care, which may have introduced selection bias in the interpretation of the results. In addition, the interpretation of LOS was somewhat ambiguous and may have been affected by delays in postoperative radiation therapy and reoperation. Furthermore, the sample size was small and the study was a retrospective pilot study, which limits the generalizability of the results. Therefore, based on the information obtained from this study, prospective trials for early recovery in elderly patients with oral cancer and the development of outcomes in elderly patients are warranted.

Conclusion

The number of elderly patients with oral cancer is expected to increase. Although clear physical differences exist in the postoperative recovery of older and non-older patients, the individual differences among older patients are large and cannot be delineated based on chronological age. This study confirmed the safety of surgical treatment in older patients; however, oral feeding, especially in older patients, was associated with hospital LOS. It is necessary to consider measures to enhance the oral functions involved in eating and swallowing and support postoperative recovery.
