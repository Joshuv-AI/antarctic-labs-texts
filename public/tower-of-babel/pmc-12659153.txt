A machine-learning-derived online screening tool for depressive symptoms in patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (CCVD): a cross-sectional study with temporal validation from CHARLS
Authors: Chun-Juan Zhang, Min-Xia Liu, Mei-Zhen Wu, Xi-Cheng Zhou, Xiao-Dong Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDepressive symptoms are significant and deleterious complication of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (CCVD), profoundly impairing patients’ quality of life. This study aimed to develop and validate an online screening model to estimate the risk of depressive symptoms among patients with CCVD.MethodsThis study utilized data from the 2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including 4,086 patients with CCVD. A total of 31 behavioral, health-related, psychological, and sociodemographic indicators were evaluated. Feature selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, Random Forest (RF), and the Boruta algorithm. Six machine learning models, Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree, RF, and XGBoost, were developed and compared. The best-performing model was used to construct a clinically interpretable nomogram, which was deployed as a real-time, web-based risk screening tool via a Shiny application. Temporal validation was conducted using CHARLS 2015 data.ResultsAmong the 4,086 CCVD patients included, 1,990 (48.70%) exhibited depressive symptoms. Feature selection identified 13 key variables. The Logistic Regression model demonstrated superior performance among the tested algorithms, with an AUC of 0.767, and showed moderate discriminative ability (C-index = 0.776, 95% CI: 0.761–0.790) and good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, P = 0.51). The model was implemented into a freely accessible online calculator for real-time risk assessment.ConclusionUsing machine learning approaches, this study developed and validated a screening model with moderate discriminative ability for estimating the risk of depressive symptoms in CCVD patients. The online Shiny-based calculator serves as a practical screening tool for clinicians, supporting early identification and intervention in high-risk populations. Further prospective validation is warranted to assess its real-world utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05294-3.Keywords: Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, Depressive symptoms, Machine learning, Screening model, Shiny web

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (CCVD), including coronary heart disease, heart failure, and stroke, represent a leading cause of global mortality and constitute a major public health challenge due to their high prevalence and mortality rates. According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, CCVD caused about 18 million deaths globally in 2023, which was 32% of total mortality [1]. In China, CCVD has emerged as the primary cause of death among both urban and rural residents. In 2023, there were 320 major cardiovascular events per 100,000 people, and the death rate was more than 150 per 100,000 person-years [2]. As the population ages faster, this problem is expected to grow.

Compounding this challenge is the increasingly prominent issue of comorbid depressive symptoms among CCVD patients. Studies show that about 20% to 40% of people with CCVD have depressive symptoms, which was 2 to 3 times higher than in the general population [3]. Overall, about 20.8% of these patients have depressive symptoms [4].Critically, depressive symptoms significantly increases the risk of all-cause mortality (Hazard Ratio [HR] 1.43; 95% CI, 1.27–1.60) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.44; 95% CI, 1.27–1.63) [5, 6].The underlying bidirectional relationship between CCVD and depressive symptoms involves complex mechanisms, including inflammation in the body, problems with hormones and nerves, and unhealthy behaviors like not quitting smoking [7]. Even though this has a big impact on patient health, there are still not enough tools to detect depressive symptoms early in CCVD patients in daily clinics. This is especially true in basic healthcare settings with limited resources. Therefore, there is a strong need for easy and accurate ways to screen these patients to help improve their health.

Machine learning (ML) is emerging artificial intelligence technology, which offers powerful support for risk stratification by building screening models from multidimensional data, like basic personal information, health habits, and life quality scores. ML algorithms excel at identifying non-linear relationships and complex variable interactions, demonstrating high accuracy and cost-effectiveness advantages in depressive symptoms risk assessment [8]. In recent years, methods that combine multiple algorithms, such as decision trees, random forests (RF), and XGBoost, have been used to screen for depressive symptoms in different groups. These approaches not only reduce misdiagnosis rates but also facilitate the integration of information from electronic health records and biomarker data [9]. Furthermore, online screening models deployed via web applications (e.g., those built using the Shiny framework) provide immediate and accessible clinical decision support, making them particularly well-suited for large-scale screening programs and telehealth scenarios [10]. Furthermore, a model’s interpretability is very important for its use in clinical practice. Recent studies on explainable AI show that understanding how a model works is as important as its accuracy. This is especially true for mental health screening. Doctors need to understand the reasons behind a risk score to trust the tool and make good decisions. A review of explainable AI [11] in health care found that interpretable models are used more often in clinics than complex “black-box” models. Clear models help doctors explain risks to patients and focus on factors that can be changed, like sleep or pain. This evidence supports our tool is not only accurate but also easy to understand and useful in real clinical settings.

It is very important to assess depressive symptoms risk in middle-aged and elderly Chinese people with CCVD. However, existing studies often rely on small-scale survey data and rarely use online tools to help apply the results in clinical practice. Based on this background, the present study used large past data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Advanced feature selection techniques, including LASSO regression, Boruta algorithm and RF, were employed to identify key covariates such as pain severity, functional impairment, and cognitive function. Multiple machine learning algorithms were integrated and optimized to enhance screening performance. Ultimately, a clinically intuitive visual nomogram was developed, and an online Shiny-based web calculator was deployed to enable real-time assessment of depressive symptoms risk in patients with CCVD, providing clinicians with a practical tool to support patient care.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study utilized data from the 2020 wave of the CHARLS, encompassing a nationally representative sample of 19,395 respondents. CHARLS employed a multi-stage probability sampling design to ensure representativeness. The inclusion criteria for CCVD patients were: (1) Age ≥ 45 years; (2) Self-reported physician diagnosis of heart disease or stroke in the 2020 survey wave; and (3) Availability of complete responses for the depressive symptoms assessment scale. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Missing information on CCVD status; (2) Missing depressive symptoms scale information; (3) Presence of implausible outliers. Implausible outliers were defined a priori based on physiological feasibility, including sleep duration ≤ 1 h or ≥ 15 h per day; and (4) Missing data on any other covariates included in the analysis. CCVD was defined according to the GBD study criteria, encompassing coronary heart disease, heart failure, and stroke. After applying these criteria, a final sample of 4,086 middle-aged and older adult CCVD patients was included (Fig. 1). Data from the CHARLS 2015 wave served as an independent temporal validation cohort. To ensure sample independence, strict deduplication was performed using CHARLS unique identifiers, such that participants in the validation cohort were not included in the development dataset. The study protocol received approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

The depressive symptoms assessment used the Chinese 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10). This shorter version is based on the original scale by Radloff. Previous studies have shown it is reliable and valid for middle-aged and older Chinese groups [12], so it was suitable for this dataset. Each CESD-10 item is rated using a 4-point frequency scale: 0 for "rarely or none of the time" (less than 1 day), 1 for "some or a little of the time" (1–2 days), 2 for "occasionally or a moderate amount of time" (3–4 days), and 3 for "most or all of the time" (5–7 days). Items 5 ("I felt hopeful about the future") and 8 ("I was happy") were reverse-scored. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The analysis only included participants who provided complete responses to all 10 CESD-10 items. Participants with any missing answers were excluded to ensure consistent and reliable assessment of depressive symptoms across the study sample. Following established standards from earlier research, a score of 10 or higher indicated the presence of clinically significant depressive symptoms [13].

Covariates

Sociodemographic and health behavior data came from standard questions [14]. This included age, sex, area of residence, current smoking status, and alcohol use. Height and weight were measured with portable height meters and digital scales. Body mass index was calculated from these measurements. Education level was grouped into: no formal education, primary school, junior/middle or high school, and college or above. Residence area was classified as rural or urban. Self-rated health and life satisfaction were each measured with a single question. Answers were grouped as "Good", "Fair", or "Poor". The presence of chronic pain was based on self-reported symptoms lasting three months or more in any body part. This included areas like the head, back, legs, or stomach and so on.

Functional status was measured using scales for basic activities (ADL) and more complex activities (IADL). The ADL scale covered six basic tasks: bathing, dressing, moving in/out of bed, using the toilet, controlling urine/feces, and eating [15]. The IADL scale covered five tasks: shopping, cooking, doing housework, managing money, and taking medicine [16]. For each task, participants reported their level of difficulty: no difficulty, some difficulty but still able to do it, have difficulty and need help, or unable to do it. Each task was then divided into two groups: "No difficulty" or "Any difficulty". A total functional limitation score was created by adding the scores from all ADL and IADL tasks. This total score was then put into three groups for analysis: 0 (No limitations), 1–2 (Mild limitations), and 3 or more (Severe limitations) [17].

Cognitive function was assessed in two areas [18]: Episodic memory and executive function. Memory was tested by reading a list of ten words. Participants recalled the words right away and again after four minutes. The memory score was the average number of words remembered correctly from both tests. Executive function were tested with tasks about time orientation, subtracting numbers, and copying a shape. Each correct answer scored one point. The total score was the sum of all correct answers, with a higher score meaning better thinking skills.

Statistical analysis and model development

To identify key variables for the depressive symptoms risk model, we employed three robust feature selection techniques: LASSO regression, the Boruta algorithm (a wrapper method built around Random Forests), and Random Forest (RF). A variable was kept only if all three methods selected it. It focuses on the strongest predictors. LASSO regression handles correlated variables well. Specifically, LASSO regression handles multicollinearity, Random Forest evaluates variable importance, and the Boruta algorithm provides robust filtering against random noise. This multi-method approach increases confidence in the final variable set.

Six machine learning algorithms were utilized to develop screening models: Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and RF. Model performance was rigorously evaluated and compared based on discrimination ability, clinical utility, and overall accuracy. The optimal algorithm selected through comprehensive evaluation was used to construct a clinically interpretable nomogram for risk visualization. To facilitate practical application, a user-friendly web-based screening tool was developed and deployed using the Shiny framework in RStudio.

Model validation was conducted as follows: (1) Internal validation: tenfold cross-validation on the 2020 development set. (2) Temporal validation: First, the 2015 wave provided a cross-sectional sample of patients with CCVD (N = 1311, after deduplication from the 2020 dataset) to validate the model's discriminative ability in an independent sample from a different time period. Second, to validate the model's performance for future depressive symptoms, a longitudinal validation cohort from participants who had CCVD but without depressive symptoms (CESD-10 < 10) at baseline in 2015 was constructed (N = 467). This cohort addresses the clinically relevant question of predicting which patients with existing CCVD will go on to develop depressive symptoms.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The following R packages were used: rms (v6.4.0) for regression modeling and nomograms, Resource Selection (v0.3–5) for the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, rmda (v1.6) for decision curve analysis (DCA), ggplot2 (v3.4.4) for visualization, pROC (v1.18.0) for ROC analysis, SHAPforxgboost (v0.1.3) for model interpretation (XGBoost), and e1071 (v1.7–13) for SVM implementation.

Feature selection using machine learning

Thirteen key variables were ultimately selected for model development through the combined application of Boruta, RF, and LASSO regression. The key features were retained only if it was independently selected by all three methods (Fig. 2). These variables included: Age, Gender, Pain, Arthritis, Digestive disease, fall down, retire, sleep duration, cognition, education, functional disability, life satisfaction, and self-perceived health status.

Multi-model analysis and performance comparison

Six machine learning models were used to screen for depressive symptoms in CCVD patients. The performance of these models were illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4, utilizing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, decision curve analysis (DCA) and confusion matrices. The LR model performed best in both the training set and the validation set. In the training set, the LR model had an AUC of 0.741, an accuracy of 0.699, a sensitivity of 0.673, a precision of 0.723, and an F1 score of 0.683. The LR model also had the smallest Brier score of 0.196 (Table S3). This shows it had the best model fit. The DCA results showed the LR model had the highest net benefit for most probability thresholds. The model was also tested on new data from the 2015–2020 CHARLS longitudinal cohort. The results for predicting new-onset depressive symptoms are in Figure S1 and Table S4. The LR model again performed best in this cohort study, with an AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.631–0.848) and a Brier score of 0.184.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis

Ten statistically significant risk factors identified in this regression were subsequently incorporated into a clinically interpretable nomogram (Fig. 5) for precise estimation of individual depressive symptoms risk. The intercept of the model was 3.7956, AIC was 4667.410. The overall model was statistically significant (Likelihood Ratio Test, P < 0.05). It demonstrated moderate discriminative ability (Harrell's C-index = 0.776, 95% CI: 0.761–0.790, P < 0.05). What’s more, a subgroup analysis stratified by gender was conducted. The results showed the model worked similarly well for men (AUC = 0.781) and for women (AUC = 0.759). This suggests the model is reliable for both genders. To facilitate immediate clinical application, an online web calculator based on this LR model was developed and deployed, enabling real-time depressive symptoms risk assessment for CCVD patients: https://zhangchunjuan.shinyapps.io/CVD_depression_model/.

Assessment of feature importance

Figure 6 shows a SHAP summary plot. This plot helps understand which factors are most important. Functional disability, self-rated health, sleep time, and cognitive function were the most important factors. Better life satisfaction and self-rated health were linked to a lower depressive symptoms risk. In contrast, having pain, being female, having functional disability, having digestive disease, and having arthritis were linked to a higher depressive symptoms risk. Discussion

This study developed and validated an online screening model for depressive symptoms in CCVD patients. The study used three methods to select important features: LASSO regression, the Boruta algorithm, and RF. This process found 13 key variables. These included functional disability, self-rated health, sleep duration and so on. Six ML models were tested. The LR model performed best, with an AUC of 0.767, and used to create an easy-to-understand nomogram. The final model was deployed as an accessible web-based calculator (https://zhangchunjuan.shinyapps.io/CVD_depression_model/), providing a practical tool for point-of-care risk assessment in clinical settings.

The LR model was considered a moderate to good level of performance, which was similar to, or slightly better than other depressive symptoms risk models made for patients with heart disease or other chronic diseases. This level of performance is representative of the AUC range (typically 0.71–0.83) commonly reported in similar studies [19], such as those focusing on cardiovascular conditions [20, 21], cancer [22], and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [23]. Notably, the model includes a wide range of factors. In particular, it uses psychosocial and functional variables like self-rated health, sleep quality, and functional disability. These factors are very relevant in medical care settings. In these settings, depressive symptoms often shows itself through physical symptoms. This multidimensional approach not only enhances predictive performance but also improves clinical interpretability and practicality, particularly when delivered through an accessible online tool.

In this study, functional disability, self-rated health, and sleep duration were emerged as the three strongest variables of depressive symptoms in CCVD patients. Specifically, functional disability was the strongest factor. It may increase risk not only by restricting activities of daily living and social participation but also through psychoneuroimmunological mechanisms [24]. Poor self-perceived health was another key factor. This measure reflects integrated cognitive and emotional processing of one’s health status [25, 26], consistent with Leventhal’s Self-Regulatory Model [27, 28]. It is a known predictor of health outcomes. In addition, shorter sleep duration was significantly associated with increased depressive symptoms risk [29, 30].This is a modifiable lifestyle factor. Less sleep was linked to higher depressive symptoms risk. Previous reports showed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms in older adults, with 6–8 h being protective [31]. Disrupted circadian rhythm and hormonal imbalance may underlie this association. The study also confirmed several clinical and demographic risk factors. Female gender was significantly associated with higher depressive symptoms risk, which may be attributable to both biological factors—such as hormonal variations and sociocultural influences [32]. Chronic pain [33, 34], arthritis [35], and digestive diseases were also prominent risk factors, with high comorbidity rates observed in the development dataset. These conditions may contribute to depressive symptoms through shared inflammatory pathways (e.g., IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6) [36] and via gut-brain axis dysregulation [37]. The link to digestive diseases is especially strong [38, 39], with depressive symptoms prevalence reaching approximately 25% in disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease, far exceeding the general population rate [40, 41]. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of depressive symptoms in CCVD patients and highlight the need for integrated biopsychosocial approaches in clinical management. The prominent roles of functional status, subjective health perception, and sleep quality point to specific areas where targeted interventions—such as cognitive behavioral therapy, sleep hygiene education, and inflammatory modulation—could prove beneficial [42, 43].

This study has several limitations. First, it relied on self-reported heart and brain vessel disease data and past records. This approach may potentially missing asymptomatic events like silent myocardial infarction or transient ischemic attack. Second, the study biomarker (e.g., CRP, IL-6) and neuroimaging data limits mechanistic exploration This makes it hard to study the underlying body mechanisms. Third, the research only involved middle-aged and older adults. This means the findings may not apply to younger patients with these diseases. Future testing should include people of more ages. Fourth, although temporal validation was performed using CHARLS 2015 data, the reverse chronological design may not fully represent future population shifts. Fifth, the cross-sectional nature of the development data limits causal inference for depressive symptoms risk variables. Sixth, the feature selection approach required consensus across three distinct methods. While this enhanced model robustness, it may have been overly conservative, potentially excluding some weaker but valuable predictors. Future studies should explore alternative feature selection strategies and verify the model's performance in real-world clinical practice. Seventh, the use of a complete-case analysis may have introduced selection bias. It is plausible that patients with more severe conditions or cognitive impairments were more likely to have missing data and also a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms. Their exclusion could lead to an underestimation of the true risk and affect the generalizability of our model to the most vulnerable subpopulations. Future studies could use multiple imputation to handle missing data, a method shown to reduce selection bias in similar CHARLS-based analyses of older adults with chronic diseases. Despite these limitations, our analysis, which employed robust feature selection and multiple ML algorithms, identified an interpretable LR model as the most suitable for clinical use. Its core value lies in its ability to synthesize multidimensional information typically already gathered during routine clinical care (e.g., demographic characteristics, existing comorbidities, functional status) to generate a data-driven, composite risk score. The resulting online calculator provides a structured, multi-factorial risk estimate to complement existing screening methods, and provide an auxiliary reference for clinical decision-making. Future research could build upon this work by exploring more advanced and sophisticated ensemble learning architectures. For instance, recently proposed methods like HeartEnsembleNet, a hybrid ensemble approach specifically designed for cardiovascular risk prediction, demonstrate the potential of integrating diverse base models and deep learning components to achieve superior performance [44], which may further improve predictive performance for complex biopsychosocial conditions like depressive symptoms in CCVD.

Conclusions

Using the CHARLS database, this study developed a risk screening model for depressive symptoms in CCVD patients based on ML methods and deployed an online Shiny calculator. This tool provides a clinically feasible approach for assessing depressive symptom risk in CCVD patients, promoting timely intervention. It offers an evidence-based foundation for precision care implementation within resource-constrained settings.
