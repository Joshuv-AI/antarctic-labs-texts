Tunable cavity coupling of a single SnV − \rm\text{SnV}^{-} center in nanodiamond across bad-emitter and bad-cavity regimes Preprint: APS/123-QED
S. Sachero 1 , R.Berghaus 1 , E. Nieto Hernandez 2 , F. Feuchtmayr 1 , N. Lettner 1 , P. Maier 1 , S. Ditalia Tchernij 2 , A. Kubanek 1 , 3 \rm{}^{1},^{3}

Abstract
Efficient coupling between quantum emitters and optical cavities is essential for scalable quantum photonic technologies. Group IV vacancy centers in diamond, particularly the negatively charged tin-vacancy center, have emerged as promising candidates due to their spectral stability, high Debye–Waller factor and large orbital splitting in ground-states. Here, we demonstrate controlled coupling of a single negatively charged tin vacancy center hosted in a nanodiamond to a fully tunable Fabry–Perot microcavity. At cryogenic temperatures, we access the weak coupling regime and observe a transition from the bad-emitter to the bad-cavity regime as the optical transition of the color center narrows. At 4 K, a Purcell factor exceeding 1.7 is measured, confirming cavity-enhanced emission. The obtained results demonstrate the potential of SnV − \rm\text{SnV}^{-} centers in nanodiamonds as a coherent single-photon source for quantum networks.



I
Introduction

Coupling a quantum emitter to a single mode of light is fundamental for various quantum technologies, such as quantum repeater and quantum networks
Kimble 2008
;
David et al. 2015
;
Ruf et al. 2021a
. A key requirement for the successful implementation of these applications is the generation of coherent single photons
Wehner et al. 2018
;
Rogers et al. 2014
, which must be emitted in a well-defined optical mode. The efficiency of coherent photon emission primarily depends on the intrinsic properties of the emitter. In this context, group IV vacancy (G4V) centers in diamond have emerged as promising candidates
Iwasaki 2020
;
Harris and Englund 2024
;
Chen et al. 2020
. The aforementioned centers exhibit a high Debye–Waller factor, with over 60% of their emission residing within the zero-phonon line (ZPL)
Harris et al. 2023
;
D’Haenens-Johansson et al. 2011
;
Iwasaki et al. 2015
;
Qiu et al. 2023
. Furthermore, their
\rm D_{3d}
symmetry renders them robust against electric field fluctuations
Santis et al. 2021
;
Hepp et al. 2014
;
Rugar et al. 2020
, ensuring stable and coherent emission. Among these centers, the negatively charged tin-vacancy (SnV
-
) center stands out due to its high quantum efficiency
Iwasaki et al. 2017
;
Rugar et al. 2019
;
Tchernij et al. 2017
and a large ground-state (GS) orbital splitting (
\rm\sim
850 GHz)
Thiering and Gali 2018
, which significantly suppresses phonon-mediated decoherence at moderate cryogenic temperatures.

To further enhance photon emission into the ZPL, color centers can be integrated into optical microcavities. Depending on the atomic polarization decay rate (
\rm\gamma
), cavity-field decay rate (
\rm\kappa
) and atom-cavity coupling rate (g), different regimes of cavity quantum electrodynamics (cQED) can be accessed. From the strong coupling regime (
\rm g\gg\kappa,\gamma
)
Tolazzi et al. 2021
, where coherent energy exchange dominates
Press et al. 2007
;
Hennessy et al. 2007
, to the weak coupling regime, where loss processes (
\rm\gamma
or/and
\rm\kappa
) dominate the atom-cavity coupling rate
Fox 2012
;
Saavedra et al. 2021
.

In the weak coupling regime, two distinct sub-regimes can be identified depending on the relative magnitudes of
\rm\gamma
and
\rm\kappa
: the bad-emitter regime (
\rm\kappa\ll\gamma
)
Albrecht et al. 2013
, where the emitter’s rapid dephasing rate dominates the emitter-cavity interaction, and the bad-cavity regime (
\rm\kappa\gg\gamma
)
Sames et al. 2014
, where the cavity decay rate is the fastest decay channel.
In the bad-emitter regime, the cavity funnels the emission into a narrower cavity mode
Riedel et al. 2017
;
Johnson et al. 2015
, thereby yielding a frequency-tunable bright and narrow single-photon source
Albrecht et al. 2013
;
Høy Jensen et al. 2020
. Further narrowing of the emitter linewidth, achievable, for example, through cryogenic cooling in solid-state defect, can bring the system into a transitioning regime where
\rm\gamma<\kappa

Sames et al. 2014
, which in this paper we refer to as bad-cavity regime. In this regime, the cavity modifies the spontaneous emission rate, leading to enhanced emission via the Purcell effect
Ruf et al. 2021b
;
Yurgens et al. 2024
;
Benedikter et al. 2017
.

The coupling efficiency is limited by competing loss channels. In particular, unwanted scattering or absorption processes, commonly denoted as
\rm\kappa^{\prime}
, contribute to additional losses that degrade the overall system efficiency. These losses compete with the desired cavity-field decay rate
\rm\kappa
, effectively reducing the fraction of photons emitted into the target optical mode. As a result, only a fraction of emitted photons is channeled into the well-defined cavity mode. Therefore, minimizing
\rm\kappa^{\prime}
is critical for optimizing the performance of cavity-enhanced single-photon sources.

Open-access Fabry–Perot (FP) microcavities are particularly well-suited for such applications due to their high spectral resolution, narrow optical linewidths
Hunger et al. 2010
, and in-situ tunability
Saavedra et al. 2021
. Recent work has demonstrated the integration of G4V centers into diamond membranes, enabling coherent optical control and cavity coupling
Zifkin et al. 2024
;
Herrmann et al. 2024
;
Rugar et al. 2021
;
Berghaus et al. 2025
. However, the fabrication of high-quality, ultra-thin diamond membranes remains technically challenging. Surface roughness and interface imperfections can significantly degrade the quality of the coupled system. A scalable and practical alternative is to host color centers in nanodiamonds (NDs)
Antoniuk et al. 2024
;
Fehler et al. 2021
;
Greentree 2016
;
Johnson et al. 2015
. NDs offer several advantages: size-selective fabrication minimizes scattering losses, pre-characterization of individual emitters and deterministic integration into the cavity, eliminating the need for lateral cavity scanning.

Here, we demonstrate controlled coupling of a single
\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
center hosted in a ND by coupling its ZPL to a fully tunable FP microcavity. The proven single-photon nature of the
\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
center under off-resonant excitation allows direct detection of coherent photons from the ZPL. The narrowing of the single
\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
optical transitions at cryogenic temperatures allows the operation of the ND-cavity system in the weak coupling regime
Fox 2012
. Further cooling to 4 K leads to an additional reduction of the linewidth transitioning the operation of the system from the bad-emitter (
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
) to the bad-cavity regime. In the latter regime, we observe a reduction in the excited-state lifetime corresponding to a Purcell factor above 1.7, confirming cavity-enhanced emission.

Assembly of the hybrid ND–Cavity System

II.1
Experimental platform

Our platform is a fully tunable hemispherical FP microcavity formed by a CO2 laser-ablated concave mirror and a macroscopic flat mirror. The measured radius of curvatures (ROC) of the spherical structure are
\rm ROC_{x}\approx

\rm 25\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
and
\rm ROC_{y}\approx

\rm 22\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
resulting in an ellipticity of 14 %. The cavity consists of two distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) mirrors symmetrically designed.
The coating presents a first stopband centered at
\rm\lambda
= 620 nm with a transmission T = 650 ppm corresponding to a coating-limited finesse of
\rm F_{ideal,618}=4800
. The second stopband in the green wavelength range, with a transmission of 6400 ppm, has a coating-limited finesse value of 400.

The cavity is embedded in a titanium bucket (purple rectangle in figure
1
a)). The flat cavity mirror is mounted on three nanopositioners (2xANPx101 and ANPz102, attocube systems AG) which enable precise tuning of the cavity length. A multimode fiber (MM), positioned below the cavity, collects the transmitted laser signal. A mode-matched achromatic lens couples the excitation laser into the cavity mode and collects, in reflection, the emitted fluorescence signal. To optimize coupling efficiency the achromatic lens is mounted on three nanopositioners allowing x,y and z tunability. The experimental setup including the microcavity, optical filters, and excitation/detection fibers is placed within a liquid helium bath cryostat (dashed rectangle).

II.3
Characterization of the hybrid ND-cavity system

After transferring the ND into the hemispherical structure, we proceed with the characterization of the resulting ND-cavity system. As a first step, we perform multiple cavity transmission scans. By evaluating the ratio of the free spectral range (FSR) to the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the transmission resonances, we extract an average finesse value of
\rm F_{exp,618}=4600\pm 500
at 618.7 nm. This value is in good agreement with the ideal finesse
\rm F_{ideal,618}
, indicating that the presence of the ND does not introduce scattering losses.

The cavity dispersion is characterized by transmission white-light diode (WLED) spectra as a function of the cavity length (upper panel in figure
1
c)). The good agreement between experimental data and the simulation of the fundamental mode wavelengths (lower panel), based on the model for an empty cavity,

\nu_{m}=\frac{c}{2L}\left(m+\frac{cos^{-1}\left(\sqrt{1-L/ROC}\right)}{\pi}\right)

(1)

confirms that the inclusion of the ND at such short cavity lengths does not significantly distort the mode dispersion (see figure
7
in Appendix
C
).

Subsequently, the setup is indirectly cooled by a surrounding liquid nitrogen chamber. At approximately
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, the system reaches thermal equilibrium, providing relative passive thermal stability for the cavity. To monitor stability, transmission WLED spectra are acquired continuously over a 2-hour period (see figure
6
c) Appendix
B
). During this time, a drift in the effective cavity length (
\rm L_{eff}
) of approximately 5 nm is observed. The drift is attributed to a gradual temperature increase over the course of the measurement. To confirm this hypothesis, the drift in cavity length is fitted as a function of temperature using a linear model (figure
1
d)). The resulting slope corresponds to a thermal expansion coefficient (CTE) of
\rm\alpha=(5.10\pm 0.03)\cdot 10^{-6}\;K^{-1}
. The good agreement of the estimated value with the reported CTE of titanium,
\rm\alpha=6.8\cdot 10^{-6}\;K^{-1}

King and Lawson 1938
supports our interpretation.

III
Cavity coupling of the single SNV
-
center

Off-resonant measurements are performed by exciting and detecting through the fundamental modes of the cavity. To enable simultaneous excitation and detection of the ZPL emission, the resonance condition
\rm L_{eff}=m\cdot\frac{\lambda_{m}}{2}
must be satisfied for both excitation (
\rm\approx

\rm 533.3\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
) and detection (
\rm\approx

\rm 618.5\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
) wavelength.

A simulation of the resonance condition, performed with the excitation wavelength fixed, reveals that for
\rm L_{eff}\approx

\rm 3.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}

\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
center (figure
2
a)). This corresponds to excitation and detection through the mode numbers
\rm m_{exc}=14
and
\rm m_{det}=12

\rm m_{exc}=14

To verify the simulation, we experimentally tune the cavity length across two excitation resonances while simultaneously recording the laser transmission signal (blue curve, figure

\rm m_{exc}=14
and
\rm m_{det}=12

\rm L_{eff}\approx

\rm 3.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}

\rm m_{det}=11

The fluorescence resonance appears broadened compared to the laser transmission due to the lower piezo-scanning speed, which is necessary to increase sensitivity to the lower photon counts. IV
Cavity-enhanced interaction of the SNV
-
center in the weak coupling regime

In the bad-cavity regime the lifetime of the emitter is reduced due to the Purcell effect. The overall Purcell enhancement can be estimated as
Janitz et al. 2020
:

F_{p}=\frac{\tau_{o}}{\tau_{p}}

(2)

where
\rm\tau_{p}
and
\rm\tau_{0}
(
3
) are the cavity-modified and the free-space spontaneous emission lifetime, respectively.

To estimate
\rm\tau_{p}
we measure power-dependent second-order autocorrelation functions (
\rm g^{2}(t)
) and fit the data with the three level model equation
Steck 2019
. Since the decay rate (
\rm\gamma_{1}
) varies linearly with the excitation power the resulting values are fitted with the model
\rm\gamma_{1}=m\cdot P+1/\tau

Berthel et al. 2015
.

Lifetimes of
\rm(14\pm 1)
ns and
\rm(19\pm 2)
ns are extracted at zero excitation power at
\rm\sim
\rm 30\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
and
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, respectively (figure
3
a)). The measurements are performed with the cavity maintained on resonance with the emitter frequency by actively adjusting the cavity length to compensate for temperature-induced drifts. The resulting lifetime values are confirmed through pulsed lifetime measurements. The OPO laser, fixed at 533.25 nm, is pulsed off with an electric optical modulator (EOM) while recording the time delay between the excitation pulse and the detected fluorescence photons. By fitting the data with an exponential decay we obtain the following lifetime values:
\rm(12.2\pm 0.3)
ns at
\rm 4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, (
\rm 15.8\pm 0.3
) ns at
\rm 40\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, and
\rm(21\pm 1)
ns at
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
(figure
3
b)). As shown in figure
3
a), the pulsed lifetime measurements (yellow and green square) intersect the linear extrapolation of the power-dependent
\rm\gamma_{1}
values at zero excitation power. The agreement confirms the reliability of the extracted lifetime values. The lifetime reduction indicates that the system operates in the weak coupling regime.

At
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, the emitter linewidth (
\rm\gamma=

\rm 210\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
) exceeds the cavity-field decay rate (
\rm\kappa=
\rm 15\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
), placing the system in bad-emitter regime (
\rm\gamma>\kappa,g
)
Albrecht et al. 2013
. In this regime, only a fraction (
\rm\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}
) of the spontaneous emission couples to the cavity mode
Kaupp et al. 2013
. As a result, spontaneous emission is funneled into the cavity mode without significantly modifying the emitter’s radiative lifetime
Grange et al. 2015
;
Høy Jensen et al. 2020
;
Häußler et al. 2021
. This explains the weak observed Purcell enhancement: the measured lifetime
\rm(21\pm 1)
ns is only marginally shorter than the free-space value (see table
1
).

In contrast, at lower temperatures, the narrowing of the emitter optical transitions enables operation in the bad-cavity regime (
\rm\gamma\;<\;\kappa
), where the cavity-field decay rate dominates. Under these conditions, we estimate a Purcell factor of
\rm F_{p}=1.78\pm 0.04
. The definition of
\rm F_{p}
(equation
2
) includes the emission into the incoherent phonon sideband and the free-space continuum
Yurgens et al. 2024
. However, since only the fraction of emission coupled into the C optical transition is enhanced by the cavity, we correct
\rm F_{p}
with a factor
\rm\epsilon=0.36
. The correction factor
\rm\epsilon
accounts for non-unity quantum efficiency (approximately 80%
Iwasaki et al. 2017
), a limited Debye–Waller factor (
\rm\sim
56%, estimated from the free-space photoluminescence spectrum), and off-resonant branching ratio into C transition (80 %
Rugar et al. 2021
). Accounting for all these effects, we estimate a corrected Purcell enhancement of
\rm F_{p,ZPL}=4.9
.

The Purcell factor (
\rm F_{p}
) follows a Lorentzian dependence on cavity detuning
Englund et al. 2005
;
Rugar et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2024
:

F_{p}=F_{cav}\left(\frac{\vec{E}(r)\vec{\mu}}{|\vec{E}_{max}||\vec{\mu}|}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{1+\left(2Q\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{cav}}-1\right)\right)^{2}}+F_{fp}.

(3)

The first term accounts for spatial and spectral mismatch between the emitter dipole
\rm\vec{\mu}
to the cavity field
\rm\vec{E}
, while
\rm F_{fp}
, accounts for background modifications to the emission rate due to the cavity environment. For perfect alignment, the enhancement is given by
\rm F_{cav}
+
\rm F_{fp}
, where the theoretical Purcell factor
\rm F_{cav}
is
Janitz et al. 2020
:

F_{cav}=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda_{c}}{n}\right)^{3}\cdot\frac{Q}{V_{mode}}

(4)

For our FP cavity, operating at a wavelength of
\rm\lambda_{c}
= 618.5 nm, an estimated quality factor of
\rm Q_{ideal}
= 56400 and mode volume of
\rm V_{mode}=21\lambda_{c}^{3}
, we obtain
\rm F_{cav}=205
. However, since the ND is located on the spherical structure rather than at the field maximum of the cavity mode, it experiences weaker coupling.
To quantify the effect of this spatial mismatch, we use the ratio
\rm(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\omega\left(L\right)})^{2}
, which effectively describes the reduction of the optimal coupling due to the position
Bayer et al. 2023
. Incorporating this spatial misalignment, we obtain a corrected Purcell factor of
\rm F_{cav}=173
.

To understand the significant discrepancy between the theoretically predicted Purcell factor and the experimentally measured value, we perform a detailed analysis of the system’s spectral properties by measuring the Purcell enhancement as a function of cavity-emitter detuning (figure
3
c)). The detuning-dependent lifetime measurements allow us to extract the effective cavity-field decay rate and assess whether additional broadening mechanisms are degrading the cavity performance. Cavity detuning is achieved by tuning the excitation wavelength and adjusting the cavity length to maintain resonance conditions for
\rm m_{det}
= 12 and
\rm m_{exc}
= 14. For every cavity detuning, we perform pulsed lifetime measurements, with increasing acquisition time for larger detuning to account for minor fluorescence counts.

Fitting the detuning-dependent data yields an effective cavity-field decay rate of
\rm\kappa_{\text{exp}}=(160\pm 30)
GHz, nearly an order of magnitude larger than the expected value of 15 GHz. This broadening arises from fast mechanical vibrations and thermal drift, which shift the cavity resonance during acquisition
Ruf et al. 2021c
. Despite manually compensating for slow drifts, rapid fluctuations reduce the effective quality factor to
\rm Q_{exp}=(3100\pm 600)
. Under these realistic conditions, the maximum achievable Purcell factor is limited to
\rm F_{\text{vib}}=10\pm 2
. This value reflects the upper bound imposed by the degraded Q factor.

Comparing
\rm F_{\text{vib}}
with the measured Purcell factor
\rm F_{p}=4.9
(formula
2
) provides insight into the efficiency of coupling between the emitter and the cavity mode. We obtain an effective spatial alignment factor of approximately 49%. This value corresponds to the fraction of the C emitter dipole moment
\rm\mu
that is aligned with the cavity electric field (E), assuming perfect spatial overlap and including. Conversely, when we do not explicitly correct for the branching ratio, we find an alignment factor of about 38%. This lower value implicitly includes both the dipole misalignment and the reduction in effective coupling strength due to the branching ratio. Thus, improving spatial alignment of the emitter within the cavity field, by moving or rotating the ND, could lead to a higher Purcell factor and further lifetime reduction.

V
Conclusion

We demonstrate the successful coupling of a single
\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
center in a ND to a FP cavity. The small size of the ND minimizes scattering losses
Albrecht et al. 2013
;
Bayer et al. 2023
;
Feuchtmayr et al. 2023
. As a result, the cavity maintains a finesse of
\rm 4600\pm 500
, comparable to that of a bare cavity. Furthermore, we show that the dispersion of the cavity modes remains unaffected by the presence of the ND and can be accurately predicted using the model for an empty cavity. The presence of a single
\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
center in the ND, as confirmed by the observed minimum of the autocorrelation function
\rm g^{2}(0)=0.21\pm 0.03
, enables the detection of coherent photons from the ZPL. This coherence is crucial for the generation and establishment of indistinguishable photons, a key requirement for scalable quantum networks and spin–photon interfaces
Görlitz et al. 2022
;
Harris and Englund 2024
;
Li et al. 2015
. By cooling the system to cryogenic temperatures, we operate the system in the weak coupling regime. Specifically, at
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, a linewidth of 210 GHz allows operation in the bad-emitter regime, where we observe single photon emission into the cavity mode over a very broad spectral range, which can be exploited to realize a continuously tunable, narrow-band single photon source
Riedel et al. 2017
;
Johnson et al. 2015
;
Høy Jensen et al. 2020
;
Albrecht et al. 2013
. Further cooling narrows the optical transition C and shifts the system into the bad-cavity regime, where a clear lifetime reduction, corresponding to a Purcell factor of
\rm 1.78\pm 0.04
is observed. This enhancement is crucial for realizing highly efficient photon sources, as it increases the probability of photon emission into the desired cavity mode, improving brightness and emission rate for quantum photonic applications. Probing of the fine structure, reveals an increased GS splitting of 1234 GHz favorable for high-fidelity microwave spin control
Brevoord et al. 2025
;
Rosenthal et al. 2023
.

By detuning the cavity from fully on resonance to off-resonance, we probe the emitter’s lifetime under varying coupling conditions. These measurements reveal a coupling efficiency of approximately 49%, indicating that precise manipulation of the nanodiamond (ND) position using an AFM could further enhance the interaction between the cavity and the C optical transition
Lettner et al. 2024
;
Fehler et al. 2021
. Moreover, we observe that rapid mechanical vibrations of the cavity degrade its performance, limiting the maximum achievable Purcell factor by approximately an order of magnitude. Therefore, improving the mechanical stability of the cavity is crucial for further increasing both the Purcell enhancement and emitter–cavity coupling efficiency
Pallmann et al. 2023
.

These results demonstrate that the integration of single
\rm\text{SnV}^{-}
centers in size-controlled NDs within tunable FP microcavities provides a practical route toward versatile and tunable quantum light–matter interfaces.

Appendix E
Bad-emitter regime

The total spontaneous decay rate (
\rm\gamma
) of an emitter can be expressed as the sum of the emitter’s natural linewidth (
\rm\gamma_{0}
), determined by its radiative lifetime, and the pure dephasing rate (
\rm\gamma*
) arising from interactions with phonons,
\rm\gamma=\gamma_{0}+\gamma*
. At
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, the phonon population in the host lattice is still substantial, leading to significant pure dephasing. In this regime, the dephasing rate dominates over the lifetime-limited contribution (
\rm\gamma^{*}\gg\gamma_{0}
), and we approximate
\rm\gamma\sim\gamma*
. Thus, we assume that the spontaneous decay rate is equal to the FWHM of the PL spectrum acquired at
\rm 100\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
. This corresponds to
\rm\gamma^{*}_{C}=(210\pm 20)
GHz and
\rm\gamma*_{D}=(410\pm 20)
GHz.

Due to the broad emitter linewidth (
\rm\gamma\gg\kappa
) only a fraction
\rm\frac{\kappa}{\gamma^{*}}
of the total emission is spectrally resonant with the cavity mode and effectively couples into it
Kaupp et al. 2013
.

In this regime, the Purcell factor can be approximated as:
Albrecht et al. 2013
:

F_{bad-emitter}\approx\frac{4g^{2}}{\kappa+\gamma_{0}}\times\frac{\kappa}{\gamma^{*}}.

(6)

Since in the bad-emitter regime
\rm\gamma^{*}>g
we expect a
\rm F_{bad-emitter}<1
. As a consequence, the cavity would not alter the emitter’s lifetime, but rather channel the emission into a well-defined cavity mode
Albrecht et al. 2013
. With this model we can explain the observation of no lifetime reduction both optical transitions.
