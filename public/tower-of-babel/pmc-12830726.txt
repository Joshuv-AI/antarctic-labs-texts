Reconstruction and analysis of pore blockage fouling in ceramic ultrafiltration membranes through FIB-SEM
Authors: Da Sheng, Tianyu Wang, Yangang Zhang, Meng Sun, Ruiping Liu, Xiwang Zhang, Huijuan Liu, Jiuhui Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pore blockage fouling significantly impairs ultrafiltration (UF) efficiency. Recent advances in volume electron microscopy have enabled nanoscale investigations of its mechanisms. Here, we developed a focused ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) tomography strategy that enables accurate 3D reconstruction of organic foulant accumulation within ceramic membrane pores. FIB-SEM achieves a voxel resolution of 2 × 2 × 5 nm3. The high-resolution imaging clearly reveals the trilayer structure of ceramic UF membranes, and captures the dynamic evolution of pore blockage fouling. The interface between the top and intermediate layers emerges as the primary site for initial membrane fouling. This localized channel constriction decreases the theoretical water permeability by 76.5%, directly explaining the rapid initial flux decline. Lattice Boltzmann simulations reveal that multilayer membrane structures create flow stagnation zones. Near-zero velocities trigger foulant accumulation, clarifying pore blockage mechanisms. 3D reconstruction and pressure distribution analysis revealed that pore blockage fouling is effectively removed during physical backwash, but could rapidly accumulate again at the initial stage of the next operation cycle. This study provides key insights into pore blockage fouling in trilayer ceramic UF membranes, and offers a theoretical basis for guiding the design of anti-fouling membranes.

Introduction

Ultrafiltration (UF) has critical applications across water treatment fields, including in drinking water production systems1, water reuse systems2, desalination plants3, and industrial separation processes4. Ceramic UF membranes show strong promise for these challenging applications because of their inherent chemical and thermal stability5,6, particularly in decentralized continuous-operation scenarios such as rural drinking water treatment and seawater desalination7. To balance membrane flux and mechanical strength, composite membranes are typically fabricated by coating a selective top layer with small pores onto a macroporous support layer. UF membranes with a fine-pored top layer often include an intermediate layer between the support and the top layer. This intermediate layer enhances the surface flatness of the substrate during the coating process, thereby reducing the formation of defects in the top layer5,8–10. However, the wide implementation and long-term operation of ceramic membranes are greatly inhibited by membrane fouling, which leads to a decrease in the flux, an increase in the operating pressure, and membrane failure11,12. Generally, UF membrane fouling occurs in two forms: surface cake layer buildup and internal pore blockage13. Owing to the complex three-layer architecture, the pore blockage fouling mechanisms in ceramic membranes remain poorly understood, hindering effective fouling control. Therefore, a key challenge is that a nanometer-resolution map depicting the 3D pore network of ceramic UF membranes is lacking.

Ceramic UF membranes exhibit nanometer-scale pore structures and micrometer-thick separation layers14. Their 3D structural characterization requires cross-scale techniques. Inroads have been made with light microscopy and mass spectrometry, but the results are resolution limited. Light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) achieves a resolution of only 3 μm13, which is constrained by the Abbe diffraction limit15. The time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS)-based method achieves only a 5 μm in-plane resolution16. Researchers have utilized transmission electron microscopy (TEM)-based technologies to achieve 3D reconstructions of nanofiltration membranes with nanometer resolution17–19. However, these techniques require samples thinned to under 80 nm. The micrometer-scale membrane structures cannot be preserved in samples of such thickness.

Focused ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) is a cross-scale technique for 3D reconstruction20. Studies have shown that this technique creates 3D cell organelle reconstructions at a 4 nm resolution in all directions while handling structures up to the micrometer scale21. Additionally, notable variations in electron scattering properties among the ceramic matrix, organic foulants, and pore structures enable their distinct visualization and differentiation via SEM secondary electron detection. These characteristics make FIB-SEM a highly promising tool for 3D visualization of ceramic UF membrane architectures.

In this study, the pore blockage mechanisms in ceramic UF membranes was investigated. We acquired successive cross-sectional images of both clean and fouled ceramic UF membranes (2 × 2 × 5 nm3 resolution) through FIB-SEM. Simultaneously, automated image segmentation via deep learning was implemented, enabling 3D reconstruction of membrane substructures at the 2 μm scale. Pore network modeling and lattice Boltzmann simulations elucidated the microscale flow characteristics within the reconstructed architecture. This work reveals four critical findings: 1) the key factors determining the pore blockage severity, 2) the dynamic processes causing flux decline during membrane fouling, 3) the structural determinants driving fouling initiation, and 4) the reversibility of pore blockage during backwash. This study offers prospective guidance for optimizing UF membrane structures and designing anti-fouling ceramic membranes. The research approach can also be extended to investigate various porous media containing nanochannels.

Results

3D reconstruction framework for ceramic UF membranes

3D tomography based on FIB-SEM was employed to reconstruct the structure of ceramic UF membranes (CM15; the molecular weight cutoff specified by the manufacturer was 15 kDa) (Fig. 1a). To minimize ion beam-induced artifacts, platinum and carbon protective layers were first deposited on the membrane surface using a gas injection system (Supplementary Fig. S1). Sequential gallium ion (Ga⁺) milling (30 kV, 300 pA) coupled with secondary electron imaging (2 kV, 500 pA) achieved a 2.0 nm/pixel resolution with a nominal slice thickness of 5.0 nm. Post-acquisition metrology revealed an actual average slice thickness of 5.2 nm (Fig. 1b), attributed to operational drift in ion beam control. The aligned slice series formed a clean volumetric dataset for subsequent computational analysis. Image segmentation was achieved through a deep learning approach22. Input datasets were generated through Otsu’s method followed by manual refinements. The U-Net 2.5D architecture in the Intelligent Segmentation module completed model training, enabling accurate segmentation of pore structures within CM15 (Fig. 1c). The segmented model subsequently reached a uniform 2.0 nm resolution in all dimensions through upsampling, allowing comprehensive 3D reconstruction of the CM15 architecture (Fig. 1d). The pore connectivity in CM15 was further characterized. The top surface and bottom/lateral surface were demarcated via surface segmentation (Supplementary Fig. S2), and Boolean operations were applied to compute pore geometries intersecting both domains, thereby defining hydraulically connected pores. Quantitative analysis revealed that the connected pore network constituted 38.7% of the total analyzed volume, whereas the isolated pores accounted for as little as 0.22%. Pore network model (PNM)-based 3D membrane analytics

The ceramic membrane cross-section was prepared using FIB milling and showed an asymmetric layered structure. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping revealed that zirconium dioxide (ZrO2) was present primarily in the top two layers and that titanium dioxide (TiO2) was present mainly in the bottom sublayer (Fig. 2a) (Supplementary Text. 1). The TiO2 supporting layer contained micrometer-sized pores formed by coarse particle packing, and this structure had no molecular sieving capability. The ZrO2 layer consisted of two separate layers with a clear interface. The top layer had a dense microstructure with nanopores, whereas the contiguous intermediate layer had substantially enlarged pores. The porosity gradients across the spatial axes were measured using a representative 2 × 2 × 2 μm3 volume covering both the top and intermediate layers (Fig. 2b). Isotropic slicing at 2.0 nm intervals produced 1000 cross-sections for porosity analysis. Along the x- and z-axes, a consistent porosity of approximately 0.25 was maintained. For the y axis, the porosity varied with depth. The porosity remained stable at approximately 0.18 in the top layer. Within the intermediate depth zone (870–1230 nm), the porosity progressively increased to 0.36 and then reached a plateau. Five randomly selected CM15 ceramic membranes were employed for systematic investigation of the top layer structure. All the samples were subjected to standardized 3D reconstruction through the above established protocols. The pore network model generated with OpenPNM23 quantified key structural descriptors (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S4). To validate the accuracy of the PNM, the CM15-1# sample was used as a representative model. A comparison of the permeability and tortuosity calculated by the PNM and the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) was conducted (Supplementary Text. 2 and Text. 3). The results show that the permeability obtained from the PNM is 2.47 × 10-7 D, while that derived from LBM is 1.35 × 10−7 D, both values falling within the same order of magnitude. The tortuosity calculated via the PNM is 1.76, compared to 1.87 from the LBM velocity field, yielding a relative error of only 5.9%. Therefore, from a hydraulic perspective, abstracting the ceramic UF membrane structure as a pore network model enables accurate quantitative analysis.

The pore size distributions across the five CM15 top layers were closely aligned, which confirmed their structural consistency (Fig. 2d). Gumbel distribution modeling effectively described the right-skewed dataset, with a central value of 19.0 nm (Supplementary Text. 4). The observed pore sizes ranged from 7.0 nm to 85.0 nm, with 95% of the sizes concentrated between 10.0 nm and 50.0 nm. This distribution model was applied to describe the structural characteristics of the CM15 top layer in follow-up research.

Membrane fouling properties

CM15 membrane fouling experiments were conducted using a cross-flow filtration system (Fig. 3a). All the experimental protocols are fully described in the Methods section. A transmembrane pressure of 1.0 bar was applied. Three model foulant categories, dextran (DEX), dextran–bovine serum albumin (DEX–BSA) composites, and polystyrene nanoparticles (PSNPs), were systematically selected to span broad size ranges while maintaining analogous physical properties. The nomenclature followed the particle characteristics: numerical suffixes denote the molecular weight (kDa) of dextran (e.g., DEX10 = 10 kDa dextran). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was used to determine the dextran particle diameter. The DEX10 to DEX2000 samples covered a size range of 1.5–28.8 nm. The diameter measurements linearly correlated with the cubic root of the molecular weight. This result corresponded with theoretical predictions24 (Supplementary Fig. S5). Synthetic DEX–BSA exhibited a broad size distribution with peaks spanning from 32.7 to 342.0 nanometers (Supplementary Fig. S6a). The PSNPs, with manufacturer-specified size ranges of 50–100 nm (PSNP50) and 200–500 nm (PSNP200), were measured by TEM to have actual sizes of approximately 70 nm and 300 nm, respectively. Figure 3b shows consistent fouling patterns across all experimental groups. The flux-time curve indicates a rapid flux decline during the initial 4 min, followed by a more gradual reduction. Based on the Hermia model25–27, fouling evolution can be inferred from membrane flux decline profiles as detailed in Supplementary Text. 5. Taking DEX800 fouling as an example (Supplementary Fig. S7), the dominant fouling mechanism shifts in two distinct phases: During the initial 4 min, combined complete blocking and standard blocking mechanisms prevail. Following a transition phase, cake layer formation dominates. Thus, the acute flux decline observed within the initial 4 min is primarily attributed to pore blockage fouling. This is corroborated by SEM imaging of the CM15 membrane surface at the 4-min mark (Supplementary Fig. S8), which shows no significant cake layer formation, further confirming that the initial flux decline was mainly caused by pore blockage.

After 4 min of fouling, PSNP200 caused the smallest flux reduction (12.8%), whereas DEX2000 produced the largest flux decline (82.8%). From 4 to 30 min, all the groups displayed similar flux reductions (5.0–12.9%) (Fig. 3c). These results demonstrate that pore blockage fouling occurring during the initial filtration stage significantly contributes to the decrease in the membrane flux, with the magnitude of this effect being determined by the foulant particle size.

As shown in Fig. 3d, the severity of membrane fouling is strongly correlated with the size of the foulant particles relative to the membrane pores. DEX10 (1.5 nm) and DEX40 (6.7 nm), with particle sizes below the pore size range of CM15, exhibited a limited capacity for complete pore obstruction. The flux decreases within 30 min were 34.8% and 49.3%, respectively. Their incomplete blocking behavior resulted in a relatively mild flux decline. The DEX150 (10.5 nm) particles matched the CM15 membrane pores, leading to direct blockage of the pores. Blocked pores formed dead-end structures, promoting foulant accumulation. This triggered an 81.1% flux decline within 30 min. DEX800 (20.2 nm) and DEX2000 (28.8 nm) intensified this trend, reaching 88.0% and 89.1% flux losses, respectively. The DEX–BSA sizes (32.7–342.0 nm) partially overlapped with those of the CM15 pores (7.0–85.0 nm). Size exclusion prevented most of the foulants from entering the pores. Consequently, the flux decline decreased to 58.4%. Both PSNPs were too large to cause internal membrane blockage fouling, resulting in flux declines of 28.3% for PSNP50 and 20.1% for PSNP200. FIB-SEM cross-section imaging verified the pore blockage patterns (Fig. 3e). After 30 min, DEX10-induced pore blockage was negligible in the CM15 membranes, whereas DEX150 and DEX800 were distinctly deposited within the pores. No internal blockage occurred with PSNP200 exposure. Thus, pore blockage requires contaminant–membrane pore size matching.

Furthermore, we validated this trend using natural organic matter (NOM), which is a common mixture of organic compounds in water and a significant contributor to UF membrane fouling. Experiments were conducted with SRNOM 2R101N at a concentration of 2 g·L⁻¹. The results indicate that the flux decline induced by NOM falls between that caused by DEX40 and DEX150 (Supplementary Fig. S9a). This intermediate behavior can be attributed to the abundance of macromolecular humic acids in NOM. Similar to the DEX–BSA system, NOM contains fewer components capable of pore blockage. Supporting evidence is provided by 3D reconstruction of membranes after 2 min of fouling (Supplementary Fig. S9b), which clearly demonstrates that NOM results in comparatively less obstruction of membrane pores.

Pore blockage formation process and flux decline mechanisms

In this study, the pore blockage formation process in ceramic membranes was investigated using DEX800 as a model foulant because the diameter of DEX800 exactly overlaps with the dominant pore size of CM15, and a rapid decrease in the flux was observed after 4 min of DEX800 exposure (ΔJ/J0 > 80%). FIB-SEM nanotomography causes permanent membrane damage, thus requiring separate UF membranes for each 3D reconstruction. Nevertheless, batch uniformity tests on CM15 membranes confirmed highly consistent structural features (Fig. 2d). These structural differences demonstrably do not affect the fouling behavior (Fig. 3b).

Three fouling stages (0, 2, 4 and 30 min) were systematically analyzed to track structural changes during membrane fouling (Fig. 4a). Cross-sectional imaging revealed distinct deposition dynamics. Initially, foulants adhered solely to the interface between the top and intermediate layers (2 min), followed by outward migration toward the membrane surface (4 min). After 30 min, the pores in the top layer approached near-complete blockage. The 3D reconstructions provide a more spatially resolved visualization of this trend (Fig. 4b). A consensus has not yet been reached regarding the initial site of membrane fouling. While the general view posits that fouling begins at the membrane surface before gradually penetrating the pores28,29, a few studies suggest it may begin internally within the membrane30,31. Given that different filtration methods (e.g., constant flux vs. constant pressure, cross-flow vs. dead-end) are used across the literature, we investigated the dead-end, constant-flux mode to help reconcile these conflicting viewpoints (Supplementary Fig. S11a). DEX800 was employed, acting as a model foulant. The flow rate was set to 60 L m−2 h−1 using a peristaltic pump. The results show that flux declined slower in constant-flux filtration than in constant-pressure filtration (Supplementary Fig. S11b). Under constant-flux filtration, pressure rose continuously from 0.40 bar to 2.05 bar at 10 min, finally stabilizing around 2.40 bar(Supplementary Fig. S11c). Two time points were chosen for structure analysis (2 min and 10 min). The 2-min point reflects the early rapid flux drop, while the 10-min point marks the start of flux stabilization.

In dead-end constant-flux filtration, a cake layer formed quickly within 2 min (Supplementary Fig. S11d). By then, flux had fallen to 55.7% of the initial value, with no clear pore blockage yet (Supplementary Fig. S11e and S11f). This confirms that cake layer fouling alone can cause significant flux loss. After 10 min of fouling, the membrane surface was completely covered by the cake layer (Supplementary Fig. S11g), and internal pore blockage began to occur (Supplementary Fig. S11h and S11i). Notably, under constant-flow filtration mode, pore blockage fouling still initially appeared at the interface between the top layer and the intermediate layer.

Based on the above findings, it can be concluded that both cake layer fouling and membrane pore blockage are capable of causing a substantial reduction in flux, and the sequence of their occurrence is closely related to the UF mode. However, regardless of the filtration mode employed, membrane pore blockage consistently initiates at the interface between the top layer and the intermediate layer.

PNMs of CM15 under 0, 2, 4, and 30 min fouling conditions were reconstructed using OpenPNM for quantitative analysis (Fig. 4c). The clean membrane demonstrated a relatively homogeneous porous structure in the top layer, which was indicated by uniform blue spherical cluster distributions. The intermediate layer showed localized pore expansion features marked by red clusters. After 2-min fouling, severe pore blockage was observed at the interface between the top and intermediate layers. A 52.1% pore blockage ratio was recorded in critical fouling zones (Supplementary Fig. S12a). A limited cluster of membrane pores remained active as hydraulic bottlenecks. After 4 min of fouling, the pore blockage had extended upward into the top layer, causing partial blocking. By the 30-min mark, the top layer was nearly entirely clogged, exhibiting a local pore blockage volume ratio of 66.0% (Supplementary Fig. S12b). The water transport pathways within this layer were reduced to a handful of remaining channels.

The theoretical permeability for the four fouling scenarios were calculated based on the pore network model. Supplementary Text. 6 contains the full computational parameters and theories. Supplementary Fig. S13 presents the calculation results. Compared to the permeability of the unfouled membrane structure, the theoretical permeability decreased to 38.1%, 15.7%, and 12.6% at 2, 4, and 30 min, respectively. The theoretical calculations involve inherent uncertainties, and the selected micrometric samples might not fully represent the entire membrane. Nevertheless, the calculated permeation patterns were highly consistent with the experimental flux reduction trends. This suggests that the structural alterations resulting from local pore blockage help to explain the overall reduction in flux.

The fouled membrane model can be divided into three distinct zones: the upper bottleneck zone (Region #1), primary bottleneck zone (Region #2), and lower bottleneck zone (Region #3) (Supplementary Fig. S14a). Pore blockage formation mechanism

Figure 2b reveals measurable porosity enlargement between the top layer and intermediate layer. This structural configuration likely initiates contaminant deposition. Quantitative membrane pore analysis confirmed persistent size variations across depth. The CM15 membrane displays three vertically stratified regions on the basis of the classification criteria established in the previous section. Two-dimensional cross-sectional analysis revealed progressive pore enlargement with increasing depth (Supplementary Fig. S14a). Pore network modeling across three membrane regions clarified this size progression through systematic distribution analysis (Supplementary Fig. S14b). Notably, the interface between the top and intermediate layers manifests dual structural adjustments: individual pore diameter expansion accompanies a collective porosity increase. These coupled modifications inevitably alter the local flow characteristics.

The LBM was applied to analyze the flow behavior in the ceramic membranes. Dimensionless parameters were used in these calculations. The Methods section fully describes the computational framework and boundary conditions. After 2 min of fouling, foulants concentrated at the interface between the top and intermediate layers and were located approximately 1 μm below the surface (Fig. 5a). LBM-generated velocity fields detected initial flow deceleration at the interface between the top and intermediate layers. Both flow monitoring (Fig. 5b) and cross-sectional evaluation (Fig. 5c) verified this phenomenon. The mean flow rates across depth-specific cross-sections were used to quantify the velocity distribution (Fig. 5d). The cross-sectional mean flow velocity was 8.07 × 10−5 at a depth of 0.176 μm, which progressively decreased to 2.87 × 10−5 at a depth of 1 μm, only 35.6% of the initial value. Furthermore, the velocity contour revealed multiple deep blue regions in cross-sectional views (Supplementary Fig. S15). These areas demonstrated localized flow stagnation, where the flow velocity approached zero. Reduced flow rates facilitated pollutant accumulation within membrane pores. Once pore blockage occurred, these passages became dead ends. The impact of backwash on pore blockage fouling

The characteristics of membrane pore blockage fouling during the backwash process were analyzed (Fig. 6). The CM15 membrane after the tenth physical backwash cycle was selected for analysis. During the fouling-backwash cycles, the fouling on the surface of CM15 was not completely removed, with noticeable pollutants still adhering to it (Fig. 6b). Cross-sectional electron microscopy images revealed that the residual fouling was mainly concentrated on the surface, while the internal membrane pore blockage was almost entirely eliminated (Fig. 6c).

A 3D reconstruction of a localized area of CM15 under backwash conditions showed that the backwash water flows from the intermediate layer to the top layer (Fig. 6d). Based on the 3D reconstruction, a pore network model was established (Fig. 6e) to calculate the pressure distribution (Fig. 6f) and determine the average pressure across different depth intervals (Fig. 6g). The results demonstrated that during backwash, the membrane interior maintains relatively high pressure. Even at a distance of 200 nm from the membrane surface, the backwash pressure remains above 0.4 bar. This is likely due to the small pore size and severe blockage in the top layer, where the pressure drop is concentrated.

Thus, backwash exerts high pressure and impact on internal pore blockage fouling, facilitating its removal. However, the membrane surface is exposed to the atmosphere during backwash, resulting in a relative pressure close to zero. Additionally, the backwash water experiences a significant pressure drop after passing through the top layer, leading to limited removal efficiency for surface fouling. Therefore, while physical backwash is highly effective at removing internal pore blockage, the primary residual fouling remains on the membrane surface.

As shown in Fig. 6a, although backwash effectively removes pore blockage fouling, such fouling rapidly reoccurs early in the subsequent filtration cycle. The incompletely cleaned membrane surface and the reoccurrence of pore blockage significantly restrict membrane flux. In this study, CM15 operated below 16% of its initial flux for more than 86% of the running time. Hence, simultaneous removal of cake layer fouling and pore blockage fouling is crucial for ensuring the efficient operation of UF.

Discussion

We achieved 3D reconstruction of ceramic UF membranes at a 2 × 2 × 5 nm3 resolution using FIB-SEM and deep-learning-based image segmentation. In this work, pore structure variations in asymmetric ceramic UF membranes during organic fouling were comprehensively visualized. The severity of internal pore blockage correlates directly with the foulant particle size: the most severe fouling occurs when foulant sizes fall within the membrane pore distribution range. The abrupt pore enlargement from the top layer to the intermediate layer causes a localized flow velocity reduction, leading to foulant accumulation. This accumulation reduces the effective flow area, creating a bottleneck effect that drives a rapid flux decline during initial filtration (e.g., 83.3% maximal flux drop at 4 min). This study, from a 3D structural perspective, confirms that membrane pore blockage is more likely to form during the initial stage of UF in a cross-flow system. This type of fouling is a major contributor to the significant flux decline that occurs initially. However, during the physical backwash process, the pressure gradient distribution makes pore blockage easier to remove, whereas the cake layer is more difficult to eliminate. Both cake layer fouling and pore blockage can cause a substantial decrease in membrane flux. Therefore, future research on anti-fouling UF membranes must consider strategies to remove both types of fouling. For example, modifying membrane surface properties such as hydrophilicity and electrical charge can reduce pollutant adhesion and suppress cake layer formation. Meanwhile, depositing catalytic materials at the interface between the top layer and the intermediate layer could allow reactive species to diffuse within a confined space. This would help suppress pore blockage at this critical location. This approach aims to remove fouling from key areas with low energy input, while maintaining the physical sieving function of the ceramic UF membrane. It avoids the need to make the entire membrane catalytic, which would compromise its sieving ability and turn it into a penetrative electrode.

Methods

Materials

Dextran with a molecular weight range of 10,000–2,000,000 g mol−1 for ultrafiltration studies was sourced from Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) and demonstrated ≥99% chemical purity. High-molecular-weight dextran could not be obtained commercially. An alternative fabrication method with established protocols was employed to synthesize dextran-coated BSA particles ( > 32.7 nm diameter), as detailed in prior literature32 (Supplementary Text. 7). No polysaccharide-based material matching the 200 nm size requirement was available. PSNPs (200–500 nm) served as surrogate foulants for these trials. Given that these dimensions exceeded the ceramic membrane pore sizes (Fig. 3c), these particles were intentionally selected to demonstrate that contaminants incapable of entering the membrane pores cannot cause pore blockage fouling. While PSNPs represent a nonideal substitute under material constraints, their use to isolate and verify this physical exclusion mechanism remains methodologically justified. This research specifically investigates how pore blockage fouling is influenced by the relative size of pollutants and pores. To target this interaction, we chose the CM15 because its pore size distribution aligns closely with the size range of the dextran molecules.

Membrane fouling and backwash experiment

The presence of surface adsorbates significantly impacts the interfacial hydrophilicity of CM15, necessitating robust surface cleansing protocols to establish predictable hydrophilic pore characteristics for subsequent permeability analyses. The CM15 test samples were secured on perforated alloy substrates inside a plasma reaction vessel in a BSET NT3 plasma reactor system (Glentech Inc., United States). Prior to hydrodynamic evaluation, the membranes were subjected to atmospheric plasma exposure at 18 W RF power for 20-min cycles to achieve optimal surface energetics. All foulant samples were uniformly dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) Tween® 80 to inhibit particle agglomeration and ensure solution homogeneity. In replicate experiments, we employed multiple CM15 membranes from the same manufacturing batch to assess whether inherent structural variations exert nonnegligible impacts on the fouling behavior.

Preceding flux characterization trials, 10 liters of deionized (DI) water underwent cross-flow circulation through the ultrafiltration membranes for 24-h stabilization conditioning. The DI water was subsequently replaced with test analytes—aqueous dextran suspensions of distinct molecular sizes. A constant transmembrane pressure (ΔP = 1.0 bar system average) was maintained during the filtration measurements. The cross-flow flow rate of the ultrafiltration system was maintained at 30 L h−1. Based on the flow cross-sectional area in the membrane module, the cross-flow velocity is estimated to be approximately 0.057–0.092 m s−1. The cross-flow flow field on the membrane surface was modeled as a cylinder with a base area of 30 mm² and a height of approximately 2 mm. By simplifying the cross-flow as a uniform flow, the velocity at the maximum cross-sectional area was estimated to be approximately 0.14 m s−1. Permeate effluents were collected in gravimetric vessels positioned on computerized analytical balances (0.01 g resolution), with the mass acquisition intervals synchronized to 5-sec increments. Backwash was performed using the same setup, with the key difference that the UF membrane was reversed. It was positioned with the support layer facing the incoming flow and the top layer facing the outflow. The backwash used DI water and the pressure was set at 1.0 bar. In each fouling-backwash cycle, the membrane was fouled for 30 min, followed by a 5-min backwash. Chemical backwash follows the same procedure as physical backwash, with the exception of using a 2 wt% NaOH solution instead of DI water. All experiments involving the measurement of membrane flux changes were performed three times, with the results averaged.

Focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy

In a FIB-SEM, an ion beam was employed for milling the UF membrane, whereas an electron beam was utilized for imaging both the surface and milled cross-sections. Prior to being loaded into the microscope chamber, the UF membrane was secured on the sample stage using a specialized adhesive. Owing to the nonconductive nature of the membrane itself, a high-angle Pt sputtering technique was implemented to deposit an approximately 3 nm-thick metallic layer on the membrane surface. This approach effectively enhances the surface conductivity while minimizing potential alterations to the native surface morphology of the UF membrane caused by the metal coating.

Following insertion into the microscope chamber, the sample stage was tilted at 54° to achieve orthogonal alignment with the ion source. Ga⁺ ions accelerated at 30 kV with a beam current of 300 pA were employed to sequentially mill the ceramic membrane in 5-nm-thick slices per iteration. Post-milling operations utilized an electron beam operating at an accelerating voltage of 2 kV and a 500 pA beam current, achieving a resolution of 2 nm. The imaging area was strategically selected on the basis of real-time observations of the milled cross-section, with priority given to comprehensive coverage of the separation layer throughout the iterative cutting–imaging sequence. Following the image acquisition process, the total cutting depth is calculated from the focal distances at the initial and final positions, and the average thickness of each slice is then determined.

Image segmentation for the UF membrane, membrane pores, and contaminants was performed using deep learning-based method. The workflow proceeded as follows: First, the Otsu’s thresholding algorithm was implemented to preliminarily delineate the membrane boundaries. Second, ten representative images were selected for manual refinement of threshold segmentation results, with distinct classifications established for the membrane matrix, pores, and foulants to generate input parameters for deep learning model training. Third, the U-Net architecture was subsequently used to process manually segmented slice data, and a 2.5D framework with 3 adjacent integrated slices configured with a depth level of 5 and an initial filter count of 64 was employed for deep learning training. Finally, the trained deep learning model processed all the raw datasets to generate 3D reconstructions.

Pore-scale numerical simulation method

Two methodologies were primarily employed for the numerical simulations at the pore scale: the PNM and the LBM. To analyze the membrane pore morphology and distribution characteristics, the PNM was adopted because of its advantages of lower computational demands and intuitive graphical representation. Conversely, to elucidate the formation mechanisms of membrane pore fouling, for which high-precision computational results are essential, the LBM was implemented because of its superior resolution capabilities. The PNM was constructed on the basis of the watershed algorithm, with a segmentation neighborhood of 26 and a marker extent of 1. In the LBM framework, the D3Q19 lattice configuration was employed, enabling comprehensive analysis and precise quantification of spatially resolved pressure and velocity profiles within critical membrane structural domains through systematic numerical resolution33. The pressure boundary condition was imposed at the inlet and outlet, whereas the no-slip bounce-back scheme was adopted for the solid boundaries. Dimensionless numerical parameters were employed in the LBM calculations. A pressure of 1.5 × 10−4 was maintained at the inlet boundary, whereas the outlet boundary operated under zero-pressure conditions. The working fluid was assigned a dynamic viscosity of 0.001 Pa·s, ensuring physiochemical consistency with water-based filtration systems. The detailed calculation theories and steps are given in the Supplementary Text 8.
