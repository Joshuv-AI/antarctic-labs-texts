Gene regulatory networks orchestrating oocyte fate bifurcation in primordial follicles revealed by single-cell transcriptomics
Authors: Hanwen Zhang, Xingsi He, Qiuzhen Chen, Min Su, Huanyu Yan, Xiao Zeng, Shicai Fan, Xi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe first wave of primordial follicle activation occurs around postnatal day 2.5 (P2.5), with the remaining follicles entering a dormant state to ensure a continuous supply of fertilizable oocytes. However, the molecular characterization and underlying mechanisms of this critical fate commitment remain poorly understood. Here, we employ SMART-seq2 to profile transcriptional dynamics in individual perinatal female C57BL/6 mouse germ cells across three developmental stages: cyst stage at embryonic day 17.5 (E17.5), primordial-follicle stage at P2.5, and primary-follicle stage at P6.5. Unsupervised clustering and trajectory inference reveal divergent transcriptional programs within P2.5 primordial oocytes, indicative of a bifurcating process in which pre-determined oocytes commit towards either dormant or activated states. Activated oocytes exhibit upregulation of genes linked to PI3K-Akt/mTORC1 signaling, extracellular matrix (ECM) disassembly, and oocyte maturation, whereas marker genes of dormant oocytes are enriched in oxidative stress response and DNA repair pathways. Transcriptional regulatory network reconstruction based on SCENIC inference identifies key transcription factors (e.g., BHLHE41, TCF3/12) orchestrating the fate bifurcation. Notably, dormant oocytes form a stable transcriptional state distinct from the continuous activation and development trajectory. Our findings provide a comprehensive landscape of gene expression and regulatory networks that orchestrate fate determination in P2.5 primordial-follicle oocytes.Subject terms: Transcriptomics, Oogenesis, Gene regulatory networks

Introduction

In female mammals, ovarian follicles serve as the fundamental units of reproductive function. The primordial follicle pool, established from embryonic day 17.5 (E17.5) through the early postnatal period, constitutes the exclusive reservoir of fertilizable oocytes1. Shortly after its completion around postnatal day 2.5 (P2.5)2,3, two divergent developmental trajectories emerge: a subset of medullary primordial follicles initiates immediate activation4, whereas cortical follicles enter a deeply quiescent state and are recruited gradually throughout reproductive life5. Although spatial positioning and microenvironmental niches are known to influence whether a primordial follicle becomes dormant or activated6, the molecular mechanisms governing the first-wave activation of P2.5 primordial follicle oocytes remain poorly understood. Deciphering these regulatory programs is essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying follicular fate commitment, with potential implications for disorders, such as premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)7.

Previous efforts to dissect this critical developmental juncture have been hindered by technical and experimental limitations. Bulk RNA-seq of size-sorted perinatal oocytes masks transcriptional heterogeneity6, whereas existing single-cell studies often lack sufficient temporal resolution or adequate sampling depth of oocyte subpopulations8–10. Consequently, the transitional states, molecular signatures, and regulatory circuits related to the fate commitment of P2.5 primordial follicle oocytes remain incompletely characterized or explored, obstructing our understanding of how first-wave activation diverges from the establishment of long-term dormancy.

To overcome these limitations, we manually isolated perinatal female germ cells and performed single-cell RNA sequencing. Focusing on the earliest oocyte fate bifurcation, we profiled P2.5 primordial follicle-stage oocytes at a high sampling depth and included E17.5 cyst-stage germ cells and P6.5 primary follicle-stage oocytes as developmental references before and after this bifurcating point. Bioinformatics analyses, including clustering, pseudotemporal trajectory inference, and RNA velocity estimation, revealed previously uncharacterized transcriptional heterogeneity and uncovered a clear fate bifurcation within P2.5 primordial follicle oocytes. By integrating SCENIC-based transcription factor (TF) activity inference, we reconstructed the regulatory networks underlying dormant and activated primordial follicles, providing mechanistic insights into the transcriptional regulation of primordial oocyte fate decisions.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of female germ cells during perinatal development

To explore the transcriptional dynamics in female germ cells during perinatal development, mouse ovaries were isolated at E17.5, P2.5, and P6.5. The ovaries were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, and individual germ cells were manually collected for single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) (Fig. 1a). Consistent with previous findings11, germ cells at E17.5 were predominantly organized in cysts (Fig. 1b). At P2.5, while germ cell cysts were still present, many oocytes assembled with their surrounding granulosa cells had developed into primordial follicles (Fig. 1c). By P6.5, some follicles remained dormant as primordial follicles, whereas some others were activated and developed into primary follicles or even a few secondary follicles with larger oocytes inside (Fig. 1d). To profile oocytes at key developmental stages, we manually collected cyst-stage oocytes at E17.5, primordial-follicle oocytes at P2.5, and primary-follicle oocytes at P6.5, using cell diameter as the primary selection criterion (Fig. 1a; see Methods for details).

After data processing and quality control, the scRNA-seq data were subjected to dimensionality reduction analysis using the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method, which revealed a clear sequential organization of germ cells corresponding to their developmental stage (Fig. 1e). Established marker genes for the cyst, primordial-follicle, and primary-follicle stages exhibited the highest expression at E17.5, P2.5, and P6.5, respectively (Fig. 1f). Specifically, meiosis-related genes, including Stra8, Meioc, and Ccnb33,12,13, were highly expressed in E17.5 cyst-stage germ cells but were markedly downregulated in P2.5 primordial-follicle oocytes, consistent with germ-cell arrest in meiosis prophase I. Genes associated with primordial follicle formation, such as Figla, Sohlh1, and Ybx214–16, reached peak expression in P2.5 primordial-follicle oocytes before subsequently declining. By P6.5, primary-follicle oocytes exhibited robust upregulation of genes involved in oocyte-granulosa cell communication (Gdf9, Bmp1517–19) as well as the zona pellucida component gene Zp111, marking transition into the oocyte growth phase. Additionally, normalization of the scRNA-seq data using External RNA Control Consortium (ERCC) spike-ins showed an increase in global RNA expression during oocyte development (Supplementary Fig. 1a), which correlated with the observed increase in oocyte size across the developmental stages (Fig. 1b–d). Moreover, each cell exhibited a high number of detected genes and low mitochondrial transcript content (Supplementary Fig. 1b), further supporting the integrity of our dataset. In addition, somatic cell markers, such as Foxl220 and Amh21 were expressed at negligible levels (Supplementary Fig. 1c), confirming the high purity of the oocyte population and the absence of detectable somatic contamination. Altogether, these results confirm the high quality and reliability of our scRNA-seq dataset, providing a robust foundation for further transcriptomic analysis of perinatal female germ cells.

Stage-specific transcriptional programs in perinatal female germ cells

To comprehensively identify marker genes associated with distinct stages of perinatal oocyte development, we performed differential expression analysis, identifying 691, 457, and 969 significantly upregulated genes for E17.5 cyst stage germ cells, P2.5 primordial-follicle stage oocytes, and P6.5 primary-follicle stage oocytes, respectively (Fig. 2a). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis uncovered stage-specific biological processes: at E17.5 cyst stage, enriched pathways included meiotic cell cycle, RNA splicing, apoptosis, and oogenesis; at P2.5 primordial-follicle stage, key processes involved oxidative stress response, cellular respiration, tricarboxylic acid cycle, and DNA damage response; and at P6.5 primary-follicle stage, enriched functions were related to cell cycle regulation, protein transport, ubiquitination, and microtubule-based processes (Fig. 2b). These findings underscore dynamic transcriptional changes and distinct developmental characteristics of oocytes at each perinatal stage.

To gain better insights into the stage-specific transcriptional programs, we examined the expression of selected marker genes in greater detail (Fig. 2c). At E17.5, genes, such as Tex12, U2af2, Ctsz, and Rhox13 were significantly elevated. Tex12, a key component of the synaptonemal complex, plays a crucial role in meiosis22; U2af2, a core spliceosome component, has been linked to cancer development and progression23. Ctsz, known to induce apoptosis via the lysosomal pathway24, may contribute to programmed cell death in E17.5 germ cells25. Rhox13, which regulates spermatogonial differentiation and proliferation26, is suggested to have a regulatory role in female gametogenesis. At P2.5, another set of genes was specifically upregulated. For instance, Park7, a key regulator of oxidative stress response, protects cells from reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced damage27. Cox4i1, a subunit of cytochrome c oxidase, is essential for mitochondrial respiration and energy production28. Slc27a2, which is involved in long-chain fatty acid transport and activation29, may contribute to the distinct metabolic state of P2.5 oocytes. Additionally, Parp2, involved in DNA repair, participates in base excision repair and maintains genomic stability30. At P6.5, highly expressed genes included Cenpn, which is essential for centromere assembly and ensures proper chromosome segregation during the cell cycle31. Rab25, a key regulator of vesicle trafficking and a member of the Rab family, shares functional similarities with Rab11a and likely plays a significant role in oocyte development32,33. Fbxl2, associated with protein ubiquitination, regulates the degradation of specific cell cycle proteins, ensuring fidelity in cell cycle processes34. Additionally, Tacc3, which regulates spindle organization, is thought to be critical for the formation of acentrosomal microtubule-organizing centers during germ cell meiosis35. The stage-specific elevated expression of the above genes underscores their dynamic and time-dependent roles in germ cell development.

To validate these stage-specific markers, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of ovarian tissues at E17.5, P2.5, and P6.5 (Fig. 2d). Both our transcriptomic data and IHC results confirmed that Tex101/TEX101 was highly expressed at E17.5, suggesting its potential involvement in female germ cells during the cyst stage (Fig. 2d, upper). Notably, TEX101 has been previously reported as critical for sperm migration and fertilization36. The expression of Fxr1/FXR1 peaked at the primordial follicle stage, and subsequently decreased in oocytes within the primary follicle at P6.5 (Fig. 2d, middle). Given that FXR1 is known as a key translational activator essential for spermatogenesis and male fertility37, our results indicate its potential role in translational regulation during primordial follicle development. Lastly, Miox, which encodes myo-inositol oxygenase (MIOX)38, exhibited elevated expression in primary follicles at P6.5 (Fig. 2d, lower), suggesting a metabolic shift from the non-grown to the growing oocyte stage.

To further characterize transcriptional changes during perinatal oocyte development, we analyzed gene expression differences between adjacent developmental stages. Comparing the numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), our analysis suggests that transcriptomic alterations were more pronounced between E17.5 and P2.5 than between P2.5 and P6.5 (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e; Supplementary Data 1). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed a predominant involvement of cell proliferation, RNA processing, and metabolic processes during perinatal oocyte development (Supplementary Fig.1f). Notably, from E17.5 to P6.5, transcriptional activity related to RNA splicing and RNA processing declined continuously, while genes associated with lipid metabolism and fatty acid metabolism showed a steady increase in expression. However, other biological processes, such as protein transport and cell division/cell cycle, did not follow a continuous pattern, suggesting the presence of stage-specific regulatory networks during perinatal oocyte development.

Transcriptomic heterogeneity in P2.5 oocytes linked to the first wave of primordial follicle activation

Unsupervised clustering analysis of germ cells collected at E17.5, P2.5, and P6.5 identified four distinct clusters (Fig. 3a). All E17.5 cells were assigned to cluster C1, and nearly all P6.5 oocytes (46 out of 47) fell into cluster C4. In contrast, P2.5 oocytes segregated into two clusters, C2 and C3, indicating pronounced transcriptional heterogeneity at this stage (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). To validate this heterogeneity, we performed immunofluorescence staining of FOXO3, a transcription factor whose phosphorylation and nuclear export mark the onset of primordial follicle activation4,39. The staining revealed two distinct patterns of FOXO3 localization: one displaying strong nuclear fluorescence, indicative of non-activated oocytes, and the other exhibiting extranuclear localization, a hallmark of activated oocytes (Fig. 3b). These findings are consistent with our transcriptomic clustering results and thus support heterogeneity among oocytes at P2.5.

To further characterize these P2.5 oocyte populations, we conducted differential expression analysis between the C2 and C3 clusters. This analysis identified 2,071 upregulated and 3,106 downregulated genes in C3 relative to C2 (Fig. 3c). Notably, the upregulated genes in C3 included key regulators of oocyte development and maturation, such as the zona pellucida (ZP) family (Zp1, Zp2, Zp3)40, components of the subcortical maternal complex (SCMC) (Khdc3, Ooep, Nlrp5, Tle6, Zbed3, Padi6)41, and essential maternal factors like Btg4 42–44 (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 2b; Supplementary Data 1). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further revealed that C3 oocytes exhibited molecular features characteristic of oocyte development, including the establishment of gamete genome methylation and enhanced Notch signaling-mediated interactions with somatic cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d–f), both of which are key hallmarks of follicular activation and development.

To further support the activation profile of C3 oocytes, we examined several genes critical for oocyte activation, including Kit45, Lin28a46, Gdf917, Bmp1519, Stat347, and Nobox48,49. As anticipated, these genes showed significantly elevated expression in C3 (Fig. 3d). Previous studies have demonstrated that PI3K and mTORC1 signaling pathways are key drivers of primordial follicle activation, whereas the TGF-β pathway maintains follicle dormancy 50–53. Consistent with this, GSEA revealed significant enrichment of PI3K and mTORC1 signaling among upregulated genes in C3, while downregulated genes were enriched for the TGF-β pathway (Fig. 3e–g). In addition to molecular signaling, recent studies have implicated mechanical stress from the extracellular matrix (ECM) in maintaining oocyte dormancy by regulating nuclear rotation54,55. Supporting this, we observed that genes upregulated in C3 were enriched for ECM disassembly processes, whereas genes associated with ECM organization and cell-substrate adhesion were significantly downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 2g–k). Furthermore, oocyte growth–related genes that were already upregulated in C3 continued to increase in expression in C4 P6.5 oocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2l). Collectively, these results strongly suggest that the transition from C2 to C3 corresponds to oocyte activation and exit from dormancy.

Furthermore, GO enrichment analyses of DEGs during the C2-to-C3 transition revealed that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in processes, such as transmembrane transport, lipid metabolism, phosphorylation, and actin cytoskeleton organization, indicating extensive oocyte-somatic cell interactions, widespread post-translational modifications, and metabolic shifts during oocyte activation. In contrast, downregulated genes were enriched in RNA processing, RNA splicing, and oxidative respiration, suggesting alterations in RNA metabolism and energy utilization (Fig. 3h). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further confirmed that activation-promoting pathways, including PI3K-Akt and mTOR, were significantly enriched among upregulated genes, whereas negative regulatory pathways, such as TGF-β, Hippo, and FoxO signaling were enriched among downregulated genes. Additionally, MAPK, Ras, and Rap1 signaling pathways emerged as potential contributors to oocyte activation, while the p53 signaling pathway appeared to be negatively associated with activation (Fig. 3i).

Dormant and activated oocyte branches revealed by trajectory inference

To investigate the developmental trajectories of perinatal oocytes, we applied single-cell trajectory inference using Monocle256. This analysis arranged cells along a pseudotime continuum and identified a bifurcating trajectory with three distinct states (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). With the time-point information and RNA velocity inference (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c), the trajectory initiated with State 1, comprising all E17.5 germ cells and a subset of P2.5 oocytes. The remaining P2.5 oocytes diverged into two branches: State 2, which consisted exclusively of P2.5 oocytes, and State 3, which progressed to include P6.5 oocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Mapping the transcriptomic clusters onto the trajectory states provided additional insight into oocyte fate specification. Notably, 90% of State-2 P2.5 oocytes belonged to the C2 cluster and 84% of State-3 P2.5 oocytes corresponded to the C3 cluster (Fig. 4b, c). Given that C2 oocytes exhibit molecular signatures associated with dormancy, including genes linked to response to oxidative stress (Fig. 3i), we infer that State 2 represents dormant oocytes. This interpretation aligns with the notion that dormant oocytes are established following follicle formation shortly after birth, constituting the ovarian reserve that supports female fertility across the reproductive lifespan57–59. In contrast, State 3 appears to represent activated oocytes that continue development, forming the trajectory towards follicular growth. Lastly, we defined State 1 as a fate pre-determined state, prior to the fate bifurcation to either dormancy or activation.

To further validate the identities of these two branches, we examined the expression of known dormancy- and activation-associated markers. Superoxide dismutase 1 (Sod1), an antioxidant enzyme highly expressed in dormant oocytes to maintain cellular homeostasis2, was predominantly enriched in the dormant branch (Fig. 4d). Conversely, Zp2, a key regulator of oocyte development, exhibited increasing expression along the oocyte growth trajectory (Fig. 4e). These findings establish that oocytes at P2.5 bifurcate into two distinct fates: one leading to dormancy and ovarian reserve formation, and the other progressing toward activation and continued development.

Gene expression dynamics orchestrating oocyte dormancy and activation

To gain deeper insight into the fate determination process between activation and dormancy in postnatal mouse oocytes, we analyzed gene expression changes along the trajectories. This analysis identified five distinct gene clusters (GCs): GC1 (221 genes), GC2 (308 genes), GC3 (540 genes), GC4 (955 genes), and GC5 (476 genes) (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 2). Representative genes from each cluster were listed and selected for detailed visualization (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 3d). Notably, GC1 exhibited high expression in the pre-determined state, with a gradual decline as development progressed. GC2 and GC3 were enriched in dormant oocytes, whereas GC4 and GC5 were upregulated in activated and developing oocytes. Based on these expression patterns, we termed GC2–GC3 genes as Oocyte-Dormancy Genes and GC4–GC5 genes as Oocyte-Development Genes.

To validate the functional relevance of the defined gene sets in oocyte dormancy and activation, we compared them to differentially expressed genes from publicly available data on small and large oocytes at P4 and P6, where small oocytes represent dormant ones and large oocytes correspond to developing oocytes57 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). As expected, genes upregulated in large oocytes were significantly enriched in the Oocyte-Development Genes, whereas those highly expressed in small oocytes were predominantly associated with the Oocyte-Dormancy Genes (Fig. 5b). These findings further support the distinct roles of two sets of genes in regulating oocyte fate.

Functional enrichment analysis using GO further elucidated the molecular characteristics of oocytes in different states (Fig. 5c). Oocyte-Dormancy Genes (GC2-GC3) exhibited gene expression signatures associated with aerobic metabolism (e.g., aerobic respiration, proton motive force-driven mitochondrial ATP synthesis), translation (e.g., translation, mitochondrial translation), and cellular protection (e.g., DNA damage response, response to oxidative stress). These findings indicate that dormant oocytes rely on unique metabolic, translational, and quality control mechanisms. In contrast, Oocyte-Development Genes showed enrichment in protein processing (e.g., protein transport, protein import into the nucleus, protein phosphorylation, and protein ubiquitination), metabolic changes (e.g., lipid metabolic processes), and cytoskeletal organization (e.g., meiotic spindle organization, microtubule-based process, actin cytoskeleton organization). These functional signatures highlight the importance of protein modifications, metabolic shifts, and active preparation of materials essential for meiosis and maturation.

Several key genes further illustrated the difference in transcriptional programs in dormant and activated oocytes. For instance, the DNA repair regulator Fen1 was highly expressed in dormant oocytes, whereas cell cycle regulators, such as Cdc25b and critical developmental genes like Uhrf1 showed elevated expression in growing oocytes (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 3d). We further validated two important genes for oocyte fate determination at their protein levels. IHC staining clearly showed that UHRF1 showed lower expression in dormant oocytes within primordial follicles but was highly expressed in activated oocytes. In contrast, FEN1 was highly expressed in dormant oocytes but exhibited significantly lower expression in growing oocytes (Fig. 5d).

It has been reported recently that the primordial-to-primary transition (PPT) is a crucial process in follicle activation and oocyte development, and the loss of key genes can prevent primary follicle formation60. To identify genes that were transcriptionally activated during this process, we intersected the Oocyte-Development Genes with the upregulated genes from the C2-C3 transition (as detailed above), resulting in 951 PPT-activated genes (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Data 3). Many of these genes were essential for oocyte development, including the ZP genes and the Oosp family genes61. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were involved in transcription activation, lipid metabolism, and protein transport and post-translational modification (Fig. 5f). Moreover, key activation pathways, including the PI3K-Akt pathway, were significantly enriched, highlighting their critical roles following the PPT process (Fig. 5f). A recent study has shown that Tcf3/12 knockout disrupts PPT, leading to the identification of a set of TCF3/12 target genes that are crucial for primary follicle formation62. Notably, over 70% of the downregulated target genes following Tcf3/12 knockout overlap with our PPT-activated genes (Supplementary Fig. 4b), further highlighting the critical role of TFs in dynamic gene regulation during and following oocyte activation.

Transcriptional regulatory network reconstruction in activated and dormant oocytes

Oocyte maintenance and activation are orchestrated by the precise TF regulation63. To further elucidate the transcriptional regulatory network in oocyte fate bifurcation, we performed regulon activity analysis using SCENIC on P2.5 oocytes. The inferred regulon activities were visualized in a heatmap, with pseudotime-based cell ordering revealing distinct patterns of TF activity that reflect dynamic changes during oocyte development (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 4). Clustering of TF activity profiles identified four primary groups: the first group showed high activity during the pre-determined state, the second was predominantly active in the dormant state, the third exhibited elevated activity in the activated state, and the fourth displayed a universal pattern. Notably, FIGLA showed high activity in the dormant oocytes, and it has been reported that female mice lacking Figla experience significant oocyte loss and infertility64. Additionally, SOHLH1 exhibited consistent activity throughout our analysis, known as a critical TF involved in oogenesis and essential for regulating fertility genes, including Lhx8 and Nobox65.

Next, we projected P2.5 oocytes on a two-dimensional space based on the inferred TF activity using the UMAP method. In parallel, clustering analysis in the TF activity space partitioned these oocytes into two branches (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Annotating the oocytes with transcriptomic trajectory states revealed that oocyte progression from the pre-determined state to activation followed a continuous transition in TF activity, whereas dormant oocytes form a distinct, separate branch (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Furthermore, examining individual TFs with high activity in either activated or dormant oocytes, we observed that many TFs exhibited activity shifts between the two TF-activity clusters, that is TF activity showed limited changes from the pre-determined state to the activated state, whereas TF activity might obviously change during the transition from pre-determined state to the dormant state (Fig. 6c). These findings suggest that, from the TF regulation perspective, follicle formation, oocyte activation and growth around P2.5 may proceed as a continuous process, whereas oocyte entry into dormancy required a unique and specialized transcriptional regulation.

Transcription factors interact with each other to establish intricate regulatory networks that mediate essential biological functions. By analyzing betweenness centrality (BC) values, we assessed the regulatory influence of TFs in P2.5 oocytes. This revealed extensive interactions between multiple TFs and identified core TFs with high BC values, including BHLHE41 and TCF3 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). In the regulatory network of highly active TFs within the activated oocytes, we identified TCF3, ELK1, and TCF12 as core TFs (Fig. 6d). Notably, TCF family proteins (including TCF3 and TCF12) have been recently shown to be critical regulators in follicle activation62. Reanalyzing public ATAC-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq data from P7 growing oocytes62, we identified ELK1 as well as TCF3, TCF12, and TFAP4 as key TF regulators governing oocyte growth, as their motifs were enriched in transcriptionally active promoters or enhancers (Fig. 6e). These epigenomic results align with TF activity inference in our SCENIC analysis, showing significantly higher activity of ELK1, TCF3, TCF12, and TFAP4 in growing oocytes than in dormant oocytes (Fig. 6f). Collectively, these results establish a regulatory paradigm in which TF networks orchestrate the fate bifurcation of primordial follicles.

Discussion

Single-cell transcriptomics has become a powerful tool for investigating gene expression dynamics, especially in resolving transcriptomic heterogeneity among phenotypically homogeneous cells9. However, the intrinsic scarcity of female germ cells has limited the sampling depth in existing transcriptomic datasets, hindering a comprehensive delineation of transcriptional heterogeneity during primordial follicle pool formation and the first oocyte fate bifurcation. To address this limitation, we enriched female germ cells at key developmental stages and employed SMART-seq2 to obtain high-coverage transcriptomics data for individual oocytes. The resulting dataset allows us to characterize transcriptional dynamics during perinatal oocyte development. Consequently, we uncovered bifurcating developmental trajectories: while a set of oocytes directly undergoes first-wave activation, the remaining cells enter dormancy. Moreover, we showed that these trajectories are predominantly orchestrated by the TF regulatory networks.

It is evident that oocytes at different developmental stages coexist at each sampling time point, thus we enriched stage-specific oocytes using their diameter as a proxy for distinct stages21,66. This approach was further supported by corresponding differences in RNA content (Supplementary Fig. 1a), consistent with prior observations that oocyte volume and maternal mRNA stores increase progressively during follicular development66,67. While this approach efficiently enriched oocytes at major developmental stages, size-based selection does not yield a completely homogeneous population. In particular, the size-selected P2.5 group may include a small fraction of oocytes from early growing follicles, while some additional transitional intermediates may have been excluded and remain to be explored in future work. More importantly, bioinformatic analyses further revealed pronounced transcriptional heterogeneity within P2.5 oocytes (Fig. 3a). Pseudotime trajectory inference identified a clear developmental bifurcation at this stage (Fig. 4a), highlighting that oocytes can transition directly from the pre-determined state to the activated state without passing through the dormant state. This branching pattern was supported by transcriptomic and regulon signatures (Fig. 5a, Fig. 6a-c), and further reinforced by RNA velocity analyses, which resolved a continuous trajectory from pre-determined towards activation while dormant oocytes deviated into a separate lineage (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Based on these observations, we proposed a fate bifurcation model for primordial-follicle stage oocytes. This interpretation is consistent with energetic logic, as maintaining full dormancy may be unnecessary for oocytes destined for immediate activation. It also aligns with physiological and experimental evidence showing that oocytes intrinsically tend toward activation unless stabilized by extrinsic cues from the ovarian microenvironment4,55,57,68,69. Together, these findings refine the conceptual framework of first-wave follicle activation, suggesting that these oocytes bypass a dormancy stage and instead commit directly to activation from a poised pre-determined state. Moreover, the discovery of the pre-determined state highlights a critical window after follicle assembly when oocytes remain uncommitted and are receptive to signals directing them towards either dormancy or activation.

Based on the fate bifurcation model, we not only recapitulated known regulators but also uncovered previously unrecognized genes and pathways that may mediate oocyte fate decisions. For oocyte activation, our analysis highlighted several critical genes, including Gdf917, Kit45, Lin28a46, Nobox49, Cdh170, and Stat347, as well as major signaling pathways, such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK47, and mTOR, together with ECM disassembly processes. Conversely, the p53 signaling pathway, which regulates diverse cellular processes71, was downregulated in activated oocytes, suggesting a potential inhibitory role in activation. Notably, classical factors regulating oocyte activation, such as Foxo372, p2773, or Cdc4274 remained unchanged in transcript abundance, suggesting that their functions are likely mediated through post-translational modification and/or subcellular localization75. Recent studies have further shown that many regulatory genes, such as Hdac6 and Yy1, were upregulated in granulosa cells to promote follicle activation76,77, underscoring the extensive oocyte-granulosa cell crosstalk. Because the present study focuses exclusively on germ cells, future work co-analyzing both oocytes and surrounding granulosa cells will be essential for a more comprehensive understanding of follicle activation. Oocyte dormancy is a unique biological process, allowing human oocytes to persist for decades while preserving their quality78,79. Consistent with previous reports2, our data reveal elevated Sod1 expression in dormant oocytes, accompanied by upregulation of other oxidative stress response genes like Sod2 and Prdx4, which jointly maintain redox homeostasis. Dormant oocytes also exhibit upregulation of DNA damage repair genes, such as Fen1, which likely preserve long-term genome stability throughout prolonged periods of meiotic arrest. Additionally, hypoxia and decreased ROS have been implicated in contributing to maintaining oocyte dormancy2,57, highlighting the importance of metabolic regulation in oocyte fate determination.

PPT is accompanied by extensive transcriptional changes80, and the loss of key genes disrupts this process81. As a complement to previous studies, we identify a number of transcriptionally activated genes following PPT, which may play important roles during later oocyte growth and maturation. Specifically, mature oocytes store maternal transcripts and proteins required for early embryonic development, with mitochondria-associated ribonucleoprotein domains (MARDO) storing translationally repressed mRNAs82 and cytoplasmic lattices (CPLs) and the SCMC anchoring necessary proteins83. Zar1, which is essential for MARDO formation82, and genes encoding SCMC components (such as Padi6) are among the PPT-activated genes. Furthermore, Uhrf1, which encodes a multifunctional epigenetic regulator for epigenetic reprogramming84, is significantly upregulated along the developmental trajectory but localized to the cytoplasm rather than the nucleus (Fig. 5d), suggesting its role as a maternal factor stored for later developmental stages. Collectively, PPT starts to trigger transcription and translation of genes critical to early embryonic development, highlighting a fine-tuned program of gene expression regulation during oogenesis.

Transcription factors play critical roles in oogenesis16. Based on SCENIC inference, we reconstruct the TF regulatory network in primordial follicle oocytes, revealing oocyte fate-associated TF activity patterns. In addition to recovering known critical regulators, such as TCF3 and TCF12 62,85,86, we identified previously unrecognized TF candidates with potential roles in directing the dormancy–activation decision. Among activation-associated regulators, ELK1 emerged as a prominent factor. ELK1 is a phosphorylation-sensitive ETS-domain TF, regulated by MAPK signaling, and is broadly involved in proliferation and differentiation87. In sheep, ELK1 directly activates BMPR1B transcription in granulosa cells, highlighting its importance in female reproduction88,89. Importantly, integrating our data with P7 oocyte ATAC-seq data62 revealed that ELK1 binding sites are highly accessible in growing oocytes, providing strong evidence for its potential regulatory role in the oocytes. Besides, we also detected high activity and network centrality of SIN3A and HDAC2 in activated oocytes. Given that these factors form the SIN3A/HDAC chromatin-remodeling complex, essential for early embryonic development90, their coordinated activity in activated oocytes suggests a potential epigenetic contribution to follicle activation. Conversely, the dormant-state regulatory network was dominated by BHLHE41, a basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) TF implicated in tissue homeostasis91. In spermatogenesis, BHLHE41 counteracts SOHLH1 to repress spermatogonial differentiation92, raising the intriguing hypothesis that it may analogously suppress activation-associated programs to preserve primordial follicle dormancy in the ovary. Nevertheless, our analysis also revealed that FIGLA maintains high transcriptional activity in dormant oocytes (Fig. 6a), suggesting a previously underappreciated role in establishing or sustaining the quiescent state. Although FIGLA is known to be expressed specifically in female germ cells and essential for primordial follicle assembly85,93, its function in dormant oocytes may be exerted in a context-dependent and synergistic manner, such that the same TF could perform distinct, stage-specific roles through dynamic rewiring of its regulatory network94. Overall, while these findings provide renewed insight into TF-mediated regulation of oocyte fate, they are mainly based on transcriptomic inference and require experimental validation. Future mechanistic studies will be essential to delineate how these TFs orchestrate the fate decision between dormancy and activation in primordial follicles.

In summary, our study provides a detailed exploration of the first fate determination of P2.5 oocytes in primordial follicles, systematically characterizing gene expression and functional differences between activated and dormant oocytes. We identified key genes and regulatory pathways governing the oocyte developmental fates, which may facilitate future research on the pathogenesis of POI and offer potential improvement for in vitro follicle activation techniques.

Methods

Mice

All female C57/BL6 mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. They were housed in a Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) environment under controlled conditions: temperature 20 °C–22 °C, 12/12-hour light/dark cycle, and 50–70% humidity. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All breeding and treatment procedures strictly adhered to the guidelines and regulations of the Animal Care and Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Ethical Number: 2410010). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Isolation of oocytes from embryonic and neonatal mouse ovaries

Female mice were paired with males each afternoon, and the presence of a vaginal plug the following morning was designated as E0.5. The day of birth was designated as P0.5. E17.5 ovaries were obtained by cesarean section. Pregnant dams were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and female embryos (n = 1) were rapidly dissected from the uterine horns. Bilateral ovaries were isolated by microdissection. For postnatal stages, P2.5 female pups (n = 6) and P6.5 female pups (n = 1) were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and bilateral ovaries were carefully excised intact. Oviducts and ovarian bursae were carefully removed under a stereomicroscope in calcium- and magnesium-free Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). The ovaries were enzymatically digested in 500 μL HBSS containing 0.25% trypsin, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 0.01% DNase I at 37 °C for 10 min with gentle agitation. Digestion was terminated by adding 500 μL HBSS supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cell suspensions were centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully aspirated, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μl HBSS. Single-cell suspensions were examined under a stereo microscope (Nikon SMZ1000), and individual germ cells were manually isolated using a mouth pipette and transferred into lysis buffer. Specifically, cyst-stage germ cells were selected from E17.5 suspensions based on a cell diameter of ~10 μm (n = 22); primordial follicle-stage oocytes were selected from P2.5 suspensions with a diameter of ~15–20 μm (n = 317); and primary follicle-stage oocytes were selected from P6.5 suspensions with a diameter of ~20–35 μm (n = 47).

Single-cell RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

Single cells were taken out from −80 °C freezer and thawed on ice. 1 μL of 10 μM oligo-dT primer, 1 μL of 10 mM dNTP mix, and 0.1 μL of ERCC spike-in RNA (1:100,000 dilution, Life Technologies, 4456740) were added to the lysis buffer. cDNA synthesis and amplification were performed following the SMART-seq2 protocol95. The cDNA libraries were then checked on a Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent), and the passed samples were finally chosen for sequencing. Sequencing libraries were constructed by using the KAPA HyperPlus Kit (Kapa Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 150-bp paired-end sequencing, generating at least 20 million reads per single-cell sample.

Single-cell RNA-seq raw data processing

Low-quality bases and adapters were removed using TrimGalore (v0.6.6). The filtered reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) using the STAR (v2.7.10b) aligner96. Sequencing reads were also mapped to ERCC spike-in sequences, which were later used to estimate the absolute gene expression levels in individual oocytes. HTSeq (v0.13.5)97 was used to generate a sample-by-gene read count matrix. RSEM (v1.3.3)98 was used to generate FPKM values matrix. The workflow for single-cell data processing is conducted according to typical analysis procedures9. In brief, file operations were conducted in R (version 4.0.4), and the FPKM matrix of cells and genes was constructed as the input for the Seurat package (v4.3.0)99, which was employed for key analyses, including expression normalization, identification of highly variable genes, data scaling, and principal component analysis (PCA). The number of principal components (PCs) used for downstream analysis was determined using the JackStraw method. Dimensionality reduction was subsequently performed using UMAP. A series of commands was executed to visualize cell clusters, including the RunUMAP function with an appropriate combination of resolution and dims.use, as well as the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions to identify and analyze cell clusters. To identify marker genes of each cluster, the FindAllMarkers function was used to identify conserved marker genes in clusters with default parameters.

Pseudotime trajectory inference

Monocle2 (v2.12.0)56 was used to analyze the pseudotime of single cells and order them along a developmental trajectory. The input for Monocle2 consisted of the FPKM matrix, with cells annotated according to the cluster numbers identified by Seurat. Size factors and dispersions were estimated for the data before trajectory analysis. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the differentialGeneTest function in Monocle2 with a p-value threshold of <0.01. The data were then reduced to two dimensions using the DDRTree algorithm. Additionally, the BEAM function was employed to identify differentially expressed genes at branch points within the trajectory.

Enhanced RNA velocity estimation

PhyloVelo (v0.1)100 was employed to estimate the velocity of transcriptomic dynamics by using monotonically expressed genes. The gene-expression count matrix and Monocle 2-derived low-dimensional coordinates were used as input. Counts were normalized to a library size of 10,000 and log transformed. Cell generations were numerically encoded according to sampling time points (i.e. E17.5, P2.5, and P6.5) to provide a temporal proxy. Velocity fields were inferred using the exact model and projected onto the Monocle 2 embedding. The directionality of the velocity vectors was inverted to align with the developmental trajectory consistent with the biological timing.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

The R package DESeq2 (v1.30.1)101 was used for differential gene expression analysis. Genes with adjusted P value < 0.01 and fold change > 1.2 or < 1/1.2 were considered as differentially expressed. The online tool DAVID102 (version 2022) was used for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis.

TF activity inference and gene regulatory network reconstruction

pySCENIC (v0.12.1)103 was used to identify potential transcriptional regulators. Gene expression FPKM matrix was used as input. Based on this matrix, we constructed co-expression modules between TFs and their potential target genes. A TF and all its direct target genes were defined as a regulon. Regulons for which more than 70% of cells exhibited an activity score of 0 were excluded from further analysis. For the regulon activity heatmap, we applied z-score normalization to the activity of each regulon. Cells were grouped into categories based on the State classification derived from Monocle2 and ordered along the trajectory according to their Pseudotime values provided by the same analysis. We identified cells using the active regulon matrix as input in Seurat to generate the UMAP plot, including cells with a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 0.1 in the analysis. Z-score normalized regulon activity was projected onto the UMAP plot. The transcription factor interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape104. BC was calculated using the CytoNCA plugin in Cytoscape to identify nodes with high connectivity across the network.

Reanalysis of ATAC-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq data

ATAC-seq reads were trimmed and filtered using Trim Galore (v0.6.6) to eliminate low-quality bases and adapter sequences. The filtered reads were aligned to the mm10 mouse genome using BWA-MEM2 (v0.7.17)105, and peaks were identified with MACS2 (v2.2.9.1)106. Promoter regions were annotated using the parameter annotatePeaks.pl in HOMER107 with a 1000 bp window size. The distal H3K27ac data representing enhancers were derived from Bofeng Liu et al62. Motif enrichment analysis was conducted using HOMER107 to identify enriched transcription factor binding sites.

Histological analysis of ovarian sections

Ovarian tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 12 h at room temperature, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Serial sections of 5 μm were cut using a microtome, mounted on glass slides, and dried at 39 °C for 12 h. Before staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated in descending concentrations of ethanol. For morphological evaluation, sections were stained with hematoxylin (Sigma, H9627-25G) to delineate ovarian structures, including cysts (aggregates of germ cells encased by pre-granulosa cells), primordial follicles (oocytes surrounded by a few flattened pre-granulosa cells), and primary follicles (enlarged oocytes encircled by cuboidal granulosa cells).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Preprocessed sections (as described above) were subjected to antigen retrieval and allowed to cool to room temperature. To minimize nonspecific binding, sections were blocked with goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Thereafter, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. Following thorough washing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with an appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibody under dark conditions. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, C1022, Shanghai, China) for 5 min at room temperature, and the slides were mounted with an antifade medium before imaging on a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM900, Zeiss, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Preprocessed sections (as described above) were subjected to antigen retrieval and natural cooling, then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 10 minutes to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. After blocking with goat serum for 30 minutes at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. Following washing in PBS, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ABclonal, AS014) for 1 hour at room temperature. The chromogenic reaction was developed using DAB peroxidase substrate (KeyGEN, KGC3201), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Sigma, H9627-25G) before examination under a light microscope.

Antibodies and reagents

Mouse monoclonal anti-MVH antibody (ab27591) was purchased from Abcam (UK). Rabbit polyclonal anti-ZP3 antibody (A13156) was purchased from ABclonal (China), and rabbit monoclonal anti-FXR1 antibody (R389286) was purchased from ZENBIO (China). Rabbit polyclonal antibodies anti-TEX101 (15447-1-AP), anti-MIOX (17623-1-AP), anti-UHRF1 (21402-1-AP), and anti-FEN1 (14768-1-AP) were acquired from Proteintech (USA). Rabbit monoclonal anti-FOXO3 antibody (D19A7) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). All the aforementioned antibodies were diluted 1:200 for immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining. Additionally, CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (SA00013-2), CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (SA00013-4), CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (SA00013-1), and CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (SA00013-3) were obtained from Proteintech (USA) and diluted 1:200.

Statistical and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.4), unless otherwise specified. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were compared between two groups using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, while categorical variables were analyzed using two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests. For differential analysis, a significance threshold of multi-testing adjusted p < 0.01 was applied. To account for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was used. For the boxplots in the figures, center bars indicate the median of the data, the box edges represent the first and the third quartiles, and the whiskers show the 1.5× interquartile range. For the violin plot, the shaded area represents the data distribution.
