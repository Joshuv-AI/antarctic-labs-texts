“If I am here, it is because the system failed”: a critical qualitative study of global commercial clinical trials for advanced cancer in Chile
Authors: Loreto Fernández-González, Gary Rodin, Jorge Sapunar, Amaya Perez-Brumer, Anne-Emanuelle Birn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe pharmaceutical industry is a key global health actor given its crucial role in the development and testing of medicines. Within Latin America, Chile currently has the highest number of multinational commercial clinical trials per inhabitant, predominantly for oncological therapies. Insufficient attention has been paid to context-specific conditions that enable clinical trials’ successful implementation in the Global South. In examining what makes Chile an attractive hub for commercial trials and why patients enroll in them, this study seeks to elucidate the interplay among the pharmaceutical industry, health systems, and societies. We analyze how trial providers, cancer practitioners, and patient participants draw from their own lived experience to make sense of local healthcare, health policies and global pharmaceutical market trends, amid global health crises.MethodsUsing a critical political economy of global health framework, we conducted a critical ethnography in 2022-23 at a tertiary cancer center that is the lead recruiter of patients into oncology commercial trials in Santiago, Chile. In-depth interviews, participant observation, and document gathering were conducted, with materials analyzed through thematic content analysis using Nvivo12.ResultsForty-seven subjects –fourteen patients, four caregivers, and twenty-nine providers– were interviewed and/or shadowed. The main theme, “A failed (health) system” reveals a perception that the growing presence of commercial clinical trials for advanced cancer works synergistically with a deficient health system. Subthemes of political economy levels of (mal)functioning, were grouped as follows: (i) the COVID-19 pandemic and the global organization of commercial trials; (ii) national factors (health services and post-trial access policies); and (iii) community/relational factors (palliative care and unaffordability of high-cost drugs). Clinical trials were experienced as an exit strategy from a broken healthcare system, even as participants regarded the pricing strategies of anticancer therapies acritically.ConclusionsThe conditions that make Chile an attractive hub for cancer commercial clinical trials are determined by an interplay of global, national, and community-level structural arrangements between public and private actors. Chile serves as an important global health case study on the role of the pharmaceutical industry in capitalizing on systemic healthcare failures to advance clinical research on metastatic cancer.Keywords: Clinical trials, Pharmaceutical industry, Advanced cancer, High-cost drugs, Global health, Ethnography, Political economy, Chile

Background

The pharmaceutical industry is a key global health actor, given its crucial role in the development, testing, commercialization and distribution of medicines [1]. However, there is a vigorous ongoing debate on whether or to what extent the largely North-based Global pharmaceutical industry, as currently configured, is an impediment or facilitator to global health equity [2]. Testing of new medications in the Global South through industry-sponsored clinical trials has been on the rise in recent decades, concomitant with the rise of neoliberal globalization. Yet the interrelation of these trials to global and local political, healthcare system, and epidemiological conditions remains understudied [3]. While biomedical literature focuses on what must remain invariable and consistent in the trials, to adhere to the study protocols regardless of their geographical location, much less attention has been drawn to the context-specific conditions which enable their successful implementation in a country. Moreover, how people in need of high-cost, life-extending medications access the standard of care remains understudied in the Global South.

Chile is the Latin American leader in registered clinical trials per capita [4]. Atop its welcoming of foreign direct investment (FDI) and growing healthcare system challenges [5], Chile’s epidemiological profile –featuring cancer as the leading cause of death since 2019 [6], makes it particularly attractive to clinical trials in oncology, the fastest growing segment of commercial clinical trials [4]. Historically the global prototype for neoliberal social and economic policy implementation [7] is an ideal setting to study these issues. Examining what makes Chile an attractive hub for commercial trials and why patients seek to enroll in them is valuable to understand the interplay among the pharmaceutical industry R&D practices, health systems, and societies.

Aim and structure of the study

This study aims to explore the views of clinical trial providers, cancer care practitioners, and clinical trial enrolled participants in the major industry-sponsored investigative site for cancer trials, on the aspects that facilitate the growing presence of oncology clinical trials in Chile. We analyze how these actors use their own lived experience with clinical trials to make sense of local healthcare and health policies, global pharmaceutical market trends, and the impact of global health contingencies, particularly the COVID-19 pandemic.

We begin by introducing the main research & development (R&D) trends of the global pharmaceutical industry and the rising costs of innovative anticancer therapies worldwide, then briefly describe Chile’s political economy and the context of clinical trials and cancer in the country. Using a critical political economy of global health framework, our critical ethnographic findings reveal the perspectives of both patients and providers through their views and experiences of enrollment in clinical trials for advanced cancer. We end by discussing how these global trends reinforce a neoliberal approach to health and illness, and how the pharmaceutical industry’s pricing strategies and systemic healthcare failures constitute major drivers for enrollment in clinical trials.

Private research & development, the globalization of clinical trials and defining the standard of care

The private pharmaceutical industry is the largest component of global research and development (R&D) spending among OECD countries [8]. Pharmaceutical R&D includes all actions related to the creation and production of new medicines during preclinical, clinical, and post-approval surveillance stages. A significant proportion of Pharma R&D allocation goes to clinical research [1], in which new chemical or biological agents are tested in clinical trials with human subjects. The World Trade Organization’s patent protection rules, in effect since 1995 [9], combined with the profit potential of bringing new drugs to market, have spurred the explosive growth in commercial clinical trials in recent decades [3, 10].

The growing complexity of preclinical and clinical studies makes trials more expensive and lengthier to complete [11]. One solution employed by the pharmaceutical industry to speed up recruitment and to cut R&D costs has been to conduct multinational trials: these are often outsourced to contract research organizations (CROs; private, for-profit companies specialized in conducting clinical trials for pharmaceutical companies) or embedded in hospitals and clinics [10].

In Latin America, this trend of offshoring and globalization of clinical trials has been met with both enthusiasm and concern [12]. Enthusiastic voices cite the region’s demographic diversity, healthcare coverage, large urban areas, and welcoming attitude towards the pharmaceutical industry [13–15]. Indeed, a number of settings regionally offer both lower operational costs and rising cancer incidence, providing a high concentration of “ideal patients” who meet eligibility criteria and are willing to enroll in a clinical trial [11]. Concerns have arisen because most Latin American trials are commercially sponsored and conducted by High Income Country (HIC)-based Transnational Companies (TNCs), whose interests may not be aligned with the needs of vulnerable populations, such as people with advanced cancer; this situation generates tensions between profit motives and the need to protect disadvantaged groups from extractive or exploitative dynamics, especially when facing limited access to the standard of care within their health systems, due to deficient commercialization pathways and coverage schemes [16].

The United States is the largest market for newly developed drugs, making regulatory approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) crucial for commercialization [1, 17]. To ensure equipoise in clinical trials [18], the FDA and other regulatory agencies call for investigational drugs to be compared to the “standard of care,” or “best proven therapy”, particularly on the basis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs)1 [19]. When global RCTs designed in the Global North offer the standard of care to the control groups, enrolling in the trial might be coercive, especially for people living in resource-constrained settings, due to deficient healthcare systems and structural coercion [20–22]. Moreover, these dilemmas endure after regulatory approval, as newly approved drugs may not be available to, or affordable for, the communities from which trial participants are recruited [23].

Oncology and the rising cost of the standard of (cancer) care

Oncology is currently the dominant field in clinical research due to global epidemiological trends of rising incidence and market (profit-seeking) strategies of the pharmaceutical industry [1]. Historically and contemporaneously, oncology has been among the most active specialties for the testing of new pharmaceuticals, with experimental treatments embedded in the clinical care of patients, and enrollment in clinical trials actively encouraged [24]. In the absence of cancer cures, R&D efforts have concentrated on the development of new antineoplastic medications along with label expansion to create new drug combinations for personalized treatments. For people with advanced cancer (metastatic or stage IV), the current standard of care might involve several sequential lines of treatment that delay progression and improve quality of life, meaning that incurable cancer is increasingly no longer an imminent terminal diagnosis [25].

High prices of newly approved therapies constitute a rising concern regarding access and the sustainability of health systems [26]. Critics have shown that despite these high prices, many innovative therapies add little value to cancer care [11, 27]. Nonetheless, this business model has persisted: for example, Merck & Co.’s monoclonal antibody Keytruda© (Pembrolizumab) [28], currently approved for over forty indications, ranked as the bestselling drug in 2023 [29]2, with dissimilar results. The high –and increasing– costs of new cancer drugs has been justified by the pharmaceutical industry as due to high R&D investments, although evidence shows that oncology R&D are recovered in a median of three to four years [27].

Only 12% of the drugs tested in clinical trials are ultimately approved by the FDA [17]. Specifically in cancer, less than 4% drugs tested in a phase I trial receive FDA approval [30], meaning that they fail to prove therapeutic superiority compared to standard treatments. This also means that only a minority of trial participants actually benefit from the investigational drug. Despite this reality, people with cancer across the world seek enrollment in clinical trials, but the majority of the evidence on enrollment motives and experiences largely derives from the Global North, high-income settings [31–36]. Thus, examining multi-site oncology clinical trials in the Global South, where “standard of care” treatment outside of trials is limited or absent, is crucial to understanding the contextual aspects shaping trial enrollment in offshore pharmaceutical trials.

Why Chile? testing pharmaceuticals as a neoliberal experiment

Within Latin America, Chile currently has the highest number of registered ongoing trials per inhabitants (20.8 per million) [4], and it ranks fourth in the number of open clinical trials according to the WHO [37]3. Cancer constituted the main therapeutic research area (N = 56; 39%) of the 143 clinical trials approved by the Chilean Public Health Institute in 2023, and all were industry-sponsored [38]. As of December 2024, there were 86 commercial cancer clinical trials, with 45 actively recruiting, predominantly RCTs [39]. Locally, clinical trials have been portrayed as providing “endless benefits” for the economy, health systems, and patients [4]. These benefits include opportunities for patients to receive innovative treatments still not available on the market and to experience better continuity of care. The Chilean Chamber for Pharmaceutical Innovation [4] also claims higher survival for clinical trial patients, although recent evidence does not support this in cancer [40].

Chile’s governance system, and its approach to clinical trials, stems from the pioneering implementation of radical neoliberal policies during the dictatorship of Augusto Pinochet (1973–1990), which turned the country into one of Latin America’s fastest-growing economies [5, 41] and “probably the “purest” neoliberal republic in the hemisphere” (p.77) [42]. Classified as a high-income country, in 2010 it became the first South American country to join the OECD. Persistent inequalities and asymmetries in power and resources have led to major waves of protests in the past two decades, resulting in unsuccessful initiatives to write a new Constitution to replace the one imposed by Pinochet that established a neoliberal state [43]. A hallmark of this neoliberal approach is represented in Chile’s three-tier health system [44]4.

On the health front, cancer is currently Chile’s leading cause of death, prompting the creation of the National Cancer Plan (2018–2028) and the passage of the National Cancer Act in 2020 [7]. Several types of cancers and palliative care are covered by the Explicit Health Guarantees (GES) Act, which is intended to ensure timely access and financial protection for treatments, regardless of insurance status. Access to high-cost drugs is covered for patients insured through FONASA either through the GES, or other public sources [7]. However, even for GES conditions, healthcare remains fragmented [43]. Civil society groups have criticized the limited access to innovative high-cost drugs, the waiting lists in the public health care system (worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic), and the overall inequality in the public versus the private system [45].

Methods

Study design and theoretical approach

We conducted a critical ethnography (CE) [46] at a private non-profit cancer center in Santiago, Chile between January 2022 and June 2023. Critical ethnography shares with traditional ethnography the use of a combination of techniques to produce a comprehensive account of the culture being studied. We employed in-depth interviewing, participant observation, and document analysis. The present article primarily reports on the results of the interviewing section of the study. CE emphasizes the ethnographer’s immersion in the same power dynamics as the participants in the field, with a focus on the potential of research for emancipatory social change [46]. Consistent with this approach, our fieldwork was characterized by the equal treatment and respect given to every participant, regardless of their professional and social background, political views, and institutional role.

Theoretically, this study uses a critical political economy (CPE) of global health framework, as defined by Birn, Pillay and Holtz [2]. This framework integrates political, social, cultural, historical, and economic processes to understand how health and illness are produced via societal structures, in this context referring to neoliberal capitalist globalization. It pays attention to the global, national, and community levels in terms of wealth distribution and power asymmetries, in order to unravel how health at the individual level is shaped by these factors. Also, it recognizes the role of local agency in challenging and changing public policies and modifying health outcomes. Through the identification and analysis of these determinants and their interrelationships, it is possible to produce an integrative account of the health issues being studied and the social processes that shape them. In our study, CPE guided every step of the inquiry, beginning with the thorough review of global pharmaceutical trends and Chile’s political economy; as well as during the data collection process via the interviewing of a range local actors, including people with cancer (see below).

Study site and settings

The study took place at a private non-profit cancer center in Santiago, the largest national investigative site of oncology commercial trials, predominantly for metastatic cancer [4]. This institution provides a range of cancer treatments and services for people with cancer countrywide, both publicly and privately insured. Commercial clinical trials are run through the Oncological Drugs Research Unit, created in 2011. This unit has over forty employees, including oncologists, trial coordinators, financial and regulatory affairs specialists, technicians, data entry clerks and administrative staff. In 2023, sixty-eight trials were active, and 129 patients were recruited for different trials, mostly Phase III [47].

The fieldwork was organized following Hammersley and Atkinson’s [48] levels of ethnographic inquiry, characterized by an open-ended, exploratory approach. Settings where data were collected included medical consultations (not trial related), tumor boards, the ambulatory chemotherapy unit, and trial coordinators/other professionals’ workplaces.

Sampling and participants

We performed an initial mapping of the actors and social relations of the field, followed by a purposive, maximum variation sampling strategy. Forty-seven subjects were interviewed, comprising fourteen patients (nine women and five men) with a variety of tumor locations (gastrointestinal, genitourinary, lung, gynecological, and skin melanoma), and four caregivers (two women and two men). Most patients (N = 11) had public insurance. Twenty-nine providers (thirteen women and sixteen men) agreed to participate: seven trial coordinators, twelve physicians and ten other professionals. Efforts to ensure diversity among participants included sampling equally, when possible, involving men and women within each category, and a variety of socioeconomic backgrounds.

Patients were invited to participate if they were 18 years or older, had a diagnosis of advanced cancer (metastatic), and were currently or had been enrolled in a commercial clinical trial at the cancer center. Sampling also focused on patients with public and private health insurance, and rural and urban backgrounds. If present at the moment of the interview, their primary caregivers were also invited to participate. Patients cognitively or physically unable to be interviewed or unaware of their diagnosis and/or prognosis were excluded. Clinicians were asked to identify patients suitable for the study and asked their permission to be contacted by the ethnographer. No patient was approached directly without a previous introduction by a member of their care team.

Providers invited to take part in the study included professionals working at the Oncological Drugs Research Unit, such as medical oncologists, trial coordinators, managers, regulatory affairs specialists, and data entry personnel. Clinicians and managers from other units were also invited to include their perspectives on having commercial clinical trials at the cancer center and their overall role in the health system. Sampling focused on inviting diverse providers in terms of general working experience, seniority at the cancer center, and familiarity with clinical trials. This sampling strategy allowed the inclusion of a diverse range of socioeconomic and professional backgrounds.

Data collection

Participants were interviewed and/or shadowed in person at the cancer center or online (vía Zoom) according to their preferences. All interviews were audio or videorecorded and later transcribed verbatim by the ethnographer (LF-G). Interviews focused on: (1) the illness/professional trajectory of the participant that led him/her to be involved in clinical trials, (2) reflecting on the role of the clinical trials in the health system, and (3) an overall appraisal of the current state of things in Chile, by asking participants to identify the main issues and problems in the health system and Chilean society. These elements enabled discussion of a variety of topics, some seemingly unrelated to the clinical trials and cancer, yet highly valuable for providing global and local contextual understanding for the CPE analysis.

In addition to the interviews, over 200 h of participant observation took place at the aforementioned settings. Fieldnotes were taken on paper and later digitally systematized for analysis [49]. Documents identified and collected included newsletters, the cancer center’s website and webinar announcements, internal documents, and public bulletins issued or co-authored by the center. Local regulatory documents were also gathered. Materials provided by patients like partial or full informed consent forms from the trials, tumor board resolution forms, and other documents, were also gathered.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed following thematic content analysis using QSR NVivo V.12 software. We employed a combination of deductive and inductive approaches to analyze and organize the findings. First, inductively, all material was coded and later grouped into a main theme, which captured the overall message that emerged from the data. A second, deductive phase consisted of organizing subthemes stemming from the main theme, clustered according to theoretical framework categories. In vivo codes were prioritized wherever possible to maximize the voice of the participants in the results.

Analysis was performed throughout the execution of the project, following an iterative process. Findings and their theoretical underpinnings were periodically discussed between the ethnographer (LF-G) and the coauthors. A main tenet of ethnography is the distinction between emic and etic perspectives [48]; equal consideration of both perspectives is essential to CE, ensuring a rigorous analysis attuned to the reflexivity of the researcher. This reflexivity entails continuous acknowledgement of the researcher’s privileges and biases [46]. Special consideration was given to how attitudes and interactions were shaped by institutional hierarchies, and how participants positioned the ethnographer as a female, non-physician employee of the cancer center, particularly when given access to observations and review of documents.

Ethical considerations

This project was reviewed and approved by the Social Sciences, Humanities & Education Research Ethics Board (REB) of the University of Toronto (protocol #00041570), and the Instituto Oncológico Fundación Arturo López Perez REB in Santiago, Chile (protocol #2021-037-PSY-SIN-INT), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the details of the study before taking part and provided written informed consent.

This project is a doctoral dissertation at the University of Toronto’s Dalla Lana School of Public Health. All recommended public health measures were taken to protect participants and the researcher (LF-G) against COVID-19 during fieldwork. The study site was contacted early in the protocol-writing process and the conditions of access were agreed upon beforehand with key actors at the cancer center. After REB approval, access to the proposed settings was renegotiated with these actors. Every step of the fieldwork was approved by both providers and patients: their suggestions were followed regarding who to approach next, and what to focus upon during observations.

Results

Findings were organized around the main theme of “A failed (health) system”, reflecting multiple levels of the political economy. Subthemes were grouped according to the levels of the political economy of global health framework. At the global level, the analysis focused on epidemiological trends and the global organization of commercial trials. Nationally, the findings revealed significant tensions and issues on the country’s health services and posttrial access policies. At the community/relational level, participants reported a dismissal of palliative care as a therapeutic option, and the challenges related to the unaffordability of high-cost drugs. Together, these interconnected levels illustrate how systemic failures shape access to care. The pharmaceutical industry was perceived to have a ubiquitous presence on all three levels. Clinical trials were often portrayed or experienced in terms of accessing a strategy to exit a broken system and pursue other options (Table 1).

Main theme: “A failed (health) system”

Across participants, there was a strong consensus about a “failed (health) system” in Chile. The account of Miguel (a pseudonym), a taxi driver from Santiago with metastatic bladder cancer enrolled in a clinical trial at the center, illustrates this. After undergoing emergency surgery at a public hospital, Miguel was put on a waiting list for further treatment. He complained to FONASA and was finally referred to a private hospital. He recounted how, after being initially given a prognosis of three months to live, he was offered enrollment in a clinical trial at the same center. Almost four years later, he was in the follow-up phase of the trial and was convinced that without it, he would be dead.

If I am here, it is because the health system (la salud) failed. I was left two months waiting for an urgent second surgery (…) and they didn’t call me. (…) I could have died (…) a lot of people die waiting for a surgery.

In this sense, the failure of the public health care system to provide timely and proper care opened the door to receiving privately sponsored experimental treatment.

For clinicians, referring patients to clinical trials was mainly a strategy for prescribing a standard of care otherwise unavailable. An oncologist who worked as the principal investigator of several trials in the Oncological Drugs Research Unit, while also running a clinic at the cancer center, shared an example of how he normally assessed the therapeutic options patients had depending on their insurance, and how entering the trial ensures access to the standard of care:

So sometimes it happens that here we cannot provide the standard treatment, but the study does offer it in one of its branches. So those are the patients that we prioritize. For example, today I sent another patient with a metastatic kidney cancer, whose standard [of care] is immunotherapy, for treatment [at the trial], because in the [public] hospital they only give her pazopanib, a pill which was used before; it used to be the standard, but is not anymore. Since I know that the [current] standard is something else, I prefer to give her access to the standard care [trial], in any way possible. Because the study branches are immunotherapy for both sides, plus or minus pills – which are different pills than the ones she will get by the GES. So, that is what I do. (medical oncologist who also works as clinical trial provider)

Hence, this failed system describes a landscape wherein the availability of commercial clinical trials works synergistically with –and indeed benefits from– a deficient national health system. This synergy did not affect every actor equally: industry-sponsored clinical trials were described as thriving in these conditions. The cancer center was committed to a strategic plan to increase recruitment, into existing and newly opened clinical trials. The Oncological Drugs Research Unit had also been experiencing a high rotation of clinical trial coordinators, data entry clerks, and regulatory affairs specialists, due to competition between CROs and local branches of global pharmaceutical companies to attract skilled professionals.

It is important to underscore that Miguel’s critique went beyond health care (“la salud”). By his account, the failure of the health care system to provide adequate care reflected a societal fracture whereby people have stopped listening to one another: “The most relevant thing here, why people go crazy, [is because] the people are unheard”. Broadly speaking, these conditions stem from a degraded state and political system, where politicians are governing for their own interests: “I have believed in those people [politicians], I voted for these people, and then you feel disillusioned. In my case, many governments have passed, and I feel the disillusion, and I just don’t believe anymore”. Miguel’s words capture the frustration and impotence of patients who are participating in clinical trials, usually after navigating public health care and/or incurring in significant bills.

Global/National level

“Obviously with the pandemic everything got worse”

The COVID-19 pandemic aggravated the waiting lists for cancer patients. At the same time, it offered an opportunity for the pharmaceutical industry to showcase the importance of R&D regarding vaccine development and clinical trials at a global scale.

According to several providers, referrals to clinical trials soared during the pandemic. Because health services were prioritized for COVID-19 patients, people with cancer (and other conditions) in the public system were left untreated and added to waiting lists. In the words of a female nurse who worked as trial coordinator: “Obviously with the pandemic everything got worse”, in the public healthcare system. Another senior trial coordinator pointed out that due to the suspension of screening and early detection initiatives, after the pandemic, a high number of patients were diagnosed and finally able to seek treatment; however, since their care was delayed, they had worse prognoses.

Notably, this crisis served as a window of opportunity for the implementation and running of trials. While most health services were either suspended or performed remotely, clinical trials continued to function in-person: “we never stopped”, as one regulatory affairs specialist stated. Reasons for this included mandatory in-person presence for paperwork and clinical appointments, as well as compliance with lab exams and treatment timings, to avoid breaches in procedures specified by research protocols. Thus, in practice, unlike the public healthcare system, trials remained open and ongoing, offering an option for patients to obtain treatment within the trials. As one medical oncologist who worked in clinical trials during the pandemic recalls:

[During the pandemic] we had an explosion of patients, who came from the public system, who had no treatment alternatives and who, basically went to be treated there, referred by their oncologists (…), from all Chile, they came to be treated (…), it was greatly influenced by the lack of timely care at the hospitals, especially the public hospitals, where you did see patients whose GES deadlines were not being met.

In Chile, the pandemic was framed by the Ministry of Science and Technology “as an opportunity to rethink” [50] and boost the local scientific community. Several trial coordinators mentioned, during participant observation conversations, news of COVID-19 vaccine trials helped to educate the public about the scientific rigor and transparency associated with the testing of pharmaceuticals.

“It is the same trial” The globalization of commercial trials not only involves the study protocol but also the monetization of the clinical trial components. Sizeable fees make the field extremely attractive to professionals and institutions. Providers reported that pharmaceutical sponsors pay higher fees than public and private insurers for services such as lab tests and hospitalization days. In other words, it is more lucrative to conduct clinical trials than to provide general cancer care services. Health professionals not working in commercial trials argued that financial incentives can translate into downplaying potential risks for patients. As one oncologist critically put it: “it is assumed that [the trial] has a benefit”.

Managers and trial coordinators justified this difference: “The medical oncologist here is worth the same or more than the one in the United States, it is the same trial” (authors’ emphasis), as a trial coordinator put it, implying that fees were negotiated having the US prices as a parameter. For insurers, too, trials are particularly enticing, as most costs during participation in the trial are covered by the sponsor, including addressing adverse events.

Providers working in trials claimed that public hospitals could benefit greatly by hosting commercial clinical trials inside their facilities –and not just refer them to external CROs, where often the same oncologists work part time.

Each hospital should have its own [clinical trials] unit, each clinic. It would be ideal; it would eliminate the eternal waiting lists (…). And many patients could be treated in the research unit with zero costs for the hospital, faster and with an income for the hospital with a benefit for the patient and with zero cost for anyone. (senior trial coordinator)

National/community level

(Unintended) consequences of the Ricarte Soto Act and post-trial access The Ricarte Soto Act (N°20850), passed in Chile in 2015, posthumously honors the journalist Ricarte Soto, who, became a patient advocate denouncing the lack of access to costly medicines. The law created a universal system of financial protection for high-cost drugs and devices. It also contained important modifications to clinical trial policies, including the implementation of the Helsinki Declaration recommendation on post-trial access to therapeutics. The law states the right of research participants to enjoy indefinite continuation of treatments received in the study, free of charge, as long as “therapeutic utility subsists”, to be paid by the trial sponsor. The law prompted the suspension of 23 trials with 1328 active participants during the first months after its promulgation, due to concerns of paying for post-trial treatment [51].

Senior trial coordinators interviewed in our study recalled the “before and after” consequences of the Soto Act, with clinical trials being relocated in other Latin American countries. These professionals viewed this trend as a flaw in the legislation forcing pharmaceutical companies to comply: to them, it reinforced the notion that the state works as a barrier to private R&D, thereby harming patients. They considered that the posttrial access mandate of the Ricarte Soto Act threatened the financial sustainability of the clinical trial industry by compelling the continuity of treatment to trial participants. As a result, most pharmaceutical sponsors switched the focus of their trials to illnesses with limited life expectancy, such as advanced cancer.

In 2015 the famous Ricarte Soto Act appeared, and it was disastrous, because it was made by a guy sitting at a desk who had no idea about how clinical trials operate (…). The sponsor is not really responsible for a cancer patient because he is not going to live forever, being super honest. (senior trial coordinator)

“A last resort”

Avoiding public health care and its problems is a major reason why clinical trials are considered a better option, according to patients and providers alike. Waiting lists, crowded services, inadequate or aging infrastructure, and mistreatment by providers were frequent characterizations of care at public hospitals. Several interviewees shared anecdotes about relatives or acquaintances who “died waiting” for cancer treatment in the public system. Accessing for free the latest standard of care (typically high-cost drugs) –plus all of the supportive care, exams and travel allowance– was highlighted as the major benefit of being a trial participant.

I think that people die of sadness, that they have to be there at five o’clock in the morning to take a number, if you are lucky, they will give it to you, and if the doctor showed up at work (…). The medicine here for the poor is awful. (patient participant)

Going private and paying out of pocket was not an option for any of the patients and caregivers interviewed, also consistent with the message repeatedly relayed by providers: “trials provide access”. Participants with public insurance often underscored that getting treatment at the cancer center outside of the scope of the trial would be impossible. Tumor board meetings frequently discussed clinical cases of people from both urban and rural areas who, after spending months seeking treatment, had been referred by public or private insurers, or were willing to copay out-of-pocket for specific treatments according to their purchasing power. Given this context, referrals for eligibility screening for a clinical trial emerged as a welcome option that promised to deliver prompt care to patients.

[The trial is] a last resort, because everything in the system has failed. There are some who have been waiting months for a call from FONASA, or from GES and they never called them. (senior trial coordinator)

Community/Individual level

“Just painkillers” All patients and clinicians were asked about the therapeutic alternatives to enrolling in a trial. A subtheme that emerged was the idea that “it was either the study or nothing”. Several patients claimed that they were “declared terminally ill at the hospital” and “were sent home to die”. In many cases, this meant that they had been referred to palliative care. Clinical trial informed consent forms, normally comprising 25 to 30 pages, included on average half a page describing alternatives to the trial and defining palliative care in three to four lines. The stance that the trial was better than usual care (including palliative care) was echoed by clinicians: “the trial will always be better” as claimed one oncologist. Another trial coordinator conveyed that outside the trial, patients would be given “toxic chemos from the 50s”. This perception was supported by the view articulated by various patients that the trial was a “salvation”, a “blessing”, and/or the only thing that kept them alive. Like Miguel, other patients mentioned that they had outlived their initial prognosis before enrolling in the trial. As the son of one trial participant narrated:

The clinical trial was like the only salvation we had, because in the [public] hospital they were super harsh, too. I mean, it was like “the only thing I’m going to give you here are pills,” he [the doctor] said, and “you don’t have a chance of being cured.”

“ Just painkillers”, his father added.

“Not even millionaires”

Participants emphasized that no one can access high-cost drugs on their own – “not even the Farkas”- (a famous Chilean millionaire and philanthropist), as one patient noted. For patients with private insurance, copayment of high-cost drugs and all related costs of treatments was considered to be unaffordable. Patients enrolled in trials tended toward feeling fortunate in being able to receive costly treatments, yet “not paying a dime” –as one patient said- even as they blamed the state for the financial hardships associated with health expenditures. These hardships were intertwined with other challenges, such as inadequate pensions, poor housing, and rising neighborhood crime. Participants strongly criticized the state and government for the country’s social and economic inequality and failure to address these issues, while remaining almost completely silent about the role of the pharmaceutical industry. In other words, patients did not question why the medicines they needed were so expensive that they had to enter a clinical trial in order to access them.

Opinions were divided among providers regarding how they felt about the pricing strategies of the pharmaceutical industry. Some justified that profits were necessary to incentivize companies to invest in R&D. This group of professionals tended to adhere to the notion that the most important purpose of clinical trials in Chile is providing access to innovative, high-cost drugs, and that trials are generally safe. Others voiced an ethical dilemma: as one professional put it: “I sometimes wonder if I am working on the right side”. Still others expressed satisfaction and pride in their work, consisting of providing costly treatments for free to patients.

In the end, what I like is having the possibility of offering a first-world medication […] to a patient who comes from a rural area or a FONASA patient, to have this opportunity to be treated with a drug which would not be possible in other circumstances. (medical oncologist who also works as clinical trial provider)

A third group of professionals (whose jobs were not related to clinical trials) were critical of the pharmaceutical industry and felt that the high cost of drugs coerced patients into taking part in the trials; they were also critical of their colleagues who worked with the trials. For example, an oncologist mentioned during participant observation exchanges that they felt their colleagues were whitewashing their financial interests by claiming they helped to bring access to patients.

Discussion

The rising incidence of cancer in Chile in recent decades has been met with insufficient health care services countrywide. Concomitantly, the country’s hosting of an increasing number of commercial clinical trials, predominantly cancer-related [38, 52], means that today Chile has the highest per capita clinical trial availability in Latin America. This critical qualitative study conducted at the main investigative site for commercial cancer trials, serves as a case study that provides nuanced perspectives on the processes enabling the successful implementation and execution of these trials for patients with advanced cancer, highlighting the interplay between systemic healthcare deficiencies and the growing influence of pharmaceutical innovations in shaping illness journeys. Findings suggest that the profound crisis in cancer care in a neoliberal healthcare system, along with increasing penetration of high-cost drugs as the standard of care for metastatic cancer, are major drivers for clinical trial enrollment.

Our results underscore that the perception of benefits of trial enrollment is context-dependent and closely tied to the gaps observed between the standard of care versus usual care provided. In other words, the attractiveness of clinical trials as a therapeutic option for patients with advanced cancer is the flip side of a health system that is experienced by patients and health providers as abusive, unequal, and poorly managed. Access to innovative high-cost drugs, lab exams, and related procedures for free, at a state-of-the-art private hospital, was contrasted by participants with waiting lists, out-of-reach (expensive) medications, and resorting to palliative care characterizing the public healthcare system. In this sense, global commercial clinical trials set the bar on what is considered the optimal standard of care and make it available under experimental conditions [11, 20].

Global pharmaceutical industry R&D trends, including offshoring trials to the Global South and commercializing new cancer therapies as high-cost drugs, collide with the ongoing crises and limitations of most public healthcare systems. Indeed, the rising cost of medicines is part of a global trend that puts a strain on all healthcare systems and has been critiqued by the World Health Organization [53].

Notably, participants perceived that the state was the key actor at fault: the responsibility of the pharmaceutical industry in the pricing of high-cost drugs was only tangentially mentioned as problematic. This acritical position coexists with strong demands for innovative, life-extending treatments. The same phenomenon has occurred in Chile regarding other health conditions, particularly rare diseases [54]. While the Ministry of Health has developed special budgets for high-cost drugs, these policies are far from a solution. Other Latin American scholars have documented how the right to health has been progressively turning into a right to pharmaceuticals, transforming people into patient-citizen-consumers [55].

In Chile, the pharmaceutical industry has pursued an active agenda to promote clinical trials and attract sponsors, highlighting benefits such as longer survival rates and improvements in the quality of health services [4], and claiming for better coverage rather than problematizing their responsibility on blocking access [56]. In this scenario, the state is portrayed as a deficient manager of the health care system, and also as a barrier to access to innovative drugs. The strong critique of the Ricarte Soto Act and its consequences illustrates this conundrum. Yet, critical discussion is missing regarding how pricing and marketing practices may deepen care disparities or the impact of potential conflicts of interest and ethical concerns, such as coercion.

Our analysis suggests that participation in commercial clinical trials reinforces and reproduces a neoliberal approach to illness and healthcare. This is consistent with what has been described by other scholars in other contexts and regarding other health conditions [10, 20, 57–60]. Such medical neoliberalism is particularly salient in the US, where underinsurance and intersectional vulnerability are behind enrollment in clinical trials among both ill people and health volunteers [57–60]. In Latin America, the ethical tensions of profit-seeking pharmaceuticals in the implementation of clinical trials have been discussed by several authors [10, 12, 16, 23], and our research adds to the evidence on the subject.

Our findings add crucial ethnographic depth to these reflections. Using the clinical trial as a strategy to access clinical care has also been reported about HIV clinical trials in Peru [61]; however, in our sample we did not find presence of mistrust, which might be explained due to the stigma surrounding both illnesses (cancer and HIV), and other structural and social phenomena.

The discontent of patients and providers with cancer care in Chile is part of a larger, generalized discontent with longstanding structural conditions that characterize the contemporary Chilean state and society. Our findings resonate with what has been theorized in sociology as a uniquely Chilean neoliberal subjectivity, traversed by ambivalence [62, 63]. Many in Chile can identify the benefits of improved living conditions, compared to prior generations, but within a “system” that feels imposed, alienated by consumerism, and associated too much with the legacy of the dictatorship. A core component of this neoliberal subjectivity is the ability to take advantage of every opportunity that is presented to individuals. This last feature captures the trajectories of several of the patients we interviewed and reflects how various trial providers felt about why patients should seek to enter a trial. Several patients expressed a personal satisfaction with the fact that the drugs they were receiving were extremely costly and articulated concern and fear of returning to the public healthcare system. The clinical trial offered an opportunity to be treated with dignity and politeness within a medical setting, despite not paying, which was experienced as exceptional.

This study, like any investigation, has both strengths and limitations. To our knowledge, these are the first data published on the subject of commercial clinical trials in Chile using qualitative methods. The study site is the national leader in cancer commercial clinical trials, actively recruiting patients, and as such, serves as a valuable case study on the subject. Our results enrich the literature examining the global pharmaceutical industry in the Global South, and specifically regarding clinical trials enrollment in oncology. As well, we have produced a rich account of both patients and providers about some of the challenges of living with metastatic cancer in Chile. The critical ethnographic design includes in-depth interviewing and observation of the social phenomena and includes valuable insights concerning the experiences and views of the participants, in their own words. Additionally, the political economy of global health framework allows for comprehensive analysis of the determinants, both distal and proximal [2], which, in combination with critical ethnography methodology, interrogate the status quo and the material conditions of individuals and their subjectivities [46].

Limitations of this study include the impossibility of shadowing participants outside the cancer center, mostly due to COVID-19 protection measures. Due to time constraints and the health status of patients, long-term shadowing was not feasible. However, the ethnographer remotely followed up with most of the interviewed patients, who shared their views and life stories, out of genuine gratitude and concern for their health. Also, we did not interview actors from the pharmaceutical industry or other clinical trial sites/CROs, which might affect the generalizability of the findings.

Future research should expand the understanding of clinical trial participation beyond cancer and in other settings. Including other investigative sites, CROs in the country, and similar data from other Latin American countries would provide a regional landscape on the association between the issues in accessing life-extending treatments, health systems deficiencies, regulatory frameworks, and the growing interest in commercial clinical trials. Critical appraisal of informed consent processes is crucial for analyzing the ethical dilemmas of using clinical trials as a strategy to access high-cost drugs in experimental conditions. The distinction between the standard of care as defined by multinational clinical trials protocols and the context-dependent usual care must be accurately appraised and taken into account by decision makers and REBs when analyzing the benefits and risks posed by clinical trials and the global testing of pharmaceuticals. Additionally, the perspectives of policymakers, health authorities, and key informants in the pharmaceutical sector and civil society would add valuable viewpoints to the issue of rising costs of cancer treatments and the role of clinical trials therein.

Conclusions

The conditions that make Chile an attractive hub for cancer commercial clinical trials do not respond to a single cause. They are determined by a complex interplay of global, national, and community structural arrangements between public and private actors that shape the living conditions of people with metastatic cancer.

The aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic in the country is concatenated with recent policy changes regarding posttrial regulations, the outrageous inequality between public and private healthcare systems, and the unaffordability of newer antineoplastic agents. While this business model is lucrative and reinforces the clinical trials market, the Chilean example shows that it does not enhance the usual care that people get within their healthcare systems. On the contrary, the gap perceived between the standard of care and the usual care available was one of the main motives for choosing to enroll in trials when facing an advanced disease. Chile serves as a case study for global health on the role of the pharmaceutical industry in capitalizing on systemic healthcare failures to advance clinical research on metastatic cancer.

Critical approaches and methods are indispensable tools for global health, serving to question dominant narratives and power asymmetries that threaten the public good in the Global South. In particular, critical qualitative inquiry enables an in-depth description of settings and their social underpinnings that are not visible to the biomedical gaze. By illuminating the taken-for-granted but not-documented social phenomena surrounding clinical trials, these approaches allow for critical examination of this essential step of the R&D process that has increasingly been conducted by Global North-based private, for-profit entities in the Global South. As clinical trials are increasingly offshored, we must interrogate whether the pharmaceutical industry shares the interests of the communities where trials are conducted or mostly benefits from their troubles. More importantly, we must question what actions governments are taking to ensure accountability from the industry and that dignified care is available to everyone without the need to enroll in an experimental protocol.
