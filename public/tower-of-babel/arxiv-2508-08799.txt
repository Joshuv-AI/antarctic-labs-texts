Measurement-Based Quantum Diffusion Models
Xinyu Liu, Jingze Zhuang, Wanda Hou, Yi-Zhuang You

Abstract
We introduce measurement-based quantum diffusion models that bridge classical and quantum diffusion theory through randomized weak measurements. The measurement-based approach naturally generates stochastic quantum trajectories while preserving purity at the trajectory level and inducing depolarization at the ensemble level. We address two quantum state generation problems: trajectory-level recovery of pure state ensembles and ensemble-average recovery of mixed states. For trajectory-level recovery, we establish that quantum score matching is mathematically equivalent to learning unitary generators for the reverse process. For ensemble-average recovery, we introduce local Petz recovery maps for states with finite correlation length and classical shadow reconstruction for general states, both with rigorous error bounds. Our framework establishes Petz recovery maps as quantum generalizations of reverse Fokker-Planck equations, providing a rigorous bridge between quantum recovery channels and classical stochastic reversals. This work enables new approaches to quantum state generation with potential applications in quantum information science.

A key advantage of our measurement-based approach is that measurement serves dual purposes: it drives the forward stochastic process while simultaneously extracting information about the quantum state. This information can be used to condition the reverse process, enabling the construction of state-dependent control Hamiltonian or quantum processes. This capability is unique to measurement-based approaches and is not available in other quantum diffusion frameworks that rely on noise channels or unitary scrambling. Admittedly, several works have explored measurement-driven quantum processes for recovering target quantum state ensembles

;
Kwun et al. 2025
;
Zhang and Chen 2026
;

Our theoretical framework establishes several important connections. We show that the average behavior of the measurement-induced SDE is described by a Lindblad master equation, providing the quantum analog of the Fokker-Planck equation in classical diffusion. We prove that Petz recovery maps serve as the quantum generalization of reverse Fokker-Planck equations.

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{traj.}}\!\ket{\psi_{t}}\!\bra{\psi_{t}}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\mathds{1}/2^{n}

This does not contradict the fact that each conditional state

Thus, randomized weak measurement can preserve purity at the trajectory level while inducing complete depolarization at the ensemble level, realizing a trivializing forward quantum diffusion.

Consequently, reverse quantum diffusion admits two natural generative objectives:

(i)

Trajectory-level recovery:
sample from or reconstruct the trajectory-level pure-state distribution
p(\psi_{t})
induced by the forward process;

(ii)

Ensemble-average recovery:
reconstruct the averaged state

at each diffusion time
t
.

Whereas much of the literature

target (ii) of reconstructing
\bar{\rho}_{t}

Crucially, prior approaches cast denoising as a state-preparation problem, seeking optimal protocols that act universally on all states (regardless of measurement record). By contrast, our measurement-based framework formulates denoising as a control problem that learns control Hamiltonian conditioned on the measurement record of each trajectory.

In this work, we address both objectives, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
For trajectory-level recovery (i), we develop a

approach that learns a time-reversal unitary evolution from measurement records.
For ensemble-average recovery (ii), we consider two complementary strategies: a

map for quantum implementation, and

for purely classical post-processing.

III
Reverse Quantum Diffusion

III.1
Control Hamiltonian Learning

III.1.1
Classical Decoder

The reverse diffusion task can be formulated as a control problem: given the current state of the system, a denoising model must condition on this state and propose control operations that drive the system backward in time. In classical systems, the state variables coincide with physical observables, which can be directly accessed, copied, and processed by the denoising model to generate classical control actions. In quantum systems, however, there is a fundamental separation between the underlying quantum state and measurable observables. The quantum state cannot be directly accessed; it can only be inferred from repeated measurements. Yet, quantum trajectories are not reproducible, making it impossible to reconstruct intermediate states along the trajectory via quantum state tomography without severe post-selection overhead. As a result, a quantum denoising algorithm does not have direct access to the state and must base its control on indirect and noisy inference, which is substantially more challenging than in the classical case.

To address this difficulty, we introduce a
classical decoder
that works alongside the quantum denoising model. The decoder is a classical algorithm that infers the sequence of Pauli expectation values
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}
from the measurement record
\{(O_{t},o_{t})\}
. Such a decoder can be implemented using a classical simulator or, alternatively, learned as a data-driven inference model. In this work, to demonstrate the feasibility of this approach, we employ a simulation-based decoder, acknowledging that it is not scalable to large quantum systems.

The Kraus-operator formulation in Sec.
II.3
provides a concrete basis for constructing a classical decoder.
Given a measurement record
\mathcal{O}
, the classical decoding algorithm proceeds as:

(i)

Infer the initial state

\ket{\psi_{0}}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{\ket{\psi}}\,\|K_{T}(\mathcal{O})\,\ket{\psi}\|^{2}

(22)

by maximum-likelihood estimation.

(ii)

Reconstruct the intermediate states by forward Kraus map

\ket{\psi_{t}}=\frac{K_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\ket{\psi_{0}}}{\|K_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\ket{\psi_{0}}\|}.

(23)

(iii)

Compute the Pauli expectation values
{\bm{z}}_{t}=\langle\psi_{t}|{\bm{P}}|\psi_{t}\rangle
for all time
t
to construct
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}
. These calculations assume the availability of a classical simulator.

While this simulation-based method might not be the most efficient form of classical decoding, it serves as a proof-of-principle. More scalable alternatives, such as data-driven learning-based decoders, will be left for future investigation.

III.1.2
Quantum Score Matching

Given the time series
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}
decoded from the weak measurement record, reversing the diffusion becomes a quantum control problem. The objective is to learn a control Hamiltonian
H_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t},t)
(with trainable parameter
\theta
) that drives
\ket{\psi_{t}}
backward along its trajectory. Since pure states remain pure under the forward diffusion

1

\mathrm{d}\ket{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}=\mathrm{i}\,H_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t)\,\ket{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(24)

This is analogous to the probability flow ODE approach in classical diffusion models

.

We introduce a
unitary score function

V_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t):=\exp(\mathrm{i}\,H_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t)
, representing the
\mathrm{d}t

\ket{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}
to
\ket{\psi_{t}}
. It is trained by minimizing the mean infidelity loss

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=1-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi_{t},\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\big|\!\bra{\psi_{t}}V_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t)\ket{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\!\big|^{2},

(25)

where
\ket{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}=K_{\mathrm{d}t}(O_{t},\mathrm{d}o_{t})\ket{\psi_{t}}
is generated by a step of forward diffusion, and

{\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}=\bra{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}{\bm{P}}\ket{\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}

is the corresponding Pauli expectation values. Both can be inferred from measurement data given a classical decoder/simulator. Once trained, the reverse process is implemented by applying
V_{\theta}({\bm{z}},t)
at each step, generating a purity-preserving backward evolution.

This formulation is formally equivalent to the score-matching denoising loss for the SDE in
eq.

7

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bm{z}}_{t},{\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\Big\|&{\bm{s}}_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}-{\bm{f}}({\bm{z}}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t,t)\\
&+\frac{{\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}-{\bm{z}}_{t}-{\bm{f}}({\bm{z}}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big\|^{2}.\end{split}

(26)

In classical diffusion models, the score function

{\bm{s}}({\bm{z}},t)=\nabla_{{\bm{z}}}\log p_{t}({\bm{z}})

is the gradient of the log-likelihood and determines the drift required to reverse the diffusion process.
A key distinction in the quantum setting is that the reverse drift takes the form

{\bm{s}}_{\theta}({\bm{z}},t)\rightarrow\nabla_{{\bm{z}}}\!\left[\Sigma({\bm{z}},t)\,\log p_{t}({\bm{z}})\right]
,
where
\Sigma_{ij}({\bm{z}},t)=g_{i}({\bm{z}},t)g_{j}({\bm{z}},t)
is a state-dependent noise covariance matrix.
This difference arises because quantum noise originates from measurement back-action that depends on the conditional state, whereas in classical diffusion—particularly in machine learning applications—Gaussian noise is added independently of the state.
Despite this modification, the modeling and training of score functions are well established

\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}
serving as the classical training data.

By identifying

\begin{split}&2^{-n}\big({\bm{s}}_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}-{\bm{f}}({\bm{z}}_{t},t),t)-{\bm{f}}({\bm{z}}_{t},t)\big)\cdot{\bm{P}}\\
&=-\mathrm{i}\,[H_{\theta}({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t)\,,\,\rho_{t}],\end{split}

(27)

where
\rho_{t}=\ket{\psi_{t}}\bra{\psi_{t}}
.
eq.

25
and
eq.

become proportional.
Thus, learning the SDE score term

III.1.3
Error Bound Analysis

Given a time-dependent control Hamiltonian

t=0

V[H]\;=\;\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}\exp\!\Big(\mathrm{i}\int_{T}^{0}H({\bm{z}}_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big),

(28)

where
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
denotes reverse time-ordering.
Acting with
V[H]
on each final state
\ket{\psi_{T}}
generates a
reverse-propagated ensemble

\mathcal{E}_{0}[H]=\big\{V[H]\ket{\psi_{T}}:\ket{\psi_{T}}\in\mathcal{E}_{T}\big\},

(29)

where
\mathcal{E}_{T}
denotes the final state ensemble
t=T
.
The corresponding
average state
at
t=0
is obtained by averaging over the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{0}[H]
,

\bar{\rho}_{0}[H]=\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{T}}V[H]\ket{\psi_{T}}\!\bra{\psi_{T}}V^{\dagger}[H].

(30)

To derive an error bound for the entire pure-state ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{0}[H]
produced by the backward unitary evolution, we employ the Wasserstein distance as a metric on the space of pure-state ensembles, since the Wasserstein distance compares full probability distributions rather than just their averages. Suppose the learned generative model (with control Hamiltonian
H_{\theta}
) produces an ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{0}[H_{\theta}]=\{\,|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|:\phi\sim p_{\theta}(\phi)\,\}
of pure states drawn from a distribution
p_{\theta}
, and that the true ensemble of pure states is
\mathcal{E}_{0}[H_{\mathrm{true}}]=\{\,|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|:\psi\sim p_{\mathrm{true}}(\psi)\,\}
for some distribution
p_{\mathrm{true}}
. The Wasserstein-1 distance (with trace distance as the cost function) between
p_{\theta}
and
p_{\mathrm{true}}
is defined as
Zhang et al. 2024a

\begin{split}&W_{1}(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})\\
&=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})}\int D(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle)\;\pi\big(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle\big)\;\mathrm{d}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\psi,\end{split}

(31)

where
\Pi(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})
denotes the set of all couplings (joint distributions)
\pi(\phi,\psi)
having marginals
p_{\theta}(\phi)
and
p_{\mathrm{true}}(\psi)
. In other words,
\pi(\phi,\psi)
satisfies
\int\pi(\phi,\psi)\,d\phi=p_{\mathrm{true}}(\psi)
and
\int\pi(\phi,\psi)\,d\psi=p_{\theta}(\phi)
. The cost function
D(|\phi\rangle,|\psi\rangle)
is the trace distance between the pure states
|\phi\rangle
and
|\psi\rangle
.

Theorem 1
.

Distribution Convergence Theorem. Let
p_{\mathrm{true}}
be the true initial-state distribution, and let
p_{\theta}
be the distribution obtained by running the reverse diffusion process for time
T
under the learned control unitary
V_{\theta}
. Assume
V_{\theta}
satisfies

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi_{t},\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\sqrt{1-\left|\langle\psi_{t+dt}|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle\right|^{2}}\leq\epsilon dt
,
and that it is Lipschitz continuous such that for any pure state
|\alpha\rangle
,

D(V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z},t)|\alpha\rangle,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}^{\prime},t)|\alpha\rangle)\leq L_{V}dtD(|\psi\rangle,|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)
, where
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
are the state vectors of
|\psi\rangle
and
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
, respectively. Then by choosing a stopping time
T=\frac{C}{L_{v}}\ln(1/\epsilon)
with
0<C<1
, the Wasserstein-1 distance between
p_{\theta}
and
p_{\mathrm{true}}
is bounded by

W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\mathrm{true}})\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{L_{v}}\epsilon^{1-C}+\epsilon^{-C}\,E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)

(32)

with
E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)
decaying exponentially in
T
as
ne^{-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}T}
. If we choose a sufficiently large measurement strength
\gamma
such that
\frac{4\gamma}{3nL_{v}}\geq 1
, the leading-order behavior of the bound for small
\epsilon
is dominated by the first term in
eq.

32
, and
W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\mathrm{true}})\;\to\;0
as
\epsilon\to 0.

Proof sketch
. The
1
-Wasserstein distance
W_{1}(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})
between the learned and true distributions accumulates over time due to the small per-step discrepancy
\epsilon dt
between their reverse diffusion processes. At each infinitesimal time step, this deviation is amplified at most by the Lipschitz factor
e^{L_{V}dt}
. Meanwhile,
E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)
quantifies how far the distribution obtained by forward diffusion up to time
T
is from a uniform random product-state distribution, and this deviation is exponentially small in
T
. Combining these results and choose
T=\frac{C}{L_{v}}\ln(1/\epsilon)
with
0<C<1
yields the bound stated in the theorem. Full proof details are provided in SM
sup
.

The above theorem means that the distribution distance

when the training error

III.2
Classical Shadow Reconstruction

III.2.1
Problem Statement

We now pivot from trajectory-wise reversal to
ensemble-average
recovery.
In this setting, the data are no longer a single measurement record
\mathcal{O}=\{(O_{t},o_{t})\}
tied to one quantum trajectory, but a collection of independent and identically distributed records
\{\mathcal{O}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{M}
generated by repeatedly performing randomized weak-measurement protocol on the unknown initial state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
.
The learning task is to reconstruct the
\bar{\rho}_{0}
given the measurement record dataset.

This is a problem of learning quantum states from classical data.
When the objective is inference—rather than coherent state preparation on a quantum device—
classical shadow tomography

Huang et al. 2020
provides an efficient solution: it associates to each measurement record a classical shadow that is an unbiased randomized estimator of
\bar{\rho}_{0}
, and averaging classical shadows yields a reconstruction of
\bar{\rho}_{0}
with rigorous concentration guarantees and favorable sample complexity.

III.2.2
Measurement-and-Prepare Channel

For each measurement record
\mathcal{O}
obtained from forward diffusion over time
t
,
define the
measurement operator

\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})=K_{t}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{O})\,K_{t}(\mathcal{O})

(33)

in terms of the Kraus operator
K_{t}(\mathcal{O})
defined in
eq.

12
,
such that the probability of observing
\mathcal{O}
on an initial state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
is

p(\mathcal{O}\mid\bar{\rho}_{0})=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\bar{\rho}_{0}),

(34)

referred to as the
posterior distribution
of
\mathcal{O}
.

Classical shadow tomography begins by repeatedly measuring identical copies of
\bar{\rho}_{0}
,
thereby generating measurement records
\mathcal{O}
drawn from the posterior distribution.
Collecting
M
such measurement shots produces the dataset

\{\mathcal{O}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{M}
.

In the classical post-processing stage, each record
\mathcal{O}
is mapped to its corresponding measurement operator
\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})
, regarded as a
classical snapshot
.
This process can be described as a
measure-and-prepare channel
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{0})&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left[\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\bar{\rho}_{0}\right]\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{O}\sim p(\mathcal{O}\mid\bar{\rho}_{0})}\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}).\end{split}

(35)

Here we intentionally do not normalize
\mathcal{M}_{t}
(so it is not trace-preserving), as this choice simplifies the analysis.

The map
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is a linear transformation from the target state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
to the average classical snapshot.
If the measurement scheme is
tomographically complete
—as in the randomized Pauli measurement case—
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is invertible, and the state can be reconstructed as

\bar{\rho}_{0}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{O}\sim p(\mathcal{O}\mid\bar{\rho}_{0})}\,\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}\!\left[\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\right].

(36)

Estimating the ensemble average requires samples from the quantum device,
while the post-processing step constructs classical snapshots and applies
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}
.
This is the basic principle of classical shadow reconstruction.
The problem thus reduces to determining the inverse map
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}
, also called the
reconstruction map
.

III.2.3
Channel Inversion and Pauli Weight

In general, computing
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}
is challenging,
but our weak-measurement scheme allows an exact solution thanks to two key features:

(i)

Pauli Twirling
.
In randomized Pauli measurement, the observable distribution is invariant under local Clifford unitaries, implying that
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is Pauli-twirled.
Therefore,
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is diagonal in the Pauli basis:

\mathcal{M}_{t}(P)=w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)\,P,

(37)

where the
Pauli weight
(as the channel eigenvalue) is defined as

w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(P\,\mathcal{M}_{t}(P))}{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\mathds{1}}.

(38)

(ii)

Locality
.
The randomized Pauli measurement is a local measurement scheme,
meaning that weak measurements act independently on each qubit.
This locality enables us to compute the Pauli weight for each qubit separately and combine the results multiplicatively.
The final closed-form expression is

w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)=w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\mathds{1})\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{16\gamma t}{3n}}}{3+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{16\gamma t}{3n}}}\right)^{|P|},

(39)

where
|P|
denotes the operator weight (support size) of the Pauli string
P
, and the factor

w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\mathds{1})=\left(3\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{4\gamma t}{3n}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{4\gamma t}{n}}\right)^{n}

(40)

is a time-dependent function.

With these results, the reconstruction map is

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(P)=\frac{1}{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)}\,P.

(41)

III.2.4
Estimating Observables and Sample Complexity

For finite sample size
M
, the Pauli expectation value can be estimated as

z_{0,i}\equiv\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left(\bar{\rho}_{0}\,P_{i}\right)\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}^{(s)})\,P_{i}\right)}{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P_{i})},

(42)

from which the initial ensemble-average state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
can be formally recovered as

\bar{\rho}_{0}=\frac{{\bm{z_{0}}}\cdot{\bm{P}}}{2^{n}}\approx\frac{1}{2^{n}M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}^{(s)})\,P_{i}\right)\,P_{i}}{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P_{i})},

(43)

effectively realizing the reverse diffusion via classical post-processing the forward diffusion data
\{\mathcal{O}^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^{M}
.

The statistical variance associated with
z_{0,i}
scales as

\mathrm{Var}[z_{0,i}]\sim\frac{\|P_{i}\|_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}}{M},

(44)

where
\|P\|_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}
denotes the
shadow norm
and is given by

\|P\|_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left(P\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(P)\right)}{\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\mathds{1})\right)}=\frac{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\mathds{1})}{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)}.

(45)

Therefore, to achieve a target variance
\epsilon^{2}
for a given Pauli observable
P
, the required sample size scales as

M\sim\frac{\|P\|_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(46)

Physically, the Pauli weight
w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)
quantifies the rate that measurement extracts information about the Pauli observable
P
.
In reconstruction, the expectation value of
P
must be reweighted by
1/w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)
, which necessarily amplifies statistical fluctuations.
A smaller Pauli weight indicates less information per sample and thus a larger amplification factor and a larger sample complexity.

The shadow norm exhibits characteristic time dependence:
for short times (
t\to 0
), it diverges as
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
, reflecting that weak measurements initially acquire little information about
\bar{\rho}_{0}
, hence requiring many samples for accurate reconstruction.
As
t
increases, the information per trajectory grows linearly before saturating.
In the long-time limit (
t\to\infty
), the shadow norm converges to
3^{|P|}
,
matching the scaling of projective Pauli measurements.

III.3
Petz Recovery

III.3.1
Global Petz Recovery

At the average state level, reversing quantum diffusion amounts to inverting the weak measurement channel
\mathcal{O}_{t}
that governs the evolution of the average state
\bar{\rho}_{t}
.
A general, near-optimal method for channel inversion is the
Petz recovery map

Petz 1986
, which guarantees exact recovery when
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is reversible on the support of
\bar{\rho}_{0}
and provides strong theoretical performance guarantees for approximate cases.

For an infinitesimal time step
\mathrm{d}t
, the Petz recovery map can be expressed in the “twirled” integral form

\begin{split}&\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{d}t}(\sigma)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\tau)\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{d}t}^{\tau}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\tau\ ,\\
&\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{d}t}^{\tau}(\sigma)=\bar{\rho}^{\frac{1-i\tau}{2}}_{t}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{d}t}^{\dagger}\left(\bar{\rho}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}^{\frac{-1+i\tau}{2}}\sigma\bar{\rho}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}^{\frac{-1-i\tau}{2}}\right)\bar{\rho}^{\frac{1+i\tau}{2}}_{t}\ ,\end{split}

(47)

where
f(\tau)=\frac{1}{2(\cosh(\pi\tau)+1)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{d}t}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{d}t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}
denotes the adjoint channel of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{d}t}
, and the Liouvillian super-operator
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is self-adjoint as defined in
eq.

14
. Physically,
eq.

47
describes how to undo the decoherence from
\mathcal{F}_{dt}
using the eigenbasis of
\bar{\rho}_{t}
as a prior. The forward channel
\mathcal{F}_{dt}
slightly suppresses off-diagonal elements of
\bar{\rho}_{t}
in its eigenbasis, corresponding to a drift toward the maximally mixed state. The Petz map applies the opposite drift by reweighting the eigencomponents of
\sigma
relative to
\bar{\rho}_{t}
, thereby restoring the lost coherence. Iterating
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{dt}
over the full evolution time yields a reconstruction of the initial average state from the final diffused state.

III.3.2
Local Petz Recovery

While the
global
Petz map is an exact recovery channel in theory, it is generally intractable for large many-body systems due to its fully nonlocal action.
A practical alternative arises when the ensemble exhibits predominantly short-range correlations, characterized by a finite
Markov length

\xi
: correlations between a region
A
and the distant complement
C
decay rapidly once a buffer region
B
(of width
\xi
) is included, as shown in Fig.
5
(a).
Formally, this corresponds to the conditional mutual information (CMI)
I_{\rho}(A\!:\!C|B)
being small for appropriate tripartitions
(A,B,C)
.
In this near-Markovian regime, the global state is well-approximated by a quantum Markov chain, and one can replace the global Petz map by a
local
version acting only on
S_{j}=A\cup B
.

The local Petz map
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{d}t,S_{j}}
is simply constructed by restricting the twirled Petz formula
eq.

47
to
S_{j}
, using the reduced density matrix
\rho_{t,S_{j}}
as the prior and
\mathcal{F}_{dt,S_{j}}
as the local measurement channel.
Overlapping regions
\{S_{j}\}
are chosen to cover the system, and the local recoveries
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{dt,S_{j}}
are applied in sequence or in parallel following the scheme of Ref.
Sang and Hsieh 2024
.
This yields a finite-depth recovery circuit implementable on quantum hardware, with accuracy controlled by the residual CMI.

Theorem 2

(Local Petz Recovery for Quantum Diffusion with Finite Markov Length)
.

We consider an
n
-qubit state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
evolving under a sequence of Trotterized measurement channels
\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}
over time
T
, leading to a fully decohered state
\bar{\rho}_{T}
. By applying a sequence of local twirled Petz recovery maps—each acting on a local neighborhood of the measured qubit—we approximately reverse this process. Assuming exponential decay of conditional mutual information with distance for every intermediate
\bar{\rho}_{t}
, we show that for sufficiently large
T
and recovery region size, the final reconstructed state
\bar{\rho}_{0}^{\prime}
satisfies
\|\bar{\rho}_{0}^{\prime}-\bar{\rho}_{0}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon.

Proof Sketch.

The error bound for Petz recovery is derived following the approach of
Sang and Hsieh 2024
: one first bounds the error of a single recovery step in terms of the conditional mutual information (CMI), then sums over
N_{T}
steps to obtain a telescoping bound. By the data-processing inequality, CMI is monotonic, implying that the total recovery error remains bounded by
\delta
. Meanwhile, repeated weak measurements drive the state towards a maximally mixed product state by rapidly damping higher-weight Pauli operators (see
eq.

21
), so the difference between the final state
\bar{\rho}_{T}
and the maximally mixed product state decays exponentially with
T
. Combining these results yields the stated bound. Full proof details are provided in SM
sup
.

The following protocol constructs local Petz recovery maps from forward-diffusion measurement data and implements them as local quantum channels on quantum hardware:

(i)

Collect data.

Repeatedly prepare the unknown target average state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
and apply the forward diffusion process via randomized weak measurements.
Record the complete measurement record
\mathcal{O}=\{(O_{t},o_{t})\}
for each trajectory, forming a dataset
\{\mathcal{O}\}
.

(ii)

Process data.

Apply weak-measurement classical shadow tomography to the dataset
\{\mathcal{O}\}
to reconstruct an estimate
\tilde{\rho}_{0}
of the initial average state.
Using this estimate, infer the intermediate states
\tilde{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}(\tilde{\rho}_{0})
via classical simulation of the known forward channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, see Fig.
5
(b).

(iii)

Compute local Petz maps.

For each overlapping local region
S_{j}
(with size exceeding the Markov length
\xi
), construct the local Petz recovery map
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{d}t,S_{j}}
by restricting the
twirled Petz
formula
eq.

47
to
S_{j}
, using the reduced state
\tilde{\rho}_{t,S_{j}}
and the corresponding local channel
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{d}t,S_{j}}
.
Arrange and stack these local maps in the reverse order of the forward schedule to obtain the overall Petz recovery protocol
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}
, as illustrated in Fig.
5
(c) (see
Sang and Hsieh 2024
for the detailed construction).

(iv)

Implement reverse diffusion.

Each step of
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{t}
is a local quantum channel that can, in principle, be implemented on quantum hardware.
Initialize the device in a state
\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{T}
(e.g., a random product state evolved forward to time
T
), then apply
\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{0}=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{T}(\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{T})
to obtain a recovered approximation to the original state
\bar{\rho}_{0}
.
This procedure enables repeated state preparation directly on the quantum device.

This protocol provides a systematic construction of the reverse process as a composition of local quantum channels, tailored for forward diffusion driven by randomized measurements.
In contrast to prior approaches to average-state recovery based on learned reverse quantum channel
Parigi et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Kölle et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Kwun et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2025
;
Zhang and Chen 2026
, the Petz recovery approach requires no learning: the recovery maps are constructed directly from data and knowledge of the forward channel.
This observation offers an important theoretical guarantee for learning-based methods—namely, that a purely classical algorithm exists to build local recovery maps from measurement data.
Therefore, any successful learning-based recovery can be viewed as approximating, in principle, a well-defined recovery process given by local Petz maps.

III.3.4
Petz Recovery as Time-Reversed Diffusion

We establish that the Petz recovery map provides a quantum generalization of classical time-reversed diffusion under weak measurements, and that in the classical large-spin limit the two become equivalent.

Consider an
n
-qubit separable state (no quantum entanglement), expressed as a mixture of tensor product of spin coherent states

\bar{\rho}_{t}=\int\mathrm{d}{\bm{n}}\;p({\bm{n}},t)\,\ket{{\bm{n}}}\!\bra{{\bm{n}}},

(49)

where the spin configuration is described by
{\bm{n}}=({\bm{n}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{n}}_{n})
with
|{\bm{n}}_{j}|=1
, and the corresponding coherent state reads

|{\bm{n}}\rangle\langle{\bm{n}}|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathds{1}+{\bm{n}}_{j}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{j}}{2},

(50)

and
p({\bm{n}},t)
is a normalized probability density over the
n
-fold product of Bloch spheres.

Consider randomized Pauli weak measurements, whose action is equivalent to applying independent depolarizing channels on each qubit.
Since such local measurements do not generate entanglement, every trajectory remains a pure product state throughout the process.
Consequently, the ensemble evolution is fully captured by the probability density
p({\bm{n}},t)
over coherent state configurations
{\bm{n}}
.
The resulting stochastic dynamics yield the forward Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}p({\bm{n}},t)=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}p({\bm{n}},t),

(51)

where
\nabla_{\perp}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla_{\perp,j}^{2}
is the sum of Laplacians on individual Bloch spheres.

Given the forward diffusion history of the average state

\bar{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{0})
,
the reverse process can be implemented as

\bar{\rho}_{0}=\mathcal{R}^{0}_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{t}),

(52)

where
\mathcal{R}^{0}_{t}
is the (untwirled) Petz map derived from
eq.

47

\mathcal{R}^{0}_{t}(\sigma)=\bar{\rho}_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\dagger}\!\big(\bar{\rho}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}^{-1/2}\,\sigma\,\bar{\rho}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}^{-1/2}\big)\,\bar{\rho}_{t}^{1/2},

(53)

which reweights
\sigma
by
\bar{\rho}_{t}^{-1/2}
through the adjoint channel.
For
\sigma=\int q({\bm{n}},t)\,\ket{{\bm{n}}}\!\bra{{\bm{n}}}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{n}}
,

\mathcal{R}^{0}_{t}(\sigma)
induces a backward diffusion of the distribution
q({\bm{n}},t)
— a quantum generalization of classical reverse diffusion.
The PDE governing this backward evolution is given in SM
sup
.

In the large-spin (classical) limit, coherent states with different
{\bm{n}}
become orthogonal,
\langle{\bm{n}}|{\bm{n}}^{\prime}\rangle\to\delta_{{\bm{n}},{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}
. The Petz equation becomes the classical backward Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}q({\bm{n}},t)=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}q({\bm{n}},t)-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}\!\cdot\!\big[q({\bm{n}},t)\,\nabla_{\perp}\log p({\bm{n}},t)\big],

(54)

exactly reversing

\frac{4\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}\log p
as the backward drift.

This result reveals a precise theoretical connection: in the classical limit, the Petz recovery map is time-reversed diffusion. This not only clarifies the physical meaning of Petz recovery, but also provides a rigorous bridge between quantum recovery channels and classical stochastic reversals, ensuring that any successful quantum Petz reconstruction has a well-defined classical counterpart.

IV
Conclusion and Discussion

We developed a measurement-based framework for quantum diffusion that unifies trajectory-wise and ensemble-average reversal. On the forward side, randomized weak measurements generate stochastic pure-state trajectories while inducing a measurement channel that is locally scrambled and diagonal in the Pauli basis. On the reverse side, we presented two complementary routes: (i) trajectory-level reversal via score-matching/control–Hamiltonian learning; and (ii) ensemble-level reversal via either Petz recovery or classical shadow reconstruction.

Summary of contributions.

(i) We formalized trajectory-level reverse diffusion as a control problem and showed its equivalence to quantum score matching, yielding a principled objective for learning the reverse flow. (ii) For ensemble averages, we introduced a practical
local
Petz recovery when correlations are short-ranged, leveraging recoverability guarantees for Lindbladian dynamics and near-optimal Petz maps; this connects quantum channel inversion to reversing measurement-induced decoherence.
(iii) We established a complementary
classical
post-processing route via classical shadow tomography, exploiting that our Pauli-twirled measurement-and-prepare channel is diagonal in the Pauli basis; its inverse gives an unbiased estimator of
\bar{\rho}_{0}
with rigorous concentration bounds for local observables.
(iv) Conceptually, we clarified that Petz recovery implements a structured quantum analogue of time-reversed diffusion and reduces to classical backward diffusion in the large-spin/separable limit, linking recovery channels to Fokker-Planck reversals. This complements recent diffusion-style generative models for quantum data that operate in either state space or density-matrix space.

Broader implications.

The measurement-driven formulation naturally couples data acquisition and control: the same weak-measurement records that drive forward diffusion also provide conditioning signals for reverse synthesis. Local Petz maps offer a hardware-friendly, finite-depth realization of ensemble reversal under finite Markov length, whereas classical shadows provide an efficient, device-agnostic alternative when the goal is inference rather than state preparation. Together, these tools place quantum diffusion models on firmer mathematical footing and connect them to established theories of recoverability and randomized tomography.

Limitations and outlook.

Our trajectory-wise reversal presently relies on classical decoders (simulation- or learning-based) to infer sufficient state information for feedback, a key difference from classical denoising where the state is directly observable. Scaling such decoders, tightening end-to-end error bounds (from Hamiltonian learning error to ensemble Wasserstein/trace-distance guarantees), and extending beyond randomized Pauli measurements to other locally scrambled schemes (e.g., Clifford- or unitary-2-design–based protocols) are immediate next steps. It is also promising to explore recovery-aware measurement schedules and noise models that optimize sample complexity and circuit depth, and to interface these methods with structure-preserving diffusion for mixed states and with recent progress on designing open dynamics with prescribed steady states.

Overall, this work provides a concise blueprint for quantum diffusion: use weak measurements to generate and learn from forward randomness; reverse trajectories via learned control when needed; and reverse ensembles via Petz recovery or classical shadows when sufficient statistics are available. We expect these ingredients to be directly useful in near-term platforms that already support weak, randomized measurements and local quantum channels.

Appendix A
Derivations for the Score Matching of the General Diffusion Dynamics

This appendix provides a detailed, self-contained derivation of the reverse-time dynamics for a general diffusion process and its application to the stochastic evolution of quantum states under weak measurement. We begin by outlining the general theory of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and score-based modeling. We then connect this mathematical framework to the physical process of weak measurement. Finally, we establish a formal equivalence between learning the reverse diffusion process for an ensemble of pure states and learning an effective time-reversed unitary evolution.

A.1
General Diffusion Dynamics

A.1.1
The generic SDE, Fokker–Planck PDE, and ODE Descriptions

A generalized diffusion process for a state vector
\bm{z}(t)
in an
N
dimensional space can be described by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) with a
M
-dimensional and state-dependent noise. The generic SDE takes the form (in which all quantities are real valued):

d\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}(\bm{z}_{t},t)d\bm{w}_{t}\ ,

(55)

where:

•

\bm{z}_{t}
is an
N
-dimensional
state vector
representing the system’s state at time
t
.

•

\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
is an
N
-dimensional
drift field
(drift force), describing the deterministic part of the evolution.

•

\bm{G}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
is an
N\times M
matrix representing the
noise amplitude
, which couples the noise sources to the state vector.

•

d\bm{w}_{t}=(dw_{t,1},\cdots,dw_{t,M})^{T}
is an
M
-dimensional vector representing a Wiener process
noise
, satisfying the a probability distribution (usually Gaussian) with the first and second moments (
\mathbb{E}
stands for mean value):

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}w_{t,i}]=0\ ,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}w_{t,i}\mathrm{d}w_{t,j}]=\delta_{ij}\mathrm{d}t\ .

The time evolution of the probability distribution
p(\bm{z},t)
of the state vector
\bm{z}
is known to be governed by the corresponding Fokker–Planck partial differential equation (PDE):

\partial_{t}p(\bm{z},t)=-\nabla\cdot\left[\bm{f}(\bm{z},t)p(\bm{z},t)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\left(\Sigma_{ij}(\bm{z},t)p(\bm{z},t)\right)\ ,

(56)

where Einstein summation convention over repeated indices are assumed, and we have introduced the
N\times N
noise covariance matrix

\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},t)=\bm{G}(\bm{z},t)\bm{G}^{T}(\bm{z},t)\ .

(57)

Using the probability distribution
p(\bm{z},t)
, the SDE of state vector
\bm{z}_{t}
is statistically equivalent to a deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE), known as the probability flow ODE:

d\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{v}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t\ ,

(58)

with the velocity field
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{N})^{T}
given by:

v_{i}(\bm{z},t)=f_{i}(\bm{z},t)-\frac{1}{2}\Big[\partial_{j}\Sigma_{ij}(\bm{z},t)+\Sigma_{ij}(\bm{z},t)\partial_{j}\log p(\bm{z},t)\Big]\ ,

(59)

or more concisely in vector form,

\bm{v}(\bm{z},t)=\bm{f}(\bm{z},t)-\frac{1}{2}\Big[\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},t)+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},t)\cdot\nabla\log p(\bm{z},t)\Big]\ ,

(60)

where
(\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma})_{i}=\partial_{j}\Sigma_{ij}
.

A.1.2
Reverse Diffusion and Score Matching

A key result from stochastic calculus is that the forward diffusion process described by Eq. (A1) has a corresponding reverse-time process. The reverse SDE, which allows for generating samples from the distribution
p(\bm{z},0)
by starting from a simple prior at time
T
, at time
\widetilde{t}=T-t
is given by:

d\bm{z}_{\widetilde{t}}=\left[-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{\widetilde{t}},{\widetilde{t}})+\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z}_{\widetilde{t}},{\widetilde{t}})+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z}_{\widetilde{t}},{\widetilde{t}})\cdot\nabla\log p(\bm{z}_{\widetilde{t}},{\widetilde{t}})\right]\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{t}}+\bm{G}(\bm{z}_{\widetilde{t}})d\bm{w}_{\widetilde{t}}\ .

(61)

where
d\bm{w}_{\widetilde{t}}
is a standard Wiener process in the reverse time direction.

The generative power of this framework hinges on knowledge of the score function incorporating the information of
\nabla\log p(\bm{z},t)
. In score-based modeling, we define a learnable model
s_{\theta}(\bm{z},t)
with variational parameters
\theta
to approximate the score-dependent part of the reverse drift:

s_{\theta}(\bm{z},t)\approx\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},t)+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},t)\cdot\nabla\log p(\bm{z}_{t},t)\ ,

(62)

by which one can implement the reverse SDE in
eq.

61
by the score function
s_{\theta}(\bm{z},t)
. The model is trained by minimizing the score-matching loss, which is the expected squared deviation between the model and the true score term:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{t}\sim p(\bm{z},t)}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)-(\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma})(\bm{z}_{t})-\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z}_{t})\nabla\log p(\bm{z},t)\right\|^{2}\right]

(63)

Directly computing this loss is often intractable. A practical and equivalent alternative is denoising score matching. Given a forward step
\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{z}_{t}+\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\bm{g}(\bm{z}_{t})d\bm{w}_{t}
, it can be shown that the above loss function in
eq.

63
is equivalent to the following denoising loss function up to a constant:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})\mathrm{d}t,t)+\frac{\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}-\bm{z}_{t}-\bm{f}\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\|^{2}\right]\ .

(64)

This objective trains the model to predict the noise term that corrupted the state, thereby implicitly learning the score without needing to compute it directly.

Lastly, starting from a backward probability distribution
q(\bm{z},{\widetilde{t}})
, the backward diffusion PDE driven by the forward probability distribution
p(\bm{z},{\widetilde{t}})
takes the form

\partial_{\widetilde{t}}q(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})=-\nabla\cdot\left[\left(-\bm{f}(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})+\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})\cdot\nabla\log p(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})\right)q(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})\right]+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\left(\Sigma_{ij}(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})q(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})\right)\ ,

(65)

where
\nabla\cdot\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})\cdot\nabla\log p(\bm{z},\widetilde{t})
plays the role of a drifting force towards the distribution
p(\bm{z},{\widetilde{t}})
.

A.2
The nonlinear SDE from the Weak Measurement Protocol

A.2.1
The Weak Measurement Protocol

We consider a quantum system initially prepared in the state
\rho_{0}
. Ancilla-assisted weak measurement of an observable
O_{t}
is implemented by coupling the system to an ancilla qubit, prepared in
|0\rangle_{A}
, via the interaction Hamiltonian

H=\lambda O_{t}\sigma_{A}^{y}\ .

(66)

After evolving for a finite time
\delta t
, the ancilla is projectively measured in the
\sigma_{A}^{x}
basis, yielding an outcome
o_{t}
. In the continuous limit, where
\delta t\to 0
and
\lambda\to\infty
such that the measurement rate
\gamma=\lambda^{2}\delta t
is constant, this interaction is described by the Kraus operator:

K_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{O_{t}}}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\gamma\,\delta t+2\gamma\,O_{t}\,\delta o_{t}\Big),

(67)

where
N_{O_{t}}
is a normalization factor such that
\sum_{\delta o_{t}}K^{\dagger}_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})K_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})=\mathds{1}.

A.2.2
Forward Measurement-Based Diffusion

Assuming the observable
O_{t}=O_{t}^{\dagger}
is Hermitian, the corresponding weak measurement of observable
O_{t}
in time
\delta t
with measurement outcome
o_{t}
will update the system’s state via a Kraus operator

\rho_{t+dt}=\frac{K_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{\delta t}^{\dagger}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})}{\mathrm{Tr}\left[K_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{\delta t}^{\dagger}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\right]}\ .

(68)

The probability of observing outcome
o_{t}
is state-dependent, and the mean value of
o_{t}
from weak measurement defined above is given by

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[o_{t}]=2\sqrt{\gamma\delta t}\langle O_{t}\rangle\ ,

(69)

where we denote
\langle O_{t}\rangle=\langle\psi_{t}|O_{t}|\psi_{t}\rangle
for short. For instance, for one step of weak measurement of spin
1/2
operator
O_{t}
with outcomes

dt
, the probability of obsereving outcome
o_{t}

p(o_{t}|\rho_{t})=\mathrm{Tr}\left[K_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{\delta t}^{\dagger}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\right]=\frac{1}{2}+o_{t}\sqrt{\gamma\delta t}\langle O_{t}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}+2\gamma\langle O_{t}\rangle\delta o_{t}\ ,\qquad\delta o_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\delta t}{\gamma}}o_{t}\ .

(70)

A.2.3
The Stochastic Process from Weak Measurement

The evolution of the quantum state
\rho_{t}
is found by expanding the update rule in

(71)

where

dw_{t}=o_{t}\sqrt{dt}-2\sqrt{\gamma}dt\langle O_{t}\rangle=2\sqrt{\gamma}\big(\mathrm{d}o_{t}-\langle O_{t}\rangle dt\big)

. The first term in

d|\psi_{t}\rangle=\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}(O-\langle O\rangle)^{2}dt+\sqrt{\gamma}(O-\langle O\rangle)dw_{t}\right)|\psi_{t}\rangle\ .

(73)

where the the drift vector

\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t},t)=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\text{Tr}\Big([O_{t},[O_{t},\rho_{t}]]\bm{P}\Big)\ ,\qquad\bm{g}(\bm{z}_{t},t)=\sqrt{\gamma}\text{Tr}\Big(\{O_{t}-\langle O_{t}\rangle,\rho_{t}\}\bm{P}\Big)\ ,

A.3
The Unitary Revserse Procedure for Pure States

A.3.1
From learning unitary to learning score function

For an ensemble of pure states, the forward diffusion process implemented by weak measurement must be purity-preserving. As is clear from
eq.

73
, a pure state remains pure along the quantum trajectory. Consequently, the time-evolution process is a unitary evolution.

For such pure state evolutions, we are therefore motivated to define a unitary score function being an infinitesimal time-
dt
unitary evolution
V_{\theta}(\bm{z},t)
(for some fixed small time step
dt
), such that it approximates the infinitesimal forward evolution

\rho_{t+dt}=\frac{K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{dt}^{\dagger}(O_{t},do_{t})}{\mathrm{Tr}\left[K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{dt}^{\dagger}(O_{t},do_{t})\right]}\approx V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t)\rho_{dt}V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t},t)=|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\langle\psi_{t+dt}|\ ,\qquad\rho_{t}=|\psi_{t}\rangle\langle\psi_{t}|\ .

(79)

Once this score function is learned, the time-reversed process can be generated by the inverse unitary transformations
V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t},t)
, which gives a purity-preserving reverse unitary evolution.

We are thus straightforwardly motivated to define a loss function

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\left\|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)\,\rho_{t}\,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)-\frac{K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{dt}^{\dagger}(O_{t},do_{t})}{\mathrm{Tr}\left[K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})\,\rho_{t}\,K_{dt}^{\dagger}(O_{t},do_{t})\right]}\right\|^{2}_{F}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\left\|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)\,\rho_{t}\,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)-\rho_{t+dt}\right\|^{2}_{F}\\
&=1-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi_{t},\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\left|\langle\psi_{t+dt}|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle\right|^{2}\ ,\end{split}

(80)

optimizing which gives the desired unitary score
V_{\theta}
. We now show that this is equivalent to the denoising loss function in
eq.

64
usually defined in the diffusion problem. Using the fact that

\left\|\bm{z}\cdot\bm{P}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}\,\mathrm{tr}(P_{i}P_{j})=2^{n}\sum_{i}z_{i}^{2}=2^{n}\left\|\bm{z}\right\|^{2}\ ,

(81)

the denoising loss function in
eq.

64
can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}&=\frac{2^{n}}{2}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\left\|\left[\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})dt,t)-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})\right]\cdot\frac{\bm{P}}{2^{n}}+\left(\frac{\bm{z}_{t+dt}-\bm{z}_{t}}{dt}\right)\cdot\frac{\bm{P}}{2^{n}}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\frac{2^{n}}{2}\,\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{z}_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\left\|\left[\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})dt,t)-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})\right]\cdot\frac{\bm{P}}{2^{n}}+\frac{\rho_{t+dt}-\rho_{t}}{dt}\right\|^{2}\ .\end{split}

(82)

Comparing with
eq.

80
, we find if we make the following identification:

\left[\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})dt,t)-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})\right]\cdot\frac{\bm{P}}{2^{n}}=\frac{\rho_{t}-V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)\,\rho_{t}\,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)}{dt}\ ,

(83)

then the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
we defined in
eq.

80
is up to a global factor equivalent to the denoising loss function in
eq.

64
.

Further, to the linear order of
dt
, the infinitesimal unitary
V_{\theta}
can be parametrized by

V_{\theta}=e^{iH_{\theta}dt}\approx I+iH_{\theta}dt\ ,\qquad H_{\theta}=\sum_{j}\eta_{\theta,j}P_{j}=\bm{\eta}_{\theta}\cdot\bm{P}\ ,

(84)

where
H_{\theta}
is a Hermitian generator. (Keeping only up to the first order in
dt
of
V_{\theta}
because our SDE is accurate in the first order in
dt
.) Thus,
V_{\theta}\rho_{t}V_{\theta}^{\dagger}-\rho_{t}\approx i[H_{\theta},\rho_{t}]dt
, and we can identify the following:

\displaystyle\left[\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})dt,t)-\bm{f}(\bm{z}_{t})\right]\cdot\frac{\bm{P}}{2^{n}}

\displaystyle=-i\left[H_{\theta},\rho_{t}\right]=-\frac{i}{2^{n}}\left[\bm{\eta}_{\theta}\cdot\bm{P},\bm{z}_{t}\cdot\bm{P}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2^{n}}\sum_{jk}\left[P_{j},P_{k}\right]\eta_{\theta,j}z_{t,k}\ .

(85)

We see that

encodes the information of the loss function
\bm{s}_{\theta}-\bm{f}
.

A.3.2
Error bound for Unitary Learning

The equivalence between learning the score function and learning a unitary generator allows us to bound the error of the generative model. Here we consider the error bound of the unitary reverse generative model.

First, for a pair of pure states
|\phi\rangle
and

D(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle)=\text{Tr}\sqrt{(\rho_{\phi}-\rho_{\psi})^{2}}=\sqrt{2(1-|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2})}\ ,\qquad\rho_{\phi}=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\ ,\quad\rho_{\psi}=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\ .

(86)

Assume the learned probability distribution from the unitary reverse generative model is
p_{\theta}
, and the true probability distribution of pure states
p_{\mathrm{true}}
.
We then define the

p
-Wasserstein distance
with trace distance cost is defined by
Zhang et al. 2024a

\begin{split}&W_{p}(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})}\int D(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle)^{p}\;\pi\big(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle\big)d\phi d\psi\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\end{split}

(87)

where
\Pi(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})
runs over all the
couplings
(joint distributions)
\pi

on pairs of states
(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle)
whose marginals are
p_{\theta}
and
p_{\mathrm{true}}
, namely satisfying
\int\pi\big(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle\big)d\phi=p_{\mathrm{true}}(|\psi\rangle)
, and
\int\pi\big(|\phi\rangle,\,|\psi\rangle\big)d\psi=p_{\theta}(|\phi\rangle)
.

We now show the following theorem:

Theorem* 1

(Restatement of Theorem
1
)
.

Distribution Convergence Theorem. Let
p_{\mathrm{true}}
be the true probability distribution of initial pure states, and
p_{\theta}
denote the learned probability distribution obtained after a reverse diffusion process of total evolution time
T
generated by the learned control unitary
V_{\theta}
.
Assume the learned control unitary satisfies

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\psi_{t},\psi_{t+\mathrm{d}t}}\sqrt{1-\left|\langle\psi_{t+dt}|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle\right|^{2}}\leq\epsilon dt\ ,

(88)

where
\bm{z}_{t}
is the state vector of
|\psi_{t}\rangle
. Also, assume
V_{\theta}
has a Lipschitz bound of constant
L_{V}dt
such that for any pure state
|\alpha\rangle
:

D(V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z},t)|\alpha\rangle,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}^{\prime},t)|\alpha\rangle)\leq L_{V}dtD(|\psi\rangle,|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)\ ,

(89)

where
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
are the state vectors of
|\psi\rangle
and
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
, respectively. Then by choosing a stopping time
T=\frac{C}{L_{v}}\ln(1/\epsilon)
with
0<C<1
, the
1
-Wasserstein distance between the learned ensemble
p_{\theta}
and the true ensemble
p_{\mathrm{true}}
is bounded by

W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\mathrm{true}})\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{L_{v}}\epsilon^{1-C}+\epsilon^{-C}\,E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)

(90)

with
E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)
decaying exponentially as
ne^{-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}T}
with respect to the diffusion time
T
. If we choose a sufficiently large measurement strength
\gamma
such that
\frac{4\gamma}{3nL_{v}}\geq 1
, the leading-order behavior of the bound for small
\epsilon
is dominated by the first term in
eq.

90
, and
W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\mathrm{true}})\;\to\;0
as
\epsilon\to 0.

Proof.

We first note that once the learned control unitary
V_{\theta}
is determined, the reversed state
|\phi_{t}\rangle
at any time
0\leq t\leq T
generated by
V_{\theta}
backward from a state
|\phi_{T}\rangle
at time
T
is deterministic:

|\phi_{t}\rangle=\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}\prod_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T-dt}V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}+dt}^{\phi},t^{\prime})|\phi_{T}\rangle=V^{\dagger}(\phi_{T},t,T)|\phi_{T}\rangle\ ,

(91)

where
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
stands for the anti-time ordering, and
\bm{z}_{t}^{\phi}
is the state vector of state
|\phi_{t}\rangle
.

We denote the true probability distribution at time
t
as
p_{\mathrm{true},t}
, which is evolved by the forward weak measurement diffusion from the initial true probability distribution
p_{\mathrm{true}}
. Similarly, we denote the learned probability distribution at time
t
as
p_{\theta,t}
, which is generated by reverse diffusion
V_{\theta}
from the true probability distribution
p_{\mathrm{true},T}
at time
T
.

Using the fact that
|\phi_{t}\rangle=V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle
,

\begin{split}&D(|\phi_{t}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t}\rangle)=D(V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t}\rangle)=D(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle)\\
&\leq D(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)+D(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle)\\
&=D(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)+\sqrt{2\left(1-\left|\langle\psi_{t+dt}|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle\right|^{2}\right)}\ .\end{split}

(92)

which holds for any state
|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle
.

Assume the Wasserstein distance
W_{1}(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})
for time
t+dt
is achieved by the coupling
\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\in\Pi(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})
, namely,

W_{1}(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})=\int D(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)\;\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}\ .

(93)

We define an induced coupling at time
t
as
\pi^{d}_{t}
:

\pi^{d}_{t}\big(|\phi_{t}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t}\rangle\big)d\phi_{t}=d\phi_{t+dt}\int_{\psi_{t+dt}}\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)}{p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)}d\psi_{t+dt}\ ,

(94)

where
|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle=V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\phi_{t}\rangle
. The function
\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)
is the true joint distribution between states
|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle
, satisfying

\int\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)d\psi_{t+dt}=p_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t}\rangle)\ ,\quad\int\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)d\psi_{t}=p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)\ .

Note that
\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)}{p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)}=p_{\mathrm{true}}(|\psi_{t}\rangle\big||\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)
is the conditional probability of
|\psi_{t}\rangle
with respect to
|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle
of the true distribution, which satisfies
\int p_{\mathrm{true}}(|\psi_{t}\rangle\big||\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)d\psi_{t}=1
.

By
eqs.

92
and
94
, we arrive at the following inequality:

\begin{split}&\int D(|\phi_{t}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t}\rangle)\;\pi_{t}^{d}\big(|\phi_{t}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t}\rangle\big)d\phi_{t}d\psi_{t}\\
\leq&\int D(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)\;\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}\\
&\ +\int\sqrt{2\left(1-\left|\langle\psi_{t+dt}|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle\right|^{2}\right)}\;\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)\frac{\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)}{p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)}d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t}\\
=&W_{1}(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})+\Delta(\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}})\ ,\end{split}

(95)

where

\Delta(\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}})=\int\sqrt{2\left(1-\left|\langle\psi_{t+dt}|V_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t}\rangle\right|^{2}\right)}\;\frac{\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)}{p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)}\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t}\ .

(96)

We assume
V_{\theta}
is Lipschitz with coefficient
L_{V}dt
, that for any state
|\alpha\rangle
,

D(V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z},t)|\alpha\rangle,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}^{\prime},t)|\alpha\rangle)\leq L_{V}dtD(|\psi\rangle,|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)\ .

(97)

Then, using
eq.

88
, assuming
\bm{z}_{t+dt}
denotes the state vector of state
|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle
, we find

\begin{split}&\Delta(\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}})\leq\sqrt{2}\epsilon dt+\int\left(D(|\psi_{t}\rangle,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)-D(|\psi_{t}\rangle,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)\right)\;\\
&\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)}{p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)}\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t}\\
&\leq\sqrt{2}\epsilon dt+\int D(V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt},t)|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,V_{\theta}^{\dagger}(\bm{z}_{t+dt}^{\phi},t)|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)\frac{\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)}{p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)}\Gamma_{\mathrm{true},t}(|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle,|\psi_{t}\rangle)d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t}\\
&\leq\sqrt{2}\epsilon dt+L_{v}dt\int D(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle)\;\pi_{t+dt}^{\text{inf}}\big(|\phi_{t+dt}\rangle,\,|\psi_{t+dt}\rangle\big)d\phi_{t+dt}d\psi_{t+dt}\\
&=\sqrt{2}\epsilon dt+L_{v}dtW_{1}(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})\ .\end{split}

(98)

Thus, by
eq.

95
and the definition of the
1
-Wasserstein distance, we find

W_{1}(p_{\theta,t},p_{\mathrm{true},t})\leq\big(1+L_{V}dt\big)W_{1}(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})+\sqrt{2}\epsilon dt\ .

(99)

This implies

e^{-L_{V}(T-t)}W_{1}(p_{\theta,t},p_{\mathrm{true},t})\leq e^{-L_{V}(T-t-dt)}W_{1}(p_{\theta,t+dt},p_{\mathrm{true},t+dt})+\sqrt{2}e^{-L_{V}(T-t)}\epsilon dt\ .

(100)

At
t=T
, we start to perform the unitary reverse from a uniform random product state distribution
p_{\theta,T}
. Meanwhile,
E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\sim n\exp\left(-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}T\right)
, because for non-identity Pauli strings, each Pauli weight decays exponentially to zero with increasing diffusion time
T
, as shown in
eq.

124
. So

W_{1}(p_{\theta,T},p_{\mathrm{true},T})=E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\sim ne^{-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}T}\ ,

(101)

as is evident from the Pauli weight of the weak measurement channel in
eq.

124
. This together with integrating
eq.

100
from
T
to
t
yields an accumulated bound:

W_{1}(p_{\theta,t},p_{\mathrm{true},t})\leq e^{L_{V}(T-t)}\Big[\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{L_{V}}\left(1-e^{-L_{V}(T-t)}\right)+E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\Big]\ .

(102)

Thus, particularly, for
t=0
, the
1
-Wasserstein distance between
p_{\theta}=p_{\theta,0}
and
p_{\mathrm{true}}=p_{\mathrm{true},0}
is bounded by

W_{1}(p_{\theta},p_{\mathrm{true}})\leq e^{L_{V}T}\left[\frac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{L_{V}}\left(1-e^{-L_{V}T}\right)+E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\right]\ .

(103)

Now if we choose a stopping time
T=\frac{C}{L_{v}}\ln(1/\epsilon)
, then
eq.

103
becomes

W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\mathrm{true}})\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{L_{v}}\epsilon^{1-C}+\epsilon^{-C}\,E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)

(104)

In the leading-order behavior of this expression for small
\epsilon,
the second term in
eq.

104
becomes

E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\sim n\exp\left(-\frac{4\gamma}{3nL_{v}}\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)=n\epsilon^{\frac{4\gamma}{3nL_{v}}}

(105)

In choosing a sufficiently large measurement strength
\gamma
, we expect

to decay sufficiently fast that
\frac{4\gamma}{3nL_{v}}\geq C,
and the leading-order behavior of the bound for small
\epsilon
is then dominated by the first term in
eq.

104
, which is a constant. As

W_{1}(p_{0},p_{\mathrm{true}})\;\to\;\frac{\sqrt{2}}{L_{v}}\epsilon^{1-C}

(106)

This means that the distribution distance
W_{1}\to 0
when the training error
\epsilon
is made arbitrarily tiny.

Appendix B
Pauli Weights Evolution under Measurement Channel and Measurement-and-prepare channel

This appendix derives the analytical expression of the measurement channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and the measurement-and-prepare channel
\mathcal{M}_{t}
we defined in the main text, by solving the differential equation of their Pauli weights.

B.1
The Measurement channel and the linear SDE

B.1.1
The measurement channel

We next examine the generic weak measurement protocol of
section

A.2.1
in the context of qubit systems. We consider a system of
n
qubits, initialized in a state
\rho_{0}
. The forward diffusion is implemented by applying a sequence of ancilla-assisted weak measurements. At each infinitesimal time step
\delta t
, a single-qubit observable
O_{t}
is measured once on a qubit
j_{t}
(either randomly chosen or consecutively). The observable is selected from the set of single-qubit Pauli operators
\{\,\sigma_{x,j},\sigma_{y,j},\sigma_{z,j}\mid j=1,\dots,n\,\}
or their linear superpositions, ensuring
O_{t}^{2}=I
. The corresponding Kraus operator is given by:

K_{\delta t}(O_{t},\delta o_{t})\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\gamma\,\delta t+2\gamma\,O_{t}\,\delta o_{t}\Big)\ ,\qquad\delta o_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\delta t}{\gamma}}o_{t}\ ,\qquad o_{t}=\pm 1\ ,

(107)

as given in Eq. (18) of the main text.

In the following derivations of this appendix, for convenience, we do not distinguish between the discrete time
\delta t
and
dt
, and we will denote
eq.

67
as

K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\exp\left(-\gamma dt+o_{t}\sqrt{\gamma dt}O_{j_{t,t}}\right)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\left(1-\frac{\gamma dt}{2}\right)I+o_{t}\sqrt{\gamma dt}O_{t}\right]\ .

(108)

Note that
K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})
differs from Eq. (10) of the main text in normalization. This is because the main text Eq. (10) corresponds to many weak measurement steps within time
dt
(thus the accumulated signal
do_{t}
can be regarded as continuous), while here in
eq.

108
, for simplicity we assume there is only one weak measurement step with discrete outcomes
do_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{dt}{\gamma}}
.

For a sequence of consecutive weak measurements on the
n
-qubit system from time
t=0
to
t=T
(
t=kdt
,
1\leq k\leq N_{T}
,
N_{T}=T/dt
), we define a trajectory
\mathcal{O}
as the sequence of measured observables
O_{t}
and their corresponding outcomes
o_{t}\in\{\pm 1\}
at each time step:

\mathcal{O}=\left\{(O_{t},o_{t})\mid 0\leqslant t\leqslant T\right\}

(109)

The associated Kraus operator up to time
t

along the trajectory
\mathcal{O}
is

\begin{split}&K_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{T}\prod_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}K_{dt}\left(O_{t}^{\prime},do_{t}^{\prime}\right)\ ,\end{split}

(110)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordering operator, and
K_{dt}
is defined in
eq.

108
. At time
t
, the system state
\rho_{t}
conditional on trajectory
\mathcal{O}
is given by

\rho_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\frac{\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})\right]}\ ,\quad\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})=2^{N_{t}}K_{t}(\mathcal{O})\rho_{0}K_{t}^{\dagger}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)\ ,\qquad N_{t}=\frac{t}{dt}\ .

(111)

where
\rho_{0}
is the initial state at
t=0
, and we have defined a tilded state
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})
with unnormalized trace. The trace of
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})
has the physical meaning of being the probability for trajectory
\mathcal{O}
to occur:

p_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\frac{1}{2^{N_{t}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})\right]\ .

(112)

As defined in the main text, the measurement channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the average of the final states of all trajectories respecting their probabilities:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{t}(\rho_{0})&=\sum_{\mathcal{O}}p_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\rho_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\frac{1}{2^{N_{t}}}\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\frac{1}{2^{N_{t}}}\sum_{\mathcal{O}}K_{t}(\mathcal{O})\,\rho_{0}\,K_{t}^{\dagger}(\mathcal{O})\ .\end{split}

(113)

Particularly, in the final expression, all the
2^{N_{t}}
trajectories
\mathcal{O}
is summed at equal weight, as their probability
p_{t}(\mathcal{O})
is readily included in the trace of
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})
. Therefore, we say that the unnormalized states
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})
of different trajectories
\mathcal{O}
effectively occur at equal probability.

B.1.2
The linear SDE

The unnormalized state
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})
defined in
eq.

111
plays an important role in the above quantum channels, so it is useful to take a closer look at it. Its evolution under the sequence of weak measurements is governed by a linear SDE
Jacobs and Steck 2006
:

d\widetilde{\rho}_{t}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[O_{t},[O_{t},\widetilde{\rho}_{t}]]dt+\sqrt{\gamma}\{O_{t},\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\}d\widetilde{w}_{t}\ ,\qquad d\widetilde{w}_{t}=o_{t}\sqrt{dt}=2\sqrt{\gamma}\mathrm{d}o_{t}\ ,

(114)

which can be derived from the expression of Kraus operators
K_{dt}
, and
d\widetilde{w}_{t}
is the stocastic noise variable. Note that for later convenience, we choose to rescale the variable
\mathrm{d}o_{t}
into
d\widetilde{w}_{t}
according to the above here. An important difference compared to the normalized state
\rho_{t}
, since each unnormalized state
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}
occurs at equal probability (
eq.

113
),
d\widetilde{w}_{t}
in the linear SDE
eq.

114
for
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}
has a probability distribution with first and second moment

\mathbb{E}[d\widetilde{w}_{t}]=0\ ,\qquad\mathbb{E}[d\widetilde{w}_{t}^{2}]=dt\ .

(115)

In the limit
dt\rightarrow 0
, it is sufficient to keep all the calculations up to order
dt\sim d\widetilde{w}_{t}^{2}
, so all probability distributions of
d\widetilde{w}_{t}
with the same first and second moments are equivalent for the SDE. Thus, we can simply set
d\widetilde{w}_{t}
as the standard random Wiener noise obeying a Gaussian distribution satisfying
eq.

115
.

in terms of
d\widetilde{w}_{t}
, the infinitesimal Kraus operator in
eq.

108
can be rewritten as

K_{dt}\left(t,d\widetilde{w}_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\exp\left(-\gamma dt+\sqrt{\gamma}O_{t}d\widetilde{w}_{t}\right)\ ,

(116)

which will be used hereafter in quantum channel calculations.

B.1.3
Solving the measurement channel

As the equal probability average over all
\widetilde{\rho}_{t}(\mathcal{O})
, the measurement channel satisfies a differential equation being the average of the linear SDE in
eq.

114
, namely, for infinitesimal evolution:

d\rho_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{dt}(\rho_{t})-\rho_{t}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}[O_{t},[O_{t},\rho_{t}]]dt\ .

(117)

Assume
O_{t}
acts on qubit
j_{t}
. By averaging
O_{t}
over all possible single-qubit Pauli operators (
\sigma_{j_{t},x}=X_{j},\sigma_{j_{t},y}=Y_{j},\sigma_{j_{t},z}=Z_{j}
each with
1/3
probability) on qubit
j_{t}
, we find

d\rho_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{dt}(\rho_{t})-\rho_{t}=-\frac{\gamma}{6}\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}[\sigma_{j_{t},\mu},[\sigma_{j_{t},\mu},\rho_{t}]]dt\ .

(118)

Particularly, this shows that
\mathcal{F}_{dt}
maps a Pauli string operator
P
to itself, and thus the measurement channel is diagonal in the Pauli basis. More explicitly, we can expand the density matrix in the Pauli basis as

\rho_{t}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}z_{t,i}P_{i}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\bm{z}_{t}\cdot\bm{P}\ ,\qquad\rho_{t+dt}=\mathcal{F}_{dt}(\rho_{t})\ ,

(119)

and the above differential equation implies

dz_{t,i}=-\frac{\gamma dt}{6}z_{t,i}\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}[\sigma_{j_{t},\mu},[\sigma_{j_{t},\mu},P_{i}]]=-\frac{4\gamma dt}{3}z_{t,i}\delta_{|P_{i}|,j_{t}}\ ,

(120)

where
|P|
denotes the support of Pauli operator
P
, while we define
\delta_{|P|,j_{t}}=1
if qubit
j_{t}\in|P|
, and
\delta_{|P|,j_{t}}=0
otherwise. Solving the above equation gives the following solution:

z_{t,i}=w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P_{i})z_{0,i}\ ,\qquad w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)=\exp\left(-\frac{4\gamma}{3}\sum_{j\in|P|}t^{(j)}\right)\ ,

(121)

where we have defined
t^{(j)}
as the total length of time that the
j
-th qubit is measured (in other words, the
j
-th qubit is measured for
t^{(j)}/dt
times), which satisfy
\sum_{j=1}^{n}t^{(j)}=t
. The coefficient
w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)
is called the
Pauli weight
of the measurement channel, which is defined as

w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)=\frac{\text{Tr}[P\mathcal{F}_{t}(P)]}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\ ,\qquad\rightarrow\qquad\mathcal{F}_{t}(P)=w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)P\ .

(122)

Thus, the action of the measurement channel on a density matrix
\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}z_{0,i}P_{i}
is

\mathcal{F}_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}z_{0,i}P_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)z_{0,i}P_{i}\ .

(123)

For weak measurements either uniformly randomly on a qubit, or sequentially on each of the
n
qubits, in the long time limit
t/dt\gg n
, each qubit is on average measured for a total length of time
t^{(j)}=t/n
. Thus, the Pauli weight in
eq.

121
becomes

w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)=w_{\mathcal{F},m}(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}mt\right)\ ,\qquad m=|P|\ ,

(124)

where
m=|P|
denotes the support size of the Pauli string operator
P
.

B.2
The Measurement-and-prepare channel

B.2.1
Definition of the channel

We now turn to the measurement-and-prepare channel, which is essential for our method to extract weak measurement shadow tomography from the outcomes gathered through weak measurements.

We define the following unnormalized classical snapshot state (
\text{Tr}[\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})]\neq 1
):

\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})=K_{t}^{{\dagger}}(\mathcal{O})K_{t}(\mathcal{O})\ ,

(125)

which tends to unnormalized random pure products state when
t\rightarrow\infty
. The random single qubit measurements then form a measurement-and-prepare quantum channel
\mathcal{M}_{t}
that maps the initial state
\rho_{0}
into

\begin{split}&\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho_{0})=\frac{1}{2^{N_{t}}}\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\rho_{0}\right]=\frac{1}{2^{N_{t}}}\sum_{\mathcal{O}}\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})p_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ \sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})p_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{O}\sim p(\mathcal{O}\mid\bar{\rho}_{0})}\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\ ,\end{split}

(126)

where
p_{t}(\mathcal{O})
is the probability of trajectory
\mathcal{O}
as defined in
eq.

112
,
N_{t}=t/dt
is the number of steps.
The channel in
eq.

126
is defined not preserving the trace of
\rho_{0}
. The randomized measurement channel maps the underlying state
\rho
to an ensemble of classical snapshot states
\sigma\sim p(\sigma|\rho)\sim\text{Tr}(\sigma\rho)
.

In practice,
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho_{0})
can be measured by summing over the output snapshot states
\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})
of all the trajectories measured. By definition, each trajectory
\mathcal{O}
occurs with probability
p_{t}(\mathcal{O})
, thus the resulting sum approaches
\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho_{0})
. Note that the output
\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})
of the measurement-and-prepare channel can be calculated solely from the measurement outcomes which determines
K_{t}(\mathcal{O})
, and no information about
\rho_{0}
is needed. By reversing the measurement-and-prepare channel, one can deduce
\rho_{0}
, or the shadow of it. Thus, it is important to derive the channel
\mathcal{M}_{t}
analytically, which we shall do below.

As local scrambled channels (because of randomized single-qubit measurements invariant under local SU(2) rotations), both channels are diagonal in the Pauli basis, and we can define their Pauli weight as:

w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)=\frac{\text{Tr}[P\mathcal{M}_{t}(P)]}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\ .

(127)

Accordingly, their action on Pauli operator
P
is given by

\mathcal{M}_{t}(P)=w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)P\ .

(128)

Particularly,
\text{Tr}\left[\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho_{0})\right]=w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\mathds{1})
is unnormalized. In the below, we derive the Pauli weight
w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)
of the unnormalized channel.

B.2.2
Pauli Transfer Matrix

For simplicity, we ignore the trajectory label
\mathcal{O}
in this subsection. We want to find a differential equation satisfied by the unnormalized Pauli weight
w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)
. To do this, we consider the time evolution of
n
steps in time
ndt
, in which we assume each of the
n
qubits is measured once. We note that the unnormalized channel in
eq.

126
at time
t
and
t+ndt
can be rewritten as

\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho_{0})=\underset{\sigma_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\,\sigma_{t}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\sigma_{t}\rho_{0}\right]\ ,\qquad\mathcal{M}_{t+ndt}(\rho_{0})=\underset{\mathcal{N}}{\mathbb{E}}\,\underset{\sigma_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\,\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{t})\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{t})\rho_{0}\right]\ ,

(129)

where we have defined a channel
\mathcal{N}
as

\sigma_{t+ndt}=\mathcal{N}(\sigma_{t})=2^{n}K_{ndt}^{\dagger}\sigma_{t}K_{ndt}\ .

(130)

Here
K_{ndt}
is a random Kraus operator corresponding to
n
steps of weak measurement, which has the form

K_{n\mathrm{d}t}=\exp\left(-n\gamma\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma}\,O_{j}\,d\widetilde{w}_{j}\right)\simeq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma}\,O_{j}\,d\widetilde{w}_{j}-\frac{n\gamma}{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\ ,

(131)

with
O_{j}
being the random Pauli operator measured on qubit
j
(
O_{j}^{2}=1
), and
d\widetilde{w}_{j}=\pm\sqrt{dt}
are random measurement outcomes.

It is clear that the channel
\mathcal{N}
above is also locally scrambled, namely, its distribution is invariant under local SU(2) rotations of each qubit. Therefore, by
Akhtar et al. 2023
, the evolution of Pauli weights from
\mathcal{M}_{t}
to
\mathcal{M}_{t+ndt}
defined above is given by a linear transformation govered by the
Pauli transfer matrix
(PTM):

w_{\mathcal{M}_{t+ndt}}(P)=\sum_{P^{\prime}}W(P,P^{\prime})\,w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P^{\prime},t)\ ,

(132)

where
P
runs over all the Pauli string operators, and the PTM is defined by

W(P,P^{\prime})=\underset{\mathcal{N}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[P\,\mathcal{N}(P^{\prime})\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\right)^{2}\ .

(133)

Calculating the PTM therefore gives the differential equations satisfied by Pauli weights
w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)
.

B.2.3
Deriving the Pauli weight differential equation

For a Pauli string operator
P^{\prime}
with support on
m^{\prime}=|P^{\prime}|
qubits, the action of channel
\mathcal{N}
to linear order of
\mathrm{d}t
reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(P^{\prime}\right)\simeq\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma}O_{j}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}-\frac{\gamma}{2}n\mathrm{d}t\right)P^{\prime}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma}O_{j}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}-\frac{\gamma}{2}n\mathrm{d}t\right)

(134)

\displaystyle\simeq(1-\gamma q\mathrm{d}t)P^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}\left\{O_{j},P^{\prime}\right\}+\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\gamma\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}^{\prime}O_{j}P^{\prime}O_{j^{\prime}}\ ,

from which we see the channel
\mathcal{N}
can map a Pauli operator
P
into another Pauli operator
P^{\prime}
with the support size (1) increase by
1
, or (2) invariant, or (3) decrease by
1
. Therefore, we expect the PTM
W(P,P^{\prime})\neq 0
only if
|m-m^{\prime}|\leq 1
, where
m=|P|
and
|m^{\prime}|=|P^{\prime}|
are the support size of Pauli operators
P
and
P^{\prime}
.

Explicitly, using the definition of PTM in
eq.

133
, up to linear order of
dt
(note that
\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}
is of order
\sqrt{dt}
), and using the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}]=0
, and
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{w}_{j^{\prime}}]=dt\delta_{jj^{\prime}}
, we find

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{N}}(P,P^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{8}{3}m\,\gamma\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\delta_{P,P^{\prime}}+4\gamma\,\mathrm{d}t\sum_{j=1}^{n}\underset{O_{j}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\delta_{P,\frac{1}{2}\{O_{j},P^{\prime}\}}\right]\ .

(135)

By further acting on
w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P^{\prime})
and averaging over
O_{j}
among all single-qubit Pauli operators with equal probability, we arrive at

\begin{split}&w_{\mathcal{M}_{t+ndt}}(P)=\sum_{P^{\prime}}W(P,P^{\prime})\,w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P^{\prime})\\
&=\left(1-\frac{8}{3}m\gamma\mathrm{d}t\right)w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)+4\gamma\mathrm{d}t\sum_{P^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{N}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{P,\frac{\left\{O_{j},P^{\prime}\right\}}{2}}w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P^{\prime})\\
&=\left(1-\frac{8}{3}m\gamma\mathrm{d}t\right)w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)+4\gamma\mathrm{d}t\sum_{P^{\prime}}w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P^{\prime})\left[\frac{1}{3}\delta_{m^{\prime},m-1}\delta_{P,OP^{\prime}}+\delta_{m^{\prime},m+1}\delta_{OP,P^{\prime}}\right]\ ,\end{split}

(136)

where we have defined
\delta_{P,OP^{\prime}}=1
if
P=OP^{\prime}
for some single-qubit Pauli operator
O
, and
m=|P|
,
|m^{\prime}|=|P^{\prime}|
are the support sizes.

The sum over
P^{\prime}
term accounts for transitions into the state
P
from other Pauli states, in the bracket of which the first term describes a weight-increasing process, where a support-
(m-1)
Pauli operator
P^{\prime}
becomes
P
, while the second term describes a weight-decreasing process, where a support-
(m+1)
Pauli operator
P^{\prime}
becomes
P
.

Dividing the above
eq.

136
by
ndt
, we arrive at a differential equation for
w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)
in the continuous-time limit:

\frac{dw_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{8m}{3n}\gamma w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)+\frac{4\gamma}{n}\sum_{P^{\prime}}w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P^{\prime})\left[\frac{1}{3}\delta_{m^{\prime},m-1}\delta_{P,OP^{\prime}}+\delta_{m^{\prime},m+1}\delta_{OP,P^{\prime}}\right].

(137)

This differential equation can be further simplified. Due to the permutation symmetry among all qubits and the initial condition symmetric among all qubits, we can assure that the Pauli weight depends only on the support size
m=|P|
of Pauli operator
P
:

w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)=w_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)\ ,\qquad m=|P|\ ,

(138)

and the Pauli weights satisfy a closed system of ODEs from
eq.

137
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{\mathcal{M},m}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{8m}{3n}\gamma\,w_{\mathcal{M},m}+\frac{4m}{3n}\gamma\,w_{\mathcal{M},m-1}+\frac{4(n-m)}{n}\gamma\,w_{\mathcal{M},m+1}\ ,\qquad(0\leq m\leq n)

(139)

This set of ODEs govern the time evolution of the Pauli weights
w_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)
(
0\leq m\leq n
) for the weak measurement-and-prepare channel, with boundary conditions
w_{\mathcal{M},-1}=w_{\mathcal{M},n+1}=0
, and initial conditions

w_{\mathcal{M},m}(0)=\delta_{m,0}\ ,

(140)

as is evident from the channel definition.

B.2.4
Analytical solution of the Pauli weights

The coupled linear ODEs in
eq.

139
can be compactly expressed in matrix form as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{w}_{\mathcal{M}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=D\ \bm{w}_{\mathcal{M}}\ ,\qquad\bm{w}_{\mathcal{M}}=(w_{\mathcal{M},0},w_{\mathcal{M},1},\cdots,w_{\mathcal{M},n})^{T}\ ,

(141)

where
\bm{w}_{\mathcal{M}}
is the column vector of Pauli weights across all support sizes
0\leq m\leq n
. The evolution is governed by the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
tridiagonal matrix
D
, the nonzero entries of which are given by:

D_{m,m^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{8}{3}\dfrac{m}{n}\gamma,&\text{if }m^{\prime}=m\\[8.61108pt]
\ \ \dfrac{4}{3}\dfrac{m}{n}\gamma,&\text{if }m^{\prime}=m-1\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{4(n-m)}{n}\gamma,&\text{if }m^{\prime}=m+1\\[8.61108pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(142)

The matrix
D
is non-Hermitian, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of which are typically complicated to obtain. However, due to its special form, an analytical solution can be obtained by mapping the problem to a well-understood physical system: a quantum spin in a magnetic field. This mapping is realized through a similarity transformation
D^{\prime}=VDV^{-1}
, which converts
D
into a Hermitian matrix without changing its eigenvalues.

More generically, we consider a tridiagonal matrix
D
of the form with nonzero entries
D_{m,m}=c_{1}m+d_{0}
,
D_{m,m+1}=c_{2}(n-m)
, and
D_{m,m-1}=c_{0}m
. We define a diagonal matrix
V
with elements

V_{m,m^{\prime}}=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\,\frac{\left(\sqrt{c_{2}/c_{0}}\right)^{m}}{\sqrt{m!(n-m)!}}\ .

A straightforward calculation of the similarity transformation
D^{\prime}=VDV^{-1}
yields the following matrix elements:

\displaystyle D^{\prime}_{m,m^{\prime}}=(VDV^{-1})_{m,m^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{c_{0}c_{2}}\sqrt{(m+1)(n-m)},&\text{if }m^{\prime}=m+1\\[4.30554pt]
\sqrt{c_{0}c_{2}}\sqrt{m(n-m+1)},&\text{if }m^{\prime}=m-1\\[4.30554pt]
c_{1}m+d_{0},&\text{if }m^{\prime}=m\\[4.30554pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(143)

These matrix elements are structurally identical to those of spin operators in a spin-
s
system, where the total number of qubits is
n=2s
, and the weight index
m
is related to the
z
-component of spin via
m=s+s_{z}
. In particular, the off-diagonal elements correspond to the matrix elements of the spin raising and lowering operators
S_{+}
and
S_{-}
, while the diagonal term matches
S_{z}
. As a result, the transformed matrix
D^{\prime}
can be written as a linear combination of spin-
s
operators:

D^{\prime}=VDV^{-1}=(c_{1}s+d_{0})\,I+c_{1}S_{z}+2\sqrt{c_{0}c_{2}}\,S_{x}\ ,

(144)

which corresponds to the Hamiltonian of a spin-
s
particle in an effective magnetic field
\bm{B}=-(2\sqrt{c_{0}c_{2}},\,0,\,c_{1})
. Particularly, it is known that the eigenvalues of such a Hamiltonian are equally spaced, given by

\lambda_{p}=p\Lambda_{1}+(n-p)\Lambda_{2}\ ,\qquad\Lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{1}\pm\sqrt{c_{1}^{2}+4c_{0}c_{2}}\right)\ .

(145)

In our case here, the constants are given by

c_{0}=\frac{4\gamma}{3n}\ ,\quad c_{1}=-\frac{8\gamma}{3n}\ ,\quad c_{2}=\frac{4\gamma}{n}\ ,\quad d_{0}=0\ .

(146)

Substituting the coefficients from the table, we obtain the exact
p
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{p}
of the evolution matrix
D
:

\lambda_{p}=\frac{4\gamma}{3}-\frac{4\gamma}{n}p\qquad\text{for }p=0,1,\ldots,n\ .

(147)

The largest eigenvalue corresponds to
p=0
, giving
\lambda_{0}=\frac{4\gamma}{3}
. The general solution for the Pauli weight vector is a linear combination of the corresponding eigenvectors
\bm{v}^{(p)}
of
D
:

\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{\mathcal{M}}(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{n}c_{p}\,e^{\lambda_{p}t}\bm{v}^{(p)},

(148)

where the coefficients
c_{p}
are determined by the initial condition
\bm{w}_{\mathcal{M}}(0)=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
. After solving for the coefficients, one arrives at the solution:

w_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)=w_{\mathcal{M},0}(t)\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{16\gamma t}{3n}}}{3+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{16\gamma t}{3n}}}\right)^{m}\ ,\qquad w_{\mathcal{M},0}(t)=w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\mathds{1})=\left(3\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{4\gamma t}{3n}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{4\gamma t}{n}}\right)^{n}\ .

(149)

We can define the Pauli weight of the channel after normalization as

\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)=\frac{w_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)}{w_{\mathcal{M},0}(t)}=\left(\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{16\gamma t}{3n}}}{3+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{16\gamma t}{3n}}}\right)^{m}\ ,\qquad\rightarrow\qquad\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)=\big(\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{M},1}(t)\big)^{m}\ .

(150)

In the long-time limit
t\to\infty
, one has

\widetilde{w}_{\mathcal{M},m}(t\rightarrow\infty)=\frac{w_{\mathcal{M},m}(t\rightarrow\infty)}{w_{\mathcal{M},0}(t\rightarrow\infty)}=\frac{1}{3^{m}}\ ,

(151)

This agrees with the fact that
\sigma_{t}/\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma_{t})=K_{t}^{\dagger}K_{t}/\text{Tr}(K_{t}^{\dagger}K_{t})
is a product state. It is a product state of mixed state unless
t\rightarrow\infty
, when it becomes a pure product state.

fig.

6
shows the Pauli weight
\tilde{w}_{\sigma,m}(t)
at finite time
t
calculated analytically from the above (see caption for detailed descriptions).

Figure 6:

Rescaled Pauli weight
w_{\mathcal{M},m}(t)/w_{\mathcal{M},0}(t)
(a) as a function of the support size
m
for different fixed time
t
(see legend), and (b) as a function of time
t
, which approaches
1/3
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

B.3
Error and shadow norm

We recover the initial state
\rho_{0}
by defining shadow of the measured trajectories
\mathcal{O}
:

\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))\ .

(152)

It is straightforward to show that the mean value of the shadow of various trajectories
\mathcal{O}
from the same initial state
\rho_{0}
is the initial state
\rho_{0}
:

\underset{p_{t}(\mathcal{O})}{\mathbb{E}}\ \hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}=\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ p_{t}(\mathcal{O})\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}\Big[\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ p_{t}(\mathcal{O})\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\Big]=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}(\rho_{0})\Big]=\rho_{0}\ .

(153)

This is the key of our method.

We now examine the variance of an operator
O
being measured from the shadow. We define

\hat{o}=\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}O)\ ,\qquad\rightarrow\qquad\mathbb{E}\ [\hat{o}]=\text{Tr}(\rho_{0}O)\ .

(154)

Its variance is given by

\begin{split}\text{Var}[\hat{o}]&=\mathbb{E}[(\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}O))^{2}]-(\text{Tr}(\rho_{0}O))^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[(\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}O))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(\text{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))^{2}]\\
&=2^{N_{t}}\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ p_{t}(\mathcal{O})(\text{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))^{2}\ .\end{split}

(155)

Note that

p_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\text{Tr}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\rho_{0})\sim\frac{\text{Tr}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\ ,\qquad\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})=K_{t}^{{\dagger}}(\mathcal{O})K_{t}(\mathcal{O})\ ,

(156)

we can estimate the Variance of
\hat{o}
as

\text{Var}[\hat{o}]\leq\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ \frac{2^{N_{t}}\text{Tr}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}(\text{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))^{2}=\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ \frac{2^{N_{t}}\text{Tr}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\text{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))\Big]\ .

(157)

If we estimate
2^{N_{t}}\text{Tr}(\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))\simeq C_{t}
as a constant, we approximately have

\begin{split}\text{Var}[\hat{o}]&\leq\frac{C_{t}}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\underset{\mathcal{O}}{\mathbb{E}}\ \sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})\text{Tr}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O}))\Big]=\frac{C_{t}}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)\mathcal{M}_{t}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O))\Big]=\frac{C_{t}}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)O\Big]\ .\end{split}

(158)

The constant
C_{t}
can be fixed by noting that the above upper bound, which is the expectation value of the square of operator
O
, should be
1
if
O=\mathds{1}
is the identity:

\frac{C_{t}}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\mathds{1})\Big]=1\ ,\qquad\rightarrow\qquad\frac{C_{t}}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}=\frac{1}{\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\mathds{1})\Big]}\ .

(159)

This gives the variance of each shadow:

\text{Var}[\hat{o}]\leq\|O\|^{2}_{\text{sh}}\ ,

(160)

where we have defined the shadow norm of operator
O
as

\|O\|^{2}_{\text{sh}}=\frac{\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(O)O\Big]}{\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\mathds{1})\Big]}\ .

(161)

For Pauli operators
P
with support size
m=|P|
, the shadow norm is

\|P\|^{2}_{\text{sh}}=\frac{\text{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(P)P\Big]}{\text{Tr}{\mathds{1}}}=\frac{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\mathds{1})}{w_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(P)}\qquad\rightarrow 3^{m}\qquad\text{as}\qquad t\rightarrow\infty\ .

(162)

For
M
shadows, the total variance will be
1/M
factor smaller:

\text{Var}[\hat{o}]\leq\frac{\|O\|^{2}_{\text{sh}}}{M}\ .

(163)

B.4
Efficient calculation of the shadow

The shadow in
eq.

152
can be calculated efficiently due to the nice properties we showed earlier. First, recall that the Kraus operator
K_{t}(\mathcal{O})
associated with trajectory
\mathcal{O}
is a product of Kraus operators over each individual qubits:

\begin{split}&K_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{T}\prod_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}K_{dt}\left(O_{t}^{\prime},do_{t}^{\prime}\right)=\otimes_{j=1}^{n}K_{t}^{(j)},\quad K_{t}^{(j)}=\mathcal{T}\prod_{j_{t^{\prime}}=j}K_{dt}\left(O_{t}^{\prime},do_{t}^{\prime}\right)\ ,\quad K_{dt}(O_{t},do_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\exp\left(-\gamma dt+o_{t}\sqrt{\gamma dt}O_{t}\right)\end{split}

(164)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordering operator, and each Kraus operator
K_{t}^{(j)}
is the accumulated contributions of measurements of observables

O_{t}
acting on the
j_{t}=j
-th qubit. Note that each
K_{t}^{(j)}
is simply a
2\times 2
matrix acting on the
j
-th qubit.

Therefore,
\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})
can be reexpressed in a tensor product form:

\sigma_{t}(\mathcal{O})=\otimes_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{t}^{(j)}(\mathcal{O})\ ,\qquad\sigma_{t}^{(j)}(\mathcal{O})=K_{t}^{(j)\dagger}(\mathcal{O})K_{t}^{(j)}(\mathcal{O})\ ,

(165)

where each
\sigma_{t}^{(j)}(\mathcal{O})
is simply a
2\times 2
matrix.

Then, because the channel
\mathcal{M}_{t}
has a Pauli weight
w_{\sigma,m}(t)\equiv\left(w_{\sigma,1}(t)\right)^{m}
, the action of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
and
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}
simply decomposes into the action on each qubit. If we decompose
\sigma_{t}^{(j)}(\mathcal{O})
in single-qubit Pauli basis as

\sigma_{t}^{(j)}(\mathcal{O})=\kappa_{0}^{(j)}I_{j}+\kappa_{0}^{(j)}X_{j}+\kappa_{0}^{(j)}Y_{j}+\kappa_{0}^{(j)}Z_{j}\ ,

(166)

then we can define the single-qubit shadow (which is a
2\times 2
matrix)

\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}^{(j)}=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{-1}(\sigma_{t}^{(j)})=\kappa_{0}^{(j)}I_{j}+\frac{1}{w_{\sigma,1}(t)}\Big(\kappa_{0}^{(j)}X_{j}+\kappa_{0}^{(j)}Y_{j}+\kappa_{0}^{(j)}Z_{j}\Big)\ ,

(167)

and the shadow of the total system is simply the tensor product:

\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}=\otimes_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\rho}_{0,\mathcal{O}}^{(j)}\ .

(168)

Appendix C
From measurement channel and reverse Petz channel to quantum diffusion

In this appendix, we show how the weak measurement channel and its Petz recovery channel correspond to the partial differential equations (PDEs) of the quantum diffusion picture of our weak measurements. The key observation is that the density matrix after the quantum channels can be understood as a quantum generalization of the probability distribution in diffusion problem, which satisfies the PDEs of the diffusion.

C.1
The measurement channel and the forward diffusion

We first focus on the forward evolution of the system by a sequence of weak measurements.

We consider a system of
n
qubits. As introduced in the main text, the measurement channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
describes the averaged state of all the weak measurement trajectories
\mathcal{O}
. Earlier, we have derived the analytical expression of the measurement channel. For a density matrix
\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}z_{i}P_{i}
, where
P_{i}
runs over all the Pauli string operators

\mathcal{F}_{t}(\rho_{0})=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P_{i})(t)z_{i}P_{i}\ ,\qquad w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)=w_{\mathcal{F},m}(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}mt\right)\ ,\qquad m=|P|\ .

(169)

Here
m=|P|
is the support size of Pauli operator
P
. Note that the Pauli weight
w_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(P)=w_{\mathcal{F},m}(t)
only depends on the support size
m
of operator
P
.

We consider the measurement channel evolved state
\rho_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}(\rho_{0})
of some initial state (mixed or pure)
\rho_{0}
. In practice, the random weak measurement along each trajectory
\mathcal{O}
leads to a random pure product state, since the single-qubit weak measurements eventually collapse each qubit to a single-qubit pure state. This motivates us to define the following pure product coherent state basis:

|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{I_{j}+\bm{n}_{j}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{j}}{2}\right)\ ,\qquad\bm{n}=(\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\cdots,\bm{n}_{n})\ ,\quad|\bm{n}_{j}|=1\ ,\quad\bm{n}_{j}=(n_{j,x},n_{j,y},n_{j,z})\ ,

(170)

where
I_{j}
is the identity matrix of the
j
-th qubit, and
\bm{\sigma}_{j}=(\sigma_{j,x},\sigma_{j,y},\sigma_{j,z})
of the
j
-th qubit.
As defined,
\bm{n}_{j}
is a unit vector living on the Bloch sphere of the
j
-th qubit (
1\leq j\leq n
). the coherent states
|\bm{n}\rangle
form an overcomplete basis of the system, satisfying

\int d\bm{n}|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|=\frac{I}{2^{n}}\ ,\qquad\int d\bm{n}=\text{Tr}\left(\frac{I}{2^{n}}\right)=1\ ,

(171)

where
d\bm{n}
is the Haar measure in the space of
\bm{n}
, and
I
is the identity operator. Generically, the density matrix
\rho_{t}
can be expanded in terms of the coherent state projector basis as:

\rho_{t}=\int d\bm{n}p(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,\qquad\int d\bm{n}p(\bm{n},t)=1\ ,

(172)

which is known as the Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation, and
p(\bm{n},t)
can be called the quasi-probability. For Hermitian operator
\rho_{t}
,
p(\bm{n},t)
is real. We also note that the choice of
p(\bm{n},t)
is not unique due to the overcompleteness of coherent state basis.

Generically
p(\bm{n},t)
is not necessarily positive. Our discussion below does not require
p(\bm{n},t)
to be non-negative; but we will show
p(\bm{n},t)
is similar to the probability distribution in the classical diffusion problem.

Physical Meaning and relation to previous considerations
. If all the
p(\bm{n},t)\geq 0
, then
\rho_{t}
has no entanglement between qubits, but only has classical correlations. In this case,
\rho_{t}
can be exactly understood as an esemble of random pure random product states
|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|
with probability distribution
p(\bm{n},t)
, which are evolved from an initial state esemble of random pure product states with probability distribution
p(\bm{n},0)
. We note that for generic states, we have been denoting the probability distribution of states as
p(\bm{z},t)
, in terms of variable
\bm{z}
which has components
z_{P}
being the coefficients of Pauli matrices
P
in the density matrix
\rho=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}z_{i}P_{i}
(see
eq.

169
). When the initial states of our setup are restricted to random pure product states
|\bm{n}\rangle
, they will remain random pure product states during the weak measurement evolution; their corresponding variable
\bm{z}
is a function of
\bm{n}
determined by
|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{i}z_{i}P_{i}
, and by changing of variables we can re-express
p(\bm{z},t)
as
p(\bm{n},t)
. Accordingly, the nonlinear SDE for variable
\bm{z}
becomes a nonlinear SDE for variable
\bm{n}
on the Bloch spheres.

C.1.1
The single-qubit example

We first consider the example of a single-qubit system, for which
\bm{n}=\bm{n}_{1}
. In this case, the state
\rho_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}(\rho_{0})
at time

is given by

\rho_{t}=\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)\frac{I_{1}+\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{1}}{2}\ .

(173)

By

169

satisfying

\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)=1\ ,\qquad\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)\bm{n}_{1}=e^{-\frac{4\gamma}{3}t}\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},0)\bm{n}_{1}\ .

(174)

We now show that this correspond to a diffusion problem of quasi-probability distribution
p(\bm{n}_{1},t)
. As we have explained,
\rho_{t}
in the form of

can be understood as the average of an ensemble of random pure single-qubit states
|\bm{n}_{1}\rangle
with a “probability” distribution
p(\bm{n}_{1},t)
. Particularly,
\rho_{0}
corresponds to an esemble of initial states
|\bm{n}_{1}\rangle
with a “probability” distribution

\bm{z}

\bm{n}_{1}

restrited on the Bloch sphere:

(175)

where
\nabla_{\perp}^{2}
is the Laplacian
\nabla_{\bm{n}_{1}}^{2}
restricted on the Bloch sphere
|\bm{n}_{1}|=1

is the diffusion constant which can be derived from the nonlinear SDE restricted on Bloch sphere. Here we shall determine

by matching the evolution of the measurement channel. Note that the PDE in

\bm{f}
in the nonlinear SDE for generic state

We can solve the diffusion equation by expanding

in terms of spherical harmonics as

p(\bm{n}_{1},t)=\sum_{lm}\widetilde{p}_{lm}(t)Y_{lm}(\theta_{1},\varphi_{1})\ ,\quad\widetilde{p}_{lm}(t)=\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)Y_{lm}(\theta_{1},\varphi_{1})\ ,\quad\bm{n}_{1}=(\sin\theta_{1}\cos\varphi_{1},\sin\theta_{1}\sin\varphi_{1},\cos\theta_{1})\ .

(176)

Here
(\theta_{1},\varphi_{1})
is the spherical coordinate of
\bm{n}_{1}
. The angular momentum
l=0,1,2,\cdots
, and
|m|\leq l
.
eq.

175
tells us

\partial_{t}\widetilde{p}_{lm}(t)=-Dl(l+1)\widetilde{p}_{lm}(t)\ ,\quad\rightarrow\quad\widetilde{p}_{lm}(t)=e^{-Dl(l+1)t}\widetilde{p}_{lm}(0)\ .

(177)

Note that
Y_{10}(\theta_{1},\varphi_{1})=\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}v_{1,z}
, and
Y_{1\pm 1}(\theta_{1},\varphi_{1})=\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}(v_{1,x}\pm iv_{1,y})

and
176

\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)\bm{n}_{1}\propto\widetilde{p}_{1m}(t)

l=1

\widetilde{p}_{1m}(t)=e^{-\frac{4\gamma}{3}t}\widetilde{p}_{1m}(0)\ .

(178)

Comparing with

\rho_{t}
from the measurement channel is equivalent to the forward diffusion problem of probability distribution

satisfying the forward diffusion PDE:

\partial_{t}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)=\frac{2\gamma}{3}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)\ .

(179)

Lastly,
\widetilde{p}_{00}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}

\int d\bm{n}_{1}p(\bm{n}_{1},t)=\sqrt{4\pi}\widetilde{p}_{00}(t)=1

C.1.2

For

C.2
The Petz recovery channel and the backward diffusion

We now consider the Petz recovery channel of the measurement channel. For states
\rho_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}(\rho_{0})
evolved by the measurement channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
defined above, the Petz recovery channel reversing from final time
T
to time
T-t
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{t}(\sigma)=\rho^{1/2}_{T-t}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\dagger}(\rho_{T}^{-1/2}\sigma\rho_{T}^{-1/2})\rho^{1/2}_{T-t}\ ,\qquad\rightarrow\qquad\mathcal{R}_{t}(\rho_{T})=\rho_{T-t}\ ,

(181)

where
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\dagger}
is the conjugate of the measurement channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, defined by
\text{Tr}(\mathcal{F}_{t}(\rho_{1})\rho_{2})=\text{Tr}(\rho_{1}\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\dagger}(\rho_{2}))
. In the case of measurement channel in
eq.

169
, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

We consider the infinitesimal Petz recovery channel from
\rho_{t+dt}
to
\rho_{t}
given by

\mathcal{R}_{dt}(\sigma)=\rho^{1/2}_{t}\mathcal{F}_{dt}^{\dagger}(\rho_{t+dt}^{-1/2}\sigma\rho_{t+dt}^{-1/2})\rho^{1/2}_{t}\ ,

(182)

and show it is analogous to the backward diffusion in the diffusion model. To the linear order of
dt
, we have

\frac{d\sigma}{dt}=\frac{\mathcal{R}_{dt}(\sigma)-\sigma}{dt}=\rho^{1/2}_{t}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{dt}-1}{dt}(\rho_{t}^{-1/2}\sigma\rho_{t}^{-1/2})\rho^{1/2}_{t}+\frac{\rho^{1/2}_{t}\rho_{t+dt}^{-1/2}-1}{dt}\sigma+\sigma\frac{\rho_{t+dt}^{-1/2}\rho^{1/2}_{t}-1}{dt}\ .

(183)

We rewrite the operators involved above in the Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation as:

\rho_{t}=\int d\bm{n}p(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,\quad\rho_{t}^{1/2}=\int d\bm{n}\eta(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,\quad\rho_{t}^{-1/2}=\int d\bm{n}\lambda(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,

(184)

and

\frac{\rho^{1/2}_{t}\rho_{t+dt}^{-1/2}-1}{dt}=\int d\bm{n}\beta(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ .

(185)

To better understand them, we first define the multiplication of operators in the Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation:

A=\int d\bm{n}p_{A}(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,\quad B=\int d\bm{n}p_{B}(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,\quad AB=\int d\bm{n}p_{AB}(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,

(186)

where we can define a multiplication operation

p_{AB}(\bm{n},t)=(p_{A}*p_{B})(\bm{n},t)\ ,

(187)

where
*
being an operation similar to convolution. In this sense, the functions defined in
eq.

184
satisfy
\eta*\eta=p
, and
\lambda*\lambda*p=1
.

By expressing state
\sigma
as

\sigma=\rho_{t}=\int d\bm{n}q(\bm{n},t)|\bm{n}\rangle\langle\bm{n}|\ ,

(188)

in terms of a function
q(\bm{n},t)
, we can formally rewrite
eq.

183
in the Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation as a PDE:

\partial_{t}q=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\eta*[\nabla_{\perp}^{2}(\lambda*q*\lambda)]*\eta+\beta*q+q*\bar{\beta}\ ,

(189)

where
\bar{\beta}
is the complex conjugate of
\beta
, and we have used the fact that the forward evolution
\frac{\mathcal{F}_{dt}-1}{dt}=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}
from
eq.

180
.

We claim that
eq.

189
is the quantum analog of the backward diffusion PDE of the classical diffusion model. To see this, we show it reduces to the classical backward diffusion in a properly defined classical limit.

We define the classical limit as the case where all the coherent states
|\bm{n}\rangle
are orthonormal, namely,

\langle\bm{n}|\bm{n}^{\prime}\rangle=\delta(\bm{n}-\bm{n}^{\prime})\ .

(190)

For qubits, this is an unphysical limit, but we can understand it as being approached when we consider many copies of the coherent states, namely,
|\bm{n}\rangle\rightarrow|\bm{n}\rangle^{\otimes k}
with
k\rightarrow\infty
, or equivalently, the large spin limit of each qubit (which is spin
1/2
). In this classical limit, the states in the Glauber-Sudarshan P-representation become effectively diagonal, and by the definition in
eq.

186
, the
*
multiplication simply becomes the normal multiplication. The functions we defined then reduces to the following (the variable
(\bm{n},t)
are omitted for simplicity):

\eta=p^{1/2}\ ,\qquad\lambda=p^{-1/2}\ ,\qquad\beta=\frac{p^{1/2}(\bm{n},t)p^{-1/2}(\bm{n},t+dt)-1}{dt}=-\frac{\partial_{t}p}{2p}=-\frac{\gamma}{3n}\frac{\nabla_{\perp}^{2}p}{p}\ .

(191)

Substituting these function into
eq.

189
, we find

\partial_{t}q=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\left[p\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)-q\frac{\nabla_{\perp}^{2}p}{p}\right]=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\left[\nabla_{\perp}^{2}q-2\nabla_{\perp}\cdot\left(q\frac{\nabla_{\perp}p}{p}\right)\right]\ ,

(192)

or in a more familiar form,

\partial_{t}q(\bm{n},t)=\frac{2\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}q(\bm{n},t)-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}\cdot\left[q(\bm{n},t)\nabla_{\perp}\log p(\bm{n},t)\right]\ .

(193)

This is exactly the backward diffusion PDE corresponding to the forward diffusion PDE in
eq.

180
, where
\frac{4\gamma}{3n}\nabla_{\perp}\log p(\bm{n},t)
plays the role of the backward drifting force.

Approaching the classical limit
. As we have argued above, if we replace each spin
1/2
qubit by a large spin
S
qudit (which has
2S+1
states),
eq.

190
will be asymptotically satisfied, and we can achieve the classical limit. In this case, state
|\bm{n}\rangle
denotes the pure product of large spin coherent states along direction
\bm{n}_{j}
on the
j
-th spin. One then prepares an esemble of initial states being pure product coherent states
|\bm{n}\rangle
with a probability distribution
p(\bm{n},t)
, and perform a sequence of weak measurement for these large spin qudits. In the large spin limit, the measurement channel
\mathcal{F}_{t}
matches exactly with the forward classical diffusion governed by
eq.

180
, and the Petz recovery channel
\mathcal{R}_{t}
matches exactly with the backward classical diffusion governed by
eq.

54
.

Lindbladian of the measurement channel

For an single

S_{j}
, the conditional mutual information is bounded by
I_{\rho_{t}}(A:C|B)\leq\mathrm{poly}(|A|,|C|)\,e^{-\mathrm{dist}(A,C)/\xi}
, where
C
is the complement of
S_{j}
. Then by requiring

\mathrm{dist}(A,C)\geq\xi\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot\,\ln\!\left(\frac{2n}{\epsilon}\right)}{\gamma\epsilon^{2}\cdot\delta t}\right)\ ,\qquad T\geq\frac{3n}{4\gamma}\ln\left(\frac{2n}{\epsilon}\right)\ ,

(208)

the final state
\rho_{0}^{\prime}
produced by applying all
N_{T}
Petz recovery steps remains close to the initial state
\rho_{0}
, with the error bounded by the trace distance:

\big\|\rho_{0}^{\prime}-\rho_{0}\big\|_{1}\leq\epsilon\ .

(209)

Proof.

The total error can be bounded by the sum of single-step errors:

\big\|\rho_{0}^{\prime}-\rho_{0}\big\|_{1}=E_{\mathrm{petz}}(T)+E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T),\qquad E_{\mathrm{petz}}(T)=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}}\big\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{S_{k}}_{\delta t}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\rho_{t_{k}})\big)-\rho_{t_{k}}\big\|_{1}\ ,

(210)

where
\rho_{t_{k}}=\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\rho_{t_{k-1}})
is the state after the
k
th forward step of duration
\delta t
.
By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, this implies

E_{\mathrm{petz}}(T)^{2}\leq N_{T}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}}\big\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{S_{k}}_{\delta t}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\rho_{t_{k}})\big)-\rho_{t_{k}}\big\|_{1}^{2}.

(211)

Each single-step recovery error is controlled by the drop in the local CMI over that step:

\big\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{S}_{\delta t}(\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\rho))-\rho\big\|_{1}^{2}\leq 2\ln 2\,\Big[\,I_{\rho}(A:C\,|\,B)-I_{\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\rho)}(A:C\,|\,B)\,\Big],

(212)

for any subsystem
S=A\cup B
(with complement
S^{C}
).

Summing these squared errors over
k=1,\dots,N_{T}
yields a telescoping sum of local CMI differences at each recovery step.
By the data-processing inequality, the conditional mutual information is non-increasing under the forward channel,

I_{\rho_{t_{k}}}(A:C|B)\leq I_{\rho_{t_{k-1}}}(A:C|B)
, so the sum of CMI differences telescopes to the initial-minus-final value:

\sum_{k=1}^{N_{T}}\Big(I_{\rho_{t_{k}-1}}(A:C|B)-I_{\rho_{t_{k}}}(A:C|B)\Big)=I_{\rho_{0}}(A:C|B)-I_{\rho_{T}}(A:C|B)\leq I_{\rho_{0}}(A:C|B),

(213)

using the fact that
I_{\rho_{T}}\geq 0
.

Meanwhile,
E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\sim n\exp\left(-\frac{4\gamma}{3n}T\right)
, because for non-identity Pauli strings, each Pauli weight decays exponentially to zero with increasing diffusion time
T
, as shown in
eq.

124
. To achieve
E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},
it suffices to require

T\geq\frac{3n}{4\gamma}\ln\left(\frac{2n}{\epsilon}\right)

(214)

Using the assumed locality bound
I_{\rho_{0}}(A:C|B)\leq\mathrm{poly}(|A|,|C|)\,e^{-\mathrm{dist}(A,C)/\xi}
and noting
N_{T}=T/\delta t,

eq.

211
becomes

E_{\mathrm{petz}}(T)^{2}\leq\frac{T}{\delta t}\mathrm{poly}(|A|,|C|)\,e^{-\mathrm{dist}(A,C)/\xi}

(215)

To achieve an error
E_{\mathrm{petz}}(T)^{2}\leq(\frac{\epsilon}{2})^{2}
, it suffices to require

\mathrm{dist}(A,C)\geq\xi\cdot\log\left(\frac{4\mathrm{poly}(n)T}{\epsilon^{2}\cdot\delta t}\right)\geq\xi\cdot\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot\,\ln\!\left(\frac{2n}{\epsilon}\right)}{\gamma\epsilon^{2}\cdot\delta t}\right)

(216)

Then we have the global recovery error

\big\|\rho_{0}^{\prime}-\rho_{0}\big\|_{1}=E_{\mathrm{petz}}(T)+E_{\mathrm{diff}}(T)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon\ .

(217)

∎
