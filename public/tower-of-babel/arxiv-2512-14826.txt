Stephan Foldes and Russ Woodroofe

Abstract
. We show that in a rank supersolvable lattice that is graded by a bounded real interval, any antichain cutset is a level set for some appropriately constructed grading. As a consequence, given an antichain cutset in any of the measurable Boolean lattice, a continuous partition lattice, or a continuous projective geometry, we may find a grading in which the cutset is a level set.



1.
Introduction

The Boolean lattice on a finite set is a basic example in lattice
theory, with almost any nice property that one could ask for. The
Boolean lattice on an infinite set, on the other hand, is much less
well-behaved. For example, it is difficult or impossible to extend
gradedness to this situation; in particular, ordinal theory gives
that there are many isomorphism classes of maximal chains. Better-behaved
is the
measurable Boolean lattice
on a complete measure space

(X,\mu)
with a finite measure. In this lattice, we restrict ourselves
to the measurable subsets of
X
, and consider two measurable subsets
to be equivalent if their symmetric difference has measure zero. If
the measure does not have any points of positive measure (or, in the
other extreme, if
\mu
is the counting measure), then
\mu
gives
an analogue of a rank function.

The situation with the partition lattice on an infinite set is similar:
the full lattice of partitions on
[0,1]
is too large to admit a
nice rank function, and the same holds even for the lattice of partitions
into measurable parts. But Von Neumann in
[
19
,
20
]

developed machinery to build continuous extensions of lattices of
subspaces and similar families of modular lattices. Indeed, the measurable
Boolean lattice on the interval
[0,1]
can be viewed as an example
of Von Neumann’s construction. Björner in
[
2
]
extended
the machinery of Von Neumann to geometric and semimodular lattices.
A main application of Björner’s extension was a continuous extension
of finite partition lattices. Haiman in
[
12
]
gave a
more concrete construction of a continuous partition lattice, built
from measurable sets in a similar manner to the measurable Boolean
lattice. Björner’s lattice embeds in that of Haiman.

The current paper came out of an effort to extend some of our earlier
work on antichain cutsets for discrete posets
[
8
]

to the continuous situation, covering (at least) all of the examples
we have so far discussed. An
antichain cutset
in a lattice

L
is an antichain
A
so that every maximal chain nontrivially
intersects
A
. Antichain cutsets were first studied by Rival and
Zaguia
[
15
]
. The structure of antichain cutsets
is much more prescribed than that of antichains more generally. For
example, the family of antichain cutsets of a graded, finite, and
strongly connected poset coincides exactly with that of the level
sets of the poset
[
8
, Theorem 2]
. We contrast
with the situation for general antichains, where e.g. a finite partition
lattice typically has antichains that are larger than any level set

[
11
]
.

Unlike the finite situation, where a rank function may be recovered
in a straightforward way from the poset structure, the continuous
lattices that we are interested in may admit gradings that are quite
different from one another. For example, let
L
be the measurable
Boolean lattice on
[0,1]
with Lebesgue measure
\mu
. Then
\mu

is a rank function (see Section
2
for a definition),
and given a measurable positive function
f
, the function
\nu(U)=\int_{U}f\,d\mu

yields another rank function. The level sets of these two rank functions
are generally quite different from each other. Since a level set of
any rank function is necessarily an antichain cutset, it is too much
to hope that every antichain cutset is a level set in some chosen
rank function.

We believe that the following question is reasonable to ask, and of
some interest.

Question 1.1
.

For which classes of graded posets does it hold
that, for each antichain cutset
A
, there is a grading
\sigma

(possibly distinct from an initially presented one) so that
A
is
a level set under
\sigma
?

The first author showed in
[
7
]
that the condition
asked for in Question
1.1
holds for space-time causality
orders.

In the current paper, we will show that the condition of this question
holds for a reasonably general class of lattices, defined as follows.
Let
L
be a lattice with an understood rank function
\rho:L\to R
,
for
R
some subset of
\mathbb{R}
, or more generally of the extended
reals
[-\infty,\infty]
. We say that an element
m
of
L
is

rank modular
if for every element
x
of the lattice, it holds
that
\rho(x\vee m)+\rho(x\wedge m)=\rho(x)+\rho(m)
. Equivalently,
the same relation holds whenever
x
is incomparable with
m
. We
say that
L
is
rank supersolvable
if it has a maximal chain
consisting of rank modular elements (a
chief chain
).

In the case of a finite lattice with the natural rank function, rank
supersolvability is one of several equivalent conditions for supersolvability

[
9
]
. We do not know if these conditions
are equivalent in the non-discrete situation; see also Lemma
3.14

and the subsequent discussion.

Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded interval. If
L

is a rank supersolvable lattice with respect to the
R
-grading
\rho
,
and
A
is an antichain cutset for
L
, then there is some grading

\sigma
under which
A
is a level set.

Under an additional continuity condition, we can extend Theorem
1.2

to unbounded intervals. A precise statement of this extension may
be found in Theorem
4.1
below.

Corollary 1.3
.

For any antichain cutset
A
in

(1)

the measurable Boolean lattice over
[0,1]
with the Lesbesgue measure,
or

(2)

the continuous projective geometries of Von Neumann, or

(3)

the continuous partition lattice of either Björner or Haiman,

there is some grading
\sigma
under which
A
is a level set.

The chief chain of
L
(with respect to
\rho
) does not necessarily
satisfy the rank modular condition with respect to the grading
\sigma

in our construction; thus, it is not necessarily rank supersolvable
in the grading
\sigma
. We do not know whether, in the situation
of Theorem
1.2
, it is possible to construct a grading

\sigma
where both
A
is a level set and also rank modularity
of the chief chain is preserved.

A recent paper of Engel, Mitsis, Pelekis and Reiher
[
6
]

considers a somewhat different extension of discrete results on antichains
to a continuous and/or analytic setting.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
,
we define
R
-gradings, and review the details of the construction
of Von Neumann and Björner. In Section
3
, we extend
facts about rank modularity and supersolvability from the discrete
case to the current setting. In Section
4
,
we prove (an extension of) Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Definitions and background

Let
R
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
, or more generally of the extended
reals
[-\infty,\infty]
. An
R
-rank function
or
R
-grading

for the poset
L
is a function
L\to R
that restricts to an order
isomorphism on every maximal chain of
L
. Thus, a finite poset is
graded (in the usual sense) if and only if it has an
[n]
-grading.

Example 2.1
.

Given a complete measure space
(X,\mu)
, we take the measurable
Boolean lattice
\mathcal{B}(X,\mu)
to be the set of measurable
sets up to the identification of pairs of sets whose symmetric difference
has measure zero. If
\mu
is a finite measure with no points of
positive measure, then
\mu:\mathcal{B}(X,\mu)\to[0,\mu(X)]
is a

[0,\mu(X)]
-grading. We notice also, on the other hand, that the
rank function on a finite Boolean lattice is exactly the counting
measure on the underlying set.

The measurable Boolean lattice on the interval
[0,1]
with Lebesgue
measure is a natural continuous analogue of the Boolean lattice on

n
points.

Our other examples come from a construction originally due to Von
Neumann for modular lattices
[
19
,
20
]
,
and extended by Björner to semimodular lattices in
[
2
]
,
see also
[
4
]
. We review this construction.
There are two basic ingredients: a direct limit, and a metric completion.

For the direct limit, we start with a graded lattice
L_{n}
of rank

n
for each positive integer
n
. It will be convenient to require
every
L_{n}
to be semimodular. We renormalize the standard rank
function on
L_{n}
by dividing by
n
: thus, the top element
\hat{1}

has renormalized rank
1
, an element in a middle rank has renormalized
rank of roughly
1/2
, etc.

Whenever
k
properly divides
n
, we consider a lattice embedding

\varphi^{k,n}:L_{k}\to L_{n}
that respects the renormalized rank.
We require that, when
k
divides
m
and
m
divides
n
, it
holds that
\varphi^{k,n}
agrees with the composition of
\varphi^{k,m}

and
\varphi^{m,n}.
Now we take the directed limit
L_{(\infty)}

of this system of lattices and embeddings over the positive integers
with the divisibility order. In other words, we take a union over
all
L_{n}
, and then identify earlier
L_{k}
’s with sublattices
in later
L_{n}
’s. The renormalized rank function carries through
to give a
([0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q})
-grading on
L_{(\infty)}
.

We now take a metric completion to get a
[0,1]
-graded lattice.
We need to have a metric for this purpose. It has long been known
that in a semimodular lattice with rank function
r
, the function

d(x,y)=2r(x\vee y)-r(x)-r(y)
is a metric
[
14
]
.
Indeed, we may view this as the length of the “up-down path” from

x
up to
x\vee y
, and from
x\vee y
back down to
y
. If every

L_{n}
is semimodular, then these metrics pass to a metric on
L_{(\infty)}
.
Now
L_{\infty}
is the metric completion of
L_{(\infty)}
under
this metric. We recover a rank function by letting
\rho(x)=d(\hat{0},x)
;
by definition of the distance function, this
\rho
extends the renormalized
rank function on
L_{(\infty)}
.

Example 2.2
.

Von Neumann defines a continuous projective geometry over a field

\mathbb{F}

[
19
, Section 5]
; see also
[
1
, Chapter X.5]
.
In the language above, this may be described as follows. Let
L_{n}

be the lattice of all subspaces of the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. When

n
is a multiple of
k
, view
\mathbb{F}^{n}
as
\mathbb{F}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{F}^{k}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{F}^{k}
.
Now define
\varphi
by mapping a subspace
W
of
\mathbb{F}^{k}
to

W\oplus\cdots\oplus W
. It is easy to see that
\varphi
preserves
renormalized rank, and is a lattice mapping. Every element of the
metric completion is rank modular with respect to
\rho
.

Example 2.3
.

The measurable Boolean lattice
on
[0,1]
may be built from this machinery, as follows
[
20
, Section 8]
.
Let
L_{n}
be the Boolean lattice of all subsets of
[n]
. When

n
is a multiple of
k
, view
[n]
as
[k]\times[n/k]
. Define

\varphi
by mapping
S\subseteq[k]
to
S\times[n/k]
, and take
the directed limit and metric completion.

The identification with subsets of
[0,1]
comes by identifying
i\in[n]

with the interval
(\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}]
. Since the Lebesgue
measure of a union of
m
intervals of length
\frac{1}{n}
is
\frac{m}{n}
,
the renormalized rank of
S\subseteq[n]
agrees with the Lebesgue
measure. Now
L_{(\infty)}
consists of all sets that may be written
as unions of half-open intervals with rational endpoints. The metric
completion gives us unions of such sets, recovering (up to measure
zero) all Borel sets.

Example 2.4
.

Björner
[
2
]
builds a measurable partition lattice
using a similar approach. He identifies elements of an underlying

(n+1)
-element set with the set of intervals

\left\{\{0\},(0,\frac{1}{n}],(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\dots,(\frac{n-1}{n},1]\right\}.

Then when
n
is a multiple of
k
, we identify each interval of
length
\frac{1}{k}
with
\frac{n}{k}
intervals of length
\frac{1}{n}

in a similar manner as in Example
2.3
.
Rank supersolvability of every finite partition lattice yields rank
supersolvability of the limit object
[
2
, Theorem 2]
.

We remark that while one might call
[0,1]
-graded or
\mathbb{R}
-graded
posets “continuously graded”, they do not appear to have any relationship
to speak of with the continuous posets of
[
10
]

that are studied in computability theory. Indeed, while the measurable
Boolean lattice on
[0,1]
is a main object for our purposes here,
it already fails to have interesting way-below relationships. When
every element of the lattice
L
is rank modular, the
R
-grading
gives
L
the structure of a metric lattice in the sense of
[
1
]
.

Haiman gives explicit descriptions of Björner’s measurable partition
lattice as well as of related lattices
[
12
]
. Haiman’s
lattices are again rank supersolvable
[
12
, Lemma 2.15]
.
A recent preprint of Contreras Mantilla and Sinclair
[
5
]

discusses the Von Neumann and Björner construction from a model theory
perspective. See also the paper
[
18
]
of
Vershik and Yakubovich, and the survey article of Björner
[
3
]
.

3.
Rank modularity and rank supersolvable lattices

3.1.
Continuity

We begin with some basic results about rank supersolvable lattices,
extending the well-known theory of finite supersolvable lattices
[
9
,
13
,
16
,
17
]
.

Throughout this section, let
R
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
, or more generally
of the extended reals
[-\infty,\infty]
. Let
L
be an
R
-graded
lattice. Let
\mathbf{m}=\left\{m_{\lambda}:\lambda\in R\right\}

be a maximal chain in
L
with
\rho(m_{\lambda})=\lambda
, which
we will take to be the chief chain of
L
when it is rank supersolvable.
We place the standard topology of
R\subseteq[-\infty,\infty]
on
each maximal chain.

We start with an easy but useful consequence of rank modularity, not
requiring rank supersolvability.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
w<z
and
m^{\prime}<m
are elements of
L
,
where
m
and
m^{\prime}
are rank modular, then

\displaystyle\rho(m\wedge z)+\rho(m\vee z)-\left(\rho(m\wedge w)+\rho(m\vee w)\right)

\displaystyle=\rho(z)-\rho(w),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\rho(m\wedge z)+\rho(m\vee z)-\left(\rho(m^{\prime}\wedge z)+\rho(m^{\prime}\vee z)\right)

\displaystyle=\rho(m)-\rho(m^{\prime}).

Proof.

Compute! We have
\rho(m\wedge z)+\rho(m\vee z)=\rho(m)+\rho(z)
,
and similarly
\rho(m\wedge w)+\rho(m\vee w)=\rho(m)+\rho(w)
. The

\rho(m)
terms cancel, giving the first equality. The second equality
is entirely similar.
∎

As in the finite case, the difference in ranks
\rho(z)-\rho(w)

may be viewed as giving the height of the interval
[w,z]
. We recover
from Lemma
3.1
several bounds on such differences.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
w<z
and
m^{\prime}<m
are elements of

L
, where
m
and
m^{\prime}
are rank modular, then the following inequalities
hold

\displaystyle\rho(m\wedge z)-\rho(m\wedge w)\leq\rho(z)-\rho(w),\qquad

\displaystyle\rho(m\vee z)-\rho(m\vee w)\leq\rho(z)-\rho(w)

\displaystyle\rho(m\wedge z)-\rho(m^{\prime}\wedge z)\leq\rho(m)-\rho(m^{\prime}),\qquad

\displaystyle\rho(m\vee z)-\rho(m^{\prime}\vee z)\leq\rho(m)-\rho(m^{\prime}).

Proof.

The left-hand sides of the inequalities are all nonnegative by order
considerations. The left-hand sides of the first row sum to the right
hand side(s) of the first row; similarly for the second row.
∎

We immediately recover two continuity properties.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{c}=\left\{c_{\kappa}:\kappa\in R\right\}

be a maximal chain in
L
, with
\rho(c_{\kappa})=\kappa
. If
m

is a rank modular element of
L
, then the following maps are Lipschitz
(hence continuous at every point in
R\cap\mathbb{R}
):

\begin{array}[]{l}R\to R\\
\kappa\mapsto\rho(m\wedge c_{\kappa})\end{array},\text{$\quad$and$\quad$$\begin{array}[]{l}R\to R\\
\kappa\mapsto\rho(m\vee c_{\kappa})\end{array}$}.

Corollary 3.4
.

If
L
is rank supersolvable with chief
chain
\mathbf{m}
, and
z
is an element of
L
, then the following
maps are Lipschitz (hence continuous at every point in
R\cap\mathbb{R}
):

\begin{array}[]{l}R\to R\\
\lambda\mapsto\rho(m_{\lambda}\wedge z)\end{array}\text{,$\quad$and$\quad$$\begin{array}[]{l}R\to R\\
\lambda\mapsto\rho(m_{\lambda}\vee z)\end{array}$}.

An alternate statement of Corollary
3.3
is
that the map formed by composing the three maps
\rho|_{\mathbf{c}}^{-1}
,
meet with
m
, and
\rho
is Lipschitz (hence continuous) as a map

R\to R
; similarly with Corollary
3.4
.

Remark 3.5
.

In the case where
L
is semimodular, so that
d(x,y)=2\rho(x\vee y)-\rho(x)-\rho(y)

is a metric, then it is a general fact that the join operation is
Lipschitz in this metric (or a product of this metric with itself)
over the entire lattice
[
2
]
. The meet operation,
on the other hand, is not continuous even in the partition lattice.
We can extend Corollary
3.3
to show that meet
with a rank modular
m
in a semimodular lattice is Lipschitz in
the metric, since

\displaystyle d(m\wedge x,m\wedge y)

\displaystyle=2\rho((m\wedge x)\vee(m\wedge y))-\rho(m\wedge x)-\rho(m\wedge y)

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho(m\wedge(x\vee y))-\rho(m\wedge x)-\rho(m\wedge y)

\displaystyle=2\rho(x\vee y)-\rho(x)-\rho(y)-2\rho(m\vee x\vee y)

\displaystyle\leq d(x,y).

It is likely that there are more general continuity statements, but
since these are incidental to our main purpose, we do not develop
this idea further.

Although infinite lattices may not be bounded, this is easy to control.
The following is immediate from definition.

Lemma 3.6
.

The lattice
L
has a (unique) maximum element
\hat{1}
if and
only if
R
has a maximum element. Similarly for minimum elements.

Notice that if
R=[-\infty,\infty]
and
\hat{1}
is a join irreducible
or
\hat{0}
is a meet irreducible, then the rank modular relation
involves an indeterminate form only when we compare the minimum
\hat{0}

and maximum
\hat{1}
elements, which causes no confusion.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
L
(and
R
) has no maximum element, then
we can extend
L
to a
(R\mathop{\cup}\sup R)
-graded lattice by
adding a maximum element
\hat{1}
to
L
and taking
\rho(\hat{1})=\sup R
.
If
L
is rank supersolvable with chief chain
\mathbf{m}
, then

L\cup\{\hat{1}\}
is rank supersolvable with chief chain
\mathbf{m}\cup\{\hat{1}\}
.

Similarly for minimum elements.

Proof.

It is immediate that the extension of
\rho
is a grading. Since

\hat{1}\vee m=\hat{1}
and
\hat{1}\wedge m=m
, the element
\hat{1}

is rank modular.
∎

Corollaries
3.3
and
3.4

give continuity of certain maps at every real point of
R
. If
R

contains
\infty
or
-\infty
, then additional care is required.
Recall from a first topology course that, since the extended reals
are homeomorphic to
[-1,1]
, a map
\varphi:[-\infty,\infty]\to[-\infty,\infty]

is continuous if and only if it respects convergence of sequences.
As an immediate consequence, we obtain the following.

Lemma 3.8
.

An increasing map
\varphi:[-\infty,\infty]\to[-\infty,\infty]

is continuous at
\infty
(or
respectively at
-\infty
) if and only if
\sup\left\{\varphi(\alpha):\alpha<\infty\right\}=\varphi(\infty)

(or respectively, if

\inf\left\{\varphi(\alpha):\alpha>-\infty\right\}=\varphi(-\infty)
).

We remark that there are supersolvable (indeed, modular) lattices
where the maps of Corollaries
3.3
and
3.4

fail to be continuous at
\pm\infty
.

Example 3.9
.

Consider the product lattice
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

with
\mathbb{R}
-grading
\rho(a,b)=a+b
, and let
L=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\cup\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}
.
Then the join (meet) is obtained by taking entry-wise maximums (minimums),
and every element of
L
is rank modular. Now
\mathbf{c}=\left\{(0,b):b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\cup\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}

is a maximal chain where
\sup\left\{(0,b)\wedge(1,0):b\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=(0,0)
,
while
\hat{1}\wedge(1,0)=(1,0)
. In particular, the map
b\mapsto\rho((0,b)\wedge(1,0))

fails to be continuous at
\infty
. An entirely similar argument
shows that the map
b\mapsto\rho((0,b)\vee(-1,0))
fails to be continuous
at
-\infty
.

Example 3.10
.

Consider the lattice of all bounded Lebesgue-measurable
sets in
\mathbb{R}
(up to measure zero), with Lebesgue measure as a
[0,\infty)
-grading.
By modularity of measures, every element is rank modular. Then the
intervals
m_{\lambda}=[-\lambda,\lambda]
form a chief chain. Since
every bounded measurable set is contained some
m_{\lambda}
, we
have that
\sup_{\lambda}\left\{m_{\lambda}\wedge y\right\}=y
.
Thus, the first map of Corollary
3.4
is continuous.

On the other hand, the intervals
c_{\kappa}=[1,1+\kappa]
also form
a maximal chain, and
\sup_{\kappa}\left\{c_{\kappa}\wedge[-1,1]\right\}=\{1\}\subsetneq[-1,1]
.
Thus, the first map of Corollary
3.3
fails
to be continuous at
\infty
.

3.2.
Real-graded vs discrete-graded supersolvable lattices

For a finite lattice, there are several conditions that are equivalent
to rank supersolvability. All are based on chains of elements satisfying
some modularity condition. Rank functions in the non-discrete case
are less rigid than those in the finite case, and that an element
that is rank modular with respect to one rank function does not generally
imply that it will be rank modular with respect to another.

Example 3.11
.

If
\rho(x)
is a
[0,1]
-grading on
L
, then
\left(\rho(x)\right)^{2}

is another
[0,1]
-grading. If
m
is rank modular with respect
to
\rho
, however, then (by the failure of the Freshman’s Dream
identity in characteristic
0
) it typically will not be rank modular
with respect to
\rho^{2}
.

Example 3.12
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly increasing function, and
\rho
is
an
R
-grading, then
f\circ\rho
is an
f(R)
-grading. If
f

is affine linear, then
m
is rank modular with respect to
\rho

if and only if it is rank modular with respect to
f\circ\rho
. If

f
is not affine linear, then it typically does not preserve rank
modularity of elements.

Going from rank modularity to other modularity conditions is easier.
The next lemma gives that a rank modular element is “left modular”.

Lemma 3.13
.

If
m
is rank modular in
L
, then for any

w<z
in
L
, it holds that

(w\vee m)\wedge z=w\vee(m\wedge z).

Proof.

Apply exactly the same argument as in
[
9
, Lemma 4.1]
.
∎

Thus, if
m
is rank modular, then we may write
w\vee m\wedge z

without parentheses.

A maximal chain consisting of rank modular elements gives somewhat
stronger lattice conditions:

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
L
be rank supersolvable with chief chain

\mathbf{m}
. For
z<w\in L
and
m_{\lambda}<m_{\kappa}\in\mathbf{m}
,
it holds that

\displaystyle(z\vee m_{\lambda})\wedge w

\displaystyle=z\vee(m_{\lambda}\wedge w),\text{ and}

\displaystyle(m_{\lambda}\vee z)\wedge m_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=m_{\lambda}\vee(z\wedge m_{\kappa}).

Proof.

The first identity is a special case of Lemma
3.13
.

For the second identity, we notice that since
(m_{\lambda}\vee z)\wedge m_{\kappa}\geq m_{\lambda}\vee(z\wedge m_{\kappa}),

it suffices to show that the ranks agree. Now, since
m_{\lambda}\vee z\vee m_{\kappa}=z\vee m_{\kappa}

and
m_{\lambda}\wedge z\wedge m_{\kappa}=m_{\lambda}\wedge z
, we
get that

\displaystyle\rho((m_{\lambda}\vee z)\wedge m_{\kappa})

\displaystyle=\rho(m_{\lambda}\vee z)+\kappa-\rho(z\vee m_{\kappa})

\displaystyle\rho(m_{\lambda}\vee(z\wedge m_{\kappa}))

\displaystyle=\lambda+\rho(m_{\kappa}\wedge z)-\rho(m_{\lambda}\wedge z).

Now Lemma
3.1
gives the difference of these ranks
to be zero, as desired.
∎

We observe in passing that since the sublattice generated by a finite
modular chain and another finite chain forms a distributive lattice

[
9
,
16
]
, the sublattice of
L

generated by (finite) combinations of
\mathbf{m}
and another chain

\mathbf{c}
is distributive.

In the finite case, the converse of Lemma
3.14
also holds
true
[
9
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.4]
. The difficulty
in extending this converse to the non-discrete case is in constructing
a nice enough rank function.

Question 3.15
.

Given an
R
-graded lattice
L
and a chain
\mathbf{m}
satisfying
the conclusion of Lemma
3.14
, is it possible to find
a rank function under which all elements of
\mathbf{m}
are rank
modular?

We close this section by extending one more result from finite lattices
to
R
-graded lattices: rank modular elements project into intervals
nicely.

Lemma 3.16
.

If
m
is a rank modular element of
L
, then
w\vee m\wedge z

is a rank modular element of the interval
[w,z]
for any
w<z

in
L
.

Proof.

It suffices by duality and iteration to show that
m\wedge z
is
rank modular in the interval
[\hat{0},z]
. For
w<z
, we desire
to have
\rho(w\vee m\wedge z)=\rho(w)+\rho(m\wedge z)-\rho(m\wedge w)
.
But the rank modular identity with
m
and
w\vee m\wedge z
gives
us that

\rho(m\vee(w\vee m\wedge z))+\rho(m\wedge(w\vee m\wedge z))-\rho(m)=\rho(w\vee m\wedge z).

Noticing that
m\vee(w\vee m\wedge z)=m\vee w
and
m\wedge(w\vee m\wedge z)=m\wedge z
,
and expanding
\rho(m\vee w)
as
\rho(m)+\rho(w)-\rho(w\wedge m)

now yields the desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Throughout this section, let
L
be a rank supersolvable lattice
with a given
R
-grading
\rho
over some interval
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
We may assume without loss of generality by Lemma
3.7

that
R
is compact (passing to the extended reals if necessary),
and so that
L
has a minimum element
\hat{0}
and maximum element

\hat{1}
. Let
\mathbf{m}=\left\{m_{\lambda}:\lambda\in R\right\}

be the chief chain guaranteed by rank supersolvability, where
\rho(m_{\lambda})=\lambda
.
Let
A
be an antichain cutset.

We will prove the following generalization of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
R\subseteq[-\infty,\infty]
be an interval.
Let
L
be a rank supersolvable lattice with respect to the
R
-grading

\rho
, with chief chain
\mathbf{m}
. Suppose further that the
following conditions are met for every maximal chain
\mathbf{c}
,

\lambda_{0}\in R
, and

:

\displaystyle\sup\big\{m_{\lambda_{0}}\wedge c

\displaystyle:c\in\mathbf{c},\rho(c)<\infty\big\}=m_{\lambda_{0}}\quad

\displaystyle\quad\inf\big\{m_{\lambda_{0}}\vee c

\displaystyle:c\in\mathbf{c},\rho(c)>-\infty\big\}=m_{\lambda_{0}}

\displaystyle\sup\big\{m_{\lambda}\wedge z

\displaystyle:\lambda\in R,\lambda<\infty\big\}=z

\displaystyle\inf\big\{m_{\lambda}\vee z

\displaystyle:\lambda\in R,\lambda>-\infty\big\}=z.

If
A
is an antichain cutset for
L
, then there is some grading

\sigma
under which
A
is a level set.

We notice that if
R
is bounded from above (in

), then the
supremum conditions are automatic; similarly if
R
is bounded from
below with the infimum conditions. Indeed, in light of Lemma
3.8

and the discussion surrounding, we see that the additional conditions
give exactly that the maps of Corollaries
3.3

and
3.4
are continuous also at
\infty
and/or
at

Lemma 4.2

Under the conditions of Theorem

,
writing
\mathbf{c}=\left\{c_{\kappa}:\kappa\in R\right\}

following maps

are continuous.

\displaystyle\lambda\mapsto\rho(m_{\lambda}\wedge z)\qquad

\displaystyle\lambda\mapsto\rho(m_{\lambda}\vee z)

\displaystyle\kappa\mapsto\rho(m_{\lambda_{0}}\wedge c_{\kappa})\qquad

\displaystyle\kappa\mapsto\rho(m_{\lambda_{0}}\vee c_{\kappa})

Remark 4.3

Examination of Examples
3.9
and
3.10

demonstrates that continuity at
\pm\infty

the lattice of Example

is isomorphic to

nonetheless holds by

We now construct the new rank function
\sigma
for Theorem

.
Consider the
good chain
through
z
given by
\left\{z\wedge m_{\lambda}\,:\,\lambda\in R\right\}\cup\left\{z\vee m_{\lambda}\,:\,\lambda\in R\right\}

We will show that the good chain through
z
is a maximal chain,

at a unique point
\alpha(z)

\sigma(z):=\rho(z)-\rho(\alpha(z)).

It is obvious by construction that
\sigma(a)=0
if and only if

,
so that
A
is a level set with respect to
\sigma
. We will show

\sigma
to be a new

-rank function,
which can then be rescaled to an

-grading. We will need to check
that
\sigma
is well-defined, strictly-increasing, and surjective
onto
[\sigma(\hat{0}),\sigma(\hat{1})]
over any maximal chain.

First, in order for
\sigma
to be well-defined, we need to check
that the good chain is maximal.

Lemma 4.4
.

The good chain through
z
is a maximal chain.

Proof.

It suffices to show that elements of the good chain achieve every

.

It follows from Lemma

that
\rho(\left\{z\wedge m_{\lambda}\,:\,\lambda\in R\right\})

is the continuous image of a connected set having upper bound

similarly for
\rho(\left\{z\vee m_{\lambda}\,:\,\lambda\in R\right\})
.
As these share the point

, the ranks of a good chain form
a connected subset of

\hat{0}=\hat{0}\wedge z
and
\hat{1}=\hat{1}\vee z
,

An entirely similar proof gives:

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\mathbf{c}
is a maximal chain
and

is on the given chief chain

of

the chain
\{m\wedge c:c\in\mathbf{c}\}\cup\{m\vee c:c\in\mathbf{c}\}

is a maximal chain.

Remark 4.6
.

By Corollary
3.3
, if
L
is
R
-graded for

a bounded subset of

, then we may relax the requirement
that

lie on the given chief chain to simple rank modularity.

We will mostly focus on elements
z
above the antichain cutset
A
;
elements below the antichain cutset may be dealt with in a completely
symmetrical manner.

We start with an easy observation:

Lemma 4.7
.

If
w<z
are above or on the antichain

\alpha(w)=m_{\lambda}\wedge w
and

\alpha(w)=\alpha(z)

Proof.

If
\lambda\leq\kappa
, then
m_{\lambda}\wedge w\leq m_{\kappa}\wedge z
.
Since both lie on the antichain

See the illustration in Figure
4.1
. We can now
obtain the increasing property:

Lemma 4.8
.

The map
\sigma
is strictly increasing.

Proof.

We need to show that if
w<z
, then
\sigma(w)<\sigma(z)
.

We first check the case where

are both above or on

.
We write
\alpha(w)=m_{\lambda}\wedge w

\alpha(z)=m_{\kappa}\wedge z

If
\alpha(w)=\alpha(z)

that
\lambda>\kappa

\displaystyle\sigma(z)-\sigma(w)

\displaystyle=\left(\rho(z)-\rho(w)\right)-\left(\rho(m_{\kappa}\wedge z)-\rho(m_{\lambda}\wedge w)\right)

\displaystyle>\left(\rho(z)-\rho(w)\right)-\left(\rho(m_{\kappa}\wedge z)-\rho(m_{\kappa}\wedge w)\right)

where the strict inequality is because

,
and the weak inequality is by Corollary

.

Finally, if both are below or on

w

Lemma 4.9
.

If
\mathbf{c}
is a maximal chain,

\hat{\alpha}=\rho\circ\alpha\circ\rho|_{\mathbf{c}}^{-1}

is a continuous map
R\to\mathbb{R}
.

That is, the rank of

varies continuously over the chain

\mathbf{c}

Proof.

We prove that
\hat{\alpha}
is continuous from the right at a point

is entirely similar, and points below or on the

By Lemma
4.2
and the fact that the continuous

m_{\lambda}\geq\alpha(w)
.
If
m_{\lambda}=\alpha(w)
, then
m_{\lambda}=\alpha(z)
also for

, and a constant function is right-continuous.

Thus, we may assume that
m_{\lambda}>\alpha(w)

4.2

and

and minimality of
\lambda

that is above the antichain cutset, below

,

By Lemma

Corollary 4.10

For any maximal chain
\mathbf{c}

holds that

We have that
\sigma\circ\rho|_{\mathbf{c}}^{-1}=\mathrm{id}_{R}-\hat{\alpha}

Example 4.11
