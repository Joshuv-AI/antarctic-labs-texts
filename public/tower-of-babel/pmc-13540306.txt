Systemic Bevacizumab for Severe Bleeding From Acquired Gastrointestinal Vascular Malformations
Authors: Nardeen E Ayad, Jacob R Anderson, Bimalangshu Dey, Rebecca Karp Leaf, Andrew B Song, Hanny Al‐Samkari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcquired gastrointestinal vascular malformations not due to congenital disorders such as hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia are a common cause of chronic and acute hemorrhage, particularly in older adults. These lesions cause severe anemia, dependence on intravenous iron and/or red‐cell transfusion, and recurring hospitalizations. Hemostatic procedures are temporizing and no standard treatment, including the somatostatin analogs, addresses the underlying angiogenic dysregulation driving vascular malformation formation and recurrence. Therefore, bevacizumab, an anti‐VEGF‐A monoclonal antibody, is a promising targeted therapeutic. In this observational cohort study, we analyzed 32 patients (median age 75 years, 44% female) with acquired gastrointestinal vascular malformations due to idiopathic angiodysplasia, chronic liver disease, or deficiencies of von Willebrand factor who were treated on a predefined institutional bevacizumab pathway. The median Hematologic Support Score (a composite endpoint integrating red‐cell units transfused and elemental iron infused) improved from 15.04 (95% CI, 11.16–21.80) red‐cell unit equivalents during 6 months pretreatment to 4.08 (4.00–8.00) during Months 1–6 of bevacizumab treatment (p < 0.001) and further to 0.00 (0.00–5.40) during months 7–12 (p < 0.001). Units of red cells transfused and quantity of elemental iron infused were analyzed independently; both significantly improved after bevacizumab initiation. Annualized hospitalization/ED visit rate improved from 3 (year before bevacizumab) to 1 (year after bevacizumab) (p = 0.01), and median hemoglobin improved by 3.1 g/dL after bevacizumab (p < 0.001). Proteinuria and hypertension occurred in 8 (25%) and 5 (16%) patients, respectively, leading to bevacizumab discontinuation in 3 (9%). In conclusion, bevacizumab is a novel, targeted therapeutic approach for acquired gastrointestinal vascular malformations that may be safe and effective.Keywords: acquired vascular malformation, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, anemia, angiodysplasia, angiogenesis, bevacizumab, gastrointestinal bleeding, iron deficiency, liver disease, von Willebrand disease

Introduction

Acquired gastrointestinal (GI) vascular malformations, a type of vascular anomaly, are abnormal, often degenerative, dilated, tortuous, overly permeable and/or thin‐walled vascular lesions occurring in the mucosa and submucosa of the GI tract [1]. These include small‐vessel lesions (angiodysplasia and angioectasia) and large‐vessel lesions (Dieulafoy lesion and gastric antral vascular ectasia). Acquired vascular malformations are a common cause of chronic occult or overt GI bleeding, potentially causing severe iron deficiency anemia and dependence on intravenous iron and/or red‐cell transfusion [2]. These lesions most commonly occur in older adults and may be idiopathic (as in idiopathic angiodysplasia) or secondary to chronic liver disease or deficiencies of von Willebrand factor (VWF) [3, 4]. These lesions may be numerous, occur out of reach of typical endoscopy, and/or recur after procedural intervention due to ongoing uncorrected angiogenic dysregulation [4, 5, 6, 7]. The GI hemorrhage provoked by these lesions often causes emergency visits or hospitalizations for severe anemia with temporizing procedural intervention and discharge, then subsequent interval readmission for the same [8, 9]. This vicious cycle, the lack of a longer‐term solution or pathophysiology‐targeting therapy and the ensuing medical and psychological morbidity can be profound, particularly in heavily comorbid elderly patients.

Few medical therapeutic options exist for these patients. Somatostatin analogs (which slow bleeding by reducing splanchnic blood flow to lesions) and antifibrinolytic agents (which improve clot integrity) are often used as initial medical therapies. While they may be effective [10], this effectiveness can be limited and does not address the underlying angiogenic overactivity frequently driving vascular lesion formation, recurrence, and spread [4, 5, 6, 7]. Therefore, systemic antiangiogenic therapy is a rational approach to target the underlying molecular drivers of acquired vascular malformations. This approach, particularly with systemic bevacizumab, has been successful in the management of GI bleeding from different vascular malformations, specifically telangiectasias and arteriovenous malformations in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. However, published data on bevacizumab for acquired GI vascular malformations are limited to case reports and small case series with short treatment duration and minimal follow‐up [17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

In recognition of the potential effectiveness of systemic antiangiogenic therapy in these patients and to address a major unmet need, we developed an institutional acquired GI vascular malformation off‐label bevacizumab treatment pathway within the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) HHT Center of Excellence for adults with severe vascular GI bleeding resulting in hematologic support dependence who previously failed standard therapies. We present here an observational cohort study describing the long‐term safety and effectiveness outcomes of this pathway.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Mass General Brigham. All adults with acquired GI vascular malformations treated on our institutional bevacizumab treatment pathway (Figure 1) were identified and included, with data for analysis collected retrospectively. Bevacizumab was dosed intravenously every 2 weeks for four induction infusions; those demonstrating bleeding improvement with induction were then treated with indefinite maintenance infusions given every 4 weeks. Initial bevacizumab doses were 5 mg/kg (most patients) or 2.5 mg/kg (age ≥ 80 years or moderate‐to‐severe bevacizumab‐relevant baseline comorbidities, such as hypertension or proteinuria) (Figure 1).

The primary endpoint was the change in the 6‐month Hematologic Support Score before and after bevacizumab initiation. The Hematologic Support Score [22] is a composite endpoint recommended for [23, 24] and previously utilized in [25] studies of chronic bleeding interventions (Figure 2). The Hematologic Support Score integrates intravenous iron and red‐cell transfusion requirements over a given time period, reporting out red‐cell unit equivalents [Hematologic Support Score = red‐cell units transfused + (milligrams of elemental iron infused/250)].

Hematologic Support Score, a composite hematologic measurement. Composite hematologic measurements are the preferred method for assessing changes in blood loss over time in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) and other chronic bleeding disorders, such as chronic gastrointestinal bleeding from vascular malformations [22, 23]. The Hematologic Support Score utilizes established principles of biological chemistry and transfusion medicine [26, 27], along with simple arithmetic, to combine milligrams of elemental iron infused and units of red cells transfused over a specified time interval into a single composite number, red‐cell unit equivalents. It is assessed over a given time interval, such as a 3‐month or 6‐month period, before and after administration of a therapy, which are compared to assess the efficacy of the therapy at reducing bleeding. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Secondary endpoints included Objective Response [defined as achieving both Hematologic Response [23] (≥ 30% reduction in Hematologic Support Score) plus Hemoglobin Response [23] (≥ 1 g/dL hemoglobin increase)], healthcare utilization [hospital admissions and emergency department (ED) visits due to GI vascular bleeding, which generally included procedural intervention], hemoglobin, red‐cell units transfused and elemental iron infused reported separately, and safety. Treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were defined according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v.5.0 (National Cancer Institute).

All effectiveness endpoints were evaluated over the first 12 months of treatment; safety was assessed over the entire treatment duration. Given that the earliest effectiveness measurement (hemoglobin) occurred at Month 3 of treatment, to be eligible for effectiveness evaluation, patients had to complete bevacizumab induction or at least 3 months of treatment; those not doing so for any reason were automatically considered early treatment failures (and defined as not achieving Objective Response). Hematologic Support Score, healthcare utilization, red‐cell units transfused, elemental iron infused, and average hemoglobin measurements were evaluated statistically using Wilcoxon signed rank tests. Serial hemoglobin measurements were evaluated statistically using a mixed model for repeated measures. p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction to maintain a familywise error rate of α = 0.05 within each dataset. Baseline hemoglobin was calculated as a 6‐month average of measured pretreatment hemoglobin values.

Results

Thirty‐two adults with acquired vascular malformations causing severe GI bleeding and hematologic support dependence [23] were initiated on systemic bevacizumab between January 1, 2020 and October 31, 2025. All 32 were included in safety analysis (32.6 patient‐years of bevacizumab treatment), and the 26 who completed bevacizumab induction or at least 3 months of treatment were included in effectiveness analysis [mean (range) treatment duration 1.2 (0.33–5.4) years]; patient disposition (including reasons for discontinuation) is illustrated in Figure 3. Median (range) age at bevacizumab initiation was 75 (40–90) years, and 44% were female. Effectiveness Analysis

Primary Endpoint (Hematologic Support Score)

The median 6‐month Hematologic Support Score significantly improved over the course of treatment, going from 15.04 (95% CI, 11.16–21.80) red‐cell unit equivalents during the 6 months pretreatment to 4.08 (95% CI, 4.00–8.00) red‐cell unit equivalents during Months 1–6 of treatment (p < 0.001) and to 0.00 (95% CI, 0.00–5.40) red‐cell unit equivalents during months 7–12 of treatment (p = 0.001) (Figure 4A).

Objective Response

Of 32 patients, 18 patients (56%) achieved Objective Response overall. Of patients completing induction or greater than 3 months of therapy, 18 of 26 (69%) achieved Objective Response.

Healthcare Utilization

The annualized rate of hospitalizations or ED visits from GI vascular bleeding significantly improved over the course of treatment, going from 3.00 (95% CI, 1.00–5.00) encounters during the year before bevacizumab initiation to 1.00 (95% CI, 0.00–1.18) encounters during the year following bevacizumab (p = 0.01) (Figure 4B).

Red‐Cell Transfusions

The median units of red cells transfused significantly improved over the course of treatment, going from 6 (95% CI, 2–13) units of packed red cells during the 6 months pretreatment to 0 (95% CI, 0–1) units during Months 1–6 of treatment (p < 0.001) and to 0 (95% CI, 0–0) units during Months 7–12 of treatment (p = 0.003) (Figure 4C).

Elemental Iron Infused

The median quantity of elemental iron infused significantly improved over the course of treatment, going from 1700 (95% CI, 1320–2020) milligrams during the 6 months pretreatment to 1020 (95% CI, 1000–1530) milligrams during Months 1–6 of treatment (p = 0.31) and to 0 (95% CI, 0–510) milligrams during Months 7–12 of treatment (p = 0.006) (Figure 4D).

Hemoglobin

The median hemoglobin significantly improved over the course of treatment, going from 8.4 (95% CI, 7.8–9.3) g/dL at baseline to 11.4 (95% CI, 9.5–13.0) g/dL at Month 3, 12.5 (95% CI, 9.7–14.3) g/dL at Month 6, 10.9 (95% CI, 9.6–13.2) g/dL at Month 9, and 10.9 (95% CI, 8.9–13.6) g/dL at Month 12 of bevacizumab treatment (p < 0.001) and representing a 2.5–4.1 g/dL improvement over baseline at each time point (Figure 4E). Median individual patient average hemoglobin during Months 1–12 of bevacizumab treatment was 11.5 (95% CI, 9.6–13.0) g/dL, significantly higher than baseline (p < 0.001).

Safety Analysis

Safety outcomes are detailed in Table 2. The most common TEAEs were proteinuria and hypertension, occurring in 8 (25%) and 5 (16%) patients, respectively; all cases of proteinuria and hypertension were Grade 1 or 2 except 2 cases of Grade 3 for each. No venous thromboembolic events occurred; two patients (6%) had Grade 3 cardioembolic ischemic strokes attributed to atrial fibrillation and adjudicated as unlikely related to bevacizumab (Table 2). Discontinuation for TEAEs occurred due to Grade 3 proteinuria in 2 patients (6%) and Grade 3 hypertension in 1 patient (3%).

Discussion

In this study, we observed substantial effectiveness of systemic bevacizumab in treating severely bleeding, non‐HHT, hematologic support‐dependent patients with acquired GI vascular malformations of diverse etiologies, with over half of patients objectively responding. Most responding patients achieved complete liberation from red‐cell transfusion and significantly improved intravenous iron requirements concurrently with normalization or near‐normalization of hemoglobin and significant reduction in annualized rate of bleeding‐induced hospitalizations and ED visits. Despite most patients being late elderly (age ≥ 75 years, with only one patient < 60 years) and with substantial bleeding and antiangiogenic therapy‐relevant comorbidities, discontinuation for severe TEAEs occurred in < 10% of patients. The incidence of proteinuria and hypertension with use of bevacizumab in this study was not excessive but also not trivial and emphasizes the need to monitor closely for these toxicities when using this agent to treat vascular bleeding. It is of note that these toxicities were readily reversed with discontinuation of bevacizumab.

While the standard of care for management of GI vascular malformations in HHT has evolved in recent years to minimize recurrent procedural intervention and shift to disease‐modifying antiangiogenic therapies [12, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32], the current standard of care for the management of acquired, non‐HHT vascular malformations remains supportive. Initial management is endoscopic and procedural, utilizing techniques such as argon plasma coagulation to locally ablate bleeding vascular lesions. This approach is anatomically limited by challenges in addressing lesions deep in the small intestine and the propensity of lesions to recur given that the underlying angiogenic dysregulation is not corrected by local ablation. Supportive medical treatments such as octreotide, which reduce splanchnic blood flow to lesions and therefore may reduce overall bleeding volume, have demonstrated efficacy in managing acquired vascular malformations. The prospective, randomized OCEAN study describes improvement in transfusion requirements in patients receiving octreotide, although to a lesser degree than patients receiving bevacizumab in the present study. This suggests potential for improved hemostasis through more deliberate targeting of angiogenesis, but of course, these studies had important differences in population and design, and a prospective study comparing the two agents would be necessary to compare efficacy. Finally, randomized trial data have been published for the use of short‐course thalidomide in this setting that must be acknowledged [33]; however, given that indefinite treatment is required to prevent recurrence of bleeding and the serious concerns with emergence of neuropathy with long‐term thalidomide (i.e., not a concern with bevacizumab) as well as other well‐described thalidomide toxicities, we disfavor the use of long‐term thalidomide in the management of vascular bleeding at our HHT Center of Excellence for patients with or without HHT. Pomalidomide is preferred when an immunomodulatory imide drug is being considered given its superior short‐ and long‐term safety profiles [25, 29, 34], but there are no significant data we are aware of for pomalidomide in non‐HHT acquired GI vascular malformations, and the efficacy of pomalidomide for GI bleeding in HHT appears inferior to that of bevacizumab in these patients.

A prospective trial of systemic bevacizumab in patients with acquired vascular malformations is the logical next step. However, it is of note that the real‐world population of this study (rather than a highly selected clinical trial population) improves generalizability of these data. The evidence of clinical benefit in the present study is strong, supporting access to off‐label bevacizumab for the treatment of severe bleeding from acquired, non‐HHT GI vascular malformations.

Cost‐effectiveness is an additional consideration in the management of these patients. While no cost‐effectiveness analysis has been done for bevacizumab in acquired GI vascular malformations, a rigorous cost‐effectiveness analysis has been done evaluating bevacizumab use in HHT versus standard of care (hematologic support and healthcare utilization) and found that bevacizumab was not only cost‐effective but rather uniquely cost‐saving, irrespective of the scenario analyzed [16]. Given that the clinical course, including hematologic support and high burden of healthcare utilization [35], is similar for patients with severe HHT‐associated vascular bleeding and severe acquired GI vascular malformation bleeding, this analysis may be informative in considerations of these patients as well.

This study has several limitations. Although patients were treated on a predefined institutional pathway outlining management and monitoring, they were not enrolled in a formal prospective study protocol, limiting adverse event reporting and necessitating retrospective data collection. While major improvements coinciding with bevacizumab initiation in all hematologic parameters and healthcare utilization were observed in many patients without any clear alternative explanation, the lack of randomization introduces potential for confounding factors that could have resulted in clinical improvements independent of bevacizumab. The patients who discontinued bevacizumab early due to lack of effectiveness or significant adverse events are reflected in the overall Objective Response rate of the study but could not be included in the granular hematologic endpoint analysis. Finally, while responders and nonresponders were observed in each of the three main subgroups of vascular malformation etiology (idiopathic, deficiency of VWF, and chronic liver disease), these subgroups were too small to allow for meaningful subgroup analyses.

In conclusion, antiangiogenic therapy with systemic bevacizumab to target the angiogenic dysregulation underlying many acquired GI vascular malformations is a novel therapeutic approach and option that may be safe and effective for a challenging clinical problem.
