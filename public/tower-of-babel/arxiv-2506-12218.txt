Abstract
Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) are central to science and engineering applications including causal inference, scheduling, and neural architecture search. In this work, we introduce the DAG Convolutional Network (DCN), a novel graph neural network (GNN) architecture designed specifically for convolutional learning from signals supported on DAGs. The DCN leverages causal graph filters to learn nodal representations that account for the partial ordering inherent to DAGs, a strong inductive bias not present in conventional GNNs. Unlike prior art in machine learning over DAGs, DCN builds on formal convolutional operations that admit spectral-domain representations. We further propose the Parallel DCN (PDCN), a model that feeds input DAG signals to a parallel bank of causal graph-shift operators and processes these DAG-aware features using a shared multilayer perceptron. This way, PDCN decouples model complexity from graph size while maintaining satisfactory predictive performance. The architectures’ permutation equivariance and expressive power properties are also established. Comprehensive numerical tests across several tasks, datasets, and experimental conditions demonstrate that (P)DCN compares favorably with state-of-the-art baselines in terms of accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency. These results position (P)DCN as a viable framework for deep learning from DAG-structured data that is designed from first (graph) signal processing principles.



I
Introduction

The increasing complexity of data and tasks calls for statistical learning from signals on non-Euclidean domains
[
2
]
. Graph Signal Processing (GSP) represents said irregular domains using graphs and leverages their topology as a prior to effectively process relational data
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. GSP advances have facilitated the principled designs of Graph Neural Network (GNN) architectures and contributed to our understanding of their stability properties, with impact to a host of network-centric machine learning (ML) applications
[
2
,
6
,
7
]
. GNNs learn graph-aware nodal representations through local message passing operations that implement graph convolutions
[
8
,
9
,
6
]
, attention mechanisms
[
10
]
, or autoencoder
[
11
,
12
]
, just to name a few notable instances from an ever-growing list.

Directed graph models of, e.g., flows, citations, precedence, or, cause-effect relationships are ubiquitous. Incorporating directionality into GNNs introduces a strong inductive bias to boost information distillation for scalable learning in this context. However, this comes with well-documented challenges
[
13
]
, which are accentuated in the case of
directed acyclic graphs
(DAGs). For example, DAGs have nilpotent adjacency matrices, which lead to a collapsed spectrum and prevent the direct use of spectral-based tools
[
14
]
. Despite these issues, DAGs are widely adopted in causal inference
[
15
]
, learning with Bayesian networks and structural causal models
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
, code parsing
[
20
]
, or performance prediction for architectural optimization of deep neural networks
[
21
]
.

In this work, we introduce a novel
convolutional
GNN specifically designed to learn from signals supported on DAGs; see Section
II-B
for a formal problem statement. Since DAGs introduce a partial order over the nodes, a central question is how to incorporate this strong inductive bias into the architecture in a principled fashion. Our answer hinges on (and with new results in Section
III-B
we contribute to) a recent framework that extends GSP techniques to DAGs and partially ordered sets (posets)
[
22
,
14
]
. This way, we arrive at a new DAG Convolutional Network (DCN) in Section
IV
. The fundamental design principles adopted (Section
II
) can be traced to linear structural equation models (SEMs), which makes DCN well suited for capturing causal dependencies among variables (provided that the DAG model at hand admits such causal interpretations
[
15
]
). In addition, the architecture supports a spectral interpretation and is permutation equivariant. Unlike related GNNs for DAGs
[
23
,
24
]
, DCN’s convolutional layers involve mainly sparse matrix multiplications, resulting in manageable computational complexity.

In Section
V
we also introduce a Parallel DCN (PDCN) architecture to address potential scalability issues, transitioning from the deep, recursive structure of DCN to a parallel (wide) implementation. Shifted input signals over the DAG are passed through a shared-parameter multilayer perceptron (MLP), and their outputs are summed to form rich nodal representations. We show that the number of learnable parameters in the PDCN model is independent of the graph size, unlike a DCN deployed with the most general class of causal graph filters. We argue that PDCN is permutation equivariant and expressive enough to distinguish between pairs of non-isomorphic DAGs (within the scope of the Weisfeiler-Lehman test
[
25
]
).

Related work and contributions in context.
GNN-based ML over DAGs has been gaining traction recently. A variational autoencoder (VAE) framework called D-VAE was proposed in
[
23
]
, to embed DAGs into a continuous low-dimensional latent space. The DAGNN model in
[
24
]
introduces a sequential aggregation scheme, where each node collects information from all its predecessors through an attention mechanism, and then updates its representation using a gated recurrent unit (GRU) to exploit the DAG-induced partial order. However, both D-VAE and DAGNN involve sequential operations that lead to high computational cost, making them less suitable for large-scale graphs. Instead, DCN efficiently captures long-range ancestor dependencies via the notion of weighted transitive closure inherent to causal graph filters
[
14
]
. Moreover, DAGNN combines the forward and reverse directions of the DAG in a way that is not straightforward to interpret. More recently,
[
26
]
extended transformer models to DAGs by encoding node ordering through a reachability-based attention mechanism. Different from
[
23
,
24
,
26
]
, the proposed (P)DCN architectures build on formal convolutional operators for signals on DAGs. Our experiments in Section
VI
provide evidence in support of this design choice, demonstrating gains in utility for different ML tasks, as well as notable computational savings. Conclusions and a discussion of limitations and future work are given in Section
VII
.

In summary, this work makes the following significant technical contributions to DAG representation learning:

•

We develop DCN, a novel architecture that brings to bear causal graph filters for efficient convolutional processing of DAG-structured signals. PDCN trades off depth for width, offering a scalable variant whose parameter count remains constant regardless of the number of DAG nodes.

•

We examine the representation capabilities of the (P)DCN models, establishing their permutation equivariance and compliance with the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) test. These properties follow from some new results we derive for the causal graph-shift operators and filters pioneered in
[
14
]
.

•

We assess the performance of the proposed DCN framework on both synthetic and real-world datasets, and show that it compares favorably to state-of-the-art baselines across various tasks. In controlled synthetic experiments, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the model under different noise regimes, number of observations, and graph topologies. We also showcase the practical value of DCN in signal interpolation tasks arising with genomic and hydrological data. We share the code to reproduce all of the results reported in Section
VI
.

Relative to the conference precursor
[
1
]
, here we consider convolutional learning on DAGs through a unified presentation along with full technical details (including extended discussions, examples, and unpublished theoretical results with their proofs), supported by comprehensive numerical experiments. Noteworthy novel pieces in this full-blown journal paper include: (i) the PDCN architecture; (ii) new properties of causal graph-shift operators and filters which are then used to (iii) establish permutation equivariance of (P)DCN as well as examine their expressive power; and (iv) a comprehensive and reproducible performance evaluation protocol. The latter offers new comparisons with DAGNN and D-VAE as well as real-data applications that include gene expression level imputation and hydrological data forecasting.

II
Preliminaries and Problem Statement

We start by reviewing the necessary graph-theoretic preliminaries to formally state the problem of learning from DAG signals. We then introduce convolutional GNNs and argue why they are unsuitable as models for the problem dealt with here, which calls for new architectures that are tailored to DAGs.

II-A

Graph-Theoretic Preliminaries: DAGs and Signals

Let
{\mathcal{D}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
be a DAG, where
{\mathcal{V}}
is the set of
N
nodes and
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
represents the set of directed edges; see Fig.
1
(left).
An edge
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
indicates a connection from node
j
to node
i
.
Since
{\mathcal{D}}
contains no cycles, it admits a topological ordering of the nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}
, such that node
j
precedes node
i
whenever
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
.
Thus, every DAG defines a unique partial order over the set
{\mathcal{V}}
, where
j<i
if node
j
is a
predecessor
of
i
, meaning there exists a directed path from
j
to
i

[
22
]
.
In a poset, not all elements are necessarily comparable.
Specifically, for any pair
i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}
such that there is no path from
i
to
j
or from
j
to
i
, we have
i\not\leq j
and
j\not\leq i
. Going back to Fig. 1 (left), e.g., we have
1<7
while nodes
3
and
4
cannot be ordered.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
denote the (possibly weighted) adjacency matrix of
{\mathcal{D}}
, where
A_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if
(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}
.
Given the partial order over
{\mathcal{V}}
, we can rearrange the entries of
{\mathbf{A}}
to obtain a strictly lower-triangular matrix; see Fig
1
(right).
The diagonal entries are zero, reflecting the fact that DAGs do not contain self-loops.
Beyond the graph structure itself, we also consider signals defined over the node set
{\mathcal{V}}
.
A graph signal is a function
x:{\mathcal{V}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, mapping each node to a real value.
This function can be represented as a vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, where
x_{i}
denotes the signal value at node
i
. For instance, the expression level measured for a gene that is part of gene-regularity network, or, the concentration of a dissolved substance recorded by one of multiple hydrological monitoring stations along a river; see also the numerical experiments in Section
VI
.
A signal processing framework for graph signals supported on DAGs, referred to as DAG signals, was proposed in
[
14
]
; see also Section
III
.

II-B

Problem Statement

This study focuses on learning from signals defined on a DAG
\mathcal{D}
. The task involves a training dataset
{\mathcal{T}}=\{({\mathbf{X}}_{m},{\mathbf{y}}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
, comprising
M
i.i.d. pairs of input-output observations derived from a network process on
\mathcal{D}
. Each multi-feature input graph signal is represented as
\mathbf{X}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times F}
, where
F
is the number of input features per node. The outputs
{\mathbf{y}}_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
are single-feature graph signals for simplicity. Extensions to multi-feature output signals or tasks involving graph-level labels are straightforward.

The objective is to learn a mapping between
{\mathbf{X}}_{m}
and
{\mathbf{y}}_{m}
using a nonlinear parametric function
f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\cdot;{\mathcal{D}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times F}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Notice how the learnable function is by design chosen to be dependent on the DAG. This way we impose a prior on signal structure to fruitfully exploit the relational inductive biases in the data. The parameters
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
of this function are optimized by minimizing the empirical risk

\min_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{y}}_{m},f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}}_{m};{\mathcal{D}})),

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes a loss function. For regression tasks, this could be the mean squared error (MSE), while for classification, the cross-entropy loss is commonly used.

II-C

Going Beyond Graph Neural Networks

When it comes to designing the parameteric model
f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\cdot;{\mathcal{D}})
, one might be initially tempted to adopt a convolutional GNN off-the-shelf. These neural networks employ learnable convolutional graph filters
[
27
]
, where the output of each layer
\ell=1,\ldots,L
, is recursively given by
[
8
,
6
]
:

{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\left(\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{r}{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell-1)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{r}^{(\ell)}\right).

(2)

In this architecture,
{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell)}
denotes the nodal features at layer
\ell
, while
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{r}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{F_{i}^{(\ell)}\times F_{o}^{(\ell)}}
contains the learnable filterbank parameters.
F_{i}^{(\ell)}
and
F_{o}^{(\ell)}
stand for the number of input and output features. In the initial layer,
{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}:={\mathbf{X}}
is assigned the input graph signals. The powers of the adjacency matrix,
{\mathbf{A}}^{r}
, determine the radius of the neighborhood from which information is aggregated. This structure enables multi-hop dependencies to be captured by the model. To encode additional graph properties, the adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
can be substituted with a more general graph-shift operator (GSO)
[
6
,
28
]
, which encodes structural relationships in the graph. Nonlinear transformations are introduced using pointwise activation functions, commonly ReLU, defined as
\sigma(x)=\max(0,x)
. This filterbank convolutional GNN (FB-GCNN) has proven effective for scalable learning from network data
[
8
,
6
]
.

However, this design is unsatisfactory for the learning problem at hand due to a couple of key reasons. First, the aforementioned graph filters are not convolutional operators for signals on DAGs. They do not satisfy the convolution theorem because the usual GSP notion of Fourier analysis based on GSO eigenvectors is rendered vacuous. Indeed, the strictly lower triangular adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
of a DAG has all eigenvalues equal to zero and, hence, lacks an eigenbasis. While it is operationally feasible to run the GNN model in (
2
) on a DAG, this collapsing spectrum makes it impossible to have a graph spectral-domain interpretation. Second, DAGs introduce a unique partial order over the set
{\mathcal{V}}
. It is thus prudent to encode this strong inductive bias in the convolutional operation used to learn nodal representations. To address these limitations that motivate our work, we build on the DAG signal processing framework proposed in
[
14
]
, which is outlined next.

III
DAG Signal Processing

Here we review convolutions for signals on DAGs
[
14
]
, which are fundamental building blocks in our new architectures. We also establish some new properties of DAG convolutional filters that we will use for subsequent analyses.

III-A

Graph-Shift Operators and Convolution for DAGs

Consider a signal
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
defined in the partially-ordered nodes of a DAG
{\mathcal{D}}
. The seminal work in
[
14
]
advocates modeling
x_{i}
as linearly aggregated (possibly causal) effects
\{x_{j}:(i,j)\in{\mathcal{E}}\}
from parent nodes, and hence all of node
i
’s predecessors
k<i
by transitivity. Let
{\mathbf{c}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
denote the vector of exogenous nodal contributions, where input
c_{i}
is associated with node
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
following our graph signal terminology. Accordingly, the relationship between the signal
{\mathbf{x}}
and the contributions
{\mathbf{c}}
is given by

x_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i}W_{ij}c_{j},\quad i\in{\mathcal{V}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{c},

(3)

where
{\mathbf{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
is the so-termed
weighted transitive closure
matrix of the DAG. An entry
W_{ij}\neq 0
indicates that there exists a directed path from node
j
to node
i
. These are also the edges in the reachability graph
\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}
associated with
{\mathcal{D}}
; see also Fig.
2
for the reachability graph of the DAG in Fig.
1
. It follows that matrix
{\mathbf{W}}
is lower-triangular, and (by design) its diagonal entries are
W_{ii}:=1
to account for the reflexivity property of posets. In terms of the signal model (
3
), this means that
c_{i}
contributes to
x_{i}
. Still, the most important implication of
\textrm{diag({$\mathbf{W}$})}=\mathbf{1}
is that
{\mathbf{W}}
is invertible, and its columns will play the role of a Fourier basis as discussed later.

We still have not addressed how to compute the non-diagonal entries
W_{ij}
from those in the adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
. There are multiple ways to do so depending on the meaning of the edge weights in
{\mathcal{D}}
; see
[
14
, Section III-B]
for a detailed treatment and algorithm building on
[
29
]
. Here, we adopt

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}+{\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{A}}^{2}+\ldots+{\mathbf{A}}^{N-1}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1},

(4)

which implies a linear SEM for
{\mathbf{x}}
, when
{\mathbf{c}}
are exogenous noises
[
15
, Ch. 6.2]
. Indeed, using (
3
) and (
4
) yields

{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{c}}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}{\mathbf{c}}={\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{c}}.

For each node
k\in{\mathcal{V}}
, a causal GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
can be defined as
[
14
]

[{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{x}}]_{i}=\sum_{j\leq i\text{ and }j\leq k}W_{ij}c_{j}.

(5)

This operation essentially filters the signal at node
i
by aggregating contributions from nodes that are
common
predecessors of
i
and
k
[cf. (
3
)]. In matrix form, (
5
) can be expressed as

{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{c}}={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}{\mathbf{x}},

(6)

where
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
is a diagonal matrix such that
[{\mathbf{D}}_{k}]_{ii}=1
if
i\leq k
and
[{\mathbf{D}}_{k}]_{ii}=0
otherwise. As argued earlier,
{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}
is guaranteed to exist and can be efficiently computed using the weighted Möbius inversion
[
30
]
. Inspecting the left- and right-hand side of (
6
), it follows that the columns of
{\mathbf{W}}
are the eigenvectors of all GSOs
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
, and the binary diagonal entries of
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
the respective eigenvalues. Consequently,
{\mathbf{W}}
provides a Fourier basis for DAG signals, while
{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}
represents the corresponding Fourier transform. From (
3
), the contributions
{\mathbf{c}}
can be interpreted as the Fourier coefficients of
{\mathbf{x}}
.

In GSP, shift-invariant graph filters are polynomials of a single GSO
[
27
]
. However, for DAGs there is one causal GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
for every
k\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and each
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
is idempotent. Accordingly, the most general form of a linear shift-invariant convolutional filter for DAGs (henceforth called causal graph filters) was introduced in
[
14
]
as

{\mathbf{H}}=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\theta_{k}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}={\mathbf{W}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\theta_{k}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{W}}^{-1},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
are the filter coefficients. For an input signal
{\mathbf{x}}
, we say
{\mathbf{H}}
implements the
convolution operation
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{h}}*_{{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}
. From (
7
) frequency response of the causal graph filter is given by the diagonal of
\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\theta_{k}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
, thus implementing a pointwise scaling operation on the frequency coefficients
{\mathbf{c}}
of the input signal
{\mathbf{x}}
.

This causal graph filter definition forms the foundation of the DCN architecture we introduce in Section
IV
. But before moving on, next we provide further insights and establish new properties of causal GSOs and filters that will inform subsequent analyses of the proposed architectures.

III-B

Properties of Causal GSOs and Graph Filters

Let us begin with the illustrative example in Fig.
2
to develop intuition about how the matrices
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
diffuse information across
{\mathcal{D}}
. Recall the toy DAG in Fig.
1
. Its reachability DAG
\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}
is shown in Fig.
2
, which corresponds to the support of the non-diagonal entries in
{\mathbf{W}}
. Indeed,
{\mathbf{W}}
also includes self-loops at every node. The other panels depict the connectivity of the directed graphs induced by the GSOs
{\mathbf{S}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{S}}_{4}
, and
{\mathbf{S}}_{5}
. These graphs are not DAGs, because the
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
have some non-zero diagonal elements corresponding to direct and indirect ancestors of
k
(including itself; these self-loops are not shown in Fig.
2
to avoid clutter).
For example, if we are interested in the value at node
v_{7}
after shifting a signal
{\mathbf{x}}
with respect to node
v_{4}
using the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{4}
, we obtain from Fig.
2
that

y_{7}=[{\mathbf{S}}_{4}]_{27}x_{2}+[{\mathbf{S}}_{4}]_{47}x_{4}+x_{7}.

(8)

A closer inspection of the connectivity in Fig.
2
suggests that using the causal GSO to aggregate information at a particular node (
v_{7}
in the previous example) only involves a subset of its ancestors and possibly itself. This intuition is formalized in the following result, which establishes a relationship between the supports of
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the weighted transitive closure
{\mathbf{W}}\!=\!({\mathbf{I}}\!-\!{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}
.
For every
k\in{\mathcal{V}}
, it holds that
\sup({\mathbf{S}}_{k})\subseteq\sup({\mathbf{W}})
.

Proof.

The off-diagonal support of
{\mathbf{W}}
corresponds to the edge set of the reachability DAG
\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}
, meaning
W_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if there exists a directed path from
j
to
i
in
{\mathcal{D}}
.
By definition, the causal GSOs are given by

{\mathbf{S}}_{k}={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{A}}).

(9)

Since
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
is a binary diagonal matrix, we consider the worst case where
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}={\mathbf{I}}
, yielding

[{\mathbf{S}}_{k}]_{ij}=\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}W_{iv}[{\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{A}}]_{vj}.

(10)

If
[{\mathbf{S}}_{k}]_{ij}\neq 0
, then there exists
v
for which both
W_{iv}\neq 0
and
[{\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{A}}]_{vj}\neq 0
.
This implies that there is a direct link from
j
to
v
and a directed path from
v
to
i
, meaning that there must also be a path from
j
to
i
.
Hence,
W_{ij}\neq 0
. Conversely, if
W_{ij}=0
, then no such
v
exists, ensuring that
[{\mathbf{S}}_{k}]_{ij}=0
.
∎

Our goal is to leverage
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
to aggregate information via convolutional operations on DAGs, as illustrated in the previous example.
With
{\mathcal{S}}\!=\!\{{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}
denotingthe set of causal GSOs, a natural question that arises is whether different DAGs can generate the same set
{\mathcal{S}}
.
The answer is negative, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

The set of causal GSOs
{\mathcal{S}}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}
derived from the weighted transitive closure
{\mathbf{W}}=({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}
are unique, i.e., distinct (non-isomorphic) DAGs yield different matrices
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
Proposition
2
ensures that the GSOs
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
define a unique aggregation mechanism within a DAG, which is crucial for analyzing the expressivity of the proposed architectures.
Additionally, a key property in learning from graph-based data is permutation equivariance, which ensures robustness to node relabeling.
Graph filters and GNNs possess this property
[
6
,
28
]
, and we now show that causal graph filters do as well; see Appendix
B
for a proof.

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
be the poset of nodes obtained by permuting with
\pi:{\mathcal{V}}\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
the DAG with node ordering
{\mathcal{V}}
. Let
{\mathbf{P}}
be the matrix representation of
\pi
. For any
q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
, the permuted GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}
is given by

{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top},

(11)

where
k=\pi^{-1}(q)
. Moreover, causal graph filters are permutation equivariant, meaning filter outputs given by
g({\mathbf{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathcal{S}})=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\theta_{k}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{x}}
satisfy

g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime})={\mathbf{P}}g({\mathbf{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathcal{S}}),

(12)

where
{\mathcal{S}}\!=\!\{{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}
is the set collecting all the GSOs,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\!=\!{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{x}}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\!=\!{\mathbf{P}}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}\!=\!\{{\mathbf{S}}^{\prime}_{q}\}_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}
.

IV
DAG Convolutional Network (DCN)

We now have all the ingredients needed to introduce a convolutional network for signals defined on DAGs, henceforth referred to as DCN.
Central to this end is the definition of causal graph filters in (
7
), which we use to implement layerwise convolutions and compose them with a pointwise nonlinearity to obtain a DAG perceptron.
The DCN is a deep learning architecture obtained by stacking multiple such DAG perceptrons. In its simplest form, the output of the layer
\ell=1,\ldots,L
is recursively given by

{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell-1)}\right),

(13)

where
\theta_{k}^{(\ell)}
denote the learnable filter coefficients, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is a nonlinear activation function, such as the
\mathrm{ReLU}
. The input
{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}={\mathbf{x}}
to the first layer is the graph signal
{\mathbf{x}}
we want to process using the DCN.
The output of the final layer
{\mathbf{x}}^{(L)}
is a graph signal collecting (here scalar, or single feature) nodal representations that can be used for different downstream tasks; see Section
VI
for several examples. Extensions to graph signals with multiple features are straightforward, and will be addressed later in this section.

The recursion (
13
) can be interpreted in two complementary ways.
From a spectral perspective, the operation
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{(\ell-1)}={\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{c}}^{(\ell-1)}
first selects the
causes
(i.e., the exogenous inputs in an SEM) of predecessors of node
k
.
These causes are then diffused across the reachabilty DAG
\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}
via the weighted transitive closure matrix
{\mathbf{W}}
, effectively infusing the inductive bias stemming from the partial ordering of vertices in the DAG.
Alternatively, this operation can be understood from the perspective of message-passing GNNs.
Specifically, for each node
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, every GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
aggregates features from common predecessors of nodes
i
and
k
, creating different messages incoming at
i
.
These messages are then combined in a nonlinear, weighted fashion to update the node representations
{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)}
at the output of layer
\ell
.

Although the single-filter DCN in (
13
) provides an intuitive foundation to understand the DCN model, it may lack sufficient expressiveness to learn complex DAG signal representations.
Moreover, since input signals often have multiple features (
F>1
), a more flexible architecture is required.
To address these shortcomings, we extend the single-filter formulation in (
13
) using a bank of learnable DAG filters, leading to [cf. (
2
)]

\mathbf{X}^{(\ell)}=\sigma\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\mathbf{X}^{(\ell-1)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{(\ell)}\right).

(14)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{F_{i}^{(\ell)}\times F_{o}^{(\ell)}}
contains the learnable filter coefficients, where
F_{i}^{(\ell)}
and
F_{o}^{(\ell)}
respectively denote the number of input and output features. Naturally,
F_{i}^{(\ell)}=F_{o}^{(\ell-1)}
. Fig.
3
a depicts this recursion for a DCN with
L
layers, where the input is
{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}={\mathbf{X}}
and the output is given by
\mathbf{X}^{(L)}=f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\mathbf{X};{\mathcal{S}})
, with
{\mathcal{S}}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}
being the set of causal GSOs and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
collects all the learnable parameters. The number of layers
L
and features
F_{i}^{(\ell)},F_{o}^{(\ell)}
are hyperparameters chosen before training. Given a training set
{\mathcal{T}}=\{({\mathbf{X}}_{m},{\mathbf{y}}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
, learning is accomplished by using mini-batch stochastic gradient descent to minimize the task-dependent empirical risk (
1
).

While the bank of learnable filters enhances the expressiveness of DCN, feature aggregation remains dictated by the causal GSOs, ensuring the model exploits the structural information of the DAG.
In fact, the attentive reader will have noticed our slight abuse of notation when using
f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\cdot;{\mathcal{S}})
instead of
f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(\cdot;{\mathcal{D}})
as introduced in Section
II-B
.
Formally these are not the same, since the causal shifts in
{\mathcal{S}}
are not uniquely defined given
{\mathcal{D}}
(recall there is a choice to be made regarding the weighted transitive closure).
In any case, what we want to convey is that the prior structural information in
{\mathcal{D}}
enters the DCN model via
{\mathcal{S}}
.
Next, we elaborate on the advantages and limitations of the DCN.

IV-A

Analysis and Discussion

The DCN convolutional layer definition in (
14
) resembles the GNN architectures designed for general (cyclic) graphs; see also the GNN recursion in (2).
However, relying on causal graph filters is essential to implement formal convolutions over DAGs, and this
innovation offers several advantages.
First, the spectrum of
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
is well defined. Endowing the DCN with a spectral representation could pave the way to studying properties such as stability, transferability, or denoising capability
[
6
,
31
]
.
Exploring these properties further presents a promising direction for future research beyond the scope of this paper.
In addition, the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
are the diagonal entries of the binary matrices
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
.
When combined with non-expansive activation functions such as the
\mathrm{ReLU}
, this ensures numerical stability even when multiple layers are stacked, avoiding issues that commonly arise in other GNN architectures.
In other words, causal GSOs are inherently well conditioned and there is no need for e.g., degree-based normalization of aggregation operators to keep the eigenvalue spread in check.

From a computational perspective, alternative architectures designed to learn from DAG-related data involve
aggregation
and
combination
operations that are computationally demanding
[
23
,
24
]
.
In contrast, in DCN the
aggregation
is determined by the causal GSOs via the products
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}^{(\ell-1)}
, and the matrices of learnable coefficients
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{(\ell)}
determine the weights of the
combination
step. Furthermore, recall that
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
is a binary diagonal matrix, so
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}={\mathbf{D}}_{k}^{2}
and it follows from (
6
) that each
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
will have all-zero columns. Fig.
2
shows that causal GSOs are typically quite sparse.
Hence, DCN layers only involve sparse matrix products, leading to an efficient computation. More formally, with
S=\max_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\|{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\|_{0}
and assuming a fixed number of features
F
for simplicity, the worst-case computational complexity of the DCN layer is
{\mathcal{O}}(NF(S+NF))
.
In contrast, related architectures such as D-VAE
[
23
]
or DAGNN
[
24
]
require more complex and computationally demanding operations, resulting in longer running times; see also the numerical tests in Section
VI
.

The labeling of the nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}
is in principle arbitrary.
Thus, to ensure model consistency, the output of a DCN should be robust to node relabelings.
Building on the permutation equivariance property of causal graph filters in Theorem
1
, the following corollary establishes that DCNs are permutation equivariant mappings as well.

Corollary 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
be the poset of nodes obtained by permuting with
\pi:{\mathcal{V}}\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
the DAG with node ordering
{\mathcal{V}}
. Let
{\mathbf{P}}
be the matrix representation of
\pi
.
The DCN output
f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}};{\mathcal{S}})
is permutation equivariant, i.e.,

f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\prime}}({\mathbf{X}}^{\prime};{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime})={\mathbf{P}}f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}};{\mathcal{S}}),

(15)

where
{\mathcal{S}}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}
is the set collecting all causal GSOs,
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{X}}
are the permuted inputs, and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}\}_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{q}\}_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}
are the permuted causal GSOs and learnable parameters, respectively.

Proof.

We prove the result for a DAG perceptron for simplicity, though the argument extends to multiple layers by induction.
Leveraging Theorem
1
, from (
14
) we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{\prime}}({\mathbf{X}}^{\prime};{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left({\mathbf{P}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}\right)={\mathbf{P}}f_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}};{\mathcal{S}}),

(16)

where, as in the proof of Theorem
1
,
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}
denotes the matrix of learnable coefficients associated with
k=\pi^{-1}(q)
. The last equality holds because
\sigma(\cdot)
is a pointwise nonlinearity.
∎

On the downside, the number of GSOs involved in the convolution, and consequently the number of learnable parameters, increases linearly with the size of the graph
N
.
This scaling poses challenges in terms of computational cost and memory requirements.
An engineering workaround to mitigate this issue is to approximate the convolution in (
14
) by restricting the summands therein to a subset of nodes
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}
with fixed cardinality, independent of
N
.
Admittedly, this reduction still leads to a convolutional filter but it may limit the expressiveness of the DCN.
However, this limitation can be alleviated by stacking multiple layers, as the cross-products of different
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{S}}_{\ell}
can give rise to a new GSO. Furthermore, when the number of selected GSOs satisfies
|{\mathcal{U}}|\ll N
, the computational complexity of the DCN reduces to
{\mathcal{O}}(F(S+NF))
. This is comparable to GNNs for general graphs with cycles. In this work, we observed that selecting the GSOs involved in the convolution via uniform sampling yields competitive performance; the numerical experiments in Section
VI
offer evidence supporting this claim. Nevertheless, developing a principled method to determine the subset of nodes in
{\mathcal{U}}
remains a promising direction for future research.
Finally, note that Corollary
1
assumes the use of all nodes in
{\mathcal{V}}
. However, the permutation equivariance of DCN using only GSOs related to a subset of nodes
{\mathcal{U}}
still holds, provided that the permutation
\pi
only acts on nodes within this set.

V
Parallel DCN

The DCN offers a principled framework for learning representations from data on DAGs. Because causal GSOs are idempotent operators
[
14
]
, the benefits of employing a deep architecture to gain expressive power are less clear, at least when using all of the GSOs. In such a case, and disregarding the (important) effect of interleaving pointwise nonlinearities, one can show that the composition of two causal graph filters can always be re-parameterized using a single causal graph filter. Building on this observation, we introduce Parallel DCN (PDCN), a variant of DCN that shifts from the sequential structure in (
14
) to a parallel (wide) implementation. We show that the number of PDCN parameters is independent of
N
.

The proposed PDCN architecture comprises
N
parallel branches, with shared input
{\mathbf{X}}
and respective outputs

{\bar{\mathbf{Z}}}_{k}=\mathrm{MLP}\left({\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}\right)=\sigma\left(\dots\sigma\left({\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}\right)\dots\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(L)}\right),

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(\ell)}
now denotes the learnable parameters of the
\ell
-th MLP layer. Notably, these weights are common to all branches. Inspired by
[
32
]
, the MLP in each branch
k\in{\mathcal{V}}
is fed the shifted signal
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}
. The PDCN output is computed by aggregating the output of every branch, namely

{\mathbf{Z}}=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\bar{\mathbf{Z}}}_{k}.

(18)

Similarly to the DCN layers in (
14
), PDCN also exploits the causal GSOs to aggregate information from the ancestors of each node in a principled way.
These DAG-aware features are then fed to an MLP, which learns how to combine them. The PDCN architecture is depicted in Fig.
3
b. Observe how, while DCN is a sequential architecture amenable to grow deeper, PDCN exhibits a parallel structure with tunable width (say by dropping a subset of causal GSOs indexed by
{\mathcal{V}}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}
). Another benefit of this alternative design is that PDCN is amenable to parallelization, leading to efficient inference.

V-A

Analysis and Discussion

If the primary concern is model expressivity, each PDCN branch could employ a different MLP with parameters
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{(\ell)}
,
k\in{\mathcal{V}}
. However, our design follows a weight-sharing strategy across all branches. Like a siamese network, all MLPs in PDCN share the same set of weights
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(\ell)}
, effectively decoupling the number of learnable parameters from the size of the graph. Assuming a fixed number of features
F
, this approximately adds to a total of
{\mathcal{O}}(F^{2}L)
learnable parameters.

Even with weight sharing, the computational complexity scales linearly with
N
. Following the same reasoning as in DCN, one can trade-off expressivity for complexity by using only a subset of the available GSOs, i.e., selecting
|{\mathcal{U}}|\leq N
branches. Moreover, in the specific case of PDCN, we can precompute the shifted signals
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}
before training, further alleviating the computational burden. Weight sharing also offers the added flexibility of training a PDCN model using only some of the branches, and then performing inference with other, more, or all GSOs enabled as desired.

Once more, performing the aggregation step using causal GSOs ensures that PDCN is permutation equivariant.

Corollary 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
be the poset of nodes obtained by permuting with
\pi:{\mathcal{V}}\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
the DAG with node ordering
{\mathcal{V}}
. Let
{\mathbf{P}}
be the matrix representation of
\pi
. The PDCN output
f^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}};{\mathcal{S}})
is permutation equivariant, i.e.,

f^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}}^{\prime};{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime})={\mathbf{P}}f^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}};{\mathcal{S}}),

(19)

where
{\mathcal{S}}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}
is the set collecting all causal GSOs,
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{X}}
are the permuted inputs, and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}\}_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}
are the permuted causal GSOs.

Proof.

From Theorem
1
we have
{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}
, for
q=\pi(k)
. Given the input
{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}
, the output of each branch is given by

{\mathbf{Z}}_{q}^{\prime}=\sigma\left({\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}\right)={\mathbf{P}}\sigma\left({\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(1)}\right)={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{Z}}_{k}.

(20)

Here, we consider a single-layer MLP for simplicity, but the same reasoning applies to an MLP with
L
layers. Finally, since

f^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}}^{\prime};{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime})=\sum_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{Z}}_{q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathbf{Z}}_{k}={\mathbf{P}}f^{p}_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\mathbf{X}};{\mathcal{S}}),

(21)

the result follows.
∎

Corollary
2
assumes a PDCN with
N
branches, but the result still holds for a smaller number of branches as long as the permutation affects only those GSOs in the model.

Finally, an important property of GNNs is their ability to distinguish between non-isomorphic graphs, as characterized by the WL test
[
25
]
. Next, we argue that PDCN satisfies this property for any pair of non-isomorphic DAGs.
Parallel GNNs distinguish non-isomorphic graphs if their parallel aggregation and readout functions are injective
[
32
, Th. 1]
. PDCN belongs to this class of parallel GNNs, with its aggregation and readout functions defined in (
17
) and (
18
), respectively.
From a node-wise perspective, this diffusion can be expressed as

\sum_{v\leq u}[{\mathbf{S}}_{k}]_{uv}{\mathbf{x}}_{v},\quad u\in{\mathcal{V}},

(22)

where each node
u
aggregates the features
{\mathbf{x}}_{v}
from its predecessors
v\leq u
(i.e., its in-neighbors in the reachability DAG
\bar{{\mathcal{D}}}
).
Since Proposition
2
establishes that the set of causal GSOs
{\mathcal{S}}
is unique for different (non-isomorphic) DAGs, neighborhood aggregation is injective when node features are one-hot encodings, as discussed in
[
9
]
.
Moreover,
[
9
, Lemma 5]
asserts that there exists a function
\phi
such that
\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\phi(x)
is unique for each multiset
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then, based on the universal approximation theorem, the MLP in PDCN can learn a mapping that ensures the global readout function is injective.

Remark 1
.

Based on the preceding discussion, PDCN satisfies the conditions of
[
32
, Th. 1]
and is therefore capable of mapping non-isomorphic DAGs, as recognized by the WL test, to distinct embeddings.

Numerical Evaluation

We demonstrate the strong performance of the DCN architecture across diverse tasks and settings, through comprehensive benchmarking against state-of-the-art models using both synthetic and real-world datasets. We compare against the DAG-agnostic GNN baselines GCN
[
8
]
, FB-GCNN
[
6
]
, GraphSAGE
[
33
]
, GIN
[
9
]
, and GAT
[
10
]
. We also consider an MLP
[
34
]
to probe the added value of leveraging prior information about the graph structure. In terms of DAG-aware baselines, we only utilize the encoders of the DAGNN
[
24
]
and D-VAE
[

models. Our GSP focus is on learning from DAG signals, rather than decoding DAG structure from learned latent representations. See Appendix
C

Synthetic experiments in Section

include two tasks: prediction of a diffused signal and source identification. In the diffusion learning task, we evaluate the ability of the model to capture signal propagation dynamics over DAGs. After training the graph signal regression model using an MSE loss

\textrm{NMSE}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}

where

{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{CE}}
is employed during training, and the performance is reported in terms of classification accuracy. Additionally, we conduct experiments on two real-world datasets: gene expression levels and hydrological measurements from the River Thames. In all cases, the data is split into 70% for training, 20% for validation, and 10% for testing. VI-A

Synthetic Data Experiments

Unless otherwise stated, for the synthetic experiments we sample graphs from an Erdős-Rényi (ER) random DAG model with
N=100
nodes and an edge probability
p=0.2
. We simulate
2000
input-output graph signal pairs using the model
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}
. Here,
{\mathbf{x}}
is a sparse input signal with non-zero values restricted to the first 25 nodes in the DAG,
{\mathbf{H}}
is a causal graph filter as in (
7
) constructed from 25 causal GSOs chosen uniformly at random, and
{\mathbf{y}}
represents the diffused output signal. The filter taps are uniformly sampled from
[-1,1]
.

We focus on two supervised learning problems, involving the above signals supported on ER DAGs; recall the general problem statement in Section
II-B
. First, we consider the system identification task of learning a network diffusion process, where the objective is to estimate the output
{\mathbf{y}}_{\textrm{test}}
given a new sparse input
{\mathbf{x}}_{\textrm{test}}
. The input and output signal observations are corrupted with zero-mean additive white Gaussian noise. To have a better control over the signal-to-noise ratio, additive noise is normalized with respect to the signal power.
We set the normalized power to
0.05
, that is, a
5\%
of the power of the signal.
The second task addresses the source identification problem, where the goal is to predict the source node of a partially observed output signal
{\mathbf{y}}_{\textrm{test}}
. For this task the input
{\mathbf{x}}
contains a single non-zero entry, constrained to be within the first 25 nodes in the topologically-ordered
{\mathcal{V}}
. Since the diffusion
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}
does not alter the signal value at the source nodes, we mask the output
{\mathbf{y}}
to exclude direct observations from potential seed nodes. Results (averaged over 25 trials) are reported in Table
I
.

Findings and discussion.
The performance of the (P)DCN architectures is compared to several baselines. The least-squares (LS) predictor captures the true linear model; it computes an LS estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from the training data and then predicts test outputs. The results tabulated in Table I show the superior performance of DCN in both tasks. In the
network diffusion task
, we observe that using a subset of 30 or 10 GSOs (DCN-30 and DCN-10) allows to decouple the number of DCN parameters from the graph size, while maintaining competitive performance. VII
Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work

This work introduced DCN, a novel GNN architecture specifically designed for convolutional learning from signals supported on DAGs. Unlike conventional GNNs, DCN layers are built from causal graph filters that inject strong inductive biases stemming from the partially-ordered nodes in a DAG. Unlike prior message-passing architectures for DAG representation learning, our GSP perspective is to exploit formal convolutions for DAG signals. These sparse operators admit graph-spectral domain interpretations (useful for analyses) and computationally-efficient implementation in the vertex domain. We further proposed PDCN, a parallel and parameter-efficient variant that retains strong predictive performance.
We established that the proposed architectures are permutation equivariant and that PDCN is capable of mapping non-isomorphic DAGs, as recognized by the WL test, to distinct representations. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets, including prediction of gene-expression levels and hydrological forecasting, confirmed the robustness, scalability, and effectiveness of the proposed architectures. DCN consistently outperformed state-of-the-art baselines in terms of accuracy, noise resilience, and computational efficiency.

Despite these strengths, our approach is not exempt from limitations. DCN’s reliance on a potentially large number of causal GSOs (and accordingly the number of learnable weights that grows with the number of nodes) may challenge scalable learning in moderate- to large-sized DAGs. Although PDCN addresses this issue in part, future work will explore adaptive or learned selection of informative GSOs to further reduce complexity. Additionally, we aim to investigate how DCN can be extended to dynamic DAGs or probabilistic causal graphs; integration within Bayesian neural network frameworks is also of interest for adding an uncertainty quantification substrate to our models. Conducting stability analyses in the presence of graph perturbations is another direction in our future research agenda, noting that studies involving directed graphs are notoriously challenging. Overall, this work contributes a principled and practical foundation for deep learning on DAGs, with broad implications across diverse domains where directional (and potentially causal) relationships are of essence.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
1

We first show how permutations affect the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
and then stablish that causal graph filters are permutation equivariant.

Permuting a graph results in a new adjacency matrix given by
{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}
.
From our choice of transitive closure, it follows that the permutation reflects on
{\mathbf{W}}
as

{\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}=({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime})^{-1}={\mathbf{P}}({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}.

(27)

Recall that the diagonal entries of the matrices
{\mathbf{D}}_{q}
encode the ordering of the nodes, which remain unchanged by relabeling.
Thus, after permutation, we have

{\mathbf{D}}_{q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top},

(28)

where
k=\pi^{-1}(q)
. This ensures that
{\mathbf{D}}_{k}
retains the ordering of the original (unpermuted) graph, while
{\mathbf{P}}
reorders its diagonal entries to match the new node labels.
Then, combining (
6
), (
27
), and (
28
), we obtain

{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}={\mathbf{W}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{D}}_{q}^{\prime}({\mathbf{W}}^{\prime})^{-1}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{W}}{\mathbf{D}}_{k}{\mathbf{W}}^{-1}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}={\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}.

Next, observe that each filter coefficient
\theta_{k}
is associated with the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}_{k}
, so both are tied to a specific node rather than its label.
Thus, for each permuted term
\theta_{q}{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}
, we have

\theta_{q}{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}=\theta_{k}{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}.

Applying this relation and the expression for causal graph filters, we conclude that the output of the graph filter when the input is given by the permuted
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
is given by

g({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime},{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime})=\sum_{q\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}\theta_{q}{\mathbf{S}}_{q}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{P}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{V}}}\theta_{k}{\mathbf{S}}_{k}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{P}}g({\mathbf{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta},{\mathcal{S}}).

Baselines Methods

We outline the baseline models and their hyperparameter used for the experimental comparisons in Section

.

GCN

[
8
]
. Implements a first-order graph convolution using a degree-normalized adjacency matrix with self-loops. Each layer aggregates neighbor features and then applies a linear transformation followed by a nonlinearity. GCNs may suffer from oversmoothing. Like DCN, our implementation uses
L=2

32

FB-GCNN

[
6
]
. Represents the convolutional GNN architecture in (
2
). In all settings, the vector of learnable parameters has dimension

L=2
layers. Unless otherwise stated, we use a filter order
R=2
. When we vary
R
, the respective models are denoted FB-GCNN-
R

GraphSAGE

[
33
]
. Learns inductive node embeddings by sampling and aggregating fixed-size neighborhoods using functions like mean, LSTM, or pooling. Unlike transductive models like GCN, it can generalize to unseen nodes after training. We implement GraphSAGE with
L=2
layers, mean aggregation, and a hidden dimension of
32
.

GIN

[
9
]
. Designed to match the expressive power of the WL graph isomorphism test by using injective sum aggregation, thus better differentiating between graph structures. Each GIN layer combines sum-aggregated neighbor features with learnable MLPs. GIN-

[
10

L=2
layers with

2

DAGNN

[
24
]
. Adapts GNN’s message passing to capture a DAG’s topological ordering. Uses an attention mechanism to weigh information from parent nodes and a GRU to sequentially integrate these messages. In our implementation, DAGNN has
L=2

.

D-VAE

[
23
]
. A VAE model to learn continuous latent representations of DAGs. Like DAGNN, it recursively encodes nodes by aggregating messages from their predecessors using a GRU. The latent space representation is decoded back to a DAG, ensuring structural consistency. We only use D-VAE’s encoder in our tests, with a hidden layer of
128

MLP

[
34
]
. A structure-agnostic model that treats node features independently, without incorporating any graph connectivity. It consists of fully connected layers applied to each node’s feature vector. Facilitates assessing the added value of using the graph structure in the models. Our MLP has

Random Graph Models

For the synthetic data experiments in Section
VI-A
, we generate random DAGs according to the following two models.

Erdős-Rényi (ER).
For given
N
, undirected edges are established independently with probability
p
. Then, the edges are randomly oriented so that
{\mathbf{A}}
is lower triangular.

Scale-Free (SF).

These graph models are based on the preferential attachment process
[
35
]
. Starting from an initial DAG with
m_{0}
nodes, new nodes are added sequentially, each connecting to
m\leq m_{0}
existing nodes with probability proportional to their degrees. Edges are oriented each time a new node is attached, resulting in a sampled DAG.
