Jailbreaking LLMs Without Gradients or Priors: Effective and Transferable Attacks
Zhakshylyk Nurlanov, Frank R. Schmidt, Florian Bernard

Abstract
As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in safety-critical domains, rigorously evaluating their robustness against adversarial jailbreaks is essential. However, current safety evaluations often overestimate robustness because existing automated attacks are limited by restrictive assumptions. They typically rely on handcrafted priors or require white-box access for gradient propagation. We challenge these constraints by demonstrating that token-level iterative optimization can succeed without gradients or priors. We introduce RAILS (RAndom Iterative Local Search), a framework that operates solely on model logits. RAILS matches the effectiveness of gradient-based methods through two key innovations: a novel auto-regressive loss that enforces exact prefix matching, and a history-based selection strategy that bridges the gap between the proxy optimization objective and the true attack success rate. Crucially, by eliminating gradient dependency, RAILS enables cross-tokenizer ensemble attacks. This allows for the discovery of shared adversarial patterns that generalize across disjoint vocabularies, significantly enhancing transferability to closed-source systems. Empirically, RAILS achieves near 100% success rates on multiple open-source models and high black-box attack transferability to closed-source systems like GPT and Gemini.

Introduction

The rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into critical domains—from healthcare
(
19
)
to software engineering
(
4
)
—has elevated the urgency of robust safety alignment. Despite extensive efforts to align these models via human feedback (e.g., RLHF
(
11
)
), they remain vulnerable to
jailbreaks
: adversarial inputs that bypass safety filters to elicit harmful content
(
10
)
. These vulnerabilities pose severe risks, ranging from the automated generation of disinformation
(
20
)
to assistance in designing biological weapons
(
8
)
.

Red teaming has emerged as a primary defense against these threats
(
7
)
. However, automated red teaming currently faces a dilemma. Gradient-based methods like GCG
(
24
)
are effective but require white-box access to model weights. Crucially, they are inherently tied to the specific tokenizer of the target model to compute gradient updates, making it difficult to generate attacks that transfer across models with disjoint vocabularies. Conversely, gradient-free methods often rely on heavy handcrafted priors
(
13
)
or “attacker" LLMs
(
3
)
, limiting their ability to discover novel, structural vulnerabilities outside their pre-defined templates.

To address these limitations, we introduce
RAILS
(RAndom Iterative Local Search), a gradient-free and prior-free optimization framework. RAILS operates solely on model logits, treating jailbreaking as a discrete optimization problem. Unlike naive random search, which fails in the astronomically large search space of discrete prompts, RAILS achieves state-of-the-art performance through two key innovations: a novel
auto-regressive loss
used in local search that enforces exact prefix matching, and a
history-based candidate selection
strategy.

Figure 1:
RAILS: A tokenizer-agnostic and gradient-free framework.
Unlike gradient-based methods which are bound to specific model weights and tokenizers, RAILS utilizes logit-based feedback from multiple open-source LLMs to generate robust adversarial prompts that effectively transfer to black-box models.

The latter addresses a critical challenge in adversarial optimization: the misalignment between the
proxy objective
(maximizing the probability of a target affirmative prefix) and the
true objective
(eliciting a fully harmful response). Low loss on the proxy does not guarantee a successful jailbreak. RAILS mitigates this by retaining a diverse history of candidates—balancing exploration (diversity) and exploitation (low loss)—to maximize the probability of bypassing safety filters during a few-shot attack.

Our study provides the following key contributions:

•

Gradient-Free Optimization Framework:
We demonstrate that iterative local search, when guided by an auto-regressive loss, acts as a potent optimizer in the discrete token space, matching the efficiency of gradient-based methods without requiring white-box access.

•

Bridging the Objective Gap:
We introduce a history-based selection strategy that bridges the gap between the proxy optimization objective and the true attack success rate. By exploiting the diversity of the optimization trajectory, this approach significantly improves robustness against safety filters compared to greedy selection.

•

Cross-Tokenizer Ensemble Attacks:
We propose a method to optimize attacks against ensembles of models with disjoint vocabularies—a capability inherently difficult for gradient-based approaches—thereby discovering shared, tokenizer-agnostic vulnerabilities.

•

Empirical Effectiveness and Transferability:
We report state-of-the-art results on open-source models (e.g., 90.2% ASR on Llama2) and show that cross-tokenizer optimization is a key driver of transferability, enabling successful attacks on closed-source systems like GPT and Gemini.

2
Related Work

The pursuit of addressing LLM vulnerabilities has spurred diverse strategies in prompt optimization. We categorize existing methods by their reliance on gradient access, handcrafted priors, and their ability to transfer across models.

2.1
Gradient-Based Optimization

A prominent class of attacks treats prompt generation as a gradient-based optimization problem, requiring white-box access to the target model’s weights. Methods such as AutoPrompt
(
18
)
, GGI
(
23
)
, and Faster-GCG
(
12
)
utilize gradient information to guide token selection. The standard baseline in this category, GCG
(
24
)
, employs a coordinate ascent strategy to optimize adversarial suffixes. While effective, these methods face two critical limitations: they are inapplicable in gray-box settings (where only logits are available) and are inherently tied to the specific tokenizer of the source model, complicating ensemble attacks across disparate architectures.

2.2
Attacks Based on Handcrafted Priors

To bypass the need for gradients, many methods rely on strong, human-designed priors. We distinguish between two sub-categories:

Template and Seed-Based Methods.
Some approaches constrain optimization to specific, handcrafted templates. Prompt-based Random Search (PRS)
(
1
)
, for example, optimizes a suffix within a lengthy, model-specific template. Similarly, AutoDAN
(
13
)
utilizes an evolutionary algorithm but depends on a carefully curated set of human-written prompts to seed its search. RAILS challenges the necessity of these manual priors, demonstrating that effective attacks can be learned from scratch.

LLM-Guided Strategies.
Another line of work shifts the burden of optimization to an “attacker” LLM. Methods such as PAIR
(
3
)
, TAP
(
15
)
, and SFS
(
16
)
use a language model, steered by meta-prompts, to iteratively refine adversarial inputs. While these methods operate without gradients, they are limited by the capabilities and context window of the attacker model, often struggling to find the precise token-level manipulations required to break robustly aligned models.

2.3
Gradient-Free Optimization

Our work falls into the category of general-purpose, gradient-free optimization. These methods aim to discover adversarial inputs using only model outputs (logits). BEAST
(
17
)
employs a beam search strategy guided by an auxiliary model to propose token substitutions. However, as shown in our experiments, BEAST struggles to jailbreak robustly aligned models like Llama2. RAILS demonstrates that a simpler approach—random iterative local search—can be significantly more potent when augmented with an auto-regressive loss and history-based candidate selection.

2.4
Adversarial Transferability

Generating attacks that transfer from open-source to closed-source models is a significant challenge, extensively studied in computer vision
(
6
;
22
;
9
)
and recently in NLP. Transfer attacks like GCG-T
(
24
)
typically optimize against an ensemble of models. However, gradient-based ensembles usually require models to share a tokenizer, severely limiting the diversity of the ensemble. While methods like TAP-T
(
15
)
improve transferability, they often rely on powerful models (e.g., GPT-4) as the source. RAILS introduces a
cross-tokenizer
ensemble approach, enabling the optimization of prompts against models with disjoint vocabularies. This capability allows RAILS to exploit fundamental, shared vulnerabilities across diverse architectures, significantly enhancing transferability to black-box systems.

3
Method

We introduce RAILS (RAndom Iterative Local Search), a framework designed to jailbreak LLMs without gradient access or handcrafted priors. We first formalize the optimization problem and the threat model, then detail our search algorithm, loss functions, and candidate selection strategy.

3.1
Problem Formulation

We operate under a
gray-box threat model
. The attacker has query access to a target LLM
\mathcal{M}
and can observe the output logits, but has no access to internal weights or gradients.
Let
{\bm{x}}_{1:i-1}
be a harmful user query (e.g., “Write a tutorial on bomb-making”). Our goal is to find an adversarial suffix
{\bm{x}}_{i:j}
of length
L
that induces the model to generate a harmful response.

The Objective Gap.
A core challenge in automated red teaming is the misalignment between the optimization objective and the true attack success.

•

Proxy Objective (Optimization):
We optimize the probability of the model generating a specific affirmative target string
{\bm{y}}
(e.g., “Sure, here is a tutorial”).

{\bm{x}}^{*}_{i:j}=\argmax_{{\bm{x}}_{i:j}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}}P_{\mathcal{M}}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{1:i-1}\oplus{\bm{x}}_{i:j})

(1)

•

True Objective (Evaluation):
An attack is successful only if the full model response is classified as harmful by a judge classifier
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
.

High probability of the target
{\bm{y}}
does not guarantee a harmful response; the model might generate the target prefix but then refuse (e.g., “Sure, here is… I cannot help with that”). RAILS addresses this gap through a specialized candidate selection strategy (Section
3.4
).

(1) In the iterative search loop, RAILS perturbs the current best string, evaluates candidates using the source model(s), and updates the history buffer
\mathcal{H}
. (2) After optimization, top candidates are selected from history for a final few-shot attack.

3.2
The RAILS Algorithm

RAILS optimizes the adversarial suffix in a two-stage process, visualized in Figure
2
.

1.

Iterative Search Loop:
We iteratively refine a single candidate string using random local perturbations guided by model logits. All evaluated candidates are stored in a history buffer
\mathcal{H}
.

2.

Few-Shot Attack:
After the search budget is exhausted, we select a diverse set of candidates from
\mathcal{H}
to query the target model, maximizing the likelihood that at least one prompts a harmful response.

3.3
Loss Functions

Standard attacks typically use the Teacher-Forcing (TF) loss, which averages the negative log-likelihood of all target tokens. However, in generation tasks, predicting the first token correctly is a prerequisite for the subsequent tokens. To reflect this, we introduce a stricter loss.

Auto-Regressive Loss (
\mathcal{L}_{AR}
).
This loss penalizes the sequence with a large constant
C
as soon as the model’s greedy generation deviates from the target
{\bm{y}}
.

\ell^{k}_{AR}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\begin{cases}C,&\text{if }\exists~i<k:\hat{y}_{i}\neq y_{i}\\
-\log P_{\mathcal{M}}(y_{k}\mid{\bm{x}}\oplus{{\bm{y}}}_{0:{k-1}}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

where
\hat{y}_{i}
is the token greedily generated by the model at position
i
.
\mathcal{L}_{AR}
forces the optimizer to prioritize exact prefix matching, preventing it from optimizing later tokens until the earlier ones are correct.

Combined Loss.
We use a convex combination of both losses to guide the search:

\mathcal{L}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{AR}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{TF}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})

(3)

3.4
History-Based Candidate Selection

To bridge the gap between the proxy objective (low loss) and the true objective (harmfulness), we employ a hybrid selection strategy for the final attack. We select
K
candidates from the history buffer
\mathcal{H}
based on two criteria:

1.

Exploitation (Lowest Loss):
We select the top candidates with the absolute lowest loss values observed during the entire search. These maximize the probability of the target prefix.

2.

Exploration (Temporal Diversity):
We sample candidates uniformly from different iterations of the search trajectory. These candidates may have slightly higher loss but represent diverse points in the embedding space.

This strategy acknowledges that an optimal string might be overfitted to the proxy objective, while a slightly suboptimal point might trigger the true harmful behavior more effectively.

3.5
Cross-Tokenizer Ensemble Attack

A key innovation of RAILS is its ability to attack ensembles of models with disjoint vocabularies. Since we do not backpropagate gradients, the perturbation generation is decoupled from the loss computation.
We generate token-level perturbations and evaluate the loss on each model
\mathcal{M}_{j}
using its
own tokenizer.
The ensemble loss is a weighted sum:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ens}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=\sum_{j}w_{j}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}_{j}}(\text{Tokenize}_{j}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})

(4)

where weights
w_{j}
are dynamically adjusted based on each model’s current success, prioritizing models that are not yet successfully attacked. This forces the search to find adversarial strings that are robust across different tokenization schemes, enhancing transferability.

4
Experiments

We evaluate RAILS on the HarmBench benchmark
(
14
)
, a standardized framework for red teaming. We assess performance on both single-model direct attacks and cross-model transfer attacks.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Dataset and Models.
We target a set of aligned open-source models: Llama2-7B-Chat, Llama3-8B-Instruct, Vicuna-7B-v1.5, Qwen-7B-Chat, and Baichuan2-7B-Chat. For transfer attacks, we target closed-source systems: GPT-3.5 Turbo (1106), GPT-4 Turbo (1106), and Google Gemini Pro 1.5. The dataset covers harmful behaviors including cybercrime, chemical weapons, and harassment.

Baselines.
We compare against gradient-based GCG, gradient-free search methods (PRS, BEAST, AutoDAN), and agent-based methods (PAIR, TAP). All baselines use standard settings from the literature.

Evaluation Protocol.

Attack success is determined by a pre-trained harmfulness classifier (
\mathcal{C}_{\text{test}}
) that evaluates the target model’s full response (up to 512 tokens), following
(
14
)
. A successful attack must elicit a response that is both harmful and relevant to the query.
Current methods like PAIR, TAP, and PRS inherently use full model responses and judge classifiers
during their optimization loop
to select prompts. To ensure a fair comparison, RAILS adopts a compatible two-phase approach:

1.

Optimization (Proxy Objective):
We optimize purely based on the proxy objective (probability of the target prefix) without expensive generation.

2.

Selection (True Objective):
We select
K=100
diverse candidates from the history buffer and filter them using a validation classifier (
\mathcal{C}_{\text{val}}
) on the source model. The top
K_{\text{test}}=20
successful prompts are then evaluated on the target model.

This setup mirrors the computational reality of an attacker who generates many low-cost candidates offline before launching a few-shot attack. In our ablations, we also report “RAILS Core” results using a single prompt from proxy objective optimization to compare head-to-head with single-shot baselines like GCG.

4.3.2
Adversarial Suffix Length.

We analyzed the impact of suffix length on attack success (Figure
4
a). We observe a distinct trade-off: while longer suffixes (40-60 tokens) maximize ASR on the white-box source model (Llama2), shorter suffixes (5-20 tokens) transfer significantly better to black-box targets (GPT-3.5, Gemini). This suggests that shorter strings act as more generic "jailbreak triggers," whereas longer strings tend to overfit the specific attention patterns or tokenization artifacts of the source model.

4.3.3
Candidate Selection Strategy.

To bridge the gap between the proxy objective (loss) and the true objective (harmful generation), we optimize the selection of the
K=100
validation candidates. During optimization, we generate a large pool of candidates (
600\text{ iterations}\times 1024\text{ batch size}
). We compared varying strategies to sample from this pool (Figure
4
b). Greedy strategies (e.g., "Best 100 iterations") that select candidates solely by lowest loss often fail due to redundancy; they select semantically identical suffixes from the same local minimum. In contrast, our
Hybrid Strategy
selects the top 50 candidates by loss (Exploitation) and 50 candidates by sampling the best suffix from random distinct iterations (Exploration). This approach significantly outperforms baselines, boosting validation success from
\sim
78% to 86%, demonstrating that temporal diversity in the optimization trajectory is crucial for finding effective jailbreaks.

4.4
Quantitative Analysis

4.4.1
Mechanisms of Transfer.

To understand why RAILS-T transfers effectively, we analyzed tokenizer structures vs. suffix consistency (Table
5
). We define directional
vocabulary overlap
as the proportion of tokens from a source model’s vocabulary that exist in a target model’s vocabulary (
|V_{i}\cap V_{j}|/|V_{i}|
). Due to vast differences in vocabulary sizes (e.g., Llama2’s 32k vs Qwen’s 152k), this metric is highly asymmetric. Averaging across all directional pairs of our evaluated models (Llama2, Qwen, Llama3, Baichuan2, GPT-4, Gemini Pro), the mean vocabulary overlap is only
49.0%
(see Supplementary
S3
). Despite this moderate vocabulary overlap, the adversarial suffixes generated by RAILS-T (Multi-Model) exhibit a high
token consistency
of
84.0%
when re-tokenized. In contrast, Single-Model attacks produce suffixes with lower consistency (81.1%). This confirms that ensemble optimization drives RAILS to discover shared adversarial patterns that map to valid tokens across disjoint vocabularies (see Supplementary
S4
and
S5
).

5
Discussion

Impact Statement.

This work demonstrates that attackers can bypass LLM safety mechanisms using only output logits, suggesting that real-world models may be more vulnerable than previously understood. While these findings highlight significant risks, RAILS also serves as a valuable tool for automated red teaming, enabling more realistic evaluations of model defenses. By improving our understanding of how harmful behaviors emerge and transfer across architectures, this work supports the development of more robust safeguards.

Limitations.

Our approach has several limitations. First, despite being more efficient than training-based methods, RAILS remains computationally expensive, requiring a large number of model queries per attack. Second, our study focuses on model-level defenses and does not evaluate system-level defenses like auxiliary input/output filtering or moderations APIs. Third, while RAILS finds effective adversarial prompts, interpreting
why
certain token perturbations generalize better than others remains an open challenge for future interpretability research.

6
Conclusion

We introduced RAILS, a gradient-free optimization framework that challenges the assumption that white-box access or handcrafted priors are necessary for effective red teaming. By leveraging random iterative search with a specialized auto-regressive loss and history-based selection, RAILS matches or exceeds the performance of gradient-based methods. Crucially, RAILS effectively bridges the gap between incompatible tokenization schemes, enabling the generation of universal adversarial prompts that transfer to closed-source models like GPT-4 and Gemini Pro. Our findings reveal structural weaknesses shared across diverse LLM architectures and underscore the urgent need for stronger, tokenizer-agnostic defenses to enhance AI security.
