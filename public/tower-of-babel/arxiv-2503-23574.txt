Abstract
. Model documentation plays a crucial role in promoting transparency and responsible development of AI systems. With the rise of Generative AI (GenAI), open-source platforms have increasingly become hubs for hosting and distributing these models, prompting platforms like Hugging Face to develop dedicated model documentation guidelines that align with responsible AI principles. Despite these growing efforts, there remains a lack of understanding of how developers document their GenAI models on open-source platforms. Through interviews with 13 GenAI developers active on open-source platforms, we provide empirical insights into their documentation practices and challenges. Our analysis reveals that despite existing resources, developers of GenAI models still face multiple layers of uncertainties in their model documentation: (1) uncertainties about what specific content should be included; (2) uncertainties about how to effectively report key components of their models; and (3) uncertainties in deciding who should take responsibilities for various aspects of model documentation. Based on our findings, we discuss the implications for policymakers, open-source platforms, and the research community to support meaningful, effective and actionable model documentation in the GenAI era, including cultivating better community norms, building robust evaluation infrastructures, and clarifying roles and responsibilities.

1.
Introduction

Model documentation like Model Cards
(
Mitchell et al. 2018
)
has long been considered as a key mechanism for enhancing AI transparency and accountability by the broader responsible AI (RAI) community
(
Winecoff and Bogen 2024
;
Liao et al. 2023
;
Liao and Vaughan 2023
)
. The emergence of Generative AI (GenAI) has amplified both the complexity and importance of model documentation. On the one hand, unlike traditional AI/ML systems designed for specific tasks, GenAI models present unique documentation challenges due to their broad capabilities, emergent behaviors, and potential for unexpected outputs. These challenges intersect with what researchers have termed the “evaluation crisis”—the growing difficulty in predicting and measuring model performance across diverse tasks and contexts
(
Xiao et al. 2024
;
Ganguli et al. 2022a
;
Chang et al. 2023
;
Wallach et al. 2024
;
Grill 2024
;
Hutchinson et al. 2022
)
. On the other hand, these unique characteristics and rapid development have also spurred new regulatory initiatives, such as the EU AI Act’s code of practice for general-purpose models
(
AIA [n. d.]
)
, which aims to standardize documentation requirements for these systems.

With the proliferation of GenAI systems, open-source platforms have emerged as central hubs for hosting, sharing, and deploying these models
(
Srikumar et al. 2024
)
. Far from serving merely as code repositories, these platforms function as dynamic ecosystems where models are collaboratively developed, modified, and applied across diverse contexts
(
Gorwa and Veale 2023
)
. This shift has driven initiatives like Hugging Face’s dedicated model documentation guidelines
(
Face 2024a
)
, which seek to align open-source practices with RAI principles.

Prior work in the HCI and FAccT communities has offered critical guidelines and frameworks to help AI/ML practitioners document their models and facilitate stakeholder communication
(
Mitchell et al. 2018
;
Crisan et al. 2022
;
Mohammad 2021
;
McMillan-Major et al. 2021
)
. Since the introduction of these frameworks, researchers have investigated their value and implementation, through both lab-based studies
(
Liao and Vaughan 2023
;
Boyd 2021
;
Nunes et al. 2022
;
Nunes et al. 2024
;
McMillan-Major et al. 2024
)
and real-world investigations
(
Yang et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2024
;
Heger et al. 2022
;
Chang and Custis 2022
;
Bhat et al. 2023
)
. While these studies provide important insights into how documentation has been performed in corporate and research settings
(
Chang and Custis 2022
;
Heger et al. 2022
)
, little is known about documentation practices in the open-source ecosystem, which might pose unique challenges—particularly with GenAI models.
Meanwhile, recent large-scale studies have highlighted widespread insufficiencies and incompleteness in documentation artifacts on open-source platforms
(
Liang et al. 2024
;
Winecoff and Bogen 2024
)
. However, these studies focus primarily on the artifacts themselves, leaving limited understanding of the underlying reasons for these gaps and how developers on these platforms approach and practice documentation. Finally, while extensive research has examined how open-source software developers document code and leverage documentation for peer knowledge sharing
(
Guzzi 2012
;
Rai et al. 2022
;
Kadhim 2024
;
Sharma et al. 2022
)
, there remains a critical gap in understanding how traditional code documentation practices adapt to the unique demands of GenAI models.

Our study addresses these gaps by exploring how GenAI developers navigate documentation practices in open-source contexts, uncovering the distinct challenges and emerging practices that characterize this rapidly evolving field. In this paper, we ask the following research questions:
How do developers of GenAI document their models on open-source platforms? What are the challenges they face in this process?

To answer these questions, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 13 developers who had experiences in documenting GenAI models on open-source platforms. Our findings reveal that despite existing resources
(
Mitchell et al. 2018
)
, developers still face significant uncertainties in three key areas: determining what content to include in documentation, figuring out how to effectively document different aspects of their models, and establishing who should be responsible for various documentation components. We find that these uncertainties are shaped by competing pressures among traditional open-source documentation norms, market-driven interests, and emerging RAI guidelines.

By illuminating these uncertainties and the underlying tensions that contribute to them, this work advances our understanding of documentation practices in the evolving landscape of GenAI development on open-source platforms. Based on our findings, we further discuss implications for policymakers, open-source platforms, and RAI researchers to support more meaningful, effective, and actionable model documentation in the GenAI era, including fostering stronger community norms, building robust evaluation infrastructures, and clarifying roles and responsibilities. More broadly, this work contributes to the growing discussions on achieving meaningful transparency and accountability in AI development within existing open-source community norms and practices.

2.
Background and Related Work

2.1.
AI/ML Documentation as Responsible AI Artifacts

AI/ML documentation has been considered as a key mechanism for enhancing AI transparency and accountability by the Responsible AI (RAI) community
(
Liao et al. 2023
;
Liao and Vaughan 2023
;
Mitchell et al. 2018
)
. Past RAI literature in CHI, FAccT, and other conferences have proposed specialized documentation approaches, including data documentation
(
Gebru et al. 2018
;
Jain et al. 2024
;
Bender and Friedman 2018
)
, model documentation
(
Mitchell et al. 2018
;
Crisan et al. 2022
;
Mohammad 2021
;
McMillan-Major et al. 2021
)
, and AI service documentation
(
Arnold et al. 2018
)
. These frameworks aim to serve multiple purposes: guiding responsible AI usage, facilitating ethical reflection during development, and strengthening governance mechanisms
(
Winecoff and Bogen 2024
)
.

Since the introduction of these frameworks, past literature has offered important insights into their value as well as the challenges associated with their implementation, through both lab-based studies
(
Liao and Vaughan 2023
;
Boyd 2021
;
Nunes et al. 2022
;
Nunes et al. 2024
;
McMillan-Major et al. 2024
)
and real-world investigations
(
Yang et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2024
;
Heger et al. 2022
;
Chang and Custis 2022
;
Bhat et al. 2023
)
. Specifically, a few empirical studies have highlighted the challenges AI/ML practitioners face when documenting traditional (predictive) AI/ML systems in real-world settings. For example,
Heger et al. 2022
found that industry practitioners often struggle with determining documentation scope, target audience, and level of detail. Similarly,
Chang and Custis 2022
highlighted how the lack of incentives and organizational support can hinder the production of high-quality model documentation.

Evaluation is an important part of AI documentation. For example, some existing documentation templates require evaluating models’ disaggregated performance, biases, and downstream impact
(
Mitchell et al. 2018
;
Arnold et al. 2018
)
. However, the emergence of GenAI models has introduced unprecedented complexities in the evaluation of model capabilities, opportunities, and risks
(
Jiao et al. 2024
;
Wallach et al. 2024
;
Ganguli et al. 2022b
;
Solaiman et al. 2023
;
Bender et al. 2021
;
Magooda et al. 2023
)
, which might further amplify and complicate challenges previously identified when documenting traditional AI/ML models. For example, recent research has highlighted how the distinct characteristics of GenAI models—from their emergent behaviors across contexts to their inherent ability to hallucinate—could significantly complicate related documentation and transparency efforts
(
Liao and Vaughan 2023
)
. Prior work has established methodological gaps in evaluations of GenAI risks and capabilities
(
Rauh et al. 2024
;
Wallach et al. 2024
;
Grill 2024
;
McIntosh et al. 2024
)
, which in turn complicates how evaluation results are communicated through documentation.
Despite these widely acknowledged challenges in the research community, there remains a critical gap in empirical understanding of how GenAI developers in real-world settings (particularly in open-source communities) encounter, perceive, and attempt to navigate these complexities in their documentation practices.

2.2.
Documentation Practices in Open Source Communities: From Software to AI Models

Open Source Software (OSS) broadly refers to code licensed to enable anyone to freely access, modify, and repurpose it with minimal restrictions
(
St. Laurent [n. d.]
)
. Beyond its technical definition on flexibility and accessibility of the software, OSS embodies the social dynamics of its creation, often rooted in a “hobbyist culture” where distributed volunteers collaborate voluntarily. This culture is driven by personal enjoyment, a sense of freedom, and loosely connected networks of contributors
(
Coleman 2012
;
Roberts et al. 2006
;
Ahmad 2021
)
.
In OSS communities, there is a long and rich tradition of documentation practices (e.g., code documentation), often evolving organically and informally to serve the needs of the developer community
(
Geiger et al. 2018
;
Pawlik et al. 2015
;
Arya et al. 2024
)
. Previous literature
(
Pawlik et al. 2015
;
Pinho et al. 2024
;
Kazman et al. 2016
)
has highlighted the user-driven and crowd-sourced nature of code documentation practices in OSS, which commonly contains essential information including project overview, implementation details, usage guidelines, and terms of use. For instance, on Git-based platforms like GitHub, README files serve as the primary entry point for projects, appearing prominently on repository homepages and usually containing these standard elements
(
Aggarwal et al. 2014
;
Longo and Kelley 2015
)
.

In recent years, open-source platforms has increasingly become a crucial site for research and development of GenAI models, shaped by multiple intersecting factors. Recent research has illustrated that open-source platforms are an essential infrastructure for model hosting and dissemination in the emerging GenAI supply chain
(
Srikumar et al. 2024
;
Ait et al. 2023
;
Gorwa and Veale 2023
)
.
Meanwhile, scholars have raised concerns that “open source AI” might serve as a strategic tool in corporate AI competition
(
Gray Widder et al. 2023
;
Srnicek 2022
)
, potentially leading to “open washing” practices, where claims of openness are used to obscure meaningful policy interventions
(
Gray Widder et al. 2023
;
Widder et al. 2024
;
Liesenfeld and Dingemanse 2024
)
.
In contrast, others have argued that open-source environments have the potential for democratizing AI development and resisting the corporate consolidation of power by potentially leveling the playing field for individuals and organizations
(
Seger et al. 2023
;
Kapoor et al. 2024
;
Howard 2023
)
.

Given that open-source platforms are becoming central hubs for GenAI development
(
Osborne et al. 2024
;
Widder et al. 2024
)
,
Gorwa and Veale 2023
argue that these platforms have effectively become model marketplaces requiring platform-level governance. Recognizing this challenge, platforms like Hugging Face have begun to develop specific model documentation guidelines designed to align with responsible AI principles
(
Face 2024a
;
Face [n. d.]
)
.
Despite these efforts, recent large-scale, quantitative analyses revealed significant gaps in the transparency and completeness of open-source AI documentation artifacts
(
Yang et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2024
;
Castaño et al. 2023
)
. For example, many documentation artifacts fail to include critical information, such as model limitations, intended uses, and potential social impacts
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
.
In our study, to expand the focus from documentation artifacts to documentation
practices
, we explored GenAI developers’ practices when documenting GenAI models in open-source environments.

3.
Methods

In this study, we examine how GenAI developers approach model documentation on open-source platforms. Following
Gorwa and Veale 2023
, we define “open-source platforms” as both general-purpose software development platforms that host AI models (e.g., GitHub) and specialized AI model marketplaces (e.g., Hugging Face) where users can upload and share AI models and AI-related datasets that others can download, modify, and build upon. We define “GenAI developers” broadly to include anyone involved in creating, adapting, or deploying GenAI models, and, where appropriate, we mention their specific roles across the development pipeline
(
Kolides et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023
)
. Following
Mitchell et al. 2018
, we define “model documentation” as publicly-available materials that report the basic information, training data, evaluation results, and intended use cases of the model, which can include a variety of artifacts, such as Hugging Face Model Cards, GitHub README.md files, or technical reports.

3.1.
Study Protocol

We conducted semi-structured interviews to investigate how GenAI developers approach model documentation on open source platforms. Our study protocol was designed to explore developers’ documentation motivations, practices, challenges, and navigation strategies.
We began by exploring participants’ background in GenAI development. Then we asked them about their past experiences with model documentation, examining their motivations, existing practices, challenges, tool and resources they used for documentation, as well as their approach to navigate different documentation sections and team collaboration practices. We also asked questions relevant to their evaluation processes, such as impact assessment, that goes into their documentation(e.g., how they decided which parts of their evaluation design and results to document). Each interview lasted between 50 and 65 minutes. All interviews were recorded and transcribed for analysis with participants’ consent. Each participant was compensated $60 USD.

(
Patton 2014

3.3.
Data Analysis

We employed a reflexive thematic analysis approach to analyze the interview data
(
Braun and Clarke [n. d.]
)
. The first author performed an initial open coding of all the interview transcripts.
Detailed memos were kept throughout the coding process to document emerging patterns and potential themes. Concurrently, the research group held regular collaborative sessions to iteratively discuss and refine the emerging codes. We continuously discussed discrepancies
and ambiguities in the codes, iteratively refining our coding based on these discussions. We then generated higher-level themes that captured patterns of shared meaning across all the codes via both inductive and deductive coding, while keeping our research questions in mind. Under the standard practice of reflexive thematic analysis, we did not calculate the reliability between coders, as repeated discussions about
discrepancies are integral to generating the codes and themes
(
Braun and Clarke 2019
)
.
Through this iterative process, we identified several key themes related to GenAI developers’ documentation practices, challenges, and navigation strategies in open source environments. Ultimately, this process generated 131 first-level themes, 13 second-level themes, and 4 third-level themes. We present our results and first-level themes in Section 4. All second and third-level themes are presented in the appendix
A
.

4.
Findings

Our findings first highlight the various motivations driving participants to document their models. Based on this, our analysis uncovers three key layers of uncertainty in the documentation practices of participants: (1) uncertainty about what content to prioritize when documenting, (2) uncertainty about how to document effectively, and (3) uncertainty about who should take responsibility for documenting.

4.1.
Why: Motivations for Model Documentation

Through our interviews, we identified a range of motivations driving developers to create model documentation. For our participants, documentation serves multiple purposes, including facilitating model re-use and maintenance, promoting the model (as a form of marketing), and reflecting on ethical considerations.

4.1.1.
Model Reuse and Maintenance.

Most participants view model documentation as a channel for providing information on model usage and communicating maintenance updates to potential users. P5 specifically noted that his model card on Hugging Face serves a similar purpose to code documentation on GitHub. That is, the model card makes the model
“easy to use,”
which is
“exactly the same [as when] I was publishing codes on GitHub where I could do a nice README [so that] that people can use it.”
Similarly, P4 shared that documentation for him is a crucial channel to communicate how to use the model:
"Once you invest your time in creating an artifact…you would invest just as much time in making sure that it’s possible for people to use it."
. Beyond initial usage instructions, developers also use documentation to communicate ongoing maintenance updates. For example, P7 mentioned leveraging model cards to notify users about technical issues:
”We had a bug with transformers… to communicate this bug, we use the model card.”

4.1.2.
Model Promotion and Marketing.

Some participants viewed model documentation as a strategic tool to demonstrate their model’s competitive advantages, positioning it against a market of potential competitor models. This motivation stemmed from a highly competitive mindset among GenAI developers, often leading to a model marketing orientation towards model documentation. In industry settings, this marketing orientation has become increasingly formalized—P13 noted that in his company, product managers are involved in how the model is presented within documentation:
“we have the PM who talks about the model as a product.”
Motivations stemming from this competitive orientation drove some developers to focus on evaluation metrics in documentation. For example, P3 argued that
“I think that’s what sells first, right? The comparative, we beat X models by Y metrics…is what everyone wants to see.”
. Likewise, P9 noted that they are often motivated to share model cards when their evaluation results are compelling, since others can use these results to
“understand how it competes [on] the benchmark… and for comparison purposes.”

4.1.3.
Conveying Ethical Considerations.

A few participants mentioned that they care deeply about the ethical use of the models they developed. As such, they try to reflect their ethical deliberations through their documentation practices. For example, P4 shared his experience collaboratively developing documentation on a foundation model with other members on his team. According to P4, the team’s emphasis on ethical documentation emerged naturally from shared values within the team rather than being imposed through formal requirements. As P4 described,
“[the document] didn’t predate the workshop. It’s not that this document was there and told people to pay attention to it, but rather the workshop started and people had the understanding that this was a mindful way of doing things.”
A clear example of how these shared values lead to documentation practices was their shared value on multicultural inclusion. As P4 explained, in his team there was
“a very active and intentional decision of having people focus on low-resource languages”
. As a result, the team documented both their efforts and limitations in achieving this goal, including explicitly noting when certain language families remained underrepresented, demonstrating their commitment to being
“self-critical, aware and trying as best as possible to address every one of these issues in low-resource languages.”

4.2.
What: Content-Level Uncertainties in Model Documentation

Our findings reveal that a significant uncertainty that participants face when documenting GenAI models is to determine “what to document” – what content to include in their documentation artifacts, even when they are aware of existing responsible AI documentation frameworks.

4.2.1.
Uncertainties in What to Document Beyond Traditional Open-Source Code Documentation Norms

While some participants may recognize the growing emphasis on responsible AI practices, they still feel uncertain in terms of what to include in their model documentation, particularly for sections that extend beyond existing code documentation artifacts in open-source communities. They question whether investing in RAI documentation is worthwhile given their community’s traditional focus on technical implementation and preference for informal knowledge sharing – especially when they observe limited engagement from their primary audience with enhanced documentation efforts.

First, participants feel unsure about whether to adopt the model documentation guidelines provided by platforms, as the informal and flexible nature of open-source knowledge sharing often conflicts with the structured requirements of RAI documentation. For instance, some developers express hesitation to adapt a lengthy, standardized documentation processes that feels at odds with the agile nature of open-source development. As P9, who is an active contributor on Hugging Face, notes:
“They do have good document[ation], but specifically for the model cards, … you literally have to read a full, a very long page… The time and the learning curve to just spend on any syntax or semantics is just a little too much.”
This process conflicts with his preferred development style, as he emphasized:
“I enjoy flexibility as a programmer … [For model documentation,] it’s an initial learning curve, but still it’s a learning curve.”
Second, developers questioned whether readers are willing to read a long and comprehensive document for the model as they felt many users would instead prefer to rely on informal communication methods available within open-source communities. For example, P6 observed that users of his model often bypass formal documentation entirely, instead seeking quick recommendations through channels like Discord. Using such channels in lieu of model documentation, open-source users often reach out to the model creator and ask questions like:
“what does the model do? Is there anything else that I need to know about the model?”
This pattern reflects the traditional hobbyist preference for direct, peer-to-peer knowledge sharing in open source. As P6 explained, developers in the open-source community prefer
“word of mouth, mouth-to-mouth talk.”

These uncertainties about whether to adapt to RAI documentation requirements to fill in different parts of their model documentation reflect a tension between established open-source documentation norms and emerging RAI documentation expectations. We further discuss the implications in Section
5.1
on how to better support the transitioning from code documentation to RAI documentation expectation within developer community.

4.2.2.
Uncertainties around What to Document for the Intended Audience.

Participants also feel uncertain about what to offer in their model documentation to meet the needs of their intended audience. Most participants initially assumed that their primary readers would be similar people in the open-source community that traditional code documentation serves, like data scientists and developers seeking to reuse models, driving them to create documentation artifacts that focus on the re-use and maintenance of their models, related to the motivation we identified in section
4.1.1
. When it comes to audiences like non-technical stakeholders, developers expressed confusion on what to document and whether it is actually meaningful to invest their time in communicating the information that is not traditionally required by the audience of open-source software. P4 noted that despite careful documentation efforts including extensively documenting the ethical considerations, he never received feedback on the bias and limitations section from the open-source community, making him feel that
“no one would’ve engaged with the model in its entirety, even though every bit of it is important.”
Similarly, P7 reflected on his team’s efforts in ethical considerations, including removing opted-out content from their training dataset and conducting red-teaming exercise. However, he observed a lack of community engagement in his model card’s sections on ethical considerations despite his team’s efforts:
“Unfortunately… we never really had any positive feedback on this [section on bias and limitations]. I think mostly people will say that it is good, but they don’t find it necessary to evaluate this part.”
This highlights developers’ uncertainty about what to document for their intended audience on open-source platforms, which may result in incomplete sections that fall outside traditional open-source code documentation practices. We further discuss implications for addressing this underlying tension between established open-source community norms and the emerging RAI requirements in Section
5.1
.

4.2.3.
Uncertainties around What Level of Detail to Report to Balance Competing Priorities.

We find that participants often feel uncertain about what content they should prioritize in their documentation, primarily driven by the competing priorities among ethical, legal and commercial considerations. This tendency has become particularly pronounced with GenAI, where intense market competition and hype cycles create additional pressure around information disclosure. Underlying these disclosure decisions are developers’ uncertainties about navigating unclear boundaries between intellectual property protection and transparency requirements, and about assessing potential legal and competitive risks of disclosure. As a result, these factors influence their decisions about what information to include in different aspects of model documentation, such as details about datasets and model properties.

When documenting training data, some express uncertainty about the boundaries of what can be shared as their datasets might contain unlicensed data. P6’s experience illustrates this uncertainty around legal boundaries:
“some of my training data is copyrighted… [which means that] for research purposes, you can use the dataset, but if you distribute it, you basically can get into legal issues.”
As a result, developers often chose to limit their documentation as a strategy for managing legal risks. For example, while they might include basic information, such as dataset size, they deliberately withhold more detailed information that might reveal any legal violations associated with their data usage.

For participants working in industry, they expressed uncertainty about how much they can disclose regarding proprietary datasets, as companies increasingly consider data as a valuable form of intellectual property. P9 articulates how withholding certain details of their datasets can be a means of defending a competitive advantage:
“if you release the dataset… it might be a little bit too open for our competitors to build a competing product”
. He further mentioned some cases where they had to take the dataset out of the open-source environment, once they realized the dataset could be monetized by others and used for commercial purpose:
“the thing is even though we are making it some of the applications open, it doesn’t necessarily mean that the company would be forgoing all the monetary benefits out of it.”

We also observed that some participants feel uncertain about how much they should provide when documenting considerations around the model’s risks, biases, and limitations, as this conflicts with marketing interests. This uncertainty arises from competing pressures: marketing considerations push developers to emphasize their models’ strengths and potential, while ethical considerations demand transparent and holistic disclosure of risks and limitations. For instance, when interviewing P3 about why he did not include detailed limitations in his documentation, he expressed his perception on the open-ended capabilities of GenAI models:
“I think it’s understood that large language models are non-deterministic in nature, right?”
He then implied ,
“why would anyone want to share their weaknesses (when you can selectively praise the model’s potential)?”
Similarly, some participants acknowledge that they could conduct safety evaluations, but choose not to prioritize them, instead favoring marketable metrics. P1, who is working on a startup branding itself as providing lightweight and efficient GenAI models, admitted that he is limiting the reporting on bias and limitations:
“I think it is technically possible to measure hallucinations or bias… I believe there are some benchmarks…”
. However, he indicated that his company’s focus on marketable efficiency metrics took precedence:
“We are first to focus on efficiency and then we can do more things about whether these models could say something which is wrong…”

These uncertainties and participants’ resulting conservative disclosure practices reflect a broader challenge in the current GenAI ecosystem: while market dynamics drive rapid model development and deployment, documentation standards have not yet evolved to provide sufficient granularity to guide complex disclosure decisions. While existing documentation guidelines specify what aspects to report, they often lack practical guidance on navigating competing interests and risks in different contexts. As a result, developers typically default to minimal disclosure as the safest approach, though this risk-averse strategy may ultimately hinder the development of responsible GenAI documentation practices. We explore the implications of this documentation gap and potential solutions in Section
5.3
.

4.2.4.
Uncertainties around What Model Characteristics are Feasible to Measure and Report Faithfully.

Finally, participants also expressed uncertainty about which model characteristics are feasible to measure and report. We find that these perceptions can lead to documentation decisions that prioritize reporting established, technically tractable measurements while potentially overlooking their limitations. This is influenced by two factors: whether they perceive the characteristic as technically feasible to evaluate and whether there exist “legitimate” (i.e., widely adopted) benchmarks for measurement. P7’s approach to choosing what to report demonstrates this consideration: he chose to report his vision-language model’s performance on Fairface
(
Karkkainen and Joo 2021
)
, an established benchmark for detecting gender, race and age bias, emphasizing the technical feasibility of the test
“it is pretty easy to run the evaluation – you just need to integrate that into the code”
, and how it is widely adopted:
“it was used by other people, so that in this way you can compare your model against them.”
However, when faced with hallucinations in vision-language models, he views it as not documentable because he
‘’rarely sees companies using’ such benchmarks”
and therefore perceives it a
“very hard problem to solve”
in his domain knowledge of vision-language models.

Similarly, P3 demonstrated a pragmatic approach to those decisions based on perceived technical feasibility and quantifiability. As he stated:
“if one were to pick a factor, I would pick whichever is the easiest and most quantifiable to document.”
P3 considers some of the model’s reliability as worthy of documentation because these factors are
“studied really nicely and there already exist quantitative measures for it,”
while viewing model characteristics such as toxicity or bias as
“way too hard”
to document because
“bias and toxicity are like sarcasm. Sarcasm across cultures is different. it is just multidimensional… there is cultural angle to it, there’s geographic angle to it… race could also become a problem.”
Although research shows that toxicity and bias can be measured across cultures
(
Davani et al. 2024
)
, developers’ documentation decisions for such evaluations appear driven more by perceived feasibility than by the actual importance of these risks.

This approach reveals a concerning disconnect between what participants consider feasible to report and what constitutes a comprehensive documentation of model capabilities and limitations. Participants often prioritize “documentability” based on the availability of quantifiable measurements or established benchmarks, while dismissing complex socio-technical issues as too challenging to document. This narrow focus risks producing superficial documentation that overlooks important model characteristics that could have significant societal impact. We discuss strategies for shifting these perceptions and establishing more meaningful practices in Section
5.1
.

4.3.
How: Process-Level Uncertainties in Model Documentation

Beyond struggling to decide what content to include, developers also face process-level challenges in model documentation. In many cases, even if they know what to document, they may still feel uncertain in how to document it.

4.3.1.
Uncertainties in How to Report Intended and Unintended Model Use Cases

Developers struggle with a fundamental documentation challenge: defining and reporting the scope of potential uses for their GenAI models. This challenge stems from two key factors: the inherent non-deterministic nature of GenAI outputs and these models’ broad applicability across different contexts. The challenge is further amplified in open-source environments, where developers have limited visibility into how their models are actually deployed downstream. Faced with these uncertainties, we found that developers typically respond in one of two ways: either they seek guidance from domain specialists such as legal advisors to help establish boundaries around appropriate use, or they provide minimal documentation about intended and unintended use cases expecting downstream users to be accountable for the model impacts.

When discussing documentation of intended use cases, developers highlighted the primary challenge posed by the open-ended, general-purpose nature of GenAI models, which makes it difficult to establish clear boundaries around appropriate use cases, as
“there are too many different possibilities.”
P9 illustrated this uncertainty by comparing GenAI to his previous work on a traditional ML model for empathy detection from text:
“In that instance, you can pretty much say… the use of the model can be determined properly.”
He contrasted this experience with the attempt to clearly define intended use with generative systems:
“Think about the large language models, it’s much more generalized. You can’t specifically say it should be used for this purpose.”

Defining unintended use cases was equally challenging for developers, especially on open-source platforms. With limited tools to comprehensively assess potential misuse scenarios, identifying unintended uses often relies on subjective intuition. According to P9, an industry practitioner, he has limited visibility into the downstream use cases in which the models can be mistakenly used, as the platform is open and
“basically everyone can download it…it is much more [of] a speculative guess of how [the model] could ever be misused.”
He noted that while he did attempt to get some insight into potential misuse through internal testing with his colleagues, this informal approach did not feel sufficiently systematic or comprehensive. As a result, he often turned to legal consultants in his company because they:
“told me that sometimes people misuse it in certain ways, which may not in line with what I’m thinking in the first place.”

4.3.2.
Uncertainties in How to Report Model Capabilities

When documenting models’ capabilities, participants expressed that they often feel uncertain about how to accurately report them due to the limitations of existing benchmarks. As a result, they often choose to rely on some “proxy metrics,” despite concerns about their actual usefulness.

For participants who work on foundation models, they shared that the lack of industry-wide standard evaluation benchmarks creates uncertainty about how to present model capabilities in a transparent and comparable way. As P13, a foundation model developer, noted:
“everyone tests their model on different tasks.”
He described how some competitors selectively report results for specific metrics (e.g., only using IFEval’s loose criteria while omitting the strict criteria
2
2

2

IFEval is a benchmark to evaluate a language model’s capability to follow instructions
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
. It has two different metrics: strict accuracy checks if instructions are followed exactly, while loose accuracy is more flexible and allows for small formatting differences.
), potentially creating misleading comparisons:
“it’s difficult to present [results] in a fair way.”
Some also worried that existing benchmarks may become inadequate due to data or concept drift
3
3

3

Data drift refers to changes in the distribution of input data over time, after a model has been deployed
. For instance, P1 mentioned that static benchmarks cannot adequately capture the model’s performance on dynamic data when models are deployed in real-world settings:
“even if there is one questioning-answering dataset, maybe later in 10 years there will be different test datasets… there will be a data shift, then the LLM will not be good anymore.”

Similarly to participants who are foundation model developers, those who fine-tune models for a specific purpose also feel uncertain about how to report the model’s capability because benchmarks often do not exist for the specific use cases they care about. That is, even if they
do
know what the specific use case is for their model, they are still uncertain about how to report it given limitations in the available evaluation methods. P6, who develops GenAI for storytelling, highlighted how existing standardized, generalized evaluation metrics such as Question Answering (QA) benchmarks, became meaningless in his model’s use case:
“We want to test our models on how well they can write certain content and how good that content is. And that’s more difficult to do with existing dataset, as there is no good benchmark for storytelling”
. He can only conduct the evaluation by observing output using the same prompt to see if the model has improved. As a result, he chose to omit standardized benchmark results from his model card, explaining:
“why do I need it? You don’t need math QA benchmarks for storywriting.”

Faced with a dearth of appropriate specific benchmarks on how to report model capability, many chose instead to rely on “proxy metrics” – widely used benchmarks that provide a general understanding of the model’s overall capabilities (e.g., reasoning). Despite the fact that P1 didn’t entirely trust the leaderboard benchmarks because they
“[don’t] give a full complete view on the LLM capacity”
, he still thought such metrics could prove useful in giving users
“some first insights about the model’s capability”
. These metrics could at least give users a general impression of, for example,
“Is that a very bad LLM or good LLM?”

4.3.3.
Uncertainties in How to Report Model Risks, Biases, and Limitations

Beyond the challenges of reporting model capabilities, participants also struggle with systematically identifying, measuring, and documenting biases and limitations in their models. While some developers may realize the importance of reporting such issues, they struggle with how to communicate them, particularly without a background or training in relevant concepts or evaluation methods..

The complexity of such safety assessments often requires specialized expertise that many development teams lack. P4 described how his team’s documentation of bias, risks, and limitations relied heavily on support from their organization’s ethics team:
“There was the ethics team that reached out to us and helped us write the section.”
This ethics team conducted specialized evaluations such as red teaming exercises,
“trying to jailbreak it to generate offensive content.”
Notably, P4 pointed out that that section is the only section
“not written by developers,”
suggesting specialized knowledge was needed to complete the report. While developers may recognize inherent model limitations – as P4 noted,
“we know that all models have problems when generating bad content”
– they often lack the training and expertise to conduct thorough safety evaluations. As P4 said,
“It’s difficult to evaluate it. People building the models, they are not necessarily trained for this task. They don’t really know where to start.”

Without such specialized support, participants face significant challenges in reporting comprehensive safety assessments. P4, who develops GenAI models in an academic research group, highlighted how the absence of systematic evaluation processes and dedicated safety teams particularly impacts their ability to assess emergent issues like hallucinations. While his team attempted to evaluate risks through informal testing akin to red-teaming – i.e., having team members interact with the model to identify problematic outputs – P4 thinks there are limitations of this approach. He emphasized the need for systematic processes to quantify and characterize issues, noting they need to understand
“what sort of hallucination [occurs], how often it happens.”
Without structured evaluation, P4 explained their documentation could only include general warnings, such as stating that
“these models are just basically known to be prone to hallucination and you should not rely on it”
and expecting users to
“have that in the back of their mind when they’re using it.”

4.4.
Who: Responsibility-Level Uncertainties in Model Documentation

In addition to deciding “what to report” and “how to report,” we find that participants also face multiple uncertainties regarding “who should report” – that is, who should take the documentation responsibilities, including reporting biases, describing model capabilities across different deployment contexts, and providing guidelines for appropriate use.

4.4.1.
Uncertainties in Who Should Report Bias

Some expressed uncertainty about who should report bias in their models, partially due to the complicated network of actors involved in GenAI model development. For example, some participants acknowledged that GenAI model biases stem from the training data, as P6 noted:
“we all know the biases coming from the datasets.”
This uncertainty led to a perception among our participants that, when datasets are made publicly available, it was unclear whether they themselves were responsible for evaluating and reporting biases present in those datasets.
For instance, P11, despite acknowledging potential gender biases where
“the model may treat different genders differently,”
felt that with published datasets and open-source models, explicit reporting results from evaluation of biases was unnecessary:
“We don’t have to (report the bias) because we already know what is being introduced or not with the (published) dataset. I don’t do that and my colleagues don’t do that.”
He further speculated that an unnamed third-party was likely already responsible for conducting bias evaluations and reporting:
“there might be people who are doing it for specific things.”
This illustrates how GenAI developers are shifting responsibility for assessing the model onto other actors in the supply chain, either upstream to foundational model providers, or downstream to model users, creating gaps in accountability and transparency.

4.4.2.
Uncertainties in Who Should Report Model Performance Across Different Deployment Contexts

A key documentation challenge participants face stems from uncertainty about who should be responsible for evaluating and reporting model performance in specific deployment contexts. Some acknowledge that model performance can vary significantly across those contexts:
“even the model efficiency can change”
(P1), yet they question whether they should be responsible in documenting model performance there.

This uncertainty is especially pronounced in open-source environments. Participants expressed greater concerns about their documentation responsibilities in these contexts, compared to customer-facing deployments, where developers create GenAI models tailored for specific customer requirements. For customer-facing deployments, the intended use environment and requirements are more established, leading to developers taking more responsibility in documentation. As P1, who worked on both customer-facing model and open GenAI models explained,
“for customer-specific models, we really need to do more extensive evaluation to prove to our customers our model can perform well in some critical use cases they care about”
. In contrast, open-source models, lacking a predefined use context, typically rely on
“some standard benchmark”
(P1) for documentation, as developers feel it’s impractical to evaluate and document performance for all possible deployment scenarios. This uncertainty often leads developers to shift the responsibility for thorough model validation to downstream developers using their models in various applications. For example, P1 shared that in their documentation’s evaluation section, they explicitly wrote that if the downstream users of the models
“want to see the quality of the model”
, they should
“recheck [the model’s performance] in the final use case that you would like to try”
. While this approach aligns with the growing consensus that model performance must be understood within its specific deployment context
(
Weidinger et al. 2023
)
, it also highlights how the diffusion of responsibility could become a potential failure mode in GenAI model documentation.

4.4.3.
Uncertainties in Who Should be Accountable for Documenting Appropriate Use

Related to the uncertainties we identified in Section
4.3.1
, we find here that some participants are not willing to document appropriate use, as they question whether this might unintentionally make them accountable for cases of misuse. For instance, P6 questioned the liability issue, stating that when the content generated by GenAI is being misused,
“who’s liable? Is it the person who creates the model or is the person actually uses the model that’s liable?”
He stated his preference for an MIT-style license, a type of open-source software license that absolves developers of liability for any harm caused by the software
(
Open Source Initiative 2024
)
:
“For AI models, I want a really broad MIT style license that basically says this model can be used, produced, and the owner does not want to take any responsibility for whatever you do with it.”
He further questioned if model documentation can do anything about the unintended use cases:
“The documentation doesn’t prevent any risk. The risk is mainly who’s going to use it. I don’t have any control over who is abusing it.”
Similarly, some try to walk around the uncertainty in who should be accountable for unintended use cases by using legal disclaimers including generic copy-pasted statement, rather than engaging meaningfully with model-specific misuse and inappropriate use cases. For example, P5 shared his experience in how his team wrote up a “disclaimer” section and copy-pasted it to every model card they produced:

“just in case [there are risky use cases] people would know that we are not liable for anything—we just provide the files.”
In other words, some developers sought to address the uncertainties in who is liable for misuse cases by advocating for an approach that shifts accountability for appropriate use to downstream users, often leading to incomplete reporting.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
Cultivating Community
Norms and Culture in Open-Source For Meaningful GenAI Model Documentation

Our findings revealed that developers grapple with the question of what to document when deciding which content to include in their documentation artifacts, despite existing resources
(
Mitchell et al. 2018
;
Face 2024a
)
and ongoing efforts in measuring GenAI models transparency in research community
(
Bommasani et al. 2023
)
. Our work builds on prior studies examining practitioners’ challenges with ML documentation in applied settings
(
Chang and Custis 2022
;
Heger et al. 2022
)
by addressing challenges unique to documentation practices among open GenAI model developers as they adapt to this paradigm shift. We observed that many developers currently approach AI documentation similarly to traditional code documentation, primarily focusing on technical specifications and implementation details that facilitate code reuse. However, such approaches are insufficient to address the broad range of considerations necessary to manage GenAI risks, which are
socio
-technical in nature
(
Weidinger et al. 2023
, cf.)
.

Notably, even on platforms like Hugging Face that have taken a leading role on providing responsible documentation guidelines compared to other open-source platforms
(
Face 2024a
;
Gorwa and Veale 2023
)
, our study suggested that developers still feel uncertain about whether and how to adhere to these guidelines. Previous work has discussed how ML practitioners often focus on the transparency needs of ML systems based on their own intuition rather than considering the intended audience’s needs, leading to calls for a broader sociotechnical understanding of AI transparency
(
Ehsan et al. 2021
;
Chang and Custis 2022
)
. Similarly, our findings revealed developers’ tendency to assume intended audiences consist of technical users, leading to their documentation focusing on technical re-usability other than socio-technical considerations. This reflects a deeper cultural challenge within the open-source development community, where the requirements and expectations of RAI clash with established open-source norms and beliefs.

This cultural challenge is particularly evident in developers’ tendency to prioritize documenting easily measurable and quantifiable aspects of GenAI development. Prior work has examined the history and culture of evaluations of supervised ML, where model performance is primarily evaluated through performance-based metrics
(
Orr and Kang 2024
)
, eschewing measurements of seemingly abstract qualities
(
Hutchinson et al. 2022
, cf.)
. However, researchers in the FAccT community have argued that AI developers
can
operationalize abstract, unobservable constructs like fairness into concrete measurements by drawing on techniques in the social sciences
(
Jacobs and Wallach 2019
;
Van der Wal et al. 2024
)
. Despite these efforts in the FAccT research community, our findings indicate that developers are not incentivized to adopt these approaches to measure and document model bias, risks, and limitations, since these techniques fall outside of their existing disciplinary training and expertise.

Therefore, we urge open-source platforms to offer developers resources that encourage a more sociotechnical approach to model documentation, helping them better align their practices with the broader considerations required to manage GenAI risks effectively. By providing examples of “best practices” for GenAI model documentation and other resources, open-source platforms can support developers’ ongoing learning about responsible AI documentation specifically and risk management practices more generally
(
Madaio et al. 2024
, cf.)
.

5.2.
Building Robust Evaluation Infrastructures For Effective GenAI Model Documentation

Our findings reveal that developers often feel uncertain about how to document GenAI-specific risks and harms due to the lack of established evaluation methods, in addition to the cultural and disciplinary norms previously described. When developers do recognize the importance of documenting model capabilities and limitations like hallucinations and toxicity, they still struggle with how to systematically evaluate and report these characteristics in their documentation. This suggests that improving model documentation practices requires not just better documentation templates or guidelines, but also robust, accessible evaluation infrastructure that can support developers in generating meaningful content for their documentation. We have seen promising attempts by regulatory entities to develop testing infrastructures for foundation models, such as the open evaluation package of the UK AI Safety Institute open evaluation package
(
AI Safety Institute [n. d.]
)
. Open-source platforms like Hugging Face are also making strides in providing evaluation support, such as the open-source LLM leaderboard
(
Face 2024b
)
. Recognizing these ongoing efforts, our findings implicate several key considerations for the development of such infrastructures.

First, model documentation guidelines often require holistic and transparent disclosure of models’ risks and limitations. However, our findings suggest that, even aside from challenges when documenting evaluation results, conducting comprehensive safety assessments requires significant resources, which may be a barrier for low-resource organizations or individual developers. To ensure that safety documentation is meaningful and feasible across the board, we need to develop evaluation methodologies and frameworks that enable low-resource safety assessments to still provide valuable insights. This could involve creating standardized templates, checklists, or question prompts that guide developers through the process of identifying and documenting potential risks, even in the absence of extensive testing resources.

Second, model documentation needs testing infrastructure that provides support for sociotechnical evaluation of models. Our findings reveal that developers particularly struggle with having adequate resources and knowledge to evaluate models at a sociotechnical level. We anticipate that the growth of testing infrastructure will help address not only the question of how to report evaluation results, but also provide resources to establish a better understanding of what is measurable and documentable in the first place.

5.3.
Clarifying Roles and Responsibilities For Actionable GenAI Model Documentation

A significant uncertainty developers face when documenting GenAI models is to determine who should document them. Prior research highlights this challenge as part of the “many hands” problem in AI accountability, where the complex network of actors involved in developing and deploying AI systems disperses responsibility, leading to gaps and inconsistencies in practices
(
Nissenbaum 1996
;
Cooper et al. 2022
)
. The issue is further compounded by the complex supply chain dynamics of developing GenAI systems in which upstream pretrained models are adapted to specific tasks by downstream actors
(
Widder and Nafus 2023
;
Cobbe et al. 2023
)
. Within this supply chain, both upstream and downstream deployers influence the model’s behavior and impact, yet their roles and responsibilities for documentation remain unclear. This ambiguity contributes to “dislocated accountability”
(
Widder and Nafus 2023
)
, where no individual or entity clearly assumes responsibility for the risks of AI systems, regardless of their origin.

Our research explores how dislocated accountability manifests itself in the documentation practices of GenAI developers, particular in the context of open-source. Our findings extend the existing literature on ethical considerations in open-source development.
Widder et al. 2022
’s work examined ethical considerations in open-source AI development, particularly focusing on “Freedom 0”—the right to use code for any purpose—and how it contributes to developer’s struggle balancing between open-source values and ethical concerns. Our findings show how the broad downstream scenarios of GenAI models further complicates this dynamic—developers feel even less able to anticipate or control harmful uses, despite the potential for greater harm. This creates a “responsibility paradox”: although multiple actors are responsible for the model and the risks it poses, no single entity assumes comprehensive responsibility for documenting and communicating those risks.

Policymakers and open-source platforms can help “interrupt the AI supply chain”
(
Kapania et al. 2024
)
and address dislocated accountability
(
Widder and Nafus 2023
)
by clarifying roles and responsibilities for documenting GenAI models. For example, foundation model developers could document pre-training data, model architecture, and pre-deployment evaluation methods, while downstream deployers could focus on documenting performance in context-specific tasks. Clearly defining documentation responsibilities ensures that the capabilities, limitations, and risks associated with models are properly captured at each stage of development, providing downstream actors with the context needed to manage emerging risks effectively.

Platforms, which play a crucial role in establishing and institutionalizing norms of practice, should also implement mechanisms for collaborative documentation while maintaining clear accountability. Early steps toward these goals are evident in policymaking, such as the initial drafts of the EU AI Act Code of Practice (CoP)
(
AIA [n. d.]
)
. However, further recommendations on best practices are needed to address implementation gaps not covered by existing policy frameworks and to ensure coverage for models that fall outside their scope. For example, while the CoP establishes baseline documentation requirements for general-purpose AI models, our findings suggest the opportunity to develop clearer guidance on documentation responsibilities for downstream developers who fine-tuning or distilling the general-purpose model.

6.
Conclusion

Model documentation has played a crucial role for fostering transparency and promoting responsible AI development. Despite its growing significance in the age of GenAI, little is known about how GenAI developers approach documentation, particularly on open-source platforms, which have increasingly become central hubs for hosting and distributing these models. Through interviews with 13 GenAI developers on open-source platforms, we identified key uncertainties developers face regarding what to document, how to document, and who should document. Building on our findings, we discuss implications for addressing these uncertainties and supporting meaningful, effective, and actionable documentation practices in the evolving landscape of GenAI.

7.
Limitations

Our study has several methodological limitations worth acknowledging. First, despite extensive recruitment efforts, including reaching out to developers of over 100 GenAI models on HuggingFace and contacting larger corporations, we faced significant recruitment challenges that have been documented in other responsible AI research
(
Deng et al. 2022
)
. Future work can expand our sample to include more developers across different organizational contexts and experience levels to further validate and refine our understanding of documentation uncertainties. Second, our study was geographically limited to participants from the US and EU due to IRB restrictions, potentially missing important perspectives from developers in other regions where open-source GenAI development is also active. While our purposive sampling approach aimed to capture diverse roles and contexts within these constraints, future work would benefit from expanded geographical representation to better understand how documentation practices might vary across different cultural and regulatory environments.

Ethical Considerations Statement

We took several measures to protect our research participants. Our study design was reviewed and approved by our organization’s Institutional Review Board. Participation was voluntary. 10.
Adverse Impacts Statement

A significant limitation of our study is its exclusive focus on developer perspectives. By only examining documentation practices through the lens of developers, our findings may inadvertently privilege their viewpoints and concerns over those of other crucial stakeholders. This could lead to recommendations that undervalue the documentation needs of affected communities, downstream users, policymakers, and civil society organizations. While we try to carefully stay in distance and examine developers’ perspectives, there is still a risk that our findings could be used to justify documentation practices that serve developer interests while failing to address the broader societal implications of GenAI systems.

Our research could contribute to an over-reliance on documentation as a solution to AI governance challenges. While comprehensive documentation is important, it should not be seen as a substitute for other crucial aspects of responsible AI development such as careful testing, ongoing monitoring, and meaningful stakeholder engagement. There is a risk that our findings could be used to justify a "checkbox approach" to model documentation that prioritizes form over substance.
