An ultrasonic inspection method for diffusion-welded components with variable curvature and thickness
Authors: Lichen Teng, Wenmin Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomated ultrasonic inspection of diffusion-welded components with variable curvature and thickness is challenging due to difficulties in focusing acoustic waves on curved weld interfaces and the small size of defects, especially when accurate CAD models are unavailable. This study aims to develop a methodology for reliable inspection of such components. An integrated system combining laser profile scanning and multi-axis robotic ultrasonic testing is developed, synchronization among surface conformal data (surface-fitting information describing the geometry of the scanned surface), ultrasonic signals, and robot motion states is resolved in the proposed system design. Adaptive ultrasonic scanning trajectories are generated based on laser profiles to accommodate unknown curved surfaces. Ultrasonic field simulations determine suitable transducer parameters, and the effects of surface curvature and scanning trajectory accuracy on defect echoes are analyzed, ensuring the reliability of inspection results. Multi-axis robotic C-scan experiments are conducted on titanium alloy diffusion-welded curved blade specimens. The proposed methodology enables automated inspection of complex curved diffusion welds. Experimental results show effective detection of internal defects, with improved inspection stability and reliability. This study introduces an integrated laser–ultrasonic inspection approach that explicitly addresses the synchronization of multi-source data and enables adaptive scanning on unknown curved surfaces, providing a reliable approach for ultrasonic inspection of complex curved diffusion-welded components.Keywords: Ultrasonic testing, Complex diffusion, Diffusion bonding defects, Variable curvature, Variable thicknessSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Diffusion bonding is a solid-state joining technique in which two properly prepared surfaces are bonded at elevated temperatures under an applied pressure1. Owing to the intrinsic complexity of the diffusion bonding process, defects may arise at the bonding interface due to factors such as inadequate surface finish, surface contamination, or improper selection of bonding parameters. Typical interfacial defects, including unbonded defects and micro-voids, can severely degrade the mechanical performance of diffusion-bonded TC4 (Ti-6Al-4 V) alloy components2, particularly in critical aerospace applications such as hollow aero-engine blades3–5. Given the stringent requirements for structural reliability and operational safety in the aerospace industry, the development of reliable non-destructive testing (NDT) techniques for TC4 diffusion-bonded joints is of paramount importance.

Among the available NDT methods, ultrasonic testing has demonstrated superior sensitivity for the inspection of diffusion-bonded joints compared with X-ray-based techniques6. In particular, ultrasonic C-scan inspection has become one of the most widely adopted approaches for evaluating diffusion-bonded interfaces7. This technique enables effective visualization of interfacial defects and their spatial distribution, thereby allowing correlations to be established between ultrasonic reflection characteristics and the bonding strength of the interface. Recent studies have further explored ultrasonic inspection of complex-shaped surfaces, highlighting methods for adaptive ultrasonic inspection and advanced imaging algorithms8–10.

Extensive studies have investigated the application of ultrasonic C-scan techniques to diffusion-bonded structures. Kumar et al.11 employed immersion ultrasonic testing to inspect cylindrical Ti-6Al-4 V diffusion-bonded specimens, using the ratio of the interface echo amplitude to the surface echo amplitude to characterize bonding quality across different regions. Lavrentyeva et al.12 introduced an interfacial stiffness coefficient to quantitatively relate the ultrasonic reflection coefficient to the microscopic dimensions of the bonding interface. Liu et al.13 utilized high-frequency ultrasonic transducers for the inspection of thin titanium alloy plates and demonstrated that increasing the inspection frequency is effective for enhancing the detectability of diffusion bonding defects. Edwill et al.14 exploited ultrasonic nonlinear effects in the vicinity of diffusion bonding defects and successfully detected a defect located 12.7 mm beneath the specimen surface. Luan et al.15 conducted ultrasonic inspections of TC4 diffusion-bonded circular specimens using transducers with different frequencies and concluded that accurate defect detection requires appropriate transducer selection in conjunction with advanced signal-processing techniques. Nevertheless, despite the substantial body of work on ultrasonic detection of diffusion bonding defects, studies focusing on diffusion-bonded components with complex or free-form surface geometries remain scarce. Although these studies have significantly advanced ultrasonic inspection of diffusion bonding defects, most of the existing work has primarily focused on transducer parameter optimization and the inspection of planar diffusion-bonded components, while relatively limited attention has been devoted to defect detection in diffusion-bonded structures with complex surface geometries.

This study proposes an ultrasonic non-destructive testing method specifically designed for diffusion-bonded components with curved surfaces. By developing an integrated inspection system that combines laser profile scanning and ultrasonic testing, coordinated motion control, inspection path planning, and ultrasonic inspection of complex surface geometries are achieved. Based on the geometric characteristics of the tested components and the inspection requirements, ultrasonic wave fields under different transducer parameter configurations are numerically computed to optimize the selection range of transducer parameters. Furthermore, using the transducer parameters adopted in the experiments, the critical transducer deflection angles and normal offset distances corresponding to different surface curvatures are systematically calculated, providing a theoretical basis to ensure the reliability and stability of ultrasonic inspection results for curved diffusion-bonded components.

Surface geometry reconstruction via laser profile scanning

To achieve accurate and efficient complex surface reconstruction, a laser profile scanner is used to project a laser line onto the target and capture the reflected contour on a sensor matrix, from which the contour points are extracted after ambient light suppression and centroid calculation, as shown in Fig. 3.

The measurement principle of laser profile scanner.

Once the receiving position of the measurement point on the sensing matrix is determined, the coordinates of the point can be calculated based on the principles of Laser Line Triangulation (LLT)17. As illustrated in Fig. 4, for the measurement point Pi, the horizontal distance(y-axis) between the sensor element receiving its reflected signal and the origin point (at the light source) is ai. The vertical (z-axis) distance between the sensor element and the origin point is ci. The angle between incident laser and reflected laser at this point is αi. Laser with different angles is received by corresponding element in sensor matrix. By querying the position of the receiving element, we can get the value of ai, ci and αi.

As shown in Fig. 4, the laser profile scanner acquires the (x, z) coordinates of points along a contour in the x-direction during each scan. The ultrasonic transducer and scanner are interchanged via a tool quick-change system on a six-axis robot. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the scanner is mounted on the robot end effector to perform 3D profiling of complex surfaces, with the y-axis as the scanning axis and the x-axis as the stepping axis. The measured contours are synchronized with the robot’s trigger positions to form a laser point cloud, referenced to the scanner’s original coordinate at the first scanning point.

A3D scanning diagram of laser profile scanner.

Following the laser scanning process, the collected laser point cloud undergoes post-processing in commercial software such as CATIA18 or within a secondary development environment utilizing Open CASCADE19. The CAD model reconstruction process based on the laser point cloud is illustrated in Fig. 6. The raw laser point cloud contains noise and irrelevant data from fixtures and surface artifacts, which are first manually removed to retain only the target surface. A radius-based filtering method (radius 0.4 mm, minimum 10 neighbors) is then applied to suppress residual reflection noise, followed by voxel-based resampling with a voxel size of 0.3 mm to reduce data volume by 40–60% while preserving geometric features. Holes and protrusions in the resulting mesh are filled and smoothed to improve surface continuity. Finally, the processed mesh is reconstructed into a surface model and exported as a *.stp file, providing a complete and accurate geometric representation for subsequent ultrasonic C-scan planning.

The calculation of the ultrasonic scanning trajectory is achieved by discretizing the surface within the defined scanning area. The u and v parameters of the testing surface are discretized to generate discrete points. These points are then relocated by one lift-off distance (water path length) along the normal direction and organized in sequence to form the scanning trajectory of the ultrasonic transducer center, as depicted in Fig. 8.

Trajectory planning begins by discretizing the surface according to a predefined scanning mode, such as zigzag or spiral. The distance between adjacent track points is defined as the scanning step. When employing trajectory planning for complex surfaces, the use of an isoparametric method for surface discretization can lead to unequal distances between adjacent trajectory points, thereby distorting the resulting C-scan image. To address this issue, the implementation of an equal arc length discretization method21 ensures a uniform distribution of trajectory points, thereby enhancing imaging quality. This study utilizes a range of surface and curve discretization techniques offered by Open CASCADE. Initially, an isoparametric line in the scanning direction is extracted from the surface at each step interval. Subsequently, each isoparametric line is discret.

Synchronization and control method for surface conformal data, ultrasonic signals, and motion states

Accurate acquisition and processing of inspection data are essential for both laser profile scanning and multi-axis actuator–based ultrasonic C-scan inspection, with a key challenge being the correct association of measured geometric or ultrasonic data with the corresponding mechanical system positions. For both laser profile and ultrasonic data, accurate data association requires position signals from the robot controller to trigger the laser scanner or the ultrasonic acquisition board. However, standard industrial robot I/O cannot directly provide the encoder-compatible signals required by these devices, which typically accept only two channels corresponding to the scanning and stepping axes. To address this mismatch, a microcontroller-based interface is introduced to convert the robot’s position-related I/O signals into encoder signals recognized by the laser scanner and ultrasonic board, thereby enabling synchronized data acquisition and improving system flexibility. To synchronize laser profile data with mechanical positions, a microcontroller-based encoder triggering method is developed. As shown in Fig. 9, the laser profile scanner communicates with the host computer via Ethernet for command transmission and data transfer, while its multifunction interface provides power and trigger signals. This interface includes 11 pins, as illustrated in Fig. 9, and enables precise synchronization between profile acquisition and mechanical position feedback.

Schematic of the position feedback synchronization method.

During curved-surface profiling, the robot carries a laser profile scanner along a predefined trajectory, while position trigger signals are sent to a microcontroller that converts robot I/O into encoder signals for the scanner. Each trigger initiates one profile acquisition, and the buffered data are transmitted to the host computer, where a dedicated thread retrieves the profiles in real time until the scan is completed.

Ultrasonic data acquisition is performed using an ultrasonic pulser/receiver and a high-speed data acquisition card. For multi-axis C-scan inspection, synchronization between ultrasonic signals and motion states is achieved through triggering strategies, including internal and external triggering. Internal triggering operates at a fixed pulse repetition frequency and relies on host-side indexing, leading to higher overhead and lower efficiency. In contrast, external triggering uses robot motion–driven encoder signals to trigger acquisition and directly associate ultrasonic data with C-scan positions, providing more efficient and accurate synchronization.

In the proposed system, the ultrasonic board is triggered by the data acquisition card, with its trigger and signal outputs connected to the synchronization and analog input channels of the card, respectively. A microcontroller-based encoder generation scheme is adopted to utilize the built-in A- and B-phase encoder inputs of the acquisition card. Robot I/O signals trigger the microcontroller, which outputs encoder signals to synchronize both laser profile and ultrasonic data with the robot positions. This unified framework enables accurate data association and reliable inspection of curved diffusion-bonded components, as shown in Figs. 10 and 11.

Experimental setup

The hardware architecture of the ultrasonic testing experimental system for complex surfaces, developed in this study, is presented in Fig. 12. The RX160 six-axis robot serves as the scanning mechanism, supporting both the ultrasonic transducer and the laser profile scanner. A tool fast-conversion system enables quick switching under the same reference coordinate system. The laser profiling module utilizes the LLT2900 line laser profile scanner, while the high-frequency ultrasonic system comprises the PRC50 ultrasonic card and the AL12260 data acquisition board. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the scanned images, both the laser profiling and ultrasonic scanning processes are externally triggered by the robot controller, based on pulse signals emitted from the current scanning position. The software interface, independently developed in C + + , facilitates functions such as laser profiling, ultrasonic scanning trajectory planning, testing scheme settings, and multi-view ultrasonic imaging.

Effect of scanning trajectory accuracy on ultrasonic detection sensitivity

During ultrasonic inspection of curved diffusion-bonded components, detection sensitivity can be degraded by manipulator trajectory deviations and transducer misalignment relative to the surface normal. Since higher surface curvature reduces echo amplitudes, detection errors caused by incidence deviation and normal misalignment were evaluated over a curvature range of − 0.05 to 0.05mm−1, providing a basis for maintaining adequate sensitivity under realistic inspection conditions.

(1) Effect of transducer incidence angle on ultrasonic detection sensitivity.

The simulation parameters used were the same as those listed in Table 1. The effect of varying transducer incidence angles on defect responses was systematically analyzed. Taking a curvature of ± 0.5 mm⁻1 as an example, the defect responses for ultrasonic waves incident on concave and convex surfaces at different angles are presented in Figs. 21 and 22, respectively. As shown, with increasing incidence angle, the echo amplitude of the defects gradually decreases for both concave and convex surfaces, with the convex surface being more significantly affected.

Defect echo response curves on convex surfaces under varying transducer incidence angles.

Defect echo response curves on concave surfaces under varying transducer incidence angles.

To quantitatively evaluate the influence of transducer incidence angle on the echo amplitude, a critical incidence angle was defined as the angle at which the defect echo amplitude drops to 5% of its nominal value. From the figures, for a curvature of ± 0.5 mm⁻1, the critical incidence angle was found to be 2.5° for the convex surface and 5° for the concave surface. To further investigate the relationship between surface curvature and allowable incidence deviation, critical incidence angles were simulated for different curvature values, and the results were plotted as a curve in Fig. 23. It can be observed that concave surfaces tolerate larger angular deviations than convex surfaces. Therefore, ensuring the scanning trajectory accuracy so that transducer deflection remains within 2° is beneficial for maintaining reliable detection sensitivity (Fig. 24).

Experimental results

In the experimental system described above, an internal weld testing experiment is conducted on a complex curved specimen with an unknown model to validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The complex specimen exhibits a variable thickness ranging from 1 mm at its thinnest region to 4 mm at its thickest, with an overall geometry characterized by varying curvature. The weld is located near the central region of the specimen. Due to the manufacturing challenges associated with diffusion-welded blades of complex curved surfaces, this specimen was prepared using the preliminary blade prototyping process, which may contain unbonded diffusion weld defects. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, the inspection procedure begins with a robot-mounted laser profile scanner, which is used to capture the surface profile of the specimen and reconstruct its three-dimensional model.

The high-precision laser profiling model of the complex surface has been successfully developed. Based on this reconstructed model, trajectory planning is conducted, and the optimal ultrasonic transducer is selected through ultrasonic field simulation calculations. This approach facilitates extensive ultrasonic C-scan imaging of the weld area on the testing sample. Leveraging the high-precision model obtained via laser profiling, fine scanning imaging is performed in regions with concentrated defects, allowing for accurate positioning and labeling of suspected defects within the model. This provides a solid foundation for nondestructive testing in subsequent processing and for optimizing the welding process.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the ultrasonic inspection of diffusion bonding defects in titanium alloy hollow fan blades and established an integrated laser profiling–ultrasonic C-scan methodology for complex curved components. A laser-based surface reconstruction approach was developed to obtain accurate geometric models of the curved inspection surfaces, enabling automatic generation of equi-arc-length C-scan trajectories based on three-dimensional solid models.

A robotic curved-surface C-scan control framework was implemented, in which trajectory pose data were converted into executable motion commands, and a synchronized control strategy was introduced to ensure precise correspondence between ultrasonic signals and spatial positions. By analyzing the influence of surface curvature on ultrasonic detection sensitivity, defect response curves were established for surfaces with varying curvature, providing a basis for selecting transducer parameters for complex variable-curvature specimens. Furthermore, the effect of scanning trajectory accuracy on detection sensitivity was systematically investigated, and critical transducer deflection angles and maximum allowable normal offsets were determined: concave surfaces allow larger deviation in transducer angle compared with convex surfaces, and for optimal detection, trajectory scanning accuracy should maintain transducer deflection errors within 2°; concave surfaces also permit larger normal offsets than convex surfaces, and trajectory scanning accuracy should ensure a maximum normal deviation within 0.5 mm.

Finally, an experimental platform was constructed and validated on a titanium alloy hollow fan blade, achieving reliable ultrasonic C-scan imaging of diffusion bonding interfaces on complex curved surfaces. The results demonstrate that the proposed integrated profiling and inspection methodology not only provides a robust theoretical and technical framework but also defines quantifiable operational criteria for high-precision ultrasonic evaluation of complex curved diffusion-bonded structures.
