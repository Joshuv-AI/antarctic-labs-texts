Design guidelines for self-healing materials in soft electronics
Authors: Chan Beom Park, Gunho Chang, Jooyeun Chong, Jiheong Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soft electronic devices require durability to endure their inherent exposure to diverse mechanical deformations, including scratches, punctures, and repeated bending. Without intrinsic damage recovery mechanisms, such deformations inevitably compromise mechanical integrity and limit device lifetime. To address this issue, the strategic incorporation of reversible dynamic bonds enables autonomous self-healing while simultaneously achieving high mechanical toughness through energy dissipation during bond rupture. To this end, optimizing the glass transition temperature and bond exchange kinetics is essential to ensure sufficient chain mobility for rapid interfacial diffusion and autonomous mechanical recovery. Building on the reversible bond nature, this review presents emerging self-healable and tough soft electronics applications in three major areas: (1) Multimodal electronic skins capable of comprehensive physiological signal sensing; (2) modularly reconfigurable systems with adhesive-free interlayer bonding that enable user-on-demand device assembly; (3) optoelectronic devices that seamlessly integrate light-emitting and pressure-sensing capabilities. These applications demonstrate that dynamic bond engineering enables elastomeric devices to simultaneously achieve mechanical robustness, functional adaptability, and autonomous self-healing. Such advancements position them as durable platforms with extended operational lifetimes, paving the way for next-generation wearable and implantable bioelectronics in real-world applications.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Elastomers have emerged as a central materials platform for emerging electronic devices that require intimate mechanical integration with soft, deformable, and dynamic environments [1–3]. Their defining characteristics, such as low elastic modulus (typically 100 kPa–10 MPa), high stretchability (often > 500%), and large, reversible deformations, originate from loosely crosslinked polymer networks in which entropic elasticity and weak intermolecular interactions dominate the mechanical response [4–6]. Under mechanical load, polymer chains can reorganize, slide, and reconfigure without catastrophic failure, enabling mechanical compliance that is fundamentally inaccessible to conventional rigid plastics or metals.

These attributes have catalyzed rapid progress in several frontier application areas. In soft robotics, elastomeric actuators and grippers present lifelike, continuum motion, enabling intimate human–robot interaction [7, 8]. Stretchable electronics use elastomeric substrates and encapsulants to accommodate large areal strains without loss of electrical function, enabling skin-mounted health monitors, deformable displays, and conformal sensor arrays that can follow complex skin surfaces [9–11]. Despite their promising potential, most soft material systems suffer from limited durability under realistic operating conditions and pose major sustainability challenges. Soft devices are inherently exposed to mechanical deformation: scratches from rough surfaces, punctures from sharp objects, frictional wear, repeated bending, and stretching at localized regions such as joints (Fig. 1a). These mechanical damages inevitably compromise device functionality and shorten service lifetime. To address these durability challenges, considerable research effort has focused on developing materials that simultaneously possess high toughness to resist damage initiation and fracture, and intrinsic self-healing capability to autonomously restore mechanical integrity after damage (Fig. 1b).Fig. 1Devices undergo diverse deformations during operation. a Mechanical damage typically causes device failure. b Self-healing enables continuous operation under deformation

Self-healing elastomers that can restore their structure and properties after damage via reversible interactions in the bulk have been widely proposed as a solution to the durability and sustainability issues. In such systems, dynamic bonds (e.g., hydrogen bonds, metal–ligand coordination, dynamic covalent linkages such as disulfides, imines, and Diels–Alder adducts) can dissociate and reform. At the same time, polymer chains diffuse across damaged interfaces, allowing cut or cracked surfaces to join and restore mechanical continuity [12, 13]. In principle, this enables extended service lifetimes, reduced replacement cycles, and even processing or reshaping under mild conditions. However, self-healing elastomers have historically suffered from a fundamental issue between healing ability and mechanical robustness. The key characteristics that drive efficient self-healing, such as weak, reversible bonds and high chain mobility, tend to weaken the material’s ability to bear high stresses and resist fracture [14, 15]. Conversely, elastomer systems designed for high stress tolerance rely on strong crosslinked domains or rigid architectures, where the reduced bond dynamicity and restricted chain mobility fundamentally limit their self-healing capacity [16, 17]. Thus, simultaneously achieving both high toughness and efficient self-healing has long been considered mutually exclusive. Recent advances, however, suggest that this apparent issue can be overcome through strategic multi-dynamic bonding [18–22]. By combining reversible bonds of different strengths and timescales within a single network, strong interactions can maintain load-bearing capacity and fracture resistance, while weaker interactions act as sacrificial bonds that dissipate energy and facilitate rapid self-repair [23–26]. This review presents emerging design principles for engineering tough, self-healing elastomers and their soft electronics application, showing that high toughness and autonomous self-healing can be systematically achieved through rational molecular and network design.

Mechanism of toughening and self-healing through dynamic bonds

Dynamic bonds enable toughening through energy dissipation

Electronic device applications require elastomers that sustain mechanical loads and resist failure under various deformation modes. Soft elastomers composed of single, non-uniform polymer networks exhibit poor mechanical performance, characterized by low stiffness and low strength [27]. Critically, low toughness severely limits their practical applications. Toughness, which is the energy absorbed before fracture (area under the stress–strain curve), remains low due to limited energy dissipation pathways in a single, non-uniform polymer network. In addition, since defects are inevitable, fracture toughness, defined as the energy required per unit of newly created crack surface area to propagate cracks to failure, is a critical factor that represents crack resistance. Likewise, due to the lack of energy dissipation mechanisms, polymers with single, non-uniform networks exhibit low fracture toughness [28–30].

A fundamental trade-off governs design: strengthening the network through dense crosslinking improves modulus and strength but sacrifices stretchability and toughness, whereas increasing chain mobility enhances stretchability at the expense of strength and stiffness (Fig. 2a). Over the past two decades, this trade-off has been progressively overcome through a paradigm shift: the strategic introduction of weak, reversible crosslinks as sacrificial bonds for energy dissipation. Rather than relying solely on permanent covalent bonds (Bond dissociation energy, Ebond > 200 kJ/mol), modern elastomers now achieve elastic modulus from sub-MPa to hundreds of MPa, strength from sub-MPa to tens of MPa, and stretch ratios from single digits to hundreds.Fig. 2Design guidelines for tough and self-healing elastomers. a Stress–strain curves based on dynamic bond strength. b Role of dynamic bond strength on network properties

The key lies in weak reversible crosslinks with bond energies of 5–50 kJ/mol, such as hydrogen bonds [31–33], electrostatic interactions [34, 35], metal–ligand coordination [36, 37], host–guest complexes [38, 39], hydrophobic associations [40], and π-π stacking [41], that preferentially break under stress while maintaining overall network integrity. As the elastomer deforms, these bonds dissociate, converting mechanical work into thermal energy, while the sparsely crosslinked backbone sustains high stretchability through entropic elasticity. Critically, broken bonds can recombine due to their reversible nature, enabling recoverable dissipation over cyclic loads and autonomous self-healing that are essential for the damage tolerance.

The dissociation energy and timescale of dynamic bonds can be engineered by modulating molecular structure and supramolecular cooperativity (e.g., multiple weak bonds forming stronger motifs) [42, 43]. This tunability permits moderate control over when and where bonds break, optimizing stress distribution and selectively activating dissipative zones. Although intrinsic fracture energy (Γ₀) is limited to ~ 10–100 J/m2, total fracture toughness (Γ_total) can vastly exceed this through additional dissipative contribution (Γ_process) arising from damage-related processes. A large plastic zone develops ahead of the crack tip where multiple mechanisms operate: (i) sacrificial bond dissociation, (ii) chain pull-out and frictional sliding, and (iii) viscoelastic deformation [44–47]. The scaling relationship Γ_total = Γ₀ + Γ_process ∝ (energy density × zone volume) explains how elastomers achieve Γ > 10,000 J/m2. Mechanical dissipation manifests as pronounced hysteresis in stress-stretch curves, with the enclosed area quantifying energy dissipated per cycle and confirming efficient process zone operation.

To realize high toughness, modern elastomers strategically combine multiple dynamic bond classes with distinct strengths and kinetics [48]. Strong reversible bonds maintain the network’s structural integrity, whereas weak reversible bonds serve as the primary dissipative elements. This functional decoupling allows the network to sustain large stresses through strong bonds and efficiently dissipate energy through weak bonds via reversible reassociation. Optimizing bond strength, type, and spatial distribution through the strategies discussed in the following sections addresses the central design challenge for achieving tough, self-healing elastomers.

Mechanism of self-healing: chain diffusion and dynamic equilibrium

Self-healing refers to the ability of a material to autonomously repair damage caused by external sources. Introducing self-healing into soft polymers enables materials to recover their mechanical properties after damage while preserving stretchability. This unique combination has attracted considerable research interest [49]. Although various mechanisms exist for self-healing, they can be broadly categorized into extrinsic and intrinsic self-healing.

In extrinsic self-healing polymers, self-healing occurs through additional healing agents such as capsules and vascular tubes incorporated within the polymer. These healing agents, which are encapsulated to avoid affecting the polymer network under normal conditions, are released upon external stimuli to repair the damaged polymer network and restore its properties [50, 51]. Since extrinsic self-healing can be implemented through facile methods and maintains the properties of the original polymer, it was widely studied in the early stages of self-healing research. Nevertheless, it has the disadvantage of being inherently single-use due to the inevitable consumption of healing agents, which limits its repeatability.

In intrinsic self-healing polymers, healing moieties exist within the polymer network itself, allowing damaged portions of the network to spontaneously recover over time after external damage. These healing moieties are mostly capable of reversible dissociation and association, forming bonds that are much weaker than the framework molecules, leading to the break and reform of these bonds under external stimuli without frame breakage [52, 53]. Consequently, intrinsic self-healing can be performed repeatedly, overcoming the single-use limitation of extrinsic self-healing, which is why most current self-healing research is based on this approach.

The most critical aspect in designing intrinsic self-healing polymers is ensuring sufficient chain mobility to allow diffusion through the polymer network (Fig. 2b). Self-healing occurs when polymer chains on one side of a damaged interface diffuse across to the opposite interface. The dynamic bonds of these transferred polymer chains reform crosslinking points with dynamic bonds of the chains beyond the interface, thereby reconstructing the network and recovering its properties [54, 55]. Thus, the self-healing efficiency is proportional to the ease of chain diffusion, which is predominantly influenced by the glass transition temperature of the polymer backbone chain and the bond strength of crosslinkers [56].

The polymer glass transition temperature represents the temperature range over which an amorphous polymer undergoes a transition from a hard and brittle glassy state to a soft and flexible rubbery state. If the polymer glass transition temperature is high compared to the commonly used temperature range of − 20 to 40 °C in many applications, the polymer becomes brittle and rigid at the macroscale, and at the microscale, internal polymer chain diffusion is suppressed [56]. Therefore, self-healing can be regarded as negligible under these conditions. In contrast, if the glass transition temperature is significantly lower than this temperature range, the polymer becomes macroscopically soft and stretchable; microscopically, polymer chains can move freely without significant restriction, resulting in self-healing. Chen et al. demonstrated that the degree of self-healing can be controlled by adjusting the glass transition temperature of acrylate-based polymer networks [57]. Particularly for electronic device applications, since the stiffness of the network itself varies with the glass transition temperature, polymers with low glass transition temperatures are generally used to satisfy soft and flexible requirements.

In addition to the glass transition temperature of polymer chains, the bond strength of crosslinkers is also a factor that significantly affects the overall mobility of the network. Even if the backbone chains have a low glass transition temperature and can flow, strong crosslinking bonds suppress the chain diffusion and prevent self-healing by restricting chain mobility. For instance, multiple hydrogen bonds, such as ureido-pyrimidinone (UPy), possess strength comparable to covalent bonds, resulting in low self-healing efficiency even in low glass transition temperature polymers [58, 59]. On the other hand, using only weak bonds to ensure chain mobility causes crosslinking points to break even under weak external stimuli, making it difficult to maintain the material’s shape during operation. Therefore, to create polymers that are both tough and healable, it is important to introduce bond strengths that are strong enough to maintain mechanical properties while enabling chain diffusion.

Strategies for designing tough, self-healing elastomers through dynamic bond control

Tough elastomers are achieved by using dynamic bonds to dissipate energy during bond rupture. Maintaining network integrity while dissipating energy using a single bond type is challenging, so hybrid strategies that control bond type, strength, and density are most commonly employed [43, 60]. This chapter introduces approaches using identical bond types with tunable binding strength, as well as methods that adjust bond types and structure to dissipate applied energy effectively, thereby enhancing toughness.

Synergistic dynamic crosslinking

Recently, toughening through synergistic crosslinking has been extensively studied. Kang et al. developed a novel design strategy for self-healing elastomers by incorporating controlled multi-strength hydrogen bonding interactions within a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) network (Fig. 3a) [61]. The key innovation lies in the synergistic combination of strong cooperative hydrogen bonds (methylenebisphenyl urea, MPU) and weak anticooperative hydrogen bonds (isophorone urea, IU) to construct a supramolecular network architecture. While strong MPU-MPU interactions confer elasticity and mechanical robustness, sterically hindered weak IU-IU interactions effectively dissipate strain energy through reversible bond breakage and reformation during mechanical deformation. By overcoming the conventional trade-off between mechanical toughness and viscoelasticity inherent in self-healing polymers, the PDMS-MPU₀.₄IU₀.₆ film achieved exceptionally high fracture energy (12,000 J/m2) with notch-insensitive stretchability up to 1,200%. Notably, autonomous self-healing occurs at room temperature even in aqueous environments and artificial sweat due to the hydrophobic PDMS backbone, demonstrating water-insensitive recovery, a significant advancement over previous hydrogen-bonded systems vulnerable to moisture. Another example of a dynamic bond for synergistic crosslinking is reported by Park et al., who demonstrated that systematic tuning of counter anion effects in metal–ligand coordination-based elastomers enables substantial mechanical toughening through synergistic anion dynamics (Fig. 3b) [62]. Using Zn2⁺-complexed 2,2′-bipyridine (BPy) ligands, they revealed that non-coordinating anions (trifluoromethanesulfonate, OTf−) promote rapid ligand exchange and efficient stress relaxation, whereas coordinating anions with multiple coordination modes, particularly acetylacetonate (acac−), introduce diverse coordination environments that broaden the relaxation time spectrum. By mixing non-coordinating OTf− anion and multimodal coordinating acac− anions, they achieved a remarkable synergistic enhancement: the blended system exhibited threefold higher mechanical toughness and significantly improved self-healing efficiency compared to single-anion counterparts, despite reducing the coordinating-anion content by half. This counter-anion-driven approach revealed that energy dissipation modes operating at different time scales work concurrently to enhance material robustness. The study underscores an overlooked design parameter in metal–ligand coordination networks, that counter-anion chemistry provides a powerful yet facile route for fine-tuning polymer dynamics without requiring major chemical modifications.

Both studies establish a unified principle: the cooperation between weak energy-dissipating bonds and strong load-bearing bonds generates mechanically superior self-healing materials. However, they diverge in implementation: Kang et al. achieved this through careful chemical design of hydrogen bonding architecture, while Park et al. accomplished it through subtle manipulation of coordination dynamics via counter-anion selection. Together, these works highlight complementary strategies for engineering tough, self-healing elastomers by leveraging multi-scale bond dynamics at the molecular level.

Structural modulation of dynamic bonds

The overall polymer dynamics are governed not only by bond strength but also by the spatial placement and structural form of the non-covalent crosslinks within the polymer network. Yang et al. pioneered the development of self-healing electronic skins functioning at extreme polar temperatures (–78 °C) by positioning the multi-strength hydrogen bonds at the side chain (Fig. 4a) [63]. In general, strong interactions such as UPy-based crosslinking restrict chain mobility, resulting in high mechanical strength but limited self-healing. However, when UPy is incorporated not directly into the polymer backbone but into a mobile side chain, the network gains mobility at the crosslinking points, enabling self-healing behavior. The authors designed two Upy-crosslinked PDMS networks with different Upy placements: side-chain UPy (S-UD) and main-chain UPy (M-UD) derivatives. Unlike M-UD, which cannot self-heal at ultralow temperatures, the S-UD exhibits superior ultralow-temperature performance due to lower binding energy variations (0.197 eV vs. 0.703 eV) and potential wells during both stretching and self-healing processes. S-UD elastomers achieved record-high elongations of 3257% at − 78 °C while maintaining 75.9% self-healing efficiency after 24 h at this extreme temperature, which had not been previously reported. Density functional theory analysis revealed that side-chain UPy placement allows stepwise breaking and reconstruction of multi-strength hydrogen bonds (single, double, and quadruple), with lower energy requirements for molecular chain reorganization compared to the main-chain UPy moieties. By integrating this elastomer with eutectic gallium-indium electrodes and solid silver flakes as a self-healing conductor, the authors fabricated a complete robotic hand e-skin capable of precise pressure sensing, shape recognition, and Braille symbol identification at − 78 °C—a milestone achievement enabling tactile feedback for autonomous polar exploration and demonstrating the feasibility of intelligent robotic systems in extreme environments.Fig. 4Dynamic bond with structural modulation a Elastomers achieving toughness and ultra-low-temperature self-healing through strategic positioning of quadruple hydrogen bond UPy units. Jin et al. also combined quadruple UPy hydrogen bonding to a movable structure to address the challenge of simultaneously achieving high toughness and room-temperature self-healing (Fig. 4b) [64]. Polyrotaxane consists of α-cyclodextrin (α-CD) rings threaded onto a polyethylene glycol (PEG) chain, where the α-CDs can freely slide along the backbone. By functionalizing the α-CDs with UPy and incorporating them into a UPy-containing polyurethane, the UPy units on the polyrotaxane can form dynamic crosslinks with those in the polyurethane. Because the UPy crosslinking points themselves are sliding, this architecture simultaneously delivers high toughness (77.3 MJ/m3) and fracture energy (127.2 kJ/m2), together with exceptional stretchability (up to 2900%) and efficient self-healing (91% at room temperature after 24 h). Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that cyclodextrins dynamically redistribute and facilitate three distinct regimes of deformation: initial load-bearing via strong inter-chain hydrogen bonding, stress dissipation through cyclodextrin sliding, and strain-hardening through reversible strain-induced crystallization of polyethylene glycol backbones. This multiscale toughening mechanism of combining molecular-level hydrogen bonding dynamics with mesoscale sliding effects and nanoscale crystallization demonstrates how hierarchical energy dissipation pathways enable both mechanical robustness and rapid room-temperature repair, establishing a paradigm for designing elastomers for flexible electronics with extended service life.

Rong et al. introduced a novel design paradigm featuring interlaced hydrogen bonds that fundamentally differ from conventional quadruple hydrogen bonding systems, delivering unprecedented mechanical performance in self-healing elastomers (Fig. 4c) [65]. Unlike traditional quadruple hydrogen bonds formed between identical molecular units, interlaced hydrogen bonds are constructed from two molecular units with different lengths and steric hindrance, resulting in lower repulsive secondary interactions and substantially higher binding energies. By incorporating chain extenders composed of oxalyldihydrazide (OD) and adipic acid dihydrazide (AD) into a polyurethane-urea backbone, the synthesized elastomer exhibited record-breaking tensile strength (117.5 MPa), superhigh fracture energy (522.46 kJ/m2), and exceptional puncture resistance (181.9 N). The supramolecular interlocking network formed by interlaced hydrogen bonds locks polymer chains at room temperature, conferring extraordinary mechanical robustness. Notably, despite achieving ultrahigh strength at ambient conditions, the elastomer demonstrated excellent self-healing properties (up to 95.8% efficiency at 100 °C) with high transparency (91% transmittance), remarkable durability, including thermal oxidation resistance and solvent resistance, making it a comprehensive material for demanding applications in protective coatings and flexible electronics.

All three studies exemplify a unifying principle: integrated dynamic bonding systems exploiting complementary bond exchange pathways enable the seemingly contradictory requirements of mechanical robustness and self-healing. Whether designed through dual cross-linking synergy, interlaced hydrogen bond topology, or strategic bond positioning, these works demonstrate that precise molecular engineering of dynamic bonds, rather than simply tuning their ratio, offers remarkable pathways to tough, self-healing materials for demanding real-world applications.

Despite these advanced design and structural strategies, a fundamental challenge remains in the trade-off between high energy dissipation and ideal elastic recovery. Since the toughening mechanism primarily relies on the dissociation of sacrificial bonds, these elastomers inherently exhibit pronounced hysteresis, which often leads to undesirable residual strain and limited fatigue resistance under cyclic loading. The lack of elasticity can compromise the long-term precision and signal reliability of soft electronic devices. Therefore, developing orthogonal dynamic networks is essential to decouple dissipation from elasticity. This approach allows for independent control over mechanical reliability and healing kinetics, effectively reducing residual strain.

Electronic device applications

The practical applications of tough, self-healing elastomers in soft electronics are categorized into three distinct classes based on the primary functional role of their dynamic bonds: autonomous repairability, modular assembly, and integrated functional stability. First, wearable devices and electronic skins demand repairability, where dynamic bonds focus on restoring mechanical and electrical integrity against unpredictable damages inherent in daily skin-mounted use. Second, modular electronics utilize the self-bonding nature of these elastomers for layer assembly, enabling the adhesive-free reconfiguration of individual building blocks into customized systems. Finally, optoelectronic devices represent a synergistic integration of both concepts; they require precise assembly to form complex architectures while necessitating rapid repair kinetics to maintain delicate optical performance, such as stable luminance, under severe mechanical stress. The development of these self-healing devices reflects the evolution of dynamic bond engineering from simple damage recovery to complex device-level construction and functional preservation.

Wearable devices and electronic skin

A tough and self-healable elastomer is an essential component of wearable devices and electronic skin [66–68]. Since both applications are primarily mounted in the exterior part, such as human skin or robotic surfaces, ensuring mechanical robustness and self-healing capability is important [69–71]. The strategies with various dynamic bonds have been widely used to obtain mechanical robustness and self-healing efficiency for these electronic interfaces [72–75].

Lee et al. achieved a breakthrough in rapid autonomous self-healing electronic skins that recover over 80% functionality within just 10 s after mechanical damage, without requiring external heat or chemical stimuli (Fig. 5a) [76]. They incorporated disulfide bonds, capable of ultrafast dynamic covalent exchange, into isophorone-urethane–crosslinked polytetramethylene ether glycol (PTMEG) to produce fibrous thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU). The asymmetric alicyclic structure of isophorone enhances polymer chain mobility, facilitating spontaneous bond regeneration even at temperatures as low as 0 °C. Beyond remarkable mechanical resilience (supporting 1 kg load immediately post-healing, 900% stretchability), the e-skin demonstrates comprehensive physiological monitoring: surface electromyography (sEMG) for muscle activity, electrocardiography (ECG) for cardiac function, facial expression recognition through periocular monitoring, voice recognition via throat contact, and multi-joint movement tracking (finger, wrist, elbow, knee, ankle). Remarkably, performance is maintained underwater across varied temperatures and humidity levels. Integration with machine learning algorithms enables on-site AI-driven fatigue classification achieving > 95% accuracy, capable of identifying muscle fatigue states with > 80% recovery of initial performance within 10 s. Ren et al. reported a high-performance, skin-inspired wearable platform characterized by exceptional mechanical toughness and autonomous self-healing through the dynamic covalent polyimine (Fig. 5b) [77]. The architecture employs a novel "soft motherboard-rigid plugin modules" (SOM-RIPs) design, which effectively isolates fragile components from external deformations, allowing the system to endure 120% longitudinal strain and repeated bending (3.5 mm radius) with a strain reduction ratio of 1200 times. This robust framework achieves fully autonomous repair at room temperature; the polyimine substrate restores its mechanical integrity within 1.5 h via dynamic bond exchange reactions. The device demonstrates superior wearable feasibility by maintaining stable electrical resistance and conformal thermal contact on complex human geometries, such as fingers and forearms, even during dynamic motion. Most significantly, these resilient material properties enabled the fabrication of a high-performance thermoelectric generator (TEG) that achieves a record-high open-circuit voltage of 1 V/cm2 at a temperature difference of 95 K, providing a durable and self-repairing energy-harvesting solution for next-generation wearable electronics.

A further example was reported by Vo et al., who developed a fully autonomous self-healing integrated circuit for skin electronics by employing identical supramolecular polymers across all active components, including the conductor, semiconductor, and dielectric layers (Fig. 5c) [78]. The breakthrough design uses a nanoweb-structured blend of semiconducting polymer (DPPT-TT) within a supramolecular self-healing elastomer (PDMS-MPU-IU), generating strain-insensitive percolating pathways that heal microscale damage (≤ 4 μm) autonomously at room temperature without manual alignment or external stimuli. All transistors in 5 × 5 active-matrix arrays maintained uniform performance under 30° biaxial strain and achieved 77% recovery efficiency after cutting damage. The silver metallization strategy creates metal-polymer nanocomposite electrodes with low Schottky barrier heights (< 0.1 eV), facilitating efficient charge injection while enabling autonomous healing of damaged electrodes within 24–80 h. Most significantly, the authors fabricated the first self-healing logic gates (inverter, NAND, NOR) capable of maintaining computational functionality after mechanical damage and continued operation after healing under 30° biaxial stretch—a milestone representing true integrated skin electronics with self-repair capabilities. Long-term ambient stability exceeding 1 year without encapsulation demonstrates practical feasibility for wearable applications.

These three studies collectively establish e-skin as a paradigm of wearable bioelectronics, from isolated sensors to integrated systems. 3D sensor architecture, ultrafast healing kinetics, or supramolecular polymer transistor integration from three works demonstrate that e-skins can now function as comprehensive physiological monitoring platforms, adaptive robotic interfaces, and eventually, bio-integrated prosthetic systems.

Modular electronics

The modular electronics are the reconfigurable electronics composed of small building blocks that can be individually assembled [79–83]. Through the reassembly, modular electronics can change their geometry and functionality, offering the advantage of customization and reusability. Self-healing elastomer is one of the most promising candidates for modular electronics, enabling a facile, durable, and repeatable assembly between two building blocks without any external adhesive [84–87].

A reconfigurable and scalable assembly (RS assembly) method enabling user-on-demand modular integration of self-healing electronic devices was demonstrated by Jang et al. (Fig. 6a) [88]. The key innovation is that all electronic components—gate dielectric, semiconductor, and electrodes—are fabricated from a single self-healing polymer (PDMS-MPU-IU), allowing adhesive-free interlayer bonding through self-bonding assembly. The 5 × 5 active-matrix thin-film transistor (AM-SS-TFT) arrays exhibited exceptional areal uniformity across 25 devices and retained full electrical functionality following damage. More significantly, individual SS-TFT modules and resistors could be rapidly reconfigured into different logic gates—inverter, NAND, and NOR circuits—by physical disassembly and reassembly without manual alignment. For example, an inverter configuration could be deconstructed and immediately recombined with an additional transistor to form a NAND gate, with automatic functional recovery occurring post-damage. The AM-SS-TFT arrays also maintained operational performance for 7 days when implanted subcutaneously in rodent models, demonstrating biocompatibility and in vivo durability. Cooper et al. also pioneered a revolutionary approach using two dynamic polymers with immiscible backbones (PDMS and polypropylene glycol (PPG)) but identical hydrogen-bonding linkers (bisurea) to achieve autonomous layer realignment during healing of damaged multilayer structures (Fig. 6b) [89]. While identical dynamic bonding of bisurea maintains strong interlayer adhesion, the disparity in surface free energies (PDMS: 23 mJ/m2, PPG: 44 mJ/m2) generates interfacial tension gradients that drive directional polymer chain diffusion to spontaneously realign misaligned layers. Interfacial widths (2–39 nm) are tunable and can be programmed via annealing temperature, then kinetically locked by cooling. In a proof-of-concept pressure-sensing capacitor, alternating layers of PDMS polymer embedded with dielectric SrTiO₃ particles and PPG polymer embedded with conductive carbon black nanoparticles underwent autonomous realignment after mechanical fracture, recovering 96% of initial capacitance. Most remarkably, core–shell magnetic fibers composed of PPG polymer containing magnetized NdFeB flakes as cores and PDMS polymer as shells demonstrated underwater circuit assembly where magnetic forces guide macroscopic alignment while interfacial tension mediates microscopic layer alignment. A complete LED circuit was assembled by simply placing magnetically coded wire segments into water—magnetic forces achieved coarse alignment, interfacial tension locked precise layer positioning, and thermal annealing completed electrical healing, resulting in full circuit functionality and light emission.

Sun et al. developed a modularly assembled self-healing thermoelectric device utilizing liquid metal electrodes and carbon nanotube-doped disulfide-containing polyurethane (DSPU) encapsulation with selective thermal conductivity optimization (Fig. 6c) [90]. Through strategic selective encapsulation of liquid metal interconnects with carbon nanotube-doped DSPU, the device achieved a normalized power density of 3.14 W/cm2·K2, representing state-of-the-art performance for self-healing thermoelectric generators. The modular assembly concept utilizes liquid metal fluidity: fractured device terminals are exposed and brought into contact, whereupon the liquid metal spontaneously reconnects and DSPU’s dynamic disulfide and hydrogen bonds reform. Users can customize output specifications by arranging modules in series or parallel configurations to achieve the desired voltage and power for specific applications. The device also enables tower-like vertical stacking to enhance cooling capacity, achieving maximum temperature differentials of 6.2 K in two-layer configurations. Enhanced mechanical compliance allows conformal application on curved surfaces with excellent fatigue resistance over 2000 bending cycles, positioning it as a wearable energy harvester and personal thermal management platform.

These three studies collectively establish a unifying paradigm for modular electronics: leveraging dynamic bonding systems achieves rapid damage recovery, user-controlled reconfigurability, and robust interlayer adhesion. Each approach demonstrates how careful materials engineering of dynamic systems enables soft electronics that are simultaneously durable, adaptable, and autonomously healable, which are critical features for next-generation wearable and implantable devices.

Optoelectronic devices

Light-emitting components are an essential element of an electronic device, as they are used to express or indicate the operational state of the device [91, 92]. The conventional LED, which is usually based on rigid electronics, however, often causes a modulus mismatch with soft substrates, even resulting in delamination or failure at the substrate interface [93–96]. In this regard, stretchable electroluminescent devices, often represented as an optoelectronic device, play a crucial role in wearable devices and soft robotics [97–99]. The stable operation of these devices depends on mechanical robustness and damage recovery, which tough self-healing elastomers can effectively satisfy [100–103].

Liu et al. also established a stabilization strategy through controlled ion–dipole interactions between CF₃ groups and perovskite cations, combined with dipole–dipole van der Waals interactions throughout the polymer network (Fig. 7a) [104]. The dual-layer protection mechanism enables unprecedented 1-month stability in water, strong acid, strong base, and seawater while maintaining autonomous self-healing through water-mediated interaction dynamics. The doubled interfacial cohesion forces at fractured surfaces (validated by AFM) confirm that accumulated dipole interactions actively promote underwater healing (55% efficiency after 24 h). The key innovation lies in coordinating complementary bond dynamics: strong urea interactions confer mechanical robustness, while weak imine bonds effectively dissipate strain energy through reversible exchange. This synergistic architecture achieves exceptionally high toughness (144.2 MJ m−3) with 900% stretchability. In addition, water can accelerate the self-healing by promoting bond dissociation/reformation, enhancing the chain mobility. The water-assisted self-healing enables rapid autonomous self-healing within 1 h underwater without external stimuli. When this polymer is integrated into light-emitting touch-responsive devices, the elastomer maintains luminance performance over repeated mechanical cycles, with complete functional recovery post-healing. Moreover, water-assisted self-healing polymer-perovskite quantum dot composites demonstrate ~ 1-year boiling water stability, exemplifying how coordinated weak and strong bonds simultaneously address optical performance and mechanical durability.

Another example was demonstrated by Tan et al., who proposed a complementary design strategy by combining high-κ dipole–dipole interactions with reversible hydrogen–fluorine bonding in a PVDF-HFP fluoroelastomer (Fig. 7c) [106]. While dipole realignment under AC fields significantly reduces activation voltage (23 V, < 1 kHz), enabling safe human–machine interaction, the incorporated reversible H-F bonding serves as dynamic physical cross-links. This molecular design not only facilitates rapid re-association of fractured interfaces but also effectively dissipates mechanical energy during deformation, resulting in exceptional toughness along with rapid mechanical self-healing (91% strength recovery within 5 min). Beyond simple structural restoration, this dual-mechanism approach enables the device to recover 89–128% of original luminance within 5 min post-cutting. Crucially, the material’s enhanced toughness prevents catastrophic failure, allowing it to maintain function even after severe puncture damage. This demonstrates that the integration of efficient energy dissipation pathways can prevent crack propagation and simultaneously optimize electroluminescent performance and mechanical resilience.

Despite these promising demonstrations, a significant challenge remains in achieving rapid, autonomous healing at room temperature without compromising mechanical robustness. Fast bond exchange kinetics often cause unintended softening and a reduction in toughness. Furthermore, many high-toughness polymers require elevated temperatures or prolonged durations at ambient conditions for effective chain diffusion. Future research should therefore focus on advanced network designs that can effectively decouple repair efficiency from bulk mechanical properties. By strategically balancing transient bond dynamics with long-term structural stability, it will be possible to facilitate rapid healing specifically at the damaged interface while maintaining the structural rigidity and toughness of the bulk material under real-world operating conditions.

Conclusion and future perspective

Dynamic bonds provide a powerful approach to creating soft elastomers that are simultaneously tough and self-healing. The fundamental concept is simple: by combining strong bonds that resist deformation with weak bonds that dissipate energy when stressed, elastomers can withstand large mechanical forces while also recovering autonomously from damage. This strategy overcomes the conventional trade-off where tough materials are brittle and unable to self-repair, while self-healing materials tend to be weak and easily damaged. Through careful engineering of dynamic crosslinking systems, structural design, and hierarchical bond arrangements, elastomers can now achieve mechanical stability under repeated stress while simultaneously healing micro-cracks and fractures without external stimuli. These fundamental principles translate across diverse applications: soft electronics can be modularly assembled and disassembled without adhesives, wearable sensors can monitor body signals continuously and recover from daily mechanical damage, and flexible light-emitting devices can function normally even after sustaining physical injuries.

Realizing a circular electronic ecosystem where intrinsic repairability enables both significantly extended durability and efficient closed-loop recycling remains a key direction for future research. With the exceptional durability of self-healing polymers, future flexible electronic devices are expected to exhibit robust cyclic performance. In addition, leveraging adhesive-free modular assembly, these materials will drive a paradigm shift in customizable systems, allowing users to reconfigure wearable and implantable electronics on-demand. Ultimately, by tuning multi-scale bond kinetics to withstand complex physiological environments, self-healing elastomers will provide a foundation for resilient, adaptable, and sustainable bio-integrated platforms with lifelong operational stability.

Furthermore, biomedical implants represent the most promising frontier for these materials [71, 107–109]. Self-healing elastomers enable cardiac patches that conform naturally to heart tissue immediately upon implantation (Fig. 8a) [110], electronic devices that adapt gracefully to biological tissue growth without triggering inflammatory responses (Fig. 8b) [111], and biosensors that continuously monitor disease biomarkers throughout their operational lifetime (Fig. 8c) [112].
