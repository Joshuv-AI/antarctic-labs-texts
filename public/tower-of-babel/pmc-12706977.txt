Radiomics-based diagnosis of carotid artery stenosis using non-contrast CT: model development and validation
Authors: Guoliang Wang, Yuhang Zhang, Liu Xu, Jianqi Ni, Yifeng Shen, Qin Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly detection of carotid artery stenosis is critical for effective stroke prevention. We investigated the feasibility and diagnostic efficacy of radiomics analysis based on non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) in identifying carotid artery stenosis, and aimed to establish a radiomics-based machine-learning model as a non-invasive, cost-effective auxiliary diagnostic tool.MethodsThis retrospective study included 260 patients who underwent both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced carotid computed tomography (CT) scans at Jiaxing First Hospital between January 2021 and December 2024. The degree of carotid stenosis was confirmed by CT angiography. Patients were enrolled according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Clinical data from 260 eligible patients were collected and stratified for comparative analysis. All CT images were acquired on a single scanner. Two experienced radiologists jointly identified the stenotic segments on NCCT images and used ITK-SNAP (software tool for manual outlining of ROIs) software to manually outline the regions of interest (ROIs) in 2D along the vessel wall of the plaques themselves. First, the stenotic segments were accurately localized on computed tomography angiography (CTA) images; after rigid registration, these reference positions were mapped to the corresponding NCCT images to guide the precise outlining of ROIs. To ensure the reproducibility of annotations, a third radiologist then reviewed all ROIs: 30 randomly selected cases were independently re-segmented, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of the extracted radiomic features was calculated. Finally, features with ICC > 0.85 were retained, and this result indicated high inter-observer consistency. Radiomic features were extracted using PyRadiomics after image preprocessing, which comprised gray-level normalization, resampling, and wavelet filtering. Feature selection was performed using Spearman correlation analysis and Lasso regression with tenfold cross-validation. The selected features were used to train multiple machine-learning classifiers, including logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), and naïve Bayes. The dataset was randomly split into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio. Model hyperparameters were optimized in the training cohort via fivefold cross-validation. Model performance was assessed using metrics such as accuracy, area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curve, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and confusion matrix. A decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility.ResultsNinety-seven radiomic features were initially extracted; after selection, 12 with non-zero coefficients remained. The SVM model achieved an AUC of 0.874 in the training set and 0.774 in the test set, indicating strong discrimination and generalizability. Confusion-matrix analysis confirmed reliable separation of positive and negative cases with a low misclassification rate. Decision-curve analysis demonstrated that the model conferred superior net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities from 0.2 to 0.8 compared with both treat-all and treat-none strategies.ConclusionsRadiomics analysis of NCCT imaging provides an effective, contrast-free means of identifying carotid artery stenosis. Our model demonstrated robust diagnostic accuracy and clinical potential, especially when contrast-enhanced imaging is contraindicated. Prospective multi-center external validation and further workflow automation are nevertheless required to support broad clinical adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03592-2.

Introduction

Carotid artery stenosis is a major pathogenic mechanism of ischemic stroke. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 80% of ischemic strokes associated with carotid stenosis result from hemodynamic disturbances, thrombosis, or artery-to-artery embolism secondary to atherosclerotic narrowing or occlusion [1–3]. Atherosclerosis plays a central role in the initiation and development of carotid stenosis, and is closely linked to conventional cardiovascular risk factors, such as smoking, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension [4–8]. With an aging population and increasing prevalence of metabolic disorders, the incidence of carotid stenosis is on the rise [2, 9]. Carotid stenosis may be asymptomatic in its early stages; however, once an ischemic stroke occurs, it is often accompanied by high rates of disability and recurrence. Therefore, the early and accurate identification of carotid artery stenosis is a key focus in primary stroke prevention [10].

Currently, several imaging modalities are used clinically to evaluate carotid artery stenosis, including carotid duplex ultrasonography (CDUS), computed tomography angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) [11, 12]. CDUS is often employed as the initial screening tool due to its non-invasiveness, real-time capabilities, and cost-effectiveness, but it is susceptible to operator dependency and anatomical variations. CTA and MRA provide high-resolution vascular imaging, with CTA providing superior visualization of atherosclerotic plaques and vascular calcification [13]. DSA is the gold standard for diagnosing vascular stenosis but is limited in routine use due to its invasiveness and the risks associated with iodinated contrast agents [14]. Due to advantages such as non-invasiveness and safety, CTA can serve as an alternative to DSA in many scenarios and has become the preferred or initial screening method for vascular diseases. Thus, there is an urgent need for imaging strategies that maintain diagnostic accuracy while enhancing non-invasiveness, objectivity, and accessibility [2, 15].

Radiomics, an emerging field of high-throughput image analysis, allows for the extraction of a large number of quantitative features from standard medical images, including first-order statistics, textural matrices [e.g., the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) and gray-level run length matrix (GLRLM)], shape descriptors, and wavelet-based features [16, 17]. These features can reveal subtle image patterns that are often imperceptible to the human eye. Radiomics has shown promising applications in disease diagnosis, risk stratification, and prognostic modeling across various fields. It has been used to differentiate between benign and malignant lung nodules, predict molecular subtypes of breast cancer, and quantify coronary artery calcification burden.

Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) is one of the most commonly used imaging modalities in clinical practice due to its accessibility, rapid acquisition, and acceptable radiation exposure [17]. Although it lacks contrast-enhanced vascular delineation, NCCT is a critical resource in emergency settings and primary care institutions. Applying radiomics analysis to NCCT data could unlock valuable diagnostic information, address the inherent limitations of conventional computed tomography (CT), and provide a novel non-invasive pathway for evaluating vascular pathologies such as carotid artery stenosis [18].

A previous study has used radiomics based on CTA images to assess coronary or carotid stenosis and plaque characteristics[19]; however, research on the use of radiomics based on NCCT images to diagnose carotid artery stenosis is limited [20]. Indeed, little research has been conducted on the feasibility and clinical value of detecting stenosis using radiomic features from NCCT, especially in patients with contraindications to contrast agent use. Given the widespread application of NCCT in resource-constrained and urgent care environments, the development of an automated, contrast-free radiomics-based diagnostic approach could have substantial clinical and translational benefits [21, 22].

The study initially attempted to use a U-Net-based algorithm for automatic segmentation. However, due to issues of vague vascular boundaries and insufficient contrast in NCCT images, the definition of the region of interest (ROI) was inaccurate, and this method was ultimately abandoned. As a result, this retrospective study examined data of patients diagnosed with carotid artery stenosis via CTA and collected their corresponding NCCT and contrast-enhanced CT images [21, 23, 24]. The anatomical location of stenosis identified on contrast-enhanced images was used as a reference for ROI segmentation on NCCT images. Multi-dimensional radiomic features were then extracted and used to train machine-learning models for diagnostic prediction. The performance, stability, and clinical utility of these models were systematically evaluated to determine the applicability of radiomics-based diagnosis using NCCT images [3, 25].

We explored the potential of radiomics analysis based on NCCT for the non-invasive identification of carotid artery stenosis. We sought to establish a low-cost, scalable, and contrast-free diagnostic tool to provide theoretical insights and technical support in clinical settings.

Methods

Study objective

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiaxing First Hospital (approval No. 2024-LY-089). Given its retrospective nature, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

In total, 260 outpatients or inpatients who underwent both NCCT and contrast-enhanced carotid CT examinations at Jiaxing First Hospital between January 2021 and December 2024 were included in the study. All patients had confirmed degrees of carotid artery stenosis via CTA.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included in the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: (I) were aged ≥ 18 years; (II) had undergone both NCCT and contrast-enhanced carotid CT scans with good image quality and no significant artifacts; (III) had original Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images suitable for radiomics analysis available; and (IV) had a time interval between CTA and NCCT scans of ≤ 1 week.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (I) had poor-quality images precluding ROI delineation; (II) had neck tumors, aneurysms, or severe calcifications affecting structural identification; and/or (III) had an unclear medical history or incomplete clinical data.

Collection of clinical indicators

We collected the clinical history of 260 patients, including 194 males and 25 females, with ages ranging from 63 to 81 years. Clinical baseline data included: gender, age, smoking history, drinking history; history of hypertension and diabetes; as well as levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (mmol/L, ref: > 1.0), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (mmol/L, ref: < 3.4), D-dimer (mg/L, ref: < 0.5), and fibrinogen. All indicators were obtained via fasting blood sampling on the day of the patients’ examination and processed by the hospital laboratory department following standardized procedures.

Training and test sets

The dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 8:2. Fivefold cross-validation was employed in the training set for hyperparameter tuning and model training.

Data collection and preprocessing

All CT scans were performed using a Toshiba Aquilion One 320-slice spiral CT scanner (Toshiba, Japan) to ensure consistency.

Image quality was assessed based on the “Chinese Guidelines for Standardized Head and Neck CT Angiography”. Two experienced radiologists independently evaluated the images. The exclusion criteria included motion artifacts scoring > 2 (on a 4-point scale), blurred vascular edges precluding ROI delineation, contrast agent filling defects, and/or severe atherosclerotic plaques obscuring the lumen.

A total of 260 paired NCCT-CTA cases (with an interval of ≤ 1 week) were included in the study. The stenosis group comprised 160 datasets (stenosis degree ≥ 50%) and the non-stenosis group comprised 100 datasets.

Image annotation [16]

The image data were exported from the hospital’s standard imaging workstation. Two experienced radiologists jointly determined the anatomical location of carotid artery stenosis along the vessel wall of the plaque itself on CTA images. After rigid registration, researchers manually delineated the ROI of the stenotic vascular segment on the corresponding NCCT images using ITK-SNAP software. To ensure accuracy and consistency, all the ROI delineations were reviewed and confirmed by a third senior radiologist. Thirty randomly selected cases were independently re-segmented, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of the extracted radiomic features was calculated. Finally, features with an ICC > 0.85 were retained, indicating high inter-observer consistency. This multi-level review process aimed to minimize human bias and enhance the stability and reproducibility of subsequent radiomic feature extraction. Feature extraction and preprocessing

Radiomic feature extraction was performed using the open-source PyRadiomics package (version 3.0.1) in the Python environment, focusing on the previously delineated ROI regions.

To reduce multicollinearity and redundant information in the high-dimensional feature space, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated between features. Features with absolute correlation coefficients > 0.9 were considered redundant, and one feature from each correlated pair was removed. Subsequently, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression method was employed for feature selection and dimensionality reduction. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined using tenfold cross-validation by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE), balancing model complexity and predictive performance. The selected features were then used for subsequent machine-learning model construction.

Model construction and evaluation

The selected radiomic features were used to construct various mainstream machine-learning models to examine their ability to identify and classify carotid artery stenosis without contrast agents.

Classification algorithms

The following classification algorithms were used:(I)Logistic regression (LR), which is a linear modeling approach;(II)Support vector machine (SVM), which is a kernel-based nonlinear learning method, and(III)Naïve Bayes (NB), which is a probabilistic reasoning-based classification method.

The dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 8:2 to ensure that the testing set remained unseen during model training, providing a realistic assessment of model generalizability. In the training set, fivefold cross-validation was employed for hyperparameter tuning and model training, optimizing model configurations to enhance robustness and reduce overfitting.

To enhance interpretability and visualization, the ROC curves were plotted, illustrating model performance across various sensitivity and specificity combinations. Confusion matrices were constructed to analyze true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, providing insights into model prediction distributions.

Further, a decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the models. By comparing the net benefits across different probability thresholds, the DCA assesses the potential clinical impact of the models, considering the consequences of false positives and false negatives. This analysis offers a framework beyond traditional statistical performance metrics, emphasizing the real-world clinical applicability of the models.

Overall, this comprehensive model evaluation framework not only validated the discriminative power of radiomic features in NCCT images for carotid artery stenosis, but also laid the groundwork for the development of non-invasive, automated diagnostic tools in clinical settings.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9), leveraging libraries such as scikit-learn, pandas, numpy, and matplotlib for data processing, modeling, and visualization.

Feature selection

LASSO regression, implemented via the glmnet algorithm, was used for radiomic feature selection. Tenfold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ), balancing model complexity and feature selection stability.

Results

Analysis of clinical baseline characteristics

A total of 260 patients consisted of 100 cases with mild-to-moderate stenosis and 160 cases with severe stenosis. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (n = 208) and a test set (n = 52) in an 8:2 ratio. Baseline characteristics such as gender, age, and underlying diseases showed no significant differences between the two groups (all P > 0.05), and the two groups were comparable. The degree of stenosis was potentially associated with clinical indicators. Compared with the mild-to-moderate stenosis group, the severe stenosis group had a significantly higher proportion of male patients (86% vs. 57%, P < 0.001) and older age (73 ± 8 years vs. 70 ± 10 years, P = 0.034). Total cholesterol (3.52 ± 0.89 vs 3.82 ± 0.99, P = 0.010) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (1.98 ± 0.71 vs 2.24 ± 0.82, P = 0.006) were significantly lower than those in the mild-to-moderate stenosis group, with statistical significance. The Spearman correlation analysis revealed a high degree of multicollinearity among several features (|r|> 0.9), and redundant features were subsequently removed based on the correlation heatmaps (Figs. 2, 3). To further reduce dimensionality and identify the most predictive variables, LASSO regression was employed. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined via tenfold cross-validation to minimize the MSE (Fig. 4). A total of 11 features with non-zero coefficients were retained for subsequent model construction (Figs. 5, 6). These selected features predominantly originated from the GLCM and first-order statistical descriptors, suggesting that carotid artery stenosis may be associated with alterations in local intensity distribution and gray-level homogeneity. These changes may reflect underlying microstructural abnormalities in the vessel wall and surrounding tissues. Among the 11 selected radiomic features, several GLCM-derived metrics such as entropy and contrast indicate increased texture heterogeneity and local intensity variation, which may reflect calcified or lipid-rich plaques. Discussion

Carotid artery stenosis is a risk factor for ischemic stroke [26]. Accurate, non-invasive, and repeatable evaluation methods are critical for early diagnosis and risk stratification [27]. Traditionally, contrast-enhanced carotid CTA and magnetic resonance imaging have been widely employed for vascular imaging [28]. However, these modalities rely on contrast agents and consume significant resources, limiting their accessibility, especially in high-risk or contrast-contraindicated populations and resource-limited settings. In recent years, the emergence of radiomics has opened new avenues for precision medicine by extracting high-dimensional, quantitative, and imperceptible features from conventional imaging data, enabling the construction of predictive models and facilitating individualized disease management [29].

Our findings are consistent with prior CTA-based radiomics studies. For instance, Zaccagna et al. [16] demonstrated that GLCM entropy from CTA could identify vulnerable plaques, while Hu et al. [29] employed dual-energy CT to classify symptomatic carotid lesions. Unlike these contrast-based approaches, our NCCT-based model eliminates contrast dependency, extending radiomics utility to broader patient populations.

To our knowledge, few studies have comprehensively evaluated the feasibility of NCCT-based radiomics for carotid artery stenosis detection. Multiple machine-learning classifiers were developed and compared. Among them, we integrated clinical indicators to construct the SVM model, and the AUC reached 0.874, indicating that the model has a strong ability to recognize positive samples; in the test set verification, the AUC was 0.774, and the AUC on the test set was slightly lower, but it still showed diagnostic stability across data sets, suggesting that SVM has a stronger discrimination ability under the current feature dimension. The diagnostic value of NCCT radiomics relies on the pathophysiological link between plaque composition and X-ray attenuation heterogeneity. Fibrocalcific plaques exhibit high local density variation, producing elevated entropy and contrast values, while lipid-rich cores yield lower mean attenuation and higher skewness. These microstructural variations translate into quantifiable gray-level patterns detectable by radiomics. Even in the absence of contrast agents, the imaging information contained in plain scan CT still has a high diagnostic value, especially in terms of vascular structural changes caused by carotid atherosclerosis. The radiomics features can capture potential pathological information such as grayscale unevenness and density changes. This finding has substantial significance for the optimization of imaging workflows, rational allocation of clinical resources, and enhancement of patient care experiences.

During the feature selection phase, LASSO regression was employed to identify a set of radiomic features highly associated with carotid stenosis [30], primarily derived from the GLCM and first-order statistical descriptors. These features reflect subtle heterogeneity in local density and texture uniformity. For instance, the GLCM-derived parameters, such as energy, entropy, and contrast, capture the spatial complexity of tissue architecture, while the first-order statistics, including the mean, skewness, and kurtosis, represent overall grayscale distribution trends. We observed greater texture irregularity and more dispersed grayscale values within the stenotic regions, patterns consistent with underlying pathological processes such as plaque deposition, calcification, and lipid core formation [31]. Thus, these radiomic features are not only statistically discriminative but may also reflect the microstructural basis of stenosis at a pathophysiological level [32]. The nomogram constructed in this study integrated clinical indicators with radiomic features for the first time, achieving a visual prediction of the risk of carotid stenosis. Clinical indicators made up for the missing information of imaging features in the "disease process background" and constructed a more comprehensive diagnostic dimension.

A key advantage of radiomics lies in its ability to non-invasively quantify tissue heterogeneity. Traditional imaging evaluation often relies on manual measurements or subjective visual interpretation, which may overlook latent high-dimensional signals. Our results demonstrate that even NCCT images—despite their relatively limited spatial resolution [17]—contain quantifiable grayscale and texture information sufficient for disease classification [18]. This is especially valuable in clinical cases in which contrast administration is contraindicated, such as in patients with iodine allergy, renal insufficiency, or frailty. From a modeling perspective, the SVM model is well suited for small-sample, high-dimensional classification tasks, offering robust decision boundaries and generalization capacity. We applied cross-validation strategies to mitigate overfitting and ensure model stability. Additionally, the clear separation between the positive and negative cases in the reduced feature space further supports the discriminative power of the selected features. SVM's ability to handle high-dimensional feature spaces with a relatively small sample size made it the best choice for this study. SVM was chosen as the primary model due to its superior ability to handle high-dimensional feature spaces, as evidenced by its performance in our results. While other models, such as Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, are also viable options, SVM demonstrated the best balance of accuracy and generalizability in this study.

In clinical practice, head and neck NCCT is frequently performed as part of acute stroke evaluation due to its accessibility, speed, and cost-effectiveness. When patients undergo routine head and neck plain CT scans, combined with radiomics analysis, a preliminary judgment on the degree of carotid artery stenosis can be made, thereby reducing reliance on contrast-enhanced CT. Furthermore, through the "Points mapping" of the nomogram, the complex radiomics model can be simplified into an intuitive tool suitable for clinical practice. Our retrospective analysis revealed that approximately 38% of patients underwent contrast-enhanced scans despite lacking strong risk indicators for stenosis, resulting in an unnecessary patient burden, radiation exposure, and contrast-related risks. The radiomics approach proposed in this study, if integrated into routine CT interpretation workflows, could automatically flag patients at high risk for stenosis following plain CT, thereby reducing unwarranted contrast use, streamlining imaging pathways, and improving diagnostic efficiency and patient compliance.

From a clinical perspective, false-negative results may delay appropriate interventions, whereas false-positive predictions could lead to unnecessary imaging or anxiety. Integrating the model into a tiered diagnostic pathway—serving as an initial screening tool before confirmatory CTA—could mitigate these risks and enhance overall workflow safety.

Compared with existing domestic and international studies, most radiomics analyses of carotid artery stenosis still rely on contrast-enhanced CTA or CT images [33]. While these modalities offer superior anatomical detail and lumen reconstruction, their acquisition is invasive and costly, limiting their utility for mass screening. Conversely, the use of NCCT as the imaging source enhances accessibility and applicability, particularly in primary care settings and in elderly or high-risk populations with contraindications to contrast agents. This represents an important advancement in expanding radiomics application from research to real-world vascular imaging.

Technically, the current study relied on the manual delineation of ROIs, albeit this was assisted by enhanced images to improve localization consistency. Nonetheless, subjectivity remains an issue, and could potentially affect feature reproducibility and standardization. Future work should explore the use of automated or semi-automated segmentation techniques [34] to enhance efficiency and inter-operator consistency.

Importantly, as artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted diagnostic systems continue to evolve, radiomics models could be embedded into radiology workstations as real-time analytical tools, aiding clinicians in the rapid detection and classification of vascular lesions [35]. In chronic disease management scenarios, such models may also support routine screening or postoperative surveillance of high-risk individuals, which in turn could reduce missed diagnoses and enhance the precision of stroke prevention strategies [36].

However, the study also contained some limitations. To begin with, a small sample size, particularly in the test set, may constrain the generalizability of the model. Second, this was a single-center retrospective study, with all scans acquired from the same CT device using consistent protocols [37]. External validation across multiple centers, scanner types, and reconstruction kernels is needed to confirm robustness. Additionally, image quality variations inherent to NCCT, such as limited spatial resolution and contrast differentiation, might have affected the feature extraction accuracy. Establishing standardized preprocessing pipelines is essential to reduce such variability. Finally, while the SVM model demonstrated strong classification performance, it remains relatively opaque from a clinical interpretability perspective. Future research could incorporate model explainability techniques, such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) or Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) [38], to identify key predictive features and enhance clinician trust in AI-based tools [39]. To enhance the generalizability of our findings, future studies will include multi-center validation using varied imaging equipment to assess the robustness of the model across different clinical settings.

From an ethical and practical standpoint, the clinical deployment of AI models must address issues such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, and responsibility attribution [40, 41]. For instance, multi-center data aggregation should adhere to data de-identification protocols and privacy regulations, while minimizing demographic or regional biases to ensure equitable diagnostic performance across populations. Moreover, a clearly defined human–AI collaboration framework is essential, whereby AI serves as a supportive tool rather than a replacement for clinical decision-making, facilitating safe, sustainable integration into medical practice [42].

In conclusion, this study proposed a radiomics-based strategy leveraging NCCT images for the non-invasive identification of carotid artery stenosis [25]. The model showed promising diagnostic performance, clinical utility, and scalability [3]. This study confirms that the "clinical indicators + SVM" strategy holds promise in real-world clinical settings. Going forward, by expanding the sample size, dynamically updating indicators (e.g., monitoring changes in clinical parameters during disease progression), optimizing feature engineering processes, and strengthening model validation, we expect to further develop the method into a generalizable auxiliary diagnostic tool. Such a tool could be particularly valuable in scenarios where contrast imaging is contraindicated, resources are limited, or frequent evaluations are needed. This work contributes to the establishment of a non-invasive, efficient, and precise paradigm for early vascular disease screening and management.

Conclusions

This research constructed a radiomics-based SVM model with routine NCCT images to effectively identify carotid artery stenosis without the need for contrast agents. The model demonstrated high diagnostic performance and stable generalizability. Our findings suggest that grayscale and texture features embedded in plain CT images contain valuable pathological information, particularly in terms of capturing vascular wall alterations, offering strong discriminatory power. This non-invasive, accessible, and cost-effective approach holds significant promise as an auxiliary tool for the early screening of carotid stenosis, especially in patients with contraindications to contrast-enhanced imaging. To further promote the model's robustness, generalizability, and level of automation, future research should focus on expanding the sample size, conducting external validation across multiple centers, and integrating automated segmentation and multimodal fusion strategies. These advancements will be critical for translating this technique into a clinically viable, widely applicable AI-assisted diagnostic tool for vascular disease screening and management. The approach is expected to evolve into a scalable auxiliary diagnostic tool—one that will demonstrate unique value particularly in clinical scenarios where contrast-enhanced examinations are limited.
