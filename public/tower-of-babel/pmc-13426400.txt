Robot-Assisted Distal Pancreatectomy for Solid Pseudopapillary Neoplasm of Pancreas
Authors: Fernando Revoredo Rego, Wilder Calmet Berrocal, Mónica Uribe León, Jorge Valdés Gómez, Alfonso Chacaltana Mendoza, Carlos Alberto Wong Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Distal pancreatectomy is the standard surgical approach for lesions located in the body and tail of the pancreas. Minimally invasive techniques, including laparoscopic and robotic approaches, have been increasingly adopted. We report a case of robot-assisted distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy performed using the Da Vinci Xi platform in a 40-year-old female patient with a solid pseudopapillary neoplasm. The operative time was 340 minutes, the intraoperative blood loss was 150 mL, and the console time was 236 minutes. The postoperative course was complicated by a clinically relevant type B pancreatic fistula, which was managed conservatively. This case demonstrates the feasibility of robotic distal pancreatectomy, even during the initial learning phase, when performed in appropriately selected patients.

INTRODUCTION

Distal pancreatectomy (DP), also known as left pancreatectomy, is the procedure of choice for the treatment of symptomatic benign (serous cystic neoplasm), premalignant (intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm, mucinous cystic neoplasm, neuroendocrine tumors), and malignant (pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, solid pseudopapillary neoplasm) lesions located in the body and tail of the pancreas.1 Traditionally, this operation has been performed using an open approach.2 However, the development of minimally invasive techniques has led to the progressive adoption of laparoscopic DP (LDP) and robot-assisted DP (RDP), the use of which has increased steadily over recent decades.3

Several randomized controlled trials1,4 have demonstrated that minimally invasive approaches are associated with reduced intraoperative blood loss, a lower incidence of delayed gastric emptying, faster functional recovery, and a shorter length of hospital stay compared with open surgery.

Despite these advantages, LDP has inherent technical limitations, including two-dimensional visualization and the use of rigid instruments with restricted mobility.5 Robotic platforms have addressed many of these limitations by providing high-definition three-dimensional visualization, instruments with an extended range of motion,6,7 elimination of physiological tremor, and improved ergonomics for the surgeon.2 These features translate into greater precision during complex procedures such as vascular dissection and intracorporeal suturing.6,7

Even though more than two decades of development in robotic surgery, its implementation in Peru remains recent and has driven primarily by private-sector investment. In this context, we present our initial experience with an RDP using the Da Vinci Xi platform.

CASE REPORT

A 40-year-old woman with a body mass index (BMI) of 25 kg/m2 was incidentally found to have a cystic lesion in the pancreatic body. Computed tomography showed a heterogeneous nodular lesion measuring 2.3 cm with peripheral contrast enhancement. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a 2.6 cm cystic lesion with an enhancing solid component with diffusion restriction (Figure 1).

(A, B) CT scan showing a 2.3 cm heterogeneous nodular lesion (yellow arrow). (C) T2-weighted MRI. (D) T1-weighted MRI showing a 2.6 cm nodular lesion (yellow arrow) with a cystic component and restricted diffusion small area (E). (F) ADC map.

Endoscopic ultrasound revealed a 2.8 cm heterogeneous lesion with a 1.4-cm solid component. Fine-needle aspiration biopsy (22G) showed high-grade dysplasia (Figure 2). Cyst fluid glucose was 96 mg/dL. Serum tumor markers were within normal limits (CEA 1.27 ng/mL, CA 19-9 4.61 U/mL). An initial diagnosis of solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas versus mucinous cystic neoplasm of the pancreas with high-risk stigmata was proposed.

(A) Endoscopic ultrasonography demonstrating a 2.8 cm anechoic lesion in the body of the pancreas, with a 1.4-cm solid component (arrowheads). (B) Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy using a 22-gauge needle. (C) Color Doppler examination showing no vascular signal.

After completion of vaccination against Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Neisseria meningitidis, a RDP with en bloc splenectomy was performed using the Da Vinci Xi platform. Total operative time was 340 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 150 mL and a console time of 236 minutes. The patient was discharged on postoperative day seven with a surgical drain in place.

On postoperative day 15, the patient developed a type B postoperative pancreatic fistula8 requiring readmission, drain replacement and intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics for five days. The subsequent course was uneventful, and the drain was removed during outpatient follow-up.

Histopathological analysis confirmed a solid pseudopapillary neoplasm with negative resection margins. Immunohistochemistry staining expressed β-catenin and SOX11 (Figure 3). At nine months of follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic and has fully resumed her usual activities.

Surgical Technique7

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned in reverse Trendelenburg (14–16°) with a 4–6° tilt to the right. Pneumoperitoneum was established at Palmer’s point. Four 8-mm robotic trocars were placed in the upper abdomen, along with one 12-mm assistant trocar and two additional 5-mm trocars for assistance and liver retraction (Figure 4).

Schematic illustration of robotic port (trocar) placement, including robotic trocars (T1, T2, T3, and T4), auxiliary laparoscopic trocars (T5 and T6) and the retractor trocar (T7).

Docking of the robotic cart was performed from the patient’s left side. A fenestrated bipolar Maryland forceps, endoscopic camera, monopolar hook and Cadiere forceps were used.

The gastrocolic ligament was opened, and the short gastric vessels were divided with LigaSure® (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), exposing the anterior surface of the lesion and distal pancreas. After identification of the left gastric pedicle and splenic artery, the splenic artery was controlled with Hem-o-lok® clips (two proximal and one distal) and divided. The inferior pancreatic border was mobilized, and a retropancreatic tunnel was developed medial to the lesion. The splenic vein was then dissected, controlled with Hem-o-lok® clips (two proximal and one distal), and divided. Pancreatic transection was performed through the 12-mm assistant trocar using an Echelon® laparoscopic stapler (Johson & Johnson, Cincinnati, OH, USA) with a blue cartridge, according to Nakamura’s prolonged-firing technique.9 Briefly, the stapler was closed gradually to compress the pancreatic parenchyma for 3 minutes, fired, and maintained closed for additional 2 minutes before completing transection. A medial-to-lateral resection was performed anterior to Gerota’s fascia, including the spleen, analogous to radical antegrade modular pancreatectomy (RAMPS).10 Lymphadenectomy include stations 11, 18, and 10. The specimen was retrieved in an Endo bag® through a Pfannenstiel incision. The pancreatic stump and operative field were inspected for hemostasis, and two closed-suction tubular drains were placed through T4 (Figure 5).

It is important to emphasize that surgeons undertaking robotic DP should have prior experience in open and LDP, ideally within high-volume centers, as well as adequate structured training in robotic pancreatic surgery.

DISCUSSION

Available evidence comparing RDP with LDP suggests that RDP is associated with lower conversion rates, higher rates of splenic preservation, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and shorter hospital stay, with comparable oncological outcomes, albeit at a higher initial cost.2,6 From an economic perspective, although RDP involves higher operative costs, these may be partially offset by a shorter hospital stay and by standardization of the technique, using a robot-assisted technique instead of a pure robotic technique (in this approach laparoscopic energy devices are not employed),11 as we describe in the present case.

Benchmarks have been proposed for measuring outcomes of RDP.12 Our results regarding blood loss, conversion, postoperative delayed gastric emptying, postoperative hemorrhage, intensive care unit stay, and length of stay were in accordance with the reported benchmarks. Our operative time was 340 minutes, exceeding the benchmark (300 minutes). As with any surgical innovation, operative time in robotic procedures depends not only on the surgeon’s technical expertise but also on the institutional learning curve, including docking, instrumentation, and bedside assistance. We believe that operative time will improve with increasing experience and case accumulation.

In this case, spleen preservation was not considered because our preoperative diagnosis was solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas (low-grade malignant pancreatic tumor)13 versus mucinous cystic neoplasm with high-risk stigmata (enhancing 14-mm solid component with diffusion restriction).14 In both cases, an oncological resection with standard lymphadenectomy is suggested.

Clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula remains the leading cause of morbidity following DP, with an incidence of up to one-third of patients,12 and there is no difference in the postoperative pancreatic fistula rate comparing the open and the laparoscopic or robotic approaches.15 This complication may arise because the pancreatic remnant continues to secrete enzymatic fluid after transection. When closure of the pancreatic stump is not completely hermetic, pancreatic juice can leak into the abdominal cavity and become activated, particularly in the setting of contamination or local inflammation. Activated pancreatic enzymes may then digest adjacent fat, vascular structures, and surrounding tissues, leading to fluid collections, abscess formation, vascular erosion, and delayed hemorrhage.16 Consequently, postoperative morbidity increases, hospital length of stay is prolonged, and patients are more likely to require percutaneous drainage, antibiotic therapy, nutritional support, reoperation, or readmission, consuming healthcare resources and thereby increasing cost.15,17 In oncologic patients, this complication may also delay the initiation of adjuvant chemotherapy.16

Recently, pancreatic fistula risk scores specific to DP (distal pancreatectomy fistula risk score [D-FRS]) have been developed in both preoperative and intraoperative settings,17 with the aim of estimating the risk of developing a postoperative pancreatic fistula. The preoperative D-FRS includes the pancreatic neck thickness and pancreatic duct size. The intraoperative D-FRS adds BMI, pancreatic texture and operative time. These values enter a formula that uses a logistic regression model, which is available at pancreascalculator.com. both values are used to estimate the absolute risk and to determine the risk category (low risk: <10%, intermediate risk: 10–25%, and high risk: >25%). In the present case, both the preoperative fistula risk score of 31% and intraoperative fistula risk score of 40% indicated a high risk, which explains the observed complication.

Despite various strategies proposed to reduce the incidence of pancreatic fistula (suture ligation of pancreatic duct, use of staple line reinforcement, tissue patches, biologic sealants or prophylactic somatostatin analogues), none has consistently demonstrated a significant reduction in this complication.18 Conservative management remains the standard approach for clinically stable patients.

In conclusion, this case report demonstrates that RDP during the initial learning phase is feasible in selected patients, however, high fistula risk remains a major limitation and should guide patient selection.
