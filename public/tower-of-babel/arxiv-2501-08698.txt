On the generalized coloring numbers
Sebastian Siebertz

Abstract
The coloring number col ⁡ ( G ) \col(G) of a graph G G , which is equal to the degeneracy of G G plus one, provides a very useful measure for the uniform sparsity of G G . The coloring number is generalized by three series of measures, the generalized coloring numbers . These are the r r -admissibility adm r ⁡ ( G ) \adm_{r}(G) , the strong r r -coloring number col r ⁡ ( G ) \col_{r}(G) and the weak r r -coloring number wcol r ⁡ ( G ) \wcol_{r}(G) , where r r is an integer parameter. The generalized coloring numbers measure the edge density of bounded-depth minors and thereby provide an even more uniform measure of sparsity of graphs. They have found many applications in graph theory and in particular play a key role in the theory of bounded expansion and nowhere dense graph classes introduced by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez. We overview combinatorial and algorithmic applications of the generalized coloring numbers, emphasizing new developments in this area. We also present a simple proof for the existence of uniform orders and improve known bounds, e.g., for the weak coloring numbers on graphs with excluded topological minors.

On the other hand, the strong

By now, the generalized coloring numbers have found many
applications in graph theory, and play
a key role in the theory of bounded expansion and nowhere

Bounded expansion and nowhere dense classes are intimately linked to first-order logic and many of the developments were driven by the question to solve the first-order model checking problem on these classes. Structural decompositions derived from the generalized coloring numbers lead to quantifier-elimination, and as a consequence to efficient model checking as shown by Dvořák, Král’ and Thomas
[
55

[

Game characterizations and sparse neighborhood covers lead to tractable first-order model checking on nowhere dense classes as shown by Grohe et al.
[
78

Hence, the classification of tractable first-order model checking on monotone graph classes is complete and leaves the question of a classification of tractability on hereditary graph classes.

The deep connections of nowhere dense classes to classical model theory (stability theory) are currently guiding an exciting development of dense but structurally sparse graph classes.
Stability is one of the key notions from classical model theory (see the monumental work of Shelah

This connection is highly fruitful and many notions from model theory can be appropriately translated to graph theory and turn out to be dense analogs of well known concepts from sparsity theory.

Another recent exciting development evolves around the graph product structure theory for planar graphs, initiated by Dujmović et al.
[
48

, which was inspired by the techniques developed for the construction of low treedepth colorings for classes with excluded minors by Pilipczuk and Siebertz
[

2.1
Treewidth and the strong
r
-coloring numbers

The
generalized coloring numbers

\col_{r}(G)
and
\wcol_{r}(G)

were introduced by Kierstead and Yang
[
94
]

as generalizations of the usual coloring number. Here, we take
a different perspective and introduce the strong
r
-coloring number

\col_{r}(G)
as a local version of
treewidth
, while the

-coloring number
\wcol_{r}(G)
will be introduced in the
next subsection as a local version of

supports our view of these parameters as
measures for the local

of a graph, and sets the stage for the study of tree decomposable graphs.

Treewidth, as a measure of the global connectivity of a graph,
plays an important role in graph theory.
It was originally introduced by Bertelè and Brioschi under the name of dimension
[
10
]
.
It was rediscovered by Halin
[
80
]
, and again rediscovered by Robertson and Seymour
[
136
]
as part of their graph
minors project.
We refer to
[
13
,
82
]

for surveys on various characterizations of treewidth and
equivalent parameters and to
[
14
,
29
]

for surveys on algorithmic applications.

Definition 2.1
.

A
tree decomposition
of a graph
G
is a pair

\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathrm{Bag})
, where
T
is a tree and

\mathrm{Bag}\colon V(T)\to\mathcal{P}(V(G))
is a mapping that assigns each node

x
of
T
to its
bag

\mathrm{Bag}(x)\subseteq V(G)
so that the
following conditions are satisfied (see Figure 4):

1.

For each
u\in V(G)
, the set

\{x\mid u\in\mathrm{Bag}(x)\}
is non-empty and induces a connected
subtree of
T
.

2.

For every edge
uv\in E(G)
, there is a node
x\in V(T)
such
that
\{u,v\}\subseteq\mathrm{Bag}(x)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=(T,\mathrm{Bag})
be a tree decomposition of
G
.
The
width
of
\mathcal{T}
is the maximum bag size minus
1
, i.e.,
\max_{x\in V(T)}|\mathrm{Bag}(x)|-1

The

of a graph

is the minimum possible width of a tree decomposition of
G
. For

we let
\mathrm{Bag}(X)=\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{Bag}(x)

A tree decomposition of a graph. No edge can cross the intersection of two bags, which form separators in

In the following, it will be convenient to assume that

is a rooted tree. A

is a tree

with a designated root vertex

minimal element:

are its true ancestors and true descendants, respectively.
The
parent

of a non-root node is its closest true ancestor.
A

is a disjoint union of rooted trees. We write

for the partial order that coincides with

on each rooted

Tree decompositions capture the global connectivity properties of
graphs, as made precise next.

.

Let

Definition 2.9
.

Let

be a graph and

Lemma 2.10
.

Let

\pi
be an order of

Let

be any path of length

We have chosen to include it as some arguments become

2.2
Treedepth and the weak

The notion of treedepth was introduced by Nešetřil and
Ossona de Mendez

[

, and

[

Where intuitively treewidth measures the similarity

of a graph with a star.

The

.

Let

same vertex set as

or
v\leq_{F}u

Let

2.4
Logic and transductions

Let us recall some basics from first-order model theory. For extensive background we refer to Hodges’ textbook
[
83
]
.
A
signature
is a collection
of relation and function symbols, each with an associated arity. Let
\sigma
be a
signature. A
\sigma
-structure

\mathbb{A}
consists of a non-empty
set
A
, the
universe
of
\mathbb{A}
, together with an interpretation of
each
k
-ary relation symbol
R\in\sigma
as a
k
-ary relation

R^{\mathbb{A}}\subseteq A^{k}
and an interpretation of each
k
-ary function symbol
f\in\sigma
as a
k
-ary function
f^{\mathbb{A}}:A^{k}\rightarrow A
.
In this work we assume that all structures are finite (i.e. have a finite universe and a finite signature).

We now define first-order logic FO and monadic second-order logic MSO (over the signature
\sigma
). We assume an infinite supply
\textsc{Var}_{1}
of first-order variables (which will range over elements) and an infinite supply
\textsc{Var}_{2}
of monadic second-order variables (which will range over sets of elements).
Every variable is a term, and if
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
are terms and
f\in\sigma
is a
k
-ary function symbol, then also
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
is a term.
First-order formulas are built from the atomic formulas
t_{1}=t_{2}
, where
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are terms, and
R(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
, where
R\in\sigma

is a
k
-ary relation symbol
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
are terms, by closing under the Boolean
connectives
\neg
,
\wedge
and
\vee
, and by existential and
universal quantification
\exists x
and
\forall x
.
Monadic second-order formulas are defined as first-order formulas, but further
allow the use of monadic quantifiers
\exists X
and
\forall X
, and of a membership atomic formula
x\in X
, where
x
is a first-order variable and
X
a monadic second-order variable.

A variable
x
not in the scope of a quantifier is a
free variable
(we do not consider formulas with free second-order variables). A formula without free variables is a
sentence
.
The
quantifier rank

\mathrm{qr}(\varphi)
of a formula
\varphi
is the
maximum nesting depth of quantifiers in
\varphi
.
A formula without quantifiers is called
quantifier-free
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is a
\sigma
-structure
with universe
A
, then an
assignment
of the variables in
\mathbb{A}

is a mapping
\bar{a}:\textsc{Var}\rightarrow A
. We use the standard
notation
(\mathbb{A},\bar{a})\models\varphi(\bar{x})
or
\mathbb{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a})

to indicate that
\varphi
is satisfied in
\mathbb{A}
when the free variables
\bar{x}

of
\varphi
have been assigned by
\bar{a}
.
We write
\models\varphi
to express that
\varphi
is a valid sentence, that is,
\varphi
holds in every structure (of an appropriate signature).
For a formula
\varphi(\bar{x})
we define
\varphi(\mathbb{A}):=\{\bar{a}\in A^{|\bar{x}|}\mid\mathbb{A}\models\varphi(\bar{a})\}
.

In this work we consider the following types of structures:

1.

Colored graphs
are
\sigma
-structures, where
\sigma
consists of a single binary relation
E
and unary relations, with the property that

E
is symmetric and anti-reflexive.

2.

Guided pointer structures
are
\sigma
-structures, where
\sigma
consists of a single binary relation
E
, unary relations, and unary functions, with the property that

is symmetric and anti-reflexive, and that every function
f\in\sigma
is
guided
, meaning that if

, then

or

.

3.

Ordered graphs
are
\sigma
-structures, where
\sigma
consists of a two binary relations
E
and

, with the property that
E
is symmetric and anti-reflexive, and

is a linear order.

4.

Partial orders
are
\sigma
-structures, where
\sigma
consists of binary relation
\sqsubseteq
that is interpreted as a partial order, in many cases a linear order or tree order.

Let
\sigma,\tau
be relational signatures and let
\mathcal{L}
be one of
\mathrm{FO}
or
\mathrm{MSO}
.
An
\mathcal{L}
-interpretation

\mathsf{I}
of
\sigma
-structures in
\tau
-structures is a tuple
\mathsf{I}=(\nu(\bar{x}),(\rho_{R}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{ar(R)}))_{R\in\sigma})

\rho_{R}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{ar(R)})

-formulas, and

|\bar{x}|=|\bar{x}_{i}|
for all
1\leq i\leq ar(R)
for all
R\in\sigma

is the arity of

.
For every
\tau
-structure
\mathbb{B}
, the
\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{A}=\mathsf{I}(\mathbb{B})

has the universe
\nu(\mathbb{B})
and each relation
R_{\mathbb{A}}
is interpreted as
\rho_{R}(\mathbb{B})
.
We say that
\nu

defines
the universe and
\rho_{R}
defines the relation

of
\mathsf{I}(\mathbb{B})
.
The number of free variables

of

of the interpretation.
A

In particular, a simple interpretation

\tau

A

[

of graphs has bounded treewidth if and only if the class of incidence graphs of graphs from

[
25
]

In this section, we present lower and upper bounds for

linked to the density of bounded-depth minors and bounded-depth topological minors. These are the central notions in the theory of bounded expansion
and nowhere dense graph classes, which were introduced by
Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[

,

]

and depth-

Throughout this work when

We then

[

[

to compute

in terms of the density of depth-

topological minors, which

[

and prove the existence

was first proved by Van den Heuvel and Kierstead
[

]
.

We then discuss the approach of Grohe et al.
[

to approximate the weak

-coloring numbers via transitive fraternal augmentations, introduced by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[

]
.

Note that if

This requires branch sets of large radius though, and as we will see, bounded-depth minors and bounded-depth topological minors behave quite similarly in important aspects.
Most importantly, the edge densities and clique numbers of graphs that we can find as bounded-depth minors and bounded-depth topological minors are functionally related.

Definition 3.3

Let

of

The

\nabla_{r}(G)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\frac{|E(H)|}{|V(H)|}\ \colon\ H\preccurlyeq_{r}G\right\},

\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\frac{|E(H)|}{|V(H)|}\ \colon\ H\preccurlyeq_{r}^{top}G\right\}.

Similarly, we define

\omega_{r}(G)\coloneqq\sup\{t\ \colon\ K_{t}\preccurlyeq_{r}G\}

as the largest clique that appears as a depth-
r
minor, and

\widetilde{\omega}_{r}(G)\coloneqq\sup\{t\ \colon\ K_{t}\preccurlyeq_{r}^{top}G\}

as the largest clique that appears as a topological depth-

Note that

is equal to the maximum edge density over all subgraphs of

and we derive the following lemma for the chromatic number

of

Lemma 3.4
.

\chi(G)\leq 2\nabla_{0}(G)+1
. If

and if

, then
\chi(H)\leq 2\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)+1

We refer to the functions

and

as the

and

of

, respectively.
As proved by Dvořák
[
49
]
, these measures
satisfy

\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)\leq\nabla_{r}(G)\leq 4\big(4\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)\big)^{(r+1)^{2}}.

Hence, expansion and topological expansion are functionally equivalent.
The key observation for the proof is that in the branch set of a high-degree vertex in a bounded-depth minor model, we will always find a vertex of large degree. We can use such a vertex as the branching vertex of a high-degree vertex in a topological minor model.

The functions

r\mapsto\omega_{r}(G)
and
r\mapsto\widetilde{\omega}_{r}(G)

are called the
\omega
-expansion
and
topological
\omega
-expansion
of

As shown by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[

also the
\omega

are functionally related, see
[

for the following bounds.

\widetilde{\omega}_{r}(G)\leq\omega_{r}(G)\leq 1+\widetilde{\omega}_{3r+1}(G)^{2r+2}.

The above notions extend to classes

of graphs
by defining
\nabla_{r}(\mathscr{C})=\sup_{G\in\mathscr{C}}\nabla_{r}(G)
,

\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(\mathscr{C})=\sup_{G\in\mathscr{C}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)

\omega(\mathscr{C})=\sup_{G\in\mathscr{C}}\omega_{r}(G)
, and

\widetilde{\omega}_{r}(\mathscr{C})=\sup_{G\in\mathscr{C}}\widetilde{\omega}_{r}(G)

We can now define bounded expansion and nowhere dense graph classes.

Definition 3.5

A class

of graphs has

Definition 3.6
.

A class
\mathscr{C}

Bounded-depth minors in classes of bounded expansion have constant edge density by
definition. It turns out that also bounded-depth minors in nowhere dense classes have small edge density.

(
[

Let

The following theorem is implicit in the work of Dvořák

Theorem 3.14

(

Let

\adm_{r}(G)

-fan set in

Proof.

If

-fan set in

.
To see this, let

be any order of

be the maximal

with one endpoint in

that are internally disjoint

and such that

How can it possibly be hard to compute the values

According to Menger’s theorem the number of vertex disjoint paths between two vertices

for

(we start with at least
k+1
paths by definition of a
(k+1,r)
-fan and by dropping the path of length
0
we are left with at least
k
paths with one endpoint in
A\setminus\{v\}
).

Choose a maximal set
\mathcal{P}
of pairwise internally
vertex-disjoint paths of length at most
2r-1
connecting pairs of
distinct vertices from
A
whose internal vertices belong to

V(G)\setminus A
such that each such pair is connected by at
most one path from
\mathcal{P}
. Let
H
be the graph with vertex set
A
and edges between all vertices
v,w\in A
connected by some path in
\mathcal{P}
. Then
H\preccurlyeq_{r-1}^{top}G
and hence

|\mathcal{P}|=|E(H)|\leq s\cdot c
. Let
M
be the set of all
internal vertices of the paths in
\mathcal{P}
, and let

m\coloneqq|M|
. Then
m\leq s\cdot c\cdot(2r-2)
.

By
Lemma

3.4

H
is
(2c+1)
-colorable and hence contains an independent set
R
of size at least
\left\lceil s/(2c+1)\right\rceil
.
For
v\in R
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{v}
be the set of initial
segments of paths in
\mathcal{P}_{v}
between
v
and a vertex in

(M\cup A)\setminus\{v\}
with all internal vertices in

V(G)\setminus(M\cup A)
. Observe that for
u,v\in R
the paths in

\mathcal{Q}_{v}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{u}
are internally vertex disjoint:
if
P\in\mathcal{Q}_{u}
and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{v}
had an internal vertex
in common, then
P\cup Q
would contain a path of length at most

2r-2
that is internally disjoint from all paths in
\mathcal{P}
,
contradicting the maximality of
\mathcal{P}
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertices of the paths in
\mathcal{P}
and the paths in
\mathcal{Q}_{v}
for
v\in R
. Let
H^{\prime}
be the graph obtained from
G^{\prime}
by
contracting for each
v\in R
all paths in
\mathcal{Q}_{v}
to single
edges. Then
H^{\prime}\preceq^{t}_{r-1}G
.
We have

|V(H^{\prime})|\leq s+m\leq s+s\cdot c\cdot(2r-2)\leq s\cdot c\cdot(2r-1)

and at least

|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\left\lceil s/(2c+1)\right\rceil\cdot k

edges. Thus, the edge density of
H^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle\frac{|E(H^{\prime})|}{|V(H^{\prime})|}\geq\frac{\left\lceil s/(2c+1)\right\rceil\cdot k}{s\cdot c\cdot(2r-1)}\geq\frac{s\cdot k}{s\cdot c\cdot(2c+1)\cdot(2r-1)}\geq\frac{6rc^{3}+1}{(2c^{2}+c)\cdot 2r}\geq\frac{6rc^{3}+1}{3c^{2}\cdot 2r}>c.

A contradiction, showing that
k\leq 6rc^{3}
.
∎

Corollary 3.18
.

A class
\mathscr{C}
of graphs has bounded expansion if and only
if there exist functions
a,c
and
w
such that for every positive

and every

we have

\adm_{r}(G)\leq a(r)
,
\col_{r}(G)\leq c(r)
and
\wcol_{r}(G)\leq w(r)
.

Observe that the function bounding the
r
-admissibility
in
Theorem

3.14
is polynomial in

and
\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G)
.
One may wonder if a similar relation holds between

and
\nabla_{r}(G)
. The following problem is attributed to
Joret and Wood in
[

.

Problem 3.19
.

Does there exist a polynomial
p(x,y)
such that for all
graphs
G
and all positive integers

we have

\col_{r}(G)\leq p(r,\nabla_{r}(G))
?

The problem appears already in bounded degree graphs.

Does there exist a polynomial

3.3
Universal orders

According to
Corollary

3.18
, if
\mathscr{C}
has bounded
expansion, then there exists a function
f
such that
for every value of
r
and every
G\in\mathscr{C}
there exists
an order
\pi
of
V(G)
such that
\adm_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
.
A universal order is an order that works for every value
of
r
at the same time.

Definition 3.21
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
f
be a function. An order

\pi
of
V(G)
is a
universal order
bounded by

f
if
\adm_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
for every non-negative integer
r
.

By
Lemma

2.21
we could equivalently ask that

\col_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
or
\wcol_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
in the
above definition. It was shown by Van den Heuvel and
Kierstead
[
148
]
that all classes of bounded expansion admit
universal orders. In this section, we provide an alternative proof.

For this, we consider a variant of
the iterative procedure presented in the proof of
Theorem

3.14

to construct the order
\pi=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
. In the
procedure of
Theorem

3.14
,
Theorem

3.17
guarantees
that in step
i
we will always find a vertex
v_{i}

with back-connectivity
b_{r}(S_{i},v_{i})\leq 6r(\widetilde{\nabla}_{r}(G))^{3}
.
We are going to prove that we do not only find one vertex with
small back-connectivity, but in fact, a large fraction of the
vertices have a
back-connectivity that is only slightly larger than the optimal
back-connectivity. For every value of
r
, there will hence be
only a few vertices violating the
r
-admissibility constraint,
so that we are still left with at least one vertex that can
be inserted as
v_{i}
without violating the constraint for
any value of
r
. The proof of the following lemma is
a variation of the proof of
Theorem

3.17
(only the numbers need to be adapted), which is presented
by Pilipczuk et al.
[
131
]
(the arXiv version of
[
132
]
).

Lemma 3.22

(
[
131
]
, Lemma 14)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
S\subseteq V(G)
. Let
\varepsilon>0

and let
r
be a positive integer. Then at most
\varepsilon\cdot|S|
vertices
v\in S
have
b_{r}(S,v)>6\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot r\cdot\bigl(\widetilde{\nabla}_{r-1}(G)\bigr)^{3}
.

We are ready to prove the existence of universal orders.

Theorem 3.23
.

Let
G
be a graph and let

f(r)\coloneqq 2^{r+1}\cdot 6r\cdot\bigr(\widetilde{\nabla}_{r-1}(G)\bigl)^{3}
.
Then
G
admits an order
\pi
with

\adm_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
for every positive integer
r
.

Proof.

We adapt the iterative procedure presented in the proof of
Theorem

3.14
to an order
\pi=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
of
V(G)

with
\adm_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
for
all positive integers
r
.

We initialize
S_{n}\coloneqq V(G)
. Now, for
i=n,\ldots,1
,
if
v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}
have already been ordered, choose

v_{i}
such that if
S_{i}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\}
, then

b_{r}(S_{i},v_{i})\leq f(r)
for all positive integers
r
. Clearly,
if such a choice for
v_{i}
is possible, which remains to be shown, then
\pi
has the
desired properties.

According to
Lemma

3.22
, for any set
S
and any
positive integer
r
,
at most
1/(2^{r+1})\cdot|S|
vertices have back-connectivity

b_{r}(S,v)>f(r)
. Hence, at most
|S|/2
vertices
conflict
f
for the value
r=0
, at most
|S|/4
vertices
conflict
f
for the value
r=1
, and so on. Hence, at most

\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{r+1}}\cdot|S|<|S|,

vertices of
S
are conflicting
f
for some value of
r
. We
conclude that among all vertices of
S
, we find
at least one vertex
v_{i}
satisfying

b_{r}(S_{i},v_{i})\leq f(r)
for all values
of
r
.
∎

Corollary 3.24
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of bounded expansion. Then there exists
a function
f
such that for all
G\in\mathscr{C}
there exists an
order
\pi
with
\adm_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
for every positive
integer
r
.

We can use the approach of Dvořák to approximate the
values
b_{r}(S,v)
up to a factor of
r
and thereby, for every fixed class
\mathscr{C}
of bounded
expansion obtain an algorithm that computes a universal order
in polynomial time.

Corollary 3.25
.

Let
G
be a graph and let

f(r)\coloneqq 2^{r+1}\cdot 6r^{2}\cdot\bigr(\widetilde{\nabla}_{r-1}(G)\bigl)^{3}
.
Then we can compute in polynomial time an order
\pi
of
G
with

\adm_{r}(G,\pi)\leq f(r)
for every positive integer
r
.

3.4
Transitive fraternal augmentations

We now come to a second approach to approximate the generalized
coloring numbers, which is based on Nešetřil and Ossona
de Mendez’s transitive fraternal augmentation technique, see
Chapter 7.4 of
[
122
]
. In the following, for clarity, we will call directed edges
(u,v)

arcs
.

Definition 3.26
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
r
be a positive integer. An

r
-fraternity function
is a function
w\colon V(G)\times V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that for all distinct
u,v\in V(G)

at least one of
w(u,v)
and
w(v,u)
is
\infty
, such
that for all
uv\in E(G)
either
w(u,v)=1
or
w(v,u)=1
,
and such that for all distinct
u,v\in V(G)

1.

either
\min\{w(u,v),w(v,u)\}=1
,

2.

or
\min\{w(u,v),w(v,u)\}=\min_{z\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}(w(z,u)+w(z,v))
,

3.

or
\min\{w(u,v),w(v,u)\}>r
and

\min_{z\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}(w(z,u)+w(z,v))>r
.

If
w
is an
r
-fraternity function for every value of
r
,
then
w
is called a
fraternity function
(see Figure 12).

Figure 12:
A fraternity function on a path
P_{6}
. All missing pairs are assigned value
\infty
.

Definition 3.27
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
r
be a positive integer and let

w
be an
r
-fraternity function for
G
.
For
1\leq i\leq r
, the directed graph
\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq i}

on the same vertex set as
G
containing all arcs

(u,v)
with
w(u,v)\leq i
is called an

i
-fraternal augmentation
of
G
.

Obviously we have
\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq 1}\subseteq\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq 2}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq r}
. The arcs
of
\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq r}
that are not arcs of
\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq 1}

are called
fraternal arcs
.

We remark that fraternal augmentations
in
[
122
]

are defined so that the fraternal arcs in the
second and third item above are introduced between vertices
that have a common
in-neighbor, that is, in that work, it is required that

\min\{w(u,v),w(v,u)\}=\min_{z\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}(w(u,z)+w(v,z))
,
or if
\min\{w(u,v),w(v,u)\}>r
then

\min_{z\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}(w(u,z)+w(v,z))>r
.
We prefer our definition, as it highlights the connection
to the generalized coloring numbers. Let us make this connection
explicit.

Lemma 3.28
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
r
be a positive integer and let

\pi
be a linear order of
V(G)
. Then
w\colon V(G)\times V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
with

w(u,v)=\begin{cases}i&\text{if $i\leq r$ and $v\in\SReach_{i}[G,\pi,u]\setminus\SReach_{i-1}[G,\pi,u]$}\\
\infty&\text{if $v\not\in\SReach_{r}[G,\pi,u]$}.\end{cases}

is an
r
-fraternity function.

Proof.

It is clear that for all distinct
u,v\in V(G)

at least one of
w(u,v)
and
w(v,u)
is
\infty
and
that for all
uv\in E(G)
either
w(u,v)=1
or
w(v,u)=1
.
Now assume
w(u,v)=i
for some
i\geq 2
, hence,

v\in\SReach_{i}[G,\pi,u]\setminus\SReach_{i-1}[G,\pi,u]
. Then there exists a path
P
of
length
i
between
u
and
v
such that
v
is
minimum among
V(P)
with respect to
\pi
and such
that all internal vertices of
P
are larger than
u
.
Let
z
be the smallest vertex of
V(P)\setminus\{u,v\}
and let

\ell_{1}
be the distance between
z
and
v
on
P
and let
\ell_{2}
be the distance between
z
and
u
on
P
. Then
v\in\SReach_{\ell_{1}}[G,\pi,z]

and
u\in\SReach_{\ell_{2}}[G,\pi,z]
. Furthermore, there
does not exist
z^{\prime}
and
d_{1},d_{2}
with
d_{1}+d_{2}<i

such that
v\in\SReach_{d_{1}}[G,\pi,z^{\prime}]

and
u\in\SReach_{d_{2}}[G,\pi,z^{\prime}]
, as otherwise,
we could concatenate the paths witnessing this to find a
path of length
i-1
between
u
and
v
such that
v
is
minimum among
V(P)
and such
that all internal vertices of
P
are larger than
u
. Such a
path does not exist as
v\not\in\SReach_{i-1}[G,\pi,u]
.
In particular,
v\not\in\SReach_{\ell_{1}-1}[G,\pi,z]

and
u\not\in\SReach_{\ell_{2}-1}[G,\pi,z]
, hence,

w(z,u)=\ell_{1}
and
w(z,v)=\ell_{2}
and there is no

z^{\prime}
with
w(z^{\prime},u)+w(z^{\prime},v)<i
.
∎

Using

Lemma 3.30
.

Let
G
be a graph, let

be a positive integer and
let

be an

-fraternity function of
G
.
Let

and assume that

is a path of

between

and

in

. Then
one of the following holds.

There are indices

is a directed

to

and for all

.

2.

There are indices
0<i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}<\ell

such that

is a directed
path from
v
to
u
in

and for all

0\leq i_{m}<i_{n}\leq\ell
we have
w(v_{i_{n}},v_{i_{m}})\leq i_{n}-i_{m}

3.

There are indices
0<i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}<\ell
and
an index

such that

and

from

and from

, respectively. Furthermore, for all

and for all

w(v_{i_{n}},v_{i_{m}})\leq i_{n}-i_{m}
.

We prove the statement by induction on

. For

,
we have
(u,v)\in E(\vec{G}^{w}_{\leq 1})
if and only if

Now assume that

and assume the statements hold for

in

and the third item holds for

Now assume we are in the second case and there are indices

is a directed path from

to

in

we have a directed path

from

to

in

, that is,
the second case holds for

.
In the latter case, we apply the argument again to the sequence

(

taking the role of

from

to
u
in

, or
we apply the argument again. In some step, we either get

from

to

in

for some

, or
a directed arc
(u,v)
in
G^{w}_{\leq r}
. The additional property on

The argument in the third case is analogous to the argument
in the second case.
∎

3.30

Lemma 3.31

(adaptation of Lemma 6.7 of
[

]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph, let

be a positive integer and let

be an
r
-fraternity function of
G
. Assume that

G^{w}_{\leq r}
has maximum out-degree
\Delta
and let

. Then
\wcol_{r}(G)\leq 4d^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\vec{H}
be the directed graph that we obtain by
adding all edges
(u,v)
such that there is a directed path
of (weighted) length at most
r
from
u
to
v
in

. As
G^{w}_{\leq r}
has maximum out-degree
\Delta
,
\vec{H}
has out-degree at most
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\Delta^{i}\leq r\cdot\Delta^{r}=d
.
Furthermore,
Lemma

3.30
implies that
for all paths
u=v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}=v
of length
\ell\leq r

between
u
and
v
in

, either

1.

(u,v)\in E(\vec{H})
, or

2.

(v,u)\in E(\vec{H})
, or

there is an index
0<i<\ell
such that

(u,v_{i}),(v,v_{i})\in E(\vec{H})
.

As
\vec{H}
has out-degree at most

, the underlying undirected
graph

is
2d
-degenerate and there exists an order
\pi
of

V(H)
such that each vertex has at most
2d
smaller neighbors.
We claim that
\wcol_{r}(G,\pi)\leq 4d^{2}
.

To see this, we count for each vertex
u\in V(G)
the number of

from

such that
the end-vertex is the smallest vertex of the path. This number
bounds

.

By our above observation, for each such path
P

with end-vertex

, we either have an edge
(u,v)
or an
edge
(v,u)
or there is

on the path and we have arcs

in
\vec{H}
.

By construction of the order, there are at most
2d

edges
(u,v)
or
(v,u)
such that

. Furthermore,
we have at most
d
arcs
(u,z)
in
\vec{H}
, as

has
out-degree at most
d
. For each such

there are at
most
2d
edges
(v,z)
such that

by
construction of the order. As

requires that

is the minimum vertex on

, these are the

Now it is easy to give upper bounds for

Lemma 4.12

Let

It remains to prove that

-minor-free graphs admit flat connected partitions of small width. The proof goes back to a decomposition of

This name

Definition 4.13

A path

It will be the key to the planar product structure theory to partition the graph into globally isometric paths. The next lemma shows that isometric paths partitions are flat partitions.

Lemma 4.14

Let

.

Finally, based on the arguments of Abraham and Andreae

(
[

Let

This immediately leads to the following theorems.

Theorem 4.16

(

Let

(

Let

. For every graph

Van den Heuvel and Wood
[

started investigating whether the structure of the excluded minor can give even more insights on the generalized coloring numbers and they proved that for every

In fact, they prove that the exponent is bounded by the

Definition 4.18

4.2
Planar graph and graphs of bounded genus

In this section, we look at upper bounds for

and

Since for every genus

First, it was shown that planar graphs have isometric paths partitions of width

Lemma 4.20

(
[
150
]
)
.

Every planar graph

has an isometric paths partition of

For a graph of genus

. By arranging these vertices first in the linear order, we obtain the following bounds.

Theorem 4.21

(
[

]
)
.

For a planar graph

\wcol_{r}(G)\leq\binom{r+2}{2}\cdot(2r+1)\in\mathcal{O}(r^{3}),

and for a graph

\wcol_{r}(G)\leq\biggl(2g+\binom{r+2}{2}\biggr)\cdot(2r+1)\in\mathcal{O}(r^{3}+gr).

In case the treewidth of

is bounded the following improved bounds hold, as proved by Hodor et al.
[

]
.

Theorem 4.22

(
[
84
]
)
.

For all non-negative integers

such that for every graph

This bound is tight for planar graphs of bounded treewidth. It is one of the main open problems whether in general the weak coloring numbers of planar graphs is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(r^{2}\cdot\log r)
.

Problem 4.23

Is it true that
\wcol_{r}(G)\in\mathcal{O}(r^{2}\log r)
for every planar graph

?

From the isometric paths partitions for planar graphs, one would already obtain good bounds also for the strong coloring numbers. A more careful construction (also based on isometric paths partitions) and analysis gives the following bounds.

Theorem 4.24

(
[
150
]
)

For a planar graph

\col_{r}(G)\leq 5r+1,

and for a graph

For quite a while it was an open question whether the

Theorem 4.25

(

The proof of

To study

4.4
Graph product structure theory

An isometric paths partition
\mathcal{P}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
of small width provides already some insight into the treelike structure of a graph.
The main shortcoming of these partitions is that a path induced by
V_{i}
is not globally isometric but only isometric in the subgraph
G-\bigcup_{j<i}V_{j}
.
In the study of
p
-centered colorings (which we will consider in
Section

6
) it was proved by Pilipczuk and Siebertz that planar graphs actually admit isometric paths partitions of width at most
8
such that each path of the partition is a globally isometric path
[
127
]
.
This result was further strengthened by Dujmović et al.
[
48
]
, who showed the following result. Consider a tree
T
rooted at a node
r
. A path
v_{1},\ldots,v_{t}
in
T
is called

if for some

and for all

we have
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(v_{i},r)=d+i
.

Theorem 4.28

(
[
48
]
)
.

Let
T
be a rooted spanning tree in a connected planar graph

. Then
G
has a partition
\mathcal{P}
into vertical paths in

such that

has treewidth at most
8
.

Note that if

is a BFS spanning tree, then all vertical paths in

are isometric paths. Hence, the result yields a strengthening of the result of
[
127
]
that has led to the groundbreaking result of Dujmović et al.
[
48
]
, the planar product structure theorem.

Definition 4.29
.

The strong product
G\boxtimes H
of two graphs

and

is the graph with vertex set

that includes the edge with endpoints
(v,x)
and
(w,y)
if and only if

and

;

and

; or

and

(see Figure 14).

Theorem 4.30

(
[

]
)
.

Every planar graph is a subgraph of

1.

for some planar graph

2.

The planar product theorem had an immense impact and was the basis to solve major open problems and to make progress on longstanding open problems. For example, it was used by Dujmović et al.
[

to show that planar graphs have queue layouts
with a bounded number of queues; this was a longstanding open problem. It was used by Dujmović et al.
[

We refer to the recent survey

4.5
\infty
-admissibility

Let
G
be a graph,
v\in V(G)
and

. Recall that a

is a

A

such that for every

According to

is equal to the largest number

-fan set in

We define a related concept. Let

be a graph and

of size at least

A maximal such set is called a

Weissauer
[

proved the following.

Theorem 4.31

(
[

]
)
.

For every graph

\frac{\adm(G)+1}{2}\leq\beta(G)\leq\adm(G).

Weissauer
[

]
proved the following decomposition theorems.

Theorem 4.32

If

has no

has a

If

We conclude the following corollary, which was first proved by Dvořák

Corollary 4.34

A graph

if and only if

We now turn to more sophisticated lower bounds for the generalized coloring numbers.
The first result by Grohe et al.

By

6
Structural decompositions

Tree decompositions offer a way of globally decomposing a graph
along small separators into well behaved pieces.
On the other hand, the generalized coloring numbers offer rather local insights.
In this section we will study another way of decomposing graphs into well behaved pieces. These turn out to be equivalent to the generalized coloring numbers but offer different insights.
The structural decompositions we discuss here were first introduced by DeVos et al.
[
33
]
in terms of proper vertex colorings.
For a positive integer
p
, a
p
-treewidth coloring
of a graph
G
is a vertex coloring such that the subgraph induced by any
i\leq p
color classes has treewidth at most
i-1
.
Hence, every single color class induces a graph of treewidth
0
, that is, an edgeless graph.
In particular, the coloring is a proper vertex coloring.
A class of graphs admitting a
p
-treewidth coloring for every positive integer
p
with a number of colors depending only on
p
is said to admit

low treewidth colorings
. De Vos et al.
[
33
]
proved that every class excluding some graph as a minor admits low treewidth colorings.
Not much later, Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez proved that these classes even admit
low treedepth colorings
,
then introduced classes with bounded expansion and proved
[
112
]
that they are characterized by the existence of low treedepth colorings.
Finally, Zhu
[
158
]
provided an alternate characterization of classes with bounded expansion by the boundedness of generalized coloring numbers.
The terminology has changed from structural colorings to structural decompositions and
after presenting Zhu’s result we will switch to this terminology.

6.1
p-centered colorings

Let us begin with the two key definitions.

Definition 6.1
.

For a positive integer
p
, a
p
-centered coloring
of a graph
G
is a vertex coloring of
G
such that for any connected subgraph
H\subseteq G
, either some color appears exactly once in
H
, or
H
gets at least
p
colors.
The least number of colors in a
p
-centered coloring of
G
is denoted
\chi_{p}(G)
.

A
centered coloring
of
G
is a vertex coloring such that, for any
connected subgraph
H
some color appears exactly once in
H
. It is easy to see that the treedepth of
G
is equal to the minimum number of colors in a centered
coloring of
G
.

Definition 6.2
.

For a positive integer
p
, a
p
-treedepth coloring
of a graph
G
is a vertex coloring of
G
such that the subgraph induced by any
i\leq p
color classes has treedepth at most
i
(see Figure 15).

Figure 15:
A

The following lemma by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[

]
shows that

-centered colorings yield

-treedepth colorings.

Lemma 6.3

(
[

]
)
.

A
q
-centered coloring for

is a
p
-treedepth coloring.

The converse is not true, however, the existence of a
p
-treedepth coloring with few colors implies the existence of a
p
-centered coloring with few colors. We did not find a reference for this fact, but we believe that it is folklore in the community.
We include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
p\geq 2
.
If

admits a
p
-treedepth coloring with

admits a

-centered coloring with

Proof.

Fix a

-treedepth coloring

of

be the directed graph with vertex set

, where

is an ancestor of

for some choice

p
colors including

in the treedepth decomposition

for the colors
I
.
There are

7
Game characterizations and wideness

In this section we are going to study game characterizations of the generalized coloring numbers as well as connections to a related notion called wideness.
Just as treewidth can be characterized by cops-and-robber games in a very intuitive way, it turns out that the generalized coloring numbers can be characterized by games. Cops-and-robber games are played on a graph by a team of cops and a robber. The robber resides on the vertices of the graph. The cops aim to catch the robber who is trying to avoid capture.
Depending on the concrete setting, e.g. visibility, speed of cops and robber, and local restrictions in the game rounds, we obtain different models that correspond to different width measures.

7.1
Cops-and-robber games

Let us first recall the classical cops-and-robber game introduced by Seymour and Thomas
[
141
]
characterizing treewidth.
The game is played by two players on a graph

G
and can run at infinite speed along paths in the graph. The second player controls a team of
k

G

The goal of the cops is to catch the robber. The game proceeds in rounds. In each round, some of the cops may step into helicopters (these cops are temporarily removed from the game) and announce the next position where they want to be placed. Now the robber can run to its new position, however, it is not allowed to run through a path that contains a vertex occupied by a cop. The cops win if they can land on the position of the robber, that is, if it has no moves left when the cops announce to land on its position.
Otherwise, the game continues forever and the robber wins.
Seymour and Thomas proved that the cops-and-robber game can be used to characterize treewidth.

In the above game, the robber is visible, agile, and can run at infinite speed.

This variant turns out to be equivalent to the

[

Otherwise,

Hence, when the robber traversed the path

Therefore, we have

∎

Corollary 7.4
.

A graph class
\mathscr{C}
has bounded expansion if and only if for every

.

.

The

, introduced by Ganian et al.
[

]
, is played like the visible-and-agile game but the cop player is not allowed to move a cop from a vertex once it has landed.
That is, in the lift-free game the cops are placed on the vertices of the graph one after another and we count the number of cops needed to catch the robber.

Theorem 7.5

(
[

]
)
.

The least number of cops needed to catch the robber
in the lift-free game on

.

In order to appropriately localize this game we take a different view on counting the number of rounds in the lift-free game.
For

, in the

r

every vertex is equipped with a counter that is initially set to

.
In the radius-

-counter game the cops are placed on the vertices of the graph one after the other until all vertices are occupied by a cop.
Denote the set of placed cops in round

by

(and

.
In round

, we increase the counters of all vertices in the

in

by

(also the counter of the vertex itself).
The

of a play is the largest number that a counter takes in the play and the

of

is the smallest counter-depth that the cops can ensure in the radius-

-counter game with optimal play.

Theorem 7.6
.

The radius-

is equal to
\wcol_{r}(G)
.

In particular, the radius-

Proof.

Let

be a graph and let

be an order of

.
Then

if and only if there is a path

is the minimum vertex on

∎

.

A graph class
\mathscr{C}

7.2
Splitter Game and Quasi-Wideness

Another way of localizing lift-free games is to restrict the game graph in each round to a local neighborhood of the last cop move.
This leads to the Splitter game, which characterizes nowhere dense classes, as shown by Grohe et al.
[
78
]
.
We present an equivalent characterization in terms of uniform quasi-wideness and derive bounds for the length of the Splitter game as well as for uniform quasi-wideness in terms of the generalized coloring numbers.

Let
G
be a graph. The
radius-
r
Splitter game
on
G
is played by two players: Connector and Splitter, as follows.
The game starts on
G_{0}:=G
.
In round
i+1
of the game, Connector chooses a vertex
c_{i+1}\in V(G_{i})
.
Then Splitter picks a vertex
s_{i+1}\in N_{r}^{G_{i}}(c_{i+1})
and deletes it, that is, the game continues on
G_{i+1}:=G_{i}[N_{r}^{G_{i}}(c_{i+1})\setminus\{s_{i+1}\}]
.
Splitter wins if
G_{i+1}=\emptyset
, otherwise, the game continues.

Observation 7.8
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then
\td(G)
is equal to the least number such that Splitter wins the radius-
\infty
Splitter game.

As shown in
[
78
]
the Splitter game characterizes nowhere dense classes.

Theorem 7.9

(
[
78
]
)
.

A class
\mathscr{C}
of graphs is nowhere dense if and only if for every positive interger
r
there exists a constant
\ell(r)
such that Splitter wins the radius-
r
Splitter game on every
G\in\mathscr{C}
in at most
\ell(r)
rounds.

The proof is based on the fact that nowhere dense classes are uniformly quasi-wide (defined below).
It was observed by Kreutzer et al.
[
97
]
that the weak coloring number bounds the number of rounds that Splitter needs to win.

Lemma 7.10

(
[
97
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then Splitter has a strategy to win the radius-
r
Splitter game on
G
in
\ell=\wcol_{2r}(G)+1
rounds.

Proof.

Given a graph
G
and an integer
r
. Let
\ell:=\wcol_{2r}(G)
and let
\pi
be a witnessing order.
Splitter plays the following strategy: in any round

, Splitter picks
s_{i+1}=\min_{\pi}(N_{r}^{G_{i}}(c_{i+1}))
.
Then for every remaining vertex

we have

, as there is a path of length at most

connecting

and
s_{i+1}
(via the central vertex

) and

is minimum on this path (compare this to the counter game).
We conclude that the game must end after at most

rounds.
∎

The fact that nowhere dense classes are characterized by the Splitter game is derived from a characterization of these classes by the notion of uniform quasi-wideness, which was introduced by Dawar

]
.
A set

is called

7.3
Flipwidth, flipper-game, and flip-flatness

We conclude this section with a short overview of how the presented games and the notion of uniform quasi-wideness have recently been generalized to dense graphs with the hinted at beautiful connections to model theory.
We begin with the
flipwidth game
, which was recently introduced by Toruńczyk
[
147
]
.
The idea of game is to (temporarily) flip adjacencies between arbitrarily large sets of vertices (instead of occupying them by cops) – an operation that is well suited to simplify dense graphs.
Note also the close connection between flips and quantifier-free transductions.

Definition 7.16
.

Let
G
be a graph and
X,Y\subseteq V(G)
.
A
flip
between
X
and
Y
results in the graph
G^{\prime}
obtained from
G
by inverting the adjacency of every pair
(x,y)
of vertices with
x\in X
and
y\in Y
:
every pair
(x,y)
that is adjacent in
G
becomes non-adjacent in
G^{\prime}
, and vice-versa.
A
k
-flip
of a graph
G
is obtained by partitioning
V(G)

into
k
parts, and then performing flips between some pairs
X,Y
of parts of the partition (possibly with
X=Y
).

The flipwidth game is defined as follows.

Definition 7.17
.

The
flipwidth game
with radius
r
and width
k
on a graph
G
is a game played by two players,
Flipper
and
Runner
, as follows.
We let
G_{0}:=G
and let Runner pick any vertex
v_{0}
of
G
.
In the
i
th round, Flipper announces a
k
-flip
G_{i}

of the original graph

G
.
Runner can move from their previous position
v_{i-1}
to a new position
v_{i}
, by traversing a path of length at most
r
in
G_{i-1}
.
The game is won by Flipper if the runner’s new position
v_{i}
is isolated in the announced
k
-flip
G_{i}
.

The
flipwidth
of radius
r
of a graph
G
, denoted
\fw_{r}(G)
, is the smallest number
k
for which Flipper has a winning strategy in the flipwidth game with radius
r
and width
k
.

A class
\mathscr{C}
of graphs has
bounded flipwidth
if
\fw_{r}(\mathscr{C})<\infty
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
It has
almost bounded flipwidth

if for every
\varepsilon>0
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
we have
\fw_{r}(H)\in\mathcal{O}_{r,\varepsilon}(|H|^{\varepsilon})
for every

n
-vertex graph

which is an induced subgraph of a graph

.

Toruńczyk proved the following connections with graph theory and model theory.
First, he proved that the flipwidth game is a robust generalization of the bounded-speed variants of the cops-and-robber game by considering weakly sparse graph classes.
A graph class is
weakly sparse
if it
excludes some fixed biclique
K_{t,t}
(balanced complete bipartite graph with parts of size
t
) as a subgraph.

Theorem 7.18

(
[
147
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of graphs.
Then:

1.

\mathscr{C}
has bounded degeneracy if and only if
\mathscr{C}
is weakly sparse and
\fw_{1}(\mathscr{C})<\infty
,

2.

\mathscr{C}
has bounded treewidth if and only if
\mathscr{C}
is weakly sparse and
\fw_{\infty}(\mathscr{C})<\infty
,

3.

\mathscr{C}
has bounded expansion if and only if
\mathscr{C}
is weakly sparse and
\mathscr{C}
has bounded flipwidth.

4.

\mathscr{C}
is nowhere dense if and only if
\mathscr{C}
is weakly sparse and
\mathscr{C}
has almost bounded flipwidth.

On dense graphs he obtained the following wonderful results.

Theorem 7.19
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of graphs. Then

1.

\mathscr{C}
has bounded cliquewidth if and only if
\fw_{\infty}(\mathscr{C})<\infty
.

2.

if
\mathscr{C}
has bounded twinwidth, then
\mathscr{C}
has bounded flipwidth. Furthermore, if
\mathscr{C}
is a class of ordered graphs then
\mathscr{C}
has bounded twinwidth if and only if
\mathscr{C}
has bounded flipwidth.

3.

if
\mathscr{C}
is structurally nowhere dense, then

has almost bounded flipwidth.

4.

if

is a first-order transduction, then

Hence, every class of bounded flipwidth is monadically dependent.

Toruńczyk conjectures that a class has almost bounded flipwidth if and only if it is monadically dependent.

We now come to a second game, the
flipper game
, which was introduced by Gajarský et al. and which can be seen as a generalization of the Splitter game, and which characterizes monadically stable classes

]

Definition 7.20
.

(

A class

[

We remark that flip-flatness is not called flip-wideness (as it should be) in order to avoid confusion with flipwidth.

(

8
Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension

In this section, we study the classical notions of
VC-dimension
and
order-dimension
and their connection to the generalized coloring numbers.
VC-dimension was originally introduced by Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
152
]
as a measure of complexity of set systems.
VC-dimension plays a key role in learning theory as it characterizes PAC-learnability by the result of Blumer et al.
[
12
]
.
Independently it was introduced by Shelah in model theory where it defines dependent theories
[
142
]
.
The connection between PAC-learning and dependence was observed by Laskowski
[
103
]
.

A second related measure is
Littlestone-dimension
(we are going to work with
order-dimension
, which is functionally equivalent), which characterizes online-learning by the result of Littlestone
[
105
]
.
In model theory it defines stability, as observed by Chase and Freitag
[
23
]
.

As mentioned before, nowhere dense graph classes are both stable and dependent, hence, set systems definable by first-order formulas in nowhere dense classes have bounded order-dimension and bounded VC-dimension.
Besides the direct applications of VC-dimension and order-dimension for definable set systems in nowhere dense classes, these connections have led to further interesting combinatorial and algorithmic applications that we will present in the following.
The generalized coloring numbers as well as uniform quasi-wideness have played a big role in the development of the theory in this direction.

8.1
VC-dimension and number of types

The VC-dimension of a set system is defined as follows.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
X
be a set and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)

be a family of subsets of
X
.
A subset
A\subseteq X
is
shattered by
\mathcal{F}
if

\{A\cap F\colon F\in\mathcal{F}\}=\mathcal{P}(A)
; that is, every subset of
A
can be obtained as the intersection of some set from
\mathcal{F}
with
A
.
The
VC-dimension
,
of
\mathcal{F}
is the maximum size of a subset
A\subseteq X
that is shattered by

\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 8.2
.

Let
X
be a set and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)

be a family of subsets of
X
.
The
order-dimension
of
\mathcal{F}
is the largest integer
\ell
such that
there exist elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\in X
and
F_{1},\ldots,F_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}
with
x_{i}\in F_{j}\Leftrightarrow i\leq j
.

The key property of VC-dimension is that it implies polynomial upper bounds on the number of different
traces
that a set system can have on a given parameter set. This is the statement of the famous Sauer-Shelah Lemma.

Theorem 8.3

(Sauer-Shelah Lemma,
[
139
,
143
,
152
]
)
.

For any family
\mathcal{F}
of subsets of a set
X
, if the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is
d
,
then for every finite
A\subset X
,

|\{A\cap F\mid F\in{\cal F}\}|\leq c\cdot|A|^{d},\qquad\textit{where $c$ is a universal constant.}

Definition 8.4
.

The
vc-density
(also called the

vc-exponent
)
of a set system
\cal F

on an infinite set
X
is the infimum of all reals
\alpha>0
such that

|\left\{A\cap F\,\mid\,F\in\cal F\right\}|\in\mathcal{O}(|A|^{\alpha})
, for all finite
A\subset X
.

The vc-density of neighborhood set systems in bounded expansion classes was first studied by Reidl et al.
[
134
,
135
]
under the name
neighborhood complexity
.

Theorem 8.5

(
[
135
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\subseteq V(G)
. Then

|\{N_{r}(v)\cap A\mid v\in V(G)\}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(2r+2)^{\wcol_{2r}(G)}\cdot\wcol_{2r}(G)\cdot|A|+1.

The problem with extending this result to nowhere dense classes in a useful way was that the weak coloring number, which can only be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n^{\varepsilon})
in nowhere dense classes, appears in the exponent in the above inequality.

As shown by Pilipczuk and Siebertz
[
128
]
, the VC-dimension of the
r
-neighborhoods set system in nowhere dense classes is very small.

Lemma 8.6

(
[
128
]
)
.

If
K_{t}\not\preccurlyeq_{r}G
, then the VC-dimension of the set system

\{N_{r}(v)\mid v\in V(G)\}

of
r
-neighborhoods is at most

.

This yields already polynomial neighborhood complexity for nowhere dense graph classes.
On the way to proving that neighborhood set systems in nowhere dense classes have in fact vc-density
1
, Eickmeyer et al.
[
57
]
first proved that also the set system of weak coloring numbers in nowhere dense classes has bounded VC-dimension.

Lemma 8.7

(
[
57
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a nowhere dense class of graphs and let
G\in\mathscr{C}
. Let
\pi
be a linear order of
V(G)
and let

\mathcal{W}_{r,\pi}=\{\WReach_{r}[G,\pi,v]\mid v\in V(G)\}

be the set system of weak
r
-reachability sets.
Then there exists a constant
vc(r)
, depending only on
\mathscr{C}
and
r
(and not on
G
and
\pi
) such that
\mathcal{W}_{r,\pi}
has VC-dimension at most
vc(r)
.

Based on this result, the proof of
Theorem

8.5
could be lifted to nowhere dense classes.

Theorem 8.8

(
[
57
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a nowhere dense class of graphs. Then there exists a function
f(r,\varepsilon)
such that for every
G\in\mathscr{C}
,
A\subseteq V(G)
,
r\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0

|\{N_{r}(v)\cap A\mid v\in V(G)\}|\leq f(r,\varepsilon)\cdot|A|^{1+\varepsilon}.

The setting studied in model theory is to consider definable set systems.
One of the key results of Shelah is that stable and dependent theories can be characterized by upper bounds on the number of types (as defined next).

For a first order formula
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})

with free variables partitioned into
object variables

\bar{x}
and
parameter variables

\bar{y}
, a structure
\mathbb{A}
,
and a subset of its domain
B\subseteq A
, define
the set system of
\varphi
-types
with parameters from
B
that are realized in
\mathbb{A}
as

\displaystyle S^{\varphi}(\mathbb{A}/B)=\left\{\big\{\bar{v}\ \in B^{|\bar{y}|}\,\colon\,\mathbb{A}\models\varphi(\bar{u},\bar{v})\big\}\colon\,\bar{u}\in A)^{|\bar{x}|}\right\}\ \subseteq\ \mathcal{P}(B^{|\bar{y}|}).

Every tuple
\bar{u}\in A^{|\bar{x}|}
defines the set of those tuples
\bar{v}\in B^{|\bar{y}|}
for which
\varphi(\bar{u},\bar{v})
is true.
The set
S^{\varphi}(\mathbb{A}/B)
consists of all subsets of
B^{|\bar{y}|}
that can be defined in this way.
We can also work with the following definitions of stability and dependence.

Definition 8.9
.

A class
\mathscr{C}
of structures is
dependent
if for every
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
there exists a constant
d
such that for all
\mathbb{A}\in\mathscr{C}
and all
B\subseteq A
the VC-dimension of
S^{\varphi}(\mathbb{A}/B)
is bounded by
d
.

Definition 8.10
.

A class
\mathscr{C}
of structures is
stable
if for every
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
there exists a constant
d
such that for all
\mathbb{A}\in\mathscr{C}
and all
B\subseteq A
the order-dimension of
S^{\varphi}(\mathbb{A}/B)
is bounded by
d
.

A class
\mathscr{C}
is called
monadically dependent
or
monadically stable
, respectively, if the class of all colorings of structures from
\mathscr{C}
is dependent or stable, respectively.
As proved by Baldwin and Shelah
[
8
]
in this case it suffices to consider formulas
\varphi(x,y)
with only two free variables, which allows the treatment of monadically dependent and monadically stable classes via transductions, as presented in
Section

6.2
.

We can now define the VC-dimension, order-dimension and vc-density of formulas over structures in the natural way.
For example, the vc-density of a formula is defined as follows.

Definition 8.11
.

The vc-density of a formula
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
over a class of structures
\mathscr{C}

is the infimum of all reals
\alpha>0

such that
|S^{\varphi}(\mathbb{A}/B)|\in\mathcal{O}(|B|^{\alpha})
,
for all
\mathbb{A}\in\mathscr{C}
and all finite
B\subset A
.

Using the locality of first-order logic, uniform quasi-wideness and neighborhood complexity, it was proved by Pilipczuk et al.
[
130
]
that the vc-density of first-order formulas in nowhere dense classes is very small.

Theorem 8.12

(
[
130
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a class of graphs and let
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
be a first-order formula.

(1)

If
\mathscr{C}
is nowhere dense, then for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists a constant
c
such that for every
G\in\mathscr{C}
and every nonempty

A\subseteq V(G)
, we have
|S^{\varphi}(G/A)|\leq c\cdot|A|^{|\bar{x}|+\varepsilon}.

(2)

If
\mathscr{C}
has bounded expansion, then there exists a constant
c
such that for every
G\in\mathscr{C}
and every nonempty
A\subseteq V(G)
, we have
|S^{\varphi}(G/A)|\leq c\cdot|A|^{|\bar{x}|}
.

As shown by Dreier et al.
[
38
]
as a key ingredient for their first-order model checking algorithm on monadically stable classes, also monadically stable classes behave very well.

Theorem 8.13

(
[
38
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a monadically stable class of graphs and let
\varphi(x,y)
be a first order formula with two free variables. Then for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists a constant
c
such that for every
G\in\mathscr{C}
and every nonempty

A\subseteq V(G)
, we have
|S^{\varphi}(G/A)|\leq c\cdot|A|^{1+\varepsilon}.

Dreier et al.
[
38
]
conjecture that the theorem generalizes to formulas
\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, just as
Theorem

8.12
for nowhere dense classes. This conjecture would be implied by the conjecture that the notions of monadic stability and structural nowhere denseness coincide
[
125
,
126
]
.
They also conjecture that
Theorem

8.13
generalizes to monadicallly dependent classes.
This is known for some special cases, e.g. for classes with bounded twinwidth (even in the general setting of
Theorem

8.12
and with the strong bounds as for classes with bounded expansion) as proved by Gajarský et al.
[
70
]
.

8.2
Distance-r domination

A
distance-
d
dominating set
in a graph
G
is a set
D\subseteq V(G)
such that every vertex of
G
is at distance at most
d
to a vertex of
D
.
The algorithmic problem of deciding whether a graph admits a distance-
d
dominating set of size at most
k
is one of the benchmark problems on nowhere dense classes and many of the combinatorics we have seen so far have been developed to approach this algorithmic problem.
Recall that a problem is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to a parameter
k
if it can be solved in time
f(k)\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
for some computable function
f
. It admits a kernel of size
g(k)
if one can compute in polynomial time an equivalent instance of size
g(k)
.

After Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
[
120
]
demonstrated first that the dominating set problem is fixed-parameter tractable based on low treedepth decompositions,
Dawar and Kreutzer
[
31
]
extended these results to nowhere dense classes and the distance-
d
dominating set problem.
They introduced the notion of
domination cores
and developed an irrelevant vertex technique that was highly relevant for the further algorithmic development of nowhere dense classes based on uniform quasi-wideness.
A
k
-domination core is a set
C\subseteq V(G)
such that every set of size at most
k
that dominates
C
also dominates the whole graph
G
.
Obviously, the existence of small domination cores is highly useful for the kernelization of the dominating set problem.
The next step by Drange et al. was to establish linear kernels for distance-
d
domination for bounded expansion classes based on neighborhood complexity combinatorics
[
36
]
.
With the development of polynomial bounds for uniform quasi-wideness functions by Kreutzer et al.
[
98
]
first a polynomial kernel
[
98
]
and then an almost linear kernel for distance-
d
domination on nowhere dense classes was developed by Eickmeyer et al.
[
57
]
.
Telle and Villanger proved that small domination cores exist even for biclique-free graphs
[
146
]
and most generally for semi-ladder-free graphs as proved by Fabianski et al.
[
63
]
(however, these can only be computed in fpt time).
Polynomial kernels for the independent set and dominating set problem
in powers of nowhere dense classes can be efficiently computed as shown by Dreier et al.
[
40
]
.
A unified view on kernelization for packing and covering problems in nowhere dense classes is provided by Ahn et al.
[
4
]
and by Einarson and Reidl
[
58
]
.
The weak coloring numbers also play a key role in the constant factor approximation algorithms for the distance-
d
dominating set problem
[
5
,
53
,
54
,
99
,
128
]

8.3
Neighborhood covers

The
dual
of a set system
\mathcal{F}
on a universe
X
is the set system
\mathcal{F}^{*}
that has one element for each set of
\mathcal{F}
and contains the sets
S_{x}=\{A\in\mathcal{F}\mid x\in A\}
for all
x\in X
. Intuitively, if we represent the set system
\mathcal{F}
by its incidence matrix, then we obtain the dual set system
\mathcal{F}^{*}
by the transposed incidence matrix.
The
dual VC-dimension
of
\mathcal{F}
is the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.
Observe that the set system
\{N_{r}(v)\mid v\in V(G)\}
is equal to its dual, hence, its VC-dimension is equal to its dual VC-dimension. In general, if the VC-dimension of
\mathcal{F}
is
d
, then its dual VC-dimension is less than
2^{d+1}

[
7
]
. The dual set system of
\mathcal{W}_{r,\pi}
considered in
Lemma

8.7
is the system
\mathcal{W}^{*}_{r,\pi}=\{X_{v}\mid v\in V(G)\}
, where
X_{v}=\{w\mid v\in\WReach_{r}[G,\pi,w]\}
. This system hence also has bounded VC-dimension in nowhere dense classes.
Its most interesting property however is that it forms an
r
-neighborhood cover
.

Definition 8.14
.

An
r
-neighborhood cover
of
G
is a set
\mathcal{X}
of vertex subsets of
G
, called
clusters
such that the
r
-neighborhood of every vertex is contained in some cluster
X\in\mathcal{X}
. The
radius
of a cluster
X
is the radius of
G[X]
. The cover
\mathcal{X}
is said to have
radius at most
d
if all its clusters have radius at most
d
. The
degree
of the cover is the maximum number of clusters that intersect at some vertex.

The existence of sparse neighborhood covers for nowhere dense classes was one of the key ingredients for the first-order model checking algorithm on these classes by Grohe et al.
[
78
]
.

Theorem 8.15

(
[
78
]
)
.

Let
\pi
be an order of
V(G)
. Then
\mathcal{W}_{2r,\pi}^{*}
is an
r
-neighborhood cover of radius at most
2r
and degree at most
\wcol_{2r}(G,\pi)
.

This result was vastly generalized by Dreier et al.
[
38
]
, who proved that sparse
neighborhood covers can be constructed from linear orders with small
crossing number
.

Definition 8.16
.

Let
(U,\mathcal{F})
be a set system and
\pi
be a linear order of
U
.
The
crossing number
of
X\in\mathcal{F}
is the number of pairs
(u,v)
of elements of
U
such that
v
is an immediate successor of
u
in
\pi
and exactly one of
u
and
v
belongs to
X
.

Theorem 8.17

(
[
38
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and assume the set system of closed neighborhoods has crossing number at most
k
. Then
G
admits a distance-
1
neighborhood cover of radius at most
4
and degree at most
k+1
.

Welzl proved that small vc-density leads to linear orders with small crossing number
[
156
]
.

Theorem 8.18

(
[
156
]
)
.

Let
(U,\mathcal{F})
be a set system with vc-density at most
d
, where
d>1
is a real.
Then there exists a linear order of
U
with crossing number bounded by
\mathcal{O}(|U|^{1-1/d}\log|U|)
.

As the
r
th-power of every monadically stable class is again monadically stable one obtains the following corollary.

Theorem 8.19

(
[
38
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a monadically stable class of graphs, let
r
be a positive integer and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then
\mathscr{C}
admits
r
-neighborhood
covers with radius at most
4r
and degree
\mathcal{O}(n^{\varepsilon})
.

Furthermore, the neighborhood covers of the theorem can be efficiently computed.

Together with the flipper-game characterization of monadically stable classes this leads to the efficient model checking algorithm of Dreier et al.
[
38
,
41
]
on monadically stable classes of graphs.

We conclude this section with one final result that Braunfeld et al. used to show that monadically stable classes have quasi-bounded bounded shrubdepth decompositions
[
21
]
.

Definition 8.20
.

An
induced
r
-neighborhood cover
of a graph
G
is a set
\mathcal{X}
of
induced subgraphs of
G
, called
clusters
, such that the
r
-neighborhood of every vertex is contained in some
X\in\mathcal{X}
. The radius of
\mathcal{X}
is the
maximum radius of the connected components of the clusters of
\mathcal{X}
. The
size
of
\mathcal{X}
is the cardinality of
\mathcal{X}
.

Note that the connected components of any induced
r
-neighborhood cover of radius
d
and size
k
yield an
r
-neighborhood cover with radius
d
and degree
k
.

Theorem 8.21

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a monadically stable class of graphs, let
r
be a positive integer and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then
\mathscr{C}
admits induced
r
-neighborhood covers with radius at most
4r+2
and size
\mathcal{O}(n^{\varepsilon})
.

Further applications

We conclude our survey with a few selected applications of the generalized coloring numbers.

9.1
Exact distance colourings

We begin with an elegant result of Van den Heuvel at al.
[
149
]
bounding the chromatic number of exact distance graphs by the weak coloring numbers.
Exact distance graphs were introduced by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez in
[
122
]
. We refer to
[
122
,
149
]
for more background on colorings of power graphs and exact distance graphs.

Definition 9.1
.

For a graph
G
and positive integer
p
, the
exact
distance-
p
graph

G^{[\sharp p]}
is the graph with vertex set
V(G)
that has an edge between vertices
u
and
v
if and only if
u
and
v
have distance exactly
p
in
G
.

For odd
p
, Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez proved that for a class
\mathscr{C}
with bounded
expansion, the chromatic number of
G^{[\sharp p]}
is bounded by an absolute constant for all
G\in\mathscr{C}

[
122
]
.
We provide the elegant proof of the following result (in fact,
[
149
]
proves a stronger result in terms of a measure called
\mathrm{dcol}
that we refrain from defining here).

Theorem 9.2

(
[
149
]
)
.

For every odd positive integer
p
and graph
G
we have

\chi(G^{[\sharp p]})\leq\wcol_{2p-1}^{*}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be a linear ordering minimizing the chromatic number of
G\langle\pi,2p-1\rangle
and let
\rho
be a corresponding coloring witnessing that
\wcol_{2p-1}^{*}(G)=:c
, hence any two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
have different colors under
\rho
whenever
u\in\WReach_{2p-1}[G,\pi,v]
or
v\in\WReach_{2p-1}[G,\pi,u]
.
For a vertex
v
write
m(v)
for the minimum vertex in the
\lfloor p/2\rfloor
-neighborhood of
v
with respect to
\pi
.
Define the coloring
\chi
such that every vertex
v
receives the color
\rho(m(v))
.
We claim that
\chi
is a proper coloring of
G^{[\sharp p]}
.

Consider an edge
uv\in E(G^{[\sharp p]})
.
By definition,
u
and
v
have distance exactly
p
in
G
and the
\lfloor p/2\rfloor
-neighborhoods of
u
and
v
are disjoint.
We have
m(u)\neq m(v)
and there exists a path of length at most
2p-1
between
m(u)
and
m(v)
.
Assume without loss of generality that
m(u)<_{\pi}m(v)
. Then
m(u)\in\WReach_{2p-1}[G,\pi,m(v)]
, hence,
u
and
v
receive different colors under
\chi
.
∎

9.2
Dimension of posets

A partial order is a reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive binary relation.
A partially ordered set (poset) is a set equipped with a partial order
\leq
.
The
dimension

\mathrm{dim}(P)
of a poset
P
is the least integer
d
such that the elements of
P
can be embedded into
{\mathsf{R}}^{d}
such that
x<y
in
P
if and only if the point of
x
is below the point of
y
with respect to the product order of
{\mathsf{R}}^{d}
.
The dimension is a key measure of a poset’s complexity.

A convenient way of representing a poset is to draw its Hasse diagram:
Each element is drawn as a point in the plane such that if
x<y
, then
x
is drawn below
y
. Then, for each relation
x<y
that is not implied by transitivity (these are called the cover relations), we draw a
y
-monotone curve going from
x
to
y
.
The diagram defines a corresponding undirected graph, where edges correspond to pairs
of elements in a cover relation.
This graph is called the cover graph of the poset.
The height of a poset is the maximum size of a chain in the poset (a set of pairwise
comparable elements).

There is a large body of literature connecting poset dimension with graph structure. Streib and Trotter proved that for every fixed heigh
h>1
, posets of height
h
with a planar cover graph have bounded dimension
[
145
]
.
In general, Kelly proved that there is no function bounding the dimension posets by their height and a planar cover graph also does not imply a bound on the dimension of the poset
[
91
]
.
In the sequel, Joret et al. showed that posets have dimension upper bounded by a function of their height if their cover graphs have bounded treewidth, bounded genus, or exclude an apex-graph
as a minor
[
87
]
, exclude a minor
[
154
]
(Walczak) or topological minor
[
106
]
(Micek and Wiechert), or come from a class with bounded expansion
[
89
]
(Joret et al.).
The following nice result was proved by Joret et al.
[
88
]
.

Theorem 9.3

(
[
88
]
)
.

Let
P
be a poset of height at most
h
and let
G
be its cover graph. Then
\mathrm{dim}(P)\leq 4^{\wcol_{3h-3}(G)}
.

This line of research culminated in the following characterization of monotone graph classes.

Theorem 9.4

(
[
88
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a monotone class of graphs. Then
\mathscr{C}
is nowhere dense if and only if for every integer
h>1
and real
\varepsilon>0
,
n
-element posets of height at most
h
whose cover graphs belong to
\mathscr{C}
have dimension
\mathcal{O}(n^{\varepsilon})
.

9.3
Directed graphs

With the great success of treewidth and related measures for undirected graphs, researchers have attempted to define similar concepts for directed graphs, see e.g.
[
9
,
11
,
71
,
85
,
123
,
138
]
.
However, the algorithmic impact of directed width measures has by far not been as successful as for undirected graphs.
For example, Ganian et al.
[
72
]
call a width measure for digraphs
algorithmically useful
if all MSO definable problems admit polynomial-time algorithms on digraphs of bounded width.
However, Courcelle’s
Theorem
[
27
]
, stating that every MSO
property can be decided in linear time on every class of
bounded treewidth, under standard complexity theoretic
assumptions cannot be extended to graph classes
of unbounded treewidth that additionally satisfy certain
mild closure conditions
[
72
,
100
]
.
Nevertheless, these lower bounds still allow the search for
classes of digraphs that are substantially different from
classes whose underlying undirected graphs have bounded
treewidth or are nowhere dense, and that still admit
efficient algorithms for individual problems.

Kreutzer and Tazari
[
101
]
introduced generalizations of bounded expansion and nowhere dense classes of graphs to the directed setting.
Excluding some tournament (a directed graph with exactly one edge between each two vertices) as a bounded depth minor does not turn out to be the right approach.

Nowhere crownful
classes of digraphs are defined by excluding one-subdivided cliques (which interestingly are the forbidden patterns in monadically dependent classes), and turn out to have a nice combinatorial theory
[
101
]
.
In particular, nowhere crownful classes can alternatively be characterized by directed uniform quasi-wideness, with immediate algorithmic applications.
Classes with
directed bounded expansion
were studied in
[
99
,
96
]
.
In particular, these classes can be characterized by directed generalized coloring numbers.

Concluding remarks and acknowledgments

The generalized coloring numbers are one of the most influential notions in the theory of bounded expansion and nowhere dense graph classes.
In this survey I have tried to give an overview over some of the most important results and applications.
Of course the presented material reflects my own preferences and I hope that I have not upset anyone by missing their results.

In the text I have included open questions about the generalized coloring numbers in the respective context.
