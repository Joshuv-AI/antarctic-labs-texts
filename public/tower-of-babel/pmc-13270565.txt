Moral character development in nursing students: a qualitative content analysis in Iran
Authors: Pooneh Yousefi, Hoda Ahmari Tehran, Ensieh Madani, Hassan Boosaliki, Neda Yavari, Mansure Madani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNursing is a moral profession requiring virtues such as compassion and integrity to deliver ethical care amid complex challenges like moral distress. Despite known influences on moral development, nursing students’ own perceptions of factors shaping their moral character remain underexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to explore nursing students’ perceptions of the factors contributing to the development of moral character.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study and conventional content analysis was used. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 16 purposive-sampled nursing students (Bachelor’s and Master’s) at Hormozgan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, between June and August 2025. Data were analysed following Graneheim and Lundman’s approach until data saturation (meaning saturation) was achieved. Trustworthiness was ensured through credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability strategies, guided by the COREQ checklist.ResultsFour overarching themes emerged, encompassing 14 sub-themes: (1) inner motivations and values (four sub-themes: intrinsic motivation, personal/religious beliefs, psychological capital, self-reflection); (2) relational dynamics and communication (three sub-themes: role modeling, peer support, family/community influences); (3) moral learning in practice (three sub-themes: formal ethics education, staged clinical experiences, simulation training); and (4) systemic and organizational influences (four sub-themes: university culture, clinical ethical climate, professional norms, resource availability).ConclusionMoral character development is a dynamic, multifaceted process shaped by personal conviction, social relationships, structured learning, and institutional contexts, underscoring the ethical significance of virtues like resilience and integrity in navigating moral distress. In this Iranian cohort, religious values and relational role models were particularly salient. These findings inform bioethics curricula and policy to strengthen moral resilience among nursing students, preparing them for ethical practice amid complex healthcare challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01443-1.Keywords: Moral character, Nursing students, Qualitative content analysis, Ethics education, Role modeling, Clinical learning environment

Introduction

Nursing is fundamentally a moral profession, where practitioners must embody virtues such as compassion, integrity, justice, and moral courage to deliver ethical, patient-centered care amid increasingly complex healthcare environments [1, 2]. These qualities, collectively referred to as moral character, enable nurses to recognize ethical dilemmas, make sound judgments, prioritize patient well-being, and act with resilience, even under institutional constraints or resource limitations [3]. In an era marked by rising moral distress—exacerbated by events like the COVID-19 pandemic—strong moral character is not merely desirable but essential for mitigating burnout, ensuring care quality, and sustaining the profession’s workforce [4].

The development of moral character in nurses is grounded in foundational theories, such as Rest’s Four-Component Model of Morality, which posits that ethical behavior arises from moral sensitivity (recognizing ethical issues), moral judgment (reasoning about them), moral motivation (prioritizing ethics over other values), and moral character (persevering in ethical action) [5]. Empirical research has identified multifaceted factors influencing this development, categorized broadly into human and structural elements. Human factors include individual attributes like self-reflection and character strengths, social influences such as role modeling by mentors, and professional experiences like handling moral distress [1]. Structural factors encompass educational strategies (e.g., simulation-based ethics training and reflective discussions), supportive clinical environments, organizational resources (e.g., ethical guidelines), and sociocultural norms [6, 7]. Notably, professional identity formation has been shown to positively correlate with moral competencies, underscoring the interplay between personal growth and occupational socialization [8].

Prior research has explored aspects of moral development in nursing, such as moral sensitivity through attributes like honest communication and compassionate practice [9], and moral courage influenced by prior experience and supervision [10]. Systematic reviews have synthesized external factors promoting moral competence, including ethics education and clinical environments [1, 11], while qualitative studies highlight moral distress from poor care, disempowerment, and theory-practice gaps [12]. However, these often adopt quantitative approaches or focus on isolated components like sensitivity or courage, limiting holistic understanding [2, 3]. Recent debates in moral pedagogy emphasize virtue ethics for cultivating habits like empathy and integrity through experiential learning [1], while virtue-based frameworks address moral integrity in ethical decision-making [13], and professional voice enactment via ethical education [14]. The review of previous studies in this introduction serves to contextualize the existing body of knowledge, highlighting gaps that justify the current research—specifically, the scarcity of qualitative, student-centered explorations that capture holistic perceptions of moral character development in culturally specific contexts like Iran, where religio-cultural factors intersect with global ethics frameworks [2, 15]. This gap leaves underexplored how nursing students themselves perceive the interplay of personal, relational, educational, and systemic factors in shaping their moral character, potentially overlooking opportunities for context-tailored interventions to enhance moral resilience.

The necessity of conducting this study stems from the high prevalence of moral distress and ethical erosion among Iranian nursing students, where cultural expectations of faith-integrated care, resource shortages in public healthcare, and hierarchical clinical settings may stifle moral agency and exacerbate burnout [12]. As researchers with extensive experience in nursing education and ethics in Iran, our concerns centered on these issues: the risk of ethical desensitization in resource-limited environments, the underutilization of student voices in curriculum design, and the potential for unaddressed moral distress to contribute to workforce attrition in underserved regions. Addressing this gap through student perceptions is crucial to inform resilient, culturally nuanced ethics training, ultimately reducing burnout and improving patient outcomes.

For nursing students—the future stewards of this profession—cultivating moral character during undergraduate education is critical, as clinical placements often serve as the crucible for ethical maturation [16]. Studies indicate that students’ moral sensitivity, a precursor to full character development, manifests through attributes like honest communication, compassionate practice, intuitive recognition of vulnerabilities, and critical awareness of decision consequences [9]. However, undergraduate experiences frequently expose students to moral distress, stemming from witnessed poor care, hierarchical disempowerment, unsafe practices, or inconsistencies between classroom ethics and real-world realities—potentially stunting growth if unaddressed [12]. Positive predictors of moral courage, such as prior healthcare experience and supportive supervision, highlight opportunities for targeted interventions [10].

This study contributes to the field by providing a qualitative, student-centered exploration of moral character factors in an Iranian context, bridging gaps in culturally nuanced insights and offering practical recommendations for virtue-based pedagogy. It advances bioethical knowledge by highlighting how religio-cultural elements intersect with global ethics frameworks, informing scalable interventions for moral resilience in diverse nursing education worldwide. This study aimed to explore nursing students’ perceptions of the factors shaping their moral character throughout professional education and clinical practice.

Methods

Design

A qualitative descriptive design employing conventional content analysis was adopted to explore nursing students’ perceptions regarding the development of moral character. This approach is suitable for providing straightforward descriptions of phenomena in participants’ own words, particularly when aiming to stay close to the data without heavy theoretical interpretation [17]. Conventional content analysis, as outlined by Graneheim and Lundman [18], involves inductive category development to identify patterns and themes from textual data, ensuring the findings remain grounded in participants’ experiences [18]. The study’s design and reporting were guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [19], which ensures methodological transparency and rigor by addressing aspects related to the research team, study methods, context, findings, analysis, and interpretations.

Participants and setting

A purposive sampling strategy emphasizing maximum variation was applied to ensure a broad spectrum of viewpoints based on gender, age, academic level, clinical experience, hometown background, and level of religiosity [20]. Purposive sampling was conducted by first reviewing university enrollment records and student profiles to identify candidates across the specified criteria (gender, age, academic level, clinical experience, hometown background, and level of religiosity). Potential participants were then invited via email or in-person during classes, with self-reported religiosity assessed during initial screening to ensure variation (e.g., aiming for a mix of high, medium, and low ratings). This objective process continued iteratively, adjusting invitations based on emerging gaps in diversity until maximum variation was achieved. Sixteen nursing students were recruited from Hormozgan University of Medical Sciences in southern Iran. All data were collected from a single institution to focus on contextual depth, though this may limit broader generalizability. The first three participants had completed between five and eight semesters of clinical practice, and subsequent recruitment proceeded iteratively to refine emerging categories through ongoing analysis. In alignment with qualitative methodological standards emphasizing data sufficiency, sampling continued until two consecutive interviews yielded no new substantial insights, indicating both thematic stability and adequate analytical depth. This point of data saturation, specifically meaning saturation, was reached when no new codes or themes emerged, ensuring richness without redundancy [21]. This approach ensured the data set was sufficiently rich and comprehensive to address the research objectives, without seeking an unrealistic notion of absolute saturation. Students with fewer than two semesters of clinical exposure or those unwilling to provide informed consent were excluded to ensure participants had adequate practical experience and provided ethical participation.

Data collection

Data were gathered using open-ended, semi-structured interviews conducted by the first author between June and August 2025. This method enabled an in-depth exploration of participants’ perceptions and experiences while preserving flexibility to pursue emerging ideas [22]. Before each session, participants received clear information about the study’s aims, procedures, confidentiality assurances, and their rights, after which written informed consent was obtained.

Interviews commenced with opening, rapport-building questions to establish trust and invite participants to share their backgrounds. For example, students were asked, “Can you describe your educational background and clinical experiences so far?” and “How would you define moral character in the context of nursing?” The focus then shifted toward factors influencing moral character development. Participants responded to questions such as, “What internal factors do you believe contribute most to building your moral character as a future nurse?” and “Can you describe experiences where relationships with mentors, peers, or family shaped your ethical growth?” Probing questions included, “Could you elaborate on how clinical training or simulations have influenced your moral development?” and “Can you walk me through a specific situation where organizational factors affected your sense of ethics?” To explore perceived facilitators and challenges, participants were asked questions like, “What role has your personal faith or values played in your moral decision-making?” and “Can you give examples of how university culture or clinical settings supported or hindered your ethical growth?” Follow-up probes included, “How does self-reflection help you maintain moral awareness?” and “What impact have role models had on your professional conscience?” Personal perspectives were examined through reflective questions such as, “How do psychological factors like resilience affect your ability to act ethically?” and “Can you share an experience where upholding moral standards was particularly challenging?” Additional prompts included, “How did that experience affect you?” and “What support helped you grow from it?” This structured sequence allowed for comprehensive, nuanced insights into students’ experiences while maintaining a flexible and participant-centered interview process.

The interview guide, developed collaboratively by the research team to align with study aims, is provided in Supplementary Material 1 for the full interview guide. Three participants took part in follow-up interviews to clarify earlier responses and expand on key themes. Data collection continued until the research team determined that data sufficiency had been reached—when no new substantive insights emerged [21].

Primary interviews lasted an average of 75 min, with subsequent ones around 35 min. Sessions occurred in secluded, serene university venues like conference rooms or designated study spaces, prioritizing ease and discretion. The team convened routinely amid collection to appraise preliminary insights, contemplate interviewing dynamics, and uphold reflexivity alongside procedural integrity. Interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ permission using a digital recorder, and field notes were taken immediately after each session to capture non-verbal cues and contextual observations. The average duration of primary interviews was 75 min (range: 60–90 min), while follow-up interviews averaged 35 min (range: 20–45 min). To enhance participant comfort, interviews were scheduled at mutually convenient times, and a quiet, private room was used to minimize interruptions.

Recordings were digitally captured and transcribed verbatim post-session by the lead author for precision [22]. Team members cross-verified transcripts against originals. Anonymity was preserved via de-identification and pseudonym assignment (e.g., P1, P2), excising identifiers.

Data resided on a secured, encrypted, password-guarded cloud repository limited to team access. Backups were routine to avert loss. Rigorous protocols ensured privacy adherence and ethical compliance, with a comprehensive audit trail logging all handling and choices for accountability [23].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the conventional content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman (2004) [18]. Post-interview, recordings underwent verbatim transcription by a specialist. Transcriptions were conducted by a professional transcriber under the supervision of the lead researcher to ensure accuracy and fidelity to the recordings. Also, transcripts were perused iteratively for holistic immersion in experiences. Meaningful units—spanning words, sentences, or segments—were discerned and encoded, yielding 452 initial codes. Iterative collation and abstraction merged these into 14 sub-themes via similitude and divergence assessments. Sub-themes coalesced into four principal themes, encapsulating the dataset holistically.

The process involved: [1] immersing in the data through repeated readings; [2] identifying meaning units relevant to the research aim; [3] condensing these into codes; [4] grouping codes into sub-themes based on similarities; and [5] abstracting sub-themes into overarching themes through iterative comparison and modification (e.g., refining a theme like ‘inner drives’ to ‘inner motivations and values’ after revisiting codes for religious influences). Disagreements were resolved through team discussions to ensure consensus. This iterative process allowed for constant comparison, where codes were revisited and modified as new data emerged, ensuring themes captured the essence of participants’ experiences without forcing preconceived categories.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) tenets of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [24]. Credibility advanced via member checking post-transcription, returning transcripts and analyses for validation and amendment, affirming representational fidelity. Transferability derived from meticulous contextual and demographic depictions (Table 1), aiding applicability judgments. Dependability stemmed from a detailed audit trail chronicling all stages for replicability and scrutiny. Confirmability was fortified by the lead investigator’s reflexive log, charting biases, sentiments, and responses to curb subjectivity.

Given the first author’s professional background in nursing education, reflexive practices were prioritized to mitigate preconceptions that could influence the framing of questions or interpretation of findings. The reflexive journal facilitated continuous self-evaluation, while peer debriefing sessions with a co-researcher encouraged critical dialogue and alternative interpretations. Disagreements in coding or theme development were resolved collaboratively, with input from a third researcher when necessary to ensure consensus. This triangulated analytical process enhanced the credibility and confirmability of findings through multiple perspectives. For instance, the first author, with a background in nursing education, maintained a reflexive journal to document potential preconceptions about students’ ethical challenges and engaged in peer debriefing sessions to challenge interpretations, such as re-evaluating themes related to role modeling to ensure they reflected participants’ views rather than personal experiences.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Qom University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.MUQ.REC.1403.177). The study complied fully with the Declaration of Helsinki principles governing research with human participants. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed oral and written information about the study’s purpose, procedures, voluntary participation, and data confidentiality. To safeguard anonymity, all personal identifiers were removed from transcripts, and participants were referred to by pseudonyms corresponding to the order of their interviews (e.g., P1, P2).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Sixteen nursing students participated in this study, including 10 females (62.5%) and 6 males (37.5%), with ages ranging from 20 to 30 years (mean age = 24.5 ± 2.8 years). Eleven participants (68.8%) were Bachelor’s students in semesters 4–8 of their nursing program (mean GPA = 16.8 ± 0.9 out of 20), while five (31.2%) were Master’s students in years 1–2 (mean GPA = 17.2 ± 0.7). Participants had 2–8 semesters of clinical exposure (mean = 5.2 ± 1.9), with 9 (56.3%) from urban areas and 7 (43.8%) from rural/small-town backgrounds. Thirteen (81.3%) self-rated their religiosity as high, 2 (12.5%) as medium, and 1 (6.3%) as low. All were recruited from a medical sciences university in Iran. In addition, all participants had GPAs of 16 or above, reflecting the study’s focus on academically engaged students capable of in-depth ethical discussions. (Table 2).

Theme 1: Inner motivations and values

This theme explores how nursing students’ inner lives—their intrinsic motivation shaped by Iranian cultural values, value systems, and reflective capacities—constitute the moral foundation from which professional character and ethical resilience emerge. Participants, particularly those with high religiosity, emphasized how these internal elements provide a personal anchor in the face of clinical uncertainties unique to nursing in resource-variable settings.

Intrinsic motivation and responsibility

Students repeatedly described a self-driven sense of moral obligation that transcended external supervision. This inward motivation, characterized by responsibility and self-accountability, empowered them to act ethically even under pressure. Many associated ethical maturity with an inner voice that guided their decisions long before institutional rules intervened.

They explained that the desire to “do the right thing” came from internal conviction rather than from professors’ monitoring. Their reflections captured the shift from compliance-based behavior to internalized professional conscience:

“When I say I must be responsible in my job, it doesn’t just mean completing tasks. It means accepting whatever results come out of my actions. That sense of duty comes from within me—I want to be this way. I don’t need a professor hovering to remind me to perform correctly; I feel accountable before anyone says a word.” (P2).

Students also described how intrinsic motivation compelled them to acknowledge and correct mistakes without hesitation, viewing errors as tests of ethical integrity rather than failures to conceal.

“The moment I realize I have made a mistake—like giving the wrong medication—I immediately inform the supervisor. I don’t even think twice. I tell myself, ‘If I stay quiet, the patient might suffer.’ Preventing harm becomes more important than protecting myself.” (P8).

This subtheme was especially prominent among Master’s students, who linked it to their advanced clinical exposure, noting how it evolves over time to prevent ethical lapses in high-stakes environments. “As I advanced, I felt a deeper responsibility—not just to complete tasks, but to ensure every action genuinely benefits the patient. It’s like an internal compass pushing me to double-check everything, even when no one is watching.” (P4).

This self-directed moral energy nurtured accountability, courage, and lifelong ethical vigilance.

Personal and religious beliefs/values

Faith and humanistic ethics intertwined as powerful internal compasses guiding behavior. Religious principles such as the distinction between halal and haram shaped students’ ethical awareness, influencing not just what they did, but how they perceived their moral obligations. Their narratives revealed that faith was not confined to ritual but integrated deeply into everyday care practices.

Many recalled evaluating their actions against spiritual standards, ensuring that every clinical task aligned with divine and moral expectations.

“From the start, they taught me about halal and haram. I always consider: ‘Is this action truly right for the patient? Is it permissible?’ This religious framework helps me weigh decisions carefully, ensuring that my care aligns with both ethical standards and spiritual integrity.” (P1).

Ritual practices also infused moments of care with compassion and reverence, showing that even technical procedures could be sacred acts when performed with moral consciousness.

“I pray for myself that my patient doesn’t get worse today. When inserting an IV, I say ‘Bismillah’ so I don’t rupture the vein, especially in elderly patients—it’s a sin to cause unnecessary pain. This practice not only calms me but also reminds me of the moral weight of avoiding harm in vulnerable situations.” (P6).

At the same time, humanistic beliefs broadened their ethical worldview, grounding professionalism in empathy and equality. “Our behavior should always remind us that the person we’re treating is human and deserves respect, not just a source of income. Viewing patients this way fosters a deeper commitment to fairness and compassion in every interaction.” (P5).

Urban-background participants often contrasted this with secular influences, while rural ones tied it strongly to community faith traditions. “My religious upbringing makes me see every patient as a trust from God; I can’t cut corners because it would violate my core beliefs, like ensuring fairness in treatment regardless of the patient’s status.” (P13).

Together, these beliefs fused spiritual integrity with professional ethics in the Iranian cultural context.

Mental health and psychological capital

Moral strength was sustained not only by values but also by emotional resilience and empathy. Students described how optimism, emotional stability, and self-compassion supported their ethical persistence in stressful clinical environments. These psychological capacities functioned as buffers against burnout, preserving their ability to provide compassionate, patient-centered care.

Several participants highlighted how understanding patients’ emotions strengthened their moral purpose, allowing them to extend care beyond duty to genuine human connection.

“In our work, you need to have feelings and understand the patient. I try to offer emotional support. An elderly woman with high blood pressure called me alone from home; I told her what medications to take and followed up. This empathy builds trust and helps me maintain ethical focus during long shifts.” (P1).

Others emphasized the importance of emotional composure and presence, suggesting that small gestures of reassurance were deeply moral acts.

“When my patient was in pain, I kept visiting, reassuring them: ‘I’m here—don’t be afraid—it’s okay.’ Such actions not only alleviate suffering but also reinforce my own resilience in facing emotional challenges.” (P16).

Female participants frequently mentioned empathy as key to handling gender-specific patient vulnerabilities in Iranian healthcare. “Building psychological strength helps me stay calm in crises. Without it, I might overlook a patient’s fear, but resilience lets me act with compassion—like holding a child’s hand during a painful procedure.” (P10).

Psychological capital, therefore, was both a moral and emotional resource, enabling students to translate empathy into sustained ethical care.

Self-reflection and ethical awareness

Self-reflection emerged as a deliberate practice for moral calibration. Students emphasized the importance of reviewing their actions, examining intentions, and learning from emotionally charged experiences. Through self-questioning, they sustained ethical sensitivity and prevented desensitization over time.

Some explained that reflection allowed them to evaluate whether their care aligned with personal and professional ideals, acting as a mirror for conscience.

“After every shift, I reflect: ‘Did I treat that patient as I’d want my family treated?’ It keeps my conscience sharp. This habit helps me identify gaps in my ethical approach and commit to improvements.” (P7).

Others described transformative insights from such reflections, where acknowledging moral hesitation or fear became an opportunity for ethical growth.

“I once hesitated during a painful procedure. When I reflected, I realized fear had clouded my ethics. I promised myself I’d prioritize compassion next time. Reflection turns what could be regrets into lessons that sharpen my moral judgment.” (P4).

This practice was described as evolving with clinical semesters, helping to integrate theory with real-world ethical dilemmas. “Reflection isn’t just thinking back—it’s asking why I felt uneasy during a family consultation, what made me hesitate, and how I can be more honest and confident next time. It helps me build better trust with patients and families.” (P11).

Reflection thus bridged ethical intention and behavior, fostering continuous moral evolution.

Overall, Theme 1 underscores the foundational role of internalized moral agency, where personal drives and reflective practices form the bedrock of ethical resilience in nursing.

Theme 2: Relational dynamics and communication

This theme captures how students’ interactions—with mentors, peers, families, and communities—served as living laboratories for moral learning, shaping ethical awareness through social experience and relational influence within Iranian nursing education. It reflects the collectivist cultural emphasis on interpersonal bonds in fostering professional ethics.

Positive (and negative) role modeling

Role modeling emerged as one of the most influential forces shaping students’ moral development. Observing compassionate and respectful instructors provided tangible examples of ethical practice, while exposure to unprofessional behavior sparked moral resistance and self-definition. Students spoke with admiration about instructors who combined expertise with empathy, leaving enduring impressions of moral excellence. One participant described the profound influence of a specific instructor’s patient interactions starting from early in their training:

“One of the instructors was so warm with patients. From semester two, I remember watching how she greeted them, how she listened, and how her tone instantly made them comfortable. I started copying her gestures without even realizing it. Over time, I noticed I felt better about myself when I acted like her—more confident, more human. She showed me that true professionalism is inseparable from kindness, and now I try to carry that into every interaction.” (P1).

Others highlighted how instructors’ humility inspired reciprocal respect and patient-centered care. A student reflected on a beloved instructor’s lack of arrogance and its ripple effect on their approach to patients:

“We had an instructor everyone loved—no superior attitude… It taught us respect is key, so we respected patients too. Her demeanor encouraged us to build trusting relationships based on equality and empathy.” (P15).

Conversely, witnessing unethical shortcuts or disrespectful conduct prompted ethical dissonance and active rejection. Another recounted an experience where poor hygiene practices were normalized in a clinical setting, initially desensitizing their moral compass:

“In one ward, staff used one dressing set for multiple patients… our conscience didn’t bother us—we thought it was normal. But over time, this made me question standards and commit to advocating for proper hygiene.” (P9).

Some participants described taking moral action in response to these negative models, illustrating their growing moral agency. One student shared how they recognized the risk of mirroring rudeness and proactively addressed it:

“The head nurse in one ward spoke harshly to students. After a few weeks, I caught myself talking the same way—I didn’t like who I was becoming. I reported it and asked to switch wards early. That situation taught me that if I don’t actively resist negative influences, they can shape my behavior without me realizing it.” (P6).

Male participants often focused on negative models in hierarchical settings, using them to define their own leadership style. “I once saw a physician dismiss a patient’s pain without even listening. It upset me deeply. I promised myself that when I’m in that position, I will always listen first. In our culture, honoring someone’s dignity starts with acknowledging their voice.” (P3).

Through these encounters, students internalized the power of example—both positive and negative—in shaping moral identity.

Social support and peer norms

Peers served as emotional anchors and moral mirrors. Students emphasized that collegial encouragement reduced anxiety, nurtured confidence, and created informal ethical communities where shared standards emerged naturally. They recounted instances where peers’ reassurance helped them regain composure during early clinical experiences, fostering moral courage. A participant recalled a moment of peer encouragement during a stressful early semester that enabled them to proceed confidently:

“In semester two, I was really stressed before approaching a patient. One of my fellow interns looked at me and said, ‘Just be precise—don’t worry, you can do it.’ That small encouragement calmed me, helped me start the procedure, and reminded me that we’re responsible not only for our own work but for supporting each other to do things correctly.” (P14).

Group discipline and collective commitment also strengthened accountability, making ethical consistency a shared goal rather than an individual struggle. Another described how their internship group’s high standards positively influenced their own performance and academic outcomes:

“In semester six, our internship group was very disciplined. Everyone came prepared, reviewed the ethics protocol, and reminded each other about patient rights. I was affected by their commitment. Even my GPA improved that term because the group motivated me to keep high standards.” (P8).

This subtheme varied by hometown, with rural students noting stronger peer bonds due to shared community values. “My friends and I, especially those from similar hometowns, agreed to always double-check each other’s tasks before leaving a patient. It built trust among us and prevented mistakes. We became a sort of moral support network—we held each other accountable.” (P7).

This collective ethos nurtured a sense of moral belonging and professional pride.

Family and community influences

Family upbringing and community values served as early blueprints for moral conduct, deeply informing students’ professional ethos. Many traced their compassion and sense of duty back to familial examples of altruism, kindness, and service. Students recalled how moral teachings at home naturally translated into patient-centered behavior in their professional lives. One student connected their mother’s neighborly helpfulness directly to their empathetic view of patients:

“My mother is a homemaker, but she has always helped our neighbors—visiting them when they’re sick, bringing food, or just offering comfort. Growing up with that made me believe caring starts at home. Now, when I see patients, I feel the same sense of responsibility. I treat them as if they are family members. That lesson has stayed with me throughout my training.” (P10).

Cultural narratives about serving others also resonated as enduring moral reminders in clinical practice. A participant drew on a traditional village proverb that continues to shape their diligent patient care:

“In my village, the elders always say, ‘Serve the weak.’ It’s a phrase I grew up hearing. Whenever I’m in the ward and I see someone vulnerable—a child, an elderly person, someone without family—I hear that saying in my head. It reminds me to slow down, pay attention, and never neglect the ones who most need our help.” (P13).

High-religiosity participants integrated family influences with faith, seeing them as intertwined. “Growing up, my father emphasized justice in all dealings; now in nursing, I apply that by advocating for underprivileged patients in our clinics, ensuring no one is overlooked. This foundation shapes my approach to equity in healthcare.” (P5).

These early moral imprints connected personal history to professional integrity.

Overall, Theme 2 highlights the relational ecosystem as a catalyst for moral socialization, where interpersonal influences either reinforce or challenge ethical growth in nursing contexts.

Theme 3: Moral learning in practice

This theme illustrates how structured educational experiences, from classroom learning to clinical immersion, transformed ethical theory into embodied professional values, particularly in the context of Iranian nursing curricula that blend theoretical ethics with practical simulations. It showcases how progressive learning addresses unique challenges like moral distress in clinical rotations.

Formal professional ethics education

Ethics courses provided the conceptual backbone for moral reasoning. Students described classroom discussions on autonomy, confidentiality, and patient rights as crucial foundations that guided daily actions. They recounted moments when theoretical instruction translated directly into ethical behavior at the bedside. One participant illustrated this by explaining how a specific classroom emphasis on informed consent became a habitual practice in routine patient interactions:

“In our ethics class, the instructor always insisted that even the smallest action requires the patient’s permission. At first, it felt unnecessary for routine tasks, but over time it became part of who I am as a nurse. Now, even if I just want to take a patient’s BP, I explain what I’m doing and ask, ‘May I?’ This habit came directly from that course, and I can see how much it builds trust. Patients feel respected when you include them, even in little things.” (P5).

Lessons on confidentiality were particularly impactful, shaping trust-based interactions and reinforcing respect for patient privacy. A student highlighted the deep internalization of this principle and its role in building patient trust:

“Confidentiality stayed with me the most. In class, we talked about privacy, but in practice I realized that patients tell us things they haven’t shared with anyone—not even their family. When I protect that information, it isn’t just following a rule; it’s safeguarding their dignity. And when they notice that, they trust you in a different way.” (P12).

Bachelor’s students viewed this as foundational, while Master’s ones applied it to complex cases. “Ethics education didn’t just teach me what the rules are, but why they matter—especially when caring for vulnerable populations. Now, during my pediatric rotations, I consciously use those principles to mediate sensitive family issues and advocate for the child’s best interest.” (P2).

This theoretical grounding fostered ethical reflexivity in clinical encounters.

Staged clinical experiences

Gradual exposure to clinical responsibility helped students integrate moral and technical skills progressively. With each stage, they gained ethical confidence, learning how to make sound judgments under supervision before acting independently. Participants reflected that early experiences were overwhelming but essential for ethical growth through practice and feedback. Reflecting on their initial challenges with patient communication, one student shared their progression from struggle to ease:

“In the second semester, I could barely take a proper history. I was so nervous that I stumbled over my words and forgot important questions. But with each rotation, I practiced more, watched how nurses talked to patients, and received feedback. Now I can communicate comfortably, educate patients, and handle difficult conversations. That growth didn’t happen overnight—it came with experience.” (P9).

Supervised environments also provided moral safety nets, where students learned accountability without fear of punitive consequences. Another described their tentative start in a high-stakes surgical setting:

“In the surgical ward, I only measured vital signs at first and felt so unsure. But supervision helped me learn without fear of making catastrophic mistakes.” (P6).

This progression helped mitigate early moral distress, as noted by participants with more semesters. “Starting with simple tasks under supervision built my moral judgment. Later, when ethical conflicts came up—like being asked to do something unsafe—I had the confidence to say no. In earlier semesters, I wouldn’t have had the courage.” (P15).

These cumulative experiences built both competence and conscience.

Simulation-based training

Simulated scenarios offered controlled spaces for ethical experimentation, allowing students to face moral dilemmas without real-world harm. These exercises fostered moral courage, critical reflection, and collaborative ethics. Participants recalled vividly how simulated errors during debriefing sessions turned into transformative lessons on integrity and responsibility. One student recounted a simulation involving medication errors and the value of post-exercise debriefing:

“In one simulation, I made a medication error. My heart sank even though it wasn’t real. During the debriefing, we talked openly about honesty, responsibility, and how to disclose mistakes to patients. That conversation changed something inside me. It made me realize that errors aren’t just technical issues—they’re moral moments.” (P11).

They also described learning advocacy through role-play, practicing assertive communication when ethical boundaries were threatened. A participant described how mock emergency scenarios honed their ability to speak up for patient rights:

“During mock codes, we practiced stopping unsafe actions. I remember raising my voice and saying, ‘This violates consent—please stop.’ It felt awkward at first, but practicing it in simulation made it so much easier to speak up in real situations later.” (P14).

Simulations were praised for cultural relevance, such as handling family involvement in decisions.

“In one simulation, we practiced how to respect religious and cultural preferences during end-of-life care. It really prepared me to balance family wishes with the patient’s autonomy in real clinical settings, which is one of the biggest challenges we face.” (P8).

Simulations thus bridged theoretical ethics with moral instinct.

Overall, Theme 3 emphasizes experiential learning as a bridge from ethical theory to practice, fostering moral competence through structured and progressive educational strategies.

Theme 4: Systemic and organizational influences

This theme reveals how moral development was not an individual pursuit alone but flourished within institutional climates that supported fairness, compassion, and professionalism, especially in Iranian healthcare systems facing resource constraints and cultural expectations. It highlights systemic factors unique to public university hospitals.

Organizational culture of the university

Students portrayed the university environment as a moral ecosystem where fairness, inclusion, and equality fostered trust. Feeling valued and respected encouraged them to replicate those behaviors in patient care. They highlighted institutional justice as a source of psychological safety that reinforced ethical standards. One student appreciated the consistent follow-through from advisors, which assured them of equal protection:

“Here in the university nobody cares if your dad is a doctor or a farmer – everyone gets the same chance. My advisor calls me personally if I miss something. That feeling of ‘I matter’ makes me want to treat my patients the same way – like they matter too, not just another bed number.” (P3).

Equitable treatment across backgrounds was another defining feature of this moral culture. Another emphasized the impartiality shown by instructors regardless of students’ socioeconomic status:

“What stood out to me was how instructors treated everyone equally—whether you’re rich or poor, from a big city or a small village, it doesn’t change anything. That fairness stays with you.” (P8).

This culture was seen as countering broader societal inequalities, promoting ethical equity. “Knowing that the university’s policies are fair gives you a sense of security. I tried to carry that same fairness into the clinics—treating every patient equally, no matter their background. Our clinics were culturally diverse, but the value I learned here was that everyone deserves the same care.” (P12).

Such an environment normalized fairness as an ethical baseline.

Ethical climate of clinical settings

Clinical settings with compassionate leaders and collegial support profoundly shaped students’ moral behavior. Witnessing acts of kindness made ethical care tangible and emotionally resonant. Students recalled instances where simple gestures of respect from supervisors set the tone for the entire ward. A participant vividly remembered a head nurse’s warm greeting that transformed patient mood:

“In CCU the head nurse calls every old man ‘dear father’ and every old woman ‘dear mother’. Patients smile so big when they hear it. I started doing the same and suddenly the grumpy grandpa who never talked started telling me about his grandchildren. Small words change everything.” (P1).

They also recognized how emotionally intelligent leadership diffused tension and modeled ethical composure. One student observed a physician’s calm handling of a difficult family situation:

“In the emergency department, a family came in angry and shouting. The emergency physician didn’t raise his voice or push them away. He spoke slowly and respectfully, explained the situation, and within minutes the family calmed down and left peacefully. Watching that taught me more about ethical composure than any lecture—how staying calm can prevent harm.” (P5).

Altruistic actions by staff served as moral exemplars, embedding compassion as the ward’s shared value. Another was moved by colleagues’ generosity toward a vulnerable patient:

“I saw nurses buy clothes and diapers for an abandoned baby with their own money. I cried in the locker room. That day I understood nursing is bigger than salary – it’s love in action.” (P7).

Positive climates were contrasted with stressful ones, emphasizing their role in preventing burnout. “A supportive ward with kind leaders encouraged me to go beyond duty, like spending extra time with isolated patients, fostering a cycle of ethical care.” (P9).

Such climates cultivated enduring ethical sensitivity.

Professional norms and regulations

Rules and standards were seen not merely as administrative constraints but as ethical safeguards ensuring consistency and justice in practice. Students recognized how written protocols translated abstract ethics into concrete actions protecting patient dignity and privacy. One participant noted a strict policy that prevented privacy breaches:

“There was a strict rule forbidding photographing records—even for learning. At first it seemed restrictive, but later I realized these regulations prevent privacy breaches. When rules preserve confidentiality so clearly, they build a culture where trust becomes part of our everyday professional conduct.” (P1).

They also recalled how small procedural routines symbolized moral respect. A student described a routine that upheld modesty during sensitive examinations:

“Whenever we examined pregnant women, our instructor insisted the curtains must be drawn completely. It felt like a small task, but I understood it was about respecting boundaries. These routines honor cultural sensitivities and turn ethical respect into visible, daily practice.” (P6).

Clear documentation requirements reinforced responsibility and accountability. Another explained the importance of verifying and recording patient data before interventions:

“We were always told to check vitals before giving medication and document everything clearly. At first, it felt repetitive, but I came to see how these requirements promote responsibility. Writing the numbers makes you accountable—you can’t skip steps when the record reflects every action.” (P3).

Regulations were viewed as culturally adaptive, aligning with Islamic ethics. “Some rules feel very connected to our values—like having to report mistakes immediately. It reflects honesty and justice. In busy public hospitals, such frameworks prevent harm by making sure we don’t hide errors but learn from them.” (P14).

Regulations, therefore, functioned as the structural backbone of moral practice.

Resource availability and workload

Students described the profound moral impact of adequate staffing and supplies, emphasizing that ethical care is inseparable from working conditions. Material and human resources shaped their capacity to uphold standards of compassion and diligence. They explained that moral shortcuts often arose not from apathy but from exhaustion and scarcity. One student candidly admitted how heavy workloads could undermine ethical priorities, contrasting it with better-staffed shifts:

“During overloaded shifts, you feel tempted to rush or skip small steps—not because you don’t care, but because you’re overwhelmed. But when staffing is good and the workload is balanced, I can slow down and prioritize ethics fully. Adequate resources prevent those unnecessary compromises and let me give holistic care.” (P15).

Conversely, resource adequacy restored their moral satisfaction and ability to care holistically. A participant reflected on the relief provided by improved supplies after previous shortages: “There were times when supplies were so limited that we had to ration basic items, and it felt awful—like we weren’t giving patients what they deserved. Now that resources have improved, I feel a sense of ethical ease again. When you have what you need, you can finally focus on patient needs without facing constant moral dilemmas.” (P16).

This subtheme was critical for rural-background students, who linked it to broader healthcare disparities. “In under-resourced wards, the workload forces hard choices—like focusing on emergencies and letting comfort care wait. It’s painful because you know what ethical care should look like. But with enough staff and supplies, you can engage fully with every patient. Having resources directly strengthens moral resilience.” (P4).

Supportive environments thus sustained both moral intention and ethical performance.

Overall, Theme 4 illustrates the institutional context as an enabler or barrier to moral development, underscoring the need for supportive systems to sustain ethical practice in nursing.

Discussion

The present qualitative content analysis elucidates nursing students’ perceptions of the multifaceted factors shaping their moral character development, yielding four interconnected themes: inner motivations and values, relational dynamics and communication, moral learning in practice, and systemic and organizational influences. These findings resonate with and extend prior empirical and theoretical work, while also highlighting contextual nuances specific to an Iranian undergraduate and graduate nursing cohort. By grounding interpretations in Rest’s Four-Component Model of Morality [25]—which delineates moral sensitivity, judgment, motivation, and character—this discussion compares the study’s results with consistent and inconsistent evidence from the literature, offering interpretive insights into mechanisms of moral maturation, cultural embeddings, and implications for ethical erosion or resilience in nursing education.

Inner motivations and values: foundations of moral agency

The theme of inner motivations and values emerged as a foundational pillar, encompassing intrinsic motivation, personal/religious beliefs, psychological capital, and self-reflection. This clarifies that inner motivation specifically refers to the self-driven impetus for ethical action in clinical care, as described by participants in their experiences of developing moral character—such as acknowledging mistakes to prevent patient harm or integrating faith into compassionate procedures. This aligns robustly with previous research, which identified individual attributes such as self-reflection and character strengths as human factors promoting moral competence in nurses [26]. Students’ descriptions of an “inner voice” guiding ethical decisions without external oversight mirror another study’s emphasis on internalized virtues like integrity and resilience, suggesting that moral character transcends compliance to become a self-sustaining ethical compass [27]. Interpretively, this intrinsic drive may buffer against moral distress, as students framed errors not as concealable failures but as opportunities for accountability—echoing Rest’s moral character component, where perseverance in ethical action persists amid adversity.

Religious and personal values further reinforced this theme, with participants integrating Islamic principles (e.g., halal/haram distinctions, Bismillah invocations during procedures) into compassionate care. This is consistent with quantitative findings in an Iranian context, demonstrating a positive correlation between professional identity formation and moral competencies, often mediated by faith-based values [8]. The fusion of spiritual integrity with humanistic empathy—viewing patients as deserving respect beyond “income sources”—parallels a scoping review on moral sensitivity development, where intuitive recognition of vulnerabilities stems from personal value systems [9]. Psychologically, students’ emphasis on resilience, optimism, and empathy as “buffers” against burnout accords with a predictive model of moral courage, where psychological capital enables ethical action under stress [10]. Self-reflection, described as post-shift conscience-sharpening, directly supports a study in this regard, which posits reflective practices as enhancers of ethical competence [28].

These consistencies interpret moral character as an internalized process rooted in virtue ethics, where personal agency initiates ethical cycles [29, 30]. In the Iranian sociocultural milieu, religiosity (81.3% high self-rating) amplifies this, transforming routine tasks into sacred acts and fostering moral sensitivity—a precursor in Rest’s model. This theme primarily corresponds to Rest’s moral motivation (prioritizing ethics through intrinsic and faith-based values) and moral character (persevering via psychological capital and reflection), suggesting educational interventions should prioritize faith-sensitive reflection to cultivate intrinsic motivation, potentially mitigating the ethical erosion noted in transitions to practice [31].

Relational dynamics and communication: social scaffolds for ethical growth

Relational dynamics and communication revealed role modeling, peer support, and family/community influences as pivotal, yet this theme introduces inconsistencies with literature emphasizing uniformly positive social scaffolds. Positive role modeling—admired instructors’ warmth and humility inspiring patient respect—aligns with a concept analysis of clinical learning environments, where mentor exemplarity fosters ethical socialization [6]. Students’ rejection of negative models (e.g., reporting rude head nurses, resisting normalized unhygienic practices) demonstrates emerging moral agency, consistent with a scoping review on moral courage, which highlights resistance to unethical norms as a developmental milestone [3].

However, inconsistencies arise in the ambivalence of relational influences. While a study categorize role modeling solely as a promotional factor [32], participants here described negative exposures as desensitizing initially (“conscience didn’t bother us”) before sparking dissonance and growth. This nuanced “moral resistance” contrasts with quantitative dominance in moral courage studies [33], which often overlook how adverse models catalyze self-definition. Peer norms provided emotional anchors and collective accountability, echoing a systematic review on nursing as a profession, where collegial influences shape ethical norms [34]. Yet, family/community imprints—proverbs like “serve the weak”—extend beyond typical literature foci on professional socialization.

Interpretively, these dynamics position relationships as “living laboratories” per Rest’s moral motivation component, where prioritizing ethics over conformity emerges through social mirrors, aligned with care ethics theories emphasizing relational interdependence. The double-edged nature—positive models accelerating growth, negative ones risking erosion if unreflected—explains vulnerabilities in a study of undergraduate moral distress from hierarchical disempowerment [12]. In Iran, community-oriented values may heighten relational moral learning, suggesting culturally tailored mentorship programs to harness positive exemplars while debriefing negatives, preventing the “cycles of distress” in clinical transitions [35, 36]. This theme maps to Rest’s moral sensitivity (recognizing ethical issues in relationships) and judgment (reasoning through social influences), with implications for moral pedagogy that fosters virtuous habits through guided interactions [37]. Furthermore, these findings align with strategies Iranian nurses use to manage moral distress through peer support and role modeling [36, 38, 39], extending this to student development by highlighting proactive relational buffers.

Moral learning in practice: bridging theory and action

Moral learning in practice—formal ethics education, staged clinical experiences, and simulation training—consistently bridged abstract principles to actionable ethics, supporting framework for professional values education, where progressive integration builds moral competencies [16]. Classroom emphasis on consent and confidentiality translating to bedside habits aligns with a study on moral sensitivity, where theoretical grounding enhances intuitive ethical recognition [2]. Staged exposures, from vital signs to independent judgment, mirror the gradual ethical maturation in another study [9], fostering judgment and sensitivity under supervision.

This theme resonates with Kohlberg’s stages of moral development [40], where structured experiences facilitate progression from rule-based to principled ethic. Simulations, as safe spaces for ethical experimentation, align with recent evidence on simulation-based training to enhance moral sensitivity among healthcare students [41], offering controlled opportunities to practice judgment without harm. This theme corresponds to Rest’s moral judgment (reasoning in practical scenarios) and sensitivity (recognizing dilemmas in simulations), underscoring the need for integrated pedagogies.

Systemic and organizational influences: institutional enablers of ethics

Systemic and organizational influences—university culture, clinical ethical climates, norms/regulations, and resources—highlighted institutional scaffolds, largely consistent with literature. Equitable university treatment fostering trust parallels findings of another study, mirroring ethical environment analysis [42]. Compassionate clinical climates with altruistic acts echo another study’s structural factors [43], modeling Rest’s motivation.

Inconsistencies surface in resource/workload impacts: students linked shortages to moral shortcuts [44], contrasting idealized views that downplay materiality. This accords with moral distress literature, where scarcity exacerbates ethical compromise [45], but diverges another study’s focus on individual predictors, underscoring systemic culpability [10]. In Iran, workload pressures may amplify this, interpreting resources not as peripherals but ethical enablers—without them, intrinsic values falter. This theme ties to virtue ethics by emphasizing environments that cultivate professional virtues like justice and benevolence [46], and aligns with ethical challenges in Iranian healthcare, where systemic factors contribute to moral distress [47]. It maps to Rest’s moral character (persevering amid systemic barriers) and motivation (prioritizing ethics in supportive climates). Additionally, these systemic influences resonate with meta-competence frameworks in nursing care, where organizational factors shape overall moral competency [26].

Overall, findings affirm moral character as contextualized agency, where individual virtues are not isolated but dynamically shaped by sociocultural embeddings and institutional affordances. In this Iranian cohort, religio-cultural elements—particularly the integration of Islamic principles into daily ethical reasoning—amplify inner motivations, transforming personal faith into a resilient scaffold for moral sensitivity and perseverance amid clinical pressures. Yet, persistent systemic gaps, such as resource scarcity and workload inequities, introduce vulnerabilities that risk misalignment with global standards of ethical practice, potentially perpetuating moral distress if unaddressed. Ultimately, these insights position nursing education as a pivotal arena for intentional moral cultivation: by harmonizing intrinsic conviction with supportive structures, programs can empower students not merely to navigate ethical complexities, but to embody the compassionate integrity that defines the profession’s enduring legacy.

Broader contributions to bioethical discourse

Overall, findings affirm moral character as contextualized agency, where individual virtues are not isolated but dynamically shaped by sociocultural embeddings and institutional affordances. In this Iranian cohort, religio-cultural elements—particularly the integration of Islamic principles into daily ethical reasoning—amplify inner motivations, transforming personal faith into a resilient scaffold for moral sensitivity and perseverance amid clinical pressures. Yet, persistent systemic gaps, such as resource scarcity and workload inequities, introduce vulnerabilities that risk misalignment with global standards of ethical practice, potentially perpetuating moral distress if unaddressed. Ultimately, these insights position nursing education as a pivotal arena for intentional moral cultivation: by harmonizing intrinsic conviction with supportive structures, programs can empower students not merely to navigate ethical complexities, but to embody the compassionate integrity that defines the profession’s enduring legacy. Beyond nursing education, this study contributes to broader bioethical discourse by highlighting cultural relativism in moral development—e.g., how faith-integrated ethics can inform universal bioethical frameworks like principlism, while addressing inequities in global health ethics. It offers insights for bioethicists on fostering moral resilience in diverse, resource-limited contexts, extending discussions on virtue-based approaches to professional ethics across healthcare disciplines.

Limitations

Limitations include single-site Iranian sampling, limiting generalizability; future longitudinal, multicenter studies could track trajectories. Additionally, transferability beyond the Iranian cultural context may be constrained due to the prominence of religio-cultural factors (e.g., Islamic principles integrated into ethical reasoning) in participants’ narratives, which may not resonate as strongly in secular or non-Islamic healthcare education systems. While core themes like role modeling and self-reflection align with global nursing ethics frameworks, adaptations would be necessary for cross-cultural application, warranting comparative studies in diverse settings. Nonetheless, student-voiced perceptions advocate proactive education: reflection portfolios, mentor certification, simulation hubs, ethics committees, and impact metrics. These position nursing programs as moral incubators, fortifying character for ethical healthcare amid complexities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, nursing students perceive moral character development as a multifaceted tapestry woven from inner convictions, relational mirrors, practical forges, and systemic soils—essential for ethical fortitude in turbulent healthcare landscapes. By illuminating these perceptions, this study advocates targeted strategies: embedding self-reflection journals, curating virtuous role models, enhancing simulation ethics, and championing resource-equitable environments. Nursing education must evolve as a moral incubator, empowering students to not only endure but exemplify compassion, integrity, and courage. Ultimately, fostering these stewards promises resilient, patient-centered care, safeguarding the profession’s noble heart amid future challenges.

To operationalize these insights, we recommend a multi-tiered framework grounded in virtue ethics: (1) Curriculum Integration: Emphasize self-reflection as a moral imperative, fostering habitual introspection to cultivate ethical awareness and resilience in decision-making; (2) Role Model Cultivation: Prioritize ethical exemplarity in mentorship as a foundational virtue, ensuring relational influences nurture moral integrity and professional identity; (3) Experiential Learning: Integrate simulation-based training as an ethical forge, enabling safe practice of moral courage and judgment to bridge theory and virtue-based action; (4) Institutional Advocacy: Promote supportive climates and equitable resources as ethical obligations, creating environments that sustain moral motivation and prevent distress; and (5) Evaluative Practices: Adopt ongoing assessments of moral sensitivity as a virtue-driven process, ensuring continuous growth in ethical competence across diverse contexts. These actionable steps, grounded in students’ voices, position nursing programs as proactive architects of moral excellence.
