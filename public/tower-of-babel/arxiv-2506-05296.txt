Control Tax: The Price of Keeping AI in Check
Mikhail Terekhov, Zhen Ning David Liu, Caglar GulcehreSamuel Albanie

Abstract
The rapid integration of agentic AI into high-stakes real-world applications requires robust oversight mechanisms. The emerging field of AI Control (AIC) aims to provide such an oversight mechanism, but practical adoption depends heavily on implementation overhead. To study this problem better, we introduce the notion of Control tax ---the operational and financial cost of integrating control measures into AI pipelines. Our work makes three key contributions to the field of AIC: (1) we introduce a theoretical framework that quantifies the Control Tax and maps classifier performance to safety assurances; (2) we conduct comprehensive evaluations of state-of-the-art language models in adversarial settings, where attacker models insert subtle backdoors into code while monitoring models attempt to detect these vulnerabilities; and (3) we provide empirical financial cost estimates for control protocols and develop optimized monitoring strategies that balance safety and cost-effectiveness while accounting for practical constraints like auditing budgets. Our framework enables practitioners to make informed decisions by systematically connecting safety guarantees with their costs, advancing AIC through principled economic feasibility assessment across different deployment contexts. 1 1 1 Website of the project: https://mikhailterekhov.github.io/control-tax/ . Code: https://github.com/MikhailTerekhov/control-tax-code . The repository also contains the full dataset of backdoors from our attacker models.



1
Introduction

General-purpose AI systems, of which Large Language Models (LLMs) are the most notable example, are quickly making their way into our daily lives. More recently, these systems are also being integrated into broader communication and information processing pipelines. This large-scale deployment, combined with AI reliability issues, brings with it a multitude of risks, coming either from malicious users
(
Greshake et al. 2023
)
or as a subversion by the AI system itself. The latter phenomenon, associated with
AI misalignment
, can be introduced by developers or emerge unintentionally during the training process
(
Betley et al. 2025
)
. To address these issues, the field of
AI control

(
Greenblatt et al. 2023
)
(AIC) seeks to safely extract useful work from highly capable but potentially misaligned AI systems by supervising stronger models with weaker ones at deployment.

Whether AIC is actually adopted by practitioners will depend on the associated overhead. In this work, we introduce the notion of
Control tax
to capture the operational and financial cost of integrating control measures into AI pipelines. We propose empirical and analytical methods to estimate the control tax for a given AI system and a desired level of safety. In Figure
1
, we show the cost-safety trade-offs produced by these methods in the standard setting of code backdooring, where the language model is tasked with introducing subtle flaws into the solutions of competitive programming problems from the APPS dataset
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
.

One key component of many AIC protocols is a
trusted monitor
—an AI, typically assumed to be weaker and safer, that is tasked with detecting misbehavior of the controlled system. The quality of this monitor plays a central role in determining the safety of the protocol. In Section
2
, we evaluate the performance of different LLMs at assigning suspiciousness scores to backdoored code generated by attacker LLMs at various strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of such discrimination abilities at scale, and we will release the code and generated data.

However, comparing ROC curves with different monitors may not be sufficient to address certain practical considerations. Specifically, given a fixed auditing budget, how should practitioners determine the optimal suspiciousness threshold for auditing? In Section
3
, we provide an answer through a novel theoretical model based on the trusted monitoring protocol introduced by
Greenblatt et al. 2023
. This protocol models an adversarial interaction between a stateless red team attempting to insert a backdoor into the system over multiple rounds and a stateful blue team striving to detect these insertions. In this context, safety is defined as the probability of successfully identifying any backdoor.

We analyze two distinct deployment scenarios:
batch
, where all outputs from the untrusted system are available in advance, and
sequential
, where outputs must be approved one by one. Our theoretical framework quantifies the safety characteristics of both scenarios under the criterion where even a single approved backdoor represents system failure.
Collectively, these results provide a rigorous connection between monitoring performance and practical deployment safety. By quantifying the monitoring overhead associated with different safety levels, our framework enables practitioners to translate safety objectives directly into actionable monitoring costs, effectively estimating the necessary control tax for safe AI system deployment.

2
Monitor Performance on Code Backdoors

AIC protocols rely on the ability of one model (the monitor) to identify malicious actions introduced by another model (the attacker).
Although stronger monitors are generally expected to improve the safety of AIC protocols, the precise influence of improved monitoring on protocol safety remains poorly understood.
Accurately characterizing the trade-off between performance gains and associated costs is crucial to guide real-world deployment decisions and to quantify the overall control tax. In this section, we investigate the performance of modern LLMs in detecting code vulnerabilities, aiming to provide deeper insights into how improved monitoring contributes to overall protocol safety and the practical implications of these advancements.

To explore this dynamic, we assess the effectiveness of modern LLMs in both introducing and detecting vulnerabilities. We utilize the APPS dataset
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
due to its extensive number of samples and test coverage, which allows us to clearly define vulnerabilities. The dataset contains
10,000
programming problems at three difficulty levels (introductory, interview, and competition) with train and test splits. We filter the test split with a procedure described in Appendix
A
, resulting in 2427 samples suitable for evaluation. Most problems come with one or more correct solutions written by a human and a set of tests. A test contains a valid input and the output that a correct solution should produce, given the input. Following the methodology outlined by
Greenblatt et al. 2023
, attacker models inject backdoors into the APPS dataset.

2.1
Backdoor Generation

Figure 2
:
A schematic representation of our pipeline to generate backdoors in APPS code.

We first define the notion of a
backdoor
in the context of the APPS dataset. Given a programming problem, we consider a solution
backdoored
if it satisfies all publicly available test cases but produces incorrect outputs for at least one input not included among these public test cases. We will refer to this input as the
backdoor input
. Under this definition, there is an inherent ambiguity between solutions containing honest mistakes and intentionally inserted backdoors. To clearly separate these categories, our pipeline uses correct solutions sourced directly from the APPS dataset, while all backdoored solutions are intentionally generated by attacker models.

Our backdoor generation pipeline, illustrated in Figure
2
, follows an iterative process leveraging LLM-based agents. For each problem, we partition the provided tests into public and private sets, ensuring at most five public tests per problem. Next, we present the attacking model with the problem statement, the public tests, and the correct solution from the APPS dataset. The attacker is tasked with introducing a backdoor into the correct solution and specifying a backdoor input, which should trigger the backdoor behavior. To verify successful backdoor insertion, we check whether this backdoor input results in an output that differs from the output produced by the original, non-backdoored solution.

Given the complexity of reliably generating backdoors, our pipeline uses intermediate steps inspired by
Greenblatt et al. 2023
. We first prompt the attacker model to propose multiple backdoor strategies, which are independently ranked by another instance of the same model. The top-ranked strategies are then returned to the attacker, instructing it to produce backdoored solutions and specify triggering inputs. Each proposed solution must (1) pass all public tests, and (2) produce outputs different from the original on specified inputs. If both conditions are satisfied, we accept the candidate. Otherwise, we provide detailed feedback to the attacker about which condition failed and iterate the process, repeating up to ten attempts per problem. We provide the prompt templates we used in the pipeline in Appendix
B.1
, and some example generated backdoors in Appendix
D
. We also explore how the specific information provided to the backdoor influences backdoor subtlety in Appendix
C.5
. Our results there indicate that the attackers cannot extract benefits from knowing which models will monitor them, nor from having a few examples of backdoors that were successful (not detected by the monitor) and examples of those that were detected.

2.2
Monitor Pipeline

For each attacker model, we select those solutions that have been successfully backdoored and validated by our pipeline. We then pair these backdoored solutions with their corresponding correct solutions from the APPS dataset, creating a balanced monitoring dataset of backdoored and correct solutions. To eliminate potential model biases, we apply a code formatter and remove all comments from the code (which LLMs often use to describe bugs explicitly).

Instead of relying on a simple binary classification, we adopt fine-grained suspiciousness scores (from 1, least suspicious, to 100, most suspicious) to capture nuanced judgments necessary for AIC protocols under limited auditing budgets (discussed further in Section
3
). Monitors evaluate whether the submitted code contains a “subtle, intentionally introduced flaw,” encouraged by chain-of-thought reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
. Prompt templates are provided in Appendix
B.2
. We will publicly release and open-source our backdooring and monitoring pipelines, along with the generated data.

2.3
Results

Success rates of attacker models using our backdoor generation pipeline turn out to be strongly correlated with the underlying performance on the APPS dataset (see Appendix
C.1
for detailed results). Here, we primarily focus on the monitor performance, distinguishing backdoored solutions from correct ones. Figure
3
shows the AUC scores across our full suite of attacker-monitor pairs, highlighting that models conventionally considered stronger perform better at the monitoring task. The strongest model we evaluated, OpenAI’s
o3-mini
, achieves high AUC scores of 0.92 - 0.96.

Interestingly, detection performance remains consistent across a large group of attackers with diverse capabilities, indicating that stronger models do not necessarily create subtler backdoors. This aligns with recent findings by
Engels et al. 2025
, although they perform the evaluations in a simpler setup without agentic scaffolding or numerical suspiciousness scores. One possible explanation could be that public unit tests serve as a pre-filtering mechanism. These tests act as a quality threshold that attacker’s proposed backdoors need to pass. We provide supporting evidence for this hypothesis in Appendix
C.3
, where we show that, among backdoors that fail the public tests, the monitor’s AUC scores clearly depend on attacker strength. It is notable that the most recent models (GPT-4.1 series and Gemini 2.5) do seem to produce subtler backdoors than the rest, indicating a step change in the backdooring capabilities in the newest generation of LLMs.

3
Safety in Long-Term Deployment

Suspiciousness distributions can serve as a starting point for evaluating the control tax, but they do not directly provide safety guarantees in long-term deployments. In this section, we develop two theoretical models that translate ROC curves into probabilities of avoiding backdoors under resource constraints. By combining these theoretical insights with the empirical results from the previous section, we derive optimal behavior both for the attacker and the defender and the corresponding success probabilities.

(f(s),t(s))

s

\mathcal{P}(s),\mathcal{Q}(s)

(1-p_{b})\mathcal{P}+p_{b}\mathcal{Q}

In our multi-step scenarios, the
red team

blue team

c\in[0,1]

3.2
Sequential not Anytime Trusted Monitoring

We now turn to another broadly applicable AIC setting, where tasks arrive sequentially and auditing decisions must be made immediately. In this scenario, we still assume that both the red and the blue team know the total horizon
N
. Following the bandit literature
(
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
, we refer to this condition as the
not anytime
setting. If the red team chooses to insert a backdoor into a given task, inserts a backdoor successfully, and the blue team does not audit, the red team wins immediately. Unlike the offline scenario, the blue team cannot reorder tasks by suspiciousness, making their optimal strategy inherently time-dependent, as detecting late vulnerabilities becomes irrelevant if the system is already compromised.

Intuitively, an effective auditing strategy should allocate a higher budget at the beginning to avoid letting a backdoor slip early and decrease it over time. To formalize this intuition, in the Appendix
E
we prove Lemma
1
, which establishes that rearranging any finite auditing strategy into one with a non-increasing budget as a function of time results in safety at least as good as the original strategy.

Motivated by this, we define the blue team’s
allocation strategy
as a non-increasing, Riemann-integrable function
p:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, representing the expected fraction of audited samples over time. For a protocol with
N
total rounds, the blue team audits on average
p(i/N)
samples at round
i
. The budget constraint requires
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(i/N)=cN
, implying
\int_{0}^{1}p(\tau)\,d\tau=c
as
N\to\infty
. Auditing
p(\tau)
samples per round corresponds to selecting time-dependent thresholds
s(p(\tau),p_{b})
, defined by (
1
), where
p_{b}
is the attacker’s backdooring rate. This time-dependence aligns with prior work, such as exponentially decaying allocations introduced by
Bhatt et al. 2025
.

The sequential not anytime control (
SNAC
) setup is a Stackelberg game where the blue team selects an allocation strategy
p
, and the red team responds with
p_{b}
. Empirically, we observe two regimes for the attacker’s optimal
p_{b}^{*}
. If the blue team allocates significant budgets initially, the attacker’s response scales as
p_{b}^{*}\sim\lambda^{*}/N
for some
\lambda^{*}>0
as
N\to\infty
. Upfront auditing (
p(\tau)=1
for
\tau\leq c
, else 0) exemplifies this regime. Conversely, if auditing allocation initially is relatively small, the attacker’s best strategy is constant backdooring (
p_{b}^{*}=1
). For instance, we analytically show in Appendix
H.3
that a constant allocation
p(\tau)\equiv c
yields
p_{b}^{*}=1
.

Our main result for SNAC setups characterizes the blue team’s winning probability in terms of their allocation strategy
p

p_{b}\sim\lambda/N
as
N\to\infty

\lambda

p
and insertion rate

P_{w}(p,N;\lambda)

\begin{split}P_{w}(p;\lambda)&\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{w}(p,N;\lambda)\\
&=\lambda p_{+}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(\tau))\bigr)\exp\left\{-\lambda p_{+}\tau-\lambda(1-p_{+})\int_{0}^{\tau}\tilde{t}(p(\theta))d\theta\right\}d\tau,\end{split}

(6)

where
\tilde{t}(x)=t(s(x,0))
. The formal statement and proof are presented in Appendix
H.4
. The value of the game for the red team can then be expressed as a variational saddle point problem:

P_{w}^{*}=\min_{p:[0,1]\to[0,1]}\max_{\lambda>0}P_{w}\left(p;\lambda\right).

(7)

subject to
p
being Riemann-integrable and satisfying
\int_{0}^{1}p(\tau)d\tau=c

4
Discussion

We evaluated LLMs for backdoor detection, showing that stronger monitors more effectively distinguish honest from backdoored solutions. This improved discrimination enhances the quantification of AIC protocol safety. Our central contribution is a characterization of the trade-off between monitoring accuracy and associated costs, the “control tax,” essential for informed auditing protocol deployment.

We found that conditioning on both the successful insertion of a valid backdoor and passing standard tests already provides robust safety against simpler backdoor attacks. This suggests that, among the current models, backdoor sneakiness does not significantly increase with stronger attackers. However, explicitly providing attackers sneakiness feedback could change this, and investigating such scenarios is an important future direction.

Our results demonstrate how sample-level monitoring performance translates directly into aggregate protocol safety. Through various safety protocol implementations, we showed that detailed evaluations offer practical safety estimation tools. In sequential (non-anytime) scenarios specifically, we observed safety benefits from concealing the total auditing horizon, mitigating potential strategic vulnerabilities. Additionally, our analysis consistently indicates that optimal adversarial behavior involves inserting a constant average number of backdoors as the sample size grows, a result proven analytically for the batch case and supported empirically in the sequential setting.

Overall, our findings clarify the interplay between model strength, auditing costs, and safety outcomes, laying the groundwork for future robust and economically viable AI safety protocols.

Related Work

Our work contributes to the emerging field of AI Control (AIC), introduced by
Greenblatt et al. 2023
. Current directions in AIC include extending the original APPS backdooring setup to other settings such as agentic tasks
(
Bhatt et al. 2025
)

Korbak et al. 2025
build upon that and propose specific recommendations for the implementation of AIC before deploying strong AI models.
The closest work to our theoretical modeling is that of
Griffin et al. 2024
, who also model AIC in a game-theoretic setting. Importantly, they focus on the safety-usefulness trade-off while we focus on safety versus costs.

Scalable oversight
(
Christiano et al. 2018
)
is a closely related area concerned with evaluating stronger experts using weaker ones, more recently using LLMs as experts
(
Bowman et al. 2022
;
Sudhir et al. 2025
)
.
Burns et al. 2023

Irving et al. 2018

(
Michael et al. 2023
;
Khan et al. 2024
;
Kenton et al. 2024

Engels et al. 2025
proposed a model for scaling laws for scalable oversight and evaluated it in several scenarios, including APPS backdooring. However, they did not use an agentic loop, which lead to underelicitation of attacker capabilities. They also evaluated classifiers that provide binary labels, while a numerical score is essential for our safety evaluations.

AIC draws on broader literature in both security and machine learning. The ability of LLMs to introduce code vulnerabilities has been studied in the context of LLM-assisted coding
(
Schuster et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Yan et al. 2024

;

)
.
Attacker LLMs in AIC are an example of a
model organism of misalignment

)

(
Hubinger et al. 2024
;
Greenblatt et al. 2024
)

6
Conclusions

Our study advances the understanding of AI oversight by building upon recent work on controlling misaligned AI behavior, notably
(
Bhatt et al. 2025
)
and
(
Greenblatt et al. 2023
)
. By analyzing trusted monitoring mechanisms and auditing strategies, we quantify the trade-offs between enhanced safety and the associated costs due to monitoring, the so-called “control tax.” Our results illustrate that stronger monitors significantly reduce malicious behaviors, delineating the relationship between safety improvements and monitoring overhead.

We highlight the particular effectiveness of fine-grained and unpredictable audit strategies, including concealed audit horizons, in mitigating adversarial attempts at evasion. These techniques force malicious agents into benign behavior or inevitable detection, addressing critical concerns around deceptive AI strategies. Furthermore, our use of automated monitoring aligns with scalable oversight frameworks
(
Bowman et al. 2022
)
, enhancing the feasibility of supervising powerful models.

In summary, our results reinforce the practicality and necessity of detailed, protocol-level safeguards, providing empirical evidence that strategic monitoring can reliably align advanced AI systems with safety objectives. We hope these insights inspire continued refinement of control mechanisms to optimize safety within acceptable performance costs.

7
Limitations

Our study has a few key limitations. First, we evaluated our approach only on the APPS dataset, which lacks agentic tasks; future work could extend validation to datasets such as SWE-bench
(
Jimenez et al. 2023
)
or SWE-Lancer
(
Miserendino et al. 2025
)
. Additionally, due to practical constraints, we focused primarily on advanced models (GPT-3.5-turbo and above), leaving room to explore weaker models and optimized prompting strategies in future research. Our honest and malicious solutions were generated differently, with honest ones directly from APPS and malicious ones via attacker models. Future studies could instead generate both solution types from the same attacker models, shifting the evaluation toward malicious solution generation from scratch. A proper theoretical treatment of the anytime monitoring case could also prove beneficial. Another limitation is the explicit use of the ROC curve values for deriving the optimal strategies. In practical deployments, we might not know the true performance of our monitors. Lastly, our analysis focused exclusively on safety and auditing costs; incorporating measures of usefulness, as discussed by
Greenblatt et al. 2023
, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of AI safety trade-offs.

Broader impact.
The techniques presented in the paper for generating backdoored code could potentially be exploited for harmful purposes. However, the paper explicitly focuses on approaches to mitigating the damage from such backdoors, aligning with the standard security practice of proactive threat modeling.

Appendix A
Filtering the APPS Dataset

The APPS dataset consists of some problems whose solutions are defined as Python functions, and some whose solutions are defined as scripts. We only use the latter category, which represents the large majority of problems in the test split. Furthermore, some problems in APPS admit more than one correct answer. For such problems, a
checker script
is necessary to verify the correctness of the answer; simple string equality check is not enough. APPS does not provide such checker scripts. To filter out these cases, we take the problems which have no less than
3
distinct solutions and run each solution on all tests. If even one solution fails on some of the tests, we filter out the problem. For backdoor generation we also only consider problems which have no less than
3
“suitable” public tests. We consider a test suitable to be a public test if its input and output jointly take no more than
1000
characters. This is necessary to fit the public tests into the attackers’ context windows.

C.2
Attackers

The success rates of various models as attackers using our backdoor generation pipeline are shown in Figure
8
. The models’ overall success rates are ordered roughly according to their general coding ability. These rates are strongly correlated with the performance on the APPS tasks themselves, which we report in the previous subsection. Somewhat surprisingly, backdooring performance does not change much w.r.t. the difficulty of the problem. For difficulties, two forces might be pulling the performance in opposite directions. On the one hand, a more difficult problem requires a deeper understanding of the solution, especially if the attacker is to avoid failing the public tests. On the other, an overly simple problem (introductory solutions are sometimes only 5 lines long) does not provide enough attack surface to introduce a subtle flaw, given that the attacker is discouraged from writing simple if-statement backdoors.

We distinguish a stronger notion of a successful backdoor, where the code not only passes the public tests, but the private ones as well (labeled “Passed all tests” in the figure). If the code fails on at least one private test, the result is labeled as “Passed only the revealed tests.” On rare occasions, there are no private tests for a given problem. For completeness, we separate success in these cases into its own category in the figure.

Failure can also happen in several ways. Most often, the backdoors are not subtle enough and fail some public tests. Note that in this case the code still contains a bug, but the bug can be attributed to model’s incompetence rather than to malicious intent. The second most popular reason of failure is that the code’s output on the backdoor input is the same as that of the correct code. In those cases, we cannot know whether a bug exists in the new code at all. More rarely, the attackers fail for other reasons, including not adhering to the expected output format (presented Appendix
B.1
). This failure mode is especially prominent for
Gemini 2.0 Flash
. A preliminary investigation showed that the model in many cases does not use the XML tags required by the prompt, and instead wraps the code and the backdoor input into triple backticks with various names, likely due to this format being much more represented in post-training data. Furthermore, given the performance of this model on the APPS dataset, we suspect that formatting prevented it from otherwise succeeding in a large fraction of the wrongly formatted outputs.

Another reason for failure is the correct code failing to run when presented with the backdoor input. This is often due to the backdoor input not being formatted according to the specifications of the problem. Failing on such an input does not qualify for our definition of a backdoor.

C.5
Attacker prompt variations

In Section
2.3
we discussed that the monitor performance seems to mostly depend on the monitor strength and that we observe no significant variations in changing the attacker strength. To investigate this further, we formed a hypothesis that the prompting did not incentivize attacker models to go for backdoors which are hard to detect, but only aimed for code that is good enough to pass the pipeline.

To motivate models to insert as good of backdoors as they can, we tried 4 different prompting strategies of varying levels of context. The first strategy is the base strategy that was used in
3
. In the second prompt, we let the attacker know that it will be monitored and that it will be competing against other models, thus it should make things as sneaky as possible. The third prompt builds on top of the previous one, by giving information about the monitor name, with the final prompt also giving explicit examples of backdoors which were detected and undetected. Naturally, we exclude these examples from the problem list that the attacker should create a backdoor for. The monitoring pipeline was kept the same throughout these ablations.

C.6
Monitor prompt ablations

In the main paper, we use the monitors that assign scores between 1 and 100. We compare this to more crude 1 - 10 scoring in Figure
12

5
, thus making the scale effectively more crude. AI control requires finely distinguishing the scores at the right tail of the distribution when operating in the realistic regime of low auditing budgets. This means that continuous scores, or at least scores with higher resolution should be beneficial. We leave an exploration of ways to achieve this to future work.

C.7
Fixing monitor miscalibration

Our monitors mostly fail by overestimating the suspiciousness of the provided code. In an attempt to circumvent this, we devise a two-step prompting strategy inspired by the filtering approach of
Davidson et al. 2025
. Our original monitor is only asked for a “suspiciousness” score
S
, which is high when the monitor thinks that the solution is backdoored. Here, we also ask the same monitor in a fresh context for a “correctness” score
C
, which should be high if the monitor thinks the solution is correct. The monitors tend to assign both
S
and
C
higher than average, likely due to sycophancy
[
Sharma et al. 2023
]
. Assuming both scores are between
1
and
100
, we can attempt to cancel both biases and compute a
compound score

\widetilde{S}:=\frac{S+(101-C)}{2}.

(9)

C.9
Non-convex ROC curves

Figure 15
:
Top:
The original ROC curve for an attacker-monitor pair and its convex hull. For both the original ROC and the hull, we plot safety of sequential and batch monitoring using the limit approximation formulation from Section
3.2
.
Bottom:
Optimal blue team strategies for sequential monitoring with the original ROC and the convex hull.

Some of the curves in Figure
28
demonstrate that occasionally batch safety is below sequential safety. Conceptually, one should expect the batch setting to always provide better safety for the blue team since the space of the actions they can take is larger. However, in our setup, the space of actions that the blue team can take in a sequential setting (time-dependent suspiciousness thresholding) is not a subset of the set of actions in the batch setting (a single suspiciousness threshold). Since we see cases where this leads to the batch setting giving worse safety guarantees, we have to conclude that sometimes hard thresholding is not the correct strategy in the batch setting.

We hypothesize that this issue is related to the convexity of the ROC curves.
Scott et al. 1998
show that, given a classifier with a known non-convex ROC curve, we can use a simple probabilistic construction to get a new classifier whose ROC curve is the
convex hull
of the original ROC. Here, we examine an attacker-monitor pair (GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Haiku) for which the ROC curve is prominently non-convex (see Figure
15
left). We then compare the batch and sequential safety with the original monitor’s ROC and its convex hull. Notably, in the second case the batch safety for small
c
goes up and now dominates sequential safety, as one would expect. This implies that the strategies that the blue team has to consider when using a monitor with a non-convex ROC should indeed be probabilistic, but this randomness only comes from the construction to obtain the classifiers on the convex hull.

Safety in the sequential case, on the other hand, is almost identical when using the original ROC or its convex hull. In Figure
15
, we also show the optimal strategies
p(\tau)
for the blue team for both cases at various budgets
c
. We can see that the strategies look similar. Notably, there is a sharp drop to near-zero values from
p(\tau)\approx 0.22
. This threshold corresponds to the FPR where the ROC merges with its convex hull. This means that the blue team should not use the values
p(\tau)
at FPR corresponding to the non-convex regions of the ROC. If these values are excluded, we should indeed expect the safety with the original ROC or its convex hull to be the same.

Appendix E
Characteristics of optimal blue team auditing

Intuitively, trusted monitoring should only work in the case where the monitor has some discriminatory capability. More concretely, in the case of an adversarial monitor, i.e. one which rates honest samples with high suspicion and backdoored samples with low suspicion, the use of the monitor should be discouraged. This intuition can be nicely formalized in a simple requirement, which leads to several implications for the behavior of our functions. For the entire document we will assume that

t\geq f,

(10)

i.e. the monitor at least as good as random guessing.

Having this assumption, we can now derive our first result.

Proposition 1
.

Let
s
be defined like in (
1
), i.e.

t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)p_{b}+f\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)(1-p_{b})=c.

(11)

Then
s
is non-decreasing in
p_{b}
and non-increasing in
c
.

Proof.

We will first show that
s
is non-decreasing in
p_{b}
.
Let us define the function

G(x)\coloneqq t\bigl(s(c,x)\bigr)x+f\bigl(s(c,x)\bigr)(1-x).

(12)

We know that for all
x\in[0,1]
,
G(x)=c
. Suppose that there exist
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
such that
\alpha<\beta
, and
s(c,\alpha)>s(c,\beta)
. Then we have the following

\displaystyle G(\alpha)

\displaystyle=t\bigl(s(c,\alpha)\bigr)\alpha+f\bigl(s(c,\alpha)\bigr)(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq t\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)\alpha+f\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle=\bigl(t\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)-f\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)\bigr)\alpha+f\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)

\displaystyle<\bigl(t\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)-f\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)\bigr)\beta+f\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)

\displaystyle=t\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)\beta+f\bigl(s(c,\beta)\bigr)(1-\beta)

\displaystyle=G(\beta).

The first inequality comes from the fact that
t,f
are non-increasing, and the second inequality comes from
t\geq f
. Due to the strict inequality, we have
G(\alpha)<G(\beta)
which is a contradiction, hence
s(c,\alpha)\leq s(c,\beta)
, and thus
s
is non-decreasing in the second argument.

For the second part we define

H(x)\coloneqq t\bigl(s(x,p_{b})\bigr)p_{b}+f\bigl(s(x,p_{b})\bigr)(1-p_{b}).

(13)

We know from (
11
) that
H(x)=x
, hence it is an increasing function. Suppose again that we have
\alpha<\beta
such that
s(\alpha,p_{b})<s(\beta,p_{b})
. Then we have

\displaystyle\alpha=H(\alpha)

\displaystyle=t\bigl(s(\alpha,p_{b})\bigr)p_{b}+f\bigl(s(\alpha,p_{b})\bigr)(1-p_{b})

\displaystyle\geq t\bigl(s(\beta,p_{b})\bigr)p_{b}+f\bigl(s(\beta,p_{b})\bigr)(1-p_{b})=H(\beta)=\beta.

Thus we obtain a contradiction, and hence
s(\alpha,p_{b})\geq s(\beta,p_{b})
, which means that
s
is non-increasing in the first argument.
∎

Recall that the exact expression for red-team winning in N turns is given by

\begin{split}P_{w}\left(p,N;p_{b}\right)&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(p(k),p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\\
&\quad\times\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(p(i),p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(p(i),p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr],\end{split}

(14)

where
p:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow[0,1]
describes the auditing budget at each turn. While solving for the exact optimal
p
that minimizes the red team win probability of any given choice of parameters is too complex, we are able to prove that optimal
p
have to be non-increasing.

Lemma 1
.

Let
N\in\N
,
p_{b},p_{+}\in[0,1]
,
t:\R\to[0,1]
a non-increasing TPR function and
s:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\R

a suspiciousness threshold. Suppose that for some auditing strategy
p:\{1,\dots,N\}\to[0,1]
we have

p(i)<p(j)\quad\text{for some }\,1\leq i<j\leq N.

Define
q
by swapping
p(i)
and
p(j)
:

\displaystyle q(m)=\begin{cases}p(j),&m=i,\\
p(i),&m=j,\\
p(m),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(15)

Then

P_{w}\left(p,N;p_{b}\right)\;\geq\;P_{w}\left(q,N;p_{b}\right).

Proof.

First note that any transposition of non-adjacent entries can be realized by a sequence of adjacent swaps, each of which strictly decreases
P_{w}
. Thus it suffices to prove the claim when
j=i+1
.

We will start by rewriting some of the terms. Set

\alpha_{p,k}\;=\;t\bigl(s(p(k),p_{b})\bigr),\qquad A_{p,k}\;=\;(1-\alpha_{p,k})\,p_{b}\,p_{+},\qquad B_{p,k}\;=\;1-p_{b}\bigl[p_{+}+\alpha_{p,k}(1-p_{+})\bigr].

From the fact that
t
is non-increasing and
s
is non-increasing in the first argument (following Proposition
1
), we have that the composition is non-decreasing in the first argument. Since we assume that
p(i)<p(i+1)
we must have

\alpha_{p,i}\leq\alpha_{p,i+1}.

(16)

A direct check shows

P_{w}\left(p,N;p_{b}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{p,k}\,\prod_{m=1}^{k-1}B_{p,m}.

Furthermore, the definition of
q
(
15
) implies:

\displaystyle\alpha_{p,m}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{q,j}&m=i,\\
\alpha_{q,i}&m=j,\\
\alpha_{q,m}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(17)

Analogous equations hold for
A
and
B
.

Consequently we know that
\prod_{m=1}^{k-1}B_{p,m}=\prod_{m=1}^{k-1}B_{q,m}
for
k\leq i
or
k>j
. Combining the observations above, we can express the difference as

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=P_{w}(p,N;p_{b})-P_{w}(q,N;p_{b})

\displaystyle=R\Bigl[(A_{p,i}+A_{p,i+1}B_{p,i})-(A_{q,i}+A_{q,i+1}B_{q,i})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=R\Bigl[(A_{p,i}+A_{p,i+1}B_{p,i})-(A_{p,i+1}+A_{p,i}B_{p,i+1})\Bigr]

where
R=\prod_{m=1}^{k-1}B_{p,i}
.

Since
R\geq 0
, it remains to show

(A_{p,i}+A_{p,i+1}B_{p,i})-(A_{p,i+1}+A_{p,i}B_{p,i+1})\geq 0.

Observing

1-B_{p,k}=p_{b}\bigl[p_{+}+\alpha_{p,k}(1-p_{+})\bigr],

one finds

\displaystyle(A_{p,i}+A_{p,i+1}B_{p,i})-(A_{p,i+1}+A_{p,i}B_{p,i+1})

\displaystyle=A_{p,i}\bigl(1-B_{p,i+1}\bigr)-A_{p,i+1}\bigl(1-B_{p,i}\bigr)

\displaystyle=p_{b}^{2}\,p_{+}\Bigl[(1-\alpha_{p,i})\bigl(p_{+}+\alpha_{p,i+1}(1-p_{+})\bigr)-(1-\alpha_{p,i+1})\bigl(p_{+}+\alpha_{p,i}(1-p_{+})\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=p_{b}^{2}\,p_{+}\,(\alpha_{p,i+1}-\alpha_{p,i})\;\geq\;0,

which we know from (
16
).

Therefore
\Delta\geq 0
, i.e.
P_{w}(p,N;p_{b})\geq P_{w}(q,N;p_{b})
, whenever the swap is adjacent. By decomposing any distant swap into adjacent ones, the general claim follows.

∎

It is noteworthy that this lemma only applies when the auditing is finite, as in the infinite case the decomposition into adjacent swaps does not hold.

is non-increasing (and thus converges to a finite limit), reflecting scenarios where scrutiny naturally decreases or resources become limited over time.

For the proofs below we will use the notation from Section

i

B_{i}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{b})
.
B_{i}=1
means that the backdoor is inserted. We will, with a slight abuse of notation, denote
B_{i}
as the event
B_{i}=1
for this and other binary random variables when the meaning is clear from the context.
C_{i}
is a binary random variable that denotes whether a correct backdoor is inserted. It only depends on
B_{i}
and has the conditional distributions
\mathbb{P}[C_{i}\mid B_{i}]=p_{+}
and
\mathbb{P}[C_{i}\mid\overline{B_{i}}]=0

R

i

A_{i}

H.1
Safety of upfront auditing

In upfront auditing, the blue audits the first
cN
samples, i.e.
A_{i}=1
for
i<cN
and
A_{i}=0
otherwise. Red team wins if no backdoored submissions were audited and there was at least one successful backdoor among the rest of the submissions:

R=\left\{\left(\nexists i<cN:\ B_{i}\right)\land\left(\exists i\geq cN:\ C_{i}\right)\right\}.

(19)

The probability of the red win will be denoted, like in the main paper, as
P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})=\mathbb{P}[R]
. In upfront auditing, we only need to check whether
B_{i}
does not occur among the first
cN
samples and
C_{i}
occurs at least once among the last
(1-c)N
samples:

P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})=(1-p_{b})^{cN}\left(1-(1-p_{b}p_{+})^{(1-c)N}\right).

(20)

We do not know of a closed-form expression for the maximizer of this expression, but we can maximize it asymptotically as
N\to\infty
. To that end, we introduce a substitution
p_{b}=\lambda/N
. We have

\displaystyle P_{w}(c,N;\lambda/N)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[cN\left(-\frac{\lambda}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right)\right]\left(1-\exp\left[(1-c)N\left(-\frac{p_{+}\lambda}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right)\right]\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=\exp[-c\lambda]\left(1-\exp[-(1-c)p_{+}\lambda]\right)+o(1).

(22)

Let
h(\lambda)=\exp[-c\lambda]\left(1-\exp[-(1-c)p_{+}\lambda]\right)
. We can find

\hat{\lambda}=\argmax_{\lambda}h(\lambda)=\frac{1}{(1-c)p_{+}}\log\frac{c+(1-c)p_{+}}{c}.

(23)

As
N\to\infty
,
P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})
uniformly converges to
h
around
\hat{\lambda}
, so we can exchange the limit and
\argmax
to find that

p_{b}^{*}=\frac{1}{(1-c)p_{+}}\log\frac{c+(1-c)p_{+}}{c}\frac{1}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(24)

If we substitute this back into (
20
), we get after some transformations

\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{w}(c,N;p_{b}^{*})=\left(\frac{c}{c+(1-c)p_{+}}\right)^{\frac{c}{(1-c)p_{b}}}\left(1-\frac{c}{c+(1-c)p_{+}}\right),

(25)

which is equivalent to (
3
) from the main paper.

H.2
Asymptotics of Batch Trusted Monitoring

In the batch setting, the blue team receives all code submissions from the red team, runs the monitor on all of them, and audits the ones whose suspiciousness is above the threshold
s(c,p_{b})
, where
s
is defined by (
1
). More formally, given a monitor with the ROC curve
\bigl(f(s),t(s)\bigr)
and the suspiciousness distributions of benign and backdoored code
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
as defined in the paper, the suspiciousness score of the
i
-th sample is a random variable
S_{i}
in
\R
defined by its conditional distributions
(S_{i}\mid\overline{B_{i}})\sim\mathcal{P}
and
(S_{i}\mid B_{i})\sim\mathcal{Q}
. The event that the
i
-th sample is audited is defined as
A_{i}=\{S_{i}\geq s(c,p_{b})\}
. Note that, the random variables
A_{i},B_{i},C_{i}
(and all derived random variables) depend on
c
and
p_{b}
, but for notational convenience, throughout the appendix we implicitly assume this dependence.

The red team has to insert at least one successfully backdoored sample that is not audited and not insert a backdoor into a sample that is audited:

R=\left\{\left(\exists i\ C_{i}\land\overline{A_{i}}\right)\ \land\ \left(\nexists i\ B_{i}\land A_{i}\right)\right\}.

(26)

Furthermore, since the blue team can reorder the samples, the order of the samples does not come into the win event definition (
26
). If the red team submits any backdoor sample with a high suspiciousness, no matter when they do it, they lose.

Theorem 1

(Asymptotics of batch trusted monitoring)
.

Consider a trusted monitoring setup with
N
rounds and a monitor with the ROC curve
\bigl(f(s),t(s)\bigr)
and the corresponding suspiciousness distributions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
. The blue team can submit a fraction
c\in[0,1]
of samples for auditing in expectation. The red team picks a backdooring probability
p_{b}
and the blue team picks a suspiciousness threshold
s(c,p_{b})
satisfying (
1
). Let the events
B_{i},C_{i},A_{i}
, the random variable
S_{i}
and the function
s(c,p_{b})
be defined as above, and the event
R
be defined as (
26
). We will define the probability of the red team winning
P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})=\mathbb{P}[R]
, where we omit the dependency of
P_{w}
on
t,f
, and
p_{+}
for clarity. Furthermore, let
p_{b}^{*}=\argmax_{p_{b}}P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})
be the optimal backdooring probability for the attacker. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle p_{b}^{*}=\frac{1}{d_{0}-t_{0}}\log\left(\frac{d_{0}}{t_{0}}\right)\frac{1}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

(27)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{p_{b}}P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})=(t_{0}/d_{0})^{t_{0}/(d_{0}-t_{0})}\left(1-t_{0}/d_{0}\right),

(28)

where
t_{0}=t(s(c,0))
and
d_{0}=t_{0}+(1-t_{0})p_{+}
.

Proof.

By the definition of TPR and FPR, the probabilities of auditing a benign and backdoored sample are given by
\mathbb{P}[A_{i}\mid\overline{B}_{i}]=f(s(c,p_{b}))
and
\mathbb{P}[A_{i}\mid B_{i}]=t(s(c,p_{b}))
respectively. Then, the probability that the blue catches red on the
i
-th turn can be expressed as

q:=\mathbb{P}[B_{i}\land A_{i}]=t(s(c,p_{b}))p_{b}
, the probability that red submits a successful backdoor on a given turn and is not audited is given by
u:=\mathbb{P}[\overline{A}_{i}\land C_{i}]=\bigl(1-t(s(c,p_{b}))\bigr)p_{b}p_{+}
. The probability that none of these two events ever happens is given by
\mathbb{P}[\nexists i\ (B_{i}\land A_{i})\lor(\overline{A}_{i}\land C_{i})]=(1-q-u)^{N}
. Then, the probability that the blue wins is given by

\mathbb{P}[\overline{R}]=(1-q-u)^{N}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-q)^{i-1}q=(1-q-u)^{N}+1-(1-q)^{N},

(29)

where the sum gives the probability that the blue audits a backdoor at least once. Consequently, substituting values for
q
and
u
back,

P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})=\mathbb{P}[R]=(1-t(s(c,p_{b}))p_{b})^{N}-\left(1-t(s(c,p_{b}))p_{b}-\bigl(1-t(s(c,p_{b}))\bigr)p_{b}p_{+}\right)^{N}.

(30)

To get the asymptotics of
p_{b}^{*}
, we will start by introducing a change of variables
p_{b}=\lambda/N
. Since
s(c,p_{b})
and
t(s)
are continuous,
t\,\circ\,s
is continuous as well. Then, we have
t(c,p_{b})=t_{0}+o(1)
, where
t_{0}=t(s(c,0))
. Here and in the following, the O-notation is written as
N\to\infty
. Substituting into (
30
),

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\lambda}\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{t_{0}\lambda}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right)^{N}-\left(1-\frac{t_{0}+(1-t_{0})p_{+}}{N}\lambda+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right)^{N}}_{\text{we will denote this as $S_{N}(\lambda)$}}

(31)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\lambda}\big(\exp\left[-t_{0}\lambda\right]-\exp\left[(t_{0}+(1-t_{0})p_{+})\lambda\right]+o(1)\big).

(32)

Let
h(\lambda)=\exp\left[-t_{0}\lambda\right]-\exp\left[-(t_{0}+(1-t_{0})p_{+})\lambda\right]
. We can find

\hat{\lambda}=\argmax_{\lambda}h(\lambda)=\frac{1}{(1-t_{0})p_{+}}\log\frac{t_{0}+(1-t_{0})p_{+}}{t_{0}}.

(33)

As
N\to\infty
,
S_{N}(\lambda)
uniformly converges to
h(\lambda)
in a neighborhood of
\hat{\lambda}
. Hence, we can exchange the limit and
\argmax
and conclude that
\lambda^{*}=\hat{\lambda}+o(1)
. Substituting back into
p_{b}
, we get

p_{b}^{*}=\frac{1}{(1-t_{0})p_{+}}\log\left(\frac{t_{0}+(1-t_{0})p_{+}}{t_{0}}\right)\frac{1}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(34)

∎

H.3
Sequential not anytime trusted monitoring with uniform allocation strategy

Before we prove properties of the sequential not anytime trusted monitoring setting, we will first define what we mean by red team winning.

Definition 1
.

Let the monitor’s TPR and FPR are given by continuous functions
t,f:\R\to[0,1]
corresponding to suspiciousness distributions
\mathcal{P}(s),\mathcal{Q}(s)
of benign and backdoored code. We define the events
B_{i},C_{i}
and a random variable
S_{i}
just like in Theorem
1
with a backdoor success probability
p_{+}
. Furthermore, let
A_{i}=\left\{S_{i}\geq s(p(i/N),p_{b})\right\}
be the event that the
i
-th code is submitted for auditing, where
s
is given by (
1
). We define the event of the red team winning as

R_{snac}=\left\{\exists i\ \left(\forall j<i\ \overline{B_{j}}\lor\overline{A_{j}}\right)\land C_{i}\land\overline{A_{i}}\right\},

(35)

i.e. a correct backdoor is submitted at a time
i
, not audited, and before that no backdoors were audited.

This event’s definition implicitly depends on
t,f,p,N,p_{b}
, which we omit for consistency with the rest of the paper.

We will now prove that, under the simple constant allocation budget strategy, the optimal behavior of the red team is to insert backdoors with probability
p_{b}^{*}=1
. Intuitively, to understand why this holds true for the online not anytime trusted monitoring scenario, one can interpret
p_{b}
as representing the trade-off between “inserting a backdoor immediately” and “waiting to insert a backdoor in the future.” When the budget is constant, the attacker gains no advantage by delaying, since the per-turn success probability remains unchanged over time. Moreover, in a scenario without the anytime property, the red team loses if no backdoor is inserted within
N
turns. Given that the success probability is constant across turns, the attacker maximizes their chance of success by attacking immediately—thus implying
p_{b}^{*}=1
. Before we prove the main theorem, we will show the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For any fixed
c\in[0,1]
the function
F:[0,1]\to\R
, given by
F(p_{b})=p_{b}t(s(c,p_{b}))
is non-decreasing in
p_{b}
.

Proof.

Recall from
1
that for all
p_{b}\in[0,1]
we have

t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)p_{b}+f\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)(1-p_{b})=c,

and therefore

p_{b}t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)=c-f\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)(1-p_{b}).

(36)

Proposition
1
tells us that
f(s(c,p_{b}))
is non-increasing in
p_{b}
, and we note that
(1-p_{b})
is decreasing in
p_{b}
. As a result the right-hand side is a non-decreasing function in
p_{b}
, proving that the left-hand side must be as well.

∎

We will now formalize this intuition with the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a trusted monitoring setup with
N
rounds. Suppose that the blue team auditing strategy is given by the constant function function
p:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
, with
p(x)=c
for all
x\in[0,1]
where
c\in[0,1]
. Then
p_{b}=1
maximizes
P_{w}(c,N;p_{b}):=\mathbb{P}\left[R\right]
.

Proof.

If the blue team on the
k
-th step uses the threshold
s(p(k/N),p_{b})
to decide whether to audit a given sample, then, by the definition of TPR, the probability of a backdoored sample being audited will be
t(s(p(k/N),p_{b}))
. Then, the total red team win probability is given as a sum over
k
of probabilities that the attacker wins on the
k
-th turn:

\begin{split}P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})&=\mathbb{P}\left[R_{snac}\right]\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(p(k/N),p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\\
&\quad\times\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(p(i/N),p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(p(i/N),p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr].\end{split}

(37)

Following our assumption that
p(x)=c
is a constant function, we obtain the simplify the expression as follows:

\begin{split}P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})&=\mathbb{P}\left[R_{snac}\right]\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\times\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr]\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr]^{k-1}\\
&=\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr]^{k-1}\\
&=\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\frac{1-\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr]^{N}}{t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}+\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}}\\
&=\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{+}\frac{1-\Bigl[1-p_{b}p_{+}-\bigl(1-p_{+}\bigr)t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}\Bigr]^{N}}{p_{+}+t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\left(1-p_{+}\right)}.\end{split}

(38)

We notice the following:

•

\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)
is non-decreasing in
p_{b}
,

•

\bigl[p_{+}+t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\left(1-p_{+}\right)\bigr]^{-1}
is non-decreasing in
p_{b}
,

•

1-\Bigl[1-p_{b}p_{+}-\bigl(1-p_{+}\bigr)t\bigl(s(c,p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}\Bigr]^{N}
is also non-decreasing in
p_{b}
.

Therefore
P_{w}(c,N;p_{b})
is non-decreasing in
p_{b}
and is maximized at
p_{b}=1
.

∎

H.4
Online not anytime trusted monitoring

Theorem 3

(Asymptotics of sequential not anytime monitoring)
.

Consider a trusted monitoring setup with
N
rounds. The monitor’s TPR and FPR are given by continuous functions
t,f:\R\to[0,1]
. The attacker picks a backdooring rate
\lambda>0
so that the corresponding backdooring probability is
p_{b}=\lambda/N
. Then, backdoor incidence over time in an appropriate sense converges to a Poisson point process over a continuous time period
[0,1]
with the rate
\lambda
. The blue team follows the auditing budget allocation strategy defined by a Riemann-integrable function
p:[0,1]\to[0,1]
. Let events
B_{i},C_{i}
and a random variable
S_{i}
be defined as in Theorem
1
with a backdoor success probability
p_{+}
. Further, let the event
A_{i}
of auditing and
R_{snac}
of the red team winning be given by Definition
1
and
P_{w}(p,N;\lambda/N):=\mathbb{P}[R_{snac}]
.

Defining
\tilde{t}(x)=t(s(x,0))
for brevity, the red team winning probability is given by

\begin{split}P_{w}(p;\lambda)&:=\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{w}(p,N;\lambda/N)\\
&=\lambda p_{+}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(\tau))\bigr)\exp\left\{-\lambda\tau p_{+}-\lambda(1-p_{+})\int_{0}^{\tau}\tilde{t}(p(\theta))d\theta\right\}d\tau.\end{split}

(39)

Denoting the expression on the RHS as
F\left[p;\lambda\right]
, the optimal rate for the attacker is then found as
\argmax_{\lambda}F\left[p;\lambda\right]
, and the optimal strategy for the blue team is found as a solution to the following minimax optimization problem:

\min_{p:[0,1]\to[0,1]}\max_{\lambda>0}F[p;\lambda].

(40)

subject to
p
being Riemann-integrable and satisfying
\int_{0}^{1}p(\tau)d\tau=c
.

Proof.

Given that the attack event at each step occurs according to an i.i.d. sample from
\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\lambda/N)
, we can use the standard construction of the Poisson point process
[
Gallager 2011
]
to show that the random number of backdoors between points
\tau_{1}N
and
\tau_{2}N
for
0\leq\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq 1
converges to
\operatorname{Poisson}(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})
in distribution, and that the required independence properties hold.

Just like in Theorem
2
, the red team win probability is given by

\begin{split}P_{w}(p,N;p_{b})&:=\mathbb{P}[R_{snac}]\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(p(k/N),p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\\
&\quad\times\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl[1-t\bigl(s(p(i/N),p_{b})\bigr)\,p_{b}-\bigl(1-t\bigl(s(p(i/N),p_{b})\bigr)\bigr)\,p_{b}\,p_{+}\Bigr].\end{split}

(41)

Since
t(\cdot)
is continuous,
t(s(\cdot,\cdot))
is also continuous. Denoting
\tilde{t}(x)=t(s(x,0))
like in the theorem statement, we have that
t(s(p(k/N),\lambda/N))=\tilde{t}(p(k/N))+o(1)
as
N\to\infty
, and this holds uniformly over the values of
p(k/N)
, since
t(s(\cdot,\cdot))
is continuous on a compact domain
[0,1]\times[0,1]
. Now we can substitute
p_{b}=\lambda/N
into (
41
):

\begin{split}P_{w}(p,N;\lambda/N)&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(k/N))+o(1)\bigr)\,\lambda\,p_{+}\\
&\quad\times\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl[1-\tilde{t}(p(i/N))\,\lambda/N-\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(i/N))\bigr)\,p_{+}\,\lambda/N+o(1/N)\Bigr].\end{split}

(42)

Due to uniform convergence, we can pull the first
o(1)
term out of the sum as
o(N)
. We can also rewrite the product as an exponential, using
\log(1+x)=x+o(x)
as
x\to 0
:

\begin{split}P_{w}(p,N;\lambda/N)&=o(1)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(k/N))\bigr)\,\lambda\,p_{+}\\
&\quad\times\exp\left[-\frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(\tilde{t}(p(i/N))+\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(i/N))\bigr)\,p_{+}\Bigr)\right].\end{split}

(43)

The outer sum
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
is a Riemann sum, which converges to the corresponding integral
\int_{0}^{1}d\tau
as
N\to\infty
, where we need to substitute
k/N
by
\tau
. The inner sum, correspondingly, is a Riemann sum of the integral
\int_{0}^{\tau}d\theta
with the substitution
i/N
by
\theta
. Applying these substitutions,

\begin{split}&P_{w}(p,N;\lambda/N)\\
&\quad=o(1)+\lambda p_{+}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(\tau))\bigr)\exp\left[-\lambda\int_{0}^{\tau}\Bigl(\tilde{t}(p(\theta))+\bigl(1-\tilde{t}(p(\theta))\bigr)p_{+}\Bigr)d\theta\right]d\tau.\end{split}

(44)

This coincides with (
39
) up to rewriting. The optimal behavior of the red team is to pick a
\lambda
that would maximize this expression, and the blue team then picks the allocation strategy for an optimal outcome for them, leading to the minimax formulation (
40
) as the game equilibrium.

∎

Appendix J
Numerical Optimization of the SNAC Problem

We implemented saddle point optimization for both the exact finite
N
expression and the limit approximation. The finite optimization problem is given by

P_{w,finite}^{*}:=\min_{p\in[0,1]^{N}}\max_{p_{b}\in[0,1]}P_{w}(p,N;p_{b})

(45)

subject to
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(i)=c
, where
P_{w}
is defined by (
14
).

The limiting approximation problem is given by

P_{w,infinite}^{*}:=\min_{p\in[0,1]\to[0,1]}\max_{\lambda>0}P_{w}(p;\lambda),

(46)

subject to
\int_{0}^{1}p(\tau)=c
, where
P_{w}
is defined by (
39
).

In the paper in Figure
6
we report the solution to the exact problem. We show how the limit approximations around the optimal
p_{b}^{*}
correspond to the exact values in Figure
23
. Already from
N=100
the limit approximation is indistinguishable from the exact expression. The limit approximation can also be computed faster and implemented easier than the exact expression. For the limit approximation expression, we still discretize
p(\tau)
into
N
equally spaced values. In Figure
5
, we report the sequential safety values with
N=1000
. We use the Adam optimizer from PyTorch with learning rate
10^{-2}
and default parameters (
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
,
\varepsilon=10^{-8}
, no weight decay).

Inner maximization over
p_{b}
or
\lambda
.
To perform the inner maximization, we make use of the fact that it happens over a scalar. For
p_{b}
, we maximize over a discrete set of values

P_{b}:=\left\{\exp\left[\log p_{b}^{min}+\frac{i-1}{M-1}\log\frac{p_{b}^{max}}{p_{b}^{min}}\right]\mid i\in\{1,\dotsc,M\}\right\},

(47)

where
p_{b}^{min}=10^{-7}
,
p_{b}^{max}=1
, and
M=500
. For the limit approximation, we pick
\lambda
from the set

\Lambda:=\left\{\exp\left[\log\lambda^{min}+\frac{i-1}{M-1}\log\frac{\lambda^{max}}{\lambda^{min}}\right]\mid i\in\{1,\dotsc,M\}\right\},

(48)

where
\lambda^{min}=0.1
,
\lambda^{max}=10
, and
M=500
. Inner maximization over discrete sets allows us to keep the outer minimization problem differentiable w.r.t.
p
.

Parameterization of
p
.
Outer minimization happens over the set

P=\left\{p_{i}\in[0,1]^{N}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=cN\right\}.

(49)

This a subset of a simplex restricted by the requirement for
p_{i}
to be between
0
and
1
. To perform first-order optimization over this set we use a parameterization
\mathcal{P}:\R^{N}\to P
which maps optimized parameters
\theta\in\R^{N}
to

\mathcal{P}(\theta)=\left[\sigma(\theta_{1}+\mu)\ \cdots\ \sigma(\theta_{N}+\mu)\right]^{\intercal},

(50)

where
\sigma(x)=(1+e^{-x})^{-1}
is the sigmoid function and
\mu\in\R
is a value selected so that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma(\theta_{i}+\mu)=cN.

(51)

Such value always exists since
\mu\to-\infty
makes the sum approach
0
and
\mu\to+\infty
makes it approach
N
. Furthermore, the search for
\mu
satisfying (
51
) can in practice be performed in a few iterations of the Newton’s method. We implement the mapping (
50
) and the Newton iteration search for
\mu
in PyTorch, which allows us to backpropagate the gradient to
\theta
. The parameterization
\mathcal{P}
can represent any element of the set
P
except for those where at least one of the values
p_{i}
is exactly
0
or
1
. In practice this does not lead to issues since sufficiently close approximations of
0
do not influence the final value of the win probability
P_{w}
. Furthermore, Lemma
1
allows us to restrict the search to non-increasing strategies
p
. We sort the values of
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
in the non-increasing order. In our experience, this allows for better convergence of the optimization. For the limiting approximation, we further enforce
p(0)=1
during optimization to avoid the
p_{b}=1
maximum as discussed in Appendix
F
.

Approximating TPR and FPR functions.
To approximate the functions
t(s)
and
f(s)
from the discrete samples of monitor suspiciousness values, we use kernel density estimation with a Gaussian kernel
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma=3
. We further approximate the kernel density values with linear interpolation on a dense set of
5000
equally spaced values of
s
between
-15
and
115
. This linear interpolation is necessary to solve (
11
) and obtain
s(p_{i},p_{b})
.
