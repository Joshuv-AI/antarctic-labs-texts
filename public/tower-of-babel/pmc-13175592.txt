Clinical Applications of Deep Learning for Glottal Area Segmentation and Glottal Area Waveform Feature Computation
Authors: Ying-Chang Wu, Sheng-Fu Liang, Ming-Chi Cheng, Hui-Chen Su, Cheng-Ming Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGoal: Analysis of the glottal area during vocal fold vibration has gained increasing attention. However, traditional analysis requires manual, frame-by-frame glottal area annotation to compute the glottal area waveform, a time-consuming, and error-prone process. Methods: This study proposes an automated system for glottal area segmentation and glottal area waveform feature extraction from 36 videostroboscopy recordings of 23 patients with vocal fold nodules. The system integrates YOLO and U-Net architecture for glottis detection and segmentation. Subject-independent 5-fold cross-validation was performed on 5017 annotated frames. Results: The system achieved an average Intersection over Union of 92.8%, and a Dice Similarity Coefficient of 95.8%, substantially outperforming thresholding and edge-based baselines. Computation time was reduced by 27.7 -folds compared with manual method. Applied to 14 patients undergoing voice treatment, the system detected consistent trends in glottal area dynamics post-treatment. Conclusion: The system enhances efficiency and accuracy of glottal area waveform analysis and demonstrates clinical utility for laryngeal assessment.Keywords: Deep learning, glottal area segmentation, glottal area waveform, glottis detection, videostroboscopy

Introduction

Voice is fundamental for human communication, and voice disorders severely impede social interaction. Phonation stems from vocal fold vibration (source) and subsequently modified by the vocal tract (filter). Therefore, disruptions to vocal fold vibration markedly impair phonatory function. [1]. Vocal fold nodules (VFN) is a common benign lesion of the vocal fold mucosa, primarily caused by excessive vocal use [2], [3]. Otolaryngologists assess vocal folds using direct tissue visualization and vibratory pattern evaluation. However, observing rapid oscillation requires specialized techniques. High-speed videoendoscopy (HSV) and videostroboscopy (VS) are endoscopic methods for capturing vocal fold morphology and vibration. HSV accurately records true vocal fold dynamics by sampling at rates exceeding 4000 Hz. In contrast, VS employs stroboscopic illumination synchronized with the vibratory frequency of sustained vowel sounds. This technique composites multiple vibratory cycles, providing a real-time visualization at an effective frame rate of 30 Hz. [4], [5], [6], [7]. VS is clinically favored due to its real-time visualization, ability to capture extended phonation samples, and practical advantages (ease of use and lower cost) [4], [8].The abbreviations are listed in Table 1.

Traditionally, obtaining Glottal Area Waveform (GAW) requires manual, frame-by-frame computation, making it labor-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to error. Consequently, recent studies have explored automated image analysis techniques for VS, including GA segmentation and lesion detection, to overcome these limitations [9], [10], [11]. However, accurate GA segmentation is quite challenging due to the low contrast between the GA and surrounding tissues, the presence of lesions, and the sudden changes in illumination during recording [12], [13]. Yan et al. proposed an automated GA segmentation method for HSV images. Their technique combines thresholding and region growing, followed by erosion to mitigate noise. This approach successfully achieved effective segmentation even under varying lighting conditions. [14]. Miranda et al. developed an active contour to "snakes" for GA segmenting. They set a threshold from the grayscale histogram, adjusting brighter pixel values. Noise was reduced using anisotropic diffusion and median filtering. A Gradient Vector Flow field was then generated to guide the initial contour to the GA boundary based on gradient values [15].

Fehling et al. integrated U-Net with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) to exploit temporal features from adjacent frames, enabling automated segmentation of the left and right vocal folds [16]. Despite advances in HSV, its adoption has been hindered by the absence of standardized hardware and the difficulty of linking imaging systems with analytical software. To address this issue, Kist et al. developed OpenHSV, which enhances the connection between the system and hardware through a U-Net-based GA segment model. However, Kist's OpenHSV system still depends on manual selection of the glottis and analyzes the entire video.

Hsu et al. documented the correlation between voice therapy and surgery with an analysis that combines ImageJ software and VS. The study included 42 patients newly diagnosed with VFN, of whom 15 received voice therapy and 27 underwent surgical intervention. After VS, the Maximum GA (Max GA) and Minimum GA (Min GA) were measured using ImageJ software. Hsu et al. found that voice therapy significantly improved Max GA, while surgery significantly reduced Min GA [17].

Schindler et al. reported functional improvement after voice therapy in 40 patients with unilateral vocal cord paralysis, although evaluation was based mainly on visual judgement. Post-treatment video laryngoscopy revealed an increase in complete glottic closure [18]. Noordzij et al. used GAW analysis in 24 surgical patients and demonstrated a significant postoperative increase in the maximum normalized glottic area, indicating the surgery's efficacy [19].

Although deep learning is promising [20], analyzing VS data remains challenging. Adjacent frames often lack meaningful information due to motion artifacts. Moreover, prior automated GA segmentation methods have focused on HSV images; direct application to VS may lead to poor segmentation and unreliable GAW measurements. Thus, we propose a real-time VS analysis system to expedite GA size calculation and derive objective GAW feature parameters.

Materials and Methods

Patients’ Data

In this study, VS videos were collected from Chiayi Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Taiwan. This project has been reviewed and approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB Approval Number: 202300824B0C502). Each video was recorded by professional physicians at the CGMH Department of Otolaryngology-Head & Neck Surgery using the Kay Elemetrics Stroboscopy Unit (Model CSL 4500, KayPentax, Lincoln Park, NJ, USA). The frame rate is 30 frames per second, the video resolution is 720 x 488, the audio sampling rate is 48 kHz, and the bit rate was about 253 kbps (±2 kbps). The videos are stored in MP4 format.

A total of 36 VS recordings from 23 patients were included in this study. Fourteen patients had both pre- and post-treatment recordings (28 recordings), and 8 patients had a single recording. All patients were diagnosed with VFN by a professional otolaryngologist. Subjects with other vocal fold diseases were excluded. A total of 5017 frames were annotated; detailed per-subject frame counts and fold assignments are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S-I). Treatments included pharmacologic therapy, surgery, and speech therapy, with follow-up evaluations performed approximately three months after treatments. The cohort comprised 6 males and 17 females aged 25 to74 years. (mean = 50.7 years, SD = 15.1). During the VS recording, the physician instructed patients to inhale and then phonate. Each participant was asked to sustain the vowel /i/ for as long as possible.

Glottis and GA Annotations

This study employed a Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) methodology to generate ground-truth training data for the deep learning pipeline, utilizing Label Studio for image annotation from video frames [21]. For YOLOv8-based glottis detection, bounding boxes delineated left and right vocal folds per frame. For U-Net-based GA segmentation, binary mask were generated (1 = glottal area, 0 = other regions). Trained research assistants performed all annotations (Fig. 1.).

YOLOv8 Model for ROI Detection

The system passes every frame through the YOLOv8 detector to localize the glottal ROI. To optimize the YOLO-detected ROI for U-Net segmentation, a preprocessing pipeline is employed to reduce noise and facilitate model convergence. Key steps include conversion to grayscale using the value channel from the HSV color space [22] and adaptive gamma correction for contrast enhancement. The ROI is then resized to 256 × 256 pixels. As shown in Fig. 3, preprocessing effectively mitigated artifacts such as jagged edges from asynchronous capture and improved brightness and contrast, thereby enhancing the visual distinction between the vocal folds and glottal area. The complete preprocessing pipeline is detailed in the Supplementary Materials (Section S1).

Images before and after processing. (a) and (b) exhibit pronounced jagged edges, which can be attributed to the asynchronous nature of the video capture. In contrast, (c) and (d) demonstrate enhanced brightness and contrast after preprocessing, improving their suitability for subsequent analysis.

U-Net Model and Training Approach

For GA segmentation, this study employs the U-Net model, originally proposed by Ronneberger et al. [23]. As illustrated in Fig. 4, U-Net adopts a symmetric encoder-decoder structure, where the encoder applies repeated 3x3 convolutions and 2x2 max-pooling to progressively extract features, and the decoder uses transposed convolutions to gradually upsample and restore spatial resolution.

U-Net model architecture. The architecture includes an encoder with 3x3 convolution, batch normalization, and ReLU activation, followed by max pooling 2x2. The decoder then uses transposed convolution and 1x1 convolution with sigmoid activation.

Skip connections concatenate encoder features with the corresponding decoder output to preserve localization accuracy [23]. A final 1x1 convolution with a sigmoid activation function is then used to generate the binary segmentation mask.

Both YOLOv8 was initialized with pre-trained weights from the COCO dataset. The U-Net model was trained using Dice Loss [24] and Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) as loss functions [25], with a greater emphasis placed on Dice Loss. The training regimen consisted of two phase: In Phase 1 the model was training for 80 epochs on a dataset comprising 1280 images, with 640 images sourced from CGMH and 640 from an online dataset [16]. This phase facilitated robust segmentation of the GA in high-quality images while also enabling preliminary segmentation in lower-quality images. In Phase 2, the model was trained on 5017 images obtained from 23 VFN patients VS recordings from CGMH. Following this training, the model was employed to segment the GA effectively. The segmented areas from 64 consecutive frames were concatenated to construct the raw GAW before normalization. A subject-independent 5-fold cross-validation was performed, ensuring that all recordings from the same patient were assigned to the same fold to prevent data leakage.

GAW Normalization

Normalization is required to account for potential camera rotation and varying distances from the glottis to enable cross-case and within-case comparisons of GA values. The vocal fold length, defined as the distance between the anterior commissure and the vocal process, normalizes the GA by dividing it by the square of the vocal fold length. While image resizing reduces the impact of distance variations, camera rotation can still introduce errors in the measured vocal fold length. To address this, the camera rotation angle is inferred from the GA's rotation angle, identified at the peaks of the smoothed GAW. The cv2.fitEllipse function [26] is employed to accurately estimate the rotation angle, enabling image realignment and subsequent object detection to delineate the ROI. The vocal fold length is then determined for each GAW peak and linearly interpolated/extrapolated to obtain the length at each time point, facilitating GA normalization and feature extraction. Evaluation Metric

The model's segmentation performance was evaluated against the ground truth using a comprehensive set of standard metrics. The Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) (1) and the Intersection over Union (IoU) (2) were employed to quantify the spatial overlap between the predicted segmentation () and the ground truth (y). Both metrics provide a measure of prediction accuracy, with the DSC placing greater emphasis on penalizing false positives (FP). To provide a more conclusive assessment, precision (3) and recall (4) were also utilized.

Result

Table 2 summarizes the segmentation results. The proposed YOLO + U-Net system achieved an mean IoU of 0.9275 ± 0.0449 and DSC of 0.9575 ± 0.0266, with precision of 0.9327 ± 0.0467, and recall of 0.9936 ± 0.098, substantially outperforming both the thresholding baseline (DSC: 0.3713 ± 0.3261) and the edge-based active contour baseline (DSC: 0.5419 ± 0.2742). Per-fold results are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S-II). The agreement between the proposed system and the manual GA measurements was assessed using regression analysis. Because the data did not follow a normal distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk test p-values < 0.05) [41], the Theil-Sen regression was applied. The regression model exhibited excellent concordance, with a slope of 0.998, and a y-intercept of 0.001. The coefficient of determination R2 = 0.985. Fig. 6 illustrates the strong agreement between the system's normalized GA values (y-axis) and manual segmentation (x-axis), where the regression line (solid) closely aligns with the y = x reference line (dashed), confirming the system's high accuracy and consistency across the measurement range.

This study investigated the effect of treatment on GAW features in 14 patients with paired pre- and post-treatment VS data. For each patient, a single pre- and post-treatment GAW was selected, with lengths ranging from 32 to 66 points at 29.94 fps (approximately 1.07 to 2.20 seconds); detailed per-patient data lengths are provided in Supplementary Table S-III. Paired t-tests and Cohen's d effect size analyses were performed to determine significant within-subject differences.

Table 3 summarizes the pre- and post-treatment results. The average Min GA across the 14 patients showed a consistent decreasing trend from 7.04 × 10−3 (SD = 8.0 × 10−3) to 4.92 × 10−3 (SD = 6.0 × 10−3), though not statistically significant (p = 0.170), corresponding to a moderate negative effect (Cohen's d = −0.39). Conversely, the average Max GA showed a consistent increasing trend from 75.7 × 10−3 (SD = 32.2 × 10−3) to 88.0 × 10−3 (SD = 71.9 × 10−3), with a p = 0.597 and a positive effect (Cohen's d = 0.15). Furthermore, the Min-Max Ratio, which reflects glottal stability and symmetry during phonation, also exhibited a consistent trend toward improvement following treatment, indicating enhanced vibratory balance between the vocal folds. It shows a post-treatment reduction (p = 0.174) with a negative effect (Cohen's d = −0.39). This decrease suggests a trend toward reduced GA variability and improved physiological stability following treatment.

Discussion

This work demonstrates high-accuracy deep-learning-based glottal segmentation on videostroboscopy recordings, enabling quantitative, non-invasive assessment of treatment response. Table 2 details the segmentation results, demonstrating the model's high accuracy and robustness. The proposed YOLO + U-Net system achieved a mean DSC of 0.9575 ± 0.0266 and an IoU of 0.9275 ± 0.0449. The low standard deviation across folds confirms strong generalization. Notably, the model exhibited an exceptionally high recall (average: 0.9936 ± 0.098), indicating that it consistently captures the true glottal area with minimal false negative. This high recall, however, was accompanied by a slightly lower precision (average: 0.9327 ± 0.0467). This imbalance suggests a slight tendency toward "over-segmentation", whereby the model generates a small number of false positives, to ensure complete target coverage. Such a trade-off represents a key area for future refinement.

Table 3 shows the pre- and post-treatment comparison of glottal dynamics for N = 14 subjects, revealing consistent directional trends across all metrics (p > 0.05; see Supplementary Material for GT validation). Clinically, the observed increase in Max GA (reflecting enhanced vocal fold abduction) coupled with the decrease in Min GA (implying improved glottal closure) suggests a consistent expansion of the glottal dynamic range after treatment. However, the limited sample size (N = 14) of this study restricts the generalizability of these findings. Future studies should therefore involve a larger patient cohort for validation and incorporate long-term follow-up. Furthermore, correlating these objective GA metrics with subjective patient-reported outcomes (e.g., VHI) or perceptual assessments (e.g., GRBAS) will be essential to fully validate their clinical relevance.

Findings from this study, particularly the consistent directional trends in Max GA and Min GA (Table 3), are consistent with prior research demonstrating that glottal area dynamics reflect therapeutic efficacy. Schindler et al. reported that voice therapy can effectively reduce glottal insufficiency [18], while Noordzij et al. documented a significant postoperative increase in Max GA [19]. Similarly, Hsu et al. documented marked differences in GA before and after both voice therapy or surgical intervention [11].To validate these algorithm-derived trends, GT GA deltas were correlated with algorithm GA deltas for subjects with overlapping GT annotations (N = 6). A significant correlation was observed for Max GA (r = 0.874, p = 0.023), supporting the validity of the algorithm measurements (see Supplementary Material).

Quantitative analysis of Max GA and Min GA is critical not only for assessing therapeutic response but also for differentiating normal from pathological phonatory patterns (such as benign versus malignant lesions) and monitoring postoperative recovery [29], [30]. When combined with real-time vocal fold imaging, these parameters can simultaneously capture amplitude, symmetry, and phase difference metrics, supporting non-invasive preliminary differential diagnosis and a comprehensive evaluation of laryngeal function.

The development of robust, non-invasive systems, such as the one we validated in this study (Table 2), is essential for advancing these clinical applications [27], [28]. This non-invasive approach is especially important given that vocal fold length and area vary significantly with gender and age, underscoring the need for the individualized reference values and long-term monitoring that our system is designed to provide [27]. Moreover, integrating an adaptive signal processing module for system calibration is crucial to mitigate inconsistencies arising from varied acquisition hardware and ambient light fluctuation, ensuring the long-term stability and comparability of the monitoring data.

The proposed system was validated in this study on patients with vocal nodules, confirming its capability in accurate glottal feature extraction and its clinical feasibility. Building on these results, future work will extend this application to normal individuals and patients with a wider variety of diseases, crucially including those with malignant tumors. By leveraging these objective GAW features, our system can assist clinicians in diagnosing diseases, reducing misdiagnosis rates, and facilitating the early detection of malignant tumors [13], [31], [32].

Compared with previous threshold-based [14], active-contour [15] baselines evaluated in this study (Table 2), the proposed YOLO + U-Net framework achieved substantially superior segmentation accuracy (DSC: 0.9575 vs. 0.3713 and 0.5419, respectively) on challenging VS data (Table 2), highlighting its robustness and clinical practicality.

Conclusion

The proposed system achieves high accuracy in glottis detection and robust performance in GA segmentation, providing a reliable foundation for quantitative analyses. Automated annotations significantly alleviated the labor-intensive burden and improved efficiency. Furthermore, the system sensitively captured consistent physiological trends between pre- and post-treatment states, underscoring its strong potential as a reliable, non-invasive, and objective tool for quantifying treatment efficacy and supporting clinical diagnosis.
