Neuroprotective effects of chrysin in a rat model of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Sana Moradi, Farzad Rajaei, Shahram Darabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chrysin, or 5,7-dihydroxyflavone, is a flavonoid with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects, which are significant in Parkinson’s disease. The objective of the study is to investigate the protective effects of chrysin in Parkinson’s disease.Materials and methods:Wistar strain rats were divided into three groups: treatment (receiving chrysin before and after OHDA-6 injection), control (injected with 0.9% normal saline into the left striatum), and lesion (injected with 0.9% normal saline and OHDA-6 into the left striatum). Behavioral testing with apomorphine hydrochloride was conducted 1 week before and 4 weeks after surgery. For histological examination, black blocks of tissue were prepared, and Nissl staining was used for neuron counting. Microscopic studies were performed by capturing images of the slides.Results:In the behavioral test conducted after apomorphine injection and before surgery, there was no significant difference in the number of rotations among the control, lesion, and treatment groups. However, after surgery, this difference was significant between the lesion and treatment groups (P ≤ 0.05). In Nissl staining, a significant difference in the number of neurons in the substantia nigra was observed between the treatment and lesion groups.Conclusion:According to the results obtained from this study, chrysin prevented the neurodegeneration caused by OHDA-6, leading to a lower rate of mortality in the neurons of the midbrain substantia nigra.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) was discovered by James Parkinson in 1817[1]. The prevalence of this disease is increasing annually due to the rising average age of humans[2]. The clinical diagnosis of PD remains complex, often requiring a nuanced understanding of clinical neurology and the overlapping symptoms of various movement disorders[3,4]. The primary treatment for this disease is the drug levodopa, which may lead to the side effect of dyskinesia, often appearing in patients who have used this medication for an extended period[6]. Understanding the functional neuroanatomy of the basal ganglia is crucial for interpreting these motor complications and the progression of the disease[7]. In addition to medication, there are other therapeutic approaches, such as unilateral pallidotomy and deep brain stimulation[8].

These methods have shown acceptable results in patients with PD whose condition is not adequately controlled through medication alone, due to various reasons[8]. However, one must also consider the numerous side effects associated with surgery[9]. Clinical evidence has shown that chrysin possesses antioxidant effects, acting directly as a scavenger of free radicals and enhancing cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms[10]. The beneficial role of chrysin in cell survival was first reported in a laboratory study, demonstrating its ability to protect against apoptosis induced by 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+) in dopaminergic cells in the substantia nigra of rats[11]. In the field of neurological disorders, chrysin exhibits antiepileptic effects by acting as a ligand for the benzodiazepine receptor, reducing pentylenetetrazol-induced tonic-clonic seizures in mice[12]. Another advantage is its neuroprotective effect in spinal cord injuries in mice, attributed to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties[13]. Additionally, chrysin may inhibit the activation of HIV-1 during latent infection[14]. One potential effective treatment for PD is chrysin (plant pigments), which is abundant in fruits and vegetables[15]. Chrysin is expected to avoid the side effects associated with other therapeutic methods (such as those mentioned for levodopa and surgery)[16]. However, there is limited reliable evidence regarding whether chrysin has anti-inflammatory effects in the central nervous system[17].

While previous in vitro and in vivo studies have established the neuroprotective properties of chrysin, including its anti-apoptotic effects on dopaminergic cells exposed to MPP+[8] and its antioxidant actions in various neurological models[18], a significant knowledge gap remains[19]. The majority of existing evidence for chrysin’s efficacy in PD is derived from cell culture experiments or studies focusing on other neurotoxic insults. Direct, comprehensive histological and behavioral validation in a classic and widely accepted neurotoxin model of PD, such as the 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) rodent model, is limited[20]. Specifically, a detailed characterization of chrysin’s ability to preserve neuronal integrity in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) and concurrently mitigate associated motor deficits in the 6-OHDA model has not been fully explored. Therefore, this study advances beyond prior reports by providing a parallel assessment of both behavioral recovery (via apomorphine-induced rotations) and direct histological neuroprotection (via Nissl staining) in the SNc following chrysin treatment in a 6-OHDA-lesioned rat model. This dual approach allows for a more robust and translational evaluation of chrysin’s potential as a disease-modifying agent for PD.

Materials and methods

In this study, 30 adult male Wistar rats (12 weeks old) were acclimatized in the Qazvin University of Medical Sciences laboratory. Housed in groups of four in transparent polycarbonate cages (20 × 59 × 38 cm), they were kept in a controlled environment (20–24°C, 45–55% humidity, 12-hour light-dark cycle) for 2 weeks. The animals had free access to a specialized diet and water via bottles. Inducing disease in these rats requires special attention to their food and water needs, ensuring continuous care. Stereotaxic surgery was performed under ketamine and xylazine anesthesia, following the Paxinos atlas. After euthanasia, the animals were buried in a designated pit.

For histological and behavioral assessments in the Parkinson’s model, 24 male Wistar rats (200–300 g) were obtained from Qazvin University of Medical Sciences. Housed in groups of four, they followed the Qazvin University of Medical Sciences care protocol in cages with controlled temperature and light conditions. The rats had unrestricted access to water and a standard rodent diet and were acclimatized for one month before being transferred to the animal house.

The animals are randomly assigned to three groups:

(1) Sham-operated control group: Animals in this group underwent stereotaxic surgery and received an injection of the vehicle solution (5 µL of 0.9% normal saline containing 0.2% ascorbic acid) into the left striatum. This group serves as a control for the effects of the surgical procedure.

(2) Lesion group (6-OHDA): Evaluation was conducted in the group receiving chrysin before and after 6-OHDA injection. Animals in this group were treated with chrysin (Sigma, USA) at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight, which was administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. This specific dose was selected based on previous studies demonstrating its neuroprotective efficacy in rodent models of neurological injury[21,22]. The treatment regimen began 1 day prior to the stereotaxic surgery to establish a neuroprotective state before the insult. Following the 6-OHDA lesion, chrysin administration continued daily for 4 weeks. This prolonged treatment period was designed to provide sustained neuroprotection against both the acute toxicity and the ongoing secondary degenerative processes induced by 6-OHDA, aligning with the study’s endpoint for behavioral and histological analyses.

(3) Treatment group (Chrysin + 6-OHDA): Animals in this group were treated with chrysin and underwent the same 6-OHDA lesioning procedure as the lesion group.

Behavioral assessment before surgery

Apomorphine hydrochloride (Sigma) at 5.0 mg/kg was used for preoperative behavioral assessment. It was administered intraperitoneally 1 week before surgery by injecting a 0.3 cc solution of apomorphine in 0.9% normal saline using an insulin syringe.

Mice were placed in a cylindrical chamber 10 minutes before the experiment. Five minutes post-injection, manual counts of complete rotations (360 degrees) were made at 10-minute intervals for 1 hour.

The net rotation was calculated by subtracting the rotations to the left from those to the right. Mice with fewer than 30 rotations in 60 minutes post-injection of 2.5 mg/kg apomorphine hydrochloride were considered healthy. Behavioral assessment was repeated 4 weeks after surgery, and the analysis compared pre- and post-surgery values.

Surgery

Mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (20 mg/kg). After the mice were placed in a stereotaxic apparatus, the injection coordinates for the striatum were adjusted. Using the Paxinos and Watson atlas for guidance, a surgical incision was made to expose the skull. After disinfection, the skull was drilled at the designated coordinates. Bregma visualization guided the 5- or 10-µL injection at 1 µL/min, followed by a gradual withdrawal of the syringe after 5 minutes to prevent substance leakage. The eyes were protected with a moistened felt cloth during surgery.

The injected substance varies among the different groups:

1. In the left striatum of animals in the lesion group, 5 microliters of the vehicle solution containing 6-OHDA (0.9% normal saline, 0.2% ascorbic acid, and 5.2 µg/µL 6-OHDA) were injected.

2. In the left striatum of animals in the sham-operated control group, 5 µL of the vehicle solution (0.9% normal saline and 0.2% ascorbic acid) were injected.

3. In the treatment group, 1 day before the striatal lesion, animals were treated with chrysin at a dose of 10 mg/kg. The treatment continued with daily intraperitoneal injections of the drug for 4 weeks.

Post-surgery, the incision was sutured and tetracycline ointment was applied to prevent infection. Mice were then moved to a warm environment to counteract the surgery-induced drop in body temperature.

Sample size

The sample size (n = 8 per group) was determined based on a priori power analysis using G*Power software (version 3.1). The calculation was performed for a one-way ANOVA with three groups, assuming a large effect size (f = 0.4) derived from preliminary data on apomorphine-induced rotations in the 6-OHDA model, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power (1-β) of 0.80.

Randomization

Following the acclimatization period, the 24 rats were randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups (sham-operated control, lesion, or treatment; n = 8 per group) using a computer-generated random-number list.

Blinding

To ensure objectivity, all data collection and analyses were performed by experimenters who were blinded to the group allocations. For the behavioral assessment, the researcher counting apomorphine-induced rotations was unaware of the treatment condition of each animal. Similarly, for the histological analysis, the slides were coded, and the investigator performing neuronal counting in the substantia nigra was blinded to the group identity. The code was broken only after all data had been collected and analyzed.

Anesthesia

The anesthetic consists of a ketamine–xylazine mixture, with doses of 100 mg/kg ketamine and 5 mg/kg xylazine. For instance, a 200-g rat receives 0.2 cc of 10% ketamine (100 mg/cc) and 0.2 cc of xylazine. Additional ketamine can be administered if the animal regains consciousness.

Histological examination of the substantia nigra

For histological examination, brain sections were processed for both Nissl staining and tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunohistochemistry. For Nissl staining, sections were stained with a 1.0% cresyl violet solution to visualize general neuronal morphology. For TH immunohistochemistry, adjacent sections were incubated with a primary antibody against TH (anti-TH, 1:1000, Merck Millipore, USA) overnight at 4°C. Following incubation with a biotinylated secondary antibody, the signal was visualized using an avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (Vectastain ABC Kit, Vector Laboratories, USA) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. TH-positive neurons in the SNc were counted at the same four anatomical levels (2.96, 3.2, 3.8, and 4.2 mm from the interaural point) using a light microscope.

Data analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Rotational behavior induced by apomorphine at two assessment periods (before and 4 weeks after surgery) was analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Significant differences led to pairwise comparisons using the Tukey test. The number of neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta was analyzed using one-way ANOVA, and Tukey post hoc tests were used for multiple-group comparisons. Graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel 2017. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Four weeks post-surgery, the Lesion group exhibited a marked increase in contralateral rotations (124.6 ± 12.5). Chrysin treatment significantly attenuated this motor deficit, reducing rotations to 26.4 ± 5.2 (P = 0.002). Histologically, the Lesion group showed a 65% loss of TH-positive neurons compared with controls (P < 0.001). Chrysin administration preserved significantly more dopaminergic neurons (284.7 ± 16.5) than the Lesion group (P = 0.002).

Apomorphine-induced behavioral test

The behavioral test was conducted during the fourth week after surgery. The results indicate that, 4 weeks post-surgery, the mean numbers of rotations (mean ± SEM) in the control, treatment, and lesion groups were approximately 0, 23, and 120, respectively.

Therefore, following surgery and induction of the Parkinson’s model in the lesion group, the average number of rotations among the animals reached 120, indicating successful lesion creation in the animal model. In the treatment group, chrysin led to a reduction in damage to dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, resulting in improved movement and a decrease in rotations compared to the lesion group. There was a significant difference in the number of rotations between the treatment and lesion groups (P ≤ 0.05; Fig. 1). Tissue examination with Nissl and TH staining

Nissl staining showed that in the chrysin treatment group, the drug prevented 6-OHDA-induced neuronal destruction, and reduced neuronal loss was observed in the midbrain substantia nigra (Fig. 2). This finding was further corroborated by the more specific TH immunohistochemistry. Quantification of TH-positive neurons revealed a significant loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) of the lesion group (120 ± 15 neurons/section) compared to the sham-operated control group (350 ± 20 neurons/section, P < 0.001). Chrysin treatment significantly attenuated this loss, with the treatment group showing a significantly higher number of TH-positive neurons (280 ± 18 neurons/section) compared to the lesion group (P < 0.01; Fig. 3). This provides direct evidence that chrysin protects the specific dopaminergic neurons that are vulnerable in PD. Discussion

The present study investigated the neuroprotective efficacy of chrysin in a 6-OHDA-induced rat model of PD. Our findings demonstrate that chrysin (10 mg/kg) significantly preserves TH-positive neurons. This aligns with modern pharmacological approaches that seek to utilize natural compounds with high antioxidant profiles to bypass the limitations of current synthetic drugs.

Dosage dynamics and prophylactic potential

Our study utilized a 10 mg/kg dose, which proved effective in the 6-OHDA striatal lesion model. However, discrepancies exist in the literature regarding the “ideal” therapeutic window. While some MPTP mouse models have reported success with similar doses (10–20 mg/kg)[25], systemic toxin models often require substantially higher concentrations, up to 100 mg/kg, to achieve behavioral recovery[26]. The efficacy of our relatively low dose may be attributed to the administration protocol; by initiating pretreatment 1 day before the insult, we likely induced a state of “prophylactic antioxidant priming.” This suggests that chrysin’s clinical strength may lie in its ability to fortify neurons against oxidative stress before irreversible damage occurs, rather than in the acute rescue of already degenerating populations.

Histological correlation with functional recovery

The reduction in apomorphine-induced rotations observed in our subjects is strongly corroborated by histological evidence. While initial Nissl staining indicated general neuroprotection, TH-immunohistochemistry confirmed that chrysin specifically safeguards the dopaminergic phenotype against 6-OHDA toxicity.

The preservation of approximately 80% of TH-positive neurons (Fig. 3) provides a clear neuroanatomical substrate for the functional recovery observed. This level of protection exceeds that in some previous reports[27], a difference potentially stemming from our specific lesion coordinates or the 4-week treatment duration. By maintaining the integrity of the nigrostriatal pathway, chrysin appears to prevent the downstream inflammatory cascade and the resulting motor asymmetry.

Mechanisms of neuroprotection: antioxidant vs. Anti-inflammatory

Although biochemical markers were not directly quantified, the chemical structure of chrysin offers a plausible mechanism for the observed results. 6-OHDA induces cell death via the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the inhibition of mitochondrial complex I. Chrysin’s 5,7-dihydroxy structure allows it to act as a potent hydrogen donor, neutralizing hydroxyl radicals.

Furthermore, our focus on primary neuronal survival in the SNc complements findings by Yao et al[28], who emphasized glial inhibition in ischemic models. This suggests that chrysin is a multi-target compound whose primary mechanism–whether anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, or antioxidant–may shift depending on the specific nature of the pathological insult (ischemic vs. oxidative). This is further supported by the work of Mercer et al., who highlighted chrysin’s role in reducing apoptotic damage in the SNc[29], and by Mehri et al, who demonstrated its ability to mitigate acrylamide-induced neurotoxicity in a dose-dependent manner[30].

Behavioral implications and limitations

While the apomorphine-induced rotation results were statistically significant (P ≤ 0.05), this “forced” motor test primarily measures nigrostriatal asymmetry. It does not fully account for spontaneous motor deficits or bradykinesia[31]. Some evidence suggests that flavonoids may improve performance on drug-induced rotation tests more effectively than on spontaneous coordination tasks, such as the Rotarod or Pole test[32]. This distinction implies that chrysin may preserve dopamine receptor sensitivity or gross pathway integrity even if fine motor control remains partially compromised[33].

Additionally, while our study focused on motor function, PD involves significant cognitive and non-motor impairments[34]. Previous studies using the Morris Water Maze (MWM) have shown that chrysin can improve learning and memory by reducing hippocampal oxidative stress[35]. The absence of cognitive testing in our protocol is a limitation; future research should incorporate MWM or stepping tests to provide a more holistic view of chrysin’s translational potential[36].

Furthermore, the challenge of delivering flavonoids like chrysin across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) remains a focal point of recent research[37,38]. Recent advances in neurotherapeutics and drug delivery systems suggest that optimizing the bioavailability of such compounds is essential for clinical translation[39]. These insights, combined with our histological evidence of neuronal preservation, support the continued exploration of chrysin as a scaffold for more potent neuroprotective agents[40].

In summary, chrysin demonstrates robust neuroprotective effects by preserving the TH-positive population and reducing mortality markers, consistent with its known antioxidant properties. However, to fully elucidate the molecular pathways involved, future studies utilizing Western blotting or ELISA are required to confirm whether this protection is mediated primarily by the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway or by inhibition of NF-κB inflammatory signaling.

Despite the significant neuroprotective and behavioral improvements observed, this study has limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the 6-OHDA model, while a gold standard for mimicking dopaminergic cell loss, represents an acute neurotoxic insult rather than the chronic, progressive proteopathic nature of human PD[41]. Consequently, the “reduced mortality” observed here refers to the rescue of neurons from an acute oxidative challenge. Second, our study focused on a 4-week window; further longitudinal research is required to determine whether these protective effects are sustained over time or whether they merely delay the degenerative process[42,43]. Future studies utilizing alpha-synuclein preformed fibrils or transgenic models would be beneficial to validate these findings across different pathological mechanisms of PD.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that chrysin treatment significantly reduces dopaminergic neuronal loss and improves motor asymmetry in a 6-OHDA rat model. These results suggest that chrysin possesses promising neuroprotective properties, likely mediated through its antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects. However, it is important to note that these results represent an acute experimental model. Clinical extrapolation remains premature until the long-term efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetic profile of chrysin are validated in chronic models and human trials. Chrysin should currently be viewed as a promising experimental scaffold for neuroprotective research rather than a ready-to-use clinical intervention.
