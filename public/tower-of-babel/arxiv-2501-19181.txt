Introduction

A common theme in the study of functions of a complex variable is that the behavior of an analytic function at an interior point is very different from the behavior at a boundary point. A good illustration of this distinction is provided by the concept of domains of holomorphy; given a bounded open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, there exists an analytic function
f_{U}
that cannot be extended to a larger domain, meaning that, in general, we cannot expect such a function to be analytic on the boundary. Since every domain in
\mathbb{C}
is a domain of holomorphy, a function that is analytic in a domain cannot be expected to be differentiable at a boundary point; however, it is possible that there is a greater degree of smoothness at the boundary than would otherwise be expected.

One such example is the existence of a bounded point derivation at a boundary point. Let
X
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
. Runge’s Theorem states that functions analytic in a neighborhood of
X
can be uniformly approximated by rational functions with poles off
X
; therefore, let
R_{0}(X)
denote the space of rational functions with poles off
X
and let
R(X)
denote the uniform closure of
R_{0}(X)
. Then
x\in\partial X
is said to admit a bounded point derivation for
R(X)
(of order 1) if the mapping
f\to f^{\prime}(x)
extends as a bounded linear functional for all
f\in R_{0}(X)
. Equivalently, there is a bounded point derivation for
R(X)
at
x
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq C||f||_{X}

for all
f\in R_{0}(X)
, where
||\cdot||_{X}
denotes the uniform norm on
X
.

While showing the existence of a non-zero bounded point derivation exhibits a semblance of analyticity at the boundary, it is not the only way to do so. Other ways that a boundary point can have additional smoothness include the existence of an analytic disk and the existence of a non-trivial Gleason part
[
1
,
6
]
; however, these are not considered in the present paper. Motivated by the properties of bounded point derivations, Wang
[
12
]
identified additional conditions that provide further ways that an analytic function might have additional smoothness at a boundary point. Before we state these conditions, we review the following definitions.

We say that
\phi(r)
is an admissible function if it is positive and nondecreasing on
(0,\infty)
and if the associated function
\psi(r)=\frac{r}{\phi(r)}
is also positive and nondecreasing on
(0,\infty)
with
\psi(0^{+})=0
. Examples of admissible functions are the power functions
\phi(r)=r^{\alpha}
where
0<\alpha<1
and the function
\phi(r)=\frac{r}{\log(1+r)}
.

Let
R_{x}^{t}f(z)
be the error at
z
of the
t
-th degree Taylor polynomial of
f
about
x
, which is defined by

R_{x}^{t}f(z)=f(z)-\sum_{j=0}^{t}\dfrac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}(z-x)^{j}.

Let
A_{n}(x)
denote the annulus
\{z:2^{-(n+1)}\leq|z-x|\leq 2^{-n}\}
and let
B(x,r)
denote the disk centered at
x
with radius
r
. Let
m
denote
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. A set
E
is said to have full area density at
x
if
\dfrac{m(B(x,r)\setminus E)}{m(B(x,r))}\to 0
as
r\to 0
.

Finally, let
\gamma(X)
denote the analytic capacity of the set
X
. For a compact set, analytic capacity is defined as follows.

\displaystyle\gamma(X)=\sup|f^{\prime}(\infty)|

where the supremum is taken over all functions
f
that satisfy the following properties:

1.

f
is analytic on
\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus X
.

2.

||f||_{\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus X}\leq 1

3.

f(\infty)=0
.

See
[
4
, pg. 196]
for additional information on analytic capacity.

Let
X
be a compact subset of the plane, let
x\in X
, let
t
be a non-negative integer, let
\phi(r)
be an admissible function, and let
\|\cdot\|_{X}
denote the uniform norm on
X
. Wang’s three conditions are the following.

(A)

For each
\epsilon>0
the set

\displaystyle\{y\in X:|R_{x}^{t}f(y)|\leq\epsilon\phi(|y-x|)|y-x|^{t}\|f\|_{X}

\displaystyle\text{ for all }f\in R_{0}(X)\}

has full area density at
x
.

(B)

There exists a representing measure
\mu
for
x
on
R(X)
such that
\mu(x)=0
and

\int\dfrac{d|\mu|(z)}{|z-x|^{t}\phi(|z-x|)}<\infty.

(C)

The series

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{n(t+1)}\gamma(A_{n}(x)\setminus X)}{\phi(2^{-n})}

converges.

These three conditions are known to be equivalent when
\phi\equiv 1
, and conditions
(B)
and
(C)
are equivalent for all admissible
\phi
; however, if
\phi\not\equiv 1
then condition
(A)
is not equivalent to the other two conditions. In this case
(A)
implies
(B)
and
(C)
but
(B)
and
(C)
do not imply
(A)

[
9
]
.

In a previous manuscript
[
3
]
we considered the analogues of these conditions for the space
R^{p}(X)
, the closure of
R_{0}(X)
in the
L^{p}
norm, and showed that the same relationships hold between these conditions and the original conditions.

In the present manuscript, we will focus on boundary problems for spaces of Lipschitz functions. Much study has been done in this area
[
7
,
10
,
11
]
, but there are still many unanswered questions and open problems. The first goal of this paper is to determine the correct analogues of Wang’s conditions for the case of analytic Lipschitz functions, which will be done in the next section. After that we will verify two implications; that
(c)
implies
(b)
in Section 3 and that
(a)
does not imply
(c)
in Section 4. The other two implications:
(b)
implies
(c)
and
(b)
implies
(a)
will be left for another time.

2
Lipschitz functions and the Lipschitz analogues of Wang’s Conditions

To determine the correct analogues of Wang’s conditions for Lipschitz functions, we have to consider the functional properties of Lipschitz spaces, which are very different from those of
R(X)
and
R^{p}(X)
. We start by defining a Lipschitz condition.

Let
0<\alpha<1
. A function
f
is said to be a Lipschitz function with Lipschitz exponent
\alpha
on
\mathbb{C}
if there exists a constant
k>0
such that for all
z,w\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle|f(z)-f(w)|\leq k|z-w|^{\alpha}.

(1)

For such a function, the Lipschitz seminorm, denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}}^{\prime}
is defined to be the smallest value of
k
that satisfies (
1
). It is only a seminorm because
k=0
whenever
f
is a constant function; however, it can be made into a norm by adding the uniform norm to the Lipschitz seminorm. Thus, the Lipschitz norm with exponent
\alpha
, denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}}=\|f\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}}^{\prime}+||f(z)||_{\mathbb{C}}.

The space of Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{C}
equipped with the above norm forms a Banach space, which we denote by Lip
{}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})
. This set contains an important subspace, the little Lipschitz class, which is the set of Lipschitz functions such that

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\sup_{0<|z-w|<\delta}\dfrac{|f(z)-f(w)|}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}=0.

The little Lipschitz class, which is denoted by lip
{}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})
, plays an important role in the theory of Lipschitz functions. In addition to these two spaces, we define a third Banach space. Let
U
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{C}
and let
A_{\alpha}(U)
denote the set of functions in the little Lipschitz class that are also analytic on
U
.

A key difference between the Lipschitz spaces and
R(X)
is the structure of the dual spaces. It follows from the work of Leeuw
[
2
]
that the dual space of
A_{\alpha}(U)
has the following form. If
L
is a linear functional acting on
A_{\alpha}(U)
and
Y
is the closure of
U
, then there exists a Borel-regular measure
\mu
on
Y\times Y
such that

\displaystyle L(f)=\int_{Y\times Y}\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\mu(z,w)

whenever
f\in

A_{\alpha}(U)
. In general
L
cannot be represented by direct integration against a measure in contrast to linear functionals on
R(X)
.

Another important difference between
A_{\alpha}(U)
and
R(X)
is the associated capacities. The theory is that every Banach space has an associated capacity and many properties of that space, such as the existence of bounded point derivations, are characterized using this capacity. For
R(X)
the associated capacity is analytic capacity, while for
A_{\alpha}(U)
the associated capacity is lower
(1+\alpha)
-dimensional Hausdorff content.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. A measure function is an increasing function
h(t)
,
t\geq 0
, such that
h(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
. If
h
is a measure function then define

M^{h}(E)=\inf\sum_{j}h(r_{j}),

where the infimum is taken over all countable covers of
E
by squares with sides of length
r_{j}
. The lower
(1+\alpha)
-dimensional Hausdorff content of
E
, denoted
M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(E)
, is defined by

M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(E)=\sup M^{h}(E)

where the supremum is taken over all measure functions
h
such that
h(t)\leq t^{1+\alpha}
and
h(t)t^{-(1+\alpha)}\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
. Furthermore, up to a constant multiplicative bound, the infimum can be taken over countable coverings of
E
by dyadic squares
[
5
, pg. 61, Lemma 1.4]
. It follows from the definition that lower
(1+\alpha)
-dimensional Hausdorff content is monotone; that is,
U\subseteq V
implies
M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(U)\leq M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(V)
. Another key property of Hausdorff content is that for a disk
B
with radius
r
,
M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(B)=r^{1+\alpha}
.

With these distinctions between
R(X)
and
A_{\alpha}(U)
understood, we can now state the analogue of Wang’s conditions for analytic Lipschitz functions. Let
\phi
be an admissible function, let
U
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{C}
and let
Y
be the closure of
U
. Let
t
be a non-negative integer. The analogues of Wang’s conditions for analytic Lipschitz functions are:

(a)

For each
\epsilon>0
the set

\displaystyle\{y\in U:|R_{x}^{t}f(y)|\leq\epsilon\phi(|y-x|)|y-x|^{t}\|f\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle\text{ for all }f\in A_{\alpha}(U)\}

has full area density at
x
.

(b)

There exists a Borel regular measure
\mu
on
Y\times Y
such that for all functions
f\in A_{\alpha}(U)

\displaystyle f(x)=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\mu(z,w)

and

\int\dfrac{d|\mu|(z,w)}{|z-x|^{t}\phi(|z-x|)}<\infty.

(c)

The series

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{2^{n(t+1)}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}(x)\setminus U)}{\phi(2^{-n})}

converges.

We conjecture that
(b)
and
(c)
are equivalent and imply
(a)
but
(a)
does not imply
(b)
or
(c)
.

We close this section with the following Lipschitz analog of Melnikov’s theorem
[
8
, Theorem 4]
for
R(X)
, which will be used several times in the following sections. See
[
7
, 2.6]
for a proof.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a piecewise analytic curve that encloses a region
U
and is free of outward pointing cusps. Let
0<\alpha<1
Then there is a constant
C
which only depends on the curve
\Gamma
such that

\left|\int_{\Gamma}f(z)dz\right|\leq CM_{*}^{1+\alpha}(K)||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(K)}.

whenever
f\in
Lip
{}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})

is analytic on
U\setminus K
, where
K
is a compact subset of
U
.

(c) implies (b)

We first prove that condition
(c)
implies condition
(b)
. By letting
\phi(r)
be an arbitrary positive non-decreasing function we may assume that
t=0
without loss of generality.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\phi(r)
be a positive non-decreasing function, let
U
be an open subset of the complex plane, let
Y
denote the closure of
U
and let
x\in U
. Suppose

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n}\phi(2^{-n})M^{1+\alpha}_{*}(A_{n}(x)\setminus U)<\infty.

Then there exists a Borel regular measure
\mu
on
Y\times Y
such that for all functions
f\in A_{\alpha}(U)
,

\displaystyle f(x)=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\mu(z,w)

and

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\mu(z,w)}{\phi(|z-x|)}<\infty.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x=0
. Let
A_{n}=A_{n}(0)
and let
T_{n}
be a linear functional on
A_{\alpha}(U\cap A_{n})
defined by

\displaystyle T_{n}(f)=-\int_{A_{n}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz

and let
f\in A_{\alpha}(U)
. It follows from Lemma
1
that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{A_{n}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz\right|\leq C2^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}\setminus U)||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}}

and thus
T_{n}
is a bounded linear functional on
A_{\alpha}(U\cap A_{n})
. Let
Y_{n}
denote the closure of
U\cap A_{n}
. It follows from De Leeuw’s representation of lip
\alpha^{*}
that there is a Borel regular measure
\nu_{n}
on
Y_{n}\times Y_{n}
such that

\displaystyle T_{n}(f)=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\nu_{n}(z,w)

and

\displaystyle\int|\nu_{n}(z,w)|\leq C2^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}\setminus U).

Let
\displaystyle\nu=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{n}
.

Let
T
be the linear functional on
A_{\alpha}(U\cap A_{0})
defined by

\displaystyle T(f)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=\frac{1}{2}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz.

Then
|T(f)|\leq 2||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}}
and thus
T
is a bounded linear functional. Hence, there is a Borel regular measure
\lambda
on
Y_{0}\times Y_{0}
such that

\displaystyle T(f)=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\lambda(z,w)

and

\displaystyle\int|\lambda(z,w)|\leq C

Since
f
is analytic in a neighborhood of
0
there exists a positive integer
N
such that
f
is analytic on the disk
|z|\leq 2^{-N}
. Hence, by the Cauchy integral formula there exists a constant
N>0
such that
\displaystyle f(0)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=2^{-N}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz
. Thus,

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=2^{-N}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=\frac{1}{2}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{\partial A_{n}}\frac{f(z)}{z}dz

\displaystyle=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\lambda(z,w)+\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d\nu_{n}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\lambda(z,w)+\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\nu.

Let
\mu=\lambda+\nu
. Then

\displaystyle f(0)=\int\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}d\mu

as desired.

Lastly, since
\nu_{n}
has support on
Y_{n}\times Y_{n}
it follows that

\displaystyle\int\frac{|d\mu(z,w)|}{\phi(|z|)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\phi(|z|)^{-1}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{n}(z,w)\right|+\int\phi(|z|)^{-1}|\lambda|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{n}(z,w)+C

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(|2^{-n}|)^{-1}\nu_{n}(z,w)+C

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(|2^{-n}|)^{-1}2^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}\setminus U)<\infty.

∎

(a) does not imply (c)

We now give an example to show that, in general, condition
(a)
does not imply condition
(c)
. In this example, we consider the case

Theorem 3
.

Let
\phi(r)
be an admissible function such that
\phi(0^{+})=0
, and let
0<\alpha<1
. Then there is an open set
U

, but

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}4^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}(0)\setminus U)=\infty.

We first describe the construction of the set

. Let

be a sequence of points on the positive real axis. We can find a subsequence which we will still denote as

such that
\phi(a_{n})<\frac{1}{2}\phi(a_{n-1})
and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{j-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}<\psi(a_{j})^{-1},

is the associated function to

. Choose a sequence of radii

such that
r_{n}^{1+\alpha}=a_{n}^{2}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})
. Let

denote the open disk centered at

and let
\Delta
denote the open unit disk in

. The counterexample to
(a)

(c)
is given by the Roadrunner set
\displaystyle U=\Delta\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\overline{B(a_{n},r_{n})}
. We first show that condition
(c)
is false for this set.

Lemma 4
.

The set
U
described above has the property that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}4^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}(0)\setminus U)=\infty.

Proof.

Let
A_{n}=A_{n}(0)
. Since
A_{n}\setminus U=B(a_{n},r_{n})
and for a disk

with radius

,

, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}4^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}\setminus U)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}4^{n}a_{n}^{2}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{16}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}n^{-1}\phi\left(\frac{3}{4}2^{-n}\right).

Since

\displaystyle\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}\phi\left(\frac{3}{4}2^{-n}\right)=\frac{3}{4}\psi\left(\frac{3}{4}2^{-n}\right)^{-1}\psi(2^{-n})\geq\frac{3}{4}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\phi(2^{-n})^{-1}4^{n}M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}\setminus U)\geq\frac{27}{64}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-1}=\infty.

∎

. By the definition of the Lipschitz seminorm and the triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f(z)}{z^{2}(z-y)}\right\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(B(a_{n},r_{n}))}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\neq w;z,w\in B(a_{n},r_{n})}\frac{\left|\frac{f(z)}{z^{2}(z-y)}-\frac{f(w)}{w^{2}(w-y)}\right|}{|z-w|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\neq w;z,w\in B(a_{n},r_{n})}\frac{|f(z)-f(w)|}{|z-w|^{\alpha}|z|^{2}|z-y|}+\sup_{z\neq w;z,w\in B(a_{n},r_{n})}\frac{|f(w)||w^{2}(w-y)-z^{2}(z-y)|}{|z-w|^{\alpha}|z|^{2}|z-y||w|^{2}|w-y|}

\displaystyle=(I)+(II).

It follows from the definition of the Lipschitz seminorm that

\displaystyle(I)\leq||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}\sup_{z\in B(a_{n},r_{n})}|z|^{-2}|z-y|^{-1}.

To get a bound for
(II)
, we first note that since
f(0)=0
,

\displaystyle(II)\leq||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}\frac{|w^{2}(w-y)-z^{2}(z-y)|}{|z-w|^{\alpha}|z|^{2}|z-y||w|^{2-\alpha}|w-y|}.

Next, a calculation shows that
w^{2}(w-y)-z^{2}(z-y)=(w-z)[w^{2}+w(z-y)+z(z-y)]
. Hence,

\displaystyle(II)\leq\sup_{z\neq w;z,w\in B(a_{n},r_{n})}\left(\frac{|w-z|^{1-\alpha}}{|z|^{2}|z-y||w|^{-\alpha}|w-y|}+\frac{|w-z|^{1-\alpha}}{|z|^{2}|w|^{1-\alpha}|w-y|}+\frac{|w-z|^{1-\alpha}}{|z||w|^{2-\alpha}|w-y|}\right)||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}

Recall that
d_{n}(y)
denotes the distance from
y
to
B(a_{n},r_{n})
. Since
z,w\in B(a_{n},r_{n})
,
|w-z|\leq|w|
,
\frac{1}{2}a_{n}\leq|z|\leq 2a_{n}
, and
\frac{1}{2}a_{n}\leq|w|\leq 2a_{n}
.Thus,

\displaystyle(I)\leq 4a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}

and

\displaystyle(II)\leq\left(4a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(y)^{-2}+4a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}+4a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}\right)||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{f(z)}{z^{2}(z-y)}\right\|_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(B(a_{n},r_{n}))}^{\prime}\leq C\left(a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}+a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(y)^{-2}\right)||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}

and

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(0)-f^{\prime}(0)y|\leq C|y|^{2}||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}+a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(y)^{-2}\right)M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(A_{n}\setminus U).

Since
A_{n}\setminus U=B(a_{n},r_{n})
and for a disk
B
with radius
r
,
M_{*}^{1+\alpha}(B)=r^{1+\alpha}
,

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(0)-f^{\prime}(0)y|

\displaystyle\leq C||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}\left(|y|^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}+|y|^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(y)^{-2}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=C||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}^{\prime}\left((A)+(B)\right).

Next, we obtain an estimate for
d_{n}(y)
, the distance from
y
to
B(a_{n},r_{n})
. If
y\in E\cap A_{N}
, then
d_{N}(y)\geq s_{N}
. However, if
n<N
, then since
r_{n}<s_{n}
we have

\displaystyle d_{n}(y)\geq(a_{n}-r_{n}-s_{n})-2^{-N}\geq\frac{5}{7}a_{n}-2^{-N}=\frac{15}{28}2^{-n}-2^{-N}\geq\frac{1}{28}2^{-n}=\frac{1}{21}a_{n}

On the other hand, if
n>N
, then

\displaystyle d_{n}(y)\geq 2^{-(N+1)}-(a_{n}+r_{n}+s_{n})\geq 2^{-(N+1)}-\frac{9}{7}a_{n}\geq 2^{-(N+1)}-\frac{27}{28}2^{-n}\geq\frac{1}{28}2^{-(N+1)}=\frac{1}{42}a_{N}.

We first bound (A). Since
y\in A_{N}
,

\displaystyle(A)

\displaystyle=|y|^{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}+a_{N}^{-2}d_{N}(y)^{-1}r_{N}^{1+\alpha}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-2}d_{n}(y)^{-1}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C|y|^{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-3}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}+a_{N}^{-2}s_{N}^{-1}r_{N}^{1+\alpha}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-2}a_{N}^{-1}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right).

Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{|y|}{\phi(|y|)}=\psi(|y|)

\displaystyle\leq\psi\left(\frac{4}{3}a_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{4}{3}a_{N}}{\phi(\frac{4}{3}a_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{4}{3}a_{N}}{\phi(a_{N})}=\frac{4}{3}\psi(a_{N}).

Hence
|y|\leq\frac{4}{3}\phi(|y|)\psi(a_{N})
and

\displaystyle(A)

\displaystyle\leq C|y|\phi(|y|)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-3}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\psi(a_{N})+a_{N}^{-1}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}s_{N}^{-1}r_{N}^{1+\alpha}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-2}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=C|y|\phi(|y|)\left[(A_{I})+(A_{II})+(A_{III})\right].

We first provide a bound for
(A_{I})
. By our choice of
r_{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle(A_{I})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-3}a_{n}^{2}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})\psi(a_{N})

\displaystyle=\psi(a_{N})\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}n^{-1}.

It then follows from the property that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{j-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}<\psi(a_{j})^{-1}

for
1\leq j<N
that

\displaystyle\psi(a_{N})\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}n^{-1}

\displaystyle=\psi(a_{N})\sum_{n=1}^{j-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}n^{-1}+\psi(a_{N})\sum_{n=j}^{N-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}n^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\psi(a_{N})\sum_{n=1}^{j-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}+\psi(a_{N})j^{-1}\sum_{n=j}^{N-1}\psi(a_{n})^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\psi(a_{N})\psi(a_{j})^{-1}+j^{-1}.

Thus, by choosing
j
sufficiently large, it follows that
(A_{I})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Next, we obtain a bound for
(A_{II})
. By the choices of
r_{n}
and
s_{n}
,

\displaystyle(A_{II})

\displaystyle=7a_{N}^{-1}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{N}^{-1}N^{1/3}a_{N}^{2}N^{-1}\phi(a_{N})

\displaystyle=7N^{-2/3}

and
(A_{II})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Finally, we bound
(A_{III})
. By the choice of
r_{n}
we have that

\displaystyle(A_{III})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-2}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{n}^{2}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle=\phi(a_{N})^{-1}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n}).

It then follows from the property that
\phi(a_{n})<\frac{1}{2}\phi(a_{n-1})
that

\displaystyle\phi(a_{N})^{-1}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle<\phi(a_{N})^{-1}N^{-1}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle<\phi(a_{N})^{-1}N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\phi(a_{N})

\displaystyle=N^{-1}.

Thus,
(A_{III})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Hence, by choosing
N
sufficiently large, we obtain

\displaystyle(A)\leq\epsilon|y|\phi(|y|).

Now we focus on getting a bound for
(B)
. Since
y\in A_{N}
,

\displaystyle(B)

\displaystyle=|y|^{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(y)^{-2}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}+a_{N}^{-1}d_{N}(y)^{-2}r_{N}^{1+\alpha}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}d_{n}(y)^{-2}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq|y|^{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-3}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}+a_{N}^{-1}s_{N}(y)^{-2}r_{N}^{1+\alpha}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}a_{N}^{-2}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right).

Since
|y|\leq\frac{4}{3}\phi(|y|)\psi(a_{N})
,

\displaystyle(B)

\displaystyle\leq C|y|\phi(|y|)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}a_{n}^{-3}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\psi(a_{N})+\phi(a_{N})^{-1}s_{N}(y)^{-2}r_{N}^{1+\alpha}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{N}^{-1}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=C|y|\phi(|y|)[(B_{I})+(B_{II})+(B_{III})].

Since
(B_{I})=(A_{I})
we already have a bound for it, so we focus on bounding
(B_{II})
. It follows from our choices of
r_{n}
and
s_{n}
that

\displaystyle(B_{II})=49\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{N}^{-2}N^{2/3}a_{N}^{2}N^{-1}\phi(a_{N})=49N^{-1/3}

and hence
(B_{II})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Lastly, we bound
(B_{III})
. By the choice of
r_{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle(B_{III})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{N}^{-1}a_{n}^{2}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle=\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{N}^{-1}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n}).

It then follows from the property that
\phi(a_{n})<\frac{1}{2}\phi(a_{n}-1)
that

\displaystyle\phi(a_{N})^{-1}a_{N}^{-1}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-1}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}N^{-1}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\phi(a_{n})

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\phi(a_{N})^{-1}N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\phi(a_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}N^{-1}.

Thus,
(B_{III})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Hence, by choosing
N
sufficiently large, we obtain

\displaystyle(B)\leq\epsilon|y|\phi(|y|).

Thus, it follows that for
y\in E
with
|y|
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle|f(y)-f(0)-f^{\prime}(0)y|\leq\epsilon|y|\phi(y)||f||_{\text{Lip}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{C})}.

∎

Appendix

In this section, we present an example to illustrate that the construction used in the proof of Theorem
3
cannot be used to disprove the conjecture that
(a)
implies
(c)
when
t=0
. Let
U
be the roadrunner set constructed by removing closed disks
\overline{B(a_{n},r_{n})}
with center
a_{n}
and radius
r_{n}
from the open unit disk; that is, let
U=\Delta\setminus\bigcup\overline{B(a_{n},r_{n})}
. Let
\phi(r)=r^{\beta}
where
0<\beta<\alpha
. We will show that every choice of radii
r_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n(1+\beta)}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}=\infty

causes
U
to be so large that
U^{C}
fails to have full area density at
0
. More precisely, for
U^{C}
to have full area density at
0
, it is required that

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{m(B(0,2^{-j})\setminus U^{C})}{m(B(0,2^{-j}))}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\pi\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}}{\pi(2^{-j})^{2}}.

Since
\displaystyle\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}>r_{j}^{2}
, it follows that for
U^{C}
to have full area density at
0
, it must be the case that
2^{2j}r_{j}^{2}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Hence, it is necessary that, for
j
sufficiently large,
r_{j}<2^{-j}
. But this implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n(1+\beta)}r_{n}^{1+\alpha}<\infty,

and thus
U^{C}
cannot have full area density at
0
.
