Examining the immunomodulatory role of nanoparticles on mast cell activation
Authors: Jessica Perez Pineda, Lisa A. DeLouise
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent studies reveal that inorganic nanoparticles (NPs) can alter allergic skin reactions in a contact hypersensitivity (CHS) mouse model. Specifically, negatively charged silica (SiO2) NPs suppressed the response whereas manganese doped titanium dioxide (mTiO2) exacerbated it. Mast cells are critically important in transducing the CHS response. In this study we investigated the effect of SiO2 and mTiO2 NPs on bone marrow derived mast cell (BMMC) activation markers, degranulation and cytokine release. Sensitized BMMC were exposed to NPs alone or NPs plus antigen. Cytokines (IL-6, IL-13, TNF-α) and degranulation (β-hexosaminidase) studies were performed. Flow cytometry was used to follow cell surface activation markers including FcεRI, CD63 (LAMP-3), and CD107a (LAMP-1). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) studies were preformed to assess NP endocytosis. Results found that mTiO2 NPs were cytotoxic to BMMC in a dose- and time-dependent manner. SiO2 NPs showed minimal cytotoxicity up to 100 µg/ml. In the absence of antigen the NPs had limited effect on sensitized BMMC degranulation or cytokine release. However, in the presence of antigen, 30 min co-culture studies (NP plus antigen) showed that SiO2 NPs protect against degranulation, and they suppressed the expression of cell surface activation markers whereas mTiO2 NPs exacerbated these.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52598-1.

Introduction

Contact dermatitis (CD) is a common inflammatory skin disease that affects up to 9.8% of the population, and it is the cause of ~ 90% of occupational skin disorders in the industrialized world1–5. Allergic contact dermatitis (ACD), a subtype of CD, is a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction triggered by skin contact with a hapten sensitizer (e.g. poison ivy, nickel, latex) and comprises ~ 20% of CD cases6. The incidence of ACD is increasing, with 17 new allergens reported yearly between 2008 and 20157–11. A compromised skin barrier increases the risk of penetration of exogenous substances such as chemicals, haptens and particulates, potentially inducing or aggravating allergic responses that can lead to severe immune reactions7,12,13.

Recent studies reveal that nanoparticles (NPs) can influence allergic skin reactions by either exacerbating or diminishing contact allergic responses14–18. NPs can be found in natural sources such as dust storms, forest fires and soil erosion19. They can also be engineered for multiple uses such as fertilizers, personal care items, electronics and health treatments8,20–26. Our prior studies demonstrated that small (< 200 nm) negatively charged engineered NPs, independent of composition, modulated the adaptive immune response in a dinitrofluorobenzene (DNFB) mouse model of contact hypersensitivity (CHS)15,27. DNFB is a potent hapten, in a class of dinitrophenyls, widely used as sensitizer to induce in vivo allergic skin inflammation28–30. We observed that negatively charged, gold (Au), silica (SiO2), and silver (Ag) NPs (20–200 nm diameter) suppressed the allergic response while similar sized positive NPs did not15. Furthermore, certain NPs including highly carboxylated carbon nanotubes (CNT) and some titanium dioxide (TiO2) exacerbated the allergic response15,31,32. However, the underlying mechanisms of how NPs can alter skin immune responses remains poorly understood. Studies report that dendritic cells are critically important in transducing the CHS response33–35. Recently, dendritic cell phenotyping and adoptive transfer studies found that NPs could modulate PD-L2 expression on dendritic cells that correlated with the CHS response36. Other studies found that these NPs impact early signaling events in the challenge phase (< 2 h) and they are capable of reducing the number of degranulated mast cells in the skin, suggesting that mast cells may also play a central role in NP CHS modulation37.

Mast cells are tissue-resident immune cells, prevalent in barrier tissues, that mediate allergic responses through IgE independent and dependent mechanisms with IgE bound to high affinity FcεRI receptors38–42. In vivo, repeated exposure to DNFB is commonly used to model atopic dermatitis and to induce IgE generation43–47. Mast cell activation initiates signaling cascades that regulate cytokine release (e.g. TNF-α, IL-6) which promotes migration of dendritic cells to lymph nodes influencing inflammation40,48–52. Mast cell deficiency in the skin has been shown to dramatically reduce the allergic response due to the impairment of dendritic cells migration to lymph nodes in the sensitization phase30,53. Understanding how NPs might alter mast cell activation, by affecting degranulation and cytokine release, is critical for deciphering the role NPs have in modulating allergic responses. Recent studies in mice using the DNFB CHS model found that mast cells (CD117+, FcεRI+) secreted IL-13 that inhibited the TH1 CHS response and the ability of skin dendritic cells to present antigen in the lymph nodes52. While it is recognized that NPs can alter mast cell cytokine release54,55, it is unknown if NPs can affect cytokine release when co-exposed with a potent hapten and how co-exposure may alter the expression of mast cell surface markers related to activation and degranulation.

In this work, we examined the effect that commercially available ~ 20 nm negatively charged silica (SiO2) and < 100 nm negatively charged 1% manganese-doped titanium dioxide (mTiO2) NPs, have on IgE-mediated activation and degranulation of bone marrow-derived (CD117+, FcεRI+) IgE sensitized mast cells (BMMC). The NPs were selected based on prior studies that found silica suppressed and mTiO2 exacerbated the allergic CHS response15,32,37. BMMC were sensitized with IgE anti-dinitrophenyl-human serum albumin antigen (anti-DNP). Sensitized BMMC were exposed to NPs alone or NPs plus DNP antigen which crosslinks IgE bound to FcεRI receptors on the BMMC to trigger degranulation and cytokine release56–61.

Using flow cytometry, cytokine and degranulation analysis, we evaluated BMMC activation after NP exposure (alone and co-culture with DNP antigen) and characterized the expression of surface markers including FcεRI, CD63 (LAMP-3), and CD107a (LAMP-1). FcεRI is a high-affinity IgE receptor and is central to initiating hypersensitivity reactions. During mast cell activation, this receptor gets internalized to activate the Fc signaling pathway that regulates degranulation and cytokine release39,62,63. CD63 and CD107 are both members of the lysosome-associated membrane protein (LAMP) family that play significant roles in mast cell activation64–67. CD63 is weakly expressed on resting mast cells but upon IgE-mediated activation it is upregulated significantly66,68. Similarly, CD107a is found in cytosolic lysosome compartments, and it is translocated to the cell membrane during mast cell activation (See Fig. S1)67–69.

In NP exposure studies, we found that mTiO2 NPs were cytotoxic to sensitized BMMC in a dose- and time-dependent manner. In co-culture (NP plus DNP antigen) studies of sensitized BMMC, we found that SiO2 NPs protect against degranulation, and they are able to suppress the expression of surface markers associated with activation. These results show that NPs co-cultured with a potent sensitizer can alter BMMC activation, consistent with the immune response observed in the CHS mouse studies, providing mechanistic insight on the immunomodulatory roles that NP have in contact allergy.

Methods

Nanoparticle characterization

Using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements (Zetasizer Nano Instrument, Malvern Panalytical) the SiO2 NPs (nanoComposix Cat #SISN20-25 M) exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 31.8 nm (± 1.67 nm), zeta potential of -26.6 mV (± 3.89 mV) and a PDI of 0.217 in ultrapure water (pH 6.5). The mTiO2 NPs (Sigma-Aldrich Cat #677469, < 100 nm) exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 530.7 nm (± 45.5 nm), zeta potential of -9.1 mV (± 2.26 mV) and a PDI of 0.296 in ultrapure water (pH 6.5). The lower surface charge of the mTiO2 NPs suggest a greater tendency to agglomerate as evidenced by the hydrodynamic diameter being larger than the vendor reported < 100 nm primary particle size. Transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi 7650) was used to measure primary NP size. The average size of the mTiO2 NPs was 56.02 nm (± 17.3 nm) and SiO2 NPs was 21.42 nm (± 2.37 nm) (Table S1, Figs. S2, S3).

Cell culture

Bone marrow cells were harvested and cultured from the femurs and tibias of male and female outbred SKH hairless mice backcrossed 6 generations with C57BL/6 mice aged 6–10 weeks70. All animal work was approved by the University of Rochester Committee on Animal Resources (UCAR) (UCAR-2010-023E). Experiments and reporting of data were carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Bone marrow cells were differentiated over 4 weeks in RPMI 1640 (Gibco Cat # 11875-093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco Cat# 10082147), sodium pyruvate (1mM) (Gibco Cat #11360-070), 1% pen strep (Gibco Cat # 15140-122), non-essential amino acids (0.1 mM) (Gibco Cat # 11140-050), HEPES (10 mM) (Gibco Cat # 15630-080) and mouse recombinant IL-3 (30 ng/mL) (Peprotech Cat # 213-re13)71,72. BMMC differentiation was monitored by the evolution of c-kit (CD117) and FcεRI marker expression73. After 4 weeks, BMMC ≥ 95% purity was achieved, as shown in (Fig. 1). BMMC were used between passages 1–4 after purity was achieved. Studies using non-sensitized BMMC showed little effect of the NPs on activation markers or degranulation. Hence, for all experiments, BMMCs were sensitized overnight at 37 °C with 1 µg/mL anti-DNP IgE (clone SPE-7, Sigma-Aldrich). After sensitization, cells were resuspended in cell media without IL-3 and exposed to the NPs74. All experiments were preformed on IgE sensitized BMMC from at least N = 3 different mice.

Cell viability and exposure to SiO2 and mTiO2 NPs

Cell viability was assessed by flow cytometry using the Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 dye (Invitrogen™) that labels dead cells. We normalized the BMMC live population against BMMC control cells that were not treated with any stressor. Sensitized BMMCs were exposed to SiO2 or mTiO2 NPs at different concentrations for 1 h and 24 h. A range of non-cytotoxic concentrations (0–100 µg/mL SiO2 and 0–25 µg/mL mTiO2) were chosen to evaluate the degranulation response in co-exposure with dinitrophenol-conjugated human serum albumin antigen (DNP, 100 ng/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich Cat # A6661).

Mast cell degranulation

Mast cell degranulation was measured based on the release of β-hexosaminidase as previously described75. Briefly, after sensitization overnight, BMMCs were washed two times and resuspended in HEPES buffer and plated at 5 × 104 cells per well in 96-well flat-bottom plates. They were exposed to SiO2 (0–100 µg/mL) and mTiO2 (0–25 µg/mL) NPs with and without DNP antigen (100 ng/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Afterwards, the plate was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min and 50 µL of supernatant from each sample was transferred into a new well of a 96-well plate to quantify the released β-hexosaminidase. Next, 150 µL of 0.01% Triton-X was added to the cells to lyse them for 10 min to determine the β-hexosaminidase remaining in the cells and 50 µL of the lysed sample was transferred into a new 96-well plate. Next, 100 µL of p-nitrophenyl-N-acetyl-β-D-glucopyranoside in 0.1 M citrate buffer pH 4.5 (PNAG) (Sigma-Aldrich Cat # 487052) was added to the cell supernatants and lysates and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Reactions were stopped by adding 150 µL of 0.2 glycine (pH 10.7). Optical density was measured at 405 nm using a Spectramax i3 plate reader (Molecular Devices). The percentage of degranulation was calculated as the amount of β-hexosaminidase secreted divided by the total (secreted plus cell content).

Cytokine release by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sensitized BMMCs were plated at 1 × 105 cells per well in a 96-well in media without IL-3 to prevent its influence on cytokine secretion. Cells were exposed to non-toxic and non-degranulating concentrations of SiO2 (10 µg/mL) and mTiO2 (1 µg/mL) NPs in the presence or absence of DNP antigen (100 ng/mL) for 1 h, 4 h and 24 h to determine the release of cytokines over time. The cell culture supernatant was collected and analyzed after the evaluated timepoints for the cytokines IL-6, TNF-⍺ and IL-1348,50,52 by ELISA (Invitrogen Cat # 88-7064, 88-7324, 88-7137, respectively) following manufacture instructions. These cytokines were selected as they represent important cytokines that can modulate skin allergy.

Surface marker expression

Mast cell activation was assessed by measuring FcεRI, CD63 (LAMP-3), and CD107a (LAMP-1) surface expression. Briefly, sensitized 1 × 106 BMMC were treated with SiO2 (10 µg/mL) and mTiO2 (1 µg/mL) NPs in the presence or absence of DNP antigen (100 ng/mL) for 30 min. Afterwards, cells were stained with 5 µg/mL Super Bright 436 anti-mouse FcεRI (clone MAR-1; ThermoFisher), APC anti-mouse CD117 (clone 2B8;ThermoFisher), PE-Cy7 anti-mouse CD63 (clone NVG-2 ;ThermoFisher) and Brilliant Violet 711 (BV711) anti-mouse CD107a (LAMP-1) (clone eBio1D4B; ThermoFisher) to 50 µL of BMMC suspension after NP exposure. BMMCs were placed on ice 30 min after NP exposure and washed twice with ice-cold FACS buffer. Afterwards, cells were stained for 15 min on ice with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780, washed twice with FACS buffer and then incubated for 10 min on ice with 1 µg/mL anti-mouse CD16/32 (clone 93; ThermoFisher) to block Fcγ receptors. Cells were then stained with markers of interest for 30 min at 4 °C and washed with ice-cold FACS buffer. Samples were analyzed on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) and FlowJo (v10.7.3). The gating strategy is shown in Fig. S4. For each experiment, FMO controls with BMMC were used. Examples of the FMO gating are illustrated in Fig. S5.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies on NP cellular uptake

BMMC were cultured alone (control) or cultured with SiO2 NPs (10 µg/ml) or mTiO2 NPs (1 µg/ml) or with A2187 (calcium ionophore) 10 µM for 30 min. Cells were washed and prepared for TEM analysis. The cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M, pH 7.2 cacodylate buffer for 24 h at 4 °C and rinsed with cold 0.1 M pH 7.2 cacodylate buffer three times. The cells were then fixed with 1% osmium tetraoxide (Ted Pella, Inc.) for 1 h at 4 °C and washed with cold distilled water three times. The cells were dehydrated using graded alcohol baths (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%) and then infiltrated with and embedded in Spurr epoxy resin with overnight polymerization at 70 °C. After embedding, the samples were cut to 1–2 μm with a glass blade and finally sliced at 70 nm with a diamond knife and placed on copper grids. Cells were imaged using a Hitachi 5100 TEM apparatus.

Statistics

We used GraphPad Prism 10.4 to analyze all statistical analysis. Ordinary one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare expression levels to non-stimulated BMMC and DNP antigen with Tukey and Sidak’s multiple comparisons. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. All data is presented with standard deviation. The experiments were replicated at least three times.

Results

Effects of SiO2 and mTiO2 on BMMC toxicity, degranulation, cytokine secretion and surface marker expression

We performed cytotoxicity studies to establish concentration ranges to examine the effects of SiO2 and mTiO2 NPs on BMMC activation and degranulation. We conducted NP only exposure studies on sensitized BMMC for 1 h and 24 h to measure cytotoxicity as a function of concentration and time using flow cytometry. Results (Fig. 2) showed a dose- and time-dependent decrease in cell viability for mTiO2 but not for SiO2. Exposing cells for 1 h to mTiO2 (> 25 µg/mL) caused a > 30% decrease in cell viability whereas exposing cells to SiO2 was not cytotoxic up to 100 µg/ml. Exposing the cells for 24 h to the mTiO2 NPs showed a decrease (> 20%) in cell viability over time starting at > 5 µg/mL. Exposure to SiO2 NPs for 24 h was considerably less toxic, exhibit 90% viability at 100 µg/ml.

Cytotoxicity studies suggest that mTiO2 may act as a proinflammatory stressor compared to SiO2. To further characterize interactions with BMMC, we evaluated degranulation response after NP exposure via the release of β-hexosaminidase. BMMCs were exposed to a range of concentrations of SiO2 (0–100 µg/mL) and mTiO2 (0–25 µg/mL) for 30 min. Results showed that SiO2 (Fig. 3A) does not affect degranulation, while mTiO2 (Fig. 3B) increases degranulation at the highest evaluated concentration (25 µg/mL). These results suggest that mTiO2 can stimulate BMMC activation at high concentrations (≥25 µg/mL). TEM studies showed that mTiO2 NP (1 µg/mL) are endocytosed by BMMC in 30 min but no evidence was found for SiO2 (10 µg/mL) NP uptake (Figs. S6-S9).

Next, we selected non-toxic and non-degranulating concentrations of the NPs and examined their effect on the release of key cytokines (IL-6, TNF-⍺ and IL-13) secreted in the supernatant by ELISA. BMMCs were exposed to SiO2 (10 µg/mL) and mTiO2 (1 µg/mL) as a function of time. Results showed that neither of the NPs increased IL-6 secretion (Fig. 4A) over time, while mTiO2 upregulated TNF-⍺ after 24 h (Fig. 4B). IL-13 was not released after exposure to the NPs (Fig. S10). These results suggest that mTiO2 affects BMMC activation, under non-cytotoxic exposure conditions, as measured by degranulation and release of TNF-⍺.

Lastly, we examined the effect of each NP on the expression of surface markers related to BMMC activation by flow cytometry. Double and subsequent single gating strategies, with FMO controls, were used (Fig. S4 – S5). Double gating on the CD117+ FcεRI+ population is performed to identify the BMMC population. Subsequent single gating assesses the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) levels of each marker of interest in the BMMC population. Results are compared to the expression of each marker on non-treated cell controls. Results show that the mTiO2 NPs downregulate the expression of both FcεRI and CD117 (Figs. 5A-B). Downregulation of the FcεRI-IgE complex confirms BMMC activation, as internalization of this high affinity receptor induces intracellular signaling that leads to degranulation and cytokine release76,77. Further, mTiO2 NP induced a slight upregulation in the expression of CD107a (Fig. 5C) which is also considered a marker for degranulation. In contrast, SiO2 NPs did not alter the expression levels of FcεRI or CD107a and only slightly reduced the expression of CD117 (Figs. 5A, B, D). While the expression level of CD117 in mast cell activation is not well understood, studies report that CD117 can enhance antigen-mediated FcεRI activation78. Neither NP altered CD63 (LAMP-3) expression with statistical significance although mTiO2 showed an increased trend (Fig. 5D). In the absence of antigen, it is plausible that exposing the cells to the NPs for longer times or to higher NP doses might cause more substantial increase in CD53 and CD107a. Nonetheless, these results suggest that in the absence of antigen, mTiO2 activate BMMCs through the FcεRI signaling pathway and confirm the β-hexosaminidase data showing mTiO2 was capable of degranulating the cells.

Effects of DNP antigen co-exposure with NPs on cytokine secretion, degranulation and surface marker expression

Previous studies have shown that NPs co-exposed with DNFB modulated the in vivo CHS response in the challenge phase15. In this work, we exposed sensitized BMMCs to DNP antigen, which is similar to the hapten complex that forms using DNFB. DNP is capable of triggering degranulation in BMMC sensitized with anti-DNP-IgE79,80 and this model has been used to study the effects of preloading cells with NPs on degranulation, cytokine secretion and the expression of the surface markers54,55,75. Here, we evaluated if NP (SiO2 and mTiO2) co-exposure with DNP (100 ng/mL) had an effect on degranulation in a dose-response manner. Results showed exposure to DNP alone induced ~ 40% degranulation which is higher than mTiO2 alone (Fig. 3B). We observe that SiO2 NPs dramatically suppressed DNP-induced degranulation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6A). At a moderate concentration (5–10 µg/mL), SiO2 NPs suppressed the degranulation by ~ 50%. At higher concentrations (≥25 µg/mL) SiO2 NPs suppressed DNP-induced degranulation with β-hexosaminidase levels equivalent to unstimulated controls. In comparison, mTiO2 NPs had no effect on degranulation caused by DNP (Fig. 6B). These results are consistent with the immunosuppressive effects induced by SiO2 NPs in the CHS model15,36,37.

Next, we examined the effects of DNP co-exposure with SiO2 (10 µg/mL) or mTiO2 (1 µg/mL) NPs on cytokine release (IL-6, TNF-⍺ and IL-13) over time (Fig. 7). Results showed DNP exposure alone upregulates these cytokines to high levels over time compared to NPs alone (Figs. 4, S5). At 4 h and 24 h, mTiO2 NP exacerbated IL-6 release by DNP (Fig. 7A). Neither NP altered the release of TNF-⍺ by DNP over time (Fig. 7B). Co-exposure with SiO2 NP reduced the amount of IL-13 released by DNP alone after 24 h (Fig. 7C). While, mast cell produced IL-13 is considered to have anti-inflammatory in Th1 hapten CHS models limiting the production CD4+IFN-γ+ cells in the skin52, for IgE-mediated allergic skin disorders, IL-13 is proinflammatory promoting Th2 cytokine production, disrupting the skin barrier and causing epidermal hyperplasia52,81. Hence, our findings are consistent with mTiO2 NP and SiO2 promoting proinflammatory and immunosuppressive effects on FcεRI mediated BMMC activation, respectively.

Lastly, we examined the effects of DNP co-exposure with NPs on the expression of surface markers related to BMMC activation by flow cytometry. Analysis of each marker showed that DNP alone downregulated the expression of FcεRI and CD117 and upregulated the expression of CD63 and CD107a consistent with its ability to activate BMMCs (Fig. 8A-D). In DNP co-exposure with NPs, SiO2 suppressed the downregulation of FcεRI, and diminished the upregulation of CD63 and CD107 caused by DNP, thus demonstrating its ability to be immunosuppressive in IgE-mediated BMMC response (Fig. 8A-D). Co-exposure of DNP with mTiO2 NPs had no effect on the expression of FcεRI, CD117 and CD107a, while having a slight significant downregulating effect on the expression of CD63 (Fig. 8A-D). These results suggest that mTiO2 NPs do not exacerbate the potent BMMC activation caused by DNP IgE allergic activation.

Discussion

Engineered NPs have been extensively used in many industries and have become a core part of biomedical research over the past few decades82,83. In recent years, NP-based therapies have emerged as a potential means to induce allergy tolerance84–93 using functionalized NPs to deliver an allergen with immune modulating signals89–91. NPs have been designed to reach specific subsets of immune cells or used as adjuvants to modify a specific immune response94,95. These strategies are more specific and superior to the use of systemic immunosuppressants, which are the most used drugs in the treatment of allergies treatment95. These drugs are capable of inhibiting immune responses; however, they have a number of adverse side effects with long term use96.

This work expands on our previous studies that showed NPs can modulate the adaptive immune response in a CHS model and they can modulate dendritic cell response in vitro and in vivo using an adoptive transfer CHS model15,36. In the previous studies, we showed that SiO2 NPs exhibited a cytoprotective effect in co-exposure with DNFB and are capable of suppressing inflammation in the challenge phase while mTiO2 NPs induced higher pro-inflammatory effects on dendritic cells and exacerbate the allergic response in mice15,36. As dendritic cells and mast cells interact to coordinate the allergic response, we decided to examine how these NPs could impact BMMC activation by quantifying BMMC cytotoxicity, degranulation, cytokine production, and the expression of key surface markers (FcεRI, CD117, CD63, CD107a) in an allergic in vitro setting.

Results of this study showed that exposure to mTiO2 NPs are cytotoxic (Fig. 2B), capable of degranulating BMMCs (Fig. 3B) and releasing higher levels of TNF-⍺ (Fig. 4B) compared to SiO2 NPs which did not induce these effects. The observed slight increase in degranulation with mTiO2 NP (25 µg/ml) in the absence of antigen (Fig. 3B) may be partially due to cytotoxicity or cell membrane damage, including disruption granule membranes, rather than direct receptor-mediated activation. The data shows that over a 24 h period the cells secrete very low levels (< 3 pg/ml) of TNFα. This suggest the cells maybe be slightly stressed and/or some cells may have died due to removal of IL-3 in the media which is required to promote cell growth and differentiation but can influence cytokine secretion.

In the short, 30 min, NP co-exposure studies with DNP antigen, results showed that SiO2 NPs reduce the effects of DNP on BMMC degranulation in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 6A) and they also reduce the amount of IL-13 released by DNP (Fig. 7C). In contrast, mTiO2 NPs increase the release of IL-6 induced by DNP (Fig. 7A) consistent with their pro-inflammatory properties. It is important to note that activated mast cells often co-release TNF-α and IL-6 which is not observed here. However, TNFα like histamine, is stored in preformed granules. Hence, mast cells can release pre-formed mediators without (or delay) release of cytokines like IL-6 that must be synthesized. This differential mediator release occurs in response to stimuli that favor rapid degranulation over transcription97. It is plausible that the stimuli used here failed to activate rapid cytokine synthesize. We specifically chose low non-toxic, non-degranulating concentrations of each NP and while we detect degranulation via β-hexosaminidase release, our stimulation conditions were likely too low to induce IL-6 synthesis and secretion.

These results add to a growing literature base98–100 demonstrating that BMMC responses can be modulated by NPs. For example, Brown et al. demonstrated that pre-exposure of BMMC to AgNPs for more than 24 h enhanced BMMC activation by DNP54. Furthermore, they demonstrated that exposure to AgNPs can trigger BMMC activation through non-IgE mediated pathways101. Here, we demonstrate that SiO2 NPs reduce BMMC activation in DNP co-exposure studies, noting that the cells were not preexposed to the NPs. This finding is consistent with our previous in vivo mouse CHS studies reporting that these SiO2 NPs suppressed the allergic response showing reduced inflammation and BMMC degranulation15,37. To achieve the immunosuppressive effect, the SiO2 NPs had to be co-applied with the DNFB challenge or applied in a short time window < 2 h post DNFB challenge, suggesting the NPs modulate a very early immune response trigger.

Analysis of surface markers related to BMMC activation suggest that DNP and mTiO2 induce BMMC (FcεRI+, CD117+) activation using the FcεRI pathway. Although non-IgE mediated activation cannot be ruled out, DNP and mTiO2 NPs downregulate FcεRI expression which is consistent with the receptor being internalized. Downregulation of the FcεRI receptor alone is not sufficient to confirm this mechanism. Supportive evidence are the changes in CD63 and CD107a (LAMP-1) expression which are established reliable surface markers of mast cell activation and degranulation, particularly when triggered via the FcεRI (IgE-mediated) pathway67,102,103. Furthermore, we found that NP exposure on unsensitized BMMC had no effect on activation, cytokine secretion or degranulation, pointing to the importance of FcεRI engagement. In contrast, SiO2 NPs had no effect on the expression of the surface markers (Fig. 5) and in co-exposure with DNP, they suppressed the effects of DNP on the markers (Fig. 8), demonstrating the inhibitory abilities of SiO2 NPs to suppress BMMC activation.

These findings suggest that NPs may modulate BMMC activation using the FcεRI pathway as they are capable of altering DNP activity. DNP is a known activator of mast cell via antigen/IgE/FcεRI complex, and its activation leads to calcium mobilization and release which in turns leads to the release of the cytokines IL-6, TNF-⍺ and IL-1338. However, it is still unknown, which specific part of the pathway is being affected by the NPs. It is of particular interest to speculate on how the SiO2 NP suppressed degranulation. The TEM images (Figs. S6-S9) support the data that BMMC exposed to SiO2 do not appear degranulated in contrast to BMMC that uptake mTiO2 which do appear degranulated. We do not believe that DNP (100 ng/ml) is selectively sorbed on SiO2 considering the many thousand-fold excess of protein in the media containing 10% FBS (30–45 mg/ml). It is plausible that the protein coronas that form on the NPs differ between SiO2 that may sorb more immunosuppressive proteins that have been identified in prior studies104. The SiO2 NP could potentially block DNP from binding the FcεRI receptors or they may engage receptors that block degranulation including adrenergic receptors and/or Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Inhibition Motifs (ITIMs). Future studies can analyze differences in protein corona compositions as well as the changes in proteins associated with the FcεRI pathway. Determining if SiO2 NPs modulates phosphorylation of tyrosine kinases such as Lyn or Fyn will help confirm the mechanism within the FcεRI pathway that inhibit BMMC activation.

In summary, this work builds on our knowledge base showing how NPs can modulate BMMC activation and related cell surface markers that can be used to predict the effects of unknown compounds on mast cell activation.
