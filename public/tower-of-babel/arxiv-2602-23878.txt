A Foundation for Differentiable Logics using Dependent Type Theory
Reynald Affeldt, Alessandro Bruni, Ekaterina Komendantskaya, Natalia Ślusarz, Kathrin Stark

Abstract
Differentiable logics are a family of quantitative logics originated in the machine learning literature. Because of their origin, differentiable logics often come equipped with analytic properties that guarantee that they are differentiable. However, they usually lack an accompanying theory that describes their algebraic and proof-theoretic properties. Meanwhile, fuzzy logics, seen as substructural logics, have been studied algebraically and proof-theoretically, and some fuzzy logics with desirable analytic properties have also been used in machine learning. Our aim is to systematically compare analytic, algebraic and proof-theoretical properties of both fuzzy and differentiable logics. To this end, we formalize differentiable and fuzzy logics in a unified framework, encoded using the MathComp library in the Rocq proof assistant. We propose a single language specification to encompass multiple logics, using intrinsic typing to only allow valid and well-typed formulas for each of the logics that we encode: Yager, Łukasiewicz, Gödel and product fuzzy logics, as well as the differentiable logics DL2 and STL. Algebraically, we show how these logics can be interpreted using residuated lattices, which are prevalent in the theory of substructural logics. Analytically, we formalise the existence of a positive derivative for certain logical connectives, and to this end we formalise L’Hôpital’s, contributing it to the MathComp library. Proof-theoretically, we formalise established sequent calculi for fuzzy logics, and we propose new sequent calculi for DL2 and STL ∞ , and formalise their soundness in our framework.

Introduction

This work aims at contributing to the field of formal verification of
machine learning seen as the
study of algorithms that learn statistically from data. Neural networks
are the most common technical device used in machine learning.
The standard learning algorithms (such as gradient descent) use a

loss function

{\cal L}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
to optimise
the neural network’s parameters (say,
\theta
) to fit the input-output
vectors given by the data in a way that the loss
{\cal L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

is minimised. This optimisation objective is usually denoted as

\min_{\theta}{\cal L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

Most approaches to verification of neural networks consist of an
automated procedure based on SMT solving, abstract interpretation, or
branch-and-bound techniques (see, e.g., Albarghouthi’s
survey
albarghouthi2021introduction
).
Verification typically applies after training because traditional
learning is purely data-driven and thus agnostic to verification
properties.
In contrast,
property-guided training
takes place once the
verification properties are stated.
More precisely, verification of neural networks consists of two parts:
statement and verification of a given property, and training
of the neural network that optimises the neural network’s parameters
towards satisfying the given property.

However, naively or manually performed mapping of a logical property
to an optimisation task results in major discrepancies (as shown by
Casadio et al.
CasadioKDKKAR22
). This suggests the need to have
principled
tools for property-guided training. One approach is to provide
programming language support for property-guided development of neural
networks that involves specification, verification, and optimisation in
a safe-by-construction environment.
As an example, Vehicle
FoMLAS2023
;
DaggittKAKS025
provides this
support in the form of
a Haskell DSL, with
(1) a higher-order typed specification
language, in which required neural network properties can be clearly
documented, and
(2) type-driven compilation, which can take care of
correct-by-construction translation of properties into both the
language of neural network verifiers it interfaces with and loss
functions.

To generate loss functions from a logical property, one can use

differentiable logics
(DLs).
One possibility for such differentiable logics are the
well-studied
fuzzy logics
that date back to the works of
Łukasiewicz and Gödel
van2022analyzing
.

Example 1.1
.

The semantics of a differentiable logic is a map
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
from a given syntax to a
real number-valued function.
Consider, as an example of a fuzzy logic, product logic
(

.

As an alternative, both verification and machine-learning communities
formulated differentiable logics such as DL2
fischer2019dl2
and
STL

.

Existing differentiable logics are very different from one another. For example, they do
not agree on the domains of the resulting loss functions: fuzzy logics
have as domain the closed interval

In
consequence, it is unclear which differentiable logic is more
suitable for property-guided training and how the differentiable logics compare.
Empirical evaluations, such as Flinkow et al.
flinkow2025comparing
,
cannot
provide a clear principled answer to the problem of developing suitable
differentiable logics.

This situation calls for a principled unified mathematical study of differentiable logics.
One way to do this is to take into account a century-long tradition of
algebraic and proof-theoretic studies of fuzzy
logics
galatos2007residuated
;
fuzzy-proof
, and show how those
well-established results extend to, and shed the light on, the new differentiable logics
proposed in the machine learning context. On the other hand, machine learning
comes with is own approach to analysing mathematical (this time
understood as
analytical
) properties of differentiable logics. Thus, the second
research question to ask is whether, and how, the century-old fuzzy logics
(rendered as differentiable logics) fit into this machine learning context.
This research program poses a challenge, due to both
the large zoo of existing differentiable logics as well as the diversity of the mathematical
methods used by
different
communities to motivate and analyse these logics.

Why reconciling algebraic and analytic traditions is hard
for differentiable logics?

On the one hand, even though differentiable logics derived from fuzzy logics can be
characterised by
the existing algebraic semantics of fuzzy
logics
galatos2007residuated
;
fuzzy-proof
,
the majority of these logics do not enjoy desirable analytic properties,
such as
smoothness
or
shadow-lifting

, which
are key for machine learning applications.
On the other hand, the new differentiable logics inspired by
machine learning
fischer2019dl2
;
varnai

are not very well understood from the algebraic point of view.
For example, Varnai and Dimarogonas

prove that
differentiable logic operations cannot satisfy simultaneously the properties of
shadow-lifting, idempotence, and associativity. Yet, associativity is
traditionally the key property in the
algebraic analysis of differentiable logics
galatos2007residuated
.

Similarly, proof-theoretic study of differentiable logics is lacking.
While some fuzzy logics enjoy sound and/or complete propositional sequent
calculi

, the new differentiable logics suggested by machine
learning communities have not been studied from the proof-theoretic
perspective.

We believe that a systematic investigation of differentiable logics is a necessary
step towards the development of a reliable neural network verification
tool, and towards formal verification of machine learning in general.
We therefore propose to formalise them in an interactive theorem prover,
providing a
unified formalisation of differentiable logics in which their properties can be
compared rigorously and in which one can verify the translation from
properties to loss functions.
For that purpose, we chose the
Rocq
prover:
firstly, because it has been used to carry out numerous formalisations of logics
and programming languages;
secondly, because the Mathematical Components project provides libraries to
handle the algebraic properties of differentiable logics (using

MathComp

) and the analytic properties such as
shadow-lifting (using
MathComp-Analysis

analysis
).

Our approach to a systematic investigation of differentiable logics is to build on
top of previous work that has already proposed a common presentation
of differentiable logics

and to advance the understanding of mathematical
properties of differentiable logics along the following three axes:

•

Algebraic
: The idea is to define algebraic semantics
for differentiable logics that guide our general understanding and comparison of
their properties.
For that purpose, we use
residuated
lattices
.
Residuated lattices are algebraic structures that have been
suggested as a suitable model for quantitative

substructural logics

galatos2007residuated
;
fuzzy-proof
.
We show which of the new differentiable logics can be mapped to residuated lattices and make
a comparative inventory
of the structural properties of the logical connectives of differentiable logics.

•

Analytic
: This axis concerns the properties of
differentiable logics that rely on the use of real analysis, because of the use of
limits and differentiation. A central example is shadow-lifting
already mentioned above. It states that the resulting functions
should have partial derivatives that can characterise the idea of
gradual improvement in training

. For example, the
translation of a conjunction should evaluate to a higher value if
the value of one of its conjuncts
increases. Example
1.1
illustrates how such a
conjunction may look.
We formally prove or disprove (the latter on pen and paper) shadow-lifting,
for each chosen differentiable logic.

•

Proof-theoretic
:
Proof theory, and in particular Gentzen sequent calculi, have long
been used to study computational properties of logics. While fuzzy
logics already have well-defined proof-theoretic
semantics

, the question of whether new differentiable logics may
enjoy such semantics is still open. We present sound sequent
calculi for DL2

and STL
∞
, and argue why
STL
ν
is
unlikely to have a Gentzen-style proof calculus (see
Sect.
4.1.2
for definitions of STL
∞
and
STL
ν
).

The approach explained above led us to the following contributions:

1.

We explain how to encode known differentiable logics in a single generic syntax
using
Rocq
, building on known techniques for logic embedding,
such as taking advantage of dependent types to achieve intrinsic
typing. The formalisation is comprehensive and extensible for
future use.

2.

We demonstrate how to use the Mathematical Components libraries
for our purpose. For example, we take advantage of algebraic
tactics

to automate algebraic and soundness
proofs, and we develop reusable lemmas, such as L’Hôpital’s rule
to establish shadow-lifting.

3.

As a result of our formalisation, we are able to find and fix
errors in the literature, and to produce original results
missing parts of the
shadow-lifting proofs, and soundness of DL2 and STL
∞
with respect
to their
new sequent calculi.

The paper proceeds as follows.

Section

In Sect.

.

3
Mathematical background on residuated lattices

In this section, we recall the notion
of residuated lattice that will be key
to organise our formalisation of differentiable logics.

Residuated lattices have been proposed as a suitable algebraic
structure to characterise a range of
substructural
logics

galatos2007residuated
, i.e., logics that are stronger than
intuitionistic logic, but weaker than classical logic.
These include linear logic, paraconsistent and relevant logics, and,
most importantly for our current study, real number-valued and fuzzy
logics
galatos2007residuated
;
fuzzy-proof
. We devote this
section to definition of residuated lattices, and will use the
knowledge of these algebraic properties as a guiding principle in
later sections.

In the below definition we use real numbers as a carrier set, since
real numbers are of specific interest in this work. Often, the below
definition is given with an abstract carrier set.

Definition 3.1
.

An algebra
\mathbf{RL}=({\mathbb{R}},\lor,\land,\odot,\Rightarrow,1)
is called
a

residuated lattice
if

•

({\mathbb{R}},\lor,\land)
is a lattice (i.e.,
\lor,\land
are
commutative, associative, and mutually absorptive, and thus give rise to
a lattice order
\leq
),

•

({\mathbb{R}},\odot,1)
is a monoid (i.e.,
\odot
is commutative and
associative with unit element 1),

•

x\odot y\leq z
iff

We divide the properties proven in this work into three groups: algebraic,

(I) Algebraic properties

Interpretation via residuated lattices

Definition 4.1

In addition, we say that

•

•

All of the chosen

and some of them have a

Alas, they lose their desirable analytic properties at the limit!
This shows that the parameter
\nu

switch between the discrete and continuous worlds.

Idempotence

The idempotence of the lattice connectives is a direct consequence of the mutual absorption axioms (R5–R6) which are part of the axiomatisation of a residuated lattice (Table
2
), hence we do not consider it separately. Take
\lor
as an example:

x\lor x\overset{\textrm{(R5)}}{=}x\lor(x\land(x\lor x))\overset{\textrm{(R6)}}{=}x.

The idempotence property for monoidal connectives, i.e.,

x\odot x=x
was proposed as one of the desirable properties by the
authors of STL
ν

. Informally, the property is justified as it
disables situations when duplication of identical data swings the
training process. However, since Varnai and Dimarogonas
varnai
show that
idempotence,
associativity, and shadow-lifting cannot be satisfied at the same
time, idempotence stands at odds with definition of residuated
lattice, that does require associativity of both monoid and lattice
operators. On the other hand STL
∞
operators are
idempotent and associative, but not shadow-lifting.

(II) Analytic properties

Varnai and Dimarogonas

introduce three properties in this
category: weak
smoothness, scale-invariance, and shadow-lifting.
The latter is the most important as it accounts for gradual improvement in
training.
We only consider shadow-lifting here as it is the most complex of those
properties and leave the remaining properties to future work.

Definition 4.2

(Shadow-lifting property
varnai
)
.

The differentiable logic satisfies the
shadow-lifting
property if, for any

\llbracket p\rrbracket_{\textrm{DL}}\neq 0
,

\left.\dfrac{\partial\bigg\llbracket\bigwedge\nolimits_{M}(p_{0},\ldots,p_{i},\ldots,p_{M})\bigg\rrbracket_{\textrm{DL}}}{\partial\llbracket p_{i}\rrbracket_{\textrm{DL}}}\right\rvert_{p_{j}=p\text{ where }i\neq j}>0

holds for all
0\leq i\leq M
,
where
\partial
denotes partial differentiation.

Notice that classical conjunction does not satisfy the
property of shadow-lifting: no matter how “true” the value of
p_{2}
is, if

p_{1}
is
false, then
p_{1}\land p_{2}
will remain false. Likewise, all differentiable logics that use

\min
or

\max
to define conjunction will fail shadow-lifting.

Shadow-lifting was originally defined for conjunction only, as STL
ν
had no
disjunction.
In our formalisation, we could, in principle, extend shadow-lifting to any
logical connective of interest.
However, we left this
extension for future work.

For differentiable logics that do not naturally have
\bigwedge\nolimits_{M}
, we prove shadow-lifting for

8.1

Real-valued logics, and in particular fuzzy logics, have been studied

significance is beyond the scope of this paper for which we refer the interested

Generally, these logics are shown to be more expressive than intuitionistic

Both Hilbert-style and Gentzen-style calculi for fuzzy logics exist. We

The distinct features of these fuzzy logic calculi come from their
unusual structural rules. Firstly, handling the hypersequents necessitates

The structural rules called

is supposed to reflect the fact that the real line forms a total order, in

usually requires some combination of the structural rules

,

, and
(MIX)
, depending on the choice of the fuzzy

need to be defined for each

While Gödel logic has both monoidal and lattice connectives it is

replaced by the multiplicative analogues).

Definition 8.2

(Sequent soundness)
.

•

•

Property-guided training, by means of an example

Neural network properties

Given a neural network
N:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the a property to be verified
usually takes the form of a Hoare triple

\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\longrightarrow{\cal S}(\mathbf{x})
,
where
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\cal S}
can be arbitrary properties
obtained by using variables
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
constants, vector, arithmetic operations,
\leq
,
=
,
\land
,
\lor
, and

\neg
. Additionally,

{\cal S}
may contain the neural network
N
as a function.

Example 9.1

(
\epsilon
-
\delta
-robustness

CasadioKDKKAR22
)
.

Given a neural network
N
and a vector
\mathbf{v}
, consider the
specification that requires that for all inputs
\mathbf{x}
that are within
an
\epsilon
distance from
\mathbf{v}
, the output of
N(\mathbf{x})
should
not deviate by more than
\delta
from
N(\mathbf{v})
:

\forall\mathbf{x}.|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\epsilon\Rightarrow|N(\mathbf{x})-N(\mathbf{v})|_{L_{\infty}}\leq\delta.

This property can be used to avoid misclassifying images when only a few pixels are
perturbed.
This particular example uses the
L_{\infty}
norm
:

|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|_{L_{\infty}}\overset{\textrm{def}}{=}\max_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}([\mathbf{x}]_{i}-[\mathbf{y}]_{i})
,
where
[\mathbf{x}]_{i}
stands for the
i
th element of
\mathbf{x}
.

Unfortunately, as demonstrated by Fischer et
al.
fischer2019dl2
, even most accurate neural networks fail
even the most natural verification properties, such as

\epsilon
-
\delta
-robustness. This motivated the search for better
ways to train the networks.

Property-guided training

Methods for property-guided
training have received considerable attention in the AI literature, as the
survey
ijcai2022p767
shows.
We will only illustrate the method that was suggested by Fischer et
al.
fischer2019dl2
, and refer the reader to the survey for more
examples.

Example 9.2

(Generating a loss function from a logical
property
fischer2019dl2
)
.

Recall that standard supervised learning trains a neural network
N
with
trainable parameters
\theta

to optimise the objective
\min_{\theta}{\cal L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,
for the loss function
{\cal L}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
.
Generally,
{\cal L}
measures the difference between
the network’s output and the given data for each input point. Examples of

{\cal L}
are cross-entropy loss or mean squared error.
But now we want to train the neural network to satisfy any arbitrary property

\forall\mathbf{x}.{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})\longrightarrow{\cal S}(\mathbf{x})
. For this,
we replace the above optimisation objective with

\min_{\theta}\left(\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{H}_{{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})}}{\cal L}_{{\cal S}}(\mathbf{x})\right)

where
\mathbb{H}_{{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
refines the type
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
to a
subset for which the property
{\mathcal{P}}
holds,
and
{\cal L}_{\mathcal{S}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is obtained by
applying a suitable

interpretation function
for
{\cal S}
.

We omit the exact details of how such optimisation algorithms are
defined: they are known and can be found in a suitable machine
learning tutorial, for example
KM18
.
Intuitively, the optimisation algorithm will search for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{H}_{{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{x})}
such that
\mathbf{x}

maximises the loss
{\cal L}_{{\cal S}}(\mathbf{x})
,
in order to train the neural network parameters
\theta
to minimise that loss.
Concretely, if the property is
\epsilon
-
\delta
-robustness, it will look for
the worst perturbation of
\mathbf{v}

that violates the property, and will optimise the neural network to classify
that bad example correctly.

Differentiable logics for loss functions

In the above
example, we did not explain how to define the interpretation function

{\cal L}_{{\cal S}}
for an arbitrary property
{\cal S}
; we need differentiable
logics for that purpose.

Related work

Neural network verification is a new field, and its nascent methods
need validation and further refinement. Better understanding,
formalisation and implementation of
differentiable logic
s is often named as one of
the main challenges in the field
CordeiroDGIJKKLMSW25
.

In terms of programming language support for neural network verification,
the CAISAR tool
girardsatabin2022caisar
, implemented as an OCaml DSL,
puts emphasis on the smooth integration of a general specification language
with many existing neural network solvers.
However, CAISAR does not support property-guided training.
The aspiration of languages like Vehicle
DaggittKAKS025
and CAISAR is to
accommodate compilation
of specifications into both neural network solver and machine learning backends.
For the former, there is an on-going work on certifying the neural network
solver backends
Des25
;
DaggittAKKA23
;
DaggittKAKS025
.

On the side of machine-learning backends,
differentiable logic
s have been previously
formalised in the Agda interactive theorem prover as part of the formalisation
of the Vehicle language
ADK24
, but did not extend to
shadow-lifting---the part that requires extensive mathematical
libraries. Property-guided training certified via theorem proving was
also proposed by Chevallier et al.
ChevallierWF22
.

Relevant work on formalisation of neural networks in ITPs includes:
verification of neural networks in Isabelle/HOL

brucker2023verifying
and Imandra
Des25
,
formalisation of piecewise affine activation functions in

Rocq

aleksandrov2023formalizing
, providing formal guarantees of
the degree to which the trained neural network will generalise to new
data in
Rocq

bagnall2019certifying
, convergence of a
single-layered perceptron in
Rocq

murphy2017verified
, and
verification of neural archetypes in
Rocq

de2022use
. The
formalisation presented here does not directly formalise neural
networks.

Prompted by the increasing demand from AI, there has been a surge of
research into mathematical foundations of quantitative logics,
of which
differentiable logic
s can be seen as a special case.
Firstly, Bacci et al.
bacci2023mfps
;
abs-2402-03543
formalised a logic of
Lawvere quantale,
over the domain
[0,\infty]
. The logic bears some resemblance to
DL2

thanks to its domain, and also
the authors have shown that the
Łukasiewicz
logic is an instance of the logics of
Lawvere quantale.
The structure of Lawvere Quantale generalises the definition of residuated
lattice, and also this logic
has a proof-theoretic interpretation
bacci2023mfps
;
abs-2402-03543
.
Bacci and Mögelberg
abs-2501-18275
further extended that logic with
the induction principle,
and showed how it can be applied to encode Probabilistic Hoare logic and Markov
Processes.

At the same time, Capucci
abs-2406-04936
presented a logic that promises
even a greater generality.
It also takes a Lawvere quantale as its interpretation, thus retaining the link
with the tradition of residuated lattices.
But at the same time it defines multiplicative and additive connectives that
yield
a sequent calculus similar to that of the Linear logic.
Notably, its monoidal operators resemble those of the
product
logic we have
considered here.
For the lattice/additive component, that logic offers to use soft additive
connectives
\land
and
\lor
.
These connectives have a softness parameter, that, at
\infty
, turns them into

\min
and
\max
; while at all other values
retains the shadow-lifting and smoothness properties. This resembles a lot the

STL
ν
logic intuition, with its use of parameter
\nu
.
Capucci’s logic is under development, yet it promises to bring a conceptually
elegant resolution
to the quest for harmonious co-existence of algebraic, analytic and
proof-theoretic properties in one logic.
We are looking into formalisation of that logic in

Rocq

marulanda-giraldo2025our
.

Different variants of the logics we use in this work---fuzzy logics in
particular---can also be considered as alternative
differentiable logic
s. The
primary example being the choice of type of
implication
van2022analyzing
.
The
implication required by the properties of residuated lattices and used through
this paper are R-implications, but S-implications as well as Yager f- and
g-implications are other well-known classes with different semantic
properties
baczynski2007yager
.
We have investigated some of the alternate semantics, in particular involving
S-implications and S-negations, residuation does not always hold.
On the other hand, the extension of
differentiable logic
s, particularly fuzzy logics, with
comparisons between real numbers is reminiscent of
SMT
barrett2018satisfiability
, as both involve adding domain-specific
theories on top of propositional logic.

A property found desirable for
differentiable logic
s which we do not consider in this work is
adequacy relative to Boolean logic (in ML works sometimes referred to as
‘‘soundness’’
fischer2019dl2
). Proving a logic to be adequate shows it
to be weaker than classical logic---a property commonly seen within the field
of substructural logics which are shown to be stronger than intuitionistic
logic and weaker than classical
galatos2007residuated
. While adequacy of
fuzzy logics is well known, the introduction of comparisons between real
numbers has a non-trivial effect on the result and is left as future work.

Lastly, we would be remiss not to mention the developments in the
application of
differentiable logic
s in machine learning. In the literature, fuzzy
logics have been introduced alongside comparative
analyses of their performance in training
tasks
van2022analyzing
. Krieken et
al.
van2025neurosymbolic
have also loosely included them in a
broader scene of neurosymbolic methods in terms of their assumptions
of independence of different concepts. More recently, Flinkow et
al.
flinkow2025comparing
conducted a comprehensive evaluation
of various
differentiable logic
s---including all logics considered in the present
work except
STL
ν
---employing a neural network verifier; although the

differentiable logic
s’ semantics differ slightly, their evaluation provides
information about the performance of
differentiable logic
s in training.

Conclusions and future work

We have presented a complete
Rocq
formalisation of a range of
existing
differentiable logic
s, making the following three main contributions:

1.

We provided a
uniform formalisation
of algebraic,
analytic, and proof-theoretic results for
differentiable logic
s that span a wide
range of substructural logic and machine learning papers, published
by different communities in the last 20 years. We showed that
unification of these three aspects is crucial for adequate
understanding and future design of logics for machine
learning. Moreover, we showed that such a unifying approach is
possible.

2.

We contribute to the
differentiable logic
community by
revisiting
semantics of STL
ν
and DL2
in a way more amenable to formal
verification. We found and fixed errors in the literature.
Indeed, our previous attempt
ldl
to do this with pen and
paper proofs was drowned in low-level case analysis and resulted in
some errors, see Section
4.3
. This complexity was our
initial motivation to undertake the formalisation.
We moreover presented a new sequent calculus for
DL2
that could
be derived thanks to our general unifying approach
to
differentiable logic
s.

3.

We proposed a
general formalisation strategy
to
differentiable logic
s based on
dependent types and formal mathematics. The proposed formalisation
is built to be easily extendable for future studies of different

differentiable logic
s.

Future work

We plan to consider other definitions of soundness, and other
differentiable logic
s,
including
STL
ν

The trade-off between idempotence, associativity, and shadow-lifting

Establishing connection of this work with the logics of Lawvere

Additionally, we have several possible improvements to the formalisation itself

Firstly, we could generalise our formalisation by introducing
a structure for residuated lattices instead of specialising to real numbers.
Furthermore, we believe that the use of multisets

better support when coupled with appropriate automation.
Namely, due to lack of exchange rules in calculi modelled on

however require substantial effort to match the level of support
enjoyed with polymorphic lists.

We leave the formalisation of completeness of the calculi, as well as

Separately from the questions of scientific curiosity and mathematical elegance,
there is a question of lacking programming language support for machine
learning.
As tools like Vehicle
DaggittKAKS025
and
CAISAR
girardsatabin2022caisar

are being proposed to provide a more principled
approach to verification of machine learning, in the long term, compilers of
these new emerging languages will require certification. And this, in turn,
will demand formalisation
of results such as the ones we presented here. The formalisation of
differentiable logic
s
would hence directly contribute to
certified compilation of specification languages to machine learning
libraries.

Finally, first-order quantification for quantitative logics generally and

differentiable logic
s in particular, has been a long-standing open problem, discussed in our
work

;
abs-2406-04936

Thanks to accumulated knowledge of these logics, we stand very close to its
resolution.
