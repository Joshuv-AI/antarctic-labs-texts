Larvicidal activity of Hippocratea excelsa extracts against larvae: Aedes aegypti, Culex quinquefasciatus, and Anopheles albimanus (Diptera: Culicidae)
Authors: Joel Daniel Castañeda-Espinoza, Juan Sánchez-Arriaga, Armando Valdez-Ramírez, Juan Ramírez-Zamora, Olivia Pérez-Valera, Guillermo Delgado, Gabriel Flores-Franco, Rodolfo Figueroa-Brito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the larvicidal activity of hexanic, acetonic, and methanolic extracts obtained from the fruits-seeds and stems of Hippocratea excelsa against larvae of Aedes aegypti, Culex quinquefasciatus, and Anopheles albimanus. Acute toxicity bioassays were conducted under in vitro conditions using a completely randomized design. Major chemical compounds were identified by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The results showed that the hexanic extracts from the stem and fruit–seed at concentrations of 20–200 ppm caused 100% larval mortality in all three mosquito species after 12 h of exposure. The hexanic stem extract showed LC₅₀ values of 4, 4, and 3 ppm at 12 h for Ae. aegypti, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and An. albimanus, respectively. Similarly, the hexanic fruit–seed extract showed LC₅₀ values of 3.7, 3.7, and 3.4 ppm at 12 h for the same species. GC–MS analysis of the hexane stem extract identified hexadecanoic acid ethyl ester (1), 5-cyclohexyl-1-pentene (2), 2-methyloctacosane (3), eicosane (4), squalene (5), β-amyrone (6), and friedelan-3-one (7). These findings suggest that hexanic extracts from the stem and fruit–seeds of H. excelsa possess larvicidal potential for the control of Ae. aegypti, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and An. albimanus larvae.

Introduction

Mosquitoes are considered important vectors due to their hematophagous nature; by feeding on the blood of infected human or animal hosts, they acquire and transmit various pathogenic microorganisms (Du et al., 2019). With each bite or blood meal, these insects can spread infectious agents to new hosts, making them key factors in disease transmission. An infected mosquito can maintain its ability to transmit pathoghout throughout its life (World Health Organization (WHO), 2020; Du et al., 2019).

The mosquito species Aedes aegypti Linnaeus, Culex quinquefasciatus Say, and Anopheles albimanus Wiedemann (Diptera: Culicidae) are recognized as important vectors responsible for transmission of various infectious agents to humans. Diseases associated with these species include dengue, yellow fever, chikungunya, zika, malaria, and filariasis, all of which are of great public health relevance (Boateng et al., 2025; World Health Organization (WHO), 2020; Du et al., 2019).

The use of synthetic insecticides is currently the primary method of mosquito control worldwide. However, their prolonged and intensive application—often involving higher dosages and increased frequency—has led to the development of resistance in mosquito populations (Nurul-Nastasea et al., 2023; van den Berg et al., 2021). The development of resistance to these insecticides poses a significant threat to control efforts and to the prevention of vector-borne diseases (Asgarian et al., 2023).

Moreover, synthetic insecticides present serious risks to human health, as their use has been associated with various cancers, diabetes mellitus, respiratory and neurological disorders, reproductive system dysfunctions, and oxidative stress (Rani et al., 2021).

Given this scenario, scientific efforts have focused on identifying effective alternatives that offer sustainable solutions for managing medically important insects (Shroff et al., 2021). Among these alternatives, the development of plant-based compounds with larvicidal properties stands out. In addition to being effective against mosquitoes, the compounds offer advantages such as being economical, environmentally friendly, biodegradable, and safe for non-target organisms (Senthil-Nathan, 2020). For this reason, botanical insecticides have emerged as viable alternatives for mosquito management (Isman, 2015).

For instance, the hexanic extract of Hippocratea excelsa Kunth leaves and the ethyl acetate extract of Hippocratea celastroides Kunth (Celastraceae) leaves have demonstrated toxicity against fourth-instar larvae of Ae. aegypti (Ruiz-Guerrero et al., 2015). Similarly, the ethanolic extract of Hippocratea africana Willd. (Celastraceae) leaves have shown high toxicity against fourth-instar larvae of Anopheles gambiae Giles (Diptera: Culicidae) (Oboho et al., 2023).

Furthermore, the ethyl acetate extract of Padina gymnospora Kützing (Phaeophyceae) has been reported to induce high mortality in Ae. aegypti, with an LC50 of 27.0 μg/mL (Aravinth et al., 2024). It has also been reported that plant extracts may reduce the activity of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and carboxylesterases in mosquito larvae, representing a potential larvicidal mechanism of action (Ragavendran et al., 2023).

In P. gymnospora, the saturated fatty acid hexanoic acid has been identified; it has also been reported in species of the genus Hippocratea. Additionally, hexadecanoic acid (palmitic acid) may interfere with larval cuticle permeability, promoting both dehydration and the penetration of toxic compounds. This effect has been associated with the biological activity of fatty acids in insects, which can disrupt cellular membrane integrity and affect essential enzymatic systems, such as acetylcholinesterase (Rahuman et al., 2008; Perumalsamy et al., 2015).

Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the toxic effects of H. excelsa extracts of varying polarity on third-instar larvae of Ae. aegypti, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and An. albimanus.

Materials and methods

Mosquito rearing

This study was conducted at the Entomology Laboratory of the Centro de Desarrollo de Productos Bióticos, Instituto Politécnico Nacional (Ceprobi-IPN), located at km 6 of the Yautepec-Jojutla highway, San Isidro, Yautepec, Morelos, México (18°49′28”N, 99°05′46”W; 1056 m). Eggs of Ae. aegypti and An. albimanus were provided by the insectary of the National Institute of Public Health (Chamilpa, Cuernavaca, Morelos), while Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae were obtained from the Vector Department, Central District, Iguala, Guerrero, México. Eggs were placed in plastic containers (15 × 26 × 5 cm), and upon hatching, larvae were fed fish food (Wardley®). Larvae were separated according to their stage to prevent predation among them. Adult mosquitoes were maintained under controlled conditions of 60–80% relative humidity, 25 ± 1 °C, and a 12:12 h light/dark photoperiod. They were fed sucrose solutions in soaked cotton (8% for An. albimanus and Cx. quinquefasciatus, 10% for Ae. aegypti), followed by heparinized rabbit blood (15 μL/mL) for 3–5 days using a glass artificial feeder with a Parafilm-M® membrane to induce egg production. The F1 generation of each species was used in bioassays (Dias et al., 2018; World Health Organization (WHO), 2005).

Plant material fruit-seeds and stems of H. excelsa

Were collected in november 2024 from El Naranjo, Iguala, Guerrero, México (18°24′11.87”N, 99°31′59.5”W). Voucher specimens were identified by M. Sc. Gabriel Flores Franco and deposited in the HUMO herbarium at the Centro de Investigación en Biodiversidad y Conservación, Universidad Autónoma del Estado de Morelos, México, under voucher number 32942.

Preparation of plant extracts

Plant material was shade-dried at temperature for 15 days and ground using an IKA® electric mill (MF 10 basic microfine grinder drive; Wilmington, NC, USA). Samples (500 g of fruit-seeds and stems) were macerated in 4 L Erlenmeyer flasks with solvents of increasing polarity (hexane H1030–17, acetone A0425–17, and methanol MG125–18; Jalmek®, reagent grade) for 72 h, in triplicate. Solvents were removed under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (Rotovapor® R-3, Büchi®, Switzerland) under the following conditions: 100 mbar at 55 °C for hexane, 540 mbar at 44 °C for acetone, and 320 mbar at 50 °C for methanol, yielding 5%, 7%, and 8% (w/w), respectively. The extracts were subsequently dried in a laminar flow hood and stored at 6 ± 2 °C until use in the bioassays.

GC–MS analysis

Hexanic extracts of H. excelsa stems were analyzed at the Laboratorio de Espectrometría de Masas, Instituto de Química, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México. The analyses were performed using an Agilent Technologies 7890 A gas chromatograph coupled to a mass spectrometer, equipped with an HP-5 capillary column (5% diphenyl/95% dimethylpolysiloxane), 30 m length, 0.32 mm internal diameter, and 0.25 μm film thickness. Helium was used as the carrier gas at 1 mL/min. Injector temperature was 300 °C, with a 1 μL injection volume. The oven temperature was programmed from 40 °C (1 min hold) to 300 °C at a rate of 8 °C/min. Extracts were dissolved in hexane at 1 mg/mL and injected in split mode (50:1). The Jeol GC Mate II mass spectrometer operated with electron ionization at 70 eV, scanning from m/z 10 to 600 every 1.3 s. The identification of the substances was made by comparison with data base of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST).

Acute toxicity bioassays

Experiments were conducted using 25 third-instar larvae of Cx. quinquefasciatus, Ae. aegypti, and An. albimanus per glass container, each considered an experimental unit. Treatments were replicated three times. The chemical insecticide Abate® (temephos, 1 mg/250 mL water; 4 ppm) was used as a positive control. The solvents hexane, acetone and methanol (1 mL/250 mL water) were used as negative controls. The stock solutions were prepared by dissolving 300 mg/10 ml of the corresponding solvent (hexane, acetone or methanol), according to the bioassay, to obtain a final concentration of 30,000 ppm. Throughout the experiment, all larvae were fed fish food (Wardley®) (Dias et al., 2018). Larval mortality was recorded at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h (World Health Organization (WHO), 2005).

Statistical analysis data

Dates were assessed for tested normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homoscedasticity using Bartlett's test. Differences among treatments were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test (p < 0.05) in Prism 10 (Graph Pad® Inc., USA). Probit analyses were conducted to estimate LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values using JMP® software version 11 (SAS Institute, Inc., 2012).

In these same hexane extracts of stem and fruit–seed, evaluated at 10 ppm, mortality increased in a time-dependent manner, with the highest values recorded at 24, 48, and 72 h. The hexanic extract produced the highest mortality in Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae, showing statistically significant differences relative to the acetonic and methanolic extracts, however, no significant differences were found between the latter two (F = 46.88; df = 3; p ≤ 0.0001). In Ae. aegypti, the hexanic extract was again the most toxic, showing significant differences among all solvent types (F = 124.52; df = 3; p ≤ 0.0001). Discussion

The larvicidal activities of the three H. excelsa extracts increased with increasing concentration and exposure time. Among them, the low-polarity (hexanic) extracts obtained from the stem and fruit-seeds of H. excelsa demonstrated exceptional larvicidal activity, achieving 100% mortality in L3 larvae of Culex quinquefasciatus, Aedes aegypti, and Anopheles albimanus within 12 h of exposure. Although acetonic and methanolic extracts showed a slower mode of acting, they produced more than 50% mortality between 24 and 48 h, and the methanolic extract reached 100% mortality in all species by 72 h.

The hexanic extract also exhibited the greatest potency, with LC₅₀ values ranging from 3 to 4 ppm, indicating significantly higher efficacy than that previously reported for leaf extracts of H. excelsa against Ae. aegypti (LC₅₀ = 610 ppm; Ruiz-Guerrero et al., 2015).

Variations in larvicidal performance among different studies may be attributed to differences in the biosynthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites, which are influenced by genetic, ontogenic, morphogenetic, and environmental factors (Verma and Shukla, 2015).

In the present study, the hexanic extract from fruit-seed showed remarkable activity (LC₅₀ = 3.7 ppm against Ae. aegypti), surpassing extracts from other Celastraceae species, such as hexanic extract of Maytenus senegalensis (Lam.) Excell (LC₅₀ = 164.53 ppm; Channi and Biradar, 2023) and the ethanolic leaf extract of Maytenus guianensis Klotzsch ex Reissek (LC₅₀ = 11.3 ppm; Martins et al., 2021) against Cx. quinquefasciatus and Ae. aegypti, respectively. These comparisons highlight the relatively high potency of H. excelsa extracts.

In addition, pristimerine and tingenone have been identified in the root bark of H. excelsa as the most active antigiardial metabolites, with IC₅₀ values of 0.11 and 0.74 μM, respectively (Mena-Rejón et al., 2007). Evidence from other Celastraceae taxa within the family supports the hypothesis that phytochemical compounds bioactive terpenoids and alkaloids are responsible for mosquito larval toxicity.

Phenolic compounds, such as tannins and flavonoids, are known to possess insecticidal properties and can act as mitochondrial poisons in insect vectors. The methanolic leaf extract of Hippocratea africana Willd caused 85% mortality in Anopheles gambiae larvae (Oboho et al., 2023). Similarly, in China, several newer commercial insecticides contain active ingredients based on dihydroagarofuran sesquiterpenes and celangulin extracted from Celastrus angulatus Maxim, which is recognized as a commercial botanical insecticide with larvicidal activity (Isman, 2020). In Maytenus oblongata Reisseek, sesquiterpene alkaloids such as 1-benzoyl-4-deoxy-alatamine and 1,2-dibenzoyl-4-deoxy-alatamine have shown activity against Ae. aegypti (Touré et al., 2016).

Phytochemical analysis of the hexanic extract from stem of H. excelsa led to the identification of the triterpenoid's squalene, friedelan-3-one, and β-amyrone. Similar terpenoids including squalene (5) and friedelan-3-one (7), have been reported in Tontelea micrantha (Mart. Ex Schult.), whose extracts exhibit both larvicidal activity and activity against the zika virus (Ferreira et al., 2019). Squalene (5) has also been eicosene (4) proposed as a candidate scaffold for design of new antiviral compounds against dengue viruses (Chandrasekaran et al., 2019). Asimismo, β-Amyrone (6) mediante viral presento actividad inhibitoria moderad frente la replicación del virus de la chikungunya (CHIKV) (Bhakat and Soliman, 2015).

Additionally, bioactive compounds identified in spore extracts of Aspergillus fumigatus, including squalene (5), 3-eicosene, and 9-eicosene, have demonstrated potential as chemical control agents against mosquito vectors, likely through disruption of physiological processes and membrane integrity in immature stages (Kabir et al., 2024). Similarly, the essential oil of Coccinia grandis has been reported to exhibit significant larvicidal activity against Ae. aegypti, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and Anopheles stephensi, an effect primarily attributed to eicosane, which may act either independently or via synergistic interactions with other lipophilic constituents in the mixture, thereby enhancing toxicity and interfering with key metabolic pathways in larvae (Mohammed et al., 2017).

Furthermore, n-hexadecanoic acid was identified in the ethyl acetate extract obtained from the mycelium of Aspergillus terreus. This compound has been reported to exhibit significant mosquitocidal. Activity, particularly when present in combination with other metabolites, likely due to synergistic interactions, against Ae. aegypti, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and An. stephensi (Chinnasamy-Ragavendran and Devarajan-Natarajan, 2015).

In our study, the hexanic extracts from stem and fruit-seed of H. excelsa were highly toxic to all three mosquito species evaluated, including Ae. aegypti, the primary vector of dengue and zika viruses, such as Cx. quinquefasciatus and An. albimanus, which transmit filariasis and malaria, respectively. These results position H. excelsa as a promising source of natural bioinsecticides. Its strong larvicidal activity, combined with biodegradability and lower environmental impact, underscores its potential for integration into vector management programs as a sustainable alternative to conventional synthetic insecticides.

Conclusion

Hexanic extracts from the stem and seed–fruit of Hippocratea excelsa showed strong larvicidal activity, achieving 100% mortality against Aedes aegypti, Culex quinquefasciatus, and Anopheles albimanus within 12 h of exposure, thus fulfilling the main objective of this study. These findings identify H. excelsa as a promising botanical source for mosquito control.

The results suggest potential application in reducing vector populations and disease transmission as an alternative to synthetic insecticides. However, further studies are required to validate these effects under field conditions, evaluate non-target impacts, and determine the active compounds and mechanisms responsible for the observed activity.
