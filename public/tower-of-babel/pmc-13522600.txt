Cis glycan–glycan interactions organize membrane nanodomains that tune receptor signaling
Authors: Kenichi G N Suzuki, Naoko Komura, Sachi Asano, Maina Takahashi, Eriko Yamaguchi, Ayano Yamazaki, Akihiro Imamura, Shusaku Shibutani, Rahul Chadda, Taka A Tsunoyama, Takahiro K Fujiwara, Koichiro M Hirosawa, Kenichi Morigaki, Koichi Furukawa, Keiko Furukawa, Yoshio Yamauchi, Yukichi Kitamura, Masataka Nagaoka, Hiromune Ando, Akihiro Kusumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe plasma membrane (PM) is generally viewed as organized by coordinated interactions among proteins and lipids, largely overlooking the cell’s most extensive surface chemistry, the glycan layer. Here, we directly detect specific cis glycan–glycan interactions using single-molecule imaging of 39 chemically synthesized fluorescent ganglioside analogs in living cells and defined lipid bilayers. All gangliosides examined form transient, metastable homodimers via homophilic glycan interactions, which are stabilized by cholesterol to generate nanoscale ganglioside homodimer rafts. They represent fundamental organizing units underlying PM nano-heterogeneity. Using EGF receptor (EGFR) as a representative receptor, we show that the interactions of paired GM3 glycans in the GM3 homodimer raft with defined N-linked glycans on EGFR suppress ligand-independent EGFR dimerization by reducing the dimer formation rate and enhancing dissociation, and continue to slow dimerization after EGF stimulation. Our findings establish cis glycan–glycan interactions as an organizing principle of PM organization and receptor regulation.Subject terms: Glycobiology, Growth factor signalling, NMR spectroscopy, Single-molecule biophysics

Introduction

The organization and signaling functions of the plasma membrane (PM) have classically been understood as emerging from protein–protein interactions whose kinetics and outcomes are modulated by the surrounding lipid environment, including nanoscale cholesterol-enriched raft domains. At the PM, protein–protein interactions can occur as simple one-to-one encounters; however, in many signaling pathways, receptors and downstream signaling molecules are incorporated into multivalent interaction networks by adaptor and scaffold proteins that ensure signaling specificity, kinetics, and pathway insulation1–6.

These protein networks operate with a spatially structured membrane environment shaped by actin-based compartmentalization and lipid-based heterogeneity, including cholesterol-dependent nano-raft domains, which provide additional, often modulatory, layers of regulation7–9. However, beyond proteins and lipids, the cell surface carries a dense and chemically diverse glycan layer, whose lateral actions remain far less mechanistically defined.

A limited number of studies have suggested that gangliosides can influence receptor activity through glycan-dependent mechanisms acting within the same PM10–14, raising the possibility that these glycans acting within the same PM might constitute an additional physical layer of the PM, capable of regulating its molecular organization and receptor signaling alongside proteins and lipids. Nevertheless, the questions of whether specific cis glycan–glycan interactions exist in living cell membranes, and whether such interactions can function as an organizing principle for PM nano-organization and signaling, have remained unanswered. This motivated us to undertake a quantitative, molecule-resolved investigation of cis glycan–glycan interactions at the PM and their functional consequences for receptor behavior. In this study, cis glycan–glycan interactions refer to intermolecular lateral interactions between glycans on membrane components within the same plasma membrane, rather than intramolecular interaction or interactions between apposing membranes or cells.

Among membrane-associated glycans, those carried by gangliosides—glycosphingolipids containing one or more sialic acids—constitute a major fraction. Gangliosides account for 0.3–4 mol% of total lipid in most cell types and reach up to approximately 37% in neurons, thereby contributing a substantial fraction of cell-surface glycans15,16. Ganglioside glycans participate in diverse trans-cellular interactions in physiological and pathological contexts, including cancer17, Parkinson disease10, virus and toxin entry18–20, bacterial pathogenesis21,22, receptor endocytosis23,24, and immune responses11. In contrast, whether and how ganglioside glycans act laterally within the same PM remains poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear whether ganglioside glycans interact with one another, with glycans on glycoproteins, and/or with amino acid residues of membrane proteins in cis.

Carbohydrate–carbohydrate interactions have long been proposed as biologically relevant modes of molecular recognition. However, such interactions are intrinsically weak and transient, rendering them difficult to detect directly in the crowded and dynamic environment of the live-cell PM. As a result, the glycan-dependent regulation of membrane organization and signaling has largely remained phenomenological, lacking a defined physical organizing principle.

Here, we use a quantitative, molecule-resolved approach to test whether specific cis glycan–glycan interactions can act as an organizing principle of PM nano-organization and signaling. We chemically synthesize 39 fluorescent ganglioside analogs representing the major ganglioside families (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) and analyze their behavior in living-cell PMs and artificial planar lipid bilayers (PLBs), using single-molecule imaging25–27. We show that gangliosides form transient homodimers through specific cis-glycan-glycan interactions and that cholesterol stabilizes these homodimers to generate nanoscale ganglioside homodimer rafts. Using the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) as a representative and biologically critical membrane receptor, we further show that paired GM3 glycans interact with defined N-linked glycans on EGFR and thereby tune EGFR dimerization and activation. These findings identify cis glycan-glycan interactions as an organizing principle of PM nano-organization and receptor regulation.

Results

Ganglioside analogs undergo transient homo-colocalization and codiffusion

All chemically synthesized fluorescent ganglioside analogs faithfully reproduced the partitioning behaviors of their native counterparts, localizing to cold-detergent resistant membrane (DRM) fractions (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and to liquid-ordered (Lo)-like domains in giant plasma membrane vesicles (GPMVs) (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The only exception was GM3-C18:1, a non-physiological, non-raftophilic analog of GM3.

Using single-molecule imaging, we tracked ATTO594-conjugated GM3 molecules (594-GM3; Supplementary Fig. 1a) in the bottom PM of GM3-null cells derived from CHO-K1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4). Virtually all 594-GM3 molecules diffused in the PM (Supplementary Fig. 5), and in addition, they frequently underwent transient homo-colocalization during diffusion, followed by separation (Fig. 1b–d and Supplementary Movie 1). Such events, in which two molecules remain colocalized while diffusing together, are hereafter referred to as homo-codiffusion.

The duration histogram of all detected homo-colocalization-codiffusion events could be fitted with a single exponential function26,27, yielding a colocalization lifetime (τCol) of 186 ± 11 ms (Fig. 1d). This measurement was further validated by enhancing the temporal resolution to 8 ms (125 frames/s; approximately 4×-enhanced from video rate; Fig. 1d; see Methods), which provides more accurate estimates of τCol (see Methods). Based on this validation, for practical purposes, video-rate imaging was employed for subsequent τCol measurements throughout the study.

To examine whether homo-colocalization reflects intrinsic interactions among gangliosides, we analyzed the behaviors of GM3 and a variety of other gangliosides in a simplified artificial membrane system, specifically a Lo-phase planar lipid bilayer (PLB) composed solely of saturated phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol (in a 65:35 mole ratio). Lo-phase membranes were employed because gangliosides are known to associate with raft-like domains preferentially25,28,29. (Ganglioside-associated raft-like domains are described in detail later in the Results in the subsections Ganglioside homodimers generate raft domains within their proximal domains and Nanoscale ganglioside homodimer rafts.) The τCol of 594-GM3 in Lo-phase PLBs closely matched that observed in the PM (Fig. 2a–e), indicating that 594-GM3 homo-colocalization can arise from interactions among GM3, saturated PC, and cholesterol, without the involvement of other cellular molecules.

Remarkably, all examined ganglioside analogs exhibited transient homo-colocalization in both PMs and Lo-phase PLBs (Fig. 2b, e), suggesting that transient homo-colocalization might be a general property of gangliosides. Among the gangliosides tested, GM3, which possesses the simplest glycan structure examined (Fig. 1a), displayed the longest τCol (186 ms), whereas 594-GM1, a structurally slightly more complex ganglioside, exhibited a shorter τCol of 115 ± 7 ms (Fig. 2b, e and Supplementary Movie 2).

To distinguish true molecular interactions from incidental colocalization events, we defined the molecular binding detected by single-molecule colocalization as events with durations at least twice the lifetime of non-associative molecules. Specifically, the incidental τCol for 594-DOPE, a monomeric, non-raftophilic molecule, was 42 ms; therefore, events lasting ≥84 ms were classified as interaction events (Fig. 1d). This two-fold criterion is an operational threshold based on the exponential decay of incidental colocalization events: for a non-associative control with lifetime τ, only e−2, or 13.5%, of incidental events are expected to last longer than 2τ. Based on this definition, we refer to τCol values meeting this criterion as τCol-dimer. Independent validation of molecular interactions is provided in subsequent subsections.

Finally, several control experiments ruled out the possibility that the observed homodimerization was an artifact due to interactions between fluorescent dyes. Despite the use of the same ATTO594 probe, the τCol-dimer values differed clearly among 594-GM3, GM2, GM1, sphingomyelin (SM), and DOPE (Fig. 2b, e; no significant difference between 594-SM and 594-DOPE). Switching the fluorophore to ATTO488 or changing the dye attachment site, from sialic acid to terminal galactose for GM1 (594-termG6-GM1) or to N-acetylgalactosamine for GM2 (594-termGN6-GM2) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1c), did not alter the τCol-dimer values (Fig. 2b, e). Together, these results indicate that the observed homo-colocalization and homodimerization are intrinsic properties of gangliosides, rather than artifacts of fluorescent labeling. Although vesicle-based solid-state NMR could be valuable for analyzing unlabeled gangliosides, we considered it unsuitable for our purpose because the limited signal-to-noise ratios expected for this approach would make it difficult to discriminate lateral cis interactions within the same membrane from trans interactions between apposing membranes.

Sialylgalactose residues provide the basic affinity for transient homo-colocalization, whereas the specificity arises from higher-order features

We next explored the molecular basis for the homo-colocalizations of GM3, GM1, and GM2, and found that sialic acid and galactose residues provide the basic affinities for these interactions. Asialylated analogs, 594-asialo-GM1, 594-asialo-GM2, and 594-asialo-GM3 ( = 594-LacCer, where Lac = GalGlc and Cer = ceramide; Figs. 1a and 2a) exhibited significantly reduced τCol-dimer values compared with their sialylated counterparts in both PMs and Lo-phase PLBs (Fig. 2b, e). Similar reductions in τCol-dimer durations were observed for globo-series and lacto-series gangliosides: 594-SSEA4 and 594-SA-nLc4Cer showed longer τCol-dimer values than their desialylated analogs, 594-SSEA3 and 594-Lc4Cer, respectively (Figs. 1a and 2b, e).

In addition to sialic acid, galactose residues contribute to homo-colocalization. A LacCer variant lacking galactose (594-GlcCer) exhibited significantly shorter τCol-dimer values than 594-LacCer (594-GalGlcCer) in both PMs and Lo-phase PLBs (Fig. 2b, e). Because the τCol-dimer values did not differ significantly between 594-LacCer molecules labeled at Gal-3 or 4 [594-Gal3- or 594-Gal4-LacCer, respectively] (Fig. 2b, e), subsequent experiments were standardized using the Gal-3-labeled form of 594-LacCer.

The specific contribution of sialylgalactose residues to ganglioside interactions was further supported by competition experiments. The addition of sialylgalactose to the medium markedly reduced the τCol-dimer of 594-GM3, whereas isomaltose addition had a much smaller effect (Fig. 2f; both sialylgalactose and isomaltose were synthesized in this study). Likewise, the presence of lactose (GalGlc) in the medium significantly reduced the τCol-dimer of 594-LacCer ( = 594-GalGlcCer) (Fig. 2f).

These results demonstrate that the sialylgalactose residues provide the basic affinity required for GM3 homodimerization. However, given the ubiquity of sialylgalactose residues across gangliosides, the observed specificities of the homophilic affinities (heterophilic affinities were generally weak; see the next section) likely arise from more complex molecular mechanisms, such as the three-dimensional conformations and dynamics of the glycan chains.

Single-molecule FRET detects metastable homodimers of GM3 and GM1 and less stable GM3-GM1 heterodimers

To directly detect dimerization at the molecular level, we performed single-molecule Förster resonance energy transfer (smFRET) experiments26,30. smFRET occurs when fluorescent probes attached to host molecules approach within a defined proximity, approximately 5.6 nm for the ATTO488 and ATTO594 pair, consistent with dimer formation. In the PM of GM3-null cells (Supplementary Fig. 4), smFRET between 488- and 594-GM3 was detected with a lifetime (τFRET-dimer) of 89 ± 6 ms (Fig. 3a–c). This lifetime is significantly longer than the τFRET-dimer of 31 ± 2 ms for the control pair of GM3 and DOPE, an unsaturated lipid, by a factor of 2.9 (Fig. 3c). GM1 exhibited similar behavior, with a τFRET-dimer of 70 ms (compared with 29 ms between GM1 and DOPE; Fig. 3c).

Interestingly, the τFRET-dimer values (Fig. 3c) were consistently approximately half of the corresponding τCol-dimer values (Fig. 2b, e). This difference likely reflects the fact that smFRET signals are detected only during specific portions of the homo-colocalization events.

Across all examined gangliosides and LacCer, the τFRET-dimer values measured in the Lo-phase PLB were comparable to those observed in cellular PMs (Fig. 3c), supporting the notion that direct homo-interactions drive homodimerization without mediation by third molecules, aside from cholesterol and other raft-associated lipids. The τFRET-dimer for GM3-GM3 is significantly longer than that for GM1-GM1 (Fig. 3c), consistent with the τCol-dimer data (Fig. 2b, e) and with prior molecular dynamics simulation results31.

In contrast, hetero-pairs of GM1-GM3 and GM1-GM2 exhibited substantially shorter τFRET-dimer values than those for homodimers of GM1-GM1 and GM3-GM3, in both the PM and Lo-phase PLBs (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, other GM3 hetero-pairs (GM3-LacCer, GM3-GalNAcGD1a, and GM3-GQ1b) also exhibited significantly shorter τFRET-dimer values compared to GM3-GM3 homodimers in Lo-phase PLBs (Fig. 3c), underscoring the specificity of ganglioside homodimerizations (Fig. 3d). Two exceptions were the GM1-GalNAcGD1a and GM1-GQ1b heterodimers, which exhibited τFRET-dimer values comparable to that of GM1-GM1 homodimers, and significantly longer than those involving GM3 (Fig. 3c). These GM1 heteropairs are targets of autoantibodies in Guillain-Barré syndrome32, consistent with their longer homodimer lifetimes found here, highlighting their clinical relevance.

The removal of sialic acid (asialo-GM3 = LacCer) significantly reduced the τFRET-dimer of GM3-GM3 pairs, yielding the following rank order: GM3−GM3 » D-LacCer−D-LacCer, L-LacCer−L-LacCer » D-LacCer−L-LacCer, D-LacCer−DOPE (control) (Fig. 3c, d, Supplementary Fig. 6a). These findings support a critical role for sialic acid in stabilizing GM3 homodimers. Notably, the τFRET-dimer values for heterodimers formed between optical isomers of LacCer were much shorter than that for the natural D-LacCer homodimer, indicating that precise molecular recognition between two lactose molecules induces LacCer homodimers, consistent with all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulation results (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d).

Chemical groups involved in GM3 homodimerization

We identified four specific interactions at the chemical group level that contribute to GM3−GM3 homodimerization.

(i) In sialic acid, the acetamide group plays a critical role in GM3 homodimerization (AcNH → H: A in Fig. 4a), whereas the carboxy group is dispensable (COOH → CH2OH: B in Fig. 4a) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7a).

(ii) Ca2+ enhances GM3 homodimerization (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Notably, this Ca2+ dependence is not due to the carboxy group in the sialic acid, the sole anionic group in GM3, because Ca2+ depletion significantly shortened the τCol-dimer of 594-hydroxymethyl-GM3 to an extent comparable to that observed for 594-GM3 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). We therefore propose that the Ca2+ dependence arises from Ca2+-mediated interactions among the hydroxy groups of the glycan chains, analogous to the Ca2+-mediated lactose-lactose interactions reported for photo-switchable glycoclusters33.

(iii) The presence of additional sialic acids in the glycan chain tends to destabilize ganglioside homodimers, probably due to the electrostatic repulsion between carboxy groups. Specifically, the homodimer lifetimes were shorter for gangliosides containing two sialic acids (594-GM3 > 594-GD3; 594-termGN6-GM2 > 594-GD2; and 594-termG6-GM1 ≥ 594-GD1b, where ≥ indicates a numerically greater value without statistical significance). Moreover, increasing the number of sialic acids from one to four resulted in progressively shorter lifetimes (594-termG6-GM1 ≥ 594-GD1b > 594-GT1b ≥ 594-GQ1b; see Fig. 1a for chemical structures) (Fig. 2b, e and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

(iv) The sphingosine backbone also contributed to homodimerization. Hydrogenation of the sphingosine backbone, which converts the double bond to a single bond (594-sat-GM3; (D) in Fig. 4a), did not affect τCol-dimer. In contrast, hydroxylation significantly reduced τCol-dimer (594-phyto-GM3 (E); i.e., GM3 with phytosphingosine, a naturally occurring lipid; Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). This reduction is likely attributable to steric hindrance and/or a loss of hydrophobic interactions.

In summary, these results provide evidence that specific and direct cis glycan-glycan interactions, particularly those involving the acetamide group of sialic acid and the Ca2+-mediated interactions among glycan hydroxy groups, play crucial roles in GM3-GM3 homodimerization.

Ganglioside homodimers generate raft domains within their proximal domains

Three key observations indicate that ganglioside homodimers are far more strongly associated with raft domains than monomers: (i) cholesterol depletion using methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) or saponin markedly reduced the τCol-dimer of 594-GM3, whereas cholesterol replenishment restored it (Fig. 4d); (ii) replacement of the C18:0 chain with C18:1 or conjugation to ATTO647N, which blocks raft formation by inducing lipid disorder due to the hydrophobicity of ATTO-647N25, similarly reduced τCol-dimer (Fig. 4d); and (iii) raftophilic molecules were preferentially recruited to ganglioside homodimers: Sphingomyelin and the GPI-anchored protein CD59 were transiently recruited to GM3-GM3 and GM1-GM1 homodimers with dwell lifetimes of 70–90 ms, whereas their dwell lifetimes at GM3 or GM1 monomers were approximately 50 ms, only slightly longer than those of control pairs, approximately 40 ms for DOPE at GM3 or GM1 homodimers (Fig. 4e, f). Such transient but significantly longer recruitment of raftophilic molecules is considered to be an indication of raft-like domain properties25,27,34; in the present case, in the proximal regions surrounding the ganglioside homodimers. Thus, raft association of gangliosides is markedly enhanced upon their homodimerization, although their monomers also weakly associate with raft domains.

These observations show that raft-lipid interactions stabilize GM3 homodimers. However, cis homophilic glycan interactions appear to be the primary driver for GM3 homodimerization. GM3 analogs with longer acyl chains (up to C24:0, encompassing most sphingolipid chains found in mammalian tissues35) formed more stable homodimers, probably because of increased raftophilicity (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Nevertheless, 594-GM3 containing a very short fatty acyl chain, C2:0, still formed homodimers with a τCol-dimer longer than that of glycan-less 594-Cer containing C18:0, 82 ± 5 ms vs 47 ± 1 ms (Fig. 4g). Thus, ganglioside homodimerization is fundamentally driven by homophilic glycan interactions and subsequently stabilized by cholesterol and other raftophilic lipids. We therefore refer to these raft-lipid-stabilized, ganglioside homodimer-induced domains as ganglioside homodimer rafts.

Importantly, ganglioside monomers associate only weakly with raft domains, as shown in the observations described in (iii). Therefore, when a phenomenon is found to involve raft-associated GM3, the involved GM3 is most likely existing in GM3 homodimer rafts.

Nanoscale ganglioside homodimer rafts

The sizes of ganglioside homodimer rafts appear comparable to those of ganglioside dimers themselves, similar to previously reported GPI-anchored protein homodimer rafts26,30. This conclusion is supported by the observation that diffusion coefficient of 594-GM3 was similar to, or even greater than, those of non-raftophilic monomeric molecules such as 594-GM3-C18:1 and 594-DOPE (Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, lipids in micron-scale raft-like Lo domains in Lo/Ld-phase-separated giant unilamellar vesicles and giant PM vesicles diffuse much more slowly than those in Ld domains, by factors of five to ten27,36 and two37, respectively. Thus, neither ganglioside monomers nor homodimers appear to be associated with micron-scale raft domains.

This conclusion is further supported by the PM picket-fence architecture, which partitions the PM into compartments of 30–200 nm (32 nm median in CHO-B1 cells, closely relevant to CHO-K1 cells used here; DOPE hops to an adjacent compartment every 1.0 ms on average8,9,38,39). If ganglioside homodimer rafts were larger than several nanometers in diameter, they would be expected to diffuse substantially more slowly than non-raftophilic monomeric molecules such as 594-GM3-C18:1 and 594-DOPE. Because this was not observed, ganglioside homodimer rafts likely consist of only a few to several cholesterol and other raftophilic molecules, and undergo hop diffusion at rates comparable to 594-GM3-C18:1 and 594-DOPE.

In other words, GM3 monomers, and probably monomers of other ganglioside, can transiently form and/or participate in nanoscale, metastable, cholesterol-containing raft-like domains within the surrounding Ld-type membrane. Upon homodimerization through cis glycan-glycan interactions, paired gangliosides more efficiently generate and/or stabilize slightly longer-lived raft-like nanodomains, likely by locally promoting cholesterol-dependent raft-like lipid organization. We note that Ld-type membrane regions outside raft domains also contain cholesterol, although at lower mole fractions than those found within raft domains.

At higher ganglioside densities (>1.0 copies/µm2), GM1, GM2, and GM3 formed cholesterol-dependent higher-order homo-oligomers (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 8a–d). However, these oligomers exhibited shorter lifetimes than homodimers (Supplementary Fig. 8e and Supplementary Table 12), suggesting that they arise from homodimers and monomers through weaker, dynamic raft-lipid interactions, rather than specific glycan-glycan interactions.

Together, these findings indicate that nanoscale ganglioside homodimer rafts would serve as basic building units for the assembly of greater, functional raft domains driven by cholesterol-mediated raft-lipid interactions. This behavior parallels our previous observations for GPI-anchored proteins, including CD59, Thy1, and DAF8,9,26,30, which form metastable nanoscale homodimer rafts, driven by extracellular homophilic protein-protein interactions and stabilized by cholesterol-mediated raft-lipid interactions. In gangliosides, raft formation is instead driven by homophilic glycan-glycan interactions and then stabilized by cholesterol-mediated raft-lipid interactions. In addition, ganglioside homodimer nanoscale rafts in the PM outer leaflet form independently of long-acyl-chain PS in the inner leaflet (Supplementary Fig. 9).

GM3 homodimer rafts reduce the EGFR dimer fraction by modulating kinetics of dimer formation and dissociation: Results before EGF stimulation

Next, we investigated the role of transient nanoscale GM3 homodimer rafts and cis glycan–glycan interactions in receptor function regulation, using the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) as a representative and biologically critical membrane receptor. Previous studies have shown that GM3 suppresses EGFR dimerization40 and activation13,40,41, but the underlying mechanism has remained unclear. Here, we examined how GM3 modulates EGFR autoactivation prior to EGF ligation and how ligand binding overcomes this inhibition.

To this end, we established two CHO-K1-based cloned cell lines stably expressing low surface densities of Halo7-tagged EGFR (EGFR-Halo7) at approximately 0.3 copies/μm2 (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). The CHO-K1 cell line was selected as the base cell line because it expresses virtually no endogenous EGFR and negligible levels of ErbB242. One cell line is based on intact CHO-K1 cells containing abundant GM3 (thus GM3 homodimers; EGFR-Halo7 expression = 0.31 ± 0.035 copies/μm2), while the other is derived from GM3-null CHO-K1 cells (GM3-null-EGFR cells; EGFR-Halo7 expression = 0.29 ± 0.023 copies/μm2). The GM3-null cell line was extensively used in the ganglioside homodimer raft analyses described in previous subsections. EGFR-Halo7 molecules were labeled with either R110 or TMR at high efficiencies (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c), without impairing receptor function (Supplementary Fig. 10d–f).

In resting cells (before EGF stimulation), single-molecule imaging made three key findings indicating that the GM3 associated with raft domains suppresses the EGFR dimer fraction by stabilizing EGFR monomers and destabilizing EGFR dimers. Under the low EGFR surface densities employed here (approximately 0.3 copies/μm2; Supplementary Fig. 10c), oligomers greater than dimers were rarely observed.

(i) Destabilization of EGFR dimers by GM3 (increased dissociation rate constant, koff).

In CHO-EGFR cells, EGFR-Halo7 molecules intermittently formed transient dimers (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Movie 3) with a τCol-dimer(EGFR) of 330 ± 14 ms, while in GM3-null-EGFR cells, the τCol-dimer(EGFR) was significantly longer (553 ± 35 ms; Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 10g). Reintroducing GM3 (or 594-GM3) into GM3-null-EGFR cells restored τCol-dimer(EGFR) to levels comparable to those observed in CHO-EGFR cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 10g). These results indicate that GM3 in the PM destabilizes EGFR dimers, likely through interactions with nanoscale GM3 homodimer rafts.

The Halo7 tag attached to the EGFR’s C-terminus had minimal effects on EGFR dimer lifetimes, as demonstrated by the comparable τCol-dimer(EGFR) values when EGFR was tagged at the extracellular N-terminus with another tag protein, ACP (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 10g, h). In addition, the very low levels of ErbB2 present in the PM were unlikely to affect the EGFR dimer lifetimes, as indicated by the much shorter colocalization lifetimes of EGFR-mGFP with ErbB2-Halo7 (68 ± 2 and 62 ± 2 ms before and after stimulation, respectively; Fig. 5d) compared with τCol-dimer(EGFR) before stimulation (330 ms).

(ii) Reduction of the EGFR dimer fraction by GM3 (increased EGFR dimer-monomer dissociation constant KD).

The ratio of EGFR dimers to monomers (the copy number ratio of the EGFR protomers existing as dimers vs. monomers) was over 2.5-fold higher in GM3-null-EGFR cells than in CHO-EGFR or GM3-rescued GM3-null-EGFR cells (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Figs. 10c and 11a–d). From these ratios and the independently determined expression levels, the equilibrium dissociation constants for EGFR dimerization (KDs) were calculated, alongside the dissociation rate constant koff (1/τCol-dimer) and the dimer formation rate constant kon (koff/KD) (Fig. 5f). These analyses demonstrate that, in the absence of EGF stimulation, GM3 suppresses the EGFR dimer fraction (increases KD) by stabilizing EGFR monomers (reducing kon) and destabilizing EGFR dimers (increasing koff). This analysis jointly quantified the equilibrium dissociation constant and the dimer formation and dissociation rate constants for EGFR dimerization.

(iii) Increase in the EGFR dimer fraction upon cholesterol depletion.

Cholesterol depletion, which destabilizes GM3 homodimers (Fig. 4d), increased the EGFR dimer/monomer ratio by a factor of 1.8 in CHO-EGFR cells. Cholesterol replenishment reversed this effect, restoring the original dimer/monomer ratio (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 11a, e, f), further supporting a role of GM3 homodimer rafts in moderating EGFR dimerization.

Collectively, these findings i-iii indicate that, in resting cells, the GM3 associated with raft domains, specifically GM3 homodimer rafts, lowers the EGFR dimer fraction by enhancing the EGFR dimer dissociation rate, koff, and suppressing the dimer formation rate, kon. These findings establish the critical role of GM3 in restraining EGFR dimerization and activation prior to external ligand stimulation.

GM3 homodimers, but not monomers, undergo frequent transient interactions with both EGFR monomers and protomers in dimers: Results before EGF stimulation

We examined the direct interactions between GM3 homodimers and non-ligated EGFR monomers/dimers before EGF stimulation. In GM3-null-EGFR cells, as in the GM3-null cells analyzed in Figs. 1–4, we distinguished 594-GM3 homodimers from monomers by examining their trajectories, in the same manner used to discern EGFR monomers from dimers (Fig. 5a–c). In CHO-EGFR cells, owing to the high concentration of endogenous GM3, the detected 594-GM3 fluorescent spots would often represent heterodimers composed of 594-GM3 and endogenous GM3 molecules. We refer to these 594-GM3 spots in CHO-EGFR cells as apparent monomers (GM3-AMs) or apparent homodimers. While individual GM3 homodimers dissociate within 0.2 s (Fig. 2b), the abundant endogenous GM3 would promote the immediate reformation of new homodimers, and therefore, GM3-AMs are expected to be predominantly GM3 homodimers. This expectation is tested in the last paragraph of this subsection.

We made the following two major findings.

(i) Colocalization lifetimes of GM3-AMs with EGFR monomers and dimers are both close to 108 ms: findings in CHO-EGFR cells.

Without EGF stimulation, single-molecule imaging revealed that GM3-AM and EGFR-Halo7 fluorescent spots intermittently and transiently became colocalized and co-diffused in the PM (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Movie 4). The colocalization lifetimes of GM3-AMs with EGFR monomers and dimers were 110 ± 10 and 103 ± 7 ms, respectively, without a significant statistical difference (bottom two bars in Fig. 6c). When EGFR monomers and dimers were not distinguished, the overall colocalization lifetime of GM3-AMs with EGFR was 108 ± 7 ms (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b), consistent with the individual measurements (with EGFR monomers and dimers). Based on this result, we distinguished EGFR monomers and dimers only when necessary.

(ii) GM3 homodimers significantly colocalize with EGFR, whereas GM3 monomers do so to a much lesser extent: findings in GM3-null-EGFR cells.

In GM3-null-EGFR cells, where 594-GM3 homodimers and monomers were distinguished by merging and splitting events in their trajectories (Figs. 1b–d and 6d), GM3 homodimers colocalized with EGFR monomers significantly longer than GM3 monomers did (121 ± 7 vs 81 ± 6 ms; GM3-D and GM3-M for GM3-null cells in Fig. 6c; in this analysis, only EGFR monomers were inspected). Furthermore, among the 594-GM3 fluorescent spots colocalized with EGFR monomer spots, approximately 71% were homodimers and 29% were monomers (83:17 in terms of 594-GM3 molecular copy numbers) (Fig. 6e). These findings highlight the preferential and robust interaction of GM3 homodimers with EGFR, compared to GM3 monomers.

We defined significant ganglioside interactions as those where the colocalization durations exceed twice the incidental colocalization period. To define EGFR-ganglioside interactions, we applied the same criterion used before for ganglioside-ganglioside interactions; namely, colocalization durations exceeding twice the incidental colocalization period. For EGFR-ganglioside interactions, the lifetime of the EGFR-(Cy3)DOPE interaction (51 ms) was used as the negative control, yielding a threshold colocalization lifetime of 102 ms (Fig. 6f). Under this criterion, the colocalization lifetime of GM3 homodimers with EGFR (121 ± 7 ms) exceeded this threshold, indicating a molecular-level interaction, whereas that of GM3 monomers was below this threshold level (81 ± 6 ms; Fig. 6c). These values further explain how the preferential EGFR binding by GM3 dimers as compared with GM3 monomers, 2.4-fold in terms of GM3 fluorescent spots (71:29; Figs. 6e) and 5.0-fold in terms of molecular copy numbers (83:17; Fig. 6e), is achieved: GM3 dimers dissociate from EGFR 1.5x more slowly than GM3 monomers (121 ms/81 ms) and bind to EGFR approximately 1.6x faster than monomers (2.4/1.5).

Moreover, the colocalization lifetime of GM3-AMs with EGFR monomers (108 ms in CHO-EGFR cells, Fig. 6f) was close (p = 0.076) to that of (true) GM3 homodimers with EGFR monomers (121 ± 7 ms; in GM3-null-EGFR cells, Fig. 6c), but substantially longer (p < 0.001) than that of GM3 monomers with EGFR monomers (81 ± 6 ms, Fig. 6c). This result strongly supports our premise that the GM3-AMs in CHO-EGFR cells are predominantly GM3 homodimers (and likely also in the parental CHO-K1 cells). Given that GM3-AMs’ colocalization lifetimes with EGFR monomers and dimers are very similar (110 ± 10 and 103 ± 7 ms, Fig. 6c), we conclude that GM3 homodimers undergo frequent and transient interactions with EGFR monomers and EGFR protomers within dimers equally well; i.e., GM3 homodimers interact with one EGFR protomer in a dimer as effectively as with an EGFR monomer.

Divalent binding of two GM3 glycan chains in GM3 homodimer rafts to EGFR is responsible for GM3-EGFR interactions

Since transient GM3 homodimers are associated with nanoscale raft domains, we examined the involvement of raft domains in GM3-EGFR interactions. Various treatments that disrupt raft domains and reduce GM3 association with raft domains, including cholesterol depletion, substitution of GM3’s saturated acyl chain (C18:0) with an unsaturated chain (C18:1), or conjugation of hydrophobic ATTO647N to the sialic acid of GM3 (Fig. 4d), destabilized the EGFR-GM3 interactions (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 12b, c). These results indicate that the nanoscale raft domains associated with GM3 homodimers, rather than GM3 monomers, contribute to stabilizing the GM3 homodimer’s interactions with EGFR, likely by enabling the divalent binding of two GM3 glycan chains in GM3 homodimer rafts to EGFR.

Single-molecule imaging further revealed that, except for GD3 and GM3, other gangliosides (GM1, GM2, GalNAcGD1a, GD1b, GT1b, and GQ1b) as well as LacCer underwent negligible interactions with EGFR (Fig. 6f). The interaction lifetime of GD3 homodimers with EGFR is similar to that of GM3-AMs in CHO-EGFR cells (fourth and second bars from the bottom, respectively, in Fig. 6c) and is longer than that of GD3 monomers (fourth and third bars from the bottom in Fig. 6c). In contrast, GM1 and GM2 homodimers, like their monomers, exhibited no significant interactions with EGFR (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 13a–c).

The inability of LacCer to bind to EGFR underscores the importance of GM3’s sialic acid for the EGFR interaction. However, the inability of other gangliosides to bind EGFR suggests that the overall glycan structure may be critical. Our results largely rule out the possibility of dye-induced artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 12a; for detailed explanations, see its caption).

Interestingly, despite the EGFR interaction with the GM3 glycans in GM3 homodimer rafts, EGFR preferentially partitioned into non-raft domains (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 14). This result suggests that the GM3-EGFR linkage might occur at the interface between raft and non-raft domains.

To further substantiate these observations, we developed a simple in vitro system consisting of Lo/Ld-phase-separated PLBs (30 °C) containing EGFR. This system additionally corroborated that (i) GM3 homodimers transiently bind to EGFR and interact significantly longer than GM3 monomers, supporting the crucial role of the divalent binding of two GM3 glycan chains in GM3 homodimer rafts to EGFR in enhancing GM3-EGFR interactions, (ii) GM1 hardly binds to EGFR, and (iii) the interaction is mediated by the sialylgalactose residue of the GM3 glycan (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 15; see the caption to Supplementary Fig. 15 for details).

Cis glycan-glycan interactions are responsible for EGFR-GM3 binding

Using three complementary approaches in the absence of EGF ligation, we found that cis glycan-glycan interactions are responsible for EGFR-GM3 binding. Specifically, complex-type N-linked glycans of EGFR at N504 and N579 bind to GM3 glycans on GM3-AMs in CHO-EGFR cells.

(i) Removal of N-glycans impairs GM3 binding.

The treatment with peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F), together with tunicamycin supplementation in the culture medium, demonstrated that the N-glycans of EGFR are essential for GM3 binding (Fig. 6f, i and Supplementary Fig. 16a–c).

(ii) Competitive inhibition by complex-type glycans.

Exogenous application of complex-type glycans that mimic those at N504 and N579 (Supplementary Fig. 16d, e) effectively suppressed the binding of 594-GM3-AMs to EGFR (Fig. 6f, i and Supplementary Fig. 16c).

(iii) Site-specific mutations reduce GM3 interaction lifetimes.

Substitution mutations at N504Q or N579Q, sites normally conjugated with highly-branched tetra-antennary or bi-antennary complex-type glycans, respectively43,44, resulted in shorter GM3 interaction lifetimes (75–80 ms vs 108 ms for wild-type EGFR; Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 17a), whereas mutations at N544 or N599, which bear high-mannose-type glycans43, did not affect GM3 binding. Importantly, the N504Q and N579Q mutants exhibited higher dimer fractions compared to wild-type EGFR, whereas the N544Q and N599Q mutants had unaffected dimer fractions (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 17b–h, i–n for post-EGF stimulation).

Moreover, sialylgalactose supplementation in the medium significantly reduced the GM3-EGFR interaction lifetimes (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 18a) and increased the EGFR dimer fractions and lifetimes compared with isomaltose (Fig. 7c–e and Supplementary Fig. 18b, c). However, because sialylgalactose also suppressed GM3 dimerization (Fig. 2f), these effects might reflect the reduced availability of GM3 homodimer rafts and/or direct competition with GM3 binding to EGFR.

Collectively, these results pinpoint the complex-type N-glycans at N504 and N579 as critical binding sites for GM3 glycans. The specific cis glycan-glycan interactions between GM3 homodimer glycans and EGFR’s complex-type N-glycans at these sites suppress EGFR dimerization and activation, unveiling a regulatory mechanism for EGFR depending on cis glycan-glycan interactions.

After EGF stimulation, GM3 associated with raft domains moderates EGFR activation by slowing EGFR dimer formation

Single-molecule behaviors of EGFR-Halo7 expressed at approximately 0.3 copies/µm2 in the PM were monitored for 30–120 s after EGF addition (100 nM). EGFR exhibited prolonged dimerization, with no dissociation events detected under these observation conditions, indicating highly stable dimer formation (Supplementary Fig. 19a). Concurrently, the fraction of immobile EGFR molecules increased 2.7-fold to 56% in CHO-EGFR cells (Supplementary Fig. 19b).

Further analyses yielded two key findings, highlighting that GM3 homodimer rafts moderate both the dimerization and activation of ligated EGFR.

(i) Moderation of ligated EGFR dimerization by GM3 associated with raft domains (GM3 homodimer rafts).

Following EGF stimulation, EGFR dimer/monomer ratios increased under all conditions examined (compare the darker and lighter bars of similar colors in Fig. 5e, g; also see Supplementary Fig. 11). However, the increases in dimer fractions were significantly greater in GM3-null-EGFR cells and in cholesterol-depleted CHO-EGFR cells than in GM3-rescued GM3-null-EGFR cells and intact or cholesterol-repleted CHO-EGFR cells, respectively. Because the ligated EGFR dimers formed before and during the observations persisted without dissociation until the end of the observed periods, the observed dimer densities do not represent equilibrium states but instead reflect kinetically slowed conditions resulting from reduced monomer densities45. These results indicate that the GM3 molecules associated with raft domains, namely the GM3 homodimer rafts, decelerate ligated EGFR dimerization.

(ii) Reduction of ligated EGFR activation by GM3 associated with raft domains (GM3 homodimer rafts).

EGFR activation was assessed at the level of individual EGFR monomers and dimers (each fluorescent spot) by measuring the recruitment frequency of single Grb2-mGFP molecules to single EGFR-Halo7 fluorescent spots (Supplementary Movie 5). Activation levels were significantly higher in GM3-null-EGFR cells and cholesterol-depleted CHO-EGFR cells than in intact CHO-EGFR cells and GM3-rescued or cholesterol-repleted counterparts (Fig. 7f).

We next examined the EGFR monomer/dimer composition of EGFR fluorescent spots recruiting Grb2. Under our experimental conditions, 83% of the Grb2-mGFP recruitment events occurred at EGFR dimers, while the remaining 17% occurred at monomers (Supplementary Fig. 20). These results suggest that a subset of ligated EGFR monomers might remain unphosphorylated or, alternatively, that phosphorylated EGFR molecules existing as dimers might recruit Grb2 more efficiently than phosphorylated monomers.

Taken together, these findings indicate that the GM3 molecules associated with raft domains, and thus the GM3 homodimer rafts, slow the ligated EGFR dimerization and thereby moderate the ligated EGFR activation, as reflected by the reduced subsequent recruitment of downstream signaling molecules, such as Grb2.

GM3 interacts with ligated EGFR monomers, but hardly with ligated dimers

We then directly observed the interactions of GM3 homodimers with EGFR monomers and dimers at the level of single molecules. Before EGF stimulation, the interaction lifetimes of 594-GM3-AMs with EGFR monomers and dimers in CHO-EGFR cells were comparable (110 ± 10 and 103 ± 7 ms, respectively; difference not statistically significant; Fig. 7g and Supplementary Fig. 21a). In contrast, after EGF stimulation, a clear divergence in these interaction lifetimes emerged: while the interaction lifetimes with ligated EGFR monomers remained comparable (103 ± 5 ms), those with ligated EGFR dimers were significantly shortened to 63 ± 4 ms (Fig. 7g and Supplementary Fig. 21b), approaching the incidental colocalization lifetime (51 ms) and indicating minimal interaction.

These findings lead to an important conclusion: 594-GM3-AMs maintain comparable interactions with ligated EGFR monomers and with non-ligated EGFR monomers and dimers, but undergo minimal interactions with ligated EGFR dimers. This suggests that the conformation of ligated EGFR dimers significantly differs from that of ligated EGFR monomers, as well as from those of non-ligated EGFR monomers and dimers, which might share similar conformations46–51.

Summarizing the GM3 interactions with ligated EGFR molecules, GM3 homodimer rafts slow the dimerization of both ligated and non-ligated EGFR molecules, thereby moderating ligated EGFR activation and reducing the recruitment of downstream signaling molecules. However, once the ligated EGFR molecules form dimers, GM3 is no longer able to destabilize the ligated EGFR dimers.

N-glycans at EGFR’s N504 and N579 remain crucial GM3 interactors after EGF stimulation and decelerate ligand-induced EGFR dimerization

As described earlier, the complex-type N-linked glycans bound to N504 and N579 were essential for the GM3 glycan interactions that suppress EGFR dimerization and activation before EGF stimulation (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 17b–h). After EGF stimulation, the dimer fractions increased for all EGFR variants; however, the increase was significantly greater for the N504Q- and N579Q-mutants than for wild-type EGFR or the N544Q- and N599Q-mutants (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 17b, i–n). These results indicate that the EGFR N-glycans at N504 and N579, which are the primary interactors with GM3 homodimers, also play a crucial role in reducing the dimerization rate constant (kon) of ligated EGFR. The amine group at K642, previously proposed to interact with GM340, was found to be dispensable for GM3-EGFR interactions (Fig. 7a, b and Supplementary Fig. 17h, n).

These observations further suggest that although EGF might preferentially bind to pre-existing EGFR dimers45, under our experimental conditions, EGF also binds to EGFR monomers52, which subsequently form dimers at rates that decrease as the monomer densities decline over time.

Integrating these results with previous experimental and molecular dynamics simulation data46–51, we propose the following model. Prior to EGF binding, the elongated extracellular domain IV of EGFR, which contains N504 and N579, fluctuates between two metastable interchanging orientations relative to the PM51. In one orientation, the long axis of domain IV lies nearly parallel to the PM surface46,49 (Fig. 8 top left and middle; non-ligated EGFR monomer and GM3-accessible non-ligated EGFR dimer). In the alternative, perpendicular orientation, domain IV extends away from the PM, reducing accessibility of the N504/N579 glycans to GM3 and allowing a weak domain II-mediated interaction in the non-ligated EGFR dimer (Fig. 8, top right). Because this domain II interaction is weak in the absence of EGF, the dimer can revert to the GM3-accessible state or dissociate into monomers. Conversely, stabilization of the membrane-proximal, GM3-accessible orientation by GM3 homodimer rafts would disfavor domain II engagement and shift the equilibrium toward EGFR monomers.

A schematic model proposing mechanisms by which GM3 homodimers enhance EGFR monomer fractions both before and after EGF stimulation.

Before EGF stimulation (top), GM3 homodimers reduce the EGFR dimerization rate and increase the dimer dissociation rate. After EGF stimulation (bottom), GM3 homodimers also reduce the ligated EGFR dimerization rate, but cannot enhance the dissociation of ligated EGFR dimers because the GM3 glycans cannot reach the glycans at N504 and N579 located in domain IV of EGFR.

Crucially, in the parallel orientation, the glycans at N504 and N579 are likely accessible for interactions with GM3 homodimer glycans, stabilizing this orientation through cis glycan-glycan interactions and promoting the monomeric state of non-ligated EGFR (Fig. 8, top). This is consistent with previous molecular dynamics simulation results, indicating that, among the four extracellular domains of EGFR, only domain IV can approach the membrane surface closely46,49. After EGF stimulation, GM3 continues to interact with ligated EGFR monomers, thereby slowing their dimerization. However, EGF binding induces strong domain II interactions that force the domain IVs of EGFR dimers into the perpendicular orientation46,53, relocating the glycans at N504 and N579 away from the PM and preventing further GM3 interactions (Fig. 8, bottom). Additional complexity might arise from the potential dimerization of the intracellular tyrosine kinase domains54.

Discussion

This study identifies cis glycan-glycan interactions as a physical layer of PM organization, complementing protein- and lipid-based mechanisms. Ganglioside homodimer rafts represent nanoscale glycan-based organizing units, and GM3-EGFR interactions demonstrate that this organization can directly tune receptor dimerization kinetics.

This behavior is analogous to our previous observations for GPI-anchored proteins, including CD59, Thy1, and DAF8,9,26, which also continually form metastable nanoscale homodimer rafts. Whereas GPI-anchored protein homodimer rafts are driven by specific extracellular homophilic protein-protein interactions, ganglioside homodimer raft formation is driven by specific homophilic cis glycan-glycan interactions. Like GPI-anchored proteins’ transient nanoscale homodimer rafts, gangliosides’ homodimer rafts can further assemble into trimer and tetramer rafts through raft-lipid interactions (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 8). Given the higher abundance of gangliosides and cholesterol in the PM compared to GPI-anchored proteins, the nanoscale ganglioside homodimer rafts are likely to represent a quantitatively dominant and substantial source of membrane nano-heterogeneity.

A central advance of this work is the direct linkage of this glycan-based organizing principle to receptor regulation, exemplified here by EGFR. Previous studies reported that GM3 suppresses EGFR activation and autophosphorylation13,40,41, and biochemical models proposed interactions between GM3 and EGFR N-glycans or basic residues. However, these studies neither directly visualized GM3–EGFR interactions in living-cell PMs nor resolved how glycan-dependent regulation is coupled to EGFR dimerization kinetics and conformational states.

Importantly, the two glycans of the GM3 homodimer in a nanoscale raft, which might be conformationally stabilized by hydrogen bonds55, bind to the complex-type N-linked glycans at EGFR’s N504 and N579. These cis glycan-glycan interactions moderate EGFR dimerization and activation both before and after EGF stimulation, providing a direct mechanistic link between ganglioside nano-organization and receptor signaling.

In the absence of EGF, non-ligated EGFR monomers and dimers are in dynamic equilibrium, and thus we determined the dimer–monomer equilibrium dissociation constant KD, as well as the dimer dissociation and formation rate constants for EGFR dimerization (koff and kon, respectively) (Fig. 5f). The measured KD of 0.89 ± 0.14 copies/µm2 indicates that, at the EGFR expression levels commonly employed in signaling studies (40–400 copies/µm2), EGFR molecules predominantly exist as dimers or higher-order oligomers even in resting cells, consistent with previous observations45,54,56. The cis glycan-glycan interactions between GM3 in GM3 homodimer rafts and EGFR reduce EGFR dimerization by decreasing kon and increasing koff, thereby stabilizing EGFR monomers and suppressing spontaneous EGFR activation. Notably, GM3 homodimers preferentially interact with EGFR compared to GM3 monomers, with approximately five-fold higher binding probability, likely reflecting the divalent nature of GM3 glycans in the nanoscale GM3 homodimer rafts for binding to EGFR glycans at N504 and N579.

After EGF ligation, GM3 homodimer rafts continue to slow EGFR dimerization and activation. However, once ligated EGFR forms a stable dimer, the GM3 glycans can no longer reach the EGFR glycans located in domain IV, and thus can no longer destabilize the ligated EGFR dimer. This ligand-dependent switch in GM3–EGFR interactions demonstrates state-dependent regulation of EGFR signaling by membrane glycans.

Although the individual binding events between GM3 homodimer glycans and EGFR N-glycans are short-lived (between 80 and 125 ms; Fig. 6c, f, h), they occur frequently owing to the abundance and rapid diffusion of GM3 homodimers in the PM (0.37 µm2/s, Supplementary Fig. 5). Consequently, these frequent transient interactions are expected to dampen unwanted ligand-independent EGFR activation and to slow ligand-induced activation kinetics. These mechanisms provide a molecular explanation for previous observations that GM3 suppresses EGFR autophosphorylation13,40,41. Modulating GM3 organization in the PM may therefore represent a potential strategy for tuning EGFR signaling strength, particularly under conditions of aberrant receptor activation57.

Although EGFR is largely excluded from raft domains (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b and Fig. 6g), it can interact with GM3 homodimers through cis glycan–glycan interactions involving highly branched complex-type N-glycans in domain IV. Such interactions could occur at raft/non-raft boundaries, allowing ganglioside homodimer rafts to modulate EGFR function without raft partitioning of EGFR.

Synergistic protein- and lipid-based mechanisms for regulating receptor activation have been reported previously26,58–60. Our findings reveal an additional regulatory layer based on lateral glycan-glycan interactions. Although sialylgalactose residues are essential for GM3 homodimerization, their ubiquitous presence among gangliosides prompts a broader consideration. The specific homophilic affinities observed across all examined gangliosides, along with the generally lower affinities observed in most hetero-pair interactions, suggest that the specific homodimerizations likely arise from the higher-order structural features of glycan chains, such as three-dimensional conformations and dynamics. Elucidating these features across diverse gangliosides remains an important subject for future investigation.

The EGFR N-glycan at N504 is also a known galectin-3 binding site61,62. Thus, galectin-3 and GM3 might compete for binding to this glycan, with galectin-3 promoting EGFR clustering and activation62 and GM3 suppressing EGFR dimerization. Understanding how such competitive glycan-mediated interactions are coordinated in living cells represents an important avenue for future research.

GM3 and EGFR are likely to coexist frequently, particularly in epithelial cells. EGFR is broadly detected in epithelial lineages63, whereas GM3 is widely distributed in mammalian cells and tissues64 and has been described as the predominant ganglioside of epithelial cells65. Tissue analyses further showed GM3 to be dominant in many non-neural organs containing EGFR-relevant epithelial compartments66. Thus, GM3–EGFR co-presence is likely common, although functional coupling should be context-dependent. This principle may extend to other RTKs, because MET, FGFR1/3, and VEGFR2 are active in GM3-containing tissues or cells, and GM3 directly inhibits VEGFR2 signaling67–70.

Together, our results demonstrate that cis glycan–glycan interactions can directly regulate receptor behavior by modulating dimerization kinetics in a ligand- and state-dependent manner. In the case of EGFR, paired GM3 glycans within nanoscale GM3 homodimer rafts interact with specific complex-type N-linked glycans at N504 and N579, stabilizing the monomeric state and suppressing spontaneous EGFR activation in resting cells. Following EGF binding, these glycan-mediated interactions persist for ligated EGFR monomers and continue to slow dimer formation, but are lost once stable ligand-induced EGFR dimers form. This loss reflects a ligand-induced conformational transition of EGFR that renders the receptor refractory to glycan-mediated destabilization, providing a molecular explanation for long-standing observations that GM3 suppresses EGFR autophosphorylation and activation13,40,41. These findings establish membrane glycans as dynamic regulators of receptor activation kinetics.

The broader implications of this work extend beyond EGFR. By establishing specific cis glycan–glycan interactions as a physical mechanism that organizes the PM nanostructure and tunes receptor signaling kinetics, this study identifies membrane glycans as an active regulatory layer operating alongside protein–protein and lipid-mediated mechanisms8,9,26,58–60. Such cis glycan-based regulation may apply broadly to other receptor systems previously implicated in glycan-dependent modulation, including the insulin receptor71 and G-protein–coupled receptors72,73. By providing direct, quantitative, single-molecule evidence in the living-cell PM, our work bridges long-standing concepts in glycobiology10–14,17–22 with modern views of membrane organization and signaling dynamics, and establishes cis glycan–glycan interactions as a fundamental organizing principle of PM function.

Methods

Ethical approval

All experiments complied with relevant institutional and national regulations. This study used established cell lines and did not involve human participants, identifiable human-derived materials, or live animals; therefore, approval by an institutional ethics committee was not required.

Cells for observing ganglioside homodimers

Wild-type CHO-K1 cells (03-402, Dainippon Pharma) were used to examine the homodimerizations of 594-GM2, 594-termGN6-GM2, 594-asialo-GM2, 594-GD1b, 594-GN6-GalNAcGD1a, 594-GD2, 594-GD3, 594-GT1b, 594-GQ1b, 594-SSEA4, 594-SSEA3, 594-GloboH, 594-SA-Lc4, and 594-Lc4 because they do not produce the endogenous counterparts of these fluorescent analogs, as confirmed by flow cytometric measurements (Supplementary Fig. 4). GM3-null CHO-K1 cells, derived from CHO-K1 cells by overexpressing GM2/GD2 synthase (using pM2T1-1 cDNA encoding β1,4N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase) to deplete GM374, were used to examine the homodimerizations of 594-GM3, 488-GM3, 594-Gal3-LacCer, 594-Gal4-LacCer, and 594-GlcCer. The absence of GM3 and LacCer in GM3-null CHO-K1 cells was confirmed by flow cytometric measurements (Supplementary Fig. 4). Wild-type CHO-K1 cells and GM3-null CHO-K1 cells were cultured in HAM’s F12 medium (Sigma-Aldrich; N6658), supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco). For GM3-null CHO-K1 cells, the medium was further supplemented with 400 μg/ml G418.

To examine the homodimerization of 594-GM1, 594-termG6-GM1, 488-GM1, and 594-asialo-GM1, we used B78/M2T1-1 cells, which do not express GM1 or GM3 but contain large amounts of GM2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). This cell line was produced by transfecting B78 cells, a subclone derived from mouse B16 melanoma and originally provided by Dr. Anthony P. Albino (Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, New York, NY, USA), with the pM2T1-1 cDNA encoding GM2/GD2 synthase, which produces GM2 and GD2 from GM3 and GD3, respectively74. B78 cells do not express GM1 because they lack GM1/GA1/GD1b synthase. B78/M2T1-1 cells were provided by K. Furukawa and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Sigma-Aldrich; D5796), supplemented with 7.5% FBS and 300 μg/ml G418. All cell lines used in this study tested negative for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza).

To examine the colocalizations of 488-SM with 594-GM3 and 594-GM1, GM3-null CHO-K1 cells and B78/M2T1-1 cells were used, respectively. To examine the colocalizations of CD59 with 594-GM3 and 594-GM1, GM3-null CHO-K1 cells and B78/M2T1-1 cells were transfected with the cDNA encoding ACP-CD59 by using a 4D-nucleofector (LONZA), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The cells were seeded in glass-base dishes, grown for a day, and then incubated with 2 ng/ml (final concentration) doxycycline for an additional day26. The cDNA plasmid encoding ACP-CD59 was prepared as previously reported26. Briefly, the cDNA sequence of ACP-CD59 was placed in the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-based episomal vector pOsTet15T3, which carries the tetracycline-regulated expression units, the transactivator (rtTA2-M2), and the TetO sequence (a Tet-on vector). ACP-CD59 molecules were covalently labeled with ATTO488-conjugated acetyl-CoA (New England Biolabs) at high efficiencies, as reported previously26.

Cells for observing EGFR-ganglioside interactions

To examine the colocalization lifetimes of EGFRs with the fluorescent analogs of gangliosides and LacCer, CHO-K1 cells and GM3-null CHO-K1 cells were employed. CHO-K1 cells do not express the EGFR family members, except for very low levels of ErbB242, and thus are advantageous for EGFR observations because virtually all the EGFR (EGFR-Halo7 and ACP-EGFR) could be fluorescently labeled. Furthermore, the interaction lifetime of single molecules of EGFR-mGFP with ErbB2-Halo7-TMR in CHO-K1 cells was between 60 and 70 ms (Fig. 5d), which was much shorter than that of two EGFR molecules (330 ms in CHO-K1 cells and 553 ms in GM3-null CHO-K1 cells; Fig. 5c). This result shows that the EGFR-ErbB2 interaction is much weaker than the EGFR-EGFR interaction, indicating that the presence of low levels of ErbB2 in CHO-K1 cells hardly affected our EGFR results.

To investigate the fluorescence intensity distribution of individual 594-GM3 fluorescence spots exhibiting colocalization with EGFR-Halo7 fluorescence spots, GM3-null CHO-K1 cells were used. Since these cells do not express GM3, virtually all the GM3 in the cell would be 594-GM3, and thus the 594-GM3-EGFR interaction would not be masked by the presence of endogenous GM3.

To examine the effect of GM3 on EGFR dimerization, the ratios of EGFR-Halo7 dimers to monomers and the lifetimes of EGFR dimers were estimated in intact, GM3-null, GM3-rescued, and 594-GM3-rescued CHO-K1 cells. GM3-rescued or 594-GM3-rescued CHO-K1 cells were prepared by incubating GM3-null CHO-K1 cells with 100 μM non-labeled GM3 with a C18:0 chain (synthesized in this work), or 100 μM 594-GM3, respectively, in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS; Nissui) buffered with 2 mM PIPES (Dojindo) at pH 7.4 (P-HBSS) at 22 °C for 15 min. The cells were then washed with P-HBSS containing 0.2% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (Wako) to remove the GM3 or 594-GM3 loosely bound to the cell surface13.

Cells were starved for 1 h in the serum-free medium before observation. Immediately before the observation, the culture medium was replaced with P-HBSS. Cells were stimulated with 100 nM human recombinant EGF (PeproTech) on the microscope stage at 37 °C, and images were taken for 30–120 s after stimulation.

To investigate the activation of individual EGFR molecules, the recruitment of single molecules of Grb2 to EGFR monomers and dimers was observed. Intact and GM3-null CHO-K1 cells were co-transfected with the cDNA plasmids encoding EGFR-Halo7 and Grb2-mGFP. The cDNA sequence encoding Grb2-mGFP was ligated into the pEGFP-N1 vector. The Grb2 recruitment index in Fig. 7f was calculated by dividing the total period of colocalization between single Grb2 and EGFR fluorescent spots by the total fluorescence intensities of Grb2 and EGFR spots for the entire movie.

Cells for determining DRM partitioning

To determine the partitioning of fluorescent lipid analogs into the detergent-resistant membrane (DRM) fraction, T24 cells provided by Dr. Masahiro Sokabe (Nagoya University, Nagoya, Japan) were cultured in HAM’s F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, and used as previously reported25,75. Partitioning of EGFR and 594-GM3 before and 120 s after EGF stimulation was examined using wild-type CHO-K1 cells expressing EGFR-Halo7 (CHO-EGFR cells; labeled with R110) and those containing 594-GM3 (see Methods section), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Cells for determining partitioning into Lo vs. Ld phases in GPMVs

To examine the partitioning of fluorescent ganglioside analogs into the Lo-like and Ld-like phases of Lo-Ld-phase separated GPMVs at 10 °C, RBL-2H3 cells provided by Dr. Barbara Baird (Cornell University, New York, USA) were used as previously reported25,75. The cells were maintained in Eagle’s Minimal Essential Medium (Sigma-Aldrich; M4655), supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich; S8636), MEM non-essential amino acids (Wako; 139-15651; diluted 100x), and 15% FBS. Partitioning of EGFR and 594-GM3 into Lo/Ld-like domains in GPMVs before and 120 s after EGF stimulation was examined using CHO-EGFR cells (R110 labeling of EGFR-Halo7), which were then labeled with 594-GM3 as described in the Methods section (Fig. 6g).

PS-depleted/replenished cells

PS-synthesis deficient CHO-K1 cells (PSA-3 cells76), provided by Dr. Osamu Kuge (Kyushu University, Fukuoka, Japan), were used to examine the effect of PS in the PM inner leaflet on the homodimerization of fluorescent ganglioside analogs (Supplementary Fig. 9). The cells carry a mutation in the Psdss1 gene, which encodes phosphatidylserine synthase 1 (PSS 1), and therefore cannot catalyze the conversion of phosphatidylcholine (PC) to PS. Accordingly, the only remaining pathway to synthesize PS in the PSA-3 cells is the pathway catalyzed by PSS II76, which uses phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and serine as substrates. Therefore, to deplete PS, we cultured PSA-3 cells in ethanolamine-depleted HAM’s F12 medium, using dialyzed FBS (dialyzed three times against phosphate-buffered saline for 12 h) to block PE synthesis. According to the published results76, the amounts of PE and PS in the whole PSA-3 cells cultured under these conditions were, respectively, decreased to 64 and 16% of those in the parental CHO-K1 cells. PSA-3 cells after PS replenishment were prepared by culturing the cells in HAM’s F12 medium, supplemented with 10% FBS and 10 mM ethanolamine.

Plasmid constructs

To generate expression vectors for EGFR-mGFP and Grb2-mGFP, the cDNAs encoding human EGFR (isoform 1) and Grb2 (isoform 1) were cloned into XhoI/KpnI-digested pEGFP-N1 and EcoRI/BamHI-digested pEGFP-N1 (Clontech-Takara Bio), respectively. The monomeric GFP mutation (A206K) was induced in the pEGFP-N1 vectors by site-directed mutagenesis. EGFR tagged with Halo7 at the C-terminus (EGFR-Halo7) was generated by inserting the Halo7 cDNA into the KpnI/NotI sites of the EGFR-mGFP vector (mGFP was replaced by Halo7). The linker sequence 5′-CGGGTACCGCGGGATCCATCTGGTGGCGGAGGGAGTGGCGG AGGTGGGTCAGGAGGTGGAGGC-3′ was inserted between the EGFR and Halo7 coding sequences (RVPRDP(SGGGG)x3). EGFR mutants, N504Q, N544Q, N579Q, N599Q, and K642G, each tagged with Halo7 at the C-terminus, were generated via PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 21.

The expression vector for EGFR tagged with ACP at the N-terminus (ACP-EGFR, where the EGFR signal sequence is replaced with the CD8 signal sequence for efficient expression) was generated by PCR using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 21. The pEGFPN1 vector for CD8 signal peptide-ACP-integrin α5, prepared as previously reported77, was used as the template. In the vector, ACP and integrin are fused with the linker sequence 5’-TCTGGTGGCGGAGGGAGTACTAGTTGTACATCTCGAGCTCAAGCTTCGTCA GGAGGCGGTGGCTCAGGAGGCGGTGGCTCAGGAGGCGGTGGCTCCGGTGGTGGAGGCGATATCGGTACC-3’ (SGGGGSTSCTSRAQAS(SGGGG)x4DIGT). The cDNA sequence encoding human EGFR was obtained by PCR, using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 21 and the EGFR-Halo7 expression construct as the template. The cDNA sequence encoding EGFR was cloned into the pEGFPN1 vector, including the signal peptide of CD8, ACP, and the (SGGGG)X5 linker, by NEB builder HiFi DNA assembly master mix.

ErbB2-Halo7 was generated by inserting the human ERBB2 (isoform 1) and Halo7 coding sequences into the NheI/NotI sites of pEGFP-N1 (mGFP was replaced by Halo7). The Halo7 cDNA was amplified from pFN21K (Promega). ErbB2-halo7 contains a 20 amino acid linker (TRSRSSGGGGSGGGGSGGGG), with part generated by PCR before ligation. All constructs were verified by sequencing.

Incorporation of fluorescent lipid analogs in the cellular PM for single-molecule imaging and tracking

The fluorescent ganglioside and lipid analogues used here are imaging tracers rather than target-modulating chemical probes; their structures, purity, characterization, and validation controls are fully reported. Fluorescent analogs of gangliosides, asialo-gangliosides, GlcCer, ceramide, and SM in methanol, and DOPE in benzene, were placed in brown glass vials, and the solvent was evaporated to form a thin lipid film at the bottom of the vial at room temperature. P-HBSS was then added to the vial (final concentration of the lipid analogs = 1 µM) and vigorously mixed using a vortex mixer for 1 min. The resulting lipid suspension was subsequently sonicated with a bath-type sonicator for 2 min. The cells were washed with P-HBSS three times, and then incubated with 1 ml of 2 nM lipid analog suspension at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the lipid analogs incorporated in the basal PM were observed at the level of single molecules by TIRF microscopy.

Transfection of cDNA plasmids and labeling of protein tags

Wild-type CHO-K1 cells and GM3-null CHO-K1 cells were transfected with the cDNA encoding EGFR-Halo7 or ACP-EGFR (125 ng and 750 ng cDNA, respectively) by using a 4D-nucleofector (LONZA), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The cells stably expressing the molecules of interest were selected and cloned, using 800 μg/ml (final concentration) of G418. The transfected cells were seeded in glass-base dishes (35 mm in diameter with a 12 mm diameter glass window, 0.15 mm-thick glass; Iwaki) and cultured for two days before observations.

To fluorescently label EGFR-Halo7, the cells expressing EGFR-Halo7 were incubated with 50 nM R110- or TMR-conjugated Halo ligand (Promega) in HAM’s F12 medium containing 10% FBS for 2 h or 30 min, respectively. Under these conditions, most EGFR-Halo7 molecules were labeled with R110 or TMR (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The R110-Halo ligand was used for estimating the homodimer lifetime of EGFR-Halo7 (τCol-dimer) and the interaction (colocalization) lifetimes of EGFR-Halo7 with ATTO594-conjugated lipid probes. The TMR-Halo ligand was used for observing the colocalization of 488-GM1 or 488-GM3 with EGFR-Halo7 (Supplementary Fig. 12a) and the recruitment of Grb2-mGFP to EGFR-Halo7 (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 20).

ACP-EGFR was labeled with ATTO488 for estimating the colocalization lifetimes of ACP-EGFR with 594-GM3, or with ATTO594 for measuring the homodimer lifetimes of ACP-EGFR (τCol-dimer(EGFR)). The fluorophores were covalently linked to the ACP-tag protein by phosphopantetheine transferase, using ATTO488-CoA or ATTO594-CoA as substrates (50 nM, New England Biolabs; incubation at 37 °C for 30 min). Under these conditions, most ACP-EGFR molecules were labeled with ATTO488-CoA or ATTO594-CoA (Supplementary Fig. 10 h).

Immunostaining of EGFR and EGFR-Halo7

Since the Halo7-tagging of EGFR at its C-terminus might modulate its key function, Tyr1068 phosphorylation, this possibility was examined by immunofluorescence. CHO-K1 cells were transfected with 1 μg of cDNA encoding wild-type EGFR or EGFR-Halo7 by a 4D-nucleofector. The amount of cDNA used here was 8 times more than that used for single-molecule experiments, to compensate for the lower sensitivities of the immunofluorescence method. The transfected cells were seeded in glass-base dishes and cultured for two days before observation. After starvation for 1 h, the cells were stimulated with 100 nM human recombinant EGF (PeproTech) at 37 °C for 120 s and then fixed and immunostained as previously reported47. Briefly, the cells before and after stimulation were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (ICN Biomedicals) for 4 min, and then after blocking with 0.2% bovine serum albumin for 30 min, incubated with mouse monoclonal anti-EGFR antibody (MI-12-1, MBL; 1:200 dilution) and rabbit anti-phospho-EGFR (Tyr1068) antibodies (2234S, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:100 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the cells were incubated with FITC-labeled goat anti-mouse antibodies (55514, Cappel; 2 μg/ml) and rhodamine-labeled goat anti-rabbit antibodies (55666, Cappel; 5 μg/ml) at 22 °C for 2 h. The stained cells were washed, mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and observed with a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 epi-fluorescence microscope equipped with a high-sensitivity CMOS sensor-based camera (Nikon, DS-Qi2).

Evaluation of PS depletion

Cold-detergent insolubility

T24 cells were first incubated with fluorescent ganglioside/glycosphingolipid analogs (1.2 μM) at 22 °C for 15 min, and then chilled on ice-water, washed with pre-chilled P-HBSS three times, and incubated in pre-chilled P-HBSS containing 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) on ice-water (2.8 °C in the medium in the glass-base dish) for 15 min25,75,83,84. The cells were then washed three times with cold P-HBSS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 90 min, and then observed with a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 epi-fluorescence microscope equipped with a high-sensitivity CMOS sensor-based camera (Nikon, DS-Qi2).

Partitioning into Lo/Ld phases in GPMVs

RBL-2H3 cells grown on a glass-base dish were washed twice with 1 ml P-HBSS. For the incorporation of fluorescent ganglioside analogs into the PM, first, 2 µl of 1 mM fluorescent lipid analog and 2 µl of 1 mM BodipyFL-C5-PC (marker for the Ld-like domains25,75,83,84; Invitrogen) in methanol were placed at the bottom of a brown glass vial and air-dried, and then 200 µl P-HBSS was added to the vial (10 µM final concentrations for both probes). After vigorous vortexing for 1 min, the mixture was sonicated for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.1 ml fluorescent lipid suspension was overlaid on the cells in each glass-base dish, followed by an incubation at 22 °C for 12 min25,75,83,84.

After washing the cells with P-HBSS three times, the membrane blebs were induced by incubating the cells in 25 mM paraformaldehyde and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, Nacalai Tesque) in P-HBSS at 37 °C for 1 h85. During this incubation time, numerous blebs were generated and then detached from the cells to form GPMVs. The dish was set on the stage of a home-built TIRF microscope (based on an Olympus IX-83) equipped with a PlanApo 100 × 1.40 NA oil objective, quietly left at 20 °C for 15 min to let the GPMVs settle on the bottom glass, and then cooled to 10 °C (measured on the top surface of the glass in the P-HBSS solution). The specimen temperature was controlled through the objective lens, which was chilled by spirally wound tubing through which a chilled water-ethanol solution (approximately −5 °C) was circulated. Water condensation on the tubing and the objective lens was prevented by the slow flow of dried air around the tubing and objective lens. The specimens were obliquely illuminated simultaneously by 488- and 594-nm laser beams. The focus was set at the equatorial plane of the blebs. Images were simultaneously recorded at video rate with two sCMOS cameras (ORCA-Flash4.0 V2; Hamamatsu Photonics) in two emission channels (520 and 630 nm). Under these conditions, the majority (>90%) of the GPMVs exhibited two coexisting domains (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

All 8-bit images were analyzed using Image J 1.52 (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/). First, the background fluorescence in the image was estimated using the rolling ball algorithm. The radius of the rolling ball was 50 pixels (much greater than the blebs86). This background image was subtracted from the original image, and all further operations were performed on the background-subtracted image. Next, using the freehand selection tool, the regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn around the domains marked by test lipid molecules and control lipid molecules. The Ld-like domains were identified by the signal from BodipyFL-C5-PC, and then the average fluorescence intensity values in the ROIs were obtained. The ratio of the average intensity in the Lo-like domain vs. that in the Ld-like domain was obtained.

Partial cholesterol depletion/replenishment

These treatments were performed as described previously26,87. Briefly, partial depletion of cholesterol in the PM was achieved by incubating the cells in 4 mM methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 30 min or in 200 μg/ml saponin (Sigma-Aldrich) on ice for 15 min. Cholesterol replenishment was performed by incubating the cholesterol-depleted cells in 10 mM MβCD-cholesterol complex (1:1) for 30 min at 37 °C26,87. Cholesterol was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The overall amounts of cholesterol per cell after cholesterol depletion with MβCD and after the subsequent replenishment were 63 ± 4% and 129 ± 7% of the original amount in CHO-K1 cells, as determined by Wako’s Cholesterol E-test kit26.

Preparation of the Lo-phase planar lipid bilayers

DMPC and DPPC were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. Small unilamellar vesicles (approximately 50 nm in diameter), composed of DMPC-cholesterol (65:35 mol/mol) or DPPC-cholesterol (65:35 mol/mol), were prepared with a Liposofast extruder (Avestin). The vesicles of DMPC-cholesterol and DPPC-cholesterol were incubated at 37 °C and 60 °C, respectively, for 10 min on a coverslip cleaned by the previously reported method88, which led to the formation of PLBs on the coverslip. After washing the PLBs on the coverslip with Milli-Q water, 1 nM fluorescent lipid analog suspension in P-HBSS (500 μl) was added. The PLBs on the coverslip were set on the microscope stage at either 20 °C (DMPC-cholesterol bilayer) or 37 °C (DPPC-cholesterol bilayer), so that the whole PLB is in the Lo phase89. Single fluorescent lipid-analog molecules in the PLBs were observed by TIRF microscopy at video rate.

PNGase F treatment to remove N-linked glycans

To observe the interaction of 594-GM3 or GM1 with EGFR-Halo7 without N-glycans, CHO-K1 cells expressing EGFR-Halo7 were cultured in HAM’s F12 medium including tunicamycin (2 μg/ml, specific inhibitor of N-linked glycosylation, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h41. To cleave the remaining N-glycans on the cell surface, the cells were incubated with 10,000 U/ml of peptide N-glycosidase F (PNGase F, New England Biolabs)41, tunicamycin (2 μg/ml), and R110 Halo-ligand in serum-free HAM’s F12 medium at 37 °C for 3 h. After washing with P-HBSS, dual-color single-molecule observations of EGFR-Halo7-R110 and 594-GM3 or 594-GM1 were performed (control: CHO-EGFR cells cultured in normal serum without PNGase F treatment). To confirm the removal of the N-glycans on the cell surface, the CHO-K1 cells before and after PNGase F treatment were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde at 22 °C for 30 min, washed three times, and then incubated with 20 μg/ml biotinylated Datura stramonium lectin (MGC), which recognizes the N-glycan core structure, at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with P-HBSS, the cells were further incubated with 2 μg/ml Dylight488 streptavidin (Invitrogen) at 22 °C for 15 min and then observed with a Nikon ECLIPSE Ts2 epi-fluorescence microscope equipped with a high-sensitivity CMOS sensor-based camera (Nikon, DS-Qi2) (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b).

Single fluorescent-molecule tracking

For single fluorescent-molecule video imaging, CHO-K1, GM3-null CHO-K1, PSA-3, and B78/M2T1-1 cells were sparsely seeded in glass-base dishes (4 × 103 cells on the 12 mm diameter glass window, 0.15 mm-thick glass; Iwaki), and grown for 2 days before each experiment. Single molecules of fluorescent analogs incorporated in the PM were observed in the bottom PM (the PM facing the cover glass) at 37 °C at video rate, using an objective-lens-type TIRF microscope, based on an Olympus IX-70 or IX-83 inverted microscope (100 × 1.49 NA oil objective), equipped with a high-speed gated image intensifier (C9016-02MLG; Hamamatsu Photonics) coupled to an sCMOS camera (ORCA-Flash4.0 V2; Hamamatsu Photonics), as described previously27,90–94. The number densities of the fluorescent lipid analogs in the PM were ≈0.5 copies/μm2. The incident excitation laser powers at the sample plane for single molecule observations with TIRF illumination were 0.36 μW/μm2 for the 488 nm line (for mGFP, ATTO488, and R110, Spectra-Physics: EXLSR-488C-50-CRW), 0.39 μW/μm2 for the 561 nm line (for TMR, Spectra-Physics: EXLSR-561-100-CDRW), 0.43 μW/μm2 for the 594 nm line (for ATTO594, Spectra-Physics: EXLSR-594-50-CDRW), and 0.42 μW/μm2 for the 647 nm line (for ATTO647N, Omicron: LuxX plus647). The precision of the position determinations for single stationary fluorescent probes was estimated from the standard deviations of the determined coordinates of the probes fixed on coverslips. For the R110, ATTO488, ATTO594, TMR, and ATTO647N probes recorded at video rate, the localization precisions of single molecules were ±18, ±17, ±12, ±17, and ±14 nm, respectively. All measurements of the signal intensities of single individual fluorescent spots were performed only for those located within the central region (20 µm in diameter) of the illuminated area, where laser illumination non-uniformities were small. When analyses based on the intensity distributions were conducted, we always performed control experiments using monomer-reference molecules.

For simultaneous two-color single-molecule observations, the images were simultaneously recorded at 37 °C at video rate, using an objective-lens-type TIRF microscope, based on an Olympus IX-83 inverted microscope (100 × 1.49 NA oil objective), equipped with two high-speed gated image intensifiers (C9016-02MLG; Hamamatsu Photonics) coupled to two sCMOS cameras (ORCA-Flash4.0 V2; Hamamatsu Photonics) as described previously27,90–94. The superimposition of images in different colors obtained by two separate cameras was performed as reported previously27,90–94.

In the present research, all of the fluorescent spot identifications and colocalization determinations were performed by in-house computer software. More details have been described previously27,90–94.

Colocalization detection of single molecules

Colocalization of two fluorescent single molecules was defined as the event where two fluorescent spots become localized within 240 nm of each other, as previously described26,90. Even when the examined molecules are bound and located at exactly the same location, the probability of scoring the two molecules as associated is limited by the localization accuracy of each molecule. The probability of scoring the two molecules as associated increases with an increase of the threshold distances between the two molecules.

In single-color, single-molecule imaging experiments, when two fluorescent spots, each representing a single fluorescent molecule, are located close together, the probability that the two spots can be discriminated will increase with an increase in the distance between the two molecules. The threshold distance for differentiating one or two spots in the same color with a probability greater than 99% was found to be 240 nm in the present experimental setup, which is predominantly determined by the image S/N (with small influences of the optical diffraction limit)26,90.

In simultaneous two-color, single fluorescent-molecule tracking experiments, employing the R110/ATTO594, ATTO488/ATTO594, or mGFP/TMR pair, the distance between the two molecules was directly measured from the coordinates (x, y-positions) of each molecule (like PALM/STORM), which can be determined simultaneously and independently for each image in different colors26,90. Even for the case of two-color experiments, where in addition to the single-molecule localization accuracies of the two different-colored molecules, the accuracy of superimposing the images of different colors needs to be taken into account, we found that, for truly associated molecules, the probability of scoring the two molecules as associated increases to >99% when the threshold distance was set to 240 nm, which happens to be the same as that for single-color, single-molecule imaging. Due to this coincidence, in both single-color and simultaneous, two-color, single-molecule imaging, we defined the colocalization of two fluorescent molecules as the event where the two fluorescent spots representing these molecules become localized within 240 nm of each other.

Measurement of colocalization lifetimes using single-molecule imaging

Each time a colocalization event was detected, its duration was measured, and after observing many colocalization events, a histogram of colocalization durations was produced. The histogram was fitted with a single exponential decay function, using nonlinear least-squares fitting by the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, provided in the Origin 2022. The fitting was started from the second bin, since the first bin includes the events lasting for less than 33 ms, which are strongly affected by high-frequency noises and incidental colocalizations. The colocalized durations were then corrected for the photobleaching lifetimes of the probes (10.2 s for ATTO594, 5.8 s for ATTO488, 8.7 s for R110, 9.8 s for TMR, 8.3 s for ATTO647N), based on the equations described previously26,90.

We examined whether the colocalization lifetime was correctly estimated by this method; i.e., video-rate observations and a single exponential decay curve fitting of the colocalization duration histogram. For this purpose, we compared the homo-colocalization lifetime measured at a frame rate of 30 Hz (33 ms time resolution; video rate) with that measured at 125 Hz (8 ms resolution), approximately 4x faster than video rate (which allowed us to perform fitting with the sum of two exponential functions for true homodimerization, as described in the following paragraphs). We observed 594-GM3, which exhibited homo-colocalization/homodimerization, and 594-DOPE, a monomeric, non-raftophilic control molecule, in the PM of GM3-null CHO-K1 cells.

The colocalization duration histograms for 594-DOPE obtained at both rates could be fitted with single exponential functions, providing very similar decay lifetimes of 42 ± 1 and 39 ± 1 ms for time resolutions of 33 and 8 ms, respectively (Fig. 1d, right), whereas they could not be fitted with the sum of two exponential functions. Since 594-DOPE is not likely to form homodimers or become entrapped in raft domains, this lifetime is considered the incidental colocalization lifetime, representing the duration when two molecules have close encounters within 240 nm, but are not bound at the molecular level.

The colocalization duration histograms for 594-GM3 were also obtained at both rates. At video rate (33 ms resolution), the histogram could be fitted with a single exponential function (note that all fitting operations were performed from the second bin), providing a decay lifetime of 186 ms (Fig. 1d, top-left). At an 8 ms resolution (125 Hz), the histogram could be fitted better using the sum of two exponential functions, one with a fixed incidental lifetime of 39 ms determined using 594-DOPE, based on the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria77, and the other (second) with a lifetime of 194 ± 9 ms (Fig. 1d, bottom-left). Importantly, this second lifetime was very close to 186 ms, obtained by video-rate observation and using a single exponential fitting for the colocalization duration histogram. Therefore, we concluded that video-rate observations and a single exponential decay curve fitting of the colocalization duration histogram are appropriate for measuring colocalization lifetimes on the order of 100 ms.

The fundamental rationale of this video-rate observation method, together with single-exponential decay curve fitting initiated from the second bin, is to confine the majority of incidental colocalization events (characterized by a 39 ms exponential lifetime) to the first bin spanning 0−33 ms, thereby ensuring that the duration histogram from the second bin onward is predominantly governed by the second lifetime component representing the molecular binding time. Since performing all the experiments at an 8 ms resolution (125 Hz) and fitting with the sum of two exponential functions are difficult, and since the lifetime obtained this way is virtually the same as the longer lifetime from 8 ms-resolution recordings, all experiments were performed at 30 Hz with the single exponential fitting from the second bin.

Incidental colocalization lifetimes were further evaluated using three monomer-reference lipid analogs, 594-Cer, 594-SM, and 594-DOPE, in the Lo-phase PLBs. They exhibited very comparable incidental colocalization lifetimes of 37–38 ms in the Lo-phase PLBs (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 3).

For single-molecule lifetime analyses, n denotes the number of colocalization events used for exponential fitting, and exact n values for each condition are provided in the corresponding Supplementary tables. N denotes the number of cells analyzed. Unless otherwise stated, all datasets were collected from at least 10 cells per condition (N > 10).

Single-molecule FRET observations

Single-molecule FRET observations were performed as previously reported26,30. The FRET-donor lipid analogs, 488-GM1, 488-GM3, 488-Gal3-D-LacCer or 488-Gal3-L-LacCer, and the FRET-acceptor lipid analogs, 594-GM1, 594-GM2, 594-GM3, 594-GN6-GalNAcGD1a, 594-GQ1b, 594-Gal3-D-LacCer or 594-DOPE, were incorporated into the PMs of intact and GM3-null CHO-K1 cells or into the PLBs of DMPC(65)/Chol(35). For the PM incorporation of the fluorescent lipid analogs, the cells were incubated with 2 nM FRET donor lipid analogs and 20 nM acceptor analogs, whereas for the PLB incorporation, half concentrations were used. FRET was performed at 37 °C for the cells and 20 °C for the PLBs. Only the FRET donor was excited by the 488 nm laser beam, and both the donor and FRET-induced acceptor signals were simultaneously observed at the level of single molecules.

Single-molecule observations of EGFR in PLBs

The proteoliposomes containing EGFR were prepared as reported previously95. Briefly, DOPC, DPPC, and cholesterol in chloroform were mixed at a 3:3:2 mole ratio in a glass vial, and by evaporating the chloroform with a nitrogen stream, a dried thin film was formed at the bottom of the vial. The dried film was then hydrated by the addition of 1.66 ml of 150 mM NaCl buffered with 20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5 (HBS buffer), to obtain the lipid suspension with a final total lipid concentration of 4 mg/ml. The lipid suspension was mixed with Triton X-100 (TX-100) at a TX-100:lipid ratio of 2 (w/w) and stirred at room temperature for 10 min. The full-length EGFR, lyophilized in the presence of 50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% TX-100, 1 mM DTT, and 10% trehalose (Sigma-Aldrich), was hydrated with Milli-Q water and added to the lipid-TX-100 mixture (2 μg protein in 100 μl) and incubated for 10 min. The final lipid concentration was adjusted to 2 mg/ml by the addition of HBS buffer, and then the mixture was very gently stirred at 4 °C for 30 min. To remove the TX-100, SM2 Bio-Beads (Bio-Rad), prepared as recommended by the manufacturer, were added to the mixture at an SM2 Bio-Beads:TX-100 ratio of 10 (w/w), and slowly stirred at 4 °C for 3 h. After the SM2 Bio-Beads were sedimented by gravity, the supernatant was transferred to another glass vial containing SM2 Bio-Beads (the same amount as the first incubation) and further incubated with gentle stirring at 4 °C for 2 h, and then the supernatant was recovered. After these processes, TX-100 was not detectable by the UV absorption measurement96. The proteoliposome suspension was ultracentrifuged at 200,000 × g at 4 °C for 2 h (Himac, CS100FNX), and the proteoliposome pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of 0.01 M phosphate buffer with 0.15 M NaCl (pH 6.6). Using an extruder (LiposoFast, Avestin), the proteoliposome suspension was passed through 100 nm and then 50 nm pore-sized polycarbonate membrane filters (15 times each). The proteoliposomes were then placed on a cleaned coverslip and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, which led to the formation of Lo/Ld phase-separated PLBs (at 30 °C) on the coverslip88,97–99. After washing the PLBs on the coverslip with P-HBSS, a mixture (500 μl) of 1 nM fluorescent lipid analog in P-HBSS and 20 nM ATTO488-labeled anti-EGFR affibody (Abcam; 1 ATTO488 probe/affibody) was added. Since the ATTO488-anti-EGFR affibody was added to the top of the PLBs, only the EGFR molecules with extracellular domains facing the top surface of the PLBs were considered detectable. ATTO594-labeled lipid analogs and ATTO488-affibody-bound EGFR in the PLBs on the coverslip were simultaneously observed at 30 °C at the level of single molecules.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

The system contained two lactose molecules (two D-lactose molecules, two L-lactose molecules, or a D-lactose molecule and an L-lactose molecule) in 1000 SPC/E water molecules in a box of (31.3 Å)3, with periodic boundary conditions, and equilibrated at 300 K under atmospheric pressure (1 atm). All MD simulations were performed using the AMBER18 software package100, with the GLYCAM06j-1 force field101 for lactose molecules, which is highly compatible with the AMBER force field and can accurately describe the rotational behavior of α/β anomers, various monosaccharide conformations, and glycosidic bonds in MD simulations of sugar chains and glycan complexes.

All simulations used a 1-fs time step for integration, with the covalent bonds to hydrogen atoms constrained via the SHAKE algorithm. The non-bonded interaction cutoff radius was 8.0 Å, and the particle-mesh Ewald method (the message passing interface [MPI] version) for electrostatic interactions was used as implemented in AMBER18. For each simulation, after energy minimization of the initial structure, we first executed the NPT MD simulation at 0 K for 200 ps and adjusted the simulation box at 300 K for 2 ns. Then we performed an equilibration NPT MD simulation at 300 K for 2 ns to prepare 20 independent restart files, assuming each configuration at every 100 ps was uncorrelated with each other. For each independent restart file thus obtained, in the second equilibrium step, a 200 ps NVT MD simulation was performed with the dimer distances constrained to 9–11 Å. Thus, we prepared the 20 independent MD trajectories generated by MD simulations for the D-D, L-L, and D-L pairs with different initial atomic velocities. Finally, production temperature replica exchange (RE) MD simulations were performed for a total of 1 μs (50 ns × 20), with the number of replicas set to 36, ranging from 296.6 K to 440.0 K. Potential energies of each replica were observed to initially change drastically for a few ns and then generally fluctuated around each constant value later. Their average value gradually decreased to remain around −108 × 102 to −107 × 102 kcal/mol, i.e., a converged value within thermal fluctuation. The exchange acceptance ratios of each replica in the REMD simulation were almost equally 40%, showing enough efficiency for convergence.

Synthesis of fluorescent SM and DOPE

ATTO594-L-α-dioleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (594-DOPE), a typical non-raft lipid probe, was synthesized according to the method described previously25,27, and stored in benzene at −80 °C. Statistics and reproducibility

Colocalization-duration histograms were fitted with a single-exponential decay function, and the fitted decay times are reported with 68.3% confidence limits. Colocalization-duration distributions were compared using two-sided log-rank tests in StatView 5.0. Fluorescence-intensity distributions of individual spots of wild-type EGFR and its mutants were fitted with the sum of two log-normal functions, based on the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria77. Where indicated, fitted parameters were compared using two-sided Welch’s t-tests. Curve fitting, Welch’s t-tests, and evaluations using the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria were performed using Origin 2022 for Windows. In cases involving multiple comparisons, significance thresholds were adjusted using the Holm-Šídák method. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless an adjusted threshold was applied. Fitted parameters, confidence limits, exact p-values, sample sizes, and complete statistical results are provided in the corresponding Supplementary Tables and Source Data.

No formal statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were selected based on pilot experiments and were generally larger than the minimum required to test the hypotheses. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. All presented micrographs (Figs. 1b, 2c, 3a, 4e, 5a, and 6a, d, g and Supplementary Figs. 3b, 9a, 10d, 10e, 14a, 15a, 16a, and 19a) are representative of at least three independent experiments that yielded similar results.
