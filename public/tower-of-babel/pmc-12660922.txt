The additive effect of hemoglobin glycation index and glycemic variability to predict mortality in cardiac intensive care patients with and without diabetes
Authors: Jinhui Zhang, Zhenkui Hu, Chao Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) and glycemic variability (GV) are important metabolic markers associated with cardiovascular diseases and their prognoses. However, the potential additive effect of these biomarkers on mortality risk remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the independent and combined effects of HGI and GV on mortality in cardiac ICU patients, both with and without diabetes. We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of 1636 individuals admitted to the cardiac ICU. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality, with 365-day mortality as a secondary endpoint. Kaplan–Meier survival curves, Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic splines, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and subgroup analyses were used to evaluate the relationship between the HGI and adverse outcomes. During the follow-up period, 163 patients (10.0%) died within 28 days, and 322 patients (19.7%) died within 365 days. ROC analysis showed that the combination of HGI and GV had better predictive accuracy for mortality compared to HGI alone (28-day mortality: 0.658 vs. 0.621, P = 0.025; 365-day mortality: 0.644 vs. 0.562, P < 0.001) and GV alone (28-day mortality: 0.658 vs. 0.622, P = 0.036; 365-day mortality: 0.644 vs. 0.618, P = 0.031). Among non-diabetic patients, low HGI combined with high GV was linked to the highest mortality risk at both 28 days (HR = 4.223, 95% CI: 2.336–7.634) and 365 days (HR = 2.504, 95% CI: 1.550–4.045). However, diabetic patients with low HGI and low GV had the greatest risk of death at 28 days (HR = 3.729, 95% CI: 1.747–7.959) and 365 days (HR = 2.287, 95% CI: 1.324–3.951). The results suggested a potential additive effect of HGI and GV on mortality risk at both 28-day and 365-day in cardiac ICU patients, regardless of diabetes status. This combined biomarker approach may guide personalized glycemic management and improve clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26574-0.

Introduction

Despite advances in prevention and treatment, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) continue to represent a major global health burden1. Patients admitted to cardiac intensive care units (ICUs) experience particularly high risks of adverse outcomes2,3, highlighting the importance of identifying prognostic factors to inform strategies to reduce mortality risk in critical care.

The increasing incidence of glycemic abnormalities and diabetes contributes to systemic metabolic dysfunction, which has been recognized as risk factors for unfavorable cardiovascular prognosis4,5. Clinically, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) are the primary biomarkers used to assess blood glucose levels. While HbA1c reflects average blood glucose over the past 8–12 weeks, offering a comprehensive view on long-term glucose management, several factors, such as ethnicity, age, genetic variation, iron deficiency, and anemia, can cause discrepancies between HbA1c values and actual blood glucose levels6–10. In recent years, the hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) has emerged as a valuable tool for quantifying the difference between observed and predicted HbA1c levels, thereby offering a more individualized assessment of glycemic status11. Prior studies demonstrated that HGI was independently associated with poor outcomes in patients with heart failure, myocardial infarction, and coronary artery disease12–14. However, current calculations of HGI depend solely on admission glucose levels, overlooking the prognostic influence of in-hospital glycemic fluctuations. Both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia frequently occur in hospitalized individuals and are associated with greater risks of complications and mortality, regardless of diabetic status15–17. In recent years, glycemic variability (GV), which refers to fluctuations in glucose levels over specific periods, has been identified as a marker of poor glycemic control and a higher risk of complications18. High GV has been identified as an independent risk factor for unfavorable cardiovascular events, including atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and myocardial infarction19–21. We hypothesized that a combined evaluation of HGI and GV could improve glycemic management and provide prognostic accuracy in critical care settings. However, to date, no studies have investigated the combined prognostic utility of HGI and GV in cardiac ICU patients. In this study, we examined the prognostic significance of HGI, GV, and their combination in cardiac ICU patients, both with and without diabetes.

Methods

Data source

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, v3.1) is an open-access clinical database that contains records of patients admitted to the intensive care units of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022. The dataset’s public availability has been approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB), consisting of members from both the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Access to the dataset was granted to the author, J.H. Zhang (ID: 65008026), who conducted the extraction of relevant clinical data.

Study population

Patients were excluded from the study based on the following criteria: (1) a non-first admission to the cardiac ICU, (2) an ICU stay shorter than 24 h, (3) fewer than three blood glucose measurements, and (4) absence of HbA1c data on the first day of ICU admission. Individuals under the age of 18 years were automatically excluded from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database. In total, 1636 patients met the these criteria and were included in the study (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of the study cohort. Data extraction

The collected covariates encompassed demographic information (e.g., age, gender, weight), severity scores on the first day after ICU admission (e.g., Acute Physiology Score III [APSIII], Simplified Acute Physiological Score II [SAPSII], Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [SOFA] score), vital signs (e.g., temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate), comorbidities (e.g., myocardial infarction, heart failure), laboratory parameters (e.g., white blood cell [WBC], platelet [PLT], red blood cell [RBC]), treatments (mechanical ventilation, hypoglycemic agents, insulin therapy, and renal replacement therapy), and clinical outcomes (length of hospital stay, 28-day mortality, and 365-day mortality). For vital signs and laboratory indicators with multiple recordings during the first 24 h of ICU admission, only the earliest recorded value was included. Variables with more than 20% missing data were excluded, and missing values in the remaining variables were imputed using the random forest method.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was 28-day all-cause mortality, while the secondary outcome was 365-day all-cause mortality. Mortality data were obtained from state or hospital death records. All individuals included in the MIMIC-IV database were followed up for at least one year.

Calculation of HGI and GV

To explore the association between FPG and HbA1c levels, a linear regression model was constructed using data from all study participants. In this model, HbA1c was treated as the dependent variable, with FPG as the independent variable. The resulting regression equation was: Predicted HbA1c = 0.0095 × FBG (mg/dL) + 4.882. The HGI was then calculated as the difference between the observed and predicted HbA1c values. The correlation between HGI and HbA1c was shown in Supplement Fig. 1. GV was expressed as the coefficient of variation (CV), defined as the percentage ratio of the standard deviation (SD) to the arithmetic mean of sequential glucose measurements obtained during ICU monitoring (CV = SD/Mean × 100%). Participants were categorized into quartiles based on both HGI (Q1: < -0.76; Q2: -0.76 to -0.35; Q3: -0.35 to 0.24; Q4: > 0.24) and GV (Q1: < 15.84; Q2: 15.84–22.63; Q3: 22.63–29.93; Q4: > 29.93). For a combined analysis, and to enhance statistical power, a binary categorization of HGI and GV was employed, resulting in four groups: [Q1: high HGI (> -0.76) and low GV (< 29.93); Q2: low HGI (< -0.76) and low GV (< 29.93); Q3: high HGI (> -0.76) and high GV (> 29.93); Q4: low HGI (< -0.76) and high GV (> 29.93)].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as either mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as numbers with percentages (%), and differences were assessed using either the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factor (VIF), with variables with VIF > 5 excluded from multivariable models. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the cumulative probability of events, with the log-rank test applied to compare the survival curves across groups. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the relationships between the HGI, GV, and mortality. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 from univariate Cox regression, as well as clinically relevant factors, were included in the multivariate analysis, with collinearity controlled (VIF < 5). Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race; and Model 3 further adjusted for weight, smoking, APSIII, SOFA, Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), myocardial infarction, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, hypertension, renal failure, severe liver disease, mechanical ventilation, hypoglycemic agents, insulin therapy, and renal replacement therapy. The potential non-linear relationships between HGI or GV and mortality were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression. To evaluate the predictive performance of various indices in predicting mortality, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was calculated. Internal validation was conducted using 1000 bootstrap resamplings. It was performed by resampling the dataset with replacement 1000 times. Subgroup analyses, stratified by age, gender, and comorbidities, were presented as forest plots showing hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (†), and P < 0.001 (‡), with all p-values based on two-sided tests. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.3.2.

Results

The association between HGI and mortality in patients with or without diabetes

Kaplan–Meier survival curves according to HGI quartiles are illustrated in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3. The results of the univariate Cox regression analysis were shown in Supplementary Table 3. Supplementary Table 4 showed the variance inflation factors, indicating that there was no multicollinearity among the variables. In the overall cohort, higher HGI was independently associated with reduced risk of both 28-day and 365-day mortality after adjustment for potential confounders (28-day mortality: HR = 0.781 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 0.685–0.890; 365-day mortality: HR = 0.888 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 0.811–0.973). A similar trend was observed in non-diabetic population (28-day mortality: HR = 0.468 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 0.359–0.609; 365-day mortality: HR = 0.673 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 0.539–0.840) and in diabetic population (28-day mortality: HR = 0.861 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 0.744–0.997; 365-day mortality: HR = 0.887 per 1-unit increase, 95% CI 0.803–0.981). When analyzed as a categorical variable, HGI did not show a significant association with mortality in the non-diabetic population, indicating a possible nonlinear relationship between HGI and mortality (Supplementary Table 5). RCS analysis indicated an L-shaped association between HGI and mortality in non-diabetic population, showing a significantly lower risk of 28-day and 365-day mortality below the thresholds of 0.110 and -0.197, respectively. In diabetic individuals, the relationship between HGI and 28-day mortality also displayed an L-shaped trend, with an inflection point at 0.460, whereas the association with 365-day mortality exhibited a negative linear trend (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4). The association between GV and mortality in patients with or without diabetes

Kaplan–Meier survival curves stratified by GV quartiles were displayed in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1. Our analysis revealed that GV was an independent risk factor for mortality in the overall cohort (28-day mortality: HR = 1.018 per-1 unit increase, 95% CI 1.009–1.027; 365-day mortality: HR = 1.016 per-1 unit increase, 95% CI 1.009–1.022). In non-diabetic population, GV significantly correlated with mortality (28-day mortality: HR = 1.026 per unit increase, 95% CI: 1.013–1.040; 365-day mortality: HR = 1.023 per unit increase, 95% CI: 1.013–1.034). However, in the diabetic population, GV showed no significant association with mortality, whether treated as a continuous variable (28-day mortality: HR = 1.014 per unit increase, 95% CI: 1.000–1.030; 365-day mortality: HR = 1.009 per unit increase, 95% CI: 0.999–1.019) or categorical variable (28-day mortality, highest vs. lowest quartile: HR = 1.018, 95% CI: 0.671–3.665; 365-day mortality, highest vs. lowest quartile: HR = 1.564, 95% CI: 0.848–2.884), as shown in Supplementary Table 6. Additionally, RCS analysis indicated a positive linear association between GV and mortality in the overall population, as well as in both diabetic and non-diabetic groups (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4). However, this association lost statistical significance after adjusting for potential confounders in the diabetic patients. A significant interaction between GV and hypertension was observed for both 28-day (p for interaction = 0.034) (Fig. 4) and 365-day mortality (p for interaction = 0.003) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The association of the combination of HGI and GV with mortality in patients with or without diabetes

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the combination of HGI and GV were presented in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that individuals with low HGI and high GV (HGI < -0.76 and GV > 29.93) had the highest risk of mortality in the overall population (28-day mortality: 23.3% vs 6.0%; HR = 3.446, 95% CI 2.189–5.425; 365-day mortality: 33.8% vs 14.4%; HR = 2.252, 95% CI 1.591–3.188) and among non-diabetic individuals (28-day mortality: 24.7% vs 5.9%; HR = 4.223, 95% CI 2.336–7.634; 365-day mortality: 31.5% vs 13.2%; HR = 2.504, 95% CI 1.550–4.045) compared with individuals in the high HGI and low GV (HGI < -0.76 and GV < 29.93) group (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 7). However, in the diabetic population, individuals with low HGI and low GV (HGI < -0.76 and GV < 29.93) demonstrated the highest risk of 28-day mortality (mortality rate: 26.7 vs 6.1%; HR = 3.729, 95% CI 1.747–7.959) and 365-day mortality (mortality rate: 42.2 vs 16.8%; HR = 2.287, 95% CI 1.324–3.951). ROC curve analysis (Fig. 6) indicated that the combination of HGI and GV provided a better prediction of mortality than HGI alone (28-day mortality: 0.658 vs. 0.621, p = 0.025; 365-day mortality: 0.644 vs. 0.562, p < 0.001) or GV alone (28-day mortality: 0.658 vs. 0.622, p = 0.036; 365-day mortality: 0.644 vs. 0.618, p = 0.031). Adding HGI and GV to the traditional risk factor model improved prognostic accuracy. In covariate-adjusted ROC curve analyses, the results were consistent even after adjusting for diabetes (Table 2). Discussion

This study investigated the prognostic utility of the HGI and GV, both independently and in combination, for predicting 28-day and 365-day mortality in cardiac ICU patients, with and without diabetes. The main findings of the study were as follows: (1) HGI was significantly associated with mortality regardless of diabetes status. (2) GV exhibited a significant association with mortality among non-diabetic patients, but this relationship did not remain independently significant after adjustment in those with diabetes. (3) Among non-diabetic individuals, those with low HGI and high GV were associated with the worst prognosis whereas in the diabetic group, the poorest outcomes were observed in those with low HGI and low GV. These findings suggested that the combined evaluation of HGI and GV could potentially enhance risk stratification for patients in cardiac ICU.

Metabolic abnormalities are commonly observed in patients with severe cardiovascular diseases, especially stress-induced hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, both of which are associated with unfavorable outcomes22,23. The body’s stress response triggers the activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in increased levels of catecholamines and cortisol that promote gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, ultimately raising blood glucose levels24,25. In critically ill cardiovascular patients, acute stress responses and medical interventions often cause significant fluctuations in blood glucose, making it challenging to accurately evaluate metabolic status based solely on HbA1c or blood glucose levels. The HGI, a novel metabolic marker, addresses these limitations by providing a more comprehensive metabolic evaluation. It integrates glucose levels, HbA1c, and their interaction, focusing on individual sensitivity to glycemic variability11. Furthermore, HGI may be associated with underlying pathophysiological mechanisms such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and insulin resistance26, which contribute significantly to poor outcomes in critically ill patients. Several studies have demonstrated the predictive value of HGI in various critically ill populations with cardiovascular diseases. For instance, Gao et al. reported a significant relationship between elevated HGI and the risk of myocardial infarction in diabetic individuals27. Wei et al. found that low HGI levels were strongly linked to higher risks for both short- and long-term mortality in patients with critical coronary artery disease14. Cheng et al. observed that in patients with acute decompensated heart failure, high HGI was directly associated with lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality28. Similarly, Cao et al. noted that low HGI was significantly correlated with increased mortality in critically ill myocardial infarction patients13. Lin et al. identified a U-shaped relationship between HGI levels and major adverse cardiac events over a three-year period in individuals with diabetes and established coronary artery disease29. Additionally, Cheng et al. found that HGI was a predictor of long-term mortality in coronary artery disease patients after undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention30. Shi et al. reported that baseline HGI displayed a U-shaped association with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality rates in U.S. adults with diabetes or prediabetes and comorbid cardiovascular disease31. In our study, we found that lower HGI levels were significantly linked to higher 28-day and 365-day mortality rates in cardiac ICU patients, suggesting that HGI may offer potential advantages in risk stratification and prognostic assessment in this population.

GV represents fluctuations in blood glucose over time, which is a predominant pattern of abnormal glucose regulation in critically ill patients18. Stress response, advancing age, and certain medications (such as insulin, steroids, and adrenaline) contribute to unstable blood glucose levels32,33. Studies have shown that GV leads to more oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction compared to persistent hyperglycemia34,35. A meta-analysis showed that higher GV correlates with poor outcomes in acute coronary syndrome patients36. Similarly, He et al. found a potential association between elevated GV and adverse outcomes in coronary artery disease patients without diabetes37. Additionally, GV was associated with the onset of atrial fibrillation in diabetic patients and higher mortality within one year in non-diabetic heart failure patients38,39. Chen et al. identified GV as a significant predictor of all-cause mortality in ICU patients with atrial fibrillation19. Wang et al. found that high GV significantly raised the risk of 30-day mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events in Chinese patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction21. Chang et al. suggested that elevated acute GV could worsen in-hospital outcomes following cardiac surgery40. Our study found a significant association between GV and mortality in non-diabetic patients, which was not observed after adjustments in diabetic patients. This is consistent with findings from Chun et al. and He et al.37,39. Non-diabetic individuals may be more vulnerable to complications from glycemic variability than diabetic patients41,42, as diabetic individuals may develop tolerance to glucose fluctuations through chronic exposure to oxidative stress, which increases their harm thresholds41,43. Consequently, diabetic patients can endure a wider range of blood glucose levels compared to non-diabetic patients.

This research was the first to combine HGI and GV to explore their prognostic utility in cardiac ICU patients. Our results showed that patients with low HGI and high GV had a significantly higher risk of 28-day mortality and 365-day mortality. The ROC analysis further showed that the AUC for the combined with HGI and GV was higher than that of HGI or GV alone in predicting mortality risk. We propose that this could be due to the complementary effects of HGI and GV: HGI reflects acute hyperglycemic stress relative to chronic glycemic control, while GV captures destabilizing glucose fluctuations that may promote oxidative stress14,18. This integrated approach could inform personalized glycemic management strategies, potentially improving clinical outcomes. However, since our study focused on a cardiac ICU population and no previous research combined HGI and GV, the superior predictive value of the combination of HGI and GV needs to be validated in other ICU settings. Future studies should explore the applicability of these findings in varied patient populations.

The biological mechanisms linking HGI and GV with mortality risk in cardiac ICU patients, along with the potential influence of diabetes status, are complex and remain incompletely understood. However, several plausible explanations exist. Firstly, HGI serves as an indicator of glycemic control and hemoglobin glycation, reflecting long-term variations in glucose levels and glycation patterns11. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs), known to cause metabolic disturbances and endothelial dysfunction, are closely related to HGI44. AGEs can form cross-links with extracellular proteins, altering the structure and function of vascular endothelial cells, ultimately leading to decreased vessel elasticity and increased stiffness45. Additionally, AGEs promote the entrapment of Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) particles in arterial walls by decreasing hepatic LDL clearance, which accelerates the development of atherosclerosis46. Furthermore, a low HGI could suggest a slower rate of HbA1c, which prolongs hemoglobin lifespan, increases oxidative stress, and raises blood viscosity, which heighten the risk of cardiovascular thrombus formation30. Secondly, GV denotes the fluctuations in blood glucose around a mean value. Previous studies have shown that large fluctuations in glucose levels lead to increase the expression of markers linked to cardiac fibrosis, such as collagen types I and III and alpha-smooth muscle actin47,48. Furthermore, GV can exacerbate oxidative stress, trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines, and impair endothelial function, which can contribute to coronary vascular abnormalities and coronary heart disease49–52. Lastly, the combined effects of HGI and GV in cardiac ICU patients indicate a synergistic interaction between metabolic dysfunction and glycemic fluctuations. Both hyperglycemia and metabolic disturbances can lead to more severe cardiac damage53. This synergy may results from the combined impact of dysregulated metabolism, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction54. Further research is needed to unravel these complex interactions and to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, due to its retrospective design, the study is susceptible to inherent biases and may not adequately account for confounding variables influencing mortality, which may affect the strength of causal interpretations. Second, our findings are based on data from the single-center MIMIC-IV 3.1 database, which could limit their applicability. Although the database includes a large cohort of cardiac ICU patients, the results may not be applicable to non-cardiac ICU patients or individuals from diverse ethnic. Third, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the evaluation of dynamic changes in clinical parameters over time. Lastly, although the initial cohort consisted of 1636 patients, only 322 were included in the final mortality analysis. This relatively small sample size limits the reliability and generalizability of the mortality predictions. Larger cohort studies are needed to further validate these findings.

Conclusions

The combination of HGI and GV provided distinct advantages, showing a synergistic effect on mortality in cardiac ICU patients, both with and without diabetes. By jointly assessing HGI and GV, clinicians can identify individuals at high risk of mortality earlier, enabling continuous monitoring and timely interventions, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and alleviating the overall burden on this patient population. More multicenter clinical studies are needed to provide stronger evidence.
