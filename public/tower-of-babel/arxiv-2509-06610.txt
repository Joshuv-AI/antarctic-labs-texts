Abstract
. Particle-based stochastic approximations of the Boltzmann equation are popular tools for simulations of non-equilibrium gas flows, for which the Navier–Stokes–Fourier equations fail to provide accurate description. However, these numerical methods are computationally demanding, especially in the near-continuum regime, where the collisions become overwhelming. On the other hand, the Fokker–Planck kinetic models offer an efficient alternative, as the binary collisions are described by a diffusive process. Despite the intuitive advantage, rigorous and efficient Fokker–Planck approximations of the Boltzmann equation remain an open problem. On one hand, the moment projection of the Fokker–Planck operator should be consistent with that of the Boltzmann operator. On the other hand, the Fokker–Planck model should be constructed in such a way that the H-theorem is satisfied. The central aim of this study is fulfilling these two categorically different constraints, i.e. moment matching and entropy dissipation, within a flexible and tractable Fokker–Planck framework. To this end, we introduce a Fisher information-based entropic constraint and demonstrate that, with a suitable polynomial expansion of the drift term, it is possible to simultaneously achieve weak moment matching while honouring the H-theorem. We support our theoretical result by numerical experiments on the shock problem, validating our Fisher Entropic Fokker–Planck framework.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Rarefied gas flows appear in a large variety of applications in physics, including atmospheric reentry flights
[
35
]
, microchannel flows
[
36
]
, and extreme ultraviolet lithography
[
42
]
. Their simulation is a relevant and active research area, as conventional fluid dynamics simulations based on the Navier–Stokes–Fourier (NSF) equations fail to give an accurate description of such processes
[
25
,
2
,
12
]
. The reason lies in the fact that rarefied gases are often in strong non-equilibrium conditions. This translates to extreme gradients and jumps in the thermodynamic quantities. Therefore, the closure implied by the Linear Response Theory, upon which NSF equations are built, is violated
[
34
,
32
]
.

There exists, however, a more accurate theoretical description for non-equilibrium gases which goes beyond the continuum
[
10
]
and takes into account the molecular description of the gas. Instead of following every molecule in the system, as carried out in Molecular Dynamics
[
1
]
, the main idea is to follow the statistical description of the gas kinetics by focusing on the molecular velocity distribution
f(v,x,t)
. The latter is linked to the probability of finding a particle at time
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and at a given position
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
with velocity
v\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
. In our case we will consider the evolution of a monatomic dilute gas, for which the molecular chaos assumption (also called
Stosszahlansatz
) holds. For this setting, an accurate description of the evolution of a gas in terms of the evolution of
f
is given by the Boltzmann equation, which takes into account binary interactions between particles through an integral collision operator.
Due to the high-dimensional support of the velocity distribution function
f
as well as the complexity of the Boltzmann collision integral, simulations based on the Boltzmann equation are prohibitively computationally expensive. To deal with the high-dimensionality, a stochastic approximation based on the Monte Carlo algorithm,called Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC), was developed by Bird in the late 1960s
[
7
]
. The algorithm is consistent with the Boltzmann equation and reproduces experimental results accurately over the whole range of rarefaction. However, since it explicitly performs binary collisions through a jump process, its computational cost scales with the collision frequency. This becomes problematic when approaching the continuum limit, where the number of collisions grows quickly, so the range of application of DSMC remains limited to a specific set of collision frequencies associated with quite large rarefactions. To tackle this problem, simplified particle collision operators have gained attention in the community, trading accuracy with computational efficiency. Diverse classes of stochastic particle methods exist in literature, based on different formulations like the BGK equation
[
6
,
43
]
, as well as the Fokker–Planck (FP) operators
[
22
,
18
,
21
,
29
,
17
]
.

Within these methods, the Fokker–Planck (FP) model aims to achieve a runtime which is independent of the number of collisions, by approximating the collisions through a drift-diffusion stochastic process.
This is in contrast to the BGK model which, despite its simplified form, still employs a jump process whose evaluation needs resolving the collision frequency.
The physical motivation behind the FP model has been justified from different perspectives. For example, the Hamiltonian dynamics can be projected onto a resolved
particle leading to the Generalized Langevin Equation, as performed in the Mori–Zwanzig formalism
[
11
]
. Alternatively, in the Kramers–Moyal expansion of the Boltzmann equation, the FP
equation can be derived after keeping the first and second order terms
[
33
]
. Because of its expressive power to describe complex distributions, the FP equation has also been used for diffusion models
[
26
]
.

Although the design of FP operators has been widely discussed and different models have been proposed, both for monatomic
[
22
,
18
,
21
,
28
,
17
,
14
]
and polyatomic gases
[
15
,
5
,
29
]
, there exist still open problems to be addressed in order to unlock the full potential of these methods. In particular, we want to address the open problem of having a FP model which obtains correct
transport properties along with honoring the H-theorem. The theorem, which is a fundamental property of gases following the Boltzmann equation, states that the evolution of the entropy functional has always a negative sign until the equilibrium distribution is attained. Therefore satisfaction of the H-theorem imposes a requirement on the kinetic models, for their consistency with the Boltzmann dynamics.
In practice, it has also been observed that the H-theorem consistency has direct impact on the accurate recovery of shock profiles
[
18
,
17
]
, its absence in the FP model leads to overly diffusive shock structures.

One notable study to enforce the H-theorem within nonlinear-drift Fokker–Planck models is the Entropic Fokker-Planck (EFP) framework
[
17
]
. In this formulation, the consistency with the H-theorem is ensured by constructing an explicit coupling between diffusion and drift coefficients. However, the resulting entropy dissipation rate lacked a clear dynamical structure: although the decay of entropy was guaranteed in sign, its evolution was not governed by a well-defined functional form.
Following this study, beyond the sign of the entropy evolution, having a well-defined rate of entropy evolution turned out to be also important for shock profiles. Specifically, the decay rate proportional to the Fisher information with respect to the equilibrium distribution turned out to give high-fidelity results
[
17
]
. Motivated by these theoretical and empirical results, we present in this paper a novel framework which honors the entropy decay proportional to the Fisher information, which we will refer to as Fisher Entropic decay rate. Our model, which we will call Fisher Entropic Fokker–Planck (FE-FP), has the advantages of bringing together the H-theorem constraint and correct relaxation rates of non-equilibrium moments, while maintaining the simplicity of implementation of the previous models.

2.2.
Review of Fokker–Planck models

The Boltzmann equation for the evolution of
f
in the monatomic dilute gas setting (in the absence of external force) reads

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=S^{Boltz}[f],

(6)

where
S^{Boltz}[f]
is the operator describing the binary collisions of the particles

\displaystyle S^{Boltz}[f]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}B\left(|v-u|,\cos\hat{\omega}\right)\left(f(v^{*})f(u^{*})-f(v)f(u)\right)\ du\ d\hat{\sigma}.

(7)

Here
(u,v)
and
(u^{*},v^{*})
are the velocities of the colliding pairs pre and post collisions,
B:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[-1,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the collision kernel,
\hat{\omega}
is the deflection angle, and
d\hat{\sigma}
is the infinitesimal solid angle
[
8
]
. For notation simplicity, we omitted the dependency on
x
and
t
. To conclude, it is important to mention that the moments of
f
give information on the macroscopic quantities of the gas. We can compute the density as
\mathrm{m}^{(0)}_{0}
, the mean velocity as
U_{i}=\mathrm{m}_{i}^{(0)}/\rho
and the temperature as
T=\ \tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(2)}_{0}/(3nk_{B})
. In the latter, we have
k_{B}
as the Boltzmann constant. For notational convenience, we refer to the quantity
\theta=k_{B}T/m=\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(2)}_{0}/(3\rho)
as well. The other relevant physical quantities depending on the moments are the scalar pressure
p=\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(2)}_{0}/3
, the pressure tensor
\Pi_{ij}=\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(0)}_{ij}
, the stress tensor
\sigma_{ij}=\Pi_{ij}-1/3p\delta_{ij}
and the heat-fluxes
\ q_{i}=\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(2)}_{i}/2
. Finally, we refer to the viscosity
\mu=\mu_{0}\left(T/T_{0}\right)^{\omega}
, where
\omega
depends on the underlying molecular model,
T_{0}=273
K and
\mu_{0}
the viscosity evaluated at
T_{0}
.

Approximating the Boltzmann operator with a simplified diffusion operator, leading to the FP equation, dates back to the works of Kirkwood
[
24
]
, Lebowitz et al.
[
27
]
, and Pawula
[
31
]
. Based on the theory of Brownian motion, these studies presented a heuristic approach to obtain a diffusive approximation of the Boltzmann collision operator. In general such approximations can be cast in an operator of the form

\displaystyle S^{FP}[f]=-\nabla_{v}\cdot(A[f](t,v)\ f)+\Delta_{v,v}(D[f](t,v)\ f).

(8)

where
A[f](t,v):\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
D[f](t,v):\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We often drop the explicit dependency on
t
and
v
and refer to
A[f]
as the drift functional and
D[f]
as the diffusion functional.

2.2.1.
Linear-drift FP

The original diffusive approximation of the linear Boltzmann equation from
[
24
]
and
[
31
]
led to a formulation with a drift which is linear with respect to the (molecular) velocity and a diffusion proportional to the temperature. In the context of simulation of monatomic rarefied gases, Jenny et al.
[
22
]
were the first ones to adopt this formulation. The resulting operator takes the form of

\displaystyle S^{FP}[f]=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left({v^{\prime}}/{\tau}\ f\right)+\Delta_{v,v}\left({\theta}/{\tau}\ f\right),

(9)

where

\displaystyle A[f]=-{v^{\prime}}/{\tau}

(10)

and

\displaystyle D[f]={\theta}/{\tau}\ ,

(11)

where
v^{\prime}=v-U
is the fluctuating velocity. Here,
\tau=2\mu/p
is a time scale. This model formulation leads to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process of McKean-Vlasov-type (since
U
and
T
depend on
f
). The linear-drift FP satisfies conservation of mass, momentum and energy, and recovers the correct viscosity in the hydrodynamic limit. This implies correct relaxation rate for the stress tensor
\sigma
. However, the model has a major shortcoming: it fails to recover the correct relaxation rate for the heat-fluxes
q
. This leads to a wrong Prandtl number and thus a wrong heat conductivity in the hydrodynamic limit
[
22
]
.

2.2.2.
Cubic-drift FP

In the following years, FP methods with correct heat-flux relaxation have been proposed for monatomic
[
18
,
28
]
and polyatomic gases
[
29
]
. In order to fulfill consistent relaxation of moments beyond the stress tensor, a fully non-linear model of the drift was postulated in
[
18
]
. In particular, the diffusion

\displaystyle D[f]={\theta}/{\tau}

(12)

maintains the form of the linear-drift model, and
A[f]
now has a cubic form given by

\displaystyle A_{i}[f](v^{\prime})=-\cfrac{1}{\tau}v^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}c_{ij}[f]\ v^{\prime}_{j}+\gamma_{i}[f]\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{j}^{\prime}v_{j}^{\prime}-3\theta\right)+\Lambda[f]\left(v_{i}^{\prime}\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{j}^{\prime}v^{\prime}_{j}-\cfrac{2q_{i}}{\rho}\right),

where
\tau
has the same form as for the linear model,
c[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is a symmetric tensor,
\gamma[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a vector, and
\Lambda[f]
is a scalar. While
\Lambda[f]
was introduced as a stability term,
c[f]
and
\gamma[f]
are updated at every timestep through a linear system of equations. The system is constructed using weak consistency with the Boltzmann operator as a constraint, i.e.

\displaystyle\langle H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),S^{FP}[f]\rangle\overset{!}{=}\langle H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),S^{Boltz}[f]\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall\ \alpha\in I_{\alpha}^{3}.

(14)

In the case of the cubic-drift (
2.2.2
),
\{H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\}_{\alpha\in I_{\alpha}^{3}}=\{1,v^{\prime},\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2},v^{\prime}\otimes v^{\prime}-1/3\mathrm{tr}(v^{\prime}),v^{\prime}\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}\}
. The objective is to enforce the projection of the FP collision operator on a given set of polynomials to be similar to that resulting from the Boltzmann operator
\forall\ t\in\mathbb{R}^{\geqslant 0}
. For this reason, the weak consistency is also known in the community as“moment matching”. This enforces both conservation laws through the moments of
1,v^{\prime},\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}
and, in addition to linear-drift FP, the relaxation rates of higher-order moments (up to the heat-fluxes). This model is the state-of-the-art for FP model thanks to the simplicity of its formulation, as well as flexibility on the moments matched. We have to notice that despite the simplicity of its implementation and its moment consistency property, the cubic-drift model has no guarantee on the structure of the entropy dynamics and its relaxation rate towards the equilibrium. As a consequence, the model can be prone to diffusive shock profiles in numerical experiments
[
18
,
17
]
.

2.2.3.
FP models with alternative structures

It is worthwhile to mention that there exists two alternative structures of FP models which can address the issue of entropy relaxation encountered by the cubic-drift FP model. The first one, introduced by Mathiaud and Mieussens
[
28
]
, leverages the anisotropic diffusion coefficient inspired by the elliptic BGK model
[
43
]
. Despite the entropy consistency, the moment consistency could not be enforced globally as the diffusion coefficient might become negative for specific non-equilibrium conditions. Another result was derived in
[
17
]
, where a scalar diffusion was constructed ensuring consistency with the Boltzmann H-theorem. The improvement over the cubic-drift model, however, remained limited due to the fact that the entropy relaxation rate could not be controlled.

3.
Problem statement and main idea

3.1.
H-theorem

Considering the Boltzmann equation in a homogeneous setting (
6
) without the gradient term on the left-hand side, it can be shown that the equilibrium is given by the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

\displaystyle f_{0}=\cfrac{\rho}{(2\pi\theta)^{3/2}}\exp{\left(-\cfrac{\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}}{2\theta}\right)}\ .

(15)

The H-theorem characterizes the evolution of
f
towards
f_{0}
via the entropy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}[f]=\langle f,\log f\rangle,

(16)

stating that

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}[f]}{\partial t}\leqslant 0,

(17)

with equality
\partial\mathcal{H}[f]/\partial t=0
if and only if
f=f_{0}
. This is an important property of the Boltzmann equation, whose physical interpretation gives rise to irreversibility of the kinetic system, giving an “arrow of time" towards the equilibrium, which corresponds to the minimum entropy
\mathcal{H}[f_{0}]=\langle f_{0},\log f_{0}\rangle

[
38
]
. Moreover, existence of
L_{1}
and Kullback-Leibler bounds with respect to the equilibrium can be proved based on contraction of
\mathcal{H}[f]

[
40
]
.

3.2.
Problem statement

Given the importance of the H-theorem in Boltzmann kinetics, it is highly desirable to devise FP models admitting this constraint. In particular, theoretical results on the trend to equilibrium in terms of entropy bounds of (
8
) have been shown in
[
4
,
3
,
39
]
. In practice, admitting the H-theorem has important impact on the ability of the model to accurately recover the shock profiles
[
18
,
17
]
.

In the following, we translate the H-theorem to a model constraint for a generic FP operator. Considering the homogeneous setting, the evolution of
\mathcal{H}[f]
following the FP equation (
8
) and assuming
D[f]
to be independent of
v
, we get

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}[f]}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle-\nabla_{v}A[f]-D[f]\ (\nabla_{v}\log f\cdot\nabla_{v}\log f),f\rangle\ .

(18)

The open question is how to choose
A[f]
and
D[f]
such that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{H}[f]
follows the constraints given by the Boltzmann H-theorem, and at the same time we keep enough flexibility to fulfill the weak consistency relationship (
14
) for an arbitrary set of moments.

3.3.
Main idea

Considering the linear-drift FP model, a key insight can be gained.
By using
A[f]
(
10
),
D[f]
(
11
) from the operator (
9
) into the expression (
18
) we find

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}[f]}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cfrac{\theta}{\tau}\left\langle\nabla_{v}\log\cfrac{f}{f_{0}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\log\cfrac{f}{f_{0}},f\right\rangle,

(19)

where the integral term on the right-hand-side is the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}(f|f_{0})=\left\langle\nabla_{v}\log{f}/{f_{0}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\log{f}/{f_{0}},f\right\rangle

[
13
]
. The quantity
\mathcal{I}(f|f_{0})
represents a distance between two probability distributions
f
and
f_{0}
. Because of its quadratic form, it is always
\geqslant 0
with equality iff
f=f_{0}
. Since
T>0
, then (
17
) holds and the H-theorem is verified. This formulation is particularly favorable, as it is consistent with the theoretical analysis of FP equations, as seen in
[
39
]
. Moreover it was observed that fulfilling the equality (
19
) leads to better recovery of shock profiles with respect to other rates
[
17
]
. However, we remind that despite this nice property, the linear-drift FP model does not give correct heat-conductivity, as shown in Sec.
2.2.1
. Therefore the main idea of our Fisher Entropic FP model is to bring together the entropy evolution (
19
) and the weak consistency relation (
14
) in the construct of drift and diffusion coefficients of the FP model.

4.
Entropy guided Fokker–Planck by Fisher information

This section is dedicated to discuss in detail the FE-FP framework. Closures of the diffusion and drift functionals, as well as important theoretical results on the model, will be covered.
The general ansatz for the drift will be introduced in Sec.
4.1
. The novel entropy constraint will be discussed in Sec.
4.2
. The moment consistency relations will be then derived in Sec.
4.3

4.4

4.1.
General formulation

We start sketching our model by constructing
A
as a gradient field which, without loss of generality, can be cast as an expansion around the linear drift.
Suppose we would like to match the moments associated with polynomials
\{H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\}_{\alpha\in I_{r}^{3}}
. Consider
\{H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\}_{\alpha\in I_{r}^{3}}\cup\|v^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2k}
, where
2k=r+2-\textrm{mod}(r,2)
. The augmented set of polynomials now include the smallest even power, larger than the highest order of moments which are going to be matched. We refer to the augmented index set by
\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}
and the corresponding polynomials by
\{H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
.
Let
\Phi^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})
be a potential function corresponding to the polynomial expansion, given by

\displaystyle\Phi^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}c_{\alpha}[f]\ H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})

(20)

with
\{c_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}
being a set of real coefficients.
We consider the following drift ansatz

\displaystyle A[f](v^{\prime})=-\cfrac{1}{\tau}v^{\prime}+A^{HO}[f](v^{\prime})-c_{d}[f]\nabla_{v}(\|v^{\prime}\|^{d}_{2}),

(21)

comprising of linear, nonlinear, and stabilizing term, where

\displaystyle A^{HO}[f](v^{\prime})=\nabla_{v}\Phi^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})\ ,

(22)

contains all higher order terms in
v^{\prime}
, up to the order
r
. Note that
\tau>0
is a time scale independent of
v
that will be fixed later.
The diffusion is kept with the same formulation as the one expressed in (
12
).

The purpose of the stabilizing term
-c_{d}[f]\ \nabla_{v}\|v^{\prime}\|_{2}^{d}
is to ensure that the drift can be expressed as a gradient of a convex function as
\|v^{\prime}\|_{2}\to\infty
. This leads to regularity of the solution of the corresponding SDE (as justified in Sec.
4.6
). We choose
c_{d}[f]\geqslant 0
(with equality at equilibrium),
where
d=2k+2
is the closest even number strictly larger than
2k
. Furthermore, as we would like to recover the linear drift model in the limit of thermal equilibrium, the coefficient
c_{d}[f]
should depend on some notion of non-equilibrium moments. We set
c_{d}[f]
in terms of
\theta_{0}
and a small empirical parameter
\epsilon_{0}\approx 10^{-3}
as

\displaystyle c_{d}[f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}\sqrt{(2\theta_{0})^{r}}\ \left\|\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(r)}_{0}(f)\ -\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(r)}_{0}(f_{0})\right\|^{2}_{2}\ \Big/\ \left\|\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(r)}_{0}(f_{0})\right\|^{2}_{2}.

(23)

The idea, following the one for the cubic-drift formulation of Sec.
2.2.2
, is to design a linear system of equations for
\{c_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
such that moment consistency and entropy production are both satisfied.

4.2.
Fisher entropic constraint

We want to derive a constraint which recovers the equality (
19
) given the formulation of the drift in (
21
). By carrying out the calculation as in (
18
), we arrive to the following result (the proof is deferred to Sec.
5.1
).

Theorem 4.1

(Fisher entropic constraint)
.

Suppose
f(v,t)
(with
\mathcal{H}[f]<\infty
) is the solution of the FP equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{FP}[f]\ ,

(24)

for some fixed initial condition, where the right-hand-side is given by (
8
), the drift follows (
21
), and the diffusion is set to (
12
). Also for simplicity (and without loss of generality)
\rho=1
. If the drift coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}c_{\alpha}[f]\ \Delta_{v,v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\right\rangle=(2k+2)(2k+1)c_{d}[f]\langle\|v^{\prime}\|^{2k}_{2},f\rangle

(25)

then, the following holds

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}[f]}{\partial t}=-D[f]\ \mathcal{I}(f\ |\ f_{0}).

(26)

Here,
\mathcal{I}
is the Fisher information defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(f\ |\ f_{0})\coloneqq\left\langle\nabla_{v}\log\cfrac{f}{f_{0}}\cdot\nabla_{v}\log\cfrac{f}{f_{0}},f\right\rangle\ .

(27)

Theorem
4.1
rests at the core of the devised model, since it shows
that by enforcing (
25
) on the drift coefficients, we obtain the
favorable entropy decay given by (
26
). Note that the time scale
\tau
, introduced
in
D[f]
, controls the entropy relaxation rate.
While well-posedness and convergence to equilibrium of the FE-FP system remains out of the scope of the current study, we emphasize that the Fisher entropic structure imposed by (
26
) leads to an upper bound on the
L^{1}
-distance of
f
from
f_{0}
as shown for non-linear FP equations in
[
4
]
.

4.3.
Weak consistency

After deriving the entropy constraint of our FE-FP model, we move on to the moment equations resulting from the weak consistency constraints (
14
).

An important result regarding relaxation of non-equilibrium moments according to the Boltzmann equation (assuming molecules with the Maxwell potential) is the theorem of the trend to equilibrium
[
41
]
, recalled below. Consider an arbitrary (regardless of whether it is central) moment of the form
\langle H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle
, whose evolution is governed by the homogeneous Boltzmann equation leading to

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle=\left\langle H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),S^{Boltz}[f]\right\rangle=:P_{\alpha}[f],

(28)

where
P_{\alpha}[f]
is called the production term for
H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})
. Then we have the following result.

Theorem 4.2

(trend to equilibrium)
.

Every moment following ODE (
28
) is analytic function of time which relaxes exponentially to its equilibrium value.

For the proof, see the reference text
[
41
]
.
The consequence of this theorem is that the system of equations constructed from the constraint (
14
) is closed for any order of moments. Following this theoretical result, we insert the FP operator (
8
) with drift (
21
) and diffusion (
12
) in the left-hand side of (
14
), leading to

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\cdot\sum_{\beta\in I_{r}^{3}}c_{\beta}[f]\ \nabla_{v}H_{\beta}(v^{\prime}),f\right\rangle=P_{\alpha}[f]+g_{\alpha}(\tau,D[f],c_{d}[f])\ ,\ \forall\alpha\in I_{r}^{3}.

(29)

Here, we have grouped all the right-hand side terms depending on
D[f]
,
\tau
and
c_{d}[f]
in the function

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}(\tau,D[f],c_{d}[f])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{\tau}\ \langle v^{\prime}\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle-D[f]\langle\Delta_{v,v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle

(30)

\displaystyle-c_{d}[f]\langle\nabla_{v}\|v^{\prime}\|^{d}_{2}\cdot\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle.

4.4.
Linear system for unknown coefficients

We have now the necessary ingredients to construct the linear system for the coefficients of the FE-FP model
\{c_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
, which include the moment matching constraints (
25
) and the entropy constraint (
29
). The construction requires special caution, though, since it is not always guaranteed that the matrix obtained from combining
\Phi^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})
with the constraints is invertible. Therefore, in order to obtain a well-posed system we proceed with the following procedure.

(1)

We split the model coefficients
\{c_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
into two sets of coefficients, and derive the potential spanned by them.

(2)

We show that by enforcing the model constraints on the new coefficients, two well-posed linear systems are obtained.

This procedure will be such that the existence of a unique solution is ensured, with a minimal additional computation of the two sets of model coefficients.

Let
\varphi\coloneqq\alpha_{2k}
,
l\coloneqq|\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}|
and note that
|I_{r}^{3}|=l-1
. Furthermore, consider the matrix
R[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times l}
with elements

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}[f]=\langle\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\ \nabla_{v}H_{\beta}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle,

(31)

and the vector
Q[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
,

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}[f]=\langle\Delta_{v,v}H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),f\rangle

(32)

with
\alpha,\beta\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}
.
Additionally, let
G[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
be a vector of zeros except for the last term

\displaystyle G_{\varphi}[f]=-2Q_{\varphi}[f].

(33)

Let us introduce
\{c^{\prime}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
and
\{\hat{c}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
, from which
\{c_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
are computed

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}[f]

\displaystyle\coloneq

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{\alpha}[f]+c^{\prime}_{\varphi}[f]\ \hat{c}_{\alpha}[f],\ \forall\ \alpha\in{I}_{r}^{3}

\displaystyle c_{\varphi}[f]

\displaystyle\coloneq

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{\varphi}[f]\ \hat{c}_{\varphi}[f]\ .

(34)

Accordingly, an equivalent formulation of the drift can be obtained by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})=\sum_{\alpha\in{I}_{r}^{3}}c^{\prime}_{\alpha}[f]\ H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})+c^{\prime}_{\varphi}[f]\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}\hat{c}_{\alpha}[f]\ H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime}),

(35)

leading to
\nabla_{v}\Phi^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})=\nabla_{v}\tilde{\Phi}^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})
.
It is now left to drive, and to show the invertibility of, the linear system of equations for
\{c^{\prime}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
and
\{\hat{c}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
, fulfilling moment and entropy constraints.
We set

\displaystyle\hat{c}[f]\coloneqq R[f]^{-1}(Q[f]+G[f])\ ,

(36)

noting that
R[f]
is invertible as shown in Appendix
A
.
Next, we identify
\{c^{\prime}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
, by plugging the potential
\tilde{\Phi}^{(r)}[f](v^{\prime})
in the moment and entropy constraints, and substituting
\{\hat{c}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{{}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}}
with its solution (
36
). We obtain

\displaystyle c^{\prime}[f]=L[f]^{-1}b[f]

(37)

with

\displaystyle L[f]=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{R}[f]&\bar{Q}[f]\\
\bar{Q}[f]^{T}&S[f]\end{pmatrix}

and

\displaystyle b[f]=\begin{pmatrix}\overline{b}[f]\\
h[f]\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(38)

where the composition of
L[f]
and
b[f]
is given in the following.
\bar{R}[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{l-1\times l-1}
is the sub-matrix of
R[f]
excluding the row
\{R_{\varphi\beta}[f]\}_{\beta\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
and the column
\{R_{\alpha\varphi}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
. In the same way,
\bar{Q}[f]\in\mathbb{R}^{l-1}
is the sub-vector of
Q
excluding its last element
Q_{\varphi}[f]
. Furthermore, we have the scalar

\displaystyle S[f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}\hat{c}_{\alpha}[f]\ Q_{\alpha}[f]=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}R_{\alpha\beta}[f]^{-1}Q_{\alpha}[f](Q_{\beta}[f]+G_{\beta}[f])

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in{\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}}R_{\alpha\beta}[f]^{-1}Q_{\alpha}[f]Q_{\beta}[f]\ -2Q_{\varphi}[f]\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}R_{\varphi\alpha}[f]^{-1}Q_{\beta}[f].

The right-hand-side vector is comprised of

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{\alpha}[f]=P_{\alpha}[f]\ +g_{\alpha}(\tau,D[f],c_{d}[f]),\ \alpha\in{I}_{r}^{3}

(40)

containing the weak consistency right-hand-side, and

\displaystyle h[f]=-c_{d}[f]\langle\|v^{\prime}\|^{2k}_{2},f\rangle\ ,

(41)

the entropy constraint right-hand-side.

With these elements set, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.3

(linear systems solution)
.

The linear systems given by (
36
) and (
37
) always admit a unique solution as long as temperature is positive i.e.
T>0
.

The proof is deferred to Sec.
5.2
.
Since both linear systems for
\{c^{\prime}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
and
\{\hat{c}_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
have a unique solution, then the coefficients
\{c_{\alpha}[f]\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}
are uniquely identified through (
4.4
), resulting in a drift fulfilling both moment and entropy constraints.

4.5.
Time scale derivation

The model will be closed once we fix the time scale
\tau>0
. Note that irrespective of the value of the time scale, the model fulfills the entropy and moment constraints. Our derivation of
\tau
will be guided by the fact that it controls the entropy decay rate. In particular, we aim for a value of
\tau
such that the entropy decay rate implied by the FE-FP model is identical to the one resulting from the Boltzmann equation for a known class of distributions. In order to do that, we limit our analysis to the case for which an analytical solution can be obtained in time.

We focus on the relaxation of elliptic (anisotropic-Gaussian) distributions of the form

\displaystyle f_{G}=\cfrac{\rho}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sqrt{\det\Pi}}\exp{\left(-\cfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}v^{\prime}_{i}\Pi^{-1}_{ij}v^{\prime}_{j}\right)}.

(42)

To simplify the derivation, and without loss of generality, we perform the calculation on a frame of reference in which
\Pi
is diagonal with components
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}=3-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
, assuming that
\theta=1
and
\rho=1
.
We derive the entropy decay rate for each of the operators

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{Boltz}[f_{G}]}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle 1,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(f_{G}\log f_{G})\right\rangle,

(43)

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}^{FE-FP}[f_{G}]}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cfrac{1}{\tau}\left\langle\nabla_{v}\log\left(\cfrac{f_{G}}{f_{0}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{v}\log\left(\cfrac{f_{G}}{f_{0}}\right),f_{G}\right\rangle.

(44)

The former, given by (
43
), follows

\displaystyle\left\langle 1,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(f_{G}\log f_{G})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\cfrac{\partial f_{G}}{\partial t},1+\log f_{G}\right\rangle

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\cfrac{\partial\lambda_{i}}{\partial t}\left\langle\cfrac{\partial f_{G}}{\partial\lambda_{i}},1+\log f_{G}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cfrac{p}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\cfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}}-1\right),

where we used the fact that
\partial_{t}\Pi=\partial_{t}\sigma=-(p/\mu)\sigma

for interaction of Maxwell molecules governed by the Boltzmann equation. On the other hand, the entropy decay rate given by (
44
) follows

\displaystyle-\cfrac{1}{\tau}\left\langle\nabla_{v}\log\left(\cfrac{f_{G}}{f_{0}}\right)\nabla_{v}\log\left(\cfrac{f_{G}}{f_{0}}\right),f_{G}\right\rangle=-\cfrac{1}{\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\cfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}}-1\right)^{2}\lambda_{i}\ .

(46)

Enforcing equality between the two entropy decay rates leads to

\displaystyle\tau=\cfrac{2\mu}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\cfrac{\left({1/\lambda_{i}}-1\right)^{2}\lambda_{i}}{\left(1/{\lambda_{i}}-1\right)}=\frac{2\mu}{p}\ .

(47)

4.6.
Well-posedeness of corresponding SDEs

Following standard results in theory of stochastic processes, it is straight-forward to derive the Langevin SDEs corresponding to the FP equation given by (
8
). Specifically, we have

\displaystyle dM_{t,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{i}[f]dt\ +\sqrt{2D[f]}\ dW_{t,i},

(48)

\displaystyle dX_{t,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{t,i}dt,

(49)

where
W_{t}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the Wiener process. For our FE-FP setting, the system belongs to a family of SDEs known as McKean-Vlasov (MKV) SDEs, for which the evolution of the stochastic processes depend also on the distribution
f
(here through moments of
f
)
[
30
,
9
]
. It is easy to see that in our case, this dependency is a direct consequence of the dependency on the moments for
A[f]
and
D[f]
. This carries additional challenges compared to classical SDEs. In particular, it is more complex to prove bounds on stability and results on well-posedness. It is out of the scope of the paper to prove regularity of our SDE in the general MKV setting . Instead, we focus on regularity of the velocity SDE with frozen model coefficients. In particular, we assume the coefficients
\{c_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}
as well as the diffusion
D
to be constant in time. For this setting, we have the following theoretical result.

Theorem 4.4

(SDE regularity)
.

Let us consider the velocity SDE (
48
), corresponding to the FP equation (
8
) with diffusion (
12
) and drift defined as (
21
), with
\{c_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}
bounded and constant in time, and stabilization coefficient
c_{d}\geqslant 0
defined as (
23
). Then, the velocity SDE admits a unique solution global in time.

The proof is deferred to Sec.
5.3
.

5.
Justifications of theoretical results

We present in this section the proofs of the theorems justifying our theoretical results on FE-FP. Only for this section, for notation simplicity and without loss of generality, we adopt the following assumptions.

(1)

We set
\langle v,f\rangle=0
, such that
v^{\prime}=v
.

(2)

We set
\rho=1
.

From this point onwards, we suppress the explicit dependence on
f
for notational convenience, unless required for clarity.

5.1.
Proof of Fisher entropic constraint theorem (Theorem
4.1
)

Proof.

The proof follows from the evaluation of (
18
). For the drift given by (
21
) we find

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cfrac{3}{\tau}-\langle\nabla_{v}A^{HO}(v),f\rangle+c_{d}\langle\Delta_{v,v}\|v\|^{d}_{2},f\rangle-\cfrac{\theta}{\tau}\langle\nabla_{v}\log f\cdot\nabla_{v}\log f,f\rangle

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cfrac{\theta}{\tau}\ \mathcal{I}(f|f_{0})-\langle\nabla_{v}A^{HO}(v),f\rangle+c_{d}\langle\Delta_{v,v}\|v\|^{d}_{2},f\rangle

using integration by part and direct calculation.
The constraint (
26
) leads to cancellation of the second and the third term on the right-hand-side, and thus

\displaystyle\cfrac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cfrac{\theta}{\tau}\ \mathcal{I}(f|f_{0})\ .

(51)

∎

5.2.
Proof of linear systems solution theorem (Theorem
4.3
)

Proof.

First, note that for the system (
36
), the proof is straight-forward as
R
is symmetric positive definite (see Appendix
A
for details).
Next, for the solution uniqueness of (
37
), we need to prove that
L
is invertible. The idea is to use the block construction of
L
and prove invertibility through Sylvester’s criterion.

Since
R
is symmetric positive definite, and thus invertible as previously mentioned, we can compute the determinant of
L
via the Schur complement of
\bar{R}

\displaystyle L/\bar{R}=S-\bar{Q}^{T}\bar{R}^{-1}\bar{Q}.

(52)

Note that

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{T}\bar{R}^{-1}\bar{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in{I}_{r}^{3}}R_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}Q_{\beta}Q_{\alpha}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}R_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}Q_{\beta}Q_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}R_{\varphi\beta}^{-1}Q_{\beta}Q_{\varphi}-\sum_{\alpha\in{I}_{r}^{3}}R_{\varphi\alpha}^{-1}Q_{\varphi}Q_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}R_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}Q_{\beta}Q_{\alpha}-2Q_{\varphi}\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}R_{\alpha\varphi}^{-1}Q_{\alpha}+R_{\varphi\varphi}^{-1}Q_{\varphi}Q_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S+R_{\varphi\varphi}^{-1}Q_{\varphi}Q_{\varphi}.

Therefore

\displaystyle L/\bar{R}=-R_{\varphi\varphi}^{-1}Q_{\varphi}Q_{\varphi}.

(54)

However the right hand side is strictly negative and therefore the system always admits a unique solution, due to the following.

(1)

Note that
Q_{\varphi}=\langle\Delta_{v,v}(\|v\|^{2k}_{2}),f\rangle
and therefore
Q_{\varphi}>0
since
\langle\|v\|_{2}^{2},f\rangle>0
(due to positivity of temperature).

(2)

We have
R_{\varphi\varphi}^{-1}>0
by observing

R_{\varphi\varphi}^{-1}=\langle\nabla_{v}(\|v\|^{2k}_{2})\cdot\nabla_{v}(\|v\|^{2k}_{2}),f\rangle\ .

(55)

∎

5.3.
Proof of SDE regularity (Theorem
4.4
)

Proof.

The proof follows Theorem 3.5 by Khasminskii in
[
23
]
. Given the fact that for fixed model coefficients, the drift is only function of
v
and the diffusion is a constant, the conditions of the mentioned theorem on drift and diffusion are fulfilled. We are left with identifying a Lyupanov function
V(v)
which behaves like a convex function as
\|v\|_{2}\to\infty
, and satisfies

\displaystyle LV(v)\leqslant aV(v)\ ,

(56)

where
L
is the generator of the SDE and
a<\infty
is some constant.
In our case, we set
V(v)=\|v\|_{2}^{2}
. We observe that as
\|v\|\longrightarrow\infty
, the stability term (
23
) becomes dominant, as
\|v\|^{d}_{2}
has a higher order than any other polynomial in
A^{HO}(v)
, and goes to
-\infty
faster than any other term leading to

\displaystyle-A(v)\cdot\nabla_{v}V(v)+\sqrt{2\theta/\tau}\ \Delta_{v,v}V(v)\leqslant aV(v),

(57)

for some constant
a>0
.
∎

6.
Solution algorithm

For practical rarefied gas flow simulations, we need to deal with the three-dimensional velocity space, where the moment consistency includes the stress tensor and the heat-fluxes. This leads to correct viscosity and heat-conductivity in the hydrodynamic limit. In this section, we present a computationally affordable numerical recipe for the FE-FP operator built on this setting.

6.1.
Model expression

In order to consider the moment consistency for the momentum, the stress tensor, and the heat-fluxes, we let
\{H_{\alpha}(v^{\prime})\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{4}}=\{1,v^{\prime},v^{\prime}\otimes v^{\prime}-1/3\mathrm{tr}(v^{\prime}),v^{\prime}\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2},\|v^{\prime}\|^{4}_{2}\}
(note that the energy conservation is included through the trace of the stress tensor). With this set of polynomials we have the following expression for the drift

\displaystyle A_{i}(v^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\cfrac{1}{\tau}v^{\prime}_{i}+A_{i}^{HO}(v^{\prime})-c^{(4)}\ v^{\prime}_{i}\|v^{\prime}\|_{2}^{4},

(58)

where

\displaystyle A^{HO}_{i}(v^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{(0)}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left((c^{(1)}_{ij}+c^{(1)}_{ji})v_{j}^{\prime}+2c^{(2)}_{j}\ v^{\prime}_{i}v^{\prime}_{j}\right)

(59)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle c^{(2)}_{i}\ \|v^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+3c^{(3)}\ v^{\prime}_{i}\|v\|_{2}^{2}.

For convenience we define the symmetric tensor
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{ij}=c_{ij}^{(1)}+c_{ji}^{(1)}
.
Note that
c^{(4)}\geqslant 0,|c^{(4)}|\ll 1
is fixed as the stabilization term (see (
23
)).
The remaining coefficients are
\{c^{(0)},\tilde{c}^{(1)},c^{(2)},c^{(3)}\}
, which depend on
f
through its moments.
The vector
c^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be readily computed from the momentum conservation

\displaystyle c^{(0)}_{i}=-3\theta c^{(2)}_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(2{\Pi_{ij}}c_{j}^{(2)}+6\cfrac{q_{i}}{\rho}c^{(3)}\right)+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\rho}c^{(4)},

(60)

with
\Delta_{i}=\tilde{\mathrm{m}}^{(4)}_{i}
. The linear system is then constructed for the symmetric tensor
\tilde{c}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, the vector
c^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and the scalar
c^{(3)}\in\mathbb{R}
leading to
10\times 10
system.

6.2.
Linear system setup

Following Sec.
4.4
, we obtain the unknowns by solving the two linear systems (
36
) and (
37
) of size
10\times 10
. The explicit expressions of the system matrices
R
,
Q
,
G
and
L
(using the naming introduced in Sec.
4.4
), as well as the one for the right-hand side vector, can be found in Appendix
B
.
To reduce the cost of inversion of the matrix
L
, we calculate
L^{-1}
through its Schur complement (which we have shown in Sec.
5.2
to be the scalar
L/\bar{R}=\bar{R}_{10,10}^{-1}Q_{10}Q_{10}
)

\displaystyle L^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{R}^{-1}-(L/\bar{R})^{-1}\bar{R}^{-1}Q^{T}Q\bar{R}^{-1}&-\bar{R}^{-1}\bar{Q}^{T}(L/\bar{R})^{-1}\\
(L/\bar{R})^{-1}Q\bar{R}^{-1}&(L/\bar{R})^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(61)

and we get

8.
Conclusion and final remarks

Kinetic models have attained growing attention as an active area of research, bridging interacting particle systems with macroscopic continuum descriptions in fields ranging from rarefied gas dynamics to plasma physics and data-driven modeling. Among these, the FP equation offers a particularly attractive formulation, arising as the diffusive approximation of the Boltzmann equation in regimes where collisions are overwhelming. Yet, even in the classical setting of a monatomic dilute gas, the rigorous derivation of such approximations remains an open challenge.

Our study addresses this problem by introducing the FE-FP equation. The model brings together a consistent moment projection of the Boltzmann equation with an entropy-decay mechanism with favorable structure. The formulation is both analytically tractable and computationally affordable, requiring only marginal computational overhead compared to standard FP models. We provide theoretical justifications for the basic aspects of the model, including the entropy decay and the well-posedness of linear system underlying the model coefficients. The framework is also validated by numerical results on shock waves confirming the role of correct entropy production in shock profiles, showing close agreement with respect to the high-fidelity solution of the Boltzmann equation.

While the present study emphasizes on the model development, relying on standard numerical solvers for the resulting SDE systems, the FE–FP formulation is designed as a flexible framework upon which efficient, high-order integration schemes can be built. Such developments, aimed at improving convergence rate of resulting particle schemes, will be pursued in future work.

The FE-FP model supports arbitrary degrees of freedom in moment matching, makes it amenable to
a posteriori
error estimates. Following this path, the FE-FP model can be employed to build cascades of FP models, extending the application range of FP models to simulations of multiscale problems. More broadly, the proposed framework provides a path to building SDE models constrained by both moment conditions and entropy laws, with potential applications extending beyond kinetic theory to areas such as diffusion-based generative modeling and the Schrödinger bridge problem
[
19
]
.

Appendix A
Positive defineteness of
R[f]

Consider an arbitrary non-zero
z\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}z_{\alpha}R_{\alpha\beta}[f]z_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}z_{\alpha}\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v)\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{\beta\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}z_{\beta}\nabla_{v}H_{\beta}(v)\right),f\right\rangle

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}z_{\alpha}\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v)\right)^{2},f\right\rangle\ .

The right-hand side is clearly non-negative, so we only need to check whether
\sum_{\alpha\in\hat{I}_{2k}^{3}}z_{\alpha}\nabla_{v}H_{\alpha}(v)=0
. This is prevented due to the linear independence of
\{H_{\alpha}(v)\}_{\alpha\in\hat{I}^{3}_{2k}}
polynomials (by construct). Therefore the summation is strictly positive for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
. Besides the fact that
R[f]
is symmetric, this concludes the proof that
R[f]
is symmetric positive definite.
