Clinical and patient-reported predictors of days alive and out of hospital in decompensated cirrhosis
Authors: Sai Kiran Kuchana, Eric S. Orman, Kelly Mosesso, Timothy Stump, Patrick O. Monahan, Naga Chalasani, Archita P. Desai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Days alive and out of hospital (DAOH) is a patient-centered outcome, but has not been studied in decompensated cirrhosis (DCC). We aimed to quantify DAOH and to identify clinical and patient-reported factors associated with DAOH.Methods:Using an ongoing prospective cohort study of adults hospitalized with DCC, DAOH was calculated by subtracting days hospitalized, transplanted, or deceased from 90 days post-discharge from index hospitalization. Univariable and multivariable negative binomial hurdle models were used to identify clinical and patient-reported variables associated with DAOH, where DAOH was treated both as a categorical (any readmission, transplant, death vs. all 90-DAOH) and continuous (proportion of event days) outcome.Results:Among 363 adults (median age 54 y; MELD 3.0 score 23), 40% experienced all 90-DAOH while 90-day readmission, death, and transplant occurred in 49%, 21%, 11%, respectively. Mean DAOH was 68.3 (SD: 29.0). In multivariable models, transplant evaluation status (listed vs. not listed/evaluated: OR 7.55, p=0.004) was associated with experiencing an event within 90 days. In comparison, when considering the number of event days, higher PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles and Activities scores were independently associated with a lower proportion of event days (rate ratio, RR 0.91 per 3-point increase, p=0.030) in addition to MELD 3.0 >25 (vs. <15, RR 2.75, p<0.001).Conclusions:DAOH is a holistic, patient-centered outcome for decompensated cirrhosis that integrates readmission, transplantation, and mortality into a single measure of post-discharge burden. By using DAOH and moving beyond readmissions, we show that lower MELD 3.0 and higher patient-reported social well-being were independently associated with more days at home after hospitalization.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with decompensated cirrhosis have a higher risk of early post-discharge complications. These patients, on average, experience 3 hospitalizations per year, and the burden of 30-day and 90-day readmissions is ~26% and 50%, respectively, which is significantly higher than for other chronic diseases, such as chronic heart failure, resulting in an increased financial burden on both hospitals and patients.123 Mortality after hospitalization is also considerably high, reaching 13%–18% within 90 days post-discharge.14

Understanding the drivers of these frequent outcomes allows health systems to better deploy costly resources involved in post-hospitalization care.567 While any readmission is an important outcome, the post-discharge journey in decompensated cirrhosis is complex with competing risks of clinical outcomes such as repeated admissions, liver transplant (LT), and death. Therefore, only measuring readmissions and total hospital days does not capture the full post-discharge experience, particularly in the presence of competing risks such as death and LT. More importantly, patients are likely to care more about the number of days they are burdened by poor outcomes than any singular event. To this end, Days Alive and Out of Hospital (DAOH) have emerged as a holistic, patient-centered endpoint that integrates survival, rehospitalization burden, and time at home into a single continuous measure.891011 DAOH has been validated in perioperative medicine (including LT), cardiology, oncology, neurology, and pulmonology, where it correlates with quality of life and healthcare utilization and improves statistical power in clinical trials.1213141516171819 In cirrhosis, a prior national cohort study quantified days at home in cirrhosis, yet the clinical and patient-reported factors associated with 90-day post-discharge DAOH after hospitalization for decompensated cirrhosis remain unknown.11 To elucidate the novel patient-centered outcome of DAOH in hepatology, we aimed to quantify DAOH after an unplanned hospitalization for decompensated cirrhosis and identify the clinical and patient-reported factors associated with DAOH using an ongoing prospective study of individuals with decompensated cirrhosis.

METHODS

Study design

Within a prospective cohort study of adults (≥18 y old) with cirrhosis admitted to Indiana University Hospital, those enrolled between July 2020 and April 2025 were included in these analyses. The diagnosis of cirrhosis was based on liver histology or clinical, laboratory, and radiological findings as previously described.2021 Patients were approached for enrollment and consent during the first hospitalization occurring during the study period. Patients were excluded if they had previously undergone LT, were unable to consent to the study during their hospitalization, did not survive the hospitalization, or were discharged to hospice care (Figure 1). Participants were followed from admission through their hospitalization and up to 90 days post-discharge, LT or death, whichever occurred first. This study was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul and was approved by Indiana University’s Institutional Review Board. Written consent was given in writing by all subjects.

Consort diagram describing the flow of study participants.

Outcomes of interest

We calculated DAOH by subtracting days hospitalized or deceased during the 90-day follow-up period from 90 days. The 90 days were truncated for individuals who underwent LT (or simultaneous liver–kidney transplant, SLK) to reflect the number of days from discharge to transplant. We also measured rates of unplanned readmissions (excluding planned admission for transplant), LT, and mortality at both 30-day and 90-day intervals. Post-discharge outcomes were obtained from phone calls to participants, review of the Indiana University Health electronic health record (EHR, which covers 18 EDs and 15 hospitals in Indiana), review of obituary data, and the Indiana Network for Patient Care. This network is one of the largest health information exchanges in the United States, connecting >110 hospitals, >18,000 practices, and >50,000 providers across the state of Indiana.122

Variables of interest

We collected relevant sociodemographic and clinical variables through self-report or review of the EHR. Self-report was used to collect gender, race, ethnicity, level of education, income, marital status, living arrangement, current employment status, and alcohol use within 3 months of admission. Complications of cirrhosis [ascites, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), esophageal varices, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)] as well as the etiology of cirrhosis, were noted at baseline. Primary and secondary reasons for admission were determined by review of the admission and discharge notes and categorized. Lab results at both admissions and discharge (sodium, creatinine, bilirubin, albumin, and international normalized ratios) were used to calculate the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease [MELD] 3.0. In addition, the Child–Pugh score (CPS) was determined using patient characteristics at admission. The Social Deprivation Index (SDI) based on zip code at time of admission was derived as previously described, with a higher score denoting more disadvantage.23 Comorbidity burden was measured using a chart review to calculate the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (liver disease excluded) and ICD-10 codes within the participants' health record using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) as previously described.24

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) data were collected from participants after consent near the time of discharge from the hospital, and participants were asked to consider the past 7 days (akin to using MELD on admission, Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493). The PROMs encompassed physical function and health-related quality of life in 3 areas: functioning and well-being in physical, social, and mental domains of health. The PROMIS-29 Profile v2.0 was used to capture domains of HRQOL, including physical function, fatigue, depressive symptoms, anxiety, ability to participate in social roles and activities, sleep disturbance, and pain interference.212526 Each domain was scored in RedCap, while physical and mental summary scores were calculated as previously described.27 For each PROMIS domain, a mean T-score of 50 with a standard deviation (SD) of 10 is expected in the general US population, and higher scores indicate more of a domain measure (ie, a score of 60 indicates more physical function or, in contrast, more pain interference than a score of 50). Health literacy was measured at the time of enrollment using the Brief Health Literacy Survey (BHLS), which was scored as previously described.28 Ability to complete activities of daily living (ADL) was measured using the Functional Status Questionnaire (FSQ), which has demonstrated high reliability in many settings, including hospitalized patients. This survey was graded using predetermined cutoffs, with scores 0–100 as previously described.29 Frailty was also assessed by the Liver Frailty Index (LFI), which was measured at the time of enrollment.30

Statistical analysis

Demographic and baseline clinical variables were described with summary statistics, including median with interquartile range (IQR), mean with standard deviation (SD), and frequencies for categorial variables. All PROMS domains were scored as described above, with frequency of categories of PROM scores assessed as appropriate.

The distribution of DAOH was skewed in our study population; 40% of participants spent all 90 days post-discharge alive, and out of the hospital and without LT, or, in other words, zero days in the hospital, transplanted, or deceased (Supplemental Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C492). Therefore, we used negative binomial hurdle models, which are able to handle these excess zeros, to model the inverse of DAOH. Importantly, the use of the hurdle model provides output from 2 models. The hurdle component estimates associations between covariates and the probability of any outcome occurrence, whereas the conditional count component estimates associations between covariates and the number of outcome days among participants with at least one event.3132 In our primary analysis, we modeled both a yes/no outcome (reported as an odds ratio) of any event versus all 90 days DAOH (ie, zero days hospitalized, deceased, or transplanted; hurdle model) as well as the proportion of days hospitalized, deceased, or transplanted during the 90-day follow-up period (reported as an event rate ratio, conditional model). As transplantation is conceptually distinct from readmission or death, we completed a secondary analysis where we aimed to truncate the 90-day follow-up period by the number of days before LT. Specifically, we used an offset equal to the number of days the patient was alive without LT, which was included in the model. In this model, the hurdle model estimates the odds of experiencing at least 1 day hospitalized within 90 days of discharge before death/LT, while the conditional count model applies to patients with at least 1 hospitalization to estimate the percentage increase or decrease in the proportion of days spent in the hospital before death/LT during the 90-day follow-up.

Variables with p-values <0.10 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable models, apart from CPS and CCI due to collinearity with MELD 3.0 and HFRS, respectively. A zero-inflated model was also considered, but the hurdle model resulted in a slightly better fit as measured by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC, data not presented). For variables with missing data, we did not impute values; listwise deletion was used for the multivariate models.

All analyses were conducted in R, version 4.5.1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study cohort characteristics

A total of 363 patients were included in our cohort (Table 1), of which 146 (40%) experienced all 90 days alive and out of the hospital without LT. The median age at admission was 54.0 years (IQR: 43.5–63.0), 196 (54%) were male, 282 (78%) were White, and 33 (10%) were Hispanic or Latino. Educational attainment was high school or more in 36%. Most were insured through Medicare (34%) or Medicaid (39%). The SDI range was wide with a median of 61.0 (IQR: 33.0–87.0). The most common cause of cirrhosis was alcohol-associated liver disease (57%), followed by metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) (28%) and hepatitis C virus infection (16%). The median CCI was 5 (IQR 4–7). The median admission MELD 3.0 score was 23.0 (IQR 17–29). When categorized, 53 (14.6%) had MELD 3.0 <15, 171 (47.1%) had MELD 3.0 15–25, and 139 (38.3%) had MELD 3.0 >25. Most (69%) were not evaluated or not listed for LT/SLK, 54 (15%) were being evaluated but not listed, and 57 (16%) were listed for LT/SLK. Fluid overload was the most common reason for hospitalization (28.9%), followed by abdominal pain or gastrointestinal symptoms (16.0%), gastrointestinal bleeding (15.4%), encephalopathy (13.2%), and acute kidney injury or electrolyte abnormalities (9.9%). Compared with those with all 90-DAOH, those experiencing any readmission, death or LT were more likely to have greater than high school education (40% vs. 29%, p=0.033), have lower prevalence of alcohol-associated liver disease (52% vs. 65%, p=0.011), more frequently had encephalopathy as complication (65% vs. 49%, p=0.001) and MELD 3.0 >25 (46% vs. 27%, p<0.001). HFRS was also higher (median 11.7 vs. 9.9, p=0.006) and was more commonly listed for LT (25% vs. 2%, p<0.001).

Outcomes

Within 90 days of discharge, 146 (40%) experienced all 90-DAOH, while 177 (48.8%) were readmitted, 76 (20.9%) died, and 38 (10.5%) underwent LT (Table 3, Supplemental Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C492, and Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493). The mean (SD) number of hospital days during the 90-day follow-up period was 5.7 (9.3), the mean number of days alive before LT was 74.0 (27.5), and the mean number of days alive out of hospital was 68.3 (SD 29.0). Among patients who died or underwent LT within 90 days, the mean (SD) number of hospital days was 7.2 (8.8), the mean number of days alive before LT was 39.1 (25.1), and the mean number of days alive out of hospital was 31.9 (23.2).

Factors associated with the occurrence of any event: Readmission, transplant, or death

In univariate hurdle models, the occurrence of any event during the 90 days post-discharge, several baseline characteristics, and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were significantly associated with the odds of experiencing at least one event (readmission, LT, or death) within 90 days of discharge (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493). Variables with a univariable p-value ≤0.10 were then included in multivariable hurdle models. This included greater than high school education, alcohol-associated liver disease, HFRS, MELD 3.0, HE as a complication, primary admission categories, LFI, mean grip strength, PROMIS Physical Function, Fatigue, Ability to Participate in Social T-score, and LT evaluation and listing status.

In the multivariable hurdle model (Table 4), LT evaluation and listing status showed strong associations with the occurrence of any event. Patients evaluated but not listed had higher odds of experiencing at least one event (OR 2.46, 95% CI 0.90–6.77, p=0.08), and those listed for LT had markedly greater odds (OR 7.55, 95% CI 1.92–29.7, p=0.004). MELD 3.0 >25 demonstrated a similar positive association, although this did not reach statistical significance (vs. <15, OR 2.41, 95% CI 0.94–6.18, p=0.067). No other covariates, including HE as a complication, primary admission diagnosis categories, HFRS, LFI, mean grip strength, or PROs, remained independently associated with experiencing at least one event (readmission, LT, or death) within 90 days of discharge.

Factors associated with the number of days experiencing any event

Among patients who experienced at least one event within 90 days of discharge, several factors were associated with the proportion of follow-up days spent experiencing days hospitalized, deceased, or post-LT out of the 90-day follow-up period (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493). In the multivariable count model (Table 4), MELD 3.0 >25 remained independently associated with a 2.75-fold greater proportion of follow-up days spent experiencing an event (RR 2.75, 95% CI 1.51–5.00, p<0.001). Higher PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles scores were also independently associated with 9% reduction in days spent experiencing an event (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, p=0.030). LT listing continued to show a positive association with event burden, though this remained non-significant. No other covariates, including Social Deprivation Index, HE as a complication, ascites as a complication, primary admission diagnoses, or LFI mean grip strength, were significantly associated with the proportion of follow-up days spent experiencing an event.

Factors associated with having at least one hospitalization day

Univariable hurdle models were used to estimate the odds of experiencing at least 1 day of hospitalization before LT within 90 days of discharge. Variables with a univariable p-value ≤0.10 were then included in multivariable hurdle models. This included alcohol-associated liver disease, MELD 3.0 (categories), high school education, HFRS, HE as a complication, the primary admission diagnosis, LT evaluation/listing status, and several PROs (Supplemental Table S4, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493). Among PROs, a 1-point lower PROMIS physical function score corresponded to 3% greater odds (OR=0.97, p=0.012), each 1-point higher PROMIS fatigue score to 4% greater odds (OR=1.04, p=0.02), and each 3-point lower PROMIS ability to participate in social roles score to 9% greater odds (OR=0.91, p=0.012).

Supplemental Table S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493, summarizes the multivariable hurdle model estimating the odds of experiencing at least 1 day of hospitalization before LT or death within 90 days of discharge. MELD 3.0 remained independently associated with having at least 1 day of hospitalization. Compared with MELD 3.0 <15, MELD 3.0 >25 was associated with nearly three-fold higher odds of experiencing at least 1 hospitalization day (OR 2.77, 95% CI 1.09–7.08, p=0.033), while MELD 3.015–25 did not remain significant (OR 1.36, 95% CI 0.58–3.16, p=0.5). LT evaluation status also remained significant: patients evaluated but not listed had higher odds (OR 3.41, 95% CI 1.22–9.53, p=0.019); those listed for LT had the highest odds (OR 8.31, 95% CI 2.79–24.8, p<0.001). No other covariates, including educational attainment, alcohol-associated liver disease, HE, primary admission diagnosis categories, HFRS, and PROs (grip strength, PROMIS-29 domains, and FSQ Basic ADL), were independently associated with experiencing hospitalization.

Factors associated with the number of days spent hospitalized

Univariate conditional count models were applied to patients who experienced at least 1 hospitalization day to estimate the relative change in the number of days spent hospitalized during the 90-day follow-up. Variables with a p-value ≤0.10 were subsequently included in multivariable models; these included ascites as a complication, MELD 3.0 categories, HFRS, PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles (per 3-point change), and LT evaluation/listing status (Supplemental Table S4, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493).

In the multivariable count model (Supplemental Table S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C493), MELD 3.0>25 remained independently associated with a higher number of days spent hospitalized during the 90-day follow-up (vs. <15, RR 2.06, 95% CI 1.10–3.83, p=0.023). Each 1-point increase in HFRS remained significant (RR 1.02, 95% CI 1.00–1.04, p=0.023), and each 3-point increase in PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles was associated with a 7% reduction in the number of days spent hospitalized (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.88–0.99, p=0.030). Ascites as a complication and LT listing status were no longer significant.

DISCUSSION

Post-discharge outcomes following hospitalization for decompensated cirrhosis remain difficult to characterize because patients may experience multiple events, including readmission, death, and LT. In this study, we examined these outcomes by separating the post-discharge period into 2 analytically distinct elements. The first element was whether patients experienced any major event within 90 days of discharge. The second was the duration of time spent in adverse states after an event, measured by hospitalization days, days related to death or LT, and the summary metric, DAOH. This framework enabled us to identify whether the factors associated with post-discharge event occurrence differed from those associated with the number of days patients were burdened by the event.

In our study, the probability of experiencing any event (readmission, death, or LT) within 90 days after discharge was primarily associated with the MELD 3.0 score and LT evaluation or listing status. These findings are consistent with a systematic review of 26 studies by Orman et al,1 which found that higher MELD scores were associated with early readmission and mortality in most studies. PROMs, including PROMIS Physical Function, Fatigue, and Ability to Participate in Social Roles, were not independently associated with experiencing any event after adjustment for MELD 3.0 score, LT evaluation/listing status, and other covariates. This aligns with prior prospective data showing that the clinical parameters predominantly drive readmission, short-term mortality, and LT,67333435 although 1 prior study did show that emotional well-being and energy independently predicted short-term mortality.36 In addition, the same clinical severity markers that determined whether any post-discharge event occurred also determined whether patients experienced at least 1 hospitalization day before LT or death during the 90-day follow-up period, reinforcing that the occurrence of any early post-discharge outcomes is primarily driven by liver disease severity.

In contrast, a distinct pattern emerged when examining the number of days participants experienced these events. Among individuals who were readmitted, transplanted, or died within 90 days of discharge, the proportion of time spent experiencing these events was not only independently associated with MELD 3.0 but also with scores in PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles and Activities. This tool is designed to assess an individual’s perceived capacity to engage in usual social activities and responsibilities, capturing role participation rather than discrete physical symptoms or fatigue. Similarly, among patients with at least 1 post-discharge hospitalization, the number of days spent hospitalized during follow-up was independently associated with MELD 3.0 score, HFRS, and PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles.

Together, these findings indicate that while liver disease severity primarily determines the occurrence of post-discharge events, social functioning and comorbidities meaningfully influence the duration and burden of these outcomes once they occur. This is in line with our prior study, which showed that self-reported functional status measures provide modest incremental value over models based on routinely collected clinical variables in identifying patients at risk of readmission.2037 In cirrhosis, prior reviews of PROs have identified social functioning and participation as core domains of patient experience and have emphasized that disease severity, complications, and hospitalization strongly shape impairments in social functioning and HRQOL. Our study adds to this literature by identifying social functioning as an independent factor associated with a greater number of DAOH. Furthermore, these data support the notion that PROs complement, rather than replace, clinical severity indices in explaining healthcare utilization.353738394041 This is not surprising given that MELD 3.0 scores and Child–Pugh classification were developed for transplant allocation and short-term survival prediction, but not to predict burden of events.42 The PROMIS social participation domain itself has undergone rigorous psychometric development and validation, including in populations with advanced chronic illness, supporting its use as a robust measure of role functioning across diverse disease states.43 Within this established framework, our findings indicate that social role participation is specifically associated with the number of DAOH, rather than with the initial occurrence of those events, even after adjusting for clinical indicators of disease severity and comorbidity burden.

Finally, our findings support the use of DAOH as a more patient-centered, continuous endpoint for outcomes studies in decompensated cirrhosis, as it allows study teams to integrate survival, rehospitalization burden, and time spent at home. Prior study by Elhence et al11 previously quantified days at home in a national claims-based cohort of patients with cirrhosis and showed that decompensated cirrhosis was associated with substantial loss of days at home across acute-care, post-acute, emergency, and observational settings. Prior studies have also looked at DAOH in the hepatology setting, but are limited to the peri-transplant setting, where DAOH was validated as an outcome.1718 We not only build on these studies, but our results are also in line with studies in other related clinical fields, where DAOH is increasingly being used as a summary measure of recovery and healthcare utilization.9101213141519 In our cohort, patients who remained alive without LT spent a mean of 85 days out of the hospital during the 90-day follow-up, whereas those who died or underwent LT spent only 32 days out of the hospital. These findings demonstrate substantial loss of time at home driven by post-discharge readmissions, death, and LT. These differences also indicate that post-discharge outcomes in decompensated cirrhosis should be measured not only by the occurrence of adverse events but also by the cumulative time patients remain alive and at home without LT, as this more accurately reflects disease severity, comorbidity burden, and health-related quality of life.44

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, inclusion of robust measurements of PROs, comorbidities, and frailty alongside clinical variables, and the use of DAOH as a novel outcome. In addition, our study is unique in exploring DAOH as a binary and continuous variable, which is important in the clinical setting of decompensated cirrhosis, where 90-day outcomes occur frequently.912 Despite these strengths, our study has a few limitations. All participants received care at a tertiary care hospital with a large LT center. Future studies validating DAOH as an outcome and the role of PROs in shaping this outcome are needed in a diverse patient population. Furthermore, our DAOH measure captured time alive and out of acute-care hospitalization before death or LT, but did not comprehensively capture time spent in skilled nursing facilities, inpatient rehabilitation, observation units, emergency department encounters not resulting in admission, home health, or other post–acute care settings. Therefore, our outcome should be interpreted as DAOH rather than a comprehensive days-at-home measure capturing the full spectrum of healthcare-contact days.11 In addition, we did not explore different durations of follow-up when evaluating DAOH, such as DAOH at 30 or 365 days. While 90 days post-discharge is a commonly used time period to understand health care outcomes in decompensated cirrhosis, it is possible that longer or shorter durations of follow-up would yield different associations.79303445 Finally, the association between PROs and DAOH was modest. This may be due to the severity of illness in a hospitalized study population. The role of PROs and the association with outcomes in outpatients with decompensated and compensated cirrhosis are urgently needed, as they may serve to target individuals most in need of targeted interventions to reduce healthcare utilization.

Patient-centered outcomes are needed as our field improves the value of interventions, especially for those with chronic and/or end-stage disease.3946 We show that DAOH is an easily measured outcome that is a valid and meaningful outcome in decompensated cirrhosis and offers advantages over traditional outcomes such as readmission or composite outcomes of death and LT. Our analytic approach of exploring DAOH through hurdle models provides guidance to future work using composite and continuous outcomes such as DAOH in decompensated cirrhosis. Through our approach, we show value in evaluating all dimensions of DAOH and provide a patient-centered approach to post-discharge risk stratification in decompensated cirrhosis by distinguishing factors associated with any event occurrence from those associated with prolonged post-discharge burden. Our study shows that liver disease severity, reflected by MELD 3.0 scores and LT evaluation/listing status, identifies patients at the highest risk for readmission, death, or LT following discharge. In contrast, the PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles identifies those at higher risk of prolonged hospitalization or transplant-free survival. Measurement of social role participation may represent a patient-centered metric associated with future hospitalization burden and healthcare use, complementing traditional disease-severity-based triage.
