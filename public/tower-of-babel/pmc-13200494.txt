Quercetin ameliorates PCOS ovarian fibrosis by modulating short-chain fatty acid synthesis via increased akkermansia muciniphila abundance
Authors: Xi He, Peiyin Yang, Shuxuan Zhang, Kailu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder causing anovulatory infertility, characterized by critical ovarian fibrosis that impairs follicular development. Current pharmacological treatments for ovarian fibrosis in PCOS are limited. Quercetin, a natural flavonoid found in plants and traditional medicine, shows therapeutic promise for PCOS, though its direct effects on ovarian fibrosis and gut microbiota-mediated mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsThe PCOS mouse model was established by administering letrozole in combination with a high-fat diet. To determine the optimal therapeutic dosage, PCOS mice were randomly assigned to three quercetin treatment groups: low-dose group (PCOS + LT), medium-dose group (PCOS + T), and high-dose group (PCOS + HT). Body weight and serum hormone levels (LH, FSH, T and AMH) were systematically measured across all groups to determine the most effective concentration. Reproductive function was assessed by monitoring the estrous cycle (vaginal smears). Insulin resistance was evaluated using glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT). After the medium dose was identified as the optimal intervention dose of quercetin, subsequent experiments were conducted to investigate the underlying mechanisms. Ovarian tissue was analyzed for morphology, inflammation and fibrosis. Additionally, the intestinal mucosal structure and permeability were examined. Feces were collected for comprehensive gut microbiota composition analysis (16S rRNA gene sequencing) and fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) analysis, followed by a correlation analysis to link gut changes with physiological and ovarian outcomes. Furthermore, a fibrotic cell model was established in KGN cells via dihydrotestosterone (DHT)-induction to investigate the regulatory effects of acetic acid at different concentrations on the TGF-β signaling pathway.ResultsOur research indicated that medium-dose quercetin restored PCOS mice estrous cyclicity, improved insulin sensitivity, and ameliorated the level of ovarian fibrosis. 16S rRNA sequencing results showed that quercetin significantly increased the proportion of Akkermansia in the mouse gut microbiota. Furthermore, quercetin promoted the content of several short-chain fatty acids, particularly acetate. HE staining of the mouse intestinal mucosa revealed that quercetin was able to alleviate the structural disorder of the intestinal mucosa. Furthermore, quercetin promoted the expression of the intestinal barrier proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in PCOS mice and significantly improved intestinal mucosal permeability. In vitro studies revealed that acetic acid modulates TGF-β1 expression through the suppression of SMAD3 phosphorylation and the promotion of SMAD7 expression.ConclusionOur findings suggest quercetin alleviates PCOS-related ovarian fibrosis by modulating gut microbiota and promoting SCFA production. This research highlights a novel mechanism for treating PCOS-related ovarian fibrosis, warranting further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02085-y.

Introduction

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine-metabolic disorder affecting women of reproductive age. It’s primarily characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology [1]. Ovarian fibrosis stands as a pivotal pathological hallmark of PCOS, significantly impairing female reproductive health. It is characterized by excessive fibroblast proliferation and the abnormal accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, particularly collagen fibers, within the ovarian parenchyma. This pathological remodeling ultimately leads to disrupted ovarian architecture and impaired physiological function [2]. Ovarian fibrosis can induce elevated cortical thickness and stiffness, which in turn establishes a mechanical barrier hindering proper follicular growth and ovulation. The resultant failure of mature follicles to ovulate leads to their accumulation in a cystic state, forming the characteristic polycystic ovarian morphology seen in PCOS [3]. Furthermore, fibrosis alters the ovarian microenvironment, impacting the transport of nutrients and signaling molecules within follicles, thereby inhibiting their further development [4]. Currently, the exact mechanisms behind the development of ovarian fibrosis are not entirely clear. It’s hypothesized to arise from the complex interplay of several contributing factors, including repeated chronic inflammatory stimuli, the overproduction of the profibrotic factor TGF-β, and an imbalance in extracellular matrix synthesis and degradation [5]. Current conventional PCOS treatments predominantly address sex hormone regulation and insulin resistance, yet a direct pharmacological intervention for ovarian fibrosis remains unavailable. Consequently, further in-depth investigation into drug therapies targeting ovarian fibrosis in PCOS is imperative. Such advancements are anticipated not only to resolve a fundamental fertility concern for PCOS patients but also to ameliorate their endocrine dysfunction and related symptomatology. This comprehensive and fundamental therapeutic strategy would hold profound implications for enhancing the quality of life and health outcomes of individuals with PCOS.

Quercetin, a prominent flavonol, is widely distributed in various dietary sources, including onions, oranges, bell peppers, and olive oil [6]. Furthermore, quercetin is recognized as a crucial functional ingredient in numerous traditional Chinese medicinal prescriptions [7, 8]. Recent clinical investigations have revealed that the inclusion of flavonoid-rich foods in daily diets significantly lowers the risk of chronic metabolic disorders [9], this may be related to quercetin’s antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory effects. Evidence indicates that quercetin exerts therapeutic effects on PCOS through diverse pathways, concurrently improving androgen levels and insulin resistance in patients [10]. Additionally, quercetin is recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties, as it can inhibit cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase, and also reduce C-reactive protein and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [11]. Due to quercetin’s low aqueous solubility, its absorption by the human body is limited. This results in a very low oral bioavailability of about 2% following a single administration [12]. Consequently, many researchers believe that the gut microbiota may be a key mediator in quercetin’s efficacy. Recent studies indicate that quercetin can improve gut microbiota composition and influence the synthesis of gut microbiota metabolites [13]. Additionally, quercetin can improve intestinal mucosal permeability, reducing the entry of substances like LPS into the bloodstream and thereby alleviating the systemic inflammatory response [14]. Previous studies have indicated that quercetin possesses the ability to ameliorate fibrosis in various tissues. For instance, quercetin can improve lung pathological tissue scores and the expression of lung fibrosis markers in animal models of pulmonary fibrosis [15]. Regarding hepatic fibrosis, research has demonstrated that quercetin confers a prominent inhibitory action. A dosage range of 5 to 200 mg, delivered over a 4–8 weeks regimen, exhibited particularly profound improvements in both liver function and the extent of liver fibrosis. Consequently, quercetin may represent a viable therapeutic compound for hepatic fibrosis [16]. The influence of quercetin on the gut microbiota distribution in PCOS mouse models, as well as the link between the synthesis of associated SCFAs and ovarian fibrosis, are currently not well understood and require further investigation.

This study aims to comprehensively elucidate the therapeutic effects of quercetin on ovarian fibrosis using both a mouse model of PCOS and a fibrotic KGN cell model induced by DHT. We will first assess the direct influence of quercetin on fibrotic progression by administering it concurrently following PCOS induction. A central objective is to then investigate how quercetin impacts the gut microbiota structure and SCFA levels, thus exploring the potential role of the gut microbiota-SCFA axis in ameliorating PCOS-associated ovarian fibrosis. To discern the underlying molecular mechanisms, we will quantify the expression of pro-fibrotic factor genes in ovarian tissues and perform a thorough correlational analysis between SCFA content and these ovarian factors. Our findings are anticipated to yield novel therapeutic strategies and mechanistic insights for PCOS management, particularly by targeting ovarian fibrosis and modulating the intestinal microbiota.

Methods

Animals

Forty SPF female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were included. The experimental animals were provided by the Animal Center of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. Forty SPF female mice were randomly divided into three groups a: the control group (CON group, n = 8); PCOS group [1 mg/kg letrozole dissolved in 1% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), continuously administered for 35 days, and fed a high-fat diet, n = 8], PCOS + LT group [62.5 mg/kg Quercetin bw orally daily for 45 days, n = 8], PCOS-T group [125 mg/kg Quercetin bw orally daily for 45 days, n = 8] and PCOS + HT group [250 mg/kg bw Quercetin orally daily for 45 days, n = 8]. The concentration of quercetin used was based on previously published literature [17]. After the last administration, all mice weights were measured. This experiment protocol for the research project has been approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics No.SYXK2023-0076).

Mouse estrous cycle monitoring

During the final 10 days of the animal experiments, the estrous cycle of the mice was monitored daily using vaginal smear cytology. To collect samples, secretions were gently obtained from the lateral vaginal wall using a sterile cotton swab lightly moistened with physiological saline (or normal saline). The collected cellular material was then thinly and evenly smeared onto a clean glass slide. The smears were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with Giemsa solution for 45 min. The slides were then analyzed under a microscope, and the cellular composition of the vaginal smears—specifically the ratio of leukocytes, epithelial cells, and cornified cells—was determined to identify the specific phase of the estrous cycle.

Collection of samples

After weight measurement, fecal samples were collected by the rectal compression method and stored at -80 °C immediately. Blood (0.5-1 mL) was collected from the posterior orbital vein plexus after cervical dislocation.

Mouse Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT)

Mice designated for the test were fasted for 16 h prior to the experiment, with free access to drinking water. A 20% glucose solution was prepared by dissolving 2 g of glucose in 10mL of physiological saline. This solution was then filtered and prepared into a concentrated sugar water for injection at a final volume of 10 µL/g of body weight. First, the fasting blood glucose (FBG) level was measured. Venous blood was collected from the mouse tail, applied to a glucose test strip, and the reading was taken using a glucometer. Following body weight measurement, the required glucose dose was calculated at 1 g/kg of body weight. The glucose solution was administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injection using a 1mL syringe. Blood glucose concentrations were subsequently measured at the following time points: 15 min, 30 min, 60 min, and 120 min post-injection. Finally, the blood glucose values at all time points were used to plot a glucose-time curve, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) was calculated.

Mouse Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT)

Mice designated for the test were fasted for 4 h, with only free access to drinking water. An insulin solution was prepared by diluting insulin with physiological saline. The mice were weighed using a precision balance, and FBG was measured by collecting venous blood from the tail vein. Insulin was then administered to the mice via IP injection using a 1mL syringe at a dose of 0.75 U/kg of body weight. Blood glucose concentrations were subsequently measured at the following time points: 30 min, 60 min, 90 min, and 120 min post-injection. The AUC was calculated to evaluate insulin sensitivity.

ELISA detection

Blood samples were allowed to naturally coagulate at room temperature for 10–20 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected and immediately stored at − 80℃ until analysis. Serum levels of Luteinizing Hormone (LH) (Mlbio, ml001984, China), Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) (Mlbio, ml001910M, China), Anti-Mullerian Hormone (AMH) (Elabscinece, E-EL-M3015, China) and Testosterone (T) (Mlbio, ml037278, China) were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intestinal barrier function assays

Mice were fasted for 4 h prior to oral gavage with 0.6 mg/g fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (MedChemExpress, China). Four hours after administration, the fluorescence intensity of plasma was determined at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and emission wavelength of 520 nm.

Detection of fecal SCFAs by gas chromatography (GC-MS)

The SCFAs in mouse fecal samples were analyzed using an Agilent GC-MS/MS (7890B-7000D). Samples were extracted by bead-milling and ultrasonication in a pH 2.0-2.5 phosphoric acid solution. After centrifugation, the supernatant was precisely aliquoted for LLE using MTBE containing an internal standard (2-ethylbutyric acid or deuterated n-hexanoic acid-d3). The organic extract was collected for GC-MS/MS analysis and stored at -20℃.

A high-polarity DB-FFAP column and splitless injection were used. The gradient temperature program (initial 2-min hold, ramped to 250℃) ensured baseline SCFA isomer separation. Mass detection was performed in MRM mode using EI ionization.

Quantitation utilized a 13-point calibration curve (internal standard concentration: 10μg/mL). Absolute SCFAs content was calculated from the analyte-to-internal standard peak area ratio using the linear equation, incorporating the sample dilution factor. Method stability was monitored by injecting one QC sample per 10 test samples.

16SrRNA high-throughput sequencing

Fecal samples were first collected from all mouse groups, followed by bacterial DNA extraction from all collected samples. The 16S rRNA gene comprises nine variable regions V1-V9. For this study, the V3-V4 region was selected for amplification, suitable for the Illumina Novaseq sequencing platform. Primers incorporating Illumina adapter sequences were designed for the V3-V4 region: the forward primer was CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG, and the reverse primer was GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC. Specific Barcodes and Illumina sequencing adapter sequences were added to the 5’ end of the primers, and 1–3 degenerate bases (Mismatches) were introduced into the reverse primer to minimize primer-dimer formation. Magnetic beads were added to the PCR product at a1:1.8(v/v) ratio. After thorough mixing and a 5-minute incubation, the mixture was subjected to magnetic adsorption, washed with 70% ethanol, and dried. The purified product was finally eluted and recovered using 20-30uL of enzyme-free water. Following purification, the product concentration was quantified using Qubit, requiring a minimum concentration of > 5ng/uL and a total amount of > 100ng per sample for subsequent library construction. The 16S rRNA sequencing-specific library was constructed based on the purified PCR products. Illumina proprietary adapters were ligated to the purified PCR product using a 1:10 − 1:20 adapter-to-product concentration ratio in a ‘Y’-shaped adapter ligation reaction to prevent self-ligation due to excess adapters. Fragment selection was performed using magnetic beads: 0.8 volume ratio of beads was used to remove short fragments, followed by a 1.2 volume ratio to recover the target library fragments. The library was then enriched via PCR, incorporating Index primers into the reaction system for demultiplexing after sequencing. Upon completion, the library fragment size was verified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, library concentration was quantified via Qubit, and effective concentration was determined by Q-PCR to ensure sequencing quality. High-Throughput Sequencing High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform using the paired-end sequencing mode, which is suitable for the V3-V4 fragment length and ensures complete coverage of the target region. The sequencing depth was set at ≥ 50,000 effective reads per sample for reliable analysis of the gut microbiota community. Prior to sequencing, multiple libraries were pooled at equimolar concentrations and mixed with 5%-10% PhiX control. All pre-amplification and sequencing procedures were performed by Nanjing Fengzi Biological Technology Co., Ltd. strictly following the Novaseq sequencer operating guidelines.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). For cDNA synthesis, the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme) was employed to generate complementary DNA. Gene expression was subsequently quantified by real-time PCR, utilizing ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. Relative mRNA abundance was normalized to GAPDH mRNA levels, serving as an internal control. The primer sequences used for qPCR were as follows. TGF-β: 5’- CCACCTGCAAGACCATCGAC-3’(forward) and 5’- CTGGCGAGCCTTAGTTTGGAC − 3’(reverse); IL-6: 5’- TACCACTTCACAAGTCGGAGGC − 3’(forward) and 5’- CTGCAAGTGCATCATCGTTGTTC − 3’(reverse); TNF-α: 5’- GGTGCCTATGTCTCAGCCTCTT − 3’ (forward), 5’- GCCATAGAACTGATGAGAGGGAG − 3’(reverse); GAPDH: 5’- CATCACTGCCACCCAGAAGACTG − 3’(forward) and 5’- ATGCCAGTGAGCTTCCCGTTCAG − 3’(reverse).

Western blot

Collect mouse ovarian tissue, add RIPA lysis buffer, and perform on-ice lysis for 30 min. Collect the samples and centrifuge at 12,000 g for 30 min. Samples were denatured by adding loading buffer and boiling at 95 °C for 10 min. Proteins were then separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane at 250 mA for 80 min.The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk powder in TBST for one hour at room temperature. Following blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting IL-6 (Proteintech, 26404-1-AP, 1:1000), TNF-α(Proteintech, 29652-1-AP, 1:1000), ZO-1(Proteintech, 21773-1-AP, 1:5000), Occludin (Proteintech, 27260-1-AP, 1:5000), TGF-β1 (Abcam, AB179695, 1:1000), Collagen 1α (Col1α) (Proteintech, 67288-1-Ig, 1:1000), α-SMA (Proteintech, 14395-1-AP, 1:1000), SMAD7 (Proteintech, 25840-1-AP, 1:1000), SMAD3 (Abcam, AB208182, 1:1000) and p-SMAD3 (Abcam, AB63403,1:1000).The next day, membranes were washed three times with TBST and subsequently incubated with an HRP-conjugated sheep anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. After three additional TBST washes, protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence. The relative expression of each target protein was quantified by determining the ratio of its grayscale value to that of Tubulin, which served as a loading control.

Cell culture

The human ovarian granulosa cell line KGN was cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA), and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The 500 nM DHT (KKL, China) in DMEM/F12 media was used in KGN cells for 24 h in 6-well cell plates. The concentration of DHT used was referenced from relevant literature [18]. After treatment with DHT, granulosa cells were incubated with acetic acid (AA) (MedChemExpress, China) at different concentrations (25 mM and 50 mM) in DMEM/F12 medium for 12 h.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 7. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA. For post-hoc analysis, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was employed to determine significant differences between groups. Multiple comparisons were controlled by adjusting the p-values within the Tukey procedure to maintain the family-wise error rate at 0.05. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) based on Brecurtis were used. Specific contents are shown in the Supporting Information. The data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations or medians and interquartile intervals. Univariate analysis was used for intergroup comparisons, and independent sample t-tests and nonparametric tests were used for pairwise comparisons. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Quercetin ameliorates the PCOS phenotype in mice

C57BL/6 female mice were used to establish a PCOS mouse model by co-treating them with letrozole and a high-fat diet. This model was subsequently utilized to investigate the effects of Quercetin on metabolism and ovarian function. Based on a review of the relevant literature, we initially tested a range of concentrations to determine the optimal effective concentration of Quercetin [17] (Fig. 1A). Comparative analysis of body weight across all groups showed that mice in the PCOS group exhibited a significant increase in mass. In contrast, PCOS mice treated with the medium concentration of quercetin displayed a reduction in body weight compared to the untreated PCOS controls, suggesting that an appropriate dose of quercetin may ameliorate PCOS-associated obesity. Importantly, this effect was not observed in the low- or high-concentration quercetin treatment groups (Fig. 1B). Serum sex hormone levels were next compared. It was found that LH levels and the LH/FSH ratio were both markedly elevated in PCOS mice compared to normal controls. Similarly, serum levels of AMH and T were also increased in the PCOS group. Treatment with both the low and medium (PCOS + LT and PCOS + T) concentrations of quercetin significantly reduced the elevated levels of LH, T, and AMH in the PCOS mice, indicating that both concentrations had a beneficial effect on key hormonal features of PCOS. However, these therapeutic effects were absent in the high-concentration quercetin (PCOS + HT) group (Fig. 1C). Based on these comprehensive findings, we selected the medium concentration of quercetin for all subsequent mechanistic experiments. The estrous cycle was then analyzed across the groups. Vaginal smears were collected from the mice and subsequently stained using the Giemsa method (Fig. 1D). Analysis of the resulting cytology revealed that the PCOS group exhibited a disrupted/irregular estrous cycle. Critically, quercetin intervention was shown to effectively restore the regularity of the estrous cycle in the PCOS mice (Fig. 1E and F).

Quercetin ameliorates insulin sensitivity and ovarian fibrosis in PCOS mice

Ovarian HE staining results showed that the ovaries in the PCOS model group exhibited polycystic changes compared to the control group, specifically characterized by cystic expansion and increased numbers of follicles, along with an increase in secondary follicles, antral follicles, and atretic follicles. In contrast, HE staining of ovaries from the PCOS + T group revealed a reduction in cystic follicles and an increase in corpora lutea, suggesting that quercetin has a beneficial effect on PCOS (Fig. 2A). Next, the insulin sensitivity of the mice was assessed across all groups. The results showed that PCOS mice exhibited reduced insulin sensitivity: blood glucose levels rapidly increased after glucose injection, but the subsequent decline was significantly slower compared to the control group. Conversely, the rate of blood glucose decline in the PCOS + T group was accelerated compared to the PCOS group, suggesting that quercetin significantly improves both glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity in PCOS mice (Fig. 2B and C). Previous studies have indicated that quercetin significantly improves inflammation in PCOS mice. Consequently, the protein expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α and TGF-β were detected in the three groups of mice. It was found that the inflammatory level in the PCOS group was significantly increased, while the inflammatory level in the PCOS + T group was decreased (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, we compared the ovarian fibrosis levels among the three groups of mice using the fibrosis markers α-SMA and Col1α. Immunohistochemical results and the Western blot results demonstrated a significant increase in ovarian fibrosis levels in the PCOS group, while the PCOS + T group showed reduced fibrosis, indicating that quercetin can ameliorate PCOS ovarian fibrosis (Fig. 2D and E).

Quercetin affects the microbial diversity and composition of PCOS mice

To begin, we analyzed the microbial diversity and richness based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The number of Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) remained largely consistent across the three groups (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we assessed α-Diversity, a measure of microbiota variety, using several indices. As illustrated in the box plots (Figs. 3B, C, D, E), the Chao1 index and the Observed Species index were significantly reduced in the PCOS group compared to the Control group (p < 0.01). Notably, the Shannon index significantly increased in the treatment group compared to the PCOS group (p < 0.05), demonstrating that quercetin positively influences gut microbiota diversity. Furthermore, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) revealed a significant difference in the overall microbiota distribution (β-diversity) among the three groups, as evidenced by the distinct clustering and clear separation of samples between groups (Fig. 3F).

The specific composition of the intestinal flora in three groups

To investigate the specific distribution and composition of the intestinal flora across the three experimental groups, a detailed analysis was conducted at both the phylum and genus levels. Taxonomic analysis at the phylum level revealed that the fecal microbiota in each group primarily consisted of Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobia, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria (Fig. 4A). Moving to the family level, the predominant families included Lactobacillaceae, Verrucomicrobiaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococcaceae (Fig. 4B). Next, the taxonomic distribution of the microbiota at the genus level was depicted across the three mouse groups, revealing distinct patterns. A higher concentration of Akkermansia bacteria and a lower percentage of Lactobacilli were observed in the PCOS group. The PCOS + T group exhibited even more pronounced variations in microbiota distribution, with a further increase in Akkermansia and a further decline in Lactobacilli proportion (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 1A). To identify the dominant flora within each group at the genus level, pairwise LEfSe analysis was performed (LDA score > 3.0 and P < 0.05). This analysis revealed distinct dominant microbial taxa among the different groups of mice. In the control group, Lactobacillaceae was predominantly present. Conversely, in the PCOS group, the dominant microbial taxa were Enterobacteriaceae and Rikenellaceae. Quercetin influences the synthesis of intestinal short-chain fatty acids and intestinal mucosal permeability

The contents of different short chain fatty acids in three groups of feces are shown in Fig. 5A. Histograms were drawn to display the difference in the content of main SCFAs among different samples. Compared with the Control group, the acetic acid content in the feces of the PCOS group decreased significantly (Fig. 5B, p < 0.01), the content of butyric acid and propionic acid also decreased (Fig. 5B). Compared with the PCOS group, the levels of butyric acid and propionic acid in the feces of the quercetin-treated group increased, suggesting that quercetin induces broader SCFA remodeling (Fig. 5B). Spearman analysis of gut microbiota and short-chain fatty acids revealed a positive correlation between Lactobacillus and PA (propionic acid) and AA (acetic acid). On the other hand, Lachnoclostridium and Lachnospiraceae exhibited a negative correlation with PA and AA (Fig. 5C). The integrity of the intestinal barrier function is closely associated with the gut microbiota. Specifically, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) provide energy for intestinal mucosal cells and promote the maintenance of mucosal barrier integrity. We collected intestinal tissue from the three groups of mice for H&E staining. The results showed that the control group exhibited intact intestinal mucosal structure, with epithelial cells being complete and regularly aligned. In contrast, the PCOS group displayed impaired intestinal mucosal structural integrity, characterized by necrosis or shedding of epithelial cells. Notably, quercetin intervention restored the integrity of the intestinal mucosa (Fig. 5D). Additionally, we assessed intestinal permeability across the groups using the FITC-dextran assay. The results showed that intestinal permeability was significantly increased in PCOS mice, suggesting that the mucosal barrier function is compromised in PCOS (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we examined the expression levels of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin. We found that quercetin treatment increased the expression levels of both proteins in the mouse intestine (Fig. 5F). This impairment facilitates the translocation of substances like LPS across the gut wall into the systemic circulation, which can lead to a range of adverse effects. Conversely, quercetin intervention reduced intestinal permeability, which is beneficial for decreasing LPS entry into the bloodstream and, consequently, mitigating inflammatory responses.

The short-chain fatty acid-acetic acid reduces TGF-β expression by regulating the SMAD3/7 pathway

Ovarian tissues were collected from three distinct groups of mice, and RNA was subsequently extracted using TRIzol reagent. The mRNA expression levels of TGF-β, IL-6, and TNF-α in these ovarian tissues were then quantified using qPCR. The results revealed a significant increase in TGF-β expression within the ovaries of mice in the PCOS group. Furthermore, the expression of the inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α also increased in the PCOS group (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Notably, quercetin treatment significantly reduced ovarian TGF-β expression in the PCOS group (Fig. 6A). Separate correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between three types of SCFAs and TGF-β. The results demonstrated that all three SCFAs were negatively correlated with TGF-β expression (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Among these, acetate displayed the strongest negative correlation coefficient (Fig. 6B), a finding consistent with previous studies on fibrosis [19]. By stimulating KGN cells with DHT, we found that the TGF-β pathway was significantly activated within the KGN cells, and SMAD3 phosphorylation was increased. Conversely, the high concentration of acetic acid (HAA + DHT) significantly increased SMAD7 expression, inhibited SMAD3 phosphorylation, and reduced TGF-β expression. Furthermore, the low concentration acetic acid group also showed similar results, but the effect was less significant than that of the high-concentration group (Fig. 6C). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between gut flora composition and both fibrosis and inflammatory factors. The findings indicated a negative correlation between Akkermansia bacteria and the expression of TGF-β and inflammatory factors. Conversely, Lactobacillus bacteria exhibited a positive correlation with these markers. Unlike previous studies, our analysis included three groups of mice, revealing a positive correlation between various Lactobacillus species and TGF-β and inflammatory factors. This could be attributed to the fact that quercetin did not increase the expression of Lactobacillus (Fig. 6D). Based on these findings, it is speculated that quercetin may promote the abundance of Akkermansia bacteria. This increase in Akkermansia, in turn, is hypothesized to enhance acetate production, thereby inhibiting PCOS ovarian fibrosis.

Discussion

PCOS is a complex endocrine-metabolic disorder primarily affecting women of reproductive age. In recent years, the incidence of PCOS has continuously risen, influenced by factors such as lifestyle stress and environmental pollution [20, 21]. It’s estimated that the prevalence of PCOS in women of reproductive age may be as high as 8–13% [22]. While the precise mechanisms of PCOS aren’t fully understood, a multifactorial progression is widely accepted. Recent studies emphasize ovarian fibrosis as a critical factor in ovulatory dysfunction associated with PCOS. This fibrosis impedes normal follicular development and ovulation, ultimately impairing oocyte quality and elevating miscarriage risk [23, 24]. Furthermore, ovarian fibrosis accelerates PCOS disease progression and increases the incidence of ovarian cancer [3]. TGF-β overexpression is the most critical factor initiating fibrosis. TGF-β activates fibroblasts to synthesize the ECM, promoting the production of Type I and Type III collagen, fibronectin, and laminin [25]. Furthermore, TGF-β upregulates the expression of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) while potentially suppressing the activity of matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs). This dual action diminishes ECM breakdown, resulting in its accumulation [26]. Research indicates that aberrant increases in ovarian TGF-β levels are associated with compromised follicular development and ovulatory defects [27]. Ovarian fibrosis impedes normal follicular growth and rupture, leading to anovulation or oligo-ovulation, which severely compromises the fertility of PCOS patients [28].The precise etiology and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of ovarian fibrosis in PCOS remain incompletely elucidated. It is hypothesized that chronic ovarian inflammation and hyperandrogenism may play contributing roles. Consequently, gaining more profound insights into the pathogenesis of PCOS-associated ovarian fibrosis and discovering new therapeutic targets are of paramount importance to reproductive medicine.

In recent years, the role of the gut microbiota in PCOS has garnered widespread attention. PCOS patients frequently exhibit alterations in microbial composition and diversity, with significant shifts in the abundance of certain specific bacterial groups. Specifically, there’s often an imbalance in the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes in the gut of PCOS patients, characterized by a decrease in Firmicutes and an increase in Bacteroidetes [29]. Additionally, PCOS is associated with a reduced abundance of beneficial bacteria, including Lactobacillus and Akkermansia species. This gut dysbiosis can impact PCOS through various pathways. For example, branched-chain amino acids, which are products of gut microbiota, can affect insulin sensitivity and thereby exacerbate insulin resistance in PCOS [30]. Furthermore, gut dysbiosis can lead to increased intestinal permeability, allowing bacterial endotoxins to enter the bloodstream. This activates the immune system, triggering persistent chronic low-grade inflammation [31]. Chronic low-grade inflammation not only triggers insulin resistance but also impairs ovarian function, exacerbating ovulatory dysfunction. Bile acids are steroid molecules synthesized from cholesterol in the liver, primarily categorized into primary and secondary bile acids. In PCOS patients, altered gut microbiota composition and function lead to changes in bile acid transformation and enterohepatic recirculation. Different bile acids exhibit varying affinities and activation capabilities for FXR (Farnesoid X Receptor) and TGR5 (Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5). A reduction in secondary bile acids in PCOS patients affects the transmission of FXR and TGR5 signaling pathways, thereby exacerbating insulin resistance and lipid metabolism disorders [32].

Quercetin, one of the most abundant flavonoids found in foods, is also present in numerous traditional Chinese herbs and medicinal formulas. Its functions are extensive, with research demonstrating its diverse effects, including antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties. For instance, in human granulosa cells, 20 µM of quercetin can significantly reduce hydrogen peroxide-induced ROS (Reactive Oxygen Species) responses [33]. Similar beneficial effects of quercetin have also been observed in granulosa cells from goats and bovine [34, 35]. Contemporary research utilizing PCOS animal models, specifically rats, has provided clear insights into the effects of quercetin. Quercetin has been shown to reduce body weight, diminish the number of ovarian cystic follicles, and enhance the counts of corpora lutea and normal follicles in PCOS rats. Furthermore, it facilitates the restoration of a normal estrous cycle in these models. A clinical study demonstrated that quercetin can lower androgen and gonadotropin levels in PCOS patients [9]. However, other research indicates that while quercetin may not significantly alter BMI and overall hormone levels in PCOS patients, the LH values in the quercetin intervention group were lower than before treatment. Furthermore, quercetin has been shown to improve insulin resistance in PCOS [36]. However, there are currently no studies investigating the impact of quercetin dosage and duration on its therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, more well-designed, multi-center, large-sample studies are needed to further clarify quercetin’s treatment effects. Furthermore, quercetin exhibits poor water solubility but is soluble in organic solvents, including ethanol. Its limited direct absorption by intestinal cells leads to low bioavailability following a single oral dose. Consequently, the effects of quercetin on the gut microbiota merit more extensive investigation. Existing studies show that quercetin strengthens the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, which consequently decreases gut permeability and blocks LPS translocation into the blood [37]. Our research confirms this as well. Recent research exploring the influence of quercetin on gut microbiota composition in obese subjects demonstrated that quercetin facilitates the proliferation of Akkermansia bacteria, leading to a significant reversal of the obese phenotype. Subsequently, Akkermansia is capable of inhibiting lipid accumulation through the activation of the ILA/m6A/CYP8B1 pathway [13]. A separate study on a fructose-induced fatty liver mouse model revealed that quercetin intervention significantly reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels in mice, lowered blood transaminase levels, and increased high-density lipoprotein concentrations. In the quercetin intervention group, the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae_uncultured, Faecalibaculum, Odoribacter, and Allobaculum was significantly decreased, while Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, Parabacteroides, and Alloprevotella were significantly increased [38]. Our research demonstrates that quercetin significantly alters the gut microbiota in PCOS mice, markedly increasing the proportion of Akkermansia bacteria and enhancing the production of various SCFAs. Akkermansia muciniphila is a Gram-negative anaerobic bacterium and a crucial component of the human gut microbiota, particularly enriched in the intestinal mucus layer. The most notable characteristic of Akkermansia is its ability to utilize mucin from the intestinal mucus layer as its sole carbon and nitrogen source for growth. By secreting various enzymes, including glycoside hydrolases and proteases, Akkermansia can degrade mucin. This process helps stimulate intestinal mucus-producing cells (goblet cells) to secrete new mucin, thereby maintaining the thickness and integrity of the mucus layer [39]. A healthy mucus layer is a vital part of the intestinal barrier, effectively preventing pathogens and harmful substances from penetrating the intestinal wall and entering the bloodstream. The abundance of Akkermansia bacteria is positively correlated with the host’s metabolic health; its numbers are often lower in individuals with metabolic disorders like obesity and type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, our study showed that quercetin affects the proportion of Lactobacillus in the PCOS mouse gut microbiota, which appears to differ from previous findings. We hypothesize that this discrepancy may be attributed to the quercetin concentration used in our study and the varied effects different concentrations may have on Lactobacillus. Alternatively, it is possible that Akkermansia is more sensitive to quercetin, causing a reduction in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus in the mouse gut, rather than indicating a direct inhibitory effect of quercetin on Lactobacillus.

During mucin degradation, SCFAs such as acetate are produced. These SCFAs can serve as an energy source for the host and participate in regulating glucose homeostasis [40]. SCFAs serve diverse functions, including regulating metabolic activity and contributing to reduced insulin resistance and improved PCOS symptoms. Current research indicates that SCFAs are primarily produced by the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes phyla. There are various types of SCFAs, and their individual functions differ. For instance, butyrate and acetate can promote the expression of intestinal barrier proteins, thereby reducing the entry of LPS into the bloodstream and consequently improving systemic inflammatory responses. However, quercetin has poor water solubility. Under normal dietary conditions, the concentration of quercetin in human plasma does not exceed 0.1-1 µmol/L, although research indicates high concentrations in the gut. Our research found that quercetin significantly alters gut microbiota distribution, particularly increasing the proportion of Akkermansia bacteria, which is highly consistent with existing studies. Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between acetate and TGF-β, suggesting that acetate may play a role in inhibiting ovarian fibrosis. Acetate is a crucial short-chain fatty acid, and multiple studies have demonstrated its anti-fibrotic effects. For example, acetate can improve liver fibrosis, possibly by activating SCFA receptors such as GPR43 or GPR41, thereby regulating host metabolic and immune responses [41, 42]. Through the GPR43 pathway, acetate may inhibit the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). HSCs are the primary producers of ECM during liver fibrosis [43, 44]. Furthermore, acetate may regulate gene expression by influencing the activity of histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby inhibiting the expression of pro-fibrotic genes [45]. Significantly, acetic acid is a key metabolite rapidly converted to acetyl-CoA intracellularly. Studies suggest that acetic acid regulates the TGF-β pathway and subsequent epithelial-mesenchymal transition EMT by inhibiting SMAD3 phosphorylation [46]. Similar findings were observed in KGN cells, suggesting a plausible partial mechanism for how acetic acid improves ovarian fibrosis associated with PCOS. Admittedly, several limitations remain in the current study, such as the absence of in vivo SCFA supplementation, Akkermansia depletion, or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments; we aim to address these issues in future research.

Conclusion

In summary, our study investigated the effects of quercetin on ovarian fibrosis in PCOS mice, as well as its impact on the gut microbiota in these models. Our findings offer novel insights into the pathogenesis of PCOS and the underlying mechanisms by which quercetin may exert its therapeutic effects in this condition.
