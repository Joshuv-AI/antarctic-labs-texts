An in-sensor communication electronic textile for imperceptible and ultrarobust silent speech
Authors: Yuchen Lin, Shifan Yu, Lei Liu, Huasen Wang, Yuhui Wang, Mengting Jiang, Gantang Su, Yue Yu, Hongyu Chen, Yanhao Luo, Zijian Huang, Chenwei Li, Junzhi Yu, Ziquan Guo, Yuhan Su, Zhong Chen, Lichao Yang, Zhenyu Zhao, Qingliang Liao, Yuanjin Zheng, Xinqin Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aphasiacs with impaired vocal organs suffer profound communication barriers, making silent speech decoding essential. Although audible speech is absent, subtle multi-muscle activities are retained. This provides a physiological basis for the sensing modality that reliably decodes silent speech. Yet conventional microphone and piezoelectric films remain susceptible to dispersion–fidelity conflict, external disturbance in the complex environments, and are further constrained by sensing–communication separation. Here, we report an in-sensor communication scarf interface that imperceptibly captures multi-muscle activities and ultrarobustly decodes silent speech. By fusing spoof surface plasmon–driven in-sensor electromagnetic physiological communication with metamaterial topological embroidery, the system establishes exquisite reconciliation between dispersion-regulated spatial selectivity and ultrahigh fidelity (7 fs²), enabling phase-resolved differentiation and precise capture of multi-muscle activities. This minimalist yet informative rich component design radically transcends limitations of sensing–communication separation, microphone, and piezoelectric films, and greatly delivers anti-motion artifact by 24,300%, anti-electromagnetic interference by 46.4 dB, and anti-noise by 33.44 dB in the complex environments. Combining with machine learning, the interface achieves 98.7% recognition and supports assistive interaction and sleep apnea monitoring. The interface bridges metamaterial electromagnetics and human electrophysiology, establishing a transformative in-sensor physiological decoding paradigm for electromagnetic physiological communication and adaptive myogenic interfacing.

Introduction

Aphasiacs with impaired vocal organs suffer profound communication barriers. The absence of vocalization not only impedes communication but also triggers psychological and social complications such as depression and isolation1. At a population level, tens of millions of people worldwide are affected, primarily after stroke or traumatic injury, resulting in long-term communication barriers2,3. More seriously, the need for communication also forces them into long-term dependence on clinical support and health monitoring4–6. This long-term clinical reliance disrupts daily routines and imposes substantial burdens on patients and caregivers. Although aphasiacs cannot produce audible speech, they often retain subtle and residual multi-muscle activities when attempting to vocalize7,8. These subtle multi-muscle activities preserve silent speech intent and offer a physiological basis for silent speech decoding. Therefore, decoding of silent speech through subtle multi-muscle activities is essential for both daily interaction and clinical assistance of aphasiacs. However, capturing such subtle multi-muscle activities and decoding for silent speech in real-world complex environments remains a key challenge. Silent speech decoding requires both spatial selectivity to separate multiple muscles and phase integrity to maintain high signal fidelity. Yet existing speech-sensing modalities cannot simultaneously satisfy these two requirements.

Existing sensing modalities for speech decoding, primarily microphone and piezoelectric films, are fundamentally misaligned with the requirements of silent speech decoding. As the relevant muscles of silent speech generate subtle and localized contraction patterns in the neck region, requiring dispersion-regulated spatial selectivity to distinguish subtle multi-muscle activities9,10. Spatial selectivity refers to a sensor’s ability to separate signals generated by different muscles within the same region11,12. However, dispersion unavoidably perturbs phase characterization, impairing the phase integrity required for high-fidelity speech processing13–15. This creates an intrinsic conflict in silent speech decoding: increased dispersion improves spatial selectivity, whereas high fidelity needed for phase integrity demands decreased dispersion. In addition to the inability to resolve intrinsic conflict, the more serious problem is that existing sensing modalities for speech decoding suffer severe distortions due to the limitations arising from their transduction mechanisms and external disturbances. Microphones, which operate through diaphragm-based mechano-electrical transduction, are intrinsically dependent on airborne propagation, rendering their signals highly susceptible to severe distortion under noise and electromagnetic interference (EMI)16–19. By contrast, piezoelectric films become the widely-adopted solution for wearable sensing owing to their flexibility and sensitivity20–23. However, their direct mechano-electrical transduction is critically dependent on intimate skin-device contact. But pores, perspiration, and continuous muscle motion destabilize the interface and cause severe distortion. Except for these sensing modalities, existing systems separate sensing and communication into isolated modules, producing discrete structures with redundant components and deficient data that are susceptible to interference and prone to distortion24–26. This separation further disrupts the phase integrity required for decoding. As a result, reliable silent speech decoding still requires a co-designed sensing–communication textile, enabling high fidelity, moderate dispersion, and comfortable long-term wearability27–29. Yet no existing system achieves it.

In this article, we report an in-sensor communication (ISC) scarf interface that imperceptibly captures the subtle multi-muscle activities and ultrarobustly decodes silent speech generated when aphasiacs attempt to vocalize. The textile fusing spoof surface plasmon (SSP)–driven in-sensor electromagnetic physiological communication with metamaterial topological embroidery design. These two achieve practical optimization of the tradeoff between dispersion-regulated spatial selectivity and ultrahigh fidelity (7 fs²), enabling precise signals of multi-muscle activities. We find that spatial information differences arising from the activity of distinct muscle groups are primarily manifested in the phase of the propagating surface wave. Benefiting from the SSP-enabled confined-field propagation and embroidery-governed modal shaping, the interface stably tracks distinct muscle–phase signatures even when activities amplitude is tiny or partially overlapped. Radically transcending limitations of sensing–communication separation, the ISC provides immunity to interference of transmission, but also a minimalist component with rich informative data. This ISC framework avoids data losses and impedance mismatches across interfaces, allowing physiological waveforms to be preserved and decoded without intermediate conversion, buffering, or analog deterioration. Transcending the inherent physical limitations of acoustic and piezoelectric, the ISC scarf interface greatly delivers anti-motion artifact by 24,300%, and anti-EMI by 46.4 dB. It also maintains anti-noise with a signal–to–noise ratio (SNR) of 33.44 dB in complex environments. Even in purely random human activities and complex environments, the metamaterial-guided conduction pathway enables stable reading of subtle myogenic signals, reflecting ultrarobust adaptability to real-world usage rather than laboratory isolation. Combined with an efficient machine learning model, the system achieves 98.7% recognition in precise silent speech decoding. The imperceptible textile architecture further supports long-term wear, breathability, and steady temperature. Capturing subtle multi-muscle activities, this interface further validates its reliability in physiological applications like sleep apnea monitoring and assistive interaction. By bridging metamaterial electromagnetics with human electrophysiology, this work establishes a transformative in-sensor physiological decoding paradigm for electromagnetic physiological communication and adaptive myogenic interfacing.

Results

Conceptual framework and functional advantages of the ISC scarf interface

Aphasiacs retained the function of articulatory muscles, which include the neck groups, yet were unable to generate acoustic output due to impaired vocal organs, leading to severe barriers in daily communication and psychological well-being. These preserved multi-muscle activities provided a physiological basis for silent speech decoding, motivating us to develop a mechanism that directly converts these subtle activities into phase characterization and then decoding. To achieve this, the ISC scarf interface generated a SSP mode along a metamaterial topological embroidery pathway (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). When excited, the SSP supports a transverse propagation and longitudinal attenuation with a tightly confined field30,31. This near-field component interacts with the superficial articulatory muscles that underlie silent speech. During attempted vocalization, the contraction and approaches of muscles, such as the masseter, suprahyoid muscle complex, and thyrohyoid modulate the electromagnetic environments (Fig. 1b). At the microscopic level, the incident SSP field induces a reverse-polarization response within the muscle tissue due to its high-water content and anisotropic permittivity. This produces power absorption through lossy dielectric coupling, leading to further attenuation of the confined field and slight redistribution of local energy density. At the macroscopic level, this manifests as a measurable decay in the transmitted signal amplitude and phase change that reflects the instantaneous mechanical state of the muscles. These processes correspond to the progression of (1) signal input, (2) power absorption, (3) signal attenuation, and (4) signal output. The transmission pattern with and without the human body further validates these propagation characteristics (Fig. 1c, d). Without a human body, the SSP propagates along the textile, maintaining a symmetric confined field profile. With the presence of the human body, however, the field concentrated near the tissue–interface region due to confined field coupling, producing a clear contrast in the propagation pathway. This interface redistributed the confined field energy density and modifies the phase of the surface-wave mode. Consequently, variations in multi-muscle activities or physiological state were encoded as measurable changes in the detected signal, establishing a direct electromagnetic pathway between muscle activity and signal modulation. This mechanism formed the physical basis of muscle–wave coupling and enabled high-sensitivity detection of subtle multi-muscle activities without requiring direct mechanical contact. The phase variations generated through confined field, imperceptible interaction, were processed and input into a signal-processing module and a convolutional neural network to decode the silent speech. The foundation of clear speech decoding lay in baseline stability and signal stability under conditions of heavy motion. Therefore, the mechanisms of generating and eliminating motion artifact were compared among the ISC scarf interface, microphones, and piezoelectric films (Fig. 1e). The former two exhibited motion artifact arising from vibrations that perturbed the microphone diaphragm capacitance and from relative sliding motion associated with adhesive skin contact (Fig. 1e-i). In contrast, the ISC scarf interface transformed conformal mechanical displacement, which originally had the same scale as human motion, into the wavelength-scale perturbation of SSP modes (Fig. 1e-ii). This scale separation dramatically suppressed motion artifact. Figure 1f compared the noise susceptibility of microphones with the ISC scarf interface. Microphones remained vulnerable to acoustic noise because diaphragm vibrations modulate the device capacitance (Fig. 1f-i). By contrast, the ISC scarf interface leveraged the linear superposition principle shared by electromagnetic and acoustic waves: while sound waves required energy transfer between media to induce mechanical motion, the ISC scarf interface relied solely on alternating electromagnetic fields, which did not undergo mechanical coupling with airborne acoustic disturbances (Fig. 1f-ii). As a result, acoustic noise did not disturb the electromagnetic sensing pathway. Another significant traditional limitation was the microphone’s low immunity to EMI, leading to the microphone’s signal distortion. The mechanisms underlying the susceptibility of microphones and the ISC scarf interface to EMI were investigated (Fig. 1g). Microphones were susceptible to external interference because incident electromagnetic fields alter the internal capacitor field distribution (Fig. 1g-i). The ISC scarf interface, however, exploited momentum-mismatch decoupling: SSP modes supported on the interface possessed substantially larger in-plane wave vectors than far-field radiation in free space, thereby violating wave-vector conservation and preventing coupling (Fig. 1g-ii). Additionally, the SSP field energy was tightly confined near the device and decays exponentially away from it, preventing spatial overlap with long-wavelength far-field interference. Wear comfort is particularly important for piezoelectric films due to their skin-adhesion sensing nature. The user comfort associated with piezoelectric films and the ISC scarf interface was compared (Fig. 1h). The former required airtight skin adhesion, which led to discomfort during prolonged wear (Fig. 1h-i), and its material choices were constrained by biocompatibility requirements. Such airtight adhesion reduced interfacial ventilation between the skin and the surrounding environment. As a result, heat dissipation and sweat evaporation were hindered, leading to local heat and moisture accumulation during extended use. In addition, continuous adhesive contact may increase skin irritation under natural body motion, further reducing wearing comfort. By contrast, the ISC scarf interface employed a textile-based substrate and a body-conformal topological embroidery structure (Fig. 1h-ii). Its breathable fibers and imperceptible sensing mechanism enabled comfortable, long-term silent speech decoding for aphasiacs (Fig. 1h-ii). The performance advantages of the system were summarized using a Sankey diagram, a table, and a multifunctional aphasiac-assisted decoding training system was subsequently developed (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). This system integrated physiological monitoring capabilities (e.g., sleep apnea monitoring) with a decoding training assistant (e.g., silent speech recognition and robot-assistive decoding training). It operated reliably under high-noise, high-EMI, and motion-rich environments, establishing a foundation for practical deployment of ISC scarf interface-based systems.

Structural design and function characteristics of the ISC scarf interface

Beyond comparisons with microphones and piezoelectric films, the ISC scarf interface also demonstrated fundamental advantages in the system. To clarify the distinction between conventional systems and the ISC system, the sensing–communication separation and ISC were compared (Fig. 2a, b). In conventional systems, sensing and communication were usually implemented as physically separated modules, so the sensed signal commonly underwent an intermediate conversion and interface transfer before communication and decoding. Such structural separation increased component redundancy and made the signal more susceptible to mismatch, interference, and distortion during inter-module transduction and transmission. By contrast, the ISC presented here formed an integrated structure with coherent transduction, in which the perturbation induced by muscles was directly mapped onto the unified SSP field and transmitted through the same physical interface. This design therefore reduced the need for an additional sensing-to-communication conversion stage and suppresses interface mismatch between discrete modules. It should be noted, however, that the output signal was still acquired by an external circuit and converted from analog to digital form before backend decoding. Therefore, the advantage of the ISC architecture was not the complete removal of digitization, but the reduction of intermediate conversion steps and cumulative distortion prior to data acquisition and decoding. As a result, multi-muscle phase information was transmitted with a pristine waveform and retained for decoding. This integrated pathway enabled high fidelity while sustaining spatial selectivity, ensuring robust decoding even under motion and external disturbance. The comparison highlighted how the ISC scarf interface departs from and surpasses conventional separated implementations. As a core component of the ISC scarf interface, the metamaterial textile structure required a precise response to the subtle activities of the neck muscles. Accordingly, the interface provided SSP modes for confined field detection while maintaining excellent signal fidelity. Furthermore, the interface overcame the drawbacks associated with nonlinear phase response in dispersion environments by adopting natural and artificial polyester fibers. The structure, schematic, and geometrical design parameters of the ISC scarf interface were shown in Fig. 2c–e. The physical structure of the ISC scarf interface and its longitudinal structural decomposition, including the metamaterial layer and the substrate, were designed and demonstrated (Fig. 2c). The interface exploited the permittivities contrast between the metamaterial and the substrate to generate SSP modes at their boundary and to engineer a confined field. The relatively small but opposite numerical permittivities of the two materials, combined with the topological embroidery design, enabled artificial control over the propagation depth and modal shape of the surface wave. This confined field coordinated the propagation velocities of different frequency components, preserving the dispersion-induce confined field while supporting high-throughput, high-fidelity transmission of broadband silent speech signals. The topological embroidery design of the waveguide produced a band-flattening effect in the localized modal coupling (Fig. 2c-iii), which facilitated wavelength compression and yields spatially directional confinement of electromagnetic energy, thereby naturally enhancing immunity to external EMI. The trapezoidal and central impedance-matching sections enabled efficient wave-vector conversion between the curved plasmonic waveguide and the 50 Ω coplanar waveguide, minimizing reflections. These transition impedance-matching sections improved the transmission efficiency by more than 1.56 dB and reduced the conversion loss between the topological embroidery design and the coplanar waveguide (Supplementary Fig. 4). The SSP exhibited a wavelength compression to approximately 38.2% the free space wavelength32. The highly confined surface-wave mode and the polyester substrate provided robustness against interference sources such as large-scale human motion, acoustic noise, and electromagnetic disturbance. The SSP modes propagated in a TM polarization along the curved interface. In the x and y plane, the field energy was concentrated along the central transmission line and gradually weakens toward the curved region. In the x and z plane, the electric field extended transversely along the transmission path while decaying exponentially in the vertical direction (Fig. 2d). Here, w denoted the SSP structure width, and p denoted the SSP structure period of the structured interface. The SSP structure width w mainly determined the SSP field confinement strength and corresponding dispersion intensity, as reflected by the degree of deviation from the light line. In contrast, the SSP structure period p mainly regulated the relative linearity of the dispersion curve and therefore governed the balance between signal fidelity and dispersion. Increasing w mainly changed the confinement-related dispersion deviation, whereas varying p primarily affected the linearity of the dispersion profile while preserving the dispersion (Supplementary Fig. 5). Whether the conflict between signal fidelity and dispersion could be resolved determines whether the interface could stably transmit signals while detecting subtle local multi-muscle activities. Normalized dispersion curves were used to characterize the dispersion strength and confined field capability of the ISC scarf interface (Fig. 2e). Compared with the light line (black line), the topological metamaterial-induced dispersion exhibited a controllable deviation from the initial to the cutoff frequency, indicating that the electromagnetic energy was strongly confined at the interface. The topological design maintained a stable dispersion increase across the desired communication band of 1.5–3 GHz, demonstrating a stable mapping to the confined field region while preserving superior signal fidelity. Among the candidate geometries, S1 was selected because its dispersion curve remained relatively closer to the light line while still maintaining clear confined-field dispersion over the operating band, regulating spatial selectivity (Supplementary Note 1). This suggested a more linear phase evolution during propagation, which helped reduce waveform distortion and preserve signal fidelity, while still ensuring sufficient field confinement. In addition, the dispersion values in this frequency response range demonstrated the dispersion modulation of the ISC scarf interface. This was strong evidence that the interface has spatial selectivity. Since the distance between the light line and the interface was a dispersion value, it indictated that the SSP was generated at the surface of the ISC scarf interface. These four substrates, namely flax, cotton, non-woven fabric, and polyester, were selected because they represent common textile categories used in wearable interfaces and cover distinct fiber compositions and structural forms, including natural woven fabrics, non-woven textiles, and synthetic fabrics. This comparison, therefore, provided a representative basis for evaluating how different textile substrates affect SSP propagation. The comparison of transmission and reflection coefficients showed that the polyester substrate used in the ISC scarf interface achieved both a lower reflection coefficient and a higher transmission coefficient at the standard 2.4 GHz communication band (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 6). A higher transmission coefficient indicated that more SSP energy was maintained in forward propagation along the interface, which was beneficial for preserving the propagating signal and its phase information. In contrast, a lower reflection coefficient indicated increased interface mismatch and more backward scattering, thereby increasing reflection-induced loss and distortion. Taken together, these results indicated that polyester provided the most favorable propagation condition among the tested substrates, enabling more stable confined-field guidance with reduced scattering and reflection loss. Group delay dispersion (GDD) and group delay were key indicators of signal fidelity. The GDD and group delay in the 1.5 to 3 GHz band were evaluated for different substrates to examine how signal fidelity in the ISC scarf interface coexisted with dispersion-enabled confined field equalization (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 7). Across this band, the ISC scarf interface exhibited an overall group delay dispersion of 7 fs², indicating a highly stable dispersion profile and ultrahigh fidelity. Previously reported numerical measurements of GDD in other systems, which separated the sensing and communication, were also examined33–36. It was found that their dispersion values remained substantially higher, typically exceeding 83 to 45,000 fs², demonstrating severe distortion and an inability to achieve high fidelity and spatial selectivity. It was incompatible with the locally confined physiological communication regime explored in this work. The minimized dispersion in the design prevented severe signal distortion while still maintaining the moderate non-zero dispersion required for confined field sensing. Other substrates exhibited either large dispersion across the entire band or acceptable performance only near 2.4 GHz, but severe dispersion elsewhere. Group delay was another critical factor for high signal fidelity. ISC scarf interface demonstrated a group delay distribution centered around 5 ns, with a narrow histogram width and a variation range of less than 1 ns (Supplementary Fig. 8).

To maintain high-fidelity and moderate dispersion signal performance after processing, the signal flow diagram of a 2.4 GHz carrier transmitted via universal software radio peripheral (USRP) and its processed phase were presented (Fig. 2h, i). The USRP transmitter (Tx) sent a 2.4 GHz carrier into the scarf interface, where the metamaterial and polyester substrate form a resonant LC-like circuit referenced to ground. This interface combined the functions of confined field detection of multi-muscle activities and communication between Tx and Rx, forming an ISC. Owing to its high water content and anisotropic dielectric behavior, muscle tissue acted as a lossy dielectric medium. When superficial muscles contracted or approached relative to the SSP-supported textile pathway, the confined out-of-plane electric field penetrated the tissue and induced reverse-polarization of its abundant dipolar molecules. Robust body sensing with the ISC scarf interface

The most pressing need for aphasiacs is reliable silent speech decoding in the real-world as well as features such as portability and flexibility, like electronic skin and wearable devices39–41. Therefore, the ISC scarf interface required the successful establishment of a complete muscle-to-electromagnetic-to-phase-to-speech mapping chain. To create a direct correspondence between articulatory muscle and silent speech, side-view and front-view postures of representative international phonetic alphabet (/ɑː/, /ɔː/, /ə/, /iː/) during silent speech were captured (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 12). Figure 3b showed the specific absorption rates (SAR) of the laryngeal and mandibular regions. The silent speech region, primarily composed of bilateral laryngeal muscles, exhibited SAR values of 0.06–0.184 W kg−1, whereas the mandibular region presented 0–0.06 W kg−1. This difference indicated that the propagating SSP mode coupled more strongly to the laryngeal region than to the mandibular region under the same excitation condition. Notably, the peak SAR in the laryngeal region (0.184 W kg−1) was about 306% higher than the upper bound of the mandibular region (0.06 W kg−1), indicating a substantially stronger local electromagnetic interaction. In this context, the SAR reflected the spatial strength of SSP energy absorption and therefore the local sensitivity of the interface to tissue perturbation, rather than the muscle activity intensity itself. As a result, muscle contractions occurring in the higher-SAR laryngeal region were expected to induce larger perturbations to the SSP propagation state, leading to more evident phase modulation than those arising from the lower-SAR mandibular region. The SAR distribution, therefore, provided a quantitative physical explanation for the spatial selectivity of the ISC scarf interface and its ability to distinguish laryngeal and mandibular configurations even in the absence of audible speech. The SAR distribution also confirmed that the ISC scarf interface met the IEEE C95.1 safety standard for both aphasiacs and the general population. To illustrate the mechanism underlying information integration, the complete activities-induced phase modulation process was mapped as the SSP propagated along the neck regions (Fig. 3c). Due to the need for physiological movement, a complete verification of the bending and twisting performance of the ISC scarf interface was essential. Its bend and twist transmission coefficients were illustrated (Supplementary Fig. 13). When the angle was in the 0°–90° range, which conformed to the human neck, the transmission coefficient remained high, at −3 to −4 dB (Supplementary Fig. 13). The bending angle of a scarf when worn fell within this range. When the ISC scarf interface was tested for electromagnetic wave absorption by the human body within this frequency and bending angle range, it was found that the transmission coefficient was not affected by the proximity of the human body at the desired communication frequency. In real life, scarf interfaces should not only conform to the human body but also account for the random twists caused by human movement. The transmission coefficient of the scarf interface, from initial flatness to extreme twist, ranges from −2 to −4 dB. In addition, cyclic bending tests further confirmed the performance stability of the ISC scarf interface, as the normalized phase response remained nearly unchanged after 10,000 bending cycles, with no evident drift or waveform degradation (Supplementary Fig. 14). This meant that its transmission performance remained stable even in these multiple bent and twisted states, adapting to some unpredictable human behaviors.

To eliminate interference from other physiological activities in silent speech and compare, the ISC scarf interface continuously monitored the same physiological assessment from the microphone to the piezoelectric film to the ISC scarf interface: swallowing, yawning, random body movement, silent speech, and phone calls (Fig. 3g). While the microphone was clearly insensitive to swallowing and yawning, it inevitably exhibited significant, high noise during random body movement and phone calls, along with low repeatability of the speech waveform. Due to the high sensitivity of the electromechanical mechanism to motion, the piezoelectric film maintained high baseline jitter throughout the entire process. The shortcomings of traditional rigid substrates, microphones, and piezoelectric films for detecting physiological activities in the human body were also analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 15). Compared to the ISC scarf interface, the piezoelectric film’s motion artifact significantly interfered with signal stability (Supplementary Fig. 16). While the repeatability of silent speech recognition was high, significant interference signals were also observed when subjected to EMI. The ISC scarf interface demonstrated clear amplitude in common physiological activities, such as swallowing and yawning, indicating that even subtle muscle contractions can trigger device feedback. Moreover, anti-EMI research was investigated46–49. Even the best-performing anti-EMI was only around 38 dB, which is 591.8% worse than the anti-EMI of the ISC scarf interface. Full verification of the ISC scarf interface’s speech decoding capabilities required simultaneous recognition of vowels and consonants, as well as some phonetically-alike words that were hard to recognize (Fig. 3h, i). Bee swarm plots of the phase distribution of vowels and consonants were used to verify the ISC scarf interface’s ability to discriminate finely across most phrases (Fig. 3h). There was a strong correlation between the mouth opening amplitude and the muscle contraction amplitude of vowels. The former two vowels /ə/ and /ɑ:/ had significant phase changes. Although they used similar muscles during articulation, with the masseter being dominant (the jaw pushes forward), the swarm phase distribution diagrams of these two vowels over multiple movements reflected their recognition differences, and there was essentially no problem of confusion. The latter two consonants, compared to the former two vowels, involved the amplitude of the thyrohyoid muscle (pulling the laryngeal downward). The amplitude of the thyrohyoid muscle pulled on the latter two consonants (/s/ and /i:/) was significantly less than that on vowels, but they can still be clearly distinguished by the ISC scarf interface. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 19 illustrated the differences between some vowels and consonants when the muscles in the laryngeal and mandibular regions were dominant during silent speech. To evaluate the practical significance of multi-phrase recognition, the phrases with similar pronunciations using the ISC scarf interface was further examined (Fig. 3i). These phrases were intentionally selected as a representation of easily confused words, not only because they were close in muscle activity, but more importantly because they probed whether the ISC scarf interface could resolve subtle yet functionally meaningful differences in silent speech. First, similar phrases often recruited partially overlapping muscle groups while differing in the contraction of the laryngeal and mandibular muscles. Therefore, successful discrimination of such phrases indicated that the interface was sensitive to physiologically distinct motor patterns rather than merely capturing coarse speech categories. Second, in practical silent-speech communication, confusion most often arose between words or phrases that were articulatorily similar rather than obviously different. Thus, distinguishing these near-confusable examples was a more relevant benchmark for real-world usability than distinguishing phrases with large articulation differences.

For example, phrases such as Chong Qing and Mao Bing, although sharing similar rhyming characteristics, involved different throat–jaw coordination patterns. The corresponding waveform differences suggested that the interface could capture distinct combinations of laryngeal and mandibular activity. More notably, for pairs such as Shi Shi and Si Shi, whose pronunciations and overall contraction patterns were highly alike, the main difference was concentrated in the initial consonant articulation (Supplementary Fig. 20). The ability to resolve such minimal articulatory distinctions demonstrated that the ISC scarf interface could detect fine variations in muscle activity. This capability was important not only for recognizing phonetically similar words, but also for supporting more reliable silent-speech decoding in realistic usage scenarios where many candidate words shared closely related articulation patterns.

Interactive feedback and assistant during silent speech decoding

Accurate language recognition and feedback for aphasiacs was not only a key reflection of the multifaceted care provided to aphasiacs, from physiological needs to health monitoring. It was also a key foundation for their healthy integration into the social environments and the reduction of psychological illnesses caused by social barriers. In the process of social interaction, accurate interaction meant accurate interpretation and timely analysis of silent speech intent through machines learning6. Human-machine interaction-based decoding training assistant was used to illustrate how the ISC scarf interface processed and implemented feedback and assistive interaction to promote the decoding training (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 25). Based on the waveform diversity brought by multi-muscle activities to the SSP, a high sampling rate of 65 kHz was designed to prevent the omission of details. However, it was very difficult to use such high sampling rate data for processing, training, and display. This was also too high a load for a portable and lightweight device such as the ISC scarf interface. Therefore, some signal processing techniques were employed in the signal processor, such as down sampling and Fourier transform, to down sample and separate the desired muscle contraction frequencies. These different muscle contraction frequencies corresponded to the spatial selectivity resulting from dispersion-regulated, which aligned with the principle of dispersion, namely frequency selectivity. Multi-muscle activities at these frequencies essentially covered the activity frequencies of the neck region. Subsequently, the activities of these muscles were mapped to different features. These features were then fed into the convolutional neural network, which performs a 3.7 s model updating to adapt to the user’s multi-muscle activities. Through extensive evaluation and comparison with state-of-the-art silent speech systems, the optimal neural network selection for this environment was identified (Supplementary Fig. 26). The updated model reliably decoded silent speech and successfully recognized the intended command “Water” with high recognition. Through this recognition, the system generated feedback to guide the interactive decoding training assistant. Such positive feedback reinforced correct articulatory attempts and facilitated the gradual re-engagement of confidence for the aphasiac. To demonstrate the clear separation of the five phrases in terms of silent speech, a two-dimensional t-SNE plot of the five phrases was shown (Fig. 5b). Within the limited x and y axis ranges, each of the five phrases had a defined dominant distribution without confusion. The confusion matrix also illustrated the recognition performance of the five phrases under this framework, with recognition generally ranging from 96 to 100% (Fig. 5c). This had a certain degree of error, but overall, it accurately recognized many phrases. The training accuracy and training loss function represented the performance of the recognition model (Fig. 5d). To demonstrate the scalable learning capabilities of the ISC scarf interface on silent speech words and sentences, leave-one-subject-out experiments were conducted on 10 subjects and added 4000 additional samples to illustrate the interface’s recognition performance and generalization across different subjects and multiple sentence types (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 27). Here, cross-entropy loss with label smoothing was adopted, and several commonly used alternative loss functions were further compared (Supplementary Fig. 28). Before epoch 50, training on all data may reach a local optimum, but after a few epochs, this optimum was broken. It was not until 75 epochs that the model’s accuracy and loss stabilized. The accuracy of the interface reached an extremely high level of 98.7%. It also achieved a rapid training time of 0.02 s per epoch, which was extremely significant for initial stabilization and improving accuracy in machine learning: it could achieve high-accuracy recognition for a model in just over 3 s. Due to the diversity of phrases required for silent speech recognition for aphasiacs and the need for multilingual adaptation, it was crucial to add required training data in real-time, achieve short training times, and improve model accuracy. The interface also conceived a human-machine interactive decoding training assistant system for aphasiacs (Fig. 5e). A medical professional or device presented a vocabulary to guide training, which the aphasiac then received through the ISC scarf interface. When the correct phrase was spoken, the robotic arm provided correct feedback and satisfied the aphasiac’s needs. This provided significant physiological satisfaction and support to the aphasiac, encouraging them to continuously practice correct phrase pronunciation while preventing them from losing confidence due to traditional, inefficient training feedback. This configuration achieved 98.7% accuracy and a training time of 3.7 s, and was used as the baseline. Notably, although reducing the model depth to two layers resulted in a clear drop in accuracy (Fig. 5f), this should not be interpreted as evidence that lightweight models performed poorly in silent speech decoding. Instead, it indicated that an excessively shallow architecture was insufficient for this task (Supplementary Note 2). Ablation experiments were used to verify the effects of different hyperparameters on the silent speech decoding system and to find a suitable silent speech decoding model configuration (Fig. 5f). Batch size referred to the number of samples involved in the forward and backpropagation computations. While a larger batch could improve the stability of gradient updates, it also increased the latency and resource demand of model updating for silent speech decoding, which was unfavorable for rapid subject-specific adaptation. In this configuration, with a batch size of 16, the accuracy gap with 64 did not improve or decrease significantly, but training time increased by nearly three times, which contradicted the goal of low-latency decoding. The number of convolutional layers represented the depth of the network and the number of convolutional operations. In this training, 2 layers showed a significant performance drop compared with 3 or 4 layers, indicating insufficient capacity to capture subtle and hierarchical muscle-activation patterns related to articulation, whereas 4 layers may introduce redundancy, longer training, and a higher risk of overfitting to subject-specific variations in the patient dataset. But it could also be temporarily invoked when an increase in recognition accuracy was needed. Similarly, the number of features, representing the number of channels in each convolutional layer, reflected the model capacity for learning discriminative decoding representations from weak silent-speech signals. Doubling the number of features significantly increased the training time, while halving the number of features made it difficult to preserve sufficient articulatory and linguistic information for accurate decoding. Weight decay applied an L2 penalty to weights and was also used to prevent overfitting. High and low weight penalties had little impact on model fit, demonstrating a high degree of fit for the dataset. This ablation experiment demonstrated that although a substantial increase in model complexity could hinder efficient training, moderate reductions in complexity and regularization did not significantly impair silent-speech decoding performance while reducing training time. This was consistent with the need for lightweight models and rapid subject-specific calibration in adaptive silent speech decoding.

Figure 5h showed a physical illustration of the human-machine interaction for aphasiacs’ silent speech. When the aphasiac issued the command SH (Shake hand), the ISC scarf interface provided feedback on the correctness of the silent speech and directed the robotic arm to assist the aphasiac. The excellent feedback and interaction with the robotic arm provided significant training feedback for the aphasiac and visualized the training process. The principal component analysis of the feedback from different word phrase recognition results were used to demonstrate our aphasiac training feedback (Fig. 5i). The five phrases were SH (shake hand), GS (grab something), PS (pass something), HD (help dress), and BB (bye bye). These phrases constituted some of the physiological and communication needs of aphasiacs in their daily lives. The curves clearly reflected the distinct characteristics between different word phrases and the accurate operation results of the robotic arm. Supplementary Video 4 and Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30 illustrate this process, as well as the frequency division curves of muscle contraction frequency detected by the ISC scarf interface when saying the five different phrases. This well reflected the success of the training feedback, and that during silent speech, the region of the neck muscles only contracted at low frequencies, and was largely unaffected by high frequencies. The study demonstrated the robust silent speech decoding across held-out subjects and progressively more complex command settings, scaling the model to substantially multilingual scenarios remain challenging. The main difficulties were expected to arise from vocabulary expansion, greater articulatory and temporal variability, and cross-language distribution shifts in silent articulation patterns. Future extensions may be supported by transfer learning from language-specific pretrained models, hierarchical coarse-to-fine decoding, and incremental adaptation strategies that enable efficient expansion to new vocabularies and additional languages without retraining the full model from scratch.

Discussion

In this work, we presented a transformative breakthrough in electromagnetic physiological communication through an ISC scarf interface that imperceptibly captures subtle multi-muscle activities to decode silent speech ultrarobustly. Grounded in the physical transduction whereby multi-muscle activities perturb SSP fields, the system demonstrated that muscle–phase characterization can serve as a stable, high-information carrier for physiological communication. By integrating SSP-driven electromagnetic physiological communication with metamaterial topological embroidery, the textile resolved the intrinsic dispersion–fidelity conflict and maintained ultrahigh fidelity (7 fs2) under multi-muscle activities. This combination enabled local muscular dynamics, otherwise inaccessible to microphones or piezoelectric films, to be precisely encoded into the SSP phase, thereby establishing a direct route from dispersive surface-wave physics to high-fidelity physiological decoding. Moreover, the ISC scarf interface exhibited strong robustness against motion artifacts, long-duration usage, and external disturbances (24,300%; 24 h; 33.44 dB), while preserving reliable physiological information during extended wear and supporting broader assistive and health monitoring functions. The interface also enabled sleep apnea monitoring and supported assistive interaction for decoding training in aphasiacs. The system thereby extended the utility of electromagnetic physiological communication from silent speech decoding to broader myogenic interfacing and continuous health assessment. By translating multi-muscle activities into digital commands, the system provided an intuitive platform for daily assistance, with further potential for early dysphagia detection, laryngeal hypomobility assessment, and neuromuscular monitoring. Looking forward, this work points toward textile-based interfaces that integrate metamaterial electromagnetics and human electrophysiology to create highly informative, near-body sensing systems. Such electromagnetic physiological communication frameworks may support future applications in rehabilitation, continuous health assessment, and naturalistic human-machine interaction.

Methods

Fabrication of ISC scarf interface

Using 280 D silver sewing thread (Dongguan Shengxin Special Rope Co., Ltd., 2.4–3.0 Ω·cm−1, silver-plated finished product 310–250 D, elongation 10%) as the top thread, the sewing was performed on a Wilcom-designed pattern using a fully automatic digital embroidery and sewing machine NV2700 (Ningbo Youkemei E-commerce Co., Ltd.). The base material was polyester fiber. Ordinary household sewing thread was used as the bottom thread, resulting in a ISC scarf interface.

Transmission process of ISC scarf interface

The ISC scarf interface uses an USRP B210 RF development board (Guangzhou Yuanxing Technology Co., Ltd.). Hardware-wise, the Tx and Rx ports are connected to the two ends of the ISC scarf interface device using SMA coaxial cables (Beiji Electronics Co., Ltd.). Using the GNU Radio Companion software 3 on an Ubuntu 18.04 system, a 2.4 GHz carrier wave is transmitted to the RF development board through the ISC scarf interface. The signal, after down-conversion by the carrier and the USRP source, undergoes complex conjugate multiplication, then passes through a Complex to Arg and bandpass filter to obtain the original phase signal with directly observable phase changes.

Characterization and measurement

Finite element simulations and SAR experiments of electric field absorption and ISC scarf interface transmission were conducted using CST Studio Suite software 2024 (Dassault Systems). Communication and electromagnetic wave performance experiments were performed using a Keysight E5071C 8.5 GHz four-port (1. Maximum frequency: 8.5 GHz 2. Harmonic: −25 dBc 3. Number of built-in ports: 4 ports 4. Maximum output power: 10 dBm 5. Maximum input power: 10 dBm) advanced vector network analyzer. The E5071C was used to detect dispersion relations of different structures, GDD relations of different substrates, linear phase response (Error bars of 5% for estimated measurement uncertainty), and Smith charts for impedance-matching, as well as SNR measurements. An electromagnetic radiation detector (Delixi Electronics Co., Ltd.) was used to quantify radiation values in extreme environments. Electromyography (EMG) signals from the larynx and mandible were measured using a Cheez sEMG wet electrode sensor (Qisheng Electronics Co., Ltd.) and an Arduino UNO R3 as a correlation reference for the ISC scarf interface.

Signal processing and neural network usage

The ISC scarf interface enables real-time signal push across software interfaces via the ZMQ PUB sink module in GNU Radio Companion 3. It utilizes Python’s deque (Python 3.11) sequence to provide parallel storage and display caching for real-time signal processing and display of large datasets. During processing of Matlab R2022a, it performs gradient down-sampling filtering and envelope calculation on the signal, reducing the sampling rate to 200 Hz. We use a three-layer convolutional neural network model and add operations such as differencing, moving average features, Gaussian noise, and random masking to enhance the model’s robustness and prevent overfitting. We truncate the training in advance during fitting by using log files and model judgment, and save the results.

Application interface for aphasiac

The interface for the aphasia application was edited using the QtWidgets module in PyQt5, clearly displaying the waveform on the left and the waveform recognition results on the right. The word recognition results are also displayed in the middle right. Additionally, a reset button is included in the lower right corner for easy recording, and a zoom function is enabled in the middle of the waveform, allowing medical staff or aphasia users to reset records and zoom in/out on the waveform.

Statistical analysis

Error bar analysis in this work represents mean ± standard deviation, showing central tendency of the data. Box plots analysis in this work were used to represent the distribution of the data. The central line in each box represented the median, the lower and upper edges of the box indicated the 25th and 75th percentiles, respectively (interquartile range, IQR), and the whiskers extended to the minimum and maximum values within 1.5 × IQR from the quartiles. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical samples in this experiment were derived from independent material samples or tests.

Reporting on sex and gender

The muscle groups in the neck involved in silent speech or internal silent speech are the same in both sexes. Anatomically and functionally, the core muscular systems controlling silent speech are homologous and similar, not separate mechanisms for both sexes. The only difference is the larynx and the vocal cords, but this applies to spoken speech scenarios and is not applicable to the silent speech scenarios in this research. A total of five male subjects participated in the experiments for anti-motion artifact and anti-EMI. The neural network training experiment involved a total of 10 male subjects. Participants’ gender was based on self-report. All participants were informed with written consent. The authors affirm that human research participants provided informed consent for publication of the images in Figs. 3a, 4e, h, i, and 5g, h and Supplementary Figs. 3, 12, 21, 22, 23, and 29. The person photographed in Supplementary Figs. 21, 22, and 29 is one of the authors.

Blinding

Blinding was not considered relevant to this study because the experiments did not involve treatment or control group allocation. All participants completed predefined silent-speech tasks and motion or EMI conditions, and the primary outcomes were objective, algorithm-derived metrics, including recognition accuracy and statistical measures of motion-artifact robustness and EMI robustness. Recognition accuracy was calculated by comparing model outputs with predefined ground-truth labels, and motion-artifact-related and EMI-related analyses were performed using signal-processing and statistical procedures. These results are based on the sensor’s output to the human body and are not related to the researchers’ subjective judgment. Therefore, the investigator’s awareness of the participant’s identity or experimental condition was unlikely to introduce subjective assessment bias.

Sample size rationale

No formal statistical sample size calculation was performed because this study was designed as an exploratory engineering validation of a non-contact scarf-based silent-speech sensing system rather than a hypothesis-driven clinical or treatment-control study. The sample size of 10 participants was chosen pragmatically to provide preliminary cross-participant validation while maintaining a feasible repeated-measures data-collection protocol. Each participant completed the same predefined silent-speech tasks and motion conditions, generating multiple repeated recordings for recognition-accuracy and motion-artifact analyses. So, this sample size was considered sufficient to demonstrate proof of concept, evaluate consistency of performance across participants, and characterize motion-artifact robustness under predefined conditions. Future work will include larger and more diverse participant cohorts.
