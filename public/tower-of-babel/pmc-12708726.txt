Comparative outcomes of robot-assisted, laparoscopic, and open radical nephrectomy: A Taiwan population-based study
Authors: Marcelo Chen, Yu-San Chien, Ching-Hu Chung, Jiun-Yi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRadical nephrectomy continues to be the established treatment for patients with large or locally advanced renal tumors. Although the use of robot-assisted techniques has grown substantially in recent years, their definitive advantages compared to traditional laparoscopic or open surgeries remain unclear.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, population-based analysis utilized data from the Taiwan Cancer Registry Database (TCRD) for patients who underwent open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted radical nephrectomy between 2018 and 2020. Postoperative outcomes were assessed using the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), with follow-up information available through 2021.ResultsThis study demonstrates that minimally invasive techniques (robotic-assisted RN (RARN) and laparoscopic RN (LRN)) were associated with lower blood transfusion rates, shorter length of hospital stays (LOS), and reduced ICU (intensive care unit) admissions compared to open radical nephrectomy (ORN), both before and after Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) adjustment. Additionally, RARN showed a lower overall complication rate compared to ORN, though reoperation and readmission rates remained similar among all groups. Despite these perioperative advantages, no significant differences in overall survival were observed among the three surgical techniques.ConclusionThe results of the study highlight the potential benefits of robotic and laparoscopic approaches in improving short-term surgical outcomes without compromising long-term survival. Interpretation is constrained by database limitations and residual selection bias.Keywords: Robotic-assisted radical nephrectomy, Laparoscopic radical nephrectomy, Open radical nephrectomy, Length of hospital stays, ICU admissions

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remains a major urologic malignancy, and radical nephrectomy (RN) continues to be the standard treatment for patients with large or locally advanced tumors when nephron-sparing surgery is not feasible [1–3]. Over the past two decades, the surgical landscape has shifted from traditional open radical nephrectomy (ORN) toward minimally invasive techniques. Laparoscopic RN (LRN) first demonstrated the feasibility of reduced surgical trauma and faster recovery, and the subsequent development of robot-assisted RN (RARN) further enhanced visualization, precision, and ergonomics, particularly in anatomically complex cases [4–6].

Although RARN is increasingly adopted worldwide, its clinical advantages over LRN and ORN remain incompletely defined. Existing comparative studies report mixed findings, with some demonstrating reduced blood loss, shorter length of hospital stay, or fewer complications with RARN, while others highlight similar outcomes but substantially higher costs [7–12]. Notably, most of these investigations originate from Western cohorts. Evidence from Asian populations—where tumor characteristics, body habitus, surgical adoption patterns, and healthcare systems differ substantially—is still limited. This gap is especially relevant in Taiwan, where robotic surgery is not reimbursed by the National Health Insurance system, potentially influencing patient selection and real-world utilization.

Moreover, few nationwide studies have simultaneously compared all three surgical modalities (ORN, LRN, RARN) using population-level data. Understanding their relative outcomes in routine clinical practice is essential for guiding surgical decision-making, resource allocation, and discussions regarding reimbursement policies.

Therefore, this study leveraged two comprehensive national datasets—the Taiwan Cancer Registry Database (TCRD) and the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD)—to evaluate perioperative outcomes and overall survival among patients undergoing ORN, LRN, or RARN. By examining real-world data from an Asian population, this work aims to address key evidence gaps and provide clinically meaningful insights into the comparative effectiveness of these surgical approaches.

Materials and methods

Data source and patient definition: Patient information was sourced from Taiwan’s NHIRD and the TCRD. The NHIRD, which covers about 99.9% of Taiwan’s population, contains extensive medical claims data encompassing outpatient visits, hospital admissions, surgical procedures, diagnoses, prescriptions, and treatments including radiotherapy and chemotherapy [13, 14]. Founded in 1979, the TCRD is a cancer registry that systematically gathers data from hospitals with a minimum capacity of 50 beds that offer cancer treatment services. It captures approximately 97% of cancer cases across Taiwan. It offers detailed clinical information such as tumor size and staging, and crucial epidemiological data for evaluating surgical intervention outcomes. Data from the TCRD was linked to the NHIRD to enhance the depth and robustness of the analysis.

Patients diagnosed with RCC were identified from the cancer registry between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2020, using the ICD-O-3 code C64. Those who underwent radical nephrectomy as their initial treatment formed the study cohort. Patients younger than 18 years or with a previous cancer history were excluded. Based on the surgical technique, patients were divided into three categories: open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted radical nephrectomy. The date of the first surgery was set as the index date, with follow-up continuing until December 31, 2021.

Clinical endpoints: In this research, the main independent variables were the types of surgical interventions: ORN, LRN, and RARN. Other variables considered included patient age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [15], clinical and pathological tumor stage, tumor dimensions, and presence or absence of lymphovascular invasion. The clinical endpoints were categorized as follows: (1) overall survival rates during the study period among patients undergoing ORN, LRN, and RARN; (2) perioperative outcomes, including blood transfusion rates, LOS, ICU stay, and rates of reoperation and readmission; (3) postoperative complication rates, both general and procedure specific.

Ethics approval

The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of MacKay Memorial Hospital, Taiwan (Protocol No. 22MMHIS360e). The ethics committee granted a waiver for informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., USA). Two-sided tests were applied with a significance threshold set at 0.05. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges based on their distribution. To minimize confounding and balance covariates among the three surgical groups, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was implemented [16]. Covariate balance was checked by standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values below 0.1 considered acceptable. Adjustments included factors such as age, BMI, clinical and pathological tumor staging, CCI, smoking status, and lymphovascular invasion. Baseline characteristics, perioperative outcomes, and postoperative complications were analyzed using chi-square tests for categorical data and t-tests for normally distributed continuous data. For skewed continuous data, the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized, and Fisher’s exact test was applied to categorical data when necessary. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves, with differences between surgical methods tested by the Log-Rank method.

Data limitations

Although the NHIRD and TCRD offer broad national coverage, they lack important operative details such as tumour complexity scores (RENAL/PADUA), estimated blood loss, operative time, surgeon and hospital volume, and postoperative recovery measures. The absence of these variables limits adjustment for case complexity and provider experience. In addition, despite IPTW balancing, residual confounding and selection bias may remain because certain clinical factors were incompletely captured or remained imbalanced. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the observed associations between surgical modality and outcomes.

Results

Baseline characteristics

From 2018 to 2021, the full NHIRD database was utilized to create the study cohort (Fig. 1). The cohort comprised 829 patients who had RN, divided by surgical procedure: 309 underwent ORN, 122 had RARN, and 398 received LRN. Median follow-up durations were 889.7 days for ORN, 905.6 days for RARN, and 961.8 days for LRN (Table 1).

Before applying IPTW, significant demographic and clinical differences were observed among the surgical groups (Table 1a). Patients undergoing RARN had a significantly higher mean age (61.81 years, SD = 10.96) compared to those in the ORN group (59.00 years, SD = 12.74; p = 0.033). Clinically, ORN patients had a higher prevalence of advanced cancer stages, with significant differences observed in stage 3 (p = 0.002) and stage 4 (p = 0.030). Similarly, pathological staging indicated a greater proportion of advanced disease in ORN patients, with stage 3 significantly more prevalent (p = 0.002).

After IPTW adjustment, baseline disparities among the groups were reduced, improving comparability. The age differences between ORN and RARN patients were no longer statistically significant, demonstrating the effectiveness of IPTW in balancing non-randomized cohorts. However, significant differences persisted in variables such as smoking status, clinical staging, and pathological staging, indicating inherent variations in patient selection across surgical techniques. Both before and after IPTW, the RARN and LRN groups were associated with lower incidences of advanced disease stages and better overall preoperative health metrics compared to the ORN group.

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative outcomes for ORN, RARN, and LRN are summarized in Table 2, with results presented both before and after IPTW adjustment. Before IPTW, significant differences were observed across surgical techniques. ORN patients had a notably higher blood transfusion rate (56.31%) compared to RARN (29.51%) and LRN (25.38%), with a significant difference between ORN and RARN (p < 0.000). The mean LOS was also significantly longer for ORN patients (9.88 days) compared to RARN (7.86 days) and LRN (7.92 days), with a significant difference between ORN and RARN (p < 0.0007). ICU admission rates varied considerably, with 27.83% of ORN patients requiring ICU care, compared to 7.38% for RARN and 21.86% for LRN (p < 0.0001 for ORN vs. RARN; p = 0.0003 for RARN vs. LRN).

Following IPTW, these trends remained consistent. Blood transfusion rates were still significantly higher in ORN patients (55.23%) compared to RARN (34.91%; p < 0.0001). While LRN had a slightly lower blood transfusion rate (25.07%) compared to RARN (28.39%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.2271). While LOS for LRN was not significantly different from RARN, a decreasing trend was observed.

Postoperative complication rates also varied among groups. After IPTW, ORN patients experienced higher overall complication rates (34.34%) compared to RARN (27.21%; p = 0.0208), indicating a potential benefit of RARN in reducing postoperative risks. While LRN had slightly lower complication rates (26.92%) compared to RARN (28.43%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.6034). Survival analysis

The IPTW-adjusted Kaplan-Meier provide insights into the survival outcomes of different radical nephrectomy techniques (Fig. 2). Comparisons between ORN and RARN (Fig. 2A) and between LRN and RARN (Fig. 2B) revealed closely aligned survival trajectories, indicating comparable survival across surgical approaches during the study period. Statistical analysis confirmed no significant differences in overall survival. The hazard ratio (HR) for ORN versus RARN was 1.223 (95% CI: 0.907–1.648, p = 0.1878), while the HR for LRN versus RARN was 0.762 (95% CI: 0.535–1.084, p = 0.1308), indicating no statistical survival benefit for any specific approach.

Discussion

This study utilized two comprehensive national databases in Taiwan, the NHIRD and TCRD, to evaluate clinical outcomes across three RN techniques: ORN, RARN, and LRN. The findings indicate that RARN is associated with superior perioperative outcomes compared to ORN and LRN, highlighting its clinical effectiveness within the Taiwanese population, emphasizing the regional applicability and benefits of robotic-assisted techniques. These results not only align with global research on RARN but also contribute to a better understanding in making surgical strategies for RCC management.

Our analysis shows that both minimally invasive techniques—RARN and LRN—were associated with lower transfusion rates, shorter hospitalisation, and fewer postoperative complications compared with ORN. These trends are consistent with previous reports showing reduced blood loss, shorter LOS, and lower morbidity with minimally invasive radical nephrectomy [17, 18]. RARN demonstrated a markedly lower ICU utilisation rate, a finding that persisted after IPTW adjustment. An important and unexpected finding of this study was the markedly lower ICU admission rate in the RARN group compared with both ORN and LRN. ICU utilisation in Taiwan is influenced by multiple system-level factors, including institutional practice norms, surgeon preference, and real-time availability of ICU beds. These factors may contribute to variation that is not entirely reflective of postoperative physiological need. Nonetheless, several biological mechanisms may also explain the observed pattern. Compared with open surgery, RARN generally results in smaller incisions, reduced surgical trauma, lower blood loss, and more stable intraoperative hemodynamics—factors that may reduce the likelihood of postoperative monitoring in the ICU. Furthermore, the enhanced visualisation and precision afforded by robotic instrumentation may help mitigate intraoperative events that otherwise prompt ICU observation, such as bleeding risk or fluid shifts. Given the inherent limitations of claims-based databases, we were unable to determine which ICU admissions were mandated by clinical instability versus institutional or logistical considerations. Therefore, while the reduced ICU utilisation associated with RARN is notable, it should not be interpreted solely as evidence of superior physiologic recovery. Rather, the differences likely reflect a combination of surgical technique characteristics and system-level practice variability.

RARN and LRN demonstrated broadly similar perioperative outcomes, which aligns with prior evidence showing limited differences between these two minimally invasive techniques [7, 19]. The notably lower ICU admission rate and reduced incidence of postoperative ileus in RARN may be attributable to improved ergonomics and more controlled tissue handling afforded by robotic assistance [20]. An additional consideration in interpreting our findings is the economic impact of robotic-assisted radical nephrectomy. In Taiwan, RARN is not reimbursed by the National Health Insurance system and requires full out-of-pocket payment. Although our results demonstrate perioperative advantages associated with RARN, these benefits must be weighed against substantially higher procedural costs and limited accessibility. The observed reductions in transfusion, ICU utilisation, or complications do not necessarily translate into overall cost-effectiveness, particularly in a publicly funded healthcare system. Therefore, while RARN may offer clinical advantages, the financial trade-offs remain significant, and future research incorporating economic analyses is needed to determine whether these perioperative improvements justify the additional expense.

A key finding is the absence of statistically significant differences in overall survival among surgical modalities. This aligns with the biological principles of radical nephrectomy—that as long as the tumour is completely excised, oncologic outcomes remain comparable regardless of approach. Our results are consistent with contemporary international data showing no survival advantage for RARN over ORN or LRN [18]. The relatively short follow-up period in our dataset may not fully capture late recurrences, but the convergence of survival curves reinforces the expected oncologic equivalence.

As mentioned, the short follow-up period is a limitation of this study. Additionally, the nature of national databases limits access to certain clinical variables, such as postoperative pain and quality of life, which are important factors in evaluating recovery outcomes. Looking ahead, extending follow-up durations and incorporating more detailed data on disease-specific outcomes could provide deeper insights into the long-term effects of surgical techniques on patient health and quality of life. Future research should integrate comprehensive clinical registries to refine clinical practices and inform policymaking. Despite the use of IPTW to reduce baseline heterogeneity, several clinically important variables—including smoking status, clinical staging, and pathological staging—remained imbalanced across treatment groups. These residual differences reflect inherent selection bias in real-world surgical decision-making, where patients with more advanced or complex disease are more often directed toward open surgery. Consequently, part of the observed perioperative advantages of RARN, including lower transfusion rates, reduced ICU utilisation, and fewer postoperative complications, may be partly attributable to differences in patient risk profiles rather than to the surgical technique alone. In addition to these residual baseline imbalances, another important limitation is the absence of key operative and perioperative variables that influence both surgical selection and postoperative outcomes. The NHIRD and TCRD do not capture tumour complexity indices (e.g., RENAL or PADUA scores), operative time, estimated blood loss, surgeon or hospital volume, perioperative analgesia, or functional recovery metrics—factors known to affect perioperative morbidity and potentially differ across surgical modalities. The lack of these variables restricts the ability to adjust for case complexity and provider expertise, and unmeasured confounding may therefore persist even after IPTW. As such, the observed differences—particularly in transfusion rates, ICU utilisation, and short-term complications—should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future studies incorporating more granular clinical and intraoperative data will be essential to confirm and refine the comparative effectiveness of robotic, laparoscopic, and open radical nephrectomy.

This study represents the first nationwide comparison in Taiwan between robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) and conventional RN regarding survival and postoperative outcomes. Importantly, during the study period, RAS was not yet covered by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance, limiting its accessibility as patients needed to cover costs themselves. Despite this, our results indicated that RAS was linked to significantly lower rates of blood transfusions and postoperative complications compared to open or laparoscopic surgery. To further confirm these findings and evaluate the wider use of RAS in managing renal cell carcinoma, additional prospective studies with extended follow-up are warranted.

Conclusions

This study compared outcomes across ORN, RARN, and LRN techniques. RARN provides perioperative advantages while maintaining the expected survival equivalence with LRN and ORN, consistent with the oncologic principles of radical nephrectomy. These results highlight the short-term benefits of RARN, positioning it as a viable option for improving immediate postoperative outcomes.
