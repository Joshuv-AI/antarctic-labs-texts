The flexible separate vertical suture fixation for comminuted inferior patella fracture with osteoporosis: a retrospective clinical study
Authors: Xiaodan Zhao, Peng Tan, Zhen Zhang, Yangmengfan Chen, Chang Zou, Xueling Yuan, Guanglin Wang, Yue Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this retrospective study was to investigate the efficacy and functional outcomes of utilizing a flexible separate vertical suture fixation technique in osteoporotic patients presenting with comminuted fractures of the inferior patellar pole.MethodsA flexible separate vertical suture fixation by braided polyester was applied. Between January 2021 and December 2022, 19 patients diagnosed as comminuted inferior patella fracture with osteoporosis were retrospectively enrolled in this study.ResultsThe mean operative time was 34.2 ± 3.2 minutes, bone union achieved in all cases at an average of 3.1 ± 0.5 months after surgery. At the last follow up, the mean ROM was 132.1 ± 2.9°, and the mean Bostman score was 29.1 ± 0.9. During follow up, no instances of wire cutting or internal fixation failure occurred among the patients, no instances of postoperative anterior knee pain or refracture of the inferior patellar pole were observed and no surgical removal of implants was performed for any of the patients.ConclusionsThe flexible separate vertical suture fixation by thread is an effective fixation method for comminuted inferior patella fracture, especially for those elderly patients with osteoporosis.Keywords: Inferior patella fracture, Flexible separate vertical suture fixation, Braided polyester suture, Wire cutting, Osteoporosis

Introduction

Fractures of the inferior pole constitute approximately 9.3% to 22.4% of all patellar fractures [1, 2]. These fractures are frequently linked to small fragments, fragile cancellous bone, and comminution [3]. As the population ages, fractures involving the inferior patellar pole, coupled with osteoporosis, are increasingly prevalent, posing significant challenges and a heightened risk of internal fixation failures [4].

Numerous surgical techniques have been developed for treating this specific fracture type. Notably, the Separate vertical wiring fixation (SVW) surgical technique is recognized as an effective treatment for securing fractures of the inferior patellar pole. The SVW surgical technique was initially introduced by Yang KH in 2003 [5]. Subsequent to Yang KH’s pioneering work, various researchers enhanced and innovated the technology. The traditional SVW surgical technique universally employed wire or titanium cable for fixation. The traditional SVW surgical technique yielded satisfactory outcomes, devoid of prolonged immobilization, infection concerns, or alterations in patellar tendon length [5–8]. Yet, in cases of comminuted inferior patellar fractures, the fragment cut by the vertical wire remains a notable factor contributing to internal fixation failure [8]. Wire cutting is particularly evident in patients with osteoporosis. Additionally, earlier studies reported a notable incidence of internal fixation breakage following SVW surgery [9]. Furthermore, there remains a risk of soft tissue irritation with the traditional SVW technique, necessitating reoperation for internal fixation removal—an intricate decision for elderly patients [10]. Despite widespread acceptance of the SVW technique among trauma surgeons, further enhancements are essential to address the aforementioned issues.

The braided polyester suture, a nonabsorbable surgical material, exhibits notable stiffness and high ultimate tensile strength in clinical applications [11]. Furthermore, braided polyester suture has demonstrated superiority over stainless steel wire in the management of transverse patellar fractures [12, 13]. Vipul observed that the fixation quality achieved with braided polyester suture was comparable to that of stainless steel wire for displaced patellar fractures, offering ample stability to withstand loads anticipated during postoperative rehabilitation [14]. Additionally, braided polyester suture is less prone to induce postoperative pain and necessitate removal compared to routine metal implants. The use of braided polyester suture for patellar fracture fixation is justified and is anticipated to lead to enhanced patient satisfaction and reduced reoperation rates [12].

Nevertheless, braided polyester sutures have not been employed in the treatment of comminuted inferior patellar fractures using the SVW surgical technique. In this study, we initially utilized braided polyester suture (No.5 Ethibond) in lieu of 1.0 mm steel wires within the SVW technique, naming it the flexible separate vertical suture fixation (SVS) technique.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate our experience with the flexible separate vertical suture fixation(SVS)technique. The surgical technique and our initial clinical experience are reported in a series of 19 consecutive patients. We assessed postoperative outcomes based on radiological outcomes, functional recovery, documented complications, and observed limitations associated with the technique.

Materials and methods

Patient demographics

The records from our hospital trauma database were retrospectively reviewed for the period from January 2021 to December 2022 to identify all consecutive patients with comminuted inferior patellar pole fractures who were treated using the flexible SVS technique. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of west china hospital of Sichuan university.

Inclusion criteria comprised the following: (1) Unilateral inferior pole of patella fracture; (2) AO/OTA type 34-A1; (3) Treatment with the flexible SVW technique by braided polyester suture; (4) Minimum follow-up duration of 12 months. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Open inferior pole of patella fracture; (2) Patients with prior patellar surgery; (3) Pathological fracture, multiple injures, insufficient radiographic or chart data. A total of 19 cases of inferior patellar pole fractures were identified at our department from January 2021 to December 2022. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Surgical technique

Anesthesia and positioning

All patients underwent general anesthesia and were placed in a supine position. A pneumatic tourniquet was utilized, and an anterior median incision was made.

Approach and exposure

A midline incision extending from the superior border of the patella to the tibial tuberosity was performed. Following hematoma removal, the knee joint cavity and fracture fragments were irrigated with normal saline.

Initially, cerclage braided polyester suture (No.5 Ethibond) was transversely passed through the patellar ligament in close proximity to the inferior pole of the patella.

Secondly, three vertical parallel bone tunnels were created using a 2.0 Kirschner wire (K-wire) through the posterior margin of the proximal fragment, extending directly to the anterosuperior border of the patella. The space between the bone tunnels is approximately 0.5 cm. If the distal bone fragment is small, the spacing is appropriately reduced. The insertion site of the K-Wire is positioned as closely as possible to the lower edge of the fracture section (the subchondral bone zone). The proximal patellar tendon is partially dissected using a sharp blade to facilitate smooth passage of the Kirschner wires through the proximal patella.

Thirdly, braided polyester suture (No.5 Ethibond) was passed through the three bone tunnels from distal to proximal. The three vertical braided polyester sutures were passed through the distal patellar tendon across this horizontal braided polyester. Subsequently, the pillow behind the popliteal fossa was repositioned to the ankle to hyperextend the knee. Reduction is performed without the use of repositioning forceps due to the small and comminuted fracture fragments.

Subsequently, separate vertical braided polyester sutures were tightened using the standard technique, and the cerclage braided polyester suture was tightened at the superior border of the patella.

Prior to wound closure, the ruptured retinaculum was repaired using absorbable sutures, and joint stability was assessed by performing a full range of knee joint motions.

Postoperative care and follow up

Radiographs were taken the day after surgery for postoperative assessment. A standardized rehabilitation protocol was followed for all patients. Initiation of knee range of motion and quadriceps strength exercises commenced on the second day postoperatively. Gradual increase in knee range of motion exercises aimed at achieving 90° flexion by week 4, 120° flexion by week 6, and full range of motion thereafter. A hinged knee brace was utilized to restrict knee movement, with early permission for full weight bearing. Compliance with physiotherapy was monitored during routine follow-up visits.

Clinical and radiological follow-ups were scheduled at 6 weeks, 3 months, and subsequently every 3 months postoperatively. All patients received regular anti-osteoporosis treatment, including calcium, alfacalcidol and bisphosphonates.

Outcomes measures

Anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs were employed for assessing bone union. Radiographic union was defined as the presence of bridging trabeculae across the fracture site on both anteroposterior and lateral radiographs, assessed by two independent orthopedic surgeons. Knee range of motion (ROM), the Lysholm Knee Score and the Bostman score were documented at 6 weeks, 3 months, and subsequently every 3 months postoperatively [15–17]. The Bostman scoring system encompasses eight sections: range of movement (ROM), pain, work, thigh circumference atrophy (10 cm proximal to the patella), assistance in walking, effusion, giving way, and stair climbing. Excellent was defined as a score between 28 and 30; good between 20 and 27; scores less than 20 were considered unsatisfactory. The outcome assessors (surgeons evaluating radiographs and clinical scores) were not blinded to the treatment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and clinical outcomes. Continuous variables (e.g., age, ROM, Bostman score) are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and range. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0, IBM Corp., USA).

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 19 patients treated by flexible separate vertical suture fixation were included in the study, 12 were females and 7 were males, with an average age of 62.8 years. The most common injury mechanism was slipping on the knee, accounting for 16 (84.2%) of the 19 cases, while fall-related injuries accounted for 3 (15.8%) of the 19. All surgeries were conducted by a single experienced trauma surgeon (ZXD). The average operation time was approximately 34.2 minutes (Table 1).

Functional outcomes

Patients fully regained knee joint range of motion (ROM) without functional deficits during follow-up(Fig. 4). The mean ROM was 132.1 ± 2.9° at 12 months post-operation. The mean Lysholm Knee Score was 89.2 ± 3.2 at 12 months post-operation. No patients exhibited a clinically significant extension lag (> 5 degrees) at the final follow-up assessment. At the latest follow-up, all patients achieved a mean Bostman score of 29.1 ± 0.9, with 15 patients receiving an excellent score and 4 patients receiving a good score(Table 3). The satisfaction rate reached 100%.

Complications and re-operation

No instances of wire cutting or internal fixation failure occurred among the patients. No instances of postoperative anterior knee pain or refracture of the inferior patellar pole were observed. Furthermore, no surgical removal of implants was performed for any of the patients. Five patients complained that the knee occasionally gave way without pain or discomfort of the joint.

Discussion

Fractures of the inferior patellar pole are primarily induced by indirect force and are more prevalent in patients with osteoporosis [4, 18]. Inferior patellar pole fractures exhibit characteristics such as comminution, thin cross-section, and coronal stratification, posing challenges for effective internal fixation, particularly in osteoporotic patients [3, 19]. Numerous studies have explored the internal fixation of inferior pole fractures of the patella; however, the majority did not specifically address the concern of osteoporosis. In contrast to earlier studies, a significant distinction in our study is the higher average age of the patients (62.8 ± 7.7), with a majority presenting osteopenia or osteoporosis.

Fractures of the inferior patellar pole result in a sustained disruption of the knee extenion mechanism. The treatment principle primarily revolves around reinstating the continuity of the knee extensor, prioritizing functional restoration over anatomical reduction [20, 21]. Partial patellectomy often leads to worse functional outcomes [17]. Adequate management of inferior patellar pole fractures is crucial for optimizing knee extension restoration. It involves achieving ample internal fixation to facilitate early functional exercises and mitigate complications [22].

Since the proposal of the SVW technique, it has been regarded as a dependable treatment for fractures of the patellar lower pole [5, 23]. The SVW technique significantly reduces the risk of patella baja, improves knee range of motion and Bostman scores compared to conventional tension band fixation [24]. Nevertheless, the conventional SVW technique exhibits three main drawbacks: (1) Wire cutting, where the vertical wire cut through the fragment, resulting in failed internal fixation in comminuted inferior patellar fractures [8]; (2) Cerclage wire breakage—Song HK et al. reported a 19% (4/21) incidence, leading to internal fixation failure and soft tissue complications [6, 9]; (3) Re-operation is necessary to remove internal fixation, posing risks for elderly patients.

Building upon prior research, this study introduces crucial refinements to the SVW technique by substituting stainless steel wire with braided polyester suture. In comparison to stainless steel wire, braided polyester suture has demonstrated comparable fixation quality [12]. In this study, the flexible SVS technique employing braided polyester suture exhibited favorable clinical and radiological outcomes. Patients demonstrated bone healing, on average, at 3.1 months postoperatively. At the 1-year postoperative follow-up, the knee joint exhibited satisfactory motion, with a mean range of motion (ROM) of 132.1°, and the Bostman score was excellent, averaging 29.1. Our findings align favorably with positive outcomes documented in numerous studies evaluating the traditional SVW technique [7, 23]. However, Regarding complications, the flexible SVS technique exhibits advantages when compared to the traditional SVW technique employing wires or titanium cables for fixation. No instances of wire cuts or Cerclage wire breakage were observed among all patients.

Furthermore, braided polyester suture demonstrates outstanding biocompatibility and tolerance. No instances of soft tissue irritation or the need for re-operation to remove the implant were reported. For comparison, Yanchun Gao reported that 1 out of 18 patients required re-operation to remove the internal fixation due to soft tissue irritation [23]. Shuang G. Yan reported that all 20 patients underwent a second operation to remove the implants, despite the absence of implant breakage and irritation [7].

What’s more, reduction is easier with the flexible SVS technique. Braided polyester suture are easier to use than wires or titanium cable. The operation time (34.2 min) is significantly shorter than that of the reported times for the traditional SVW technique by K. H. Yang (79 min), Shuang G.Yan(45.7 min) and Shuangjian He (43.5 min) [5–7], representing a substantial advantage, particularly for elderly patients with underlying health conditions.

Recently, W. Fan et al. described a “candy box” technique for the fixation of inferior pole patellar fractures using sutures and Nice knots [25]. Similarly, Ming Zhou et al. reported a technique employing Krackow suturing combined with a suture bridge [26]. Both retrospective studies reported satisfactory clinical outcomes. Our technique shares conceptual similarities with these methods, particularly in the use of high-strength sutures for internal fixation, which biomechanical studies have shown to provide sufficient mechanical strength while mitigating stress concentration at the fracture site.

However, our flexible separate vertical suture (SVS) technique offers several distinctions. Most notably, it appears to be a simpler and more efficient procedure. This is reflected in a considerably shorter mean operative time in our cohort (34.2 ± 3.2 min) compared to that reported by W. Fan et al. (59.76 ± 5.80 min) and Ming Zhou (55.5 ± 5.1 min) [27]. Furthermore, our study focused specifically on a geriatric population with osteoporosis, with a mean patient age of 62.8 ± 7.7 years. This is significantly older than the populations in the studies by W. Fan et al. (51.33 ± 16.74 years) and Ming Zhou et al. (50.1 ± 14.5 years), suggesting that the SVS technique may be particularly suited for managing these complex fractures in osteoporotic bone [25, 27].

There are sveral crucial considerations should be noted: (1) The insertion site of the K-Wire should be as close as possible to the lower edge of the fracture section (the subchondral bone zone). (2) Nonabsorbable braided polyester sutures for cerclage should be passed directly posteriorly from the site of the bone–tendon union of the patellar tendon, with the three vertical braided sutures passing through it. (3) Circular and vertical braided polyester sutures must be tightened in stages. Initial tightening of the cerclage braided polyester suture assists in fracture repositioning, while secondary tightening of the vertical braided polyester sutures aids in compressing the fractured area. (4) After individually knotting the vertical braided polyester sutures, they are knotted with each other to enhance stability.

The study presented a novel surgical technique for inferior pole patellar fractures, yielding excellent clinical outcomes. The flexible SVS technique well solved the shortcomings of traditional SVW technique. However, certain disadvantages must be acknowledged:1. The knots in our suture configuration are tied anterior to the patella and consist of non-absorbable material. While no cases of soft tissue irritation have been observed in our cohort to date, the potential for irritation remains a consideration, particularly in elderly patients with fragile or thin skin where a prominent knot may become palpable or protrude over time. 2. The application of this technique is likely subject to specific patient selection criteria. A recognized limitation of our study is the lack of inclusion of younger patient populations. Consequently, the applicability of our findings and the efficacy of the presented technique are primarily confined to elderly patients with osteoporotic bone. Further investigation in younger, more physically active individuals with higher bone mineral density is necessary to comprehensively evaluate the generalizability of this technique.

It is crucial to acknowledge limitations of this study. Its retrospective design introduces potential for selection and information bias. The sample size, while sufficient for an initial technique description and early outcome report, is relatively small. The absence of a direct comparison group limits our ability to make definitive comparative claims about superiority. Furthermore, the mean follow-up period of 17.5 months, while adequate for assessing fracture union and early functional recovery, is insufficient to evaluate long-term outcomes such as osteoarthritis progression, or need for subsequent surgery. Future prospective, randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to validate these preliminary findings and directly compare the efficacy of polyester suture fixation against established metallic techniques.

Conclusion

This study outlines a flexible SVS technique utilizing braided polyester suture for the management of comminuted inferior patellar fractures with osteoporosis. The technique has demonstrated satisfactory outcomes in our early follow-up assessments and presents clear advantages. This technique is simple and easy to perform, offering stable fixation and yielding positive functional outcomes. The flexible SVS technique presents several advantages: (1) Facilitates easy reduction and reduces surgery time; (2) Provides ample fixation strength, enabling early functional exercise; (3) Significantly reduces the risks of internal fixation failure, soft tissue irritation, and re-operation due to wire cutting; (4) Eliminates the need for re-operation to remove internal fixation. It is particularly suitable for elderly patients with comminuted inferior patellar fractures and osteoporosis.
