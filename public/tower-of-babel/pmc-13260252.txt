Harnessing the seaweed holobiont: antibacterial and antioxidant lead compounds from epiphytic microbiota
Authors: Manpreet Kaur, Felix Bast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeaweed-associated epiphytic bacteria represent a promising source of bioactive metabolites with antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. In this study, 26 culturable epiphytic bacterial isolates were obtained from five marine macroalgae, namely Ulva lactuca, Amphiroa anceps, Halimeda opuntia, Kappaphycus alvarezii, and Sargassum wightii. Preliminary screening using the agar overlay method revealed that six isolates (EB-30, EB-38, EB-42, EB-47, EB-48, and EB-50) exhibited antibacterial activity against at least two pathogenic test strains, accounting for 23% of the total isolates. Crude extracellular extracts demonstrated inhibitory effects in the disc diffusion assay, with inhibition zones ranging from 8 to 21 mm at 1 mg/mL. Among these, EB-38 showed the strongest antibacterial activity against Bacillus subtilis MTCC 441, Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96, and Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432, with minimum inhibitory concentrations ranging from 62.5 to 250 µg/mL. Antioxidant activity assessed by the DPPH radical scavenging assay showed concentration-dependent effects, with EB-38 extract exhibiting the highest scavenging efficiency (71.13 ± 1.03%) and the lowest IC₅₀ value (0.41881 ± 0.02544 mg/mL). Total phenolic content ranged from 7.7 ± 0.78 to 16.98 ± 1.73 mg GAE/g extract. Molecular identification based on partial 16 S rRNA gene sequencing revealed the isolates as Halobacillus sp. (EB-30), Exiguobacterium aestuarii (EB-38), Bacillus licheniformis (EB-42), Rossellomorea aquimaris (EB-47), Bacillus sp. (EB-48), and Pseudomonas sp. (EB-50). GC–MS analysis revealed bioactive compounds, including Pyrrolo[1,2-a] pyrazine-1,4-dione derivatives, fatty acid methyl esters, tert-butylated phenols, and 13-Docosenamide. However, further mechanistic investigations are required to elucidate the molecular basis of the observed antibacterial and antioxidant effects.Keywords: Antibacterial activity, Antioxidant activity, Epiphytic bacteria, GC-MS, Total phenolic content, Seaweeds

Introduction

Seaweeds, including red, green, and brown macroalgae, are one of the key primary producers in shallow intertidal coastal marine ecosystems (Raven 2018). In natural habitats, most macroalgal species offer a nutrient-rich microhabitat on their surface for microbial colonization and biofilm formation (Egan et al. 2013; Selvarajan et al. 2019). The macroalgae (basibiont) and associated epiphytic microbiome (epibiont) evolved as a functional unit known as the ‘macroalgal holobiont’, characterized by a complex chemical interaction (Saha et al. 2024). Epiphytic bacteria, as a part of the biofilm associated with algae, are popularly studied for their host benefits and diversity (Pattigundla et al. 2025). They are also regarded as rapid colonizers because of their ability to quickly metabolize the exudates of algae, which helps them to dominate the colonization of algal surfaces (Lachnit et al. 2009). It has been claimed that they can be found in all categories of seaweeds, including green, red, and brown seaweeds (Karthick and Mohanraju 2018), with different surface densities depending on lifespan, the seasons, and the other regions of the thallus (Bengtsson et al. 2010). Moreover, the normal morphological development and growth of seaweed is largely influenced by associated bacteria by providing CO2, fixed nitrogen required for its photo-autotrophy, survival, and growth (Menaa et al. 2020).

In recent years, researchers have shown interest in epiphytic marine bacteria as they are recognized as rich reservoirs for the production of various bioactive compounds that show broad-spectrum antimicrobial (including antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, antidiabetic, antimalarial, and anticancer) and antioxidant activities. Consequently, the extraction of these compounds is considered a priority and a new approach in the pharmaceutical and biotechnological industries (Singh et al. 2015). It was found that the secondary metabolites and allelochemicals secreted by seaweed-dwelling epiphytic bacteria that prevent microbiota and macrobiota colonization are effective against a wide variety of multidrug-resistant bacteria, such as vancomycin and methicillin-resistant bacteria, several fungal species, and cancer diseases as well (Penesyan et al. 2010). These secondary metabolites are predominant sources that enable large-scale production of valuable and promising therapeutic agents within a short time under controlled conditions. Moreover, Firmicutes and Gammaproteobacteria are the most studied among seaweed-associated bacteria, as they are abundant and possess excellent capabilities (Longford et al. 2007).

According to the literature, several studies revealed that many antibacterial activities were recorded from seaweed-associated bacteria. For example, Ismail et al. (2016) demonstrated that five bacterial isolates closely related to Bacillus pumilus, Pseudoalteromonas, and Pseudomonas sp., obtained from the seaweed Padina pavonica, exhibited antibacterial effects against MRSA, Salmonella typhimurium, Vibrio alginolyticus, and V. vulnificus with inhibition zones ranging from 7 to 25 mm. Similarly, Chakraborty et al. (2017) reported that Bacillus subtilis MTCC10403, isolated from the brown seaweed Anthophycus longifolius, showed strong antibacterial activity against Aeromonas hydrophila, Vibrio vulnificus, and V. parahaemolyticus with inhibition zones of 27, 25, and 22 mm, respectively. This activity was associated with the polyketide synthase gene (KC589397), signifying its capacity to generate bioactive polyketide molecules.

Moreover, antioxidant properties have also become a very crucial subject over the past two decades and the topic of exhaustive research all over the world because of food and pharmaceutical industry demands, which have investigated natural antioxidant sources to produce efficient antioxidant biomolecules as anti-carcinogenic and anti-aging agents. For a long time, most of the antioxidant ingredients, such as phenolic compounds, pigments, polysaccharides, and carotenoids, were extracted from plants, fruits, and seaweeds (Olszowy 2019; Hentati et al. 2018; Alwaleed et al. 2024). However, recent research has increasingly shifted toward marine bacteria because they offer a sustainable and continuous source of novel antioxidants, can be cultivated rapidly under controlled conditions, and often produce unique bioactive metabolites with higher stability, potency, and industrial scalability compared to traditional plant-based sources. For instance, George et al. (2022) demonstrated that an actinobacterial strain (Micromonospora sp.) isolated from marine sediment produced extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) with potent antioxidant activity, showing efficacy comparable to ascorbic acid. Similarly, Horta et al. (2014) reported that Bifurcaria bifurcate, an epiphytic bacterium, is an exceptional source of natural antioxidant and antimicrobial compounds. Recently, Mathew et al. (2026) reported that Gelidium pusillum harbors diverse epiphytic and endophytic bacterial communities capable of producing bioactive secondary metabolites with antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, highlighting their potential as promising sources of therapeutic agents.

In the present study, epiphytic bacterial strains were isolated from the surfaces of five different seaweed species and taxonomically identified using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. After isolation and identification, crude extracts prepared from the selected six bacterial strains were evaluated for their antioxidant and antimicrobial activities using standard in vitro assays. Antimicrobial activity was assessed by the agar disc diffusion method, while antioxidant activity was determined using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay. The primary objective of this investigation is to identify culturable seaweed-associated bacteria with promising bioactive properties and assess their potential applications in aquaculture, pharmaceuticals, and other biotechnological fields. Unlike earlier studies focused mainly on seaweed extracts or non-epiphytic microbes, this study integrates antibacterial and antioxidant screening with GC–MS-based metabolite profiling and molecular identification to support the development of sustainable microbial resources as alternatives to conventional natural product sources.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Five different seaweeds representing all three groups (Ulva lactuca (9.270067 N, 79.125133 E), Halimeda opuntia (9.291947 N, 79.144308 E), Amphiroa anceps (9.272283 N, 79.135800 E), Kappaphycus alvarezii (9.272283 N, 79.135800 E), and Sargassum wightii (9.270067 N, 79.125133 E) were collected from the coastal area of Mandapam, Tamil Nadu, on the southeast coast of India in May 2023, were then transported to the laboratory under cold (4℃) conditions (Fig. 1), and were identified as stated by Gabrielson et al. (2006), Jha et al. (2009), and Silva et al. (1996).

Five different seaweeds representing all three groups: (a) Ulva Lactuca, (b) Halimeda opuntia, (c) Amphiroa anceps, (d) Kappaphycus alvarezii, (e) Sargassum wightii

Isolation of epiphytic bacterial strains

In the laboratory, seaweed samples were washed three times with autoclaved seawater to get rid of sand particles, loosely bound epiphytes, and other attached settlements on the surface of the thallus (Burgess et al. 2003). Epiphytic bacteria were isolated as described by Akrong et al. (2023) with some modifications. Five grams of a washed seaweed sample were aseptically placed in a sterile 50 mL centrifuge tube (Tarsons, Kolkata, West Bengal), and 20 mL of sterile artificial seawater was added to it. The tubes containing the individual seaweeds were put in the shaker (Exome Innovation Lab 431 Shaking Incubator), which was set to rotate at 250 rpm and run for 30 min at room temperature to make sure that the bacterial cells detached from the surfaces of the seaweeds. After that, each centrifuge tube was vortexed (Vortex Mixer (S0200-230 V-EU, DLAB Scientific, Beijing, China) for 1 min, and by using a sterilized spreader, an aliquot of 100 µL was spread in triplicate on Zobell marine agar (2216, Himedia, Mumbai, Maharashtra) and was followed by incubation at 30 °C for 2–7 days (New Brunswick™ Innova® 42/42R; Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) till the growth of bacterial colonies was fully formed. After incubation, based on morphological features, single colonies were picked and restreaked till pure cultures were obtained. The purified colonies were then used for further analysis.

Antibacterial activity

Screening of antibacterial activity by the agar overlay method

The isolated bacteria were preliminarily screened for antibacterial activity against four test strains, i.e., Escherichia coli MTCC 443, Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96, Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432, and Bacillus subtilis MTCC 441, using the agar overlay assay method as described by Callewaert et al. (1999).

Extraction of metabolites

The six bacterial isolates that showed a significant antagonistic effect were selected for the extraction of metabolites as described by Priyanto et al. (2023) with little modifications. The bacterial colonies were inoculated into a flask containing 500 mL of Zobell marine broth (ZMB) and placed in a shaker (New Brunswick™ Innova® 42/42R; Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 120 rpm (± 28 ℃ for 3 days). Then, the culture broth was centrifuged (Centrifuge 5430 R; Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) (at 5,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C) and the supernatant was extracted thrice with an equal amount of ethyl acetate (1:1 ratio). The solvent phase was evaporated in a rotary evaporator (RV 10; IKA, Staufen, Germany) to obtain the bacterial metabolite’s crude extract. The collected extract was dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), filtered, and then stored in a refrigerator until required for further use.

Antibacterial assay by the disc diffusion method

A total of 20 µL of each extract (1 mg/mL) derived from six selected isolates was added dropwise to the 6-mm paper disk and placed into the NA plate inoculated with the target strain (1 × 108 CFU/mL). About 1% DMSO and 100 µg/mL chloramphenicol were used as a negative and positive control, respectively. All plates were incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. The clear zone diameter around the paper disk was measured and quantified in millimeters (Diale et al. 2018).

Determination of MIC and MBC

MIC was conducted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) broth microdilution guidelines. Initially, the Muller Hinton broth (100 µL) was distributed in the required wells of a 96-well microtiter plate. After that, 100 µL of stock solution (1000 µg/mL) of each bacterial extract was added in row 1, followed by carrying out a two-fold serial dilution, i.e., 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.2, 15.6, and 7.8 µg/mL, up to 8 rows. Later, each well (except the sterility control) was inoculated with 100 µL of test strains (0.5 McFarland concentrations, 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). Chloramphenicol (100 µg/mL) and 1% DMSO were used as positive and negative controls, and broth media as sterility control. The plates were incubated at 37 ± 1℃ for 18–24 h. Following incubation, 20 µl of 0.015% resazurin was added to each well, followed by an incubation period of 4 h. The resazurin dye indicates the presence of active bacterial cells, and its color changes upon a redox reaction caused by active cell metabolism. A color change from blue to pink after adding the dye signifies the presence of active bacteria. The MIC value per extract was assigned to the lowest concentration, where a color change was not observed (blue). Concentrations where bacteria did not grow served as the MBC (Gupta and Rana 2021).

Antioxidant activity

DPPH (2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay

The antioxidant potential of the six selected bacterial extracts was determined by the DPPH radical scavenging assay, as suggested by Kumar et al. (2008), with a few modifications. A 1 mM methanolic stock solution of DPPH was prepared by dissolving 7.88 mg of DPPH (Sigma Aldrich, Sweden) in 20 mL of HPLC-grade methanol. A working DPPH solution of a concentration of 0.1 mM for the radical scavenging assay was made. 1 mg of extract of each bacterial isolate was dissolved in 1 mL of methanol to prepare a stock solution of 1 mg/mL. The extract stock solution was diluted to prepare various concentrations of 1 mg/mL, 0.8 mg/mL, 0.6 mg/mL, 0.4 mg/mL, and 0.2 mg/mL. 500 µL of the extract solution was mixed with 1 mL of 0.1 mM methanolic DPPH solution and vortexed (Vortex Mixer (S0200-230 V-EU, DLAB Scientific, Beijing, China) vigorously. The samples were incubated in the dark for 30 min, and absorption was taken at 517 nm with methanol as a blank. A control solution was prepared by adding 500 µL of methanol and 1 mL of 0.1 mM methanolic DPPH. A methanolic solution of ascorbic acid of various concentrations was used as the reference standard. Total phenolic content

The total phenolic content of bacterial secondary metabolites was assessed using the Folin-Ciocalteu method, with gallic acid as the standard (Kumar et al. 2008). Briefly, 100 µL of the extract solution was added to 400 µL of distilled water, and 150 µL of Folin-Ciocalteau reagent (HiMedia Labs, India) was mixed in clean and well-labelled Eppendorf tubes. After 5–10 min, 500 µL of 20% Na2CO3 was added to the mixture and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min in dark conditions. The absorption was measured at 765 nm using MilliQ water as a blank. All the experiments were performed in triplicate. Phenolic compound levels were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (mg GAE/g extract).

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

The selected epiphytic bacterial isolates were identified based on 16S rRNA sequences. Genomic DNA of bacterial cells was extracted using the HiPurA® Bacterial genomic DNA purification kit (Himedia, Mumbai) as per the manufacturer’s protocols. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR in a final volume of 25 µl by using the bacterial consensus universal forward and reverse 16S rRNA primers (27F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’) (Lane 1991). The reaction mixture contained 1 µl of template DNA (approximately 25 ng), 12.5 µl of GoTaq® Green Master Mix, 2x (Promega), 1 µl each of primers (10 pmol), and the rest is water. The amplification profile consisted of an initial three-minute denaturation at 94 °C, followed by 35 cycles of one minute at 94 °C, one minute at 52 °C, and two minutes at 72 °C. After that, one last extension stage lasted eight minutes at 72 °C. The PCR products were purified using the SpiNXT gel extraction kit (Genes2me) and sequenced by an automated sequencer at Apical Scientific Sdn. Bhd., Malaysia. Accession numbers were assigned to the sequences upon submission to GenBank. To know the closest phylogenetic relatives of each isolate, 16 S rRNA gene sequences were then compared to the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST). The neighbor-joining method was used to perform the phylogenetic analysis of isolates using MEGA software version 11 (Tamura et al. 2021). The numbers at nodes represent the percentages of 1,000 bootstrap replications.

GC-MS analysis

The chemicals found in metabolites were identified using GC-MS that was performed using a Shimadzu QP 2010 Ultra (Japan). Helium gas was employed as the carrier fluid in a capillary column with a 30 m fused silica of 0.2 m internal diameter and 0.25 m film thickness. For GC-MS detection, an electron ionization system with an ionization energy of 70 eV was used in the electron impact mode. The injector temperature was kept constant at 250 °C, and the ion-source temperature was kept constant at 200 °C. The oven temperature was programmed to begin at 110 °C, then increased by 10 °C/min to 200 °C, and then increased by 5 °C/min to 280 °C, finishing with a 9-minute isothermal at 280 °C. Fragments ranging in size from 45 to 450 kDa were used in the mass spectra. The total GC–MS run time was 35 min, with a solvent delay of 0–2 min. The relative percentage quantity of each component was determined by comparing the average peak area of each component to the total areas of compounds in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Mass Spectra reference library (Sivaranjani et al. 2021).

Statistical data analysis

All antibacterial and antioxidant assays were carried out in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Antibacterial activity was analyzed in SigmaPlot 15.0 using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Total phenolic content and DPPH assay were evaluated using one-way and two-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 10. Further analysis was performed using the Tukey test, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Epiphytic bacteria isolated from seaweeds

A total of 26 epiphytic bacterial isolates were obtained from different seaweeds. Six isolates were obtained from Ulva lactuca, seven from Amphiroa anceps, three from Halimeda opuntia, one from Kappaphycus alvarezii, and nine from Sargassum wightii. Conversely, no bacteria were observed from the sterilized seaweed surface.

Antibacterial activity of bacterial isolates by the agar overlay method

Among 26 bacterial isolates, only six (EB-30, EB-38, EB-42, EB-47, EB-48, and EB-50) exhibited antibacterial activity with different spectra against at least two of the tested bacteria. Thus, 23% of the bacterial isolates were considered active against test strains. Isolate EB-38 showed a broad spectrum of antibacterial activity towards all four test strains. The other four isolates (EB-38, EB-42, EB-47, and EB-48) could inhibit three test strains. However, EB-50 isolate inhibits only two test strains, such as Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96 and Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432.

Antibacterial activity of epiphytic bacterial extract by disc diffusion

The crude extract derived from six selected isolates (EB-30, EB-38, EB-42, EB-47, EB-48, EB-50) varied in their ability to inhibit the growth of the test strains, with the inhibition zone diameter ranging from 8 to 21 mm, by applying 1 mg/mL extract (Table 1). The largest inhibition zone was shown by EB-38-derived extract against B. subtilis MTCC 441 (21 ± 0.30 mm). This isolate also exhibited the strongest antibacterial activity against two other tested strains, i.e., Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96 (18.86 ± 0.23), Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432 (17.06 ± 0.50) (Fig. 2). However, the extract of EB-48 isolate showed the weakest effectivity against Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96 (8 mm). As a positive control, chloramphenicol (1 mg/mL) also displayed an inhibition zone with a diameter ranging from 2 to 25.6 mm, while no inhibition zone was found in 1% DMSO as a negative control.

MIC and MBC

Depending on the target bacteria, the six potential extracts have diverse MICs and MBCs (Table 2). The extract from EB-38 showed a very low MIC for Bacillus subtilis MTCC 441 (62.50 µg/mL), Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96 (125 µg/mL), and Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432 (250 µg/mL). However, E. coli MTCC 443 was strongly inhibited by the extract of isolate EB-48, as indicated by the lowest MIC (125 µg/mL) among other isolates. For MBC, higher concentrations than MIC were needed to inhibit the tested bacterial growth for all extracts.

Antioxidant activity

DPPH (2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay

The results of DPPH radical scavenging activity of bacterial extracts are depicted in Fig. 3. Bacterial extracts showed 15.64 ± 0.79%-71.13 ± 1.03% of DPPH free radical scavenging activity at 200–1000 µg/mL concentration, which was modestly lower than the reference, ascorbic acid (75.95 ± 0.30%-76.8 ± 0.00%). The activity of each bacterial extract was increased with an increase in concentration, except EB-38 extract, which showed maximum activity (71.13 ± 1.03) at 600 µg/mL, after which a slight reduction was observed. In this assay, the extract of EB-38 also showed higher activity than other bacterial extracts, while the extract of EB-50 showed the lowest activity. Furthermore, the lower IC50 value of EB-38 extract indicates its highest potency to scavenge the free radicals as compared to other bacterial extracts (Table 3).

The total phenolic content of epiphytic bacterial extracts was determined from the calibration curve of gallic acid (Fig. 4a), and the results are expressed as gallic acid equivalents per gram of extract (GAE/g extract). The total phenolic content for bacterial extracts ranged from 7.7 ± 0.78 to 16.98 ± 1.73 mg GA/g (Fig. 4b). Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

The six bacterial isolates (EB-30, EB-38, EB-42, EB-47, EB-48, and EB-50) that showed a wide range of antibacterial and antioxidant activities were identified by partial 16 S rRNA gene sequencing (Table 4). The isolate EB-30 showed 100% similarity to Halobacillus sp., EB-38 to Exiguobacterium aestuarii, and EB-42 and EB-47 were identified as Bacillus licheniformis and Rossellomorea aquimaris, respectively. The isolate EB-48 was identified as Bacillus sp. with 99.92% similarity, and EB-50 was identified with 99.93% similarity as Pseudomonas sp. The sequences of these isolates have been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) under accession numbers PQ443979 (EB-30), PQ443986 (EB-38), PQ443990 (EB-42), PQ443994 (EB-47), PV563686 (EB-48), and PQ443996 (EB-50). Chemical Profiles of Selected Bacterial Extracts

The chemical constituents from each extract were identified using GC–MS analysis (Fig. 6). The identified compounds belonged to a variety of chemical classes, such as phenolic compounds, fatty acid methyl esters, amides, hydrocarbons, aldehydes, ketones, pyrroles, and terpenes. Several of the detected molecules in the crude extracts exhibited known antimicrobial and antioxidant properties (Table 5), such as Pyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione, various methyl esters, Octadecene, Phenol 3,5-bis(1,1-dimethylethyl)-, and 13-Docosenamide. Notably, recurring substances such as Pyrrolo[1,2-a] pyrazine-1,4-dione, tert-butylated phenol derivatives, fatty acid methyl esters, and 13-Docosenamide were repeatedly identified in the extracts, indicating their significant role in the observed bioactivities.

Bioactive compounds identified through GC–MS analysis of epiphytic bacterial extracts

Discussion

The excessive abuse of antibiotics in medical treatments has driven viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites to develop resistance, reducing the effectiveness of these medications. Consequently, this leads to a threatening phenomenon known as antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Therefore, it is essential to explore novel alternative therapies that can help to combat or reduce the incidence of drug-resistant bacterial infections. Marine ecosystems, particularly seaweeds, have recently gained recognition as promising reservoirs of unexplored microbial diversity (Singh and Reddy 2014). Seaweeds naturally serve as ecological niches that support a broad spectrum of heterotrophic and epiphytic bacteria, many of which play crucial roles in maintaining the health and resilience of their hosts (Egan et al. 2013; Singh and Reddy 2014). These bacteria contribute to the seaweed’s defense by inhibiting colonization by pathogenic or fouling organisms, suggesting the presence of biologically active metabolites with antimicrobial properties (Asharaf et al. 2022). This ecological interaction highlights the potential of seaweed-associated bacteria as producers of chemically diverse and functionally potent secondary metabolites. Many of these metabolites exhibit antibacterial, antifungal, antibiofilm, and even antiviral activities, making them promising candidates for therapeutic development (Penesyan et al. 2010). Thus, exploring these microorganisms not only enhances our understanding of seaweed-microbe symbiosis but also contributes to the discovery of innovative bioactive molecules capable of combating antimicrobial resistance.

In the present study, 26 bacterial isolates were obtained from different seaweed species. Out of the 26 isolates, six isolates exhibited considerable antibacterial activity against four test strains, namely Escherichia coli MTCC 443, Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96, Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432, and Bacillus subtilis MTCC 441. Based on the 16 S rRNA sequence, these isolates were closely related to Halobacillus sp. (EB-30), Exiguobacterium aestuarii (EB-38), Bacillus licheniformis (EB-42), Rossellomorea aquimaris (EB-47), Bacillus sp. (EB-48), and Pseudomonas sp. (EB-50). The isolates displayed a wide range of antibacterial activity, demonstrating variable inhibitory potential against the investigated microorganisms. To confirm the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of six isolates (EB-30, EB-38, EB-42, EB-47, EB-48, and EB-50), their metabolites were extracted and tested for antibacterial activity using the disk diffusion method. By applying epiphytic-derived extracts at 1 mg/mL, these epiphyte extracts showed an inhibitory effect on all tested strains.

Among these, isolate EB-38 (E. aestuarii) exhibited strong inhibitory activity against Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and B. subtilis), along with moderate inhibition of Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and K. pneumoniae), supporting the broad-spectrum nature of its antibacterial metabolites. These results found harmony with an earlier study conducted by Cavanaugh et al. (2021), who found that an environmental Exiguobacterium isolate produced extracellular metabolites capable of inhibiting both Gram-positive (S. aureus, B. subtilis) and Gram-negative (E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa) bacteria, confirming its broad-spectrum antibacterial potential. Similarly, Mekala et al. (2020) reported potent bactericidal activity of a bioactive pigment produced by an environmental Exiguobacterium strain against several food-borne pathogens, with membrane disruption identified as the primary mode of action. The reported low MIC values (62.5–500 µg/mL) further demonstrated the strong antibacterial potency of the compounds produced.

In addition to Exiguobacterium aestuarii (EB-38), the other five isolates—Halobacillus sp. (EB-30), Bacillus licheniformis (EB-42), Rossellomorea aquimaris (EB-47), Bacillus sp. (EB-48), and Pseudomonas sp. (EB-50)—also exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against all four test strains. Among these, Bacillus licheniformis (EB-42) and Bacillus sp. (EB-48) showed comparatively stronger inhibition, consistent with their known capacity to produce diverse antimicrobial compounds, including lipopeptides, bacteriocins, and exopolysaccharides (Shleeva et al. 2023). These compounds exert antibacterial effects through multiple mechanisms: lipopeptides such as surfactin and fengycin can disrupt bacterial membranes and inhibit biofilm formation; bacteriocins act as ribosomally synthesized peptides that target closely related or pathogenic bacteria; and exopolysaccharides interfere with bacterial adhesion and colonization, thereby limiting biofilm development. Additional studies on marine Bacillus isolates confirm broad-spectrum antagonism, including against multidrug-resistant pathogens, supporting their potential as sources of novel antibiotics (Kiran et al. 2018; Kamilari et al. 2025). The isolates Halobacillus sp. (EB-30), Rossellomorea aquimaris (EB-47), and Pseudomonas sp. (EB-50) also displayed clear inhibitory effects, though with variable potency against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. These findings collectively highlight that seaweed-associated epiphytic bacteria represent a rich reservoir of antimicrobial metabolites, reinforcing the potential of these isolates for applications in pharmaceutical development and biocontrol strategies.

Bacteria are increasingly recognized not just for their antimicrobial potential but also as producers of natural antioxidant compounds. Several studies have documented that bacterial extracts and metabolites can scavenge free radicals, reduce oxidants, and chelate reactive metal ions, thus protecting cells from oxidative stress (Arslan et al. 2025). For example, a marine Actinobacteria strain from the Gulf of Khambhat exhibited strong antioxidant activity (free-radical scavenging) along with antibacterial effects (Dholakiya et al. 2017). In salt-tolerant bacteria (including species of Bacillus) isolated from saline environments, high salinity stress triggers enhanced production of antioxidant metabolites and enzymes (such as phenolics, flavonoids, or glutathione), helping these microbes to cope with oxidative damage (Hassan et al. 2020).

Moreover, some extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) produced by marine bacteria have shown radical-scavenging capacity, metal-chelation ability, and inhibition of lipid peroxidation characteristics associated with strong antioxidant potential (Shleeva et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024). These bacterial-derived antioxidants are significant because they may serve dual roles: protecting the producing bacteria under environmental stress (e.g., salinity, UV, reactive oxygen species) and offering bioactive compounds with potential therapeutic, nutraceutical, or cosmeceutical applications for humans (e.g., combating oxidative stress-related diseases, preventing oxidative damage) (Arslan et al. 2025). In our study, Halobacillus sp. (EB-30) and Exiguobacterium aestuarii (EB-38) displayed comparatively high potency to scavenge the free radicals as compared to other bacterial extracts, as indicated by the lower IC50 value (EB-30: IC50–0.59363 ± 0.01036 mg/mL, EB-38: IC50–0.41881 ± 0.02544 mg/mL). Although the DPPH assay demonstrated notable free-radical scavenging activity, it provides only a preliminary estimation of antioxidant potential and does not fully reflect physiological antioxidant mechanisms. The assay is sensitive to the solvent system, extract concentration, and incubation conditions, and primarily detects hydrogen-donating antioxidants. Therefore, additional complementary assays (e.g., metal chelation, reducing power, or cellular antioxidant assays) and in vivo validation are necessary to comprehensively evaluate antioxidant efficacy and therapeutic relevance.

To obtain detailed knowledge of the chemical profiles of six epiphytic bacterial extracts, GC-MS was used. The results showed that some chemical classes, such as phenolic compounds, fatty acid methyl esters, amides, hydrocarbons, aldehydes, ketones, pyrroles, and terpenes, were found as the major components in the extracts. Several antibacterial/antimicrobial and antioxidant compounds, such as Pyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione, various methyl esters, Octadecene, Phenol 3,5-bis(1,1-dimethylethyl)-, and 13-Docosenamide, were detected in the extracts, likely responsible for the antibacterial and antioxidant activity of those extracts. These results are consistent with previous GC-MS-based studies, which have reported the presence of similar bioactive compounds in marine bacterial extracts. For instance, Kiran et al. (2018) reported the isolation of pyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione, hexahydro from the marine bacterium Bacillus tequilensis MSI45 and demonstrated its potent antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. Similarly, Ser et al. (2015) detected pyrrolo[1,2-a]pyrazine-1,4-dione, hexahydro in a newly isolated Streptomyces sp. and highlighted its significant antioxidant and free-radical-scavenging potential.

In addition, other metabolites identified in our study have been documented in earlier research, further supporting their role as key bioactive constituents in marine bacterial extracts. For example, in a study of Nocardiopsis dassonvillei from a marine source, GC-MS analysis identified 1-octadecene (along with other compounds) among the principal metabolites in the bioactive extract linked to antimicrobial potential (Ngema et al. 2023). Chemical profiling of extracts of endophytic bacterium Pseudomonas campi commonly identifies phenolic antioxidants such as phenol, 3,5-bis(1,1-dimethylethyl)-, a well-established contributor to both antioxidant and antibacterial activities (Bhuiyan et al. 2025). Moreover, Conde-Martínez et al. (2017) reported that the halophilic bacterium Vibrio sp. A1SM3-36-8, isolated from a solar saltern, produced 13-cis-docosenamide as one of its predominant metabolites, which was linked to notable antibacterial and anticancer activities.

Importantly, the presence of these metabolites may reflect adaptive responses of epiphytic bacteria to the chemically rich seaweed surface environment. Recent GC–MS profiling and in silico studies of seaweed-derived extracts have identified phenolics, fatty acid derivatives, and terpenoids with antibacterial and antibiofilm activities, suggesting that host metabolites can influence the functional potential of surface-associated microbiota through ecological selection (Alwaleed et al. 2025). Likewise, biochemical studies have shown that seaweeds are rich in nutraceuticals and diverse secondary metabolites (Alwaleed 2019), which may support the growth of metabolically versatile epiphytic bacteria capable of producing pharmacologically important compounds.

Conclusions

Seaweed-associated epiphytic bacteria provide unique and novel metabolites of unprecedented structures that display significant antibacterial and antioxidant properties. The present study reported that 6 out of 26 epiphytic bacteria isolated from green (Ulva lactuca, Halimeda opuntia), red (Amphiroa anceps, Kappaphycus alvarezii), and brown (Sargassum wightii) seaweeds showed antibacterial activity against four test strains (Escherichia coli MTCC 443, Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 96, Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 432, and Bacillus subtilis MTCC 441) with different spectra. EB-38 showed the strongest antibacterial activity against B. subtilis MTCC 441 (MIC-62.5 µg/mL) and S. aureus MTCC 96 (MIC-125 µg/mL), while EB-48 was most active against E. coli MTCC 443 (MIC-125 µg/mL). Furthermore, in the DPPH assay, the lower IC50 (0.41881 ± 0.02544 mg/mL) value of EB-38 extract indicates its highest potency to scavenge the free radicals as compared to other bacterial extracts. GC-MS analysis showed that several biologically active compounds were found in all extracts of bacteria. Thus, these bioactive compounds may provide high-quality drug candidates for pharmaceutical applications, as well as agricultural and industrial applications.

However, the study was limited to culturable isolates, crude extract evaluation, preliminary in vitro assays, and tentative GC–MS identification. In addition, environmental factors such as season and geographic variation were not extensively controlled. Future investigations integrating metagenomic and high-throughput genomic approaches, compound purification, advanced structural characterization (LCMS/MS and NMR analysis), mechanistic studies, and in vivo validation will be essential to fully explore and exploit the biotechnological potential of seaweed-associated bacteria.
