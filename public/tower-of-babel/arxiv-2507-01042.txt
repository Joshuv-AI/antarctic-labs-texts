Can Argus Judge Them All? Comparing VLMs Across Domains
Harsh Joshi, Gautam Siddharth Kashyap, Rafiq Ali, Ebad Shabbir, Niharika Jain, Sarthak Jain, Jiechao Gao, Usman Naseem

Abstract
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are advancing multimodal AI, yet their performance consistency across tasks is underexamined. We benchmark CLIP, BLIP, and LXMERT across diverse datasets spanning retrieval, captioning, and reasoning. Our evaluation includes task accuracy, generation quality, efficiency, and a novel Cross-Dataset Consistency (CDC) metric. CLIP shows strongest generalization (CDC: 0.92), BLIP excels on curated data, and LXMERT leads in structured reasoning. These results expose trade-offs between generalization and specialization, informing industrial deployment of VLMs and guiding development toward robust, task-flexible architectures.

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) such as CLIP
Sun et al. 2024

Radford et al. 2021
, BLIP
Dai et al. 2023

Current evaluation practices

To address this, we benchmark CLIP, BLIP, and LXMERT across diverse datasets covering retrieval, caption generation, and visual reasoning. We introduce task-specific metrics and a novel Cross-Dataset Consistency (CDC) score to assess generalization.

Multimodal VLM Benchmarking Protocol

3.3
Modeling

To conduct a comprehensive empirical benchmarking of VLMs, we systematically executed a multi-stage experimental protocol encompassing diverse models and heterogeneous multimodal datasets as illustrated in Algorithm
1
. Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}\}
represent the set of evaluated models—each characterized by distinct architectural designs and pretraining regimes. The dataset collection is denoted as
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},D_{4},D_{5}\}
. Each dataset
D_{i}
consists of instances
x_{i}=(v_{i},t_{i})
, where
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
denotes visual inputs (e.g., images or region features), and
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
corresponds to textual inputs (e.g., captions, questions, or descriptive annotations). Given the heterogeneity in data structures and annotation formats, we designed modality-specific preprocessing pipelines
\mathcal{P}_{i}
for each dataset to ensure compatibility across all models. Specifically,
\mathcal{P}_{i}
performs image resizing to fixed resolutions, text tokenization aligned with each model’s vocabulary, and normalization of input feature scales, formalized as according to Equation (1).

\tilde{x}_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{i}(x_{i})=\big(\tilde{v}_{i},\tilde{t}_{i}\big),\quad\tilde{v}_{i}=\phi_{v}(v_{i}),\quad\tilde{t}_{i}=\phi_{t}(t_{i})

where
\phi_{v}
and
\phi_{t}
denote the respective visual and textual preprocessing functions. Subsequently, each preprocessed instance
\tilde{x}_{i}
was fed into every model
M_{j}
, which can be formally described as a parameterized mapping as shown in Equation (2).

f_{j}:\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\to\mathcal{Y}_{i},\quad\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{i})

where
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
is the task-specific output space. Due to the diverse architectures, including transformer-based encoders and cross-modal attention mechanisms, we ensured that input formatting adhered strictly to each model’s required modality embedding schemes and positional encodings. Therefore, for uniformity and reproducibility, inference for all models was conducted under controlled hardware conditions, with batch processing performed to optimize throughput while maintaining consistent latency measurements. Model inputs were batched as
\mathbf{X}_{i}=\{\tilde{x}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{x}_{i}^{(2)},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{i}^{(B)}\}
with batch size
B
, facilitating parallel computation as shown in Equation (3).

\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{i,j}=f_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{i})=\{f_{j}(\tilde{x}_{i}^{(b)})\}_{b=1}^{B}

where
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{i,j}
denotes the set of predictions generated by model
M_{j}
on dataset
D_{i}
. Finally, to capture performance variability stemming from dataset characteristics and model design, we repeated inference over multiple randomized data splits when applicable, enabling statistical rigor in subsequent analyses.

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Evaluation Metrics

To assess the performance of VLMs across heterogeneous tasks and datasets, we employ a comprehensive suite of evaluation metrics tailored to the objectives of various multimodal tasks, including image-text retrieval, caption generation, and visual reasoning.

Retrieval Tasks:

For image-to-text and text-to-image matching on COCO, Flickr30k, and Visual Genome, we report standard ranking metrics.
Recall at
K
(R@K)
for
K\in\{1,5,10\}
quantifies the proportion of correct results among the top-
K
retrieved items. In addition,
Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)
averages the inverse rank of the first correct result, while
Median Rank (MedR)
denotes the typical rank position of the first relevant item, capturing robustness to outliers.
Note:
We evaluate Image
\rightarrow
Text retrieval for COCO and Flickr30k because both datasets provide multiple captions per image, making them well-suited for retrieving the most relevant textual description given an image. In contrast, we use Text
\rightarrow
Image retrieval for Visual Genome, which contains fine-grained textual region descriptions associated with complex scenes, making it more appropriate to retrieve the most relevant image (or region) for a given text query.

Visual Question Answering:

We evaluate visual reasoning performance using the CLEVR and VCR datasets. For CLEVR, we report overall
classification accuracy
. For VCR, we follow the standard evaluation protocol and report performance on two subtasks:
Q→A
, measuring the model’s ability to choose the correct answer given a question, and
QA→R
, evaluating the model’s capacity to select the correct rationale for a chosen answer.

Caption Generation.

On COCO and Flickr30k, we assess language generation using widely adopted natural language metrics.
BLEU-
n
scores (for
n=1,2,3,4
) capture n-gram precision, but are known to penalize legitimate paraphrases and lack semantic grounding. To address this, we also report
METEOR
, which incorporates stemming and synonym matching for better alignment with human judgment, and
CIDEr
, which emphasizes consensus with human references through tf-idf-weighted n-gram matching. Where applicable, we further evaluate factual consistency using the
SPICE
metric, which parses captions into scene graphs and compares semantic content, or conduct qualitative human assessments for hallucination detection.

Efficiency Metrics.

Given the increasing demand for real-time deployment on edge devices, we measure computational efficiency through
inference latency
(ms/sample),
memory footprint
(MB),
FLOPs
, and
throughput
(samples/sec).

Generalization Metric.

To quantify robustness across diverse datasets and tasks, we introduce a novel metric called
Cross-Dataset Consistency (CDC)
. Let
a_{i,j}
denote the accuracy of model
M_{j}
on dataset
D_{i}
, and
\bar{a}_{j}
its average accuracy across all datasets. Then, CDC is defined as according to Equation (4).

\text{CDC}(M_{j})=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left|\frac{a_{i,j}-\bar{a}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{j}}\right|

A higher CDC value indicates lower performance deviation across datasets, reflecting greater generalization and stability.
Note:
In all result tables, ↑ indicates that higher values are better, while ↓ indicates that lower values are better. For all accuracy-based metrics, we report the mean and standard deviation over multiple random seeds unless otherwise specified.

Theoretical Properties of CDC:

To ensure that our proposed
CDC
metric is mathematically grounded and interpretable, we provide several theoretical guarantees that establish its behavior under known conditions.

Lemma 4.1

(Boundedness)
.

Let
a_{i,j}\in[0,1]
for all datasets
D_{i}
, and let
\bar{a}_{j}
be the average accuracy of model
M_{j}
across all datasets. Then:
0\leq\text{CDC}(M_{j})\leq 1
.

Proof.

By definition,
\text{CDC}(M_{j})=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}|}\left|\frac{a_{i,j}-\bar{a}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{j}}\right|
. Each term inside the summation is non-negative. The maximal deviation occurs when
a_{i,j}=0
or
a_{i,j}=1
, and since all
a_{i,j}\in[0,1]
, the absolute relative deviation is bounded above. Hence, the CDC score lies within the interval
[0,1]
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Perfect Consistency)
.

If a model
M_{j}
achieves identical performance across all datasets, i.e.,
a_{i,j}=\bar{a}_{j}
for all
i
, then:
\text{CDC}(M_{j})=1
.

Proof.

Substituting
a_{i,j}=\bar{a}_{j}
into the definition yields:
\left|\frac{a_{i,j}-\bar{a}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{j}}\right|=0\quad\forall i
. Thus, the summation is zero and
\text{CDC}(M_{j})=1
, indicating perfect consistency across datasets.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Monotonicity under Uniform Scaling)
.

Suppose each dataset accuracy is scaled uniformly by a constant factor
\epsilon>0
, i.e.,
a_{i,j}^{\prime}=\epsilon\cdot a_{i,j}
. Then the CDC remains unchanged:
\text{CDC}(M_{j}^{\prime})=\text{CDC}(M_{j})
.

Proof.

Let
\bar{a}_{j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{i}a_{i,j}
, and define
\bar{a}_{j}^{\prime}=\epsilon\cdot\bar{a}_{j}
. Then:
\left|\frac{a_{i,j}^{\prime}-\bar{a}_{j}^{\prime}}{\bar{a}_{j}^{\prime}}\right|=\left|\frac{\epsilon a_{i,j}-\epsilon\bar{a}_{j}}{\epsilon\bar{a}_{j}}\right|=\left|\frac{a_{i,j}-\bar{a}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{j}}\right|
. Hence, the relative deviations are preserved and
\text{CDC}(M_{j}^{\prime})=\text{CDC}(M_{j})
.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Sensitivity to Outliers)
.

If one dataset
D_{k}
exhibits large deviation while all other accuracies are close to
\bar{a}_{j}
, then the CDC score degrades proportionally to the relative deviation of
a_{k,j}
.

Proof.

Let
a_{i,j}\approx\bar{a}_{j}
for all
i\neq k
, and let
a_{k,j}
differ significantly. Then:
\text{CDC}(M_{j})=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\left(\sum_{i\neq k}\left|\frac{a_{i,j}-\bar{a}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{j}}\right|+\left|\frac{a_{k,j}-\bar{a}_{j}}{\bar{a}_{j}}\right|\right)
. Thus, the deviation introduced by a single outlier dataset reduces the CDC score linearly in proportion to its normalized error.
∎

Interpretation.
From an industrial standpoint, the theoretical properties of the CDC metric offer strong assurances for its use in real-world AI evaluation pipelines, particularly in scenarios involving multimodal and cross-domain applications. The boundedness of CDC guarantees interpretability on a standardized scale
[0,1]
, enabling consistent benchmarking across internal QA systems, production-grade models, and external benchmarks. The reward for uniformity ensures that models demonstrating balanced performance across datasets—rather than excelling on one and failing on others—are favored, aligning well with enterprise needs for robust, fair, and reproducible AI systems across diverse user segments and geographic regions. Further, CDC’s sensitivity to instability makes it an effective signal for detecting brittle models, which may overfit to certain dataset characteristics but generalize poorly in deployment environments with distributional shifts (e.g., novel camera angles, linguistic variation, or unseen demographic data). This is crucial for safety-critical applications in domains such as autonomous driving, healthcare diagnostics, or financial document analysis, where uneven performance can result in regulatory risk or customer dissatisfaction. Importantly, the metric’s invariance to linear scaling of accuracy values implies that CDC reflects true consistency, independent of baseline performance inflation. This is especially relevant in edge and embedded deployments, where models may be compressed or quantized—affecting absolute performance but not consistency.

4.2
Hyperparameters

All models were evaluated under a unified inference setup to ensure fairness and reproducibility. Experiments were run on an NVIDIA A100 GPU (40GB VRAM) using a batch size of 32. Visual inputs were resized to
224\times 224
pixels, and text inputs were tokenized using model-specific tokenizers—BERT for LXMERT and VisualBERT, and byte-level BPE for CLIP and BLIP. The maximum text sequence length was capped at 40 tokens, with truncation and padding applied. Inference used mixed-precision (FP16) for efficiency, with 10 warm-up iterations before measurement. Dropout (rate 0.1) was retained but models were run in evaluation mode. No test-time augmentation or ensembling was applied. Implementations used PyTorch 2.0.1 and HuggingFace Transformers 4.31, with preprocessing via OpenCV 4.8.0 and SpaCy 3.6.1. Visual features were normalized per-channel, and all pretrained weights were sourced from official repositories to ensure fidelity.

5
Results and Analysis

5.1
Image-Text Retrieval

Image-text retrieval evaluates a VLM’s ability to align visual inputs with textual descriptions. We benchmarked CLIP, BLIP, and LXMERT on COCO, Flickr30k, and Visual Genome using standard metrics—Recall@K (
K=1,5,10
), MRR, and MedR (see Table
1
). BLIP outperformed others across COCO and Flickr30k. On COCO, BLIP achieved R@1 of 78.9%, surpassing CLIP (74.0%) and LXMERT (71.2%), with consistent gains in R@5/10, MRR (0.84), and MedR (1). On Flickr30k, BLIP reached R@1 of 76.5%, ahead of CLIP by 4.4% and LXMERT by 7.6%, reflecting its robust alignment and ranking capabilities. CLIP showed strong generalization due to its large-scale contrastive training, but lagged in fine-grained alignment. LXMERT, while weaker on COCO/Flickr30k, showed relative strength on Visual Genome’s text-to-image retrieval (R@1 = 70.4%), likely due to its structured reasoning modules. BLIP followed closely (68.3%), and CLIP trailed (66.8%) with higher MedR (3). BLIP’s consistent MRR and low MedR values suggest superior retrieval fidelity, attributed to its fusion of generative modeling and vision-language pretraining. In contrast, LXMERT’s inconsistent scores highlight its sensitivity to visual complexity and distractors.

5.2

We evaluate VLMs on CLEVR and VCR (see Table

On CLEVR, LXMERT achieves 96.3% accuracy, outperforming BLIP (89.7%) and CLIP (84.5%), thanks to its explicit relational reasoning modules that effectively handle structured, logic-driven queries. The synthetic nature of CLEVR benefits LXMERT’s architecture, which incorporates object-level attention and spatial reasoning. For VCR, which tests real-world commonsense reasoning, BLIP leads in the
Q→A
task with 74.8%, driven by its strong vision-language pretraining. In the
QA→R

Q→A

QA→R

Caption Generation

5.4
Computational Efficiency

As shown in Table
4
, CLIP is the most efficient model across all benchmarks, offering the lowest latency (28–35 ms), minimal memory usage (0.8–1.1 GB), and the fewest FLOPs (33–38 GFLOPs). This efficiency stems from its streamlined architecture and contrastive training paradigm. In contrast, BLIP and LXMERT incur significantly higher costs. LXMERT reaches up to 180 ms latency and 4.5 GB memory usage on Visual Genome, due to its heavy multimodal fusion and cross-attention modules. BLIP, while more efficient than LXMERT, still demands 3.2–4.1 GB memory and over 120 GFLOPs on average. Throughput metrics confirm this trend: CLIP handles 29–35 samples/s, BLIP manages 11–15, and LXMERT trails with 8–11, especially struggling with reasoning-heavy datasets like VCR. These results underscore CLIP’s practicality for real-time and resource-constrained deployments.

Note:
All computational efficiency results were obtained using NVIDIA A100 GPUs to ensure consistent benchmarking across large-scale models. Edge devices such as Jetson Nano or Raspberry Pi, while relevant for deployment, lack the memory and compute capacity to run models like BLIP and LXMERT in full precision without aggressive quantization or architectural pruning. Evaluating on A100 offers a reliable upper-bound analysis of efficiency and throughput in realistic server-side or cloud-inference scenarios, aligning with industry practices for scalable vision-language applications.

Conclusion

We benchmarked BLIP, CLIP, and LXMERT across key multimodal tasks. BLIP leads in retrieval and captioning due to its generative pretraining and strong vision-language alignment. LXMERT excels in reasoning-heavy tasks like CLEVR and VCR (
QA→R
), benefiting from its cross-modal attention design. CLIP, optimized for contrastive alignment, shows robust generalization but lags in generation and reasoning. These results underscore the trade-offs between generative, contrastive, and reasoning-centric VLMs, and highlight the potential of hybrid models that integrate these complementary strengths.

Limitations

This evaluation focuses on standard benchmarks, which may not reflect real-world diversity, noise, or domain-specific constraints. We use fixed model checkpoints without task-specific fine-tuning, limiting adaptability insights. While performance metrics are covered, production-critical aspects like model size, hardware cost, and energy efficiency are not analyzed. Tasks such as multimodal dialogue and grounded generation remain outside the scope. Lastly, aggregate scores may mask edge-case or rare-language failures, which are important in high-stakes applications.

Ethics Statement

VLMs like CLIP, BLIP, and LXMERT are trained on large-scale web data, which can embed and amplify societal biases or misinformation during deployment. Generative features also pose risks of hallucination, misinformation, or deepfake misuse. Benchmark datasets (e.g., COCO, Flickr30k) may reflect cultural or demographic biases, limiting global applicability. Industry deployment should prioritize dataset transparency, bias audits, and safety mechanisms to ensure responsible and equitable use.
