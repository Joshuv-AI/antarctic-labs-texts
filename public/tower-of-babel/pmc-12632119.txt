Combined association between phenotypic age acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality in middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors
Authors: Huanrui Zhang, Wen Tian, Guoxian Qi, Baosen Zhou, Yujiao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examined the associations of phenotypic age (PhenoAge) acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality risk among middle-aged and older adult cancer survivors.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 2958 cancer survivors aged 40 and older using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2003-2018. Dietary fiber intake was assessed via two 24-hour dietary recalls, calculated using the USDA database. High dietary fiber intake was defined as the top quartile. Biological age was estimated using the PhenoAge algorithm with 8 biomarkers, defining accelerated aging as PhenoAge >0. Mortality status was determined through linkage with the National Death Index. Cox regression models and Kaplan-Meier curves were used to examine the separate and combined association of dietary fiber intake and PhenoAge acceleration on mortality, adjusting for multiple covariates. Multiplicative interaction was assessed by including a product term in the cox regression models, while additive interaction was examined using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion (AP). Restricted cubic spline models were applied to estimate the linear/non-linear relationships between PhenoAge acceleration, dietary fiber intake, and mortality risk. Sensitivity analysis was used to validate previous findings.ResultsThe study included 2,958 cancer survivors with a baseline mean age of 65.58 years. During a 17-year follow-up (median [IQR]: 79 [42-125] months), 915 participants died. Accelerated aging had elevated risk of all-cause (HR (95% CI), 2.12 (1.78, 2.52), P<0.001), cardiovascular (HR (95% CI), 1.72 (1.18, 2.51), P=0.005), cancer (HR (95% CI), 2.68 (2.00, 3.58), P<0.001), and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality (HR (95% CI), 2.04 (1.58, 2.63), P<0.001). Low dietary fiber intake was linked to increased all-cause (HR (95% CI), 1.31 (1.05, 1.63), P=0.015), cancer (HR (95% CI), 1.59 (1.11, 2.27), P=0.011), and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality (HR (95% CI), 1.64 (1.11, 2.43), P=0.013). The "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group had the highest all-cause mortality risk (HR (95% CI), 2.45 (1.81, 3.33), P<0.001), cancer mortality risk (HR (95% CI), 3.40 (2.16, 5.37), P<0.001), non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality risk (HR (95% CI), 3.18 (1.91, 5.29), P<0.001), compared to the “Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber” group. Furthermore, a significant additive interaction was observed between PhenoAge acceleration and low dietary fiber intake on cancer mortality (RERI = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.23–2.64; AP = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.08–0.76). The results of the sensitivity analysis excluding samples that died within two years are consistent with the main analysis.ConclusionsPhenoAge acceleration and lower dietary fiber intake are associated with increased mortality risk in middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors. Notably, dietary fiber intake significantly modified the association between PhenoAge acceleration and cancer mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01245-y.Keywords: Accelerated aging, Dietary fiber, Cancer survivor, Mortality, Adult

Introduction

Cancer, being a disease associated with aging, continues to present a substantial menace to global public health. The incidence of cancer is projected to be approximately 60% among individuals aged 65 and above, while around 70% of cancer-related deaths are expected to occur in this age group [1]. Given the intricate and multifaceted nature of middle-aged and elderly cancer patients, they necessitate a greater allocation of social and medical resources, which will lead to heavy burden on both individuals and society [2]. The global incidence and mortality of cancer are gradually increasing due to the aging demographic trend, highlighting aging as the primary risk factor for both cancer development and mortality [3, 4]. However, significant disparities in vulnerabilities to age-related illnesses and mortality may exist among individuals sharing the same chronological age, potentially due to variances in the biological mechanisms that drive their aging processes [5]. Biological age has been demonstrated to be a more precise predictor of remaining lifespan as opposed to chronological age, as it offers deeper insights into an individual's physiological state and vulnerability to age-related mortality [6, 7]. Recently, there have been several emerging approaches for assessing biological age, which rely on indicators from molecular biology, clinical laboratory data, or composite measurements [8, 9]. The development of biological age indicators based on the association between clinical laboratory measures and chronological age has been suggested to possess superior predictive power for mortality and age-related diseases compared to other approaches used for assessing biological age [6, 10, 11]. Phenotypic age (PhenoAge), derived from clinical information obtained in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), demonstrates strong predictive capabilities for both morbidity and mortality outcomes, providing a more dependable means of forecasting the aging process [5]. PhenoAge acceleration is a quantitative measure that assesses the disparity between an individual's biological age and their chronological age, serving as potential indications of accelerated aging [6]. The process of accelerated aging is intricately linked to overall health, significantly heightening the susceptibility to a multitude of chronic ailments such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, respiratory disease and cancer [12–15], ultimately elevating the risk of mortality [16]. While previous research has identified biological aging as an independent prognostic factor for mortality in cancer survivors [17], no studies have investigated the integration of nutritional factors with biological aging metrics to uncover their synergistic association on mortality in this population.

Dietary fiber is widely acknowledged by nutritionists and health experts as an essential constituent of a well-balanced diet, playing a pivotal role in the preservation of general well-being [18, 19]. The lack of dietary fiber has been linked to a heightened susceptibility to different forms of cancer, including colon, liver, breast, renal, pancreatic, gastric, and esophageal adenocarcinoma. Moreover, it has been found to contribute to higher overall cancer mortality rates [20, 21]. However, inconsistent conclusions have also been reported [22, 23]. Several researches have confirmed a clear correlation between the consumption of dietary fiber and the length of telomeres, as well as a reduction in age acceleration [24–26]. However, there is still a lack of studies validating the association of dietary fiber on mortality risk among middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors, as well as the combined association of dietary fiber and accelerated aging on this population.

This study aimed to examine the combined associations of phenotypic age acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality risk among middle-aged and older adult cancer survivors. Using data from the NHANES database, we analyzed their relationships with both all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Furthermore, we sought to evaluate the potential interaction between phenotypic age acceleration and dietary fiber intake in relation to mortality outcomes.

Methods

Participants

The study included cancer survivors aged 40 and older based on 8 cycles of NHANES from 2003 to 2018. NHANES is a large, multi-stage complex sampling design study, assesses the health status of a representative population of U.S. residents. We included cancer survivors aged 40 and older, and after excluding samples with missing data for PhenoAge (n=554), dietary intake assessment (n=434), follow-up information (n=1), and covariates (n=56), 2958 participants were finally included (Fig. 1). All participants signed informed consent, and the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics approved the NHANES series of studies.

Flowchart of study population selection

Dietary fiber intake assessment

The intake of dietary fiber was assessed through two 24-hour dietary recall questionnaires in NHANES. The USDA's Food and Nutrient Database was utilized to compute the intake of dietary fiber in the diet reported in the questionnaire. If two dietary recalls were available, the mean of the two dietary fiber intakes was used; otherwise, a single intake was utilized. High dietary fiber intake was categorized as the top quartile of dietary fiber intake in this study, as previously mentioned [27].

PhenoAge acceleration assessment

Biological age was measured using the most effective PhenoAge algorithm, based on the BioAge R package. This calculation involved 8 biomarkers: white blood cell count, lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, albumin, creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, red cell distribution width, and glucose, consistent with prior research [28, 29]. PhenoAge acceleration was determined as biological age minus chronological age. PhenoAge acceleration >0 is defined as accelerated aging, and ≤0 is defined as non-accelerated aging [28].

Ascertainment of mortality and follow-up

The mortality status was determined by linking with the National Death Index (NDI). Follow-up time was computed from the MEC date to death or December 31, 2019. Three cause-specific mortality rates were assessed: cardiovascular disease (I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69), cancer (C00-C97), and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular disease mortality.

Covariates

All-cause and cause-specific mortality were adjusted for the same set of covariates: age, sex, ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American/Hispanic, and other), marital (married/with a partner, and widowed/divorced/separated/other), education (less than high school, and high school and above), smoking (never smoking, ever smoking, and now smoking), body mass index (BMI), physical activity, diagnosed hypertension, diagnosed diabetes, diagnosed cardiovascular disease, and total dietary energy intake. Following the guidelines of US PA, physical activity was defined as active if participants engaged in more than 150-minute moderate or 75-minute vigorous activity per week; otherwise, it was classified as inactive. Per WHO criteria, BMI <25 kg/m2 was categorized as underweight/normal, 25–30 kg/m2 as overweight, and ≥30 kg/m2 as obese. Hypertension was defined as self-reported hypertension, systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or/and diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or reported use of medication to lower blood pressure. Diabetes mellitus was defined as self-reported diabetes, hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, fasting plasma glucose level ≥126mg/dl, or the use of oral glucose-lowering medication or insulin. Participants were classified as having cardiovascular disease if diagnosed by a doctor or other health professional with conditions: congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, or stroke.

Statistical analysis

This study incorporated appropriate sample weights and conducted statistical analyses using the “survey” package. Descriptive data were presented as weighted means±standard error or numbers (weighted percentages). Restricted cubic spline models were applied to explore linear/non-linear relationships between PhenoAge acceleration, dietary fiber intake, and mortality risk, based on “rms” package. Weighted cox regression models were applied to investigate separate and combined associations of PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality. To assess combined association of PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality, we constructed four mutually exclusive groups: 1) Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber, 2) Non-accelerated aging & low dietary fiber, 3) Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber, 4) Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber, and participants with non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber served as the reference group in subsequent analyses. Weighted Kaplan-Meier curve was used to compare cumulative survival rates at four mutually exclusive groups, and Log-rank test was applied. In all cox regression analyses, we constructed an initial model adjusting for age and sex, as well as a full model adjusting for all covariates. To explore the multiplicative interaction association between PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake on mortality, we included a multiplicative interaction term in the cox regression model. Additionally, we evaluated the additive interaction between these two factors by assessing two indices: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion (AP). Additive interaction was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval for either RERI or AP did not include zero. Furthermore, to validate the robustness of our primary findings, we repeated the main analysis after excluding participants who died within the first two years of follow-up. Finally, we conducted exploratory subgroup analyses by major types of site-specific cancers, including prostate cancer, breast cancer, and colorectal cancer, to investigate potential variations in associations across different cancer types. Statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.0.3, considering P <0.05 as statistically significant.

Tables S3 and S4 presented the results of the sensitivity analysis excluding samples that died within two years. The separate and combined associations of PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality were generally consistent with the main analysis results. In the fully adjusted model, individuals exhibiting accelerated aging demonstrated a significantly higher risk of mortality from all causes (HR (95% CI): 1.93 (1.58, 2.36)), cardiovascular diseases (HR (95% CI): 1.61 (1.06, 2.43)), cancer (HR (95% CI): 2.46 (1.72, 3.52)), and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular conditions (HR (95% CI): 1.86 (1.39, 2.49)) compared to those without accelerated aging. Similarly, participants with lower dietary fiber intake were associated with a 29% increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR (95% CI): 1.29 (1.01, 1.66)), and a 80% increased risk of cancer mortality (HR (95% CI): 1.80 (1.16, 2.79)). As for combined association, the "Accelerated aging & high dietary fiber" group exhibited a higher all-cause mortality risk (HR (95% CI): 1.83 (1.17, 2.86)), as did the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group (HR (95% CI): 2.28 (1.62, 3.22)), relative to the "Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber" group. The cancer mortality risk was notably elevated in the "Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber" (HR (95% CI): 2.24 (1.09, 4.63)) and "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" (HR (95% CI): 3.84 (2.21, 6.66)) groups compared to the "Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber" group. Additionally, the "Accelerated aging & high dietary fiber" group, and the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group showed increased risks of non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality (HR (95% CI): 2.39 (1.30, 4.40), and 2.71 (1.55, 4.75), respectively) when compared to the "Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber" group. No statistically significant interaction was observed between PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake regarding mortality (P>0.05).

Among prostate cancer participants (n=520), the combined association of PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality was presented in Table S5. After full adjustment for covariates, the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group exhibited a significantly elevated risk of all-cause mortality (HR 2.17, 95% CI: 1.31–3.60) compared to the reference group ("Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber"). For cancer-specific mortality, a markedly higher risk was also observed in the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group (HR 3.48, 95% CI: 1.39–8.69). Importantly, a significant additive interaction between PhenoAge acceleration and low dietary fiber intake was detected for cancer mortality. The RERI was 2.42 (95% CI: 0.05, 4.78), indicating a synergistic effect between the two factors. The AP was 0.69 (95% CI: 0.27, 1.12), suggesting that 69% of the combined excess risk resulted from their interaction. Additionally, the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group was associated with a substantially increased risk of non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality (HR 3.85, 95% CI: 1.74–8.54).

Among breast cancer participants (n=477), the combined association of PhenoAge acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality was summarized in Table S6. After comprehensive covariate adjustment, individuals in the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group demonstrated a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 2.45, 95% CI: 1.08–5.52) relative to the reference group ("Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber"). Similarly, a elevated risk of non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality was observed in the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group (HR 3.81, 95% CI: 1.37–10.64). Among colorectal cancer participants (n=220), the association between PhenoAge acceleration combined with dietary fiber intake and mortality was detailed in Table S7. After full covariate adjustment, the "Accelerated aging & low dietary fiber" group showed a substantially elevated risk of cancer mortality (HR 8.81, 95% CI: 1.07–72.87) when compared to the reference group ("Non-accelerated aging & high dietary fiber").

Discussion

In this research, an examination was performed on a cohort of 2958 cancer survivors aged ≥ 40 over a median follow-up duration of 79 months using data from the NHANES database (2003-2018). PhenoAge acceleration was significantly associated with increased mortality from all-cause, cancer, cardiovascular, and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular causes. A similar trend was observed for low dietary fiber intake, except in the case of cardiovascular mortality. Individuals exhibiting both accelerated aging and low dietary fiber intake consistently showed the highest mortality risk across all endpoints when compared to those with non-accelerated aging and high fiber intake. A significant additive interaction between dietary fiber intake and PhenoAge acceleration was observed specifically for cancer mortality. Although no significant interactions were found for all-cause or other causes of mortality, lower dietary fiber intake was associated with elevated mortality risk across all strata of PhenoAge acceleration. This pattern was not observed for cardiovascular mortality. The study underscores the potential importance of managing accelerated aging and dietary fiber intake as part of long-term health strategies for cancer survivors. Notably, higher fiber intake may help mitigate the adverse association between accelerated aging and cancer mortality.

Recently, there has been a notable increase in the number of people who have survived cancer, owing to notable progressions in the field of cancer therapy. Cancer survivors exhibit accelerated aging in comparison to age-matched individuals who have not been diagnosed with cancer [30]. Both the cancer and its treatments contribute to the accelerated aging seen in cancer survivors [31, 32]. Cancer and its treatment may trigger key features associated with aging, including inflammation, cellular senescence, telomere shortening, epigenetic alterations, and mitochondrial dysfunction, collectively contributing to an accelerated aging process. The accumulation of stress caused by cancer and its therapy contribute to an accelerated aging process [33]. Our study findings indicate that 37.6% of cancer survivors demonstrate signs of accelerated aging, highlighting the prevalent challenge of accelerated aging in this population.

In a study conducted on cancer survivors, it was found that an increased risk of all-cause mortality over a period of 4 years was associated with PhenoAge acceleration in the Health and Retirement Study [34]. In a breast cancer survivors cohort study, the presence of accelerated aging, as indicated by low energy levels, depression, and poor self-rated health conditions, showed a significant correlation with elevated all-cause mortality or mortality/recurrence specific to breast cancer [35]. Indeed, the burden of cancer and aging concurrently confronts middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors in comparison to their younger counterparts. Allostatic load (AL) serves as a crucial indicator of biological accelerated aging [36]. A study conducted on cancer survivors aged 60 years or older from NHANES 1999 to 2010 revealed a notable rise in the adjusted hazard ratio for all-cause mortality (high vs. low AL: HR=1.52, 95%CI=1.17–1.98.17.98) and cancer-specific mortality (high vs. low AL: HR=1.80, 95% CI=1.12–2.90.12.90) with higher AL. However, no significant correlation was found between AL and cardiovascular mortality (high vs. low AL: adjusted HR=1.59, 95% CI=0.86–2.94.86.94) [37]. Our study examined the relationship of phenotypic accelerated aging on all-cause, cancer, cardiovascular, and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality risks among middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors, confirming the adverse association of accelerated aging on the mortality risk. This study reveals that the relationship between accelerated aging and mortality exhibits an L-shaped, nonlinear pattern with a threshold effect. A study utilizing a physiological age index (PAI) constructed from 36 clinical biomarkers demonstrates that 25 of these biomarkers have a significant nonlinear association with mortality, characterized by U-shaped or J-shaped curves [38]. Similarly, epigenetic age acceleration studies have identified a threshold effect, exemplified by the "acceleration-platform" feature observed between DNA methylation age acceleration (Δage) and all-cause mortality [39]. This threshold effect aligns with the clinical characteristics of individuals exhibiting "accelerated aging," such as elevated inflammatory markers and metabolic disorders, indicating that these pathways may cause irreversible damage to organ function once a critical threshold is reached [39, 40]. Our research underscores the importance of assessing phenotypic accelerated aging in middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors in clinical practice to identify high-risk individuals and develop and implement precise intervention strategies.

It has been demonstrated that both cancer and anti-cancer treatments can induce accelerated aging, yet there is currently no effective anti-aging therapy to address this issue. Our study confirmed the correlation between accelerated aging and a heightened mortality risk among individuals who have survived cancer. However, there is a dearth of research investigating strategies to mitigate the deleterious associations of accelerated aging on this population. Dietary fiber, recognized as an essential component of a healthy diet, has demonstrated a favorable influence on the prognosis of the elderly population in our previous studies [41–43]. Therefore, we hypothesized that dietary fiber and accelerated aging may exert a combined association on mortality risk among middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors, and further evaluated whether a potentially interaction exists between them in relation to mortality. Our findings revealed a significant interaction between dietary fiber intake and accelerated aging specifically for cancer mortality, though not for other mortality outcomes, suggesting that higher fiber intake may serve as a practical dietary strategy to mitigate the adverse effect of accelerated aging on cancer-related death. Our findings did not reveal significant interactive associations between dietary fiber intake and accelerated aging for mortality outcomes other than cancer. However, upon stratifying the population based on dietary fiber intake, we observed a graded increase in mortality risk in the association between accelerated aging and mortality risk across all models, ranging from the healthiest group (Non-accelerated aging + High dietary fiber) to the least healthy group (Accelerated aging + Low dietary fiber), with the exception of cardiovascular mortality. These results indicate that higher dietary fiber intake is beneficially associated with reduced mortality risk of all-cause mortality, cancer mortality, and non-cancer/non-cardiovascular mortality among middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors. Therefore, promoting adequate dietary fiber intake may still yield clinical benefits as part of a combined strategy to reduce mortality risk associated with accelerated aging in middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors.

A study examined the correlation between 38 dietary nutrients and PhenoAge acceleration, revealing a noteworthy negative association (β: −1.190, 95% CI: −1.708, −0.672, p = 0.001) [25]. Another study assessed the correlation between macronutrient and age acceleration, finding a negative association with dietary fiber for accelerated aging (the Klemera-Doubal method-Age acceleration: β – 0.53, 95% CI – 0.62, – 0.43, P< 0.05; PhenoAge acceleration: β – 0.30, 95% CI – 0.35, – 0.25, P< 0.05) [26]. A meta-analysis was explored the potential correlation between dietary fiber intake and the occurrence and mortality rates of various cancers. In the analysis of combined data, A significant reduction in the risk of cancer, by 22%, was found to be linked with a higher intake of dietary fiber (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.74–0.83.74.83, p < 0.001) and a significant decrease of 17% in mortality (RR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.78–0.90.78.90, p < 0.001) [44]. The processes of aging and cancer development share common mechanisms, and a mutually reinforcing relationship exists between the two phenomena [32]. The exact cause of the inverse correlation between dietary fiber and age acceleration and mortality in cancer survivors remains uncertain, although there exist several plausible hypotheses. The protective properties of dietary fiber are attributed to its significant involvement in anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms. Numerous studies established a link between increased consumption of dietary fiber and a decrease in markers associated with inflammation and oxidative stress [19, 45]. The benefits of dietary fiber could be attributed to the modification in the composition and functionality of gut microbiota, thereby influencing the synthesis of microbial metabolites that play a crucial position in regulating metabolic, immune, behavioral, and neurobiological outcomes [46, 47]. The consumption of dietary fiber enhances metabolic health by eliciting the secretion of diverse gut hormones, adipokines, bile acids, and amino acid metabolic profiles, thereby improving glucose and lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, body weight regulation, and abdominal adiposity [48, 49]. Additionally, the consumption of plant-based foods, which are rich in dietary fiber, is frequently associated with an increased intake of other essential micronutrients [20]. Based on the guidelines, it is suggested that females who are 50 years old or older in the United States should incorporate 21 grams of dietary fiber into their daily diet, while males are advised to aim for an intake of 30 grams [50]. However, the actual consumption of dietary fiber among the elderly population falls significantly below the recommended levels [51]. Our study also revealed that the median daily consumption of dietary fiber was 15.10 g/day, with the high dietary fiber intake group only reaching 20.35 g/day, significantly below the recommended guideline standard. Our study did not find a significant association between dietary fiber intake and accelerated aging related to increased cardiovascular mortality risk among middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors. This result could be explained by the limited sample size, comparatively low occurrence of cardiovascular mortality, and the observation that even in the high dietary fiber group, the intake of dietary fiber remained comparatively modest. Certainly, further investigation is imperative to validate our findings.

The interpretation of the outcomes obtained in our study should consider several limitations. Firstly, the generalizability of our research findings may be limited by factors such as geographic and ethnic diversity, age-related differences, and disease-specific characteristics. Given that our results have not yet been validated in other independent populations, it is prudent to exercise caution when extrapolating these findings to different groups, taking into account the distinct attributes of each population. Secondly, the utilization of self-reported data for evaluating cancer history and dietary fiber intake might introduce a potential bias. Thirdly, the study did not provide further insights into the specific characteristics of cancer, including its stage, and treatment, which may introduce confounding variables that could potentially impact the results. Additionally, residual confounding factors should also be considered as limitations. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, taking into account these potential biases. We fully acknowledge that the sample sizes within the specific cancer subgroups remain limited, which affects the statistical power for detecting associations. Therefore, these subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory in nature. We recommend that future studies with larger, site-specific cancer populations further explore the potential variations in the associations between PhenoAge acceleration, dietary fiber intake, and mortality. Fourthly, distinguishing between soluble and insoluble fiber or identifying specific fiber sources could provide deeper insights. However, the NHANES dietary database does not systematically categorize fiber types, limiting our ability to conduct such detailed analyses. Finally, due to the observational nature of this study, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Furthermore, the NHANES database does not include continuous recording of dietary fiber intake and PhenoAge related indicators, thus the study lacks sufficient evidence to confirm a conclusive causal relationship between dynamic changes and the outcome. Despite the limitations inherent in our study, it still possesses significant merits. To our understanding, our study is the first examination to evaluate any potential link between PhenoAge acceleration and mortality risk in a large national sample of middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors, thereby offering novel insights into the relationship between accelerated aging in this population and their risk of mortality. Furthermore, we incorporated dietary fiber as a key variable to examine both its combined association with accelerated aging on mortality and their potential interaction, thereby extending the current understanding of nutritional factors in aging-related health outcomes.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates a combined association of phenotypic age acceleration and dietary fiber intake with mortality risk among middle-aged and elderly cancer survivors. Notably, a significant interaction between these two factors was observed specifically for cancer mortality, indicating that dietary fiber intake may modify the association of accelerated aging on this cause of death. Although no significant interactions were found for other mortality outcomes, the consistent beneficial association of higher fiber intake across mortality outcomes supports its protective role. These findings highlight the clinical importance of integrating both phenotypic age assessment and dietary evaluation in the management of cancer survivors. Specifically, increasing dietary fiber intake may represent a practical nutritional intervention for those exhibiting accelerated aging, with particular potential to reduce cancer-related mortality. Further research is warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms and to validate these findings in prospective interventions.
