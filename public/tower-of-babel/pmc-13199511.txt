A multi-context fusion-aware graph modelling for group activity recognition using pose-conditioned spatial encoding and actor relations
Authors: M R Tejonidhi, K R Raghunandan, B Uma, C K Madhu, A M Vinod
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroup activity recognition requires a holistic understanding of individual actions, their spatial relationships, and the surrounding environment. Traditional methods that focus solely on isolated movements often fail to capture the complex inter-player and scene-level dependencies inherent in sports and crowd scenarios. In this research work, a model for group activity recognition is developed. The proposed model combines various contextual features through the integration of poses of individual actors in the scene with the pose-aligned spatial scene context for relational reasoning. Pose features of individual actors are extracted using mmPose, while the scene-level context is encoded through pose-conditioned spatial feature aggregation rather than explicit semantic segmentation. These pose and scene context features extracted are combined and used to construct Actor Relation Graphs (ARGs) using Zero Normalized Cross Correlation (ZNCC) which improves robustness to appearance and variations in illumination. Further, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) are modelled using relationships between individual actors in a scene and their group activities. The proposed framework explicitly combines pose-level and scene-level contextual features into a single relational graph, in contrast to previous ARG-GCN approaches that mainly rely on appearance features. The model is evaluated on two benchmark datasets: the Collective Activity dataset (CAD) and the Volleyball dataset (VD). The model exhibits classification accuracies of 95.02% and 94.81% on CAD and VD, respectively. On a TITAN-XP GPU, the average time per video clip with 41 frames is approximately 0.2 s. The results show that the combination of pose and scene contexts features enhances graph-based relational learning and improves recognition accuracy.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The rapid growth of technology and its applications in various fields have led to the development of advanced computing environments for real time decision making. Video processing has attracted substantial global interest owing to its ability to analyse real-time videos, resulting in efficient monitoring and decision-making systems. To achieve these goals in efficient monitoring and decision-making systems, an increasing number of surveillance cameras are being deployed in various places like hospitals, public events, sporting venues, and strategic infrastructure1.

Context serves as unseen yet an important feature that unites individual actions into a single entity in group activity recognition (GAR). When individuals are considered independently, broader group information might be overlooked. For instance, several people in a scene may appear unrelated if they are in different poses, such as one actor standing, another actor raising a hand, and a third one turning away, even though they are participating in same activity. This emphasizes the importance of modelling human interactions with scene context to achieve accurate group activity recognition1. Conventional approaches that focus only on individual actions frequently fail in group environment, where interactions between human-human and human-object define the group activity2. As a result, GAR is still a challenging task because identifying group activity requires analysing intricate interactions between several individual actors in a scene.

Researchers emphasize that understanding group activities depends on relations between individuals and their spatial positions in the scene. For example, Ibrahim et al.3 demonstrated that using a hierarchical deep learning model to model individual actions and their relative arrangements enhances the recognition of group sports activities like “attacking” or “defending.” In the same way, Bagautdinov et al.4 highlighted the importance of temporal context by capturing coordinated action dynamics over time, enabling discrimination between activities that are similar visually. Environmental cues like a basketball court, classroom, or protest setting further limit the range of conceivable group activities, which leads to more accurate interpretations. For this reason, scene-level context is equally important5.

Context information provides the relational and environmental framework that gives meaning to individual actions. Without context, recognition systems struggle to produce fragmented or ambiguous interpretations; with context, they can infer collective behaviour efficiently.

By providing complete information about actors’ positions, their behaviour in a scene, spatial and temporal dynamics and background environment, further improve recognition performance. Li et al.6, proposed a GAR framework called *GroupFormer*, which used a clustered spatial-temporal transformer to enhance individual and group representations. The model’s accuracy is increased, and complex intra-group interactions can be captured. Du and Wang7 incorporated global contextual information and position-aware actor representations through a Contextual Transformer Network (CTN) and Clustered Position Embedding (CPE), enhancing the spatial structure encoding of actors. Yuan and Ni8 introduced a Spatial-Temporal Bilinear Pooling (STBiP) module combined with Transformer-based Context Encoding (TCE), where TCE encodes global context into individual features and STBiP models inter-feature relationships. Their two-branch design demonstrated the value of fusing complementary contextual information for GAR.

The pose-conditioned scene context mechanism is designed to complement actor pose features by encoding spatial information about the environment in which interactions occur. While pose features describe the motion dynamics and skeletal configuration of individual actors, they do not explicitly capture how actors are spatially arranged within the scene. In many group activity scenarios, the spatial configuration of actors and their interaction regions provide critical cues for interpreting collective behaviour.

To address this, the proposed framework constructs scene context representations that are conditioned on the spatial distribution of actor keypoints. By projecting pose keypoints into the spatial domain and generating pose-aligned activation maps, the model captures the layout of actors within the environment rather than relying solely on generic global scene descriptors. This conditioning strategy ensures that contextual information reflects actor positions and interaction regions that are relevant to the activity being performed.

Consequently, pose features and pose-conditioned scene context provide complementary information. Pose features encode individual motion patterns, while scene context encodes environmental structure and actor spatial relationships. Their combination enables the relational graph to reason jointly about actor behaviour and scene layout, thereby improving the modelling of collective activities.

Other approaches have explored handcrafted or hybrid feature representations. Smita et al.1 introduced a new descriptor for GAR, combining the main actor’s action descriptor with the joint context descriptors of nearby individuals, and classified them using Support Vector Machines (SVM). Dasgupta et al.9 proposed a two-stream architecture that integrates person-level information with contextual cues. Their framework uses a CNN-based appearance network with bounding boxes to capture visual features, and an HR-Net–based context network to extract pose and scene context. Li et al.10 further advanced GAR with a Graph Diffusion Convolutional Network (GFCN), dynamically constructing adjacency matrices to better model actor relationships. Similarly, Vahora and Chauhan11 designed a CNN–RNN hybrid with probabilistic inference: CNNs learn individual action poses while RNNs capture contextual dynamics, followed by a SoftMax classifier for final group activity classification. The proposed research introduces a unified multi-context relational framework wherein pose dynamics, pose-aligned spatial scene context, and inter-actor relations are jointly encoded prior to graph reasoning. Despite extensive research on components like pose estimation and graph convolutional networks, this work analyses the impact of structured contextual representation on relational inference in group activity recognition. The proposed method incorporates pose-based scene context and ZNCC-based relational similarity, enhancing robustness to actor appearances in heterogeneous environments and variations in illumination, in contrast to ARG–GCN methods that predominantly depend on appearance features.

It is important to note that the novelty of the research does not lie in introduce a new backbone architecture, instead, it involves structuring contextual information and examining whether it can explicitly improve relational reasoning without increasing the complexity of the model. The proposed model aims to improve consistency between pose, spatial relationships, and scene context in group activity recognition by integrating pose- and scene-level features into a combined relational graph.

Despite significant progress in group activity recognition, many existing approaches primarily rely on appearance-based features extracted from actor bounding boxes or global scene representations. While these features provide useful visual cues, they often fail to explicitly capture structured contextual relationships between actor pose dynamics and the surrounding environment. Consequently, relational reasoning models may struggle when actor appearances vary or when contextual cues such as spatial arrangement and scene layout play an important role in defining collective behaviour.

The proposed work addresses this limitation by explicitly structuring contextual information prior to relational graph modelling. Instead of relying solely on appearance similarity, the framework integrates pose-based actor representations with pose-conditioned spatial scene context to construct a unified relational embedding. This design enables the Actor Relation Graph to encode interactions among actors while simultaneously incorporating environmental context. As a result, relational reasoning is guided not only by actor appearance similarity but also by spatial context and pose dynamics, providing a more structured representation for group activity recognition.

The following sections provide a detailed review of state-of-the-art methods for context-aware activity recognition and identifies the research gap in the current approaches.

Literature survey

The primary components of early methods of GAR are the handcrafted features and probabilistic graphical models. For example, GAR methods employed hierarchical structures such as probabilistic grammars or AND-OR graphs for modelling relations between actors12–15. Even though these methods are effective in small-scale scenarios, they often lack the robustness in real-world environments that are dynamic.

With the development of deep learning, significant progress has been achieved in this domain4,16–22. Ibrahim et al.3 proposed a two-stage temporal model to capture fine-grained dynamics, meanwhile Shu et al.21 extended temporal reasoning with an energy-based framework to improve prediction accuracy. Hierarchical relation networks18 further enhanced relational representations among actors, and Bagautdinov et al.4 introduced a unified framework for joint actor detection and GAR. Li et al.19 explored semantic approaches that first generated video captions, which were subsequently used to infer group activity. Wu et al.22 introduced Actor Relation Graphs (ARGs) with Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), effectively capturing relational dependencies among individuals.

Beyond actor-centric modelling, contextual cues are employed in images and videos to improve recognition23–28. Contextual information enhances both local and global recognition. For instance, object detection benefits from penalizing predictions inconsistent with the surrounding scene29, while segmentation-based methods use contextual feedback to guide region proposals30. Scene context has also been applied in trajectory prediction25,26, where modules encode the nearby environment to anticipate human movement. Similarly, pose information has been used to encode behavioural cues25,31 or as auxiliary context for daily activity recognition31. Ulutan et al.32 demonstrated improvements in human–object interaction detection by incorporating scene-level context. More broadly, contextual modelling remains central to semantic segmentation33,34.

Despite these advances, most existing GAR methods emphasize relational modelling among people, with relatively limited attention to fine-grained contextual cues. Deng et al.35 employed CNN representations of the entire scene, while Wang et al.36 modelled higher-order interaction context across individuals. Unlike these methods, the proposed research explicitly exploits multi-level pose and scene contextual information that are inherently present in videos. By combining these with appearance features10, an enriched Actor Relation Graphs are constructed, and Graph Convolutional Networks is trained for context-aware GAR.

Several recent methods have further advanced group activity recognition through hybrid and transformer-based architectures. Tejonidhi et al.37 proposed an optimized GAR pipelines by integrating Actor Relation Graphs with GCN-LSTM networks, demonstrating improved temporal reasoning. Similarly, Qi et al.21 introduced *StagNet*, an attentive semantic RNN that effectively captures long-range temporal dependencies, while Chappa et al.38 proposed *SPARTAN*, self-supervised spatiotemporal transformer designed for robust representation learning in GAR tasks.

Other major contributions for group activity recognition task include hierarchical and relational models. Ibrahim et al.3 presented an hierarchical deep temporal model, while Ibrahim and Mori19 developed hierarchical relational networks to explicitly capture multi-level actor dependencies. Shu et al.39 proposed the confidence-energy recurrent network (*CERN*), introducing energy-based reasoning to reduce uncertainty in temporal predictions.

Different methods have explored advanced and effective prediction techniques. Wu et al.23 expanded ARG-based GAR with enhanced graph learning techniques, while Yuan et al.40 proposed a spatio-temporal dynamic inference network (LiteDIN) to adaptively refine group representations. Hajimirsadeghi et al.41 earlier applied a multi-instance cardinality kernel to encode activity patterns from multiple actors.

More recently, Kim et al.42 proposed detector-free weakly supervised GAR framework, removing reliance on bounding-box annotations, and Deng et al.36 designed structure inference machines using recurrent neural networks to analyze higher-order relationships.

Finally, foundational contributions such as Choi et al.43 provided early collective activity recognition benchmarks using spatio-temporal relations, which continue to serve as a baseline for evaluating modern GAR models. Proposed methodology

In addition to the above methods, several efforts have been made to advance group activity recognition through hybrid deep learning architectures and transformer-based models. For instance, hybrid models have integrated Actor Relation Graphs (ARGs) with recurrent reasoning modules such as GCN–LSTM networks, enabling improved temporal representation of collective behaviours.

Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture of the proposed GAR model. Video frames are extracted and provided as input to the system, which jointly predicts both group activities and the corresponding individual actions of participants. The proposed model consists of a dual-branch structure which comprises a Pose Context Branch and a Scene Context Branch, each representing complementary aspects of contextual information followed by ARG construction and GCN based relational reasoning. The proposed method encodes scene context via pose-conditioned spatial feature aggregation, maintaining consistency between pose and environmental cues without increasing architectural complexity, unlike solutions that depend on explicit semantic segmentation.

Block diagram of the proposed method.

In Pose Context Branch, pose features of individual actors in a scene are extracted from the fully connected (FC) layer of mmPoseNet. Subsequently, spatial features are derived from the FC layer by applying Region of Interest (ROI) align method to the bounding boxes of individual actors. In parallel, Scene-level context was modelled by constructing pose-conditioned scene context map derived from keypoint distributions that encodes global environmental information. Scene context features are then extracted from the FC layer that is followed by ROI Align on the segmented regions.

The pose and scene context features are combined to form a feature vector, which encapsulates both local actor-level features and global scene semantics. These features are fused using feature concatenation. This fusion aligns heterogeneous contextual representations into a common embedding space and enables joint reasoning over actor-level dynamics and environmental semantics prior to Actor Relation Graph construction. Using this feature vector, an Actor Relation Graph (ARG) is constructed via a ZNCC operation to compute appearance relation values among actors37. The constructed ARG is subsequently used to train a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) for joint recognition of context-based individual actions and group-level activities.

Each component of the proposed model contributes to context-aware understanding: the pose context encodes fine-grained skeletal information, the scene context provides environmental cues, and the ARG–GCN module performs relational reasoning to infer collective behaviour from individual interactions.

Graph-based reasoning continues to remain a dominant paradigm, as extensions of Actor Relation Graphs (ARGs) combined with GCNs have refined relational learning between individuals, while spatio-temporal dynamic inference networks adaptively update node representations to capture evolving group structures. Foundational contributions that modelled spatio-temporal relationships among actors continue to serve as baselines for evaluating contemporary GAR models.

Collectively, these advanced methods, outline the evolution of group activity recognition from handcrafted probabilistic models to CNN-driven frameworks, graph-based relational learning, and most recently transformer and weakly supervised models. Thus, the proposed method explicitly fuses fine-grained pose context and scene context features with relational graph modelling, thereby enhancing the context-aware GAR.

For example, if the pose of an actor is “raised arm”, it often means a spike, and “crouched legs” of an actor may mean a defensive block in volleyball.

To ensure that the ZNCC value falls between − 1 and 1, the resultant value is then normalized by the denominator. ZNCC with value − 1 denotes a total negative correlation, and 1 denotes perfect positive correlation.

ZNCC is effective for recognizing group activities because it eliminates mean bias, which means that relational similarity doesn’t change with change in illumination or feature scale shifts. This property is very important for coordinated group activities, where consistency in relative motion and interaction is more useful than similarity in absolute appearance. Compared to dot-product similarity, ZNCC is more effective at modelling relationships between heterogenous actor appearances.

The choice of Zero-Normalized Cross-Correlation (ZNCC) is motivated by its statistical normalization properties, which provide stable similarity estimation between feature embeddings. In contrast to conventional similarity functions such as dot-product attention, ZNCC removes the mean intensity component of feature vectors before computing correlation. This normalization reduces the influence of illumination variations and appearance inconsistencies between actors that frequently occur in real-world scenes.

From a relational modelling perspective, ZNCC provides a deterministic similarity formulation that does not introduce additional learnable parameters into the Actor Relation Graph. This design aligns with the objective of the proposed framework, which emphasizes structured contextual representation rather than increasing architectural complexity through additional attention modules. While transformer-based relational models typically rely on learnable attention weights, they also introduce a significant number of parameters and require large-scale datasets for stable optimization.

To validate this design choice, multiple similarity measures including dot-product similarity, NCC, SAD, and ZNCC were experimentally evaluated during ARG construction. The comparative results reported in the ablation study demonstrate that ZNCC consistently yields the best recognition performance across both CAD and Volleyball datasets. These findings indicate that mean-normalized relational similarity improves robustness to appearance variations while preserving computational efficiency.

The similarity values computed using ZNCC are used to construct the adjacency matrix of the Actor Relation Graph. For each pair of actors, the ZNCC score determines the strength of the edge connecting the corresponding nodes in the graph. Higher similarity values produce stronger graph connections, allowing contextual information to propagate more effectively between actors that exhibit related motion patterns or spatial interactions. During Graph Convolutional Network message passing, these weighted connections control how relational information flows between actors, enabling the model to emphasize coordinated behaviours while reducing the influence of unrelated individuals.

The improvement observed when combining pose and scene context features can be explained by the complementary nature of these representations. Pose features capture fine-grained skeletal motion patterns of individual actors, enabling the model to recognize action dynamics such as jumping, blocking, or moving. However, pose information alone does not explicitly encode how actors are positioned relative to each other or within the surrounding environment.

Scene context features provide this missing spatial information by representing the global arrangement of actors and interaction regions within the scene. These features encode environmental structure and spatial layout cues that are often critical for interpreting collective activities.

When pose and scene context features are fused, the resulting node representations used in the Actor Relation Graph contain both motion dynamics and spatial context. This enriched representation enables the Graph Convolutional Network to model interactions among actors more effectively, leading to improved relational reasoning and higher accuracy in group activity recognition.

Conclusion

This work proposes a new context-aware GAR model that uses both pose and scene-level context features. Using mmPose with Inception-V3 as the backbone network, pose features for each actor in a scene are extracted. Scene context feature is extracted through pose-conditioned scene context map rather than explicit semantic segmentation. These structured pose and scene context features are combined into a single feature vector, which is then used to construct an Actor Relation Graph (ARG) using ZNCC as similarity function. Then, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) are modelled to perform relational reasoning, which trains the model to learn complex spatial and temporal relationships between actors.

Thorough experiments demonstrated that the proposed method exhibited competitive performance with recognition accuracies of 95.02% and 94.81% on two widely used benchmarks, the Collective Activity Dataset (CAD) and the Volleyball Dataset (VD), respectively. The results show that explicitly modelling both individual-level pose and scene-level contextual features enhances recognition accuracy. The proposed model extends beyond academic benchmarks, identifying practical applications in areas such as sports analytics, intelligent video surveillance, and the comprehension of crowd behaviour. Subsequent research will concentrate on enhancing this study via transformer-based graph reasoning, self-supervised pretraining on large-scale video datasets, and the incorporation of multimodal signals such as audio and text features for better activity interpretation.

Due to computational and annotation constraints, experiments on AVA-Kinetics/NBA could not be added.
