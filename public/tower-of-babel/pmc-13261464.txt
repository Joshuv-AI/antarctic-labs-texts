Experimental Evaluation of Reducing Water Cut and Increasing Oil Recovery Using Multiphase Mixed Fluid
Authors: Shu Tang, Rui Shen, Wei Xiong, Shengchun Xiong, Jiayi Ma, Shuang Wang, Yanyan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemical profile control has long been an effective method for reducing water cut and increasing oil production in heterogeneous reservoirs. However, its application has been limited due to high costs, economic constraints, and difficulties in adapting to complex reservoir environments characterized by high temperatures and high salinity. To address this, this paper proposes a multiphase mixed fluid system in which the aqueous phase serves as the continuous phase and high-viscosity oil droplets as the dispersed phase. Through parallel sand pack and transparent visualization plate displacement experiments, the mechanisms by which this multiphase mixed fluid system enhances oil recovery in heterogeneous reservoirs were systematically investigated. Experimental results indicate that the multiphase mixed fluid system forms dynamic blockages in high-permeability channels via the Jamin effect. In the plate experiments, the permeability of the high-permeability layer decreased from 2227 to 178 mD, effectively achieving fluid redirection and increasing the recovery factor in the low-permeability zone by 8.5%; the system continues to function during the subsequent accelerated displacement phase; visualization experiments intuitively revealed the dynamic process of “water channeling-blockage-redirection,” while sand pack experiments confirmed the significant increase in recovery factor through precise measurement. The study confirms that multiphase mixed fluid technology, through intelligent blockage and energy redistribution, can effectively address the challenge of low sweep efficiency in heterogeneous reservoirs, providing a new technical direction for the development of high-water-cut oilfields.

Introduction

As oilfield development progresses into the mid- to late stages, the heterogeneity of underground reservoirs increases, and the problem of water channeling becomes increasingly severe. When large volumes of injection water or formation water flow along high-permeability channels, bypassing the oil-bearing zones, it prevents the oil and gas from being fully extracted, resulting in a significant reduction in recovery factors. , At the same time, to address low-efficiency production, oilfields need to increase water injection volumes and adjust equipment parameters, which not only consumes additional energy but also drives up production costs in areas such as drilling, operations, and water treatment. Therefore, the development and application of efficient profile-adjustment and water-shutoff technologies have become key to ensuring sustained and stable oilfield production and optimizing development benefits. ,

Currently used profile control and water-blocking technologies each have their limitations. Among them, polymer gel-based methods rely on polymer cross-linking to form a sealing structure, but they suffer from poor heat and salt resistance and are prone to aging over time; − surfactants are easily adsorbed by rocks or undergo chromatographic separation, resulting in insufficient concentrations during deep profile control; , microbial-based technologies rely on microbial metabolites for plugging, but the microorganisms have strict requirements for the formation environment, and the effectiveness of these methods is not consistently reliable. , To overcome the limitations of traditional technologies, multiphase mixed fluid technology offers a new direction in profile control and water blocking.

Multiphase mixed fluid refers to a system in which two or more phases of mattersuch as liquids, gases, and solid particlescoexist and flow together. In profile adjustment and water blocking applications, the synergistic interaction of these different phases can be leveraged to precisely alter the direction of fluid flow within the formation and seal high-permeability channels. Among these, a water–gas dispersion system consists of water as the continuous phase, with the gaseous medium stably dispersed in the water in the form of bubbles. By increasing flow resistance through clusters of microbubbles, this system can temporarily block pore throats, redirecting the displacement fluid from the main water-flooded flow path into untreated zones and thereby expanding the flooded volume. − Wu et al. investigated the mechanism by which the synergistic interaction between the anionic surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and hydrophilic SiO2 nanoparticles enhances foam performance, focusing on the enhancement effects in terms of foam stability and oil resistance, and revealed the microscopic mechanism of synergistic foam stabilization by nanoparticles and surfactants. Zhang et al. utilized nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology to conduct visualization experiments on nanoparticle-stabilized foam flooding under core conditions with varying permeability. They systematically investigated the oil displacement characteristics of the foam in porous media, changes in pore structure, and their impact on recovery factors, providing intuitive evidence for understanding the migration behavior of foam in heterogeneous reservoirs.

Particulate materials in solid–liquid dispersion systems offer highly tunable properties in terms of their size, morphology, and surface characteristics. They can be endowed with smart properties such as temperature, pH, or magnetic responsiveness and can integrate multiple functions including viscosity reduction, enhanced permeation, and tracer applications. , For example, temperature-responsive particles are typically constructed from poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) and its copolymers. When the polymer chains reach the lower critical solution temperature (LCST), they undergo a hydrophilic–hydrophobic phase transition, shifting from a hydrophilic to a hydrophobic state. This causes the particle network to dehydrate and contract, thereby altering its retention behavior within the pores.

In this study, a multiphase mixed fluid system is proposed, in which the aqueous phase serves as the continuous phase and high-viscosity oil dropletswhose throat dimensions match those of the dominant flow channels in the target reservoirserve as the dispersed phase. Compared to conventional technologies, the multiphase mixed-fluid technology offers significant advantages: first, it is more adaptable, capable of accommodating complex and variable formation temperature, pressure, and pore structure conditions; second, it achieves higher plugging and oil recovery efficiency, enabling effective plugging while expanding the waterflooding reach; and third, it is more environmentally friendly, as most of its components pose a lower risk of contamination to the formation and the environment.

To systematically validate the macroscopic application value and microscopic mechanisms of multiphase mixed fluid, this study employs a combined experimental approach using sand packs and transparent visualization plates: The sand pack experiments simulate the actual three-dimensional pore environment of a reservoir to quantify key engineering indicatorssuch as the plugging efficiency of profile-shaping agents and the increase in recovery factorthereby verifying their practical application potential; the transparent visualization plate experiments allow for direct observation of the migration, blocking, sweep patterns, and profile improvement processes of multiphase mixed fluid within the experimental setup. This provides direct visual evidence for formulation optimization, injection parameter design, and verification of blocking effectiveness, thereby offering scientific support for the performance validation and field application of multiphase mixed fluids.

Experimental Methods

Materials and Preparation of Multiphase Mixed Fluid Systems

The multiphase mixed fluid system used in the experiment consists of the following components: an aqueous phase, accounting for 85–95% of the total system mass, prepared using water with a salinity similar to that of formation water; an oil phase, accounting for 5–15% of the total system mass, consisting of high-viscosity oil that has undergone impurity removal and dehydration, with oil droplet sizes controlled within the range of 0.5–50 μm via microfluidic emulsification technology; and a stabilizer consisting of 0.1–0.5% SDS to ensure the stability of the oil droplets in high-pressure formation environments. The oil phase is a biodegradable vegetable oil, the stabilizer SDS is a well-known biodegradable surfactant, and the system contains no organic solvents or heavy metals, posing a very low risk of contamination to the formation and the environment. The high-viscosity oil droplets and stabilizer were injected simultaneously into the aqueous phase, with the shear rate controlled at 2000–5000 r/min to form a uniformly mixed system. Parallel Sand Pack Experiment

Experimental Materials

The simulation fluid used in the experiment was a mixture of white oil and kerosene in equal proportions, while the displacement fluid was a simulated formation water prepared from deionized water and NaCl. The multiphase mixed fluid was prepared in the laboratory. For the experiment, 80–160 mesh quartz sand was selected to construct two sand pack models with different permeability values in tubes measuring 80 cm in length and 2 cm in diameter. Parallel sand packs can more realistically simulate the direct fluid exchange between high-permeability channels and low-permeability zones in heterogeneous reservoirs. Moreover, the permeability of sand packs can be flexibly adjusted; they offer good repeatability and low cost and are particularly suitable for comparative studies of multiple scenarios. The specific procedure is as follows:

First, each sand pack was weighed to determine its dry mass. Residual air in the pores was then removed via a vacuum treatment, and the tubes were fully saturated with water. After saturation, the wet masses were measured. Finally, the pore volume (PV) for each sample was calculated by using the density of saturated water and the difference between the dry and wet masses of the sand pack. Subsequently, water permeability tests were conducted on both sand packs to determine their permeabilities. The experimental data for the sand pack are listed in Table .

Experimental setup of parallel sand packs.

First, the sand pack was saturated with crude oil, and then a parallel water flooding experiment was conducted under a pressure of 0.0051 MPa and a flow rate of 0.15 mL/min, while simultaneously recording data such as the injected PV value, water cut in the high- and low-permeability reservoirs, and total water cut. Additionally, we monitored fluid production characteristics at the outlet of both samples, including inlet pressure, time intervals, total fluid production, oil production, and water production, and concurrently recorded the time, pressure, and oil production data during the displacement process. Once the water cut at the outlet of one of the samples reached 98% or higher, the displacement pressure was incrementally increased to 0.07 and 0.1 MPa to observe whether additional oil production occurred while continuing to record relevant experimental data. Once the recovery factor stabilizes, a 0.5 PV multiphase mixed fluid is injected into the highly permeable reservoir, and the influence of this mixture on the recovery factor is recorded; once the recovery factor stabilizes again, the water flooding rate is increased to 0.3 mL/min, and the time, pressure, and oil production are continuously monitored during the displacement process. The experiment ends when the water cut at the outlet of three consecutive tubes reaches 96% or higher, and all of the experimental data are summarized and statistically analyzed.

Results and Discussion

Performance Evaluation of Multiphase Mixed Fluid in Parallel Sand Packs

To investigate the enhanced oil recovery (EOR) effects of multiphase mixed fluids in heterogeneous reservoirs, parallel sand pack experiments were conducted. First, a conventional water drive was performed as an initial development stage of the experiment. Conventional pressure water injection was used to establish a basic displacement system, achieving preliminary displacement of the original residual oil in the reservoir and initiating the production capacity. The experimental results are listed in Table . As the PV value increased, the liquid and oil production rates of the high-permeability sand pack were significantly higher than those of the low-permeability sand pack, and its water cut curve rose rapidly. The total water cut increased rapidly, with the high-permeability sand pack reaching a 100.00% water cut sooner. The rapid rise in the total water cut indicates that water flooding efficiency is declining. The cumulative recovery factor curve increased rapidly at the beginning but soon leveled off. Based on this, a pressurized water flooding operation was implemented to expand the water injection sweep volume by increasing the injection pressure, thereby overcoming displacement resistance in the low-permeability zones and further recovering crude oil that is difficult to mobilize under normal pressure. The experimental results for the pressurized water flooding phase are shown in Table . Experimental Results of Parallel Sand Packs during Pressurized Water Flooding Stage

Next, the multiphase mixed fluid waterflooding stage was initiated. By using the multiphase mixed fluid to block high-permeability flow pathways and adjust the water-absorption profile, the injected water was forced to flow toward low-permeability zones, thereby resolving issues of waterflooding, flow migration, and uneven flooding. Table shows changes in recovery factor and other parameters following the injection of the multiphase mixed fluid. After the injection system was activated, due to the additional capillary resistance caused by the Jamin effect, the inlet pressure rose sharply. Subsequently, the liquid production profile underwent a fundamental change: the proportion of liquid production and oil production from the low-permeability sand pack increased significantly, and the recovery factor curve exhibited a marked “step-like” increase, rising from 49.96 to 53.50% (an increase of 3.54 percentage points), while the recovery factor of the high-permeability sand pack remained unchanged. The overall recovery factor curve showed an upward trend. The total water cut of the entire system exhibited a temporary plateau followed by a gradual upward trend, decreasing from 92.0 to 84.1% (a decrease of 7.9 percentage points) and then rising to 97.7%. The significant decrease in water cut directly reflects the increase in oil production, indicating that the displacement fluid has effectively entered the low-permeability zone and expanded the sweep volume. Following injection into the multiphase mixed fluid system, a dynamic and effective blocking effect was formed within the high-permeability channels, thereby restructuring the macroscopic flow field of the parallel heterogeneous reservoir. This blocking effect significantly increased the flow resistance in the high-permeability zone, leading to a marked decrease in the permeability of the high-permeability channels. This forced a fundamental shift in the pressure gradient and flow path of the subsequent displacement fluid, thereby redistributing the injected energy to the previously unaffected low-permeability zones. This process not only substantially improved the macroscopic displacement efficiency but also effectively mobilized the residual oil trapped in the low-permeability zones due to insufficient initial driving pressure, ultimately achieving a significant increase in recovery factor.

Performance Evaluation of Multiphase Mixed Fluid in Transparent Visualization Plate Model

To visually observe the migration, blockage, and propagation patterns of multiphase mixed fluid, as well as the process of profile improvement within the experimental setup, transparent visualization flat-plate experiments were conducted. First, a conventional water drive was conducted. A baseline displacement channel was established in the transparent visualization flat-plate model using conventional water injection to observe the original oil–water migration patterns, laying the foundation for subsequent comparisons. Figure shows the displacement patterns in the transparent visualization flat-plate model for conventional water drives with different injection PV values. Experimental camera recordings clearly show that the injected water forms a distinct “dominant flow channel” within the high-permeability zone, rapidly traversing the entire plate. In contrast, the low-permeability zone largely retains the dark color of the crude oil, with the water front advancing slowly, forming a typical “unflooded zone.” This visually reveals the microscopic root cause of conventional water flooding’s inefficiency: seepage resistance in the high-permeability channel is far lower than in the low-permeability region. Fluids preferentially flow along the high-permeability path, resulting in residual oil in the low-permeability region not being effectively swept. This precisely corresponds to the macroscopic data from the sand pack experiments“low pressure, high water cut, and low recovery factor”and confirms the limitations of single-phase water flooding at the microscopic level.

Displacement images of the transparent visualization plate under conventional water flooding at different injected PVs.

Based on this, a pressurized water displacement test was conducted to increase the injection pressure and observe the expansion of the displacement front in real time using a transparent model, thereby evaluating the effectiveness of the increased pressure in improving the reach within the model. Figure shows the transparent visualization of displacement in a flat plate during different stages of the pressurized water displacement test. The visualization images reveal that while the leading edge of the water front in the low-permeability zone has advanced somewhat, it has failed to form an effective displacement pathway. At the same time, the reach within the high-permeability channel has significantly expanded, with the blue water zone becoming darker in color, indicating that the dominant flow pathway has been further strengthened. The fundamental reason for this phenomenon is that simply increasing system pressure can only generally enhance the reservoir’s driving capacity but cannot alter the inherent seepage patterns of fluids in heterogeneous pore-throat media. Since the flow resistance in high-permeability channels remains significantly lower than that in low-permeability zones, most of the newly added driving energy is preferentially dissipated along this path, resulting in the actual effective pressure gradient in the low-permeability zone remaining far below its activation threshold. Consequently, crude oil in low-permeability zones is difficult to effectively mobilize. In high-permeability channels, although increased pressure can drive isolated residual oil trapped in pore corners or narrow throats, resulting in limited enhancement of oil displacement and color deepening, its contribution to the overall system recovery factor remains relatively limited. This clearly demonstrates that, in the absence of flow control mechanisms, simply applying pressure cannot fundamentally block high-permeability flow paths; consequently, it is difficult to achieve an effective redirection of the driving fluid flow or a substantial improvement in sweep efficiency.

Displacement images of the transparent visualization plate model at different injection stages during pressurized water flooding.

Finally, a multiphase mixed fluid water drive was conducted by injecting the mixture into the model. Using a transparent plate, the process of high-permeability channel blockage and fluid flow redirection was visually observed. The primary objective was to evaluate the blocking and profile adjustment capabilities of the multiphase mixed fluid. Figure shows the visual displacement diagram on the transparent plate before and after the injection of the multiphase mixed fluid. Upon entering the high-permeability channel, the multiphase mixed fluid effectively blocked or occupied most of the flow path space due to the Jamin effect caused by oil droplets. At this point, we conducted water drive permeability tests on our high-permeability plate; the experimental data are shown in Table . The results indicate that the permeability of the high-permeability plate decreased from 2227 to 178 mD, preventing the subsequent displacement fluid from easily entering the high-permeability channels and instead “forcing” it into the low-permeability regions that had previously been minimally affected. This plugging effect is mainly attributed to the Jamin effect caused by oil droplets in the high-permeability pore throats, and it represents a dynamic physical plugging. During the subsequent accelerated water flooding stage, the system maintained its plugging effectiveness without significant permeability recovery, indicating good dynamic stability. However, under higher flow rates or pressure pulse conditions, some oil droplets may be displaced, leading to partial permeability recovery, which suggests that the plugging is reversible and self-adaptive.

Conclusions

This study employs a combined experimental method using sand pack and transparent visualization plates in parallel displacement tests. Through dual verification via “quantitative characterization and visual observation,” it systematically investigates the flow patterns in heterogeneous reservoirs and the mechanisms underlying enhanced oil recovery. The sand pack experiments accurately captured quantitative parameters such as the injection PV factor and oil production, while the visualization experiments intuitively captured the fluid flow redirection process. The data from both experiments corroborated each other, fully demonstrating the scientific validity and reliability of this combined experimental method in the study of displacement mechanisms in complex reservoirs.

The results of the sand pack experiments show that, in a reservoir model with a heterogeneity coefficient of 2, during the initial water flooding stage, the high-permeability channels reached 100.00% water saturation first, with a stable recovery factor of 56.82%, while the recovery factor in the low-permeability zones was significantly lower; after simple pressurization, cross-flow in the high-permeability channels was not fundamentally resolved, and permeability in the low-permeability channels increased only slightly; after injecting a multiphase mixed fluid system and increasing injection rate, the recovery factor in the low-permeability zones increased from 49.96 to 61.42%, and the overall recovery factor showed a significant upward trend, quantitatively confirming the improvement in development performance of this system for heterogeneous reservoirs.

Through real-time monitoring with dynamic imaging, the visualized bench test clearly captured the evolution of injection water, rapidly forming dominant flow channels in high-permeability zones within a heterogeneous reservoir. While simply increasing the displacement pressure could slightly advance the waterfront in low-permeability zones, it failed to overcome the flow dominance of the high-permeability channels, making it impossible to achieve an effective fluid flow redirection or a substantial improvement in recovery efficiency. Upon injection into a multiphase mixed fluid system, the high-permeability channels exhibited a dynamic plugging effect, with their permeability decreasing from 2227 to 178 mD. This resulted in an 8.5% increase in the recovery factor in the low-permeability zones, and the visualization plate clearly demonstrated the fluid redirection process.

The results of the transparent visualization plate experiments and the quantitative data from the sand pack experiments corroborate each other, clarifying the core mechanism of the multiphase mixed fluid system: “blocking high-permeability channelsforcing the displacement fluid to redirect to low-permeability zonessimultaneously improving sweep efficiency and oil displacement efficiency.” Meanwhile, this study did not include validation of adaptability under different temperature and salinity conditions. Future work will include multifactor adaptive experiments under varying temperatures, salinities, oil droplet sizes, and injection volumes, establish a matching relationship between oil droplet size and pore throat diameter, and conduct numerical simulation sensitivity analyses based on experimental data to provide theoretical guidance for field application. In addition, regarding the injection process, this study used continuous injection with a constant-flow pump, whereas field application would require consideration of equipment selection, injection pressure control, and real-time matching of oil droplet size with reservoir pore throats. With respect to economic feasibility, the raw material costs are relatively controllable, but large-scale application would still require economic evaluation based on oil prices. These issues will be further addressed in subsequent research to provide a more comprehensive theoretical basis for the large-scale implementation of this technology.
