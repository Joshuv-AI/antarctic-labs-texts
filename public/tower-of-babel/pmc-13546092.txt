Rural and Non-Rural Pediatric Health Care Providers’ Youth Suicide Prevention Beliefs, Attitudes, Training, and Practices
Authors: Brooke E. Streicher, John L. Cooley, Jenna G. Sims, Cole Marvin, Destiny Rojas, Sarah M. Wakefield, Sean M. Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:A substantial number of youth experience suicidal thoughts and behaviors, and those living in rural areas are at even greater risk. Pediatric health care providers tend to be the first point of contact for youth at risk for suicide. Thus, there is a need to understand rural and non-rural providers’ beliefs, training, and practices in youth suicide prevention.Objective:We examined providers’ beliefs, previous training, and current practices in youth suicide prevention, with attention to potential differences across providers working in rural and non-rural areas.Method:Participants were 171 pediatric health care providers in Texas, working in primary care or pediatric settings, who completed an online survey.Results:Overall, 70% of participants reported having never participated in suicide risk assessment/intervention training for youth patients, with no significant difference between rural and non-rural providers. Nearly all providers expressed an interest in additional training, with many reporting a willingness to engage in a virtual training. Rural and non-rural providers were not significantly different in their self-reported knowledge and skills related to suicide risk assessment/intervention. However, providers’ responses differed in confidence ratings; that is, non-rural providers’ ratings of their confidence in conducting youth suicide risk assessment/intervention were higher compared to rural providers.Conclusions:Our findings highlight a clear gap between pediatric health care providers’ previous training and current responsibilities in treating child and adolescent patients experiencing suicidal ideation and behavior. Implications and future directions are discussed.

Introduction

Children and adolescents are at elevated risk for suicidal thoughts and behaviors (Babeva et al., 2020), with suicide being the second leading cause of death among youth ages 10 to 14 and the third leading cause of death for adolescents and young adults ages 15 to 24 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021). In 2023, 20% of high school students in the U.S. reported “seriously” considering suicide within the past year (Verlenden et al., 2024), and suicide rates among youth have increased over recent decades (Curtin & Garnett, 2023). Of concern, adolescents who report suicidal ideation carry a greater lifetime risk for suicidal behavior (Fergusson et al., 2005; Kessler et al., 1999). Furthermore, youth living in rural areas are at an even greater risk for suicide (Margolis et al., 2018). Rural areas are generally characterized by low population density, dispersed residential development, and limited access to infrastructure and services, with residents living at greater distances from commercial, educational, and healthcare resources. Non-rural areas include urban communities, which are characterized by high population density and significant infrastructure and public services, as well as suburban communities that are located on the periphery of urban centers and have moderate population density. Previous research has shown that suicide rates are elevated among youth in rural compared to urban areas (Hirsch & Cukrowicz, 2014), and barriers to mental health services in rural areas include provider shortages, financial concerns, and stigma (Crumb et al., 2019; Guerrero et al., 2019; Schroeder et al., 2021). Over 100 million U.S. residents live in mental health professional shortage areas, which are geographic regions with insufficient numbers of providers relative to population needs (Kaiser Family Foundation, 2025).

Pediatric health care providers (PHCPs) tend to be the first point of contact for youth at risk for suicide (Ayer et al., 2022). A review of health system contacts across eight states found that up to 80% of youth visited a health care provider within the year before their death by suicide, and the majority did not have a recorded psychiatric diagnosis (Ahmedani et al., 2014). Primary care settings are a common entry point for mental health services due to their accessibility, a shortage of specialists, and patients’ reported comfort in discussing concerns with their primary care provider (Trude & Stoddard, 2003). These factors may be particularly relevant for youth and families living in rural communities.

Unfortunately, suicide risk assessment and management training is rare among PHCPs. Mental health training during residency was not previously required by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education for pediatric residents before July 2025, and continued education opportunities remain limited (Cross et al., 2022; Schoen et al., 2019; Wintersteen & Diamond, 2013). Moreover, many primary care practices lack comprehensive suicide prevention policies (Saini et al., 2014).

Current effective suicide risk assessment and management practices in primary care include educating practitioners, screening for suicide risk and mood disturbances, implementing safety planning, managing depression symptoms, and facilitating referrals to mental health services (Dueweke & Bridges, 2018; Horowitz et al., 2009). Despite the importance of these practices, PHCPs face challenges and systemic barriers that need to be addressed to enhance the effectiveness of suicide prevention efforts (Elzinga et al., 2020; Saini et al., 2010). Specifically, PHCPs’ self-perceived incompetence, time constraints, and competing professional demands can hinder their utilization of these practices (Davis et al., 2023; Elzinga et al., 2020). Difficulties coordinating care with schools and collaborating with other community providers have also been identified as barriers (Davis et al., 2023). Additionally, a lack of fidelity to evidence-based practices has been linked to insufficient training provided by organizations, decreased comfort in discussing suicide risk, and inadequate systems for referral and follow-up (Bostwick & Rackley, 2012).

The dearth of training in suicide assessment and intervention leaves PHCPs ill-equipped to provide effective help and resources to high-risk individuals. For instance, because of their lack of sufficient training, risk assessment practices may be heterogeneous between providers, potentially hindering effective management of suicidal patients (Saini et al., 2014). This trend highlights the need for improved integration of mental health services into primary care, enhanced training for PHCPs, and better collaboration between PHCPs and mental health specialists to ensure adequate patient care (Kates et al., 2019).

Previous research has documented limited suicide prevention training among primary care providers broadly (Schulz et al., 2024; Sudak et al., 2007); however, less is known about whether pediatric providers working in rural and non-rural settings differ in their training experiences, confidence, and implementation of evidence-based suicide prevention practices. Understanding these differences may help identify areas where additional training and systems-level support are most needed.

Current study

Pediatric health care settings represent essential avenues for youth suicide prevention, especially in rural areas where access to mental health services may be limited. The current study aimed to investigate PHCPs’ beliefs, previous training experiences, and current practices in youth suicide prevention, with attention to potential differences across PHCPs working in rural and non-rural areas in Texas. Specifically, this study sought to better understand providers’ experiences with suicide prevention training, their confidence and knowledge related to suicide risk assessment and intervention, and their use of evidence-based suicide prevention practices in pediatric health care settings. Findings from this work may help inform future training and support efforts for pediatric health care providers.

Method

Participants

Participants were 171 PHCPs in Texas who reported working in primary care or pediatrics. Participants were recruited through the Child Psychiatry Access Network (CPAN; 2019) provider registry. CPAN is an initiative funded by the Texas Child Mental Health Care Consortium (TCMHCC). The TCMHCC was created by the 86th Texas Legislature and funds multi-institutional research to improve mental health care for children and adolescents in Texas. Note that the content in this manuscript is solely the responsibility of the authors and does not necessarily represent the official views of the TCMHCC.

Individuals were not eligible if they were not listed in the CPAN registry. A total of 1,322 providers listed in the CPAN registry were contacted and invited to participate in the study. Two hundred e-mail addresses were invalid, resulting in 1,122 providers successfully contacted. Of those, 171 providers completed the survey and met eligibility criteria for inclusion in the current study (response rate = 15.2%). The study sample size was determined by the number of eligible providers who responded to the survey during the data collection period; no a priori sample size calculation was conducted.

Approximately 67% of the final sample of PHCPs (n = 114) worked in a rural area. Distance from rural counties to the nearest non-rural city ranged from 21 to 234 miles (M = 136.1, SD = 56.9), and PHCPs estimated that they treated patients within a radius of 10 to 450 miles (M = 104.2, SD = 74.0). The final sample of participants was predominantly women (73.1%), White (84.2%), and non-Hispanic/Latino (84.2%), and ranged in age from 26 to 78 years (M = 40.4; SD = 10.6). Further, the majority of PHCPs identified as either a Doctor of Medicine or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (59.6%) or a Nurse Practitioner (33.3%). Participants could select more than one professional specialty, degree, or service setting category. Socioeconomic status was not assessed in this sample. In the Online Supplementary Materials, see Table S1 for additional demographic information and Table S2 for details regarding PHCPs’ specialties, education, and settings.

Measures

Demographic and training history questionnaire

Demographic information, such as age, sex, years practicing, degree obtained, and professional specialty, was reported by providers. The principal investigators created questions regarding training history that asked participants about their experiences working with suicidal patients. Participants then completed items regarding their previous training, beliefs, attitudes, and current practices in youth suicide prevention, which were developed for the current study (see the Online Supplementary Materials).

Rural dweller status

Participants provided the zip code for the clinic/practice where they worked. These zip codes were then recoded to reflect rural and non-rural (urban and suburban) geographic areas using data available through the Health Resources & Services Administration (Health Resources and Services Administration [HRSA], 2024). For the purposes of the current study, “non-rural” included both urban and suburban geographic areas. Using HRSA rural classification files, zip codes were categorized by population density and proximity to urban centers, ensuring consistent geographic classification across participants and allowing examination of potential differences in practices across settings.

Modified Approach to Death and Dying patients Attitude Scale (MADDPAS)

The MADDPAS measures comfort and avoidance of working with suicidal patients (Kavas & Oztuna, 2011; Mitchell et al., 2020). The 12-item measure contains two subscales for discomfort with suicidal individuals and avoidance of suicidal individuals using a 5-point Likert scale with responses ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree); items are summed to calculate the scores. The MADDPAS was adapted from the original Approach to Death and Dying Patients Attitude Scale (ADDPAS; Kavas & Oztuna, 2011), which was previously validated among nurses treating terminally ill patients and demonstrated acceptable internal reliability (α = .72; Yildirim et al., 2023). In the current study, the Cronbach’s alphas for the comfort (α = .84) and avoidance scales (α = .79) indicated acceptable to good internal consistency and reliability.

Mental Illness: clinicians’ attitudes scale (MICA-4)

The MICA-4 measures PHCPs’ attitudes toward people diagnosed with mental illness and mental health treatment more broadly (Gabbidon et al., 2013). The 16-item measure is scored by summing PHCPs’ responses after the necessary reverse coding. The measure utilizes a 6-point Likert scale with responses ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 6 (Strongly Agree). The MICA-4 has been validated among nursing students and has shown good internal reliability (as indicated by adequate internal consistency [α = .79]; Wei et al., 2017). In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha (α = .72) was acceptable.

Procedures

Data were collected using an online survey administered to pediatric health care providers. Recruitment e-mails, postcards, and faxed flyers were sent to all enrolled providers in CPAN, which offers free access to a multidisciplinary network of mental health experts who provide peer-to-peer consultations by phone, personalized referrals, resources, and behavioral health continuing medical education. PHCPs who attempted to engage in the survey but were not enrolled in CPAN were given the option to enroll and then participate. Enrollment was free and primarily involved completing a brief registration process to access consultation and training resources. After accessing the survey link, eligible PHCPs were asked to provide informed consent and were then routed to the survey assessments. Five attention checks were randomly distributed throughout the survey to assess inattentive response patterns. Data from five participants were excluded because they had two or more failed attention checks during the survey. Participants received up to a $200 electronic gift card as compensation for their participation based on the amount of the survey they completed. To be included in the current study, providers had to report working in primary care or pediatric clinical settings. Data were collected between June 2023 and June 2025. The university institutional review board approved all procedures. As no patient (parent or youth) data were collected, and patients were not directly involved in the study, parent/guardian consent and child assent procedures were not applicable.

Analytic plan

All analyses were conducted using SPSS (Version 29). First, descriptive statistics were estimated for demographic information (see Table S1). Pearson’s chi-square tests were then conducted to assess differences between rural and non-rural PHCPs’ training experiences and preferences, as well as their current use of evidence-based suicide prevention practices for youth. Fisher’s Exact test was used in cases with small cell counts. A series of independent samples t-tests were conducted to test differences between rural and non-rural PHCPs in their past-year exposure to suicidal patients, knowledge and confidence in youth suicide prevention practices, and attitudes toward suicide and mental illness. Missing data were minimal across study variables (range: 0.0%–4.1%). Analyses were conducted using listwise deletion, such that participants with missing data on variables included in a given analysis were excluded from that analysis.

Results

Descriptive statistics regarding PHCPs’ training experiences and preferences are presented in Table 1. Overall, 69.6% of participants reported having never participated in suicide risk assessment/intervention training for youth patients, with no significant difference between rural and non-rural PHCPs, χ2 (1) = 0.68, p = .41, Cramer’s V = 0.06. The vast majority of PHCPs (91.8%) indicated that they were interested in receiving additional training in youth suicide prevention practices, which did not differ between rural and non-rural PHCPs; due to the small number that indicated they were not interested in additional training, Fisher’s exact test was used, but the results indicated no significant difference was present (p = .56, φ = −0.06). In addition, 86.6% of all participants expressed a willingness to participate in a virtual training on youth suicide risk assessment/intervention.

Descriptive statistics regarding PHCPs’ knowledge and confidence in conducting youth suicide prevention practices are presented in Table 2. Overall, 98% of participants reported having treated at least one suicidal patient during the past 12 months, with an average of 9.3 patients (SD = 16.0). Rural PHCPs reported encountering slightly more suicidal patients in the past year compared to non-rural PHCPs, although this difference was not significant, t(168.16) = −0.17, p = .87, d = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.30]. Rural and non-rural PHCPs similarly rated their knowledge and skills, t(122.98) = 1.38, p = .17, d = 0.22, 95% CI [−0.10, 0.54]; however, providers’ responses differed on confidence ratings, t(120.11) = 2.05, p = .04, d = 0.33, 95% CI [0.00, 0.65]. Specifically, non-rural providers’ ratings of their confidence in conducting suicide risk assessment/intervention for youth patients were higher compared to rural providers. On average, PHCPs reported moderate knowledge and skills (2.9 out of 5) and confidence (2.8 out of 5) in their youth suicide prevention practices.

Descriptive statistics regarding PHCPs’ current use of evidence-based youth suicide prevention practices are presented in Table 3. Because relatively few participants endorsed practices at lower frequency levels, the “In half of my cases,” “In a few of my cases,” and “Never” response options were combined into a single category and compared with “In most cases” in subsequent chi-square tests. Notably, only 55.6% of PHCPs reported using evidence-based suicide risk assessments and evidence-based safety planning in most cases when working with patients who are thinking about suicide, have engaged in a suicidal behavior, or have engaged in non-suicidal self-injury. There were no significant differences in utilization rates of evidence-based suicide risk assessments, χ2 (1) = 1.14, p = .29, Cramer’s V = 0.08, or evidence-based safety planning, χ2 (1) = 0.25, p = .62, Cramer’s V = 0.04, between rural and non-rural PHCPs. Most PHCPs (86.5%) reported referring patients to mental health services in most cases, and this rate did not differ between rural and non-rural PHCPs, χ2 (1) = 0.19, p = .66, Cramer’s V = 0.03.

Descriptive statistics regarding PHCPs’ attitudes toward suicide and mental illness are presented in Table 4. Nearly all PHCPs endorsed some level of agreement that suicide is a major problem among youth (99.4%), with no difference between rural and non-rural PHCPs t (162) = .09, p = .93, d = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.31, 0.34]. Nearly all also agreed that primary care providers play a critical role in preventing suicide among youth patients (98.8%), with similar rates observed between rural and non-rural PHCPs, t (162) = .53, p = .60, d = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.24, 0.41]. Rural and non-rural PHCPs exhibited similarity in their relatively low (on average) ratings of discomfort with suicidal patients, t(162) = −.11, p = .91, d = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.30], avoidance of suicidal patients, t(162) = −.36, p = .72, d = −0.06, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.30], and negative attitudes toward people with mental illness and the mental health field, t(161) = −.09, p = .93, d = −0.02, 95% CI [−0.34, 0.31].

Discussion

The purpose of the current study was to examine PHCPs’ beliefs, previous training experiences, confidence, attitudes, and implementation of evidence-based youth suicide prevention practices, with attention to differences between rural and non-rural providers. Overall, findings suggested that providers across settings frequently encountered youth experiencing suicidal thoughts and behaviors despite limited prior formal training in suicide risk assessment and intervention. Notably, rural and non-rural providers reported similar levels of prior training, knowledge, and use of evidence-based suicide prevention practices. However, rural providers reported lower confidence in conducting suicide risk assessment and intervention with their youth patients. Together, these findings highlight the importance of examining not only provider training exposure, but also provider confidence and implementation challenges across geographic settings.

Nearly three out of four PHCPs reported having never previously received training in youth suicide risk assessment and intervention. Despite this, PHCPs reported treating an average of nine patients experiencing suicidal ideation or behavior within the past year. Of particular concern, only half of PHCPs indicated that they regularly utilize evidence-based suicide risk assessments and interventions (i.e., safety planning) with their suicidal patients. Despite increased calls for suicide risk screening in primary care by the American Academy of Pediatrics, American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, and Children’s Hospital Association (2021), few providers consistently implement these recommendations (Horowitz et al., 2022). This could be due to the barriers many providers report facing in screening for suicide risk, including self-perceived lack of competence, limited time, competing professional demands, and insufficient collaboration with mental health services (Davis et al., 2023; Elzinga et al., 2020).

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of additional training efforts in suicide prevention among PHCPs to mitigate and address the current gap in evidence-based implementation. It is encouraging, however, that most PHCPs expressed an interest in receiving additional training in this area (92%). Furthermore, most PHCPs expressed willingness to engage in training via a virtual modality (87%). These findings may have important implications for increasing access to suicide prevention education across pediatric primary care settings, particularly in rural or underserved communities where geographic and workforce barriers may limit access to in-person training opportunities (LeCloux, 2018).

Youth living in rural areas are at increased risk for suicide and encounter multiple barriers to accessing mental health services (Crumb et al., 2019). Thus, rural PHCPs may play a particularly critical role in identifying and responding to suicide risk among youth. Although previous literature has found that providers report low confidence when conducting suicide risk assessments (LeCloux, 2018; Plax et al., 2024), current results extend this work by revealing that rural providers reported lower confidence in conducting suicide risk assessments and intervention for youth compared to non-rural providers.

PHCPs in rural areas face specific challenges when it comes to youth suicide, such as higher risk factors and limited access to mental health services; therefore, suicide prevention may be inherently more difficult in these settings and may decrease providers’ confidence in their ability to respond effectively (Fontanella et al., 2015; Singh et al., 2013). A lack of resources (e.g., financial support, shortage of providers) could also place pressure on rural providers to adequately assess and manage suicide risk, with an increased perceived responsibility for any adverse outcomes (Fontanella et al., 2015; Goldman-Mellor et al., 2018). Closer relationships within rural communities may further contribute to this perceived responsibility, as providers in rural pediatric primary care settings may navigate dual relationships and overlapping community roles with youth and families (Jaques-Leonard et al., 2021). The present findings suggest that enhancing provider confidence, particularly among rural PHCPs, should be a key focus of future suicide prevention training efforts.

Importantly, previous studies have found that suicide prevention training led to an increase in providers’ knowledge and self-perceived competence in conducting suicide risk assessments (Pisani et al., 2021; Solin et al., 2021). Although these components were not directly assessed in the current study, future suicide prevention training efforts may benefit from incorporating more applied and skills-based components, such as case-based learning, role-play practice, interdisciplinary collaboration, and ongoing consultation opportunities designed to strengthen provider confidence in managing suicide risk among youth patients in real-world clinical settings. Importantly, such multi-component trainings have previously been associated with increased skill acquisition and clinical competency (Herschell et al., 2010; Valenstein-Mah et al., 2020). These trainings may be particularly important for rural PHCPs, who may have fewer opportunities for immediate mental health consultation or collaborative support.

Encouragingly, both rural and non-rural providers reported low levels of stigma and avoidance of suicidal patients, reflecting a broad recognition of suicide prevention as an important component of pediatric care. Given providers’ strong interest in additional training, future research may benefit from improving access to suicide prevention training across multiple pathways, including integrating it into graduate and residency education, expanding fellowship and supervision opportunities, and increasing the availability of low-cost or flexible continuing medical education offerings tailored to primary care settings. Improving access to high-quality training, particularly for rural providers facing time and resource constraints, may be a critical step toward strengthening their confidence and capacity to implement evidence-based suicide prevention practices.

Clinical implications

The present findings have several important implications for clinical practice in pediatric primary care settings. Providers in this study frequently encountered youth experiencing suicidal thoughts and behaviors, despite the majority reporting no prior formal training in suicide risk assessment or intervention and only moderate levels of perceived knowledge and confidence. This pattern highlights a clear mismatch between clinical responsibilities and preparation, suggesting that improving access to targeted training is a critical step toward strengthening suicide prevention efforts within pediatric primary care settings.

At the same time, these findings should be interpreted with caution when applied to clinical practice. Given the cross-sectional and self-reported nature of the data, results do not reflect observed provider behavior or the effectiveness of specific practices. Instead, the findings identify several factors that may influence implementation of evidence-based suicide prevention practices, including provider confidence, access to training, and broader system-level barriers.

Although only about half of providers reported consistent use of evidence-based suicide risk assessment and safety planning, nearly all reported referring patients to mental health services and expressed strong interest in additional training, including virtual formats. This combination of high clinical demand and strong interest in training suggests that providers may be particularly receptive to accessible, flexible training opportunities that can be integrated into existing workflows.

Differences in confidence between rural and non-rural providers further highlight the need to prioritize not only knowledge acquisition but also provider self-efficacy. Although rural and non-rural providers reported similar training experiences, knowledge, and current practices, rural providers endorsed lower confidence in conducting suicide risk assessment and intervention. This suggests that training efforts, particularly in rural settings, may benefit from incorporating applied, skills-based components to support confidence in real-world clinical situations (Herschell et al., 2010; Valenstein-Mah et al., 2020). In rural or resource-limited settings, strategies such as telehealth consultation, flexible training formats, and streamlined referral processes may also improve feasibility.

With respect to generalizability, these findings are most applicable to pediatric and primary care settings, where providers often serve as the first point of contact for youth experiencing mental health concerns (Sheldrick et al., 2011). However, generalizability may be more limited in settings with different structures or resources, such as specialty mental health or emergency care environments. In addition, because all providers in this study were enrolled in a consultation network that offered access to mental health resources and training, the observed gaps may underestimate those present in less-supported systems.

Limitations and future directions

There are some limitations to consider when interpreting these findings. First, the study relied on self-report assessments and a cross-sectional design, which may be subject to self-presentation bias (Rosenman et al., 2011) and limit causal conclusions. Second, all PHCPs in this study were enrolled in a network that provides child and adolescent mental health consultation services and regular training opportunities, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The support and resources available to PHCPs could have bridged the gap between rural and non-rural PHCPs’ current practices in youth suicide prevention, as well as yielded a sample that is particularly invested in youth mental health. Participants were only primary care providers; therefore, findings may differ across other professions or medical specialties. Thus, replication within a more diverse sample of pediatric medical providers is warranted. We also did not collect data on the individuals who chose not to complete the survey; therefore, comparisons between responders and non-responders were not possible.

Future research should investigate the preferred modalities and specific needs of PHCPs when seeking youth suicide prevention training opportunities and examine whether ongoing consultation models contribute to provider confidence and implementation of suicide prevention practices over time. Investigating the effectiveness of online or in-person suicide prevention training for PHCPs is key for future work in youth suicide prevention efforts. Examining youth’s feelings regarding PHCPs’ involvement in suicide prevention may be helpful in bridging the gap in the relationship between the provider and the patient (Bellairs-Walsh et al., 2020). Lastly, examining confidence in more densely populated urban areas may help further clarify contextual differences between rural and non-rural providers and improve understanding of how geographic setting may influence suicide prevention practices.

In summary, this study highlights the critical need for additional training in evidence-based suicide risk assessment and management practices among PHCPs, given their indispensable role in suicide prevention. Ensuring that all PHCPs have the necessary skills and resources to effectively manage risk among their child and adolescent patients is crucial for preventing suicide across the nation. Improving PHCPs’ practices in suicide risk assessment and management requires not only individual training but also systemic changes to facilitate better integration with mental health services and to provide adequate time and resources for PHCPs to engage in these critically important care practices. Although the current study utilized a regional sample, these findings may help inform future suicide prevention training and implementation efforts across pediatric primary care systems in other states and health care settings.
