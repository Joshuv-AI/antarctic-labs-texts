Abstract
While the Koopman operator represents a nonlinear system as a linear operator in a function space, its definition does not involve inputs. For controller synthesis, an operator model is needed to describe the effect of feedback laws on closed-loop systems, so that the desired state-feedback law can be computationally searched based on such a predictive model. To this end, this paper proposes a Koopman–Nemytskii operator, defined as a linear operator that maps canonical features of state–policy pairs in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) to that of succeeding states. Under regularity conditions on the dynamics and kernel selection, this operator is definable on suitable Sobolev-type RKHSs, and its data-based estimation guarantees bounded errors in single-step prediction, multi-step prediction, and accumulated cost under control. The controller synthesis problem is thus formulated as a convex kernel-based optimization one and efficiently solved in a sample-based manner.

1.2
Motivation for a policy–state operator model: Controller synthesis

Consider the problem of finding an optimal controller with minimized cost:

J(k)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[-1,1]}\int_{0}^{\infty}(4x_{t}^{2}+a_{t}^{2})\mathrm{}t
.
By a black-box search over one dimension (
k
), the optimal linear feedback law is found as
k=k^{\ast}\approx 2.34
; clearly, such a search relies on an underlying sufficiently smooth relation between
k
and
J(k)
. In fact, the controller synthesis always boils down to
policy optimization
, namely the search over the parameters in the feedback law, and all feedback laws in scope are usually restricted in an admissible class.
As is well-known, for nonlinear systems, especially when the governing equations are unknown, the computation for an optimal controller is generally difficult
[
29
]
. Hence, an operator model that involves feedback policies (instead of involving the open-loop input values) would bring simplification, because with this model, the controller synthesis can be treated as a
direct search over policy features
. It will be shown that its solution can be well approximated in a data-driven (sample-based) manner.

In contrast, suppose that the open-loop input–state operator model (
4
) is used. When the controller is restricted to a parameterized family of laws, e.g.,
u(x_{t})=\mathrm{sat}(-kx_{t})
, the synthesis problem would query the state prediction by this operator in composition with the controller parameter:
(\phi_{x_{t}},\phi_{\mathrm{sat}(-kx_{t})}^{\prime})\mapsto\phi_{x_{t+1}}
.
Still, only the predictions on the state–input pairs that conform to the parameterized laws are unused.
Hence there is no saving on the information needed for learning the policy–state operator (
6
), and in fact, the choice of an input-value kernel
\phi^{\prime}
to meaningfully define the composition and relate the policy to performance brings back the notion of (
6
).
On the other hand, if an open-loop model (
4
) is used for an unrestricted controller synthesis, then the resulting solution is difficult due to the requirement of composition with undetermined nonparametric functions.

1.3
Contributions of this work

In this paper, we propose a novel concept of
Koopman–Nemytskii operator
, which is a generic linear representation of nonlinear systems in discrete time without input affinity. It characterizes the closed-loop flow under feedback laws from a
policy space
and is useful for controller synthesis as a search over the policy features.

1.

To the end of capturing closed-loop flows with a fixed equilibrium point at the origin, the mathematical construction of Koopman–Nemytskii operator relies on a
linear–Sobolev RKHS

\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
associated with a
linear–radial kernel

\kappa
.
This “equilibrium-preserving” technique was introduced in the author’s work on Koopman spectrum–stability relation
[
30
]
, extended to Lyapunov and dissipativity analysis
[
31
,
32
]
, and used for open-loop bilinear Koopman modeling
[
27
]
.
Such an RKHS comprises only of state-dependent functions that are “locally at least linear” and “globally Sobolev”, thus suitable for the analysis of dynamics with an equilibrium point.
This work first shows that with smooth enough dynamics and under smooth enough policies, the Koopman operator
K_{f_{u}}
is a well-defined and bounded linear operator between RKHSs.

2.

Under regularity assumptions on the policy parameterization, the dependence of the Koopman operator on the policy is continuous (despite nonlinear). Hence, a universal kernel
\varkappa
is defined on the policy space
\mathbb{U}
(with feature map
\varphi
). The superposition mapping
u\mapsto K_{f_{u}}
, namely the relation of policy to closed-loop dynamics, then gives rise to a linear operator
T
:

T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})\otimes\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U}),\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})),\kern 5.0pt\phi_{x}\otimes\varphi_{u}\mapsto\phi_{f_{u}(x)},

(8)

which we call as the
Koopman–Nemytskii operator
. Such an operator can be interpreted as a linear description of the evolution of
canonical features
(on the RKHSs) with the nonlinear dynamics, thus enabling prediction of states and state-dependent functions in closed loop.

3.

Provided a finite dataset of state–policy–successor snapshots
(x_{i},u_{i},y_{i})
(
y_{i}=f(x_{i},u_{i}(x_{i}))
,
i=1,\dots,m
),
a finite-rank approximation of the operator
\hat{T}
can be learned
via kernel extended dynamic mode decomposition (kernel EDMD).
This paper points out that the learned Koopman–Nemytskii operator has bounded generalized state prediction error, bounded multi-step state prediction error, and bounded accumulated cost errors for policy evaluation, which are linearly/quadratically proportional to the norm of initial state.
The error is analyzed via the theory of kernel interpolation
[
33
]
using the concept of fill distance of sample points, which thus scales down with increasing sample size.

4.

Finally, the Koopman–Nemytskii operator is used for controller synthesis. It is shown that the optimization of policy, considered as the search for the optimal policy parameter feature, can be formulated as a
kernel-based optimization
problem and approximated in finite dimensions for efficient data-driven (sample-based) solution. Numerical implementation on a chemical reactor is used to demonstrate the proposed approach in comparison to true model-based MPC, LQR, open-loop Koopman MPC, and open-loop Koopman synthesis strategies.

Related works

System identification

In classical literature, identifying linear transfer-function and state-space models from data has been discussed, in open-loop identification (where the system is perturbed by experimental input signals) and closed-loop identification (where the feedback controller is kept on)
[
34
]
.
Nonlinear structures such as Volterra series, kernel, and neural methods have been considered as flexible alternatives
[
35
]
.
Many recent works, pertaining to physics-informed machine learning, focused on discovering underlying differential equations
[
36
]
.
In principle, the present work can be considered as a closed-loop nonlinear system identification method that is generically applicable (under mild regularity assumptions).

Stability considerations in the learning of nonlinear dynamics

While there exist a wide range of machine learning algorithms for learning model equations, constraining it to accommodate prior information is desirable.
In a control context, stability is a primary concern. To the end of enforcing stability, reparameterization of Koopman operator that guarantees Hurwitz or Schur properties has been used
[
37
,
38
]
.
Another idea to preserve stability is to collect orbits over long horizons and set the loss metric or kernel to account for long-term errors
[
39
]
.
More general problem settings include the detection of bifurcation from data-driven models
[
40
]
, understanding the spectrum–stability relation in Koopman operator models
[
30
]
, and learning the Lyapunov stability/dissipativity
[
31
,
32
]
.

Hilbert space formulations in infinite-dimensional systems and stochastic control theory

Dynamical systems governed by partial differential equations and delayed differential equations can be conveniently described in infinite-dimensional Banach and Hilbert spaces. The properties of the operators describing the dynamics have been found useful for the analysis of the controllability, observability, and existence of optimal control in classical studies
[
41
]
.
This is also the case with dynamics governed by stochastic differential equations, interpreted by Fokker–Planck equations that describe the evolution of density functions
[
42
]
and hence have a functional state space.
From a computational point of view, convex optimization tools can often be enabled by operator-theoretic models
[
43
,
44
]
; this paper shares the same rationale.

Data-driven controller synthesis

Without explicit models, it is possible to design controllers from data with guaranteed specifications. Such data-driven controllers
[
45
,
46
]
can be found based on the Willems’ fundamental lemma, which states that persistently exciting trajectories can fully recover the behavior of linear systems
[
47
]
.
For nonlinear systems, a multitude of approaches including polynomial approximation, kernel regression, linear parameter-varying embedding, and nonlinearity cancellation have been proposed
[
48
,
49
]
.
Another promising approach is to learn dissipativity from data, which can lead to guaranteed performance even without the information of a complete model
[
50
,
51
]
.
The approach in this paper is data-driven and does not involve an explicit first-principles structure, but a nonparametric empirical model.

2.1
Hilbert space and Sobolev–Hilbert space

The following definition of Hilbert spaces is commonly known (see, e.g., Lax
[
52
]
). Let
\mathcal{H}
be a linear space on the field of real or complex numbers.
\mathcal{H}
may not be finite-dimensional. If an
inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
(a sesquilinear form on two arguments) is defined on
\mathcal{H}
, then this space is called an inner product space. If further the norm induced by the inner product (
\|h\|=\left\langle h,h\right\rangle^{1/2}
,
\forall h\in\mathcal{H}
) makes
\mathcal{H}
a complete metric space, then
\mathcal{H}
is said to be a
Hilbert space
.
If such an inner product is not defined, but
\mathcal{H}
is normed and complete, then
\mathcal{H}
is a Banach space.
We say that two Hilbert spaces coincide (
\mathcal{H}_{1}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{2}
) if
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
as sets and there exist positive constants
c_{2}\geq c_{1}>0
, such that
c_{1}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\leq\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\leq c_{2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
for any element
h
.

A typical example of Hilbert space is the following
Sobolev–Hilbert space
. Let
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty and
H^{0}(\mathbb{X})=L^{2}(\mathbb{X})
. First,
H^{0}(\mathbb{X})
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\left\langle h_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle=\int{X}h_{1}h_{2}\mathrm{d}x
. Then, for
s\in\mathbb{N}
, let
H^{s}(\mathbb{X})
be the space of functions whose weak derivatives up to degree
s
exist and belong to
L^{2}(\mathbb{X})
.
4
4

4

The weak derivative of
h
with multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
, whose degree is
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}
, refers to the function
\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h
satisfying

\int{X}\phi\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h\mathrm{d}x=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int{X}h\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}\phi}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}\mathrm{d}x,

for all
\phi
that are infinitely smooth and compactly supported in
\mathbb{X}
.

Then
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\left\langle h_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}{\left\langle\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h_{1},\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h_{2}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}
. The definition can be extended to all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
5
5

5

For fractional
s=\lfloor s\rfloor+r
,
\lfloor s\rfloor\in\mathbb{N}
,

, the norm is defined as:

\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq\lfloor s\rfloor}\|\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}^{2}+\sum_{|\alpha|=\lfloor s\rfloor}\iint_{\mathbb{X}^{2}}\frac{|\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h(x)-\mathrm{D}^{\alpha}h(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2r}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

In fact,
H^{s}
is an
interpolation space
between
H^{\lfloor s\rfloor}(\mathbb{X})
and
H^{\lceil s\rceil}(\mathbb{X})
.

A Sobolev–Hilbert space on
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be equivalently defined as the space comprising of functions
h
whose Fourier transform
\hat{h}
satisfies
c_{1}(1+|\omega|^{2})^{-s}\leq|\hat{h}(\omega)|\leq c_{2}(1+|\omega|^{2})^{-s}
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
)
[
53
]
. In other words, the space
H^{s}
defined by Fourier transform and the

defined by generalized derivatives coincide.

Given two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
, their
tensor product
is

\mathcal{G}\otimes\mathcal{H}=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\left\{g\otimes h:g\in\mathcal{G},h\in\mathcal{H}\right\}

where the elementary tensors
g\otimes h
, called the
Kronecker product
of
g
and
h
, are the ordered pairs of elements
(g,h)
endowed with inner product:
\left\langle g_{1}\otimes h_{1},g_{2}\otimes h_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle g_{1},g_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle h_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle
.
For clarity, a different notation
g\times h
is used to refer to the rank-
1
operator from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{G}
, defined by

(g\times h)h^{\prime}=\left\langle h,h^{\prime}\right\rangle g,\kern 5.0pt\forall h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}.

2.2
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)

The concept of RKHS is useful for regression or classification tasks. Without prior knowledge for defining a convenient parametric structure of the function in scope, the learning problem is often defined on an RKHS. Such a machine learning technique is known as the kernel method
[
54
]
.
Let
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
contain an infinite number of points. A continuous bivariate function
\kappa:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be a
Mercer kernel
, if for any finite number of points
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{X}
, the
m\times m
matrix
G_{\kappa}=[\kappa(x_{i},x_{j})]
is positive semidefinite. This implies that
\kappa(x,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
\kappa(x,x^{\prime})=\kappa(x^{\prime},x)
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}
.
The following space:

\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{\kappa(x,\cdot):\,x\in\mathbb{X}\}

endowed with an inner product
\left\langle\kappa(x,\cdot),\kappa(x^{\prime},\cdot)\right\rangle=\kappa(x,x^{\prime})
, is called the
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
.

We write
\phi:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
,
\phi(x)=:\phi_{x}=\kappa(x,\cdot)
as the
canonical feature map
. It satisfies
\left\langle\phi_{x},\phi_{x^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\kappa(x,x^{\prime})
and in fact, for all
f\in\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
,
\left\langle\phi_{x},f\right\rangle=f(x)
(which is known as the reproducing property).
The feature map
\phi:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
is continuous. Any
f
in the RKHS
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
is also continuous and bounded, due to the continuity of the canonical feature map
\phi
. Hence
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})\subset C_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbb{X})
. Furthermore, provided that
\kappa(x,x)^{1/2}
is bounded over
x\in\mathbb{X}
, the embedding from RKHS to
C_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{X})
is bounded, since
\|\phi_{x}\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\kappa(x,x)^{1/2}
and

\|f\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}|\left\langle\phi_{x},f\right\rangle|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}.

We say that the Mercer kernel is
radial
if
\kappa(x,x^{\prime})=\rho(|x-x^{\prime}|)
for some function
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The following fact, seen in Wendland
[
33
, Th. 10.35]
and Köhne et al.
[
55
, Th. 4.1]
, confirms the equivalence between the RKHS associated with a radial kernel and the Sobolev–Hilbert space. Let us refer to this kernel as the Sobolev kernel and denote by
\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}
.

Fact 2.1

(Sobolev kernel and Sobolev space)
.

If
\hat{\rho}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(r)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega r}\mathrm{d}r
is such that
c_{1}(1+|\omega|^{2})^{-s}\leq|\hat{\rho}(\omega)|\leq c_{2}(1+|\omega|^{2})^{-s}
for constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
and
s>n/2
, and if
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a Lipschitz boundary, then for the radial kernel
\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}(x,x^{\prime})=\rho(|x-x^{\prime}|)
, it holds that
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}}(\mathbb{X})\simeq H^{s}(\mathbb{X})
.
6
6

6

A construction of such a radial function
\rho
was given by Wendland
[
33
]
in the following way. First, let
\rho_{l}(r)=\max\{1-r,0\}^{l}
(
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
) for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
. By defining operator
I
on the space of polynomials supported within
[0,1]
:
(Ig)(r)=\int_{r}^{\infty}r^{\prime}g(r^{\prime})\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}
, one can denote
\rho_{n,k}=I^{k}\rho_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+k+1}
and let
\kappa_{n,k}^{\mathrm{Wen}}(x,x^{\prime})=\rho_{n,k}(|x-x^{\prime}|)
on
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 1
, where
n\geq 3
if
k=0
). Then
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa_{n,k}^{\mathrm{Wen}}}(\mathbb{X})\simeq H^{\frac{n+1}{2}+k}(\mathbb{X})
.
Clearly, we may also choose a constant
\sigma>0
to rescale the kernel, i.e., to use
\tilde{\rho}(r)=\rho(r/\sigma)
as the radial function.

However, when the system is known to have an equilibrium point at the origin and its stability or stabilization is of interest, Sobolev kernel may not be suitable, as it would lead to the property:
\|\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}(x,\cdot)\|^{2}=\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}(x,x)\equiv\rho(0)
(
\forall x\in\mathbb{X}
), i.e., any state is lifted onto the same sphere in the RKHS.
It is needed, hence, to introduce a new “equilibrium-aware” kernel. The following fact was given in the author’s earlier work
[
30
]
.

Fact 2.2

(Linear–radial kernel and linear–Sobolev space)
.

The following function is a Mercer kernel, called the
linear–radial kernel
:

\kappa(x,x^{\prime})=(x^{\top}x^{\prime})\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}(x,x^{\prime}).

Under the condition that
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a Lipschitz boundary, its associated RKHS
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
coincides with

\textstyle\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}{}_{k}g_{k}:g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{X})\right\},

where
e_{k}:x\mapsto x_{k}

(k=1,\cdots,n)
are the component mappings.
\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
is a Hilbert space endowed with inner product
\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}e_{k}g_{k},\sum_{k=1}^{n}e_{k}g_{k}^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\langle g_{k},g_{k}^{\prime}\right\rangle
and norm
\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}e_{k}g_{k}\|_{\mathring{H}^{s}}=\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|g_{k}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}
.

It is then direct to verify the property:
\|\phi_{x}\|=\kappa(x,x)^{1/2}\propto|x|
, which says that any state is lifted to an infinite-dimensional vector whose norm is proportional to its distance from the origin. One can also see that the space
\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
, as an RKHS, contains only functions that are valued
0
at the origin and have linear terms.
Thus colloquially, the RKHS of the linear–radial kernel is the
linear–Sobolev space
containing
“locally at least linear”
functions.
In this paper, the use of
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})\simeq\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
is two-fold.
First, given that the system (
3
) has an equilibrium point at the origin, we will define the Koopman operator on the linear–Sobolev type of RKHSs. Second, in the policy evaluation and synthesis, we will define the cost function as quadratic forms of
\phi_{x}
under the linear–radial kernel, as
“locally at least quadratic”
functions.

Another instrument that we will use later is the tensor product (as defined in the previous section) of two RKHSs:
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
, with canonical feature map
\phi
, and
\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U})
, with canonical feature map
\varphi
.
Due to the reproducing property and the tensor definition, for any
g\in\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
and
h\in\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U})
, we have
\left\langle g\otimes h,\phi_{x}\otimes\varphi_{u}\right\rangle=\left\langle g,\phi_{x}\right\rangle\left\langle h,\phi_{u}\right\rangle=g(x)h(u)
. Hence,
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})\otimes\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U})
is an RKHS with reproducing kernel
\bar{\kappa}((x,u),(x^{\prime},u^{\prime}))=\kappa(x,x^{\prime})\varkappa(u,u^{\prime})
and canonical feature
\bar{\phi}_{(x,u)}=\bar{\kappa}((x,u),(\cdot,\cdot))
.

2.3
Koopman operator and its learning

Definition 2.1
.

The composition operator, or
Koopman operator
, for an autonomous system
x_{t+1}=f(x_{t})
, where
f:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}
is continuous and
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{}_{x}}
, is

K_{f}:C_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{X})\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbb{X}),\kern 5.0ptg\mapsto g\circ f.

(9)

C_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{X})
is the space of bounded continuous functions on
\mathbb{X}
.

The Koopman operator is indeed a well-defined bounded linear operator on
C_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbb{X})
.
7
7

7

Since
C_{\mathrm{b}}(X)
is a Banach space with norm
\|g\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}}=\sup_{x\in X}|g(x)|
. Clearly, for any
g\in C_{\mathrm{b}}(X)
, we have
\|K_{f}g\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}}=\sup_{x\in X}|g(f(x))|\leq\sup_{y\in X}|g(y)|=\|g\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}}
. Hence
\|K_{f}\|\leq 1
.

From a learning point of view, it would more convenient if
K_{f}
can be defined directly on some RKHS. Indeed, this can be guaranteed by the regularity of the dynamics
f
. For
K_{f}
to be a bounded operator on
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa_{\mathrm{Sob}}}(\mathbb{X})\simeq H^{s}(\mathbb{X})
, the key condition given in Köhne et al.
[
55
]
is
f\in C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
,
s>d_{x}/2
.
8
8

8

Here
C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
for
s\in\mathbb{N}
refers to the space of functions that have bounded derivatives up to order
s
. It is a Banach space with norm

\textstyle\|g\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}|g(x)|.

For fractional
s=\lfloor s\rfloor+r
,
r\in(0,1)
, the space
C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
is understood as an interpolation space between
C_{\mathrm{b}}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}
and
C_{\mathrm{b}}^{\lceil s\rceil}
.

In the same spirit as
[
55
]
, the following conditions are given for
K_{f}
to be a bounded operator on
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})\simeq\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
. Here, let us denote

\textstyle\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{{}_{x}}{}_{k}g_{k}:g_{k}\in C_{\mathrm{}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})\right\},

and endow it with norm:

\textstyle\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{d_{x}}e_{k}g_{k}\right\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})}=\max_{k=1,\cdots,d_{x}}\|g_{k}\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})}.

Fact 2.3

(Koopman operator on the linear–Sobolev space
[
30
]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{}_{x}}
is compact,
f\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
,
s>d_{x}/2
and its Jacobian
\mathrm{D}f
is such that
\inf_{x\in X}|\mathrm{det}\,\mathrm{D}f(x)|>0
. Then
K_{f}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}))
.

Given an independent and identically distributed sample of states
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and successors
\{y_{i}=f(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the learning of the Koopman operator becomes the estimation of a linear mapping
\hat{K}_{f}
on the RKHS
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
such that under the estimated operator, the images of
\{\phi_{y_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, when evaluated on
\{x_{j}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, coincides with the actual Koopman operator:

(\hat{K}_{f}\phi_{y_{i}})(x_{j})=(K_{f}\phi_{y_{i}})(x_{j})=\kappa(y_{i},y_{j}),\enskip i,j=1,\dots,m.

To this end, we only need to let the estimated Koopman operator be specified by

\textstyle\hat{K}_{f}\phi_{y_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{ij}\phi_{x_{j}},

where the coefficients
\theta_{ij}
(
i,j=1,\dots,m
), in a matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, satisfies
\Theta G_{xx}=G_{yy}
. Here the Gramian matrix
G_{xx}=[\kappa(x_{i},x_{j})]
and
G_{yy}=[\kappa(y_{i},y_{j})]
.

Hence, if applied to any observable
g\in\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
to predict the value of
(K_{f}g)(x)
at any
x\in\mathbb{X}
, the estimated Koopman operator acts as if
g
is first interpolated by
\{\phi_{y_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Thus,

\langle\hat{K}_{f}g,\,\phi_{x}\rangle=\langle K_{f}Sg,\,\phi_{x}\rangle,

(10)

where the
kernel interpolation
operator
S:\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})\rightarrow\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{y_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is defined by
Sg=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(G^{-1}g_{y})_{i}\phi_{y_{i}}
, in which
g_{y}=(g(y_{1}),\cdots,g(y_{m}))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The error of such a prediction is bounded as follows Köhne et al.
[
55
, Th. 3.4]
.

Fact 2.4

(Prediction error of the learned Koopman operator)
.

If
K_{f}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X}))
, then it holds for (
10
) that:

\|K_{f}-\hat{K}_{f}\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{b}}}\leq\|\mathrm{id}-S\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{b}}}\|K_{f}\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}.

The first factor on the right-hand side is a uniform interpolation error on the RKHS, which is related to the
fill distance

\eta_{\mathbb{X}}
of the sample
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
on
\mathbb{X}

[
33
]
:

\|\mathrm{id}-S\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{b}}}\leq c\eta_{\mathbb{X}}^{s-d_{x}/2},\kern 5.0pt\eta_{\mathbb{X}}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{X}}\min_{i=1,\dots,m}\|y-y_{i}\|,

where
c
is a constant dependent only on
s
,
d_{x}
, and
\mathbb{X}
.
Given the scaling law of fill distance
\eta_{\mathbb{X}}\propto m^{-1/d_{x}}
, we can write

\|K_{f}-\hat{K}_{f}\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{b}}}\lesssim m^{-\left(\frac{s}{d_{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}.

3
Koopman–Nemytskii Operator

Now we consider an (unknown) nonlinear system in the form of (
3
). We make the following standing assumptions for the well-definedness of the Koopman operator under all feedback laws in consideration.

Assumption 3.1
.

For all policies
u\in\mathbb{U}
,
f_{u}\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
, with
s>d_{x}/2
,
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{X},u\in\mathbb{U}}|\mathrm{det}\,\mathrm{D}f_{u}(x)|>0
, and
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{}_{x}}
is a compact set with Lipschitz boundary.

Thus, by using the linear–radial kernel
\kappa
such that
\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X})\simeq\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
, we have

K_{f_{u}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}))\simeq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X}),\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})).

Now that the Koopman operator
K_{f_{u}}
describes the closed-loop dynamics under a given policy
u
, we examine the dependence of
K_{f_{u}}
on
u\in\mathbb{U}
. Out of this examination, the concept of Koopman–Nemytskii operator will arise.

Remark 3.1

(Special case of state-independent policies)
.

The discussions in the sequel aims to derive the operator model (
6
) for policies
u
in a prespecified class
\mathbb{U}
. This construction contains the special case of state-irrelevant constant-valued policies, i.e.,
\mathbb{U}=\{x\mapsto a:a\in\mathbb{A}\}
. This special case reduces to modeling the open-loop system under different input
values
instead of
policies
, i.e., will result in the “input–state operator model” (
4
).

3.1
Dependence of the Koopman operator on the policy

Clearly, the closed-loop dynamics
f_{u}=f(\cdot,u(\cdot))
depends on the policy
u
. If the function
f
is sufficiently smooth, then the dependence of
f_{u}
on
u
is naturally continuous.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose that
f\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A},\mathbb{X})
for some
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The substitution operator, or
Nemytskii operator
, of the controlled system (
3
) refers to:

N_{f}:\,\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{A})\rightarrow\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X}),\,u\mapsto f(\cdot,u(\cdot))=f_{u},

(11)

namely the mapping from the set of feedback laws to corresponding closed-loop dynamics.

The definition requires that
f_{u}\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
whenever
u\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{A})
. This is indeed true, briefly proved below.

Proof.

By the condition that
f\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A},\mathbb{X})
we can write

\textstyle f(x,a)=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{x}}x_{k}p_{k}(x,a)+\sum_{l=1}^{d_{u}}a_{l}q_{l}(x,a)

with all
p_{k},q_{l}\in C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A},\mathbb{X})
. Thus

\textstyle f(x,u(x))=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{x}}x_{k}p_{k}(x,u(x))+\sum_{l=1}^{d_{u}}u_{l}(x)q_{l}(x,u(x)).

Since
u_{l}\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
, we have
u_{l}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{x}}x_{k}w_{lk}(x)
with
w_{lk}\in C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
. Thus

\textstyle f(x,u(x))=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{x}}x_{k}[p_{k}(x,u(x))+\sum_{l=1}^{d_{u}}w_{lk}(x)q_{l}(x,u(x))],

where
p_{k}(\cdot,u(\cdot))+\sum_{l=1}^{d_{u}}u_{lk}q_{l}(\cdot,u(\cdot))\in C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
. Therefore
f(\cdot,u(\cdot))=f_{u}\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
.
∎

Proposition 3.1
.

At any fixed
u\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{A})
, the Nemytskii operator
N_{f}
is continuous, if
f\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A},\mathbb{X})
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, only consider
d_{x}=d_{u}=1
. The proposition is proved by induction. When
s=0
, suppose that
f\in\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
. Then in a similar way to the previous proof, let
f(x)=xp(x,a)+aq(x,a)
. Consider
u(x)=xw(x)
and
(u+\tilde{u})(x)=x(w(x)+\tilde{w}(x))
. Then
f(x,u(x))=x[p(x,xw(x))+w(x)q(x,xw(x))]
, which implies that

\textstyle\|f_{u+\tilde{u}}-f_{u}\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}}=\|p_{w+\tilde{w}}-p_{w}+(w+\tilde{w})q_{w+\tilde{w}}-wq_{w}\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}},

where
p_{w}(x)=p(x,xw(x))
and
q_{w}(x)=q(x,xw(x))
. Now that
p,q\in C_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}(\mathbb{X})
by Assumption
3.1
, obviously, by

\textstyle\|p_{w+\tilde{w}}-p_{w}\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}}\leq\|p\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}}\|\tilde{w}\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}}

and the analogous inequality for
q
, we obtain
\|N_{f}(u+\tilde{u})-N_{f}u\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}}\|\tilde{u}\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}}
. The proposition holds for
s=0
.

Suppose that the proposition holds for all
0\leq r\leq s-1
:

\|N_{f}(u+\tilde{u})-N_{f}u\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{r}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{r+1}}\|\tilde{u}\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{r}}.

For
(N_{f}(u+\tilde{u})-N_{f}u)(x)=x[p_{w+\tilde{w}}(x)+(w+\tilde{w})q_{w+\tilde{w}}(x)-p_{w}(x)-wq_{w}(x)]
, consider a partial derivative
\mathrm{D}^{s}
acted on the bracketed term after the
x
-term on the right-hand side.
By chain rule, this partial derivative comprises of differences in counterpart terms resulting from
w+\tilde{w}
and
w
, each being a product of partial derivatives of
p
,
q
, and
\mathrm{D}^{r}w(x)
(
r\leq s
). All these differences are bounded by constant multiples of
\|\tilde{w}\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}}=\|\tilde{u}\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s}}
, in which the coefficient involves
\|f\|_{\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s+1}}
. Therefore, the proposition then holds true for
s
.
∎

Then, we consider the dependence of the Koopman operator
K_{f_{u}}
on the dynamics
f_{u}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
. The
“Koopmanizing” operator
is

M:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})),\enskip f\mapsto K_{f},

where
K_{f}
is considered as a mapping from
\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X})
to
\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
, and
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{X})
is a family of functions that guarantees
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{X}}|\mathrm{det}\,\mathrm{D}f(x)|>0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under the conditions given in Definition
3.2
,
M
is a continuous operator.

Proof.

Induction is used to show the conclusion. First consider
s=0
. At any fixed
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we check
\|M(f+\tilde{f})-Mf\|
due to a small variation
\tilde{f}
in
\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{1}
such that
f+\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{F}
:

\textstyle\|K_{f+\tilde{f}}-K_{f}\|=\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathring{H}^{1}}\leq 1}\|g(f(\cdot)+\tilde{f}(\cdot))-g(f(\cdot))\|_{\mathring{H}^{0}}.

Without loss of generality, consider the case with
d_{x}=1
and
d_{u}=0
. Then
g(x)=x\gamma(x)
and
f(x)=xp(x)
. Thus,

\textstyle g(f(x)+\tilde{f}(x))-g(f(x))=x[\bar{\gamma}(x,p(x)+\tilde{p}(x))-\bar{\gamma}(x,p(x))]

where
\bar{\gamma}(x,p):=p\gamma(xp)
. Letting
\bar{\gamma}_{p}(x)=\bar{\gamma}(x,p(x))
, we get

\textstyle\|M(f+\tilde{f})-Mf\|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(\bar{\gamma}_{p+\tilde{p}}-\bar{\gamma}_{p}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\gamma
(and hence
\bar{\gamma}
) is in
\mathring{H}^{1}(\mathbb{X})
,
\mathrm{D}_{p}\bar{\gamma}(x,p)=\gamma(xp)+xp\mathrm{D}\gamma(xp)
, we have the integrand upper-bounded by a constant multiple of
\|\tilde{p}\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}^{0}}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(\gamma(xp(x))^{2}+\mathrm{D}\gamma(xp(x))^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x
. With the assumption that
|\mathrm{det}\,\mathrm{D}f(x)|\geq\epsilon>0
for some
\epsilon>0
, we have the integration further upper bounded, through change of variables
y=f(x)
, by
\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(\gamma(y)^{2}+\mathrm{D}\gamma(y)^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}y
, namely
\|\gamma\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|g\|_{\mathring{H}^{1}}^{2}
.
Therefore, upon
\|g\|_{\mathring{H}^{1}}\leq 1
, we obtain
\|M(f+\tilde{f})-Mf\|\lesssim\|\tilde{f}\|_{\mathring{C}_{0}}
.

Suppose that for all
0\leq r\leq s-1
:

\textstyle\|M(f+\tilde{f})-Mf\|_{\mathring{H}^{r+1}\rightarrow\mathring{H}^{r}}\lesssim\|\tilde{f}\|_{\mathring{C}^{r}}.

Then in the case of
s
, for
g(f(x))=xp(x)\gamma(xp(x))=x\bar{\gamma}(x,p(x))
, the action of
\mathrm{D}^{s}
on it results in terms that involve the
r
-th partial derivatives of
\bar{\gamma}
(hence
\gamma
) for
0\leq r\leq s
. When taking the difference between
f+\tilde{f}
and
f
, each of the comprising terms, upon squared integration, will be bounded by a constant multiple of
\|\gamma\|_{H^{s+1}}^{2}
(and hence
\|g\|_{\mathring{H}^{s+1}}^{2}
) multiplied by
\|\tilde{p}\|_{C^{s}(\mathbb{X})}
. Therefore, upon
\|g\|_{\mathring{H}^{s+1}}\leq 1
,

\textstyle\|g(f(\cdot)+\tilde{f}(\cdot))-g(f(\cdot))\|_{\mathring{H}^{s}}\lesssim\|\tilde{f}\|_{\mathring{C}^{s}}.

The proof is then completed.
∎

Composing the two operators, the operator
MN_{f}:u\mapsto K_{f_{u}}

becomes a nonlinear but continuous operator from
\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{A})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}))
.
The Sobolev index of the codomain is
1
lesser than the domain. This is inevitable since the effect of any variation in policy on the closed-loop dynamics is embodied on the observable
g
through the derivatives of
g
.
As the norm in
\mathring{H}^{s+1}
is stronger than the norm in
\mathring{H}^{s}
, it becomes impossible to deem
MN_{f}
as a continuous operator to
\mathring{H}^{s+1}
or an operator from
\mathring{H}^{s}
. This issue does not arise for autonomous systems (e.g., in
[
55
]
or
[
30
]
).
Due to this reason, next, instead of considering the continuous dependence of
K_{f_{u}}
on
u
, we focus on its adjoint operator
K_{f_{u}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}))
.

3.2
Definition of the Koopman–Nemytskii operator

By the adjoint operator
K_{f_{u}}^{\ast}
, we refer to the one such that

{\left\langle K_{f_{u}}^{\ast}h,h^{\prime}\right\rangle}_{\mathring{H}^{s+1}}={\left\langle h,K_{f_{u}}h^{\prime}\right\rangle}_{\mathring{H}^{s}},\forall h\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}),h^{\prime}\in\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X}).

The adjoint operator of the Koopman operator can be called as
Perron–Frobenius operator
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The operator defined by

T_{0}:\mathbb{U}\subset\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X}),\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})),\enskip u\mapsto K_{f_{u}}^{\ast}

is continuous under Assumption
3.1
, and has the property:

(T_{0}u)\phi_{x}=\phi_{f_{u}(x)},\enskip\forall u\in\mathbb{U},x\in\mathbb{X},

(12)

where
\phi
is the canonical map of the linear–radial kernel, the reproducing kernel of
\mathring{H}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X})

Remark 3.2

(Stochastic interpretation)
.

The interpretation of the Koopman–Nemytskii operator is intuitive. Given a state
x\in\mathbb{X}
and a policy
u\in\mathbb{U}
, represented by their canonical features, the Koopman–Nemytskii operator returns the canonical feature of the succeeding state
\phi_{f_{x}(u)}
.
Given a “stochastic mixture of states”
\sum_{i}p_{i}\phi_{x_{i}}
and
\sum_{j}q_{j}\varphi_{u_{j}}
as a “stochastic mixture of policies” (which may not be necessarily normalized to
\sum_{i}p_{i}=\sum_{j}q_{j}=1
), the Koopman-Nemytskii operator returns a corresponding “stochastic mixture of updated states”
T\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\phi_{x_{i}},\sum_{j}q_{j}\varphi_{u_{j}}\right)=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}p_{i}q_{j}\phi_{f_{u_{j}}(x_{i})}.

Hence, if the canonical feature of the initial state
x
can be approximately seen as
\phi_{x}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\phi_{x_{i}}
, and the policy
u
is considered as a combination of sampled policies’ features:
\varphi_{u}=\sum_{j}q_{j}\varphi_{u_{j}}
, then the prediction follows the “mixture” formula.

Remark 3.3

(Regularity of the system)
.

The construction of the Koopman–Nemytskii operator requires sufficient smoothness of the dynamics and non-degeneracy of Jacobian.
The former condition is posed due to the need for the equivalence between the Sobolev–Hilbert space and an RKHS used in learning
[
55
]
.
This can be well satisfied by systems whose governing equations arise from physical laws that are smooth.
The second condition is also mild — if the physical dynamics is continuous-time and discretized with a short sampling time, the non-degeneracy of Jacobian is guaranteed by the existence and regularity theory of ordinary differential equations.

Remark 3.4

(Black-box nature and hybrid modeling)
.

The “lifting” of a nonlinear system into an operator model, by itself, overlooks the physical meanings of the underlying dynamics. Hence, the interpretation of the Koopman–Nemytskii operator is only “empirical”.
Users who are concerned with the physical interpretability can devise a hybrid modeling strategy. Possible approaches include (i) collecting simulation data from a low-fidelity first-principles model, and then training the operator on a mixture of low-fidelity simulations and high-fidelity plant data, and (ii) training a reference operator on the simulation data from a low-fidelity first-principles model, and then regularizing the learned operator near the reference when learning from high-fidelity data.

Remark 3.5

(Parameterization of policy space and need for prior knowledge)
.

While in principle, the Koopman–Nemytskii operator can be defined for a wide policy class
\mathbb{U}
, or even the entire
\mathring{C}_{\mathrm{b}}^{s+1}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{A})
, practically, if such an operator is learned from data, the policy class
\mathbb{U}
must be (low-dimensionally) parameterized.
Essentially, this is due to the fact that the policy’s effect on the closed-loop dynamics is characterized in a “black-box” manner from a Koopman operator learning perspective. In other words, one is offered with no oracle other than the regularity of the defining spaces.
Hence, if there is prior information on the forms of satisfactorily performing controllers (based on user’s common sense of the system’s governing physics), then the policy space
\mathbb{U}
can be chosen to contain such forms, and the policy kernel
\varkappa
can be defined on the controller parameters.

3.3
Koopman–Nemytskii operators in continuous time

The approach above is proposed for discrete-time systems mainly due to its formal and computational simplicity. Here we comment on the case of a continuous-time system:

4.1

sectorial error bound
in state prediction is guaranteed.
Such a result is clearly brought by our selection of the linear–radial kernel, with which
\|\phi_{x}\|
is proportional to
|x|
.
Considering the scaling with sample size
m
, we have

\lvert\widehat{x^{+}}-x^{+}\rvert\lesssim m^{-\frac{s-d_{x}/2}{d_{x}+d_{v}}}\|T\||x|.

The capability of approximating the Koopman–Nemytskii operator therefore strongly depends on the smoothness of the kernel and also the dimensions of the states and the policy space. Smoother systems provide better scaling laws; if
s>d_{x}+d_{v}/2
, then the error in proportion to
|x|
decays at a rate of at least
m^{-1/2}
.

Remark 4.1

(
L^{\infty}
versus
L^{2}
error)
.

If one is concerned only with the
single-step prediction error
, a mean-squared error (
L^{2}
-error) can be used as a performance metric.
By
[
22
]
, the
squared
L^{2}
-error
can be bounded by
O(m^{-1}\log(1/\delta)+\sqrt{m^{-1}\log(1/\delta)})
for a confidence of
1-\delta
.
This appears to have a better scaling with sample size
m
if the smoothness index
s
is low, insensitive to dimensionality due to the irrelevance of fill distance.
However, such a non-uniform error bound cannot be easily extended to multi-step predictions.
Potentially, a “reconciled” error, e.g., uniform
on trajectories
and
mean-square among trajectories
, may be useful for multi-step prediction and policy evaluation. However, a suitable learning formulation to enforce such a bound is lacking.

Remark 4.2

(Dimensionality reduction and decomposition)
.

When the state space and/or policy space have high dimensions, two possible remedies may be helpful to the user. (i) Dimensionality reduction methods can be used to remove the empirically redundant variables. (ii) The system can be possibly decomposed into interconnected subsystems, each having a smaller dimension, so that the learning is performed separately on these subsystems.

Remark 4.3

(Online learning and reinforcement learning)
.

The operator learning here is based on a fixed dataset and hence an offline routine. Due to the pursuit of an
L^{\infty}
-type error, the error bound relies on the fill distance that does not “nicely” scale down, if
s
is too small or
d_{v}
is high.
Hence, the user may use an online numerical interpolation or online learning approach to improve the quality of the estimator operator as new data arrives.
Potentially, one can iteratively update the Koopman–Nemytskii operator and synthesize the controller (as we will discuss later), conceptually in a reinforcement learning manner to improve the control performance.
This is beyond what can be addressed in the current paper.

4.3
Error in multi-step prediction and accumulated cost

Following the previous subsection, if the estimated operator offers a uniform error (scaled by
|x|
) in predicting the succeeding states over a single time step, then the prediction over multiple time steps is anticipated to be correspondingly bounded.
For any fixed
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
u\in\mathbb{U}
, let us denote by
\phi_{x}^{0}=\hat{\phi}_{x}^{0}=\phi_{x}
, and for
t=0,1,\dots
, denote
\phi_{x}^{t+1}=T(\phi_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u})
and
\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t+1}=\hat{T}(\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u})
.

Theorem 4.2

(Error bound for multi-step prediction)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.1
, for all
g\in\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
and
t=1,2,\cdots
,

\left|\left\langle g,\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}-\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\eta^{s-d_{x}/2}t\beta^{t-1}\|g\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}|x|,

(18)

where
\beta:=\max\{\sup_{u\in\mathbb{U}}\|K_{f_{u}}\|,\|\hat{T}\|\}
.
If further
\beta<1
, then

\left|\left\langle g,\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}-\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\eta^{s-d_{x}/2}\|g\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}|x|,\kern 5.0ptt\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

We write for any
t=1,2,\cdots
:

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle g,\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t+1}\right\rangle-\left\langle g,\phi_{x}^{t+1}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle Sg,T(\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u})\right\rangle-\left\langle g,T(\phi_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u})\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\langle(S-\mathrm{id})g,T(\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u})\right\rangle\right|+\left|\left\langle g,T((\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}-\phi_{x}^{t})\otimes\varphi_{u})\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\eta^{s-d_{x}/2}\beta^{t}\|g\|\|\phi_{x}\|+\left|\left\langle K_{f_{u}}g,\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\right\rangle-\left\langle K_{f_{u}}g,\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle\right|.

The last inequality holds due to the single-step error bound in (
17
) and the fact that
\|\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\|\leq\|\hat{T}\|\|\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t-1}\otimes\varphi_{u}\|\leq\|\hat{T}\|\|\hat{\phi}_{x-1}\|
. Hence, starting from any
t\in\mathbb{N}
and track backwards, we have

\textstyle\left|\left\langle g,\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\right\rangle-\left\langle g,\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle\right|\lesssim\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\eta^{s-d_{x}/2}\beta^{\tau}\|K_{f_{u}}^{t-1-\tau}g\|\|\phi_{x}\|.

where
\beta^{\tau}\|K_{f_{u}}^{t-1-\tau}g\|\leq\beta^{t-1}\|g\|
and
\|\phi_{x}\|\propto|x|
.
∎

Remark 4.4

(Contraction)
.

To have a uniform multi-step prediction error, the above theorem provides the condition that the Koopman spectrum lies inside the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
.
As discussed in
[
30
]
, such contraction is impossible if the state-RKHS is the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{X})
, because the transition on the equilibrium point
\phi_{0}\mapsto\phi_{0}
with
\|\phi_{0}\|\neq 0
would result in
\|T\|\geq 1
.
Instead, by using the linear–Sobolev space
\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
associated with the linear–radial kernel (as in this paper) enables contraction, if there is a homeomorphism on the state space
\mathbb{X}
that maps the nonlinear dynamics to the linear dynamics governed by the Jacobian at the origin, and the Jacobian has all eigenvalues inside the unit circle.

An alternative to the linear–Sobolev space formulation is to adopt the weighted
C_{\mathrm{b}}
-space (and an associated weighted RKHS)
[
60
]
. Specifically, if an exponentially decaying smooth positive function
w(\cdot)
is known:

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{X}}w(f_{u}(x))/w(x)\leq\alpha<1
,
then we define the
weighted
C_{\mathrm{b}}
space
:
C_{\mathrm{b},w}(\mathbb{X})=\{w\cdot h:h\in C_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbb{X})\}
, with norm defined by
\|w\cdot h\|_{C_{\mathrm{b},w}}=\|h\|_{C_{\mathrm{b}}}
.
The Koopman–Nemytskii operator
T
is then contractive:
\|T\|_{C_{\mathrm{b},w}\times\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}\rightarrow C_{\mathrm{b},w}}\leq\alpha<1
.

In addition to the bound on multi-step prediction, we also provide a bound on the accumulated cost for
policy evaluation
. In an optimal control language, the stage cost (i.e., the cost at each time in the horizon) is defined by positive functions on the states and input actions, respectively:
q(x)+r(u(x))
, and the terminal cost by a positive function
q_{\mathrm{f}}(x)
.
Considering these costs as “kernel quadratic forms”:

q(x)=\left\langle\phi_{x},Q\phi_{x}\right\rangle
,
r(u(x))=\left\langle\bar{\phi}_{(x,u)},R\bar{\phi}_{(x,u)}\right\rangle
,
q_{\mathrm{f}}=\left\langle\phi_{x},Q_{\mathrm{f}}\phi_{x}\right\rangle
, with
Q
,
Q_{\mathrm{f}}
, and
R
being trace-class positive operators, the accumulated cost is expressed as:

\textstyle\psi(x,u)=\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}\gamma^{t}\left(\left\langle\phi_{x}^{t},Q\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t},R\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t}\right\rangle\right)+\left\langle\phi_{x}^{\tau},Q_{\mathrm{f}}\phi_{x}^{\tau}\right\rangle

(19)

where
\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t}=\phi_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a discount factor.
We can now consider the difference between
\psi(x,u)
and its approximation under the data-based estimation:

\textstyle\hat{\psi}(x,u)=\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}\gamma^{t}\left(\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t},Q\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle+\langle\hat{\bar{\phi}}_{x,u}^{t},R\hat{\bar{\phi}}_{x,u}^{t}\rangle\right)+\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{\tau},Q_{\mathrm{f}}\hat{\phi}_{x}^{\tau}\rangle.

Theorem 4.3

(Error bound for policy evaluation)
.

Under the conditions in Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{\psi}(x,u)-\psi(x,u)|\lesssim\eta^{s-d_{x}/2}|x|^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\gamma^{t}t(\beta^{t-1}+\beta^{2t-1})

(20)

for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
u\in\mathbb{U}
.
If further
\beta<1
, then

\displaystyle|\hat{\psi}(x,u)-\psi(x,u)|\lesssim\eta^{s-d_{x}/2}|x|^{2}.

Proof.

By writing the positive operator
Q
as
Q=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bar{q}_{j}\times\bar{q}_{j}
, where
\bar{q}_{j}\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
, we have

\textstyle\left|\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t},Q\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle-\langle\phi_{x}^{t},Q\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\langle\bar{q}_{j},\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle^{2}-\left\langle\bar{q}_{j},\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle^{2}\right|.

Due to Theorem
4.2
,

|\langle\bar{q}_{j},\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle-\langle\bar{q}_{j},\phi_{x}^{t}\rangle|\lesssim\eta^{\nu}\|\bar{q}_{j}\||x|t\beta^{t-1}
, while
|\langle\bar{q}_{j},\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle+\left\langle\bar{q}_{j},\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle|\leq\|\bar{q}_{j}\|(\|\phi_{x}^{t}\|+\|\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\|)\lesssim\|\bar{q}_{j}\|(1+\beta^{t})|x|
.
Since
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\bar{q}_{j}\|_{\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(Q)<\infty
,

\textstyle\left|\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t},Q\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle-\langle\phi_{x}^{t},Q\hat{\phi}_{x}^{t}\rangle\right|\lesssim\eta^{\nu}|x|^{2}t\beta^{t-1}(1+\beta^{t}).

In an analogous way we can bound the error between
\langle\hat{\bar{\phi}}_{x,u}^{t},R\hat{\bar{\phi}}_{x,u}^{t}\rangle
,
\langle\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t},R\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t}\rangle
, as well as the error between
\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{\tau},Q_{\mathrm{f}}\hat{\phi}_{x}^{\tau}\rangle
and
\left\langle\phi_{x}^{\tau},Q_{\mathrm{f}}\phi_{x}^{\tau}\right\rangle
in the exactly same form.
∎

5
Feedback Controller Synthesis

Now let us return to the rationale for proposing an operator that takes in the canonical features of state and
policy
for prediction and policy evaluation. By using the policy feature instead of a possible input action feature as conceptualized in (
4
), in its definition, the Koopman–Nemytskii operator avoids the composition of the input action with the policy.
Hence, in controller synthesis, i.e., when monolithically finding an optimal feedback law within a parametric policy class, the search can be considered as a
search over the policy features
, without using the composition operation to relate the policy to its performance evaluation.

5.1
Objective formulation

Starting at any given initial state
x\in\mathbb{X}
, the performance of a policy
u\in\mathbb{U}
is evaluated by the accumulated cost over an infinite horizon:

\textstyle\psi_{\infty}(x,u)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left[q(x_{t})+r(u(x_{t}))\right],

where
x_{0}=x
,
x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u(x_{t}))
(
t\in\mathbb{N}
). As in §
4.3
, we assume that the stage cost can be expressed as a “kernel quadratic form”, i.e.,
q(x_{t})=\left\langle\phi_{x}^{t},Q\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle
and
r(u(x_{t}))=\left\langle\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t},R\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t}\right\rangle
with
\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t}=\phi_{x}^{t}\otimes\varphi_{u}
. The evaluation of the policy is thus taken as the average over a probability distribution of states:

J_{\infty}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\psi_{\infty}(x,u)\right].

While
J_{\infty}(u)
may not be exactly evaluated at any
u
, due to the possibly continuous distribution
\mathbb{P}
and the infinite horizon in
\psi_{\infty}
, there always exists a discrete distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
supported on finitely many points
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{m_{x}^{\prime}}\}
and a sufficiently long horizon
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\psi_{\tau}(x,u)\right]:=\frac{1}{m_{x}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{x}^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}\gamma^{t}\left[q(x_{i,t})+r(u(x_{i,t}))\right]+q_{\mathrm{f}}(x_{i,\tau})\right]

with a terminal cost
q_{\mathrm{f}}
, is a precise estimation of
J_{\infty}(u)
with an arbitrarily small uniformly bounded error. This is due to the continuity of
q
and
r
in addition to the boundedness of state space
\mathbb{X}
and action space
\mathbb{A}
.
In a data-driven setting, the evaluation of
\psi_{\tau}(x_{i},u)
can be approximately performed by the Koopman–Nemytskii operator estimated from kernel EDMD. As proven in Theorem
4.3
, the estimated
\hat{\psi}_{\tau}(x_{i})
has an error proportional to
|x|^{2}
(and hence uniform over
\mathbb{X}
), which is further independent of
\tau
if contraction holds, i.e., if
\beta:=\max\{\|\hat{T}\|,\sup_{u\in\mathbb{U}}\|K_{f_{u}}\|\}<1
.
Thus, we denote

J(u)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\hat{\psi}_{\tau}(x,u)\right]

(22)

as the practically computable, Koopman–Nemytskii learning-based policy evaluation. Therefore, the problem of minimizing
J(u)
over
u\in\mathbb{U}
is actually of interest.
For any precision
\epsilon>0
, we have sufficiently large
m_{x}^{\prime}
,
\tau
, and
m
(in kernel EDMD), such that as long as the optimizer
u^{\star}
of
\hat{J}(u)
is found, we have
J_{\infty}(u^{\star})-J(u^{\star})<\epsilon
.

Proposition 5.1

(Regularity of the objective function)
.

Suppose that
q(x_{t})=\left\langle\phi_{x}^{t},Q\phi_{x}^{t}\right\rangle
and
r(u(x_{t}))=\left\langle\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t},R\bar{\phi}_{x,u}^{t}\right\rangle
with
Q
and
R
being trace-class operators in
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{X})
and
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{\kappa}}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{U})
, respectively. Let
\varkappa
be such that
\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U})\simeq H^{s}(\mathbb{U})
. Then
J\in\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U})
.

Proof.

By decomposing
Q=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bar{q}_{j}\times\bar{q}_{j}
, we have
q(x_{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bar{q}_{j}(x_{t})^{2}
, where each
\bar{q}_{j}\in\mathring{H}^{s}(\mathbb{X})
. The same decomposition can be written for
R
. Hence, it only remains to prove that each
x_{t}
(
t\in\mathbb{N}
) is an
H^{s}
-class of
u
, since it would justify that
\bar{q}_{j}(x_{t})^{2}
and
\bar{r}_{j}(x_{t},u_{t})^{2}
are in the
H^{s}
class as a function of
u
. This is a direct implication of the
C^{s}
property of
f
.
∎

Hence, the problem is to minimize an objective function that resides in an RKHS, while it may be nonconvex and lacks an explicit algebraic expression, although it can be evaluated on sample points.

5.2
Kernel-based optimization algorithm

Conceptually, one can reformulate the global optimization of a nonconvex function as the optimization of probability distributions on
\mathbb{U}
, i.e.,
\min_{\mathbb{Q}}\int J(u)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(u)
, so that the optimal solution corresponds to any distribution supported on global optima. The reformulated problem is indeed convex, but should be interpreted as an
infinite-dimensional linear programming problem
. If the objective is polynomial, the problem is to optimize the moments of the distribution, and the dual problem is to find the maximum constant
c
such that
J-c
is a nonnegative polynomial. The resulting momentum/polynomial optimization problem allows a disciplined solution via hierarchical finite-dimensional approximations
[
43
,
62
]
.

Generalizing to a non-polynomial RKHS setting, the formulation of Rudi et al.
[
63
]
considers
J-c
as a nonnegative kernel quadratic form, i.e., a kernel sum-of-squares form, and enforces this constraint on some sample points
\{u_{1},\cdots,u_{m_{u}^{\prime}}\}
:

\displaystyle\max_{c\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{S}_{+}}

\displaystyle c-\lambda\operatorname{tr}A

(23)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle J(u_{i})-c=\left\langle\varphi_{u_{i}},A\varphi_{u_{i}}\right\rangle,\,i=1,\cdots,m_{u}^{\prime}.

where
\mathcal{S}_{+}
stands for the space of positive self-adjoint trace-class operators on
\mathcal{N}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{U})
. Since the constraints are on sample points only, it suffices to find a positive semidefinite matrix
B
such that

J(u_{i})-c=g_{i}^{\top}Bg_{i},\,i=1,\cdots,m_{u}^{\prime}

where
g_{i}
is the
i
-th column of the Gram matrix
G_{\varkappa}=[\varkappa(u_{i},u_{j})]
, and replace
-\lambda\operatorname{tr}A
with
-\lambda\operatorname{tr}B
. The regularization parameter
\lambda>0
serves the purpose of leveraging the smoothness of the kernel — when
\lambda=0
, the optimized
c
is simply the sample minimum regardless of the “complexity” of
J-c
as a quadratic form, while using
\lambda>0
penalizes such a complexity and thus constrains the variation of
J-c
from the sample points.
The accuracy of (
23
) was proved in
[
63
, Th. 6]
, which we translate into our setting as below.

Theorem 5.1

(Accuracy of kernel-based optimization)
.

Suppose that (i) the controller parameter set
\mathbb{V}=\cup_{v\in\mathbb{V}_{0}}\mathbb{B}(v,r)
for some
\mathbb{V}_{0}\subset\mathbb{V}
and
r>0
, (ii)
s>d_{v}/2+2
and
\mathcal{N}_{\varkappa}(\mathbb{U})\simeq H^{s}(\mathbb{V})
, (iii)
\mathbb{V}
contains finitely many global optima, all of which are isolated points and not on the boundary of
\mathbb{V}
, and at which the Hessian matrices are all positive definite.
Then, with sufficiently large

We have the following observations.

•

As expected, the computational time needed by offline synthesized controllers with explicit laws is negligible vis-à-vis MPC strategies.
Using a kernel-based operator model in MPC is especially computationally cumbersome, since its state prediction model in the MPC is not only nonlinear, but also contains hidden states that are associated with the training data, which are further repeated throughout the prediction horizon (thus of number
m\tau=25000
).
From this point of view, it is more desirable to pursue a synthesis method than MPC.

•

The achievement of asymptotic stability primarily depends on the capability of capturing local dynamics around the equilibrium point, which requires the use of a linearized Jacobian model or a linear–radial kernel-based operator model.
Even for true model-based nonlinear MPC (without using terminal constraints or input bounds), the performance is lost. As this should be attributed to the non-globality of nonlinear MPC solver, from a practical point of view, using a linear–radial kernel provides more robust performance.

•

Both the learned open-loop Koopman operator (under the linear–radial kernel), composed with any assigned parametric class of policies, and the Koopman–Nemytskii operator directly learned on this policy class, can be used for state prediction and hence policy evaluation. Because of this, the synthesized controllers via strategies 5/6 and strategies 7/8 are similar.
However, in the “open-loop Koopman synthesis” approach, it is noteworthy that (i) the regularity conditions for the existence of Koopman–Nemytskii operator are already tacitly assumed to hold, (ii) the information used for policy evaluation essentially does not differ from that used for Koopman–Nemytskii policy evaluations, and (iii) the kernel-based optimization still relies on policy parameterization and a policy kernel.

By introducing the notion of Koopman–Nemytskii operator and its learning, the policy evaluation and optimization procedures are explicated.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, a Koopman-like linear operator representation of nonlinear controlled systems, named Koopman–Nemytskii operator, is proposed, and its data-based estimation, generalization errors, and use for controller synthesis are discussed.
Under regularity conditions, the
Koopman–Nemytskii operator
is a well-defined, bounded linear operator from the tensor product of an RKHS of states and a RKHS on the space of feedback laws to the foregoing RKHS, mapping the canonical features of a state and the canonical feature of a policy to that of the succeeding state.
As such, one-step or multi-step state predictions, as well as the prediction of accumulated cost under control, can be performed. In particular, the prediction by the Koopman–Nemytskii operator estimated under kernel EDMD, provided sufficiently small fill distance, is found to give bounded errors scaled with
|x|
. This proportionally bounded error further depends on the dimensions of the state and policy spaces as well as the kernel smoothness that fits the system’s regularity. Per discussions in §
4.2
, the estimation error is in the order of
O(m^{-\nu/d})
(assuming a smoothness index of
d_{x}/2+\nu
and
d
is the total dimension of state and policy parameters).
Practically, one may resort to auxiliary reduction, decomposition, or online learning approaches to alleviate the curse of dimensionality, if the smoothness index
s
is low.

To the end of controller synthesis, based on the RKHS concept, the paper adopts a
kernel-based optimization
formulation over the policy space. The problem is solvable in a data-driven (sample-based) manner that involves policy evaluation, using multi-step predictions based on the learned Koopman–Nemytskii operator.
At this point, it remains an open question whether it is possible to rigorously
synthesize controllers over an unrestricted nonparametric class
based on some operator model, although such a model essentially treats the nonlinearity in a black-box linearity in infinite dimensions.
For discrete-time systems, where input affinity cannot be expected, our formulation is yet restricted to a prespecified parametric policy class.
The author hypothesizes that for continuous-time input-affine systems, convex formulations for controller synthesis based on semigroup notions should exist. This is left for a separate work.
