Causal Inference Under Network Interference
Subhankar Bhadra

Abstract
We review and conceptualize recent advances in causal inference under network interference, drawing on a complex and diverse body of work that ranges from causal inference, statistical network analysis, economics, the health sciences, and the social sciences. Network interference arises in connected populations when the treatment assignments of units affect the outcomes of other units. Examples include economic, financial, and public health interventions with spillover in connected populations, reinforcement learning in connected populations, and advertising on social media. We discuss the design of experiments, targets of causal inference, interpretations and characterizations of causal effects, interference tests, and design- and model-based estimators of causal effects under network interference. We then contrast inferential frameworks based on fixed networks (finite population inference) and random networks (super population inference) and the generalizability afforded by them. We demonstrate that expected outcomes can depend on the network structure (e.g., the absence or presence of superstars and communities) and could be different if another network were observed, highlighting the need to understand how network structure affects causal conclusions. We conclude with a selection of open problems.



Keywords:

Interference Graph,
Finite Population Inference,
Super Population Inference,
Treatment Spillover,

1.1
Causal inference without interference

We do not review the literature on causal inference without interference,
which has been reviewed elsewhere
[

,

,

,

,

,

.
Having said that,
we sketch the two most popular approaches to causal inference based on Pearl
[
81
]
and Rubin
[
45
]
,
because both can be adapted to causal inference under interference:

•

Pearl’s framework facilitates causal inference when the number of variables is large and provides a graphical representation of a causal model in the form of a directed acyclic graph
[
51
]
.

•

Rubin’s framework assumes the existence of potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
,
which are the potential outcomes of unit
i
were
i
assigned to the control group (0) or the treatment group (1) in an experiment (with random assignments) or quasi-experiment (without random assignments).
The potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
can be fixed or random.
Rubin’s framework has at least three attractive properties.
First,
it does not require models for potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
,
assuming that
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
are fixed.
Second,
the difference in the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
can be attributed to the treatment of unit
i
,
provided that there is no interference.
Third,
causal inference can be framed as a missing data problem,
because the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
cannot be both observed,
so one of them is missing.

We review advances in causal inference under interference through the lens of Rubin’s framework.
Rubin’s classic framework assumes that there is no interference,
which is known as the Stable Unit Treatment Assumption (SUTVA)
[
93
]
.
In other words,
the potential outcome
Y_{i}(x_{i})
of unit
i
depends on
i
’s own treatment assignment
x_{i}
,
but it does not depend on the treatment assignments
x_{j}
of other units
j
.
To adapt Rubin’s framework to causal inference under interference,
we allow the potential outcome
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of unit
i
to be a function of the treatment assignments
\bm{x}
of all units in the population of interest.

2.1
Treatment spillover

The bulk of research on causal inference under interference considers treatment spillover.
Treatment spillover occurs when the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of units
i
are directly affected by the treatment assignments
x_{j}
of other units
j
.
In the running example,
treatment spillover occurs when teenager
i
’s friend
j
is exposed to advertisements of designer clothes and
j
shares these advertisements with
i
.

2.2
Outcome spillover

An alternative form of spillover is outcome spillover,
which can occur regardless of whether treatment spillover occurs.
Outcome spillover occurs when the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of units
i
are indirectly affected by the treatment assignments
x_{j}
of other units
j
via their potential outcomes
Y_{j}(\bm{x})
.
Consider an experiment that divides units into a control group and a treatment group,
without revealing who is assigned to which group.
In such scenarios,
unit
i
does not know whether unit
j
has been treated,
so
i
’s potential outcome
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
cannot be directly affected by
j
’s treatment assignment
x_{j}
,
but it can be indirectly affected by
j
’s treatment assignment
x_{j}
via
j
’s potential outcome
Y_{j}(\bm{x})
.
In the running example,
outcome spillover occurs when teenager
i
’s friend
j
is exposed to advertisements of designer clothes and purchases the advertised designer clothes.
While
i
may not be exposed to these advertisements and may not be aware that
j
was exposed to them,

i
may observe
j
wearing designer clothes and may like them,
so
i
may purchase them as well.

A related distinction between direct interference (treatment spillover) and interference by contagion (outcome spillover) was made by
Ogburn and VanderWeele 2014
,
focusing on repeated observations of outcomes.
We provide a brief review of the literature on repeated observations of outcomes in Section
8.5
,
but we focus here on observations at a single time point and capture outcome spillover via dependence among outcomes,
assuming that the outcomes constitute a single observation from the stationary distribution of a time-indexed stochastic process
[
53
,
74
]
.

2.3
Interference graphs

The channels that enable spillover can be represented by an interference graph.
A convenient representation of an interference graph is a
N\times N
-matrix
\bm{Z}\in\{0,\,1\}^{N\times N}
with elements
Z_{i,j}
,
where
Z_{i,j}=1
indicates that
j
can affect the potential outcomes of
i
and
Z_{i,j}=0
otherwise.
We consider undirected connections and exclude self-connections,
so that
Z_{i,i}\coloneqq 0
and
Z_{i,j}=Z_{j,i}
.
In a mild abuse of language,
we refer to
\bm{Z}
as the interference graph.
The interference graph can be fixed or random.
We discuss implications of fixed and random interference graphs in Section
7
.
In principle,
the interference graph can have weighted connections,
where the weights quantify the number of interactions or the strength of the connections among units.
In the running example,

Z_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
could be an indicator that teenagers
i
and
j
are friends,
while
Z_{i,j}\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
could be a count of the number of interactions of
i
and
j
during a specified time period,
and
Z_{i,j}=\exp(-d_{i,j})\in(0,\,1]
could quantify the strength of the relationship between
i
and
j
as a function of (spatial) distance
d_{i,j}\in[0,\,\infty)
between them.
Having said that,
we consider
\bm{Z}\in\mathscr{Z}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
throughout the manuscript,
with the exception of Section
8.6
.

2.4
Exposure mappings

A related approach to specifying interference is based on exposure mappings
[
3
]
,
which are popular in the literature on fixed potential outcomes and design-based estimators.
Exposure mappings are functions
f_{i}:\mathscr{X}\mapsto\mathscr{F}
from the set of all possible treatment assignments
\mathscr{X}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{N}
of size
2^{N}
to a low-dimensional set
\mathscr{F}
.
It is then assumed that the potential outcome
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of unit
i
depends on the treatment assignments
\bm{x}
only through its exposure mapping
f_{i}
,
which can be stated as follows:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\text{If }f_{i}(\bm{x})=f_{i}(\bm{x}^{\prime}),\text{{ }then }Y_{i}(\bm{x})=Y_{i}(\bm{x}^{\prime}).\end{array}

For example,
the potential outcome
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of unit
i
may depend on
i
’s treatment assignment
x_{i}
,
the number of
i
’s treated neighbors
\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}
,
and the number of
i
’s neighbors
\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}

[
114
,
109
,
75
,
46
,
59
,
62
, e.g.,]
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}f_{i}(\bm{x})&=&(x_{i},\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j},\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}).\end{array}

An example is a model capturing treatment spillover:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Y_{i}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq&\alpha+\beta\,x_{i}+\gamma\,\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}},\end{array}

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
The above model assumes that the potential outcome
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
is fixed and is a function of
i
’s treatment assignment
x_{i}
and
i
’s proportion of treated neighbors
\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}\,/\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}
.

3
Targets of causal inference

The primary target of causal inference is the causal effect of treatments on outcomes.

In the absence of interference,
the difference in the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
can be attributed to the causal effect of the treatment on the outcome of unit
i
,
assuming that the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(0)
and
Y_{i}(1)
are fixed.
In accordance,
the average treatment effect is defined by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{N}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)).\end{array}

In the presence of interference,
at least three causal effects can be distinguished:

•

the direct causal effect of treatments on outcomes;

•

the indirect causal effect of treatments on outcomes,
arising from treatment spillover or outcome spillover or both;

•

the total causal effect of treatments on outcomes.

There are no universal definitions of causal effects under interference.
We review selected causal effects in Section
3.1
(with fixed potential outcomes) and Section
3.2
(with random potential outcomes).
We then interpret and characterize causal effects in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.
The characterization of causal effects provides insight into causal effects by disentangling the contributions of treatment,
treatment spillover,
and outcome spillover.

3.1
Causal effects: fixed potential outcomes

We first introduce causal effects in scenarios with interference and fixed potential outcomes.

One of the simplest causal effects is the all-or-nothing causal effect
[
114
]
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{ALL,N}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}(\bm{1}_{N})-Y_{i}(\bm{0}_{N})).\end{array}

The all-or-nothing causal effect compares potential outcomes in two extreme scenarios:

•

the scenario in which no unit is treated (
\bm{0}_{N}
);

•

the scenario in which all units are treated (
\bm{1}_{N}
).

The all-or-nothing causal effect is useful in applications involving governmental interventions (including economic, financial, and public health interventions):
e.g.,
a government may be interested in assigning the treatment to all eligible population members and may therefore wish to compare
(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\bm{1}_{N})
to
(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\bm{0}_{N})
.

By contrast, in the running example, advertisers may be interested in intermediate treatment regimes in which some but not all teenagers are targeted with advertisements of designer clothes:
Targeting all teenagers may not be cost-effective,
because teenagers may purchase designer clothes without being exposed to advertisements,
by virtue of spillover.
To introduce causal effects in intermediate scenarios in which some but not all units are treated,
define

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}D_{j}(\bm{x}_{-i})&\coloneqq&Y_{j}(x_{i}=1,\,\bm{x}_{-i})-Y_{j}(x_{i}=0,\,\bm{x}_{-i}),\end{array}

where
j
can be equal to
i
.
The total causal effect can then be defined by
[
42
]
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}\,D_{j}(\bm{X}_{-i}).\end{array}

In other words,
the causal effect of
i
’s treatment assignment
x_{i}
on
j
’s potential outcome
Y_{j}(x_{i},\,\bm{x}_{-i})
is quantified by
Y_{j}(x_{i}=1,\,\bm{x}_{-i})-Y_{j}(x_{i}=0,\,\bm{x}_{-i})
,
averaging over all possible treatment assignments
\bm{x}_{-i}
of all units excluding
i
.
The total causal effect can be decomposed into a direct and indirect causal effect:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}&\coloneqq&\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X}}+\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X}},\end{array}

where the direct causal effect is defined by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}\,D_{i}(\bm{X}_{-i}),\end{array}

whereas the indirect causal effect is defined by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\,\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\neq i}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}\,D_{j}(\bm{X}_{-i}).\end{array}

The direct causal effect quantifies the direct effect of treatments on outcomes,
whereas the indirect causal effect quantifies the indirect effect of treatments on the outcomes of other units.
The indirect causal effect can arise from treatment spillover or outcome spillover or both,
as we demonstrate in Section
3.4
.

An alternative is to define causal effects using exposure mappings,
comparing exposure to levels
d_{1}
and
d_{2}

[
3
]
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{N}(d_{1},\,d_{2})\;\coloneqq\;\mu_{N}(d_{1})-\mu_{N}(d_{2}),\end{array}

where

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\mu_{N}(d)\;\coloneqq\;\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(d),&d\in\mathscr{F}.\end{array}

Here,

Y_{i}(d)
is the potential outcome when unit
i
is exposed to level
d\in\mathscr{F}
.
Direct and indirect causal effects can be defined via exposure mappings along the lines of
Forastiere et al. 2021
.

3.2
Causal effects: random potential outcomes

If there is interference and the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
are random while the interference graph
\bm{Z}
is fixed,
one can define direct and indirect causal effects by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}\,D_{i}(\bm{X}_{-i})\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\,\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\neq i}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}\,D_{j}(\bm{X}_{-i}),\end{array}

which average over treatment assignments and potential outcomes.
The total causal effect is then defined by
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}\coloneqq\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}+\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
.

If the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and the interference graph
\bm{Z}
are both random,
one can define direct and indirect causal effects by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}\,D_{i}(\bm{X}_{-i})\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\,\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\neq i}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}\,D_{j}(\bm{X}_{-i}),\end{array}

which average over treatment assignments, interference graphs, and potential outcomes.
The total causal effect is then
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}\coloneqq\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}+\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
.

If the large-
N
limits of these causal effects exist,
we denote them by

\tau_{D}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
,

\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
,

\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
when
\bm{Z}
is non-random
and

\tau_{D}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
,

\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
,

\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
when
\bm{Z}
is random.

3.3
Interpreting causal effects

An insightful interpretation of causal effects is offered by
Hu et al. 2022
.
The following result is based on Theorem 1 of
Hu et al. 2022
.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
X_{i}\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny iid}}\mbox{Bernoulli}(\pi_{i})
(
\pi_{i}\in(0,1)
),
then the total causal effect
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
can be expressed as

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}&=&\dfrac{1}{N}\,\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\,\pi_{i}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}\,Y_{i}&+&\dfrac{1}{N}\,\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\,\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\neq i}^{N}\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\,\pi_{j}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}\,Y_{i},\end{array}

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}
is the expectation with respect to the Bernoulli product measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{X}}
with support
\mathscr{X}\coloneqq\{0,1\}^{N}
.

Therefore,
the total causal effect
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
quantifies how sensitive expected outcomes are to changes in

•

the treatment assignment probability
\pi_{i}
of unit
i
(direct causal effect),

•

the treatment assignment probabilities
\pi_{j}
of other units
j
(indirect causal effect),

averaged over all units.

While Theorem 1 in
Hu et al. 2022
focuses on the total causal effect
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
based on fixed potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and interference graphs
\bm{Z}
,
it can be extended to the total causal effects
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
and
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
based on random potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and interference graphs
\bm{Z}
under mild technical conditions.

3.4
Characterizing causal effects

To provide insight into causal effects and disentangle the contributions of treatment,
treatment spillover,
and outcome spillover,
we characterize causal effects.

To capture treatment spillover along with outcome spillover,
we use a Gaussian Markov random field,
which is a natural generalization of linear models for dependent outcomes
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
;
note that outcome spillover implies that the outcomes of units affect the outcomes of other units,
so outcomes are dependent when there is outcome spillover (regardless of whether there is treatment spillover).
A Gaussian Markov random field capturing both treatment spillover and outcome spillover
[
11
]
implies that the conditional distributions of outcomes
Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{-i},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{z})
are Gaussian with means

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{-i},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{z})]&\coloneqq&\beta\,x_{i}+\gamma\,c_{N,i,1}(\bm{z})\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}+\delta\,c_{N,i,2}(\bm{z})\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}\,z_{i,j}.\end{array}

It is worth noting that Markov random fields can be specified by specifying the joint probability density function of all outcomes
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
or by specifying the conditional probability density function of each outcome
Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{-i},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{z})
,
as explained in the seminal work of
Besag 1974
.
The quantities
c_{N,i,1}(\bm{z})
and
c_{N,i,2}(\bm{z})
are scaling constants that may depend on the number of units
N
,
unit
i
,
and the interference graph
\bm{z}
(e.g.,
c_{N,i,1}(\bm{z})=c_{N,i,2}(\bm{z})=1/\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}
),
while
\beta
,

\gamma
,
and
\delta
are parameters independent of
N
:

•

\beta\in\mathbb{R}
captures the effect of the treatment assignment
x_{i}
of unit
i
on the outcome
Y_{i}
of unit
i
;

•

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
quantifies the effect of the treatment assignments
x_{j}
of other units
j
connected to
i
(
z_{i,j}=1
) on the outcome
Y_{i}
of unit
i
(treatment spillover);

•

\delta\in\mathbb{R}
quantifies the effect of the outcomes
y_{j}
of other units
j
connected to
i
(
z_{i,j}=1
) on the outcome
Y_{i}
of unit
i
(outcome spillover).

Gaussian Markov random fields capture the causal effect of the treatment assignments
X_{i}
on the outcomes
Y_{i}
of units
i
and the outcomes
Y_{j}
of other units
j
who are either directly or indirectly connected to
i
(see Lemma
7.2.1
in Section
7.2.1
),
in addition to capturing correlations among outcomes arising from outcome spillover (
\delta\neq 0
);
note that correlations among outcomes do not admit causal interpretations,
which would require more data
[
115
,
24
,
71
, e.g., repeated observations of outcomes:]
.

To disentangle the contributions of treatment
\beta
,
treatment spillover
\gamma
,
and outcome spillover
\delta
to the causal effects
\tau_{D}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
,

\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
,
and
\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
,
we assume that the treatment assignments
\bm{X}
are generated by a Bernoulli randomization design:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}X_{i}&\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny iid}}&\text{Bernoulli}(\pi),&\pi\in(0,\,1).\end{array}

The interference graph
\bm{Z}
is generated by a random dot product graph model
[
110
,
5
]
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Z_{i,j}&\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny ind}}&\text{Bernoulli}(P_{i,j}),&P_{i,j}&\coloneqq&\rho_{N}\,\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}_{j}.\end{array}

The parameter
\rho_{N}\in[0,\,1]
is a sparsity parameter while
\bm{\alpha}\coloneqq(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
is a matrix of rank
K\geq 1
satisfying
\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}_{j}\in[0,\,1]
.
The sparsity parameter
\rho_{N}
may depend on
N
and determines the sparsity of the interference graph
\bm{Z}
,
i.e.,
the expected numbers of neighbors
\mathbb{E}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{i,j}
.
The vectors
\bm{\alpha}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
capture unobserved heterogeneity in the propensities of units
i
and
j
to be connected.
Random dot product graphs include stochastic block models
[
32
]
,
degree-corrected block models
[
127
]
,
and mixed membership models as special cases
[
1
]
,
which are popular in statistical network analysis
[
50
]
and help characterize causal effects.

Define

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}a(\delta)&\coloneqq&\delta\lim\limits_{N\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\;a_{N}(\delta),\end{array}

where

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}a_{N}(\delta)&\coloneqq&(\bm{1}_{N}^{\top}\,\bm{\alpha})\left(\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{2}^{2}\,\bm{I}_{K}-\delta\;\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}\right)^{-1}(\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{1}_{N})\end{array}

and
\left|\!\left|\!\left|\bm{\alpha}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{2}
is the spectral norm of
\bm{\alpha}
.

The following result is based on Theorem 1 of
Bhadra and Schweinberger 2025
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that the interference graph
\bm{Z}
is generated by a random dot product graph of rank
K\geq 1
satisfying sparsity and eigenvalue conditions
[
11
, see Theorem 1 in ]
.
Then:

(a) The direct causal effect is
\tau_{D}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}=\beta.

(b) The indirect causal effect is

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}&=&\gamma+(\beta+\gamma)\;a(\delta).\end{array}

(c) The total causal effect is

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}&=&(\beta+\gamma)+(\beta+\gamma)\,a(\delta).\end{array}

The characterization of causal effects as explicit mathematical functions of
\beta
,
\gamma
, and
\delta
helps disentangle the contributions of treatment
\beta
,
treatment spillover
\gamma
,
and outcome spillover
\delta
(dropping the superscripts of
\tau_{D}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
,
\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
, and
\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
to ease the presentation):

•

The total causal effect can vanish (
\tau_{T}=0
) despite the fact that the direct causal effect does not vanish (
\tau_{D}\neq 0
),
which occurs when the effect of treatment spillover cancels the effect of treatment:

\gamma=-\beta
.

•

A non-vanishing total causal effect (
\tau_{T}\neq 0
) can arise in the absence of a direct causal effect (
\tau_{D}=0
),
as long as there is treatment spillover (
\gamma\neq 0
).

•

The indirect causal effect can vanish (
\tau_{I}=0
) despite outcome spillover (
\delta\neq 0
),
which occurs when

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\gamma&=&-\beta\,\dfrac{a(\delta)}{1+a(\delta)},\end{array}

where
a(\delta)\neq-1

[
11
]
.

•

An indirect causal effect (
\tau_{I}\neq 0
) can arise even when the direct causal effect vanishes (
\tau_{D}=0
),
as long as there is treatment spillover (
\gamma\neq 0
).

•

If there is treatment spillover (
\gamma\neq 0
) without outcome spillover (
\delta=0
),
the indirect causal effect reduces to
\tau_{I}=\gamma
.

•

If there is outcome spillover (
\delta\neq 0
) in addition to treatment spillover (
\gamma\neq 0
),
the value added by outcome spillover to the indirect and total causal effects
\tau_{I}
and
\tau_{T}
is
(\beta+\gamma)\,a(\delta)
.

•

The effect of outcome spillover
\delta
on the indirect causal effect
\tau_{I}
and the total causal effect
\tau_{T}
is magnified by
\beta+\gamma
,
underscoring that the effects of treatment
\beta
,
treatment spillover
\gamma
,
and outcome spillover
\delta
are intertwined.

4
Design of experiments

The design of experiments is concerned with the treatment assignment mechanism.
Two of the most common treatment assignment mechanisms are:

•

the Bernoulli randomization design,
which assigns units independently to the treatment group with known assignment probabilities
\pi_{i}
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}X_{i}&\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny ind}}&\text{Bernoulli}(\pi_{i}),&\pi_{i}\in(0,\,1);\end{array}

•

the complete randomization design,
which samples
n\leq N
units at random and assigns them to the treatment group.

In some applications,
it is desirable to assign treatments with an eye to achieving specific objectives,
such as reducing the variance of estimators or maximizing an objective function of interest.
We discuss them in turn.

4.1
Goal: reducing variance of estimators

Design-based estimators of causal effects (e.g., the Horvitz-Thompson estimators described in Section
6.2
) can have large variance under the Bernoulli and complete randomization designs in the presence of interference.
Attempts have been made to reduce the variance of design-based estimators by developing alternative approaches to assigning treatments.
For example,

Ugander et al. 2013
proposed a cluster randomization scheme,
where units are assigned to clusters and treatments are assigned at the cluster level instead of the unit level.
Under such designs,
the neighborhood structure of units remains similar for different possible treatment assignments,
reducing the variance of outcomes

[
24
,
61
]
.
Other examples include a randomization design based on quasi-coloring by
Jagadeesan et al. 2020
and a two-wave experimental design by
Viviano 2020
.

Sussman and Airoldi 2017
focused on linear design-based estimators and proposed optimality criterion to achieve minimum variance.

4.2
Goal: maximizing expected outcomes

An interesting question is how to assign treatments to units with a view to maximizing an objective function of interest.
Two examples are maximizing expected welfare in economics and maximizing expected rewards in reinforcement learning in connected populations.

The welfare derived from treatment assignments
\bm{x}
can be defined as

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\mu_{N}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{Y}}\,Y_{i}(\bm{x}),\end{array}

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{Y}}\,Y_{i}(\bm{x})
is the expected potential outcome
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of unit
i
when the treatments assignments are
\bm{x}
.
One can then find the optimal treatment assignments
\bm{x}^{\star}
,
which may depend on attributes of units:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\bm{x}^{\star}&\in&\argmax\limits_{\bm{x}\in\mathscr{X}}\,\mu_{N}(\bm{x}).\end{array}

In practice,
one can replace
\mu_{N}(\bm{x})
by a well-chosen estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{N}(\bm{x})
.
Details can be found in
Viviano 2025
.

A related goal is to maximize expected rewards in reinforcement learning under interference,
studied by
Xu et al. 2024
and
Gleich et al. 2025
.
In reinforcement learning under interference,
interacting agents make decisions and receive rewards.
The goal is to find optimal actions,
which may depend on attributes of agents,
and maximize the average expected reward.

5
Detecting interference

Causal inference under interference comes at a cost,
which raises the question:
Is there interference at all?

There is a growing body of work on tests for interference based on conditional Fisher randomization tests,
starting with
Aronow 2012
.
This line of research views potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
as fixed,
so the treatment assignment mechanism is the sole source of randomness.
If potential outcomes were random,
one could apply randomization tests conditional on potential outcomes,
and the tests would remain valid under the assumption of ignorability described in Section
6.1
.

Consider testing the null hypothesis that the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of units
i
are unaffected by the treatment assignments
x_{j}
of other units
j
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}H_{0}^{A}:\ Y_{i}(x_{i},\,\bm{x}_{-i})=Y_{i}(x_{i}){}\text{{ } for all }i,\,x_{i},\,\bm{x}_{-i}\end{array}

against

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}H_{1}^{A}:\ Y_{i}(x_{i},\,\bm{x}_{-i})\neq Y_{i}(x_{i}){}\text{{ } for some }i,\,x_{i},\,\bm{x}_{-i},\end{array}

recalling that the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of units
i
are fixed.
The hypothesis
H_{0}^{A}
is not sharp,
so regular randomization tests are not useful.

Aronow 2012
proposes conditional randomization tests,
which are constructed by conditioning on the treatment assignments of a subset of focal units
\mathscr{U}
.
Let
\mathscr{V}
be the set of all possible treatment assignments conditional on
\bm{X}_{\mathscr{U}}
and
T(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
be a suitable test statistic capturing the dependence between the outcomes
\bm{Y}_{\mathscr{U}}
in the focal units and treatment assignments
\bm{X}_{\mathscr{U}^{c}}
of non-focal units.
Assuming that all treatment assignments in
\mathscr{V}
are equally likely (e.g., in a completely randomized design),
one can compute the
p
-value

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}p(\bm{X}^{obs})&=&\dfrac{|\{\bm{X}\in\mathscr{V}:\,T(\bm{X},\bm{Y}^{obs})>T(\bm{X}^{obs},\bm{Y}^{obs})\}|}{|\mathscr{V}|},\end{array}

where
\bm{X}^{obs}\in\mathscr{X}
and
\bm{Y}^{obs}\in\mathscr{Y}
are the
N
-vectors of observed treatment assignments and outcomes,
respectively.

Two questions arise:
First,
which test statistic should be chosen?
Second,
how should the focal units be selected?

With regard to the test statistic,

Aronow 2012
suggests using the rank-correlation between
\bm{Y}_{\mathscr{U}}
and
\bm{D}_{\mathscr{U}}
,
where
\bm{D}_{\mathscr{U}}
are distances of units in
\mathscr{U}
to the corresponding nearest treated unit in
\mathscr{U}^{c}
.

Athey et al. 2018
propose the test statistic

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}T_{U}(\bm{X},\bm{Y}^{obs})\coloneqq U(\bm{X},\bm{Y}^{obs})-U(\bm{1}_{N}-\bm{X},\bm{Y}^{obs}),\end{array}

where

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}U(\bm{X},\,\bm{Y}^{obs})\;\coloneq\;\dfrac{\sum_{i\in\mathscr{U}}\sum_{j\in\mathscr{U}^{c}}X_{j}\,Y_{i}^{obs}\,Z_{i,j}}{\sum_{i\in\mathscr{U}}\sum_{j\in\mathscr{U}^{c}}X_{j}\,Z_{i,j}}.\end{array}

Selecting focal units is non-trivial.
In the simplest case,
the design of the experiment may suggest a natural choice of focal units:
e.g.,
if the study involves households and interference can only occur within households,
a natural choice of focal units would be to select one member from each household at random
[
4
]
.
In other cases,
focal units can be selected at random.

If there is reason to believe that there is interference,
one may be interested in testing for specific forms of interference.
For example,
one may be interested in testing whether the potential outcomes of a unit only depend on the treatment assignment
x_{i}
of unit
i
and the treatment assignments
\bm{x}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}
of
i
’s neighbors

\mathcal{N}_{i}\coloneqq\{j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{i\}:\;Z_{i,j}=1\}
:

\displaystyle H_{0}^{B}\ :\ Y_{i}(\bm{x})=Y_{i}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\;\;\text{for all }i,\,\bm{x},\,\bm{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \text{such that }(x_{i},\,\bm{x}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}})=(x^{\prime}_{i},\,\bm{x}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}).

The idea of
Aronow 2012
was advanced in the works of
Athey et al. 2018
,
Basse et al. 2019
and
Puelz et al. 2021
,
among others.

Basse et al. 2019
laid the foundations for a general testing procedure as follows.
Consider any null hypothesis
involving interference (e.g.,

H_{0}^{A}
and
H_{0}^{B}
).
Testing such a hypothesis via randomization tests is possible by carefully choosing a conditioning event
\mathscr{E}=(\mathscr{U},\mathscr{V})
,
where
\mathscr{U}
is a subset of focal units and
\mathscr{V}
is a subset of all possible treatment assignments,
such that the null hypothesis is sharp when restricted to the conditioning event
\mathscr{E}
.
Suppose that
\mathscr{E}
is sampled from a carefully constructed probability law
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{E}\mid\bm{X}^{obs}}
.
Then we have the following result,
based on Theorem 1 of
Basse et al. 2019
.

Theorem 5.1
.
Consider null hypothesis
H_{0}^{A}
or
H_{0}^{B}
and a conditioning event
\mathscr{E}=(\mathscr{U},\mathscr{V})
.
Let
T(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
be a test statistic that depends on
\bm{Y}
only via
\bm{Y}_{\mathscr{U}}
,
and assume that under the null hypothesis,
\bm{Y}_{\mathscr{U}}(\bm{x})=\bm{Y}_{\mathscr{U}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
holds for all
\bm{x}\in\mathscr{X}
and all
\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{X}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{E}\mid\,\bm{x}}>0
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{E}\mid\,\bm{x}^{\prime}}>0
.
Draw
\mathscr{E}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{E}\mid\bm{X}^{obs}}
and compute the
p
-value

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}p(\bm{X}^{obs})&\coloneqq&\mathbb{E}_{\bm{X}\mid\mathscr{E}}[\mathbb{I}(T(\bm{X},\bm{Y}^{obs})>T(\bm{X}^{obs},\bm{Y}^{obs}))].\end{array}

Then
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{X}\mid\mathscr{E}}(p(\bm{X}^{obs})\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
for all
\alpha\in[0,\,1]
.

In the works of
Aronow 2012
and
Athey et al. 2018
,
the conditioning event
\mathscr{E}
is chosen independently of treatment assignments
\bm{X}
.
That said,

Basse et al. 2019
demonstrate that sampling
\mathscr{E}
dependent on
\bm{X}^{obs}
can increase the power of tests in certain scenarios.
Following the foundational work by
Basse et al. 2019
,
Puelz et al. 2021
propose a general method to construct the conditioning event
\mathscr{E}
using a graph-theoretic approach,
and suggest guidelines on how to construct tests with high power.
We refer the interested reader to
Puelz et al. 2021
for details.

6
Estimating causal effects

If there is interference,
the direct and indirect causal effects arising from treatment and outcome spillover can be estimated by design- or model-based estimators.
We first discuss common assumptions of estimators in Section
6.1
,
review design- and model-based estimators in Sections
6.2
and
6.3
,
and compare them in Section
6.4
.

6.1
Assumptions

To estimate causal effects from data,
assumptions are required.
We sketch common assumptions on treatment assignments
\bm{X}
,
outcomes
\bm{Y}
,
and interference graph
\bm{Z}
.

6.1.1
Treatment assignments

To enable causal inference,
one needs to identify the causal estimands of interest via the probability law of the observed data.
To do so,
the bulk of the literature assumes that the treatment assignment mechanism either satisfies ignorability:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\bm{X}&\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}&\{\bm{Y}(\bm{x}):\;\bm{x}\in\mathscr{X}\},\;\bm{Z},\end{array}

or conditional ignorability given confounders
\bm{C}
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\bm{X}&\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}&\{\bm{Y}(\bm{x}):\;\bm{x}\in\mathscr{X}\},\;\bm{Z}\mid\bm{C}.\end{array}

In other words,
the treatment assignments
\bm{X}
are independent of the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
under all possible treatment assignments
\bm{x}\in\mathscr{X}
and connections
\bm{Z}
(conditional on confounders
\bm{C}
).
Ignorability can be enforced in experimental settings,
while conditional ignorability may hold in observational settings.
In the running example,
the exposure of teenagers to advertisements on social media is ignorable as long as the exposure is not affected by

•

the purchasing decisions
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
that teenagers would make given exposure
\bm{x}
to advertisements;

•

the network of friendships
\bm{Z}
among the teenagers.

Conditional ignorability is satisfied when the exposure of teenagers to advertisements depends on confounders (e.g., purchasing power and time spent on social media),
but is not affected by the purchasing decisions
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and the friendship network
\bm{Z}
given the confounders.

6.1.2
Interference graph

The bulk of the literature considers the interference graph
\bm{Z}
to be fixed rather than random,
with limited exceptions
[
62
,
20
,
12
,
11
, e.g.,]
.
At first glance,
the distinction between fixed and random interference graphs may appear to be inconsequential,
but we explain in Section
7
that it has important implications.

Whether the interference graph
\bm{Z}
is fixed or random,
most of the literature imposes restrictions on
\bm{Z}
.
The most common restriction concerns the number of neighbors
\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{i,j}
:
e.g.,
it is often assumed that the (expected) number of neighbors is bounded.
The bounded neighborhood assumption can be relaxed,
at the cost of other assumptions (e.g., mean-field conditions, see Section
6.1.3
).

6.1.3
Outcomes

To obtain theoretical guarantees,
one needs to control the dependence among outcomes,
using local dependence assumptions or mean-field conditions.

•

Local dependence conditions

leverage additional structure to determine which units are close,
and then constrain spillover to units who are close.
There are at least two approaches to imposing local dependence,
based on independence graphs
[
3
]
and conditional independence graphs
[
112
]
.
The absence of a connection in an independence graph implies independence of outcomes,
whereas the absence of a connection in a conditional independence graph implies conditional independence of outcomes given other outcomes
[
21
,
51
]
.

•

Mean-field conditions

constrain the “budget for interaction“ available to units:
e.g.,
the “budget for interaction” available to unit
i
can be constrained by assuming that the scaling constants
c_{N,i,1}(\bm{z})
and
c_{N,i,2}(\bm{z})
in Equation (
3.4
) are inverse functions of the number of neighbors,
so that
c_{N,i,1}(\bm{z})=c_{N,i,2}(\bm{z})=1/\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}

[
12
,
11
]
.

6.2
Design-based estimators

Design-based estimators take advantage of the sampling design,
without making assumptions about the model generating outcomes and interference graphs.

An example is Horvitz-Thompson estimators,
sometimes called Inverse Propensity Weighted estimators.
Without interference,
the Horvitz-Thompson estimator of the average treatment effect
\tau_{N}
is

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widehat{\tau}_{N}^{HT}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left(\dfrac{Y_{i}\;\mathbb{I}(X_{i}=1)}{\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)}-\dfrac{Y_{i}\;\mathbb{I}(X_{i}=0)}{\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=0)}\right).\end{array}

If there is interference,
the Horvitz-Thompson estimator is an unbiased estimator of the direct causal effects
\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
,
\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
, and
\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
as long as the treatment assignment mechanism is ignorable
[
98
,
62
]
.

Horvitz-Thompson-type estimators can be constructed using exposure mappings
[
3
]
.
For example,
the average potential outcome at exposure level
d
,
defined by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\mu_{N}(d)\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(d),\end{array}

can be estimated by the Horvitz-Thompson estimator

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widehat{\mu}_{N}^{HT}(d)&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{Y_{i}\,\mathbb{I}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)}{\mathbb{P}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)}.\end{array}

In experimental settings,
the exposure probabilities

\mathbb{P}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)
are known and the Horvitz-Thompson estimator is unbiased under ignorability.
In observational settings with confounders
\bm{C}
,
the exposure probabilities
\mathbb{P}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)
can be replaced by propensity scores
[
26
]
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\psi(d;\,c)&\coloneqq&\mathbb{P}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d\mid C_{i}=c).\end{array}

If the number of exposure levels is finite,
one can estimate propensity scores using multinomial regression.
Then
\mu_{N}(d)
can be estimated by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widehat{\mu}_{N}^{HT}(d)&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{Y_{i}\;\mathbb{I}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)}{\widehat{\psi}(d,\,C_{i})},\end{array}

where
\widehat{\psi}(d;\,C_{i})
is an estimator of
\psi(d;\,C_{i})
.

A doubly-robust estimator can likewise be constructed
[
67
,
117
]
.
Let
m(d;\,c)
be the true conditional mean of
Y_{i}
at exposure level
d
and confounder level
c
:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}m(d;\,c)&\coloneqq&\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{Y}}[Y_{i}\mid f_{i}(\bm{X})=d,\,C_{i}=c].\end{array}

A doubly robust estimator of
\mu_{N}(d)
is given by

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widehat{\mu}_{N}^{DR}(d)&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left[\dfrac{(Y_{i}-\widehat{m}(d;\,C_{i}))\,\mathbb{I}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)}{\widehat{\psi}(d;\,C_{i})}+\widehat{m}(d;\,C_{i})\right],\end{array}

where
\widehat{m}(d;\,C_{i})
is an estimator of
m(d;\,C_{i})
,
which can be obtained via regression of outcomes
Y_{i}
on exposures
f_{i}(\bm{X})
and confounders
C_{i}
.
The double robustness stems from the fact that if either
\widehat{\psi}(d;\,C_{i})
or
\widehat{m}(d;\,C_{i})
is consistent,
then so is
\widehat{\mu}_{N}^{DR}(d)

[
117
]
.

A relative of the Horvitz-Thompson estimator is the Hájek estimator,
which replaces the exposure probabilities
\mathbb{P}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)
by moment estimators and can be useful when the exposure probabilities are unknown or hard to estimate.
The Hájek estimator of
\mu_{N}(d)
is

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widehat{\mu}_{N}^{HAJ}(d)&\coloneqq&\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}\;\mathbb{I}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(f_{i}(\bm{X})=d)}.\end{array}

Sävje et al. 2021
and
Li and Wager 2022
compare Horvitz-Thompson and Hájek estimators.

The described design-based estimators of mean potential outcomes
\mu_{N}(d)
help estimate direct and indirect causal effects,
because both can be viewed as differences in mean potential outcomes
\mu_{N}(d)
.

6.3
Model-based estimators

More often than not,
design-based estimators have been used in scenarios in which the potential outcomes are fixed,
with limited exceptions
[
59
,
62
, e.g.,]
.
If the potential outcomes are random and generated by a model,
it is natural to use model-based estimators.
There are many models and hence many estimators.
We do not discuss them here,
but common estimators include maximum likelihood estimators
[
11
]
,
Bayesian estimators
[
27
]
,
maximum pseudo-likelihood estimators

[
112
,
12
]
,
and targeted maximum likelihood estimators
[
75
]
.

6.4
Design- versus model-based estimators

We compare design- and model-based estimators in terms of assumptions,
properties,
and the insight these estimators provide into the data-generating process.

6.4.1
Assumptions

Design-based estimators do not require assumptions about the model generating potential outcomes and interference graphs.
As a result,
design-based estimators are not prone to model misspecification.
By contrast,
model-based estimators are sensitive to model misspecification,
which can result in bias.

6.4.2
Properties of estimators

We contrast design- and model-based estimators in terms of statistical properties.

First,
design-based estimators are unbiased in most settings,
although there exist scenarios in which design-based estimators are biased
[
8
, e.g.,]
.
Second,
design-based estimators can have large variance under interference
[
114
,
62
, e.g.,]
,
and reducing the variance of design-based estimators is an active area of research,
as we discuss in Section
4.1
.
Third,
Horvitz-Thompson estimators of
\mu_{N}(d)

require a large enough number of observations at exposure level
d
to be consistent
[
27
,
59
]
.
If the number of exposure levels is large,
it may not be possible to have enough observations at all exposure levels.
To address these sample size issues,

Forastiere et al. 2021
suggested sub-classification techniques.

Model-based estimators tend to be consistent and enjoy higher rates of convergence under weaker conditions on the interference graph than design-based estimators,
provided that the model specification is correct.
For example,
model-based estimators allow the number of neighbors to increase as a function of
N
:
Using mean-field conditions described in Section
6.1.3
,

Bhattacharya and Sen 2025
and
Bhadra and Schweinberger 2025
obtain consistency results when the number of neighbors is as large as
N-1
,
the maximum number of neighbors.
The nonparametric estimator proposed by
Ogburn et al. 2024
is likewise shown to be consistent with unbounded neighborhood sizes,
provided that the neighborhoods are of size
o(\sqrt{N})
.
In a nutshell,
when it is known that the data is generated by a specified class of models,
there is additional information about the nature of dependencies among outcomes,
enabling theoretical guarantees under weaker conditional independence assumptions.

6.4.3
Insight into data-generating process

Model-based estimators have an additional advantage over design-based estimators,
by providing insight into the process generating outcomes.
For example,
model-based estimators can disentangle the contributions of treatment,
treatment spillover,
and outcome spillover to the indirect and total causal effects
\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X,Y,Z}}
and
\tau_{I}^{\mathscr{X,Y,Z}}
by taking advantage of Theorem
3.4
in Section
3.4
and estimating the effects
\beta
,

\gamma
,
and
\delta
of treatment,
treatment spillover,
and outcome spillover under Gaussian Markov random fields.

6.5
Unknown interference

It is possible that the interference graph
\bm{Z}
is not known.
In such scenarios,

Choi 2017
proposed confidence intervals to bound the average baseline effect
(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\bm{0}_{N})
from above,
which bounds the attributable average treatment effect
[
90
]

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}-Y_{i}(\bm{0}_{N}))\end{array}

from below.
The proposed method relies on a monotone treatment effect assumption (
Y_{i}(\bm{x})\geq Y_{i}(\bm{0}_{N})
for all
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
) and uses optimization to compute estimates.
That said,
these estimates can be conservative.

Yu et al. 2022
address the problem of unobserved network effects under a linear model of the form

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Y_{i}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq&\alpha+\beta\,x_{i}+\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{i,j}\,x_{j},\end{array}

where the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
are fixed and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
\gamma_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Yu et al. 2022
studied properties of linear estimators to estimate the all-or-nothing effect
\tau_{ALL,N}
and proposed linear unbiased estimators,
provided that information about the average baseline effect
(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\bm{0}_{N})
is available.

Sävje et al. 2021
established rates of convergence for Horvitz-Thompson and Hájek estimators for the direct effect
\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
under restrictions on interference,
assuming that the interference graph
\bm{Z}
is unknown.

Egami 2021
studied
causal inference in settings where interference can occur through multiple networks, and one or more of these networks are unobserved.

6.6
Exposure misspecification

While exposure mappings help construct estimators and draw causal conclusions,
an important question is how sensitive causal conclusions are to misspecifications of exposure mappings.

Leung 2022a
and
Sävje 2024
study the properties of the Horvitz-Thompson estimator of the causal effect
\tau_{N}(d,\,d^{\prime})\coloneqq\mu_{N}(d)-\mu_{N}(d^{\prime})
under exposure misspecification.

Under misspecified exposures,

\mu_{N}(d)
is not well-defined,
because the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
can no longer be represented as
Y_{i}(d)
.
Instead,
one can consider the following modified version:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widetilde{\tau}_{N}(d,\,d^{\prime})&\coloneqq&\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(d)-\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(d^{\prime}),\end{array}

where

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(d)&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}[Y_{i}\mid f_{i}(\bm{X})=d].\end{array}

Note that
\widetilde{\mu}_{N}(d)
reduces to
\mu_{N}(d)
under the correct specification of exposure mappings.

Leung 2022a
assumed that the misspecified exposure mappings
f_{i}(\bm{X})
depend on
\bm{X}
only via the
K
-neighborhoods of units in the interference graph
\bm{Z}
and established conditions under which the Horvitz-Thompson estimator is a consistent estimator of
\widetilde{\tau}_{N}(d,\,d^{\prime})
.

Sävje 2024
considered more general forms of misspecified exposures and established conditions in terms of the specification errors
Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}}[Y_{i}\mid f_{i}(\bm{X})=d]
to obtain variance bounds on Horvitz-Thompson estimators and establish consistency.

7
Finite and super population approaches

The preceding sections demonstrate that there are many approaches to causal inference under interference.
Having said that,
there is value in distinguishing these approaches according to the generalizability provided.
To do so,
we review finite and super population approaches to causal inference under interference in Sections
7.1
and
7.2
,
respectively.
We then discuss the advantages and disadvantages of finite and super population approaches in Section
7.3
,
which are comparable to those of finite and super population approaches to survey sampling
[
37
]
,
statistical network analysis
[
99
]
,
and regression under network interference
[
30
]
.

7.1
Finite population approach

A finite population approach to causal inference under interference assumes that the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and the interference graph
\bm{Z}
are fixed,
so that the sole source of randomness stems from the treatment assignments
\bm{X}
.
As a result,
differences in potential outcomes

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Y_{i}(x_{i}=1,\,\bm{x}_{-i})-Y_{i}(x_{i}=0,\,\bm{x}_{-i})\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Y_{j}(x_{i}=1,\,\bm{x}_{-i})-Y_{j}(x_{i}=0,\,\bm{x}_{-i})\end{array}

can be attributed to the treatment assignment
x_{i}
of unit
i
.
A finite population approach is the simplest possible approach to causal inference under interference,
and has been popular in the literature building on Rubin’s framework.
The target of causal inference in the finite population approach may be
\tau_{D,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
,

\tau_{I,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
,
and
\tau_{T,N}^{\mathscr{X}}
.

7.2
Super population approach

A super population approach assumes that the treatment assignments
\bm{X}
,
potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
,
and interference graph
\bm{Z}
are random and generated from a super population of treatment assignments,
potential outcomes,
and interference graphs
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}

[
113
,
59
,
62
,
20
,
12
,
11
, e.g.,]
.
A super population approach requires assumptions about the data-generating model.
At least two distinct modeling approaches can be distinguished:

•

conditional probability models for outcomes
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
and interference graph
\bm{Z}
;

•

joint probability models for
(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}
.

We review them in Sections
7.2.1
,

7.2.2
,
and
7.2.3
.

7.2.1
Conditional probability models for outcomes

Conditional models for outcomes
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
can be distinguished according to whether models capture treatment spillover or outcome spillover or both.
Models with treatment spillover but without outcome spillover assume that outcomes are independent conditional on treatment assignments,
and can be parametric or nonparametric
[
62
, e.g.,]
.
Models with outcome spillover allow outcomes to be dependent conditional on treatment assignments,
and most of them can be viewed as generalizations of regression for dependent outcomes.
Examples are discrete and Gaussian Markov random fields
[
112
,
74
,
12
,
11
]
.
Gaussian Markov random fields,
which generalize linear models for dependent outcomes,
are described in Section
3.4
and are used in the simulation studies in Sections
5
and
7.3.3
.

The bulk of the literature imposes the restriction that there exists an integer
d\geq 1
(independent of
N
) such that the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of units
i
depend only on
i
’s treatment assignment and
i
’s neighbors within geodesic distance
d
in the interference graph
[
60
]
;
note that the geodesic distance between two units
i
and
j
is the length of the shortest path connecting
i
and
j
.
By comparison,
Markov random fields are capable of representing more general forms of interference.
Under Markov random fields,
the probability law of potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
can depend on the treatment assignment
x_{j}
of any unit
j
who is connected to
i
by a finite path,
i.e.,

j
does not need to be within a fixed distance from
i
.
The following result demonstrates the expressive power of Markov random fields
[
11
, see Lemma 1 in]
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider any unit
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Under the Gaussian Markov random field for outcomes
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
described in Section
3.4
,
the conditional mean of
Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
can be written as

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})]&=&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{d=0}^{\infty}(\delta\,c_{N}(\bm{z}))^{d}\sum_{a=1}^{N}(\bm{z}^{d})_{i,a}\left(\beta\,x_{a}+\gamma\,\dfrac{\sum_{b=1}^{N}x_{b}\,z_{a,b}}{\sum_{b=1}^{N}z_{a,b}}\right),\end{array}

where
(\bm{z}^{d})_{i,a}
counts the number of paths of length
d
between units
i
and
a
in the interference graph
\bm{z}
.

Lemma
7.2.1
implies that if the expected outcome
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})]
of unit
i
depends on the treatment assignment
x_{j}
of unit
j
,
then there exists an integer
d\geq 1
such that

•

either
i
is connected to
j
by a path of length
d
;

•

or there exists a third unit
k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{i,\,j\}
such that
i
is connected to
k
by a path of length
d
and
k
is connected to
j
.

In both cases there exists a finite path between
i
and
j
in the interference graph.
These observations suggest that Markov random fields can capture more general forms of interference than other models.
In the running example,
the treatments (exposure to advertisements) of teenagers may affect friends as well as friends of friends,
and all others connected to exposed teenagers directly or indirectly by a finite path.

additive and multiplicative effects models

7.2.3
Joint probability models for outcomes and interference graphs

In addition to conditional models for
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})

,

Joint probability models for

in addition to outcome-outcome dependencies (due to outcome spillover) and connection-connection dependencies (due to transitivity and other network phenomena),
and have additional advantages

7.3
Finite versus super population approach

At first glance,
the question of whether the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and interference graph
\bm{Z}
are fixed or random may appear to be inconsequential,
and it may be tempting to assume that
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{Z}
are fixed,
motivated by the desire to minimize assumptions about the data-generating model.
Having said that,
whether
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
and
\bm{Z}
are fixed or random has important implications.
We discuss them in Sections
7.3.1
and
7.3.2
and showcase them using simulations in Section
7.3.3
.

7.3.1
Advantages

We discuss advantages of super population approaches by comparing inferential frameworks based on fixed and random interference graphs
\bm{Z}
,
assuming that the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
are random.

A super population approach based on random interference graph
\bm{Z}
comes with the following benefits,
compared with fixed interference graphs
\bm{Z}
:

•

If the interference graph
\bm{Z}
is fixed,
causal conclusions are limited to
\bm{Z}
,
which is a single element of a super population of interference graphs
\mathscr{Z}
.
Since the expected outcomes can depend on the interference graph,
predictions based on a fixed interference graph can be misleading when the interference graph is misspecified or the causal effects are sensitive to the structure of the interference graph.
In the running example,
if influencers with millions of followers emerge,
the causal effect of advertisements on purchases of designer clothes can change.
As a result,
causal conclusions based on the specified interference graph can be misleading.

•

A super population approach helps generalize causal conclusions to the super population of treatment assignments,
potential outcomes,
and interference graphs
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}
and helps improve the predictive power of models.
Indeed,
the super population model
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
assigns probabilities to all measurable subsets of
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}
and,
as long as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
can be estimated from data,
causal conclusions extend to the super population
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}
.

•

A super population approach provides insight into the probability model
\mathbb{P}_{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y},\mathscr{Z}}
that generates treatment assignments,
potential outcomes,
and interference graphs from the super population
\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{Y}\times\mathscr{Z}
.
In the running example,
super population models help understand how teenagers connect,
how connections depend on attributes of teenagers,
and how the structure of the friendship network among the teenagers affects outcomes.

7.3.2
Disadvantages

The increase in generalizability afforded by a super population approach comes at the cost of additional assumptions about the data-generating model,
which can be parametric
[
112
,
20
,
12
,
11
, e.g.,]
or nonparametric
[
62
,
75
, e.g.,]
.
In addition,
the super population approach may require more data.
For example,
the interference graph
\bm{Z}
cannot be too sparse,
because otherwise it may not be possible to learn network-related features and causal estimands from data:
e.g.,
the expected numbers of neighbors
\mathbb{E}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{i,j}
may have to be at least of order
\log N

[
11
, see, e.g.,]
.

7.3.3
Simulations

To demonstrate the advantages of a super population approach based on random interference graphs rather than fixed interference graphs,
we generate 1,000 data sets
(\bm{X},\bm{Y},\bm{Z})
with
N=
1,000 units.
The treatment assignments
\bm{X}
are generated by sampling

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}X_{i}&\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny iid}}&\text{Bernoulli}(\pi=1/2).\end{array}

The interference graph
\bm{Z}
is generated by a
\beta
-model
[
16
]
,
which is one of the simplest models for generating superstars in graphs (e.g., influencers with millions of followers).
The
\beta
-model generates
\bm{Z}
by sampling

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Z_{i,j}&\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny ind}}&\text{Bernoulli}(P_{i,j}),&P_{i,j}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{\exp(\beta_{i}+\beta_{j})}{1+\exp(\beta_{i}+\beta_{j})}.\end{array}

The weights
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
can be interpreted as the propensities of units
i
and
j
to connect with others.
We generate these propensities by first sampling

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}U_{i}&\mathop{\rm\sim}\limits^{\mbox{\tiny iid}}&\text{Bernoulli}(1/1000),\end{array}

and then setting

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\beta_{i}&\coloneqq&\begin{cases}-2&\mbox{if }U_{i}=0\\
20&\mbox{if }U_{i}=1.\end{cases}\end{array}

A unit
i
with
U_{i}=1
is interpreted as a superstar,
in the sense that
i
has a high propensity
\beta_{i}=20
to connect with others and will therefore be the center of a large star in the interference graph
[
51
]
.
The
\beta
-model creates two worlds:
a world without superstars and a world with
1,2,\ldots
superstars.
The outcomes
\bm{Y}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{z})
are generated by Gaussian Markov random fields with means

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}&\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid(\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{-i},\bm{Z})=(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{z})]\\
&\coloneqq\beta\,(1+\epsilon\,u_{i})\,x_{i}\;\;+\;\gamma\,\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}}\;+\;\delta\,\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_{j}\,z_{i,j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}},\end{array}

using
\beta=2
,

\gamma=2
,

\delta=0.9
,
and
\epsilon=10
.
The idea of the model is that the outcomes
Y_{j}
of treated superstars
j
(
X_{j}=1
and
U_{j}=1
) are boosted by
\epsilon=10
,
while the outcomes
Y_{i}
of units
i
connected to superstars
j
are boosted by
\delta\,y_{j}/\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}
:
e.g.,
in the running example,
if advertisers target
influencers with millions of followers (superstars) with advertisements of designer clothes (treatments) and offer them financial incentives for purchasing designer clothes (outcomes),
then other teenagers may likewise purchase designer clothes (spillover).
We present the average outcome
(1/N)\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}
’s based on 1,000 simulated data sets in Figure
4
.

These simulations results highlight two salient features of causal inference under interference:

1.

Expected outcomes can depend on the structure of the interference graph (e.g., the absence or presence of superstars, communities, and other structural features of interference graphs).

2.

If another interference graph were observed,
the expected outcomes could be different.
In particular,
if the interference graph changed over time (as most real-world networks do),
the changes in the interference graph could invalidate conclusions based on the specified interference graph.

These results highlight the need to understand what would happen if another interference graph were observed,
and how it would affect conclusions.
In the running example,
the network of friendships among teenagers may change:
e.g.,
teenagers may create additional friendships and some of the teenagers may turn into influencers with millions of followers,
which could affect spillover and hence purchases of designer clothes.

8
Discussion and open problems

We conclude with a selection of open problems.

8.1
Detecting outcome spillover

The simulation studies in Section
5
demonstrate that existing interference tests can detect treatment spillover,
but may require large data sets with strong signals.
Worse,
existing tests appear to be unable to detect interference arising from outcome spillover.
A promising avenue for future research is the development of tests with more power for detecting interference arising from outcome spillover,
in addition to treatment spillover.

8.2
Internal versus external validity

An open problem in causal inference under interference is the external validity of causal conclusions.
A well-designed experiment ensures internal validity,
in the sense that the causal conclusions are valid in the sample on which the causal conclusions are based.
The problem of external validity concerns the question of whether—and how—the causal conclusions can be generalized from the sample to the population from which the sample is taken (generalizability)
[
58
]
or other populations of interest (transportability)
[
82
]
.
In the running example,
advertisers of designer clothes might be interested in predicting the expected number of purchases in the entire population of interest (e.g., the worldwide population of teenagers with purchasing power),
not limited to the subset of teenagers targeted on social media.

First steps towards external validity under interference were made by
Leung 2020
and
Reeves et al. 2024
,
but external validity under interference remains a wide open problem:
e.g.,

Leung 2020
requires the sample size
n
to be of the same order as the population size
N
,
and makes other assumptions that restrict the range of applications.
In addition,
the mentioned works have not considered outcome spillover,
which complicates external validity:
Outcome spillover implies that the outcomes are dependent conditional on treatment assignments.
How to generalize findings from a sample of observed outcomes that depend on unobserved outcomes without losing theoretical guarantees is an open problem,
not restricted to causal inference under interference.
Indeed,
the same problem arises in the broader literature on dependent data,
including network,
spatial,
and temporal data
[
50
,
121
]
.
This problem has not been addressed except in special cases
[
105
, e.g.,]
,
under assumptions that limit the range of applications (e.g., the presence of lattice structure and other assumptions motivated by applications in physics and spatial statistics).

8.3
Effect of network on causal effects

In other areas in which networks mediate outcomes of interest,
it is known that the structure of networks can affect outcomes.
For example,
the structure of population networks can affect the spread of infectious diseases (e.g., HIV/AIDS, COVID-19) and can therefore affect individual and public health.
If a population consists of non-overlapping subpopulations without connections between them and an infectious disease breaks out in one of the subpopulations,
then all members of the subpopulation in question can be infected,
but the rest of the population is unaffected by the outbreak.
By contrast,
if a population includes a superstar who is connected to all other units and can therefore infect all others,
the disease can spread within and between subpopulations and can therefore affect the entire population
[
47
]
.
As a consequence,
the structure of the population network can affect individual and public health,
and it is imperative to understand the structure of the population network and its impact on individual and public health.

In the domain of causal inference,
the effect of network structure on causal effects is unclear:
The vast majority of causal effects is invariant to the structure of the interference graph (e.g., the absence or presence of superstars and communities).
For example,

Hu et al. 2022
show that the total causal effect is

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}&=&\beta+\gamma,\end{array}

assuming that

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Y_{i}&=&\alpha+\beta\,x_{i}+\gamma\,\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}}+\epsilon_{i},\end{array}

where
\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{i}\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})=0
and the interference graph
\bm{Z}=\bm{z}
is considered fixed.
The result of
Hu et al. 2022
reveals that the total causal effect
\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
does not depend on the structure of the interference graph.
In the running example,
the model implies that the total causal effect
\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
is the same,
regardless of whether there are influencers with millions of followers,
and regardless of what these influencers do.
The invariance of
\tau_{T}^{\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}}
to the structure of the interference graph is rooted in the model,
which reduces the treatment assignments
\bm{x}
and the interference graph
\bm{z}
to a one-dimensional statistic: the proportion of treated neighbors
\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\,z_{i,j}\,/\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{i,j}
.
As a result,
each neighbor
j
affects the expected outcome of unit
i
by the same amount,

•

regardless of the attributes of
j
(e.g., the prestige of
j
or the purchasing power of
j
);

•

regardless of the position of
j
in the network (e.g., whether
j
is a superstar,
a member of a desirable community,
or a “bridge” between communities);

•

regardless of the outcome of
j
.

While invariance properties can be desirable,
assessing the effect of the interference graph
\bm{Z}
on the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}(\bm{x})
can be of interest in applications.
In the running example,
advertisers might be interested in assessing whether targeting influencers with millions of followers (and providing them with financial incentives for purchasing designer clothes) affects purchases of designer clothes in the population of interest.
The simulation study in Section
7
suggests that influencers can affect causal inference,
but the question of how to capture the effect of network structure along with attributes on outcomes remains an open one.

8.4
More than means

Most literature on causal inference under interference focuses on the effect of treatments on the means of potential outcomes,
assuming that the potential outcomes are random.
In principle,
treatments can affect other features of the distribution of potential outcomes:
e.g.,
treatments may affect the spread of the distribution in addition to its mean,
as well as the tails and the number of modes.
In the running example,
some teenagers may be susceptible to peer pressure in the sense that the potential outcomes
Y_{i}(\bm{x})
of susceptible teenagers
i
are affected by the treatment assignments
x_{j}
and the potential outcomes
Y_{j}(\bm{x})
of other teenagers
j
,
while other teenagers may not be susceptible to peer pressure.
As a result,
the distribution of potential outcomes may be multimodal.
A wholesome assessment of the causal effect of treatments on the distribution of potential outcomes constitutes an interesting direction for future research.

8.5
Time-indexed data

Causal processes are temporal processes:
The effects of treatment,
treatment spillover,
and outcome spillover cannot materialize at the same time.
In the running example,
the effect of treatment (advertisements) on outcomes (purchases of designer clothes) and the effect of spillover (friends of exposed teenagers purchasing designer clothes) can take weeks or months to materialize.

If there are no repeated observations of treatment assignments
\bm{X}
and outcomes
\bm{Y}
,
then
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
can be viewed as a single observation of the stationary distribution of a time-indexed stochastic process
[
53
,
74
]
.
Otherwise,
when there are repeated observations of outcomes,
it is natural to consider a time-indexed stochastic process as a model of the causal process of interest.

Eckles et al. 2017
and
van der Laan 2014
made first steps towards causal inference under interference based on repeated observations of outcomes.
For example,

Eckles et al. 2017
study data observed at time points
0,1,\ldots,T
(
T\geq 1
) generated by a time-indexed stochastic process satisfying

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}Y_{i,t}&=&f_{i,t}(X_{i},\,Y_{i,(t-1)},\,\{Y_{j,(t-1)}:\,Z_{i,j}=1\},\,\bm{U}_{t}),\end{array}

where
f_{i,t}
is a function of the treatment assignment
X_{i}
of unit
i
,
the past outcome
Y_{i,(t-1)}
of unit
i
,
the past outcomes
Y_{j,(t-1)}
of other units
j
connected to unit
i
,
and
exogenous errors
\bm{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Eckles et al. 2017
considered estimating the all-or-nothing effect

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\tau_{ALL,N}^{T}&\coloneqq&\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{Y}}[Y_{i,T}(\bm{1}_{N})-Y_{i,T}(\bm{0}_{N})].\end{array}

van der Laan 2014
considered a more general data-generating process,
with both treatment assignments
X_{i,t}
and outcomes
Y_{i,t}
evolving over time:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}X_{i,t}&=&f^{X}_{t}(s^{X}_{i,t}(\mbox{pa}(X_{i,t})),\,U^{X}_{i,t})\\
Y_{i,t}&=&f^{Y}_{t}(s^{Y}_{i,t}(\mbox{pa}(Y_{i,t})),\,U^{Y}_{i,t}),\end{array}

where
\mbox{pa}(X_{i,t})
and
\mbox{pa}(Y_{i,t})
consist of all past observations that can affect
X_{i,t}
and
Y_{i,t}
,
respectively,
which can be viewed as the parents of
X_{i,t}
and
Y_{i,t}
in a directed acylic graph
[
51
]
.
The functions
s^{X}_{i,t}(.)
and
s^{Y}_{i,t}(.)
project the past observations into a low-dimensional space,
facilitating inference.

van der Laan 2014
developed targeted maximum likelihood estimators to estimate the average expected potential outcomes at the final time point
T
.

Despite these advances,
causal inference under interference based on repeated observations is an open area of research that holds great promise.

8.6
Space- and time-indexed data

A related,
rich area of research with many open questions is spatial interference
[
86
]
:
e.g.,

Christiansen et al. 2022
study the effect of conflict on forest loss in Colombia
[
19
]
while
Papadogeorgou et al. 2022
study the effect of airstrikes on the insurgency in Iraq.
In both cases,
there is spatial interference,
in the sense that the outcomes of a region can be affected by the treatments or outcomes of neighboring regions due to treatment spillover or outcome spillover.
Spatial interference can be viewed as a special case of network interference,
where the interference graph has weighted connections and the weights of connections may be functions of the spatial distance
d_{i,j}
between units
i
and
j
,
e.g.,

Z_{i,j}\coloneqq\exp(-d_{i,j})
.
We refer the interested reader to
Wang et al. 2025
and
Leung 2022b
for statistical theory and to
Christiansen et al. 2022
and
Zigler and Papadogeorgou 2021
and
Papadogeorgou et al. 2022
for case studies
with spatial interference.
