Ecological nuance and comparative parasitology of sympatric canids in the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone
Authors: C.N. Love, S.C. Webster, D. Shamovich, M.E. Byrne, T.G. Hinton, P.E. Schlichting, J.C. Beasley, V. Dombrovski, S.L. Lance
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anthropogenic environmental contamination, including ionizing radiation, can reshape host-parasite dynamics through direct and indirect ecological pathways, yet parasite communities in radioactively contaminated landscapes remain poorly characterized. Wildlife from the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone (CEZ) are chronically exposed to elevated ionizing radiation across a heterogeneous contamination gradient and human-abandoned landscape, offering a rare opportunity to examine host-parasite-environment interactions under long-term radiation exposure. We characterized pathogen and parasite communities in two sympatric wild canids, gray wolves (Canis lupus) and raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides), from the Polesye State Radiation Ecological Reserve, Belarus, and generated individual total 137Cs dose estimates to explore dose-infection relationships. Using fecal flotation, RT-qPCR, and serology, we identified 18 and 9 parasite taxa in wolves and raccoon dogs, respectively. Preliminary correlations between individual radiation dose and gastrointestinal parasite infection were uniformly variable. Parasite taxonomy and transmission strategy also appear to modulate dose-response direction in a host-specific manner. Thus, ecological differences between wolves and raccoon dogs may be more important than radiation alone in influencing parasite community structure. Our results establish foundational baseline data for future studies on CEZ canid parasite assemblages and underscore the complexity of disentangling radiation effects from ecological drivers in contaminated landscapes.

Introduction

Many disease dynamics, including host response and the emergence of novel infectious diseases, may be influenced by ecosystem changes such as those driven by anthropogenic environmental shifts (Gilbert, 2021; Mora et al., 2022; White and Orly, 2020; Wilke et al., 2021). Among the most pervasive drivers of such anthropogenic changes are environmental contaminants, such as those from agriculture and industry. All of these drivers can directly affect hosts or parasites (Buck et al., 2015; King et al., 2010; Zuther et al., 1999), while also reshaping landscapes and leading to broad ecological modifications that indirectly influence host-parasite dynamics (Marcogliese, 2005; Marcogliese et al., 2005; Marcogliese and Pietrock, 2011; Morley, 2012; Morley et al., 2006).

A notable yet largely underexplored contaminant from the standpoint of parasite communities is ionizing radiation, stemming from both natural sources as well as anthropogenic activities. Wildlife frequently encounter low exposure to naturally occurring radiation from elements such as uranium and radon (Sazykina and Kryshev, 2006) as well as ubiquitous exposure to UV radiation (Acevedo-Whitehouse and Duffus, 2009; Martin, 2001) and elevated exposure from anthropogenic contamination from sources such as nuclear power and weapons testing (Hinton et al., 2019; Johansen et al., 2016). Elevated radiation exposure from either natural or anthropogenic sources can have significant effects, influencing physiology (Bonisoli-Alquati et al., 2010; Hiyama et al., 2012; Kesäniemi et al., 2019a, Kesäniemi et al., 2019b), life histories (Adamovich, 1998), and biodiversity (Kakiuchi et al., 2020, 1; Romanovskaia et al., 1998), potentially altering parasitism patterns by modifying hosts’ immune systems, directly impacting parasite or host behavior or survival, or interactively by impacting transmission rates. Consequently, the interactive effects of elevated ionizing radiation exposure and parasitism rates are of increasing interest, particularly as nuclear power capacity continues to be debated and expanded in many parts of the world (Energy, 2024).

One mechanism by which ionizing radiation can impact parasitism is through the host, with immune modulatory and/or behavioral shifts. Radiation exposure has been shown to alter immune cell composition in humans (Burnette and Weichselbaum, 2013; Lumniczky et al., 2021) and immune regulatory pathways in wildlife (Kesäniemi et al., 2019; Love et al., 2026). Birds from the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone (CEZ) exhibit diminished immune function and variation in specific antimicrobial activity across levels of radiation exposure (Camplani et al., 1999) and gray wolves from the CEZ display a wholesale shift in blood cell composition (Love et al., 2026). Radiation can also change feeding and movement behaviors, as seen in species like trout (Oujifard et al., 2015), bumblebees (Burrows et al., 2022), daphnia (Baylor and Smith, 1958; Golz and Bradshaw, 2019), and mice (Maier and Landauer, 1990), potentially affecting contact rates between hosts and parasites. Increased radiation exposure can additionally drive community-level ecological shifts, such as altered species richness (Horiguchi et al., 2016; Møller et al., 2015; Romanovskaia et al., 1998) and shifts in resource availability, with some reports suggesting diverse communities with increased abundance of host species in human abandoned radiation contaminated zones (Deryabina et al., 2015; Kudrenko et al., 2026; Lyons et al., 2020; Nikiforov et al., 2022; Schlichting et al., 2019; Webster et al., 2016), possibly further influencing host movement and parasite transmission dynamics.

Additionally, direct effects of increased radiation on parasites could be a contributing factor in host-parasite dynamics. Trematodes, for example, have been shown to accumulate higher levels of radionuclides than their hosts (Booth and Schulert, 1968; Nansen et al., 1976), at times reducing their survival and reproduction (Chai et al., 1995). Protozoans like Trypanosoma species exhibit decreased growth under radiation exposure (Halberstaedter, 1938), though some show evidence of adaptation to exposure over time (Vieira et al., 2014). While somewhat limited in taxonomic breadth, these data suggest radiation exposure could have parasite specific impacts which further alter host-parasite dynamics.

Predicting parasitism patterns in radioactive areas is complex due to the varying impacts on hosts and parasites and there is renewed interest in integrating ecological science with radioecology to better understand these dynamics (Rhodes et al., 2020). Few studies describe parasite communities and infection patterns in areas with naturally elevated background levels of radiation (Maslov, 1972; Waite et al., 1990). However, landscapes contaminated due to nuclear reactor releases, as seen at Chornobyl and Fukushima, exhibit higher levels of radiation, are equally understudied, and suggest dynamic parasitism patterns. For example, decreased prevalence of a plant fungal pathogen was documented in the more highly contaminated regions of Chornobyl, but no differences in viability or fertility of the pathogen were observed (Aguileta et al., 2016). Additionally, Kesäniemi et al.(Kesäniemi et al., 2019) found no relationships between contamination level and viral prevalence, load, or virulence in bank voles of Chornobyl, despite immune modulation being previously reported in this species (Kesäniemi et al., 2019).

To address these knowledge gaps, this study establishes a baseline for host-pathogen comparative research by characterizing parasite community composition and infection patterns in two sympatric wild canid species in the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone (CEZ), gray wolves (Canis lupus) and raccoon dogs (Nyctereutes procyonoides), incorporating individual host radiation dose estimates alongside parasitological data to provide an initial framework for understanding how chronic radiation exposure may shape host-parasite relationships in this uniquely contaminated landscape. The CEZ is a well-suited model system for host-parasite-environment interactions given its diverse wildlife communities (Dombrovski et al., 2022; Schlichting et al., 2019; Webster et al., 2016). Prior work shows CEZ wolf density exceeds that of surrounding reserves (Deryabina et al., 2015) and our own previous research found mammal occurrence rates highest in the innermost, most contaminated zones (Webster et al., 2016). While this does contribute possible confounding factors when comparing rates of parasitism to populations in and around of the CEZ, there is an opportunity to begin addressing the interactive effects of radiation, host density, human activity (or lack thereof) and their possible interactive effects. Our long-term goal is to separate the effects of human abandonment, species density, and radiation exposure on infection patterns, and a necessary first step is to rigorously quantify parasite communities in CEZ wildlife. Here we examine infection patterns in gray wolves and raccoon dogs from the Polesye State Radiation Ecological Reserve (PSRER), present foundational parasite community data for these species, provide rudimentary individual radiation dose models, and offer guidance for future studies. For brevity, we use the term “parasite” throughout to refer collectively to the macroparasites (helminths, protozoans) and microparasites (bacteria, viruses) screened here, while noting that these groups differ fundamentally in transmission mode, life history, and expected response to radiation exposure, differences we address in more detail below.

Methods

Site description

The Chornobyl reactor explosion in 1986 released large amounts of radioactive material, much of which settled over a large portion of Europe and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, with the highest concentrations falling in what is now northern Ukraine and southern Belarus (IAEA, 2006). To mitigate further damage from the disaster, a 4762 km2 exclusion zone (CEZ) was established around the reactor and the most contaminated regions, displacing more than 200,000 people. The portion of the CEZ in Belarus is managed by the PSRER and is solely inhabited by wildlife and visiting PSRER employees who rotate their time in and out of the CEZ. The soils within the PSRER have wide spatial heterogeneity in radiation contamination (137Cs soil contamination levels of 40 – >7000 kBq/m2 and 90Sr levels of 37.0–111.0 kBq/m2 (Izrael and IM, 2009)). This contaminant gradient, a diverse mammal community (Deryabina et al., 2015; Schlichting et al., 2019; Webster et al., 2016), and reduced human activity in the zone provide an ideal habitat to investigate the long-term implications of highly mobile species living in a radioactively contaminated landscape.

For comparative purposes, we also included blood samples from a region with background levels of radiation (Izrael and IM, 2009) north of the CEZ in Belarus. All samples collected from the reference region were collected from sites with similar forest and freshwater system characteristics as what is found in the PSRER.

Blood-borne pathogen quantification

To collect blood samples, we used modified foothold traps to humanely capture wolves and raccoon dogs across the contamination gradient found in the PSRER. Traps were checked at minimum every 24 h to minimize animal stress. Animal capture and handling was carried out in 2014 and 2016 in accordance with University of Georgia Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and approved under Animal Use Protocols A2012 08-004 and A2015 05-004, as well as applicable Belarusian regulations governing wildlife capture. All capture and handling procedures were conducted by trained personnel with veterinary supervision for chemical immobilization and blood collection. We collected blood samples via venipuncture from immobilized hosts, preserved samples in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) or RNAprotect (Qiagen), and stored samples at −20°C until transfer to the University of Georgia's Savannah River Ecology Lab, USA, where they were stored at −80°C until processing.

To procure blood samples from individuals residing in environments characterized by background radiation levels, we partnered with hunting clubs north of the PSRER in Belarus. We collected blood samples from individuals that had been shot by hunters during legally permitted hunting activities in the region and preserved the samples following the same procedure as described above. No animals were killed for the purpose of this research. We additionally collected approximately >30 g of hindlimb muscle tissue from hunter-killed animals, preserving them at −20°C for subsequent quantification of 137Cs levels to verify background estimated radiation doses.

We utilized a reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) approach to identify presence/absence of 28 pathogens in wolves and raccoon dogs from inside (wolves N = 10; raccoon dogs N = 9) and outside (wolves N = 8; raccoon dogs N = 3) the CEZ (Table 1). We extracted RNA using extraction protocols matched to each sample storage strategy: for whole blood stored in RNAlater we used the RiboPure Blood Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and for whole blood stored in RNAProtect we used the RNeasy Protect Animal Blood Kit (QIAGEN Group). We synthesized total cDNA using 255 ng total RNA and the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad), performing all steps according to manufacturers’ protocols. We sent cDNA to IDEXX Laboratories (Sacramento, CA) for RT-qPCR analyses using the IDEXX Canine Fever of Unknown Origin RealPCR™ Panel (Comprehensive) and Canine Respiratory Disease (CRD) RealPCR™ Panel (Comprehensive). Sensitivity and specificity data for these panels are manufacturer-validated for canid samples. To indirectly test pathogen exposure we used serological testing on serum from CEZ individuals (wolves n = 9; raccoon dogs n = 3; Table 1). We screened for canine parvovirus and distemper using TiterCHEK CDV/CPV (Zoetis Diagnostics), and for four vector-borne pathogens (Ehrlichia canis and/or E. ewingii, Borrelia burgdorferi, Anaplasma phagocytophilum and/or A. platys, and Dirofilaria immitis) using the IDEXX 4Dx SNAP kit. We note that the 4Dx SNAP kit detects D. immitis circulating antigens specifically; D. repens is not reliably detected by this kit, which is a limitation given documented D. repens presence in Eastern European canids. All analyses were performed according to manufacturer's protocols. Due to the low number of positive tests, we present these data as raw numbers.

Gastrointestinal parasite identification and quantification

We collected fecal samples from wolves and raccoon dogs along three road transects in the CEZ, each measuring >50 km, distributed across the contamination gradient within the PSRER (Fig. 1). Species identity of fecal samples was confirmed by morphological assessment, which is an effective alternative to genetic identification with low error rates (Lonsinger et al., 2015). Scat from other species were encountered infrequently, as supported by camera trapping during the 2014 season demonstrating that gray wolves and raccoon dogs were the most common carnivores present in the area (Webster et al., 2016). Before initiating scat surveys, we cleared all scat samples from targeted roadways, then performed surveys every three days for a total of six collections per transect using standard techniques (Gese, 1998). We preferentially collected only samples estimated to be < 24 h old based on moisture content, odor, and surface appearance, collected each sample with sterile forceps into sterile polypropylene containers, and stored them at −20°C until processing. For gastrointestinal parasite characterization, we examined fecal samples microscopically using a passive sugar flotation technique with Sheather's sugar solution (specific gravity 1.27), following Foreyt (2002). In brief, we took 3 g thawed subsamples and rinsed them with distilled water before filtering through cheesecloth. We mixed samples with 10 mL Sheather's sugar solution, centrifuged the samples in 15 mL conical tubes for 5 min at 1500 rpm in a swinging-head centrifuge, placed a coverslip on the top of each tube's meniscus to create a seal for centrifugation (Dryden et al., 2005; Foreyt, 2002). We then transferred the coverslip to a microscope slide for identification and counting of parasites. For each sample we counted total egg or oocyst counts and converted to eggs/oocysts per gram feces, though flotation-based egg/oocyst counts, as used here, provide a semi-quantitative index of shedding intensity and are not equivalent to exact eggs-per-gram counts. The sensitivity of sugar flotation for taeniid eggs and Trichuris spp. eggs is reported at ≤50% (Liccioli et al., 2012), and sensitivity for ascarid eggs may also be reduced by the filtration step. These limitations are considered throughout the interpretation of prevalence estimates.

Parasite identification followed standard morphological keys (Thienpont et al., 1986; Zajac et al., 2021). Several taxa were identified only to group level due to morphological constraints: eggs consistent with taeniid cestodes are recorded as “taeniid eggs” as Taenia spp. and Echinococcus spp. eggs are morphologically indistinguishable by light microscopy, a diagnostic limitation consistent with those reported in the broader wild canid parasitology literature (Christaki, 2020; Zajac et al., 2021). The presence of Echinococcus spp. cannot be excluded. Diphyllobothriid cestodes are referred to as Dibothriocephalus spp. per current taxonomy. “Coccidia” refers to undifferentiated coccidian oocysts that may include Cystoisospora spp., Hammondia spp., Neospora spp., or Sarcocystis spp. Molecular confirmation was not performed for gastrointestinal parasites and this is an important limitation worthy of future work. Additionally, summed counts across different parasite taxa can reflect differences in shedding rates as much as differences in true parasite burden, so any cross-taxon totals should be read as rough comparisons, not precise measures. Because the carnivores in this study eat a range of prey, some of the parasites found in feces may have come from prey rather than from infection of the study host itself (pseudoparasitism), which could affect prevalence or intensity estimates for certain taxa.

Canid host-specific infection patterns

To characterize differences in parasite communities between the gray wolf and raccoon dog host species, we conducted a multi-faceted comparative analysis. First, we calculated prevalence (percentage of positive samples) for each parasite taxon within each host species, along with standard errors assuming binomial distributions. Statistical differences in prevalence among hosts were assessed using chi-square tests of independence when expected counts were ≥5, or Fisher's exact tests (with 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations) for sparse contingency tables. We applied a significance threshold of α = 0.05 with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction for multiple comparisons. Our goal was exploratory pattern detection rather than confirmatory hypothesis testing and isolated significant findings should therefore be interpreted appropriately.

We visualized community structure differences using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordination based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices of parasite abundance data, using the metaMDS function in the vegan package in R (Oksanen et al., 2025) with k = 2 dimensions and a maximum of 100 random starts. Ordination quality was assessed using stress values (Clarke, 1993). Prior to ordination, we removed individual samples with zero parasite detections.

Parasite diversity

To assess overall parasite diversity, we calculated three complementary metrics for each individual using the vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2025) and limited analyses to gastrointestinal parasites exclusively, given small sample sizes available for other detection methods. We calculated species richness (number of parasite species present), Shannon diversity index (H' = −Σpᵢln pᵢ, where pᵢ is the proportional abundance of species i), and Simpson's diversity index (D = 1 − Σpᵢ2). Differences in diversity metrics between host species were evaluated using Mann-Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank-sum), which do not assume normality and are appropriate given the small, non-normally distributed samples; 95% confidence intervals were obtained via the Hodges-Lehmann estimator.

Rarefaction and coverage-based standardization

Given the difference in sample size between the two host species we also estimated diversity at a common level of sample completeness (95% coverage) rather than equal raw sample size. To do so we quantified diversity using three Hill numbers (Chao et al., 2014): species richness (q = 0), the exponential of Shannon entropy (q = 1, an effective-species measure weighting species by relative abundance), and the inverse Simpson index (q = 2, weighting toward dominant species). We then inspected observed sample coverage for each host prior to selecting a standardization target, to ensure comparisons relied on interpolation rather than extrapolation beyond observed data. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were obtained via the bootstrap method implemented in iNEXT (200 replicates). These Hill-number-based diversity estimates are monotonic transformations of, but not numerically identical to, the raw Shannon (H′) and Simpson (D) indices reported elsewhere in this manuscript, which were calculated directly from unstandardized community matrices using the vegan diversity function.

To evaluate whether population-level (gamma) diversity differences between hosts were attributable to beta diversity among individuals, we partitioned diversity multiplicatively across the same three Hill number orders used in the coverage-standardized analysis (q = 0, 1, 2); βq = γq/mean(αq), where αq at each order was calculated as the mean of per-individual Hill numbers (richness; exp(Shannon entropy); inverse Simpson index) rather than the Hill number of the mean raw index, as these differ under Jensen's inequality for q ≥ 1. We additionally tested for differences in multivariate dispersion between hosts using the betadisper function in the R package vegan, applied separately to abundance-weighted (Bray-Curtis) and presence-absence (Jaccard) dissimilarity matrices derived from the site-by-species community matrix, as a complementary measure of beta diversity. Because Jaccard distances did not embed in strictly Euclidean space (indicated by a substantial proportion of negative eigenvalues in the initial principal coordinates decomposition), we applied the Cailliez square-root correction (sqrt.dist = TRUE) to the Jaccard-based dispersion analysis; this fully resolved the negative eigenvalues and is reported as the primary result for that distance metric. Group differences in beta diversity (dispersion) were tested using a permutation test (permutest, 999 permutations) for each distance metric separately.

Parasite abundance modelling

Differences in overall parasite abundance between canid host species were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis tests (kruskal.test from the stats package in R) on total parasite abundance per individual (summed across all taxa), which does not assume normality or homogeneity of variance and is appropriate given unequal group sizes.

Radiation dose quantification

Accurately measuring dose in wild organisms is difficult as they move across a highly heterogeneously contaminated landscape and both internal and external components contribute to total dose (Bontrager et al., 2024). In Chornobyl, internal dose occurs from the consumption of 90Sr and 137Cs contaminated food, water, or air. Conversely, external doses occur from radioactive components in the environments (e.g., soil, vegetation, litter) while wildlife move through the environment. Strontium-90 is a beta emitting particle that contributes to internal doses but lacks sufficient energy to be a major contributor to external dose. Cesium-137 is the most prominent contaminant still in the CEZ and emits a relatively high energy gamma photon which contributes to both the internal and external dose received by Chornobyl wildlife. Here we provide preliminary individual dose estimates focused on 137Cs, and recognize these are not full total dose estimates for each individual.

Previous work has shown a correlation of home range soil contamination with internal radiation dose (Hinton et al., 2019). Concentration Ratios (CRs) can be used to convert soil radioactivity concentrations (Bq/kg) to animal tissue radioactivity concentrations (Bq/kg). These dose coefficients (DCs) can then be applied to convert radioactivity concentrations in an animal to an internal dose rate (μGy/h). Similarly, DCs are used to convert soil contamination levels to dose rates from external exposures. Empirical ratios, such as CRs and DCs, are radionuclide- and organism-specific and have been tabulated as well as incorporated into commonly used wildlife dose models (e.g., ERICA Tool (Brown et al., 2016); BiotaDC (Ulanovsky, Copplestone, and Vives I Batlle, 2017)). Estimating dose rates with empirical ratios is a pragmatic solution to a complex problem, but one that generates massive uncertainties. Typically, CRs vary over several orders of magnitude (AgencyInternational Atomic Energy, 2010) and DCs do not account for the spatial-temporal aspects of external radiation exposures to free-ranging wildlife (Bontrager et al., 2024).

To estimate dose for each fecal samples, we used area-weighted mean soil 137Cs concentrations (Bq kg−1) from a 1000 m or 500 m radius for wolf and raccoon dog samples respectively as a proxy for estimated core home range around each sample's collection location. We estimated area-weighted means for each buffer radius by referencing UTM coordinates against geo-referenced CEZ 137Cs soil contamination maps (Izrael and IM, 2009; Webster et al., 2016) Buffer radii were selected to approximate core-use areas based on home range estimates for each species as we are unable to track full home-range telemetry of hosts from which fecal samples originated. As such, this approach cannot capture explicit range-wide variation in exposure.

To begin to disentangle relationships between radiation dose and parasite infection within the CEZ, we computationally simulated individual host total dose rates (μGy h−1) from the most prevalent radioisotope in the CEZ, 137Cs, using the ERICA Assessment Tool (Brown et al., 2016). We performed ERICA models to simulate radiation dose rate for each of our study species, gray wolves and raccoon dogs. ERICA models different wildlife species based on their size but does not include default data for wolves or raccoon dogs. Therefore, we created custom reference mammals within the ERICA program for wolves (50 kg mass; 80 cm length; 60 cm height; 45 cm width) based on previous wolf ERICA models (Hinton et al., 2019), and raccoon dogs (6.89 kg mass; 64.9 cm length; 33.6 cm height; 13.93 cm width) based on field measurements. We ran ERICA simulations with a soil bulk density of 1400 kg m−3 (Taira et al., 2013) and a soil contamination depth of 0.1 m to estimate the total, internal and external 137Cs, dose rates (μGy h−1) to which hosts were exposed.

We previously (Hinton et al., 2019) found that external exposure in CEZ wolves was most strongly associated with contamination within core-use areas, however, that same study found internal 137Cs body burdens were better predicted by contamination across an animal's full range, which our buffer-based approach does not capture. The dose estimates presented here integrate both exposure pathways, though our estimates likely represent external exposure more reliably than internal exposure. Measurement error from this innately indirect quantification of dose estimates will tend to attenuate true dose-parasite correlations.

Infection patterns across radiation exposure

We used Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) (cor.test function from the stats package in R) to explore associations between radiation dose and gastrointestinal parasite infection, since these data are overdispersed and non-normal, and ρ is a standard non-parametric tool for this kind of exploratory work. Correlations were run at two levels: (1) species-level, correlating dose with individual infection status (0/1) for each parasite species with ≥10 sampled individuals and at least one positive case, run for all hosts combined and separately per host species; and (2) group-level, where parasites were pooled into transmission-mode categories (direct vs. indirect) or broad taxonomic categories (Nematoda, Cestoda, Trematoda, Protozoa). Infection status was collapsed to the individual sampled animal (positive if that animal carried at least one parasite from the group), and ρ was calculated between dose and this collapsed infection status, again for all groups with ≥10 observations and ≥5 infected individuals. Significance was assessed with asymptotic p-values (α = 0.05, cor.test in R); 95% CIs for ρ used Fisher's z-transformation.

Estimations of significance

Across our analyses we primarily focus on estimation-based inference, reporting effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals, following recommendations to move beyond dichotomous significance testing (Amrhein et al., 2019; Wasserstein et al., 2019).

No correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the dose correlation comparisons, consistent with the exploratory aim of detecting patterns rather than confirming hypotheses and statistically significant findings from these tests should be treated as hypothesis-generating and interpreted with corresponding caution. P-values are interpreted as continuous measures of evidence rather than binary decision thresholds. All observed effects contain uncertainty, particularly given the sample sizes available and limited ability to provide exact total dose estimates per individual.

Results

Host-specific infection patterns

Blood-borne pathogens

Of the 28 pathogens tested by RT-qPCR (see Supplemental Table S1 for complete panel), we detected evidence of seven across the two host species. Gray wolves harbored five blood-borne pathogen detections (Anaplasma spp., Babesia spp., canine distemper virus, coronavirus, and Hepatozoon spp.), while raccoon dogs had three (Babesia spp., Rickettsia spp., and Streptococcus spp.). Babesia spp. was the only parasite detected in both raccoon dogs and wolves, and only in CEZ individuals (Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1).

Spatial variation in pathogen presence was observed in both hosts. In wolves, Hepatozoon canis occurred in both CEZ and northern Belarus individuals, whereas all remaining blood-borne detections were geographically restricted to one region or the other. The most prevalent blood-borne pathogens were Hepatozoon spp. and Babesia spp., both requiring tick vectors for transmission.

We performed field-based rapid serological testing on a CEZ canid subset, revealing antibody and antigen responses to three common canid pathogens: Borrelia burgdorferi and Dirofilaria immitis (indirectly transmitted) and canine parvovirus (directly transmitted) (Table 1). Wolves demonstrated seroprevalence to all three, with antibody-positivity for B. burgdorferi and CPV and antigen-positivity for D. immitis. Raccoon dogs showed antigen-positivity only for D. immitis (Table 1, Supplemental Table S1). We note that serology confirms prior exposure but cannot confirm active infection. The 4Dx SNAP kit detects D. immitis antigen specifically; D. repens is not reliably detected by this assay, representing a limitation for Eastern European canid studies where D. repens is documented.

Of the seven blood-borne detections via RT-qPCR, three pathogens exhibited direct transmission (canine distemper virus, coronavirus, Streptococcus spp.) while four require tick vectors (Anaplasma spp., Babesia spp., Hepatozoon spp., Rickettsia spp.). Combined with serological data, our survey identified four directly transmitted and six indirectly transmitted blood-borne pathogens across CEZ canid populations.

Parasite diversity differs between hosts

Parasite diversity differed between hosts (Fig. 2C). Wolves had higher mean richness per sample (3.29 ± 1.46) than raccoon dogs (2.05 ± 0.82; Mann-Whitney U: W = 666, p = 3.2 × 10−4). Shannon and Simpson diversity did not differ (Shannon: W = 520, p = 0.17; Simpson: W = 477.5, p = 0.47). NMDS showed broad community overlap between hosts (stress = 0.181; Fig. 2D). Egg/oocyst count per sample was far higher and more overdispersed in wolves (37.9 ± 306) than raccoon dogs (0.52 ± 2.59), a 73-fold difference (Kruskal-Wallis: H = 11.718, df = 1, p = 0.0006).

These sample-level comparisons do not account for unequal sampling effort between hosts (63 wolf and 34 raccoon dog). To test whether the richness differences held at the host population level, independent of effort, we standardized diversity to equal sample coverage (95%; (Chao and Lou, 2012)). Observed coverage exceeded this target for both hosts (wolves: 99.4%; raccoon dogs: 100%), so estimates reflect interpolation, not extrapolation. Wolves showed non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals above raccoon dogs across all three Hill orders (richness (q0: 8.05 [7.05–9.05] vs. 4.32 [3.32–5.33]), Shannon (q1: 6.41 [5.82–6.99] vs. 3.46 [2.89–4.03]), and Simpson (q2: 5.57 [5.07–6.07] vs. 3.00 [2.47–3.52])) indicating this pattern is robust to sampling effort and not driven solely by rare taxa.

Despite wolves' greater host-level diversity at every Hill order, the direction of this difference reversed for beta diversity at the abundance-weighted orders. Multiplicative partitioning showed higher beta diversity in wolves at richness (q0: 3.04 vs. 2.50), while raccoon dogs showed higher beta diversity at the abundance-weighted orders (q1: 1.71 vs. 1.23; q2: 2.14 vs. 1.48). Multivariate dispersion partially supported greater turnover among wolves, significant under Bray-Curtis (F1,62 = 24.41, p = 0.001) and marginal under Jaccard (F1,62 = 4.02, p = 0.054). Together, this indicates wolves' higher host-level diversity reflects a larger, more variable species pool at the host level rather than more diverse individual infections.

Infection patterns, transmission strategies, and parasite diversity across radiation exposure

To begin addressing the question of interactions between radiation dose and infection patterns, we assessed dose-detection correlations for gastrointestinal parasites semi-quantitative shedding indices across 97 individual samples. Blood-borne pathogen sample sizes preclude statistical comparison with radiation dose, so we focus exclusively on gastrointestinal parasites. All patterns reported here are exploratory and the following descriptions should be understood as descriptive summaries rather than definitive correlations.

Cross-host parasite correlations with radiation

When examining parasite patterns across both canid hosts, all gastrointestinal parasites showed weak to negligible correlations with radiation dose, with correlation coefficients ranging from ρ = −0.16 to ρ = 0.15 (Fig. 3). Taeniid eggs (1 infection, 1.0% prevalence), Dibothriocephalus (7 infections, 7.2% prevalence), and Moniezia (5 infections, 5.2% prevalence) exhibited correlation coefficients of ρ = 0.15, ρ = −0.09, and ρ = −0.04, respectively, but confidence intervals all span zero.

Between host parasite correlation with radiation

Given the distinct parasite communities and ecology for each host species, we analyzed correlations separately for gray wolves and raccoon dogs. Gray wolves harbored 10 gastrointestinal parasite taxa. Correlations with radiation dose were uniformly weak to negligible (ρ range: −0.158 to 0.20; Fig. 3A). Alaria, the most prevalent parasite in wolves (34 infections, 54.8% prevalence), showed a weak positive trend (ρ = 0.199, 95% CI: −0.053 to 0.428, p = 0.12). Weak negative correlations were observed for Paragonimus (ρ = −0.090, 95% CI: −0.332 to 0.163, p = 0.49), and weak positive correlations for Toxocara (ρ = 0.028, 95% CI: −0.224 to 0.275, p = 0.83); Dibothriocephalus showed a weak negative correlation (ρ = −0.074, 95% CI: −0.318 to 0.179, p = 0.57). No parasites in gray wolves reached statistical significance. Low-prevalence parasites (taeniid eggs, n = 1; Eucoleus, n = 2; Physaloptera, n = 7; Sarcocystis, n = 5) had wide confidence intervals including zero and are reported for completeness but lack statistical power for interpretation.

Raccoon dogs harbored 5 parasite taxa, a simpler community than gray wolves. Coccidia showed a significant negative correlation with radiation dose (ρ = −0.402, 95% CI: −0.651 to −0.074, p = 0.019), the only parasite in either host species with evidence of dose-dependent effects. This pattern suggests that raccoon dogs exposed to higher radiation had lower Coccidia prevalence. Alaria, which overlapped with gray wolves, showed no significant association (ρ = −0.006, 95% CI: −0.343 to 0.333, p = 0.973), contrasting with the weak positive trend in wolves. Trichuris spp. and Moniezia also showed non-significant correlations (ρ = 0.144, p = 0.42; ρ = −0.174, p = 0.33, respectively). Nematodirus had only one infection (n = 1) and is not interpretable.

The three parasites found in both host species displayed markedly different dose-response patterns (Table 1, Fig. 3C). Alaria showed contrasting associations: a weak positive correlation in gray wolves (ρ = 0.199, p = 0.12) but no correlation in raccoon dogs (ρ = −0.006, p = 0.973). Trichuris spp. exhibited near-zero correlations in both hosts (gray wolves: ρ = 0.031, p = 0.81; raccoon dogs: ρ = 0.144, p = 0.42). Coccidia was the exception: only raccoon dogs showed a significant negative dose relationship (ρ = −0.402, p = 0.019), while gray wolves exhibited no association (ρ = 0.143, p = 0.27). These host-specific responses suggest that intrinsic host factors, such as immune competence, foraging behavior, radiation exposure pathways, and/or habitat use, are modulating radiation-parasite interactions independent of shared parasite exposure.

Discussion

This study characterizes gastrointestinal parasite and blood-borne pathogen communities in sympatric gray wolves and raccoon dogs within the CEZ, a landscape shaped simultaneously by chronic low-dose radiation exposure and nearly four decades of reduced human disturbance. Radiation's relationship with parasite communities is unlikely to have a single direct dose-response effect. More plausibly, it likely operates through several interacting and confounded pathways, including host immune modulation, radiation-driven changes in host and intermediate host density, movement, physiology, and landscape-level effects of human abandonment on host availability and vector density.

Generally, we documented 18 and 9 parasite and pathogen taxa in CEZ wolves and raccoon dogs, respectively, with parasite communities dominated by generalist taxa requiring indirect transmission through intermediate hosts. The observed difference in parasite richness between hosts is broadly consistent with the wider literature. A continental-scale review of published wolf helminth surveys recorded 72 species from 40 genera across Nearctic and Palaearctic populations, with Palaearctic (including European) wolves showing helminth diversity at least as high as, and often higher than, larger, less disturbed North American populations (Craig and Craig, 2005). Raccoon dog surveys elsewhere in Europe, by contrast, typically report a comparatively narrower core parasite fauna dominated by a handful of common taxa, most consistently Uncinaria stenocephala, Toxocara canis, Alaria alata, Mesocestoides spp., and Echinococcus multilocularis (Duscher et al., 2017; Pilarczyk et al., 2022). The differences in richness between host species in the CEZ persisted after controlling for sampling effort, and most likely reflect fundamental differences in diet, habitat use, movement ecology, radiation exposure, and intermediate-host exposure between the two canids, though Shannon and Simpson diversity indices were comparable between them. This is consistent with cross-continental wolf parasite research showing that diet breadth, prey diversity, and host density, rather than host species identity alone, structure parasite communities in free-ranging canids (Stronen et al., 2021).

These broader patterns set up a useful comparative framework with which to place the CEZ data. Several parasite prevalence patterns in these two CEZ canids differed from published findings elsewhere in Europe, though disentangling radiation effects from behavioral and ecological context remains challenging. Alaria spp. infections were prevalent in CEZ wolves, exhibiting higher intensity and prevalence than previously reported in southern Belarus (Shimalov and Shimalov, 2000). This trematode requires aquatic intermediate hosts (snails, tadpoles) and typically infects definitive hosts through consumption of infected amphibians or paratenic hosts (Möhl et al., 2009). The elevated Alaria spp. prevalence in wolves may reflect consumption of prey species such as beaver (Gable and Windels n.d.; Nitsche, 2016) which could occur at higher densities within the CEZ due to reduced human persecution, consistent with documented higher mammal occurrence rates in the CEZ interior (Nikiforov et al., 2022; Webster et al., 2016). The comparable Alaria spp. prevalence we observed in raccoon dogs is notable on its own terms, since the species is repeatedly described elsewhere in Europe with substantially lower prevalence. Austrian raccoon dogs sampled near wetlands, and floodplain habitats with abundant paratenic hosts showed Alaria alata prevalence around 30% (Duscher et al., 2017). Additionally, Polish surveys report prevalence as high as 25% (Pilarczyk et al., 2022), compared to the 50% in CEZ raccoon dogs. Diet is a plausible explanation, given raccoon dogs' regular consumption of amphibians (Sutor et al., 2010). Whether this reflects habitat-specific differences in amphibian or paratenic host density within the CEZ, radiation response, sampling limitations, or a genuine departure from the pattern reported elsewhere warrants further investigation.

Three parasites, Dipylidium spp., Toxocara spp., and taeniid eggs were not identified in raccoon dogs, despite being identified in CEZ wolves, and prior work confirming that raccoon dogs are competent hosts for all three elsewhere in their invasive European range (Pilarczyk et al., 2022). Toxocara canis, taeniid eggs and Dipylidium caninum were recorded at 18.75%, 19.79%, and 6.25%, respectively, in Polish raccoon dogs (Pilarczyk et al., 2022) and Austrian raccoon dogs showed comparable rates for Toxocara canis (20%), taeniid eggs (20%), and Dipylidium caninum (10%) (Duscher et al., 2017). A direct comparative study from Denmark offers a plausible mechanism (Al-Sabi et al., 2013). Toxocara canis and taeniid eggs were markedly less prevalent in raccoon dogs than in sympatric red foxes (13.1% vs. 60.9%, and 2.0% vs. 30.7%, respectively), consistent with raccoon dogs' comparatively lower reliance on rodent prey, the principal intermediate or paratenic hosts for both parasites (Kauhala and Kowalczyk, 2011; Sutor et al., 2010). Wolves, as larger obligate predators with a diet skewed toward ungulates but retaining opportunistic small-mammal predation, may simply encounter these transmission routes more often than the more omnivorous, invertebrate- and plant-heavy raccoon dog diet. Dipylidium caninum is mechanistically distinct, since transmission occurs via flea ingestion during grooming rather than through diet, and, in the same Danish dataset, it was in fact more prevalent in raccoon dogs than foxes (5.1% vs. 0.3% (Al-Sabi et al., 2013)). Given the modest sample sizes of these CEZ collections and the comparatively low prevalences reported for these three parasites in raccoon dog populations elsewhere, a false-negative from limited sampling cannot be excluded and should be weighed against a genuine ecological or exposure-driven absence.

Blood-borne pathogens such as canine parvovirus (CPV) also showed seroprevalence in CEZ wolves (57%) comparable to values reported in Iberian wolves using standard antibody assays (61% (Oleaga et al., 2015)). Both figures substantially exceeded CPV-2 detection rates from Italian and French wolf populations (15.2% and 12.1%, respectively (Molnar et al., 2014), but that comparison needs a caveat as Molnar et al. used fecal PCR to detect active viral shedding, not antibody-based seroprevalence, so the two measures are not directly interchangeable, and a lower PCR-detected shedding rate does not necessarily imply lower lifetime exposure. Molnar et al., (2014) attribute cross-population differences in their own data to the density and spatial distribution of free-ranging dogs as a reservoir, which is a plausible general driver here as well, alongside strain diversity and immune history. The absence of CDV and coronavirus in CEZ wolves, despite prior detections in northern Belarus, may reflect stochastic dynamics in spatially structured populations, or simply differences in sample size and timing relative to outbreak cycles; CDV in particular circulates episodically rather than at stable endemic levels even in well-studied populations (Oleaga et al., 2015). As such, these are patterns worth further investigation.

Tick-borne pathogens were prevalent across both CEZ wolf and raccoon dog hosts. This is consistent with documented high tick densities and elevated pathogen prevalence in questing ticks within the CEZ (Dillon et al., 2024; Rogovskyy et al., 2019). Dillon et al. (2024) found over half of Ixodes ricinus ticks sampled at the CEZ were positive for at least one pathogen, and Rogovskyy et al. (2019) similarly reported Rickettsia raoultii and other zoonotic agents circulating at comparatively high rates in CEZ-collected ticks. The presence of diverse tick-borne pathogens likely reflects habitat quality and reduced human activity rather than radiation exposure per se, consistent with similar patterns documented in protected wildlife refugia with limited human disturbance (Hassett et al., 2022; Movila et al., 2012). Taken together, these taxon-specific comparisons show CEZ canid parasite communities are broadly recognizable against the European baseline, with a small number of departures, elevated wolf and raccoon dog Alaria burden, and comparatively high wolf CPV seroprevalence, that are plausible candidates for landscape or host-density effects but cannot yet be attributed to radiation specifically.

Immune and hematological data (Love et al., 2026) offer a second, mechanistically distinct line of evidence worth considering alongside these prevalence patterns. Observed shifted leukocyte profiles in CEZ wolves, reduced T and B lymphocytes alongside elevated granulocytes and monocytes, is consistent with radiation-induced hematopoietic and immunological stress reported in this wolf population (Love et al., 2026), and in irradiated human cohorts (Lumniczky et al., 2021). Functionally, lymphopenia is linked to reduced capacity to mount novel antigen responses and heightened susceptibility to viral disease, which is relevant given that CDV and CPV already circulate and cause mortality in wild wolf populations elsewhere (Justice-Allen and Clement, 2019). A radiation-suppressed lymphocyte pool could plausibly lower the threshold at which these endemic viruses cause clinical rather than subclinical disease, though this is inference from general immunology rather than something demonstrated in CEZ canids here directly.

The trematode, protozoan, and cestode burden in this population adds an additional possible interacting axis. Helminth infection can skew host immunity toward Th2/Treg responses (IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, TGF-β) at the expense of the Th1/IL-17 responses needed to control intracellular and viral pathogens, a mechanism shown to worsen outcomes in helminth-virus and helminth-mycobacterial coinfection models (Chin et al., 2023; García-Bernalt Diego et al., 2025). Combined with a granulocyte-dominant, lymphocyte-depleted baseline observed in the CEZ wolves, this raises a hypothesis that these canids may face compounded immune compromise. With radiation-driven lymphopenia potentially narrowing antiviral and anti-tick-borne-bacterial defenses while concurrent helminth-driven Th2 skewing may further blunt Th1-dependent clearance. None of this establishes causality in CEZ canids, but could begin to provide a mechanistically plausible framework built from leukocyte and coinfection literature, and would need pathogen-specific serology or infection outcome data in these populations to test directly.

Interpreting any of these patterns as radiation effects, however, runs into a structural problem with the CEZ landscape itself. A critical interpretive challenge is distinguishing radiation effects from spatial heterogeneity in canid density. The CEZ is a protected area where human persecution is minimal, creating a gradient where canids concentrate in highly contaminated interior zones while avoiding human-modified edge habitats (Tigas et al., 2002; Webster et al., 2016: Kudrenko et al., 2026). Higher host densities in contaminated regions could elevate transmission rates for density-dependent parasites, potentially masking radiation-mediated suppression or creating spurious positive dose-response relationships. Many documented parasites, particularly those transmitted via fecal-oral routes or requiring definitive host contact, exhibit density-dependent transmission (Arneberg et al., 1998). Given these confounding factors, any dose-correlation results themselves warrant further investigation. Most all observed dose-parasite correlations observed here were weak to negligible and statistically uncertain, consistent with the current data being insufficient to resolve radiation-parasite effects from ecological background variation. The directional patterns observed, while uncertain, could be ecologically interpretable as preliminary hypotheses warranting targeted follow-up.

To begin to assess dose associated patterns of parasite dynamics, a broad parasite transmission-mode grouping (direct vs indirect) served as a first-order analytical framework for beginning to explore possible ecological and natural history drivers in CEZ infections. These analyses yielded intriguing, if preliminary, results. Host-specific patterns describe a significant negative association between dose and direct-transmission parasites in raccoon dogs but only weak, non-significant associations for indirectly transmitted parasites. Additionally, wolves displayed weak, non-significant associations in both transmission modes. If real, the raccoon dog pattern runs counter to hypotheses of increased transmission in the higher-density CEZ interior, or of immune-compromise-driven increased infection for directly transmitted parasites. It is, however, consistent with several non-exclusive alternative explanations involving host behavior, host physiology, or intermediate-host and parasite-specific characteristics. Raccoon dogs have smaller home ranges and may move less in a resource-rich environment, potentially reducing direct-transmission opportunities; alternatively, there may be confounding effects of radiation on directly transmitted parasites that remain poorly understood.

Taxon-specific dose correlations, examined independently of the broader transmission-mode grouping, were similarly weak to negligible for most parasites in both hosts. Three exceptions showed a pattern of note. If real, the patterns observed in Coccidia and Nematodirus spp. in raccoon dogs, and Eucoleus spp. in wolves, each showed a negative correlation with radiation dose. All three parasite groups are directly transmitted, fecal-orally shed taxa, which is at least broadly consistent with the negative direct-transmission signal already noted in raccoon dogs, though Eucoleus's appearance in wolves complicates a simple host-specific story. Given the limited sample sizes, particularly for Nematodirus and Eucoleuss spp., number of taxa tested and uncertainly in dose estimates, these three associations should be treated cautiously. If robust, a shared negative direction across three directly transmitted taxa in two host species is nonetheless a pattern worth flagging for targeted follow-up, since it runs counter to the density-driven transmission increase that might otherwise be expected in high-density interior zones, and instead is more consistent with either reduced host contact behavior or a direct effect of dose on parasite viability or shedding at the taxon level.

Wolves' larger territories and pack-based social contact could potentially contribute to buffering against fine-scale dose variation, which would mask an equivalent behavioral or physiological signal even if one exists, consistent with their weak correlations across both parasite groups and radiation dose. The broader NMDS ordination additionally shows substantial overlap between parasite loadings and host positions. That overlap, together with the non-linear structure of the ordination itself, argues against transmission mode being a clean, unified axis of radiation response in this dataset.

Even though the dose estimates utilized here are just proxies of actual total dose, the modest explanatory power of radiation dose relative to host-associated ecological factors suggests that habitat use, behavior, physiology, vector availability, and intermediate-host populations are all plausible structuring forces for parasite communities in the CEZ landscape. These interpretive limits are heightened by methodological constraints inherent to non-invasive sampling. Fecal-based surveys necessarily underestimate total parasite diversity relative to necropsy-based methods, and individual detections may not accurately represent true host-level infection status. Because this is a cross-sectional, non-invasive survey without genetic host or parasite identification, precise individual-level dose quantification, or longitudinal tracking, the patterns reported here should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Repeated sampling of the same host individuals, and more spatially and temporally robust dose estimates, would be needed to build confidence in these dynamics. Future work should prioritize large, temporally replicated sampling with genetic individual confirmation, surveys of intermediate and paratenic hosts, GPS-based tracking to link movement with fine-scale radiation exposure, and genomic analyses of both hosts and parasites to test for radiation-associated selection.

Conclusions

Wolves and raccoon dogs inhabiting the CEZ harbor a diverse array of parasites and pathogens broadly consistent with those expected in canids from the region, with community composition, intensity, and prevalence to seemingly vary primarily as a function of host-associated ecological differences. Dose-infection correlations were consistently weak, uncertain, and are exploratory findings rather than evidence of a definitive radiation effect on parasite communities.

Certain methodological approaches would strengthen future work in this system. Genetic confirmation of fecal sample identity at the individual host level would resolve uncertainty introduced by non-invasive sampling and molecular parasite identification would sharpen taxonomic resolution beyond what coproscopy allows. Necropsy-based surveys would additionally complement and validate the coproscopic data reported here, and surveys of intermediate hosts would help characterize transmission potential across the contamination gradient. Integrating these approaches with more robust, spatially and temporally resolved dose estimation will be essential to rigorously assess the ecological and epidemiological processes shaping host-parasite dynamics in radioactively contaminated landscapes.

By documenting baseline parasite diversity and infection patterns in this unique system, our work provides essential context for understanding wildlife disease dynamics in radioactively contaminated landscapes. These data help to deepen our understanding of host-parasite-environment interactions in the context of human-induced environmental change, and contributes essential knowledge toward characterizing disease dynamics, optimizing conservation efforts, predicting emerging infectious threats, and mitigating zoonotic risks in an increasingly disrupted world (Allen et al., 2017; EcoHealth Alliance, 2019).
