Effect of micropillar surface topography on the antibacterial performance of zinc oxide coated polymer films
Authors: Quang Hung Nguyen, Tien Thanh Nguyen, Vi Khanh Truong, Avinash Baji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, nanoimprint lithography was employed to fabricate micropillars on the surface of a poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) film. In the next step, a hydrothermal method was used to grow zinc oxide (ZnO) nanostructures on the PVDF-HFP film. The antibacterial properties of the resulting films were evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli). The sharp ZnO nanostructures exhibited strong antibacterial activity through both physical membrane disruption and chemical interactions, including ion release and oxidative stress. The effect of micropillar structures on the antibacterial behavior of PVDF-HFP films was also investigated. The results show that the presence of micropillars enhanced the antibacterial efficacy. The increase in surface area enabled a higher concentration growth of ZnO nanostructures, which contributed to more efficient bacterial inactivation. ZnO-coated PVDF-HFP films with micropillars outperformed ZnO nanostructures grown on neat PVDF-HFP films, particularly against E. coli. Viability assays revealed a substantial reduction in bacterial survival, with only 3.80% of S. aureus and 1.36% of E. coli cells remaining viable on PVDF-HFP films with surface micropillars and ZnO. These findings highlight the potential of ZnO-coated, microtextured PVDF-HFP films as effective antibacterial materials for future biomedical and food packaging applications.

Introduction

Foodborne disease has recently become a major global public health issue [1]. The World Health Organization defines foodborne diseases as illnesses caused by food contamination at any point in the production, delivery, and consumption [2]. Among these, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O157) are major contributors, causing approximately 241,000 and 73,000 illnesses annually in the United States, respectively [3, 4]. However, the actual number of S. aureus and E. coli foodborne disease cases is likely much higher due to underreporting and diagnostic limitations [5, 6]. The economic burden associated with these pathogens is substantial, with foodborne illnesses caused by S. aureus and E. coli costing an estimated $167 million and $405 million annually in the United States, respectively [4, 7].

The development of antibiotic resistance in S. aureus and E. coli significantly exacerbates the risks associated with these pathogens, as traditional antibacterial treatments become less effective [8]. S. aureus has acquired multiple resistance genes that confer resistance to various antimicrobial drugs, including aminoglycosides, diaminopyrimidines, tetracyclines, and β-lactams [9, 10]. Similarly, E. coli has demonstrated high resistance to multiple drug classes, such as β-lactams (except aztreonam), tetracyclines, sulfonamides, amide alcohols, and rifamycin, while exhibiting lower resistance rates to aztreonam, aminoglycosides, macrolides, doxycycline, quinolones, polymyxins, nitrofurans, and fosfomycin [10, 11]. Additionally, S. aureus strains can produce a variety of toxins, including staphylococcal enterotoxins (SEs) [12], which are highly stable, heat-resistant, and resilient to common food preservation conditions such as freezing and drying [13]. Meanwhile, E. coli exhibits remarkable survivability against food processing methods by forming biofilms that shield bacterial cells from antibiotics and disinfectants, reducing their effectiveness [14]. These properties allow both S. aureus toxins and E. coli biofilms to persist even after extensive food processing, posing a significant threat to food safety and public health [10, 13, 14].

Addressing the health risks posed by S. aureus and E. coli, particularly antimicrobial-resistant strains [15], requires innovative solutions. One promising strategy is the development of antibacterial materials. Research has shown that surface texturing can impart antibacterial and antifouling properties to materials [16, 17]. Likewise, inorganic nanomaterials have been proposed as effective antibacterial agents due to their ability to physically interact with bacterial cells, leading to membrane rupture and cell destruction [18]. Among these nanomaterials, zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles are one of the emerging antibacterial materials due to their ability to combat bacteria by releasing toxic Zn²⁺ ions [19]. ZnO nanoparticles are found to kill bacteria without inducing high levels of oxidative stress, unlike other antibacterial agents such as hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [20]. Other mechanisms associated with ZnO nanoparticles include bacterial membrane disruption through electrostatic interactions and enzyme inhibition by penetrating bacterial cells [20–22].

In this study, zinc oxide (ZnO) nanostructures are grown on the surface of poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) films using a hydrothermal method and its antibacterial properties are evaluated. Prior to the use of hydrothermal method for growing ZnO, a thin film of PVDF-HFP is prepared and micropillars are produced on its surface using nanoimprint lithography. For comparison, a control PVDF-HFP@ZnO film without micropillars is also prepared to evaluate the impact of surface topography on antibacterial performance. The antibacterial properties of both film types are subsequently evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli).

Experimental

Materials

Poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP), with an average molecular weight of 450,000 (g/mol), triethylamine, zinc acetate dihydrate, hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA), and zinc nitrate hexahydrate were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (New South Wales, Australia). Acetone and dimethylformamide (DMF) were used as solvents and procured from CSA Scientific (Gilman, Australia).

Sample preparation

A solution of DMF and acetone was prepared by mixing 3.44 g of DMF with 1.48 g of acetone to establish a 70/30 (w/w) ratio. Following this, 1.08 g of poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) was added to the solution mixture to obtain 18% (w/w) PVDF-HFP concentration. The solution was heated to 80 °C with continuous stirring to ensure complete dissolution of the PVDF-HFP polymer. Once dissolved, the solution was cooled to ambient temperature and poured onto a smooth silicon wafer. The solvent within the solution was allowed to evaporate under ambient conditions until a solid film was formed. To ensure complete removal of residual solvent, the film and the silicon wafer were then heated at 60 °C for 1 h. After drying, the PVDF-HFP film was carefully peeled off the silicon wafer. This process took up to 48 h. The obtained films were used for subsequent micropillar fabrication via the nanoimprinting technique.

The PVDF-HFP film was transferred to the Compact Nanoimprinting tool (CNI, NIL Technology ApS, Kongens Lyngby, Denmark). A commercial template having 5-micron pores was placed on top of the film. A vacuum pressure of 1 bar was applied during the imprinting step to enable the flow of PVDF-HFP into the pores of the template. The setup was heated to 160 °C for 1 min. This temperature exceeded the melting point of PVDF-HFP, ensuring that the polymer flowed into the template’s cavities. When the setup was brought to ambient temperature, the polymer that flowed into the cavities solidified and took the shape of the cavities present in the template. In the next step, the PVDF-HFP film was peeled off the template.

Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanostructures

A hydrothermal method was used to grow ZnO nanostructures on the prepared PVDF-HFP film. For this purpose, the imprinted PVDF-HFP film was initially treated with ZnO seed solution, and was then treated with the growth solution. ZnO seed solution was prepared by dissolving 1.0975 g of zinc acetate dihydrate (0.1 M) in 50 mL of isopropanol. This solution was stirred vigorously at 85 °C for 15 min. Then, triethylamine (0.1 M) was added dropwise to this solution until it turned transparent. The reaction temperature was maintained for another 10 min before allowing the mixture to age for 3 h at room temperature. Equimolar solutions of zinc nitrate hexahydrate and hexamethylenetetramine were prepared for the growth solution. Initially, 0.3505 g of hexamethylenetetramine (0.025 M) was dissolved in 100 ml of deionized water. Once fully mixed, 0.7436 g of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.025 M) was added, and the solution was stirred continuously for 24 h.

The PVDF-HFP films (both with and without the micropillars) were immersed in ZnO seed solution to grow the ZnO nanostructure for 8 min. The films were rinsed with ethanol to remove excess material, heat-treated at 120 °C for 1 h, and left to dry in the air for 24 h. The prepared films were immersed in a zinc oxide (ZnO) growth solution, placed in a sealed glass vial, and heated at 95 °C for 8 h. During this stage, the seed layer interacts with the growth solution, initiating the hydrolysis of hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA). This process releases hydroxide ions (OH⁻), which react with zinc ions (Zn²⁺) to form zinc hydroxide (Zn(OH)₂). This compound subsequently dehydrates to produce ZnO. During the hydrothermal process, the pre-deposited ZnO seed nanoparticles on the surface of PVDF-HFP films served as nucleation sites. Exposing the films to the growth solution resulted in the growth of ZnO. In the next step, the vial was cooled to room temperature for approximately 12 h. The samples were then thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove any residual reagents and dried under ambient conditions.

Antibacterial property characterization

Four samples were investigated for their antibacterial properties. The first was a neat PVDF-HFP film without surface micropillars, which was used as the control sample. The second sample was PVDF-HFP film with surface micropillars. In the third sample, ZnO nanostructures were grown on the neat PVDF-HFP film using a hydrothermal method. This sample is referred to as PVDF-HFP@ZnO film without micropillars. The fourth sample consisted of PVDF-HFP film with surface micropillars and ZnO nanostructures, referred to as the PVDF-HFP@ZnO film with micropillars. The antibacterial properties were evaluated against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) ATCC 25923 and Escherichia coli (E. coli) ATCC 11303.

The bacterial strain was cultured in Lysogeny Broth (LB) overnight under controlled conditions (37 °C, 200 rpm). The bacterial suspension was then diluted in LB medium to achieve an OD600 of approximately 0.01 for the antibacterial inhibition assay. The samples were cut into small squares (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) and were exposed under UV light for 15 min on each side. Each sample was placed in one well (48 well-plate) before being immersed in 300 μl of the bacterial suspension to test antibacterial activity. The 48-well plate was incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Following incubation, the samples were rinsed with PBS to prepare them for confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

The viability of bacterial cells was analyzed using CLSM after staining with the Live/Dead™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (L7012, Invitrogen, USA) containing SYTO 9 and propidium iodide (PI). A 10 μl aliquot of the dye solution was applied to the sample surface, followed by a 15-min incubation in the dark. SYTO 9 penetrates all bacterial cells, binding to nucleic acids and emitting green fluorescence, whereas PI selectively binds to nucleic acids in compromised or dead cells, emitting a red fluorescence. The CLSM was configured with a dual-emission filter to distinguish live cells (green, SYTO 9: Ex/Em 480/500 nm) and dead cells (red, PI: Ex/Em 490/635 nm). For each sample, three surface images were randomly selected to quantify the viable-to-dead cells ratio. The antibacterial activity was assessed by analyzing these images using Zen Black (v2.3) and ImageJ (v1.54 f) software, calculating the green and red fluorescence percentages to determine bacterial viability. The bacterial viability was generated by using GraphPad Prism software.

Results and discussion

The PVDF-HFP film with surface micropillars provides a uniform and well-defined foundation for the growth of ZnO nanostructures, resulting in ZnO microcrystal flowers distributed uniformly across the film surface. This uniform distribution ensures an even surface structure and prevents the clustering of ZnO in localized areas [23, 24]. Both the physical and chemical properties of ZnO microcrystal flowers can effectively contribute to the antibacterial efficacy of the material against various types of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, such as S. aureus and E. coli [25, 26]. The sharp edges of these microcrystals act as physical blades, cutting through bacterial membranes, while the chemical properties of ZnO facilitate the release of Zn²⁺ ions, causing further disruption to bacterial cells [19, 27].

Figure 1 shows the scanning electron microscopy image of the surface topography of the film produced using nanoimprint lithography. The presence of surface micropillars on the film is evident from the SEM image. The diameters of the micropillars are determined using ImageJ software. The average diameter of the micropillars is found to be ~5 µm.

SEM image of the surface micropillars produced on PVDF-HFP films

The micropillars produced on the surface of the PVDF-HFP film using nanoimprint lithography are highly uniform and consistent across the sample surface. This uniformity ensures that the film provides a well-defined platform for ZnO nanostructure growth [24, 28]. The film sample is subsequently used to grow ZnO structures via a hydrothermal method. Figure 2 shows the SEM image of the sample with ZnO nanostructures. It is evident from Fig. 2 that ZnO microcrystal flowers are uniformly distributed across the film’s surface. This should help to enhance the overall surface area available for antibacterial action [29]. The uniformity of the ZnO distribution is critical for enhancing the contact area by ensuring that a higher surface area is presented to interact with bacterial cells [30]. It will also ensure that a consistent antibacterial performance is displayed across the surface of the sample [31].

ZnO nanoparticles grown on the micropillars of the PVDF-HFP film

Following this, the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) spectrum is recorded to determine and confirm the formation of ZnO. Figure 3 shows the EDX spectrum recorded for the sample. It is clear from the spectrum that the sample has the presence of various elements. For example, the presence of zinc (Zn), oxygen (O), and chlorine (Cl) is clearly visible. The spectrum shows strong signals corresponding to Zn and O elements. The peaks that are observed at 0.5 keV and 1.0 keV can be attributed to the emissions from the K-shell of O and the L-shell of Zn, respectively. Thus, the result confirms that the hydrothermal method led to the formation of ZnO structures on film samples.

EDX spectrum of PVDF-HFP/ZnO film

In the next phase of the study, the antibacterial properties of PVDF-HFP films featuring ZnO nanostructures (PVDF-HFP@ZnO) and surface micropillars were evaluated and compared against PVDF-HFP without surface micropillars, PVDF-HFP with surface micropillars, and PVDF-HFP@ZnO without surface micropillars. Antibacterial efficacy was assessed using the Live/Dead™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit to stain bacterial cells, followed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. 4). Staphylococcus aureus was found to be densely populated on both the neat PVDF-HFP film without micropillars and the neat micropillar-structured PVDF-HFP film (Fig. 4A and C), with bacterial viability recorded at 95.72% ± 0.74% and 91.3% ± 0.84%, respectively. In contrast, the PVDF-HFP films incorporating ZnO nanostructures exhibited markedly enhanced antibacterial activity. Both PVDF-HFP@ZnO samples with and without surface micropillars effectively inhibited bacterial colonization, with significantly reduced bacterial viability of 3.80% ± 6.0% and 4.76% ± 8.2%, respectively (P < 0.0001). These findings underscore the strong antibacterial effect of ZnO nanostructures and confirm that their integration onto PVDF-HFP substrates substantially enhances antimicrobial performance against S. aureus. Notably, the PVDF-HFP@ZnO film with surface micropillars exhibited the highest antibacterial efficacy, which is attributed to the increased surface area and more uniform distribution of ZnO nanostructures enabled by the micropillar architecture.

A similar trend is observed with Escherichia coli (Fig. 4B and C). The PVDF-HFP@ZnO film with surface micropillars exhibited markedly enhanced antibacterial activity against the Gram-negative pathogen. The viability of E. coli on this surface is reduced to just 1.36% ± 0.98%, indicating near-complete bacterial inhibition. In contrast, the PVDF-HFP@ZnO film without surface micropillars demonstrated significantly lower antibacterial efficacy, with E. coli viability recorded at 59.5% ± 3.7% (P < 0.0001). Meanwhile, both the neat PVDF-HFP film without surface micropillars and the neat micropillar-structured PVDF-HFP film without ZnO nanostructures showed substantial bacterial survival, with viability levels of 91.8% ± 2.69% and 91.6% ± 7.0%, respectively. This performance highlights the synergistic contributions of both physical and chemical mechanisms in combating bacterial pathogens [32]. The dual antibacterial mechanisms of the PVDF-HFP@ZnO film are particularly noteworthy. Thapliyal et al. and Hamouda et al. report similar results. [33, 34]. Their results show that the sharp edges of the ZnO microcrystals act as physical blades and help to disrupt bacterial membranes upon contact, mechanically [33, 34]. This physical damage compromises the structural integrity of bacterial cells, making them more vulnerable to additional antibacterial effects [35]. Concurrently, the chemical properties of ZnO contribute to the release of Zn²⁺ ions, which penetrate bacterial cells and interfere with vital enzymatic and metabolic processes [19, 36]. These physical and chemical mechanisms of ZnO effectively neutralize S. aureus and E. coli bacterial pathogens, significantly reducing bacterial viability [20, 37, 38]. Our results show that the neat PVDF-HFP film without surface micropillars and the neat PVDF-HFP film with surface micropillars do not display any antibacterial properties. When ZnO nanostructures are grown on the surface of these samples, they display good antibacterial performance.

The observed antibacterial performance of the PVDF-HFP@ZnO films aligns with previously reported mechanisms of ZnO’s antibacterial action, including membrane disruption, ion release, and enzymatic inhibition [19–21]. However, this study uniquely demonstrates the effectiveness of combining ZnO nanostructures with micropillar-imprinted PVDF-HFP substrates, showcasing the advantages of surface texturing in enhancing material performance. The imprinted micropillars increase the effective surface area available for ZnO deposition, allowing for a denser and more uniform distribution of ZnO nanostructures [24, 39, 40].

Notably, PVDF-HFP@ZnO films with micropillars reduced E. coli viability to just 1.36% ± 0.98%, compared to 59.5% ± 3.7% for films without micropillars. This highlights the role of surface patterning in improving antibacterial efficacy, especially against Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli, which possess an outer membrane that can limit the interaction with antibacterial agents [41]. The micropillars overcome this barrier by enhancing contact-mediated stress and enabling more effective penetration of Zn²⁺ ions and ROS [29]. Integrating micro- and nanoscale features improves antibacterial efficacy and offers a scalable and reproducible approach for fabricating advanced antibacterial materials [42–45]. It is interesting to note that the PVDF-HFP@ZnO films reduced E. coli viability more effectively than S. aureus. This can be attributed to the way ZnO interacts with the bacterial cells. E. coli has a thinner peptidoglycan layer, which suggests that it is more susceptible to mechanical rupture by high-aspect ratio ZnO nanostructures. Additionally, ZnO nanostructures generates ROS that readily affect E. coli bacterial cells. The combined effect may explain the better performance of the samples against E. coli. Nevertheless, our results show that PVDF-HFP@ZnO film’s possesses the ability to combat both major foodborne pathogens S. aureus and E. coli [13]. They show tremendous potential for use as antibacterial materials in food packaging.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the antibacterial behaviour of zinc oxide (ZnO) nanostructures grown on PVDF-HFP micropillars. These samples are shown to effectively combat both S. aureus and E. coli. The results show that the antibacterial properties are enhanced when surface micropillars are produced on the PVDF-HFP film before growing ZnO nanostructures. The presence of micropillars on PVDF-HFP film increases surface area and promotes closer contact between the bacterial cells and ZnO nanostructures. This played a role in membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and the interaction of zinc ions with the bacterial cells. Thus, the combination of microtopography and ZnO nanostructures enhances the overall antibacterial activity of ZnO. Interestingly, E. coli is more susceptible than S. aureus. One plausible reason is that E. coli has a thinner peptidoglycan layer, making it more vulnerable to mechanical rupture and more sensitive to ROS and ion effects. These findings demonstrate the potential of using PVDF-HFP@ZnO films on surfaces prone to microbial contamination, such as medical devices and food packaging.
