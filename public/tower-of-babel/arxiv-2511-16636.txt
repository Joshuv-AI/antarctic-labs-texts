Abstract
. The lonely runner conjecture of Wills and Cusick asserts that if n n runners with distinct constant speeds run around the unit circle, starting at a common time and place, then each runner will at some time be separated by a distance of at least 1 n \frac{1}{n} from all other runners. A weaker lower bound of 1 2 ​ n − 2 \frac{1}{2n-2} follows from the so-called trivial union bound, and subsequent work upgraded this to bounds of the form 1 2 ​ n + c n 2 \frac{1}{2n}+\frac{c}{n^{2}} for various constants c > 0 c>0 . Tao strengthened this to 1 2 ​ n + ( log ⁡ n ) 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) n 2 \frac{1}{2n}+\frac{(\log n)^{1-o(1)}}{n^{2}} . In this paper, we obtain a polynomial improvement of the form 1 2 ​ n + 1 n 5 / 3 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) . \frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n^{5/3+o(1)}}.



1.
Introduction

The Lonely Runner Conjecture is an influential research question that was first raised by Wills
[
34
]
in the context of Diophantine approximation, and independently by Cusick
[
13
]
in the context of view-obstruction problems. Its name comes from the following imaginative reformulation due to
Goddyn
[
6
]
. Consider
n
runners moving around the unit circle
with distinct constant speeds, starting at a common time and place. Then the conjecture
states that each runner will at some time be separated by a distance of at least
\frac{1}{n}
from all other runners. Let us identify the unit circle with
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
, and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbf{T}}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer. This allows us to formulate the Lonely Runner Conjecture as follows.

Conjecture 1.1

(Lonely Runner Conjecture)
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathbf{N}
be any
n
distinct positive integers. For each
i\in[n]
, there exists a ‘time’
t\in\mathbf{R}
such that

\min_{j\neq i}\lVert t(w_{i}-w_{j})\rVert_{\mathbf{T}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}.

The original version of this conjecture asserts that such a conclusion holds whenever the
w_{j}
are distinct real numbers. It is well-known however (see for example
[
7
, Section 4]
) that this seemingly more general version is equivalent to the integer version in Conjecture
1.1
. As the problem is invariant under translations of the speed set
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\}
, we may without loss of generality subtract the speed of the runner under consideration, thereby reducing their speed to zero. Hence, Conjecture
1.1
for
n+1
runners is equivalent to proving that, given any
n
distinct positive integers
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
, there is a ‘time’
t\in\mathbf{R}
such that

\displaystyle\min_{j=1,\dots,n}\lVert tv_{j}\rVert_{\mathbf{T}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n+1}.

Since all speeds
v_{j}
are integers, it suffices to consider
t\in\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
. This suggests defining the following quantity
1
1

1

This notation is taken from a paper of Kravitz
[
19
]
.
for any set
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
of
n
distinct positive integers:

(1)

\displaystyle\ML(V)=\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\vcentcolon=\max_{t\in\mathbf{T}}\left(\min_{j=1,\dots,n}\lVert tv_{j}\rVert_{\mathbf{T}}\right),

where ‘ML’ stands for the maximum loneliness.
This leads us to the following succinct formulation.

Conjecture 1.2

(Lonely Runner Conjecture)
.

If
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
are
n
distinct positive integers, then
\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{n+1}
.

We remark that, if true, this lower bound would be optimal since a simple check confirms that
\ML(1,2,\dots,n)=\frac{1}{n+1}
. There is a vast literature on the Lonely Runner Conjecture, spanning a wide range of topics such as Diophantine approximation (e.g.
[
5
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
), view-obstruction/billiard trajectory problems (e.g.
[
13
,
31
,
11
]
), chromatic numbers
of distance graphs (e.g.
[
17
,
9
,
20
,
21
,
36
]
), and flows in matroids (e.g.
[
6
]
). We refer the reader to the very recent survey of Perarnau and Serra
[
26
]
for a more comprehensive overview.

The principal lines of attack on the Lonely Runner Conjecture can be grouped into three categories.

(I) Few runners:
Conjecture
1.2
has been confirmed for
n\leqslant 7
(corresponding to at most eight runners in Conjecture
1.1
), see
[
7
,
1
,
27
,
12
,
5
,
28
]
.

(II) Stronger assumptions on the speeds:
It has been established that Conjecture
1.2
holds for sets
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
satisfying one of various additional assumptions, such as being lacunary (e.g.
[
30
,
2
,
24
,
16
,
15
]
), or random-like (e.g.
[
14
]
).

(III) Increasing the ‘gap of loneliness’:

The starting point here is the classical observation that for any
n
positive integers
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, a trivial union bound shows that

\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{2n},

which gets within a factor of two of Conjecture
1.2
. To see this, suppose for a contradiction that
\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})=\delta<1/2n
. This means that for every
t\in\mathbf{T}
, there exists some
j
such that
tv_{j}\in I:=[-\delta,\delta]\subset\mathbf{T}
. However, the interval
I
has measure
2\delta<1/n
and hence, if we pick
t\in\mathbf{T}
uniformly at random, then a union bound shows that

\mathbb{P}(tv_{j}\in I\text{ for some }j\in[n])\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(tv_{j}\in I)=n\times 2\delta<1,

giving the required contradiction. Surprisingly, for all large
n
, only small improvements over this crude union bound
\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}
have been obtained.
Chen
[
11
]
showed that

\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{2n-1+\frac{1}{2n-3}},

and Chen and Cusick
[
10
]
improved this to

\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{2n-3}
if
2n-3
is prime. Pararnau and Serra
[
25
]
established the bound

(2)

\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{2n-2+o(1)}.

Note that all of the bounds above are (essentially) of the form
\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{c}{n^{2}}
for various constants
c
. Tao
[
32
]
was the first to show that one may replace this
c
by a function
(\log n)^{1-o(1)}
which grows with
n
, proving that

(3)

\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+c^{\prime}\frac{\log n}{n^{2}(\log\log n)^{2}}

for an absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
Our contribution is the following improvement for the ‘gap of loneliness’, strengthening the factor
(\log n)^{1-o(1)}
to polynomial growth of order
n^{1/3-o(1)}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
V
be a set of
n
distinct positive integers. Then

\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n^{5/3+o(1)}}.

Along the way, we prove several intermediate results which may be of independent interest, such as Lemma
4.1
which confirms the Lonely Runner Conjecture for every dissociated set (see Definition
2.2
), and Proposition
2.3
which proves strong bounds for
\ML(V)
whenever
V
has rather large additive dimension (also see Definition
2.2
).

2.
Overview of the proof

We continue with an overview of the main ideas in our approach. So consider an arbitrary fixed collection
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathbf{N}
of
n
distinct positive integers, and let
\delta:=\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
. Define the Bohr sets

B(v_{j};\delta):=\{t\in\mathbf{T}:\lVert tv_{j}\rVert_{\mathbf{T}}\leqslant\delta\}.

Observe that as
\delta=\ML(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, there must for every
t\in\mathbf{T}
exist a
j\in[n]
such that
\lVert tv_{j}\rVert_{\mathbf{T}}\leqslant\delta
, and hence the Bohr sets
B(v_{j};\delta)
cover all of
\mathbf{T}
:

(4)

\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}B(v_{j};\delta)=\mathbf{T}.

Pararnau and Serra
[
25
]
obtained their improvement (
2
) over the trivial bound for the gap of loneliness by analysing the rank 2 Bohr sets

B(v_{j},v_{k};\delta):=B(v_{j};\delta)\cap B(v_{k};\delta).

Tao’s approach further exploits control on rank 3 Bohr sets, showing that sets
V
for which
\ML(V)
is ‘close’ to the trivial bound must be additively structured. He then uses a direct argument based on an analysis of the possible prime factors of such structured sets
V
to see that
\ML(V)
still exceeds the trivial union bound. However, Tao remarks (see the discussion following Theorem 1.2 in
[
32
]
) that his methods would require significantly more effort to allow one to improve the single power of
\log n
in (
3
).

The approach in this paper is rather different, and partially inspired by the structure vs. randomness dichotomy based on additive dimension that appears in the author’s recent work
[
3
]
on sum-free sets. Recall that
\delta:=\ML(V)
. Define the indicator function
\phi:\mathbf{T}\to\{0,1\}:\phi(x)=\mathbf{1}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(x)
, and let

(5)

\Phi_{V,\delta}(x):=\sum_{v\in V}\phi(vx).

We shall often simply write
\Phi(x)=\Phi_{V,\delta}(x)
, and it will be clear from context what
V
is (and we always take
\delta=\ML(V)
). Observe that
\phi(vx)=\mathbf{1}_{B(v;\delta)}(x)
and hence that (
4
) is the equivalent to the assertion that
\Phi(x)\geqslant 1
for all
x\in\mathbf{T}
. The proof of the trivial union bound can therefore be rewritten as

1\leqslant\int_{\mathbf{T}}\Phi(x)\,dx=\sum_{v\in V}\int_{\mathbf{T}}\phi(vx)\,dx=n\int_{\mathbf{T}}\phi(u)\,du=2\delta n,

so that
\ML(V)=\delta\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}
. The starting point of our approach is to exploit the stronger fact that
\Phi(x)\geqslant 1
holds pointwise, by testing
\Phi
against an arbitrary probability measure (rather than the uniform measure on
\mathbf{T}
that is used in the trivial bound). We write
M(\mathbf{T})
for the set of regular Borel measures on
\mathbf{T}
, and recall that
\mu\in M(\mathbf{T})
is said to be a probability measure if it is nonnegative and
\int_{\mathbf{T}}\,d\mu(x)=1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbf{N}
have size
n
, and suppose that
\ML(V)=\delta
. Let
\Phi
be the function defined in (
5
). Then any probability measure
\mu\in M(\mathbf{T})
satisfies

\int_{\mathbf{T}}\Phi(x)\,d\mu(x)\geqslant 1.

Proof of
Lemma

2.1
.

As
\ML(V)=\delta
and
\phi(vx)=\mathbf{1}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(vx)=\mathbf{1}_{B(v;\delta)}(x)
for each
v\in V
, we see that

\Phi(x)=\sum_{v\in V}\phi(vx)=\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{1}_{B(v;\delta)}(x)\geqslant\mathbf{1}_{\bigcup_{v\in V}B(v;\delta)}(x)=1,\quad\forall x\in\mathbf{T},

by (
4
). Hence, we find that
\int_{\mathbf{T}}\Phi(x)\,d\mu(x)\geqslant\int_{\mathbf{T}}\,d\mu(x)=1
as
\mu
is a probability measure, so nonnegative with
\mu(\mathbf{T})=1
.
∎

The structure vs. randomness dichotomy that we shall employ is based on the additive dimension
\dim(V)
of
V
. In this paper, it is convenient (though not necessary) to work with the notion of
2
-dissociativity and
\dim_{2}(V)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbf{N}
. A set
D\subseteq\mathbf{Z}
is
k
-dissociated
if whenever

\sum_{d\in D}\varepsilon_{d}d=0

for some
\varepsilon_{d}\in\{-k,\dots,-1,0,1,\dots,k\}
, then all
\varepsilon_{d}=0
. The
additive
k
-dimension
of a set
V\subset\mathbf{Z}
, denoted by
\dim_{k}(V)
, is the size of its largest
k
-dissociated subset
D\subset V
.
2
2

2

We point out that the maximal (with respect to inclusion)
k
-dissociated subsets of
V
might not all have the same size.

The standard notions of dissociativity and additive dimension
\dim(V)
correspond to the case where
k=1
. Throughout this paper however, we shall exclusively work with
\dim_{2}(V)
and so we shall simply refer to this quantity as the additive dimension of
V
.

If
V
has rather large additive dimension, then we are able to construct an explicit probability measure
\mu
(more precisely, a Riesz product, see (
13
)) which, through applying Lemma
2.1
, witnesses that
\delta=\ML(V)
is large.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
V
be a set of
n
distinct positive integers. Then

\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{\dim_{2}(V)^{2}}{n^{3+o(1)}}.

If
\dim_{2}(V)
is rather small, we take a different approach. Using similar ideas as in
[
3
]
, we will show that sets
V
with small additive dimension have a ‘denser model’
V^{\prime}
. In the context of the Lonely Runner Conjecture, we consider a set
V^{\prime}
to be a model for
V
if
\ML(V)=\ML(V^{\prime})
, (up to a negligible error). We remark that Tao
[
32
, Theorem 1.3]
proved the interesting fact that in order to confirm Conjecture
1.2
for sets of size
n
, it suffices to check all sets
V
of size
n
that are contained in
[n^{O(n^{2})}]
, thus in principle reducing the Lonely Runner Conjecture for a fixed number of runners to a finite computation. His argument essentially proceeds by showing that every set
V
of size
n
has a model
V^{\prime}
(in the sense we described above) where
V^{\prime}\subset[n^{O(n^{2})}]
. This bound was recently improved by Malikiosis, Santos and Schymura in
[
23
]
, showing that it suffices to check sets
V^{\prime}\subset[n^{2n}]
, and it is this fact and a partial computer verification that was recently used by Rosenfeld
[
28
]
to verify Conjecture
1.2
for
n=7
.

The content of the next proposition is that one may find a considerably denser model if
V
has small additive dimension.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
V
be a set of
n
distinct positive integers. Then there exists a set
V^{\prime}\subset\mathbf{N}
of
n
distinct integers satisfying:

(i)

V^{\prime}\subset\{1,2\dots,T\}
where
T=e^{\dim_{2}(V)(\log n)^{O(1)}}
,

(ii)

\ML(V)\geqslant\ML(V^{\prime})-O(\frac{1}{n^{100}})
.

Remark
.

The choice of the error term
n^{-100}
is rather arbitrary, and could be replaced by
n^{-C}
for any large constant
C
. In either case, this term is negligible as all improvements for
\ML(V)
are at least of order
n^{-2}
.

The final step in the argument then consist of proving a strong bound for
\ML(V)
for sets
V
which are contained in a relatively short initial interval
\{1,2,\dots,T\}
of
\mathbf{N}
. Note that we only need to consider
T\leqslant e^{n^{1+o(1)}}
by using the trivial bound
\dim_{2}(V)\leqslant|V|=n
in the previous proposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds. Let
V
be a set of
n
distinct positive integers, and suppose that
V\subset\{1,2,\dots,T\}
for some integer
T\leqslant e^{n^{1+o(1)}}
. Then
\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{c}{n\log T}
.

We note that some related results already exist in the literature: Tao
[
32
, Proposition 1.6]
proves that if
V\subset[Cn]
for some constant
C>0
, then one gets the linear improvement over the trivial union bound
\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1+\Omega_{C}(1)}{2n}
. This is very similar to the conclusion of Proposition
2.5
, and we shall show at the end of Section
5
that a minor modification of our argument recovers this result of Tao, in fact with a better dependence on
C
. We also note that the argument of Tao only works when
T\leqslant Cn=O(n)
, i.e. when
V
genuinely has positive density as a subset of
[T]
, whereas for our application it is crucial that our version permits
T
to be as large as
e^{n^{1+o(1)}}
. Finally, we remark that Tao
[
32
, Proposition 1.5]
showed that if
V\subset[6n/5]
is very dense in an initial interval of
\mathbf{N}
, then
V
satisfies the conjectured bound
\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{n+1}
.

Let us finish this overview by showing that Theorem
1.3
can be deduced from Propositions
2.3
,
2.4
and
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
, assuming Propositions
2.3
,
2.4
, and
2.5
.

Let
V
be an arbitrary set of
n
distinct positive integers. If
\dim_{2}(V)\geqslant n^{2/3}
, then Proposition
2.3
shows that
\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n^{5/3+o(1)}}
. If
\dim_{2}(V)\leqslant n^{2/3}
on the other hand, then
Proposition

2.4
allows us to find a model
V^{\prime}\subset[e^{n^{2/3+o(1)}}]
such that
\ML(V)\geqslant\ML(V^{\prime})-O(1/n^{100})
. By applying Proposition
2.5
with
T=e^{n^{2/3+o(1)}}
to
V^{\prime}
, we see that
\ML(V^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n^{5/3+o(1)}}
. This also yields the claimed lower bound

\ML(V)\geqslant\ML(V^{\prime})-O\left(\frac{1}{n^{100}}\right)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1}{n^{5/3+o(1)}}.

∎

3.
Notation and prerequisites

We use the asymptotic notation
f=O(g)
,
f\ll g
, or
g=\Omega(f)
if there is an absolute constant
C
such that
|f(y)|\leqslant Cg(y)
for all
y
in a certain domain which will be clear from context. We write
f=o(g)
if
f(y)/g(y)\to 0
as
y\to\infty
, and we write
f\asymp g
if both
f\ll g
and
g\ll f
.

We write
\mathbf{N},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{C}
for the natural, integer, real, and complex numbers, respectively. For two sets
E_{1},E_{2}
, we define the sumset
E_{1}+E_{2}:=\{e_{1}+e_{2}:e_{1}\in E_{1},e_{2}\in E_{2}\}
and difference set
E_{1}-E_{2}=\{e_{1}-e_{2}:e_{1}\in E_{1},e_{2}\in E_{2}\}
. We use the standard notation
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
for
x\in\mathbf{R}
, and, as this function is
1
-periodic, it is natural to consider the domain of the variable
x
to be
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
. For a suitably integrable function
g:\mathbf{T}\to\mathbf{C}
we denote, for
p\in[1,\infty)
, its
L^{p}
-norm by

\lVert g\rVert_{p}\vcentcolon=\left(\int_{0}^{1}|g(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p},

and
\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}
is the smallest constant
M
such that
|g(x)|\leqslant M
holds almost everywhere. Its Fourier coefficients are given by
\hat{g}:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
which is defined by
\hat{g}(n)=\int_{\mathbf{T}}g(x)e(-nt)\,dx
. If
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
is a function, we shall denote its Fourier transform by
\hat{f}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}f(n)e(nx)
which is a priori simply a formal series.

For two functions
g,h\in L^{2}(\mathbf{T})
we define
\langle g,h\rangle=\int_{\mathbf{T}}g(x)\overline{h(x)}\,dx
and we shall frequently make use of Parseval’s theorem which states that
\langle g,h\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}\hat{g}(n)\overline{\hat{h}(n)}
. We also define their convolution to be the function
g*h=\mathbf{T}\to\mathbf{C}
given by
(g*h)(x)=\int_{\mathbf{T}}g(x-y)h(y)\,dy
, and we note the basic fact that
\widehat{g*h}(n)=\hat{g}(n)\hat{h}(n)
.

4.
Lonely Runner for sets with large additive dimension

In this section, we prove Proposition
2.3
which provides strong bounds for the gap of loneliness whenever the set
V\subset\mathbf{N}
of speeds has rather large additive dimension. More precisely, we will show that any
V\subset\mathbf{N}
of size
n
satisfies

(6)

\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{\dim_{2}(V)^{2}}{n^{3+o(1)}}.

As a warm-up, we employ this strategy to give a short proof of the following lemma, establishing the Lonely Runner Conjecture
1.2
for dissociated sets
V
(the notion of
1
-dissociativity is enough here). In fact we will show not only that
\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{n+1}
for dissociated sets
V
, but that dissociated sets in fact have a much larger gap of loneliness
\ML(V)\gg 1/\sqrt{n}
. We remark that this lemma (or a minor variation) already recovers several results in
[
24
,
14
,
30
]
, which study the Lonely Runner Conjecture under various lacunarity assumptions. For example, it is an elementary fact that sets
V
satisfying
v_{j+1}\geqslant 2v_{j}
for all
j
are dissociated, and hence the following lemma immediately implies the main result of
[
24
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
V\subset\mathbf{N}
be a dissociated set of size
n
. Then
\ML(V)\gg 1/{\sqrt{n}}
.

Proof.

Consider the Riesz product
R(x)=\prod_{v\in V}(1-\cos(2\pi vx))
which is clearly a pointwise nonnegative function on
\mathbf{T}
. As
\cos(2\pi vx)=\frac{e(vx)+e(-vx)}{2}
, expanding out the product shows that

R(x)=\sum_{(\varepsilon_{v})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{V}}\prod_{v\in V}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{|\varepsilon_{v}|}e(\varepsilon_{v}vx).

The only contributions to the constant term come from those
V
-tuples
(\varepsilon_{v})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{V}
for which
\sum_{v\in V}\varepsilon_{v}v=0
, which is only possible if all
\varepsilon_{v}=0
because
V
is dissociated. So
\widehat{R}(0)=1
. Hence,
R(x)
is a nonnegative function with
\int_{\mathbf{T}}R(x)\,dx=1
, so
d\mu(t):=R(t)\,dt
is a probability measure. Let
\delta=\ML(V)
and define
\Phi(x)
as in (
5
). Lemma
2.1
then shows that
1\leqslant\int_{\mathbf{T}}\Phi(x)R(x)\,dx
. On the other hand, note that for any
v\in\mathbf{N}
we can bound

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbf{T}}\phi(vx)(1-\cos(2\pi vx))

\displaystyle=\sup_{u\in[-\delta,\delta]}\big(1-\cos(2\pi u)\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\cos(2\pi\delta)\ll\delta^{2},

because
\phi(vx)=\mathbf{1}_{[-\delta,\delta]}(vx)
by definition. This allows us to bound

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbf{T}}\Phi(x)R(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\int_{\mathbf{T}}\bigg(\phi(vx)(1-\cos(2\pi vx))\bigg)\prod_{w\neq v}(1-\cos(2\pi wx))\,dx

\displaystyle\ll\delta^{2}\sum_{v\in V}\int_{\mathbf{T}}\prod_{w\neq v}(1-\cos 2\pi wx)\,dx

\displaystyle=n\delta^{2},

where the final equality uses that each of the sets
V\setminus\{v\}
is trivially still dissociated, and so has a Riesz product with constant term
1
(using exactly the same argument as above). This yields the claimed bound that
\ML(V)=\delta\gg\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
∎

Let us now move to the general case, so consider an arbitrary fixed set
V\subset\mathbf{N}
of size

p>Cn\geqslant\max(V)
,
p
trivially does not divide any element of
V
, so applying Corollary
5.4
with
c=1/2
shows that

\ML(V)\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}+\frac{1/2}{p}\geqslant\left(1+\Omega\left(\frac{1}{C}\right)\right)\frac{1}{2n}.

Appendix

In this appendix, we provide a short combinatorial proof of the following lemma, giving the following slightly weaker (but still sufficient) bound in (
19
) compared to Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma
.

Let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a
2
-dissociated set, and define

E_{k}=\left\{\sum_{a\in A}\varepsilon_{a}a:\varepsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}\text{ and }\sum_{a}|\varepsilon_{a}|=k\right\}.

Then any arithmetic progression
P
contains at most
|E_{k}\cap P|\leqslant(C_{1}(\log|P|)(\log|A|))^{k}
elements of
E_{k}
,
where
C_{1}
is an absolute constant.

Our argument is based on the recent progress in
[
4
]
on the sunflower problem of Erdős and Rado. A
sunflower
of size
r
is a collection of sets
U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{r}
such that all their pairwise intersections
U_{i}\cap U_{j}
with
i\neq j
are equal. This is equivalent to the existence of a set
X
, called the
kernel
of the sunflower, which is a subset of each
U_{i}
and such that the sets
U_{1}\setminus X,\dots,U_{r}\setminus X
are pairwise disjoint. Let
S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell}
be any collection of sets of size
k
. The main result of
[
4
]
states that
\{S_{1},\dots,S_{\ell}\}
either contains a sunflower of size
r
, or else that

(30)

\ell\leqslant(C_{2}r\log k)^{k},

where
C_{2}
is an absolute constant that works for all
k,\ell
.

Proof of (
19
).

Let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be
2
-dissociated, and define
E_{k}
as in (
18
). The case where
k=1
is easy: if
B=A\cap P
, then the
2^{|B|}
distinct sums
\sum_{b\in S}b
, with
S
ranging over all subsets of
B
, are contained in
\underbrace{P+\dots+P}_{|B|}
, which is itself an arithmetic progression of size at most
|B||P|
. As
A
is
2
-dissociated (in fact,
1
-dissociativity suffices here), these
2^{|B|}
subset sums are distinct so that
2^{|B|}\leqslant|B||P|
. This shows that
|B|\ll\log|P|
.

Now suppose that
k\in[2,|A|]
and that
B=P\cap E_{k}
. By definition of
E_{k}
, each
b\in B
is a sum of
k
elements of
E_{1}=A\cup-A
:

(31)

b=\sum_{z\in S_{b}}z,

for some subsets
S_{b}\subset E_{1}
of size
k
. As
A
is
2
-dissociated, these ‘support’ sets
S_{b}
are uniquely determined. Now we apply the sunflower result (
30
) to the collection of sets
\{S_{b}:b\in B\}
, noting that all
S_{b}
have size
k
, and with
r=C_{3}\log|P|
for some absolute constant
C_{3}
to be chosen later. Hence, we either find that

|B|\leqslant(C_{2}C_{3}(\log|P|)(\log k))^{k}\leqslant(C_{2}C_{3}(\log|P|)(\log|A|))^{k}

in which case we are done, or else we can find a sunflower
(S_{b})_{b\in B^{\prime}}
for some
B^{\prime}\subset B
of size
|B^{\prime}|=r=C_{3}\log|P|
. So it suffices to show that the second option is impossible, for some sufficiently large fixed constant
C_{3}
. Suppose for a contradiction that the second option were true, so that all the sets
S_{b}
with
{b\in B^{\prime}}
contain a fixed kernel
X
, but are otherwise disjoint. Defining
t:=\sum_{x\in X}x
, we can rewrite (
31
) as

(32)

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in S_{b}\setminus X}z=b-t\in P-t

for every
b\in B^{\prime}
. As the sets
(S_{b})_{b\in B^{\prime}}
form a sunflower with kernel
X
, the numbers
b-t
with
b\in B^{\prime}
have pairwise disjoint ‘supports’
S_{b}\setminus X
, and they all lie in
P-t
, which is itself an arithmetic progression of size
|P|
. We will prove the following claim.

Claim
.

There exists a subset
B^{\prime\prime}\subset B^{\prime}
of size
|B^{\prime\prime}|\geqslant|B^{\prime}|/2
for which the set
\{b-t:b\in B^{\prime\prime}\}
is
1
-dissociated.

Note that as
\{b-t:b\in B^{\prime\prime}\}
is then a dissociated subset of
P-t
, repeating the argument in the first paragraph (for
k=1)
shows that
|B^{\prime\prime}|\ll\log|P-t|=\log|P|
. On the other hand,
|B^{\prime\prime}|\geqslant|B^{\prime}|/2=\frac{C_{3}}{2}\log|P|
, and hence this indeed gives the required contradiction upon choosing the constant
C_{3}
to be sufficiently large.

It only remains to prove the claim. Recall that the sets
S_{b}\subset A\cup-A
with
b\in B^{\prime}
form a sunflower with kernel
X
, so that the sets
S_{b}\setminus X
with
b\in B^{\prime}
are pairwise disjoint. We describe an algorithmic procedure for finding
B^{\prime\prime}
, which we initialise by taking
B^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
and
C=B^{\prime}
. While
|C|\geqslant 1
, we choose an arbitrary element
b\in C\subset B^{\prime}
and add it to
B^{\prime\prime}
. The sunflower condition shows that
S_{b}\setminus X
is disjoint from
\bigcup_{c\in C\setminus\{b\}}(S_{c}\setminus X)
.

Case I:
If
-(S_{b}\setminus X)
is also disjoint from
\bigcup_{c\in C\setminus\{b\}}(S_{c}\setminus X)
, then we simply remove
b
from
C
and go back to the first step (selecting again an arbitrary next element from the new set
C
).

Case II:
Else, there exists a
z\in A\cup-A
such that
z\in S_{b}\setminus X
and
-z\in(S_{b^{\prime}}\setminus X)
, for some unique
b^{\prime}\in C\subset B^{\prime}
. There may be multiple such
z\in A\cup-A
, in which case we choose one arbitrarily, but note that for each
z
the corresponding element
b^{\prime}
is unique (if it exists) since the sets
S_{b^{\prime}}\setminus X
with
b^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
are pairwise disjoint. In this case, we remove both
b
and
b^{\prime}
from
C
, and then go back to the first step.

The procedure starts with
C=B^{\prime}
and continues until
C
is empty. At each step, we add a new element
b
from
C
to
B^{\prime\prime}
while, in both cases I and II, we remove
b
and at most one other element from
C
. So clearly
|B^{\prime\prime}|\geqslant|B^{\prime}|/2
. To see why the set
\{b-t:b\in B^{\prime\prime}\}
is
1
-dissociated, suppose that

\sum_{b\in B^{\prime\prime}}\varepsilon_{b}(b-t)=0,

for some
\varepsilon_{b}\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Then by (
32
), we see that

(33)

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in B^{\prime\prime}}\varepsilon_{b}\sum_{z\in S_{b}\setminus X}z=0.

The sets
S_{b}\setminus X
with
b\in B^{\prime\prime}
are pairwise disjoint subsets of
A\cup-A
, so the relation above produces a relation between elements
a
of
A
with coefficients
\eta_{a}\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
. Note that even though
\varepsilon_{b}\in\{-1,0,1\}
for all
b\in B^{\prime\prime}
, it is still possible that
\eta_{a}\in\{-2,2\}
for some
a\in A
because of the possibility that
a\in S_{b_{1}}\setminus X
and
-a\in S_{b_{2}}\setminus X
, for two distinct
b_{1},b_{2}\in B^{\prime\prime}
. As
A
is
2
-dissociated, this implies that all
\eta_{a}
are
0
, and we will show that this forces all
\varepsilon_{b}
to vanish too. Let us write
B^{\prime\prime}=\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{|B^{\prime\prime}|}\}
, ordered such that
b_{1}
was chosen first in the algorithm above, then
b_{2}
, and so on. By the way we have constructed
B^{\prime\prime}
, for every
b_{j}\in B^{\prime\prime}
there exists a
z_{j}\in S_{b_{j}}\setminus X
such that

z_{j}\notin\bigcup_{i>j}((S_{b_{i}}\setminus X)\cup-(S_{b_{i}}\setminus X)).

So in particular,
z_{1}\in S_{b_{1}}\setminus X
but
z_{1}
does not lie in
\pm(S_{b}\setminus X)
for any other
b\in B^{\prime\prime}
, so it is clear from (
33
) that
\eta_{z_{1}}=\varepsilon_{b_{1}}
, and hence
\varepsilon_{b_{1}}=0
. Next, given that
\varepsilon_{b_{1}}=0
and that
z_{2}\in S_{b_{2}}\setminus X
while
z_{2}
does not lie in
\pm(S_{b_{i}}\setminus X)
for any
i>2
, we similarly deduce from (
33
) that
\eta_{z_{2}}=\varepsilon_{b_{2}}
, so that
\varepsilon_{b_{2}}=0
. Continuing in this way, we see that
\varepsilon_{b}=0
for all
b\in B^{\prime\prime}
, confirming our claim that
\{b-t:b\in B^{\prime\prime}\}
is
1
-dissociated.
