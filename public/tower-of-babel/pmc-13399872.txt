Trochleoplasty Is Associated With High Rates of Patellofemoral Arthritis: A Systematic Review
Authors: Cailan L Feingold, Eric H Lin, James C Dawahare, Andrew B Barcenas, Brandon A Young, Pranit Kumaran, Austin V Stone, Joseph N Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify 2‐ to 5‐ and >10‐year complications of trochleoplasty and define rates of postoperative patellofemoral arthritis.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were queried for terms related to trochleoplasty. Included studies were clinical studies using trochleoplasty to manage patellofemoral instability and were published in English in peer‐reviewed journals. Excluded studies were systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, cadaveric studies, animal studies, and case reports. Patient demographics, follow‐up time, concomitant procedures, complications, and subsequent surgeries were collected. Studies were grouped by follow‐up time into <2, 2 to 5, 5 to 10, and >10 years.ResultsThirty‐five studies met inclusion criteria with 1336 patients (64% female). There were 142 (10.6%) patients with less than 2 years of follow‐up, 871 (65.2%) with 2 to 5 years, 221 (16.5%) with 5 to 10 years, and 102 (7.6%) with more than 10 years. The complication rates ranged from 0% to 33%. Seven (20%) studies specifically reported on postoperative arthritis. In patients with <2 years, the most common complication was arthrofibrosis at rates of 0% to 6.3%. Patellofemoral arthritis was the most common complication in patients with 2 to 5 (0%‐35.3%), 5 to 10 (0%‐17.4%), and >10 years of follow‐up (0%‐92.3%). In patients with more than 10 years of follow‐up, rates of patellofemoral arthritis worse than Iwano grade 2 were as high as 65%. Recurrent instability rates varied widely across studies and follow‐up times from 0% to 33.3%.ConclusionsPatients undergoing trochleoplasty for patellar instability may experience low rates of recurrence but are prone to postoperative stiffness at <2‐ and 2‐ to 5‐year follow‐up. Complications following trochleoplasty in isolation are worse than when concomitant procedures are done. After 10 years, the incidence of all patellofemoral arthritis is as high as 92%, and more severe patellofemoral arthritis is as high as 65%, which is greater than the progression in patients with patellofemoral instability.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, systematic review of Level II to IV studies.

Patellar instability is a common orthopaedic condition affecting approximately 42 in 100,000 people with a high risk of recurrence. 1 Patellar instability can cause significant functional impairment and pain, resulting in worsened quality of life. One common risk factor for patellar instability is trochlear dysplasia, which can be found in approximately 10% of the general population and approximately 68% of patients with patellar instability. 2 Trochlear dysplasia is typically diagnosed using ultrasound, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or radiographic imaging. Measurements such as sulcus angle and trochlear depth are used to determine the severity of the dysplasia with the Dejour classification system of A‐D grading being the most widely used categorical system. 2 , 3 , 4 Trochlear dysplasia is an anatomic risk factor for patellar instability and may be surgically addressed with trochleoplasty in specific patients.

Trochleoplasty can be an effective surgical option that can be used to improve patellar tracking by reshaping the trochlear groove. 5 This procedure is indicated in patients with high‐grade trochlear dysplasia with recurrent instability who have failed nonsurgical treatment or prior surgical techniques such as medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction and tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO). 6 There are multiple trochleoplasty techniques, including Dejour, Bereiter, recession wedge, and sulcus deepening trochleoplasties, all of which aim to correct different variations of abnormal trochlear morphology. 7 MPFL reconstruction is often performed concomitantly to further address patellar malalignment and instability when indicated, and there are reports of combined trochleoplasty and TTO with MPFL reconstruction. 8 , 9

Multiple case series have further supported the use of trochleoplasty. 10 , 11 Its complication profile and timeline, including the progression to patellofemoral osteoarthritis, have only recently been examined in greater detail. 12 Thus, the purpose of this study was to identify 2‐ to 5‐ and >10‐year complications of trochleoplasty and define rates of postoperative patellofemoral arthritis. We hypothesized that patellofemoral arthritis would be found in half of the patients undergoing trochleoplasty at >10‐year follow‐up.

METHODS

The literature search was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines. 13 PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched using the following search strategy: “Trochleoplasty” AND (“patellar instability” OR “patellar dislocation” OR “trochlear dysplasia”). Studies were compiled in Covidence (Melbourne, Australia). Two authors (C.L.F., E.H.L.) independently screened studies in 2 phases: first by title and abstract and then by full text. If any disagreements arose, they could be settled by a third author (B.A.Y.).

Eligibility

Studies were included if they were clinical studies investigating trochleoplasty in the management of patellar instability and reported complications. Excluded studies were case reports, systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, studies using cadaveric or nonhuman subjects, or studies not published in English or peer‐reviewed journals. Studies with no full text or an inaccessible full text were also excluded. If 2 studies were reporting on the same cohort of patients at different time points, only the study with the longer follow‐up time would be included.

Data Collection

Study quality assessment was performed using the Methodological Index for Non‐Randomized Studies. 14 Patient characteristics collected included age and gender breakdown. Mean follow‐up time and any concomitant procedures were recorded. Complications, recurrent instability, and revision or subsequent procedures were collected. Studies were grouped by follow‐up timelines of less than 2, 2 to 5, 5 to 10, and greater than 10 years. Patient data within each of these groups were pooled to determine rates of complications, recurrent instability, and revision procedures by follow‐up time. Data was recorded descriptively in Excel (Version 16.88, Microsoft, Redmond, WA). Comparisons between complication rates were performed using chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests with statistical significant set to <0.05. Continuous variables were reported as ranges, as meta‐analyses and pooling were not carried out due to high heterogeneity.

RESULTS

Following the literature screen, 35 studies were included for analysis with 1336 patients (64% female). Figure 1 shows the screening process. Only 1 study was comparative and had a Methodological Index for Non‐Randomized Studies score of 18. 15 For the other 34 noncomparative studies, the average Methodological Index for Non‐Randomized Studies score was 11.3 ± 1.7. All but 5 (85.7%) studies were of level of evidence (LOE) IV, 2 (5.7%) were LOE II, 3 (8.6%) were LOE III, and none were LOE I. Complications, Recurrence, and Subsequent Procedures

Recurrent instability rates varied widely across studies and follow‐up times; the largest rates of recurrent instability were identified in the 2‐ to 5‐year follow‐up group, which ranged from 0% to 33.3% (Table 3). Studies with less than 2 years of follow‐up time had the lowest rates of complications at 0% to 9.8% (Figure 2). The highest rate of complications was seen in the studies with more than 10 years of follow‐up. Arthrofibrosis was a common complication among patients with <2, 2 to 5, and 5 to 10 years of follow‐up at rates of 0% to 6.3%, 0% to 20%, and 0% to 14.3%, respectively. Patellofemoral arthritis was first identified in studies with 2 to 5 years of follow‐up at rates of 0% to 35.3%. For patients with more than 10 years of follow‐up, patellofemoral arthritis was the most common complication. In these long‐term studies, 65.6% of all patients showed levels of patellofemoral arthritis (Iwano grades 1 and 2, or 2+), with studies reporting up to 92.3% of patients developing or worsening patellofemoral arthritis. Tibiofemoral arthritis was also identified in 1 long‐term study in up to 53% of the patients.

The subsequent procedure rate was lowest among patients with less than 2 years of follow‐up at 0% to 10.4% and highest in patients with 2 to 5 years of follow‐up at 0% to 87% (Table 3). The most common subsequent procedures were manipulation under anesthesia in patients with less than 2 years of follow‐up, implant removal in patients with 2 to 5 years, and arthroscopic lysis of adhesions in patients with 5 to 10 years. In studies with more than 10 years of follow‐up, manipulation under anesthesia was reported at rates of 0% to 17.6% and lysis of adhesion at rates of 0% to 5.9%, but it was clarified that these procedures occurred within the first postoperative year. Patellofemoral arthroplasty and total knee arthroplasty were performed at rates of 0% to 15.4% and 0% to 17.6%, respectively, in studies with more than 10 years of follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

Patients undergoing trochleoplasty exhibit relatively low recurrence of patellar instability and low complication rates until 10 years postoperatively, at which point patellofemoral arthritis is observed in greater than 50% of patients. Recurrent instability rates varied widely across studies and follow‐up times. The most common complications were postoperative stiffness and patellofemoral arthritis. The most frequent reoperations were implant removals and procedures related to arthrofibrosis, including manipulation under anesthesia and arthroscopic arthrolysis. Total knee arthroplasties and patellofemoral arthroplasties were the most common subsequent procedures at long‐term follow‐up. Trochleoplasty worsens the natural course of patellofemoral arthritis in the long term.

Recurrent patellar instability varied widely across follow‐up points for patients undergoing trochleoplasty, and given the high rates of concomitant procedures within these studies, it is difficult to evaluate trochleoplasty in its efficacy for preventing instability. A recent study comparing recurrent instability following isolated MPFL reconstruction (MPFLr), isolated trochleoplasty, and combined MPFLr and trochleoplasty reported the highest rate of recurrent instability following isolated trochleoplasty at 13.5%. 44 Residual instability for the isolated MPFLr and combined MPFLr and trochleoplasty groups was 12.0% and 4.9%, respectively. 44 Ren et al. 45 echoed this finding by showing an objective redislocation rate of 2.06% in patients undergoing trochleoplasty alone versus 0% in trochleoplasty combined with MPFLr. Phillips et al. 46 also found that failure rates were the highest following isolated trochleoplasty compared with isolated MPFLr and isolated TTO (5.3%‐40.0%, 0%‐33.3%, and 0%‐30.8%, respectively). As our patient cohort included patients undergoing concomitant procedures, higher recurrent instability rates may occur following trochleoplasty when performed in isolation. Only 3 studies included in this analysis investigated trochleoplasty performed in isolation with objective instability recurrence rates of 2%, 6.8%, and 33%. 20 , 32 , 43 Combined trochleoplasty with TTO, including MPFL reconstruction, has been reported by Wind et al. 9 in 22 patients to have improved pain scores and stability, with very few other studies if any reporting the combined procedure. This may be a potential topic for future studies to be performed. Based on the existing literature, trochleoplasty alone provides inferior results compared with other common patellar stabilization procedures.

In addition to poorer performance in reducing residual instability, trochleoplasty may also worsen the natural course of patellar instability. The high rate of patients who develop patellofemoral arthritis following trochleoplasty in the long term is concerning, as they appear to have a higher risk than counterparts who do not undergo trochleoplasty. Sanders et al. 47 evaluated patients who experienced a lateral patellar dislocation, with the majority (89.2%) foregoing patellar stabilization surgery. This study found that these patients not only showed a low incidence of patellofemoral arthritis 10 years after first‐time dislocation at 2.7% but also a steady progression. 47 Patellofemoral arthritis increased to 8.1% at 15 years and 14.8% at 20 years, not taking a substantial jump until 25 years (48.9%). A similar study focusing on skeletally immature patients, with only 16% undergoing surgical management, reported an incidence of patellofemoral arthritis of 2% at 10 years after the first instability event and 39% at 25 years. 48 The natural progression of patellar instability in patients managed with limited surgical intervention is associated with a significantly lower incidence and slower development of patellofemoral arthritis compared with those who undergo trochleoplasty. Prior studies have also shown lower long‐term rates of patellofemoral arthritis following isolated MPFLr and isolated TTO at rates of 34% and 31%, respectively. 43 , 49 Trochleoplasty carries the highest risk by far of patellofemoral arthritis among the main patellar stabilization procedures.

Patellar instability has been reported in up to 85% of patients who have trochlear dysplasia. 50 Although this does not mean that patellar instability is a direct result of the dysplasia, it has been linked to recurrent dislocation. Other factors including lateral tracking, congenital malalignment, or even osseous constraints impact the further development of instability. 51 Trochleoplasty has also been a topic of rising interest in the past decade, with systematic reviews reporting unique findings. Patient‐reported outcome measures such as Kujala score and International Knee Documentation Committee score were frequently used metrics, and stiffness and subsequent surgeries were reported. Davies et al. and Bedeir et al. both found that among their included studies, Kuja scores increased significantly postoperatively. 52 , 53 Meanwhile, Leclerc et al. 54 discovered that stiffness, patellofemoral arthritis, and subsequent surgery are elevated postoperatively, findings that are similar to our own review. Our study stratified these complication rates into the different follow‐up periods reported by our included studies as shown in Table 1, as well as reported the rates of concomitant procedures among the studies to show how the outcomes of trochleoplasty evolve over time.

The development of patellofemoral arthritis following trochleoplasty may be a product of changes in joint congruity. Elias et al. 55 showed that trochleoplasty significantly decreased the contact area of the patellofemoral joint and increased contact pressures. Trochleoplasty alters the shape of the trochlear groove but does not affect patellar shape, thereby changing the congruity of the joint. 55 , 56 Although the patella is not addressed during trochleoplasty, its shape is affected by the underlying trochlear dysplasia it developed with. Multiple studies have found that the shape of the patella in patients with trochlear dysplasia is narrower and has a smaller medial facet. 56 , 57 Meissburger et al. 58 confirmed that patellar dysplasia's severity is associated with trochlear dysplasia's severity; therefore, those with severe trochlear dysplasia contributing to patellar instability likely have a high level of accompanying patellar dysplasia. These findings have shown the mechanical impact of trochleoplasty on surrounding structures, but it is important to acknowledge the risk of trochleoplasty patients having prior recurrent dislocations impacting arthritis formation. As one of the major indications for trochleoplasty includes recurrent dislocation unable to be managed through other surgical repair, these patients have at baseline an increased chance of patellofemoral arthritis formation. Thus, patellar dysplasia in patients undergoing trochleoplasty remains uncorrected, and alterations in the patellofemoral joint, including contact and pressure on the cartilage, as well as the potential of prior dislocations, likely contribute to the development of patellofemoral arthritis.

Limitations

This study has notable limitations. Most of the studies included were of LOE IV, limiting the LOE of this study. Patients were not separated by concomitant procedures, creating a potential confounding variable. Importantly, not all the studies included here reported the time points at which specific complications occurred. Therefore, complications from studies with longer follow‐up time may have occurred in the earlier postoperative period. This might explain some of the more surprising results, such as increasing rates of postoperative stiffness over time or lysis of adhesion used as a subsequent procedure in studies with 5 to 10 years of follow‐up. Included studies may have also failed to report significant outcomes as well as complications, impacting overall rates. Studies included could not be analyzed for heterogeneity statistically as patellofemoral arthritis was not widely reported amongst the included studies. Another major limitation is the characteristics of individuals in the included studies and the heterogeneity of these characteristics. Older age and dislocations due to the underlying anatomical abnormalities leading to trochleoplasty independently impact the rates of arthritis formation, which vary widely among the included studies and likely contribute to our findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients undergoing trochleoplasty for patellar instability may experience low rates of recurrence but are prone to postoperative stiffness at <2‐ and 2‐ to 5‐year follow‐up. Complications following trochleoplasty in isolation are worse than when concomitant procedures are done. After 10 years, the incidence of all patellofemoral arthritis is as high as 92%, and more severe patellofemoral arthritis is as high as 65%, which is greater than the progression in patients with patellofemoral instability.
