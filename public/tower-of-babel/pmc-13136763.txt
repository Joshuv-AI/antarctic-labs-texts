Cultural adaptation and validation of the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale
Authors: Raghib Abusaris, Dimah Alsaqabi, Alyaa FA Ibrahim, Ashraf A’aqoulah, Mona Alanazi, Manal Alsheikh, Fai Albawardi, Mohammed Awawdeh, Samah Saad Salem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale.MethodsThe study was conducted via an online survey across all regions of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, using a cross-sectional validation design. A non-probability consecutive sampling method was used to recruit 71 non-cancer adults aged 18 years or older. The Fear of Cancer Scale was translated and adapted into Arabic in accordance with published guidelines after obtaining permission from the developers. Data was analyzed using SPSS software.ResultsFollowing the established content validity of the Fear of Cancer Scale Arabic translated version, the scale depicted a very good internal consistency. Overall, with 95% CI = (0.777, 0.858), indicating a high level of reliability. Based on the principal component analysis method and using the average variance extracted (F1: 0.480, F2: 0.328) and composite reliability (F1: 0.901, F2: 0.783), the scale depicted an acceptable discriminant validity.ConclusionThe validation of the Fear of Cancer Scale in Arabic can help in identifying cancer-related fear/anxiety among the Arabic-speaking population. This study fills a gap in the literature and provides a valuable tool for researchers and clinicians in the Arabic-speaking community. Decision-makers are encouraged to integrate the Arabic Fear of Cancer Scale into cancer clinics to enhance cancer care strategies and better understand the psychological consequences of cancer on patients and their families.Keywords: Alternate-form reliability, Discriminant validity, Fear of Cancer Scale, Internal consistency

Introduction

Cancer, a globally significant public health concern and one of the most feared illnesses, induces fear among not only patients with cancer but also individuals without the disease, including family members, caregivers, and communities at large (Brice et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2020). The World Health Organization (WHO) has published estimates of global mortality data in 2019, representing 127 countries. Cancer was identified as the leading cause of premature deaths from noncommunicable diseases in 57 of these countries (Bray et al., 2021). In addition to its substantial health burden, survey data from 30 countries demonstrate that cancer is also a prominent fear within the general population, identified by 13.7% of participants as a major concern (Li et al., 2024). Importantly, fear of cancer and fear of cancer recurrence, although closely related, represent two different psychological experiences. Fear of cancer refers to the anxiety and distress associated with having cancer itself and its consequences, such as diagnosis, treatment, or mortality, despite a lack of symptoms or evidence to support a cancer diagnosis, whereas fear of cancer recurrence specifically reflects the concern that cancer may return or progress after successful treatment. The distinction is crucial, as fear of cancer recurrence often persists despite the absence of active disease, and it’s associated with specific predictors, patterns, and effects on patients’ quality of life (Lebel et al., 2016; Simard et al., 2013).

Fear of cancer is considered a multidimensional psychological construct that encompasses both emotional and behavioral responses to the perceived threat of cancer (Coutts-Bain et al., 2022). The literature commonly conceptualizes this construct as including direct fear, such as fear of being diagnosed with cancer, fear of pain, suffering, or death, and emotional distress when thinking about the disease, as well as indirect fear, which reflects broader concerns related to the consequences of cancer, including fear of hospitals or medical examinations, worries about financial burden, and concerns about the potential emotional impact on family members. Understanding these different dimensions is important because they may influence individuals’ psychological well-being and health-related behaviors in different ways (Feng et al., 2022; Nelissen et al., 2015; Vrinten et al., 2015).

Previous studies have focused on the fear of cancer recurrence or progression in individuals dealing with cancer, commonly called the fear of cancer recurrence. Elevated fear of cancer recurrence levels are prevalent in recently diagnosed cancer patients, adult cancer survivors, and individuals who have undergone hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) for hematologic tumors. There is a remarkable positive correlation between fear of cancer recurrence levels and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress, as well as the use of psychotropic drugs (Brice et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2020; Vandraas et al., 2021).

Fear of cancer recurrence is not limited to patients; it also profoundly affects the family members of individuals dealing with cancer. This heightened fear among relatives is associated with a detrimental effect on their overall quality of life, establishing a negative association between the fear of cancer recurrence and the well-being of those closely connected to cancer patients (Peikert et al., 2020).

An inadequate understanding of cancer can induce anxiety about its symptoms, disease progression, as well as the broader social, therapeutic, and psychological ramifications for both patients and their families (Morowatisharifabad et al., 2020). Furthermore, fear of cancer predicts a range of behaviors, including cancer information seeking, scanning, and avoidance (Nelissen et al., 2015). Previous findings also support the notion that various aspects of cancer fear can have distinct behavioral effects on cancer screening participation (Vrinten et al., 2015). This highlights the importance of understanding the fear of cancer, as it can help to identify factors associated with public health and improve cancer-related behaviors within Arabic-speaking populations.

Assessing fear of cancer or closely related constructs such as fear of cancer recurrence or cancer worry is important in oncology, prevention research, and psychological care. Several standardized tools are commonly used; the Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory (FCRI) is one of the most widely used muti-dimensional instruments to assess fear of cancer recurrence among cancer patients and measures both intensity and functional impact of fear (Simard et al., 2013; Simard and Savard, 2009). The instrument has strong psychometric properties across multiple populations, often used in survivorship research and clinical screening. However, it is lengthy, time-consuming, and limited for use among cancer survivors. The Cancer Worry Scale (CWS) is another brief tool commonly used in studies involving cancer risk perception and screening behaviors. The scale assesses frequency and impact of worry about developing or having cancer. It is frequently used in population screening and prevention studies; however, it focuses mainly on worry frequency providing limited insight into behavioral or emotional consequences (Maheu et al., 2021). The Concerns About Recurrence Scale (CARS) evaluates the nature and severity of fear of cancer returning. It is commonly used to measure multiple domains such as health worries, role concerns, and mortality concerns among breast cancer survivors (Vickberg, 2003; Maheu and Galica, 2018).

More recently, the Fear of Cancer Scale (FOCS) has been developed to measure general fear related to cancer, including emotional, cognitive, and behavioral responses in broader populations (Feng et al., 2022). The Fear of Cancer Scale is concise and practical, suitable for both community and clinical settings, as it captures general fear, not limited to recurrence, which makes it suitable for screening purposes and large-scale surveys. The scale has good psychometric properties, including strong internal consistency and a stable factor structure.

The Fear of Cancer Scale, originally developed in Chinese, has been identified as a valuable tool for assessing cancer-related fear and has been recommended for translation to expand its use across diverse populations. It has been translated to different languages, such as Turkish. A Turkish study assessed the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the Fear of Cancer Scale (FOCS) for measuring cancer fear in non-oncological individuals (Öztürk and Kuday, 2025). The original questionnaire consists of 17 items grouped into two subscales: direct fear (8 items) and indirect fear (9 items) (Feng et al., 2022). The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties. In the original development study, Kendall’s concordance coefficient for content validity across seven experts was 0.542 (p < .001), and the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.807 to 0.990, indicating good content validity. Additionally, Pearson correlation coefficients for F1, F2, and the total Fear of Cancer Scale score were 0.874 to 0.896, respectively (p < .001), reflecting strong reliability.

The Fear of Cancer Scale has demonstrated strong utility in evaluating fear of cancer among non-cancer individuals, providing a reliable method to assess cancer-related fear in the general population.

Despite the global burden of cancer, including in many Arabic-speaking countries, validated Arabic instruments that comprehensively assess fear of cancer may influence how individuals perceive and report fear, highlighting the need for culturally appropriate assessment tools. Without proper linguistic translation and psychometric validation, the use of existing instruments may lead to inaccurate measurement and reduced comparability across populations. Therefore, adapting and validating an Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale is essential to ensure reliable assessment of cancer-related fear in Arabic populations. Such validation would facilitate across-cultural research, support the identification of individuals experiencing heightened cancer-related distress, and inform the development of targeted psychological and public health interventions aimed at improving cancer prevention and early detection behaviors. Accordingly, the present study aims to culturally adapt the Fear of Cancer Scale and evaluate the validity and reliability of its Arabic version, thereby providing an evidence-based instrument to strengthen future assessments and interventions within Arabic-speaking communities.

Methods and materials

Study area/settings

The study was conducted through an online survey across all 13 regions of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Each region, in addition to its regional headquarters, is further subdivided into several governorates (Interior, 2025).

Study subjects

The study subjects were non-cancer adults ≥ 18 years old living in Saudi Arabia from March 6, 2024, to December 1, 2024.

Study design

The current study employed a cross-sectional validation design and collected data from March 6, 2024, to December 1, 2024.

Sample size

The minimal sample size was determined following the rule of a minimum of subject-to-item ratio ≥ 2 (Anthoine et al., 2014). Meaning, since we have 17 items, the minimal sample size is 34. The study sample was 71 individuals. According to earlier methodological research, 15 to 30 participants are typically included in pilot testing and preliminary validation of questionnaires in order to evaluate its feasibility, reliability, and clarity. Research evaluating internal consistency frequently deems 15 to 30 respondents to be adequate for reliability analysis (Gunawan et al., 2021; Bujang et al., 2024). In this study, the collected sample size of 71 participants exceeded the minimum subject-to-item ratio of 2:1 and met a more conservative ratio of approximately 4:1 for the 17-item scale. While this supports preliminary exploratory factor analysis, the sample size remains modest relative to commonly recommended thresholds.

Sampling technique

A non-probability consecutive sampling was used to recruit participants from the target population. The questionnaire was designed using Google Forms and distributed through social media and emails.

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

The original Fear of Cancer Scale (FOCS) has 17-items. It has been used to measure the fear of cancer in healthy people. It is divided into two dimensions: direct fear and indirect fear, with a score ranging from 0 to 68 points. Higher scores indicate higher fear levels towards cancer in the participants (Feng et al., 2022). In addition, age, gender and region information were added to collect demographic data.

The item order and dimensional structure of the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale have been maintained consistent with those of the original version (Chinese/English) of the Fear of Cancer Scale. Similar to what has been done by the first author and his collaborators in translating the Index of Dental Anxiety and Fear, Alsanawi et al. (2019) and after obtaining the permission from the Fear of Cancer Scale developers, Feng et al. (2022) for cross-cultural adaptation and validation. The Fear of Cancer Scale was translated and adapted into Arabic following published guidelines Beaton et al. (2000); Boateng et al. (2018); Guillemin et al. (1993); Wild et al. (2005) which involved the following steps (Fig. 1):

Initial step

The original English version of the Fear of Cancer Scale was translated into classical Arabic by two independent and expert translators. Since “spoken” Arabic varies from one country to another, translating the Fear of Cancer Scale to classical Arabic ensures the usefulness of the tool to all Arabic nations. Following translation, the multilingual specialists were asked to review the linguistic suitability of the translated version. Backward translation to English of the reviewed version of the Arabic Fear of Cancer Scale was done by the same expert translators who performed the forward translation, and consistency was maintained with the original version. To ensure content validity, the resulting Arabic Fear of Cancer Scale was then checked by three experts for completeness, clarity, accuracy, and cultural suitability and sensitivity. After that, following the rule that 5–10 is a good starting point for testing if scale items make sense (Kishore et al., 2021), the final version was used in pilot testing to establish face validity and confirm that every single phrase used was reasonable and to make any changes, as required. The pilot testing was conducted on a small group of volunteers (n = 9) who met the inclusion criteria of being adults, fluent in Arabic, and representative of the target population to ensure clarity, comprehension, and appropriateness of the scale items.

Cognitive debriefing phase

To assess the acceptability, clarity, and understandability of the translated version, 9 non-cancer participants with strong language and comprehension skills from the research area were recruited and invited to engage in the cognitive debriefing phase of the Fear of Cancer Scale. During this phase, participants were briefed about the study's contents, and those who chose to participate were required to submit written informed consent. The survey was conducted online, and the participants were guided to focus on what they understood, the unclear aspects of each question or statement on the questionnaire, and their degree of agreement with the questionnaire rather than the accuracy of their responses. At the end, the participants were asked to provide a general open comment regarding the questionnaire to evaluate its overall acceptability, understandability, and clarity. Every comment was documented in a specific format.

Reconciliation phase

In the reconciliation phase, based on the cognitive debriefing findings, the translated version was reviewed and discussed prior to its development into the final Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale.

Statistical analysis

All calculations presented below were performed using SPSS software version 30, and statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05. Reliability was measured using alternate-form reliability and internal consistency using a two-way mixed model for consistency intra-class correlation coefficient and Cronbach α, respectively. On the other hand, validity was established using face, content, and discriminant validity, which was explored using the principal component analysis (PCA) with a cut-off of ≥ 0.4, the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) The sufficiency of sample size for factor analysis was established by the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett's test of sphericity should be described.

Results

Face validity and cultural adaptation

The Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale was deemed clear and culturally appropriate by the 9 participants in the pilot study.

Content validity

The three experts unanimously agreed on the clarity of the translation of the 17 items. On the other hand, two experts contended that the translation of the scale is accurate. Nevertheless, the third experts suggested a few minor modifications such as maintain the declarative-sentence nature in Item 2 and replace the word “أخاف” by “أخشى” in Items 2, 3, and 13. Their suggestions were adopted.

Discriminatory validity

To examine the discriminatory validity of the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale, we employed exploratory factor analysis using the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) approach. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) was 0.834, and the chi-square value of Bartlett’s sphericity test was 495.623 (p < .001), indicating adequacy of sampling and suitability for factor analysis. To reduce high-dimensional data into fewer, uncorrelated, principal components while retaining maximum variance analysis of the two components, the principal component analysis with no rotation and eigenvalues >1 was used. Discussion

Investigating cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale was the goal of the current study. To maintain the outcome's theoretical content validity across cultural boundaries, outcome measures that are intended for usage in other cultures undergo a thorough linguistic translation and cultural adaptation. This process is referred to as cross-cultural adaptation (Beaton et al., 2000; Boateng et al., 2018; Guillemin et al., 1993; Wild et al., 2005). Creating uniformity between source and target content is the primary goal of this approach. The process of cross-cultural adaptation ensures that the scale's metric qualities, such as validity and reliability, are maintained (Beaton et al., 2000; Boateng et al., 2018; Guillemin et al., 1993; Wild et al., 2005). A backward translation stage is also included in this method to draw attention to any theoretical mistakes or dissonance in the translation. In the current investigation, these protocols and guidelines were appropriately followed. The study's findings indicated that the translation and adaptation procedures were successful, as the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.886), acceptable alternate-form reliability (ICC = 0.777–0.858), and a two-factor structure explaining 46.756% of the total variance, consistent with the original conceptual framework. These findings provide empirical support for the reliability and preliminary construct validity of the Arabic version.

As far as we are aware, this is the first study to translate and evaluate the Fear of Cancer Scale in Arabic. The Arabic version demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in this sample, including internal consistency, construct validity, and alternate-form reliability. In the present study, alternate-form reliability was assessed by administering a reordered version of the same items to minimize recall bias while maintaining content equivalence. Because of carcinophobia, or the Fear of Cancer, (Medicover, 2024) appears to be temporary, especially in this region of the world where belief in predetermination is common, alternate-form reliability was preferred over test-retest reliability, in contrast to the study conducted by Feng et al. (2022). This approach was selected mainly due to practical considerations related to participant follow-up rather than definitive evidence regarding the short-term instability of fear of cancer. However, we acknowledge that item reordering may still be influenced by memory or order effects. Our analysis demonstrated a high degree of alternate-form reliability with ICC‾=0.817 with 95% Confidence Interval = (0.777, 0.858). Our study is consistent with the Turkish study that confirm the Turkish version of Fear of Cancer Scale as a valid and reliable instrument for measuring cancer fear in non-oncological individuals (Öztürk and Kuday, 2025).

When compared with existing instruments such as the Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory (FCRI) (Simard and Savard, 2009), the Cancer Worry Scale (CWS) (Maheu et al., 2021; Custers et al., 2014), and the Concerns About Recurrence Scale (CARS) (Vickberg, 2003), the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale (FOCS) demonstrates a broader conceptual focus and wider applicability. The Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory and the Concerns About Recurrence Scale primarily assess fear related to cancer recurrence among patients or survivors, whereas the Cancer Worry Scale mainly measures the frequency and impact of cancer-related worry. In contrast, the Arabic Fear of Cancer Scale captures both direct and indirect fear, thereby reflecting the emotional and behavioral dimensions of fear in non-clinical populations. The preservation of the original two-factor structure in the Arabic version supports its conceptual consistency across cultures while extending its relevance to Arabic-speaking communities. From a nursing perspective, this broader structure enhances the utility of the Arabic Fear of Cancer Scale as a screening tool for identifying cancer-related psychological distress in community and preventive care settings, thereby supporting early psychosocial assessment, patient education, and culturally responsive nursing interventions.Our study evaluated the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale in a sample of adult non-cancer community participants. In contrast, Feng et al. assessed the psychometric properties of the original Chinese version of the Fear of Cancer Scale using samples that included both university students and community residents (Feng et al., 2022). Despite these differences in study populations, the internal consistency observed in our study (α = 0.886) was comparable to that reported in the original validation study (α = 0.916).

The Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale's discriminant validity was supported by the factor analysis. The tool's suitability for evaluating cancer-related anxieties among Arabic speakers was demonstrated by its two-factor structure, which included direct and indirect fear components, remaining consistent with the original scale's structure. This structure was further validated by a cumulative variance contribution of 46.756% and the lack of substantial cross-loading, which is in line with recommended methods in psychometric validation (Cheung et al., 2024).

Interestingly, when compared to the original Fear of Cancer Scale, the results showed minor variations in item distributions within the derived factors. These differences most likely reflect cultural differences in the way the Saudi population sees fear of cancer, where beliefs about predestination may affect how people respond to questions about control and personal susceptibility (Morowatisharifabad et al., 2020). The low corrected item–total correlation for Item 3 and the borderline value for Item 17 may reflect cultural and conceptual differences rather than measurement weakness. In another study, misconceptions such as cancer being contagious were strongly rejected, indicating relatively accurate medical knowledge (Abusaris et al., 2025). This limited variability likely reduced the correlation of Item 3 with the overall scale (Feng et al., 2022). Similarly, Item 17 assesses health-seeking behaviour rather than fear, and responses may be shaped by contextual factors such as access to care and cultural attitudes toward screening, which do not always align with emotional fear (Deliktas Demirci et al., 2025; Richardson-Parry et al., 2023). Despite this, the minimal change in Cronbach’s α supports retaining these items for capturing broader dimensions of cancer-related perceptions (Feng et al., 2022).

The study emphasizes how useful the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale may be in both clinical and research contexts. It fills a significant void in culturally appropriate evaluation instruments by making it possible to identify cancer-related anxiety and its psychological effects in Arabic-speaking communities (Beaton et al., 2000). To lessen cancer-related misery in these areas, the scale also serves as a basis for creating specialized mental health interventions and public health initiatives.

Despite the aforementioned strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size (n = 71), along with the high proportion of female participants (71.8%), may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the sample size met minimal subject-to-item ratio recommendations for preliminary psychometric evaluation, it remains modest for exploratory factor analysis of a 17-item scale. Therefore, the factor structure identified in this study should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution, as larger samples are generally required to ensure stable and replicable factor solutions. Future studies should aim to validate the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale in larger and more diverse clinical and demographic populations. Increasing the sample size would also strengthen the robustness of factor analytic findings and enhance the scale’s generalizability. Additionally, longitudinal studies are recommended to assess stability over time. Moreover, an important direction for future research is the examination of further aspects of construct validity, including convergent and criterion validity. Implications for practice

The validated Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale allows healthcare providers to more effectively assess cancer-related fear and anxiety in Arabic-speaking patients. The use of the Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale in clinical settings can facilitate early detection of anxiety and support the development of comprehensive mental health programs to improve overall well-being for patients facing cancer-related fear and anxiety. Moreover, by identifying individuals with elevated fear and anxiety levels, healthcare providers can deliver more personalized care and implement targeted interventions to alleviate psychological burdens. This tool enables the integration of culturally sensitive approaches into patient care, ensuring that mental health strategies are tailored to the unique needs of Arabic-speaking countries.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the validation of the Fear of Cancer Scale (FOCS) for Arabic-speaking populations provides a scientifically validated tool for assessing cancer-related fear and anxiety within this community. The Arabic version of the Fear of Cancer Scale enables the identification of individuals experiencing heightened distress and facilitates the evaluation of their stress levels, laying the foundation for the development of culturally sensitive and targeted interventions. This study addresses an important gap in the literature by translating a reliable and valid instrument for use in research and clinical settings, ultimately contributing to a deeper understanding of cancer-related psychological distress and supporting the development of tailored mental health strategies for Arabic-speaking populations. Decision-makers are encouraged to integrate the Arabic Fear of Cancer Scale into cancer clinics to enhance cancer care strategies and better understand the psychological consequences of cancer on patients and their families.
