Christophe Hohlweg, Viviane Pons

Abstract
. In this article, we discuss the notion of partition of elements in an arbitrary Coxeter system ( W , S ) (W,S) : a partition of an element w w is a subset 𝒫 ⊆ W \mathcal{P}\subseteq W such that the left inversion set of w w is the disjoint union of the left inversion set of the elements in 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . Partitions of elements of W W arises in the study of the Belkale-Kumar product on the cohomology H ∗ ​ ( X , ℤ ) H^{*}(X,\mathbb{Z}) , where X X is the complete flag variety of any complex semi-simple algebraic group. Partitions of elements in the symmetric group 𝒮 n \mathcal{S}_{n} are also related to the Babington-Smith model in algebraic statistics or to the simplicial faces of the Littlewood-Richardson cone. Moreover, we state and discuss the conjecture that the number of right descents of w w is the sum of the number of right descents of the elements of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . In particular: (1) we state an equivalent conjecture in term of the number of atoms in the interval [ e , w ] R [e,w]_{R} of the right weak order, with a word-metric flavour; (2) we give a direct proof that this conjecture holds in the cases of symmetric groups (type A A ) and hyperoctahedral groups (type B B ).

The computations to check the validity of Conjecture

Partitions of elements in a Coxeter system

For a general reference on Coxeter groups, we refer the reader to
[
2
]
. For

, we say that a product

is

In the weak order

if and only if

is a complete meet-semilattice. For

\bigwedge_{R}X
and, if it exists, the

\bigvee_{R}X

2.1.
First properties of partitions of elements of
W

Companion to the right descent set is the
left descent set of
w\in W
:

D_{L}(w)=\{s\in S\mid\ell(sw)<\ell(w)\}=\{s\in S\mid s\leq_{R}w\}\subseteq T(w).

We denote by
d_{L}(w)=|D_{L}(w)|
. The following proposition states, in particular, that the statement in Conjecture
1
obtained by replacing right descent sets by left descents easily holds.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a partition of
w\in W
, then:

(1)

D_{L}(w)=\bigsqcup_{u\in\mathcal{P}}D_{L}(u)
. In particular
d_{L}(w)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{P}}d_{L}(u)
;

(2)

\ell(w)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{P}}\ell(u)
;

(3)

u\leq_{R}w
for all
u\in\mathcal{P}
;

(4)

\bigwedge_{R}\mathcal{P}=e
and
\bigvee_{R}\mathcal{P}=w
.

Proof.

(1) The statement is a direct consequences of the definition of partitions of
w
. (2) The statement is a consequence of the definition of partitions of
w
and of the fact that
|T(g)|=\ell(g)
for any
g\in W
. (3) The statement is a consequence of the definition of partitions of
w
and of the fact that
u\leq_{R}w
if and only if
T(u)\subseteq T(w)
. (4) Assume by contradiction that
\wedge_{R}\mathcal{P}\not=e
. Then there is
s\in S
such that
s\leq_{R}u
for all
u\in\mathcal{P}
. So

s\in\bigcap_{u\in\mathcal{P}}D_{L}(u)\subseteq\bigcap_{u\in\mathcal{P}}T(u),

contradicting that the union
T(u)
for
u\in\mathcal{P}
is disjoint. So
\bigwedge_{R}\mathcal{P}=e
. Finally, by (3), the set
\mathcal{P}
is bounded by
w
so
z:=\bigvee_{R}\mathcal{P}
exists and
z\leq_{R}w
. So in particular,
T(z)\subseteq T(w)
. For all
u\in\mathcal{P}
, we have
u\leq_{R}z
so
T(u)\subseteq T(z)
. Therefore:

T(w)=\bigsqcup_{u\in\mathcal{P}}T(u)\subseteq T(z)\subseteq T(w),

implying
w=z
.
∎

Let

Proof.

Suppose

is finite. Since the only finite standard parabolic subgroups of a universal Coxeter system are the

2.2.
Roots and reflections

To discuss further properties of bipartition of elements of
W

From now on we consider a
geometric representation of
(W,S)
over a quadratic space
(V,B)
. More precisely,
V
is a real vector space,
B
is a symmetric bilinear form that is preserved by the action of
W
, and
S
is mapped into a set of
B
-reflections
associated to a
simple system

\Delta=\{\alpha_{s}\mid s\in S\}
; see for instance
[
8
, §2.1]
for more details. Such a representation is a generalization of the
Tits canonical representation
as presented in
[
2
, §4.1]
.

For
X\subseteq V
, we denote by
\operatorname{cone}(X)
the set of nonnegative linear combinations of vectors in
X
. We consider the associated root system
\Phi=W(\Delta)
, the positive root system
\Phi^{+}=\Phi\cap\operatorname{cone}(\Delta)
and the negative root system
\Phi^{-}=-\Phi^{+}
. It is well-known that
\Phi=\Phi^{+}\sqcup\Phi^{-}
and that the set of reflections
T
is in bijection with the set of
B
-reflections
associated to
\Phi^{+}
. In particular:

T=\{s_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\}.

The inversion set (as a subset of positive roots) of an element
w\in W
is then:

\Phi(w)=\{\alpha\mid s_{\alpha}\in T(w)\}=\Phi^{+}\cap w(\Phi^{-}).

In particular
\ell(w)=|\Phi(w)|=|T(w)|
.

Subsets of
\Phi^{+}
that are inversion sets are characterized by the notion of
biclosed sets
: a subset
A
of
\Phi^{+}
is
biclosed
if the two following conditions are met: (a) for any

.

For any word

The proof of Conjecture

in type

relies on the properties of inversion sets. We need, in order to prove Theorem

, to translate the notion of biclosed sets and some of the results of Proposition

Let

be a set of pairs

with

for some

. The set

. Denote

if and only if both

and

are transitive sets. There is an alternate useful way to verify the transitivity of

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

with

(2)

Definition 3.9

Let

\mathcal{S}_{n}
with

For an example, if

Lemma 3.10

Let

, then
\{\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}\}

Note that for

\invs(\widetilde{w})=\{(a,b)\in\invs(w);b\neq n\}
. In other words, we remove the inversions containing

\invs(w)=\invs(u)\sqcup\invs(v)

\invs(\widetilde{w})=\invs(\widetilde{u})\sqcup\invs(\widetilde{v})

∎

Let
w
be a word on
\mathbb{N}
and

. We write

the subword of

containing only the letters in

. If

is not in general a permutation, but

and

.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\{u,v\}
be a bipartition of a permutation
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. The
right letters
of
w
are defined as the set of letters in

at the right-hand side of the letter

:

{\overrightarrow{n}}(w):=\{a\mid(a,n)\in\invs(w)\}\cup\{n\}

The
left letters
of
w
are the complement in
[n]
of the right letters, i.e., the set of letters in
w
at the left-hand side of the letter
n
:

{\overleftarrow{n}}(w):=\{b\mid 1\leq b<n,(b,n)\not\in\invs(w)\}.

The
left
(resp.
right
)
words
of

,
u
, and

are respectively:

\displaystyle w_{\overleftarrow{n}}

\displaystyle=\std(w_{|{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)}),

\displaystyle w_{\overrightarrow{n}}

\displaystyle=\std(w_{|{\overrightarrow{n}}(w)})

\displaystyle u_{\overleftarrow{n}}

\displaystyle=\std(u_{|{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)}),

\displaystyle u_{\overrightarrow{n}}

\displaystyle=\std(u_{|{\overrightarrow{n}}(w)})

\displaystyle v_{\overleftarrow{n}}

\displaystyle=\std(v_{|{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)}),

\displaystyle v_{\overrightarrow{n}}

\displaystyle=\std(v_{|{\overrightarrow{n}}(w)}).

In other words, we
cut

into two parts (left and right) just before the maximal letter. Then we select those letters in

and

and standardize to form new permutations. Note that

is by definition a

of left letters might be empty, in this case the left words are the empty word and the right words are the original words

. If

, the set

of right letters is never the empty set as it contains at least the letter

. Note also that the left (resp. right) words of

and

depend on the left (resp. right) letters of

.

The proof of Theorem

in the case of symmetric groups relies on the two following propositions, which are proven in the following sections.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
\{u,v\}
be a bipartition of a permutation

. Then the left words
\{u_{\overleftarrow{n}},v_{\overleftarrow{n}}\}
form a bipartition of
w_{\overleftarrow{n}}
and the right words
\{u_{\overrightarrow{n}},v_{\overrightarrow{n}}\}
form a bipartition of
w_{\overrightarrow{n}}
and

(2)

\displaystyle d_{R}(w)

\displaystyle=d_{R}(w_{\overleftarrow{n}})+d_{R}(w_{\overrightarrow{n}}),

(3)

\displaystyle d_{R}(u)

\displaystyle=d_{R}(u_{\overleftarrow{n}})+d_{R}(u_{\overrightarrow{n}}),

(4)

\displaystyle d_{R}(v)

\displaystyle=d_{R}(v_{\overleftarrow{n}})+d_{R}(v_{\overrightarrow{n}}).

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\{u,v\}
be a bipartition of a permutation
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that

and
w_{n}\neq n
. Then the right words

and

satisfy the following equation:

(5)

d_{R}(\widetilde{u_{\overrightarrow{n}}})+d_{R}(\widetilde{v_{\overrightarrow{n}}})=d_{R}(u_{\overrightarrow{n}})+d_{R}(v_{\overrightarrow{n}})-1.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
for
W=\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

The proof is by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. The result is obviously true in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
that contains only the identity with 0 descents and inversions. Now, let
\{u,v\}
be a bipartition of a permutation
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
with
n\geq 2
. We suppose that the statement of Theorem
3.1
is satisfied for all
1\leq k<n
. Write

in one-line notation.

If
w_{n}=n
, then
\invs(w)
does not contain any inversion
(*,n)
. It is also true for
\invs(u)
and
\invs(v)
since
\{u,v\}
is a bipartition of
n
. Therefore
u_{n}=v_{n}=n
. In particular, there is no descent in position
n-1
for neither
u
,
v
, nor
w
. So
w
,
u
and
v
are in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
viewed as a subgroup of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. By Lemma
3.10
,
\{\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}\}
is a bipartition of
\widetilde{w}
. So by induction
d_{R}(w)=d_{R}(\widetilde{w})=d_{R}(\widetilde{u})+d_{R}(\widetilde{v})=d_{R}(u)+d_{R}(v)
.

Now suppose that
w_{n}\neq n
. This means that
w
is an element of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that is not contained in any
\mathcal{S}_{k}
with
k<n
viewed as a subgroup of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We consider the decomposition given by Definition
3.11
. Let
k<n
be the position of the letter
n
. We have two cases.

Case
k\neq 1
.

This implies that
w_{\overleftarrow{n}}
,
u_{\overleftarrow{n}}
and
v_{\overleftarrow{n}}
are not empty words. Therefore, by Proposition
3.13
,
\{u_{\overleftarrow{n}},v_{\overleftarrow{n}}\}
is a bipartition of
w_{\overleftarrow{n}}
with
w_{\overleftarrow{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{k-1}
, and
\{u_{\overrightarrow{n}},v_{\overrightarrow{n}}\}
is a bipartition of
w_{\overrightarrow{n}}
with
w_{\overrightarrow{n}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-k+1}
. We have
k-1<n
and
n-k+1<n
, since
1<k<n
,. By induction, we obtain
d_{R}(w_{\overleftarrow{n}})=d_{R}(u_{\overleftarrow{n}})+d_{R}(v_{\overleftarrow{n}})
and
d_{R}(w_{\overrightarrow{n}})=d_{R}(u_{\overrightarrow{n}})+d_{R}(v_{\overrightarrow{n}})
. Using Eq. (
2
), Eq. (
3
), and Eq. (
4
) of Proposition
3.13
, we obtain that
d_{R}(w)=d_{R}(u)+d_{R}(v)
.

Case

This implies that the left words are empty,
i.e.
,
w=w_{\overrightarrow{n}}
,

, and
v=v_{\overrightarrow{n}}
and we cannot use directly the induction hypothesis. Nevertheless, we now have

. In particular, as

, this means that there is a descent in position

in
w
which disappears if we remove

. In other words,
d_{R}(\widetilde{w})=d_{R}(w)-1
. Moreover, by Proposition
3.14
, we have
d_{R}(\widetilde{u})+d_{R}(\widetilde{v})=d_{R}(u)+d_{R}(v)-1
. By Lemma
3.10
, we know that
\{\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v}\}
is a bipartition of
\widetilde{w}\in\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
. Therefore, by induction,
d_{R}(\widetilde{w})=d_{R}(\widetilde{u})+d_{R}(\widetilde{v})
. We conclude that:

d_{R}(w)=d_{R}(\widetilde{w})+1=d_{R}(\widetilde{u})+d_{R}(\widetilde{v})+1=d_{R}(u)+d_{R}(v).

∎

Proof of Proposition

We start with a simple, yet fundamental, lemma.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\{u,v\}
be a bipartition of a permutation

. Then there is no inversion
(a,c)
of

or

such that

is a left letter of

This is readily seen on the word

does not contain an inversion

with

and

. The lemma follows since inversions of

and

are contained inversions of

, by definition of bipartitions.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

A more ¨Coxeter-like’ argument to prove this lemma is with the transitivity of inversions (bipodal sets). Indeed, as

, then by Definition

in

. If

.

.

(or in

) between two letters

a,c\in{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)
and the descent is kept in
u_{\overleftarrow{n}}
, still in positive position. Or
a,c\in{\overrightarrow{n}}(w)
and the descent is kept in
u_{\overrightarrow{n}}
. Finally, if
a,c\in\mathcal{F}(w)
, then
u
also has a descent in negative position between
\bar{a}
and
\bar{c}
in
{\overrightarrow{n}}(w)
which was not counted in
d_{R}(u)
but is kept in
u_{\overrightarrow{n}}
and counted in
d_{R}(u_{\overrightarrow{n}})
.

We now claim that Proposition
4.24
implies:

d_{R}(u_{\overleftarrow{n}})+d_{R}(u_{\overrightarrow{n}})\leq d_{R}(u).

Suppose that there is a descent
ca
in
u_{\overleftarrow{n}}
in nonnegative position. This means that
u
is of the form
\dots c^{\prime}\dots a^{\prime}\dots
where
a^{\prime}<c^{\prime}
are letters of
{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)
which are standardized into
a
and
c
in
u_{\overleftarrow{n}}
. As they are consecutive in
u_{\overleftarrow{n}}
, this means that all letters between
c^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}
are not in
{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)
. Then Proposition
4.24
forces the word between
c^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}
to be empty. Therefore
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
is a descent of
u
.
Moreover, since
{\overleftarrow{n}}(w)
is a symmetric subset of
[\![\bar{n},n]\!]^{*}
, the
B
standardization cannot change a negative position into a positive one and the descent
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
in
u
is then in nonnegative position; therefore
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
is counted in
d_{R}(u)
.

Now suppose that there is a descent
ca
in
u_{\overrightarrow{n}}
(so in positive position). With similar arguments, using Proposition
4.24
, we get that there is a descent
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
in
u
with
a^{\prime},c^{\prime}\in{\overrightarrow{n}}(w)
. If
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
is in nonnegative position,
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
is then counted in
d_{R}(u)
. If
c^{\prime}a^{\prime}
is in negative position, then
\overline{a^{\prime}}~\overline{c^{\prime}}
is a descent in positive position with letters in
\mathcal{F}(w)
which is counted in
d_{R}(u)
and was not counted before. This implies the desires inequality and concludes the proof.
∎
