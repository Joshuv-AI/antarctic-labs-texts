Machine-Learned Sampling of Conditioned Path Measures
Qijia Jiang, Reuben Cohn-Gordon

Abstract
We propose algorithms for sampling from posterior path measures 𝒫 ⁡ ( 𝒞 ⁡ ( [ 0 , T ] , ℝ d ) ) \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})) under a general prior process. This leverages ideas from (1) controlled equilibrium dynamics, which gradually transport between two path measures, and (2) optimization in ∞ \infty -dimensional probability space endowed with a Wasserstein metric, which can be used to evolve a density curve under the specified likelihood. The resulting algorithms are theoretically grounded and can be integrated seamlessly with neural networks for learning the target trajectory ensembles, without access to data.



1
Introduction

Simulating trajectories through a space, according to a given distribution, is a problem which appears in a broad range of scientific applications.
A general formulation of the problem is given by considering a prior path measure that admits a representation as a stochastic differential equation (SDE):

dX_{t}=u_{t}^{\text{ref}}(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\quad\text{Denote this path measure as }(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}})_{t\leq T}\,,

(1)

where the
u_{t}^{\text{ref}}
part of the drift is assumed known (e.g., with
u_{t}^{\mathit{ref}}=-\nabla V_{t}
, for
V_{t}
a double-well potential). Suppose we then observe, at multiple time points
t_{1},\dots,t_{K}
, potentially noisy observations of the dynamics of the form

y_{k}=x_{t_{k}}+\sigma z_{k},\quad k=1,\dots,K\,,

which is an additive Gaussian noise model. Given such a prior and data
\{y_{k}\}_{k}
, the goal is to
infer the posterior path measure
q
, which is known to take the form
[
29
]
:

dX_{t}=[u_{t}^{\text{ref}}(X_{t})+b_{t}(X_{t})]dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},X_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\quad\text{Denote this path measure as }(q_{t})_{t\leq T}\,.

(2)

Equivalently, given methods to sample from
(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}})_{t\leq T}
, we wish to design algorithms to sample from
(q_{t})_{t\leq T}
. Such problems find applications in trajectory inference in biology
[
13
]
, time series Bayesian analysis, among many others. Notably, this is a generalization of Transition Path Sampling (TPS) from chemistry
[
8
]
, since one can simply take
t_{K}=T
to be a single observation at time
t=T
with
y_{K}=B
,
\rho_{0}=\delta_{A}
and
\sigma
small, i.e., we aim to sample from the “bridge"
\mathbb{P}(x_{T-h},x_{T-2h},\dots,x_{h}|x_{0}=A,x_{T}=B)
in TPS with
J(x;y)\approx\delta(x_{T}\neq B)
.

The fact that under mild regularity conditions, the posterior path measure (conditioned on the observations
\{y_{k}\}
) can always be represented with an additional drift
b_{t}^{\theta}

[
29
]
means that it can be understood as a “controlled diffusion process”. Thus, the task amounts to inferring the drift of the posterior SDE given a prior, assuming both (
1
), (
2
) have the same initial
\rho_{0}
that is perfectly known.

Mathematically, we write the conditional density in path space for
\{x(t)\}_{t\leq T}
as
[
3
, Section 4.1]

Q(x)\propto\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp(-I(x)-J(x;y))

(3)

where
I(x)
is the Onsager-Machlup action functional for the unconditional density of path

I(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}-u_{t}^{\text{ref}}(X_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot u_{t}^{\text{ref}}(X_{t})\right)dt

and
J(x;y)
is the likelihood term

J(x;y)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|y_{k}-x_{t_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|y_{k}-x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\delta(t-t_{k})\,dt=:\int_{0}^{T}J_{t}(x_{t})\,dt\,.

(4)

Above, the observations
y
parameterize the posterior
Q(x)
we are interested in sampling from, and it is a
global
conditioning. We assume that (1) the observation at time
t_{k}
,
y_{k}
, depends only on
x_{t_{k}}
, and not other time points; (2)
(\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}})_{t\leq T}
in (
1
) is a Markov process throughout the paper. Note that the resulting quantity of interest here is not a deterministic trajectory but rather a probability measure over path ensembles. Maximizing the functional
Q(x)
will give the most probable path under observations.

Contributions

In this work, we are interested in identifying the optimal
b_{t}^{*}
, which provides a convenient way to draw additional samples / infer the trajectory, rather than just producing a set of sampled trajectories from the target.
In particular, we explore novel ways to adapt recent ML ideas from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
sampling to
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d}))
sampling. Our two proposals are presented in Section
3
and
4
. Although both methods rely on the posterior nature of the measure rather than just sampling from a generic path measure, they take different viewpoints on the problem. Thus, we expect them to perform well under different likelihood models, and are suitable for complementary use cases. Experimental results can be found in Appendix
F
.

Related Work

With regards to the infinite-dimensional path-space sampling problem on
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists a longstanding body of research concerning at least two different types of methods.

Existing Method 1: Langevin Stochastic PDE

With the target
Q(x)
defined as above, one can write down a Langevin SPDE on
x(t,s)
:

\frac{\partial x(t,s)}{\partial s}=\delta_{x}\log Q(x)+\sqrt{2}\frac{\partial W}{\partial s}\quad\text{for}\;(s,t)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,T)

(5)

where
s
is the algorithmic time (we expect as
s\rightarrow\infty
relying on ergodicity of the Markov chain it should converge to the desired equilibrium
Q(x)
) and
t\in[0,T]
is the physical time that simply index the path. The variational derivative
\delta_{x}\log Q(x)
can be worked out analytically (as done in
[
3
]
). Together with appropriate boundary conditions, this can serve as a sampler for the problem (assuming observation at endpoint
T
):

\displaystyle\delta_{x}\log Q(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}x_{t}}{dt^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}u(x)\nabla u(x)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\nabla\cdot u(x))+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(y_{k}-x(t_{k},\cdot))

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x(0,s)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle-u(x(0,s))+2\nabla\log\rho_{0}(x(0,s))=0,\quad t=0

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x(T,s)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle-u(x(T,s))-\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}[y_{K}-x(T,s)]=0,\quad t=T

There have been attempts to pursue different variants of MCMC methods from Stuart et al.
[
32
,
7
]
(possibly with MH adjustment), with a connection to data assimilation generally.

Existing Method 2: learning
b_{t}(X_{t})

Rather than working directly with paths, which can be memory-intensive, another approach is to directly learn the additional drift
b_{t}(X_{t})
in (
2
), which in fact takes a gradient form. This often reduces to an optimal control problem or alternatively a high-dimensional PDE problem. It is known, since the posterior is a reweighting of the prior path measure, the drift admits closed-form in a path integral representation
[
29
, Theorem 1]
:

b_{t}(z)=2\nabla\log\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}}}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{t}^{T}J_{s}(x_{s})ds\right)\Big|x_{t}=z\right]=:-\nabla v_{t}(z)

(7)

for the conditioned SDE, where the expectation is taken over the prior SDE with drift
u_{t}^{\text{ref}}
. This is something one may hope to simulate to estimate and recovers Doob’s
h
-transform
2\nabla_{x}\log p_{T|t}(X_{T}=B|X_{t}=x)
when we only observe at time
T

[
28
]
. But since the quantity under estimation is typically a rare-event under the reference measure, even with importance weights, it is generally hard to do well; many paths will have negligible weights and the relevant paths are exponentially rare. There is a PDE one can write down for
v_{t}
in (
7
) as well using Feynman-Kac:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v_{t}(z)+u_{t}^{\text{ref}}(z)^{\top}\nabla v_{t}(z)+\Delta v_{t}(z)+J_{t}(z)=\|\nabla v_{t}(z)\|^{2}

(8)

but it isn’t clear that solving a high-dimensional PDE would be any easier. Some other related work along these lines (with possibly marginal variational approximation) include
[
19
,
15
,
33
]
. A method based on score-matching for learning this drift has also been proposed
[
18
]
.

In the case of TPS, this is a bona-fide Schrödinger bridge (SB) problem with two delta terminal constraints, for which one can do path-space Sinkhorn / IPF by iteratively minimizing
\text{KL}(\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\delta_{A},u}\|\overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\delta_{B},v})
over the two slots alternatively. It amounts to setting
u_{0}=u^{\text{ref}}
, and perform a sequence of score-matching time reversals to learn the drifts
u^{*},v^{*}
. However, this is not as general as our setup, and methodology from
[
26
]
that is also based on an alternating projection for SB is difficult to generalize when
u^{\text{ref}}\neq 0
since simulating from the conditional bridge would be challenging. We note that compared to the series of work on flow matching
[
34
]
, which aim to match the time marginals for a prescribed path (often set up as mixtures of diffusion processes), we aim for the more ambitious goal of sampling from the true target path measure.

Crucially, our setup does not assume access to data, which distinguishes it from the proposals in
[
35
]
that leverage diffusion model for conditional sampling from a function-valued probability density over
\{X_{t}\}_{t}
.

3
Controlled Transport from Prior to Posterior

3.1
Tracking
b_{t}

Building on
[
24
]
and
[
1
]
, we introduce a sequence of path measures as

\pi_{s}(x)=Z_{s}^{-1}\cdot(\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp(-I(x)-s\cdot J(x;y)))\quad s\in[0,1]

where
Z_{s}=\int\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp(-I(x)-s\cdot J(x;y))dx
. It is clear from the definition that
\pi_{s}
interpolate between the prior and the posterior. Similarly, we introduce a family of SDEs parametrized as

dX_{t}^{s}=b_{t}^{s}(X_{t}^{s})dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},\;X_{0}^{s}\sim\rho_{0}\;\;\text{and}\;\;b_{t}^{0}=u_{t}^{\text{ref}}

inducing corresponding path measures

\rho_{s}(x)=Y_{s}^{-1}\cdot\left[\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}-b_{t}^{s}(x_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot b_{t}^{s}(x_{t})\right)dt\right)\right]\,.

If we can learn the drift change in
b
at each step
s
as the solution of (holding for all path
x
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\pi_{s}(x)}{\partial s}=\pi_{s}(x)\left(-J(x;y)+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{s}(x)}[J(x;y)]\right)

(9)

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}\frac{\partial\rho_{s}(x)}{\partial s}=\rho_{s}(x)\left(h_{s}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}(x)}[h_{s}(x)]\right)

\displaystyle\text{where }h_{s}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(b_{t}^{s}(x_{t})-\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial b_{t}^{s}}{\partial s}(x_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial b_{t}^{s}}{\partial s}(x_{t})\,dt

then the resulting drift
\{b_{t}^{s}\}_{t}
will follow the path measure
\pi_{s}(x)
for all
s\in[0,1]
– thus
exactly
hitting target at time
s=1
. Unlike MCMC (
5
), this is “controlled", which means we don’t need to run until
s=\infty
.
Operationally, one starts by drawing samples from the prior
\pi_{0}(x)
, estimate the expectation in

h_{s}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{s}(x)}[h_{s}(x)]=-J(x;y)+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{s}(x)}[J(x;y)]

(10)

using empirical samples, and solve for
\partial b^{s}/\partial s
on the LHS to update the drift. This is followed by re-simulate paths using this new drift

b^{s+\delta s}\approx b^{s}+(\partial b^{s}/\partial s)\cdot\delta s

for again imposing the consistency equation
\pi_{s+\delta_{s}}(x)=\rho_{s+\delta_{s}}(x)
in (
10
) and repeat until
s\approx 1
since
\pi_{1}(x)=Q(x)
. Note that estimating (
10
) does not require knowing the normalizing constant. In practice, one can parametrize
\{\partial b_{t}^{s}/\partial s\}_{t}
as a Neural Network taking
(x_{t}^{s},t,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]\times[0,1]
as input and outputting a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued vector.

To correct for the possible lag
\rho_{s}\neq\pi_{s}
, one may optionally simulate the trajectories using the current drift
b^{s}
, followed by a few steps of MCMC w.r.t the density
\pi_{s}
, after which we learn the update
\partial b^{s}/\partial s
using the latest trajectories, and repeat – this is somewhat similar to Algorithm 2 from
[
24
]
where SVGD is used in between for
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
sampling.

The intuition behind this approach is that it is easier to conceptualize the algorithmic dynamics on the path measure space
q^{n}\rightarrow q^{n+1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow q^{*}
and work out the corresponding drift update for the SDE
b^{n}\rightarrow b^{n+1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow b^{*}
for implementation purpose. At a high level, this is a transport idea in the path measure setting.

Path-space Normalizing Constant Estimator

With the
\rho_{s}=\pi_{s}
interpolation method above (i.e., maintaining equilibrium dynamics w.r.t
\pi_{s}\;\forall s\in[0,1]
), one can consider thermodynamic integration in path space and calculate the partition function for
Q
as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Z_{s}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle=\int-J(x;y)\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp(-I(x)-s\cdot J(x;y))dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\int-J(x;y)\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp(-I(x)-s\cdot J(x;y))dx}{\int\rho_{0}(x_{t_{0}})\exp(-I(x)-s\cdot J(x;y))dx}Z_{s}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{s}}[-J(x;y)]\cdot Z_{s}

therefore
Z_{T}=Z_{0}\cdot\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{s}}[-J(x;y)]\,ds\right)
assuming the initial
Z_{0}
is analytically tractable. Quantity of such type is often of interest in chemistry applications. The end-to-end method is summarized in Algorithm
1
(c.f. Appendix
E
).

3.2
Kernel Method

Without NN, we can solve (
10
) with Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) by assuming
b
belongs to a RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. The benefit of this is that everything now becomes closed-form. For each
s
, denote
v^{s}(x_{t},t):=\frac{\partial b^{s}}{\partial s}(x_{t},t)
as a vector field mapping
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where we have an extra
t\in[T]
dimension compared to
[
24
]
because we are working in path-space. Introduce the linear operator
\mathcal{S}
mapping the function
v^{s}
and path
x^{i}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
to a scalar
h_{s}(x^{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}h_{s}(x^{j})
. Assuming
N
trajectories, at each
s\in[0,1]
, one solves

\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathcal{S}(v^{s})(x^{i})-j(x^{i}))^{2}+\lambda\|v^{s}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2}

(11)

by letting
j(x^{i}):=-J(x^{i})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}J(x^{j})
that takes a path
x^{i}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
as input and outputs a scalar.

Lemma 1

(Kernel Method)
.

KRR method for solving (
10
) with empirical samples, written in (
11
) for each
s\in[0,1]
, can be implemented analytically by solving a linear system of size
N\times N
at each
t\in[T]
.

Remark 1
.

The way we wrote it, it may seem that the
T
problems to solve are independent from each other, but they are in fact coupled through terms like
dx_{t}^{i}/dt
in the proof.

3.3
Equilibrium vs. Non-Equilibrium Thermodynamics

Our methods from Section
3.1
-
3.2
, and the alternative “path-based drifts" discussed in Appendix
A
, being based on having the dynamics exactly following the interpolation curve, are equilibrium methods. Alternatively, one can use the annealed gradient
\delta_{x}\log\pi_{s}(x)
from the SPDE, together with MH adjustment, as a time-inhomogeneous transition kernel, followed by
weighting
on the samples to track the
\pi_{s}
curve (Algorithm
2
). Since the MCMC dynamics alone does not impose
\rho_{s}=\pi_{s}
exactly (a single step of MCMC with changing target will result in a delay), these fall in the category of non-equilibrium methods. The result below bears a resemblance to that in
[
30
, Section 4]
, and is a generalization of Jarzynski’s equality to path space.

Lemma 2

(Time-discrete Jarzynski)
.

The analogue of the Jarzynski equality in discrete time in path space is: for
s\in[0,1]
along the Algorithm
2
dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{s}}{Z_{0}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(X_{t}\sim\pi_{t}(x))_{t\leq s}}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{s}J(X_{t})dt\right)\right]\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\underbrace{\exp\left(-\sum_{t=0}^{s/\delta_{s}}\delta_{s}\cdot J(X_{t}^{i})\right)}_{\exp(-W^{i,s/\delta_{s}})}

where we obtain path
X_{t}
from
X_{t-\delta_{s}}
using a numerical discretization of a SPDE dynamics leaving
\pi_{t}(x)
invariant through MH adjustment, with
X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}=\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}}
as initialization. Setting
s=1
, the average of a statistics
h(\cdot)
over the path w.r.t the target
Q(x)
can be computed as

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}h(X_{n}^{j})e^{-W^{j,n}(X^{j})}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{-W^{j,n}(X^{j})}}

on the final set of
M
trajectories
\{X_{n}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
, with
n=1/\delta_{s}
as the number of switching steps.

In practice, we also have to discretize the
X_{t}
path along the
[0,T]
physical time, as shown in Algorithm
2
in detail. Some drawbacks of this non-equilibrium approach: (1) simulating SPDE with MH in general is computationally intensive; (2) the weights may have high variance depending on how fast
\pi_{s}
changes (compared to equilibrium methods, where each path has weight
\approx 1
and the switching can be faster). All proofs and additional details for this section can be found in Appendix
A
.

4
Reformulating Gibbs Variational Principle via Wasserstein Dynamics

There is a variational formulation for the posterior path measure (that can be obtained by generalizing from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) so in our case we can rewrite the problem as

\arg\min_{\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}:\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}_{0}=\rho_{0}}\;\;\text{KL}(\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}\|Q^{*})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\arg\min_{\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}:\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}_{0}=\rho_{0}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}}[J(x;y)]+\text{KL}(\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}})

(12)

where
Q^{*}
is the target from (
3
) and the solution is unique under mild assumptions. Equivalently in terms of the drift, using Girsanov’s theorem we have

\arg\min_{b^{\theta}}\;\;\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}^{\theta}\sim q_{t}^{\theta}}\left[J_{t}(x_{t}^{\theta};y)+\frac{1}{4}\|b_{t}^{\theta}(x_{t}^{\theta})\|^{2}\right]\,dt\,.

(13)

While the loss (
13
) can in principle be estimated via empirical samples and used to optimize over
b^{\theta}
, there are a few challenges: (1) the loss is complicated as a function of the drift
b^{\theta}
(even though in terms of
\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}
in (
12
) it’s convex); (2) one needs to calculate
\partial x_{t}^{\theta}/\partial\theta
generated with SDE (
2
) for updating
b^{\theta}
, which requires back-propagating through the solver. This approach is related to some of the Neural SDE methods for fitting SDEs to data
[
20
,
25
]
.

Taking a different route based on convex optimization, we first leverage the Benamou-Brenier
[
4
]
reformulation to pose (
12
) as joint learning over
(q_{t},b_{t})_{t}
, which yields insights that will allow us to simplify further later. For our specific case, it becomes

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\min_{q_{t},b_{t}}\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\frac{1}{4}\|b_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}+J_{t}(x_{t};y)\right]q_{t}(x_{t})\,dxdt

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\;\partial_{t}q_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(q_{t}(b_{t}+u_{t}^{\text{ref}}))+\Delta q_{t},q_{0}=\rho_{0}

(14)

Now if one follows the augmented Lagrangian approach in
[
4
]
that via reparameterization turns the problem into a convex optimization, the Laplacian
\Delta q_{t}
from the diffusion in Fokker-Planck introduces a challenge as we have to deal with a Bilaplacian type of quantity in one of the updates.

4.1
Approximation

For (
14
), there is a way to move the Laplacian term into the objective that is used in various works (e.g., Eqn (4.25) in
[
12
]
). Followed by the similar
(q_{t},b_{t})\mapsto(q_{t},m_{t})
for
m_{t}=q_{t}\cdot b_{t}
transformation as done in
[
4
]
, we end up with our
jointly convex
objective with an extra (relative) Fisher information in the objective but a continuity equation as the constraint.

Proposition 1

(Convex Reformulation)
.

For a general reference
u_{t}^{\text{ref}}\neq 0
, (
14
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{q_{t},m_{t}}\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\frac{1}{4}\left\|\frac{m_{t}}{q_{t}}(x_{t})-(u_{t}^{\text{ref}}-\nabla\log\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}})(x_{t})\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}}}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}+J_{t}(x_{t})\right]q_{t}(x_{t})dxdt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q_{T}(x_{T})\log\frac{q_{T}}{\mathbb{P}_{T}^{\text{ref}}}(x_{T})\,dx_{T}

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\;\partial_{t}q_{t}=-\nabla\cdot m_{t},\;q_{0}=\rho_{0}\,.

(15)

When the reference process
\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}}
is in equilibrium (i.e.,
\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}}_{t}=\nu\propto e^{-V}\;\forall t
) the problem reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{q_{t},m_{t}}\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\frac{1}{4}\left\|\frac{m_{t}}{q_{t}}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\nabla\log\frac{q_{t}}{\nu}(x_{t})\right\|^{2}+J_{t}(x_{t})\right]q_{t}(x_{t})\,dxdt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}q_{T}(x_{T})\log\frac{q_{T}}{\nu}(x_{T})\,dx_{T}

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\;\partial_{t}q_{t}=-\nabla\cdot m_{t},\;q_{0}=\rho_{0}

(16)

In this case,
\rho_{0}=\nu=\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\text{ref}}
for the initial distribution.

Reformulation in terms of marginal density:

Compared to the dynamic formulation of
\mathcal{W}_{2}
optimal transport from
[
4
]
, in Lemma
1
we have an extra (relative) Fisher information and potential energy term involving
J
, and removal of the terminal
\rho_{T}
constraint for a penalty on
q_{T}
. In what follows, we adopt the following approximation, which crucially only involves the evolution of a
single
variable, instead of two. Since the objective (
16
) is (jointly) convex and (almost) separable over time
t\in[0,T]
we propose to solve a sequence of “Wasserstein Gradient Flow"/JKO steps between adjacent marginals as (initialized at
q_{0}=\rho_{0}
)

q_{t+h}\leftarrow\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\;\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(q,q_{t})+F(q)

(17)

for advancing the solution over
t\in[0,T]
with stepsize
h
, where
F(q)
is the convex relative Fisher information functional
I(q;\nu):=\frac{1}{2}\int\|\nabla\log q(x)/\nu(x)\|^{2}q(x)dx
+ term linear in
q:\frac{2}{h}\int J_{t+h}(x)q(x)dx
.
To see one can work with
\{q_{t}\}_{t}
only, observe that in (

(or equivalently

) only appears in the first part, and the rest is a function of the density

only. Therefore given a curve of optimal measure

one can solve classical OT between adjacent pairs to get the corresponding vector fields

that follow the trajectory with

kinetic energy (hence divergence-free). This is an Action Matching problem
[

if provided with samples from the

curve.

Conceptually, this is a dynamical system perspective on the problem as a sequence of transformations

q_{0}\xrightarrow{b_{h}}q_{h}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow q_{T-h}\xrightarrow{b_{T}}q_{T}\,,

and we study the joint distribution over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
at a time, as the Markovian representation (

distribution to a sequence of

that repeatedly updates the path measure

, it is a one-pass method evolving
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
from

to

, local in nature. Alternative approximations on (
16
) and more justification for our approach based on (

) are discussed in Section

.

We view the methods in the following sections as more principled than simply writing out the Lagrangian for (
14
) and fitting
2
NNs to solve a min-max problem over the
2
variables across

. This is the approach adopted in
[

where gradient descent/ascent is done on the

functions (solution of 2 coupled PDEs), subject to (multi-)marginal constraints: specializing to the case of SB it is (c.f. eqn (28) therein)

\inf_{q_{t}\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}\sup_{\phi_{t}}\quad\int\phi_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(x)dx-\int\phi_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(x)dx-\int_{0}^{1}\int\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\phi_{t}+\partial_{t}\phi_{t}\right)q_{t}\,dxdt\,,

(18)

which can be viewed as a PINN regularizer for the dynamical dual formulation of SB:

\min_{Q_{0}=\rho_{0},Q_{1}=\rho_{1}}\text{KL}(Q\|W)=\sup_{\phi_{t}}\;\int\phi_{1}(x)\rho_{1}(x)dx-\int\phi_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(x)dx\quad\text{s.t.}\;\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\phi_{t}+\partial_{t}\phi_{t}=0

with collocation points drawn from
q_{t}
. Operationally, (

) amounts to a series of Action Matching and WGF updates, with both
\phi,q
parametrized as NNs. The re-writing, although involving only

4.2
Low Dimensional Case: Composite Optimization for (
15

For the most general case (
15
), since the resulting optimization is jointly convex in the variables – the system in (
15
) should be contrasted with Theorem 1 in
[

]
– one may discretize the space and apply convex optimization methods on the resulting objective.

The formulation in (
15
) requires
\nabla\log\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}}
for the reference process, which we typically don’t know analytically. But assuming (1) one can sample from the prior trajectories; (2)
u_{t}^{\text{ref}}=-\nabla V_{t}
takes gradient form, an Action Matching loss
[
22
, Algorithm 1]
can be used to learn this, where we expect
\nabla s_{t}^{*}(x)\approx-\nabla V_{t}(x)-\nabla\log\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}}(x)
. In the case of TPS with the Brownian bridge as reference, we have
\nabla\log\mathbb{P}_{t}^{\text{ref}}(x_{t})=-\frac{x_{t}-A}{2t}

analytically available.
[
28
, Lemma B.1]
gives another way to compute this score, which also only requires simulating from the reference process and fitting a least squares objective.

With (
15
) in mind, we can adapt the iterative procedure in
[
10
]
to solve this convex problem via a primal-dual method (a related Augmented Lagrangian method for WGF is also considered in
[
6
]
). Our method reduces to minimizing a finite-dimensional composite objective of type

\min_{y}\;\Phi(y)+\Psi(\Lambda y)

for each time stepping from
t
to
t+h
– therefore we solve in total
T/h
problems of such type. Above

are both convex functions that admit an easy-to-compute proximal operator. Departing from the min-max formulation
[

]
, this is generally simpler, with convergence ensured. We give the details of this Eulerian solver in Appendix
B

The method above, though NN-free, requires updates on a

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(since it uses an Eulerian representation of the discretized density over space), so may run into scalability issues in high dimension.

4.3
High Dimensional Case: Lagrangian Methods for (
16
)

In the high-dimensional regime, we will work with the simpler problem of (
16
) that conditions an equilibrium process with observations, and advocate for parameterizing

as either an interacting particle system, or as a parametrized push-forward map
[
9

that allows us to infer

easily. In both cases, we leverage the perspective put forth in (

) so optimization is only done between adjacent marginals to track its evolution, and not joint learning over

. We will heavily rely on the representation of Fisher information as

\displaystyle R(q_{t}):=\frac{1}{2}\int\|\nabla\log q_{t}(x)\|^{2}q_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=2\int\|\nabla\sqrt{q_{t}(x)}\|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\|\nabla q_{t}(x)\|^{2}}{q_{t}(x)}dx

\displaystyle=\sup_{\frac{1}{2}\|\beta(x)\|^{2}+\alpha(x)\leq 0}\int\nabla q_{t}(x)^{\top}\beta(x)+q_{t}(x)\alpha(x)dx

(19)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\frac{1}{2}\|\beta(x)\|^{2}+\alpha(x)\leq 0}\int(\alpha(x)-\nabla\cdot\beta(x))\cdot q_{t}(x)dx

(20)

and its approximation with empirical samples below. All proofs for this subsection are given in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 3

(FI Estimator)
.

Given
n
samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim q_{t}
, one can efficiently estimate the Fisher Information functional as
\hat{R}(q_{t})\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\|y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{4}}\right)
for
y_{i}^{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I),j=1\dots,m
, and
\sigma
a bandwidth parameter.

4.3.1
Sequence of Pushforward Maps

The first Lagrangian approach we consider is based on Brenier’s Theorem for
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-OT, which says that the optimal transport map is given by the gradient of a convex function that solves a Monge-Ampère equation. To leverage this push-forward map representation
[
9
]
, we rewrite (
17
) as

\displaystyle\phi_{t}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\phi\,\text{convex}}\;\;\frac{1}{2h^{2}}\int\|\nabla\phi(x)-x\|_{2}^{2}\cdot q_{t}(x)dx+F(\nabla\phi_{\#}q_{t});\quad q_{t+h}\leftarrow\nabla\phi_{t\#}q_{t}

(21)

and propose to use an Input-Convex NN architecture
[
2
]
to optimize over a family of convex functions
\phi^{\theta}
that takes
t,\{x_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim q_{t}
and output
\{x_{t+h}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim q_{t+h}
. The gradient
\nabla\phi^{\theta}
gives us the desired transport map. This allows us to evolve an empirical measure
\hat{q}
in time and space.

In order to evaluate
F(\nabla\phi_{\#}q_{t})
for optimization, the "internal energy" term
\int\|\nabla\log q_{t}(x_{t})\|^{2}dq_{t}(x_{t})
that is nonlinear in
q_{t}
seems challenging to estimate with empirical measure
\hat{q}_{t}
, and using the change of variables formula involving determinant along the dynamics is computationally intensive. However, here one can linearize via bi-level optimization using (
19
) as we illustrate in Algorithm
3
(the rest of the terms in
F
are easily linearizable with possible integration-by-parts). Lemma
3
gives a
0
th-order method for approximating the “gradient", and luckily here we only need to draw samples from
q_{t}
for implementation,
without
evaluating its density. The particular form (
20
) is a special feature of Fisher Information – other nonlinear energies may be difficult to re-parameterize to be linearizable.

4.3.2
Interacting Particle System

To understand the iterative scheme (
17
), which is better termed acceleration in Wasserstein geometry with

functional, we see that although one description on the optimal marginal density evolution in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is through the pair of PDEs Fokker-Planck (evolved till time

\partial_{t}q_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(q_{t}(b_{t}+u^{\text{ref}}))+\Delta q_{t},\,q_{0}=\rho_{0}

and HJB on

Proposition 2

(Optimality Condition)
.

The Euler–Lagrange equations for (

[0,T]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(q_{t}\nabla\theta_{t}),\,q_{0}=\rho_{0},\nabla\theta_{0}=b_{0}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\theta_{t}\|^{2}=-2(\Delta\sqrt{q_{t}})/\sqrt{q_{t}}+2\Delta\sqrt{\nu}/\sqrt{\nu}+2J_{t}

\displaystyle 2J_{T}(X_{T})+\log\frac{q_{T}}{\nu}(X_{T})+\theta_{T}(X_{T})=0\,.

Therefore for

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\theta_{t}(X_{t})

\displaystyle\ddot{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\left[\frac{\partial\theta_{t}(X_{t})}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\theta_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]=-\left(2\nabla\frac{\Delta\sqrt{q_{t}}}{\sqrt{q_{t}}}-2\nabla\frac{\Delta\sqrt{\nu}}{\sqrt{\nu}}-2\nabla J_{t}\right)(X_{t})

\displaystyle=-\nabla\left(\Delta\log q_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\log q_{t}\|^{2}+\Delta V-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla V\|^{2}-2J_{t}\right)(X_{t})=:-\nabla F(q_{t})(X_{t})\,.

\displaystyle X_{h}=X_{0}+h\nabla\theta_{0}(X_{0})-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{s}\nabla F(q_{t})(X_{t})\,dtds=X_{0}+h\nabla\theta_{0}(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{h}\nabla F(q_{t})(X_{t})(h-t)\,dt\,.

Remark 2

5
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed two classes of new methodologies for bringing NN training to the problem of posterior path sampling. The first is based on a
controlled
dynamics over path space in order to progressively learn the optimal
b_{t}^{*}
vector field in a memory-efficient way (Sec
3.1
), and is guaranteed to hit target
Q
at
finite
time without additional reweighting, or solving high-dimensional PDEs. The second is based on convex optimization in Wasserstein space and OT, where we gradually evolve a curve of marginal densities over time. We propose grid-free stochastic methods, based either on push-forward maps (Sec
4.3.1
), or an interacting particle system (Sec
4.3.2
), that can avoid the curse of dimensionality, while maintaining generality and good approximation properties (Sec
4.3.3
).

\displaystyle=-\int\alpha^{*}(x)\nabla(\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x))dx+\int\nabla\cdot\beta^{*}(x)\nabla(\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x))dx

\displaystyle=-\int\alpha^{*}(x)(\nabla\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x)+\mathcal{K}(x-z)\nabla q_{t}(x))dx-\int\beta^{*}(x)\nabla\cdot[\nabla(\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x))]dx

\displaystyle=-\int\alpha^{*}(x)(\nabla\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x)+\mathcal{K}(x-z)\nabla q_{t}(x))dx

(42)

\displaystyle-\int\beta^{*}(x)\nabla\cdot\nabla\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x)+2\beta^{*}(x)\nabla\mathcal{K}(x-z)^{\top}\nabla q_{t}(x)dx-\int\beta^{*}(x)\mathcal{K}(x-z)\nabla\cdot(\nabla q_{t}(x))dx

This suggests an implementation as

\displaystyle X_{t+h}^{i}

\displaystyle=X_{t}^{i}+hV_{t}(X_{t}^{i})+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\cdot\left(\mathcal{K}\nabla\delta R(\hat{q}_{t})(X_{t}^{i})+2\nabla J_{t}(X_{t}^{i})\right),\;\;i\in[n]

\displaystyle V_{t+h}(X_{t+h}^{i})

\displaystyle=V_{t}(X_{t}^{i})+h\cdot\left(\mathcal{K}\nabla\delta R(\hat{q}_{t})(X_{t}^{i})+2\nabla J_{t}(X_{t}^{i})\right)

for

\hat{q}_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)(x)\;\text{ with }x_{i}\sim q_{t}

so the particle system with
n
particles will approximately follow the optimal
(q_{t})_{t\leq T}
curve with stepsize
h
.

In (
42
), earlier calculation from (
40
) shows that e.g.,
\int\alpha^{*}(x)\nabla\mathcal{K}(x-z)q_{t}(x)dx
can be approximated as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\int\alpha^{*}(x)\nabla\mathcal{K}(x-z)\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)(x)dx}_{\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)}[\alpha^{*}(y)\nabla\mathcal{K}(y-z)]}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}-\frac{\|y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{4}}\nabla\mathcal{K}(y_{i}^{j}-z)\right)

(43)

since on a random grid
\{y_{i}^{j}\}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)
with
x_{i}\sim q_{t}
, we showed

\alpha^{*}(y_{i}^{j})=-\frac{\|y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{4}},\;\;\beta^{*}(y_{i}^{j})=\frac{x_{i}-y_{i}^{j}}{\sigma^{2}}\,.

The only term that’s not in terms of
q_{t},\nabla q_{t}
in (
42
) is the last one – the rest can be similarly estimated as above. However, one can show that the divergence term also takes a rescaled Gaussian form:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\nabla\hat{q}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\frac{d}{\sigma^{2}}\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)(x)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|x-x_{i}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{4}}\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)(x)\,,

which makes the last term expressible as an expectation w.r.t a Gaussian mixture density – hence readily estimable provided samples from
\{y_{i}^{j}\}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{i},\sigma^{2}I)
for
x_{i}\sim q_{t}
, as done in (
43
).

The additional KL part needed for the update at
T
can be kernelized as

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{K}(x_{T}-z)\nabla

\displaystyle\log\frac{q_{T}}{\nu}(x_{T})q_{T}(x_{T})\,dx_{T}=\int-\nabla_{1}\mathcal{K}(x_{T}-z)q_{T}(x_{T})+\mathcal{K}(x_{T}-z)\nabla V(x_{T})q_{T}(x_{T})\,dx_{T}\,,

followed by replacing
q_{T}
with
\hat{q}_{T}
. This is similar to what’s done in SVGD
[
21
]
.

Compared to Algorithm
3
that leverages push-forward maps, this particular approach, although also evolves a density over time through space, does not require NN training. Other particle methods for implementing dynamics in Wasserstein space can be found in e.g.,
[
11
]
. We note that most of the literature focuses on Wasserstein Gradient Flow (à la JKO), whereas here we consider
2
nd-order dynamics.

Remark 5
.

A rewriting of the acceleration field makes it clear what the driving forces behind the dynamics are:

\ddot{X}_{t}=\nabla_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}\left(\|\nabla_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}\text{KL}(q\|\nu)\|_{q}^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{q}[J]\right)(X_{t})\,.

Since the squared norm of the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-gradient for the KL divergence is the relative Fisher Information, we expect the particles to undergo rapid speed change when either
q
differs significantly from
\nu
or if the external potential
J
is exerting large force.

Appendix D
Alternative Decompositions & Approximations with Entropic-OT

In this section, we give additional details on Section
4.3.3
. Our earlier reductions operate in the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
endowed with a Wasserstein
\mathcal{W}_{2}
geometry. We explore other geometries here.

As mentioned in the main text, with the grouping (
25
), we can afford a larger time-stepping, i.e., solve between each adjacent pair of observation time
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
– this is different from the
o(h^{2})
approximation error we incur when using
\mathcal{W}_{2}
. For reversible Brownian motion
W
as reference, it becomes iteratively solving between
2
observations:

q_{t_{i+1}}\leftarrow\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\;\mathcal{W}_{2,t_{i+1}-t_{i}}^{2}(q,q_{t_{i}})+\underbrace{2\int J_{t_{i+1}}(x)q(x)dx}_{\bar{F}(q)}

(44)

with entropy-regularized quadratic
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance

\mathcal{W}_{2,t_{i+1}-t_{i}}^{2}(q,q_{t_{i}})=\min_{\pi^{i}\in\Pi(q_{t_{i}},q)}\int\frac{1}{2(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi^{i}(x,y)+\text{KL}(\pi^{i}\|q_{t_{i}}\otimes q)

as the distance-measuring metric and the linear likelihood term as the objective functional
\bar{F}
, which is arguably simpler than that based on (
17
)

q_{t+h}\leftarrow\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\;\frac{1}{2h}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(q,q_{t})+h\cdot\underbrace{\int\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\log\frac{q}{\nu}(x)\|^{2}q(x)dx+2\int J_{t+h}(x)q(x)dx}_{F(q)}

involving
I(q;\nu)
.

Eulerian Method

Using ideas from
[
27
]
gives a proximal splitting first order method in low dimension for (
44
). It results in a sequence of Sinkhorn-like updates on the dual potentials. But being confined to a fixed grid, such methods will run into scalability problem in high dimension.

Lagrangian Method

One candidate here for implementing e.g.,
t=t_{i}\rightarrow t_{i+1}
update in (
44
) in high dimension can be:
let
\hat{q}_{t_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{j}^{t_{i}}}
be given as
n
particles distributed as
q_{t_{i}}
, since the first variation of
q\mapsto\mathcal{W}_{2,t_{i+1}-t_{i}}^{2}(q,q_{t_{i}})
is the optimal dual potential
\varphi
for the coupling, for iteration
k\geq 1
perform

\displaystyle x_{j}^{t_{i+1}}[k+1]

\displaystyle\leftarrow x_{j}^{t_{i+1}}[k]-\eta\cdot\left(\frac{1}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}\nabla\varphi^{\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}}(x_{j}^{t_{i+1}}[k])+2\nabla J_{t_{i+1}}(x_{j}^{t_{i+1}}[k])\right),\quad j\in[n]

(45)

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{j}^{t_{i+1}}[k]}\;\text{ with }\;\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{1}=\hat{q}_{t_{i}}\,.

In the above we expect
\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}\rightarrow q_{t_{i+1}}
, the optimal solution of (
44
), as
k\rightarrow\infty
. To get
\varphi^{\hat{q}}
needed in (
45
), one can solve an empirical Entropy-regularized OT problem, using its dual formulation: the optimal dual potential
\varphi^{\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}}
for the marginal
\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}
(with the other being fixed at
\hat{q}_{t_{i}}
) solves

\arg\max_{\varphi,\psi}\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi(X_{j})+\psi(Y_{j})-(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\varphi(X_{j})+\psi(Y_{j})-\frac{\|X_{j}-Y_{j}\|^{2}}{2}}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}\right),\quad\text{for }X_{j}\sim\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k},Y_{j}\sim\hat{q}_{t_{i}}

From this we take the optimal
\{\psi^{*}(Y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
, and set

\varphi^{*}(x)=-(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\log\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{\frac{\psi^{*}(Y_{j})-\|x-Y_{j}\|^{2}/2}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}}\right)

to be the soft
c
-transform, after which we can take its derivative to get (with this expression
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be evaluated anywhere)

\nabla\varphi^{\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}}(x)=\nabla\varphi^{*}(x)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x-Y_{j})e^{\frac{\psi^{*}(Y_{j})-\|x-Y_{j}\|^{2}/2}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{\frac{\psi^{*}(Y_{j})-\|x-Y_{j}\|^{2}/2}{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}}}

in order to iterate the algorithm (
45
). It is known that the optimal potentials
\varphi^{*},\psi^{*}
are related through soft
c
-transforms at optimality.

The procedure described above requires evolving
n
interacting particles over the iteration
k\geq 1
and between each pair of time
(t_{i},t_{i+1})\in[T]
. Each update
k
requires running the Sinkhorn algorithm to find the optimal dual potential
\{\psi^{*}(Y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
for the updated empirical density
\hat{q}_{t_{i+1}}^{k}
. Repeatedly running such subroutines may not be cheap computationally.

But as already commented in Remark
2
, the more serious concern here is that decomposition (
44
) cannot be generalized when we don’t know the transition kernel of the reference process – the cost function is fully determined by it.

Multi-marginal Schrödinger Bridge

More generally, using
[
5
, Lemma 3.4]
on the
\text{KL}(\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}})
term in the second equation of (
12

\arg\min_{\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}:\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}_{0}=\rho_{0}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}}[J(x;y)]+\text{KL}(\mathbb{Q}^{\theta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\text{ref}})\,,

we have that the objective over
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d}))
above can equivalently be written as

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\{q_{t_{i}}\}_{i}}\;\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int J_{t_{i}}(x)q_{t_{i}}(x)dx+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}\text{KL}(q_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\|R_{t_{i},t_{i+1}})-\sum_{i=2}^{K-1}\text{KL}(q_{t_{i}}\|R_{t_{i}})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\arg\min_{\{q_{t_{i}}\}_{i}}\;\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int J_{t_{i}}(x)q_{t_{i}}(x)dx+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}\text{KL}(\pi^{*}(q_{t_{i}},q_{t_{i+1}})\|R_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}q_{t_{i}}\otimes q_{t_{i+1}})+H(q_{t_{i}})+H(q_{t_{i+1}})

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=2}^{K-1}H(q_{t_{i}})+\sum_{i=2}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{t_{i}}}[\log\nu]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\arg\min_{\{q_{t_{i}}\}_{i}}\;\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int J_{t_{i}}(x)q_{t_{i}}(x)dx\quad\quad(\text{assuming Brownian motion reference})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}\underbrace{\min_{\pi^{i}\in\Pi(q_{t_{i}},q_{t_{i+1}})}\int\frac{1}{2(t_{i+1}-t_{i})}\|y-x\|^{2}d\pi^{i}(x,y)+\text{KL}(\pi^{i}\|q_{t_{i}}\otimes q_{t_{i+1}})}_{\text{SB}(q_{t_{i}},q_{t_{i+1}})}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}H(q_{t_{i}})\,.

This implies that the posterior sampling problem we consider can be exactly reduced to a multi-marginal SB over the
K
observation times (i.e., joint minimization over
\{q_{t_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{K}
), with additional parts of the loss involving simple functionals on a single marginal only. However, beyond the simple cases of e.g., Ornstein–Uhlenbeck or Brownian motion, it is not possible to specify the cost in
\text{SB}(q_{t_{i}},q_{t_{i+1}})
(transition probability density of the reference measure unknown generally). Such approach, in the case of the Wiener reference, is adopted in
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
where the authors propose a mean-field Langevin algorithm to solve this reduced problem over the joint
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{K}
space, after which one samples from the conditional Brownian bridge between each neighboring pair to reconstruct the entire trajectory. Such a simulation is also challenging for the non-linear reference process that we consider.

F.2

We set up the experiment below so
\nu
corresponds to the stationary distribution of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

dX_{t}=-\beta X_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t}

with
\beta=1/4
– this is considered to be the prior reference process in this example. Moreover, we initialize the
n=200
particles as
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\beta)
at stationarity. The likelihood
J_{t}
is chosen so that we impose a soft constraint in the form of
\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}\|x(t)-x_{\text{fixed}}\|^{2}
at the midpoint with a big
\gamma

\gamma
, at two different pre-specified locations

.

In the experiment below (Figure
6

h=0.2
, length of trajectory

and the perturbation variance
\sigma^{2}=0.4^{2}
with #perturbations
m=30
for the

estimator. This general Bayesian posterior path example using Algorithm
3
is conducted with a
7

\nabla\phi^{\theta}
. With
x_{\text{fixed}}=-1
at the midpoint, and

-1
