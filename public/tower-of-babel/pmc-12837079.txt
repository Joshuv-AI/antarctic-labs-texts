The clinical characteristics and outcomes of anaerobic spondylodiscitis: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Kai Tang, Yejun Zha, Chen Chen, Shuai Lu, Xieyuan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyogenic spondylodiscitis is rarely caused by anaerobic organisms. This retrospective study evaluated the clinical manifestations and treatment outcomes in patients affected by anaerobic spondylodiscitis.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis of medical records from individuals diagnosed with anaerobic spondylodiscitis between January 2018 and January 2024.ResultsFrom January 2018 to January 2024, 373 cases of pyogenic spondylodiscitis were identified in our department. Twenty-three patients (6.2%; 16 males and 7 females), with a mean age of 63.7 years (63.7 ± 14.3 years), were diagnosed with anaerobic spondylodiscitis during this period. All patients presented with persistent back pain lasting over one month, 5 patients (21.7%) had fever, and 3 patients (13.0%) had sciatica. The lumbar spine affected 18 patients (78.3%), and the thoracic spine affected 5 (21.7%) patients. Computed tomography images usually reveal erosion and destruction of adjacent endplates and vertebral bodies. T1-weighted Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a reduction in the signal intensity of the vertebral body, endplate and intervertebral disc. T2-weighted images demonstrate increased signal intensity of the vertebral body and/or disc. Cultures of specimens obtained by percutaneous fluoroscopically guided biopsy or surgery were positive for pathogens for 10 (43.5%) of 23 patients, and Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was positive for 19 (95.0%) of 20 patients. Introduction

Pyogenic spondylodiscitis is infection of the spine caused by pyogenic bacteria, which may involve the vertebral body, intervertebral disc, and epidural potential space. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common causative pathogen [1]. Anaerobic spondylodiscitis is caused by anaerobic bacteria, which is extremely uncommon and appears to account for less than 3% of pyogenic spondylodiscitis [2]. Anaerobes are the predominant components of normal human skin and mucous membrane bacterial flora [3] and therefore are a common cause of bacterial infections of endogenous origin. Many patients with osteomyelitis caused by anaerobes have an anaerobic infection elsewhere in the body that is the source of the organisms involved in osteomyelitis [4]. Among them are otitis media, decubitus ulcers, sinusitis, odontogenic infections and vaginitis. Spread to bone can be by a contiguous route or through the bloodstream during the course of sustained or intermittent bacteremia [4].

Because of their fastidious nature, these organisms are difficult to isolate from the blood and infectious sites. Therefore, the prevalence of anaerobic spondylodiscitis has long been underestimated before the advent of molecular identification. There have been few studies on anaerobic spondylodiscitis. In the present study, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics, imaging findings, and therapeutic outcomes of patients with microbiologically confirmed anaerobic spondylodiscitis through conventional culture methods and/or next-generation sequencing (NGS) over the last 6 years.

Methods

Patients

The medical records of patients with pyogenic spondylodiscitis were reviewed retrospectively from January 2018 to January 2024. Anaerobic spondylodiscitis was characterized as spinal infections encompassing vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, or epidural abscess formation attributable to anaerobic bacteria. When a patient was suspected of having infectious spondylodiscitis, at least two sets of blood cultures were obtained at the initial presentation. The diagnosis of anaerobic spondylodiscitis was established when the results of the culture tests or next-generation sequencing were positive for anaerobic bacteria in infected samples obtained through C-arm fluoroscopy or CT-guided biopsy or during surgery.

Bacterial culture

The collected fluid specimens from the focus obtained through puncture or surgical procedures were promptly transferred into BACT/ALERT FA and FN plus (aerobic and anaerobic) bottles using sterile syringes. Tissue specimens underwent mechanical homogenization prior to inoculation. BACT/ALERT FA and FN plus (aerobic and anaerobic) bottles were used for aerobic and anaerobic bacterial cultures respectively. Following standardized procedures outlined in the manufacturer’s protocol, the samples were incubated for 7 days in a BACT/ALERT 3D instrument (bioMérieux, Marcyl’Étoile, France). Subcultures were streaked onto Columbia sheep blood agar (Autobio Diagnostics, Zhengzhou, China) incubated under anaerobic conditions for 2 days. The identification of bacteria was performed with a VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France).

Next-generation sequencing

The specimens acquired through C-arm guided puncture or surgical procedures were stored in sterile tubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen at -80 °C, and subsequently transferred to Beijing GensKey Clinical Laboratory for further analysis. The DNA of each pretreated samples and the negative batch control samples were extracted with Genskey Micro DNA Kit (1901, Genskey, Tianjin, China) and measured using Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit. The NGS library construction kit (2012B, Genskey, Tianjin, China) was used to constructed the DNA library. The Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) was used to perform the quality control-related procedures for the DNA library. The concentration of the constructed DNA library was determined via Quantitative PCR (qPCR) and must be greater than 1 nmol/L. The pooled libraries underwent sequencing with single-stranded circular DNA templates, with 2–3 quantitative replicates incorporated to generate DNA nanospheres. The DNA nanospheres were loaded on the sequencing chip and sequenced via the MGISEQ-200 sequencing platform (MGI Tech Co., Shenzhen, China).

Histopathological examination

Tissue specimens underwent fixation in 10% formaldehyde solution followed by sequential processing including decalcification, dehydration, paraffin embedding, microtome sectioning, and hematoxylin-eosin staining. Additional diagnostic procedures such as immunohistochemical analysis and Ziehl-Neelsen staining were conducted when clinically indicated.

Management

Patients suspected of having pyogenic spondylodiscitis underwent a C-arm guided biopsy or surgery. The samples were subjected to microbiological culture or next-generation sequencing. When anaerobic infections were diagnosed, patients received a 6–12 week course of antimicrobial therapy. Surgical treatment involved antibiotic therapy coupled with various surgical procedures, such as debridement, abscess drainage, and decompression, with or without spinal stabilization. Management indications for surgery include the development of neurologic deficits or symptoms of spinal cord compression, bony destruction with spinal instability, and a poor response to antibiotic therapy [5]. In our research, patients with bone lesions affecting ≥ 25% of the vertebral body were recommended for surgery, which was beneficial for early recovery of mobility. There are various surgical approaches, including anterior, posterior, or combined approaches, as well as single-stage or two-stage surgeries, with or without instrumentation. The choice of surgical approach depends on the location of the lesion and the surgeon’s preference. When patients with underlying conditions precluded surgical therapy, CT-guided drainage serves as the primary intervention for addressing psoas muscle abscesses and the presence of purulent fluid collections within vertebral body or intervertebral disc space. After the patients discharged from the hospital, they were assessed longitudinally at 1-month intervals at the first 3 months, and 3-month intervals up to 12 months. Another 24 month’s follow-up was conducted every 6 months. On each assessment, the following indexes were recorded: (1) visual analogue scale (VAS) score; (2) wounds healing and sinus formation; (3) Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP); (4) Plain film, CT and MRI. According to the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of pyogenic spondylodiscitis [6-7], the treatment failure or recurrent infection was often based upon persistent pain, fever, elevated markers of systemic inflammation (such as ESR and/or CRP), and radiographic findings(new abnormal signals or new bone destruction, abscess, and/or bone graft nonunion). A patient is considered “cured” if there is no recurrence within one year after discontinuation of antibiotics.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software was utilized for conducting statistical analysis (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables including age and laboratory parameters were expressed as the means and standard deviations (SDs) or medians with ranges, while categorical variables were expressed as frequency counts and percentages. The differences among the categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 373 patients with pyogenic spondylodiscitis were identified between January 2018 and January 2024. Twenty-three patients (6.2%; 16 males and 7 females), with a mean age of 63.7 years (63.7 ± 14.3 years), were diagnosed with anaerobic spondylodiscitis during this period. The median follow-up period was 12 months (range, 12–36 months). The clinical data of the patients are shown in Table 1. The mean erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 71.0 mm/h (71.0 ± 27.2 mm/h), and the median C-reactive protein (CRP) level was 31.18 mg/L (12.44–188.48 mg/L) at admission. Eleven (47.8%) patients had at least one immunocompromised disease, and the remaining 12 patients had no comorbidities. All patients had persistent back pain lasting over one month, 5 patients (21.7%) had fever, and 3 patients (13.0%) had sciatica. The lumbar spine was affected in 18 patients (78.3%), and the thoracic spine was affected in 5 (21.7%) patients. In addition to a single vertebral body being affected in one patient, 22 patients had single-level spinal (two adjacent vertebrae and the intervening disc) infection. One patient had a positive blood culture for the same pathogen identified in this patient’s pus culture. With respect to the route of infection, 18 patients (77.8%) developed hematogenous spread, and 5 patients (22.2%) were infected after spinal surgery. Four patients were infected after percutaneous vertebral augmentation, and 1 patient was infected after spinal instrumentation.

Imaging presentation

All patients completed X-ray, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations. Radiographically, intervertebral space narrowing was identified in 16 patients (69.6%). On Computed tomography (CT) images, erosion and destruction of adjacent endplates and vertebral bodies were identified in 22 patients (95.7%), and psoas or paravertebral abscesses were identified in 8 patients (34.8%). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the most sensitive tool for imaging anaerobic spondylodiscitis. The T1-weighted MRI images of all patients revealed a reduction in the signal intensity of the vertebral body, endplate and intervertebral disc, and the T2-weighted images revealed increased signal intensity of the vertebral body and/or disc. Epidural abscess was identified in 9 patients (39.1%) (Figs. 1 and 2).

Microbiological findings

Microbiological analysis results are detailed in Table 2. Cultures of specimens obtained by percutaneous fluoroscopically guided biopsy or surgery were positive for pathogens for 10 (43.5%) of 23 patients, and NGS was positive for 19 (95%) of 20 patients. The positive rate of NGS is higher than that of cultures (P<0.01). The samples of 3 patients (cases 1, 3, and 5) were only subjected to bacterial culture and not to next-generation sequencing, as our hospital had not yet conducted next-generation sequencing routinely at that time. In addition, anaerobic isolates at the species level of these 3 specimens could not be identified by 16 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequencing, which is considered the gold standard [8]. Among the other 7 samples with positive bacterial culture results, 6 samples had the same identification results by next-generation sequencing with the culture method. Among the 13 patients with negative bacterial culture results, NGS yielded positive results in 13 cases. The most common gram-positive coccus is Parvimonas micra, and the most common gram-negative bacilli is Bacteroid fragilis.

Outcome analysis

In addition to antibiotic therapy, 3 patients received CT-guided drainage, and 13 patients underwent surgical therapy (Table 3). The average visual analog scale (VAS) score was 7.5 (7.5 ± 0.79) at admission and 2.3 (2.3 ± 0.82) at the last follow-up (p < 0.01). At the time of admission, 5 patients had Grade D ASIA classification, and the rest had Grade E ASIA classification. At the last follow-up, all patients had Grade E ASIA classification. One patient in the conservative treatment group relapsed, while no patient in the surgical treatment group relapsed (Table 3). There was no significant difference in the cure rate between the two groups (P > 0.05). One patient (number 1) treated with a 6-week course of antibiotics relapsed 1 month after treatment withdrawal. Recurrence was identified through MRI with hypointensity on T1-weighted images, hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, and paravertebral abscess. This patient was cured with antibiotics for another 3 months.

Discussion

Anaerobes are the predominant components of normal human skin and mucous membrane bacterial flora. These inhabited regions encompass the oral cavity, nasal passages, pharynx, lower gastrointestinal tract, vagina, and outer portion of the urethra vaginal canal. Under normal physiological conditions, these organisms maintain a noninvasive symbiotic relationship with their host, and there are considerable evidences that they perform essential protective functions and contribute to maintaining skin and mucous membrane barrier integrity [9]. However, when host defenses are impaired or when breaches occur in the skin or mucous membranes, anaerobes are capable of causing serious invasive infections as the primary pathogen and even causing death.

This study is a relatively large case series to discuss the clinical presentation of anaerobic spondylodiscitis identified by bacterial culture and next-generation sequencing at a tertiary center in China. The medical records were analyzed retrospectively from January 2018 to January 2024, and 23 (6.2%) cases of anaerobic spondylodiscitis were identified among the 373 patients with pyogenic spondylodiscitis. The constituent ratio was greater than that reported in previous studies, which was less than 3% [2, 10, 11]. The possible reason is that we included patients confirmed by NGS. Anaerobes have difficulty growing on solid agar plates, which require longer incubation times and careful regulation of the anaerobic environment to achieve adequate growth [12]. Molecular techniques such as next-generation sequencing have allowed for rapid detection of many rare anaerobic species, which has improved diagnostic efficiency [13, 14]. We found that anaerobic bacteria were isolated from only 43.5% (10/23) of patients via bacterial culture. It is difficult to recover anaerobic bacteria, which require more stringent growth conditions in conventional microbial cultures, leading to the missed diagnosis of anaerobic spondylodiscitis. In this study, we believe that next-generation sequencing could detect anaerobic bacteria with better sensitivity than traditional culture media.

Pyogenic spondylodiscitis exhibits a higher prevalence among males, with a male/female ratio of 1.4-3:1 [5, 15–17]. The results we obtained confirm this trend, with a male/female ratio of 2.3:1. Notably, contrasting findings emerged from Chen CT’s research, which reported female predominance (77.8%) in anaerobic spondylodiscitis cases [10]. This may be the result of selection bias due to the small sample size. We found that, like aerobic spondylodiscitis, anaerobic spondylodiscitis affects predominantly people older than 50 years. The average age of the patients was 63.7 years, with 20 (87.0%) patients over 50 years. In the present study, all patients presented with back pain, and the average VAS score was 7.5 at admission. The pain may manifest as acute or chronic, progressing and worsening over several weeks to even months. Compared with back pain, fever was much less common, occurring in 21.7% (5/23) of cases. We found that 5 (21.7%) patients had neurological deficits. Lower limb weakness, dysesthesia and urinary retention developed in 1 patient, and sciatica developed in 4 patients. The incidence of neurological deficits is similar to that of aerobic spondylodiscitis [5, 18]. In addition, this study found that the lumbar spine is the most frequently affected site (78.3%), followed by the thoracic spine (21.7%), which aligns with findings reported in other studies of anaerobic [2] or aerobic spinal infections [19].

The clinically important anaerobic infections are classified as gram-negative rods (Bacteroides, Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, Bilophila and Sutterella spp.); gram-positive cocci (mainly Peptostreptococcus spp.); gram-positive spore-forming cocci (Clostridium spp.) and no spore-forming bacilli (Actinomyces, Propionibacterium, Eubacterium, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium spp.); and gram-negative cocci (mainly Veillonella spp.) [20]. Bacteroid fragilis is the most common cause of causative bacteria in cases of vertebral osteomyelitis and spondylodiscitis [2, 10, 21, 22]. Other organisms implicated in anaerobic spondylodiscitis include Peptococcus and Peptostreptococcus species, Propionibacterium acnes and Fusobacterium species [23]. Similarly, the most common pathogen in this study was Parvimonas micra, known as Peptostreptococcus micros and Micromonas micros, before its final reclassification in 2006 [24], followed by Bacteroid fragilis and Fusobacterium. The pathogenesis of infections with different anaerobes appears to differ. Spontaneous anaerobic spondylodiscitis is typically associated with hematogenous spread in the oral cavity and the gastrointestinal and female genital tract flora due to bacterial (such as Bacteroid fragilis, Prevotella, Fusobacterium, and Peptostreptococcus) translocation [9]. However, Uçkay reported a strong causal correlation between Propionibacterium acnes infection and previous invasive procedures in 97% of patients [25].

Imaging technique plays a crucial role in diagnosing of anaerobic spondylodiscitis and distinguishing it from nonspecific pyogenic spondylodiscitis. The typical radiographic signs are loss of disc space height, end-plate erosion, bony destruction, reactive sclerosis, and paravertebral soft tissue mass. The atypical characteristics on plain films include single vertebral destruction with preservation of the disc height, mimicking vertebral compression fracture, which occurs in patient number 1. CT is very useful for evaluating the extent of bony changes, such as end-plate erosion, sequestra, bone defects, reactive sclerosis, and reactive ossification. The resolution of bone and soft tissue on CT is better than that on radiography. CT has improved the assessment of affected soft tissue paravertebrally but has difficulty detecting epidural abscesses. There were no patients with intraosseous gas in the medullary cavity of the affected bone on CT imaging, which is the most common feature of Fusobacterium necrophorum [26, 27]. The classic MRI findings are uniform, T2-weighted images hyperintense signal and T1-weighted hypointense signal alterations of the disc and adjacent vertebral bodies. It provides detailed structural insights: the disc, neural structures, epidural space, and surrounding soft tissue are clearly depicted. Therefore, epidural abscess and paravertebral abscess can be observed via MRI [28]. In our study, epidural abscess was identified in 9 patients (39.1%).

Research evidence for the treatment of anaerobic spondylodiscitis is rare. In Brook’s opinion, the treatment of arthritis and osteomyelitis involving anaerobic bacteria includes symptomatic therapy, immobilization in some cases, adequate drainage of purulent material, and antibiotic therapy effective against these organisms [4]. Effective antibiotic therapy is essential for controlling infection and favorable outcomes, and the choice of antibiotic should be based on the results of culture and in vitro susceptibility testing. However, many hospitals in China including our hospital currently lack the capability to conduct specialized antimicrobial sensitivity testing (AST) for anaerobic pathogens. Similarly, a 2008 clinical survey by Goldstein’s research team revealed that less than 50% of American hospitals performed anaerobic AST, and only 21% of these facilities did so in-house, while the remainder sent the isolates out to yet another reference lab for testing [29]. Owing to the lack of anaerobic AST, our empiric therapeutic decisions relied upon antimicrobial resistance patterns reported in the medical literature [4, 20, 30]. Empiric selection of antimicrobials for anaerobic infections is made even more challenging by the increased rates of resistance reported in the literature, leading to recommendations to increase susceptibility testing to guide therapy [31]. The optimal duration of antibiotic therapy is not well defined. We usually treat patients with intravenous antibiotics until the CRP level is normal, which takes approximately two to four weeks, and then the antibiotics are changed to oral antibiotics for a total of 12 weeks.

Our study had several limitations. Although it included a greater number of patients with anaerobic spondylodiscitis than other studies did, the number of patients who were divided into surgical and nonsurgical groups was still small. We could not perform a multivariate regression analysis for the outcome. Furthermore, this was a retrospective study, in which there may have been unrecognized confounding factors associated with outcomes.

Conclusion

The incidence of anaerobic spondylodiscitis might be underestimated, as it is difficult to recover anaerobic bacteria, which require more stringent growth conditions in conventional microbial cultures. We found that next-generation sequencing could detect anaerobic bacteria with better sensitivity than traditional culture media. Patients often present with back pain and a lack of fever. Anaerobic spondylodiscitis generally exhibits a favorable prognosis, considering that appropriate antibiotic therapy is administered and suitable surgical treatment is performed if necessary.
