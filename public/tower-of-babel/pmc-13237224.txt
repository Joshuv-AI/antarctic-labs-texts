HS-RankFormer for efficient and robust RGB-to-Hyperspectral image reconstruction across domains
Authors: Bandi Vamsi, Mohan Mahanty, B Omkar Lakshmi Jagan, Hari Mohan Rai, Saurabh Agarwal, Wooguil Pak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperspectral image reconstruction from RGB inputs is a compelling yet complex task due to high spectral dimensionality, scarcity of labeled data, and limitations of traditional models in capturing both spectral and spatial dependencies. Existing approaches often exhibit poor domain generalization, high computational cost, and degraded perceptual quality, particularly when transferring across scenes with different spectral distributions. HS-RankFormer is introduced as a lightweight transformer-based framework for robust RGB-to-HSI reconstruction. The framework incorporates three key components: a rank-aware transformer generator that encourages low-rank spectral structure while learning global spectral–spatial correlations; an edge-guided fusion strategy that augments RGB inputs with Sobel-derived edge maps to enhance structural awareness; and a self-supervised masked pretraining scheme that enables effective representation learning without paired annotations. Together, these components improve spectral accuracy, spatial consistency, and training efficiency. Performance is evaluated on four benchmark datasets: ARAD 1 K, BGU-HS, CAVE, and KAUST-Skin. Comparative experiments against representative state-of-the-art methods, including MST++, AWAN, Restormer, and pix2pix, show competitive or superior performance across MRAE, RMSE, PSNR, SSIM, SAM, and ΔE2000 metrics. Qualitative results further illustrate improved perceptual fidelity and strong cross-domain robustness. By combining transformer-based modeling with spectral regularization and edge-enhanced learning, HS-RankFormer offers a robust and generalizable approach for RGB-to-HSI reconstruction, with consistent performance across remote sensing, biomedical imaging, and conservation monitoring datasets.Keywords: Hyperspectral imaging, Self-supervised learning, Rank-based attention, Edge fusion, Cross-domain generalizationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) has become an essential tool across a broad spectrum of applications, ranging from precision agriculture and mineral exploration to remote sensing, medical diagnostics, and environmental monitoring1. Unlike conventional RGB imaging, which captures visual information using three broad color channels, HSI collects data in hundreds or dozens of narrow spectral bands2. The high spectral resolution facilitates detailed analysis of material properties and subtle spectral signatures, making it invaluable for tasks such as classification, anomaly detection, and surface analysis3. Despite its benefits, the widespread deployment of HSI systems is constrained by practical challenges4. High-resolution hyperspectral cameras are typically expensive, bulky, and limited by their acquisition speed5. These limitations hinder their integration into compact or mobile imaging platforms, such as drones or smartphones. Consequently, evolving research has focused on the restoration of hyperspectral images through standard RGB images, aiming to simulate HSI data using cost-effective and widely accessible hardware6.

Early efforts in hyperspectral reconstruction were primarily driven by traditional machine learning and convolutional neural networks (CNNs). These models learn to map RGB inputs to their hyperspectral counterparts by exploiting local spatial features7. While CNNs have demonstrated decent performance, they inherently focus on local context due to their fixed receptive fields and often struggle to capture long-range spectral dependencies, which is an essential requirement for accurate hyperspectral synthesis8. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) introduced an adversarial training paradigm that enhanced reconstruction quality and realism, but also brought notorious issues such as mode collapse, gradient instability, and high computational complexity9. More recently, transformer-based architectures have shown promise in vision tasks by leveraging global self-attention mechanisms10. However, these models tend to be resource-intensive and often fail to generalize effectively when confronted with domain shifts that involve variations in lighting, texture, or color statistics between the training and test data11.

A key challenge that remains underexplored in current literature is domain robustness; the ability of a model trained on one dataset to perform consistently across others with different visual and spectral characteristics. Furthermore, most existing models rely exclusively on RGB input, missing an opportunity to incorporate auxiliary structural cues that could enhance spatial awareness during reconstruction. To address the challenges above, we propose HS-RankFormer, a novel hyperspectral image reconstruction framework designed to be lightweight, generalizable, and robust across domains. The proposed model introduces a rank-aware transformer generator that efficiently captures spectral-spatial correlations while reducing computational burden. To further enhance spatial feature learning, we fuse RGB images with gradient-based edge maps, providing the model with additional structural guidance for reconstruction. In this work, “lightweight” is used in a comparative sense: HS-RankFormer is designed to be lightweight among transformer-based RGB-to-HSI reconstruction models, reducing parameter count, FLOPs, and latency relative to recent transformer baselines while maintaining strong accuracy and cross-domain robustness. We also report CNN baselines (e.g., HSCNN+), which may have fewer parameters but typically underperform in spectral fidelity and generalization.

Unlike previous works that rely solely on supervised learning, HS-RankFormer incorporates a self-supervised masked pretraining strategy. This enables the model to learn generalizable features from unlabeled data before fine-tuning under an adversarial setting. Additionally, we introduce a rank-preservation loss that regularizes the reconstruction toward spectral hierarchy consistency across reconstructed bands, thereby helping maintain intrinsic spectral relationships.

The “low-rank” refers to an explicit spectral subspace constraint implemented through a learnable projection-attention-expansion operator (Sect. “Rank-aware spectral attention”), which yields a provable rank upper bound on the reconstructed spectral representation.

Our method is evaluated on four hyperspectral datasets, including ARAD 1 K, BGU-HS, CAVE, and KAUST-Skin, which exhibit diverse scene content, brightness levels, and color distributions. Experimental results show that HS-RankFormer achieves competitive or superior performance compared to representative state-of-the-art methods in terms of accuracy and generalization capability.

Although many RGB to HSI reconstruction models rely on pixel losses, these losses often produce overly smooth spectra, since they mainly penalize per-pixel intensity differences. Fine variations along the spectral dimension, particularly near material boundaries, are often insufficiently captured by existing approaches. To address this, we include a light adversarial module. The goal is not to introduce strong GAN-based supervision, but to act as a soft regularizer that encourages sharper spatial structure and better alignment between the generated spectral curves and the local texture patterns in the RGB input. The discriminator is guided by cross-attention features, which help it focus on spectral consistency rather than arbitrary texture differences. This design maintains stable training while providing complementary supervision that enhances the spectral quality of the reconstructed hyperspectral cube.

Prior RGB-to-HSI methods typically optimize pixel-wise spectral errors and rely on fully supervised training, which can limit cross-domain robustness when scene statistics shift (illumination, texture, material composition). Transformer-based models improve global modeling but often incur higher complexity and still exhibit sensitivity to domain changes. HS-RankFormer addresses these gaps by introducing (i) rank-aware spectral attention to bias reconstruction toward low-dimensional spectral structure, (ii) edge-guided input fusion to provide explicit structural cues that RGB alone may under-represent, and (iii) masked self-supervised pretraining to learn transferable representations before adversarial fine-tuning. This combination targets cross-domain generalization and efficiency simultaneously.

Related work

Research on hyperspectral image reconstruction from RGB inputs has grown rapidly because it offers a practical way to obtain high-resolution spectral information without the expense of expensive sensors. Early work explored CNN models that capture local spatial patterns but struggle to model long-range spectral relationships. GAN-based approaches improved structural realism but often introduced instability and weak spectral accuracy. More recently, transformer architectures have been studied for their ability to capture global spectral and spatial dependencies through self-attention. Although these models achieve high reconstruction quality, many are computationally intensive and do not generalize well across datasets. Only a few studies have examined how self-supervised learning can enhance cross-domain robustness in spectral reconstruction. In this section, we review research that is closely related to our work, focusing on RGB-to-HSI reconstruction, spectral super-resolution models, and transformer-based approaches that handle spectral-spatial interactions.

Hyperspectral image synthesis and reconstruction

This section explores recent advances in generating high-dimensional spectral data from limited or conventional inputs using deep learning architectures. Early RGB-to-HSI reconstruction studies were based on convolutional architectures such as HSCNN12, HSCNN+13, HRNet14, and AWAN15. These models learned local spatial features effectively but struggled to capture long-range spectral relationships due to limited receptive fields. To address these limitations, several recent works have proposed hybrid or multi-stage designs. HASIC-Net16 introduced a hybrid attentional convolutional framework that reinforced the structural information consistency across spectral bands. Progressive spatial information guided aggregation networks further improved spectral detail by refining feature representations stage by stage. Approaches based on frequency-domain modeling demonstrated that learning spectral features in the Fourier domain can help preserve high-frequency spectral variations that CNNs tend to lose17. Self-supervised and unsupervised fusion frameworks have also contributed to spectral reconstruction. A notable example is the modality decoupling approach, which learned a unified representation for hyperspectral and multispectral images without paired supervision. Modality decoupling has been explored to disentangle shared and modality-specific representations from hyperspectral and multispectral data under unsupervised settings. For example, the MossFuse framework explicitly decomposes modality-shared and modality-complementary features and integrates them into a compact representation to enhance fusion accuracy and reduce redundancy18. More recently, diffusion-based spectral super-resolution models, such as the spectral cascaded diffusion approach, have shown strong potential for generating smooth, realistic spectral signatures in remote sensing applications19. Other recent studies incorporated spectral priors and lightweight architectures. Sun et al. proposed a transfer learning based spectral domain enhancement model that exploited complementary channel priors for hyperspectral super resolution20. Li et al. introduced an unsupervised hyperspectral noise estimation and reconstruction framework based on interband-invariant learning21. Recent studies have also explored implicit neural representation (INR) models for spectral fusion. One example is the Mamba-aware spatial spectral cross-modal network introduced by Zhu et al.22, which learns continuous spectral-spatial mappings for remote sensing classification. While this approach improves cross-modal representation learning, it requires both hyperspectral and multispectral inputs and therefore does not apply to single-RGB reconstruction tasks. Moreover, the implicit neural field formulation increases computational overhead due to the need for continuous function optimization. In contrast, HS-RankFormer reconstructs hyperspectral information directly from RGB inputs using lightweight transformer blocks and rank preservation mechanisms, making it more efficient and broadly applicable.

These developments highlight the need for methods that combine spectral spatial attention, domain adaptability, and efficient learning. HS-RankFormer addresses these goals through rank-aware transformer blocks, edge-guided input fusion, and self-supervised masked pretraining.

Transformers for spectral-spatial modeling

Transformers have gained attention for hyperspectral modeling due to their ability to capture long-range spectral and spatial dependencies. Tensor Transformer models demonstrated that self-attention can effectively represent high-dimensional spectral structures2. Spectral spatial Mamba-based models also explored selective state space designs for compact spectral modeling. Graph-based transformer frameworks combined spectral information with spatial graph topology to enhance feature learning for hyperspectral data23. Hybrid and multiscale transformer approaches have also been proposed. Cross multiscale transformer fusion networks integrated convolutional blocks with transformer modules to learn both global and local dependencies for hyperspectral and multispectral fusion24. Implicit transformer-based fusion methods used continuous representations to merge spectral information across resolutions25. Efficient transformer designs, such as PCET26, introduced patch quality assessment and additive attention to reduce computational cost while preserving spectral spatial structure. PatchOut combined CNN and transformer modules for large-scale hyperspectral datasets27. SAM-driven transformer models, such as SAM CTMapper28, demonstrated the effectiveness of combining adaptive convolution and self-attention for robust spectral spatial learning. These transformer-based advances provide strong baselines but often depend on dual-input fusion or large model complexity. HS-RankFormer reduces these limitations by offering lightweight transformer blocks with rank-aware modeling and single-RGB input synthesis.

Another relevant direction is the use of dual-domain implicit guidance models for hyperspectral and multispectral fusion. Zhu et al.29 proposed a spatial-frequency implicit fusion framework based on the Kolmogorov–Arnold Network, which combines spatial-domain cues with frequency-domain representations. While this dual-domain implicit learning improves cross-scale consistency, it still relies on multi-sensor fusion (HSI + MSI) and does not support RGB-only reconstruction. The KAN formulation also introduces a higher computational cost due to frequency-space mapping. HS-RankFormer differs by providing a single-RGB reconstruction pipeline supported by rank-aware attention and self-supervised pretraining, offering a more lightweight and generalizable alternative for hyperspectral synthesis.

Self-supervised learning and rank-based architectures

Self-supervised learning has become increasingly important for hyperspectral modeling due to the scarcity of labeled data. Several recent studies proposed spectral self-supervised frameworks using masking, reconstruction, or contrastive learning. A unified self-supervised framework for hyperspectral classification28. Spectrum Transformer-based self-supervised learning showed that masked spectral modeling can capture wavelength dependencies without labels. Progressive learning approaches improved generalization by gradually increasing task difficulty during training. Double structure transformer-based self-supervised clustering methods further demonstrated that structural masking improves spectral representation learning. Spatial spectral contrastive learning was also used to enhance target detection30. Unsupervised spectral reconstruction approaches have also explored feature disentanglement. UNER31 learned interband invariant representations to restore noisy hyperspectral bands using an unsupervised reconstruction strategy. While these methods improve spectral representation learning, most focus on classification or denoising. HS-RankFormer extends self-supervised learning to hyperspectral synthesis by combining masked pretraining with rank-aware transformers.

The hyperspectral image reconstruction HSCNN12, and its improved version HSCNN+13 focus on local feature learning through convolutional neural networks (CNNs)7. However, these approaches often struggle to capture long-range spectral dependencies. Similarly, HRNet, which leverages high-resolution representations for image reconstruction, has been shown to be effective in certain applications but lacks domain generalization when applied to heterogeneous datasets. AWAN addresses the challenges of spectral reconstruction by incorporating weighted attention mechanisms, which improve spectral consistency but remain computationally expensive. Moreover, HASIC-Net has demonstrated the benefits of hybrid attentional mechanisms for hyperspectral image synthesis. While these methods have achieved impressive results, they still suffer from limitations in cross-domain robustness and efficiency.

Degradation-learning-based methods

Another important approach in the field of spectral super-resolution is degradation-learning-based methods, which learn to map degraded or low-quality spectral data to high-quality spectral information. One notable method is the Degradation-Aware Unfolding Network32,33, which handles spectral reconstruction by modeling the degradation process, thereby improving the accuracy of reconstructed hyperspectral data. These methods, though effective in certain scenarios, do not always handle domain shifts as robustly as our approach. HS-RankFormer, with its rank-aware transformer and self-supervised learning strategy, addresses these challenges by focusing on both spectral fidelity and domain generalization.

Recent degradation-learning-based approaches explicitly model degradation processes to improve the fidelity of spectral reconstruction. The ‘Degradation-Aware Unfolding Network’ for Spectral Super-Resolution introduces a deep unfolding framework that learns to invert spectral degradations by incorporating degradation modeling into the reconstruction pipeline34. Similarly, ‘Unsupervised Hyperspectral and Multispectral Image Fusion’ via Self-Supervised Modality Decoupling proposes a modality decoupling strategy that disentangles degradation effects between hyperspectral and multispectral data in a self-supervised setting35. While effective in controlled degradation scenarios or multi-sensor fusion tasks, these methods typically assume known degradation models or require auxiliary inputs. In contrast, HS-RankFormer does not explicitly model degradation; instead, it relies on masked self-supervised pretraining and rank-aware spectral attention mechanisms to learn stable and transferable spectral-spatial representations across diverse and unseen domains.

Methodology

This section presents the detailed architecture and workflow of the proposed HS-RankFormer framework, designed for lightweight and generalizable hyperspectral image synthesis from RGB inputs. HS-RankFormer integrates three core components: a rank-aware transformer-based generator, a cross-attention discriminator, and a self-supervised pretraining strategy. The framework leverages edge-enhanced RGB inputs and spectral-spatial modeling to address the challenges of domain shift, high-dimensional feature learning, and limited label availability. The following subsections describe the datasets used, the preprocessing pipeline, the overall architecture, and the input fusion method.

ARAD 1 K dataset

The ARAD 1 K dataset serves as the primary source for training and validation. It contains approximately 1,000 hyperspectral images captured under diverse inside and outside lighting conditions. Every image has 31 evenly spaced spectral bands in the 400–700 nm visible spectrum, recorded at 12-bit resolution. Synthetic RGB images are derived from hyperspectral cubes using the CIE 1964 10° Color Matching Functions. The dataset’s diversity in object types, textures, and lighting makes it a strong benchmark for training RGB-to-HSI synthesis models.

CAVE dataset

This CAVE dataset comprises 32 hyperspectral images captured under controlled indoor lighting conditions. Each image contains 31 bands in the 400–700 nm range and is stored at 16 bits per pixel, providing high radiometric precision. RGB images are synthesized from hyperspectral cubes using spectral integration methods. Due to its relatively simple scene structure and controlled illumination, CAVE is used as a cross-domain test set to assess a model’s performance under domain shift from training data, such as ARAD.

BGU-HS dataset

The BGU-HS dataset includes approximately 200 outdoor hyperspectral images, captured under varying real-world conditions. With around 31 spectral bands and 12-bit resolution, this dataset introduces challenges such as inconsistent lighting, shadowing, and natural scene complexity. RGB counterparts are not provided directly and are estimated from the hyperspectral data using standard colorimetric transformations. BGU-HS serves as an outdoor generalization test set, enabling evaluation of the model’s performance under unconstrained environmental conditions.

KAUST-skin dataset

The KAUST-Skin dataset is a hyperspectral medical imaging dataset used for analyzing skin tissue, including dermatological diagnostics and lesion detection. It contains approximately 500 images (varying by study), covering 65 to 80 spectral bands across a broad range of 400–1000 nm, which includes both visible and near-infrared wavelengths. The data is stored in 10- to 16-bit depth, depending on the imaging sensor. RGB images are not typically provided. This dataset serves as a medical cross-domain benchmark, allowing us to test HS-RankFormer’s generalization to anatomically distinct and spectrally rich biomedical inputs.

ARAD 1 K and CAVE are publicly available and are used following their standard RGB synthesis protocols. For datasets where RGB is not directly provided, RGB is generated from the hyperspectral cube via a consistent colorimetric integration pipeline described in Sect. “Data preprocessing”. For all datasets, we evaluate on aligned spatial crops/patches and report the preprocessing choices explicitly to support reproducibility.

Overview of HS-RankFormer framework

The model consists of three integrated components: a rank-aware transformer-based generator, a cross-attention discriminator, and a self-supervised pre-training strategy. Together, these components enable spectral reconstruction from RGB inputs while capturing both spatial gradients and spectral correlations, and supporting cross-domain generalization. The model’s input is a normalized RGB image patch, optionally fused with its corresponding edge map extracted by a Sobel operator. This fused input is passed through the generator network, which learns to reconstruct the corresponding hyperspectral image. The output is evaluated by a cross-attention-based discriminator that compares spectral fidelity and spatial configuration between the ground truth and the predicted HSI. During pretraining, this model undergoes masked self-supervised learning to enable robust feature learning even in the absence of labels.

Input fusion

In hyperspectral image reconstruction from RGB inputs, preserving spatial features such as object boundaries and textures is critical. However, RGB data alone often cannot emphasize fine structural details, especially when objects share similar color spectra. To address this, we enhance the RGB input by fusing it with a corresponding ‘edge map’ derived from spatial gradients. This fusion not only guides the generator in learning high-frequency details but also helps disambiguate structures in complex scenes or low-contrast regions. The edge maps are computed using the Sobel operator, a classical method for detecting edges by calculating the first-order derivatives of image intensity. Input Fusion Architecture.

Although more sophisticated structural priors, such as learned gradient prediction networks or multi-scale Laplacian pyramids, can offer richer spatial representations, they often increase architectural complexity and may overfit to dataset-specific textures or imaging conditions. In this work, we adopt Sobel-based edge extraction as a deterministic and parameter-free structural prior. This choice reflects a deliberate trade-off that prioritizes computational efficiency and cross-domain robustness while still providing effective boundary and texture guidance for hyperspectral reconstruction.

Cross-attention discriminator

In the HS-RankFormer framework, the discriminator plays a pivotal role in enforcing spectral and structural realism in the reconstructed hyperspectral images. Unlike traditional discriminators that focus only on pixel-wise differences, the proposed ‘cross-attention discriminator’ performs ‘joint spectral-RGB validation’ using a ‘multi-branch attention mechanism’. This design enables the discriminator to assess the authenticity of both spectral continuity and spatial alignment in the generated hyperspectral cube.

The discriminator receives two inputs: the reconstructed hyperspectral image from the generator and the corresponding real HSI . To assess their consistency, both tensors are projected into a shared latent space using 3D convolutional encoders, followed by positional encoding to preserve spectral ordering. To enhance representational power, the discriminator employs a ‘multi-branch architecture’ in which each branch specializes in learning attention across a different dimension. Each branch outputs an attention-weighted feature map that is concatenated and sent to a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and a final sigmoid layer for real/fake classification. This discriminator setup ensures that the generator receives highly informative feedback not only on the spectral fidelity of the output but also on the structural alignment and inter-channel consistency. By validating both global coherence and local accuracy, the cross-attention discriminator improves the perceptual quality of the generated hyperspectral images.

The adversarial module is used in a controlled manner to provide additional structure-aware supervision. In practice, models trained only with pixel-based losses tend to over-smooth the spectral dimension and lose subtle high-frequency variations. To reduce this issue, we introduce a discriminator that receives cross-attention features from the generator. The discriminator learns to check whether the spectral profile around edges matches the spatial structure of the RGB input. Since the discriminator operates on fused attention maps rather than raw images, it provides a stable and focused signal. The adversarial loss is used with a small weight, so the generator continues to be dominated by reconstruction and rank-preservation losses. The adversarial part, therefore, works only as a soft constraint that encourages sharper spatial boundaries and more realistic spectral transitions.

Self-supervised pretraining strategy

HS-RankFormer incorporates a self-supervised pretraining stage to reduce dependence on large labeled hyperspectral datasets and to improve generalization across multiple domains. This stage trains the model to reconstruct missing spectral information from partially visible RGB inputs, enabling the network to learn meaningful spectral–spatial dependencies before supervised fine-tuning. For this stage, we combine RGB images from ARAD 1 K, CAVE, and BGU-HS. Only the RGB inputs are used, so the model does not rely on ground-truth hyperspectral supervision. This provides a diverse set of scenes, strengthening cross-dataset robustness. A fixed fraction of RGB patches is randomly masked prior to encoding. The masked input is defined as , where is a binary mask with values of 0 in hidden regions and 1 in visible regions. We use a masking ratio of 35–45% so that the model cannot rely on simple interpolation and must infer structural information from context.

The aim is to reconstruct the missing spectral features corresponding to the masked RGB patches. During pretraining, we use a coarse pseudo-HSI representation as the target. This pseudo-HSI is generated using a shallow RGB-to-spectrum mapping that provides approximate wavelength structure. Although not perfectly accurate, it gives enough spectral guidance for the model to learn the relationships between visible and hidden patches. The reconstruction loss is computed only over the masked positions and is given by Eq. 7.

This masked MSE encourages the encoder–decoder to reason about spectral structure from incomplete information and improves feature consistency across wavelengths. The pretrained weights serve as initialization for the supervised reconstruction stage. This reduces overfitting, improves stability, and accelerates optimization. As reported in Sect. “Ablation studies”, including the pretraining stage reduces MRAE by 6–9% on ARAD 1 K and CAVE, demonstrating its effectiveness.

Results and discussion

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed HS-RankFormer framework across multiple hyperspectral reconstruction benchmarks. We outline the training environment and implementation details, followed by rigorous quantitative and qualitative analyses. The effectiveness of HS-RankFormer is assessed on datasets from diverse domains, including ARAD 1 K37, BGU-HS38, CAVE39, and KAUST-Skin40. We benchmark our results against state-of-the-art baselines such as MST++41, AWAN15, Restormer42, and pix2pix43. Finally, ablation studies are performed to examine the impact of every module, namely edge input fusion, self-supervised pretraining, and rank-aware loss formulation, demonstrating their impact on generalization and reconstruction fidelity. We also include a qualitative discussion of DRCR-Net36 as an additional recent baseline in Sect. “Baseline consideration: DRCR-Net”.

Ablation studies

To examine the contribution of each major component in HS-RankFormer, we performed a series of ablation studies on the ARAD 1 K and CAVE datasets. The evaluation metrics include MRAE, PSNR, RMSE, and the perceptual color difference (ΔE2000). Each experiment disables a single module while keeping all other components unchanged. We first removed the edge map input and trained the model using only the three-channel RGB image. This modification led to higher MRAE and ΔE2000 values. The performance drop was more noticeable in scenes containing fine textures and high-frequency boundaries. This analysis indicates that the fused RGB and edge input contributes to improved preservation of spatial structure and reduced perceptual color distortion. In the second experiment, we disabled the self-supervised pretraining stage and trained the model from scratch. This resulted in a clear decline in reconstruction quality across all metrics. The MRAE increased from 0.118 to 0.158, and PSNR dropped from 35.7 dB to 33.5 dB. This indicates that the masked patch reconstruction task learned during pretraining provides meaningful spectral guidance and improves model robustness under domain shift. These results also align with the earlier quantitative improvement, where pretraining improved performance by 6% to 9% across datasets. We also replaced the proposed rank-aware transformer block with a standard ViT-based transformer. This led to inferior spectral consistency and weaker long-range spectral modeling. The RMSE increased from 2.91 to 3.14, and the PSNR dropped by approximately 1.6 dB. Visual inspection of RGB back-projections further showed that the standard transformer produced noisier wavelength responses, highlighting the importance of the reduced-rank attention mechanism. The complete HS-RankFormer architecture, which integrates edge fusion, self-supervised pretraining, and rank-aware attention, achieves competitive or superior performance across the evaluated metrics and datasets. This analysis suggests that each component contributes meaningfully to overall spectral accuracy, spatial detail preservation, and generalization. Computational complexity and efficiency analysis

To justify the lightweight nature of HS-RankFormer, we report the parameter count, floating point operations (FLOPs), memory usage, and inference speed. All measurements were taken using an RGB input size of 256 × 256. We compare HS-RankFormer with widely used hyperspectral reconstruction models, including HSCNN+12,13, AWAN15, MST++41, and Uformer-SR44. HS-RankFormer has 3.42 million parameters, which is significantly smaller than MST++ (7.8 M) and Uformer-SR (20.1 M). The FLOPs are also reduced due to the low-rank spectral attention design. The model requires 29.6 GFLOPs, while MST + + needs 51.3 GFLOPs and Uformer-SR requires 92.4 GFLOPs. The average inference time of HS-RankFormer is 11.8 ms per image, which is faster than both AWAN (17.5 ms) and MST++ (23.1 ms). These results demonstrate that HS-RankFormer achieves a better balance between reconstruction accuracy and computational efficiency. Baseline consideration: DRCR-Net

Recent spectral super-resolution studies have introduced DRCR-Net36, a dense residual channel recalibration network with non-local purification. This architecture strengthens spectral learning through feature recalibration and long-range filtering, and it has demonstrated high performance in RGB-to-HSI enhancement tasks. DRCR-Net serves as a strong, modern baseline relevant to our work. Due to the lack of standard RGB-to-HSI training weights and complete implementation details for cross-dataset evaluation, we were unable to produce quantitative results for this revision. Instead, we provide a qualitative comparison of its design with HS-RankFormer. DRCR-Net focuses on deep residual channel expansion and non-local refinement, while HS-RankFormer uses rank-aware attention and self-supervised masked pretraining to improve generalization and reduce computational cost. In future work, we plan to include a full quantitative evaluation of DRCR-Net and other advanced spectral super-resolution models once unified RGB-to-HSI training pipelines are available. Conclusion and future scope

In this work, we implemented HS-RankFormer, a lightweight, generalizable transformer framework for hyperspectral image reconstruction from RGB inputs. The proposed architecture addresses key limitations of prior methods by integrating three synergistic innovations: a rank-aware transformer generator to encourage preservation of intrinsic spectral structure, edge-guided input fusion for enhanced spatial awareness, and a self-supervised pretraining strategy for robust representation learning in low-label settings. Extensive evaluations across four diverse datasets: ARAD 1 K, BGU-HS, CAVE and KAUST-Skin, show that HS-RankFormer achieves competitive or superior performance compared to representative baselines such as MST++, AWAN, Restormer, and pix2pix across key metrics, including MRAE, RMSE, PSNR, SSIM, SAM, and ΔE2000. Qualitative visualizations further demonstrate the framework’s ability to maintain spectral fidelity, structural detail, and perceptual color consistency across natural and medical domains. Despite its high accuracy, the framework maintains a compact design and efficient training schedule, indicating its potential suitability for deployment in resource-constrained environments. Building on the current framework, several directions can be explored to enhance its utility and scalability. Future extensions may incorporate additional sensing modalities, such as depth data or near-infrared channels, to further improve spectral reconstruction in complex environments. To address data privacy and label scarcity, we aim to investigate semi-supervised, self-training, and federated learning adaptations. These approaches would allow training across distributed datasets while preserving data confidentiality and reducing the need for dense annotations.
