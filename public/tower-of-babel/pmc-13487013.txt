EEG signatures of psychotropic medications: opportunities and challenges for translational psychiatry
Authors: Louisa Krile, Khadijeh Raeisi, Andrea B. Protzner
Published: 2026

Over the past few decades, neuroimaging has been increasingly investigated as a tool for identifying individualised markers to support the diagnosis and treatment of mental health disorders. Studies of brain function using electroencephalography (EEG) have provided important insights into the pathophysiology and effects of treatment in various psychiatric disorders. However, emerging evidence suggests that disorder-specific neural features may not be contained within discrete diagnostic boundaries; rather, psychiatric disorders may be better characterised by transdiagnostic neurobiological phenotypes.1 For example, EEG research has identified spectral trends that are common across groups of psychiatric disorders2 as well as functional connectivity patterns that differentiate subtypes of treatment-resistance within a single diagnostic group.3 These findings highlight the substantial heterogeneity and comorbidity that are not fully captured by categorical diagnostic systems, supporting a shift toward dimensional4 and transdiagnostic5 models and suggesting that shared factors may contribute to EEG patterns across diagnoses.

Importantly, this clinical and neurophysiological overlap is also reflected in current pharmacological treatment approaches. Given the lack of well-defined neural boundaries between disorders, it is unsurprising that different psychotropic medications can be used to treat the same disorder, while the same medication may be prescribed across multiple disorders. Psychotropic medications are commonly used to treat a wide range of conditions, including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, and schizophrenia, and are categorised under several broad classes that exert their effects on distinct neurochemical pathways, including antidepressants (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs]), anxiolytics (e.g., benzodiazepines), and antipsychotics. EEG research has revealed spectral features associated with different medications. For example, several classes of antidepressants have been shown to reduce alpha power6 and antipsychotics have been shown to increase delta and theta power.7 Although studies of individual medications give valuable insight into their neural effects, the literature is still mixed and electrophysiological signatures that distinguish or converge across psychotropic classes remain poorly understood. Given the substantial overlap between disorders and their treatments, large-scale investigations with clinically heterogeneous populations are needed to better characterise these signatures.

In the August issue of eBioMedicine, Szponar and colleagues8 examined the neural correlates of psychotropic medications in a very large (>20,000) sample of patients and drug-naïve individuals. The patients were diagnostically diverse and receiving treatment across medication classes. Szponar et al. calculated various metrics of spectral power, functional connectivity, and non-linear measures, and used a one-vs-rest comparison scheme and principal component analysis to identify EEG signatures associated with the different medications ranked by effect size. Six medications showed significantly distinct neural profiles after controlling for age, sex, and diagnosis, including benzodiazepines, SSRIs, noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressants (NaSSAs), atypical and typical antipsychotics, and anticonvulsants that block sodium channels. Their results replicate and extend current knowledge of electrophysiological medication effects, with the components exhibiting the largest effect sizes showing that SSRIs were associated with increased gamma coherence, benzodiazepines with decreased theta-alpha and increased beta power, and anticonvulsants and antipsychotics with altered signal complexity and increased slow-wave power. Although the study was not designed to provide mechanistic explanations for drug effects nor can it currently inform clinical practice, it has several significant attributes that should be noted. First, the scale of the EEG sample, the inclusion of major psychotropic drug classes, and the simultaneous examination of multiple EEG characteristics allows for a comprehensive description of both shared and unique drug effects on resting-state EEG. Second, to aid in reproducibility and hypothesis generation, the authors made their full set of results freely available in an interactive database with a visualisation tool. The study therefore represents an important progression in the literature by providing population-level information, which is not possible with smaller-scale investigations.

As with all studies, there are limitations that are especially relevant for future work linking basic science findings into clinical practice. The observed effect sizes hover in the small to moderate range, which limits their clinical significance. Further, given the constraints of a naturalistic design, the authors were unable to explore specific treatment mechanisms or assess the impact of clinically relevant factors that could provide a more comprehensive understanding of treatment effects, including the severity, stage, and symptom profiles of the patients’ disorders. Perhaps most importantly, the absence of outcome data limits clinical interpretation of the identified neural profiles, as it remains unknown how these neural changes associated with medication use correspond with treatment success, the magnitude of treatment response, and how these associations differ within and across clinical groups.

These limitations highlight a broader challenge in translational neuroscience. Bridging the gap between neural changes with treatment, underlying physiological mechanisms, and relevant clinical outcomes is essential for such research to inform patient care. Future work should co-ordinate efforts to collect and leverage large, heterogeneous datasets and engage in open science practices to maximise reproducibility. Emerging evidence from the neuromodulation literature in mood disorders suggests that treatment response may be predicted by baseline brain features, whereas treatment-associated brain changes are fairly consistent across both responders and non-responders.9 These findings underscore the importance of collecting longitudinal data to track individuals from baseline through treatment and long-term follow-up. Combined with advanced machine learning approaches, large-scale longitudinal datasets can generate data-driven insights that may remain inaccessible to traditional hypothesis-driven methods.

Another central step toward translating these findings into clinical care is through co-design of prospective studies within transdisciplinary partnerships including neuroscience, psychiatry, pharmacology, and psychology, to ensure that both mechanistic and clinical questions are addressed. Equally important is meaningful engagement with patient partners and policy makers throughout the research process, to incorporate the perspectives and priorities of the populations these research efforts intend to serve.
