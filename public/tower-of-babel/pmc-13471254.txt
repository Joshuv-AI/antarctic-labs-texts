Why evidence struggles to shape musculoskeletal health care and surgical practice in Australia: insights from an Australian clinician survey
Authors: Josephine Yu, Jillian P. Eyles, Anelise Moreti Cabral Silveira, Eric J. Weiss, Natalie Fung, Ho Tsun Anthony Yue, Manuela L. Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence based medicine is a critical cornerstone in contemporary medicine, yet uptake and adoption of evidence remain suboptimal. This is prominent in many fields of healthcare, including musculoskeletal health and surgery. Understanding the drivers of this translational lag is critical to ensure high-quality care and improved patient outcomes.MethodsA survey questionnaire, informed by the Theoretical Domains Framework was distributed to musculoskeletal clinicians and healthcare professionals, including surgeons, physiotherapists and nurses, around Australia. We assessed the barriers and enablers to translating evidence into practice, preferred sources of evidence for clinical decision-making, and the main drivers of change and improving implementation. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and qualitative data were assessed using inductive thematic analysis.Results771 clinicians were invited to participate, of whom 82 completed the survey. Participants were from Australia and covered a range of disciplines, including surgeons, nurses and physiotherapists. In general, participants were supportive of evidence-based medicine and more than 90% agreed that engagement with literature was essential for determining the best treatment options. All respondents also considered delivery of evidence-based care to be a professional responsibility. Nonetheless, multiple barriers to translating evidence into practice were identified: poor support from leadership and professional culture (n=33, >40%), policy reform (n=18, >20%), improving access to research and the prioritisation of clinically relevant and practical evidence (n=25, >30%).ConclusionThis survey study highlights the barriers surrounding the translation of research evidence into musculoskeletal care. Improving implementation will require multifaceted strategies including building stronger clinical and executive leadership, fostering supportive professional cultures and updating policy frameworks. Future research should evaluate these approaches to determine the most effective method for embedding evidence-based medicine into routine musculoskeletal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14843-1.

Introduction

Evidence-based medicine is the cornerstone of contemporary healthcare, aiming to integrate the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and patient preferences [1–3]. While evidence-based medicine is widely endorsed as the gold-standard for clinical decision-making, studies have often reported that the average time lag between the generation of research evidence and its integration into clinical practice is approximately 17 years [4, 5]. This underscores a persistent challenge in the implementation of evidence-based care, contributing to variations in quality of care, inefficiencies in workflow and, importantly, could lead to suboptimal patient outcomes [6].

Despite the continual proliferation of high-quality research and clinical trials, a persistent gap remains between evidence generation and its translation into clinical practice in the field of musculoskeletal health care, including surgery [7, 8]. The reasons underlying this translational lag are complex and multifactorial. While systemic and infrastructure barriers (e.g., institutional inertia and reimbursement constraints) have recently garnered some attention [9], the cognitive and behavioural determinants at the clinician level remain comparatively unexplored. It is often thought that clinician knowledge, beliefs and attitudes towards evidence-based medicine and their perception of applicability in real-world contexts critically influence whether evidence is adopted into practice. Across diverse healthcare settings, a small number of studies have shown that positive attitudes toward evidence-based medicine are associated with best-practice care [10–12]. These factors are also applicable to the musculoskeletal surgical care field and may be especially important as surgeons operate in fast-moving, high-pressure and high-autonomy environments.

Implementation research within musculoskeletal health has demonstrated that simply publishing guidelines is not sufficient to change practice. Passive dissemination of recommendations and guidelines (such as one-off lectures or mailed guidelines) has rarely led to a meaningful shift in clinician behaviour or surgical decision-making [13–15]. Instead, strategies targeting individual, social, and contextual determinants can enhance the effectiveness of strategies designed to drive research evidence into practice. For example, multifaceted interventions which engage clinicians in interactive education modalities, audit and feedback, and the encouragement from opinion leaders, have positively influenced adherence to evidence-based care in the fields of knee osteoarthritis and low back pain [16, 17]. This included benefits such as a reduction in low-value imaging and unnecessary arthroscopies, with a shift towards conservative management strategies such as weight loss recommendations and exercise [16]. It is important to note that improvements in professional practice did not always directly translate to patient benefit [14]. However, sustained and tailored strategies which combine clinician training with system-level support and patient engagement appear most promising as they not only strengthen clinician adherence to evidence-based care, but when sustained over time, also yield measurable benefits in patient outcomes such as pain, function and quality of life [15, 16].

Despite these insights, empirical data on these behavioural determinants is lacking in the musculoskeletal surgical care space. Notably, the current literature – including the primary studies featured in systematic reviews – has primarily focused on physiotherapists, chiropractors and other allied health professionals. This leaves a critical knowledge gap regarding how surgeons engage with evidence and translation. To address this gap, we conducted a survey of clinicians involved in musculoskeletal care to explore multiple domains central to the translation of evidence.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to explore clinician beliefs, attitudes, and perceived barriers and enablers related to the translation of research evidence into practice in musculoskeletal surgical care. The survey was deployed online using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). Survey questions did not include any identifiable information, and all responses were thus anonymous. This study was approved by the UNSW Human Research Ethics Committee (iRECS7477), and informed consent was collected from all participants. Findings were reported in accordance with the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting Of Survey Studies [18].

The survey was distributed electronically to healthcare professionals and clinician-researchers across various disciplines in Australia, including surgeons, physiotherapists and general practitioners. To ensure sufficient representation of surgeons, a minimum quota of 25 surgeon respondents was set a priori and met. This threshold was reflective of a projected ~ 25% surgeon representation based on the anticipated total sample size. The quota aligned with commonly used pragmatic ranges for adequate capture of surgeon perspectives, and the ability to derive meaningful conclusions surrounding the barriers and facilitators to surgical decision making. Recruitment took place between November 2024 and May 2025 using convenience sampling and snowballing through professional networks and clinical mailing lists. Invitations were disseminated through The Royal Australasian College of Physicians, Australasian Faculty of Rehabilitation Medicine, Rehabilitation Medicine Society of Australia and New Zealand, Australia and New Zealand Bone and Mineral Society, Australian and New Zealand Society for Geriatric Medicine, Australian Rheumatology Association, and New Zealand Rheumatology Association. To support quota sampling and enhance diversity across clinician groups, targeted email invitations were also distributed to health professionals in different occupations such as nurses, physiotherapists and surgeons. To be eligible, participants needed to be a healthcare professional currently practicing in Australia and who had cared for patients with musculoskeletal conditions in the past 12 months, or a clinician-researcher working in the field of musculoskeletal health for at least 12 months whilst meeting the same patient care criteria as other healthcare professionals. Exclusion criteria included those who did not meet the inclusion criteria, and those who have previously responded to the survey. Incomplete responses (those who completed less than 80% of the mandatory items) were also excluded from analyses. Participants read and acknowledged the consent document (containing the above information) before self-administering an English-language survey. The survey took approximately 10 min to complete.

Survey measures

A custom survey was developed by the study team that included both closed- and open-ended items designed to assess (i) health professional demographic characteristics, (ii) perceived barriers and enablers to translating research evidence into practice, (iii) preferred sources of evidence for clinical decision-making, and (iv) main drivers of change and improving implementation.

The demographic questions captured age, gender, and professional background, including clinical discipline, years in practice, and highest qualification. The items assessing barriers and enablers to translating research evidence into practice were based on the Theoretical Domains Framework, a tool developed and used widely to identify barriers and enablers to health professionals implementing evidence-based recommendations [19], and the Determinants of Implementation Behaviour Questionnaire, [20]. The Determinants of Implementation Behaviour Questionnaire has 93 questions mapping to the theoretical domains framework and is designed for researchers and clinicians to select the relevant questions and adapt them to their context [20]. See Table 1 that maps the survey items to the 14 domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (v2) [21]. There were 20 items in the survey: 17 statements to which respondents were asked to rate their agreement using a five-point Likert scale (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree); one multiple-response question with various types of evidence to better understand what clinicians perceived as high-quality evidence (Fig. 1), from which respondents could select as many as they wanted; and two open-ended questions about their perceptions of the main drivers for change, and the key strategies to improve research implementation. A full version of the survey is available in Additional File 1.

Once the questionnaire was drafted it was circulated for review amongst the study team which included two physiotherapists who are content experts in low back pain and osteoarthritis. The survey was piloted by two musculoskeletal surgeons who completed the survey and provided feedback which was incorporated in the final version.

Analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all demographic variables and survey items using SPSS Version 29.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Results were expressed as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations where appropriate. For Likert-scale items assessing clinician beliefs, attitudes, self-efficacy, and organisational support for translating research evidence into practice, responses were collapsed into three categories: ‘Agree’ (Strongly agree/Agree), ‘Neutral’, and ‘Disagree’ (Disagree/Strongly disagree) for reporting purposes.

Responses to the multiple-response questions were summarised as proportions. Responses to open-ended questions were analysed using thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [22]. Generation of initial codes was conducted independently by two researchers, using an inductive approach, before identification of recurring themes relating to drivers of change and strategies to enhance research translation. Codes and themes were reviewed, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a consensus codebook was developed. Themes were then summarised with illustrative quotes presented in the results.

Results

Participant demographics

A total of 82 healthcare professionals (10.6% response rate; calculated as a percentage of total email invitations) from around Australia completed the survey. Participants were predominantly physiotherapists (46.3%, n = 38), and surgeons (35.4%, n = 29). One participant (1.2%) did not provide a response for their occupation group. Most participants (64%, n = 55) identified themselves as male (Table 2), with a mean age of 45 years (SD 13.9, range 23–77). Respondent characteristics are presented in Table 2. The rate of completion from the respondents for each Likert-type item in the survey was 99.8%. The rate of completion for the open-ended questions regarding the main drivers of change was 97.6%, and improving translation was 89%.

Barriers and enablers for the translation of evidence into practice

Figure 2 shows the responses to the questions relating to barriers and enablers to translating research evidence into practice. Overall, participants demonstrated a strong commitment to evidence-based practice, with universal agreement that delivering up-to-date, evidence-based care was a professional responsibility. Most respondents reported their clinical decision-making was guided by current evidence, with 91.5% (n = 75) of participants using guidelines to inform care delivery. Attitudes towards the broader research landscape were similarly positive, with most participants optimistic that future clinical research would improve practice and that incorporating the latest evidence enables delivery of the best possible care. Most respondents (88.9%, n = 72) felt confident in using the most up-to-date research, perceived evidence as accessible (76.8%, n = 63), and felt supported by their workplace to practice evidence-based medicine (78%, n = 64).

Despite these favourable attitudes, respondents identified several persistent implementation barriers. The most prominent was that there are better ways to implement research findings into practice (72.0%, n = 59). Resource constraints were notable with only 61% (n = 50) reporting sufficient resources to evidence translation. Concerns of treatment-related harms (40.2%, n = 33), financial barriers (38.3%, n = 31) and regulatory requirements (25.6%, n = 21) were also commonly identified obstacles.

Open-ended responses identifying additional barriers were broadly coded into six overarching themes: organisational culture and systems, professional dynamics, time and workload constraints, resource limitations, patient-related factors, and evidence-related concerns (Table 3). For ‘organisational and system factors’, several participants described resistance to change (particularly amongst senior staff) and a lack of consensus among colleagues as key organisational culture challenges. This was highlighted in one participant’s response, who noted, “The general system aspiration [is] to do the same thing next year as we did last year”.

In some cases, “challenging seniority” or the need for “collegiate agreement on treatment protocols” were perceived as barriers. There was also acknowledgement of medico-legal concerns, insurer restrictions, and policy gaps e.g., “Barriers in the approval of evidence-based treatments from insurers, particularly with workers compensation or CTP patients.”.

‘Time and workload constraints’ were also widely cited whereby clinicians often reported insufficient time to review the literature or translate evidence into patient care – particularly for generalist roles such as GPs.

‘Resource limitations’ were identified as not only did healthcare practitioners face high equipment and training costs, but funding shortfalls and the cost to patients as well. For example, one respondent mentioned the difficulties associated with best care “if they [the patients] are under Medicare only”. This tied in with ‘patient-related factors’ in which socioeconomic constraints of patients were also raised as a potential barrier. Interestingly, patients seemed to favour specialist advice over evidence-based recommendations: “Patients will not accept evidence-based treatment as they have been told otherwise by their specialist”.

‘Evidence-related concerns’ revolved around both quality of evidence and applicability particularly perceived high levels of bias and the lack of clear recommendations in clinical practice guidelines. In particular, one participant noted that “there is a big difference in evidence from trials “what to do” and then translating that to clinical skills “how to do it”.

Preferred sources of evidence for clinical decision-making

The survey asked respondents what type of evidence would prompt a change in their own clinical practice (Fig. 3). A majority of respondents believed that systematic reviews and meta-analyses (89%, n = 73), and randomised controlled trials (84.1%, n = 69) would influence their practice decisions. In contrast, most respondents reported that quasi-experimental studies (93.9%, n = 77), non-experimental studies (93.9%, n = 77) and meta-syntheses (86.6%, n = 71), qualitative studies (70.7%, n = 58), and expert opinion (61%, n = 50) would be unlikely to change how they practice.

Main drivers of change and improving implementation

Two open-ended questions were posed to participants to better understand how healthcare practitioners envision integrating evidence-based medicine into clinical practice. Many responses reflected or addressed the enablers and barriers mentioned in the previous section. There were a total of 79 responses coded for the first question and 73 responses coded for the second question. Overall, consensus across the qualitative responses were limited, with only four themes raised by a third or more of the participants, while most were mentioned by fewer than one in five.

For the first question, six key themes were identified as the main drivers of change (Table 4). The first key theme was ‘Leadership and Professional Culture’ which was identified by almost half of survey respondents, recognising key opinion leaders and clinical champions as essential for motivating practice change as reflected in “Having senior clinicians who are progressive and up to date, who encourage you to change your own practice”. A second commonly raised theme was ‘Patient-Related Factors’, with almost a third of the respondents noting that “patient perception of perceived value of certain treatments” can strongly encourage (or discourage) the translation of research evidence into practice.

Several other themes were less frequently identified. Some respondents emphasised ‘Policy Changes and Resource Availability’ whereby regulatory factors (e.g., “national adoption on clinical care standard”) could help to drive change. Others highlighted ‘Evidence and Dissemination’ and improving ‘Education and Access’ such as through training as key drivers. Finally, a few participants described the effectiveness of ‘Financial and Market Forces’ with a focus on cost-effectiveness and commercial influences.

The second open-ended question focused on strategies to improve the translation of evidence-based medicine which aligned closely with the drivers identified above (Table 4). The most frequently raised themes were improving ‘Access to Research’ and increased ‘Relevance and Practicality’ of research findings, with each being identified by approximately one-third of respondents. Participants described the need to remove journal paywalls, centralize results in “a comprehensive quick location”, enhance dissemination through “regular journal clubs”, and prioritise “clinically significant treatment effects… relevant in a clinical setting”.

Other themes were less frequently mentioned. Some respondents emphasised ‘Organisational and Policy Support’ highlighting the importance of “embed[ding] the concept of research implementation into all policy and funding decisions”. Others focussed on ‘Peer Influence’ and ‘Education and Training’, such as ongoing continuing professional development (CPD), as ways to improve uptake of evidence-based practice. A smaller number suggested ‘Incentivize Evidence-Based Medicine’, including defunding low-value care, to further improve adoption.

Discussion

Over 700 Australian clinicians were surveyed about their agreement with statements concerning their attitudes to evidence-based medicine. The survey had a response rate of 10.6% and respondents mostly consisted of physiotherapists and surgeons. Respondents demonstrate a strongly positive attitude towards evidence-based medicine, particularly regarding professional responsibility, use of research and the value of clinical guidelines. However, despite this generally favourably orientation towards the adoption and translation of research into practice, important organisational, resource and contextual barriers remain, highlighting a critical gap between clinician attitudes and the practical realities of implementing evidence into routine care. This study provides novel insights into the beliefs and attitudes of healthcare professionals involved in musculoskeletal care in Australia, and their perceptions of the barriers and enablers to translating research evidence into practice. While evidence-based medicine is widely accepted as a professional standard and has been taken up in other specialties, adoption into the field of musculoskeletal practice and surgery is complex. In the present study, participants expressed overwhelmingly positive beliefs and attitudes towards the value of evidence-based medicine. All respondents unanimously agreed that providing evidence-based care is a professional responsibility, and most reported the use of current literature to inform their decisions. However, despite this positive commitment, a persistent gap remains in the adoption of evidence-based medicine into clinical practice [23]. This has also been shown across other healthcare disciplines, in which a positive attitude towards evidence-based medicine does not necessarily translate into routine practice [24–26]. For example, in the field of gastroenterology, Buscaglia and colleagues demonstrated that although physicians presented favourable attitudes towards evidence-based medicine concepts, they had poor knowledge of evidence-based practice [26].

The reasons for this translational gap are multifactorial and encompass individual and organisational factors. At the individual level, most respondents felt confident in using the most up-to-date research, but many lacked a clear plan for clinical workflow integration, which may be particularly important for some clinical groups, including surgeons, who face high-stakes, high-pressure decision-making. In these environments, decisions may be guided by clinical experience, peer norms and intuition – which can often be seen to exert a greater influence on decision making in comparison to formal evidence [27, 28]. Additionally, concerns about the external validity of research findings, the perceived limited applicability of guidelines to heterogeneous patient populations, and institutional constraints such as time, support, or alignment with workflow have all been reported as barriers to evidence uptake [29]. This suggests that perhaps knowledge and self-efficacy alone may not be enough to create the shift in evidence-based medicine uptake, emphasizing the need for structured implementation strategies across health systems and policy [14, 15]. Similar patterns have been observed in other clinical domains, where, without the support of practical tools for evidence integration, knowledge gains are insufficient to modify workflows [25, 30, 31].

The inaccessibility of research was seen as a substantial barrier not only related to journal access paywalls, but also the time and workload required to interpret research evidence. However, it is important to note that previous work has already demonstrated that education strategies alone result in only small changes in clinical practice for health professionals in the musculoskeletal space [13–15]. Given high patient volumes, complex presentations of disease and patient expectations, ensuring research is easily digestible was considered essential. Without concise and clinically relevant summaries or decision support tools to facilitate integration, the translation of research into practice remains inconsistent and heavily reliant on individual capacity. Data accessibility has been positively associated with the capacity to foster evidence-based decision-making [32]. Therefore, tying in with the lack of tools to support evidence-based medicine uptake, in addition to the small effects of one-off education interventions, a practical strategy would be the development of point-of-care decision aids with interactive platforms to provide evidence-based suggestions for common musculoskeletal conditions. These tools have been well-accepted by clinical end-users and have facilitated uptake of evidence, as demonstrated by positive shifts in consultation habits [33, 34].

Another point for consideration was the survey respondents’ perceptions of what constitutes “high-quality” evidence to prompt change in clinical practice. There was a strong preference for systematic reviews, meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials, mirroring the traditional hierarchy of evidence [35]. There was also a reluctance to engage with other study designs, such as qualitative studies, which may be considered lower quality in the traditional sense. Interestingly, our survey results indicated that one important factor influencing the translation of research evidence into practice was related to patient expectations around surgical procedures. Qualitative studies provide clinicians valuable insights into the lived experiences, attitudes and expectations of people with musculoskeletal conditions, and their preferences for surgical versus conservative management [36–38]. Paying more attention to the results of robust qualitative studies could provide crucial context to improve shared decision-making and personalized care.

The crucial role of supportive senior clinicians, executives, key opinion leaders, and peer endorsement as key drivers of change was also identified by survey respondents. Evidence from social network research indicates that physicians who were most favourable to evidence-based medicine tended to be less central in professional networks, while those who occupy influential core positions within their network were less inclined to adopt evidence-based medicine [39]. Together, this suggests that diffusion of evidence-based medicine acceptance and adoption may be significantly limited within clinical communities. In contrast, others have shown that having the capacity to leverage internal expertise and engage stakeholders [32], leaders and managers [40, 41] is associated with better adoption of evidence-based care. Particularly in the field of musculoskeletal surgery, where decisions are often guided by peer norms, established traditions, and senior surgeon authority [27, 28], the importance of system-wide evidence-based medicine uptake cannot be overstated.

System-level barriers also emerged as key determinants for evidence-based medicine uptake in musculoskeletal health. Survey respondents identified that translation of research evidence into practice is more likely when clinicians are educated, supported by policy and funding models, and have adequate infrastructure. Drawing on lessons from other clinical fields, embedding evidence-based education within professional training offers an opportunity to foster the skills and habits needed for effective and sustainable integration of evidence in future decision making [42–44]. However, research evidence was more successfully translated into practice when evidence-based medicine principles were culturally embedded, informing everyday clinical decisions and practice [43]. Policy and funding are also considered pivotal in shaping how research evidence is adopted. Without supportive frameworks, even motivated clinicians may face challenges in translating research evidence into practice. For example, policy or insurer models that incentivize procedures over conservative management may inadvertently discourage evidence-based care [23]. In musculoskeletal health, where there is often variation in practice and use of low-value interventions, alignment between education, policy, and funding for evidence-based medicine is particularly important to shift research evidence into practice.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is our approach, which combines a quantitative evaluation of the barriers and enablers to the translation of evidence into practice, in conjunction with qualitative insights into contextual and behavioural determinants, and potential drivers for change. Additionally, the inclusion of a predefined surgeon quota ensured that key perspectives from surgeons were adequately represented, enabling clear identification of factors which influence surgical decision making. The survey was designed to be completed electronically (via phone, tablet, or computer) and with a low time burden to maximise the amount of survey completions, given the workload of the target health care professionals.

However, there were a few limitations of this study. Firstly, the self-selected participation may have introduced self-selection bias (respondents may have been more interested in evidence-based practices) and we caution the reader when interpreting the results. Moreover, recruitment was conducted using convenience and snowball sampling via professional networks, mailing lists and targeted email invitations. Response rate was low (~ 11%) and with most responses from two professional groups (physiotherapists and surgeons). Given the survey link was shared with potential participants beyond the initial contacts, the total number of individuals receiving and refusing participation in the survey cannot be reliably determined. Consequently, the true response rate cannot be calculated. Responses were self-reported and may have introduced expectation bias, whereby clinicians may have provided overwhelmingly positive endorsement of the concept of evidence-based medicine in line with perceived professional norms rather than actual behaviours. Finally, whilst the analysis identified key themes for the main drivers of change and ways to improve implementation that broadly aligned with the evidence translation literature, the findings may not be generalizable beyond the musculoskeletal context in Australia.

Conclusion

Within the context of musculoskeletal care in Australia, although clinicians including surgeons, physiotherapists and nurses, endorse the use of scientific evidence to inform clinical practice, the translation of evidence to practice process is often constrained by a complex interplay of behavioural, cultural, and organisational factors. Musculoskeletal clinicians in Australia identified that overcoming these barriers requires multifaceted and context-specific strategies that can integrate accessibility, leadership from senior, opinion-leading clinicians, and organisational support for resources and policy. These strategies may include improved access to evidence resources, fostering leadership in senior opinion-leading clinicians and strengthening support systems for both individuals and at an organisational level. These should be explored and evaluated in future research.
