Uncovering a neglected canine filarioid in Asia: First evidence of Cercopithifilaria bainae in Thailand and a tale of the evolutionary history within the Cercopithifilaria lineage
Authors: Duriyang Narapakdeesakul, Patchana Kamkong, Frederic Beugnet, Piyanan Taweethavonsawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We recorded the first confirmed case of Cercopithifilaria bainae, a tick-borne filarioid of canids, in a stray dog from Bangkok Province, Central Thailand. Microscopic examination of thin blood films revealed unsheathed microfilariae, measuring on average 171.59 μm in length and 3.54 μm in width, characterized by a straight, slender, or slightly curved body. The body is relatively uniform in width, tapering gradually at the posterior end. The microfilariae presented with a rounded head, an elongated cephalic space containing one nucleus, and an internal column of nuclei extending across the majority of the body length. Molecular analysis of the cox1 sequence (843 bp) confirmed species identity (99.84%) as C. bainae. Bayesian phylogenetic and molecular clock analyses estimated the origin of Cercopithifilaria lineage to the Middle Miocene (∼11.73 Mya, 95% HPD: 8.65–15.11 Mya), coinciding with the origins of key host families, Canidae (∼14.06 Mya) and Cervidae (∼12.80 Mya). Cercopithifilaria bainae shared a most recent common ancestor with the progenitors of Cercopithifilaria japonica, Cercopithifilaria grassii, and Cercopithifilaria roussilhoni at ∼10.55 Mya (95% HPD: 6.78–14.51 Mya) in the Middle Miocene. Meanwhile, the radiation of extant C. bainae haplotypes occurred much more recently during the Early Pleistocene (∼0.71 Mya, 95% HPD: 0.28–1.23 Mya), suggesting a history of ghost lineages, perhaps persisting in prehistoric caniforms, before adaptation in domestic dogs. These findings significantly expand the geographical range of C. bainae into Asia and highlight the importance of combining morphological identification with molecular phylogenetics for diagnosing and investigating neglected filarioid.

Introduction

Canine vector-borne diseases represent a significant veterinary and public health concern across Southeast Asia, particularly in tropical rainforest countries such as Thailand, where favorable climatic conditions facilitate the proliferation and population dynamics of arthropod vectors (Nguyen et al., 2021; Junsiri and Taweethavonsawat, 2025). Among these, filarial nematodes of the family Onchocercidae are well-documented and clinically significant pathogens (Jitsamai et al., 2021a, 2021b; Kamkong et al., 2023). Historically, diagnostic and research efforts in Thailand have prioritized species with high clinical severity or zoonotic implications, particularly Brugia malayi, Brugia pahangi, Dirofilaria immitis, and Dirofilaria repens-like (tentatively named Dirofilaria asiatica) (Somsap et al., 2021; Thongpiya et al., 2021; Sarasombath et al., 2024; Colella et al., 2025). However, the actual species diversity of filarioids infecting canids extends beyond these lymphatic, cardiovascular-dwelling, or subcutaneous species and encompasses neglected filarial nematodes that are often overlooked in routine diagnostics.

One such neglected filarial genus circulating in canids is Cercopithifilaria, which currently comprises at least three species (Cercopithifilaria bainae, Cercopithifilaria grassii, and Cercopithifilaria sp. II) that inhabit the subcutaneous tissues of dogs and red foxes (Otranto et al., 2013; Maia et al., 2017; Nguyen et al., 2021). Among these, C. bainae is a particular filarioid of dogs widespread in Asia, Australia, the Middle East, South Africa, Europe, and both North and South Americas, primarily transmitted by the brown dog tick, Rhipicephalus sanguineus (sensu lato) (Brianti et al., 2012; Ramos et al., 2013, 2014; Cortes et al., 2014; Latrofa et al., 2014, 2016; Angelou et al., 2020; Lineberry et al., 2021; Bezerra-Santos et al., 2022; Sazmand et al., 2022). Additionally, prior studies led by Nguyen et al. (2020) and Bezerra-Santos et al. (2022) detected the parasite’s DNA in Ctenocephalides felis in Vietnam and Rhipicephalus haemaphysaloides in India, bringing questions about a potentially broader range of hosts and vectors; nonetheless, the competence of fleas as vectors for C. bainae remains to be experimentally investigated, as previously conducted with R. sanguineus (Brianti et al., 2012).

Typically, adult worms of C. bainae reside in the subcutaneous tissue of dogs and produce their microfilariae in the superficial dermal tissues of infected hosts. As a result, this filarial species is usually examined morphologically via skin-snip biopsies, whereas molecular identification is performed using genomic DNA extracted from positively scored skin sediments (Cortes et al., 2014; Ramos et al., 2014, 2016; Boyd et al., 2019; Angelou et al., 2020; Sazmand et al., 2022). Nevertheless, although C. bainae is biologically characterized as a dermal-dwelling filarioid, its microfilariae have also been detected in canine blood and synovial fluids (Gabrielli et al., 2014; Tutija et al., 2020). Pathologically, while often subclinical, the presence of C. bainae has been implicated in chronic inflammatory responses, including dermal papules (giant cutaneous cysts), polyarthritis, and lymphohistiocytic perivascular dermatitis, suggesting a broader clinical spectrum than previously recognized (Gabrielli et al., 2014; Boyd et al., 2019; Soares et al., 2020).

Notwithstanding the widespread distribution of the principal tick vector, R. sanguineus (s.l.) throughout the Indochinese Peninsula (Dantas-Torres et al., 2024; Soe et al., 2026), data regarding the occurrence of Cercopithifilaria in canid hosts in Thailand remain virtually non-existent, representing a biogeographical gap in the Asian epidemiological context. Conversely, C. bainae has been extensively documented in Rhipicephalus spp. ticks associated with companion animals from neighboring and regional countries, including India, China, Vietnam, Taiwan, Malaysia, Indonesia, and the Philippines (Latrofa et al., 2014; Bezerra-Santos et al., 2022), as well as in skin-snips of dogs from Iran (Sazmand et al., 2022). Despite the prior surveys by Latrofa et al. (2014) and Bezerra-Santos et al. (2022), which examined a total of 54 R. sanguineus (s.l.) ticks gathered from Bangkok Province, Central Thailand, C. bainae infections were absent among the analyzed specimens.

Therefore, to address this gap, we present here the first confirmed case of C. bainae infection in a stray dog residing in Bangkok Province in late 2025, combining morphological examination of microfilariae in the blood and molecular identification through the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene. Furthermore, we conducted the phylogenetic speciation and molecular clock analyses for deciphering the potential origin and evolutionary history of the Cercopithifilaria lineage, along with host-parasite interactions.

Materials and methods

Dog case and microscopic examination

On December 7, 2025, a 5-year-old female stray dog residing in Bangkok Province, Central Thailand (13°49′52.0″N, 100°45′14.0″E), was hospitalized in the veterinary service center in Nonthaburi Province for a health examination. Overall, this dog was healthy, and no erythematous papular rash, cutaneous cysts, or nodules were observed on the skin except for an umbilical hernia. After a thorough examination, no ectoparasites were found on the dog. Ectoparasite control was administered orally to the dog. However, it should be emphasized that this dog lived in an area where the brown dog ticks (R. sanguineus (s.l.)) and several tick-associated microorganisms (Anaplasma platys, Ehrlichia canis, Mycoplasma haemocanis, Babesia vogeli, and Hepatozoon canis) were previously reported between March and June 2015 (Do et al., 2021). At the veterinary service center, thin blood films were prepared and initially stained by applying the Wright-Giemsa staining technique. Technicians incidentally detected unidentified microfilariae that were very faintly visible and did not resemble the Dirofilaria spp. or Brugia spp. parasites frequently reported in dogs in Bangkok Province (Jitsamai et al., 2021a; Kamkong et al., 2023). Subsequently, a blood sample (70 μl) from the infected dog was delivered to the Laboratory of Parasitology, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University, for further investigation of filarioids under the biosafety regulations (Permit No. 2531014).

Staining protocol and DNA extraction

In our laboratory, thin blood films were fixed in absolute methanol for 2 min and then stained with a 10% Giemsa solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) for 40 min. We likewise observed the microfilariae that were slightly tinged purple, making them difficult to examine. As a result, the staining protocol was therefore modified by fixing in absolute methanol for 2 min and staining with a 2% Giemsa solution (Merck) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) for 1 h. This modified protocol enhances the Giemsa staining of microfilariae to a more pronounced purple coloration. For further molecular analysis, 50 μl of dog blood were subjected to genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction using a NucleoSpin® Blood Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions, except for the elution step. To achieve a high yield of gDNA, Buffer BE, which had been preheated to 70 °C, was reduced by 75% from 100 μl to 25 μl and thereafter added to the filtered column prior to the elution step. The sample was preserved at −20 °C until the PCR preparation.

Molecular detection of filarioids and DNA sequencing

Molecular detection of onchocercid filarioids was performed with a nested PCR (nPCR) assay employing universal primers: (i) OnchoCOI_F1, OnchoCOI_R1 and (ii) OnchoCOI_F2, OnchoCOI_R2 (Garrido-Bautista et al., 2023), which effectively amplified a ∼900-bp fragment of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene of the parasites. Amplification was performed following the protocol established by Narapakdeesakul et al. (2025) in a total volume of 25 μl of the PCR reaction mixture, which contained 2.5 μl of 10× Buffer KOD Plus Neo, 2.5 μl of each dNTP (2 mM), 1.5 μl of MgSO4 (25 mM), 0.5 μl of each primer (10 pmol), 0.5 μl of 1.0 U KOD Plus Neo DNA Polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), 15 μl of UltraPure Water (Canvax, Valladolid, Spain), and 2 μl of gDNA template. During the amplification, gDNA of Brugia pahangi isolated from a dog in Bangkok Province, Central Thailand, served as a positive control. Primary PCR products were diluted 5-fold using UltraPure Water (Canvax) and then used as a template in the secondary PCR. All PCR reactions were carried out in a MiniAmp™ Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems™, Waltham, MA, USA).

PCR products were examined on a 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and then visualized with a UV transilluminator. PCR amplicon bands of the expected size were excised from an agarose gel and purified using a NucleoSpin® Gel and PCR Clean-up (Macherey-Nagel) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Subsequently, the amplicon sample was sequenced bidirectionally through Sanger sequencing service provided by U2Bio Company Limited in South Korea (https://www.u2bio.com), which was performed in the ABI 3730XL DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems™).

Sequence processing and phylogenetic speciation analysis

Sequencing results were rigorously assessed for the existence of ambiguous or mixed chromatograms using BioEdit v7.2.5 (Hall, 1999). The primer sequences were then removed from a consensus sequence, and the newly generated cox1 sequence of Cercopithifilaria sp. (849 bp) (GenBank: PX909678) was compared to those in the GenBank database utilizing the BLASTN search. Thereafter, our sequence was aligned with other 155 reference sequences for species of the Spiruromorpha globally through the G-INS-i algorithm in MAFFT v.7 (Katoh et al., 2017) before being trimmed to uniform length using trimAI (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009). Due to the presence of degenerate nucleotides (R and Y) in some reference sequences (GenBank: PV023865 and OK360722), these ambiguous positions were removed from the alignment dataset before constructing the phylogenetic tree to mitigate Bayesian posterior probability and topology noise.

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Bayesian inference (BI) method in BEAST v.2.7.7 (Bouckaert et al., 2019), and the best-fitting evolutionary model (GTR+F+I+G4) was selected by bModelTest (Bouckaert and Drummond, 2017). Because of the relatively high genetic diversity and potential complex rate variations observed in the cox1 sequences of Cercopithifilaria spp. (Agatsuma et al., 2005; Otranto et al., 2012), which occasionally disrupt evolutionary signals, an optimized relaxed clock (ORC) model (Douglas et al., 2021) was applied in the analysis. This approach solves the typical problem of poor mixing in relaxed clock and BI analyses and permits individual branches of a phylogeny to exhibit independent evolutionary substitution rates. Additionally, a Yule model tree prior, which assumes that every species has an equitable probability to undergo a speciation event at any given time (Yule, 1925), and the Bactrian operator, a transition kernel employed to achieve a higher effective sample size (ESS) for continuous parameters (Yang and Rodríguez, 2013), were applied. The BI analysis was performed in the subscribed CIPRES Science Gateway (https://www.phylo.org). The posterior probability support for each node was derived from 100,000,000 MCMC runs, with sampling performed every 10,000 generations from two independent chains. The initial 25% of MCMC runs were discarded as “burn-in” following the assessment of ESS values in Tracer v.1.7.1 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/tracer). The phylogenetic reconstruction was visualized in FigTree v.1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree).

Molecular dating of Cercopithifilaria and parasite-host interactions

To ascertain the potential origin of the Cercopithifilaria lineage, the cox1 sequence dataset (577-bp excluding gaps), which contained 33 Cercopithifilaria haplotypes as assessed by DnaSP v.6.12.03 (Rozas et al., 2017), was used to estimate the divergence times. We performed a BI analysis implemented in BEAST v.2.7.7, incorporating a Yule model tree prior and the Bactrian operator as previously outlined. The evolutionary rate applied in the ORC model was fixed at 0.01 substitutions per site (base pair) per million years ago (Mya), in accordance with a substitution rate anticipated for the mitochondrial cox1 gene in animals (Brown et al., 1979; Zarlenga et al., 2006). Unexpectedly, bModelTest predicted two best-fitting evolutionary models for our dataset: (i) GTR+F+I+G4 and (ii) TN+F+I+G4; thus, two independent runs were initially performed, each involving 100,000,000 MCMCs and sampling every 10,000 generations. Upon assessment in Tracer v.1.7.1 with the first 10% discarded, ESS values yielded by the first model (ranging from 3424 to 8129) surpassed those of the second model, which estimated certain parameters, notably Tree.height, BMT_ProportionInvariable, ahasInvariableSites, and ActivePropInvariable, falling below 200. Therefore, the final maximum clade credibility time tree derived from the post “burn-in” by TreeAnnotator v2.7.7 (Bouckaert et al., 2019) under the best model, GTR+F+I+G4, was selected. The scale axis was visualized in units of Mya.

Given that carnivores and ungulates constitute the predominant hosts of the presently recognized Cercopithifilaria spp., the time trees of order Carnivora (including suborders Caniformia and Feliformia) and infraorder Pecora (including families Bovidae, Cervidae, Giraffidae, and Antilocapridae), retrieved from the TimeTree 5 database (Kumar et al., 2022) (accessed on January 20, 2026), were used to establish the parasite-host interactions. Afterwards, lines connecting taxa between parasite and host phylogenies, as well as geological time scales, were generated employing the phytools and deeptime packages in RStudio v.2023.9.1 Build 494 (available at https://posit.co/download/rstudio-desktop) and then modified in Microsoft PowerPoint v.2203. The scale axis was also set in a unit of Mya.

Results

Microscopic examination and molecular identification of microfilariae in the dog blood

Microfilariae that exist in the Giemsa-stained thin blood films were exclusively observed in unsheathed forms (n = 12), measuring on average 171.59 μm (range: 154.88–190.94 μm) in length and 3.54 μm (range: 3.08–4.11 μm) in width, characterized by a straight, slender, or slightly curved body (Fig. 1). The body is relatively uniform in width, tapering gradually at the posterior extremity. The anterior extremity featured a rounded head, marked by an elongated cephalic space (CS) of approximately 18–20 μm, with one nucleus (ON) positioned laterally. The internal structure displayed the nuclei as a dense, dark-purple stained column extending across the majority of the body length. The nuclear column is disrupted by three distinct, non-staining areas at the nerve-ring (NR), excretory pore (EP), and anal pore (AP). Phylogenetic speciation analysis

The BI analysis integrated with speciation and relaxed clock models, based on the partial cox1 sequences of species of the Spiruromorpha globally, indicated that our DCB01 isolate is classified within the family Onchocercidae (Fig. 2). It was clustered with C. bainae and Cercopithifilaria sp. SW-2011 (presently designated as C. bainae) from dogs in Italy (Otranto et al., 2011; Solinas et al., 2014; Lefoulon et al., 2015) and the USA (Boyd et al., 2019), as well as from R. sanguineus tick in French Guyana (Binetruy and Duron, 2023) and Ct. felis flea in Vietnam (Nguyen et al., 2020), as previously mentioned. Obviously, the lineage comprising C. bainae evidently shared a sister group relationship with the ancestral clade of Cercopithifilaria japonica, Cercopithifilaria grassii, and Cercopithifilaria roussilhoni, which are hosted by Asian black bears (Ursus thibetanus), dog, and African brush-tailed porcupine (Atherurus africanus), respectively (Ferri et al., 2009; Otranto et al., 2013). Molecular dating and parasite-host interactions

The phylogenetic reconstruction, together with molecular clock analysis, dated the origin of Cercopithifilaria lineage to the Middle Miocene, approximately 11.73 Mya, with a 95% highest posterior density (HPD) ranging from 8.65 to 15.11 Mya (node no. 1 in Fig. 3A). This temporal origin is concordant with the major diversification periods of its primary hosts, specifically the families Canidae (∼14.06 Mya, 95% HPD: 7.50–24.35 Mya) and Cervidae (∼12.80 Mya, 95% HPD: 8.50–17.70 Mya) (Fig. 3B and C), as previously proposed by Kumar et al. (2022). Cercopithifilaria bainae shared a most recent common ancestor (MRCA) with its sister taxa (C. japonica, C. grassii, and C. roussilhoni) roughly 10.55 Mya (95% HPD: 6.78–14.51 Mya) in the Middle Miocene, shortly following the emergence of the Cercopithifilaria lineage (node no. 2 in Fig. 3A), suggesting its early speciation. Besides C. bainae, a distinct clade of Cercopithifilaria sp. II DO-2012 (haplotypes 5–7), which disseminated among canids (dog and red fox) and their associated ticks (R. sanguineus) in Europe, shared the MRCA with lineages specialized to ungulate hosts of families Bovidae (Cercopithifilaria bulboidea, Cercopithifilaria tumidicervicata, Cercopithifilaria minuta, Cercopithifilaria multicauda, and Cercopithifilaria shohoi) and Cervidae (Cercopithifilaria longa and Cercopithifilaria crassa) at ∼10.89 Mya (95% HPD: 7.73–14.31 Mya) (node no. 3 in Fig. 3A), situating it in the Middle Miocene. It is important to note that the tribe Vulpini (Canidae: Caninae), which encompasses the red fox, arose during this period (∼10.48 Mya, 95% HPD: 7.63–23.41 Mya) (Fig. 3A–C). Meanwhile, the ungulate-specific parasites themselves formed a well-supported monophyletic lineage that originated in the Late Miocene, dated back to 9.58 Mya (95% HPD: 7.06–12.36 Mya) (node no. 4 in Fig. 3A).

Discussion

This study recorded the first case of naturally acquired C. bainae infection in a stray dog from Thailand, therefore significantly bridging the geographical gap of this parasite in Asia (Latrofa et al., 2014; Bezerra-Santos et al., 2022). The initial diagnosis was established through the detection of microfilariae in the blood, an unusual localization of this parasite beyond the subcutaneous region; nevertheless, staining modifications were required for precise observation. We noticed that a typical 10% Giemsa solution applied for 40 min, as commonly used in routine diagnostics (Kamkong et al., 2023; Narapakdeesakul et al., 2025), yielded microfilariae of Cercopithifilaria that were barely discernible. This aspect is probably attributed to its unique biological structure, like a dense hydrophobic cuticle that serves as a barrier to aqueous dyes, resulting in a translucent appearance if the staining duration is insufficient for the dye to infiltrate the internal structures. The high solvent concentration may cause surface precipitation on the parasite cuticle, perhaps forming a barrier that prevents the dye infiltration. Our modified protocol using a diluted 2% Giemsa solution for 1 h proved enhanced effectiveness. This prolonged, low-concentration exposure allowed the dye to gradually permeate the cuticle and bind to the nuclei without clogging the surface, displaying the classical morphological characteristics of C. bainae, akin to those described by Otranto et al. (2011, 2013). Similarly, Sazmand et al. (2022) utilized a 5% Giemsa solution (1:20 dilution) to stain the microfilariae of this parasite for 1 h, elucidating their morphological appearances distinctly. As a result, we encourage researchers and technicians adopt this protocol, employing low Giemsa concentrations and a prolonged staining time of 1 h or longer, to examine the blood films in the unlikely case that Cercopithifilaria microfilariae are inadvertently observed.

The speciation phylogeny integrated with molecular clock analysis demonstrated that the evolutionary history of the lineage of Cercopithifilaria spp. predates the emergence of its modern domestic hosts (Fig. 3). Despite the lineage originating in the Middle Miocene (∼11.73 Mya), a period marked by the expansion of open grasslands (savanna-like habitats) and the radiation of grazing or herbivorous mammals (Casas-Gallego et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025), the parasites of bovid and cervid hosts likely evolved later from those that primarily infected carnivores, as they established a lineage interspersed among carnivorous Cercopithifilaria spp. Since the upper 95% HPD of the lineage’s origin (∼15.11 Mya) is situated in the transitional climatic period from the Early to Middle Miocene (∼14.7–17 Mya) (Baldassini et al., 2021), when forests were supplanted by grasslands, we hypothesize that host-switching events of carnivorous parasites to ungulate hosts occurred when the infected ancient or extinct predators migrated out of the forests into sympatry with the abundant grazers (Sotnikova and Rook, 2010). Because the origin of stem-Rhipicephalus ticks is probably associated with host shifts between early carnivores (e.g. hyaenodonts) and early ungulates (e.g. condylarths) (Bakkes et al., 2021), transitions of parasites from carnivores to ruminants (or vice versa) may involves shared tick vectors that have fed on both groups during their ancient timelines of predator-prey food webs. Furthermore, we observed that seven Cercopithifilaria species of ungulates are likely restricted to Japan (Agatsuma et al., 2005; Ferri et al., 2009). Although their actual epidemiology being ambiguous due to surveys conducted only in one country, they established a uniform lineage since ∼9.58 Mya (95% HPD: 7.06–12.36 Mya). This coincides with the period when the Japanese Archipelago earlier underwent a geological transformation (7.8–9.5 Mya), resulting in the separation of the island from the Eurasian continent (Matsuzaki et al., 2018).

Our morphological identification is robustly supported by the phylogenetic speciation finding (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), which resolved the Thai isolate (DCB01) as C. bainae. A striking finding in our molecular dating is the significant temporal gap between the divergence of C. bainae from its sister taxa (C. japonica, C. grassii, and C. roussilhoni) (∼10.55 Mya) and its internal radiation (∼0.71 Mya). This nearly 10-million-year interval signifies a ghost lineage, further hypothesizing that the ancestors of C. bainae survived in unknown hosts, potentially prehistoric wild caniforms as its lineage coincided with the origin of Vulpini (∼10.48 Mya), before diversifying into the haplotypes seen today. The recent radiation period (0.28–1.23 Mya) falls squarely within the Early Pleistocene, a time of intense climatic oscillation (Pisias and Moore, 1981). It is plausible that population bottlenecks during the Pleistocene ice ages, together with the later domestication (at least ∼33,000 years ago) (Wang et al., 2016) and worldwide spread of domestic dogs (Canis lupus familiaris), facilitated the recent demographic expansion of C. bainae into the region. Furthermore, since the DNA of this parasite species was detected in the cat flea Ct. felis in Vietnam (Nguyen et al., 2020), it is plausible that C. bainae can adapt to coexist with a variety of carnivore hosts, including feliforms; however, the vector competence of fleas requires further experimental investigation. To better understand the parasite-host interactions and evolutionary history, additional exploration of C. bainae in carnivore hosts, as well as their associated ectoparasitic vectors, is necessary.

Despite the significance of confirming C. bainae in Thailand for the first time, this study has limitations that must be acknowledged. Our findings are based on a convenience sampling of a stray dog in a specific location (Bangkok Province) and time (December 7, 2025), which underrepresents the actual prevalence and geographical distribution of this parasite in the country. Importantly, C. bainae is classified as a dermal filarioid, where its adults and microfilariae typically inhabit the subcutaneous tissues of the infected hosts. Although our study inadvertently detected microfilariae in the dog blood, which is uncommon (Tutija et al., 2020), skin-snip examination continues to be the standard method in routine diagnostics and is notably more sensitive than blood films, particularly if the animals have cutaneous or subcutaneous lesions and multiple erythematous plaques (Boyd et al., 2019; Soares et al., 2020). Furthermore, although R. sanguineus (s.l.) being highly populated in Bangkok Province in 2015 (Do et al., 2021), we did not conduct a survey of ticks or other ectoparasites inhabiting nearby the infected dog. Further investigations should prioritize large-scale epidemiological surveys that include both domestic and wild animals, alongside the vectors, to fully elucidate the transmission dynamics of this neglected Cercopithifilaria filarioids in Thailand and globally.

Additionally, our phylogenetic inference and molecular dating relied primarily on a singular genetic marker (cox1). While this locus is highly effective for species identification of filarioids (Narapakdeesakul et al., 2025), it solely reflects maternal inheritance history and may lack the resolution required to resolve deep evolutionary nodes robustly. Further studies should focus on integrating the mitochondrial genomes and nuclear genes; this multi-locus approach would provide a more rigorous framework for enhancing MRCA estimations and validating the potential origin and ghost lineage hypotheses. Moreover, it is noteworthy that the GenBank database (accessed on April 2, 2026) presently contains only cox1 sequences of parasites infecting hosts of the Canidae, Ursidae, Hystricidae, Bovidae, and Cervidae (Fig. 2). In fact, other Cercopithifilaria species were also discovered in Old-World monkeys (Bain et al., 1982) and marsupials (Spratt and Varughese, 1975) since the 20th Century; however, their DNA sequences have not been isolated to date. Consequently, additional reanalysis of molecular dating, integrating these missing sequences upon their availability in the GenBank database, could bring a broader view of the evolutionary history of Cercopithifilaria lineage.

Conclusion

This study provides the first evidence of C. bainae in a stray dog in Thailand, bridging a significant geographical gap in Asia. Our optimized 2% Giemsa protocol, with a 1-h staining period, enhances diagnostic accuracy for microfilariae of this neglected filarioid in the dog blood, revealing the classical morphological characteristics for C. bainae. Molecular clock analysis dated the origin of Cercopithifilaria lineage to the Middle Miocene (∼11.73 Mya), aligning with its key host families, Canidae (∼14.06 Mya) and Cervidae (∼12.80 Mya). Cercopithifilaria bainae shared the MRCA with the progenitors of C. japonica, C. grassii, and C. roussilhoni, roughly 10.55 Mya, before diversifying during the Early Pleistocene (∼0.71 Mya). These findings underscore the importance of integrated morphological and molecular approaches for identifying this neglected filarial species and exploring insights into its potential origin.
