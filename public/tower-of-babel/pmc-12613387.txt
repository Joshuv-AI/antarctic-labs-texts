Equity-focused barriers and facilitators to implementing a prenatal yoga intervention in a healthcare system: patient and provider perspectives
Authors: Sara Santarossa, Erin N Haley, Paige Coyne, Celeste Pappas, Jordan M Braciszewski, Lisa R Miller-Matero, Maya Zreik, Laurel Hicks, Amy M Loree
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with a history of depression are at increased risk for postpartum depression. Further, racial and ethnic minority groups have higher rates of postpartum depression and are less likely to initiate mental health treatment. Prenatal yoga presents a promising avenue for preventing postpartum depression among these high-risk groups. The purpose of this study was to gather feedback from pregnant patients and healthcare professionals to identify facilitators and barriers to the equitable implementation of an 8-week prenatal yoga intervention to prevent postpartum depression.MethodsAn equity-based framework for Implementation Research (EquIR) informed data collection and analysis. Participants were given an overview of the intervention and invited to provide feedback on the design and study procedures. Pregnant patients also participated in an abbreviated version of the intervention. The Framework Method was used to analyze transcripts.ResultsFour virtual focus groups with pregnant patients (N = 18) and individual interviews with healthcare professionals (N = 11) were conducted. Facilitators and barriers to implementation were grouped into themes aligned with EquIR outcomes: (1) knowledge and attitudes; (2) logistics; (3) intervention (content and instructor); (4) cost; and (5) advertising. Common barriers included safety concerns (Theme 1), time constraints (Theme 2), potential discomfort when participating (due to no prior experience, physical limitations, or limited diversity reflected in the group) (Theme 3), financial constraints (Theme 4), or perceptions about the limited safety or appropriateness of prenatal yoga (Theme 5). Conversely, key facilitators included recognizing the benefits of prenatal yoga (Theme 1), the option to participate virtually (Theme 2), providing relevant modifications (Theme 3), no more than $25 per session (Theme 4), and providing safety information and receiving referrals from a trusted medical provider (Theme 5).ConclusionsThis study represents an important step towards optimizing the equitable implementation of a prenatal yoga intervention to prevent postpartum depression for those at risk. Future work will test the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of the intervention to prevent postpartum depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08383-5.Keywords: Postpartum depression, Prenatal yoga, Equity, Equity-focused implementation, Perinatal mental health, Prevention

Background

Postpartum depression (PPD) affects approximately 13% of women and disproportionately impacts certain groups [1–3]. For example, women with a history of depression are 21 times more likely to experience PPD [1], and Black women are 1.4 times more likely to experience PPD than other racial and ethnic groups [3]. PPD adversely impacts maternal and infant health and may have longstanding impacts on a child’s language, emotional, and behavioral outcomes [4]. Intervening early in pregnancy, particularly for high-risk groups, may prevent the onset of PPD [2, 5]. This may be especially important given common challenges to initiating mental health treatment during the postpartum period [6, 7]. Both structural (i.e., financial concerns, systemic racism, and long wait times) and individual barriers (i.e., limited awareness of treatment options, stigma, and preference for other forms of support) exist and may impede the initiation of PPD treatment, particularly for at-risk groups [6, 7]. Thus, preventive interventions that are accessible and amenable for those at increased risk for PPD are needed [8].

Prenatal yoga may reduce the risk of PPD and circumvent treatment barriers to traditional mental health treatment for certain patients [9–11]. Prenatal Yoga to Prevent Postpartum Depression (PRY-D) is an 8-session, virtually delivered group-based intervention (a complimentary mobile Wi-Fi hotspot is provided to participants, if needed, for the duration of the study) [12], and has preliminary empirical support among pregnant patients with active clinical depression [13, 14]. In addition to physical activity, PRY-D incorporates aspects of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy [15, 16] - empirically supported modalities for reducing depressive symptoms and improving psychological health more broadly [13, 14]. However, additional research is warranted on the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of PRY-D as a preventive intervention for PPD. Further, barriers and facilitators to intervention feasibility and acceptability, particularly among high-risk groups, is needed to increase equitable reach and scalability.

The equity-based framework for Implementation Research (EquIR) guides the equity-focused implementation of health interventions, from the early stages of intervention development to later stages of implementation [17]. Populations that experience disparities in the intended intervention outcome (i.e., PPD) due to various social determinants of health (e.g., socioeconomic status, sex/gender, racial or ethnic background, place of residence, or mental health history) [1, 3] are considered throughout the intervention development and evaluation process. Given that those with a history of depression and racially minoritized groups experience disproportionately high rates of PPD [1–3, 5], efforts to promote intervention feasibility and acceptability among these groups are considered throughout the design and delivery of the PRY-D intervention [17]. Several implementation outcomes are examined, including acceptability (i.e., perception that the intervention is agreeable or satisfactory), adoption (i.e., intention to utilize or engage in the intervention), appropriateness (i.e., perceived relevance or usefulness of the intervention), feasibility (i.e., ability to be delivered across settings without many barriers), fidelity (i.e., adherence to the intervention), implementation cost (i.e., adjusted cost effectiveness), and sustainability (i.e., maintenance or durability of the intervention) [17]. There may be several barriers and facilitators to the equitable implementation of PRY-D within each of these domains. Thus, the aim of this study was to prospectively identify barriers and facilitators to implementing PRY-D within a healthcare system, particularly among pregnant patients at high-risk for PPD. Ultimately, findings from this study may inform the design and delivery of future prenatal yoga or alternative, preventive interventions for PPD within a large, integrated healthcare system.

Methods

Design

This paper analyzes data from focus groups with pregnant patients and one-on-one interviews with healthcare professionals at a large Midwestern, US healthcare system. These focus groups and one-on-one interviews are part of a larger study, the protocol has been published elsewhere [12], aimed at preventing PPD through the delivery of a virtual prenatal yoga intervention for at-risk (history of depression), racially diverse pregnant patients within a healthcare system (NIMH: R34MH131573). Specifically, focus groups and individual interviews were conducted to gather feedback that could identify barriers and facilitators to the equitable implementation of the aforementioned PRY-D intervention to prevent PPD, with particular attention to factors that may be especially salient to disadvantaged groups. The Institutional Review Board of Henry Ford Health (HFH) granted ethical approval (16309-01) for this study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. This study was registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov. The registration identification number is NCT06004232 (Registration Date: 2023-08-16). The 32-item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) was used for reporting [18].

Participants

Aligned with Braun and Clarke’s view on data saturation [19], this was not our main goal of sample size selection, rather achievement of our study goals (gathering feedback and ideas for the study, wherein no new themes emerged) and ensuring that diverse perspectives were captured was our aim.

Eligibility for focus group participants included being aged 18 years or older, currently pregnant, had a history of depression, and were receiving prenatal care at HFH. HFH, a nonprofit organization in Detroit, serves a diverse, underserved population where 30% of patients (and 27.8% of pregnant patients) are Black; 8.7% are uninsured; 58.6% use public health insurance [20]; 22.6% lack a car [21]; and 37% live below the poverty line [22]. History of depression was determined by International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions, codes, which were abstracted from the patient’s electronic medical record. Exclusion criteria included (a) not being fluent in English, since yoga sessions will be conducted in English, and (b) physician or obstetric provider recommendation against participation in physical activity. Eligibility criteria for individual interviews included being a healthcare professional employed by HFH who works with pregnant patients.

Procedures

Recruitment

Non-probability volunteer (self-selection), purposive sampling was used to recruit pregnant patients for focus groups. Patients who met eligibility criteria using data extracted from HFH’s electronic medical records were identified and contacted via email. Eligible and interested participants were then asked to electronically consent via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure web platform for building and managing online databases and surveys. Convenience sampling was used to recruit healthcare professionals. Targeted recruitment emails were sent out to healthcare professionals known to the research team. Additionally, snowball sampling, whereby healthcare professionals who partook in an interview were asked to recommend potentially eligible colleagues, was also used. Again, eligible and interested professionals were then asked to electronically consent via REDCap.

Conduct of focus groups and individual interviews

All focus groups and one-on-one interviews were facilitated by PC (PhD, Epidemiologist) and conducted online via WebEx (Cisco, San Jose, CA), with observation and note-taking by LH for the focus groups (PhD, Scientist and Prenatal Yoga Expert). The facilitator, PC, who identifies as a woman, has considerable training and experience executing qualitative research, specializes in patient engagement, and conducting focus groups and one-on-one interviews. Only the researchers and study participants were present for the focus groups and interviews. Informed consent was obtained prior to each discussion.

On average, focus groups with pregnant patients lasted approximately 1 h, whereas one-on-one interviews with healthcare professionals lasted approximately 30 min. Pregnant patients who participated in a focus group were given $50.00 via ClinCard (prepaid debit card) as compensation for their time. Healthcare professionals who completed an interview were compensated $25.00 e-Visa gift cards for their time. Compensating research participants has the potential to create bias through undue inducement and social desirability. However, this is most likely to occur when payment offered is substantial to the point that it could influence participants’ decision to enroll. The compensation amounts of $50.00 and $25.00 for this study are reasonable and only meant to offset the time commitment and inconvenience of study participation. Participants were encouraged to turn on their video but doing so was not a requirement. The facilitator also took time to make field notes during and after each focus group or one-on-one interview. The facilitator had no prior relationship with any pregnant patient or healthcare professional.

Semi-structured moderator guides [22] were developed to help guide focus groups and one-on-one interview discussions. Moderator guides for both groups, including key questions and subsequent probes, were developed based on EquIR [17]. Specifically, EquIR’s implementation outcomes (acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, coverage, and sustainability) were utilized to guide focus groups and interview discussions. Both guides were created by PC with oversight from SS (PhD, Scientist) and AL (PhD, Scientist), the study’s principal investigators who have qualitative methodology and subject matter expertise. Additionally, although neither moderator guide was piloted prior to its use, both were reviewed extensively by all members of the research team.

The focus group moderator guide for pregnant patients contained 7 sections [22] : (a) introduction of the facilitator—which included a brief overview of her professional background and reasons for conducting the research; (b) background for the study and purpose of the focus groups; (c) icebreaker activity with participant introductions; (d) overview of the PRY-D intervention (including the Participant Manual) presented by the facilitator; (e) real-time participation in mini-version of PRY-D yoga session (15-minute experiential practice involving aspects of the intervention, such as mindful meditation, yogic breathing and postures, restorative savasana); (f) main questions/discussion period; and (g) a wrap-up whereby the facilitator summarized main points of the discussion and elicited final feedback from participants. The main questions/discussion section included (a) soliciting thoughts about prenatal yoga to prevent PPD and the PRY-D intervention; (b) discussing the content and design of the Participant Manual; (c) obtaining feedback on participants’ experiences engaging in the PRY-D yoga session; (d) thoughts on program logistics; and (e) a discussion regarding best ways of engaging diverse pregnant patients.

The individual interview moderator guide for healthcare professionals contained similar sections to the focus group moderator guide for patients. Specifically, it contained the following 5 sections [22] : (a) introduction of the facilitator (as described for the focus groups); (b) background for the study and purpose of the focus groups; (c) overview of the PRY-D intervention (including the Provider Manual) presented by the facilitator; (d) main questions/discussion period; and (e) a wrap-up whereby the facilitator summarized main points of the discussion and elicited final feedback from participants. The main questions/discussion section included (a) soliciting thoughts about prenatal yoga to prevent PPD and the PRY-D intervention; (b) discussing the content and design of the Provider Manual; (c) thoughts on program logistics; and (d) a discussion regarding best ways of engaging diverse pregnant patients.

Data analysis

Focus groups and one-on-one interviews were audio/video recorded (with informed consent). All identifying information was removed or changed and all participants were assigned pseudonyms to maintain anonymity and confidentiality. Together, transcripts were analyzed using the Framework Method [23] and included the following steps: (a) transcription; (b) familiarization with transcripts; (c) coding (inductive and deductive); (d) developing a working analytical framework, applying the analytical framework; (e) charting data into the framework matrix; and (f) interpreting the data. PC led the analysis, with coding support from EH (PhD, Postdoctoral Researcher) and oversight from SS. No specialized software was used to manage or analyze the data (i.e., managed via Microsoft Word and Excel) (Redmond, WA). Using a deductive approach, the initial organizational framework was informed by the moderator guide questions and the associated EquIR implementation outcomes. Using an inductive approach, preliminary descriptive codes to identify concepts expressed by participants were identified, discussed, revised, and applied to the transcripts. After initial coding, transcripts were reviewed again to identify any additional subthemes and to check consistency of the coding. Although, using deductive coding may constrain the emergence of unanticipated themes, despite the addition of inductive coding, a combination of deductive and inductive techniques has been suggested as most accurate in qualitative analysis, as almost all studies are designed based on previous theory and research [22, 24].Using a negotiated approach [25], all coding disagreements between PC and EH were discussed and resolved. To enhance dependability [26], the creation of a rigorous comprehensive log (audit trail) documenting the decisions made throughout the research process, was used. The audit trail was discussed and confirmed at a bi-weekly study team meeting and in all IRB documentation. To strengthen confirmability [26], PC and EH created a preliminary data analysis report, which was reviewed by the larger study team and study’s Data Safety Monitoring Board, as a means of peer debriefing.

To enhance reflexivity, Colaizzi’s method [27] of asking participants to review themes identified through the coding process and provide feedback was employed. Specifically, to accomplish Colazzi’s method, we used a time-efficient and practical Synthesized Member Checking process [28] that has been successful our previous work [29], focus group and one-on-one interview participants were sent an email containing PDF attachments of the draft data analysis (but not full transcripts) and the framework that guided the analysis (i.e., EquIR framework), as well as a link to a short digital survey. Participants were instructed to review the data analysis and framework documents and then fill out the survey. The survey contained 3 questions, with the first asking participants to rank (from 1 to 100) the accuracy of the researcher team’s themes, followed by 2 free-text response questions asking about feedback on the researcher team’s interpretation of the data (e.g., were themes missed) and how the findings of the data could be implemented. Based on the feedback collected from participants, the researchers could go back and modify earlier steps in the analysis, if needed. This “respondent validation” or member-checking is highly valued by the authors as it allows the participants the opportunity to analyze the data alongside the research team and provides a meaningful and intentional experience in the study’s process. The survey was left open for 4 weeks. Reminders to complete the survey were sent twice. No repeat interviews were necessary, and no additional compensation was provided to the participants for this member-checking process.

Results

A total of 42 individuals consented to participate in focus groups. A total of 15 individuals consented to participate in an interview. Not all who consented actually participated in a focus group (24 either did not sign up for a time slot or did not attend) or an interview (3 did not sign up for a timeslot), and information regarding specific reason for attrition was not collected, as it was not in the study protocol. Anecdotally, reasons for attrition included participants’ schedule changes or they forgot. Ultimately, 4 focus groups with pregnant patients and 12 individual interviews with healthcare professionals were conducted.

Focus groups were composed of patients (n = 18, 2–6 per focus group) who were primarily aged 30–39 years, identified as White, with gestational age between 10 and 14 weeks (Table 1). The sample recruited is reflective of our HFH pregnant patient population, as per an Electronic Medical Record pull, which is 27.8% Black and 54% aged 30–39 years. No data was collected on income or education of patients. Moreover, the income and education data from the Electronic Medical Record is sparse and inconsistent. Healthcare professionals (n = 12) were primarily women, identifying as White, Black, or Asian, and held various roles within the health system – such as physician, nurse, or therapist (e.g., occupational, behavioral, physical) (Table 1).

EquIR equity-based framework for Implementation Research

Theme 1: knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and experiences

This theme recognizes that the knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs, as well as past experiences, of pregnant patients and healthcare professionals can impact PRY-D’s equitable implementation by facilitating or impeding its acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, and sustainability. For instance, pregnant patients and healthcare professionals who know the benefits of (prenatal) yoga perceive it positively, or have previously practiced or recommended it, were agreeable towards PRY-D (acceptability), interested in partaking in it/recommending it (adoption and sustainability), and recognized its usefulness (appropriateness). Example comments include:

“I suffered from postpartum really bad with my first child, so I [think] starting [prenatal yoga] early, it could help” [Patient 18],and [Healthcare Professional 1] said “I recommend yoga to my pregnant patients, to my non pregnant patients, all the time […]has a lot of physical and mental benefits […] I can’t really think of any specific instance where I wouldn’t recommend it”.

Conversely, pregnant patients or health professionals having no experience, or a bad experience, with (prenatal) yoga may result in negative implications for PRY-D’s adoption, acceptability, appropriateness, and sustainability. For example, Patient 7 said

“[…] I don’t feel that, at the time, my experience with my first two pregnancies, that the prenatal practice necessarily helped with my postpartum mental health. And to be honest, I was having a hard time finding 10 minutes to go for a walk, let alone do yoga practice” and Patient 15, after partaking in a mini-version of PRY-D, said “I felt like my experience is so low in yoga I couldn’t keep up, and my breathing techniques were always off.”

Additional identified barriers to PRY-D’s adoption, acceptability, appropriateness, and sustainability, particularly amongst diverse populations, included viewing (prenatal) yoga as unsafe and perceiving (prenatal) yoga as an exclusionary practice. Example comments include:

“Even if it is yoga and it is less strenuous than strength training and running and all of that, you still are going up and down in some ways. So, the blood is flowing. So, safety for sure” [Patient 4] and “I was really anxious and really nervous the first time I went to a yoga class thinking everybody’s going to look at me, it’s going to be a bunch of, you know, what you think of when you think of a yogi” [Patient 9].

Theme 2: logistics

This theme acknowledges that logistics can impact PRY-D’s equitable implementation by facilitating or hindering its acceptability, adoption, feasibility, fidelity, and sustainability. Namely, for pregnant patients to enroll in PRY-D (adoption), be satisfied with its delivery (acceptability), see their participation as manageable (feasibility), and remain adherent (fidelity) to the program (all of which contribute to long-term sustainability), PRY-D’s logistics need to be accommodating and flexible for pregnant patients with diverse backgrounds and schedules. Identified ways this could be accomplished included making the sign up/referral process straightforward, offering multiple start dates/class times (especially on weekends and evenings), providing options for missed classes (e.g., recordings and attending different class time) and ensuring classes last no more than an hour and had a weekly cadence. Example comments include:

“please just put it right in my lap. Like, tell me how to sign up and I’m going to sign up” [Patient 6] and “I think people have wildly different schedules. So I would say, if you can offer multiple sessions […]” [Patient 5].

Pregnant patients and healthcare professionals also recognized that although in-person (e.g., at a yoga studio) might be ideal, virtual delivery would likely be more accommodating, as it reduces barriers like transportation, transit time, childcare, and parking. For example, Healthcare Professional 2 said

“I think there’s definitely a huge benefit to it being virtual in terms of access” and Healthcare Professional 4 said “I think it’s more convenient for moms for them to just hop on from wherever they are and spending that time traveling back and forth is maybe too much for them.”

However, both pregnant patients and healthcare professionals expressed concerns regarding the potential for distractions at home (e.g., kids), less motivation/accountability, and difficulty creating a sense of community when participating online. For example:

“Is that door going to keep opening with other kids, other people, et cetera? And then also, if part of the goal of the group is to create a sense of community, I think you’re much more likely to create a sense of community if it’s in-person versus virtual” [Healthcare Professional 3].

Theme 3: content and instructor

The third theme recognizes that the program’s content and instructor can impact PRY-D’s equitable implementation by facilitating or impeding its acceptability, appropriateness, adoption, fidelity, and sustainability. Pregnant patients and healthcare professionals need to view PRY-D’s content as agreeable (acceptability) and suitable (appropriateness) in order to participate/recommend it (adoption and sustainability). They also need to know that the instructor is delivering the content as intended (fidelity). For instance, pregnant patients and healthcare professionals both liked that the curriculum covered mindfulness, embodiment, and breathwork, while simultaneously encouraging connection with body and baby. Example comments include:

“I think the mindfulness and also connection with the baby is really great” [Patient 6] and “I really enjoyed the breathing technique” [Patient 8].

Additionally, through their participation in a mini-session of PRY-D, pregnant patients noted liking that the instructor was knowledgeable and experienced in prenatal yoga and were extremely receptive to the fact that the instructor had been pregnant herself and was able to offer several modifications and options for poses. Example comments include:

“I like that she gave us a different option” [Patient 2] and “prefer they have either gone through or going through what I’m going through, just to have the relation” [Patient 10].

Yet, concerns regarding flow/pacing (too fast), difficulty/safety of certain poses, and preferences for racial concordance with the instructor and/or other participants were seen as potential barriers. For example, Patient 12 said:

“I know just from my experience as an African-American woman, a lot of the yoga classes I’ve been to, there aren’t a lot of people who necessarily look like me and the instructor doesn’t look like me. So, I think that will make a lot more minority women comfortable if the instructor was a woman of color or there were other people in the class that looked like her. It just makes you feel more comfortable to not be just you and, like you said, ten other white women in the class.”

Theme 4: cost

This theme acknowledges that pricing (for participants) and funding (to cover operational costs) can impact PRY-D’s equitable implementation by facilitating or hindering its adoption, feasibility, and sustainability. For diverse pregnant patients to take-up PRY-D (adoption) and see their participation as doable (feasibility), PRY-D needs to ensure that participation is affordable. For example, Patient 8 noted that

“[…] because everything is so expensive these days. So, I know maybe you have to charge something, but costs will definitely be– I think it should definitely be a big consideration” and later suggested “maybe 20–25 dollars [per session].”

Pregnant patients and healthcare professionals were also very enthusiastic about the possibility of providing yoga materials to all participants at no cost, acknowledging that buying yoga materials is an added cost of participation. Example comment:

“I’m happy to see that you provide some of the mats and materials” [Healthcare Professional 7].

Moreover, pregnant participants and healthcare professionals both recognized that not all pregnant patients would be able to attend each session and that paying per session (if there was a cost) would be preferred to an up-front payment for all sessions. Example comment:

“maybe there would be an option to pay as you go, so you don’t have to upfront pay so much money, especially if you run into complications or you end up not being able to complete it, then you’re not out that money” [Patient 13].

Costs related to the first session being in-person (transportation, childcare, and parking) were also mentioned as barriers that should be carefully considered. For example, Healthcare Professional 11 said

“it’s gonna be the childcare, the transportation, that’s gonna be what you encounter.”

Finally, both pregnant patients and healthcare professionals acknowledge that operational costs would likely be PRY-D’s biggest barrier to long-term sustainability and brainstormed ways in which PRY-D could cover these costs. Brainstormed ideas included partnering with prenatal yoga studios to use their space at no/reduced-cost, having a prenatal yoga instructor offer their time at no/reduced-cost, applying for additional grant funding/seeking philanthropic donations to cover costs and pushing for yoga to be recognized as a form of medically trained care. For example, Healthcare Professional 1 said

“if there is opportunities to have grants that would subsidize these things” and “well, get Medicaid to recognize yoga as a form of medically trained care.”

Theme 5: advertising

This theme acknowledges that advertising can impact PRY-D’s equitable implementation by facilitating or impeding coverage, adoption, and sustainability. Specifically, to create awareness (coverage) and garner enrollment (adoption and sustainability) across a diverse population of pregnant patients, PRY-D’s advertising needs to be inclusive and highlight the safety of prenatal yoga. Moreover, pregnant patients and healthcare professionals agreed that recommendations for PRY-D would do best coming directly from providers, such as during a prenatal appointment or psychotherapy session. For example:

“I feel like hearing it from your providers should be the first place. Because honestly, people are really precautionary, be it your first pregnancy, or your third pregnancy. Everybody’s pregnancy is different. And some of us take more precautions than others. So, I would feel more comfortable getting it from my provider than I would just anybody else to be honest” [Patient 2].

Additional suggestions related to advertising PRY-D included advertising more generally in doctor’s offices/clinics, advertising in “mommy” groups on social media, and in local yoga studios. Example comments include:

“maybe even offering it through social media” [Healthcare Professional 9], and “I think you should connect with ‘Detroit moms.’ It’s a Detroit mom blog. They are a great Facebook community and resource” [Patient 1].

However, it was also noted that if enrolling diverse pregnant patients is a priority, PRY-D could advertise in areas where diverse pregnant patients live, play, and work. Example comment:

“I’m Chaldean, and I belong to [Charity Name] and they are a 501c3 that actually does have, they do some stuff with perinatal, prenatal women, teaching them how to eat better, exercise and everything, so maybe connecting to something like that so that you could get those people involved” [Healthcare Professional 9].

Finally, when trying to advertise PRY-D to providers, advertising during grand rounds and department meetings, presenting to residents, and holding open information sessions about the program were frequently suggested. For example, Healthcare Professional 7 said

“consider doing a grand rounds presentation, because that’s all of the Women’s Health providers, our residents, will know about it and be able to really talk to our patients.”

Discussion

We prospectively examined equity-focused barriers and facilitators of implementing a prenatal yoga intervention to prevent PPD for a high-risk patient population within a large, integrated healthcare system. Pregnant patients and healthcare professionals provided valuable insights on the potential strengths and challenges of implementing PRY-D. Pregnant patients provided their perspectives on PRY-D following an abbreviated session and referenced relevant experiences in their current or previous pregnancies. Healthcare professionals’ responses were informed by their personal experiences or knowledge of yoga and mindfulness, clinical insights related to their work with this patient population, and familiarity with the structural aspects of working in the healthcare system. Both pregnant patients and healthcare professionals identified barriers that were consistent with common hurdles to perinatal mental healthcare engagement more broadly (i.e., time or financial constraints, limited awareness of the availability or utility of certain services) [6, 7], as well as barriers and facilitators that were unique to PRY-D. Facilitators and barriers were grouped into 5 themes aligned with EquIR outcomes: (1) knowledge, beliefs, or attitudes; (2) logistics; (3) content and instructor; (4) cost; and (5) advertising. While some responses related to logistics and advertising were specific to the healthcare system where this study is taking place, most responses could be applicable to other healthcare systems or settings. Findings illustrate potential strategies for improving the equity-focused implementation of PRY-D in a health system, which may serve as a valuable preventive option for individuals at risk for PPD.

Among both pregnant patients and healthcare professionals, a common barrier to intervention uptake included reservations about safety, or limited buy-in, regarding the potential benefits of prenatal yoga for PPD. These are consistent with some of the barriers that have been identified for traditional perinatal mental healthcare services [6]. However, in contrast to traditional services, PRY-D is a physical practice, and there were unique concerns related to safety and physical limitations. Safety concerns, in addition to relevant physical changes and associated limitations during pregnancy (e.g., fatigue, discomfort), have been described as barriers to exercise among pregnant patients in previous studies [30, 31]. These concerns may be heightened in pregnant patients who are unfamiliar with yoga [9, 23, 32]. Thus, pregnant patients and healthcare professionals indicated that it would be essential to ensure that PRY-D is safe and appropriate at various stages of pregnancy, that modifications are provided to accommodate various skill levels and pregnancy-specific changes, and that this is communicated during patient recruitment. Relatedly, some patients indicated that they would prefer to receive a referral or information about PRY-D from their medical provider - given their expertise, familiarity with their pregnancy, and any associated risks or contraindications that may be relevant for them personally. This may be particularly important for patients with high-risk pregnancies or a history of fertility challenges—as safety concerns were especially relevant to this subpopulation, and for some were related to their providers’ recommendations [33]. Further, some patients and providers had reservations about the capacity for prenatal yoga alone to sufficiently reduce the risk for PPD. However, many acknowledged the value of mindfulness and prenatal yoga during pregnancy, and this was an important facilitator to adoption and acceptability. Future findings on the effectiveness of PRY-D as a standalone or adjunctive intervention will further inform the clinical scope (i.e., potential benefits or limitations for preventing PPD) and how it will be described to patients during the referral process. Thus, receiving education on the empirical support, benefits, safety, and appropriateness of PRY-D for both healthcare professionals and patients may minimize knowledge or belief-related barriers to intervention uptake.

Logistical constraints (i.e., time, travel, and costs) were identified as potential barriers to participating in the 8-session group-based PRY-D intervention—consistent with barriers to yoga participation identified in prior work [34]. Thus, a hybrid format with both in-person and virtual sessions was identified to potentially facilitate intervention uptake and greater feasibility. Having an initial in-person session with the remaining sessions held virtually was perceived to be most accommodating for various participants’ schedules. The first in-person session would allow for program materials (e.g., yoga mat) to be distributed and the option for more detailed/personalized feedback on poses from the instructor while fostering a sense of community among participants and the yoga instructor. Finally, acknowledging competing demands of time as a barrier, may lead to future iterations of PRY-D to consider recorded or make-up sessions available for participants who may need to miss a scheduled virtual session.

In contrast to traditional perinatal mental healthcare, there were also unique considerations related to the location and space of the first in-person yoga session. Identifying a space in an accessible, central location, spacious enough to comfortably accommodate several participants for the movement-based practice, and having a relaxing atmosphere (e.g., soft lighting) were all thought to be important features. Virtual sessions were perceived to minimize the barriers of transportation, parking, and travel time for 7 of the 8 sessions. Virtually-delivered yoga and health behavior interventions are shown to be acceptable, feasible, and beneficial and demonstrate promise for delivery within a health system as a time-saving and cost-effective alternative to in person interventions [34, 35]. However, identified drawbacks of the virtual sessions included the potential for greater distractions at home (e.g., interruptions from kids and difficulty finding a quiet space) and decreased sense of community with the other participants. Given that some may prefer in-person yoga due to opportunities for pose corrections from the facilitator, distractions at home, or greater connection with other participants [36], having the initial session in person may provide a foundation for some of these components (i.e., the opportunity to receive technical feedback or support and opportunity for establishing rapport with the facilitator and other participants). If, after the randomized controlled trial, some participants identify a preference for more in-person sessions, a hybrid option for all sessions may be considered (i.e., the option to participate either virtually or in-person will be provided based on preference and sessions will be set up to accommodate both options accordingly). This may minimize safety concerns or support those who would prefer greater feedback or more detailed instruction on poses throughout the program [34, 36].

Regarding the duration and pacing of sessions, while 1 session per week seemed appropriate, many expressed the importance of the sessions being 60 min or less for increased feasibility. Although longer session times (i.e., 150 min) and intervention periods (i.e., 12 weeks) may be more effective for improving certain outcomes, programs with weekly 60-minute sessions may still be efficacious and may be more feasible for some, particularly for marginalized patient populations [32, 34, 37]. An additional barrier to uptake and acceptability of PRY-D identified by pregnant participants was lack of comfort and familiarity with prenatal yoga or beliefs about who does yoga. Some described the potential for feeling out of place, due to their perceived fitness ability or limited diversity reflected in the group. While mental health stigma did not emerge as a barrier to participating in PRY-D, perceptions about the potential for limited inclusivity were relevant. Emphasizing that PRY-D is designed to be accessible to those of varying backgrounds, fitness levels or experience, body types, etc. and that modifications are provided in the sessions to accommodate this individual variability would be important during patient recruitment and in program advertisements (e.g., brochures and posters). Additionally, efforts toward increased representation and efforts to promote inclusivity throughout the program would likely be important to retention, as noted by both patients and healthcare professionals.The importance of increased accessibility, representation, and cultural inclusivity within mindfulness and yoga classes has also been acknowledged among a multigenerational sample of Black/African American women [32].

Regarding paying program costs per session (~$25 or less) was noted as preferrable to paying a larger upfront fee, particularly as many may not be able to attend every session. Additionally, the option to participate virtually in most sessions may reduce costs of attending sessions in person (e.g., transportation, parking, and childcare) [6, 34, 37]. These barriers were noted to be particularly salient from marginalized or at-risk populations from both patients and healthcare professionals, consistent with prior work among these populations [6, 37]. When advertising the program, patients indicated that receiving information about the program from a medical provider knowledgeable of their pregnancy or a trusted referral source would be particularly important. This would also be relevant if they had certain physical contraindications that would merit approval from their provider. While the importance of personalized, contextually appropriate, and evidence-based prenatal physical activity advice has been identified [38–40], healthcare providers have acknowledged several barriers to incorporating this in routine patient care (i.e., insufficient time, inadequate knowledge or relevant training, and lack of appropriate resources) [38]. Thus, while several providers had familiarity with the benefits of prenatal yoga and were comfortable recommending this to patients, ensuring that all providers are informed of the benefits and potential contraindications for prenatal yoga through specific training, education, and resources may be needed to facilitate effective and personalized recommendations/patient referrals to PRY-D [38, 40].

Limitations

There are limitations to this study that may be addressed in future research. Although we recruited patients at high-risk for PPD, specifically those with a history of depression, greater representation from other marginalized or at-risk groups could be prioritized in future work. Importantly, more detailed demographic information, such as income and education, should be collected. Additionally, pregnant patients and health professionals were recruited from a single metropolitan healthcare system in the Midwest United States. While findings may have implications for similar healthcare systems, expanding this research to other healthcare systems is needed for greater generalizability. Future studies may consider using a specialized qualitative software to manage the data and coding, this may further strengthen transparency, reproducibility, and auditability of the findings. Although the Synthesized Member Checking process [28] was a strength employed in the data analysis, only a third of participants chose to provide feedback, raising the risk of participation bias. Future studies may consider tying compensation to the member checking process or using Dialogic Member Checking approach [41], which is more robust but time-intensive. Lastly, pregnant patient participants only participated in one abbreviated session from PRY-D. Thus, patient focus groups and feedback on barriers and facilitators may be more comprehensive after participating in the full, 8-session intervention.

Conclusions

The current study adds to the literature by identifying unique barriers and facilitators to engaging in a prenatal yoga intervention, a potentially valuable alternative to traditional mental healthcare to support perinatal mental health and reduce the risk for PPD. Information gleaned from these focus groups and interviews will inform modifications to the intervention and the development of implementation strategies to optimize the equity-focused implementation of PRY-D. Findings may also inform related intervention studies, particularly prenatal yoga, other alternative intervention options, or equity-focused programs intended to reduce the risk of PPD for at-risk patients within healthcare systems. Ultimately, this study represents an important step towards optimizing the equitable implementation of a prenatal yoga intervention to prevent PPD for those at risk. Future work will test the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of the intervention to prevent PPD.
