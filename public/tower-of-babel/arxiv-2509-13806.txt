Metastable transition times of the 1D dynamical sine-Gordon model
Petri Laarne

Abstract
We study the dynamics of a stochastic heat equation with γ ​ sin ⁡ ( β ​ u ) \gamma\sin(\beta u) nonlinearity on one-dimensional torus. We show an Eyring–Kramers law for the jump rate between potential wells in the small-noise limit, and that the transition state undergoes a bifurcation at γ ​ β = 1 \gamma\beta=1 . The argument follows the potential-theoretic approach of Berglund and Gentz [Electron. J. Probab. 2013]. Introduction

We study the
dynamical sine-Gordon equation

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=-\gamma\sin(\beta u)+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\xi,

(1.1)

where
\gamma,\beta>0
are fixed parameters,

\xi
is space-time white noise,
and the strength of the noise
\varepsilon
is taken to be very small.
The spatial domain is the one-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}
, which we fix to have length
2\pi
.

When
\varepsilon=0
, the equation has stable stationary points
at constant solutions
u\equiv 2\pi k/\beta
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The stochastic forcing turns these potential minima
metastable
:
a solution will spend a long time near
2\pi k/\beta
,
but will occasionally jump to a neighbourhood of
2\pi(k\pm 1)/\beta
.

We will answer the following two questions in this article.

Question 1.

How often, on average, does a solution to (
1.1
)
jump from the neighbourhood of one minimum
2\pi k/\beta
to another?

Question 2.

What does the solution look like in the middle of a transition?

The first question goes back to the works of Eyring and Kramers on chemical reaction rates

[
19
,
31
]
;
see the survey
[
28
]
for more on the history.
The so-called
Eyring–Kramers law
states that the transition rate
is proportional to
\exp(-h/\varepsilon)
,
where
h
is the depth of the potential well.

There have been several mathematical approaches to proving the Eyring–Kramers law,
e.g. the large deviations theory of Freĭdlin and Wentzell
[
23
]
.
We discuss it in
??
.
Our main argument however builds on the potential-theoretic approach of Bovier, Eckhoff, Gayrard, and Klein

[
11
,
12
]
,
which provides a sharp subexponential prefactor.
We outline it in
??
.

The potential-theoretic method assumes a finite-dimensional diffusion,
but an approximation argument for stochastic PDEs
was developed by Berglund and Gentz
[
8
]
.
There they study a double-well model given by a fourth-order potential,
with either Neumann or periodic boundary conditions.
As the cosine potential underlying (
1.1
) has similar locally quadratic behaviour,
the present argument is a minor modification to theirs.

In other lines of development, the recent article
[
1
]

rephrases the potential-theoretic method in terms of function-theoretic quantities,
but the results are not directly applicable to our case.
There are also spectral approaches to metastability,
as the expected transition time is related to the spectral gap of the generator of the diffusion
(see e.g.
[
12
]
).

The second question can be answered with tools from Hamiltonian mechanics
and the deterministic version of (
1.1
).
As the likelihood of a transition is exponentially related to the potential barrier height,
a typical transition passes by the
unstable
stationary solution of minimal energy.
Such solutions are called
transition states
or
instantons
.

The result derived in
??
can be summarized as:

•

When
\gamma\beta<1
, the transition state between
2k\pi/\beta
and
2(k+1)\pi/\beta

is the constant function
(2k+1)\pi/\beta
.

•

When
\gamma\beta>1
, the transition states are kink–antikink pairs
given by an explicit formula, unique up to translation in
x
.

The sine-Gordon equation becomes much more interesting in two spatial dimensions.
There the space-time white noise is so irregular that equation (
1.1
)
does not make sense as is.
The nonlinearity must be renormalized, and the relevant equation is instead the
\delta\to 0
limit of

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=-\gamma_{\delta}\sin(\beta u)+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\xi_{\delta},

(1.2)

where
\xi_{\delta}
is a suitably mollified noise
and
\gamma_{\delta}\propto\delta^{-C\varepsilon\beta^{2}}
as
\delta\to 0

(see e.g.
[
26
, Theorem 1.1]
for precise definitions).
This equation arises in mathematical physics (e.g. in relation to Coulomb gas),
and is an interesting test case for renormalization techniques due to the non-polynomial nonlinearity.
We refer to
[
14
,
15
,
26
,
25
]

for more background.

A renormalized double-well model, with nonlinearity of form
-u^{3}+\varepsilon C_{\delta}u
,
was studied by Berglund, Di Gesù, and Weber
[
6
]
;
see also
[
3
]
.
There the divergent
C_{\delta}u\to\infty u
term compensates for the vanishing product
in the prefactor (
1.6
).
Even though the potential wells are formally infinitely far apart,
the dynamics of the equation still matches the deterministic model.

In our case the renormalization is multiplicative and not additive.
It is an interesting open question whether the renormalized model can still be analyzed in a similar way.
In particular the dependence on
\beta
would be interesting to understand,
as the 2D wellposedness theory also features
\beta

The literature on the 2D double-well model is currently
restricted to the case where a constant function is the transition state.
The non-constant case is also an interesting open problem.

Stochastic and deterministic equation

2.1
Spectral Galerkin approximation

Let us first introduce some notation.
Since the functions are real-valued,
it is natural to equip
L^{2}
with the sine-cosine basis

e_{0}(x)=1,\;e_{-n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin(nx),\;e_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cos(nx)\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(2.1)

Here
e_{0}
is unnormalized so that
u(x)\equiv\pm 2\pi/\beta
corresponds to
\hat{u}(0)=\pm 2\pi/\beta
.
The Parseval identity then takes the form

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{u}(n)e_{n}(x)\bigg)^{\!2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=2\pi\hat{u}(0)^{2}+\sum_{n\neq 0}\hat{u}(n)^{2}.

(2.2)

We denote by
H^{s}
and
\mathcal{C}^{s}
the Besov spaces

B^{s}_{2,2}
and
B^{s}_{\infty,\infty}
respectively;
see e.g.
[
3
, Section 2.2]
for their definitions.

We will use the following notation for decomposition of functions into mean and oscillatory parts:

u=\bar{u}+u_{\perp},\text{ where }\bar{u}(x)=\hat{u}(0)\text{ and }u_{\perp}(x)=\sum_{n\neq 0}\hat{u}(n)e_{n}(x).

(2.3)

The operator
\projN
truncates the Fourier series to
\left|n\right|\leq N
,
and
\projNperp=\operatorname{Id}-\projN
.
Additionally,
\projperp u=u_{\perp}
in the above notation.

In
??
we will implicitly truncate all Fourier series to
\left|n\right|\leq N
.
We then identify a function

with its Fourier coefficients
\hat{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N+1}
.

Let us then consider the wellposedness and approximation of the sine-Gordon equation.

2.2
Galerkin approximation.

Consider the solutions
u
to (
1.1
) and
u^{(N)}
to (
1.3
),
where both have Fourier-truncated initial data
u_{0}=\projN u_{0}
and the same realization of the noise.
Then for any
\kappa>0
and
0\leq\alpha<1/2-\kappa

\E\left\|u(t)-u^{(N)}(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim N^{\alpha-1/2+\kappa}C(t),

uniformly in
N
, where
C(t)
is continuous and increasing in
t
.

Proof.

By the definition of mild solution,

u(t)-u^{(N)}(t)=-\gamma\int_{0}^{t}\!e^{(t-s)\Delta}\!\left[\sin(u(s))-\projN\sin(u^{(N)}(s))\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\projNperp(\mathcal{O}_{t}),

(2.4)

where
e^{t\Delta}
is the heat semigroup
and
\mathcal{O}_{t}=(e^{\cdot\Delta}\ast\xi)(t)
is the stochastic convolution of the white noise.
The linear
e^{t\Delta}u_{0}
terms are cancelled out.

We can then use Besov embeddings and Bernstein’s theorem to estimate

\displaystyle\left\|u(t)-u^{(N)}(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\projN e^{(t-s)\Delta}[\sin(u(s))-\sin(u^{(N)}(s))]\right\|_{H^{\alpha+1/2+\kappa}}\!\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+N^{\alpha-1/2+\kappa}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\projNperp e^{(t-s)\Delta}\sin(u(s))\right\|_{H^{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(2.5)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}N^{\alpha-1/2+\kappa}\left\|\mathcal{O}_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1/2-\kappa}}.

The heat semigroup satisfies

\|{e^{t\Delta}\phi}\|_{H^{r}}\leq(1+C_{r}t^{-r/2})\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{2}}

for any
r\geq 0
(see e.g.
[
5
, Lemma 3.3.3]
).
Using this and Lipschitz continuity of sine we get

\displaystyle\left\|u(t)-u^{(N)}(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}(1+(t-s)^{-\alpha/2-1/4-\kappa/2})\left\|u(s)-u^{(N)}(s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+N^{\alpha-1/2+\kappa}\int_{0}^{t}(1+(t-s)^{-1/2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(2.6)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}N^{\alpha-1/2+\kappa}\left\|\mathcal{O}_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1/2-\kappa}}.

Then Grönwall’s inequality implies

\begin{split}\left\|u(t)-u^{(N)}(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}}&\lesssim N^{\alpha-1/2+\kappa}[C(t)+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}\left\|\mathcal{O}_{t}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1/2-\kappa}}]\\
&\qquad\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{t}(1+(t-s)^{-\alpha/2-1/4-\kappa/2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\!.\end{split}

(2.7)

The expectation of the
\left\|\mathcal{O}_{t}\right\|
term is bounded by
parabolic Hölder regularity of the stochastic convolution

[
5
, Corollary 3.3.11]
,
so we get the claim.
∎

The potential-theoretic approach requires us to consider the topology of the energy landscape.

Let

3
Estimating transition times

3.1
Potential-theoretic approach

As mentioned in the introduction, the exponential asymptotics of transition times
were first studied by Freĭdlin and Wentzell
[
23
]
.
We present a brief summary of their method in
??
,
as we use it to bootstrap a better estimate in the
\gamma\beta>1
case.

The potential-theoretic approach of Bovier, Eckhoff, Gayrard, and Klein
[
11
]

is based on computing the ratio of two quantities:
the
L^{1}
integral of the equilibrium potential and the capacity.
Roughly put, the first quantity sees the geometry of the potential wells,
and the second quantity the geometry near the saddle.

3.1
Equilibrium potential.

Given two disjoint sets
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

h_{X,Y}(z)=\Prob_{z}(\tau_{X}<\tau_{Y}),

where
\tau_{X}
and
\tau_{Y}
are the first hitting times of sets
X
and
Y
respectively
under the diffusion started at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3.2
Capacity.

The capacity of two disjoint sets
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined in three equivalent ways
(see
[
1
, Lemma 2.4]
):

\displaystyle\capa(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\inf_{g}\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla g(z)\right|^{2}\exp(-F(z)/\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla h_{X,Y}(z)\right|^{2}\exp(-F(z)/\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial X}\exp(-F(z)/\varepsilon)e_{X,Y}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z).

On the first line the infimum is taken over
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

g|_{X}\geq 1
and
g|_{Y}=0
.
The measure
e_{X,Y}
on
\partial X
is the
equilibrium measure
.

Hitting times of reversible Markov diffusions can be expressed as elliptic PDE problems,
which in turn can be analyzed with potential theory.
A good summary of the derivation is given in
[
8
, Section 3.1]
.
The hitting time
\tau_{A}
of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies the relation

\int_{A^{c}}h_{B,A}(z)\exp(-F(z)/\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=-\int_{\partial B}[\E^{z}\tau_{A}]\exp(-F(z)/\varepsilon)e_{B,A}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z).

(3.1)

In the method of
[
11
]
,
one then uses the Harnack inequality to estimate the right-hand side by

[\E^{x}\tau_{A}]\capa(A,B)
, when
B
is an
\varepsilon
-ball around
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The Harnack inequality depends on the dimension,
and hence cannot be used for uniform-in-
N
bounds.
Berglund and Gentz
[
8
]
instead consider initial data from the measure
that comes from normalizing the right-hand side.
This leads to the following statement:

3.3
Expected transition time.

\E_{\mu}\tau_{A}=\frac{\int_{A^{c}}h_{B,A}(z)\exp(-F(z)/\varepsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}{\capa(A,B)},

(3.2)

where
\E_{\mu}
is over the probability measure
\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)\propto\exp(-F(z))e_{B,A}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z)

supported on
\partial B
.
