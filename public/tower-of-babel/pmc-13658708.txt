Narrative Review of Two-Drug Regimens in Modern HIV Care: Driving Innovation, Flexibility, and Quality of Life
Authors: Lucia Taramasso, Antonio Di Biagio, Simona Di Giambenedetto, Vincenzo Esposito, Roberta Gagliardini, Giovanni Guaraldi, Camilla Muccini, Diego Ripamonti, Roberto Rossotti, Stefano Rusconi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment goals in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) care have expanded beyond virological suppression to encompass long-term tolerability, treatment satisfaction, adherence, quality of life and person-centred care. Within this evolving framework, two-drug regimens (2DRs) with oral and long-acting drugs have emerged as an important therapeutic strategy, demonstrating high virological efficacy in selected clinical settings, while reducing cumulative drug exposure and expanding treatment options. Dolutegravir/lamivudine, dolutegravir/rilpivirine and long-acting cabotegravir/rilpivirine are the regimens studied the most, supported by randomised trials and real-world evidence. Beyond efficacy, 2DRs can improve flexibility of care, support adherence, reduce treatment burden and positively affect patient-reported outcomes. Long-acting injectable strategies may be particularly valuable for individuals who face challenges with daily oral adherence or treatment-related stigma. This narrative review summarizes current evidence on the role of oral and injectable 2DRs in key domains including innovation, flexibility, tolerability, adherence and quality of life, while discussing current limitations and future perspectives.Keywords: Dolutegravir, Flexibility, Innovation, Long-acting, Quality of life, Two-drug regimen

Key Summary Points

Evidence from randomised clinical trials and real-world studies consistently shows that oral and long-acting injectable two-drug regimens (2DRs), maintain high rates of virological suppression with low rates of virological failure and treatment-emergent resistance in appropriately selected people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).The evolution of 2DRs demonstrates that effective HIV management can be achieved while reducing cumulative antiretroviral drug exposure, supporting long-term treatment optimization without compromising virological efficacy.Oral and injectable 2DRs provide complementary therapeutic options that increase treatment flexibility, allowing care to be tailored to individual clinical characteristics, adherence needs and personal preferences.Long-acting injectable regimens may offer particular benefits for individuals facing challenges with daily oral therapy, including adherence difficulties, treatment fatigue and HIV-related stigma, while improving treatment satisfaction and patient-reported outcomes.Treatment selection should integrate virological and resistance history, comorbidities, individual preferences and the capacity of the clinical service to support safe long-term delivery.Future research should further evaluate the long-term effectiveness, safety, patient-reported outcomes and implementation of 2DR strategies in broader and more diverse populations to support increasingly personalized HIV care.

Introduction

The advent of modern antiretroviral therapies (ART) has brought the life expectancy of people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (PWH) closer to that of the general population [1]. Concurrently, the clinical focus has shifted from controlling viral replication to encompassing broader goals such as overall well-being, quality of life, reducing long-term adverse effects and simplifying therapy.

Two-drug regimens (2DRs) were developed within this evolving framework. Rather than representing a simple reduction in the number of agents, 2DRs reflect a more individualised therapeutic approach enabled by potent antiretroviral compounds with favourable pharmacokinetic profiles and high resistance barriers.

Among currently available strategies, dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC) and long-acting cabotegravir + rilpivirine (LA CAB + RPV) are the most studied. In randomised clinical trials and real-world studies, these regimens have shown high efficacy in selected populations, with additional potential benefits in treatment satisfaction, convenience and patient-reported outcomes (PROs).

This narrative review critically examines the current role of oral and injectable 2DRs in modern HIV care, focusing on innovation, flexibility, tolerability, adherence and the perspective of PWH according to PROsEvidence is considered according to regimen, clinical setting, population and outcome, with explicit attention to areas in which support is strong, evidence remains preliminary or implementation introduces additional risks and service requirements.

Methods

This narrative review was informed by a structured search of PubMed for English-language publications available through March 2026. Search terms included ‘two-drug regimen’, ‘dual therapy’, ‘dolutegravir/lamivudine’, ‘dolutegravir/rilpivirine’, ‘cabotegravir plus rilpivirine’, ‘long-acting antiretroviral therapy’, ‘injectable antiretroviral therapy’, ‘treatment satisfaction’, ‘patient-reported outcomes’ and ‘quality of life’, used alone and in relevant combinations. Reference lists of key trials, systematic reviews, international guidelines and selected review articles were also examined to identify additional studies. Publicly available abstracts from major international HIV conferences held in 2025 and 2026 were considered when they reported clinically relevant findings not yet available as full publications; conference-derived evidence is identified as such in the text and was interpreted cautiously.

Eligible evidence included randomised clinical trials, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, implementation studies and systematic reviews evaluating currently approved two-drug regimens on the basis of dolutegravir/lamivudine, dolutegravir/rilpivirine or long-acting cabotegravir plus rilpivirine. Studies were selected according to their relevance to virological efficacy, safety and tolerability, flexibility of use, adherence, implementation, treatment satisfaction, treatment preference, stigma or health-related quality of life. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance by the authors according to their assigned thematic sections. Priority was given to pivotal randomised trials, longer-term follow-up, systematic reviews, large or clinically informative real-world cohorts and studies addressing populations underrepresented in registration trials. Because this was a narrative rather than a systematic review, study selection was not based on a prespecified protocol, duplicate screening or a formal risk-of-bias tool, and no quantitative evidence grading was performed. The strength of evidence was interpreted qualitatively according to study design, sample size, consistency across studies, duration of follow-up, relevance of the population and whether findings were derived from peer-reviewed publications or conference abstracts. Authors drafted assigned sections, after which the complete manuscript was centrally harmonised to remove duplication, standardise terminology and ensure that evidence was interpreted consistently across sections.

Innovation

In recent decades, HIV has evolved from a rapidly fatal disease to a manageable chronic condition, thanks to innovations in ART. The first ART was approved for the treatment of AIDS in 1987, and since then other drugs have become available, although they required complex treatment that involved multiple pills a day, had high toxicity and poor tolerability [2]. From 1995 onwards, Highly Active Anti-Retroviral Therapy (HAART) began, and thereafter ART gradually shifted towards more effective, simpler and better tolerated regimens, until reaching the possibility of once-daily single-tablet regimens (STR) [2]. The first STRs to become available were made up of three drugs in a single pill, with different combinations becoming available in subsequent years, until the advent of STR comprising second-generation integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs). Owing to their high efficacy, combined with excellent tolerability and the convenience of a simple dosing regimen, modern STRs have allowed PWH to reach virological suppression in most cases [3]. However, modern innovation goes beyond viral suppression alone, to address the needs of PWH with an holistic approach [4]. In this context, it may be useful to differentiate between incremental innovation and process innovation. Incremental innovation refers to therapeutic advances that improve established treatment goals, such as efficacy, safety, convenience and long-term tolerability. Process innovation refers to the adoption of new, more effective and efficient methods for delivering therapies, moving away from rigid, syndrome-specific protocols toward flexible, tailored and technology-enabled strategies.

The development of 2DRs, particularly those based on second-generation INSTIs, represents a clear example of an incremental innovation. These regimens maintain high virological efficacy while reducing overall drug exposure, and may help address selected unmet needs, including treatment simplification, lower cumulative toxicity and achievement of good quality of life [5]. On the same line, the introduction of LA CAB + RPV in 2021 provided incremental benefits such as reduced pill burden and maintained efficacy. Long-acting strategies additionally represent a process innovation, because they modify how treatment is administered, monitored and experienced. This first complete LA injectable regimen not only demonstrated high efficacy and empowered people to choose their preferred treatment route [6], but also fulfilled some of the challenges related to adherence, stigma and daily reminder of HIV, with a positive impact on overall quality of life. [7–11].

Thus, modern ART can be started with a single pill a day. In the absence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) coinfection or resistance-associated mutations (RAMs), this could be a 2DR with DTG/3TC from day one. This 2DR regimen has been shown to be as effective as a three-drug regimen (3DR) for first-line therapy and to have a low risk of emerging treatment resistances in the rare cases of virological failures [12, 13]. The following chronic maintenance therapy can be oral, with different combinations of 2DR with high efficacy in the long-term, such as DTG/3TC or DTG/RPV[14–17], but also injectable, with efficacy demonstrated in various contexts of early and late switch and of different levels of adherence and access to care [10, 11, 18–24]. Oral and long-acting 2DR regimens now represent two complementary approaches, allowing treatment to be tailored to the needs of PWH. Future research will focus on 2DR and long-acting regimens in various forms and dosing schedules, further expanding the available options and improving convenience. This will enable innovation in therapy management, with a holistic approach aligned with the concept of healthy ageing while receiving chronic treatment.

Flexibility

Flexibility means offering treatments that can be adapted to the different needs of PWH or to their changing needs at different stages of life, from the moment of diagnosis onwards. These include first-line therapy, early switching and optimisation of regimens after years of treatment when people age and new comorbidities or needs may occur.

At present, the only 2DR that is recommended as first-line treatment is the STR with DTG/3TC which proved to be non-inferior to DTG-based 3DR in the large randomised GEMINI trials [12]. The principal prospective studies supporting DTG/3TC across first-line and rapid-start settings are summarised in Table 1. Such a regimen has also been studied in ART-naive people with low CD4 cell counts or high viremia at the time of diagnosis. In fact, in the open-label, multicentre, randomised DOLCE study, its efficacy has been compared with DTG associated with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) and lamivudine or emtricitabine (XTC) in ART-naive people with CD4 cell counts ≤ 200 cells/mm3 [13]. Among 152 participants randomised to receive DTG/3TC, the median CD4 cell count was 109 cells/mm3 (interquartile range [IQR]: 49–177) and the median HIV RNA was 180,000 copies/mL (IQR: 53,309–468,691), with 31.4% of them classified as being in Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) stage C. At week 48, the frequency of HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL was comparable between the DTG/3TC (82.2%) and the DTG/TDF/XTC arm (80.5%). The per protocol analysis further showed a response rate of 91.9% among those who received 2DR [13]. The same strategy has also been evaluated in a retrospective study comparing first-line treatment with DTG/3TC with bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (B/F/TAF) in 259 participants enrolled in 15 sites in Germany, Spain and Portugal [25]. After the matching procedure, 62 people per group were evaluated, with a mean baseline CD4 cell count of 121 cells/mm3 (standard deviation [SD] 65), including 21% in CDC stage C. At week 48, HIV RNA was < 50 copies/mL in 96.2% and 91.8% of people on 2DR and 3DR, respectively (p = 0.244), without significant differences in discontinuation rates between the two study arms [25].

The efficacy of DTG/3TC in people with very high HIV RNA levels at diagnosis has also been examined in two retrospective studies. The multicentre DOLAM 500 study, conducted in 23 HIV centres in Türkiye, enrolled ART-naive people with baseline HIV RNA levels exceeding 500,000 copies/mL. After 96 weeks, data were available for 83 participants, of whom 91.6% achieved HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL, including 93.0% of those with CD4 cell count < 200 cells/mm3. Furthermore, an Italian single‑centre study that included 108 people with baseline HIV RNA levels between 100,000 and 500,000 copies/mL showed that HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL was achieved after 12 months in more than 95% of the participants, with comparable rates in those treated with DTG/3TC and those receiving a 3DR [26].

Finally, the 2DR strategy with DTG/3TC has also been studied in the challenging scenario of ART-naive people starting treatment without available results of the resistance test. The STAT study for the first time approached the hypothesis of a test-and-treat approach with DTG/3TC [27]. In this study, DTG/3TC was started before baseline test results became available and was later modified when evidence of DTG or 3TC resistance, HBV coinfection or creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min per 1.73 m2 emerged. At week 48, 97% of 110 participants with available data achieved HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL, including 17 of 19 (89%) participants with initial HIV RNA ≥ 500,000 copies/mL, and 9 of 9 (100%) with baseline HIV RNA > 1,000,000 copies/mL. In total, five participants had chronic HBV coinfection and required ART modification; of the three individuals with available HBV resistance testing, no treatment‑emergent HBV resistance was detected. Furthermore, the only participant with baseline resistance to M184V achieved suppression of HIV RNA despite the presence of this mutation, and when ART was modified according to the resistance test results, there was no evidence of treatment emergent mutations. Treatment-emergent mutations were not detected in any study participant, including two cases of confirmed virological failure [27]. A high proportion of HIV RNA suppression has also been achieved in 214 ART-naive participants without available resistance test in the randomised D2ARLING trial, in which 106 received DTG/3TC and 108 DTG/TDF/XTC [28]. At week 48, HIV RNA < 50 copies/mL was achieved in 92% of the participants in the 2DR group and 89% of those in 3DR, showing non-inferiority of DTG/3TC. None of the participants had virological failure and none developed treatment-emergent resistance associated mutations (RAMs) to any of the study drugs [28].

Shifting the focus from first-line therapy to optimisation strategies, several real-world experiences have been reported from different countries and some even in the presence of historical resistance to lamivudine as summarised in a systematic review and meta-analysis [29]. Five observational and five interventional studies were identified for analysis, and common- and random-effects models were used to estimate the rates of virological failure. In this review, no treatment-emergent resistance mutations were reported at failure. For those with historical M184V/I, the proportion virological failure at 48 weeks was 0.03 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.01, 0.06) in observational studies, while the common-effects models of randomised clinical trials estimated zero observed virological failures [29].

Despite the evidence of oral 2DR efficacy in diverse populations, the greatest expression of the flexibility of 2DR in the modern era is the possibility to choose between oral and injectable regimens. LA CAB + RPV, administered intramuscularly every 8 weeks, is currently recommended as switch strategy in virologically suppressed PWH without HBV infection and without resistance to CAB or RPV. Many real-world studies demonstrated high efficacy of LA CAB + RPV and adherence to injections, as well as low rates of virological failure, risk of emergent resistance and treatment discontinuation.

A recent meta-analysis that included 1,269 participants in eight real-world studies, the estimated proportion of virological suppression was 93.4% (95% CI 88.7–96.9), the proportion of adherence to injections was 95.1% (95% CI 89.3–98.7) and the proportion of discontinuation for any reason or for injection-site reactions was 7.3% (95% CI 4.4–10.9) and 2.6% (95% CI 1.5–3.9), respectively [30]. The same meta‑analysis estimated the proportion of virological failure to be 1.3%, while virological failure associated with resistance was observed in approximately 0.6% of participants [30].

LA CAB + RPV has also been studied as early switch, soon after the achievement of virological suppression with DTG/3TC. The VOLITION study enrolled 171 participants who, after achieving HIV RNA below 50 copies/mL, were given the option (defined the day of choice) to continue with oral treatment or to switch to LA CAB + RPV[11]. The median time to suppression with DTG/3TC was 4.1 (95% CI 4.1–4.3) weeks, and then 89% of the participants chose to switch to LA. At month 11, high rates of virological suppression were observed after the early switch to LA CAB + RPV, with 88% (n = 113/129) of the participants maintaining virological suppression, according to the modified Snapshotand 95% (n = 113/119) among participants with available virological data in the window, highlighting the feasibility of an early transition from oral to injectable therapy [11].

Evidence from real-world studies also shows high effectiveness in different populations, including people with a long history of ART exposure before switching to LA [24], and older individuals [23].

The possibility of using LA CAB + RPV was explored even in the absence of historical genotype in the RELATIVITY study, from Spain [31]. In this cohort, 1682 out of 3146 participants (53%) did not have available genotypic resistance testing (GRT). Compared with participants with genotypic data, those without available genotyping had a lower viral load at diagnosis, a longer duration of ART and a more prolonged period of virological suppression before starting LA treatment. After a median follow-up of 13 months in the group without genotype, the observed rate of virological failure was 0.5%, with no difference compared with that of people with available GRT (1%, p = 0.476). This approach allowed people on long-term ART who had maintained virological suppression since resistance testing became standard practice, as well as those who came from centres where resistance testing was still unavailable owing to cost or organisational issues, to be included [32]. Also, other initial real‑world experiences suggest that the use of LA CAB + RPV may be feasible in individuals without historical GRT. In these cases, proviral GRT performed at the time of the switch, combined with other clinical and treatment‑related characteristics, can help stratify the risk of virological failure. This approach could reduce the a priori exclusion of individuals with incomplete historical data and warrants further investigation [33–35].

The LA option has also been explored in the context of extensive resistance to NNRTI, such as the African regions. In the randomised CARES trial, participants without available resistance testing, but with a complete history of known ART and no previous failure during exposure to NNRTI or INSTI classes, were enrolled in eight clinical research sites in Uganda, Kenya and South Africa [19]. The trial demonstrated non-inferior long-term efficacy of LA treatment in this setting up to 96 weeks, compared with oral ART, with maintenance of virological suppression in 97% of participants in both arms, despite retrospective evaluation of GRT in proviral DNA that showed a frequency of RAMs archived at baseline for RPV and CAB as high as 8% and 4% [19].

Tolerability

To achieve a long-term success of ART [36], it is crucial that an ART regimen is well tolerated [36]. Recurrent adverse events (AEs), even if not severe, and an unfavourable cumulative metabolic burden can undermine adherence and lead to repeated treatment changes. In this context, the aim of 2DRs is to reduce drug exposure while maintaining high efficacy, with the potential to decrease cumulative toxicity and drug interactions over time. This is particularly relevant because older PWH may develop non-AIDS-related comorbidities associated with premature ageing [37], experience comorbidities more frequently than the general population and consequently be exposed to a higher number of concomitant medications [38]. 2DR regimens can be a strategy for reducing potential drug interactions because they limit the number of molecules to which a person is exposed; in the case of injectable 2DR, they also eliminate first-pass metabolism through parenteral administration, thus avoiding drug-drug interactions related to oral absorption [33]. Furthermore, 2DR, particularly DTG/3TC and LA CAB + RPV, have demonstrated favourable safety and tolerability profiles in clinical trials and real-world studies.

In the GEMINI-1 and -2 trials, in ART-naive people initiating first-line ART, fewer drug-related AEs were observed with DTG/3TC versus DTG plus TDF/FTC throughout week 144 (relative risk 0.76; 95% CI 0.63–0.92)[39]. In switch studies, DTG/3TC also showed a favourable tolerability profile, despite the fact that a higher rate of AE was reported when the comparator was a regimen that had already been established and was clearly based on the clinical needs and tolerability history of the individual participant [14, 16]. By contrast, a tendency towards a lower burden of AEs with 2DR was observed in studies in which both treatment arms switched to new regimens, either 2DR or 3DR. In PASO DOBLE, grade 3–4 adverse events were reported in 1% of participants in DTG/3TC compared with 4% of those receiving 3DR, while drug-related AEs occurred in 7% and 10% of participants in the two arms, respectively, after 48 weeks, and AEs leading to DTG/3TC discontinuation were very rare (< 1%)[17]. Discontinuations due to AE were consistently low in clinical trials, usually below 1–1.5% in switch studies [14, 16, 17, 40], and, in real-life studies, 2DR based on DTG has been reported to have a lower risk of discontinuation due to AE with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.41 (95% CI 0.22–0.77) compared with DTG-3DR [40]. A recently published meta-analysis considered 43 real-world studies with consistent and comparable outcomes, representing a total of 1480 ART-naive individuals and 12,234 ART-experienced individuals who started a 2DR with DTG/3TC [41]. In this systematic review of the literature the estimated rates of virological suppression at 48 weeks were 0.96 (95% CI 0.94–0.98) and 0.97 (95% CI 0.95–0.98) in ART-naive and -experienced participants, while discontinuation for any reason was 0.05 (95% CI 0.02–0.09) and 0.13 (95% CI 0.08–0.18), respectively [41]. Discontinuation of DTG/3TC treatment appeared to be driven by sporadic and nonspecific adverse events rather than by a consistent toxicity profile, reinforcing the favourable tolerability of this regimen.

From a cardiometabolic perspective, DTG/3TC has also shown good tolerability, without any effect on blood lipids and no increase in total or LDL cholesterol compared with other regimens in clinical trials [42] and in real-world studies, where it also showed minimal impact on renal function and bone health markers [43].

In addition, weight gain has become a relevant metabolic consideration in recent years with modern ART, including DTG-containing regimens [44, 45]. In treatment-naive populations treated with DTG/3TC, the mean weight increase reached + 3.7 kg at week 144 in GEMINI trial [39], while weight change in switch studies such as TANGO and SALSA appeared to be comparable to that of other modern ART therapies [14, 16].

However, in the PASO-DOBLE study, where both arms switched to a new regimen, the mean adjusted weight gain at 96 weeks was higher with B/F/TAF (2.35 kg; 95% CI 1.61–3.09) versus DTG/3TC (0.84 kg; 95% CI 0.09–1.60) [difference: 1·52 kg; 95% CI 0·74–2·29] and more participants experienced a clinically significant (> 5%) weight increase on B/F/TAF (n = 96, 34·8%) versus DTG/3TC (n = 55, 20·1%) (adjusted odds ratio (OR) 1.90; 95% CI 1.25–2.88),[46] although this has not been confirmed in other studies [47].

Similarly, also the first fully injectable 2DR regimen with LA CAB + RPV has demonstrated a favourable tolerability profile, with no safety signals identified in pivotal trials and real-world cohorts [48]. The most common AEs reported in the course of LA treatment were injection-site reactions (ISRs) [7]. ISRs were generally mild in clinical trials, had a mean duration of about 3 days, were well tolerated and decreased in impact over time, and with an adequate injection technique [49]. Despite the appearance of ISRs, the acceptability of LA CAB + RPV injections was very high, as determined by the perception of injection (PIN) questionnaire, which assesses the experience with injectable therapies, including acceptance of pain, ISRs, and tolerability following injections [50]. Furthermore, the acceptability of the injections showed an upward trend over time. In SOLAR, after 12 months, the LA group reported significant improvements in treatment satisfaction and psychological outcomes (less anxiety about adherence, fear of disclosure and status reminders), and 90% of respondents preferred the LA formulation, compared with 5% who preferred the oral formulation[10]. However, ISRs remain the first cause of discontinuation of LA treatment in real-world studies, suggesting that people’s preferences could also be strongly influenced by mild ISRs and that perceptions of this kind of AE can differ from those of clinicians, requiring customised counselling and follow-up [51–53].

Excluding ISRs, other reasons for discontinuation of LA CAB + RPV were generally infrequent and not typically associated with significant safety concerns. Discontinuations were primarily attributed to systemic adverse events not related to intramuscular administration, including headache, pyrexia, fatigue, myalgia, dizziness rash or central nervous system (CNS) symptoms such as depressive disorder or insomnia, although these events were reported in very limited numbers [48]. Renal and bone safety outcomes are also reassuring, with stable or improved biomarkers and no clinically significant toxicities observed over 12 months [7, 53].

Additional causes of discontinuation included events not necessarily related to treatment, such as acute infections or intercurrent conditions, consistent with observations from registration trials, in which no new safety signals emerged and most serious events were not considered drug-related [48]. In real-world settings, discontinuations have also been influenced by nonclinical factors, such as people’s preferences, convenience, treatment logistics or loss to follow-up, [48, 52, 53] emphasising that individual factors, not just virological ones, must always be taken into account when selecting a treatment regimen to maximise adherence and durability.

In addition, the metabolic impact of LA CAB + RPV has been studied, and the metabolic effects appeared neutral, without significant changes in weight, lipid parameters, or insulin resistance across multiple studies [48, 54, 55]. The amount of weight gain reported in clinical trials was minimal, approximately 1–2 kg in 48–96 weeks, and comparable with that of oral ART [48]. Additionally, a reduced incidence of metabolic syndrome has been reported in PWH after switching to LA compared with their previous regimen (aHR 0.03; 95% CI 0.009–0.10)[56]. This observation is also in line with other observational studies that reported an improvement in HDL cholesterol during exposure to LA CAB + RPV, although the results were not univocal in other observational studies and in clinical trials [48, 55, 57, 58]. Overall, the metabolic data are reassuring and confirm the safety profile of LA CAB + RPV from a metabolic perspective, confirming that it can also be used by people considered at increased cardiovascular or cardiometabolic risk.

In conclusion, both oral and injectable 2DR strategies maintain a low incidence of adverse events, representing an important approach to reducing long-term toxicity in PWH, with high tolerability and a neutral metabolic profile, favouring the potential to support adherence and persistence. L

Adherence

Adherence to treatment includes starting HIV treatment, keeping all medical appointments and taking HIV medicines exactly as prescribed [59]. However, many people who are in chronic treatment, including people on ART, may experience suboptimal adherence throughout their life [60]. This phenomenon may result in a lack of virological control during the course of ART if treatment adherence falls below a certain threshold [61–63]. Although cases of suboptimal adherence can remain undetected, it has been suggested that, even in PWH with undetectable viral load, incomplete adherence can be associated with adverse immunological and clinical outcomes that go beyond viral control, such as residual inflammation, persistent immune activation, coagulopathy, progression of coronary stenosis and increased non-cardiovascular mortality [64, 65]. Interventions to promote adherence remain key to the health of PWH and should include counselling beyond ART education, including approaches to mental health and well-being [66]. From a more strictly ART-related point of view, multiple data suggest that adherence to single-tablet regimens is better than that to past multitablet regimens [7, 9, 67]. This includes high adherence to 2DRs such as DTG/3TC, which is likely to reflect the regimen simplicity, as well as its good tolerability and manageability [63]. However, because adherence challenges vary between individuals, ART selection may require a change in personalized treatment approach, with long‑acting injectable therapies representing a novel and effective alternative to daily oral dosing. LA treatment allows observed, scheduled adherence very close to 100%, linked to administration in a clinical setting. In selected cases, this can translate into a concrete reduction in the risk associated with incomplete adherence [20, 33]. Both clinical trials and real-world studies demonstrate that LA CAB + RPV therapy ensures high levels of adherence across various populations and settings, including those who had previously struggled to maintain adherence to therapy [21, 22]. In the open-label, randomised trial LATITUDE, people with inadequate adherence to ART (persistent HIV RNA level of > 200 copies/mL or loss of follow-up) received adherence support, conditional economic incentives and standard care with oral ART. If, after these measures, participants had an HIV RNA of 200 copies/mL or less, they were randomised to continue standard care or switch to monthly CAB + RPV [21, 22]. Overall, 306 participants were randomly assigned, of which 152 were selected to receive CAB + RPV. In this study, monthly injections of LA treatment were superior to standard oral ART in reducing the risk of regimen failure, with a cumulative incidence of regimen failure at week 48 of 22.8% in the CAB + RPV group and 41.2% in the oral ART group (difference, −18.4 percentage points; 98.4% CI −32.4 to −4.3).

Another piece of evidence comes from the large US ABOVE study, in which data have been retrospectively extracted from the administrative claims database to evaluate adherence to different types of ART. Adherence to treatment was estimated as the percentage of PWH with ≥ 0.9 days covered over 12 months. A total of 442,091 PWH were identified from January 2020 to August 2023. People who switched to LA CAB + RPV showed higher adherence (72% versus 43%) and longer persistence (424 [IQR 201–537] versus 393 [IQR 174–431] days), compared with those who remained on oral therapy, with 8 times higher odds of being adherent compared with the oral ART cohort (adjusted odds ratio (OR), 8.06; 95% CI 6.62–9.81) [68]. The potential of LA treatment to improve treatment adherence also results in high viral efficacy in vulnerable populations. In the JABS study, 60% of the participants had one or more complexity or vulnerability factors, including lack of social support (50%), mental health issues and alcohol or drug use (30%), and financial hardship or instability (13%). Despite this, 53 of the 54 participants (98%) who completed 12 months of follow‑up achieved virological suppression [18]. Similarly, data from the WARD 86 clinic, a publicly funded academic HIV clinic in San Francisco serving PWH with high levels of psychosocial and economic vulnerability, showed that, in PWH initiating LA CAB + RPV with initial viremia, 48-week viral suppression was reached in 92% [69]. Encouraging data has also been published on another population facing a key adherence challenge, namely adolescents living with HIV, including those with vertical transmission and an extensive treatment history. In the IMPAACT 2017 (MOCHA) phase I/II clinical trial, 144 virologically suppressed adolescents (12 to < 18 years) switched to LA CAB + RPV every 8 weeks; 137/144 (95%) completed week 96. No confirmed virological failure (two consecutive viral loads ≥ 200 copies/mL) occurred through week 96, indicating sustained viral suppression, and strong participant preference was observed [70, 71]. Among people who started LA CAB + RPV in the CoRISpe Spanish pediatric cohort and in its adult extension (n = 26), all achieved and maintained virological suppression after 11 months of follow-up [72]. Similarly, 100% engagement in care and viral suppression was achieved in the 25 adolescents enrolled in the Children’s National Hospital cohort in Washington, DC, during the 11.8-month study follow-up period in LA CAB + RPV [73].

The issue of initiating long‑acting CAB + RPV in individuals with viremia – often in the context of incomplete treatment adherence – has also been addressed in the multicountry IMPALA randomised clinical trial, conducted in Uganda, Kenya and South Africa. The study enrolled 540 participants with recent HIV RNA levels > 1000 copies/mL and/or poor engagement in care [74]. In this trial, LA CAB + RPV was not only non‑inferior to oral DTG‑based ART in terms of efficacy, but a confirmed virological failure (CVF) in sensitivity analysis demonstrated the superiority of CAB + RPV compared with oral DTG (CVF 2.6% [7/268] versus 6.7% [18/269]; risk difference − 4.1%; 95% CI − 7.6 to − 0.6). Consequently, international guidelines state now that injectable LA treatment can be considered for people with viremia who cannot take oral ART consistently, are at high risk for HIV disease progression, and do not have resistance to CAB or RPV [20, 75].

Taken together, this evidence in people with adherence challenges and poor engagement in care may suggest that a person-centred approach may be key to improving adherence, also relying on the new innovative 2DR and offering LA option even to people with extensive treatment history and nonadherence to oral treatment.

Patient Reported Outcomes (PROs) in People Treated with 2DR

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has become an important goal in HIV management alongside virological suppression and resistance prevention [76]. HRQoL refers to the perceived effects of health and treatment on physical, psychological and social functioning. Systematically collecting patient-reported outcomes (PROs), particularly related to quality of life, is now key to support therapeutic decisions and customise follow-up, to take into account people’s different needs while on treatment, with the final aim of improving treatment strategies and HRQoL. [8–10, 16, 77–81].A PRO is any report on the status of a person’s health condition that comes directly from the person, without the interpretation of the response by a clinician or anyone else. PROs can investigate multiple domains, such as treatment satisfaction, convenience, preference, acceptability, pill burden, perceived stigma and HRQoL. All these aspects may influence one another, but investigate independent domains that address different needs. PRO data can be used in a multitude of ways to improve care and outcomes at the individual, institutional, and population levels [82]. At the individual level, there is good evidence that routine use of PROs such as quality of life and self-reported symptoms can improve clinical decision making, symptom recognition, communication between people with HIV and clinicians and help clinicians identify and address quality-of-life-related problems [36, 51]. When used at the institutional level, PROs enable comparisons of provider performance to stimulate improvements in service. At the population level, PROs can identify health disparities between different population groups and monitor the health system response to HIV[76]. PROs have been evaluated in studies of both oral [16, 77, 79] and long-acting injectable 2DRs [7, 11, 19, 83], with outcomes interpreted according to the specific domain and instrument assessed.

The PAIRED study used validated questionnaires to assess treatment satisfaction, adherence, and HRQoL in people who had started DTG/3TC treatment after a therapeutic change with suppressed viral load [77]. A total of 474 PWH, diverse in terms of gender, sexual orientation, age, ethnicity, education level, region of residence and employment, completed the survey. Treatment satisfaction, adherence, and quality of life scores were high, with 83% of the participants saying that DTG/3TC therapy had a more positive impact on their overall health than 3DR therapy. A subgroup of participants also agreed to qualitative interviews, and reported that the use of fewer drugs was perceived as a treatment giving a lower risk of long-term exposure toxicity, maintaining efficacy and tolerability [77].

The phase 3 SALSA study demonstrated the non-inferiority of the 2DR switch strategy compared with continue the current three- or four-drug regimen (CAR) [16].In this study,participants in the DTG/3TC group (n = 246) and the CAR group (n = 247) reported comparable score in the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire for HIV (HIVTSQ), at baseline, with respective means of 55.2 (SD 6.5) and 55.8 (SD 5.5). From week 4 onward, the mean HIVTSQs scores increased further in the DTG/3TC group compared with the CAR group. These increases were maintained through week 48. Moreover, also the Symptom Distress Module (HIV-DSM) was evaluated in this study, with baseline scores comparable between the 2DR and CAR groups, at 9.0 (SD 9.9) and 7.9 (SD 9.3), respectively. Small improvements in the SDM score were observed at weeks 4 and 24, and were maintained at week 48, although they did not reach statistical significance, with similar results in people older than and under 50 years of age [78].

The PROBI study also aimed to evaluate PROs after switching from standard multidrug therapy to DTG/3TC in adults. This was achieved by administering questionnaires on perceived toxicity, treatment acceptability, and its impact on HRQoL.

A total of 260 PWH on DTG/3TC therapy were enrolled. Significant improvements were observed in scores related to the impact of therapy on quality of life, acceptability of treatment, and the number of self-reported symptoms 1 and 6 months after starting DTG/3TC therapy [79].

There is also evidence for LA CAB + RPV, which suggests treatment satisfaction, preference and convenience, as well as a reduced perceived treatment burden.. In the phase 3 ATLAS study, 616 virologically suppressed PWH were randomly selected to take LA CAB + RPV or continue CAR. [84]. Similarly, in the phase 3 FLAIR study, people receiving first-line abacavir/3TC/DTG treatment who achieved an HIV RNA level of less than 50 copies/mL at week 16 were randomly assigned to continue CAR (n = 283) or switch to LA CAB + RPV (n = 283) [85]. Treatment preference, measured by HIVTSDs scores, was analysed by combining the results of the FLAIR and ATLAS studies [8]. On a maximum scale of 66 points, the mean baseline score was 57.1 (SD 8.4) for the CAR (oral control) group and 57.1 (SD 8.6) for the LA group. The combined data at W24 and W44 showed a statistically significant greater increase in treatment satisfaction in the LA group compared with the CAR group. This difference was mainly owing to the ATLAS results, in which participants receiving LA treatment showed a large, stable increase in mean score compared with the CAR group: 5.4 (95% CI 4.2–6.6) at week 24 and 5.7 (95% CI 4.4–7.0) at week 44. Using an approach based on the distribution of individual values, these differences at both time points were considered clinically significant, as the difference between the two groups exceeded half of the baseline standard deviation. Changes from baseline were more pronounced for the LA group for all variables in the HIVSTQs questionnaire, particularly with regard to questions about overall satisfaction, ease, convenience and flexibility. No differences were observed in the ATLAS or FLAIR studies with regard to the SF-12 questionnaire (quality of life), from a psychological/mental or physical point of view. In both studies, baseline values were higher than the US national average of 50, with no significant changes observed in any domain at week 48 [8].

The CARES study was conducted at eight clinical centres in Africa and enrolled 512 participants with virological suppression.. In this trial, people randomised to LA treatment had non-inferior virological efficacy compared with CAR, and at week 96, more than 99% of them stated that they preferred LA CAB + RPV to previous oral therapy. The mean HIVTSQs score was 27.1, which was significantly higher (p < 0.0001) than that of the oral control group (17.9), despite frequent injection-site reactions [19].

The CUSTOMIZE study evaluated the implementation of LA CAB + RPV therapy in eight different types of HIV clinics in the USA. It also investigated the satisfaction of 109 participants who received this treatment. One year after starting the regimen, 74% of the respondents reported no interference of any kind with LA therapy, and 92% said they preferred LA therapy to their previous daily oral therapy and wanted to continue with it (97%) [83].

In the phase 3b SOLAR randomised trial, conducted in 118 centres in 14 different countries, PWH with viral loads < 50 copies/mL were randomised to receive LA CAB + RPV (n = 447) or to continue oral therapy with B/F/TAF (n = 223), demonstrating non-inferiority of LA CAB + RPV. [9]. PRO-based endpoints in this trial included HIVTSQs, acceptability of injections (Questionnaire on Perception of Injections) and three single questions designed to explore psychological challenges related to HIV therapy: fear of disclosure of one’s status, anxiety related to adherence and constant reminders of HIV status. At month 12, treatment satisfaction had improved significantly with LA CAB + RPV compared with B/F/TAF, increasing by an average of 3.36 points (95% CI 2.59–4.13) compared with an average decrease of 1.59 points (95% CI −2.71 to −0.47) (p < 0.0001). Participants who reported at least one psychological barrier at the beginning of the study experienced statistically and clinically significant improvements in treatment satisfaction within the LA group compared with the oral control group (adjusted difference: 7.96; 95% CI 5.65–10.26; p < 0.0001). Most (90%; 382/425) of the respondents indicated a preference for LA therapy over B/F/TAF (5%; 21/245) [10]. It should be noted that the top five reasons supporting the preference expressed by PWH were all psychosocial reasons, rather than indicating clinical variables, perception of efficacy, or tolerability factors: I do not have to worry so much about taking my medication every day; it is more convenient for me to receive an injection; I do not have to carry my medication with me; I do not have to remember my condition every day; I do not have to worry about others seeing me taking my pills [10].

The preferences of PWH have also been investigated in the context of recent ART initiation. The results of the VOLITION study described an early switch strategy to LA CAB + RPV, implemented immediately after virological suppression was achieved with DTG/3TC in ART-naive adults with HIV[11]. Overall, 151 participants enrolled in the study were offered the choice of switching to LA CAB + RPV, and 129 (85%) opted to switch. On the day they made their decision, the most common reasons for switching to LA were avoiding the worry of missing daily doses (80%), convenience in travel (68%) and a better fit to their lifestyle (64%). Treatment satisfaction improved after the day of choice and remained high throughout month 11 with LA CAB + RPV, with 89% of the participants planning to continue LA treatment after the study.

The available evidence indicates that oral and injectable 2DRs may improve treatment satisfaction and treatment preference and may reduce perceived treatment burden in selected populations. Evidence for broader improvement in HRQoL is less uniform because baseline HRQoL is often high, instruments vary, and several trials were not powered primarily for HRQoL outcomes. Routine assessment of clearly defined PRO domains may nevertheless support shared decision-making and help align treatment delivery with individual priorities.

Limitations, and Evidence Gaps

This review has several limitations. First, it was designed as a narrative review rather than a systematic review; therefore, although a structured search strategy was used, the literature search, study selection and evidence synthesis were not conducted according to a formal systematic-review protocol. As a result, relevant studies may have been missed, and the conclusions should be interpreted as a qualitative synthesis rather than as a comprehensive quantitative assessment of all available evidence.

Second, the included evidence is heterogeneous. The studies summarized in this review differ in design, population characteristics, treatment history, duration of follow-up, comparator regimens and outcome definitions. This heterogeneity limits direct comparisons across studies and precludes formal pooled estimates for most outcomes.

Third, much of the evidence on LA CAB + RPV and PROs derives from open-label clinical trials, observational cohorts, implementation studies or conference abstracts. These sources are valuable for understanding effectiveness, feasibility and patient experience in real-world settings, but they may be affected by selection bias, reporting bias, limited follow-up and incomplete outcome ascertainment. Conference abstracts in particular should be interpreted cautiously until full peer-reviewed publications become available.

Fourth, many studies enrolled selected populations, often excluding individuals with active hepatitis B virus infection, known resistance to regimen components, or complex adherence and social challenges. Although emerging data support the use of long-acting strategies in more vulnerable populations, the generalizability of some findings to all people living with HIV remains limited.

Fifth, quality of life, treatment satisfaction, adherence, and PROs were assessed using different instruments across studies and were not always primary endpoints. This variability limits the ability to compare results directly and may underestimate or overestimate the patient-perceived benefits of 2DR strategies.

Finally, long-term data remain limited for some newer treatment strategies, particularly early switch approaches, use in viremic individuals, adolescents, older adults and people with incomplete historical resistance data.

Remaining evidence gaps include longer-term resistance outcomes, effectiveness in people with ongoing viremia or unstable engagement in care, outcomes in those without complete resistance histories, scalability across resource-limited and high-volume services and comparative effects on rigorously measured HRQoL. Evidence is also less extensive in pregnancy, adolescents outside specialist programmes, people with advanced immunosuppression and individuals with complex multi-morbidity. Conclusions

Two-drug regimens are now established components of contemporary HIV care rather than simply reduced versions of three-drug therapy. DTG/3TC has a strong evidence base for first-line treatment and maintenance in appropriately selected people, whereas LA CAB + RPV expands the route and experience of treatment and may be particularly valuable when daily oral therapy is poorly aligned with an individual’s needs. Their benefits, however, are conditional on careful assessment of HBV coinfection, resistance history, previous virological outcomes, comorbidities and individual preferences. For long-acting therapy, successful implementation also requires reliable appointment attendance and healthcare services capable of managing missed injections, oral bridging, monitoring and the pharmacokinetic tail. Evidence consistently supports virological efficacy and treatment satisfaction in selected populations, while effects on HRQoL are more heterogeneous and should not be inferred from preference or convenience alone. The clinical value of 2DRs therefore lies in informed selection and shared decision-making, not in universal simplification. Future research should prioritise long-term resistance outcomes, implementation across diverse health systems, underrepresented populations and prospectively defined HRQoL endpoints.

Collectively, 2DR approaches – both oral and injectable – provide complementary and adaptable solutions that support long-term treatment success, promote well-being and reflect a paradigm shift toward holistic and personalized HIV care.
