Rates of cervical spine surgery and repeat epidural injections after cervical transforaminal epidural steroid injections for patients with cervical radiculopathy utilizing a large national database
Authors: Andrew R Stephens, Ramzi El-Hassan, Ashley Rogerson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical transforaminal epidural steroid injections (CTFESI) are commonly performed to treat cervical radicular pain and decrease the need of surgery. The literature is mixed regarding the efficacy of CTFESI in obviating the need for cervical spine surgery.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to assess the rates of cervical spine surgery and spinal interventions after initial CTFESI for patients with radicular pain.MethodsTriNetX, a large national database, was queried from 2004 to 2025 for patients who underwent CTFESI for cervical radiculopathy. The rates of treatment progression after initial CTFESI were calculated. Treatment progression included: no additional treatment, repeat CTFEI, cervical interlaminar epidural steroid injection (CIESI), and cervical spine surgery. Cervical spine surgeries analyzed included anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF), foraminotomy, and total disc replacement (TDR). Treatment rates were also calculated for patients after a second CTFESI.ResultsThere were 28,345 patients who underwent CTFESI during this study period. After initial CTFESI, 57 % of patients did not undergo any additional procedures/surgeries, 23.7 % had a repeat CTFESI, 5.6 % received a CILESI, 7.4 % underwent ACDF, 1.6 % underwent a foraminotomy, and 1.8 % underwent a TDR. Together, after initial CTFESI, 10.8 % of patients went on to cervical spine surgery as a second line treatment. Of the 6,711 patients who received a repeat CTFESI, 74.5 % had no additional interventional treatment, 8.2 % underwent a CIESI, 10.6 % underwent ACDF, 2.5 % underwent a foraminotomy, and 2.2 % underwent a TDR. In total, 15.3 % of patients underwent cervical spine surgery after a second CTFESI. A third consecutive CTFESI was not attempted in any patients.ConclusionThis study demonstrates rates of surgery after an initial CTFESI and a repeat CTFEIS to be low.Keywords: Cervical, TFESI, Epidural steroid injection, Cervical spine surgery

Introduction

Cervical transforaminal epidural steroid injections (CTFESI) are commonly used to treat cervical radicular pain for patients refractory to conservative treatments. Multiple studies have demonstrated the efficacy of CTFESI [1,2]. In addition to treating pain, CTFESIs are often employed as a means to decrease the need for spine surgery. Reported rates of surgery after CTFESI vary in the literature from approximately 5 %–40 % [3,4].

Several spine surgeries can be utilized to treat pathology for which patients may also receive a CTFESI. Common surgeries include anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF), foraminotomy, and total disc replacement (TDR). Complications of cervical spine surgeries include nerve injury [5], infection [6], accelerated adjacent disease [7], hardware failure requiring reoperation [8] and stiffness [9]. Recovery time after cervical spine surgery can take 4–12 weeks [10] with studies demonstrating that 82 % of patients return to work within 9 months [11]. Spinal cord injury has been reported as occurring in 0.2 % of patients [12]. Complications after CTFESI are very rare. Though case reports of major complications such as spinal cord injury and stroke after CTFESI exist [[13], [14], [15]] large cohort studies adhering to updated guidelines have not demonstrated these risks [[16], [17], [18]]. In a large database study, embolic stroke only occurred in 0.03 % of patients and paralysis occurred in 0.04 % of patients [19]. Understanding the rates of patients who progress to ultimate spinal surgery after CTFESI will aid physicians in discussing treatment options and prognosis after these treatments.

The objective of this study is to evaluate the percentage of patients who required cervical spine surgery or additional spine procedures after an initial CTFESI.

Material and methods

This study utilized a large database, TriNetX. TriNetX houses de-identified data of over 130 million patients across 100+ healthcare systems throughout the United States. TriNetX has previously been utilized in interventional spine research [19,20]. Given that this is a national database with de-identified data, IRB approval did not need to be obtained.

Patients who received CTFESI from January 2004–August 2025 were identified in the database using CPT code 64479 with associated ICD-10 codes for cervical radiculopathy. The “treatment pathways” analytic feature of TriNetX was utilized to determine the rates of procedures and surgeries after an initial CTFESI. The first CTFESI was selected as the index event. Subsequent treatments were selected utilizing CPT codes and included a repeat CTFESI, cervical interlaminar epidural steroid injections (CILESI; CPT 62321), ACDF (CPT 22551), posterior extradural laminotomy or foraminotomy (CPT 63045) and TDR (22856). The treatment pathways feature also calculates the proportion of patients who did not receive any additional treatments. The rates of these treatments were also calculated after a 2nd CTFESI.

Statistical analysis

Categorical demographics were listed as count and percent and age was reported as mean and standard deviation (SD). Patients who received additional treatments were reported as percent with associated 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI).

Rates of 2nd and 3rd line cervical spine surgery and injections after initial cervical transforaminal epidural steroid injections.

2nd line treatment

There were 18,883 (56.4 %, 95 % CI: 55.9 %, 56.9 %) patients who did not undergo any additional procedures/surgeries after the index CTFESI leaving 14,608 who received additional procedures/surgeries. Of these remaining patients, 8,215 (24.5 %, 95 % CI: 24.1 %, 25.0 %) underwent an additional CTFESI and 1,875 (5.6 %, 95 % CI: 5.4 %, 5.8 %) received a CILESI. After initial CTFESI, 2,366 (7.1 %, 95 % CI: 6.8 %, 7.3 %) underwent ACDF, 616 (1.8 %, 95 % CI: 1.7 %, 2.0 %) underwent a foraminotomy, and 844 (2.5 %, 95 % CI: 2.4 %, 2.7 %) underwent TDR. Together, after initial CTFESI, 3,826 (11.4 %, 95 % CI: 11.1 %, 11.8 %) of patients went on to cervical spine surgery as a 2nd line treatment.

3rd line treatment

Of the 8,215 patients who received a second CTFESI, 817 (9.9 %, 95 % CI: 9.3 %, 10.6 %) underwent ACDF, 220 (2.7 %, 95 % CI: 2.3 %, 3.0 %) underwent a foraminotomy, and 205 (2.5 %, 95 % CI: 2.2 %, 2.8 %) underwent a TDR. In total, 1,242 (15.1 %, 95 % CI: 14.3 %, 15.9 %) of patients underwent cervical spine surgery as a 3rd line treatment after a second CTFESI. There were 657 (7.9 %, 95 % CI: 7.4 %, 8.6 %) patients who received a CILESI after two attempts of CTFESI. A third consecutive CTFESI was not attempted in any patients. There were 6,316 (76.9 %, 95 % CI: 76.0 %, 77.8 %) patients who did not require any additional procedures after a repeat CTFESI. Of the 1,875 patients who underwent a CIESI as a second line treatment, 270 (14.4 %, 95 % CI: 12.8 %, 16.0 %) underwent a cervical spine surgery. All of these were an ACDF. Therefore, for the 10,090 patients who had a repeat cervical epidural steroid injection (CTFESI or CIESI) after an initial CTFESI, 1512 (15.0 %, 95 % CI: 14.3 %, 15.7 %) underwent cervical spine surgery.

Eventual surgery

Of the initial 33,491 patients who underwent an initial CTFEIS, 5,223 (15.6 %; 95 % CI: 15.2 % 16.0 %) ultimately required a cervical spine surgery. These included 3,385 (10.1 %; 95 % CI: 15.2 % 16.0 %) who had an ACDF, 994 (3.0 %, 95 % CI: 2.8 %, 3.1 %) who had a foraminotomy, and 844 (2.6 %, 95 % CI: 2.4 %, 2.7 %) who had a TDR.

Discussions

This study demonstrates that the majority of patients did not require surgery after a CTFESI for a diagnosis of cervical radiculopathy. Specifically, 84 % (95 % CI: 84.0 %, 84.8 %) of patients never had a cervical spine surgery after CTFESI.

Rates of spine surgery after CTFESI vary in the literature. Kumar and Gowda reported surgical rates to be around 6 % at 2 years [3]. Goyal et al. reported rates to be upwards of 30 % [4] and Slipman et al. reported rates as high as 40 % [21]. Both Conger et al. and Stephens et al. demonstrated that about 18 % of patients underwent a spine surgery after CTFESI [[22], [23]]. In this work, only 11 % of patients who received an initial CTFESI underwent surgery and only 15 % of patients who needed a second CTFESI underwent a cervical spine surgery. A possible explanation for lower rates may be that a greater proportion of patients underwent repeat CTFESI in this work (about 25 %). In Conger et al.’s study, only 9 % of patients had a repeat injection and in Goyal et al.’s study, only 16 % had a repeat injection. It is unknown how many of these patients went on to surgery compared to the patients who only had one injection. Rates may also be higher given that these above authors are associated with spine centers. It is likely that a large proportion of their patients are referrals from surgeons with surgical pathology and a possible surgical plan already in place. Some surgeons use CTFESI diagnostically to confirm the appropriate surgical level. These studies were also limited in their power with the largest study being Goyal et al. with a little over 200 patients included. This current work represents the largest study to date in assessing surgical rates after CTFESI with over 30,000 patients included. This study suggests that a repeat CTFESI has the potential to decrease the need of future spine surgery.

The most common surgery patients underwent was an ACDF. ACDF is a commonly performed spine surgery with over 100,000 performed annually [24]. Prior work has demonstrated ACDF to be effective in treating patients with cervical radiculopathy with high long-term success [[25], [26]]. Complications have been reported occurring in 10–18 % of patients [27]. Though many of these are minor complications, serious devastating complications such as spinal cord injury have been reported in about 0.2 % of patients [12]. Cervical spine surgery also places a significant cost on patients and the health care system. Martin et al. found the 90-day cost of ACDF to be around $40,000 [27]. After ACDF, patients may be out of work for 4–12 weeks [10]. Approximately 25 % of patients will have adjacent segment surgery after initial ACDF within 10 years [28] thus incurring greater cost and more time out of work. Given that the majority of patients in this cohort did not progress to surgery, CTFESI likely has the ability to decrease health care costs for patients with cervical radiculopathy. Prior work has demonstrated CTFESI to be cost-effective and improve quality of life in the short term compared to conservative measures [29].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Inherent with database studies, specific patient charts were unable to be reviewed. Subsequent cervical procedures and surgeries may have occurred at healthcare systems outside of the institutions that contribute to this database. These would have been missed in this work and rates of surgery are possibly higher than what was reported here. This study was unable to assess if CTFESI provided enough relief to prevent surgery from occurring in patients who otherwise would have received surgery. It does suggest, however, that a large proportion of patients treated with CTFESI will not need surgery or other treatments. This may be due to patients having sufficient pain relief to maximize greater participation in physical therapy. This work was unable to evaluate surgical candidacy of patients. There may have been patients who would have pursued surgery after CTFESI but were unable to do so given comorbidities or non-surgical pathology. Surgical rates reported in this work may not apply to certain patient populations.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that approximately 85 % of patients did not progress to surgery after CTFESI.
