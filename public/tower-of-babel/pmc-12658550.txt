Effect of first visit to different levels of medical institutions on diagnostic and treatment delays in pulmonary tuberculosis in Ningxia, China: a cross-sectional study from 2015 to 2019
Authors: Wenxuan Li, Juan Lei, Ying Zhang, Libo Liang, Xiaolin Wang, Xiaomei Tian, Wei Liu, Zehui Li, Longjia Wu, Siqi Wu, Hui Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the effect of first visit to different levels of medical institutions on diagnostic and treatment delays of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients, assess the current situation and potential problems of different-level medical institutions, and put forward constructive recommendations for policy-making to reduce the delayed behaviours among PTB patients in the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (NHAR).SettingNHAR, China.DesignWe collected information on all PTB patients in the NHAR identified through China’s National Tuberculosis Information Management System (TBIMS) between 2015 and 2019. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to create balanced data excluding the effects of covariates. The inverse probability of treatment weight was used to verify the robustness of the PSM results. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the correlation between first-visit medical institution levels and diagnostic and treatment delays.Main outcome measuresDiagnostic and treatment delays of PTB patients.ParticipantsWe collected 11 202 confirmed PTB cases from TBIMS during 2015–2019.ResultsAmong 11 202 confirmed PTB cases, 1497 (13.4%) had diagnostic delays, with a median diagnostic delay of 1 day (IQR: 0–7 days). Among 7593 PTB cases, 1056 (13.9%) had treatment delays, with a median treatment delay of 0 days (IQR: 0–1 days). After adjustment by propensity value matching, the possibility of diagnostic delay (OR=0.84, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.99) and treatment delay (OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.44) of county medical institutions was significantly higher than that of above-municipal medical institutions.ConclusionsMore effective measures should be implemented to improve rapid diagnosis and treatment technology and the capacity of county medical institutions, clarify the referral process and reduce the occurrence of delayed behaviours.

Background

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis that spreads from person to person through the air.1 Currently, PTB has returned to its status as the world’s leading cause of death, following the COVID-19 pandemic.2 In 2023, an estimated 741 000 people fell ill with PTB in China, accounting for 6.4% of the global PTB case burden.3 China has become the country with the second-highest PTB burden in the world.4

PTB control currently relies mainly on the early detection of cases and completion of DOTS-based treatment to control transmission and reduce incidence.5 It requires the timely identification of patients as suspected PTB cases and rapid referral to PTB dispensaries for diagnosis. However, delayed behaviours often hinder the aforementioned processes, lead to terrible treatment results for patients with PTB, increase the risk of PTB transmission and eventually result in a high disease burden.6

Currently, China primarily implements two approaches for the prevention and control of PTB patients: The first approach is that primary healthcare institutions identify individuals with suspected PTB symptoms through routine diagnosis, treatment and screening of high-risk populations, who are then referred to county-level medical institutions. County-level institutions are responsible for the confirmation and treatment of mild PTB cases, assuming the core role within their jurisdiction.7 And complex cases and patients suspected of drug-resistant PTB will be referred to medical institutions at the municipal level or higher for further diagnosis and management. The second approach is the ‘patient-initiated pathway’, which means patients can autonomously choose to directly seek treatment at medical institutions at any level based on their own medical condition or personal preferences. Therefore, medical institutions at the municipal level or higher also have the responsibility for the diagnosis and treatment of all types of PTB patients.810 It leads patients to choose different levels of medical institutions at their first visit, which may directly affect their diagnosis, treatment and referral processes within China’s TB prevention and control system, thereby resulting in varied delay outcomes.

But previous studies have found that lower-level first-visit institutions might lead to more delays due to poor medical conditions and lack of knowledge of PTB, leading to serious consequences for patients with PTB. However, patients who first visit higher-level medical institutions might receive better health services so that patients can be diagnosed and treated earlier.11 However, even if the levels of first-visit medical institutions are extremely crucial for the rapid identification, referral and timely treatment of patients with PTB, few studies have explored the specific relationship between first-visit medical institutions and diagnostic and treatment delays.

The Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region (NHAR) is located in the northwestern region of China, and the prevention and treatment of TB in this region still need to be improved.12 The study aims to investigate the impact of first visit to different levels of medical institutions on diagnostic and treatment delays, analyse the current situation and potential problems of different-level medical institutions, and provide meaningful suggestions for policy to improve the aforementioned two delayed behaviours of patients with PTB in the NHAR.

Methods

Data

We recruited all patients with PTB diagnosed and treated during 2015–2019 in NHAR, which were identified through China’s national TB Information Management System (TBIMS). As the ‘core database’ for TB control in China, TBIMS operates through a six-tier network spanning national, provincial, municipal, county, township and village levels, vertically integrating all TB prevention and control institutions from the central to grassroots levels. Furthermore, designated TB medical institutions are legally required to record full-course data in real time on patient diagnosis via TBIMS, thereby achieving nationwide comprehensive coverage of the entire management process and all institutions involved in PTB patient care.13 Compared with other systems, the TB management efficiency of this data management system has been significantly improved.14 The data captured in the TBIMS included patient demographics such as age, sex and nationality, reasons for seeking care, symptoms, medical history and subsequent diagnosis and treatment.

Study sample

A total of 11 506 patients with PTB were included in this study. Patients were excluded at baseline as follows: (1) missing baseline information (The level of the first-visit medical institution, timing of the first visit, definitive diagnosis and the initiation of anti-TB therapy, sex, age, nationality, occupation, floating population, patient source and key clinical characteristic variables); (2) patients aged <18 years (unlike adults, the symptoms of paediatric PTB patients are often atypical, and small sample size in this study would compromise the validity of the conclusions); (3) outliers with delay days exceeding 1 year (cases with a diagnostic and treatment delay of over 1 year were inconsistent with the study’s focus on ‘early curability’ and were also associated with a risk of systematic documentation errors). The patient selection process is illustrated in figure 1. Considering missing data, the dataset was divided into two subsets for diagnostic delay and treatment delay, respectively. Finally, 11 202 patients were eligible for diagnostic delay analysis and 7593 patients were eligible for treatment delay analysis.

Outcome measures

The diagnostic and treatment delays of patients with PTB were used as the outcome measures for this analysis. For diagnostic delay, we adopted the definition established by Chinese Ministry of Health, which specifies it as an interval exceeding 2 weeks between a patient’s first visit and the definitive diagnosis of PTB.15 For treatment delay, we followed the definition outlined in the Tuberculosis Control and Assessment Protocol, which specifies it as an interval of more than 1 week from PTB diagnosis to the initiation of anti-TB therapy.16

Explanatory variables

The primary explanatory variables in this study were the first visit to medical institution levels of patients with PTB. We divided them into county and above-municipal medical institutions (including municipal and provincial medical institutions) to explore the differences.

Covariates

Based on the literature and the TBIMS, we used 11 covariates to model the propensity of patients with PTB in different medical institutions and adjusted the models. The covariates included demographic and socioeconomic variables, such as sex,17 age,18 nationality,19 floating population20 and occupation,21 and clinical characteristic variables, such as complications,22 other forms of TB,23 sputum smear results and X-ray results.24 As the principal pathway to TB case detection, we innovatively used the patient source (patient-initiated pathway or screening pathway) as covariates.25

Statistical analyses

First, we analysed the probability of delayed behaviour and investigated the related delay factors in patients with PTB with different first-visit institutions after adding control variables using binary logistic regression.

The NHAR data from TBIMS in 2015–2019 are panel data, and the logistic regression might not be able to adequately decrease and control the bias caused by confounding variables. Propensity score matching (PSM) is a type of statistical method that uses non-experimental or observational data for intervention effect analysis. The theoretical framework for PSM is the ‘counterfactual inference model’. It is an approach to reduce the confounding bias by matching individuals according to similar propensity scores between different groups,26 then dividing the data into an observation group and a control group to achieve a balance of important covariates in addition to the explanatory variable between the two groups, and then comparing the differences in dependent variables. In this study, we established a multivariate regression model with the important covariates to calculate the propensity scores of diagnostic and treatment delays in patients with PTB. The propensity scores were defined as follows:

PSi=P(Xi) = Pi (Di=1 | Xi) =exp(βXi)1+exp(βXi)where Xi is the feature vector related to treatment and diagnostic delay; Di is the virtual variable of first-visit medical institution levels; and β is the logistic regression coefficient of Xi.

Then, we used the nearest propensity score to match patients at the county and above-municipal medical institutions with a 1:1 ratio to generate the observation and control groups. The balance test was performed by χ2 test between the above two groups, and all covariates were not significantly different between the two groups (p>0.05). Binary logistic regression was used to analyse the probability of diagnostic and treatment delays in patients with a first visit to the county and above-municipal medical institutions.

Finally, the inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTW) was used for sensitivity analyses. IPTW was calculated as the inverse of the propensity score for patients in county institutions and as the inverse of (1– propensity score) for the patients in above-municipal institutions. Then, the covariables were controlled by weighted logistic regression to exclude the influence of covariables.

PSM and IPTW were performed with the R project for statistical computing (V.4.1.2).

Patient and public involvement

This study was based on data from existing national systems. All information was anonymised, with no involvement of human experiments or biological specimens, and participants were not involved in defining research questions, developing outcome measures or designing the survey. Therefore, no participant consent was required for this study.

Results

After screening, 11 202 PTB patients had a median diagnostic delay of 1 day (IQR: 0–7 days), with 13.4% experiencing a delay of more than 2 weeks between their first visit and the confirmed PTB diagnosis. Among 7,593 PTB patients, the median treatment delay was 0 days (IQR: 0–1 days), with 13.9% experiencing a delay of more than 1 week between diagnosis and initiation of anti-TB therapy (figure 2). The characteristics of the patients with PTB and the results of the logistic regression analysis before matching are shown in table 1. Among all 11 264 patients with PTB, 7253 (64.4%) patients chose health services at county medical institutions for the first time. The analysis indicated that several factors were associated with a higher likelihood of diagnostic delay, including having positive X-ray results (p<0.01), floating population (p<0.01) and occupation involving light manual labour (p<0.01) and pure manual labour (p<0.01). Conversely, patients who first visited the above-municipal medical institutions (p<0.05), positive sputum smear (p<0.01) and patient-initiated pathway (p<0.01) were significantly associated with a lower risk of diagnosis delay. Treatment delay was more likely when the sputum smear results were positive (p<0.01), the X-ray result was positive, the patient-initiated pathway was involved, and patients were part of a floating population (p<0.01). Conversely, when patients first visited the above-municipal medical institutions, were older than 60 years (p<0.01), and had complications (p<0.05), they had a lower incidence of treatment delay.

Table 2 shows that before PSM, significant differences were observed between the county-level and above-municipal-level groups in terms of nationality, occupation, floating-population status, patient source and sputum smear results (p<0.05). After matching, the diagnostic delay cohort included 6006 patients and the treatment delay cohort comprised 4580 patients. The mean propensity scores were well-aligned between the two cohorts (0.40±0.18 for the diagnostic delay cohort; 0.41±0.15 for the treatment delay cohort), and no statistically remarkable differences existed across all baseline characteristics (p>0.10). These findings confirm the baseline covariates were successfully balanced postmatching.

According to the PSM model, the OR of diagnostic delay in patients who first visited the above-municipal medical institutions was significantly lower than that in patients who first visited the county medical institutions (OR 0.84; 95% CI 0.72 to 0.99). Similarly, compared with patients who first visited county medical institutions, the OR of treatment delay in patients who first visited above-municipal medical institutions was significantly lower (OR 0.37; 95% CI 0.32 to 0.44).

Discussion

We included all patients with PTB diagnosed and treated during 2015–2019 in NHAR. The results from PSM showed that first visit to different levels of medical institutions had significant effects on the diagnostic and treatment delays of patients with PTB. Specifically, patients with PTB who first visited county medical institutions had a greater possibility of diagnostic and treatment delays than those who visited the above-municipal medical institutions.

According to our results, the median days of diagnostic delay and treatment delay of patients with PTB were 1 day (IQR: 0–7 days) and 0 days (IQR: 0–1 days). This indicates that more than half of PTB patients were diagnosed within 1 day of symptom onset, and more than half initiated treatment immediately after diagnosis. This reflects the high efficiency of the local diagnostic process, enables patients to access standardised treatment earlier, reduces the risk of disease progression, lowers infectivity and aligns with the core principle of ‘early diagnosis and early treatment’. In the same way, these results were relatively low compared with other parts of China and some developing countries.2731 A possible explanation for our finding is that the NHAR began to implement the transformation of designated hospitals in 2015, which gave the designated medical institutions the function of diagnosis and treatment and improved the referral and tracking rate of patients with PTB.32 At the same time, the NHAR signed the third-phase cooperation agreement of the China–Ghana Tuberculosis Project in 2015, which established a multichannel financing model to effectively reduce the economic burden of treatment of patients with TB in the NHAR.33

Our results are consistent with previous studies, which noted that nationality, occupation, floating population, sputum smear results and X-ray results were associated with diagnostic delay; age, nationality, floating population, sputum smear results and X-ray results were associated with treatment delay.182034 Among these, the overlap of imaging features between X-ray-positive TB and other diseases increases the risk of misdiagnosis, while the presence of pulmonary cavities may delay sputum smear detection, both contributing to diagnostic delays. This indicates that Ningxia needs to strengthen rapid diagnostic technology to accelerate the identification of pathogens and shorten the diagnosis cycle of PTB. Furthermore, X-ray negative results cannot fully exclude PTB, thus requiring further high-sensitivity testing; its rapid feedback reduces delays caused by ‘waiting for imaging’ or ‘repeated examinations’.35 36 And although patients with positive X-ray results or with positive sputum smear results necessitate expanded diagnostics, integrating concurrent rapid drug susceptibility testing accelerates treatment initiation by furnishing critical treatment-related information at confirmation.37 Moreover, due to more classical radiological manifestations and clinicians’ heightened awareness of the ‘clinical urgency’ in elderly patients, treatment delays are further mitigated.38 39

In particular, we also found that patients on the patient-initiated pathway had a lower risk of diagnostic delay but a higher incidence of treatment delay. The main reason might be that patients on the patient-initiated pathway generally had higher health cognition, and patients with higher health cognition paid more attention to their own health level.40 Therefore, these patients were more inclined to choose higher-level medical institutions for treatment under the same circumstances, leading to a shorter diagnostic delay.41 In contrast, patients on the patient-initiated pathway tended to have a more severe disease condition and were more likely to have complications that might require additional treatment prior to anti-TB treatment, resulting in treatment delay.42

Our results showed that first visiting county medical institutions might lead to a higher incidence of diagnostic delay. Although the policy of conducting free sputum smear and chest X-ray examinations for people with suspected symptoms of PTB had been implemented in the two levels of medical institutions, a gap in the diagnostic delay still existed between different levels of medical institutions. Previous studies showed that poor medical facilities and inadequate staffing and capacity of medical staff might be the main reasons for this result.11 The M. tuberculosis rifampicin test could effectively be used to simplify patients’ access to early and accurate diagnosis, thereby potentially decreasing the morbidity associated with diagnostic delay, dropout and mistreatment.43 However, most county medical institutions in NHAR could only provide sputum smears and chest X-rays to patients with PTB, lacking technologies to accurately diagnose early.32 Furthermore, evidence showed that less than 80% of patients with symptomatic PTB attending primary institutions received PTB-related tests, and less than 25% received sputum tests, suggesting that the capacity of primary staff needs to be improved.44

Additionally, our study showed that first visiting county medical institutions was associated with a greater likelihood of treatment delay than first visiting above-municipal medical institutions. The underlying causes of this result might be the lack of treatment capacity and poor referral process efficiency in county medical institutions and poor supervision and coordination by health authorities. Most of the above-municipal medical institutions in NHAR had the ability to treat PTB, but not all the county medical institutions had the ability to treat PTB.45 As a result, patients first visiting county-level institutions might have longer treatment delays due to referrals. Poor efficiency of the referral process was also found in a PTB delay study in other regions in China.46 Meanwhile, the medical staff of the county medical institutions had an insufficient understanding of the treatment, and the referral mechanism of patients with PTB still needed to be improved. In a PTB diagnostic study in Ethiopia, its medical staff were also able to refer patients suspected of having PTB to general hospitals or keep them in the hospital for treatment.47 In addition, it was essential for the health administration to give full play to its supervisory and coordinating role. However, the current cooperation between different levels of medical institutions is not good enough, leading to treatment delay.48

In short, this study seeks to find insufficient PTB prevention and control in the NHAR and provide a basis for policy implications. To improve the health status of patients with PTB in the NHAR, the government and medical institutions should make more efforts to improve PTB diagnosis and treatment capabilities and clarify the referral process to minimise diagnostic and treatment delays, thus improving the effectiveness of PTB prevention and control.

Limitations

This study had some limitations due to the conditions, which need further improvement in follow-up research. First, the data we used were panel data, and the treatment and control groups could not be randomised in our study, so we were unable to strongly verify causal relationships. Although we used PSM to correct the deviation, there are still unobserved characteristics affecting the delays of patients with PTB that should not be considered. Second, socioeconomic status is an important covariate influencing delays. Due to limitations in the TBIMS, there is a lack of important socioeconomic indicators, such as education and income, in the research. However, occupation is a widely used variable representing social status and class, and previous studies have used occupation as the representative of socioeconomic status.49 50 Therefore, this study clarified occupations according to the proportion of mental work referring to the classification method of previous studies so that occupations have a certain ability to represent socioeconomic status.

Conclusions

Applying PSM to partly address potential selection bias issues, we found that patients with PTB who first visited the county medical institutions were more likely to have diagnostic and treatment delays than those visiting the above-municipal medical institutions. The findings of this study provide a better understanding of the differences in PTB prevention and treatment at different levels of medical institutions, suggesting that continuous efforts should be made to improve the effect of PTB prevention and control.
