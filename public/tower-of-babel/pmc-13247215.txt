Unlocking shale gas potential in the Sembar Formation through multi-analytical assessment and basin modeling, Central Indus Basin, Pakistan
Authors: Adeeb Ahmed, Tahseenullah Khan, Khairul Azlan Mustapha, Samina Jahandad, Ahmed E. Radwan, Muhammad Asif Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding source-reservoir characteristics is essential for evaluating shale gas potential, yet the Sembar Formation in the Central Indus Basin remains underexplored in this regard, which limits accurate assessment of its hydrocarbon generation potential and regional energy significance. While earlier studies have focused mainly on basic source rock evaluation, a comprehensive geochemical-mineralogical-basin modeling framework has not yet been employed, leaving significant gaps in understanding the formation’s shale gas potential. This study bridges this gap by integrating novel multi-analytical techniques, including organic and inorganic geochemistry, vibrational spectroscopy, microscopy, and basin modeling, to comprehensively assess the shale gas potential of the Sembar Formation. The results reveal total organic carbon values ranging from 0.23 to 1.76 wt%, suggesting poor to good source rock potential, with Type III kerogen indicating gas-prone characteristics further corroborated by vibrational spectroscopy showing aromatic hydrocarbon dominance. Biomarker results indicate that the Sembar shale received mixed organic matter, primarily from higher plants, along with planktonic and bacterial inputs, deposited under open marine-suboxic conditions. Basin modeling results (up to 68% transformation ratio) and vitrinite reflectance values (Ro=1.05–1.16%) confirm late-stage maturity, consistent with gas generation. The combination of high quartz content, favorable clay assemblage, and stratigraphically controlled carbonates yields a high brittleness index (0.77–0.86), indicating strong mechanical competence and reservoir stability, while nanopores and microfractures enhance gas storage capacity. In conclusion, these findings demonstrate that the Sembar shale possesses favorable source-reservoir characteristics for unconventional gas development, underscore its regional energy significance, and highlight the effectiveness of an integrated multi-analytical approach for shale-resource evaluation in underexplored basins.

Introduction

Shale source-reservoirs refer to geological formations in which shale rock serves as both the source and reservoir for hydrocarbons1. Efficient extraction of hydrocarbons from shale formations requires a comprehensive understanding of their source-reservoir characteristics2. Declining conventional hydrocarbon production and the rising global energy demand have made shale resources, also called unconventional reservoirs, a viable alternative3. Pakistan is estimated to possess approximately 3000 bcm (billion cubic meters) of shale gas reserves4, with the Lower Indus Basin (Central and Southern regions) holding 164 million barrels of oil and 24.6 trillion cubic feet of natural gas5. The Sembar, Ranikot, Ghazij, Mughal Kot, and Lower Goru Formations have been identified as key shale-bearing units5–8. Yet, despite numerous evaluations, no commercial shale gas discoveries have been achieved in Pakistan, highlighting the need for a comprehensive and integrated investigation. Without such studies, the country’s substantial unconventional resources may remain underutilized, prolonging dependence on imported energy. Shale intervals of the Sembar Formation, herein denoted as “Sembar shale,” were chosen for this study from the Zindapir-1 well located at the Zindapir anticline structure in the Central Indus Basin, Pakistan (Fig. 1a, b2). The study area was selected due to the favorable shallow depth (1410–2167 m) of the Sembar Formation at the Zindapir-1 well, which could provide economic benefits for hydraulic fracturing.

The key parameters for evaluating shale source-reservoirs include total organic carbon (TOC), maturity level, organic matter type, mineralogy and thickness9. In the context of resource evaluation, shale oil resources typically require a TOC threshold of 2 wt% to ensure sufficient hydrocarbon generation through thermal maturation10–14. However, for shale gas resources, the TOC threshold can be lowered to 1 wt% due to advancements in extraction technologies that enable efficient gas recovery from shales with lower organic content12,15. According to Zhang14, the following values are used as cut-offs for commercial shale gas development, in addition to TOC: vitrinite reflectance (Ro) greater than 1.1%, brittle minerals greater than 40%, clay minerals less than 30%, and net thickness of organic-rich shale greater than 30–50 m. Furthermore, a brittleness index (BI) greater than 0.48 is considered favorable for hydraulic fracturing16. Previous research has indicated that nanopores, along with micropores, are well-suited for retaining hydrocarbons in shale gas reservoirs17. Additionally, the types and quality of hydrocarbons produced in unconventional shale resources are ultimately controlled by variations in organic matter, which are analyzed using a biogeochemical/biomarkers approach18. Post-depositional conditions subsequently determine the extent to which organic matter is preserved and evolves into hydrocarbons13. The shale gas potential of a basin is influenced by the kerogen types in its source rocks, with Type III kerogen, derived from terrestrial plants, being a major contributor to gas generation19. While typically linked to gas production, in certain environments like delta plains, Type III kerogen can also generate minor quantities of oil20. Variations in sedimentary conditions and organic matter sources result in different hydrocarbon generation characteristics across basins21–24. Despite extensive studies on Type I and II kerogens, research on the gas-generation potential of Type III kerogen remains limited25–27. Recent findings in basins such as Xihu and Lishui Sags highlight its significant shale gas potential28–30. Prior investigations have mainly targeted the southern Indus Basin through conventional screening analyses such as TOC, Rock-Eval, and Ro31–38. One-dimensional (1D) maturity modeling lacks robustness unless validated with laboratory-based Ro/borehole-temperature data39–41. Biomarker results suggest predominantly terrestrial organic input and peak maturity for Sembar Formation42,43, yet research on the Zindapir-1 well remains limited to basic parameters, with little focus on mineralogy, brittleness, or pore-structure evaluation44–46. The mineralogical composition of the Sembar Formation, particularly clay content and brittleness index, remains understudied44. Although studies from the southern Indus Basin have provided valuable groundwork, they reveal a significant gap in comprehensive evaluations, particularly in the Zindapir anticline region of the Central Indus Basin, which remains largely underexplored. Accurate shale gas assessment requires integrated analyses encompassing hydrocarbon screening, basin modeling, mineralogical and porosity characterization47, yet most existing works are limited to preliminary screening without biomarker, vibrational spectroscopic, and detailed mineralogical data. Furthermore, the Zindapir-1 well was drilled and later abandoned, suggesting that while structural traps exist, hydrocarbon migration or generation may have been insufficient45,46. In summary, earlier investigations provided valuable preliminary insights but were constrained by the lack of integrated geochemical, mineralogical, and basin modeling approaches and by insufficient data validation. This study explicitly addresses these limitations through a unified, multi-analytical workflow designed to reassess the shale gas potential of the Sembar Formation.

The present study hypothesizes that the Sembar Formation at the Zindapir-1 well possesses significant shale gas potential that can be effectively evaluated using a multi-analytical approach. This work integrates organic and inorganic geochemistry, vibrational spectroscopy, microscopy, and 1D basin modeling to characterize source-reservoir properties comprehensively. For this purpose, the current study aims to assess the shale gas potential of the Sembar Formation by achieving the following objectives: (a) to assess organic matter richness, kerogen type, thermal maturity, and petroleum generation potential; (b) to analyze source input and paleoenvironmental depositional settings; (c) to model the timing of hydrocarbon generation and kerogen transformation through 1D basin modeling; and (d) to evaluate detailed mineralogy, including brittleness, and assess porosity in the Sembar shale. Unlike previous studies, this research employs integrated techniques such as TOC and Rock-Eval pyrolysis analysis, vitrinite reflectance, 1D modeling, vibrational spectroscopy, bulk and clay X-ray diffraction (XRD) mineralogy, and FESEM-EDS analyses. Since core data was unavailable, the study uses well-cuttings, providing significant insights into the source-reservoir characteristics and shale gas potential. By adopting this novel multi-analytical approach, the study not only enhances understanding of the shale gas potential of the Sembar Formation but also demonstrates the effectiveness of an integrated methodological framework for future unconventional resource exploration in the Central Indus Basin, Pakistan.

Geological setting

The major sedimentary basins in Pakistan are Indus and Balochistan. The Indus Basin is subdivided into two distinct basins: the Upper and Lower Indus basins48. According to Kadri48, the Lower Indus Basin is divided into the Central and Southern Indus basins. The Central Indus Basin (study area) lies between two regional structural highs, the Sargodha High to the north and the Mari-Kandhkot and Jacobabad Khairpur highs to the south49. This basin is characterized by two types of structures: a passive roof sequence in the Sulaiman Fold Belt composed of large anticlines and domes, and a gently dipping sequence in the Punjab Monocline, where tectonic folds and faults are relatively scarce49. There are three broad tectonic units within the Central Indus Basin: (1) the Punjab Platform, (2) the Sulaiman Depression, (3) the Sulaiman Fold Belt (Fig. 1a)50. These units are bordered to the east by the Indian Shield and to the west by folded mountain belts. Punjab Platform (with no sedimentary outcrop) is the broad monocline dipping gently toward the Sulaiman Depression, marking the eastern part of the Central Indus Basin48. Sulaiman Depression provides complete stratigraphy in the Central Indus Basin. The Sulaiman Range in the Central Indus Basin includes the Sakhi Sarwar, Zindapir, and Fort Munro folds, where Neogene molasse, Paleocene, and Cretaceous strata occur in areas of structural culmination. In the Central Indus Basin, the Sulaiman Depression is considered a hydrocarbon kitchen that charges nearby anticlines and other structural traps45,51.

Zindapir anticline

The Zindapir anticline is part of the Zindapir anticlinorium, which extends across the Sulaiman Range52. The Zindapir anticline represents the southernmost and longest segment (over 60 km) with width of ~ 14 km. Paleocene rocks are exposed at the core of the Zindapir anticline, and the Zindapir-1 well was drilled through these rocks to reach Triassic strata at a total depth of 4406 m. The formation of Zindapir structure as an anticlinal stack can be seen in the structural cross-sections (Fig. 1b)52. The Zindapir anticlinal stack consists of two duplex horses bounded between a roof thrust in the Cretaceous Sembar shale and a floor thrust above the basement at a depth of approximately 10 km (Fig. 1b2).

Zindapir anticline experienced a total shortening of 17 km and generally exhibits flat molasse strata that follow a flat-ramp-flat pattern. Approximately 4 km of pre-Paleocene succession was eroded in the Zindapir anticline, leading to the exposure of Paleocene strata at the surface (Fig. 1b1, b2)52,53. Figure 1c compares the generalized stratigraphy adopted from the Sulaiman Range with the stratigraphy encountered in the Zindapir-1 well.

In Zindapir-1 well, the oldest rocks are shale and siltstone of the Triassic Alozai Formation, which are overlain by limestone and marl from the Jurassic Loralai Formation (Fig. 1c). The Jurassic Chiltan Limestone conformably overlies the Loralai Formation. An unconformity exists between the Sembar Formation and the Chiltan Limestone. The Early Cretaceous Sembar Formation is 757 m thick, with a top burial depth of 1410 m and a bottom burial depth of 2167 m. The dominant lithology of the Sembar Formation at Zindapir-1 consists of shale and sandstone with interbedded siltstone. The Sembar Formation is overlain by the Goru Formation, followed by the Parh and Mughal Kot Formations. The Pab Sandstone is marked by an unconformity at its base, with the Paleocene Ranikot Formation unconformably overlying it. The Paleocene Dungan Formation conformably overlies the Ranikot Formation and represents the uppermost unit encountered in the borehole stratigraphy of the Zindapir-1 well (Fig. 1c).

Methodology

The study involved the analysis of 28 samples obtained from the Zindapir-1 well in the Central Indus Basin, Pakistan. A multi-analytical approach was employed, involving four key types of analyses: organic geochemistry, inorganic geochemistry, microscopic examination, and vibrational (FTIR) spectroscopy. The workflow depicting the integrated multi-analytical approach used in this study is shown in Fig. 2. A combination of screening analyses and basin modeling was employed to assess organic matter (OM) richness, kerogen type, and thermal maturity. Biomarker analysis provided insights into depositional environments and organic matter sources, X-ray diffraction (XRD) determined mineralogy and brittleness index, microscopic analyses characterized pore morphology, and FTIR evaluated shale and hydrocarbon composition at the molecular level. These integrated analyses, following standard protocols, were used to evaluate source-reservoir characteristics and petroleum generation potential, as depicted in Fig. 2. Organic geochemical analysis

The total organic carbon (TOC) content of the 28 samples (burial depths: 1425–1955 m) was analyzed after washing, hand-picking, and pulverizing the samples to a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. TOC analysis (~ 100 mg per sample) was performed using a LECO CS-300 after treatment with dilute HCl to remove inorganic carbon. The same samples (~ 65 mg) were subsequently analyzed using Rock-Eval 6, and selected samples were analyzed with Rock-Eval 7, to determine S1 (free hydrocarbons), S2 (pyrolyzable hydrocarbons), and Tₘₐₓ (temperature of maximum S2 generation). Derived parameters included the hydrogen index (HI = S2/TOC × 100), oxygen index (OI = S3/TOC × 100), production index (PI = S1/S1 + S2), and genetic potential (GP = S1 + S2).

Five samples were selected for vitrinite reflectance (Ro) measurements. Well cuttings were ground (1–2 mm), embedded in Serifix resin, and polished using successive grades of silicon carbide papers (320, 800, 1200 grit), followed by alumina (1 μm, 0.3 μm) and a colloidal silica suspension. Measurements and organic petrography were performed on oil-immersion polished blocks using a LEICA DM6000 M microscope and DISKUS Fossil software.

Extraction and biomarker analysis

Seven powdered samples (3–5 g each) were extracted for 72 h using a Soxhlet apparatus with a 93:7 (v/v) dichloromethane-methanol solvent mixture. One sample (1900–1905 m) was analyzed at HDIP, and six were analyzed at the University of Malaya. The extractable organic matter (EOM) was separated using a 150 mm chromatography column (¾ silica + ¼ alumina). Aliphatic and selected aromatic hydrocarbons (1 µL) were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC) and GC-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) under the following conditions: the oven was held at 40 °C for 1.5 min, ramped to 300 °C at 4 °C/min, then held for 5 min. Multiple-ion detection mode was used to observe biomarker ions, and peak heights were used to calculate ratios and parameters.

FTIR analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was subsequently performed on three shale samples using a PerkinElmer Spectrum 400 spectrometer equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory in transmittance mode, covering the wavenumber range of 550–4000 cm−1. Approximately 0.2 mg of powdered sample was pressed onto the diamond surface using an attached press. Each spectrum was acquired using four scans with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ using a DTGS (deuterated triglycine sulfate) detector under ambient temperature conditions (20 ± 2 °C). The spectra were processed using PerkinElmer Spectrum 6.1 software for baseline correction, smoothing, and peak integration.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

For bulk-rock mineralogy, 10 powdered samples (2–3 g) were analyzed using a Malvern Panalytical Empyrean XRD instrument at the X-Ray Lab, University of Malaya. For clay separation, powdered samples were dispersed in centrifuge tubes (3 mL) with Calgon and distilled water, sonicated for 15 min, and the clay fraction was then pipetted onto glass slides. Slides were air-dried, glycolated (80 °C overnight), and heated to 400 °C and 550 °C (30–40 min each) before final scanning. Mineral identification was carried out using X’Pert HighScore Plus v3.0.4 software, and quantitative Rietveld refinement was performed using clinker, default, and size-strain models to achieve the best peak matches.

Microscopy (FESEM-EDS)

Two samples (~ 1 × 1 cm) were examined using a Hitachi SU8220 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) equipped with an Oxford EDS system at the Chemistry Department, University of Malaya. The instrument used an electron-beam raster scan to capture high-resolution images of shale microtexture and pore structures.

Basin Modeling

PetroMod 1-D modeling was used to simulate the burial and thermal histories inferred from the Zindapir-1 well in the Central Indus Basin, Pakistan. Several parameters, including formation stratigraphy, lithologic properties, rock thickness, and sedimentary succession maturity, were used to constrain the models. Source rock maturation modeling incorporated heat flow (HF) and temperature gradient data54,55. The models were validated with measured vitrinite reflectance and bottom-hole temperature data40,56–60. The Easy%Ro kinetic model of Sweeney & Burnham (1990) was applied for Type III kerogen, matching measured and modeled Ro-temperature profiles using default boundary conditions and a constant heat flow of ~ 50 mW/m2.

Results

Organic geochemistry results

A total of 28 samples from Zindapir-1 well (located in the Central Indus Basin) were analyzed for total organic carbon (TOC) from burial depths of 1425 to 1955 m. The TOC values of Zindapir-1 range from 0.23 to 1.76 wt% (averaging 0.98 wt%). The S1, S2, S3 and GP values of Sembar shale lie in the range of 0.01–0.23, 0.06–2.32, 0.09–0.56 and 0.08–2.47 mg/g, respectively. HI and OI values range from 15 to 132 mg/g TOC and 9–222 mg/g CO2, respectively, with S2/S3 values ranging from 0.15 to 8.92. Tmax and PI values range from 446 to 466 °C and 0.04–0.32, respectively. The measured vitrinite reflectance (Ro) values of shale samples (n = 5) from Zindapir-1 range from 1.05 to 1.16% (Table 2). Organic petrographic results show vitrinite and inertinite as the dominant macerals in the Sembar shale, with pyrite framboids and grains commonly associated with organic matter (Fig. 3a-d). Quartz occurs as angular grains, and clay forms the fine-grained matrix (Fig. 3a-d).

The chromatograms in Fig. 7e show aromatic biomarkers (m/z 178, 184, and 192) in the representative Sembar shale sample. Inorganic geochemistry results

The results of the bulk and clay XRD analysis for 10 shale samples from Zindapir-1 are displayed in Figs. 8 and 9 and summarized in Table 5. Quartz is the dominant mineral in all analyzed samples, with concentrations ranging from 66.1 to 77.8%. In the upper interval (1440–1460 m) of Zindapir-1, calcite accounts for 12.8% to 15.7%, making it the second most abundant mineral, followed by kaolinite (3.4–6.6%). The middle and bottom intervals (1755–1955 m) show kaolinite as the second major mineral, comprising 12.4 to 16.6% (Fig. 8). Gypsum is encountered in four samples at depths 1810–1905 m with 2.3 to 3.5%. Rutile is also shown with a smaller quantity of 1.8–1.9%. Only six samples contain dolomite, with a maximum value of 18.8% at a depth of 1950–1955 m. Additional minerals in Sembar shale at Zindapir-1 are glauconite (2.0–7.7.0.7%), siderite (1.5–6.1%), and magnetite (0.7–1.4%) (Table 5). The results of 10 samples of clay XRD indicate that top interval of Zindapir-1 is dominated by illite layer followed by kaolinite clay mineral (Fig. 9A, B). The middle and bottom interval of studied samples at Zindapir-1 represents dominance of kaolinite followed by illite. These intervals of Sembar shale at Zindapir-1 well also contain montmorillonite (Fig. 9C-J).

1D basin modeling results

One-dimensional (1D) basin models incorporate stratigraphic sequences, details of any unconformities, geothermal gradients (temperatures), and source rock characteristics (TOC and HI). It is necessary to incorporate both the upper and lower boundaries of each stratigraphic unit, along with details regarding lithology, depositional and erosion ages, and petroleum system elements (PSE)12. These input parameters for basin modeling are presented in Table 7. Besides, it is essential to include any measured data for comparison with modeled values, such as vitrinite reflectance and subsurface temperatures12. Figure 13c presents depth plots depicting computed and bottom-hole temperatures. Likewise, depth plots in Fig. 13d illustrate the correlation between the computed Easy %Ro and the laboratory-determined Ro. The depth plots illustrate the correspondence of the computed values with the Ro and bore-hole temperatures (Fig. 13c, d). The Zindapir-1 well is located at the uplifted anticline and has a surface erosion of ~4000 m. Also, about four kilometers of the area have been uplifted52,53. Sembar shale in Zindapir-1 entered the early oil generation window in the Early Eocene. It reached the main oil window in the Middle Eocene. The Sembar shale in Zindapir-1 entered the late oil window in the Middle Eocene and remained in late oil window till present (Fig. 13e). It is evident from the burial history plot that the Sembar Formation was buried at sufficient depth to attain maturity before uplifting to the present burial depth (1410–2167 m). According to North61 the oil generation phase in source rock could be at temperature from 60 °C to 120 °C and gas generation could be from 120 °C onward. The temperature plot indicates that the Sembar Formation in Zindapir-1 has a temperature range of 46 °C to 75 °C at present depth but reached 160 °C before the uplift (Fig. 13b).

Discussion

Source-rock characterization

Organic richness and kerogen type

Geochemical screening involves total organic carbon analysis, Rock-Eval pyrolysis, and vitrinite reflectance12,62. Only four shale samples from Zindapir-1 at top burial depth intervals of 1425 m, 1440 m, 1445 m and 1455 m represent TOC of less than 0.5 wt% and indicate poor source rock potential. The remaining 24 samples represent fair to good source rock potential, with TOC range from 0.56 to 1.76 wt% having an average value of 1.08 wt%62. Moreover, the samples in the bottom zone (1860 to 1950 m) are found to have more than 1 wt% TOC. The highest TOC value of 1.76 wt% in Zindapir-1 is observed at 1615–1620 m intervals. Fourteen samples surpassed the 1 wt% threshold required for shale gas development in Zindapir-1 well15. TOC against depth plot and GP vs. TOC plot indicate fair to good potential for dominant samples (n = 24) and poor potential for only four samples (Fig. 14a).

In evaluating source-reservoir rocks, organic matter quality (kerogen type) is considered the second significant parameter after organic richness63. In this study accurate kerogen types were determined by assessing HI values, plotting data on modified van Krevelen diagrams, and evaluating the S2/S3 ratio. The HI values for 28 Sembar shale samples from Zindapir-1 range from 15 to 132 mg HC/g rock, suggesting kerogen Types III and IV62. However, the HI values drop as the sediments get mature. So, to determine the accurate kerogen type HI vs. OI and HI vs. Tmax plots are used62. These modified van Krevelen diagrams represent the dominant Type III kerogen for Sembar Formation at Zindapir-1 along with Type II/III mixed kerogen and Type IV kerogen (Fig. 14c, d). Furthermore, these kerogen types were further confirmed using S2/S3 ratio.

The S2/S3 ratios of all the shale samples (n = 28) in Zindapir-1 range from 0.15 to 8.92 (Table 2). It is noted that samples (n = 7) from the top interval (1425 to 1460 m) of the Sembar shale at Zindapir-1 are found to have S2/S3 ratio of less than 1, indicating kerogen Type IV. The S2/S3 ratio of the lower interval (1830 to 1955 m) of Sembar shale at Zindapir-1 is greater than 1, indicating the presence of kerogen Type III. It is found that the S2/S3 ratio was highest at 1615–1620 m, representing mixed kerogen Type II/III62. The FTIR signals observed in the Zindapir-1 well, particularly the pronounced aromatic signals in the range of 1600–1655 cm− 1 in Fig. 10d, are indicative of Type III kerogen64. The dominance of vitrinite in the analyzed Sembar shale samples also indicates the presence of Type III kerogen (Fig. 3a, b, d)62.

The C23T/C24Te (C23 tricyclic terpane/C24 tetracyclic terpane) and C24Te/C26 (C24 tetracyclic terpane/C26 tricyclic terpane) ratios, both greater than 1, confirm mixed marine and terrestrial organic matter contributions, whereas moderate gammacerane values (G/C30 = 0.07–0.12) indicate episodic water-column stratification. Hopane and aromatic biomarker ratios (C29/C30 = 0.42–0.70; Ts/Tm > 1; DBT/P = 0.017–0.09) suggest deposition of a clay-rich, siliceous marine shale under open-marine to shallow-coastal suboxic conditions65,72,73. The ternary diagram of C27, C28, and C29 steranes (Fig. 7d) further supports deposition in open-marine settings with intermittent shallow-marine or coastal influence74. In addition, the DBT/P ratios (0.017–0.09) and the DPT vs. Pr/Ph relationship (Fig. 7f) place the Sembar shale within Zone 3, characteristic of marine shale lithology75.

The FTIR spectra, showing both aliphatic and aromatic absorption bands in one sample, indicate a mixed marine-terrestrial organic input (Table 6). In contrast, other samples predominantly exhibit aromatic peaks, reflecting a primarily terrestrial origin for the Sembar shale (Table 6; Fig. 10d). Complementary FTIR signatures, characterized by minor aliphatic and dominant aromatic absorptions, together with vitrinite-dominated macerals, confirm a terrestrial organic source with minor marine influence64,71. These findings are corroborated by organic petrography, where the dominant vitrinite macerals typically associated with terrestrial organic matter reinforce the interpretation of a predominantly terrestrial source (Fig. 3). Overall, the integrated biomarker, spectroscopic, and petrographic evidence indicates a mixed, suboxic marine depositional system periodically influenced by stratification and restricted circulation.

The conceptual model for the deposition of the Sembar shale (Fig. 15) in a rift tectonic setting illustrates the influence of nutrient and terrigenous input from paleohighs driven by paleoweathering, as outlined by Wandrey et al76 in relation to the tectonic processes of the Indian Plate. The warm humid climate promoted high paleoproductivity, as reflected by the higher kaolinite content in the FTIR spectra (Table 6), leading to organic matter accumulation in the photic/oxic zone40,43. In this zone, silicon (Si) and aluminum (Al), along with terrigenous material, contributed to the deposition of siliceous shale. This zone is characterized by higher concentrations of Si, contributing to the siliceous composition of the shale (Fig. 15). The model highlights how these processes promoted the preservation of organic matter, while kaolinite and other minerals indicate significant terrestrial input, shaping the mineralogy of the Sembar shale (Fig. 15; Table 6).

Thermal maturity of organic matter

The amount of organic matter in sediments that have been converted into oil and gas depends on temperature and time13. Source rock maturation has been estimated based on a variety of parameters. Rock-Eval pyrolysis data, such as Tmax and production index (PI), are among these parameters. In addition, vitrinite reflectance values are commonly used to determine the maturity of organic matter62. The temperature at which S2 is generated at a maximum level is known as Tmax and is generally used to evaluate the thermal maturity of shales. However, for lower values of S2 (< 0.2), Tmax is unreliable40,77. The S2 values at Zindapir-1 for the majority of samples (n = 21) exceed 0.2. This indicates that the Tmax values from shale samples at Zindapir-1 can be used as a reliable indicator of organic matter maturity. The Tmax values of all the shale samples (n = 28) from Zindapir-1 range from 446 °C to 466 °C (average 456 °C), indicating that the samples are at the peak to late stages of maturity. The depth plot against Tmax indicates that Tmax values increase with depth (Fig. 14e). The measured vitrinite reflectance values of shale samples (n = 5) range from 1.05 to 1.16% indicating late mature stage for these intervals62. A calculation of additional vitrinite reflectance (cal. Ro) values can be made using Rock-Eval Tmaxdata by applying Jarvie et al78. equation: Ro = (0.018 × Tmax) − 7. S2 peak is generally a key determinant of Tmax in multiple Rock-Eval pyrolysis instruments, with the best Tmax values being observed for samples with S2 greater than 0.50 mg/g rock78. Additional Ro values are calculated for samples (n = 12) from Zindapir-1 whose S2 value exceeds 0.5 mg/g rock. The average calculated Ro value for samples from Zindapir-1 is 1.08% indicating late maturity (Table 2; Fig. 14f).

Thermal maturity was further assessed using molecular indicators, with the summarized parameters presented in Table 8. The OEP (odd-to-even predominance) ≈ 1 and CPI (carbon preference index) ≈ 1 values indicate that the organic matter has reached thermal equilibrium, reflecting a mature stage of hydrocarbon generation. Biomarker indices, including M30/C30 (0.08–0.16), Ts/Tm > 1, and MPI-1 (0.69), collectively indicate that the Sembar shale is late-mature and gas-prone (Fig. 7c; Table 8). The prevalence of 2-MP and 3-MP compared to 9-MP and 1-MP indicates the organic matter maturity of the selected Sembar shale (Fig. 7e)79. The FTIR characteristics play a crucial role in assessing the maturity level of marine source rocks, as demonstrated by64, where higher mature samples displayed enhanced aromatic signals (1600–1655 cm− 1), while less mature samples showed more pronounced aliphatic signals (2800–2950 cm− 1). In the Zindapir-1 well, the samples exhibit more pronounced aromatic signals, indicating a higher maturity of organic matter (Fig. 10d). Additionally, abundance of aromatic signals in this range increase with depth, as observed in the higher-maturity samples from 1430 to 1930 m of burial depth (Fig. 10a-c).

The thermal maturity of the organic matter in the Sembar shale at Zindapir-1 was further assessed using 1D basin modeling (Fig. 13; Table 7). The time vs. %Ro plot indicated that Sembar Formation in Zindapir-1 entered early maturity (early oil window) in Early-Middle Eocene (55 − 50 Ma). It remained in the peak maturity (main oil window) phase from 50 to 38 Ma. The Sembar Formation attained the late oil window (late maturity) at 38 Ma and remained in this window till present (Fig. 13a, e). The time plots are used to answer questions about generation of hydrocarbon and transformation ratio (TR) that are vital for evaluating potential source rocks12. The time profile of Zindapir-1 indicates that Sembar shale started generation of hydrocarbons at around 55 Ma and reached their maximum in the Oligocene at 28 Ma. In the lower Sects. (1736–2167 m), the TR is 68%, while in the upper Sects. (1410–1736 m), it is 53%. The Sembar Formation contains hydrocarbons primarily derived from Type III kerogen, indicating that the formation is predominantly gas-prone. Thus, the integration of geochemical, spectroscopic, and basin modeling evidence confirms that the Sembar shale has attained the gas-generation stage and retains significant potential for unconventional hydrocarbon development.

Reservoir characterization

This section discusses the key characteristics of the Sembar shale reservoir, focusing on its mineralogical composition, porosity, and the geological factors that influence its potential for unconventional gas extraction.

Reservoir implications of quartz- and clay-rich lithofacies

The mineralogical composition of the Sembar shale, as determined by bulk XRD analysis, includes quartz (66.1–77.8%), kaolinite (3.4–16.6%), calcite (12.8–15.7%), dolomite (3.7–18.8%), glauconite (2.0–7.7.0.7%), magnetite (0.7–1.4%), gypsum (2.3–3.5%), siderite (1.5–6.1%), and rutile (1.8–1.9%). The respective percentages of these minerals in Sembar shale are presented in Table 5. The presence of quartz, kaolinite, and carbonate minerals was also identified through FTIR analysis, complementing the XRD results (Table 6).

The bulk mineralogical composition of the Sembar shale from the Zindapir-1 well is dominated by quartz (66.1–77.8%) and includes notable kaolinite (3.4–16.6%), suggesting a mechanically competent lithofacies well-suited for hydraulic fracturing. High quartz content is a key indicator of brittleness, essential for enhancing fracture networks in tight shale gas reservoirs80.

Clay mineralogy, determined by clay XRD analysis, identifies kaolinite, illite, and minor montmorillonite. Kaolinite and illite, being non-swelling clays with low to moderate cation exchange capacities, are advantageous for gas production as these minerals reduce the risk of pore throat blockage and facilitate desorption81,82. Montmorillonite, known for its swelling behavior, is present in trace amounts, minimizing its impact on permeability and structural stability81.

Carbonate minerals exhibit vertical variation: calcite (12.8–15.7%) is concentrated in the upper interval, which coincides with zones of lower TOC, suggesting limited hydrocarbon potential and a higher likelihood of porosity reduction due to cementation. In contrast, dolomite (3.7–18.8%) dominates the middle and lower intervals, contributing to a more stable and fracture-prone matrix, supporting better reservoir conditions. Siderite (1.5–6.1%) also appears in minor amounts and may locally influence porosity through cementation. Accessory minerals include glauconite (2.0–7.7%), magnetite (0.7–1.4%), gypsum (2.3–3.5%), and rutile (1.8–1.9%) (Table 5). While these minerals have limited influence on reservoir quality, they are important indicators of the geological history. Glauconite points to slow sedimentation in a marine shelf setting, kaolinite suggests humid continental input, and siderite and gypsum reflect early diagenetic conditions under fluctuating redox and evaporitic regimes83–86. Hence, the high quartz content, favorable clay composition, and stratigraphically controlled carbonate distribution collectively enhance the Sembar Shale’s potential as a brittle, moderately porous, and diagenetically mature unconventional gas reservoir.

Brittleness evaluation

In shale, brittleness is a key property that determines how easily the rock can fracture during processes like hydraulic fracturing, which is essential for shale gas extraction. It is defined as the ability of a solid material to rupture easily with little or no ductile deformation87. The bulk XRD results can be used to identify the brittle and ductile zones in the shale rocks16. The bulk XRD results of clay-quartz-carbonate minerals are represented on a ternary diagram, where all the samples fall within the brittle, quartz-rich zone (Fig. 16b). These results indicate that shale intervals of the Sembar Formation can be considered suitable for hydraulic fracturing (Frac), as all samples occupy the quartz-rich brittle zone of the ternary diagram (Fig. 16b).

Porosity evaluation

The porosity characteristics of the Sembar shale, as observed through FESEM imaging and EDS analysis, are closely linked to its mineralogical composition and diagenetic history. The dominance of quartz (66.1–77.8%) and kaolinite (3.4–16.6%) plays a critical role in shaping the pore architecture. Quartz, being a rigid and brittle mineral, supports the development and preservation of intergranular and fracture-related porosity93. Meanwhile, kaolinite contributes to nanoporosity through its platy structure and the formation of loosely packed clay floccules, which are evident in the SEM images (Fig. 11d)94.

Based on the pore classification by Loucks et al17, the pore types in shale can be categorized into the picopore (< 1 nm), nanopore (1–1000 nm), and micropore (1–62.5 μm). FESEM analysis reveals that nanopores are the dominant form in Sembar shale, ranging between 70 and 300 nm, with occasional micropores (1–2.5 μm) and pores associated with clay aggregates. These nanopores are crucial for gas storage, especially for adsorbed gas, which typically occupies internal surfaces of clay minerals and organic matter (Fig. 11a-d). The irregular packing of kaolinite floccules further indicates the potential for increased surface area and enhanced diffusion pathways, reinforcing their importance in gas retention and migration (Fig. 11d). The clay mineralogy, dominated by non-swelling kaolinite and illite, with minor montmorillonite, supports the development of stable pore systems (Figs. 8 and 9). Unlike swelling clays, which can obstruct pore throats upon hydration, kaolinite and illite preserve pore integrity under burial conditions. This is favorable for gas mobility and long-term reservoir performance.

EDS mapping confirms that these pores are primarily hosted within quartz, kaolinite, and illite, which aligns with the mineralogical data from XRD (Fig. 12a-d). The high percentage of oxygen and silicon supports the idea that the shale contains primarily silicate minerals. Quartz is a common silicate mineral composed primarily of silicon dioxide (SiO2), and the presence of silicon (Si) (up to 17.33% in this sample) strongly suggests that quartz is a significant component in the shale (Fig. 12d). According to Loucks et al17, porosity in shales is broadly classified as matrix and non-matrix. The matrix porosity in the Sembar shale includes interparticle and intraparticle pores, while non-matrix porosity is associated with natural microfractures observed in certain intervals (Fig. 11a-d).

Further supporting the reservoir potential, Zhang14 reported that 20–85% of total gas in shales is adsorbed onto matrix grain surfaces, while free gas is stored in inorganic pores and microfractures. The Sembar shale exhibits features consistent with this model, suggesting that it can effectively store both adsorbed and free gas. The mechanical competence imparted by quartz-rich lithofacies aids fracture development, enhancing permeability and gas deliverability.

Implications for shale gas exploration

This study elucidates the key factors governing the potential of the Sembar Formation for shale gas exploration and development. As predicted by Rock-Eval pyrolysis, the dominant organic matter type in the analyzed shale samples is kerogen Type III (Fig. 14c, d). Sembar shale sediments contain a significant proportion of vitrinite maceral, reflecting the input of terrestrial organic matter during deposition (Fig. 3). Dominant samples, with a C24Te/C26 ratio greater than 1, futher confirm terrestrial organic matter contributions (Table 4). FTIR results reveal predominantly aromatic spectra, supporting a primarily terrestrial origin in the Sembar shale (Table 6; Fig. 10d). These rocks are also thermally mature, falling into the late maturity stage based on Tmax and Ro values (Table 2; Fig. 14e, f), and further supported by biomarker ratios (Fig. 7b, c). Based on these geochemical results, the Sembar shale is expected to generate significant quantities of gas. The chemical correlation between Rock-Eval HI and TOC content further supports this conclusion (Fig. 17b). Samples from the bottom interval (1800–1955 m) at Zindapir-1, with organic richness close to or exceeding 1 wt%, are more likely to contribute to gas generation compared to the upper interval (Fig. 14a, b). The relatively high free petroleum yield (S1) may result from contamination by well-drilling mud or natural hydrocarbon migration57,77. However, the relationship between free petroleum yield and total organic carbon suggests that the organic matter in the Sembar shale samples is indigenous and uncontaminated (Fig. 17a).

The 1D basin modeling of the Sembar Formation at Zindapir-1 reveals key maturity and kerogen transformation trends, which are critical for shale gas implications. The Sembar Formation entered early maturity during the Early-Middle Eocene (55 − 50 Ma) and reached peak maturity (main oil window) by 50 Ma, with the late oil window starting at 38 Ma. Hydrocarbon generation started around 55 Ma, peaking at 28 Ma during the Oligocene. The lower Sects. (1736–2167 m) generated 1.18 Mtons (million tons), while the upper Sects. (1410–1736 m) generated 1.41 Mtons. Gas generation was predominantly from Type III kerogen, with transformation ratios (TR) of 68% and 53% in the lower and upper sections, respectively (Fig. 13e, f). These findings highlight the potential for shale gas development in the Sembar Formation based on its thermal maturity and hydrocarbon generation history.

According to Hantschel and Kauerauf95, TR greater than 50% indicates that the source rock has generated the maximum quantity of hydrocarbons and become saturated, therefore, expelling the hydrocarbons beyond its storage capacity. Based on 50% TR threshold, the Sembar shale in Zindapir-1 began hydrocarbon expulsion in the Late Eocene. At its present depth, the Sembar shale in Zindapir-1 is unlikely to produce additional hydrocarbons due to uplift and erosion in the area (Fig. 13e, f). The Sembar shale demonstrates strong shale gas potential due to its high quartz content, which enhances brittleness and fracturing ability, and the presence of kaolinite and illite, which support gas storage with minimal permeability loss. FESEM imaging reveals dominant nanopores, along with some micropores and microfractures, suggesting capacity for both free and adsorbed gas. Vertical variations in carbonate content suggests better porosity in deeper zones, further reinforcing the shale’s suitability for unconventional gas development (Table 5).

Previous studies on the Zindapir anticline indicate that the Zindapir-1 well was drilled and abandoned as a dry hole, suggesting that although structural traps may exist, hydrocarbons either did not migrate effectively into these traps or were not generated in sufficient quantities to fill them45,46. While these studies focused on migration and accumulation challenges, the current findings emphasize the significant gas-generation potential of the Sembar shale at the Zindapir-1 well. However, due to uplift and erosion in the study area, it is likely that hydrocarbons did not effectively migrate or accumulate within the structural traps. Consequently, the Sembar Formation does not contribute to the accumulation of conventional hydrocarbons in this region, despite its gas-generation potential.

The Sembar shale’s TOC content exceeded the 1 wt% threshold necessary for shale gas development (Fig. 14a; Table 2). It demonstrates significant gas-generation potential in the lower intervals (1800–1955 m) of the Zindapir-1 well, characterized by Type III kerogen and thermal maturity (Ro) exceeding 1.1%. These intervals also exhibit low clay content and higher proportions of brittle minerals, such as quartz and carbonates, which enhance the shale’s brittleness and suitability for hydraulic fracturing. The brittleness index values of the Sembar shale in the Zindapir-1 well range from 0.77 to 0.86, indicating favorable characteristics for hydraulic fracturing in the study area (Table 5; Fig. 16a). The porosity of the Sembar shale is controlled by its mineralogical framework and depositional-diagenetic evolution. High quartz content ensures brittleness and promotes fracture generation, while kaolinite-rich zones with flocculated textures offer nanoporosity critical for gas adsorption. Minor carbonate cements may locally reduce porosity, particularly in the calcite-rich upper interval, but do not dominate the system. Additionally, the matrix porosity supports the storage of adsorbed gas, while microfractures facilitate retention of free gas (Fig. 11). Collectively, these characteristics indicate that the Sembar shale reservoir has moderate to good storage capacity and strong mechanical suitability for stimulation.

The innovation of this study lies in its application of an integrated multi-analytical approach to evaluate the source-reservoir characteristics and assess the shale gas potential of the Sembar Formation. By integrating organic-inorganic geochemical data, spectroscopic and microscopic analyses, and basin modeling, the study provides an accurate and comprehensive understanding of the formation’s potential for unconventional gas development. Despite these implications, a limitation arises from the absence of core samples, which were not acquired by the companies, restricting the analysis to well cuttings and potentially limiting the broader applicability of the porosity data. Future research should expand the dataset to include core samples and employ advanced geophysical techniques to investigate the lateral continuity of gas-prone intervals. Moreover, hydraulic fracturing simulations could provide further insights into the economic feasibility of exploiting the Sembar shale as an unconventional prospect. The Sembar Formation in Zindapir-1 exhibits favorable characteristics for unconventional gas development, including significant organic richness, high thermal maturity, and Type III kerogen. Additionally, the brittleness of the Sembar shale and its favorable pore structure further enhance its suitability for hydraulic fracturing. However, structural complexities and migration challenges limit its contribution to conventional hydrocarbon accumulations. Given the ongoing advancements in shale gas extraction technologies, the Sembar shale demonstrates substantial potential to emerge as a highly promising and economically viable target for future energy exploration and development.

Comparison with global shale analogs

When compared with globally productive shale plays, such as the Barnett and Marcellus shales (United States) and the Longmaxi Shale of the Sichuan Basin (China), the Sembar shale exhibits comparable source-rock and reservoir characteristics. Its TOC (up to 1.76 wt%) and vitrinite reflectance (1.05–1.16%) fall within the dry-gas maturity window, consistent with values reported for the Barnett (2–6 wt%, Ro 1.1–1.5%), Marcellus (1–12 wt%, Ro 1.0–3.0%), and Longmaxi (1.2–6.2 wt%, Ro 1.1–3.0%) shales96–98. Biomarker results, particularly sterane distributions, indicate a marine depositional setting for the Sembar shale, consistent with the Bakken, Marcellus, and Barnett shales99. The Sembar shale’s elevated quartz content is comparable not only to the Barnett and Marcellus shales but also to the lower Longmaxi Shale, where quartz commonly exceeds 50 wt% and contributes to high brittleness and efficient fracture propagation8,96,97. FESEM observations reveal that nanopores are the dominant pore type in the Sembar shale, ranging between 70 and 300 nm, with occasional micropores (1–2.5 μm) associated with clay aggregates. This is comparable to the Barnett Shale, where nanoscale pores (5–750 nm; median ≈ 100 nm) are primarily developed within kerogen particles and occur in irregular bubble- to elliptical-shaped forms100. However, unlike the kerogen-dominated porosity of the Barnett, the Sembar shale exhibits a greater contribution from interparticle and clay-associated pores. Similarly, in the Longmaxi Shale of the Sichuan Basin, nanoscale organic pores (typically 10–200 nm) coexist with abundant intergranular clay-related pores, reflecting comparable diagenetic compaction and thermal evolution processes97. Although the Sembar shale’s organic richness is somewhat lower, its dominance of Type III kerogen, mixed marine-terrestrial input, and favorable mineralogy suggest comparable gas-generation potential under appropriate stimulation conditions. These analogies underscore the Sembar Formation’s significance as Pakistan’s closest counterpart to successful global shale-gas plays and highlight its strategic value for advancing national energy independence and unconventional resource development.

Conclusions

A novel multi-analytical approach integrating organic-inorganic geochemistry, vibrational spectroscopy, microscopy, and basin modeling was employed to characterize the source-reservoir properties and assess the shale gas potential of the Sembar Formation in the Central Indus Basin, Pakistan. This study validates an integrated geochemical-mineralogical-basin modeling, multi-analytical workflow for assessing underexplored shale formations. Although the absence of core samples limits detailed porosity and mechanical analyses, results from well cuttings provide a reliable assessment of source-reservoir characteristics. The findings position the Sembar Formation as a promising shale-gas target in the Central Indus Basin, supporting Pakistan’s broader shale development and energy independence strategy. Moreover, the proposed workflow offers a transferable framework for evaluating other basins with comparable geological settings.
