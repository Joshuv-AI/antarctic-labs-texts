Structural identification of the RY12 domain of RyR1 as an ADP sensor and the target of the malignant hyperthermia therapeutic dantrolene
Authors: Kookjoo Kim, Huan Li, Qi Yuan, Zephan Melville, Ran Zalk, Amédée des Georges, Joachim Frank, Wayne A Hendrickson, Andrew R Marks, Oliver B Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant hyperthermia (MH) is a pharmacogenetic condition triggered by volatile anesthetics, which activate MH susceptible mutants of ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1). The precise binding sites and mechanisms of the therapeutic dantrolene and MH triggers have remained unclear. Here, we present structures of RyR1 bound to dantrolene and the MH trigger 4-chloro-m-cresol (4CmC), revealing the dantrolene and 4CmC binding sites in atomic detail. Dantrolene binds stacked with ATP or ADP in the RY12 domain at the corner of the receptor, inducing a conformational change. Functional analyses confirm ATP or ADP is required for dantrolene inhibition, and a single point mutation disrupting this peripheral ATP site abolishes this ATP/ADP-dependent inhibition. Strikingly, in the absence of dantrolene, this site selectively binds two ADP molecules even in the presence of excess ATP, indicating a role in ATP/ADP ratio sensing. Additionally, we identified four 4CmC binding sites, revealing one primary binding site in an amphipathic cavity in the core solenoid, and three auxiliary binding sites in the EF-hand, transmembrane, and RY12 domains. These findings offer insights into the binding modes and mechanisms of MH-linked molecules, and reveal the RY12 domain’s regulatory role as a promiscuous sensor for adenine nucleotides and small ligands.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Calcium channels, Permeation and transport

Introduction

Ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1) is an intracellular Ca2+ channel that plays a key role in excitation-contraction coupling by mediating efflux of Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) to the cytoplasm, which triggers skeletal muscle contraction1,2. Gating of RyR1 is regulated by numerous ligands, ions, and binding partners. These include metal ions such as Ca2+ and Mg2+; endogenous ligands like adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP); exogenous compounds like caffeine3; and regulatory proteins such as calstabin1 (Cs1, also known as FKBP12)4,5 and calmodulin (CaM)6,7. The C-terminal domain (CTD) of RyR1 (human RyR1 amino acids 4958–5038) contains binding sites for Ca2+, ATP/ADP, and caffeine/xanthine8. In addition to this central ligand-binding hub, the RYR repeats 1 and 2 (RY12) domain, located at the corner of the RyR1 tetramer has been reported to bind a class of RyR-targeting therapeutics known as Rycals, in conjunction with ATP9.

Stringent regulation of intracellular calcium release is essential, as uncontrolled calcium release can have severe physiological consequences, as exemplified by malignant hyperthermia (MH). MH is a pharmacogenetic disorder linked primarily to RyR1 mutations. Patients with MH susceptibility (MHS) experience muscle rigidity and a rapid rise in body temperature when exposed to certain volatile anesthetics. These MH trigger agents, which do not affect wild-type RyR1 at therapeutic doses, abnormally activate mutant RyR1, leading to a hypermetabolic state. The resulting increase in compensatory ATP hydrolysis causes hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, acidosis, and, if untreated, often death. Dantrolene is the only approved therapeutic agent for MH and is known to inhibit RyR1-mediated Ca2+ release10–12. However, its precise binding site and molecular mechanism of action have remained elusive. Early experiments with tritiated azidodantrolene suggested the binding site lies within the first 1400 amino acids of the receptor12, and other studies have shown that ATP, Mg2+ and CaM may potentiate its inhibitory effect13–16.

Similarly, the binding sites and mechanisms of MH trigger agents, such as halothane anesthetics and 4-chloro-m-cresol (4CmC), remain largely unelucidated. Perhaps the most well-studied MH trigger is 4CmC, which is not an anesthetic but was originally used as a preservative in succinylcholine17, prior to being identified as a potent RyR1 activator. 4CmC both activates RyR1 and increases its calcium affinity at the binding site in the channel’s core18–20. While two key residues for activation by 4CmC, Q4020, and K4021, have been identified21, their roles have been unclear. In previous structures, K4021 projects from the surface of the core solenoid into the solvent, while Q4020 lines a mostly hydrophobic cavity in the core of the domain, with no obvious entry points, leaving the exact binding site of 4CmC and mechanism unresolved.

In this study, we obtained multiple high-resolution single-particle reconstructions of the RyR1-calstabin2 (Cs2; FKBP12.6)-CaM complex with various ligands, including dantrolene and 4CmC. Our goal was to probe the binding sites and mechanisms of MH triggers, therapeutics, and adenine nucleotides. We localize the dantrolene binding site to the RY12 domain at the corner of the receptor, where it forms a ternary complex with either ADP or ATP. This binding mode is similar to that of Rycal therapeutics9 and is accompanied by a closure of the cleft between two subdomains and remodeling of the interprotomer interface. The high local resolution of 2.5 Å in this region after local processing allows us to confidently assign atomic details of the interaction. Consistent with this structure, single-channel lipid bilayer recordings show that ATP or ADP is required for RyR1 inhibition by dantrolene, and a single point mutation (W882A) that disrupts nucleotide binding abolishes this effect. Furthermore, in the absence of dantrolene, we show that the RY12 domain selectively binds two ADP molecules, even in the presence of a substantial excess ATP, and adopts a conformation that is strikingly similar to the dantrolene-bound conformation. Our structures with 4CmC confirm that it directly interacts with Q4020, occupying a cryptic pocket in the inaccessible center of the core solenoid (CSol) domain. This site is separated from the high-affinity Ca2+-binding site by a single layer of α-helices, providing a potential explanation for how 4CmC modulates RyR1 sensitivity to Ca2+. We also identify three auxiliary 4CmC binding sites located in the transmembrane (TM) domain, the EF-hand domain, and the RY12 domain.

Dantrolene binds to the RY12 domain in an ATP/ADP-dependent manner

Following masked local refinements with a local mask around the three SP1a/ryanodine receptor (SPRY) domains (SPRY1-3) and the RY12 domain, which includes amino acid residues 628–1656 in rabbit RyR1, dantrolene was identified in the RY12 local EM maps of DanADP and DanATP datasets, at local resolutions of 2.4 and 2.7 Å, respectively. Dantrolene was observed binding with either ADP (Fig. 1) or ATP (Supplementary Fig. 1) in a stacking manner between the two α-helices in the RY12 clamp. In this cleft, dantrolene and the co-bound nucleotide (ADP/ATP) are flanked by W996, which stacks with the hydantoin moiety of dantrolene, and W882, which stacks with the adenine moiety of ADP/ATP. The binding pocket is lined with positively charged amino acid residues, such as R886, R897, H904, R1000, W1005, and R1020, that accommodate the terminal phosphates of the adenine nucleotides (Fig. 1B, C). Dantrolene binds to the inner side of the cleft, while the nucleotide is positioned on the outer side.

We then investigated whether the presence of ATP or ADP is required for RyR1 inhibition by dantrolene. For these experiments, we incorporated the purified RyR1-Cs2-CaM complex into proteoliposomes for planar lipid bilayer single-channel recordings (See “Methods” for details). The addition of 100 µM dantrolene decreased the open probability (Po) of RyR1 channels that were first activated by 1 µM Ca2+ and either 2 mM ATP or ADP. However, dantrolene did not decrease Po in the absence of ATP or ADP (Fig. 1D). The current-time traces of these single-channel recordings are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Based on the RyR1-dantrolene-ATP/ADP ternary complex structures and the functional requirement of a nucleotide for inhibition, we hypothesized that the binding of ATP or ADP at this peripheral site (as opposed to the central site in the CTD) is required for dantrolene’s inhibitory effect. Because ATP and ADP both stack with the indole ring of W882, we reasoned that mutating this residue would weaken or abolish nucleotide binding at the peripheral site. Accordingly, the RyR1-W882A mutant, expressed in HEK293T cells, was tested using single-channel bilayer recordings. While we acknowledge the inherent limitations of heterologous expression systems compared to native rabbit muscle preparations, RyR1-W882A was still activated by ATP and Ca2+, but no longer showed ATP/ADP-dependent inhibition by dantrolene (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 2). Our structural observations demonstrate that dantrolene and adenine nucleotides co-bind in the RY12 domain. Based on these structures and the W882A mutant recordings, we hypothesize that dantrolene inhibition depends on the cooperative binding of an ATP or ADP molecule at the W882 site. However, further functional validation is required to definitively establish the exact mechanism. In order to provide additional structural support for this model, we collected a dataset of RyR1 in the presence of dantrolene and Mg2+ but without nucleotides (DanMg dataset), in which we can see no evidence of dantrolene binding or RY12 closure (Supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, a dataset collected with RyR1 and Mg2+-ATP (ATPMgCa), but no dantrolene or ADP, showed a wide open RY12 domain, with no evidence of RY12 closure or stacked ligands in the RY12 cleft.

The RY12 domain selectively binds ADP in the absence of dantrolene

In the ADP4CmC dataset, which lacked dantrolene, we observed two ADP molecules binding to the RY12 domain. The molecules were stacked, bridged by a Mg2+ ion, and their binding induced a closure of the RY12 domain similar to that observed with dantrolene. This finding prompted us to investigate whether the RY12 domain could selectively bind ADP even in the presence of excess ATP, a condition that mimics the intracellular environment of a myocyte. To test this, we solved structures under two conditions with different [ATP]:[ADP] ratios: one representing a physiological resting condition (Physiological dataset) and another representing muscle fatigue (Fatigue dataset). During muscle fatigue, rapid ATP hydrolysis decreases cellular [ATP] while increasing [ADP]. The fatigue dataset yielded a 2.2 Å consensus map (Supplementary Fig. 13). After masked local refinement, the RY12 local map clearly showed a stack of two ADP molecules stabilized by a bridging Mg2+ ion (Fig. 2A), and the existence of the Mg2+ is supported by a map-model difference map calculated using a model lacking Mg, which shows a clear positive difference density peak at the putative Mg site (Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, no bound ATP was evident at the peripheral site, despite a 6:1 ATP-to-ADP ratio in the sample, while ATP was bound at the central site at the CTD-S6 boundary. Conversely, under conditions mimicking a resting state (Physiological dataset) with a much larger excess of ATP, we found no evidence of high-occupancy ADP binding in the RY12 domain, although a small sub-class with bound ADP could still be isolated through further classification. The striking structural similarity between the ADP-bound and dantrolene-bound states of RY12 strongly suggests that ADP itself can inhibit RyR1 by binding to this peripheral site. This raises the intriguing possibility that the RY12 domain functions as a sensor for the ATP:ADP ratio in muscle, acting to dampen RyR1 activity when cellular ATP levels are depleted during muscle fatigue.

4CmC binds to Q4020 in a cryptic pocket within the core solenoid

In all datasets containing 4CmC (DanATP, DanADP, ATP4CmC, and ADP4CmC), three distinct 4CmC binding sites for the molecule were identified on each RyR1 protomer, with a fourth site only present in the ATP4CmC dataset. The primary site is located in the center of the CSol domain, where 4CmC directly interacts with Q4020, a residue previously shown to be essential for its activating effect21. Additionally, two auxiliary sites were located in all 4CmC datasets: one on the surface of CSol at the base of the EF-hand domain, and a second within the TM region of the receptor (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The primary 4CmC molecule, bound to Q4020, is buried in a cryptic pocket without a direct access pathway from the solvent, implying that a conformational change in the CSol domain is required for ligand binding. The two 4CmC binding sites within the CSol are in proximity to the pore-forming domain and the CTD, which contains the binding sites for ATP, Ca2+, and caffeine. At the first auxiliary site, a second 4CmC molecule interacts with Q4133 (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 5). Both sites are lined with multiple hydrophobic amino acid residues (Fig. 3C, D). The third 4CmC molecule, located in the TM domain, is wedged in between the S5 and P helices of one RyR1 protomer and the pore-lining S6 helix of the adjacent protomer (Fig. 3D).

In the RY12 local refinement originating from the ATP4CmC dataset, we could fit a model of 4CmC with an ATP molecule, identifying a fourth 4CmC binding site that is only present in this dataset (Supplementary Fig. 5). This arrangement is comparable to the binding modes of dantrolene with ADP or ATP, where the nucleotide consistently stacks on the W882 sidechain. It appears the other member of the molecular stack (4CmC in this case) is interchangeable, accommodating either dantrolene or 4CmC. However, since 4CmC is not observed at this site when dantrolene or ADP is present, its affinity for the RY12 site is likely lower than that of the other known ligands.

3D variability analysis reveals altered conformational coupling between BSol and CSol in the presence of dantrolene

After obtaining high-resolution reconstructions, we performed 3D Variability Analysis (3DVA) in cryoSPARC22 on each dataset to characterize conformational heterogeneity. Our analysis focused on the core-pore region (amino acid residues 3667–5037) and the corner region (amino acid residues 628–1656). Previous studies have established that a downward movement of the bridging solenoid (BSol) domain toward the membrane is coupled to the opening of the TM pore in both RyR1 and RyR28,23. In the two datasets containing dantrolene (DanATP and DanADP), 3DVA revealed a principal component of motion representing an inverted coupling mode. In this inverted mode, a slight downward movement of the BSol domain is coupled to closure of the TM pore, contrary to the established activation mechanism (Fig. 4). To quantify the pore dimensions in these states, the Cɑ–Cɑ distance of the hydrophobic gate residue, I4937, were measured a proxy for the pore diameter. A comparison between the putative open conformations of the normal and inverted modes showed no significant difference in their pore diameters (Fig. 4B).

The Rycal ARM210 and dantrolene both bind to the inner face of the RY12 cleft

The observation of a dantrolene-nucleotide complex in the RY12 domain, combined with the higher resolution achieved by using the subparticle processing strategy in this report prompted us to reprocess the raw data from EMPIAR-10997 (RyR1-ARM210-ATP). In the original analysis of this dataset, the Rycal drug ARM210 was modeled into the outer ligand density, while ATP was placed in the inner density of the RY12 domain9. Our improved cryoEM data processing pipeline, incorporating 3D classification and 3DVA on the RY12 subparticles, produced a clearer visualization of the bound ligands. After processing of the subparticles, the reconstructed map now clearly shows the ATP molecule stacking on W882. A density for ARM210 is situated above the ATP, flanked by W996 and the adenine moiety of the nucleotide (Supplementary Fig. 6). The tail of ARM210 remains unresolved, leaving a certain degree of ambiguity in the precise orientation of the therapeutic, but the assignment of the ATP is unambiguous.

To compare the conformational states of the RY12 domain across multiple datasets, we measured the interhelical angle between the two helices forming the clamp (residues 915–934 and 979–1002) and the Cα-Cα distance between E915 and Q1003. In all datasets featuring a ligand-bound, closed RY12 state (DanATP, DanADP, ATP4CmC, ADP4CmC, Fatigue, and the reprocessed ARM210), the domain exhibited a similar conformation, with an average interhelical angle of 42.7° and an E915-Q1003 Cα distance of 17.2 Å. In contrast, the unbound RY12 domain in the Physiological, ATPMgCa, and DanMg datasets displayed an open conformation with a wider average interhelical angle (54.4°) and a longer E915-Q1003 distance (24.5 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Analysis of binding stoichiometry using cryoEM

Our local processing workflow involves classification of the RY12-containting subparticles after symmetry expansion, necessitating additional steps to assess binding stoichiometry on the tetramer in the presence of heterogeneity. In order to address this, we used a CryoSPARC Tools Python script24 to map individual subparticles back to their originating tetramers, and thereby calculate the estimated ligand occupancy per tetramer (from 0 to 4 sites occupied).

Unlike the datasets containing dantrolene (DanATP and DanADP), which were largely conformationally homogeneous, the Fatigue, Physiological, ATP4CmC, ADP4CmC, and ARM210 datasets displayed significant conformational heterogeneity in the RY12 domain. Specifically, we observed a mixture of open (unbound) and closed (bound) states, with the closure being dependent on binding of ligands in RY12. No evidence for cooperative binding between protomers was observed. In DanATP and DanADP datasets, over 90% of the C4 symmetry-expanded RY12 subparticles were in a closed, ligand-bound state. This high occupancy is likely attributable to the 0.1 mM concentration of dantrolene used in the structural studies. Consequently, the majority of RyR1 tetramers in these datasets adopt three-bound or four-bound states (Supplementary Fig. 8). In contrast, the datasets featuring two bound ADP molecules (ADP4CmC and Fatigue) showed a broader distribution of occupancies. While 40–50% of particles were in high-occupancy states, a significant population had fewer sites bound. The ATP4CmC and ARM210 datasets showed even lower occupancy states, with most RyR1 particles having zero or only one out of four RY12 sites bound. In the Physiological dataset, the vast majority of RY12 domains were in an open conformation, and ~60% of unexpanded RyR1 particles were in either zero-bound or one-bound states. A small subset of RY12 subparticles in Physiological dataset was isolated (79,453 of 709,248 subparticles) representing a bound RY12 conformation. Full-particle occupancy analysis confirmed that, similar to ATP4CmC and ARM210 datasets, most channels under physiological conditions were in low-occupancy states (Supplementary Fig. 8). Local refinement with a mask around the RY12 domain on the small subset of bound subparticles identifies two weakly resolved ADP molecules in RY12 (Supplementary Fig. 9), and the RY12 clamp assumes comparable interhelical angle to ones in DanATP, DanADP, Fatigue, ATP4CmC, and ADP4CmC datasets (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

The RY12 domain hosts binding sites for both endogenous ligands and therapeutics

The structural and functional data presented in this report provide a complementary and detailed view of the molecular mechanism of dantrolene inhibition, as well as identifying the RY12 site as a putative sensor for ADP. During the preparation of this work, cryoEM structures of an MHS mutant RyR1 (Y522S) bound to dantrolene were reported in open and closed states, in the presence of ATP25. ATP was not assigned in the atomic model, with only dantrolene present in the RY12 cleft, but inspection of the density map suggests that the assignment observed in our wild-type structures would also be consistent with the density in the Y522S mutant. While recent crystal structures of the isolated RyR3 RY12 domain have identified dantrolene bound with ATP or AMP-PCP26, our work provides key advances through high-resolution (2.4–2.8 Å) local reconstructions of the RyR1 RY12 domain in the context of the full-length structure, bound to multiple sets of ligands. This allowed for the unambiguous assignment of a ternary complex containing both dantrolene and a co-bound ATP/ADP molecule, as well as the identification of an ADP-inhibited state where two ADPs are stacked in the RY12 cleft, bridged by a Mg2+ ion. Functionally, our single-channel recordings with wild-type and W882A mutant RyR1 confirmed that dantrolene does not inhibit RyR1 in the absence of ATP or ADP (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 2). Structurally, we found no evidence of dantrolene binding to RY12 without a co-bound nucleotide (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with previous photolabeling studies where the labeling of RyR1 by [3H]azidodantrolene was significantly enhanced by AMP-PCP, a non-hydrolyzable ATP analog27.

Mechanism of dantrolene inhibition and the role of the RY12 domain

Upon binding of the dantrolene-nucleotide complex, the RY12 domain undergoes a significant conformational change, closing like a clamp to secure the ligands. While it is not fully understood how this local change at the corner of the tetramer propagates to the channel pore, the proximity of the binding site to the interprotomer interface offers a plausible hypothesis. This interface is often destabilized by MHS mutations28, suggesting that the conformational change of the RY12 domain may inhibit the channel by stabilizing this interface. Occupancy analysis of RY12 corner subparticles provides further insight into this mechanism. In datasets containing dantrolene, over 90% of RY12 domains were in a ligand-bound state with most channels having three or four sites occupied. In contrast, the Fatigue dataset, containing a functionally similar dual-ADP complex, showed significantly lower occupancy at RY12. Furthermore, the two-ADP bound state was not observed in the DanADP dataset, despite the presence of an excess of ADP, indicating that dantrolene must bind to the RY12 site with a substantially higher affinity than ADP.

While dantrolene inhibited the channel in lipid bilayer recordings, the effects on open probability under cryo-EM conditions were minimal (Supplementary Table 1), suggesting that although the dantrolene binding site has been identified, more work will be required to fully characterize the mechanism of inhibition.

RyR1 channels form a paracrystalline, checkerboard-like array on the SR membrane in situ and in vitro29, and it has been recently reported that this paracrystalline arrangement is preserved in mouse extensor digitorum longus muscle in situ probed by cryoET30. The arrangement of the arrays indicates that the RY12 domain is likely involved in interactions between adjacent channels within this array. Therefore, ligands that stabilize the RY12 domain may not only affect a single channel but also modulate coupling between neighboring channels. Finally, our observation that in the absence of dantrolene, the RY12 domain selectively binds ADP in an inhibitory conformation, even in the presence of a physiologically relevant excess of ATP, raises the intriguing possibility that the RY12 domain may function as an endogenous sensor of the cellular ATP:ADP ratio.

Prokaryotic and viral RY12 domains encode putative nucleotide sensors

Prokaryotic homologs of the RY12 domain have been identified previously, but their function has remained unclear. The identification of the RyR1-RY12 domain as a nucleotide binding domain prompted us to revisit the prokaryotic and viral RY12 homologs using modern machine-learning based structure prediction approaches, in order to assess whether they too may be plausible nucleotide sensors. Using Foldseek31 with the ADP-bound RY12 domain as a search model, we identified prokaryotic and viral homologs in a variety of domain architectures, ranging from isolated soluble domains to putative tetrameric cation channels, and repredicted them using AlphaFold 3 (AF3) in the presence and absence of nucleotides. One such homolog is Uniprot A0A8E4ZF99, the RY12 domain containing protein from the Olleya phage Harreka 1 (Supplementary Fig. 15). AF3 prediction in the presence of various nucleotides identifies a singly AMP-bound state as the highest confidence prediction, with binding predicted to be accompanied by a similar conformational change to that observed for the RyR1 RY12 domain; this allows us to tentatively suggest that prokaryotic and viral RY12 domains may also act as nucleotide sensors, although further work will be required to demonstrate this experimentally, and to characterize the potential biological roles of these putative nucleotide sensors.

4CmC binds to a cryptic pocket in the CSol and several auxiliary sites

4CmC is a potent RyR1 activator, initially identified as an MH trigger when it was used as a preservative in succinylcholine preparations17. Although Fessenden et al. showed that Q4020 and K4021 are required for RyR1 activation by 4CmC, the structural basis for this interaction remained unclear, even after the initial structures of RyR1 were solved20,21. Our cryoEM maps confirm that Q4020 directly interacts with one of the two 4CmC molecules in the CSol domain. This primary binding site is not readily accessible to the solvent (Fig. 3C), implying that a substantial conformational rearrangement of the cytosolic domain is required for ligand entry. The functional role, if any, of the auxiliary 4CmC binding sites remains unclear. However, previous studies on isolated RyR1 using single-channel bilayer recordings indicate that 4CmC produces an additive effect on RyR1 activity when it is added to both the cytoplasmic (cis side of the bilayer) and luminal (trans side of the bilayer) sides—an observation inconsistent with a single cytosolic activation site21. The TM binding site we identified may therefore explain this luminal effect, though further investigation is required.

Methods

Purification of RyR1 from rabbit skeletal muscle

Frozen New Zealand White Rabbit skeletal muscle tissue was purchased commercially from BioIVT. 20–30 g of this tissue, collected from back and thighs, was blended with 150–200 mL of buffer H containing 10 mM Tris Maleate pH 6.8, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM benzamidine hydrocholoride (Accela ChemBio), and 0.5 mM AEBSF (GoldBio), using a Waring blender for 1.5 min. The lysed homogenate was centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 min to remove large debris, then the supernatant was filtered through three layers of cheesecloth before the second centrifugation at 36,000 × g for 30 min. Membrane pellets from the second centrifugation were resuspended in 10 mL buffer S (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 0.8 M NaCl, 0.5% CHAPS (GoldBio), 0.1% phosphatidylcholine (Avanti Polar Lipids) solubilized in 10% CHAPS, 1 mM EGTA, 2 mM DTT (GoldBio), 0.5 mM AEBSF, 1 mM benzamidine hydrochloride, and a half of a protease inhibitor tablet (Pierce)) with 10 up and down strokes in a Dounce glass homogenizer. After the 10 strokes, 10 mL buffer D (buffer S without NaCl and protease inhibitors) was added and mixed via another round of 10 up and down strokes. The solubilized membrane homogenate was then subjected to a round of ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter before it was loaded onto a 5 mL HiTrap Q HP anion exchange column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with buffer WA (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 400 mM NaCl, 0.25% CHAPS, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM TCEP (GoldBio), 0.5 mM AEBSF, 1 mM benzamidine, and 0.01% 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC; Avanti Polar Lipids)). After the filtered supernatant was loaded onto the anion exchange column, contaminant proteins were washed with five column volumes of WA. Resin-bound protein was eluted with a linear gradient of 480–520 mM NaCl using buffers WA and WB (buffer WA with 600 mM NaCl). The eluted fractions were checked via SDS-PAGE for bands with high molecular weight corresponding to RyR1, and fractions which contain RyR1 were pooled and subjected to incubation with excess CaM and calstabin2 (Cs2; FKBP12.6) at a 1:40 molar ratio for 1 h in 4 °C to allow formation of RyR1-Cs2-CaM complexes. Calstabin2 was used to form the complex because it has a higher affinity for RyR1 compared to calstabin1, and it has been shown to sufficiently form a complex with RyR18,32. Calstabin1 (FKBP12) and Cs2 share the same binding site, and both stabilize the closed conformation of RyR1. Concentration of RyR1 in the combined fractions was measured with a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific; 1 abs @ 280 nm = 1 mg/mL). After incubation, the sample is concentrated to 0.5–1 mL using an Amicon centrifugal filter unit with 100 kDa MWCO pore size (Millipore Sigma). The concentrated sample was filtered through a 0.22 µm centrifugal filter unit (Corning) before loading onto a TSKgel BioAssist G4SWXL column (Tosoh Biosciences) pre-equilibrated with buffer E (10 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 1 mM EGTA, 0.25% CHAPS, and 0.001% DOPC) for size exclusion chromatography. Fractions were eluted at 0.5 mL/min, and the fractions containing the RyR1-Cs2-CaM complex were pooled and concentrated to 7–9 mg/mL using 100 kDa cut-off Amicon centrifugal filter units. Ligands were added to the concentrated sample before cryoEM grid preparation. ATP, ADP, and free Mg2+ concentrations were calculated using MaxChelator33. The concentrations of added ligands are summarized for each condition in Supplementary Table 1.

Since dantrolene has poor solubility in H2O, dantrolene sodium salt (Sigma) was solubilized in dimethylformamide (DMF), then the dantrolene DMF solution was diluted with H2O. Briefly, DMF was first purged for 30 min with inert nitrogen or argon gas. 12 mM dantrolene in DMF (4.04 mg/mL) was prepared, then the solution was diluted 20-fold with H2O to prepare a stock solution of 600 µM dantrolene. 100 µM dantrolene was added to the sample to prepare cryoEM grids.

CryoEM grid preparation

UltrAuFoil R0.6/1 Au-300 mesh holey gold grids (Quantifoil) were glow-discharged (PELCO easiGlow), and 3 µL of purified RyR1-Cs2-CaM with ligands was deposited onto the hydrophilized EM grid. The sample was blotted for 4.5 to 5.5 s at 4 °C and 100% relative humidity in the chamber of a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher). After blotting, the sample EM grid was plunged into liquid ethane for vitrification. The grids were first screened with either a Glacios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) operated at 200 kV or a Tecnai F20 electron microscope equipped with a K2 direct electron detector (Gatan) operated at 200 kV. Screened grids were loaded to a Titan Krios (Thermo Fisher) electron microscope equipped with a K2 (1.06 Å/pixel) or a K3 (0.83 Å/pixel) direct electron detector (Gatan) operated at 300 kV in counting mode using Leginon and Appion data collection software suite34,35. Movies were collected at exposure rates of 8 e−/pix/s for 8 sein 40 frames (200 ms/frame) (DanATP), 8 e−/pix/s for 10 s in 50 frames (200 ms/frame) (ATP4CmC), or 16 e−/pix/s for 2.5 s in 50 frames (50 ms per frame) (the rest of the datasets) using a defocus range of −0.5 to −1.5 µm. A 100 µm objective aperture was used for all collections except the ADP4CmC dataset. RY12 local refinement processing and validation statistics

CryoEM data processing

Most of the image processing steps was run in cryoSPARC22 unless stated otherwise. Movie frames were aligned with Patch Motion job type in cryoSPARC. After CTF estimation with Patch CTF, low-quality micrographs with high relative ice thickness, high motion pixel distances, and low CTF fit resolution were removed from each dataset. RyR1 particles were picked with crYOLO36 or template-based particle picker in cryoSPARC. Low-resolution ab-initio models were generated with the initial sets of particles that went through a round of 2D classification. Iterative rounds of 2D classification and heterogeneous refinement further removed particles that do not contribute to high-resolution reconstructions. After cleaning up the particle sets for each dataset, global consensus reconstructions of RyR1 were carried out with Non-Uniform refinement in cryoSPARC37 with C4 symmetry enforced. For DanATP, DanADP, ATP4CmC, ADP4CmC, Fatigue, and Physiological datasets, the particle stacks from consensus Non-Uniform (NU) refinement were subjected to reference-based motion correction (RBMC) in cryoSPARC. RBMC resulted in 0.1–0.4 Å nominal resolution improvements for the six datasets. After RBMC, particles were grouped into 25–225 groups by similar beam shift/image shift coordinates recorded during data collection38.

In consensus homogeneous/NU refinement maps, the cytoplasmic domain of RyR1, especially BSol, exhibits a significant degree of motion suggested by smeared or unresolved local map quality. To improve the local resolution, five local soft masks (described in Supplementary Table 2) were generated using UCSF ChimeraX39 and cryoSPARC. The particle stacks were C4 symmetry-expanded and were subjected to focused refinements in cryoSPARC with local masks. These local masks, especially the corner mask (SPRY1-RY12-SPRY2-SPRY3 domains; amino acids 628–1656), were also used for rounds of masked 3D classification without alignment. After an initial local refinement with the corner mask, the C4-expanded particles were re-centered to the centers of the masks at each of the four corner regions of RyR1. These recentered particles were then downsized in a smaller box size (256 px) to shorten processing time. The horizontal diameter of the cytoplasmic compartment of RyR1 is ~400 Å. Since the four RY12 domains are in the corners of RyR1, adjusting per-particle defocus of the corner subparticles made improvement in the nominal resolution of local reconstructions of the corner subparticles40. Briefly, the Z component of a rotated recentering vector during a volume alignment was subtracted from the defocus for each subparticle. The defocus-adjusted subparticles were subjected to 3D classification and 3DVA after an initial focused local refinement (Supplementary Fig. 12).

For DanATP, DanADP, Physiological, and ADP4CmC datasets, initial volumes for 3D classification were generated from on-the-fly PCA results. For ATP4CmC and Fatigue datasets, 3D classification was initiated from intermediate frame reconstructions generated from a component representing the opening and the closing conformational changes on the RY12 domain calculated by 3DVA. The corner subparticles with the closed (bound) RY12 conformation were used for final local refinements with focus masks containing just the RY12 domain (amino acid residues 850–1054). These final local reconstructions of RY12 exhibit well-resolved ligands in the binding site as well as the distal strands of RY12 for Fatigue dataset.

After we separated the corner subparticles with the closed conformation, we counted how many times UIDs of unexpanded particles are referred in a selected subset of corner subparticles in a dataset41. This enabled analysis of occupancy and comparison of occupancy distributions of RyR1 particles in different datasets. RY12 occupancy was quantified and compared for all datasets except ATPMgCa and DanMg datasets, in which RyR1s do not assume the fully closed RY12 conformations.

The local maps from focused refinements of C4-expanded particles with different local masks around NTD-NSol, Cs2-JSol-CaM, and BSol-RY34 domain regions were aligned to the global consensus maps. The local maps were then combined in UCSF ChimeraX by taking the maximum value at each map voxel. The atomic structures of RyR1 were built and fitted in cryoEM maps in Coot42, and phenix.douse was used to build water in the maps with scc = 0.7–0.8, cc_mask_filter_threshold = 0.8, and map_threshold_scale = 0.5 parameters. The structures and EM maps were visualized using UCSF Chimera43 or ChimeraX. LocSpiral44 was used to obtain a map with improved connectivity at the very peripheral region of RY12 for the Fatigue dataset.

Reconstitution of purified RyR1-Cs2-CaM complex into liposomes

We used an on-column liposome formation protocol45 to incorporate RyR1 into proteoliposomes. Excess calstabin2 and CaM were added to RyR1 after the anion-exchange chromatography purification step. Proteins were incubated together overnight in 4 °C. A mixture of lipids (phosphatidylethanolamine (PE):phosphatidylcholine (PC) = 5:3, Avanti Polar Lipids) in 10% CHAPS was made and added to the proteins in a 1000:1 lipid:protein ratio. The sample was concentrated to 500 µL with a 100 kDa MWCO Amicon centrifugal filter unit and was then loaded onto a hand packed G50 column pre-equilibrated with buffer F (buffer E without CHAPS and DOPC). Proteoliposomal RyR1 was eluted at 0.25 mL/min in buffer F, and fractions corresponding to a single Gaussian peak were pooled and checked with SDS-PAGE. The pooled fractions were then concentrated to 5–6 mg/mL, flash frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored in −80 °C for later usage.

Single-channel recordings

Purified RyR1 proteoliposomes were fused to planar lipid bilayers formed by painting a lipid mixture of PE and PC in a 5:3 ratio in decane across a 200 µm hole in polysulfonate cups (Warner Instruments) separating 2 chambers. The trans chamber (1.0 mL), representing the intra-SR (luminal) compartment, was connected to the head stage input of a bilayer voltage clamp amplifier. The cis chamber (1.0 mL), representing the cytoplasmic compartment, was held at virtual ground. Asymmetrical solutions used were as follows for the cis solution: 1 mM EGTA, 250/125 mM HEPES/Tris, 50 mM KCl, pH 7.35; and for the trans solution: 53 mM Ca(OH)2, 50 mM KCl, 250 mM HEPES, pH 7.35. Purified RyR1 proteins were added to the cis side and fusion with the lipid bilayer was induced by making the cis side hyperosmotic by the addition of 400–500 mM KCl. After the appearance of potassium and chloride channels, the cis side was perfused with the cis solution. At the end of each experiment, 10 µM ryanodine was added to block the RyR channel. Single-channel currents were recorded at 0 mV using a Bilayer Clamp BC-525D (Warner Instruments), filtered at 1 kHz using a Low-Pass Bessel Filter 8 Pole (Warner Instruments), and digitized at 4 kHz. All experiments were performed at room temperature (23 °C). Data acquisition was performed by using Digidata 1322 A and Axoscope 10.1 software (Axon Instruments). The recordings were analyzed using Clampfit 10.1 (Molecular Devices) and Graphpad Prism software.

Expression of RyR1-W882A in HEK293T cells

A construct expressing RyR1-W882A was generated by introducing the mutation into fragments of rabbit RYR1, as described previously9. To briefly describe, HEK293T cells grown in 100-mm dishes with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen), penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 μg /ml), were transfected with 15 μg per dish of RyR1-W882A cDNA using PEI MAX (Polysciences). Cells were collected 48 h after transfection. ER vesicles from HEK293 cells expressing RyR1-W882A were prepared by homogenizing cell pellets on ice using a Teflon glass homogenizer with two volumes of solution containing 20 mM tris-maleate (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors (Roche). Homogenate was then centrifuged at 8000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 40,000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C. The final pellet, containing the ER fractions, was resuspended and aliquoted in 250 mM sucrose, 10 mM MOPS (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors. Samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.
