Explainable machine learning for prognostic modeling of waitlist mortality in cirrhotic liver transplantation

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) stands as the definitive intervention for patients with end-stage liver disease, offering a chance of extended survival and improved quality of life. Nevertheless, the scarcity of donor organs underscores the critical need for efficient prioritization strategies to manage transplant waitlists effectively.

Current organ allocation protocols for LT prioritize candidates by their predicted waitlist mortality, typically using statistical scoring systems that integrate key laboratory variables such as bilirubin, sodium, creatinine, and albumin. Since the introduction of the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) [1] in 2002 for donor liver allocation in the United States, efforts to refine predictive models for waitlist mortality have been unceasing. While MELD and its successors, MELD-Na [2] and MELD 3.0 [3], prioritize candidates based on objective clinical measures, they have inherent limitations. These regression-based models (or risk scores) assume a log-linear relationship between predictors and mortality [4]. However, variables like bilirubin or serum sodium may have nonlinear effects on risk [3]. To manage this, upper and lower bounds are often applied, potentially reducing accuracy, especially for patients with extreme values. Moreover, the performance of these scores varies across patient subgroups, with gender disparity and MELD exceptions limitations leading to inequities in LT allocation [5], [6], [7]. This suggests that the model’s fixed coefficients, assuming stable relationships between predictors and mortality over time and across patient populations, may not accurately and equally capture mortality risk. The use of fixed weights restricts the model’s adaptability and can oversimplify the complex and dynamic interaction of predictors that influence mortality risk assessment. Thus, further advancements in predictive modeling are needed to enhance accuracy and fairness.

Data science and machine learning (ML) have emerged as powerful tools for predicting LT candidate prioritization and waitlist mortality [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Unlike traditional models, ML models can capture complex nonlinear relationships among clinical variables and temporal variability in patient data. In addition, they can incorporate a larger set of predictors, potentially enhancing individual-level risk estimation across different prediction time frames. However, existing ML-based studies face key limitations that restrict their clinical applicability. For instance, in Ref. [10], demographic biases arise due to the severe under-representation of women (only 3 % of the study population), limiting model generalizability to a broader population. In Ref. [11], the study suffers from data leakage, where patient data appear in both training and test sets, inducing a bias in performance estimates. In addition, in Ref. [11], [12], [14], [16], ML algorithms are applied to numerous features without assessing their relevance to the decision-support process. This can lead models to rely on highly correlated features, masking important relationships and complicating clinical interpretability.

Additionally, most prior works overlook two critical issues. First, many studies lack transparency in their models’ decision-making processes, making it challenging for clinicians to understand and act upon model predictions [11], [12], [13], [14]. Therefore, the lack of built-in explainability limits clinical trust and real-world applicability. Second, although 3-month mortality remains the primary criterion for liver allocation in many countries, longer-term risk estimation (e.g., at 6 and 12 months) is also clinically relevant [10], [16]. Indeed, patients who survive past the initial high-risk period may still experience significant deterioration, and understanding mortality risk beyond 3 months can support dynamic waitlist management and strategic allocation planning.

Thus, building on prior studies on ML, particularly [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], this study aims to advance both accuracy and explainability of waitlist mortality predictions in LT by: (i) proposing a novel mortality score using tree-based ensemble learning, developed through a comprehensive comparative evaluation of traditional MELD-based scores and various ML models; (ii) exploiting a model-agnostic explainable method [17] to enhance transparency in model decision-making and unveil key mortality risk factors; (iii) expanding the prediction horizons to 3, 6, and 12 months to assess both the predictive accuracy and the influence of clinical factors on mortality risk over time. To our knowledge, this is the first in-depth study that jointly addresses these different aspects. Our work contributes to refining waitlist mortality predictions and reinforcing data-driven decision-making in LT by promoting model explainability; thereby improving the usability and real-world applicability of our model.

Materials and methods

UNOS/OPTN registry data description

This study uses data from the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) and the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS), as found in the Standard Transplant Analysis and Research (STAR) file. The OPTN/UNOS database continuously updates patient records, leading to the creation of multiple entries for a single patient. We considered adult patients listed from February 27, 2002, to September 30, 2023 (N=259,081).

For the study, patients were included if they (i) died while on the waitlist or were removed for being too sick for transplant within the specified time period (waitlist mortality class), or (ii) survived on the waitlist beyond the designated period (On waiting list class). We excluded patients who received the transplant before each studied period, as their true waitlist outcome could not be ascertained in the absence of transplantation. Patients with insufficient follow-up to determine outcome were excluded to avoid outcome misclassification. Candidates with MELD exceptions, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), or those listed for multi-organ transplants were also excluded [11], [12], [13], [14].

To study mortality risk predictions over 3, 6 and 12 months, we retrieved three corresponding subsets. Cohort composition, including the number of patients and observations (obs., visits), is reported in Fig. 1, with detailed characteristics in Table S1. For numerical variables, missing values (less than 7 % of the data) were imputed using the MissForest algorithm, a non-parametric random forest-based multiple imputation method [18]. For categorical variables, we removed observations with missing data because they represented less than 0.02 % of the total data. To capture the dynamic nature of patient health, we computed the difference between consecutive measurements for each patient for five key laboratory variables, namely albumin (1st variable in Table 1), bilirubin (2nd), international normalized ratio (3rd), creatinine (4th), and serum sodium (5th). This resulted in dynamic variables tracking the direction of intra-patient change (positive for increases, negative for decreases, and null for stagnation). Additionally, variables related to various forms of life support and medical interventions were combined into a single variable, “life_sup_comb” (20th). Categorical variables were transformed using one-hot encoding [19], with the first category omitted to prevent multicollinearity, resulting in the creation of a binary column for each category.

Methodology

This study targets predicting mortality within 3, 6 and 12 months on the waiting list, using on one hand the three MELD-based scores (MELD, MELD-Na and MELD 3.0), and on the other hand four ML-based classifiers: (i) Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) [20], (ii) Neural Network architecture for tabular data including sequential attention mechanisms (TabNet) [21], (iii) Random Forest (RF) [22], and (iv) Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) [23]. We used different ML-based models to investigate the predictive power of a linear classifier (LDA), as well as more complex and non-linear models relying on neural networks (TabNet) and tree-based ensemble models (RF and LightGBM). Fig. 2 illustrates the end-to-end methodology used to develop and explain ML-based models in our study.

Due to class imbalance, the majority class was down-sampled into 23 partitions with equivalent size to minority class. Each partition was designed to contain the same number of observations as those present in the minority class and to include different patients from the majority class. Thus, multiple subsets of balanced data were generated for each period. Then, for each subset, we performed a 3-fold cross-validation procedure to train and evaluate the classifier, ensuring that all observations from a single patient were either in the training or test set, thereby avoiding data leakage. These evaluation metrics were computed for each of the three folds for all 23 balanced subsets and then averaged across the resulting 69 training–test evaluations. The optimal decision threshold for models was determined to maximize both sensitivity and specificity in the ROC curve using Youden Index [27].

After evaluating the ML models using the full set of 27 features, we further investigated the best model’s predictive performance when restricted to the most relevant subset of features. For this purpose, we employed the SHapley Additive explanation (SHAP) method [17], derived from game theory, for feature selection. Specifically, the computed SHAP values assess a particular feature’s contribution to the prediction outcome of the classifier by comparing such predictions with and without the feature across all possible feature combinations. This allowed the identification of the most important features and provided insights into how they influence the model’s predictions [28], [29]. To ensure robust feature selection, SHAP rankings were computed across cross-validation folds and aggregated to form a consensus order. Features were then added sequentially to verify that including more than the top-ranked subset did not significantly improve performance.

Statistical analysis

Each model was trained and evaluated on 23 balanced data partitions using 3-fold cross-validation, yielding a total of 69 values per metric. These values were used to assess the significance of performance differences between models. Comparisons between models were conducted for each performance metric using the Kruskal-Wallis test (α = 0.05) [30], [31]. If significant differences were detected, pairwise Dunn’s tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (α = 0.05) were applied to control the false discovery rate [32].

SHAP for model-agnostic explainability

To understand the contributions of individual variables to the best ML model’s predictions, SHAP values were computed. SHAP analysis clarifies the model’s decision-making process and highlights key mortality predictors [28], [29].

SHAP is a post hoc explanation method, operating at the observation level (local level, namely a visit in our case) or at the population level (global level) [17]. It computes the SHAP values for the feature i at visit j, given a model f and a vector x of features to be explained:

where ϕij(f,x) denotes the SHAP value for feature i at visit j, with S representing subsets of all features excluding i. fx(S∪{i}) and fx(S) are respectively the model predictions with and without feature i.

Therefore, SHAP values quantify each feature’s impact on the model’s output by calculating a weighted average of the feature’s marginal contributions across all possible combinations of features [17].

where n is the number of observations.

Because SHAP computations are intensive, we used TreeExplainer [33], suitable for tree-based models. It computes the Shapley values only for relevant nodes, significantly enhancing the computational speed.

Results

MELD-based scores vs. ML models

Table 3 shows that MELD-Na significantly outperforms both the original MELD score and MELD 3.0 in terms of AUROC within the 3-month period (0.887 compared to 0.881 for MELD (p = 7.63 × 10–11) and 0.883 for MELD 3.0 (p = 3 × 10–6)). MELD-Na and MELD 3.0 also show a good balance between sensitivity and specificity, both significantly surpassing MELD in sensitivity (p = 1.97 × 10–18 for MELD-Na and p = 1.38 × 10–14 for MELD 3.0). These findings indicate that MELD-Na and MELD 3.0 outperform the original MELD score in identifying high-risk cases within 3 months. For the 6- and 12-month periods, the performance of the three MELD-based scores declined, consistent with longer-term uncertainty. However, MELD-Na and MELD 3.0 maintained significantly higher AUROC values than MELD.

When training LDA, TabNet, RF and LightGBM on the full set of 27 variables, all ML models achieved better predictive performance than MELD-based scores across all time periods. In particular, ML models showed significantly higher sensitivity, along with improved precision, indicating superior capability to detect high-risk cases. Although the performance of ML models decreased slightly over longer horizons, the decline was less pronounced than with the MELD-based scores.

Among the ML classifiers, LightGBM consistently achieved the best performance, with the highest AUROC and sensitivity values, followed by RF, TabNet and LDA. LightGBM reached AUROC values of 0.923, 0.897, and 0.877 for the 3-, 6-, and 12-month periods, respectively. Notably, these gains in discrimination with LightGBM were attained without sacrificing balance between sensitivity and specificity, and its optimized design reduces computational and memory requirements. These findings suggest that LightGBM could help clinicians to more accurately identify cases at higher risk of waitlist mortality, supporting fair and effective organ allocation.

Identification of relevant risk factors across the three periods

We assessed the relevance of the 27 variables for mortality prediction in each time period using the SHAP method. Since LightGBM outperformed the other models, we conducted feature analysis only on this model. Fig. 3 displays the feature importance rankings based on the corresponding SHAP values. Variables are displayed following the order obtained in the 3-month period. For comparative purposes across periods, we applied a min-max scaler to normalize the absolute SHAP values of variables to a range between 0 and 1 for each time period.

Feature importance of LightGBM with SHAP method over the three periods. The 27 variables are displayed following the order obtained for the 3-month period.

SHAP analysis revealed consistent top predictors across time periods, with MELD components such as bilirubin, INR, serum creatinine, serum sodium and albumin emerging as critical. Interestingly, age at registration (INIT_AGE), patient’s functional state and degree of ascites emerged as important predictors, although they are not included in MELD scores (refer to Section 2.4). This finding highlights a gap in traditional models and emphasizes the importance of considering these variables in future risk scoring systems.

Among all predictors, bilirubin consistently ranked as the most influential feature across the 3-, 6-, and 12-month periods. However, the relative importance of other features varied over time. Specifically, the contribution of serum sodium, age at registration, and albumin increased at longer prediction windows. In contrast, the influence of variables such as functional status and dialysis decreased over time. The evolving importance of risk factors across different time periods underlines the importance of dynamic modeling strategies.

Feature selection for an optimized LightGBM-based score

Based on the SHAP-derived feature ranking, we analyzed the performance of the LightGBM model by progressively incorporating the relevant features one by one. Then, we identified the point at which additional features no longer significantly enhanced performance. The analysis showed that beyond the top 10 features for the 3-month period, and 9 features for the 6- and 12-month periods, no significant improvement was observed across any performance metrics, when compared to using the full set of 27 features (p > 0.05 for all metrics). This indicates that additional features offer minimal benefits and can potentially introduce noise or overfitting. Based on these findings, these feature numbers were selected as the optimal cutoff points for each period.

Table 4 presents the predictive performance of the LightGBM when trained with the selected feature set for each period. Notably, these reduced sets of variables achieved nearly the same AUROC scores as the 27 variables, with AUROCs of 0.921, 0.892, and 0.872 for the 3-, 6-, and 12-month periods, respectively. This highlights the substantial predictive value of the selected variables thanks to SHAP. We thus selected this reduced set of features to design our optimized ensemble learning-based score, hereafter referred to as the ELTM (Ensemble Learning Transplant Mortality) score.

Evaluation of the ELTM scoring system

The ELTM score showed the lowest Brier scores and the highest Brier Skill Scores for 3-, 6-, and 12-month horizons, reflecting superior probability estimation compared to MELD-based scores (see Table 5). Fig. 4 shows that all scores were reasonably well calibrated with slopes close to 1 and intercepts close to 0 across all horizons. However, MELD-based scores exhibited an increasingly S-shaped pattern with longer prediction horizons, underestimating low risks and overestimating high risks, whereas ELTM predictions closely followed the 45∘ reference line. Decision curve analysis consistently demonstrated higher net benefit for ELTM across a wide range of decision threshold probabilities (see Fig. 4). This advantage was also evident at intermediate decision thresholds, as frequently encountered in clinical decision-making. This highlights that ELTM not only enhances risk discrimination but also delivers more clinically actionable predictions for patient management.

Validation on excluded transplanted patients

To further evaluate model generalizability, we performed an internal validation on patients excluded from the main study cohorts because they underwent LT before the 3-, 6-, or 12-month prediction horizons. These patients, not used in model development, represent an independent validation population with distinct clinical trajectories. Indeed, they reflect patients who progressed rapidly to transplantation and thus were among the sickest on the waitlist. Fig. 6 shows the evolution of the estimated waitlist mortality risk over time leading up to transplantation for these patients.

Estimated waitlist mortality probability over time for patients who underwent liver transplantation prior to the (a) 3-, (b) 6-, and (c) 12-months prediction horizons.

Across all prediction periods, estimated mortality risk increased as transplantation approached, with narrowing confidence intervals, indicating reduced uncertainty in advanced illness stages. This pattern reflects that physiological signs become stronger and more informative in the period immediately preceding transplant, increasing model confidence during this critical period. Importantly, ELTM consistently predicted higher risk than MELD, MELD-Na, and MELD 3.0, suggesting greater sensitivity to early and subtle decompensation. Moreover, despite these patients being excluded from model training, the ELTM trajectories remained well-separated from the MELD-based trajectories, underscoring ELTM’s robustness and ability to generalize beyond the derivation cohorts.

Assessing 3-month patient prioritization on the transplant waitlist

We further assessed the clinical relevance of the ELTM risk score for LT waitlist prioritization through a comparative analysis against each of the three conventional MELD-based scores—MELD, MELD-Na, and MELD 3.0. The analysis focused on 3-month mortality outcomes, to align with standard clinical practice and allocation policies, which assess short-term risk when making listing and organ-offer decisions.

To enable patient-level comparisons, visit-wise predictions were aggregated by averaging predicted probabilities (soft voting), producing a single summary risk score per patient. The final classification results for each model are reported in Table 6.

At very low prioritization thresholds (top 1–2 %), mortality rates in patients uniquely prioritized by ELTM and MELD-based scores were similarly high (near 100 %). Overall, our findings underscore the robustness of our score across various prioritization scenarios and its enhanced ability to identify patients at greatest risk of waitlist mortality, aligning with the core objective of liver allocation policy.

Notably, our LightGBM-based score accurately recovers the clinically accepted normal range (135–145 mmol/L) purely from data, further supporting its validity and reinforcing its trustworthiness [34]. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of several ML models for predicting 3-, 6-, and 12-month waitlist mortality among LT candidates. Such evaluation was performed in comparison to traditional MELD-based scores. The aim of the study is threefold: (i) to assess the ability of the ML models in predicting mortality on the waiting list across three prediction windows; (ii) to identify at each period the most important prognostic factors among the clinical, laboratory, and disease-related variables; (iii) to explain the models’s decisions based on the identified risk factors.

Our results showed that MELD-Na and MELD 3.0 significantly outperform the original MELD score at 3 months. Although the accuracy of all three declines at 6 and 12 months, MELD-Na and MELD 3.0 consistently maintain their advantage over MELD. This confirms the pertinence of revising the MELD score by adding serum sodium, albumin and gender information.

In prior work [13], we showed that ML models, specifically LDA, TabNet, RF and LightGBM, trained only on MELD components already outperformed the MELD scores for 3-month waitlist mortality. Here, we retrained the same models using a richer set of 27 clinical variables and observed even greater gains in AUROC and sensitivity at 3, 6, and 12 months. This underscores two key limitations of MELD scores: their linear modeling framework and their restricted feature set. Indeed, MELD scores rely on linear models that assign a fixed weight to each variable independently of its value or its interaction with others.

Among the ML approaches, non-linear models (TabNet, RF and LightGBM) outperformed the linear LDA, highlighting the need to capture complex non-linear interactions in liver disease progression. LightGBM, the best performing model, naturally models such nonlinearities, aligning with recent calls for more data-driven and personalized risk assessment [8], [12], [13]. Moreover, its superior performance over TabNet aligns with broader findings that tree-based models are particularly well-suited to handle heterogeneous tabular data and actually outperform deep learning approaches [36].

Furthermore, we evaluated our LightGBM model across patient subgroups and found that it consistently outperformed MELD-based scores across all time periods. Notably, it demonstrated more balanced performance across ethnic and gender subgroups, suggesting a potential role in reducing disparities in liver allocation. By incorporating a broader range of risk factors, LightGBM enables a more comprehensive and equitable risk assessment in transplant prioritization. Nonetheless, because our model is trained on historical allocation data, it may inherit existing biases; prospective validation and careful monitoring will be essential to ensure that ELTM mitigates rather than maintains inequities in access to transplantation. Several recent models address sex bias in liver allocation (e.g., GEMA/GEMA-Na [37] and MELD-GRAIL-Na [38], [39]). These could not be evaluated in our study because UNOS lacks blood urea nitrogen (BUN/urea) and other inputs required for RFH-GFR computation. Future work will compare ELTM with these models in datasets containing the required variables.

Our study also contributes to model explainability in medical applications. Actually, unlike previous studies that only displayed the importance of features [11], [12], [13], [14], our study exploited SHAP to clarify the impact of the variables on the model’s predictions. We detailed how specific features contribute to the model’s decision-making process. This approach helps clinicians to better understand the model’s underlying mechanisms and quantify the influence of individual variables. These local explanations and dependence plots revealed that individual risk factors contribute differently across visits, whereas traditional scores apply fixed weights to all individuals, resulting in a loss of personalization.

By considering only the top relevant features identified through SHAP to train the LightGBM model, we achieved similar predictive performance as when 27 variables were considered. Our proposed ELTM score was derived from this optimized model. It is noteworthy that the number of top variables remains almost the same across the time periods.

Our feature analysis with SHAP affirmed that MELD-based components, namely bilirubin, international normalized ratio, serum creatinine, serum sodium and albumin have a high contribution in predicting waitlist mortality, and that across the three periods. However, other factors not included in MELD scores appeared to be of high interest, such as patient’s age at registration, degree of ascites, bilirubin dynamics, and functional status as measured by the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale in UNOS registry. These findings suggest that broader systemic complications and overall physiological condition are critical determinants of waitlist mortality, revealing important limitations in the current MELD-based scoring systems. Indeed, recent analyses and cohort studies have independently validated that ascites severity [40], [41], advanced age [42], [43], KPS-measured functional status [44], and temporal changes in bilirubin [11], [45] each exert significant, independent effects on waitlist mortality.

Furthermore, our results showed different feature relevance over time: while factors such as functional status and dialysis declined in importance, others such as serum sodium, age at registration, albumin, and ascites severity became more important. This dynamic shift in feature importance over time highlights a key limitation of traditional MELD-based models, which rely on fixed weights and assume stable relationships between variables across time periods. All these results underscore the robustness and efficacy of our ELTM score in capturing the complex interactions and dynamics among risk factors for more accurate risk scoring systems.

The patient-level comparative evaluation demonstrated that the ELTM score improves short-term risk assessment compared to all three MELD scores. Indeed, ELTM improved both sensitivity and specificity, reducing missed deaths and avoiding unnecessary prioritizations; thereby increasing overall classification accuracy. In head-to-head comparisons across multiple prioritization thresholds, ELTM outperformed the MELD scores’ ability to identify the sickest patients by uncovering additional high-risk cases as the priority threshold increased, resulting in a significant advantage for allocating scarce organs. Together, these findings underline that our ELTM score can improve risk assessment, enhance equity in organ allocation, and better direct transplants to those most likely to benefit. Notably, when evaluated on excluded transplant patients, an independent validation population, ELTM score demonstrated earlier and clearer risk signals than MELD-based scores, thus supporting its ability to detect clinically meaningful deterioration prior to late-stage laboratory changes. In addition, ELTM provides a continuous probability of waitlist mortality that can be integrated into existing allocation systems through validated risk thresholds. Initially, ELTM could complement MELD-based scores to guide prioritization decisions pending further prospective validation.

Our study suffers from some limitations. One limitation of this study is the absence of external validation. While our internal validation strategy (cross-validation across multiple balanced subsets) supports the robustness of our findings within the UNOS registry, external validation on other cohorts from different healthcare systems (e.g., in Europe or Asia) will be necessary to confirm the generalizability of our model. Additionally, this study excluded patients with MELD exception scores and those with HCC to focus on comparing the performance of the explainable ML model with MELD-based scores. While methodologically consistent with prior work, this may bias results toward candidates whose risk is captured by laboratory-based variables. Patients with HCC and exception listings often have unique trajectories and may not be captured with the same efficacy by models trained on standard cohorts. Future work should focus on validating the model in excluded populations or developing tailored models. Moreover, as the dataset spans more than two decades during which allocation policies and clinical practices evolved, temporal drift may have influenced model performance. Future analyses using era-specific stratification or contemporary cohorts will be important to confirm robustness over time. Also, class imbalance was addressed by downsampling the majority class into balanced subsets. While this approach improved minority class representation, future work could explore methods such as class weighting, or cost-sensitive learning. Also, we used fixed 3-, 6-, and 12-month outcomes rather than a full time-to-event competing-risks model, which does not fully account for censoring or transplantation as a competing event. Future work will extend the model to survival-based approaches. Lastly, although we computed differences between consecutive observations, this approach does not capture the full evolution of a patient’s health over time. A time-series analysis would provide a more comprehensive view of a patient’s condition, enabling better predictive outcomes.

Conclusion

Our work demonstrated the potential of ML models, particularly LightGBM, to outperform traditional scoring systems in predicting LT waitlist mortality. By leveraging the power of SHAP, we offered a granular and a more nuanced understanding of mortality risk factors, paving the way for advancements in LT allocation policies. Our study advocated for a paradigm shift toward incorporating ML techniques in clinical decision-making, promising a future where organ allocation is both more precise and equitable.
