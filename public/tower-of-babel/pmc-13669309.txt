Efficient power generation near room temperature in earth-abundant thermoelectric tin sulfide via lattice ordering
Authors: Muhammad Yusuf Fakhri, Abhishek Ghosh, Wei-Ching Lai, Duc-Long Nguyen, Hans Hong-Ming Jheng, Suneesh Meledath Valiyaveettil, Peramaiyan Ganesan, Raman Sankar, Ching-Yueh Huang, Chien-Ching Chang, Cheng-Maw Cheng, Chien-Neng Liao, Bo-Chia Chen, Hsin-Jay Wu, Zi-Liang Yang, Ya-Ping Chiu, Ming-Hao Lee, Ming-Wen Chu, Shin Sato, Kei Hayashi, Carmelo Prestipino, Emmanuel Guilmeau, Li-Chyong Chen, Kuei-Hsien Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The inefficient carrier transport of earth-abundant tin sulfide (SnS), arising from its intrinsically low crystal symmetry, limits its thermoelectric performance at low temperatures. Here, the substitutional Ag+ doping modifies the Na-doped SnS crystal structure by reinforcing lattice dynamical anisotropy and enhancing in-plane transport properties. Concurrently, the reconstructed valence-band structure evolves into a modulated multiband configuration, increasing the density-of-states effective mass while preserving high carrier mobility. The resulting highly metallic conductivity combined with high thermopower yields the room-temperature power factor of ~101 µW cm−1K−2 in Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S single crystals. Coupled with the intrinsically low thermal conductivity of SnS, the optimized crystal achieves the figure of merit (zT) of ~1.06 at 300 K and ~2.0 at 573 K, delivering a conversion efficiency of ~3.3% and an output power density of ~0.31 W cm−2 in a single-leg module under a 243 K temperature gradient. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of crystal structure modification and multiband engineering in SnS and establish it as a highly promising low-cost thermoelectric material for near-room-temperature energy conversion.

Introduction

Thermoelectric (TE) devices offer a unique solid-state route for heat-to-electricity conversion, enabling compact and maintenance-free energy harvesting via the Seebeck effect. Conversely, the Peltier effect provides on-chip and electronics cooling without the need for refrigerant1,2. Despite these inherent advantages, the widespread deployment of thermoelectrics has been hindered by the sluggish progress in developing the conversion efficiency of the semiconductor materials, which is determined by the dimensionless figure of merit, zT = σS2T/(κl+κe), where T is the absolute temperature3,4. The three transport parameters, electrical conductivity (σ), Seebeck coefficient (S), and the thermal conductivity from the electronic part (κe), are strongly interdependent, making the optimization challenging. Therefore, gaining zT typically focuses on minimizing the independent variable of lattice thermal conductivity κl5, while simultaneously maximizing the power factor (PF = σS2)6,7.

In recent years, investigations on high-performance TE materials operating near room temperature (RT) have intensified, as evidenced by the substantial progress in several systems, including Mg3(Bi, Sb)2–based alloys8,9, Heuslers10,11, and other emerging chalcogenides12,13. Parallel efforts in flexible and microscale TE platforms have further expanded the scope of thermoelectrics for wearable and on-chip cooling applications14. However, to date, the feasibility of commercialized devices still relies predominantly on Bi2Te3-based materials, whose dependence on tellurium presents critical challenges due to its scarcity, high cost, and toxicity15,16. This urgency to move away from tellurium has motivated the exploration of alternative systems based on earth-abundant and environmentally benign elements capable of delivering competitive performance.

In this regard, layered tin chalcogenides, including tin selenide (SnSe)17–19 and tin sulfide (SnS)20,21, have emerged as highly attractive candidates delivering high zT values for low to mid-temperatures. SnS, while structurally analogous, offers practical advantages, including lower toxicity, lower cost, and greater elemental abundance20,21. Aliovalent doping by substitutional cations (Na+ and Ag+) has been proven effective for tailoring the carrier density and enhancing p-type TE efficiency22–25. On the other hand, isovalent doping at the S site by Se promotes the enhancement of PF through band structure modification and κl reduction by lowering the phonon group velocity26,27. However, in conventional polycrystalline SnS, the peak TE performance typically appears at elevated temperatures, mainly due to strong carrier scattering at grain boundaries that limits carrier mobility (μ) near RT. This effect is further reinforced by the structural phase transition from the low-symmetry Pnma phase to the high-symmetry Cmcm phase at high temperature, which enhances μ and shifts optimal performance toward higher temperatures25,27. In contrast, the single-crystalline counterpart eliminates grain-boundary scattering and exposes the intrinsic transport behavior of the lattice, enabling superior TE performance near RT. For example, SnS0.9Se0.1, optimized by controlled sulfur enrichment through SnS2 addition, exhibits a power factor of ~58 μW cm−1 K−2 at 300 K and reaches a peak zT of ~1.6 at 573 K28. However, these strategies, which primarily relied on increasing the carrier concentration (n), inevitably compromised μ due to Sn vacancies, thereby limiting further advancement near RT.

This limitation motivated us to implement an aliovalent co-doping strategy in single-crystalline SnS that preserves efficient carrier transport even at elevated n, owing to valence-band reconstruction. Na⁺ is first introduced in this SnS to regulate the hole concentration, providing a direct handle on band degeneracy. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, pristine SnS exhibits four valence bands (VBs) distributed along the Y–Γ–Z–U directions in momentum space, with their maxima located relatively close to the Fermi level. However, n achieved by Na doping alone is insufficient to activate all VBs for multi-valley transport, due to the relatively large band offset (~0.1 eV ≈ 4 kBT). Higher Ag concentrations enhance the band evolution, where VB3 begins to converge and merge with VB1 into a single dispersive feature, as confirmed by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES). Qin et al. reported a similar phenomenon in Pb-alloyed SnSe, known as a “pudding-mold” band structure arising from multiband synglisis18. While similar modulation has been demonstrated in SnSe, this work presents an effective strategy for valence-band reconstruction in lower-cost, more environmentally friendly SnS single crystals29. Additionally, the merging band flattens the valence-band maximum, enhancing the effective mass (m⁎) while preserving high μ by suppressing intervalley scattering, with additional μ gains arising from the convergence of the light band (VB3) at the Y–Z valleys19,27. The band convergence, arising from crystal structure modification, is accompanied by a reduced deformation potential (Ξ), enabling efficient carrier transport even within a flattened band structure. This modulated valence-band configuration in SnS simultaneously promotes higher carrier mobility (μ) and enhances the density-of-states (DOS) effective mass, md⁎. As a result, the intrinsic coupling between μ and md⁎ is effectively mitigated, yielding a high weighted mobility, μw ≈ μ (md⁎me) 3/2, of ~1286 cm² V−1 s−1 at RT.

Within this low-temperature range, the lattice thermal conductivity (κₗ) exhibits a modest reduction arising from lattice-structure modification, specifically Sn–S bond-angle distortion and direction-dependent phonon softening, which strengthens phonon scattering while leaving σ essentially unaffected. As shown in Fig. 1b, Sn0.99−xNa0.01AgxS single crystals exhibit a significant enhancement in μw/κl, yielding a high quality factor (B ∝ μw/κl) at RT. The pronounced increase in μw/κl with Hall carrier concentration (nH) indicates a synergistic balance between charge transport and phonon scattering. Consequently, our p-type Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S achieved a zT peak of ~2 at 573 K (Fig. 1c), surpassing those of previously reported single crystals20,21,27,28 and establishing this system as the best-performing tin sulfide–based TE material to date.

Results and Discussion

Enhanced TE transport properties

Figure 2 summarizes the TE transport properties measured along the crystallographic b-axis, which exhibits the highest performance in this system20,21. High-quality single crystals were synthesized using a modified Bridgman method and subsequently cut into well-defined geometries for transport measurements (see Supplementary Fig. 1). Phase confirmation and crystallographic orientation were confirmed by X-ray diffraction and Laue back-reflection analysis (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3), which verified the orthorhombic Pnma structure and accurately resolved the principal crystal axes. Ag incorporation induces a pronounced evolution of S (Fig. 2a), which increases from ~224 to ~246 µV K−1 at RT as Ag concentration rises from 0 to 0.04, despite the simultaneous rise in measured nH. Moreover, σ at RT improved gradually up to ~1700 S cm−1 for x = 0.03 (Fig. 2b), which is the highest value reported to date among SnS single crystals27,28. The modest decrease of σ with temperature reflects the expected acoustic-phonon transport, consistent with T−1.5 behavior of μw shown in Fig. 2b inset. Despite the large Ag nominal (x) in this compound, the positive trend of σ and nH confirmed the successful cationic substitution in the crystals. However, with further increase in Ag concentration, Ag aggregation becomes more pronounced, as revealed by the elemental mapping results (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5 and Supplementary Table 1). In particular, microscale Ag-rich regions become clearly observable for x = 0.04, leading to reductions in both nH and μw, likely due to the reduced efficiency of electrically active Ag acceptors with enhanced carrier scattering induced by the aggregated regions. Meanwhile, Agx Sny -related impurity phases are barely detectable in the synchrotron radiation powder X-ray diffraction (SR-PXRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 6). The possible SnS2 secondary phase associated with Sn deficiency coincides with the main (400) diffraction peak of SnS, making its identification challenging. Overall, the substantial improvement of σ and S resulted in the enhancement of PF through this Ag incorporation (Fig. 2c). For thermal transport measurements, the total thermal conductivity (κtot) was determined from the measured thermal diffusivity, heat capacity, and density (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 2). As shown in Fig. 2d, κtot decreases with increasing temperature for all compositions due to increased phonon–phonon scattering (Umklapp). Ag incorporation leads to a slight change of κtot across the entire temperature range, though the variation remains modest compared to the SnS crystal (x = 0). The electronic contribution to thermal transport was evaluated by calculating the Lorenz number (L) using the multiple-parabolic-band (MPB) model and estimating the electronic thermal conductivity (κe = LσT), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. The corresponding sensitivity analysis of the extracted lattice thermal conductivity (κl) with respect to the Lorenz-number estimation is provided in Supplementary Fig. 9. Applying the relation (κl = κtot – κe), the extracted κl reveals a clear reduction with increasing Ag concentration, particularly for x = 0.03. This suppression arises from enhanced phonon scattering due to mass fluctuation and local lattice strain introduced by Ag substitution30,31, consistent with Callaway’s simulation (Supplementary Methods) of κl. The observed T−1 further confirms Umklapp scattering as the dominant heat transport mechanism32.

Modifications in the crystal structure

To confirm the TE properties associated with crystal structure modifications, we investigated the structural evolution using high-resolution SR-PXRD with quantitative structural analysis, through Rietveld refinement, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 10. The lattice parameters were evaluated by Le Bail fit to decorrelate the information with respect to the structure (see results in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). A key indicator of structural change is the angle (θ) between the Sn–S bond and the a-axis, calculated from the refined atomic positions. As shown in Fig. 3a, θ progressively decreases with increasing Ag content, confirming a systematic modification of the crystal structure. Further structural insight with Sn K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) of Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S shows a subtle shift of the spectrum edge toward higher energy relative to Ag-free SnS (Fig. 3b). This behavior indicates only a slight modification of the local Sn electronic environment by dopant incorporation, consistent with a small increase in Debye-Waller factor (σ2) in Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S sample (See fitting results in Supplementary Figs. 11–13 and Supplementary Table 5). Figure 3c compares the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra at both the Sn and Ag K-edges for the Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S sample. Both Fourier transforms of the EXAFS signal show strong similarities, suggesting the successful substitution of Ag on the Sn site. Based on such observations, both signals have been fitted using SnS as a structural model, allowing an isotropic and linear expansion of interatomic distances. In this model, a contraction of ~3.7% of Ag–S bond distance with respect to Sn–S distances is observed (see Supplementary Table 5). This contraction is consistent with the reduction in unit-cell volume determined by X-ray diffraction.

Raman spectroscopy provides further evidence of lattice dynamical modifications induced by Ag incorporation in Sn0.99-xNa0.01AgxS. Figure 3d, e present the Raman spectra and the extracted peak positions of the out-of-plane (Ag1and Ag3) and in-plane (Ag2 and B3g) vibrational modes as a function of Ag content, with schematic illustrations of the corresponding vibrational patterns shown in Fig. 3f. With increasing Ag concentration, all four Raman-active modes exhibit a systematic red shift, indicating phonon softening associated with the progressive introduction of chemical defects32,33. Notably, the in-plane vibrational modes display only slight softening, whereas the red shifts of the out-of-plane modes are more pronounced. This anisotropic phonon renormalization reflects enhanced vibrational anisotropy at higher Ag content, characterized by a stronger in-plane vibrational component. Such behavior is consistent with the observed structural distortions and is expected to reduce the phonon mean free path, thereby suppressing the κₗ while preserving favorable electrical transport properties34–36.

In addition, microstructural analysis of the Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S crystal by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) reveals local structural features associated with lattice modification induced by Ag incorporation (Fig. 4a–c). High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) STEM imaging at higher magnification clearly shows the displacement of Sn atoms (Fig. 4b), consistent with the systematic evolution of the Sn–S bond angle θ and the accompanying lattice modulation. Atomic-resolution imaging in Fig. 4c further identifies dislocation cores and locally distorted Sn–S coordination environments, which are corroborated by elemental mapping using electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS). The Sn atomic displacements resolved by EELS provide direct structural evidence for lattice reconstruction in Ag-doped SnS. Such distortions are expected to introduce spatial fluctuations in local strain gradients, leading to systematic deviations of θ in layered crystal systems37,38. Electronic band structure measurement via ARPES

ARPES measurements were performed at RT using a photon energy of 92 eV to experimentally probe the evolution of the valence-band structure in Sn0.99−xNa0.01AgxS for selected compositions (x = 0, 0.01, and 0.03), as shown in Fig. 5a, b. The energy dispersion (E) along the Γ–Z momentum path (k) was resolved to examine the first valence-band maximum (VBM1), which predominantly governs the electrical transport properties of SnS (Fig. 5a). For all compositions, the VBM lies close to the Fermi energy (EF), indicating that SnS is already in a degenerate regime. Upon the introduction of substitutional Ag, the valence bands become more clearly distinguishable, revealing a pronounced evolution of the band structure. Along the Γ–Z k-path, another light band appears at relatively lower binding energy (around −0.2 eV from EF), here labeled as VB3 (based on the schematic in Fig. 1a). The corresponding energy distribution curve (EDC) analysis is provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. This band is separated from VB1 by approximately 0.2 Å−1 in k-space. Once Ag is introduced, VB3 begins to converge, especially for x = 0.03, where it approaches close to EF with a significantly reduced band offset (ΔE) between VBM1 and VBM3. This evolution shifts VBM1 away from the Z-point (by ~0.06 Å−1), confirming the k-space alignment of this extremum and indicating the formation of a pudding-mold–type band structure18. Such band convergence is expected to enhance the effective valence-band degeneracy (Nv). The noticeable band flattening results in an increased band effective mass (m∗/me), particularly at higher Ag concentrations. By fitting the second derivative of the ARPES intensity maps using the E(k) dispersion relation (see Eq. (1) in Methods), m∗/me values were extracted for different Ag contents (see Supplementary Table 6). The Na-doped SnS sample (x = 0) exhibits m∗/me ≈ 0.34 ± 0.02, which increases to 0.45 ± 0.08 in Sn0.96 Na0.01Ag0.03S at VBM1. Consequently, band flattening together with enhanced degeneracy (Nv) effectively increases S, consistent with the Pisarenko plot of nH vs S based on the single parabolic band (SPB) model (Supplementary Fig. 15). In this plot, Ag-rich samples fall along the theoretical line corresponding to a larger DOS effective mass (md* = Nv2/3m*) of 1.97me. Electronic band structure calculation via DFT

To elucidate the electronic band structure evolution induced by crystal structure modification and Ag incorporation in Sn0.99-xNa0.01AgxS single crystals, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed, as shown in Fig. 5c, d. The extended band structure along Z-U-X and the elemental contributions to the band structure are provided in the supplementary Figs. 16 and 17. The corresponding unfolded band structures were constructed using 144-atom supercells to directly accommodate the experimental compositions (see Supplementary methods for the details). For comparison, the SnS band structure is calculated and shown in Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19. Relative to the SnS unit cell, the unfolded band structure of Sn70Na1Ag1S72 (Fig. 5c) exhibits clear valence-band convergence along the Γ–Y direction, where the energy offset (ΔE) is reduced from 0.142 to 0.064 eV (Supplementary Table 7). With further Ag incorporation in Sn69Na1Ag2S72 (Fig. 5d), the band features become broader, reflecting enhanced band mixing near the band edge. The elemental analysis from DFT reveals that the valence-band edge is predominantly contributed by Sn atoms, whereas Ag atoms provide only a minor contribution in this energy region (see the Supplementary Fig. 17). In pristine SnS, the valence-band edge is dominated primarily by the s orbital of Sn atoms. Accordingly, the modification of the band edge is consistent with the experimentally observed shift in binding energy at the Sn K-edge XANES of Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S (Fig. 3b).

To further confirm the band-structure evolution induced by crystal-structure modification, particularly the variation of the Sn–S bond angle (θ), we performed additional DFT calculations based on the experimentally refined crystal structure of SnS at x = 0. The corresponding unit-cell and first Brillouin-zone (BZ) schematics are presented in Fig. 6a, b, respectively, while the atomic-position information associated with θ variation is provided in Supplementary Table 8. The calculated band structures with progressively reduced θ reveal the emergence of valence-band convergence, as shown in Fig. 6c. Figure 6d summarizes the evolution of the energy offsets (ΔE) between the valence bands, where a pronounced convergence of VBM3 toward VBM1 together with a slight divergence of VBM2 is observed. Multivalley transport simulation

To correlate the experimentally measured transport properties with the band modulation observed by ARPES and DFT, we analyzed the relationships among S, Hall carrier concentration (nH), and Hall mobility (μH). The μH values were calculated using Eq. (3.15), with the Hall coefficients provided in Supplementary Fig. 20. Multiple-band transport simulations based on the acoustic deformation potential (ADP) model40,41 were performed to compare the electrical transport behavior of pristine SnS with that of a three-band configuration. In this model, the three bands comprise VB(1 + 3), VB2, and VB4, whereas pristine SnS contains four separated valence bands with expanded energy offsets (ΔE) for VB3 and VB4, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. Details of the calculations are provided in the Supplementary Methods, while the relevant parameters, including ΔE, m*, and Ξ in Supplementary Tables 7 and 9–11. Further discussions regarding the valence-band (VB) coordinates and the principal m* are presented in the Supplementary Discussion.

As shown in Fig. 7a, the experimental S of the Ag-doped samples closely follows the curve corresponding to the modulated three-band configuration, indicating that the enhanced S originates from band flattening associated with the convergence between VB1 and VB3. The introduction of Ag also substantially increases μH, bringing the measured values into good agreement with the predictions of the modulated multiband model, as shown in Fig. 7b. The reduced energy offset (ΔE) between the two extrema (VB1 and VB4) along the Y–Γ–Z direction increases the contribution of the light-transport valley (VB4), which possesses a lower Ξ, resulting in a maximum μH of of ~327 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ in Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S. In addition, Boltzmann transport calculations (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22) further support the experimentally observed Ag-dependent transport evolution (see Supporting Methods for details). Although the calculated conductivity and mobility are lower than the corresponding experimental values because of uncertainties in the state-dependent relaxation-time model and the distinction between calculated drift mobility and measured Hall mobility, the calculations reproduce the key experimental trend: Ag-induced band reconstruction enhances carrier transport relative to x = 0, with x = 0.03 providing the optimum balance between σ and S.Fig. 7Multivalley transport simulation comparing theoretical and experimental electrical transport properties resulting from multiple-band modulation.a Pisarenko plot of Seebeck coefficient (S) versus Hall carrier concentration (nH), compared with theoretical curves from the multiband model at 300 K. b Pisarenko plot of Hall mobility (μH) versus nH, with benchmarks from recent reports on SnS single crystals included27,28.

To explain the high μH in this material, the valence-band edge of SnS exhibits a highly anisotropic electronic structure, as revealed by the constant-energy contours and three-dimensional Fermi surfaces (Supplementary Figs. 23–27). The hole pocket is strongly dispersive along the (ky) direction, relatively flat along (kx), and non-parabolic along (kz), resulting in anisotropic effective masses and non-spherical carrier pockets. Such an anisotropic band topology is advantageous for thermoelectric transport because the flatter directions enhance md* and S, while the more dispersive directions preserve efficient carrier transport. Together with the increased nH and activation of the light bands (VB3 and VB4), this anisotropic multivalley electronic structure provides a plausible origin for the simultaneous enhancement of m* and μH (see the supplementary discussion for the details of band anisotropy and degeneracy).

The room-temperature Hall measurements further reveal the effectiveness of the doping strategy, whereby both μH and nH increase simultaneously, indicating a mitigation of the conventional trade-off between these typically interdependent transport parameters. These simulations demonstrate that the improved electrical transport properties in Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S originate from the multiband configuration induced by band convergence and flattening, highlighting its decisive influence on TE performance41–43.

Figure of merit (zT) and single-leg module performance

Through this electronic band structure evolution, a high zT was achieved, as shown in Fig. 8a, where Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S crystals attained a zT of ~1.06 at 300 K and a zT peak of ~2 at 573 K. Moreover, the zT average of ~1.57 exceeds the latest reported value of p-type SnS single crystal27,28. Notably, the maximization of zT at RT arises from the modulated three-band configuration, as supported by the zT simulations shown in Supplementary Fig. 28. Orientation-dependent measurements further reveal the b-axis as the optimal transport direction, providing the highest zT for this compound, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 29. The high zT obtained in Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S is reproducible across independently prepared crystals synthesized using the same Bridgman growth procedure, where the room-temperature zT values range from 0.98 to 1.08, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 30. To further evaluate the thermal stability of the material, repeated heating–cooling cycle measurements were performed and showed stable thermoelectric performance over multiple cycles up to 573 K (Supplementary Fig. 31). In addition, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were carried out to evaluate the thermal robustness and sulfur volatility of the crystals, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 32. The TGA results show negligible mass loss within the investigated operating temperature range, confirming the good thermal stability of the material below 573 K. Despite the restricted operating temperature window, the achieved zT peak is substantially superior, establishing Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S as one of the best-performing tin-sulfide and sulfide-based TE materials reported to date35,36,44–49 as summarized in Fig. 8b. Despite the high PF obtained for the optimized SnS crystal, the experimentally achieved Pd reaches only approximately 23% of the ideal value predicted from the measured PF (Supplementary Figs. 33b and 34), indicating that only about 18–23% of the intrinsic PF capability is effectively utilized in the present device configuration50. Moreover, the device ZT, inversely estimated from the experimental η in Eq. To further clarify this discrepancy, we analyzed the resistance contributions in the fabricated single-leg module. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 35a, the internal device resistance (Rint) consists of the bulk resistance of the SnS leg (Rleg) and the parasitic/contact resistance (Rc). The extracted Rc/Rint ratio is approximately 66–71% over the measured temperature range (Supplementary Fig. 35b), indicating that the present module performance is primarily limited by contact resistance. Therefore, further optimization of the SnS/Ni(P)/solder/Cu contact structure is expected to significantly improve the practical conversion efficiency.

In summary, the distorted crystal structure associated with the bond angle (θ) in SnS governs the transport physics over a wide temperature range, and reconstructing this structural motif enables highly metallic transport that benefits low-temperature TE performance. Upon structural modification through Ag incorporation, multivalence-band convergence facilitated efficient charge transport in flattened bands with a low Ξ, preserving high μ while increasing m*. Consequently, the strong coupling effect between σ and S was mitigated in Sn0.96Na0.01Ag0.03S single crystal, enabling the realization of a high PF of ~101 µW cm−1 K−2 at RT. In this study, the aliovalent co-doping strategy with Na⁺ and Ag⁺ successfully established an emerging platform for SnS-based crystals, in which structural modulation directly gave rise to favorable TE properties. Notably, a high zT peak of ~2 at 573 K was realized within a sulfide-based system, highlighting a significant advance in earth-abundant and environmentally friendly thermoelectrics near RT.

Methods

Single crystal synthesis

Purified Sn pellets (99.99 wt.% pure) and Na ingot (99.99 wt.% pure) from Ultimate Materials Technology, S powder (99.98 wt.% pure) from Sigma-Aldrich, and Ag powder (99.999 wt.% pure) from Thermofischer Puratronics were weighed carefully and calculated according to the stoichiometry of (Ag or x = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 at. %) Sn0.99-xNa0.01AgxS. All weighted precursors were prepared inside the carbon-coated quartz tube and sealed under ~50 mTorr vacuum with an outer larger quartz tube to avoid oxidation from the high-temperature synthesis inside the furnace. The mixed materials were annealed to 1273 K using a slow heating ramp and held at this temperature for 3 days. Subsequently, the samples were cooled to room temperature inside the furnace. The resulting polycrystalline SnS ingots were crushed, ground, and sieved through a 200-mesh screen to ensure compositional uniformity. Approximately 20 g SnS fine powder was then transferred into a 13 mm inner-diameter carbon-coated conical quartz tube and double-sealed with a larger outer quartz tube. For single-crystal growth, the sealed samples were placed in a vertical furnace. The sample was held in the hot zone and heated to 1273 K for 24 h, then maintained at this temperature for an additional 2 days to ensure complete melting, followed by transfer to the cold zone at 1073 K using a pulling parameter corresponding to a cooling rate of 1 K h−1. The entire single-crystal growth process required approximately 14 days to obtain a crystal with a length of ~5 cm. After growth, the crystals were naturally cooled to room temperature inside the furnace for 1 day.

Material characterizations

The phase purity of the as-synthesized crystals was characterized using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) performed on a Bruker D2 diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Crystal orientation was examined using electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) combined with field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; JEOL JSM-7800F) and Laue X-ray diffraction. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy combined with high-resolution electron energy-loss spectroscopy (HAADF-STEM-EELS) was performed using a JEOL JEM−2100F transmission electron microscope equipped with aberration correction. STEM specimens were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) milling at iTek Technology Co., Ltd.

Raman spectroscopy and fitting procedure

Raman spectra were measured using a custom-built confocal fiber microscope integrated with a HORIBA iHR550 spectrometer, equipped with a Syncerity BI-NIR CCD camera (wavelength range: 400–1075 nm) and a 532 nm diode laser as the excitation source. The scattered signals were collected using a photomultiplier detector (PicoQuant PMA192; wavelength range: 230–920 nm). Measurements were performed on the basal plane of small SnS bulk crystals with dimensions of approximately 4 × 4 mm2 in size. For each sample, spectra were acquired from up to five different locations with an exposure time of approximately 90 s per spot. The final Raman spectrum was obtained by averaging the spectra collected from different spots. Raman peak positions were determined by Gaussian fitting using OriginLab software, yielding a fitting quality with residuals corresponding to R² ≈ 0.99.

Synchrotron radiation measurements

Most synchrotron-based experiments were carried out at the Taiwan Photon Source (TPS) of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC), Taiwan. High-energy (20 keV) synchrotron X-ray diffraction was used to collect powder diffraction data. For Rietveld refinement, crystal powders were prepared by hand grinding and sieved through a 400-mesh screen to ensure uniform particle size for all samples. For elemental mapping, bulk single-crystal specimens were analyzed using nano-focused X-ray fluorescence (XRF) at TPS beamline 21 A with a polychromatic X-ray source covering an energy range of 5–30 keV. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements at the Sn K-edge were conducted at TPS beamline 44A1 in transmission mode using ground SnS powder as the specimen. An energy scan operating in step mode was employed to acquire optimized absorption spectra at the Ag K-edge in fluorescence detection.

XRD refinement, XAS fitting and crystal structure modeling

PXRD patterns were refined by Rietveld analysis using the FullProf and WinPlotr software packages51,52. The shape of the diffraction peaks was modeled using a Thompson−Cox−Hastings pseudo-Voigt profile function. Zero-point shift and asymmetry parameters were systematically refined, and the background contribution was manually estimated. Lattice parameters, atomic positions, and isotropic displacement parameters (i.e., Debye−Waller factors: Biso) were finally refined. For extracting the EXAFS fitting parameters, reduction and modeling of the data from XAS measurements were realized using the IFEFFIT code as implemented in the Athena and Artemis software53. Crystal-structure models presented in the figures were generated using VESTA from the crystallographic information files (CIFs) obtained from the structural refinement54.

Band structure measurement via angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)

ARPES measurements were performed at beamline BL21B1 of the Taiwan Light Source (TLS) and beamline BL39A1 of the Taiwan Photon Source (TPS) at the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC). Photoemission spectra were collected using hemispherical analyzers (Scienta R4000 at TLS and DA30L at TPS) with a ± 15° collection angle and an angular resolution of 0.5°. The base pressure during measurements was better than 9.1 × 10−11 torr. Single-crystal samples were cleaved in situ and immediately transferred to the measurement chamber. The beam spot sizes were approximately 120 μm at TLS BL21B1 and 10 μm at TPS BL39A1.
