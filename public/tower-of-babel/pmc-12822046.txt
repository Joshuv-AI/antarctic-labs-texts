3D-TEE: an essential tool for percutaneous occlusion of atrial septal defects with anomalous band

Background

Atrial Septal Defect (ASD) is one of the most common congenital heart defects, accounting for approximately 10%–15% of all congenital heart diseases [1]. It is characterized by persistent opening in the atrial septum, the wall separating the left and right atria [2]. This defect allows abnormal blood flow between the atria, leading to clinical manifestations such as shortness of breath, fatigue, and, in severe cases, complications like heart failure and pulmonary hypertension. The presence of left atrial anomalous band, an anatomical variation, can further complicate diagnosis and treatment approach, as they may mimic additional defects on conventional 2D imaging or obscure the true margins of a single defect. These fibrous bands, distinct from the chordae tendineae of the mitral and tricuspid valves (which are located within the ventricles and therefore are not directly involved in atrial septal procedures), can affect the mobility of the atrial septum and influence the procedural strategy for ASD closure. Anomalous bands in the left atrium are distinct from Chiari’s network; Chiari’s network is a separate embryologic remnant that is located in the right atrium. These structures, composed of collagen fibers, can vary in length and may intersect with the atrial septal defect, making their identification essential for surgical planning [3]. Although they are relatively uncommon, recognition of left atrial anomalous band is important because they may alter device selection, deployment strategy, and intra-procedural imaging needs.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old female patient was referred to our institution for percutaneous ASD occlusion following the detection of a cardiac murmur during a routine examination four years prior, while she was pregnant. A grade 3/6 systolic murmur was auscultated at the left sternal border between the second and third intercostal spaces, accompanied by an accentuated P2 and a fixed split. The patient occasionally experienced palpitations but reported no additional discomfort. Recently, she noticed mild swelling in both feet, which prompted her visit. She denied any history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, diabetes, or tuberculosis, and had no record of infectious diseases. Three years ago, she underwent a cesarean section with an uneventful recovery. She had no history of major surgeries, trauma, blood transfusions, or known drug or food allergies, except for a documented anaphylactic reaction to vitamin K1. Upon admission, her blood pressure was 135/85 mm Hg, and her pulse was 88 beats per minute. Transthoracic color Doppler echocardiography, as shown in Fig. 1, revealed two left-to-right shunts at the atrial level, suggesting multiple atrial septal defects. Preoperative two-dimensional and color Doppler transesophageal echocardiography identified multiple ASDs (Fig. 2). However, 3D-TEE provided comprehensive face and multiplanar views and revealed that the apparent multiple defects resulted from a single ostium secundum ASD measuring approximately 24 × 18 mm in diameter with smooth, well-defined edges.Additionally, the defect demonstrates sufficient length to both the aortic root and the root of the anterior mitral valve leaflet. A mobile anomalous band was also observed, attached to the mitral side of the defect (Fig. 3). This corrected interpretation directly altered the procedural plan: the team shifted from an initial strategy of deploying two separate occluder devices to selecting and deploying a single device under real-time 3D imaging guidance. Given the patient’s right-sided volume overload, auscultatory findings, symptoms of palpitations and peripheral edema, and the size of the defect (10 mm with significant left-to-right shunting), percutaneous closure was indicated in accordance with current guideline recommendations. In accordance with best practices, the case was jointly reviewed by an adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) specialist. The ACHD specialist emphasized that, despite the unusual presence of a left atrial anomalous band, the patient met standard criteria for percutaneous closure, and 3D-TEE imaging confirmed a safe anatomical window for single-device deployment. This multidisciplinary input further validated the treatment decision.

The procedure commenced with the administration of general anesthesia, followed by TEE probe insertion. The patient was positioned supine, and a 12 F delivery sheath, preloaded with an A24 mm Amplatzer septal occluder, was prepared. A right femoral vein puncture was performed, followed by the insertion of a 6 F arterial sheath. A 6 F right heart catheter was advanced into the right atrium, and under TEE guidance, the catheter traversed the large ASD opening into the left atrium. A super-stiff guidewire was placed into the left upper pulmonary vein, followed by the advancement of a 12 F delivery sheath.

The A24 mm occluder was deployed, followed by the sequential deployment of both left and right umbrellas, which were securely positioned under 3D-TEE guidance. The device was confirmed to be correctly positioned, with no residual shunt, and there was no impact on surrounding structures (Fig. 4). The post-deployment confirmation showed that the anomalous band remained visible in a separate plane from the septum. The device was subsequently released. Discussion

In this case report, the patient’s two-dimensional transesophageal echocardiogram (2D-TEE) revealed multiple ostium secundum ASDs with a significant distance between the defects. Preoperative discussions concluded that two separate occluder devices should be used to close the ASDs. However,3D-TEE, by offering frontal views of the septum and multiplanar reconstructions, revealed a single ostium secundum ASD with a mobile anomalous band attached to the mitral side of the defect, significantly altering the procedural approach. The anomalous band, measuring approximately 3 cm, did not interfere with catheter maneuvering or device deployment. Nevertheless, its presence altered the interpretation, shifting from multiple defects to a single defect with an intersecting band, thereby influencing device selection and procedural strategy. This case serves as an instructive example for operators learning procedural 3D echocardiography: careful 3D inspection can prevent unnecessary device use and reduce procedural complexity. During surgery, the ASD was not closed with direct suture or patch, as 3D-TEE clearly identified a single ASD, thereby altering the initial surgical plan. No short-term complications, including device embolization or residual defects, were observed. Postoperative follow-up revealed that the occluder device was well-positioned, showing no evidence of residual shunting. Long-term imaging follow-up is planned to monitor the device’s position and to rule out late complications, such as migration or thrombosis. Such detailed visualization is crucial for ASD occlusion procedures, especially when anomalous bands are present.

This case underscores two key points for proceduralists: (1) 3D-TEE can alter the operative plan by clarifying whether apparent separate defects are genuine multiple ostia or a single ostium distorted by adjacent structures (e.g., anomalous band); (2) 3D-TEE serves as a highly effective educational tool for operators learning to perform image-guided ASD closure, offering spatial context that complements the higher temporal resolution of 2D imaging. While 2D-TEE provides high-frame-rate guidance useful for rapid device maneuvers, 3D-TEE provides spatial information that can prevent misinterpretation. Therefore, a complementary approach—utilizing 3D-TEE for spatial mapping and 2D-TEE for fine temporal guidance—remains the preferred practice in many centers [4]. Furthermore, this case emphasizes the importance of incorporating an ACHD specialist into the decision-making process. The ACHD specialist’s assessment confirmed that the patient met both clinical and echocardiographic criteria for closure, reassuring that the anomalous band did not contraindicate percutaneous device deployment. The specialist also recommended that careful imaging follow-up is essential to monitor long-term device stability. This collaborative approach ensured that the closure strategy was evidence-based and tailored to the patient’s unique anatomy.

In this patient, ASD closure was clearly indicated due to the defect size (approximately 24 × 18 mm) and significant left-to-right shunting, which had already led to clinical symptoms (palpitations, peripheral edema) and physical signs of right heart overload. Without intervention, these patients remain at risk for progressive right heart dilation, atrial arrhythmias, and pulmonary hypertension. Therefore, percutaneous closure was the appropriate therapeutic option, in line with guideline-based indications for secundum ASD closure.

The presence of a left atrial anomalous band, a relatively rare and often overlooked anatomical variation, can complicate ASD closure. An anomalous band can pose additional challenges in device positioning and occlusion, as it may affect the relationship between the ASD and surrounding tissues. The detailed visualization provided by 3D-TEE was pivotal in identifying this anatomical variation, enabling the surgical team to select the most appropriate occlusion device and prevent complications from incorrect device deployment. For clarity, left atrial anomalous bands should not be confused with pectinate muscles (intracavitary structures typically found in the left ventricle) or with Chiari’s network (a right atrial embryological remnant). Importantly, differentiating a left atrial anomalous band from other similar interatrial structures is crucial for precise surgical and interventional planning. TTE or TEE imaging can distinguish these variations by assessing their location, morphology, and relationships with adjacent structures. For example, a left atrial anomalous band can be differentiated from other conditions, such as a single-hole or multi-fenestrated ASD, residual septal tissue, or a septal aneurysm, which may share overlapping features. Furthermore, it is important to differentiate this structure from other anatomical variants, such as cor triatriatum sinistrum or remnants of pulmonary venous valves, which may necessitate distinct surgical approaches or interventions. Such anatomical insights are crucial when planning transcatheter interventions involving atypical structures, such as Chiari’s network, as they directly influence the success of the procedure and long-term patient outcomes.

While 2D-TEE provides valuable information, it often falls short in assessing complex spatial relationships within the heart, especially when unusual anatomical features, such as anomalous bands, are present. 3D-TEE provides a three-dimensional view of the ASD, including its size, shape, edges, and spatial relationship with nearby structures, enabling a more accurate and comprehensive evaluation. A recognized limitation of current 3D-TEE technology is its relatively lower temporal resolution compared to 2D-TEE. This reduced frame rate may hinder precise real-time tracking of catheters or devices during rapid interventional maneuvers. Thus, in contemporary practice, 3D-TEE is primarily employed for diagnostic clarification and anatomical assessment, while 2D-TEE remains indispensable for guiding fine interventional steps. In many centers, a complementary approach is adopted: 3D-TEE provides the spatial ‘road map,’ while 2D-TEE ensures the high-temporal-resolution guidance necessary for device deployment. Furthermore, 3D-TEE aids in identifying and differentiating between various types of ASDs, facilitating the selection of the appropriate occlusion device. Additionally, the ability to differentiate between various types of ASDs and other anatomical variants, such as residual septal tissue, septal aneurysms, or cor triatriatum sinistrum, is essential for selecting the appropriate occlusion device and ensuring optimal patient outcomes.

Furthermore, 3D-TEE can assist in assessing the suitability of the patient for percutaneous closure, thereby reducing the need for open heart surgery [5]. In this case, it was determined that percutaneous closure was feasible, thereby obviating the need for surgical intervention. The use of 3D-TEE not only aids in preoperative planning but also serves as an intra-procedural confirmation tool and a crucial component of postoperative imaging surveillance.

Conclusion

The integration of 3D-TEE into clinical practice, particularly in complex cases involving atypical anatomical features such as left atrial anomalous bands (distinct from pectinate muscles and Chiari’s network), has significant diagnostic, procedural, and educational implications. Its ability to provide detailed, three-dimensional insights enhances surgical precision, informs decision-making, and ultimately contributes to improved patient outcomes. This case specifically demonstrates how 3D-TEE altered the treatment strategy (from a planned two-device closure to a successful single-device deployment) and serves as an instructive teaching case for operators learning procedural 3D echocardiography guidance. Furthermore, this case underscores the importance of involving an adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) specialist in procedural planning, as their expertise ensures that complex anatomical and clinical considerations are thoroughly integrated into the decision-making process.
