Cell surface ADGRG1 and CD86 expression identifies antitumor CD4+ T cells in human cancer
Authors: Kyle J. Hitscherich, Aaron J. Dinerman, Abraham A. Hakim, Victoria Dulemba, Sivasish Sindiri, Billel Gasmi, Alakesh Bera, Jared J. Gartner, Todd D. Prickett, Zhiya Yu, Yong F. Li, Nivedita M. Ratnam, Alexandra M. Gustafson, Aarushi Bhasin, Maria R. Parkhurst, Nicholas D. Klemen, Mei Li M. Kwong, James C. Yang, Paul F. Robbins, Stephanie L. Goff, Steven A. Rosenberg, Sri Krishna, Frank J. Lowery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceStudies have demonstrated that adoptive cell transfer with antitumor CD4+ T cells can be effective in treating advanced stage cancer patients. Improving methods to identify antitumor CD4+ T cells through cell surface protein expression remains an unmet challenge. Here, by studying experimentally validated human antitumor, mutation-reactive CD4+ T cell receptors identified in tumors from patients with advanced solid cancers, we demonstrate that tumor mutation-reactive CD4+ T cells can be substantially enriched using cell surface protein markers ADGRG1 and CD86. Cell surface protein-based isolation of tumor-reactive CD4+ T cells using ADGRG1 or CD86 may facilitate the evaluation of their phenotypic and functional states in vitro and enable the development of effective gene engineered cell therapies against cancer.

Adoptive cell transfer (ACT) of autologous lymphocytes targeting tumor antigens has mediated durable tumor regressions in patients with metastatic solid tumors (1–7). Although the importance of CD8+ T cells in mediating ACT response is well established (8–11), the critical role of CD4+ antitumor T cells in mediating durable antitumor responses following ACT has only recently come to the forefront (3, 7, 9, 12–17). In one patient with metastatic cholangiocarcinoma, an ACT infusion product composed almost entirely of neoantigen-specific CD4+ T cells resulted in a durable response (3). ACT using peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBL) genetically modified to express tumor reactive TCRs (TCR-T cell therapy) has also shown efficacy in the treatment of patients with advanced stage malignancies (9, 12, 18). In a recent clinical trial, TCR-T cell therapy based on a human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-class II-restricted TCR specific for the cancer germline antigen MAGE-A3 mediated regression of metastatic melanoma, further demonstrating that ACT of CD4+ T cells can lead to regressions of solid tumors in patients with advanced stage cancer (12).

We and others have previously demonstrated success in defining the unique transcriptomic states of antitumor CD8+ and CD4+ T cells from freshly dissociated human tumor samples (noncultured) (19–23). We recently described a CD4+ TIL transcriptomic profile (NeoTCR4 signature) that facilitates the identification of neoantigen-reactive CD4+ TCRs from metastatic solid epithelial tumors using single cell sequencing (21). While transcriptomic analysis allows the identification of validated antitumor T cells, the identification of cell surface markers that can enrich antitumor CD4+ T cells would allow expansion of viable cells for downstream analysis of their function both in vitro and in vivo in ACT clinical trials. Previous work has demonstrated that reactivity to tumor cell lines, oncoviral proteins, and patient-specific neoantigens are reliably enriched when selecting for fresh CD8+ TIL expressing specific surface markers such as PD-1, CD39, and CD103 (21, 24–27). Although enrichment for neoantigen reactivity among CD4+ TIL has been investigated using hypothesis-driven markers (e.g., PD-1 or ICOS), an unbiased analysis of the cell surface protein expression profile of antitumor CD4+ TIL has yet to be defined (28, 29). In contrast to melanoma, where HLA class I-restricted tumor recognition by CD8+ T cells appears to play a dominant role, studies in common solid epithelial cancers have indicated that HLA class II-restricted CD4+ antitumor neoantigen-specific T cells may play a more significant role in mediating ACT responses to these cancers (7, 30). We therefore carried out an unbiased interrogation of single cell transcriptional and cell surface protein states to identify markers that characterized tumor reactive CD4+ T cells within epithelial tumors.

Results

Transcriptional States of Antitumor CD4+ TIL in Resected Tumors.

We selected three retrospective epithelial tumor samples (4342, 4393, 4283) based on availability of cryopreserved fresh tumor digests (FTD) and previously identified antitumor CD4+ T cell clonotypes (NeoTCR4s) within these samples (SI Appendix, Tables S1 and S2) (21). Samples were prepared for fluorescence-associated cell sorting (FACS) and single cell VDJ sequencing, along with staining of 130 DNA-barcoded cell surface antibodies for cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes (CITE-seq), and single cell RNA sequencing (Methods, Fig. 1A and Datasets S1 and S2). Acquired data were filtered for CD4+ protein expression and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis was conducted to define single cell transcriptomic states of the isolated CD4+ TIL from metastatic tumors (Fig. 1B and Dataset S1). The analysis of these samples revealed the presence of T cells in multiple phenotypic states that included T cells with markers of dysfunction and exhaustion (clusters 8 and 9), potentially cytotoxic states (cluster 4), regulatory states (clusters 0 and 7), and relatively less differentiated states (clusters 1, 3, 10) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (20–22). Clusters 4, 8, and 9 demonstrated strong expression of component genes from our previously reported NeoTCR4 signature that enriches for antitumor CD4+ T cells (CXCL13, ENTPD1, PDCD1, TIGIT, and HAVCR2) (Fig. 1B and Dataset S1) (21). These three clusters (4, 8, and 9) also exhibited lower transcriptomic expression of FOXP3 as well as T regulatory gene signatures, indicating that these phenotypic states represented dysfunctional but not T regulatory CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2).

T cells in cluster 9 possessed a transcriptomic profile with high expression of markers of exhaustion that was most similar to our previously described signature marking antitumor CD4+ T cells [CD4+ NeoTCR Exhausted (NeoTCR4-Ex), Dataset S1] (21). In contrast, cluster 8 shared a number of these markers along with higher expression of TCF7, possibly associated with a less exhausted tumor-reactive phenotype [CD4+ NeoTCR Precursor Exhausted (NeoTCR4-Pex), Datasets S1 and S3]. Cluster 4 shared mRNA expression of markers of exhaustion and neoantigen reactivity in addition to cytolytic genes that included GZMA, GZMK, GZMB, GZMH, and PRF1, designated here as the CD4+ NeoTCR Cytotoxic cluster (NeoTCR4-Cyt, SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2, Dataset S1). Additionally, our previously published NeoTCR4 transcriptomic signature demonstrated higher scores within clusters 4, 8, and 9 (Fig. 1 B and E) (21). Thus, we identified three clusters of potential neoantigen-reactive CD4+ TIL with unique phenotypic states in three colorectal cancer tumor samples. We noted that gene signatures for these three unique CD4+ TIL states (NeoTCR4-Cyt, NeoTCR4-Pex, and NeoTCR4-Ex) expand on our previously published antitumor NeoTCR4 signature; the intersection of these three states are included in what we term the NeoTCR4-Core signature (Dataset S3) (21).

Known Antitumor TCRs are Found Within Cells Scoring Highly for NeoTCR4 Signature Within the Transcriptomic Map.

Sixteen previously discovered antitumor NeoTCR clonotypes (NeoTCR4s) (6 TCR clonotypes from 4342, 8 from 4283, and 2 from 4393) were identified in the current analysis (SI Appendix, Table S2). Backprojecting these TCR sequences onto our mRNA UMAP revealed that a total of 96 T cells expressing known neoantigen-reactive TCRs were present within our sample: 36 from 4342, 54 from 4283, and 6 from 4393 (Fig. 1 C and D).

We found that these clonotypes were largely concentrated within our identified candidate NeoTCR4 clusters [4 (NeoTCR4-Cyt), 8 (NeoTCR4-Pex), and 9 (NeoTCR4-Ex)] (Fig. 1 C–E and Dataset S3). Cluster 4 contained 52% of all present NeoTCR4s, followed by cluster 9 (25%), then cluster 8 (20%) (Fig. 1D). Cluster 11 contained only 2% of all identified NeoTCR4s with all the other clusters containing very low frequencies of NeoTCR4 clonotypes (Fig. 1D). Notably, cluster 4 also contained the highest number of unique NeoTCR4s, comprising 12 different clonotypes, while clusters 8 and 9 both contained eight (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Thus, within these three patient samples, antitumor CD4+ TIL were enriched in these related transcriptional states as previously reported (Fig. 1E) (20–22). To confirm these observations, we performed a TCR-sequence search of 4069 experimentally derived MHC class II-restricted public viral TCR clonotypes (from VDJdb) to identify six public viral clones belonging to Influenza A, CMV, and SARS-Cov2 antigens and mapped their phenotypic states in the TIL-UMAP. These clones largely mapped to clusters 1, 2, 6, 7, and 10 reconfirming prior reports of distinct transcriptomic states enriched with tumor-reactive human CD4+ T cells within a tumor (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B) (19–26).

Based on the relative enrichment of known NeoTCRs within clusters 4, 8, and 9, we tested an additional 46 candidate NeoTCR4s identified within samples 4342 and 4393 (Fig. 1 E–G). Fifteen TCRs from 4342 and nine from 4393 screened positive for reactivity against patient-specific pooled mutated peptide epitopes (Fig. 1 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S3). Reactive TCRs were then parsed for precise neoantigen recognition by repeat coculture with individual mutant peptide epitopes demonstrated by IFN-γ release (ELISpot) and CD137 (4-1BB) upregulation (via flow cytometry) as depicted for private tumor neoantigen PCNTP2122L and tumor driver neoantigen NRASQ61R (Fig. 1 F and G). In sum, we identified 29 previously unknown neoantigen-specific TCR clonotypes based on transcriptomic data. Their projection back onto the transcriptomic UMAP showed a marked bias toward presence in NeoTCR4 clusters 4, 8, and 9 (Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Unbiased Definition of the Cell Surface Proteome of CD4+ Antitumor TIL in Metastatic Epithelial Cancers.

Next, to determine the markers of antitumor CD4+ TIL, we investigated the cell surface expression of 130 immune-related proteins using DNA-barcoded antibodies (CITE-seq) applied to the same samples. Most surface proteins corresponded with their transcriptional states such as high CD25 (IL2RA) protein expression on clusters 0 and 7, marking Treg states, and high IL7R protein expression on clusters 1 and 3 denoting less exhausted TIL (Dataset S2).

Among the previously discussed NeoTCR4 TIL transcriptomic states, the cell surface proteins most highly overexpressed on T cells in cluster 4 included CD57 (B3GAT1), ADGRG1, CD39 (ENTPD1), and CD86 (Fig. 2 A and B and Dataset S2). Cluster 8 showed high expression of PD-1, BTLA, TIGIT, and CD62L (SELL); cluster 9 cells demonstrated expression of ADGRG1, PD-1, TIGIT, CD39, and CD38 (Dataset S2). Examination of all proteins among these clusters (4, 8, and 9) revealed several candidate markers shared across 2 or more transcriptional states comprising known antitumor TIL (Dataset S2). These included markers ADGRG1 and CD86 (Fig. 2 A and B). In addition, TIM-3 was noted to show promising transcriptomic expression (Dataset S1) but was not included in the CITE-seq antibody panel. Although CD57 was not shared across multiple clusters of interest, it was the highest differentially expressed protein within cluster 4 and has been previously reported as a potential marker of CD4+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) (Fig. 2 A and B) (21, 30). Additional transcriptomic markers previously identified for antitumor NeoTCR8 T cells did not appear to be enriched in CD4+ antitumor TIL in the current study, and in particular CD39 cell surface expression was also found to be highly expressed by Treg CD4+ TIL (Fig. 2 A and B) (21, 26, 31). These results indicate that antitumor CD4+ TIL possess unique cell surface profiles that do not necessarily align with that of antitumor CD8+ TIL cell surface markers (26, 32, 33). Additionally, CITE-seq was uniquely able to identify ADGRG1, CD86, and CD57 as potential protein markers for NeoTCR4s which scRNA sequencing alone was unable to reliably identify.

Assessing Single Cell Surface Protein Markers for Enrichment of NeoTCR4 TIL.

An initial FACS evaluation of fresh tumor digests from samples 4342 and 4393, which contained relatively high frequencies of NeoTCR4 reactivity, demonstrated variability between samples from patients 4393 and 4342, with CD57 marking the largest populations with a median of 8.7% of CD4+ T cells, followed by ADGRG1 (5.5%), CD39 (4.3%), TIM-3 (4.2%), and CD86 (3.7%) (Fig. 2C).

We then evaluated the presence of our expanded library of 41 NeoTCR4s among the TCRs identified from T cells sorted using the individual markers ADGRG1, CD39, CD86, CD57, and TIM-3 (Fig. 2D). Among these markers, ADGRG1 had the highest enrichment for NeoTCR4 reactivity with a mean of 22.1% of sorted T cells expressing experimentally validated neoantigen-specific NeoTCR4, compared to bulk CD4 TIL, in which the frequency of NeoTCR4 reactivity was 10.4%. This was followed by CD39 (14.3%), and CD86 (13.8%), with other markers performing poorer than bulk CD4 (Fig. 2D). ADGRG1-sorted cells contained the most unique NeoTCR4 clonotypes (n = 10) followed by CD39 (n = 8), CD57 and CD86 (n = 5), and TIM-3 (n = 4), with the CD4 bulk population containing seven clones (Fig. 2D). Of note, most NeoTCR4 T cells were found as singleton cells within a marker population (Fig. 2 D, Middle and Right) suggesting very low frequency and low clonal expansion within resected tumors. These data indicate that single cell surface protein markers can enrich for NeoTCR4 cells compared to baseline populations, with ADGRG1 acting as a proficient marker for enrichment in retrospective TIL samples.

Performance of Single Cell Surface Protein Markers for Enrichment of NeoTCR4s.

To further evaluate candidate cell surface marker performance to enrich NeoTCR4s, we selected six independent metastatic tumor samples for single cell FACS-based isolation and TCR sequencing (Fig. 3A); four patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (4285, 4400, 4432, and 4513), one with metastatic pancreatic cancer (4520), and one with metastatic esophageal cancer (4560) (SI Appendix, Table S1). Samples were selected based on availability of cryopreserved tumor material without consideration of known antitumor CD4+ T cell reactivities within samples.

The initial FACS-based analysis of cell surface candidate proteins expressed by CD4+ T cells from these samples revealed that CD57, ADGRG1, CD86, and TIM-3 represented a mean of 4.4%, 3.8%, 2.4%, and 1.1%, respectively of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 3B). Following single-cell sorting and TCR sequencing and analysis of 48 to 96 cells per population, an average of 25 to 50 wells contained productive TCR sequences. Across the sequenced populations, 34 to 66 TCR constructs per sample derived from tumor samples 4400, 4520, 4513, 4432, and 4560 were screened against patient-tumor mutations (total mutations tested = 1,326, median = 227) presented by autologous antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Additionally, previously vetted NeoTCR4s from 4400 and 4285 were evaluated to determine if they were present among the TCRs identified above (21). Across all samples, experimentally validated NeoTCR4s were identified in samples 4285 (four TCR clonotypes), 4400 (11 TCR clonotypes), and 4520 (six TCR clonotypes) (Fig. 3 C and D) (SI Appendix, Table S4). Antitumor TCRs by reactivity screening were confirmed with repeat coculture against individual long peptides followed by mutant-wild type peptide avidity titrations (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Screening also included cell lysates of patient-derived tumor lysates pulsed onto APCs as described in our methods (Fig. 3 C and D). The exogenous expression of HLA class II antigens and limited tumor expression of HLA class II precludes direct recognition of tumor cell lines by CD4+ TCRs. All 15 novel clonotypes demonstrating peptide reactivity in screening assays were found to have validated mutation-specific reactivity across parsing experiments to 6 unique neoantigens (TP53R175H, KRASG13D, ERBB3G266R, PCGF1R210C, SOX9Y442fs, SUPT3H-3R210S) and one cancer-specific fusion protein (PTPN18-BBS9) (SI Appendix, Table S4). Additionally, five TCR clonotypes recognized xenograft or organoid lysate but did not recognize any of the corresponding candidate neoantigens, indicating that these TCRs may recognize other classes of tumor antigens (Fig. 3 C and D and SI Appendix, Table S4).

Across our validation samples, FACS-based isolation using CD86 cell surface protein expression was most successful in enriching NeoTCR4s with a mean of 9.9% of isolated cells containing an antitumor reactive CD4+ T cell, followed by ADGRG1 (7.0%), and CD57 (5.0%) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Among our samples containing NeoTCR4s, only one NeoTCR4 clonotype was found within our bulk CD4 populations consistent with baseline NeoTCR4 T cell frequencies of 1.6%, < 5.3%, and < 3.4% for samples 4285, 4400, and 4520 respectively, based on the limit of detection in the plate-based sequencing (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Of note, for the three samples that our markers did not enrich for functionally validated NeoTCR4s (4432, 4513, 4560), only one sample (4513) had validated neoantigen reactivity identified via conventional in vitro TIL reactivity methods (7), suggesting that it may have been poorly immunogenic.

Across these three validation samples containing NeoTCR4s (4285, 4400, 4520), 20 NeoTCR clonotypes were identified within either the Bulk-, CD86-, or ADGRG1-sorted cells. Compared to the CD4+ Bulk T cell population, protein marker cell isolation based on CD86 and ADGRG1 expression showed a significant enrichment of experimentally validated NeoTCR4s across these samples (P < 0.05, Kruskal–Wallis multiple comparisons test, Fig. 3E). CD86-sorted CD4+ T cells yielded 9/20 tumor-reactive TCR clonotypes and ADGRG1-sorted CD4+ T cells yielded 11/20 tumor-reactive clonotypes. The majority of these clonotypes were significantly enriched as only one NeoTCR4 clonotype (n = 1/20) was captured by the CD4+ bulk T cell population. Comparing individual NeoTCR4 clones at the clonotype level, CD86 and ADGRG1 again demonstrated statistically significant enrichment for these NeoTCR4s compared to bulk populations (P < 0.01, Paired t test, Fig. 3F). Taken together, these results validate the utility of candidate cell surface markers, particularly CD86 and ADGRG1, for the identification and enrichment of NeoTCR4s from solid epithelial TIL.

Receiver Operating Characteristic Analysis.

To better assess performance of single markers we performed a receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis across our five NeoTCR4-containing samples. Designating all isolated cells expressing a confirmed NeoTCR4 as true positives and screened negative TCRs as false positives, we determined performance across these single markers. Minimal MFI was determined at time of FACS-based isolation via isotype control-stained samples. ADGRG1 was found to have the best concordance via ROC analysis with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.70 (Fig. 4A). Using these analyses, we determined that the positive predictive value (PPV) of a cell expressing ADGRG1 to identify any neoantigen was 59.6% and negative predictive value (NPV) of 76.1%. The next highest concordance was with CD86 as a single marker with AUC 0.62. Use of single marker CD86 resulted in a PPV of 57.9% and NPV 65.7%. These results further suggest that ADGRG1 has the highest sensitivity and specificity among single surface protein markers in enriching for antitumor CD4+ TCR clonotypes from solid tumors.

Coexpression of Cell Surface Protein Markers on CD4+ Neoantigen-Reactive T Cells.

To gain further insight into distinct NeoTCR4 TIL cell surface marker profiles, we analyzed the unique neoantigen reactivities observed within TIL populations sorted based on individual cell surface markers for their expression of other markers of interest (Fig. 4B). All neoantigen reactivities arose from CD4+ T cell clonotypes that were validated in coculture reactivity. Some, but not all, of these neoantigen reactivities are polyclonal in nature (>1 CD4+ T cell clonotype against the same neoantigen reactivity, SI Appendix, Tables S2–S4). We then analyzed cell surface marker expression on single wells capturing confirmed antitumor CD4+ TCR sequences using FACS-based index sorting analysis (Methods). Briefly, these single wells expressing defined TCR sequences were analyzed for cell surface protein signals that coexpressed with their original FACS-sort marker gate designation.

ADGRG1 and CD86 were individually expressed at the cell surface of 94% (15 of 16) unique neoantigen-reactive T cell populations, while coexpression of ADGRG1+ and CD86+ was observed in 69% (11 of 16) of neoantigen reactivities (Fig. 4B). Additionally, we observed that individual neoantigen reactivities exhibited differences in their combined cell surface expression profiles (Fig. 4B). For instance, YAP1T154I neoantigen-reactive T cells coexpressed ADGRG1 and CD86, but not CD57. SUPT3HR210S-reactive TIL expressed CD86 and CD57 but not ADGRG1 while CD86 was expressed on all neoantigen-reactive T cells except for those recognizing PCGF1R210C (Fig. 4B). Therefore, although certain surface protein markers and combinations thereof may increase NeoTCR4 enrichment, they may do so at the expense of detecting all unique NeoTCR4 clonotypes, indicating that a tight definition of neoantigen-reactive cell states may generally involve a trade-off of sensitivity vs. specificity (Fig. 4A). These results also indicate that the number and type of neoantigens identified using cell surface FACS-based isolation differed depending upon the marker used for isolation. Nevertheless, taken together, ADGRG1 was the most effective single marker at enriching antitumor CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4 A and B).

Prospective Sample Identification of NeoTCR4s Utilizing Combination Cell Surface Markers.

Since ADGRG1 and CD86 coexpression was observed in 69% of unique CD4+ neoantigen-reactive TIL (Fig. 4B), we evaluated the utility of ADGRG1 and CD86 FACS-based sorting to prospectively identify antitumor NeoTCR4s in additional patients’ tumor samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). We performed ADGRG1 and CD86 cell surface marker staining on CD4+ TIL from a metastatic rectal cancer patient (4612). Screening candidate NeoTCR4s for their ability to recognize 158 candidate neoepitopes led to the identification of one TCR recognizing a mutant CHCHD6P63L neoantigen and two recognizing organoid lysates pulsed onto autologous APCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Two of the NeoTCR4s were also enriched by the marker combination ADGRG1 and CD86. The neoantigen-reactive NeoTCR4 demonstrated mutant specificity on mutant-wild type avidity testing (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

These results indicate that prospective surface marker-based sorting can enrich for identification of tumor reactive CD4+ T cells in a patient with metastatic cancer (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), although their efficiency in successfully capturing all possible tumor-reactive TIL for clinical use may need to be further evaluated in more samples (Fig. 4B).

Pan-Sample scRNA Analysis Identifies ADGRG1-Enriched Clonotypes as Predominantly Antitumor NeoTCR4 T Cells.

We next interrogated the cell surface marker-based TCR sequencing data from eight tumor digests from patients evaluated in this study (retrospective and validation: 4285, 4342, 4393, 4400, 4432, 4513, 4520, and 4612) by comparing their frequencies in the CD4+ bulk population to the ADGRG1+ population and then mapping the clonotypic CDR3β TCR sequences onto the combined scRNA data from all patients (Fig. 5 A and B). Any TCR clonotype present at higher frequencies in the ADGRG1+ sorted wells than in the CD4+ bulk sorted wells was denoted as “ADGRG1-Enriched” with all other clonotypes defined as “ADGRG1 Nonenriched.” All known TCRs from these patients found in scRNA (n = 62 TCR clonotypes, 25 below detection limit in the pooled scRNA analysis) were then projected and visualized on the transcriptomic maps (Fig. 5C).

Cluster 5 comprised a population of CD4+ T cells that were enriched for CXCL13 and ADGRG1 mRNA expression and negative for the cardinal Treg marker FOXP3 (Fig. 5B). ADGRG1-enriched clones appeared to map to cluster 5 in concordance with known NeoTCR4s and in contrast to ADGRG1 nonenriched clones (Fig. 5 C and D). Importantly, ADGRG1-enriched clones also demonstrated significantly higher NeoTCR4 signature scores when compared to CD4+ bulk clones indicating high enrichment of both known and untested antitumor NeoTCR4s from patient tumors (Mann–Whitney P < 0.0001, Fig. 5 D and E). Taken together, these data indicate that ADGRG1 cell surface expression can potentially be used, either alone or in combination with additional markers, to enrich CD4+ neoantigen-reactive TCR clonotypes within metastatic tumor deposits from patients with solid epithelial cancer.

Isolation of ADGRG1- or CD86-Expressing Cells Enables Direct Transcriptomic Analysis and Functional Reactivity of Neoantigen-Specific CD4+ TIL Upon Expansion.

Finally, we directly tested the populations obtained by FACS-sorting CD4+ TIL based on either ADGRG1 or CD86 cell surface protein expression from four biological specimens: metastatic colorectal cancers 4285 and 4342, which were previously studied in other experiments, as well as new samples 4637 and 4644, metastatic colorectal and ovarian cancers, respectively (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8). Flow cytometric analysis of these four samples showed that cell surface ADGRG1 and CD86 were much more limited in their protein expression compared to previously described markers of CD4+ neoantigen-reactive TIL, with ADGRG1 showing the most restricted expression at only 4% of all CD4+ TIL (Fig. 6B). Despite prior studies, notably, in these samples, ICOS expression was nearly universal, suggesting limited utility as a discriminatory marker for CD4+ neoantigen-reactive TIL (29). Coexpression analysis of ADGRG1 and CD86 showed a very limited overlap between the two markers, with only around 1% of all CD4+ TIL expressing both markers (Fig. 6C).

FACS-sorting of these samples allowed direct sequencing-based transcriptomic comparisons of the marker-expressing cells versus their bulk CD4+ TIL populations on 9883 TIL encompassing 6426 clones (Fig. 6 D–F, Dataset S4, SI Appendix, Fig. S9, and Methods). For ADGRG1, we were able to analyze samples 4342, 4637, and 4644. For CD86, we obtained populations from 4285, 4342, and 4637. A broad spectrum of CD4+ TIL transcriptomic states were captured by scRNA from the combined populations, ranging from naïve and memory TIL (C1, C2, C5) to TIL-Tregs (C0), to pre-exhausted and exhausted TIL (C3 and C6 respectively) to cytotoxic CD4+ TIL (C4) and proliferating TIL (C9) (Fig. 6F and Dataset S4). This analysis showed that ADGRG1 and CD86 selectively enriched cells within clusters 0, 4, 6, and 9 (Fig. 6E), representing Tregs, cytotoxic CD4+ TIL (NeoTCR4-Cyt), exhausted CD4+ TIL (NeoTCR4-Ex), and Ki67+ proliferative NeoTCR4, respectively (Fig. 6F and Datasets S3 and S4). Notably, neither marker enriched for the pre-exhausted TIL phenotype KLF2+ NeoTCR4-Pex (Fig. 6 F and G). Gene signature analyses confirmed that ADGRG1 and CD86 both independently enriched for the newly established NeoTCR4-Core signature along with NeoTCR4-Cyt- and NeoTCR4-Ex-expressing cells compared to bulk CD4 (Fig. 6G and Dataset S4). Interestingly, in these analyses, CD86-based FACS enriched for CD4+ cytotoxic TIL (NeoTCR4-Cyt) better than either bulk or ADGRG1-based sorting (Fig. 6G), suggesting the markers may enrich for phenotypically distinct CD4+ antitumor TIL. Importantly, among the 25 previously known CD4+ neoantigen-reactive TCR clonotypes detected in the scRNA experiment (Fig. 6H), ADGRG1 sorting enriched their relative frequency 2.92-fold while CD86 sorting enriched them 3.45-fold with no significant differences between the two markers at the clonal level (Fig. 6I). These data reinforce the utility of these two markers in enriching for tumor-reactive CD4+ TCR clonotypes in humans.

Last, although ADGRG1 did not appear to enrich NeoTCR4-Pex, we tested whether FACS and in vitro TIL expansion of ADGRG1+ CD4+ TIL enriched their functional reactivity within samples 4637 and 4644 (CD86-enrichment did not yield enough FACS-sorted TIL to set up these experiments). For these samples, we sorted both ADGRG1+ and ADGRG1− CD4+ TIL and expanded them via a Rapid Expansion Protocol (REP). Following the REP, the populations were cocultured with autologous dendritic cells (DCs) that had been incubated with pools of all the patients’ tumor mutation-encoded candidate neoantigen peptides and flow cytometry was performed to analyze the expression of CD4+ TIL activation marker OX40 (Fig. 6J). In sample 4637, the ADGRG1+ CD4+ TIL showed a single reactivity to a neoantigen within peptide pool 19 (~12% of cells showing OX40+, Fig. 6 J, Top). In sample 4644, the ADGRG1+ TIL showed a single CD4+ TIL-reactivity to a neoantigen within peptide pool 4 (~9% of cells showing OX40+, Fig. 6 J, Bottom). In neither sample did the ADGRG1− cells demonstrate specific reactivity, showing that ADGRG1+ sorted, in vitro-expanded CD4+ TIL can harbor functional neoantigen-reactive T cells.

Discussion

Adoptive cell transfer of neoantigen-reactive TIL has led to durable long-term responses in multiple patients, including a patient with advanced epithelial cancer who received infusions of an ACT product that appeared to be composed entirely of CD4+ neoantigen reactive T cells (3). The ability to isolate neoantigen-reactive CD4+ TIL from tumor samples using a cell surface protein marker would allow for enrichment of antitumor CD4+ TIL, either to serve as direct a source of ACT or as a population from which antitumor CD4+ TCRs (NeoTCR4s) can be identified. While CD8+ neoantigen-reactive TIL have been well studied due to available reagents such as class I HLA tetramers and their ability to directly recognize tumor cells via HLA class I, studies on markers of CD4+ TIL have been relatively limited (29, 33). Although PD-1 and ICOS have been shown to enrich CD4+ tumor-reactive T cells, in our metastatic solid epithelial cancer TIL samples the near universal expression of these markers may preclude selective enrichment of neoantigen-specific CD4+ TIL (Fig. 6B) (26, 29, 33). Within our previous work identifying molecular signatures for both NeoTCR4 and NeoTCR8 lymphocytes based on transcriptional states, successful identification rates (validated TCR reactivity against candidate neoantigens expressed in APCs) among CD4+ was 35.4% across four prospective patient samples, compared to 69.0% for CD8+ T cells (21), emphasizing the need to identify additional markers of tumor-reactive CD4+ T cells. Our unbiased examination of potential surface protein markers from the set of 130 CITE-Seq antibodies uncovered two markers, ADGRG1 and CD86, that could be used to enrich tumor-reactive CD4+ T cells.

Recent investigations from our group and others have included evaluation of the reactivity of patient CD8+ T cells with autologous organoid and xenograft tumors (34, 35), but this does not address the issue of whether CD4+ T cells, which generally recognize exogenous antigens, could recognize mutant or overexpressed gene products derived from tumor cells. Therefore, we evaluated the ability of T cells transduced with candidate CD4 TCRs to recognize autologous antigen-presenting cells pulsed with relevant or irrelevant tumor cell lysates. Using this approach, we identified 12 TCRs that appeared to specifically recognize autologous tumor cell lysates (Fig. 3 C and D), eight of which did not recognize neoantigen candidates. The presence of T cells bearing these TCRs in the NeoTCR4 cluster is indicative of their tumor specificity, but further studies are needed to explore the nature of these antigens.

CD86 and ADGRG1 were the most successful single markers in enriching NeoTCR4 cells via FACS-based isolation. ADGRG1 (GPR56) is a G-protein-coupled receptor which binds transglutaminase 2. ADGRG1 has been described on CD4+ tissue effector memory cells associated with markers of cytotoxicity such as KLRB1, KLRG1, and KLRF1 (36). It has additionally been found on cytotoxic cells such as NK and CD8+ lymphocytes in association with the production of cytolytic mediators (37). Previous studies including our own have commented on ADGRG1’s potential within mRNA as a marker for neoantigen reactivity, as ADGRG1 is the top-ranked gene in CD4 T-terminally exhausted cells in melanoma (20) and the fourth-ranked gene in the pan-cancer NeoTCR4 signature (21). This study, however, highlights its potential as a surface protein for enriching NeoTCR4 clonotypes from TIL.

ADGRG1-enriched populations further demonstrated concordance with both antitumor CD4+ T cell populations and high-scoring NeoTCR4 populations in meta-analysis of scRNA data from all patients utilized in this study. This confirmatory analysis solidified ADGRG1 as a promising surface marker for identification of antitumor CD4+ T cells with a nearly 10-fold enrichment of reactive CD4+ T cells.

The performance of CD86 in enriching for NeoTCR4 TIL was unexpected. Prior single cell transcriptomic studies including ours did not detect high gene expression of CD86 in TIL populations, highlighting the utility of the unbiased cell surface proteomic analyses to identify unanticipated proteins for tumor-reactive T cell enrichment as done in this study. CD86 is a membrane-bound protein of the immunoglobulin superfamily that functions primarily on antigen-presenting cells as a ligand for CD28 and CTLA-4 in mediating T cell interactions and costimulation (38), but its function on CD4+ lymphocytes is underexplored. One possible explanation for CD86 expression on antitumor CD4+ TIL could be a function of autostimulation of CD4+ T cells, or a result of translocation from antigen-presenting cells to CD4+ T cells (39, 40), indicating this might represent a marker of cellular interaction with appropriate antigen-presenting cells among the tumor microenvironment in the process of neoantigen recognition. This interpretation is suggested by our scRNA transcriptomic data which revealed a disparity in CD86 mRNA expression compared to protein expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Dataset S2), although, for many gene products, it is difficult to associate gene expression levels with protein levels. A complete understanding of the function of this protein is beyond the scope of our current investigation, although it does warrant future study.

Use of CD86 or ADGRG1 as single surface markers for NeoTCR4 enrichment appears to represent a viable strategy along with other reported approaches using cell surface marker expression such as PD-1 and ICOS, although in our hands widespread staining of both PD-1 and ICOS at the cell surface was observed (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, coexpression of ADGRG1 and CD86 markers indicate minimal overlap in four patient TIL samples (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8). Further investigation into the populations selected by such markers, however, does reveal some distinctions, as ADGRG1 and CD86 both enrich for NeoTCR4-Ex and NeoTCR4-Cyt cells but appear to select against the better phenotypic population NeoTCR4-Pex (Fig. 6). There also appears to be a preference for enriching CD4+ cytotoxic antitumor TIL (NeoTCR4-Cyt) by CD86-enrichment relative to ADGRG1-enrichment, although these observations need to be validated in a larger cohort analysis (Fig. 6H). Nonetheless, sorting and subsequent expansion of ADGRG1+ cells did enrich for functional NeoAg reactivity in two distinct samples, showing that the ADGRG1+ may not be completely exhausted. Combinatorial use of surface protein markers may even further increase NeoTCR4 enrichment capabilities, although such a stringent definition will yield very few cells overall and may come at the expense of unique NeoTCR4 clonotypes and even certain neoantigen reactivities entirely (Fig. 4B).

In summary, using unbiased high dimensional protein and mRNA analysis across retrospective and prospective samples, we found the unexpected cell surface markers CD86 and ADGRG1 were the most efficient single markers for enriching antitumor CD4+ T cells. Analysis of surface protein coexpression (Fig. 6C) reveals ADGRG1+ CD86+ as a potential highly specific combinatorial marker for NeoTCR4 cell enrichment. Given the high success rate in enriching for tumor-reactive CD4 TCR clonotypes based on ADGRG1 and CD86 cell surface protein expression we propose that these markers should be evaluated to isolate tumor-reactive CD4 TCR clonotypes for TCR-engineered cell therapies that may overcome the limitations of TIL exhaustion. Future studies should further evaluate these markers and their efficiency in isolating and expanding antitumor CD4+ TIL, and whether the quality and yields of these cells may be sufficient for ACT.

Methods

Tumor Samples.

All human tumor samples and blood products used in generation of lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells were obtained from patients enrolled on NCT00068003 and NCT00001823 clinical protocols at the NIH. Both protocols received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National Cancer Institute (NCI) at the NIH. All tumor samples were processed with a combination of mechanical separation, enzymatic digestion, and finally gentleMACS dissociation technology (Miltenyi Biotec, Gaithersburg, MD) as previously described. Resulting single cell suspensions were cryopreserved within the NCI tissue bank until time of experimentation.

Antibodies and FACS-Based Isolation.

All fluorescently labeled antibodies used for FACS-based isolation were purchased from BD Bioscience (Franklin Lakes, NJ) and Biolegend (San Diego, CA). Samples were analyzed either by flow cytometry or CITE-seq as indicated in individual experiments.

Cells were isolated via flow cytometry via Sony MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter (Sony Biotechnology, San Jose, CA). Mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) gates for each population were determined according to isotype controls for each individual patient sample.

Single-Cell FACS-Based Isolation and Transcriptomic Analysis.

Following FACS-based isolation, single cell sequencing was performed on the original three samples (4283, 4342, 4393) in separate captures via bulk TIL populations. Single-cell gene expression libraries were sequenced with a NextSeq 550 sequencer with output processed using the Cell Ranger V.9.0 pipeline (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) and analysis completed with R package Seurat V.5.1.0 as previously described (10, 21, 26). Data shown in this paper were computationally filtered onto only CD4+ cells based on CD4+ CITE-seq. For data in Fig. 6, cell hashing antibodies were used to coanalyze up to four samples in a single capture, and hashtag oligonucleotide (HTO) demultiplexing was used to define the sample identity of individual cells prior to analysis.

T Cell Receptor Sequencing.

For FACS-based isolation performed on 4285, 4342, 4393, 4520, 4513, 4400, 4432, 4342, 4560, and 4612, individual cells were isolated into either 96- or 384-well plates based on expression of individual markers of interest (ADGRG1, CD86, CD57, TIM-3) or combination (ADGRG1/CD86. The 96- or 384-well plates underwent TCRα/β pair sequencing with the SMARTer® Human scTCR a/b Profiling Kit (Takara Bio United States). In short, cDNA was obtained from sorted single cells prior to amplification of TCRα and TCRβ transcripts. Paired-end 2 × 300 bp reads were then captured using an Illumina MiSeq® instrument. TCR counts were generated using the MiXCR software package.

T Cell Receptor Reconstruction and Cloning.

Following TCR sequencing analysis, full-length TCRα/β pairs were cloned into pMSGV1 vector by Genscript (Piscataway, NJ). These plasmids were then transfected into 293GP cells and retrovirus was transduced into healthy donor PBL (41). TCR expression was verified by flow cytometry prior to use in experiments.

T Cell Culture and Rapid Expansion Protocol.

T cells derived from digestion of tumor tissue as described previously were maintained in T cell medium that is a 50:50 mixture of complete medium [RPMI1640 with 10% human AB serum (Valley Biomedical Inc.), 1× Penicillin/Streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 25 mM HEPES] and serum-free AIMV medium with no cytokines prior to use. TCR-transduced T cells were also maintained in the same T cell medium but with IL-2 (300 IU/mL). For the rapid expansion protocol (REP), T cells were incubated in T cell medium with anti-CD3 (OKT3, 30 ng/mL), IL-2 (3,000 IU/mL) and gamma irradiated (4,000 Gy) allogeneic feeders in 1:100 T cell to feeder ratio for 2 wk with periodic media exchange to maintain enhanced growth of the T cells as described previously (18).

T Cell Functional Assays.

TCR-transduced T cells or TIL were evaluated for functional reactivity via overnight coculture. T cells were incubated overnight within ELISpot plates along with patient-derived APCs pulsed with patient-specific mutant peptides constructed based on tumor sequencing (21). Following overnight coculture, cells were evaluated by flow cytometry for activation markers CD137 (4-1BB) and CD134 (OX40) and by ELISpot assay for IFN-γ release.

Patient-Derived Tumor Xenograft, Organoid, and Cell Line Preparation and Functional Screening.

All patient derived xenograft (PDX) tumors were implanted on mice approved under the NIH IRB animal protocol number SB-194. Fresh patient tumor samples were mechanically separated into fragments from the initial tissue harvest and implanted subcutaneously at the flank of NOD-scid-IL2rgnull (NSG, NCI, Frederick, MD) mice using a 20-gauge needle.

Simultaneously, fragments from initial tumor resection were processed via combination of mechanical, enzymatic, and finally gentleMACS dissociation technology (Miltenyi Biotec) for isolation of tumor cells and establishment of tumor organoid cultures.

For cell lysate experiments, freshly harvested PDX tumor, PDX derived cell lines or organoid-derived cell lines were mechanically separated and resuspended in deionized water. Cells were then freeze-thawed and resulting lysate was added to autologous DCs for coculture experiments.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical calculations and figure construction were performed via Prism program v8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis was performed via Prism software.
