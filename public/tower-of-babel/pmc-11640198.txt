Bilateral Lung Transplantation in Patients With Severe Chest Asymmetry: A Case Series From a Single Center
Authors: Paolo Mendogni, Alessandro Palleschi, Giacomo Grisorio, Alessandra Mazzucco, Cristina Diotti, Letizia Corinna Morlacchi, Valeria Rosetti, Gianluca Bonitta, Mario Nosotti, Lorenzo Rosso
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSuppurative lung diseases leading to end‐stage respiratory failure are typical indications for bilateral lung transplantation (LuTx). Some cases may present severe chest asymmetry because of recurrent infections or previous surgical procedures, and the most used surgical options are single LuTx and contralateral pneumonectomy or bilateral transplantation with graft downsizing. Our purpose is to evaluate our treatment protocols for these patients and review surgical strategies reported by others.We prospectively collected clinical data of patients with significant pleural cavity asymmetry who underwent bilateral LuTx at our center from 2017 to 2022. Clinical reports of all patients who underwent LuTx for end‐stage suppurative disease in the same period were reviewed as the control group.During the study period, 74 patients underwent bilateral LuTx for suppurative disease; seven of them presented with severe thoracic asymmetry, and all of them were extubated by the second postoperative day. The mean intensive care unit stay was 4 days. The postoperative radiological evaluation did not show clustering or atelectasis of the graft implanted in the smaller hemithorax. No perioperative major complications were recorded, and the average length of stay was 23 days. The perioperative course appeared remarkably good, and both the short‐ and long‐term follow‐up were similar to that of the control group.

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LuTx) is an established therapeutic option for end‐stage lung disease [1]. Suppurative lung diseases, such as bronchiectasis (BR) and cystic fibrosis (CF), should be treated with bilateral LuTx; this results in an improved pulmonary function and allows the removal of all contaminated lung tissue as a source of recurrent infections. However, some cases could show an important thoracic asymmetry due to previous infections or lung resections. Pneumonectomy and single LuTx could be an option to treat this condition but are associated with a high risk of complications and a need for extracorporeal support [2, 3, 4]. Another surgical option is bilateral LuTx with possible graft volume reduction by anatomical or non‐anatomical lung resection.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the surgical strategy we undertook in this subgroup of patients and discuss alternative solutions practiced in other centers.

Methods

This study is an electronic health record‐based retrospective observational case series on seven patients with suppurative lung diseases and severe chest asymmetry who underwent bilateral LuTxs at our institution between 2017 and 2022. Chest asymmetry was defined as a prominent difference in the diameters of the rib cage assessed on examination and the presence of mediastinal shift at computer tomography (CT). The electronic database was updated with clinical follow‐up by pulmonologists at least every 3 months. Extracted variables of interest included demographic data, surgical parameters, primary graft dysfunction occurrence (PGD), intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, hospital length of stay, and clinical outcomes as infections occurrence, acute and/or chronic rejection, and overall survival.

To get a comparison with our institutional benchmark we also extracted the data of 67 patients with CF or BR who underwent bilateral LuTx in the same period.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, categorial variables were represented as absolute and percentage frequencies. We deliberately avoided any inferential analysis given the small number of patients. This study was approved by the Milano‐2 Ethical Committee (n.181/24.01.2017)

Case 1

A 40‐year‐old female patient diagnosed with CF had a history of recurrent respiratory infections and progressive respiratory failure. Chest CT showed an important thoracic asymmetry with the mediastinum shifted to the left. Following an episode of massive hemoptysis, clinical conditions rapidly worsened. The patient needed Extra Corporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) and was enlisted in the National Emergency Lung Transplant Program with a Lung Allocation Score (LAS) of 62.11. The donor was a 36‐year‐old female 160 cm in height and 63 kg in weight with a total lung capacity (TLC) of 5.6 L. The donor/recipient height ratio was 0.98. The patient underwent bilateral LuTx through a clamshell thoracotomy. The ECMO shifted from veno–venous (VV) to veno–arterial (VA) during the surgery due to hemodynamic instability. Dense pleuropulmonary adhesions were found especially in the left hemithorax where we intended to transplant the first lung. Despite the smallness of left pleural cavity, we avoid any graft reduction by repositioning the mediastinum. Extubation was performed on the first postoperative day and the patient was discharged from ICU on 6th postoperative day (POD). Total hospitalization time was 41 days for acute kidney injury and a deep vein thrombosis, but no surgical complication occurred and the patient was discharged in optimal respiratory conditions. Seven years after transplantation, the patient is alive and in good clinical conditions; her best forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) is 126%.

Case 2

A 17‐year‐old female patient diagnosed with CF at birth had a history of recurrent respiratory infections causing lung parenchyma disruption and progressive respiratory failure. Thoracic computed tomography showed diffuse BR and lung atelectasis causing a severe thoracic asymmetry with the mediastinum shifted to the right, an almost complete right lung destruction, and a relevant dislocation of heart and great vessels (Figure 1). The patient was included in the waiting list with a LAS of 40.81 and underwent bilateral LuTx through a clamshell incision. The donor was a 51‐year‐old female with a 4.3 L of TLC, the donor/recipient height ratio of 0.96. Intraoperative tenacious pleuropulmonary adhesions on the right hemithorax were found, which made the surgery extremely challenging. Despite the severe mediastinal shift, the hemodynamic conditions remained stable and ECMO support was non needed. Median realignment of the mediastinum occurred without hemodynamic alterations during the transplantation of the right graft. Postoperative mechanical ventilation time was less than 24 h. ICU stay and hospital stay were 5 and 22 days, respectively. Six months after the transplantation an EBV‐related lymphoma was diagnosed and treated by chemotherapy with a complete response. Two years after the transplantation, the patient developed acute cellular rejection and chronic lung allograft dysfunction treated with steroids at high dose. Forty‐nine months after transplantation the patient developed a mild chronic allograft dysfunction (CLAD) after an episode of acute rejection. Almost 5 years after transplantation the patient is alive, in good clinical condition and with current FEV1% of 68%.

Case 3

A 27‐year‐old male diagnosed with CF in childhood had a history of multidrug‐resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonization, progressive loss of pulmonary function, need for non‐invasive ventilation and recurrent pneumothorax. Chest x‐ray and CT scan showed a thoracic asymmetry with the mediastinum shifted to the left (Figure 2). The patient was included in the lung transplant waiting list with a LAS of 34.3. The patient underwent a bilateral lung transplant via clamshell incision. There were firm pleuropulmonary adhesions in the left hemithorax where the first lung was transplanted without any parenchymal reduction. Due to hemodynamic instability, VA‐ECMO was required between the first and second lung; the extracorporeal assistance was removed at the end of the operation. The donor/recipient height ratio was 1.08 with a considerable difference in weight 70 kg versus 53 kg. The mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and hospital stay were 1, 3, and 19 days, respectively. The patient is alive, in good clinical condition, and with good respiratory function, 55 months after transplantation.

Case 3: Preoperative CT scan showing left lung destruction with left pneumothorax and postoperative computed tomography scan showing mediastinal realignment.

Case 4

A 48‐year‐old female patient affected by diffuse BR and advanced obliterating bronchiolitis with progressive loss of pulmonary function and need for supplemental oxygen therapy was referred to our center. CT scan showed diffuse BR and an asymmetric thorax, with the mediastinum greatly shifted to the right (Figure 3). After an acute respiratory infection, clinical conditions rapidly worsened. The patient needed extracorporeal life support by VV‐ECMO and was enlisted in the National Emergency Lung Transplant Program, with a LAS of 81.16. The donor was a 28‐year‐old female with a height ratio of 1.06 and TLC of 5.8L. The patient underwent bilateral LuTx via clamshell incision. The ECMO support was not shifted from VV to VA despite the significant bleeding during right pneumonectomy due to the adhesiolysis. In this case, we avoid any graft reduction. The patient was extubated on POD 2 and discharged from ICU on POD 4. Total hospitalization time was 19 days, and the postoperative course was uneventful. Four years after transplantation, the patient is alive and in good clinical conditions.

Case 4: Preoperative CT scans showing bronchiectasis associated with total right lung destruction and severe mediastinal retraction to the right and postoperative axial and coronal CT sections showing an adequate realignment of airways and mediastinal structures.

Case 5

A 28‐year‐old female affected by CF with a clinical history of persistent respiratory infections, P. aeruginosa colonization, recurrent hemoptysis, and progressive respiratory failure was referred at our Center. CT scan showed a relevant thoracic asymmetry with an important mediastinal shift to the left (Figure 4). Despite the clinical conditions, the patient successfully carried out her pregnancy before the transplantation. A respiratory exacerbation after the first administration of COVID‐19 vaccine caused a sudden worsening of respiratory failure, with the need of VV‐ECMO support as a bridge to transplantation. The patient was immediately included on the National Emergency Lung Transplant Program with a LAS of 67.63; the donor was a 52‐year‐old female 150 cm height (ratio 0.98) with a TLC of 5.8 L. The patient underwent bilateral LuTx via clamshell incision and VA‐ECMO support was needed during surgery. A left lung reduction through wedge resection of the upper lobe was needed due to significant size mismatch between lung and pleural cavity despite the mediastinal realignment. The patient was extubated within 24 h and discharged from ICU on POD 2. The postoperative course was regular and the total hospital stay after the transplantation was 20 days. The 34‐month follow‐up was uneventful and the patient the patient is still in good clinical and respiratory conditions.

Case 5: Preoperative CT scans showing a destroyed left lung with extensive cavities with mediastinum shifted to the left and postoperative axial and coronal CT sections showing a complete mediastinal realignment.

Case 6

A 41‐year‐old male affected by CF with a clinical history of recurrent respiratory infections and P. aeruginosa and Achromobacter colonization leading to chronic respiratory failure was referred at our Center. CT scan showed a relevant thoracic asymmetry with heart and mediastinum greatly shifted to the right due to the almost complete atelectasis of the right lung and subsequent hyperinflation of the left lung. The patient was included in the lung transplant waiting list with a LAS of 40.99 and underwent bilateral LuTx via clamshell. Donor was a 53‐year‐old female 165 cm in height (height ratio 1.0) and 47 kg in weight with 4.3 L of TLC. The presence of dense pleural adhesions, bulky hilar lymph nodes, and the relevant mediastinal shift made the surgery extremely challenging; however, intraoperative hemodynamic conditions remained stable also after right pneumonectomy and there was no need of extracorporeal oxygenation support. Mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and total hospital stay were 2, 4, and 17 days, respectively. During the hospitalization, the patient tested positive for SARS CoV‐2, without severe respiratory symptoms, and was efficiently treated with Remdesivir. Twenty‐five months after the transplantation, the patient is well, in good clinical conditions currently at his best of respiratory function with a FEV1 4.31 L (137%).

Case 7

A 37‐year‐old male affected by CF was referred to our Centre after a significant increase in number of respiratory infections in the previous 6 months conditioning hospitalization. He had P. aeruginosa, Achromobacter xylosodoxidans, and Escherichia coli respiratory colonization and a progressive respiratory failure leading to domiciliary non‐invasive ventilation need. The patient was enlisted with an LAS of 36.09; he underwent bilateral LuTx via clamshell incision with no need of ECMO support despite the significative adhesion syndrome and the remarkable left‐side mediastinal shift. The donor was a 64‐year‐old male with a TLC of 4.7 L (height ratio of 1.0). No graft reduction was performed. Mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and total hospitalization were 1, 4, and 18 days, respectively; postoperative course was characterized by prolonged air leak treated conservatively by drainage aspiration. Seven months after discharge the patient was hospitalized in another institution for acute respiratory failure based on P. aeruginosa multi‐drug resistant and E. coli pneumonia from which he fully recovered. Almost a year after transplantation the patient needed hospitalization for a SARS‐CoV2‐related infection conditioning respiratory failure from which he is still recovering.

Control Group

From 2017 to 2022 a total of 67 patients underwent bilateral LuTx for suppurative end‐stage lung diseases without significative chest asymmetry (control group) (Figure 5). Table 2 shows the aggregate data of the control and study group. In summary, the chest asymmetry group median age was 34.0 years, three patients were female (42.9%); the control group had a median age of 34 with 49.3% of female patients. The patients with chest asymmetry were enlisted with a median LAS of 53,5 compared with a LAS of 41.6 of the control group. V‐A ECMO support was required intraoperatively for four of the seven patients with thoracic asymmetry (57.1%) and for 34 (50.7%) of the patients in the control group. No patients with thoracic asymmetry needed major anatomic resections due to size mismatch but one patient received an atypical resection of the lingula; within the control group seven patients required volume adjustments (four anatomical and three atypical resections).

Discussion

Bilateral LuTx is the treatment of choice for patients affected with end‐stage lung suppurative diseases, such as CF and BR; in these cases, single LuTx is not recommended because chronical bacterial infections coming from the native remaining lung can infect the graft and compromise its function. In recent years, the overall number of bilateral LuTxs has been constantly increased also in non‐suppurative lung diseases, due to better functional results and the overall survival [1, 5, 6].

In some cases, patients affected with suppurative diseases, recurrent infections can cause an important destruction of lung parenchyma, resulting in cavities, fibrosis, atelectasis, bronchial dilatation, airways distortion, and chest wall retraction that significantly modify mediastinal realignment and volume of pleural cavities.

Severe chest asymmetry caused by previous surgeries or recurrent infections destroying the parenchyma is not considered an absolute contraindication for transplantation; however, bilateral transplantation can be technically challenging, even if performed in high‐experienced transplant centers [7]. As proof of this, some of our patients had already been excluded by other Italian transplant centers. Therefore, a careful preoperative evaluation is mandatory to assess surgical risks. Dense pleuropulmonary adhesions can make difficult the pneumonectomies and cause severe bleeding; furthermore, the dislocation of heart and great vessels associated with the mediastinum shift can induce serious intraoperative hemodynamic instability. In these cases, extracorporeal circulatory support could be required despite the higher risk of bleeding complications. In our experience, ECMO support was necessary in the case of severe hemodynamic instability: four patients needed intraoperative ECMO, a slightly higher rate than the control group. To limit the use of ECMO, our strategy was to remove the destroyed lung and transplant the first graft into the reduced pleural cavity, allowing the realignment of the mediastinum.

To note, donor/recipient size match can be challenging in these patients, considering the significant retraction of one hemithorax and the mediastinal shift [2]. To limit the impact of the size discrepancy, donors selected for our patients with asymmetry were very similar in size based on gender, height and weight. Calculation of predicted total lung capacity (p‐TLC) and p‐TLC ratio between donor and recipient was also used to limit the need for lobar transplantation with a 20% discrepancy accepted. In contrast, 10% of patients in the control population received organs in need of volume reduction. Two recent studies from our institution demonstrate how lobar transplantation can increase the donor pool for patients with a small chest cavity with comparable postoperative and survival outcomes to patients undergoing standard transplantation [8, 9]. Despite this experience, the case series presented in this study demonstrates how it is possible to achieve the realignment of the mediastinum and the return to the symmetry of the thorax using the entire two lungs of a donor with dimensions equal to the recipient.

In case of chest asymmetry, our strategy in unusual and various surgical options have been reported by others [10]. A retrospective single‐center study by Sinn et al. including 13 patients from the Vienna Lung Transplant Center proposed different surgical alternatives: unilateral full‐size and contralateral lobar transplantation, standard bilateral transplantation after mobilization/repositioning of the mediastinum, oversized single transplant followed by pneumonectomy on the smaller contralateral side and single transplant after a previous contralateral pneumonectomy. Results showed higher perioperative complications in the asymmetric group (especially in single lung transplant groups) if compared with patients without chest asymmetry [2].

Single LuTx after a previous contralateral pneumonectomy has also been described. However, higher risks of surgical complications such as bronchopleural fistula or contralateral empyema and a higher perioperative mortality related to this surgical strategy were reported [4, 6].

In our center, sequential bilateral LuTx is preferred in patients affected with suppurative diseases, even in the case of significant asymmetric thorax. In all the cases with chest asymmetry, the planned surgical access to the thorax was a bilateral anterolateral trans‐sternal thoracotomy (clamshell incision). We recommend clamshell incision specifically in this subset of patients as it allows complete access to the mediastinum and the pleural cavities; the complete release of the mediastinum from the sternal plane was comfortable, the pericardial release was easily achieved allowing its mobilization toward the median area after the first graft transplant. We always started surgery with the pneumonectomy of the worst affected lung, which, in the case of asymmetric chest, commonly corresponds to the side of the retracted hemithorax.

We do not consider previous lung resection as a contraindication even if a tailored surgical plan is recommended. In this regard, a patient with severe asymmetry and previous left inferior lobectomy recently received a bilateral transplantation at our center; this approach proved to be successful once again as mediastinal mobilization was achieved, no volume reduction was needed, and surgery was only longer than usual; postoperative course was uneventful. Considering patient with chest asymmetry and previous thoracic surgery special attention must be given to any sign of fibrothorax (e.g., previous talcage or decortication); as in these patient's adequate mobilization of mediastinum and recovery of chest mobility are not feasible we advise against bilateral transplantation.

One limitation of this study is the impossibility of inferential statistical analysis due to the small number of patients with asymmetry treated. Nevertheless, both short‐ and medium‐term outcome of these patients was remarkably good even in presence of high‐risk conditions.

In our case series, bilateral transplantation was successfully performed in seven patients with severe chest asymmetry without major intra, peri, and postoperative complications: our approach seems a feasible and promising procedure in this selected cluster of patients, even without extracorporeal support. We believe that standard bilateral transplantation is the best choice for patients with end‐stage lung suppurative diseases and it should always be considered as first approach even in presence of severe chest asymmetry.
