Numerical Analysis of Stabilization for Random Hyperbolic Systems of Conservation Laws
Shaoshuai Chu, Michael Herty, Alexander Kurganov

Abstract
This paper extends the deterministic Lyapunov-based stabilization framework to random hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, where uncertainties arise in boundary controls and initial data. Building on the finite-volume discretization method from [ M. Banda and M. Herty , Math. Control Relat. Fields, 3 (2013), pp. 121–142], we introduce a stochastic discrete Lyapunov function to prove the exponential decay of numerical solutions for systems with random perturbations. For linear systems, we derive explicit decay rates, which depend on boundary control parameters, grid resolutions, and the statistical properties of the random inputs. Theoretical decay rates are verified through numerical examples, including boundary stabilization of the linear wave equations and linearized shallow-water flows with random perturbations. We also present the decay rates for a nonlinear example and for the linearized Saint-Venant system with source terms.



Key words:
Random hyperbolic systems of conservation laws; exponential stability; boundary feedback control; Lyapunov
functions.

AMS subject classification:
65M12, 93D05, 65M08, 35L53, 35L65.

Introduction

The study of stabilization for hyperbolic systems has been an active field of research, particularly in the context of control theory and
numerical analysis. A key aspect of the stabilization analysis in hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs) involves feedback
boundary conditions, which have been extensively studied in various contexts; see, e.g.,
[
8
,
4
]
. These studies
highlight the importance of well-posed boundary conditions in ensuring controllability and stabilizability. The interplay between analytical
techniques and numerical discretization methods has also been explored in
[
3
]
, where finite-volume schemes are employed to
preserve the stability properties of the continuous problem. Building on these insights, later studies have broadened the stabilization
toolbox by developing approaches based on Lyapunov functions, feedback boundary control, and further refined numerical discretization
techniques; see, e.g.,

[
5
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
36
,
1
]
. In particular, for the stabilization of Saint-Venant system of shallow-water equations,
substantial progress has been achieved in the analysis of related models that incorporate source terms. For instance, dissipative boundary
conditions for one-dimensional (1-D) nonlinear hyperbolic systems were investigated in
[
9
]
, while a Lyapunov-based
framework for exponential stability of 1-D linear hyperbolic balance laws was developed in
[
14
]
. Extensions to more complex
systems, such as the Saint-Venant-Exner model, were studied in
[
15
]
, where backstepping-based stabilization techniques were
proposed. More recently, numerical boundary feedback stabilization for non-uniform hyperbolic balance laws was introduced in
[
2
]
.
These works highlight the importance of accounting for source terms and provide valuable insights into the stabilization of the Saint-Venant
system and related models.

Recent studies have introduced Lyapunov-based approaches to establish exponential stability for deterministic hyperbolic balance laws. These approaches provide a rigorous framework for analyzing the long-term behavior of solutions in the presence of well-posed boundary conditions; see, e.g.,
[
23
]
. The application of discrete Lyapunov functions has been particularly successful in proving the stability of numerical schemes, as it allows for explicit stability bounds and decay rates under controlled settings; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
4
,
34
]
.

This paper aims to bridge this gap by extending the Lyapunov-based stabilization framework introduced in
[
1
]

to hyperbolic balance laws with stochastic perturbations. This extension is motivated by the increasing interest in stochastic PDEs and their applications in modeling uncertainties in physical systems, including fluid dynamics, traffic flow, and networked systems, where random variations must be accounted for in the stability analysis; see, e.g.,
[
22
,
17
,
21
,
18
]
. While previous studies have extensively addressed deterministic stabilization techniques, the extension to random systems remains relatively underdeveloped. The incorporation of randomness into the stabilization framework introduces new mathematical challenges, particularly in the construction of appropriate Lyapunov functions that account for stochastic perturbations. Similar approaches were developed in the context of stochastic control theory, where Lyapunov techniques were adapted to random dynamical systems; see, e.g.,
[
28
]
. By incorporating randomness into the framework established in
[
1
]
, this study advances the theoretical understanding of stabilization for stochastic hyperbolic balance laws and provides computational results that support the theoretical findings.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. §

Finally, we give some concluding remarks and potential directions for future research in §

2
An Overview of the Studied Hyperbolic Systems

In this section, we give a brief introduction to the studied problem and refer the reader to
[
1
]
for more details.

We consider the following initial-boundary value problems for 1-D nonlinear hyperbolic PDEs:

\bm{u}_{t}+\bm{f}(\bm{u})_{x}=\bm{0},\quad x\in[0,1],\,\,t\in(0,T],

(2.1)

with the initial conditions

\bm{u}(0,x,\xi)=\bm{\psi}(x,\xi),\quad x\in[0,1],

(2.2)

and the boundary conditions, which will be specified later. In (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),
t
is the time,
x
is the spatial variable,
\xi
is the random variable,
\bm{u}=\big(u^{(1)},\ldots,u^{(p)}\big)^{\top}
is the vector of unknowns, and
\bm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes a possibly nonlinear smooth flux function. The system (
2.1
) is assumed to be strictly hyperbolic, meaning that the Jacobian matrix
F(u)={\partial\bm{f}}/{\partial\bm{u}}
has real distinct eigenvalues. For smooth solutions
\bm{u}(x,t,\xi)
, (
2.1
) can be rewritten in a quasi-linear form as

\bm{u}_{t}+F(\bm{u})\bm{u}_{x}=0,\quad\bm{u}(0,x,\xi)=\bm{\psi}(x,\xi).

(2.3)

The eigenvalues of
F(\bm{u})
are denoted by
\Lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,p
, and we assume that there exists
m
such that for any
\bm{u}
,

\Lambda_{p}<\ldots<\Lambda_{m+1}<0<\Lambda_{m}<\ldots<\Lambda_{1}.

This classification allows us to define the decomposition of
\bm{u}
into components associated with positive and negative eigenvalues:

\bm{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}^{+}\\
\bm{u}^{-}\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{u}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\bm{u}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-m}
correspond to the positively and negatively propagating characteristics, respectively. Similarly, we decompose the flux function:

F(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}F^{+}(\bm{u})\\
F^{-}(\bm{u})\end{pmatrix},

where
F^{+}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
,
F^{-}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{(p-m)\times p}
. In addition,
G:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

is a possibly nonlinear boundary operator

G(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}G^{+}(\bm{u})\\
G^{-}(\bm{u})\end{pmatrix},

which determines how information propagates at the domain boundaries with
G^{+}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and

G^{-}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p-m}
representing the corresponding flux components.

As in
[
1
]
, we adopt a general feedback-type boundary condition, which reads as

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}^{+}(t,0,\xi)\\
\bm{u}^{-}(t,1,\xi)\end{pmatrix}=G\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}^{+}(t,1,\xi)\\
\bm{u}^{-}(t,0,\xi)\end{pmatrix}.

3
The Discretization Scheme

In this section, we study the numerical schemes for boundary
L^{2}
-stabilization of 1-D nonlinear random hyperbolic systems. We first consider a simple case with
m\equiv p
, that is,
\Lambda_{i}>0
,
\forall i
,
F(\bm{u})
is diagonal, and
G
is a linear operator with
G_{ij}=0
,
i\neq j
.

Proposition 3.1

Assume that
F
is diagonal for all
\bm{u}\in B_{\varepsilon}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
where
B_{\varepsilon}(0)
is an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
centered at the origin and with radius
\varepsilon
. Also assume that

F(\bm{u})={\rm diag}\big(\Lambda_{1}(\bm{u}),\ldots,\Lambda_{m}(\bm{u})\big),\quad\Lambda_{i}>0,\quad\Lambda_{i}(\bm{u})\neq\Lambda_{j}(\bm{u}),\quad\forall i\neq j,

(3.1)

and the boundary conditions are prescribed as

\bm{u}(t,0,\xi)=\mathcal{K}\bm{u}(t,1,\xi),

(3.2)

where

\mathcal{K}={\rm diag}\big(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{m}\big),\quad\kappa_{i}>0,\,\,\forall i.

If
\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,m}\kappa_{i}<1
, then the equilibrium
\bm{u}\equiv 0
for (
2.3
) with (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) is exponentially stable.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
immediately follows from the fact that
\rho(\mathcal{K})=\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,m}\kappa_{i}<1
.

We now introduce the numerical discretization. We assume that
\delta>0
is sufficiently small such that
M_{\delta}(0)\subset B_{\varepsilon}(0)
with

M_{\delta}(0):=\{\bm{u}:|u^{(i)}|\leq\delta,\,\,i=1,\dots,m\}.

We introduce a uniform grid
(x_{j},\,\xi_{k})
for
j=0,\ldots,M
and
k=0,\ldots,K
with
x_{j+1}-x_{j}\equiv\Delta x
,
\xi_{k+1}-\xi_{k}\equiv\Delta\xi
. The temporal grid is chosen such that the CFL condition holds

\lambda\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\leq 1,\quad\lambda:=\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,m}\max\limits_{\bm{u}\in M_{\delta(0)}}\Lambda_{i}(\bm{u})

(3.3)

and
t^{n}=n\Delta t
for
n=0,1,\ldots N
, where by possibly further reducing
\Delta t
, one can enforce
N\Delta t=T
.

Assume that the initial conditions
\bm{u}^{\,0}_{j,k}=\bm{\psi}(x_{j},\xi_{k})
,
j=1,\ldots,M
,
k=0,\ldots,K
are available. We then use the boundary conditions (
3.2
) to obtain

u^{(i),0}_{0,k}=\kappa_{i}u^{(i),0}_{M,k}
,
k=0,\ldots,K
, and then evolve the solution in time by the upwind scheme used in
[
1
]
:

\displaystyle u_{j,k}^{(i),n+1}

\displaystyle=u_{j,k}^{(i),n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{i}\left(\bm{u}_{j,k}^{n}\right)\left(u_{j,k}^{(i),n}-u_{j-1,k}^{(i),n}\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,M,\,k=0,\ldots,K,\,i=1,\ldots,m,

(3.4)

\displaystyle u_{0,k}^{(i),n+1}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}u_{M,k}^{(i),n+1},\quad k=0,\ldots,K,\,i=1,\ldots,m.

(3.5)

Notice that the scheme (
3.4
)–(
3.5
) is nonconservative, but this is not a problem since in this paper we only consider smooth solutions of (
2.1
)–(
2.2
). It needs to be noted that if
{u}^{(i),n}_{j,k}\in M_{\delta(0)}
then

0<D^{(i),n}_{j,k}:=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{i}\big(\bm{u}^{n}_{j,k}\big)\leq\frac{\Lambda_{i}\big(\bm{u}^{n}_{j,k}\big)}{\lambda}\leq 1,

(3.6)

and the scheme (
3.4
)–(
3.5
) can be rewritten as

u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}=u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)+u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}D^{(i),n}_{j,k},\quad u^{(i),n+1}_{0,k}=\kappa_{i}u^{(i),n+1}_{M,k}.

(3.7)

Proposition 3.2

Assume (
3.1
), (
3.2
),
\big|u^{(i),0}_{j,k}\big|\leq\delta
for
i=1,\ldots,m
,
j=1,\ldots,M
,
k=0,\ldots,K
, and
\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,m}\kappa_{i}\leq 1
. Then the scheme (
3.4
)–(
3.5
) satisfies

\big|u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big|\leq\delta,\quad\forall\,i,j,k,n.

Proof
. We prove this proposition by induction. Assume that
\big|u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big|\leq\delta
and therefore
D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\in(0,1]
. Then, (
3.7
) implies

\displaystyle\big|u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big|

\displaystyle=\big|u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)+u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big|\leq\big|u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big|\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)+\big|u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}\big|D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\leq\delta,

for all

,

, and

, which completes the proof of the proposition.

\hfill\square

,
k=0,\ldots,K
, and
j=1,\ldots,M
(for
j=M
, one needs to also use (
3.20
) to establish the last inequality in (
3.22
)).

Therefore, one obtains

\displaystyle D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{1.24})}{\leq}\Big(D^{(i),n}_{j,k}+\frac{\mu_{i}\Delta x}{2}D^{\rm min}_{i}\Big)e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}

(3.23)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{1.14})}{\leq}D^{\rm min}_{i}\big(e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}-1\big)+\frac{\mu_{i}\Delta x}{2}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}\leq-\frac{\mu_{i}\Delta x}{2}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}.

Next, using (
3.23
) in (
3.21
) leads to

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal L}^{n+1}-{\cal L}^{n}}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle\leq-\dfrac{\Delta x\Delta\xi}{2\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}\mu_{i}\Delta xe^{-\mu_{i}x_{j}}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k})

(3.24)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta x\Delta\xi}{\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(D^{(i),n}_{1,k}\kappa_{i}^{2}e^{-\mu_{i}x_{1}}-D^{(i),n}_{M+1,k}e^{-\mu_{i}(x_{M}+\Delta x)}\Big)\big(u^{(i),n}_{M,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k}).

The last term on the RHS of (
3.24
) is nonpositive since

\displaystyle D^{(i),n}_{1,k}\kappa_{i}^{2}e^{-\mu_{i}x_{1}}-D^{(i),n}_{M+1,k}D^{(i),n}_{M+1,k}e^{-\mu_{i}(x_{M}+\Delta x)}\overset{(\ref{1.13})}{\leq}D^{\rm max}_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}e^{-\mu_{i}x_{1}}-D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}(x_{M}+\Delta x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}(x_{M}+\Delta x)}\Bigg(\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{D^{\rm max}_{i}}{D^{\rm min}_{i}}}\kappa_{i}\Bigg)^{2}e^{\mu_{i}(x_{M}+\Delta x-x_{1})}-1\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}(x_{M}+\Delta x)}\Bigg(\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{D^{\rm max}_{i}}{D^{\rm min}_{i}}}\kappa_{i}\Bigg)^{2}e^{\mu_{i}}-1\Bigg)\overset{(\ref{1.18})}{\leq}0.

Hence,

\frac{{\cal L}^{n+1}-{\cal L}^{n}}{\Delta t}\leq-\dfrac{\Delta x\Delta\xi}{2\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}\mu_{i}\Delta xe^{-\mu_{i}x_{j}}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k})\overset{(\ref{1.19a})}{\leq}-\nu{\cal L}^{n}.

Therefore, we obtain

{\cal L}^{n+1}-{\cal L}^{n}\leq-\nu\Delta t{\cal L}^{n},

and by recursively applying the previous inequality, we end up with

{\cal L}^{n}\leq(1-\nu\Delta t)^{n}{\cal L}^{0}\leq e^{-\nu t^{n}}{\cal L}^{0}=e^{-\nu t^{n}}{\cal L}^{0}.

Finally, the inequality (
3.18
) follows from (
3.16
) using the definition (
3.12
) and estimate (
3.13
).

\hfill\square

Remark 3.1

From the proof of Theorem
3.4
, we observe that its statement is also true in the case

-\sqrt{\frac{D^{\min}_{i}}{D^{\max}_{i}}}<\kappa_{i}<0
, and we omit the proof for the sake of brevity. The case
\kappa_{i}=0
is a special case.
Here, the boundary condition for the component
i
is equal to zero and this state is propagated through the domain. From the practical
point of view, this is not interesting since the system would be controlled by precisely the state (namely zero), which it is
intended to reach. The previous proof yields in the case
\kappa_{i}=0
no bound on the component
\mu_{i}
. In the case
\kappa_{i}=0
for all

i=1,\ldots,m
, we obtain exponential convergence for any rate
\nu>0
.

Remark 3.2

Note that the results are independent of the choice of either a general or uniform probability density function since the decay rate
\nu

is independent of the values of the probability density
\rho(\xi)
function.

4.1
Extension of Theorem
3.4
to the Case of Linear
F

In the linear case, a stronger result can be obtained. Assuming

F(u)={\rm diag}(\Lambda_{i})^{m}_{i=1},\,\Lambda_{i}>0,\,\Lambda_{i}\neq\Lambda_{j},\,i\neq j

(4.1)

holds, then (
3.3
), (
3.8
), and (

), yield

\lambda=\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,m}\Lambda_{i},\,\,\,D^{(i),n}_{j,k}=D^{\rm max}_{i}=D^{\rm min}_{i}=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{i}.

Note that in this case,
D^{(i),n}_{j,k}
is independent of
j
,
k
, and

. Therefore, in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, the estimate (
3.11
) on the discrete derivative of

is not needed. Hence, one can use only Proposition

to derive the following estimate as in the proof of Theorem

\frac{{\cal L}^{n+1}-{\cal L}^{n}}{\Delta t}\leq-\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\min\limits_{i=1,\ldots,m}\big\{\mu_{i}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}\big\}{\cal L}^{n}.

Observe that compared with (

) an improved bound on the decay rate is obtained. Note that (

) reduces now to

0<\kappa_{i}<1,\quad\mu_{i}\leq\ln(\kappa_{i}^{-2}).

As one can see, all bounds are now independent of
\delta
, and hence a finite terminal time
T
is not needed. The results of the discussion above are summarized in the following Theorem.

Theorem 4.1

Assume that (
4.1
) holds. For any
\kappa_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
such that
0<\kappa_{i}<1
, there exists
\mu_{i}>0
,

the numerical solution

)–(

4.2
Extension of Theorem
3.4
to the Case
m\neq p

Recall that in §
3
, we assumed that

, that is, all transport coefficients
\Lambda_{i}(\cdot)
were positive. When

{\cal L}^{n}=\Delta x\Delta\xi\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{-\mu_{i}x_{j}}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k})+\Delta x\Delta\xi\sum_{i=m+1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k}),

(4.2)

where, as before,
\mu_{i}>0
,
\forall i
, and it is assumed that for all
\bm{\eta}\in M_{\delta(0)}

\Lambda_{i}(\bm{\eta})>0,\,\,i=1,\ldots,m,\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\,\Lambda_{i}(\bm{\eta})<0,\,i=m+1,\ldots,p.

(4.3)

Furthermore, the discrete boundary conditions need to be modified accordingly, namely, we set

(4.4)

and

u^{(i),n}_{M+1,k}=\kappa_{i}u^{(i),n}_{1,k},\,\,i=m+1,\ldots,p,\,\,k=0,\ldots K.

The upwind scheme is also different now. While (

)–(
3.5
) still can be used for
i\leq m
, for

, the upwinding direction changes and thus (

)–(

) should be replaced with

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359ptu^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=u^{(i),n}_{j,k}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{i}(\bm{u}^{\,n}_{j+1,k})\big(u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}-u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big),\,\,j=1,\ldots,M,\,k=0,\ldots,K,\,i=m+1,\ldots,p,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359ptu^{(i),n+1}_{M+1,k}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}u^{(i),n+1}_{1,k},\quad k=0,\ldots,K,\,i=m+1,\ldots,p.

(4.7)

Similarly, the CFL condition (
3.3
) and the definitions of
D^{(i),n}_{j,k}
,
D^{\rm max}_{i}
, and

in (

), (

), and (

) should be adjusted to

\lambda\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\leq 1,\,\,\lambda:=\max\limits_{i=1,\ldots,p}\max\limits_{\bm{u}\in M_{\delta(0)}}|\Lambda_{i}(\bm{u})|,

(4.9)

respectively.

We now consider
i\geq m+1
, and rewrite (
4.6
) as

u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}=u^{(i),n}_{j,k}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}|\Lambda_{i}(\bm{u}^{\,n}_{j+1,k})|\big(u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}-u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)\overset{(\ref{1.40})}{=}u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)+D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k},

(4.10)

which can be derived precisely the same way (
3.19
) was obtained. We then multiply (
4.10
) by
e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}
and sum over

to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Big(\big(u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big)^{2}-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\Big)e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\Big(\big(u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)^{2}-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\Big)e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\big(D^{(i),n}_{j,k}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}-D^{(i),n}_{1,k}\big(u^{(i),n}_{1,k}\big)^{2}e^{\mu_{i}x_{0}}+D^{(i),n}_{M,k}\big(u^{(i),n}_{M+1,k}\big)^{2}e^{\mu_{i}x_{M}}

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\overset{(\ref{1.22}),(\ref{1.33})}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\big(D^{(i),n}_{j,k}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}+\big(u^{(i),n}_{1,k}\big)^{2}\big(D^{(i),n}_{M,k}\kappa_{i}^{2}e^{\mu_{i}x_{M}}-D^{(i),n}_{1,k}e^{\mu_{i}x_{0}}\Big).

Next, assuming similar bounds on the discrete derivative of the initial and boundary values as in Proposition
3.3
, the first term on the RHS of (

D^{(i),n}_{j,k}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\leq\frac{\mu_{i}\Delta x}{2}D^{\rm min}_{i}.

The second term on the RHS of (

\displaystyle D^{(i),n}_{M,k}\kappa_{i}^{2}e^{\mu_{i}x_{M}}-D^{(i),n}_{1,k}e^{\mu_{i}x_{0}}\leq D^{\rm max}_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}e^{\mu_{i}x_{M}}-D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{\mu_{i}x_{0}}=D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{\mu_{i}x_{0}}\bigg(\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{D^{\rm max}_{i}}{D^{\rm min}_{i}}}\kappa_{i}\bigg)^{2}e^{\mu_{i}}-1\bigg)\overset{(\ref{1.18})}{\leq}0.

Combining the last two estimates and proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta x\Delta\xi}{\Delta t}\sum_{i=m+1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big(\big(u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big)-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\Big)e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}\rho(\xi_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta x\Delta\xi}{2\Delta t}\sum_{i=m+1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}\mu_{i}\Delta xe^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\nu\Delta x\Delta\xi\sum_{i=m+1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\mu_{i}x_{j}}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\rho(\xi_{k}),

where
\nu
is defined by

\nu=\frac{\Delta x}{2\Delta t}\min\limits_{i=m+1,\ldots,p}\Big\{\mu_{i}D^{\rm min}_{i}e^{-\mu_{i}\Delta x}\Big\}.

Hence, under suitable assumptions on the discrete gradients, there is an exponential decay without restriction on the sign of
\Lambda_{i}
.

Remark 4.1

In this paper, we do not provide a detailed discussion of the well-posedness of random hyperbolic systems. We note that for each fixed
realization of the random variable, the system reduces to a deterministic hyperbolic system of conservation laws with smooth flux functions
and well-posed initial–boundary value problems, for which the classical theory (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
) applies. Since the random
input enters only through the initial and boundary data, the standard well-posedness results can be invoked for each realization, and our
primary focus here is the numerical stabilization analysis based on discrete Lyapunov functions.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have extended the Lyapunov-based stabilization framework to random systems of hyperbolic conservation laws,
where uncertainties arise from boundary controls and initial data. By integrating a stochastic discrete Lyapunov function into the numerical
analysis, we rigorously established exponential stability for the finite-volume discretization of these systems. Theoretical decay rates
were derived, demonstrating their dependence on boundary control parameters, grid resolution, and the statistical properties of random
inputs. We experimentally checked the stability of one nonlinear system, and the obtained results demonstrate convergence as well even
though the rigorous proof is currently out of reach. We have applied the second-order central-upwind (CU) scheme to the linearized
Saint-Venant system of shallow-water equations and compared its performance with the performance of the first-order upwind scheme. The
conducted numerical experiments confirm that the CU scheme achieves much smaller values of
||e^{-\nu t}{\cal L}^{0}-{\cal L}||_{\infty}
(which
means that the obtained stability estimates are sharper) while maintaining stability. We have also experimentally checked the
stability in one numerical example of the random hyperbolic system of balance laws, which demonstrates that the applied boundary control
successfully stabilizes the system. Future research directions include extending this framework to more complex nonlinear (nonconservative)
systems and high-order numerical schemes.

Appendix A
Central-Upwind (CU) Scheme

In this appendix, we briefly describe the semi-discrete CU scheme, which was introduced in
[
26
,
25
]
(see also
[
6
,
7
,
27
]
for recent low-dissipation modifications of the CU schemes) as a “black-box” solver for general hyperbolic systems of conservation laws.

As CU schemes are finite-volume methods, the computed quantities are the cell averages,
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}\bm{u}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x
,
j=0,\ldots,M-1
. The semi-discrete CU scheme from
[
25
]
for the system (
2.1
) reads as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\bm{u}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{{{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}}_{j+1,k}(t)-{{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}}_{j,k}(t)}{\Delta x},

(A.1)

where the CU numerical fluxes are given by

{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}_{j,k}(t)=\frac{a^{+}_{j,k}(t)\bm{f}\big(\bm{u}^{-}_{j,k}(t)\big)-a^{-}_{j,k}(t)\bm{f}\big(\bm{u}^{+}_{j,k}(t)\big)}{a^{+}_{j,k}(t)-a^{-}_{j,k}(t)}+\frac{a^{+}_{j,k}(t)a^{-}_{j,k}(t)}{a^{+}_{j,k}(t)-a^{-}_{j,k}(t)}\big(\bm{u}^{+}_{j,k}(t)-\bm{u}^{-}_{j,k}(t)\big).

(A.2)

Here,
\bm{u}^{\pm}_{j,k}(t)
are the right/left-sided values of a global (in space) piecewise linear interpolant

\widetilde{\bm{u}}(x;t)=\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(x;t):=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)+\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(x-x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),\quad x\in(x_{j},x_{j+1}),

(A.3)

at the cell interface
x=x_{j}
, namely,

\bm{u}^{-}_{j,k}(t)=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k},\quad\bm{u}^{+}_{j,k}(t)=\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)-\frac{\Delta x}{2}\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}.

In order to ensure a non-oscillatory nature of this reconstruction, one needs to compute the slopes
\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}
in (
A.3
) using a nonlinear limiter. In the numerical experiment reported in §
4
, we have used a minmod limiter

[
33
,
35
]
:

\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}={\rm minmod}\bigg(\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j-\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)}{\Delta x},\,\,\frac{\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{j+\frac{3}{2},k}(t)-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t)}{\Delta x}\bigg),\quad j=1,\ldots,M-2.

(A.4)

Here, the minmod function is defined as

{\rm minmod}(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots):=\begin{cases}\min_{j}\{z_{i}\}&\mbox{if}~z_{i}>0\quad\forall\,i,\\
\max_{j}\{z_{i}\}&\mbox{if}~z_{i}<0\quad\forall\,i,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

and applied in a component-wise manner.

We note that in order to apply the minmod limiter (
A.4
) at the boundary cells, one needs to use the ghost values
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{-\frac{1}{2},k}(t)
and
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{M+\frac{1}{2},k}(t)
, which may be unavailable for certain boundary conditions including those considered in this paper. Therefore, at the boundary cells, one can use a different minmod limiting:

\displaystyle\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{\frac{1}{2},k}={\rm minmod}\Bigg(\frac{\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(t)-\bm{u}^{-}_{0,k}(t)}{\Delta x/2},\,\,\frac{\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{\frac{3}{2},k}(t)-\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{\frac{1}{2},k}(t)}{\Delta x}\Bigg),

(A.5)

\displaystyle\big(\bm{u}_{x}(t)\big)_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}={\rm minmod}\Bigg(\frac{\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t)-\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{M-\frac{3}{2},k}(t)}{\Delta x},\,\,\frac{\bm{u}^{+}_{M,k}(t)-\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t)}{\Delta x/2}\Bigg),

where
\bm{u}^{-}_{0,k}(t)
and
\bm{u}^{+}_{M,k}(t)
are the one-sided boundary point values, which can be computed using the prescribed boundary conditions.

The one-sided local speeds of propagation
a^{\pm}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}(t)
are estimated using the largest and the smallest eigenvalues of the Jacobian
F(u):\Lambda_{p}<\ldots<\Lambda_{1}
. This can be done, for example, by taking

a^{+}_{j,k}(t)=\max\big\{\Lambda_{1}\big(\bm{u}^{+}_{j,k}(t)\big),\Lambda_{1}\big(\bm{u}^{-}_{j,k}(t)\big),0\big\},\quad a^{-}_{j,k}(t)=\min\big\{\Lambda_{p}\big(\bm{u}^{+}_{j,k}(t)\big),\Lambda_{p}\big(\bm{u}^{-}_{j,k}(t)\big),0\big\}.

Finally, the ODE system (
A.1
) has to be numerically integrated using a stable and sufficiently accurate ODE solver. In the numerical experiment reported in §
4
, we have used the three-stage third-order strong stability preserving Runge-Kutta (SSP-RK3) method (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
20
]
) and use the CFL number 0.45.

Appendix B
Boundary Conditions for the CU Scheme

In this appendix, we consider the linearized Saint-Venant system of shallow-water equations (
5.1
)–(
5.2
) to show how to compute the boundary values
\bm{u}^{\pm}_{M,k}
and
\bm{u}^{\pm}_{0,k}
needed to evaluate the CU numerical fluxes (
A.2
) at the boundaries.

We assume that the solution (its cell averages and all of the cell interface values, including the boundary ones) is available at all times until a certain time level
t
. We also assume that at the time level
t+\tau
(for the SSP-RK3 ODE solver
\tau
may be either
\frac{\Delta t}{2}
or
\Delta t
) the cell averages
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\bm{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{{j+\frac{1}{2}},k}(t+\tau)
have been already computed and our goal now is to obtain
u^{(1),\,\pm}_{M,k}(t+\tau)
and
u^{(2),\,\pm}_{0,k}(t+\tau)
since
u^{(1),\,\pm}_{0,k}(t+\tau)
and
u^{(2),\,\pm}_{M,k}(t+\tau)
can then be directly computed from the prescribed boundary conditions. Here, we show how to compute
u^{(1),\,\pm}_{M,k}(t+\tau)
since
u^{(2),\,\pm}_{0,k}(t+\tau)
can be obtained in a similar manner.

We begin with
u^{(1),\,+}_{M,k}(t+\tau)
, which can be calculated using the method of characteristics, namely,

u^{(1),\,+}_{M,k}(t+\tau)=\widetilde{u}^{\,(1)}(x_{M}-\tau\Lambda_{1},t).

(B.1)

Due to the finite speed of propagation and the CFL restriction on
\tau
, the RHS of (
B.1
) can be easily computed, which results in

u^{(1),\,+}_{M,k}(t+\tau)=\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$u$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,(1)}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t)+\big(u^{\,(1)}_{x}(t)\big)_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}\bigg(\frac{\Delta x}{2}-\tau\Lambda_{1}\bigg).

To obtain the left-sided value
u^{(1),\,-}_{M,k}(t+\tau)
, we reconstruct the linear piece in the cell
(x_{M-1},x_{M})
:

\widetilde{u}^{\,(1),\,-}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t+\tau)=\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$u$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,(1)}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t+\tau)+(u^{\,(1)}_{x}(t+\tau))_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(x-x_{M-\frac{1}{2}}),

(B.2)

where the slope is computed as in (
A.5
), namely, by

\big(u^{\,(1)}_{x}(t+\tau)\big)_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}={\rm minmod}\Bigg(\frac{\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.54996pt\hbox{\kern-0.46252pt$u$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,(1)}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t+\tau)-\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.54996pt\hbox{\kern-0.46252pt$u$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,(1)}_{M-\frac{3}{2},k}(t+\tau)}{\Delta x},\,\,\frac{u^{(1),+}_{M,k}(t+\tau)-\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.54996pt\hbox{\kern-0.46252pt$u$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,(1)}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t+\tau)}{\Delta x/2}\Bigg).

(B.3)

Finally, substituting
x=x_{M}
into (
B.2
)–(
B.3
) results in

u^{(1),\,-}_{M,k}(t+\tau)=\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$u$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{\,(1)}_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}(t+\tau)+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\big(u^{\,(1)}_{x}(t+\tau)\big)_{M-\frac{1}{2},k}.

Appendix C
Extension of Theorem
4.2
to Random Hyperbolic Systems of Balance Laws

In this appendix, we study the
L^{2}
-stabilization of 1-D random hyperbolic systems of balance laws. We consider a simple case, which is
given by

\bm{u}_{t}+F(\bm{u})\bm{u}_{x}=-B\bm{u},

(C.1)

where the matrix
B
is diagonally positive definite, that is,

B={\rm diag}(b^{(1)},\dots,b^{(m)}),\qquad b^{(i)}>0.

(C.2)

Making the same assumptions as in Theorem
4.2
, the corresponding upwind scheme for the system (
C.1
)–(
C.2
) reads as

\displaystyle u_{j,k}^{(i),n+1}

\displaystyle=u_{j,k}^{(i),n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\,\Lambda_{i}\!\left(\bm{u}_{j,k}^{n}\right)\Big(u_{j,k}^{(i),n}-u_{j-1,k}^{(i),n}\Big)-\Delta t\,b^{(i)}u_{j,k}^{(i),n},

(C.3)

\displaystyle u_{0,k}^{(i),n+1}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\,u_{M,k}^{(i),n+1},\qquad i=1,\dots,m,

and

\displaystyle u_{j,k}^{(i),n+1}

\displaystyle=u_{j,k}^{(i),n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\,\Lambda_{i}\!\left(\bm{u}_{j+1,k}^{n}\right)\Big(u_{j+1,k}^{(i),n}-u_{j,k}^{(i),n}\Big)-\Delta t\,b^{(i)}u_{j,k}^{(i),n},

(C.4)

\displaystyle u_{M+1,k}^{(i),n+1}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\,u_{1,k}^{(i),n+1},\qquad i=m+1,\dots,p.

The scheme (
C.3
)–(
C.4
) can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\Big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}-\Delta t\,b^{(i)}\Big)+u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}D^{(i),n}_{j,k},

(C.5)

\displaystyle u^{(i),n+1}_{0,k}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\,u^{(i),n+1}_{M,k},\qquad i=1,\dots,m,

and

\displaystyle u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\Big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}-\Delta t\,b^{(i)}\Big)+u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\,D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k},

(C.6)

\displaystyle u^{(i),n+1}_{M+1,k}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{i}\,u^{(i),n+1}_{1,k},\qquad i=m+1,\dots,p.

Applying Jensen’s inequality to (
C.5
)–(
C.6
) leads to

\displaystyle\big(u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\Delta t\,b^{(i)})\Big[\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}-\Delta t\,b^{(i)}\big)\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}+D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}+D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}\big)^{2},\qquad i=1,\dots,m,

and

\displaystyle\big(u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\Delta t\,b^{(i)})\Big[\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}-\Delta t\,b^{(i)}\big)\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}+D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\big(1-D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}+D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big(u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)^{2},\qquad i=m+1,\dots,p.

One can then obtain

\displaystyle\big(u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big)^{2}-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\leq D^{(i),n}_{j,k}\Big(\big(u^{(i),n}_{j-1,k}\big)^{2}-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\Big),\qquad i=1,\dots,m,

(C.7)

\displaystyle\big(u^{(i),n+1}_{j,k}\big)^{2}-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\leq D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\Big(\big(u^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}\big)^{2}-\big(u^{(i),n}_{j,k}\big)^{2}\Big),\qquad i=m+1,\dots,p.

The estimates (
C.7
) hold provided

D^{(i),n}_{j,k}+\Delta t\,b^{(i)}\leq 1,\qquad D^{(i),n}_{j+1,k}+\Delta t\,b^{(i)}\leq 1,

which can be ensured by choosing a sufficiently small time-step
\Delta t
in the upwind scheme. Continuing the proof of Theorem
4.2
after
formula (
4.10
), one arrives at the same estimated decay rate as in Theorem
4.2
.
