Red Teaming Large Language Models for Healthcare
Vahid Balazadeh, Michael Cooper, David Pellow, Atousa Assadi, Jennifer Bell, Mark Coastworth, Kaivalya Deshpande, Jim Fackler, Gabriel Funingana, Spencer Gable-Cook, Anirudh Gangadhar, Abhishek Jaiswal, Sumanth Kaja, Christopher Khoury, Amrit Krishnan, Randy Lin, Kaden McKeen, Sara Naimimohasses, Khashayar Namdar, Aviraj Newatia, Allan Pang, Anshul Pattoo, Sameer Peesapati, Diana Prepelita, Bogdana Rakova, Saba Sadatamin, Rafael Schulman, Ajay Shah, Syed Azhar Shah, Syed Ahmar Shah, Babak Taati, Balagopal Unnikrishnan, Iñigo Urteaga, Stephanie Williams, Rahul G Krishnan

Abstract
We present the design process and findings of the pre-conference workshop at the Machine Learning for Healthcare Conference (2024) entitled Red Teaming Large Language Models for Healthcare , which took place on August 15, 2024. Conference participants, comprising a mix of computational and clinical expertise, attempted to discover vulnerabilities – realistic clinical prompts for which a large language model (LLM) outputs a response that could cause clinical harm. Red-teaming with clinicians enables the identification of LLM vulnerabilities that may not be recognised by LLM developers lacking clinical expertise. We report the vulnerabilities found, categorise them, and present the results of a replication study assessing the vulnerabilities across all LLMs provided.

Introduction

The Red Teaming Large Language Models for Healthcare pre-conference workshop at the 2024 Machine Learning for Healthcare (MLHC) conference aimed to explore the limitations of current large language models (LLMs) in realistic healthcare scenarios. The goal of the workshop was to use the expertise of the physicians and clinicians at the conference, working together with participants from computational, machine learning (ML), engineering and other backgrounds, to identify vulnerabilities exhibited in LLMs’ responses to clinical queries. A secondary goal was to expose the physicians to both the capabilities and vulnerabilities of these tools in clinical contexts. In this paper, we present the results of the workshop, and the set of prompts identified as potentially harmful or wrong in clinical settings. We categorise the vulnerabilities elicited by these prompts, and present our categorisation of potentially harmful behaviours the models may exhibit when used for clinical purposes.

The 46 workshop participants were divided into 12 groups of 3-5 with at least one clinical expert in each group. Eighteen participants identified themselves as having a clinical background.
Participants were instructed to think of realistic clinical use cases for which they would want to query an LLM and use their expertise to evaluate the answers. Groups had approximately two hours and could choose to test queries using Llama-30B, Mistral-7B, GPT-4o, and Gemini Flash 1.5.

Unlike other red teaming activities, in which the red team acts adversarially to discover flaws that could be exploited by malign actors, we assume that clinical users will not attempt to elicit harmful responses. Rather, in our activities, we asked participants to assume positive intent to assist in patient care while using the LLMs in realistic clinical scenarios and determine whether the LLM responses were potentially harmful. We identified a range of harmful responses, from incorrect interpretation of images to hallucination and providing over-optimistic prognoses or risk assessments. One significant challenge in evaluating the performance of LLMs on medical tasks is the frequent fine-tuning and rapid evolution of these models, which may limit the long-term applicability, reproducibility, and relevance of any single study. We assessed changes in the vulnerabilities found in our workshop by attempting to replicate all prompts and assessing if the vulnerabilities were still observed across all models 2-4 months after the workshop.

Related Work

Red-teaming has been used extensively to discover unsafe or undesirable behaviour of LLMs, one extensive survey covering these uses is
Lin et al. 2025
.
Feffer et al. 2024
critiqued the effectiveness of some uses of red-teaming, and in our workshop we followed the guidelines and checklist recommended in that work.
Bullwinkel et al. 2025
reported lessons and suggestions for red-teaming based on red-teaming 100 generative AI based products at Microsoft, our work reinforces a number of the lessons shared in their report.
There is a growing body of work that evaluates the performance and safety of LLM behaviour specifically in medical and healthcare contexts, including several red team exercises that have been reported. Survey articles can provide a more extensive overview of the use
Li et al. 2024
and evaluation
Chen et al. 2025
of LLMs in healthcare applications.

In an assessment of the use of LLMs in high-risk domains,
Hung et al. 2023
note that in the medical field, “existing evaluation metrics … often fall short when it comes to detecting potentially harmful medical advice". Indeed, recent evaluations of the performance and use of LLMs in real medical scenarios primarily rely on the judgments of human evaluators.
Dash et al. 2023
evaluated the ability of ChatGPT3.5 and 4 to answer 66 real clinical informatics requests, with evaluators assessing that none of the responses posed harm, but that the majority of the responses were discordant with that of the informatics consult. Using 2400 real cases of common pathologies from MIMIC IV,
Hager et al. 2024
demonstrated the poor performance of open-source LLMs on real-world diagnostics, treatment plans, and referrals for testing.
Williams et al. 2024
used 10,000 real ED visits from UCSF to evaluate the ability of GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4-Turbo to recommend admission, radiological scans, and antibiotic treatment, demonstrating poor and overly conservative performance compared to physicians.
The work that is most closely related to ours is
Chang et al. 2025
, which presents results of a medical red-teaming workshop evaluating GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Teams of clinical and technical participants assessed potential harms to safety or privacy and hallucinations or bias present in responses to medical queries. That exercise identified 143 prompts that produced an inappropriate response from at least one of the models.

Specific vulnerabilities may not be reproducible or may change over time; for example,
Chang et al. 2025
observed 45 of the vulnerabilities in one of the GPT-4 models were not present in the older GPT-3.5 model. Thus it is important for clinicians to understand the types of vulnerabilities and failure modes exhibited by LLMs in the healthcare context. Previous works have categorised vulnerabilities in a large variety of ways.
Huang et al. 2025
classify hallucinations into factual inconsistencies, factual fabrications, instruction inconsistency, context inconsistency, and logical inconsistency, and classify their causes as imitative falsehoods, duplication bias, social bias, domain knowledge deficiency, outdated factual knowledge, co-occurrence bias, long tail knowledge, and complex reasoning failures. In the domain of medical summarisation,
Asgari et al. 2024
categorise hallucinations into fabrications, negation of relevant facts, unsupported speculation of causality, and non-contextually relevant information. In their investigation of types of errors output by GPT-4 on USMLE questions,
Roy et al. 2024
classify errors as sticking with the wrong diagnosis, incorrect or vague conclusions, ignoring missing information, non-medical factual errors, unsupported medical claims, incorrect understanding of the task, and hallucination of information. When categorising biases that cause
human
diagnostic error by physicians,
Wellbery 2011
cites anchoring, availability, confirmation, and framing biases, and premature closure. In this work, we draw from these sources to define categories for the vulnerabilities found in the red-teaming exercise.

3
Methods

3.1
Design Considerations

Native Interaction.
Where possible, we sought participants to be able to interact with the language models via the user interface created by the organisation that developed the model. Participants were provided access to a ChatGPT interface to interact with GPT-4o, and a Gemini interface to interact with Gemini Flash 1.5. These two interfaces enabled participants to provide both text and images as input to the model. Participants were provided access to a custom portal designed by collaborators at the Vector Institute to interact with Llama 3 70B and Mistral 7B. As these two models do not support image data as input, the Vector platform did not allow participants to upload images to those models.

Vulnerability, Threat Model, and Team Composition.
We employed the question bank designed by
Feffer et al. 2024
to understand the threat model and to scope workshop goals. The complete table of our answers to the questions in Table 1 of
Feffer et al. 2024
is provided in the supplementary materials (Appendix
A
). To highlight key elements of our responses to the guiding questions, the principle artifact of evaluation at this workshop was a collection of pre-trained language models. To our knowledge, these models have not been specially fine-tuned to handle clinical data (unlike, for example, Clinical Camel
(
Toma et al. 2023
)
), although it is likely that in the course of their training they have been exposed to a substantial volume of tokens generated from clinical processes (e.g., clinical notes, patient care guidelines, medical textbooks, etc.). Rather than testing for a specific vulnerability, this exercise aims to test for a broad swathe of potential vulnerabilities, to be identified by the participants over the course of the workshop. Crucially, unlike standard language model red teaming exercises, which may operate under the assumption of a malicious user, the vulnerabilities we seek to identify should correspond to well-intentioned users – as we assume that the healthcare practitioners that would use these models are well-intentioned and seek to do right by their patients. Each team of participants ranged from three to five members, and was comprised of both participants with a clinical background (e.g., clinicians) and participants with a technical background (e.g., computer scientists). Clinical expertise at the workshop ranged a span of subspecialties, including oncology, hepatology, emergency medicine, and pediatrics, among others.

3.2
Identification of Vulnerabilities

Orientation.
Because no prior experience with language models was assumed, participants were guided through a simple warmup exercise to familiarise themselves with the operation and behaviour of language model-based chatbots. Throughout the warmup, participants were guided through exercises that, despite the abilities of these models to generate complex, stylistically consistent text, they are not omniscient and are limited by the data on which they were trained. Participants were further instructed to ask the model a public health question (the top 10 causes of death in the United States), and to upload an image to acquaint themselves with the image interpretation features of the interfaces. A complete list of the instructions and questions used in the orientation exercise is provided
on the workshop website
.

Activity.
After the warmup, participants were instructed to ask one of the clinicians in their group to describe their clinical workflow, and to brainstorm ways in which one of these language models may integrate into relevant tasks. This instruction aimed to ensure that participants focused their prompts primarily on realistic clinical scenarios grounded in the lived experiences of the clinicians in their groups. Then, participants were instructed to brainstorm a list of reasonable prompts to service the identified use case(s), and to report any responses from the language model which may present “harm” (defined broadly) in the relevant clinical setting. After identifying a vulnerability, participants were asked to perform a degree of simple, local exploration to investigate whether small modifications to the provided prompt could mitigate or eliminate the identified vulnerability.

3.3
Post-Activity Analysis of Vulnerabilities

After the conclusion of the activity, the organising team manually reviewed and categorised each of the submitted vulnerabilities into one of the following categories. The categories were not determined
a priori
; rather, we permitted the empirical list of submitted findings to guide the determination of the relevant categories of vulnerability.

1.

Hallucination
: the model’s response contains details or references that are not grounded in the basis of fact.

2.

Image Interpretation Failure
: the model does not correctly interpret (or generate) images.

3.

Incorrect Medical Knowledge
: the model’s response contains a factual claim regarding medical knowledge that is incorrect.

(a)

Incorrect Long-Tail Medical Knowledge
: the case in question is either a rare disease or a complex case that requires deep expert-level knowledge to correctly identify the correct response.

4.

Omitted Medical Knowledge
: the model’s response ignores relevant medical information provided in the prompt, or does not contain crucial, relevant elements of medical knowledge.

5.

Anchoring
: the model’s response fixates on potentially irrelevant or misleading information provided in the prompt.

6.

Sycophancy
: the model’s response provides a response aligned with the user’s implied desires, at the expense of providing a correct or useful response.

7.

Prioritisation Error
: the model’s response poorly prioritises the urgency of, for example, diagnosis, testing, or treatments, when asked a question of prioritisation.

8.

Vaguery
: the model’s response is vague or generic, to the point where it is not appropriate for the (specific) context.

9.

Training Bias
: the model’s response is biased by the (overwhelmingly non-medical) training data. For example, the model may draw an incorrect analogy using reasoning from beyond the medical field that is not applicable to the setting in question.

Discussion

In this work, we documented vulnerabilities found by workshop participants across all tested language models spanning various prompt types and clinical subject areas. We identified 32 unique prompts resulting in medical vulnerabilities, with hallucination, image interpretation failure, anchoring bias, and incorrect medical knowledge being among the most common vulnerability categories. The workshop provided clinicians with hands-on experience of potential errors they may encounter when incorporating LLMs into clinical practice. We categorised the vulnerabilities found to provide further guidance for clinical LLM users as to these pitfalls.

There are a number of limitations to this work. First, there is some selection bias in the vulnerabilities as participants chose to test ChatGPT more than the other models. Second, our data collection process emphasized reporting vulnerabilities rather than documenting cases where LLMs provided adequate responses, preventing assessment of the prevalence of harmful responses.
Third, despite participant diversity, many clinical specialties were not represented, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of our findings. Finally, as LLM adoption in healthcare is ongoing, the scenarios envisaged by participants may not reflect the ultimate use cases where these technologies see greatest adoption.

Despite the selection bias, our replication study shows that many of the vulnerabilities originally found with GPT-4o also exist for the other models, while far fewer of the vulnerabilities found in the other models exist for GPT-4o. On the other hand, many vulnerabilities also did not replicate, in some cases even on the model in which they were originally reported. This highlights that understanding the vulnerabilities of LLMs is a moving target that cannot easily be captured by a static set of benchmarking prompts. By providing a set of categories into which vulnerabilities can be classified, we allow for a broader understanding of the vulnerabilities of LLMs in healthcare settings, even as specific instances of vulnerable prompts in those categories may disappear over time.

Our findings highlight several critical implications for the development and deployment of LLMs in healthcare settings. The pervasiveness of vulnerabilities such as anchoring bias and sycophancy make LLM responses inherently unstable. Differently phrased prompts with the same intentions can result in very different responses or vulnerabilities, while clinical applications require LLMs to be robust to these re-phrasing.
Our study underscores the need for dynamic, continuous evaluation frameworks rather than relying on static benchmarks. The variability in replication across models and over time suggests that vulnerability assessment should be an ongoing process rather than a one-time certification.
For clinical practice, practitioners should develop awareness of these vulnerability categories to better anticipate and identify potential errors when using LLMs.

Some safeguards against identified vulnerabilities have already emerged. Participants noted that hallucinated references or information in summaries could be mitigated by explicitly instructing the LLM not to fabricate information. We also observed built-in safeguards in some models, such as Gemini’s refusal to interpret medical images—a strategy that could prevent image interpretation vulnerabilities but may limit utility in specialized clinical applications.

Is the activity meant to illustrate a handful of possible vulnerabilities?

Specific vulnerability

How was this vulnerability identified as the target of this evaluation?

Target vulnerabilities will be identified in the first stage of the workshop wherein clinician team members will share their clinical workflows and decision-making tasks with their teams, and will brainstorm with their teams use cases for LLMs within these workflows.

Why was the above vulnerability prioritised over other potential vulnerabilities?

We prioritise vulnerabilities associated with the workflows specific to the participants in our workshop as those individuals will be best positioned to evaluate the harm associated with the responses provided by the LLMs.

What is the threshold of acceptable risk for finding this vulnerability?

This is dependent on the specific clinical workflow being studied.

Criteria for success

What are the benchmarks of comparison for success?

The success of an individual vulnerability will be gauged by the extent to which (a) it surfaces in a realistic clinical use case (as adjudicated by clinician workshop participants), and (b) may plausibly lead to downstream clinical harm. The success of the workshop will be gauged based on the number and severity of vulnerabilities that are uncovered.

Can the activity be reconstructed or reproduced?

Workshop materials will be publicly released so that our workshop can be reproduced; however, due to the time-varying nature of some of the closed-source language models that we evaluate (GPT-4o), it is uncertain whether future replications of our workshop will identify similar vulnerabilities.

Team composition

What were the criteria for inclusion/exclusion of members, and why?

Workshop members were selected on the basis of interest and domain expertise; participants in the 2024 Machine Learning for Healthcare (MLHC) Conference were invited to sign up for a pre-conference workshop that would include our red-teaming activity.

How diverse/homogeneous is the team across relevant demographic characteristics?

Each team will be comprised of both healthcare practitioners and computer scientists. Subject matter expertise is the most relevant demographic characteristic for this workshop.

How many internal versus external members belong to the team?

Each team is comprised only of external members, in the sense that (to our knowledge) none of our participants are affiliated with the groups developing the models being red-teamed.

What is the distribution of subject-matter expertise among members?

A range of clinicians from different clinical sub-specialties will be present at our workshop. Similarly, a range of computer scientists with different machine learning specializations will take part.

What are possible biases or blindspots the current team composition may exhibit?

It is possible that our participants do not comprehensively represent the distribution of clinical sub-specialties in practice. To appropriately acknowledge limitations of our exercise, we will gather information from workshop registrants on their respective clinical specialties.

What incentives/disincentives do participants have to contribute to the activity?

Workshop participants are those who have proactively elected to sign up for the activity. We hypothesise that our participants are largely driven by a desire to improve clinical processes and out of a sense of interest in the subject domain.
