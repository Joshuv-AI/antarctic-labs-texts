Granulomatosis with polyangiitis involving both ischemic and hemorrhagic cerebrovascular disease: A case report and literature review
Authors: Jiuyang Xu, Sizhao Li, Yongpeng Ge, Xiaoyang Cui, Guochun Wang, Fang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGranulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) is a necrotizing small-vessel vasculitis associated with antineutrophilic cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs). GPA can have multisystem involvement; however, central nervous system (CNS) manifestations are uncommon. Here, for this first time, we report a rare case of GPA with both ischemic and hemorrhagic CNS involvement.Care presentationA 67-year-old previously healthy male presented with intermittent fever, malaise, hemoptysis, skin lesions, and hearing loss. Chest computed tomography revealed multiple nodular lesions, which later enlarged and merged into consolidations. His serum ANCA titer was 1:80 with high PR3-ANCA activity. Methylprednisolone was initiated at a dose of 40 mg/day; however, only a partial response was observed. He complained dizziness and awkwardness in moving his left extremities during hospitalization, and subsequent imaging tests revealed ischemic attacks and intracranial hemorrhage in different locations. Steroid pulse therapy and rituximab were administered to induce remission. The neurological and pulmonary lesions resolved gradually. There were no signs of recurrence at 12 months post treatment.ConclusionOur case highlights the involvement of CNS manifestations in GPA, and suggests potential role of rituximab in treatment of GPA patients with CNS involvement.Keywords: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Antineutrophilic cytoplasmic antibodies, Central nervous system, Cerebrovascular disease, Case report

Introduction

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) is a vasculitis associated with antineutrophilic cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs), especially cytoplasmic proteinase 3 (PR3) [1]. It is characterized by necrotizing vasculitis in small vessels and granuloma formation, and usually involves the upper and lower respiratory tracts, kidneys, skin, and the ears, nose, and throat (ENT). Patients may present with non-specific symptoms, such as fever, malaise, and weight loss, before progressing to organ-specific symptoms. Therefore, misdiagnosis with infections or malignancies is frequent.

Although peripheral neuropathy is a common manifestation in patients with GPA, central nervous system (CNS) involvement is uncommon [[2], [3], [4]]. Approximately 3–5% of patients present with cerebral vasculitis, which is usually associated with intracranial hemorrhage or ischemic infarctions [3], which typically present as multiple lesions affecting the distal penetrating vessels; they do not usually respond to antiplatelet therapy and may recur in the absence of immunosuppressive treatments. Fewer common hemorrhagic events affect the brain parenchyma. Other CNS manifestations involve the pituitary gland, spinal cord, and cranial neuropathy [2]. Furthermore, CNS involvement can appear either at disease onset or later in the disease stage, accompanied by recurrence [5].

We report a case of GPA with both hemorrhagic and ischemic cerebral vascular manifestations complicated by multidrug-resistant bacterial pulmonary infections. The patient only partially responded to initial steroid treatment (1 mg/kg/day). Steroid pulse therapy and rituximab were administered following an adequate antibacterial regimen. The patient was followed-up for 12 months without disease recurrence.

Case report

A 67-year-old Han Chinese male visited our center with fatigue, fever and hemoptysis. The patient was otherwise healthy except for gradual hearing loss during the past year.

The patient complained of fatigue and productive cough with white sputum 3 months before admission. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed multiple pulmonary nodules (Fig. 1). Arterial blood gas test was unremarkable. Pulmonary infection was suspected and the patient was empirically administered moxifloxacin, although microbiological tests of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were negative. There was no improvement in his cough. He then developed fever (maximum temperature 39 °C) with hemoptysis and nose bleeds. A complete blood count revealed a normal white blood cell count; however, the C-reactive protein (CRP) level was notably elevated (82.18 mg/L, normal <10 mg/L). Paranasal sinusitis was revealed on CT (Fig. 2H). Empirical antibiotic therapy was changed to ceftazidime and voriconazole but fever and hemoptysis did not alleviate. It was later changed to levofloxacin and linezolid (Fig. 1). The only effective antipyretic therapy was dexamethasone, which was administered intravenously at 5 mg three times at the local clinic before admission to our center. The microbiological tests were all negative, and the CRP level further increased to 231 mg/L, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 91 mm/h and a ferritin level of 1346 ng/ml. The patient was tested for serum autoantibodies and cANCA was positive at a 1:32 dilution. He was suspected of having ANCA-associated vasculitis, and steroid therapy was initiated with methylprednisolone (40 mg/day). Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered at 20 g/day for 5 days. The patient was soon afebrile. However, his body temperature increased again 2 days later, which was alleviated by additional oral prednisone 20 mg/day. The hemoptysis did not resolve, and repeat chest CT revealed further enlargement of the pulmonary lesions.

The patient was then transferred to our hospital and was admitted to the rheumatology ward. Physical examination revealed a skin rupture on the right lower leg with sinus tract formation (Fig. 2L). Neurological examination results were unremarkable. Laboratory tests (Table 1) revealed elevated levels of inflammatory markers, including CRP, ESR, ferritin, and D-dimer. Anemia and hematuria with an abnormal red blood cell appearance were also noted. Serum autoantibodies, cANCA, and PR3-ANCA all tested positive. His estimated glomerular filtration rate was within the normal range. Bronchoscopy revealed fresh bloody excretions in multiple lobes, and the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid culture was positive for drug-resistant Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Enterobacter cloacae. Transbronchial lung biopsy revealed infiltration of inflammatory cells, micro-abscess formation, and destruction of small vessel walls with neutrophil infiltration in all layers.

The patient was diagnosed with GPA, according to the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations against Rheumatism classification criteria [6], complicated by a mixed drug-resistant bacterial pulmonary infection. He was given nasal cannula oxygenation (2L/min) and administered intravenous methylprednisolone (80 mg/day), piperacillin tazobactam, levofloxacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole to treat the pulmonary infection based on the culture results. Steroid pulse therapy was not administered at that time, considering the uncontrolled bacterial infection. His body temperature was normal, but hemoptysis persisted. Chest CT revealed bilaterally diffused lesions (Fig. 2A–D).

The patient complained of dizziness 2 weeks after admission. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed a fresh cerebral infarction in the left corona radiata, and magnetic resonance angiography suggested narrowing of the left middle cerebral artery (Fig. 2E–G). Oral statins were administered following consultation with a neurologist. Aspirin was contraindicated because of hemoptysis and the high risk of bleeding. His dizziness did not resolve, and he complained of newly-onset difficulty moving his left leg 1 week later. A neurological physical examination revealed weakened muscle strength in the left arm and leg, decreased pain in the left leg, and a positive Babinski sign in the left leg. Brain CT and subsequent brain susceptibility weighted imaging revealed a cerebral hemorrhage in the right internal capsule (Fig. 2I–K).

After a multi-disciplinary treatment (MDT) consultation with neurologists and respirologists, steroid pulse therapy was initiated with methylprednisolone 500 mg/day for 3 days, followed by 80 mg/day intravenous methylprednisolone. Rituximab 600 mg on day 1 and 1000 mg on day 15 were administered for induction–remission. His muscle strength gradually improved, and no new cerebral hemorrhage was observed on repeat brain CT scans (Fig. 2J). The patient was discharged 2 months after admission, and steroid therapy was changed to tapering oral methylprednisolone.

The patient was followed up for 12 months. His fatigue and hemoptysis completely resolved, and left arm and leg movements recovered. Chest CT revealed complete absorption of the pulmonary lesions, and brain CT showed no new hemorrhage or infarction lesions. His sinusitis also partially resolved. The skin rupture and subcutaneous sinus tract in right leg healed after removing the dead tissue and subsequent vacuum-sealing drainage. There was no sign of GPA remission at 12 months. He did not complain cough, dizziness or fatigue. A second rituximab regimen was given at 6 months as remission maintenance therapy.

Discussion

CNS involvement is an uncommon manifestation for ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) [7,8]. Typical clinical presentations include transient/sustained headaches, motor impairment related to ischemic attacks or intracranial hemorrhage, neuropsychic/mood disorders, hearing and visual loss, and diabetes insipidus. De Luna et al. distinguished CNS involvement in GPA according to clinical-radiological presentations into granulomatous (G-CNS) and vasculitic (V-CNS) phenotypes [7], suggesting a potentially different pathogenesis for each.

We present a rare case of GPA complicated by both ischemic and hemorrhagic cerebral vascular manifestations. No previous studies have reported patients with GPA complicated with ischemic and hemorrhagic simultaneously. The patient was previously healthy without risk factors for cardiovascular diseases. The diagnosis of GPA was made based on typical manifestations in the lungs, kidneys, skin, and ENT, as well as lung biopsy pathology and positive serum PR3-ANCA. His Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score was high (24). Induction therapy was methylprednisolone (40 mg/day) instead of pulse therapy because the patient had a complicated mixed drug-resistant bacterial pulmonary infection; however, the initial steroid therapy was only partially effective, and the steroid dose was increased to 80 mg/day to alleviate the fever and hemoptysis. The patient still developed neurological involvement in the presence of steroid therapy, further suggesting that vasculitis was active. Diagnosis of CNS vasculitis can be challenging because histo-pathological confirmation is needed [9]. For this patient, the diagnosis was made considering multiple pieces of clinical together with imaging evidence and absence of other potential causes. Firstly, the patient was previously healthy and the blood pressure and serum lipid levels were within the normal range at CNS symptom onset, suggesting that the risk of primary cerebral vascular disease was low. Secondly, the fact that these CNS manifestations resolved with subsequent strong immunosuppressive therapy indicated that they were associated with the primary disease. Further, the hemorrhage was believed to be independent of the ischemic event because it was in a different location and no antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy was used.

There are currently no recommendations for remission induction treatment of AAV with CNS involvement [2,10]. Generally, CNS involvement is recognized as an organ-threatening complication, and a combination of high-dose steroids and cyclophosphamide/rituximab has been used previously [4,8,[11], [12], [13], [14]]. There have been no direct comparisons between the effects of cyclophosphamide and rituximab. A few reports exist on the use of rituximab for G-CNS in AAV with CNS involvement [15,16], but evidence in V-CNS is insufficient. In this case, we administered a combination of steroid pulse therapy and rituximab, considering that the patient's serum IgG level and CD19+ B cell counts were elevated. The treatment was accompanied by 2-week broad-spectrum anti-bacterial therapy. No enlargement of the CNS lesions was observed, and the clinical manifestations gradually improved. There were no signs of recurrence at 12 months post-therapy, and the oral steroid dose was gradually tapered, suggesting that this therapy is effective for AAV patients with severe CNS complications. We administered a second course rituximab at 6 months for remission maintenance therapy, following the guideline recommendation for all GPA patients in the 2016 EULAR guideline.

Conclusion

Our case highlights the involvement of CNS manifestations in GPA, including both ischemic and hemorrhagic lesions. The case also suggests potential role of rituximab in treatment of GPA patients with CNS involvement. More clinical evidence is needed to further verify the experience gained from this case report.
