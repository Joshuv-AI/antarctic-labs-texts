Patient- and Caregiver-Reported Burden and Quality of Life in Generalised Myasthenia Gravis: A Multinational Real-World Survey in the Asia–Pacific Region
Authors: DaeYoung Yu, Mami Kasahara-Kiritani, Bolam Lee, I Ching Tsai, Shiva Lauretta Birija, Joe Conyers, Hannah Connolly, Gregor Gibson, Yoon-Ho Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding patient- and caregiver-reported burden of generalised myasthenia gravis (gMG) is essential to inform region-specific management and policy decisions. However, real-world data from the Asia–Pacific region remain limited.MethodsData were drawn from the Adelphi Real World gMG Disease Specific Programme™, a cross-sectional survey of neurologists and their patients with gMG conducted in Australia, China, Japan, Malaysia, South Korea, and Taiwan between July 2023 and January 2024. Neurologists reported patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and perceived patient quality of life (QoL). Patients, and patients’ caregivers provided answers to self-reported outcome measures, including the EQ-5D-5L, corresponding to status at the time of survey. Analyses were descriptive other than weighted kappa analyses used to measure neurologist-patient agreement on subjective patient QoL at the time of survey.ResultsOverall, 163 neurologists reported data for 434 patients, of whom 205 self-reported data alongside 151 caregivers. Most patients were female (58.5%), with a mean (SD) age of 51.4 (15.2) years, and received caregiver support (71.4%). Patients reported deficits across all EQ-5D-5L domains, including usual activities (79.4%), and moderate to extreme anxiety/depression (29.4%), with a mean (SD) index score of 0.72 (0.23). Most patients (54.6%) had a FACIT-Fatigue score < 30, indicating severe fatigue, and 45.0% of patients had reduced employment due to gMG. A kappa value of 0.2692 indicated a fair agreement between neurologist and patient perceived patient QoL. Mean (SD) patient absenteeism was 11.8% (20.8%), presenteeism 39.2% (22.5%), overall work productivity loss of 42.9% (23.9%), and activity impairment of 51.3% (23.9%). Caregivers reported a mean (SD) 27.6% (19.6%) overall work productivity loss and 47.0% (24.1%) activity impairment.ConclusionPatients and their caregivers reported substantial burden to QoL, including impaired work productivity, limitations in activities of daily living, fatigue, and anxiety/depression. Further research into health-policy initiatives is warranted to more effectively address these unmet needs in the Asia–Pacific.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00929-9.Keywords: Generalised myasthenia gravis, Quality of life, Productivity loss, Activities, Of daily living, Asia Pacific

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Generalised myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a rare autoimmune disorder that has well-reported negative impacts on both patient and caregiver quality of life.Region-specific data on patient- and caregiver-reported gMG burden is required to inform management and policy decisions in the Asia–Pacific. However, real-world data in the region is limited.What was learned from the study?A third of patients self-reported a subjectively poor quality of life. Patient EQ-5D-5L utility values indicated health-related quality of life impairment, and over half had a FACIT-Fatigue score < 30, indicating severe fatigue. On the basis of self-report data, activity and work impairment due to gMG were notably present for both patients and their caregivers.Both patients with gMG and their caregivers reported substantial quality of life burdens, as well as notable overall work productivity loss and activity impairment. Introduction

Generalised myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a rare, autoantibody-mediated, neuromuscular junction disorder, characterised by fatigable weakness of skeletal muscles [1, 2]. Global estimates of gMG prevalence have increased over the last five decades with recent estimates ranging from 12 to 20 cases per 100,000 population [2, 3]. Recently reported prevalences for Taiwan and Japan range from 11 to 24 per 100,000 population [4–6].

The symptoms of gMG and greater disease severity can limit activities of daily living, cause excessive fatigue, and significantly impair patients’ quality of life (QoL) [1, 2, 7]. A systematic literature review found a general association between those with greater symptom severity or longer hospitalisation days and worse depression and anxiety outcomes [8]. Psychiatric comorbidities not only affect patients’ health-related QoL (HRQoL) but also impact their caregivers. Disease severity and patient symptoms of depression have been associated with increased caregiver burden [9], with similar findings reported in a study conducted in China [10]. On the basis of an international online observational study, one-third of patients reported needing caregiver assistance [11].

Despite increased awareness and the availability of treatment options, recent studies continue to show that patients with gMG experience reduced HRQoL across physical, mental, and social domains [12, 13]. Additionally, a global literature review showed low employment rates [14], with greater work impairment observed among those with more severe symptoms [15].

Despite the existence of diverse healthcare systems, and differences of epidemiological characteristics and cultural environments across the Asia–Pacific region, regional empirical evidence is essential to strengthen the evidence base and facilitate meaningful cross-country comparisons. This regional approach highlights both common challenges and unique regional requirements. Studies from Australia, China, and Japan have shown that gMG disease severity, impact on activities of daily living, and employment status were associated with reduced patient QoL [16–18]. Negative illness perception has also been shown in a cohort of patients with MG in China [19]. However, the number of real-world studies remains limited. Many lack caregiver perspectives, and generalisability is often constrained by small sample sizes, and selection bias from single health centre samples.

Aims and Objectives

There is a paucity of real-world data regarding QoL impact and burden of gMG on patients and their caregivers in the Asia–Pacific region. To address these gaps, the present large multinational study aimed to describe the demographic characteristics of a cohort of patients with gMG in the Asia–Pacific region, as well as their burden of illness and self-reported outcomes, including impact on HRQoL, employment, and activities of daily living. Additionally, caregiver burden and self-reported outcomes were assessed.

Methods

Study Design

This study was an analysis of secondary data derived from the Adelphi Real World gMG Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™, a large cross-sectional survey, with elements of retrospective data collection, conducted in six regions in Asia–Pacific, namely Australia, China, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, and Taiwan, from July 2023 to January 2024. The DSP methodology has been previously described [20], validated [21], and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [22]. This approach is well established, with studies implemented globally across many different disease areas [23], including MG [24–26]. The DSP is a wholly owned Adelphi Real World product, which offers data access via subscription. Subscribers do not influence DSP design or data collection, and publication of survey results are not contingent on the subscriber’s approval or censorship.

A sample of neurologists from each country was recruited to participate in the DSP by local fieldwork agents, following completion of a short screening questionnaire. Neurologists were eligible to participate in this survey if they were personally responsible for treatment decisions and management of at least one patient with a confirmed diagnosis of gMG. Neurologists were instructed to complete a patient record form for up to four consecutively consulting patients who visited them for routine care, completed at the time of their most recent consultation to mitigate against selection bias. Neurologists received fair‑market reimbursement upon survey completion.

Patients were eligible for inclusion in the survey if they were 18 years or older, had a neurologist-confirmed diagnosis of gMG, were Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) class II–IV at time of survey, and were not enrolled in a clinical trial at time of survey. Caregivers were eligible provided they attended the consultation for a patient whom a neurologist reported data for, and were aged 18 years or older. Caregiver was defined as someone that provided support beyond what would be expected for a person of a particular age. Neither patients nor caregivers were compensated for participation.

Neurologist-Reported Data

Upon recruitment, neurologists completed a workload and attitudinal survey to investigate general workload, treatment practices, and attitudes and perceptions about gMG.

The patient record form contained detailed questions on demographic and clinical characteristics, including maintenance treatment, comorbidities, autoantibody status, myasthenic events, and hospitalisations. Also included were questions on perceived patient QoL, patients’ use of physical aids, home modifications, the requirement of caregiver(s), patient pain due to their gMG, and fatigue affecting patients’ activities of daily living. A gMG maintenance treatment was defined as a treatment prescribed for long-term use, rather than short-term rescue treatment.

For myasthenic events, classification relied on the neurologists’ clinical judgement. Myasthenic exacerbation was defined as weakening of some, or all, muscles throughout the body which did not require breathing assistance, whereas myasthenic crisis was defined as weakening and rapid fatigue of voluntarily controlled muscles which required intubation.

Disease severity measures were also reported by neurologists. MGFA is a disease severity classification scheme, where class I–V designates the least to most severe MG [27]. To reflect clinical status during the study period rather than patients’ historical worst, neurologists were asked to assess MGFA class based on the patient’s health at the time of survey. Additionally, two other MG specific severity scores were reported: the Myasthenia Gravis—Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) scale and the Myasthenia Gravis Composite scale (MGC). The MG-ADL is an eight-item survey where each item is rated 0 to 3, giving each individual an overall range from 0 (normal) to 24 (most severe) [28, 29]. The MGC is a ten-item survey where each item has a specific four-choice score, weighted based on factors such as QoL impact. This gives an overall range from 0 (normal) to 50 (most severe) [30, 31].

Patient-Reported Data

Eligible patients for whom the neurologist completed a record form were invited to voluntarily complete a patient-reported questionnaire. These contained detailed questions on disease burden, QoL, and other patient-reported outcome measures.

The EQ-5D-5L health utility system was used to assess patients HRQoL [32, 33]. An EQ-5D-5L value set for China was used to generate scores [34]. These scores range from 1 (perfect health) to below 0 (a health state equivalent to death). The EQ-Visual Analogue Score (VAS) score [35] was also used to assess patients generic HRQoL. This ranges from 0 to 100, with 0 being the worst imaginable health state and 100 being the best imaginable health state. Additionally, from the EQ-5D-5L domains, patients could respond via a five-option Likert scale (no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems, or extreme problems) on the areas of mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression.

The MG-QoL-15R is a 15-item questionnaire derived from the 60-item MG-QoL [36, 37]. Each item is scored from 0 to 2, where 0 = not at all, 1 = somewhat, and 2 = very much. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with a higher score indicating a greater negative impact on QoL.

The 13-item Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue Scale (FACIT-Fatigue) measurement system [38, 39] provided a score for HRQoL in relation to patient self-reported fatigue. The potential score ranges are 0–52, with higher scores indicating lesser fatigue and its subsequent impact upon daily activities. Scores of less than 30 were considered indicative of severe fatigue.

Impact on patients work and general activities was assessed using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment instrument (WPAI), with the proportion of time absent from work, time impaired at work, overall work impairment, and overall activity impairment scored out of 100% [40].

Caregiver-Reported Data

The caregivers of eligible patients for whom the neurologist completed a record form were invited to voluntarily complete a caregiver self-complete questionnaire. These contained detailed questions on self-reported outcome measures including the EQ-VAS and EQ-5D-5L, and a patient proxy report of the EQ-5D-5L.

Caregivers could also complete a short form version of the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-12), a 12-item questionnaire that can be used to assess the burden of caring in caregivers. Each question scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always) with total scores ranging between 0 and 48 [41]. Greater scores indicate greater burden, with a total score > 20 considered indicative of high caregiver burden.

Ethical Considerations

Using a checkbox, patients and caregivers provided informed consent to take part in the survey. Patient- and caregiver-reported questionnaires were completed independently from their neurologist and were returned in a sealed envelope, ensuring the responses were kept confidential from their neurologist.

No identifiable protected health information was extracted during the course of the survey. Data were collected in such a way that patients and neurologists could not be identified directly, with data aggregated before being shared with the subscriber and for publication.

Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines [42]. Exemption from ethics approval was sought and granted from PEARL Institutional Review Board (IRB; study number AG9525).

In addition, each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [43], the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [44], and the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and subsequent revisions.

Data privacy regulations in South Korea meant that only retrospective DSP data could be collected. Prospective patient or caregiver self-reporting was therefore not conducted. No patient-reported data for Australia was presented due to no response. As a result of a lack of participation, there was no caregiver-reported data for Australia or Taiwan.

Data Analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed on data from the patient record forms, patient-reported questionnaires, and caregiver self-complete forms.

Patients that had a purely ocular MG (MGFA class I) diagnosis at the time of survey were excluded from the analysis to focus the study on patients with gMG. MGFA class V was also excluded from the analysis due to a low base and to keep the focus on patients with gMG not actively experiencing a crisis.

Mean with standard deviation (SD) and/or median with range was calculated for continuous variables, where appropriate. Frequency counts and percentages were used for categorical variables. Unordered categorical data of subjective patient QoL were analysed using chi-squared tests, with a p value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Weighted kappa analyses (linear weights) were used to measure neurologist–patient agreement on subjective patient QoL at the time of survey, where matched neurologist- and patient-reported data were available. Kappa values were interpreted using the Cohen kappa statistic scale: ≤ 0 = no agreement, 0.01–0.20 = slight, 0.21–0.40 = fair, 0.41–0.60 = moderate, 0.61–0.80 = substantial and 0.81–1 = almost perfect agreement [45]. To analyse the significance of a fair agreement between neurologist and patient, Bowker’s test was used.

All analyses were conducted in UNICOM Intelligence Reporter version 7.5.1 (UNICOM Systems 2021). Missing data were not imputed; therefore, the number of patients included in each analysis varied depending on the variable and was reported separately for each analysis.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Data from 163 neurologists across six Asia–Pacific countries were collected for 434 patients with gMG, of whom 205 completed the patient questionnaire and 151 had caregiver reports. For the MGFA classes excluded from the analysis, 155 patients were in class I and one patient was in class V. Data disaggregated by whether a patient was reported male or female in their medical records are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Patients were predominantly female (58.5%, n = 254), with a mean (SD) age of 51.4 (15.2) years, and 80.3% (n = 347) were anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) positive. The mean (SD) time since gMG diagnosis was 4.9 (5.8) years and a median 3.1 (range 0.0–43.1) years. Of those with known comorbidity status at time of survey (n = 434), close to three-quarters (71.4%) had at least one comorbidity, most commonly hypertension, anxiety, or dyslipidaemia (Table 1).

The majority of patients (77.4%, n = 336) were classified as MGFA class II at the time of survey, with mean (SD) MG-ADL and MGC scores, 5.2 (4.0) and 8.9 (8.2) respectively, indicating mild-to-moderate functional limitation (Fig. 1). Overall (n = 434), most patients (97.9%) reported gMG symptoms at the time of survey, with the most frequently reported symptoms being ptosis (76.3%), diplopia (63.4%), and dysarthria (53.7%; Supplementary Table S1).

Over 90% of patients (93.8%, n = 407) were receiving maintenance treatment, yet 37.0% (n = 155) had experienced at least one exacerbation of symptoms and 22.2% (n = 93) had experienced a myasthenic crisis since diagnosis. Additionally, a quarter of patients (25.1%, n = 106) had received three or more maintenance treatment regimens (Table 2). The reason for patients’ most recent hospital admission was commonly due to gMG (64.5%, n = 91), with a mean stay of 11 nights, and 21.3% (n = 30) requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission (Supplementary Table S2).

Neurologist-Reported Physical Aids, Caregiver Assistance, and Patient Pain and Fatigue

For patients with data on aids used due to gMG (n = 406), about one-third required physical aids, most commonly a cane or walking stick (15.0%), dark glasses or sunglasses (8.1%), or a wheelchair (7.4%). For patients with known home modifications (n = 367), use of shower chairs (16.6%), bathtub bars (14.7%), and grab bars or support railings (14.7%) was also reported. In total, 31.3% (n = 127) relied on at least one physical aid, and 35.1% (n = 129) had made home modifications to support daily living (Table 3).

Patient-Reported Outcomes

As shown in Fig. 2a, most patients reported problems across all EQ-5D-5L domains, most often with usual activities (79.4%, n = 162). More than half (56.4%, n = 115) described having slight problems with usual activities. Moderate problems were common in anxiety/depression (21.6%, n = 44), and nearly one-third of patients (29.4%, n = 60) reported moderate to extreme anxiety/depression. Severe problems were reported between 2.5% and 5.9% for the EQ-5D-5L domains. Caregiver proxy reports confirmed that most patients faced difficulties across domains (Fig. 2b).

Overall level of problems in each EQ-5D-5L domain, a self-reported by patient and b a caregiver-reported patient proxy. No patient self-completion data available in Australia (no completions) and South Korea (retrospective analysis only, no self-reporting collected)

Patient-reported outcomes highlighted substantial impairment, where the mean (SD) EQ-VAS score was 60.8 (19.7), the EQ-5D-5L index was 0.72 (0.23) and the MG-QoL-15R score was 14.3 (6.6). Fatigue was prominent, with a mean (SD) FACIT-Fatigue score of 27.7 (10.9), and over half (54.6%, n = 112) meeting criteria for severe fatigue (score < 30; Table 4).

Caregiver-Reported Outcomes

No problems were reported by over 90% of care partners for mobility (93.4%, n = 141), self-care (91.4%, n = 138), and usual activities (90.7%, n = 137) in their own EQ-5D-5L assessment; however, 32.5% (n = 49) reported slight, moderate or severe problems with anxiety/depression (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Caregivers reported better overall health status with a mean (SD) EQ-VAS of 80.3 (11.0), and EQ-5D-5L of 0.95 (0.10), yet a notable proportion (39.7%, n = 60) had ZBI-12 scores > 20, indicative of high caregiver burden (Table 4).

Patient- and Neurologist-Reported QoL Ratings

Subjective QoL assessments revealed different proportions between matched patients and neurologists (n = 205, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3a): 12.2% of patients rated their QoL as “very poor” compared with 1.5% of neurologists, while 28.8% of neurologists rated patient QoL as “good” versus only 18.0% of patients. “Poor” QoL was self-reported by 22.0% of patients. Based on a weighted kappa analysis and using the Cohen interpretation scale for the degree of agreement, the kappa value of 0.2692 indicated only fair agreement. Of the 205 matched patient-neurologist reported QoL ratings, 42.4% of cases had the patient reporting a subjectively lower QoL than their respective neurologist (p < 0.0001; Fig. 3b).

Employment and Work Productivity

Overall, 29.9% (n = 126) of patients were reported to be in full-time employment, ranging from 22.7% (n = 5) in South Korea to 40.4% (n = 23) in Malaysia. Of those not in full-time work (n = 218), 45.0% reported working less or not working at all due to their gMG (Table 1).

Among patients, mean (SD) absenteeism was 11.8% (20.8%), while presenteeism was 39.2% (22.5%). This translated to a mean (SD) overall work productivity loss of 42.9% (23.9%) and an activity impairment of 51.3% (23.9%; Fig. 4a).

Caregivers also experienced notable impacts, with mean (SD) absenteeism of 4.8% (12.0%) and presenteeism of 25.6% (17.2%), overall work productivity loss of 27.6% (19.6%) and activity impairment of 47.0% (24.1%; Fig. 4b).

Discussion

This multinational study provides the first region-wide characterization of the patient and caregiver burden of gMG in the Asia–Pacific region. Although most patients were classified as MGFA class II and received maintenance therapy, more than one-third had experienced recent exacerbations and one-fifth a prior crisis, underscoring that symptom control remains suboptimal. Patients and caregivers continue to experience substantial functional limitations and psychosocial distress, confirming that even clinically ‘mild’ disease can have profound effects on daily life.

Compared to ocular MG, gMG is associated with a broader range of symptoms that vary in severity and can fluctuate over time, potentially leading to myasthenic exacerbations or life-threatening crises [2, 8]. Over 90% of our cohort had to stay for one night or more during their most recent hospitalisation, with around one-fifth admitted to an ICU. The unpredictable nature of gMG negatively impacts patients’ social and emotional well-being [46], highlighting the need for treatment regimens that provide sustained disease control. Comorbidity, experienced by three-quarters of our patient cohort, can further complicate treatment, with high comorbidity prevalence in late-onset MG [47]. The number of comorbidities at onset was not available; therefore, research into the relationship of comorbidity and age at disease onset would be of value. It should be noted that, despite the reported disease burden, MGFA classified disease severity in our cohort appeared lower than in other published studies from Asia–Pacific and Europe [16, 18, 25, 26, 48].

Across EQ-5D-5L domains, the greatest challenges were anxiety/depression and performing usual activities, consistent with findings from a previous international real-world MG study [7]. Additionally, the majority of patients had FACIT-fatigue scores below 30, indicating severe fatigue. These findings demonstrate that, despite most patients being classified as MGFA class II at survey, substantial disease burden persisted for our patient cohort, particularly in emotional well-being and functional capacity. Observational research has shown that patients with gMG have a significantly lower QoL compared to the general population [11], with roughly a third of patients in our study self-reported a subjectively poor or very poor QoL. A recent review suggested that current treatments are not adequate in preventing the physical, mental, and social impacts that lead to decreased QoL [12], aligning with the patient-reported data from our study.

A significant discrepancy was observed between patients’ self-rated and neurologists’ perceived QoL, with neurologists tending to underestimate the extent of patient impairment. Additionally, 12% of patients were reported by their neurologists to have depression as a clinical comorbidity, but nearly one-third of patients self-reported experiencing moderate to extreme anxiety or depression in the EQ-5D-5L assessment. Such perceptual gaps may hinder timely psychosocial support or rehabilitative interventions, and highlight the importance of incorporating patient-reported outcomes into routine care. Further studies are needed to investigate the strategies to reduce this perception gap and to integrate patient perspectives more effectively into clinical management.

Almost half of patients not in full-time employment were working less or not working at all due to their gMG, and those employed missed over 10% of their work time on average. Work impairment for patients with gMG has been previously documented in Europe and the USA [15], revealing negative impacts on patient QoL. As gMG often affects individuals below typical retirement age, and is more prevalent in female patients [2, 49, 50], continued research into employment status, work impairment, and the associations with QoL is important.

Most patients with gMG were reported to receive additional support from caregivers. Close to 40% of caregivers had a ZBI score over 20, indicating high burden, with anxiety/depression appearing to be the most significant personal impact. A previous study based on patients with MG in Germany found that caregiver burden is negatively influenced by symptoms of depression in the patient receiving their care [9]. This highlights the importance of mental health support for both the patients and their caregivers. Caregivers also reported significant work and productivity impact, with nearly half experiencing activity impairment and one-quarter reporting work-related impairment due to the patient’s gMG. As found in our study and others [11], caregivers are often spouses, partners, or their children and caring for a family member can place additional strain on these relationships.

Taken together, these findings reveal a multidimensional burden affecting patients and caregivers. Neurologists may, in some cases, be underestimating impact on patient’s daily life and well-being. This also aligns with the clinical disease burden of this cohort, where unmet needs in relation to treatments prescribed and corticosteroid overreliance were reported [51]. Therefore, region-specific health-policy initiatives focusing on patient and caregiver support are warranted.

Strengths and Limitations

This study had a number of strengths and limitations. Minimal inclusion criteria were applied to neurologist selection, aiming to provide a geographically diverse and pragmatic sample. Participation was influenced by willingness to complete the survey. The cross-sectional design of this study limited the ability to draw causal inferences. While neurologists were asked to provide data for a consecutive series of patients to reduce selection bias, those who visit more frequently may have more severe disease than patients with milder disease who consult their neurologist less often. While recall bias is a common limitation of surveys, the data for these analyses were collected at the time of each patient’s appointment, which is expected to reduce this likelihood. In addition, neurologists had access to medical records for data extraction to ensure accuracy.

The serological test used to determine autoantibody status was not collected in the survey, so no comment could be made on specific testing technique. Neither myasthenic exacerbation nor crisis is defined on an international level [52]; therefore, definitions used for this study have been provided in the Methods. It should be noted that reappearance of minor symptoms was not excluded from the definition of exacerbation. Also important to note is the possibility that formal clinical diagnoses failed to capture the full spectrum of depressive symptoms or associated cognitive impairments experienced by patients in their daily lives, meaning the presence of depression may have been under-reported. Regarding the presented caregiver-reported data, this may be skewed toward patients with greater disease severity, as those with milder symptoms may have attended clinical visits unaccompanied, leading to potential underrepresentation in the dataset. Additionally, the age of the patients and the familial relation of caregivers may explain the high proportion of caregiver involvement for our cohort.

As a result of data privacy regulations in South Korea, and lack of response in Australia, no prospective patient self-reporting data could be presented from these countries. A lack of response was also the reason there was no caregiver-reported data for Australia or Taiwan. Demographic differences may limit the generalisability of these results outside of the Asia–Pacific region. While it appears patient cohorts in Europe and the USA had comparable gender proportions (tended to be majority female), they may be on average of a greater age (with patients in their late 50s) [25, 26, 53–55]. Patient data may not have captured proportionally similar samples from each country’s respective gMG population, and caregiver data collected were heavily weighted toward China, which may limit the generalisability to other Asia–Pacific countries. Across the six regions evaluated, the total number of patients living with MG is estimated at roughly 100,000, based on published prevalence data and country-specific population assumptions [4, 6, 56–58]. Our study includes 434 patients that are 18 years or older living with gMG, and MGFA class II–IV at time of survey. Despite these limitations, the data presented here reflect clinical practice at the time of the survey and provide valuable insights into the lived experience of patients and caregivers.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that, despite the availability of gMG treatments in the Asia–Pacific region, many patients continue to experience a range of substantial burdens beyond clinical symptoms, and that neurologists may underestimate the impact on patients. Patients with gMG and their caregivers reported poor QoL, impaired work productivity and limitations in daily activities alongside notable levels of fatigue, anxiety, and depression. To manage daily life, patients often require home modifications, assistive tools, and caregiver support. The burden of gMG extends beyond the individual patient to societal levels, highlighting the need for comprehensive support systems. Further research into treatment strategies and care support systems is warranted to more effectively address the burden and unmet needs.
