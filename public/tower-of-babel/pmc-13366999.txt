Single Rhupus syndrome in a young man complicated by class IV lupus nephritis and pleural effusion: complete remission with cyclosporine-based therapy: a case report
Authors: Nawwar Fallouh, Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Yousef Al Jabban, Raneem Ahmad, Wasfi Sahloul, Bassam Darwish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRhupus syndrome, the coexistence of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), is an uncommon overlap disorder. Severe SLE-type organ involvement—particularly class IV lupus nephritis and clinically significant serositis—has been reported less frequently than in isolated SLE, and optimal immunosuppressive strategies in patients with concurrent renal and hematologic disease remain poorly defined.Case presentationWe describe a 28-year-old Arab man who presented with fatigue, weakness, knee effusion, and laboratory evidence of autoimmune hemolytic anemia with leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. Serologic evaluation revealed antinuclear antibodies, anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, hypocomplementemia (low C3/C4), positive rheumatoid factor, and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies, supporting an RA–SLE overlap. Urinalysis demonstrated proteinuria and microscopic hematuria, and kidney biopsy confirmed ISN/RPS class IV-G (A/C) diffuse lupus nephritis with full-house immunofluorescence. The patient initially improved on oral prednisolone, hydroxychloroquine, and mycophenolate mofetil but subsequently re-presented with orthopnea, severe fatigue, and fever. Imaging showed a large unilateral pleural effusion; pleural fluid was repeatedly exudative with negative microbiologic studies, and thoracoscopic biopsies demonstrated nonspecific chronic pleuritis, consistent with lupus pleuritis. Treatment was escalated with intravenous methylprednisolone pulses and intensified oral corticosteroids, although background therapy was continued. Cyclosporine was added primarily to address immune cytopenias in the setting of bone-marrow hypocellularity (approximately 20–40%) with preserved trilineage maturation and no evidence of abnormal or monoclonal proliferation. Clinical, hematologic, and renal remission was observed following treatment escalation, although causality cannot be inferred from a single case.ConclusionThis case illustrates that Rhupus syndrome may present with aggressive SLE-type organ involvement, including class IV lupus nephritis, pleural effusion, and immune cytopenias. In this patient, remission was observed after treatment escalation with a cyclosporine-based regimen; however, this should be interpreted as an observed association rather than evidence of causality or generalizable efficacy. Cyclosporine may represent a feasible immunomodulatory option in selected high-risk patients, provided careful monitoring for toxicity, particularly in the setting of lupus nephritis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-026-06144-2.

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases are familial, chronic, systemic inflammatory disorders driven by dysregulation of immune tolerance. Within this spectrum, rhupus syndrome denotes the coexistence of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in the same patient [1], A 7000-patient epidemiological study found that the prevalence of RA was 15% and that of SLE was 8.9%. Therefore, 1.2% is the expected probability of a coincidence between RA and SLE [2]. The entity was formally described by Peter Schur in 1971, who introduced the term “rhupus” for patients fulfilling classification criteria for both RA and SLE [3]. Clinically, rhupus combines features of both diseases, most often symmetric polyarthritis of the hands, wrists, and knees together with malar rash, rheumatoid nodules, and constitutional symptoms such as fatigue and malaise [4]. Because no dedicated diagnostic criteria exist, clinicians rely on established frameworks for SLE (SLICC 2012) and RA (ACR/EULAR 2010); in practice, diagnosis is supported by erosive polyarthritis on imaging, elevated rheumatoid factor (RF), positive anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP), and lupus-associated serologies ( ANA, anti-double-stranded DNA [anti-dsDNA]) [5]. The absence of official classification criteria places patients with rhupus at a disadvantage and complicates consistent case classification and research comparability [6]. Distinguishing rhupus from isolated RA or SLE is clinically important, as therapeutic strategies and outcomes differ [7]. Here, we present a case of a 28-year-old Arab male with rhupus syndrome complicated by class IV lupus nephritis and pleural effusion.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old Arab man with a 20-year smoking history, no prior surgical history, no known drug or food allergies, and no relevant family history of autoimmune disease presented with fatigue, weakness, and inflammatory arthritis involving both large and small joints over a 7-month period, with knee effusion at presentation. Although prior knee imaging was reportedly suggestive of inflammatory joint damage, erosive disease could not be independently confirmed radiographically because the original images were unavailable owing to local archiving limitations. Initial studies demonstrated hypochromic microcytic anemia with hemolysis; the white-blood-cell count was approximately 4.4 × 103/µL and platelets were reduced. Urinalysis showed proteinuria and microscopic hematuria with 30 RBC/mm3 of predominantly glomerular morphology (80% dysmorphic, 20% isomorphic). Random-urine testing revealed creatinine 277 mg/dL, protein 78.6 mg/dL, and a protein/creatinine ratio of 0.28 mg/mg, with CRP elevated at 12.23 mg/L. Autoimmune serology was notable for ANA (HEp-2) positive, homogeneous, at 1:160; anti-dsDNA positive at 1:160; anti-CCP positive; RF positive; and hypocomplementemia with C3 42 and C4 4 mg/dL. Viral hepatitis screening was negative (HBsAg 0.8, anti-HBc total 1.70 nonreactive, anti-HCV 0.40). Bone-marrow aspirate was performed to evaluate cytopenias and showed hypocellularity (approximately 20–40%) with preserved trilineage maturation, without evidence of abnormal/monoclonal cell proliferation; clinical correlation was recommended. Ancillary studies (e.g., flow cytometry, cytogenetics, or viral stains) were not performed due to local resource limitations.

Given active urinary sediment despite modest proteinuria (UPCR 0.28 mg/mg), measured prior to renal biopsy and before initiating immunosuppressive therapy, kidney biopsy confirmed ISN/RPS class IV-G (A/C) diffuse lupus nephritis with an activity index of 5/24 and chronicity index of 3/12; light microscopy described endocapillary hypercellularity with interstitial lymphocytic infiltrates, and immunofluorescence demonstrated granular “full-house” staining (IgG 3+, C3 3+, C1q 2+, IgA 1+, IgM 1+). The working diagnosis was systemic lupus erythematosus overlapping rheumatoid arthritis (Rhupus Syndrome) complicated by autoimmune hemolytic anemia. The diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis was further supported by the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, with involvement of more than 10 joints including small joints, symptom duration of 7 months, and positive RF and anti-CCP serology, yielding a score of at least 8/10. He was started on hydroxychloroquine 200 mg daily, mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice daily, folic acid 5 mg daily, and prednisolone 10 mg daily, with clinical improvement.

One month later he re-presented with 15 days of orthopnea, severe fatigue, and intermittent fever. Computed tomography demonstrated a large pleural effusion; thoracentesis removed 600 mL with immediate relief (Fig. 1). Pleural fluid was exudative on two occasions: initially WBC 160/µL (85% lymphocytes, 15% neutrophils), protein 4.0 g/dL, LDH 400 U/L, glucose 72 mg/dL, GeneXpert negative; ten days later after thoracoscopy and repeat drainage, WBC 570/µL (90% neutrophils, 10% lymphocytes), protein 4.5 g/dL, LDH 408 U/L, glucose 72 mg/dL. During the flare, hemoglobin declined from 9.6 to 7.6 g/dL, platelets were approximately 32 × 103/µL, WBC fell from 10.0 to 5.8 × 103/µL with neutrophils around 70%, albumin was 2.3 g/dL with total protein 5.0–6.3 g/dL, ESR 35 mm/h, LDH 342 U/L, and renal indices transiently worsened with creatinine 1.2 to 1.8 mg/dL and urea 54 to 70 mg/dL; electrolytes were unremarkable (Na 134 mmol/L, K 4.1 mmol/L). Management was escalated with intravenous methylprednisolone pulses and an increase in oral prednisolone to 40 mg daily although hydroxychloroquine and mycophenolate mofetil were continued; cyclosporine was added primarily for immune cytopenias in the setting of bone-marrow hypocellularity (approximately 20–40%) with preserved trilineage maturation and no morphologic evidence of abnormal/monoclonal proliferation, rather than as primary therapy for lupus nephritis, and because alternative agents were not readily available locally at that time. Serial thoracenteses removed approximately 600 mL of serous fluid each time with symptomatic improvement. Over follow-up, respiratory symptoms resolved, kidney function stabilized, and hematologic parameters improved. Blood pressure and renal function were monitored every 2 weeks during the first 3 months of cyclosporine therapy and monthly thereafter once the patient was stable, in line with standard cyclosporine safety recommendations. The presentation raised consideration of Rhupus Syndrome versus isolated SLE or RA; the combination of inflammatory polyarthritis involving large and small joints, positive RF and anti-CCP serology, ANA/anti-dsDNA positivity, hypocomplementemia, and biopsy-proven class IV lupus nephritis favored RA–SLE overlap (Rhupus Syndrome). The pleural effusion differential included lupus pleuritis, infection (notably tuberculosis), and malignancy; exudative chemistry with negative GeneXpert and thoracoscopic biopsies showing fibrosis with nonspecific chronic pleuritis and no granulomas, necrosis, or neoplasia supported inflammatory lupus-related pleuritis over infection or cancer. Cytopenias with hemolysis suggested autoimmune hemolytic anemia, although aplastic anemia was unlikely given preserved trilineage maturation without morphologic evidence of marrow aplasia, despite overall hypocellularity.

Discussion

Rhupus syndrome (RhS) is a rare autoimmune overlap disorder characterized by the coexistence of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [8]. It exhibits a female predominance and frequently presents initially with RA-like features before the emergence of systemic manifestations attributable to SLE [9, 10]. An increased prevalence of HLA-DR1 and HLA-DR2 alleles has been reported among patients with Rhupus syndrome (RhS). Moreover, the presence of rheumatoid nodules in individuals with SLE has been proposed as a risk factor for subsequent Rhupus syndrome (RhS) development. Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies may further stratify risk, as their presence in SLE is associated with erosive arthritis and is thought to drive the clinical overlap with RA characteristic of Rhupus syndrome (RhS) [5]. Clinically, RhS is defined by a deforming, erosive, and symmetric polyarthritis accompanied by signs and symptoms of SLE, supported by serologic markers with high diagnostic specificity, including anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), anti-Smith (anti-Sm), and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies [11, 12]. Diagnosing and distinguishing Rhupus syndrome (RhS) from isolated RA or SLE is challenging; thus, serologic biomarkers are central to evaluation. In particular, concurrent positivity for anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies supports a true RA–SLE overlap rather than either disease alone [13]. Although anti-CCP antibodies have been implicated in the pathogenesis of articular erosions and may align Rhupus syndrome (RhS) with an RA-like inflammatory arthritis phenotype, erosive disease was not independently confirmed radiographically in our patient. Additional biomarkers that may be detected include anti-Smith (anti-Sm), anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La, anticardiolipin antibodies, rheumatoid factor, and anti-ribonucleoprotein (anti-RNP) antibodies [14]. In our patient, serology showed anti-CCP positivity together with ANA, documented anti-dsDNA positivity (1:160), positive rheumatoid factor, and hypocomplementemia (low C3/C4), supporting a true RA–SLE overlap. The diagnosis of RA was further supported by the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, with involvement of more than 10 joints including small joints, symptom duration of 7 months, and positive RF and anti-CCP serology, yielding a score of at least 8/10. Class IV lupus nephritis, pleural effusion, and autoimmune cytopenias/hemolysis provide clinico-pathologic confirmation of active SLE, together supporting the diagnosis of Rhupus syndrome (RhS). Notably, the patient had a nephritic presentation with active urinary sediment despite only modest baseline proteinuria (UPCR 0.28 mg/mg, obtained prior to renal biopsy and before initiating immunosuppressive therapy), illustrating that severe histologic lupus nephritis can occur even when proteinuria is not markedly elevated. In Rhupus syndrome (RhS), classic SLE organ involvement, particularly lupus nephritis and neuropsychiatric disease, appears less frequent and, when present, typically less aggressive [11, 13]. Peripheral cytopenias are seen in both RA and SLE, but bone-marrow hypocellularity is rarely reported [14]. Autoimmune hemolytic anemia has been described yet remains uncommon, and overt aplastic anemia is rarer still; when it occurs, it is attributed to immune-mediated destruction of hematopoietic stem cells, producing a hypocellular marrow [14]. In our patient, organ involvement was unusually prominent, with biopsy-proven class IV lupus nephritis and pleural effusion, contrasting with the generally milder visceral involvement described in many reports. Hematologically, he had peripheral cytopenias with autoimmune hemolysis, consistent with but uncommon in Rhupus syndrome (RhS), while bone-marrow evaluation demonstrated hypocellularity (approximately 20–40%) with preserved trilineage maturation and no morphologic evidence of abnormal/monoclonal proliferation, making malignant/infiltrative marrow pathology less likely and arguing against overt aplastic anemia. At diagnosis, patients typically receive systemic corticosteroids plus one to three conventional DMARDs, most commonly methotrexate, sulfasalazine, azathioprine, or leflunomide, together with hydroxychloroquine. High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulses are used less frequently in Rhupus syndrome (RhS) than in SLE. For organ-threatening disease, additional immunosuppressants such as cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, or cyclosporine are employed to limit damage [15]. In cases refractory to conventional therapy, biologics have been considered; although TNF-α inhibitors raise safety and efficacy concerns, rituximab and abatacept appear more promising in this overlap population [13, 15]. In our patient, therapy followed standard practice: systemic corticosteroids plus HCQ, with mycophenolate mofetil for class IV lupus nephritis. An acute flare with pleural effusion warranted pulse methylprednisolone and steroid escalation; cyclosporine was added primarily to address severe immune-mediated cytopenias/hemolysis in the setting of bone-marrow hypocellularity, rather than as primary therapy for lupus nephritis. Mechanistically, cyclosporine was selected for its calcineurin-inhibiting effect, which suppresses T-cell activation and downstream cytokine signaling, a rationale considered particularly relevant to the patient’s hematologic involvement. Alternative agents (e.g., cyclophosphamide or rituximab) were not readily available locally at that time, and clinical and hematologic improvement was subsequently observed during short-term follow-up up to hospital discharge. We acknowledge that cyclosporine may be limited by non-response and potential toxicities, particularly nephrotoxicity and hypertension; therefore, in our patient, blood pressure and renal function were monitored every 2 weeks during the first 3 months of therapy and monthly thereafter once the patient was stable, consistent with standard cyclosporine safety recommendations. Conventional RA DMARDs (MTX/SSZ/AZA/LEF), cyclophosphamide, and biologics were not used in this case.

Conclusion

This case underscores that Rhupus syndrome (RhS) can present with aggressive SLE-type organ involvement**, including class IV lupus nephritis and pleural effusion,** despite reports of milder visceral disease in overlap syndromes. Anti-CCP positivity with ANA, positive RF, hypocomplementemia, and documented anti-dsDNA positivity (1:160) supported RA–SLE overlap, while bone-marrow evaluation showing hypocellularity (approximately 20–40%) with preserved trilineage maturation and no morphologic evidence of abnormal/monoclonal proliferation made malignant/infiltrative pathology less likely and did not support overt aplastic anemia. A regimen of systemic corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine, mycophenolate for nephritis, and cyclosporine for hematologic disease was associated with clinical, renal and hematologic remission at short-term follow-up (up to hospital discharge); however, given the single-case design, this finding should be interpreted as an observed association rather than evidence of causality or generalizable efficacy. Cyclosporine may represent a case-dependent immunomodulatory option for immune-mediated hematologic involvement, particularly through T-cell–directed effects, provided that patients are carefully monitored for toxicity, especially nephrotoxicity and hypertension.
