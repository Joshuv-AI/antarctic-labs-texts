Large systems of symmetrized trapped Brownian Bridges and Schrödinger processes


Abstract
. Consider a large system of N N Brownian motions in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} fixed on a time interval [ 0 , β ] [0,\beta] with symmetrized initial and terminal conditions, under the influence of a trap potential. Such systems describe systems of bosons at positive temperatures confined in a spatial domain. We describe the large N N behavior of the averaged path (that is, their empirical path measure) and its connection with a well known optimal transport problem formulated by Erwin Schrödinger. We also explore the asymptotic behavior of the Brownian motions in terms of Large Deviations. In particular, the rate function that governs the mean of occupation measures turns out to be the well-known Donsker-Varadhan rate function. We therefore prove a simple formula for the large N N asymptotic of the symmetrized trace of e − β ​ ℋ N e^{-\beta{\mathcal{H}}_{N}} , where ℋ N {\mathcal{H}}_{N} is an N N particle Hamilton operator in a trap

1.
Introduction

In this paper we study a model that examines how a large system of particles at positive temperature behaves when the particles are trapped and influenced by specific constraints. When this system gets very large, the average path behavior of the particles becomes such that we can approximate it by a single diffusion. The main objective of this paper is to contribute to a probabilistic interpretation of the Bose-Einstein condensation. In Chapter 4 we will further elaborate on the relevant literature and future directions toward this goal. Let us give a brief overview of the model and its connection to quantum statistical mechanics.

We consider a system of
N
non-interacting bosons in a trap potential
W
. The system is described by the Hamilton operator, defined on symmetric functions of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{Nd})

{\mathcal{H}}_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(-\Delta_{i}+W(x_{i})\big),\qquad x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.1)

where the
i
-th Laplace operator,
\Delta_{i}
, represents the kinetic energy of the
i
-th particle, and
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
is the trap potential. The trace of the operator
{\rm e}^{-\beta{\mathcal{H}}_{N}}
is the canonical partition sum of the system at temperature
1/\beta
. However, the characteristic property of bosons is expressed by the symmetry of any
N
-particle wave function under permutation of the coordinates. This in turn means that the partition sum of a system of
N
bosons is given by the trace of the restriction of
{\rm e}^{-\beta{\mathcal{H}}_{N}}
to the subspace of symmetric wave functions, denoted by
{\rm Tr}_{+}({\rm e}^{-\beta{\mathcal{H}}_{N}})
. Via the Feynman-Kac formula, this trace is given as

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{+}\big({\rm e}^{-\beta{\mathcal{H}}_{N}}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{dN}}{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{N}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}}\Big(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mu^{\beta}_{x_{i},x_{\sigma(i)}}\Big)({\rm d}\omega)\,\exp\Big\{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{\beta}W(\omega_{s}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({i}})}})\,{\rm d}s\Big\},

(1.2)

where
\mu^{\beta}_{x,y}
is the canonical Brownian bridge measure with initial site
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and terminal site
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see (
2.7
) for a detailed definition of the Brownian bridge measure), and we wrote
\omega=(\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}},\dots,\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({N}})}})
.

We therefore consider a system of
N
Brownian motions under symmetrized conditions, and we are interested in rigorously describing the large
N
behavior of the system in the presence of a trap potential. In this paper, we show that the averaged path behavior (in the sense of empirical measures) of the Brownian motions can be described by a simple stochastic diffusion.

We also show that it solves a large deviation problem introduced by Schrödinger, which involves minimization of relative entropy with given marginals. This problem is known in the literature as the Schrödinger bridge problem. Let us give an overview of this concept: Fix a time horizon
\beta>0
,
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
probability measures in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a Markov process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with underlying probability measure
\mathbb{P}
. The Schrödinger bridge problem consists of finding a measure
Q
on the space of all paths
\omega:[0,\beta]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

H(Q|\mathbb{P})=\min_{Q_{0}\sim\nu_{1},Q_{\beta}\sim\nu_{2}}H(Q|\mathbb{P}),

(1.3)

where
Q_{0}
and
Q_{\beta}
are the distributions of the initial and final time of Q respectively and
H(Q|\mathbb{P})
is the relative entropy of
Q
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
(see (
2.13
).) The authors of [FG97] have expressed the solutions of the Schrödinger bridge problem as an integral of bridge measures: Let
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}
be the conditional probability measure
\mathbb{P}
, given that
X_{0}=x
and
X_{\beta}=0
. Then we can write
\mathbb{P}
as

\mathbb{P}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{P}_{x,y}\mu({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y),

where
\mu
is the joint law of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
under
\mathbb{P}
. Let now
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginal distributions
\nu({\rm d}x,\mathbb{R}^{d})=\nu_{1}({\rm d}x)
and
\nu(\mathbb{R}^{d},{\rm d}y)=\nu_{2}({\rm d}y)
. If
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
under the measure

Q:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{P}_{x,y}\nu({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)

remains Markovian, then
Q
is called a Schrödinger process. According to
[
FG97
]
a Schrödinger process is a solution to the problem (
1.3
). In the Appendix
5.5
we provide a detailed definition of a Schrödinger process.

Finally, we extend the Large deviations results of
[
AK08
]
and we provide an explicit formula for the rate function that controls the mean of occupation measures defined in (
2.11
).

The paper is organized as follows: In section 2 we set up the model and state some auxiliary results, in section 3 we state our main results, in section 4 we discuss the connection of our problem with the existing literature and in section 5 we provide proofs of our main results. The appendix consists of proofs of the auxiliary results and detailed definitions of Large Deviations and Schrödinger processes.

2.
Set up and auxiliary results

We study the large-
N
behavior of a system of
N
symmetrized trapped Brownian motions
B_{1},\dots,B_{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on a fixed time interval
[0,\beta]
, i.e., the behavior of the system under the measure defined as follows: For a tuple of
N
paths
\omega=(\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}},\ldots,\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({N}})}})
with
\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({i}})}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}:=C([0,\beta];\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
C([0,\beta];\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of all continuous paths
\omega:[0,\beta]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we set

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathfrak{m}},N,W}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}({\rm d}\omega):=\frac{1}{Z_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}}\exp\left(-H_{N}(\omega)\right)\mathbb{P}_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}({\rm d}\omega),

(2.4)

where

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathfrak{m}}({\rm d}x_{1})\cdots{\mathfrak{m}}({\rm d}x_{N})\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}^{\beta}_{x_{i},x_{\sigma(i)}},

(2.5)

H_{N}(\omega):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{\beta}W(\omega^{(i)}(s)){\rm d}s

(2.6)

and
Z_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}
is the partition function defined as

\mathbb{Z}_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}}[e^{-H_{N}}].

Here
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
is the set of all permutations of
1,\dots,N
,
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}^{\beta}
the normalised Brownian bridge measure on the time interval
[0,\beta]
with initial site
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and terminal site
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall that the canonical (non-normalised) Brownian bridge measure on the time interval
[0,\beta]
with initial site
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and terminal site
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

\mu_{x,y}^{\beta}(A)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x}(B\in A;B_{\beta}\in{\rm d}y)}{{\rm d}y},\qquad A\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}\mbox{ measurable,}

(2.7)

where
\mathbb{P}_{x}
is the probability measure under which
B=B^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}}
starts from
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
\mu_{x,y}^{\beta}
is a regular Borel measure on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}
with total mass equal to the Gaussian density,

\mu_{x,y}^{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}_{\beta})=p_{\beta}(x,y)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x}(B_{\beta}\in{\rm d}y)}{{\rm d}y}=(4\pi\beta)^{-d/2}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{4\beta}|x-y|^{2}}.

(2.8)

The normalized Brownian bridge measure is defined as
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}^{\beta}=\mu_{x,y}^{\beta}/p_{\beta}(x,y)
, which is a probability measure on
{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}
. Moreover,
{\mathfrak{m}}
is the initial probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Hence, the terminal location of the
i
-th motion is affixed to the initial location of the
\sigma(i)
-th motion, where
\sigma
is a uniformly distributed random permutation. Any of the
N
paths is a Brownian motion with initial distribution
{\mathfrak{m}}
, but with a peculiar terminal distribution at time
\beta
. We can conceive
\mathbb{P}_{{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}
as a two-step random mechanism: First we pick a uniform random permutation
\sigma
, then we pick
N
Brownian motions with initial distribution
{\mathfrak{m}}
, and the
i
-th motion is conditioned to terminate at the initial point of the
\sigma(i)
-th motion, for any
i
.

The function
W:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
is the so-called ”trap potential” and we assume that

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\inf_{|x|>R}W(x)=\infty,

(2.9)

it is bounded from below, and it is continuous on the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:W(x)<\infty\}
which is either
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or a connected compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We are interested in the large-
N
behavior of the empirical path measure defined as follows: For a tuple of
N
paths
\omega=(\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}},\ldots,\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({N}})}})
, we define
L_{N}:{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}^{N}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{\beta})
as

L_{N}(\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({i}})}}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}),

(2.10)

where for any topological space
X
we denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(X)
the set of all probability measures defined on
X
and for
x\in X
we denote by
\delta_{x}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(X)
the point-mass probability measure.

More precisely, we show that the distributions of
L_{N}
under
\mathbb{P}_{W,{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}
as
N\to\infty
, converge in probability to a unique measure
\mu^{*}_{W,{\mathfrak{m}}}
, and under further assumptions on the initial distribution
{\mathfrak{m}}
, we show that
\mu^{*}_{W,{\mathfrak{m}}}
belongs to a class of measures that solve the so-called Schrödinger bridge optimal transport problem: an entropy minimization problem on path measures with fixed initial and terminal distributions (see Section
6.12
for a detailed introduction to Schrödinger processes and the Schrödinger bridge problem). To reach this goal we extend the Large Deviations results in
[
AK08
]
. In Section
6.2
, we recall the notion of a Large Deviations Principle. In particular, we obtain a Large Deviations Principle for
L_{N}
under the measure
\mathbb{P}_{W,{\mathfrak{m}},N}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({{\rm sym}}})}}
and for the means of the normalized occupation measures, defined as follows: For a tuple of
N
paths
\omega=(\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}},\ldots,\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({N}})}})
we define
Y_{N}:{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}^{N}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

Y_{N}(\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}{\rm d}s\,\delta_{\omega^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({i}})}}(s)}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(2.11)

We also study an important special case: We set
{\mathfrak{m}}
to be the Lebesgue measure and we replace
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}^{\beta}
by the canonical, non-normalized, Brownian bridge measure,
\mu_{x,y}^{\beta}
(see (
2.7
)). In this case, the rate function for the means of occupation measures
Y_{N}
turns out to be equal to
\beta
times the well-known Donsker-Varadhan rate function, which we define in Section
6.2
.

The first step toward our results is to study Large Deviations Principles for the distributions of
L_{N}
and
Y_{N}
under the measure defined in (
2.4
). Throughout the paper, we fix
\beta>0
; we endow
{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}
with the topology of uniform convergence and the corresponding Borel
\sigma

N

B^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({1}})}},\ldots,B^{{\scriptscriptstyle{({N}})}}
, taking values in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}

[
AK08
]
, we work with Brownian motions having generator
\Delta

\frac{1}{2}\Delta

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\tilde{f}(\omega):=\int_{0}^{\beta}f(\omega(s)){\rm d}s,\qquad\omega\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}.

Additionally, we endow
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{\beta})
with the Lévy metric defined as

{\rm d}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\{\delta>0\colon\mu(\Gamma)\leq\nu(\Gamma^{\delta})+\delta\,\mbox{ and }\,\nu(\Gamma)\leq\mu(\Gamma^{\delta})+\delta\,\mbox{ for all }\,\Gamma=\overline{\Gamma}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}\},

(2.12)

where
F^{\delta}=\{\omega\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\beta}\colon{\operatorname{dist}}(\omega,F)\leq\delta\}

as

. Hence, the Lévy metric generates the weak topology of probability measures. The latter is the topology under which we develop Large Deviations Principles. We also use the notation ”
\mu_{N}\Rightarrow\mu
” to denote the weak convergence of probability measures.

6.2.
Large Deviations

In this chapter we will see an overview of some of the tools we will be using. The main concept of Large deviations theory is the asymptotic computation of small probabilities on an
exponential scale. On this essay we will have to study complicated and decaying families of probability measures, so a proper understanding of Large deviations theory is essential. Proofs of the results can be found in [DZ98]

Recall that a family of measures
(\mu_{\epsilon})_{(\epsilon\geq 0)}
on
(\mathcal{X},{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathcal{X}})
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a topological space and
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathcal{X}}
is the Borel sigma-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
, satisfies the Large Deviation Principle (LDP) with speed
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
and rate function
I:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
if the level sets
L(a)=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:I(x)\leq a\}
are closed in
\mathcal{X}
for every
a\geq 0
and the following holds:

Upper bound: For every
F\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
closed

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\epsilon\log\mu_{\epsilon}(F)\leq-\inf_{x\in F}I(x).

Lower bound: For every
G\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
open

-\inf_{x\in G}I(x)\leq\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\epsilon\log\mu_{\epsilon}(G).

Moreover, if the level sets are compact, we call
I
good rate function.

G\subset{\mathcal{X}}
open, we define
\phi_{G}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\phi_{G}=\begin{cases}\phi(x),\quad x\in G\\
-\infty,\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

then
\phi_{G}
is lower semi continuous, because
\{x:\phi_{G}(x)>a\}=G\cap\{x:\phi(x)>a\}
which is open for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
. From theorem
6.7
and from lemma
6.9
we have

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\nu_{\epsilon}(G)=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{G}e^{\phi(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)-\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\phi(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)

=\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\phi_{G}(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)-\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\phi(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)

\geq\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\{\phi_{G}(x)-I(x)\}-\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\{\phi(x)-I(x)\}=-\inf_{x\in G}J(x)

Upper Bound: If
\phi:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
is upper semi continuous and
F\subset{\mathcal{X}}
closed, we define
\phi_{F}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\phi_{F}=\begin{cases}\phi(x),\quad x\in F\\
-\infty,\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

then
\phi_{F}
is upper semi continuous, because
\{x:\phi_{F}(x)\geq a\}=F\cap\{x:\phi(x)\geq a\}
which is closed for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
. From theorem
6.7
and from lemma
6.10
we have

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\nu_{\epsilon}(F)=\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{F}e^{\phi(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)-\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\phi(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)

=\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\phi_{F}(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)-\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon\log\int_{\mathcal{X}}e^{\phi(x)/\epsilon}\mu_{\epsilon}({\rm d}x)

\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\{\phi_{F}(x)-I(x)\}-\sup_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\{\phi(x)-I(x)\}=-\inf_{x\in F}J(x)

J is a good rate function: Obviously
J
is non negative. We suppose that there exist a
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}
such that
I(x_{0})-\phi(x_{0})<\infty
. For every
y\in{\mathcal{X}}
have that
I(y)-\phi(y)\geq-\sup\phi:=-b
. If
c=\inf_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\{I(x)-\phi(x)\}
then

I(x_{0})-\phi(x_{0})\geq c\geq-b

So if
\phi
is bounded, then
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, since
I
and
-\phi
are lower semi continuous, then
J
is too. Finally, for
a>0

\{x:J(x)\leq a\}\subset\{x:I(x)\leq a+b+c\}

since I is good rate function, the latter set is compact, so J is good rate function as well
∎

6.3.
Schrödinger processes

Here we refer to the paper [FG97] and the book [F88].

Definition 6.12
.

Let
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
S
be a state space, which we assume that is Polish, and let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(with
\beta>0
) be a Markov process with underlying probability measure
\mathbb{P}
, and state space
S
. Moreover, let
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(S\times S)
be the joint initial and terminal distribution of
\mathbb{P}
starting from
t=0
and terminating at time
t=\beta
. Define the measure

{\rm d}\nu(x,y):=\varphi(x,y){\rm d}\mu(x,y)

where
\varphi:S\times S\to(0,\infty)
is a positive, normalized under the measure
\mu
function. Finally let

{\rm d}Q:=\varphi(X_{0},X_{\beta}){\rm d}\mathbb{P}

(6.90)

The process
(X_{t})
under
Q
is said to be a Schrödinger process if
(X_{t})
has the Markov property under the measure
Q
.

6.3.1.
Background.

Let
X_{t}
above be the Brownian motion on
S=\mathbb{R}^{d}
with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
(that is,
\mathbb{P}
is the Wiener measure with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
) and let
P_{s,t}(x,{\rm d}y)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
0\leq s<t\leq\beta
be its transition probabilities. Schrödinger initiated the study of processes of the form of
Q
by considering
the following problem of Large Deviations. Let
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{\beta}
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and look for the most likely behavior of a large collection of independent Brownian motions governed by
\mathbb{P}
under the constraint that the empirical distributions at times
t=0
and
t=\beta
are close to
\nu_{0}
and to
\nu_{\beta}
. In the limit of an infinite particle system, this behavior can be described by independent motions governed by
Q
where the measure
\nu
minimizes the relative entropy
H(\nu|\mu)
under the constraints that
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{\beta}
are the marginal distributions of
\nu
. See (1.3 page 161 [F88]) for the proof. The density of the entropy minimizing measure admits a factorization of the form

\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\mu}(x,y)=f(x)g(y),

(6.91)

where
f,g
are measurable, non negative functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That implies that
Q
has the structure of
h
-path process, i.e., it is the integral of a function
h
times the transition probabilities ) where
h
is a space-time harmonic function of Brownian motion in the sense that
h(s,x)=\int h(t,y)P_{s,t}(x,{\rm d}y)
. In particular, we obtain the Markov property and
Q
is a Schrödinger process.

For a measure
\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

\nu\approx\mu
, with
H(\nu|\mu)<\infty
, the Markov process of the associated measure
Q
, is equivalent both to the factorization of
\varphi
of the form (
6.91
) and the minimization of
H(\nu|\mu)
. Such implications are also valid if we replace the Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a general Markov process, but not all of them. The difficult part is to derive the factorization (
6.91
).

Let
(X_{t})
be a Markov process with undrelying measure
\mathbb{P}
, and
Q
be a measure with density
\varphi
as in definition
6.90
. Then for
\tau\in[0,\beta]
, we have that

\frac{{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}Q}\Big|_{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\phi(X_{0},X_{\beta})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]=\int_{S}\phi(X_{0},y)p(t,\beta,X_{t},{\rm d}y):=\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},X_{t}),

where for
x\in S
we denote by
E^{x}_{\mathbb{P}}
the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}
when the process starts from
x
, and for
s,t\in[0,\beta]
, with
t>s
,
x\in S,A\in{\mathcal{B}}(S)
we denote by
p(s,t,x,A)
the transition probabilities of
\mathbb{P}
.

Fix
t,s\in[0,\beta]
with
s<t
. Then we have for every continuous and bounded function
h:S\to\mathbb{R}
that

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[h(X_{t})|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(X_{t})\frac{\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},X_{t})}{\varphi_{0,s}(X_{0},X_{s})}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}]

To see that, choose a random element of S,
Y

{\mathcal{F}}_{s}
measurable. Then

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[h(X_{t})Y]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Yh(X_{t})\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},X_{t})]=

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[Y\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(X_{t})\frac{\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},X_{t})}{\varphi_{0,s}(X_{0},X_{s})}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}]\varphi_{0,s}(X_{0},X_{s})]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}[Y\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[h(X_{t})\frac{\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},X_{t})}{\varphi_{0,s}(X_{0},X_{s})}|{\mathcal{F}}_{s}]].

So we deduce

Q(X_{t}\in{\rm d}y|{\mathcal{F}}_{s})=\frac{1}{\varphi_{0,s}(X_{0},X_{s})}\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},y)p(t-s,X_{s},{\rm d}y).

Thus a prediction given
{\mathcal{F}}_{s}
in general will involve both
X_{s}
and
X_{0}
, so
Q
is not necessarily a Markov process.
However, if
\phi(x,y)=f(x)g(y)
, where f,g are positive, measurable functions on S, then we show that
Q
is a Schrödinger process. Define the function
h:S\times[0,\beta]\to\mathbb{R}
as

h(x,t):=\int_{S}g(y)p(1-t,x,{\rm d}y),

then,
h
is space-time harmonic in the sense that

h(x,s)=\int_{S}h(y,t)p(t-s,x,{\rm d}y)

for
0\leq s<t\leq\beta
. The density of Q with respect to
\mathbb{P}
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
can be expressed as

\varphi_{0,t}(X_{0},X_{t})=f(X_{0})h(X_{t},t)

where
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
are the initial distributions of
\mathbb{P}
and Q respectively. This shows that
Q
has the structure of an h-path process, in particular, Q is a Markov process since h-path transforms preserve the Markov property.
