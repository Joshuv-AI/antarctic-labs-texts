Rare extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumor of the vulva: a case report

Case presentation

A 53-year-old perimenopausal female with a past medical history notable for invasive ductal carcinoma of the left breast presented with a three-month history of a tender, non-draining left labial lump initially presumed to represent cellulitis/folliculitis. Conservative antibiotic management failed, and the lesion was excised piecemeal at an outside institution. Pathologic findings of the resection were interpreted as gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST).

One month following the initial excision, the patient was referred to gynecologic oncology. On examination, the surgical site demonstrated a 1 × 2 cm open wound with areas of unhealed tissue but no palpable lymphadenopathy or discrete residual mass. Pelvic MRI with and without contrast demonstrated a 3.5 × 2.5 cm enhancing perineal lesion located at the posteromedial margin of the vulva, just left of midline. No metastatic disease or pelvic lymphadenopathy was identified. Upper endoscopy and colonoscopy did not demonstrate a gastrointestinal primary. Repeat clinical evaluation one month later demonstrated progression at the site of prior excision near the introitus: a 2 cm area of thickening slightly left of midline near the hymenal ring with a 0.5 cm circular area of erythematous granulation tissue, prompting recommendation for definitive wide radical excision.

Operative details

Under general anesthesia, the left posterior vulva and lower vagina were marked, and a 5-inch incision was carried down through scarred tissue and thickened tumor bed using scalpel and electrocautery. Digital rectal exam confirmed proximity to the rectum. Careful sharp and blunt dissection allowed complete gross excision without entry into the rectal lumen. During the resection, partial disruption of the superficial external anal sphincter occurred, necessitating intraoperative consultation with colorectal surgery; proctoscopic evaluation revealed no injury to the anterior rectal wall, and the sphincter muscle was successfully reconstituted (Heinrich et al., 2003). Plastic surgery performed reconstructive closure using a local soft tissue flap elevated from the anterior labia majora, advanced to meet the posterior remnant of the labia majora, providing durable coverage and restoration of labial contour (Agaimy et al., 2007). The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged home on postoperative day 1.

Pathologic findings

The initial piecemeal resection consisted of a tan-white rubbery soft tissue. The slides from the outside institution were reviewed at our institution. Histologic evaluation demonstrated a spindle-cell neoplasm with focal necrosis, a mitotic rate of up to 7 mitoses per 50 high-power fields, and positive margins. Immunohistochemical profiling revealed strong positivity for c-KIT (CD117), DOG-1, and CD34, while immunostains for desmin, smooth muscle myosin, smooth muscle actin, STAT6, SOX10, S-100, p63, CD10, multiple cytokeratins, and estrogen receptor were negative (Fig. 1). The findings supported a diagnosis of gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) and militated against diagnoses of smooth muscle neoplasm, solitary fibrous tumor, malignant melanoma, metastatic breast carcinoma, and sarcomatoid carcinoma (Miettinen and Lasota, 2006, Fletcher et al., 2002, Medeiros et al., 2004). Molecular testing performed by next-generation sequencing at ARUP Laboratories identified a KIT exon 11 mutation (Hirota et al., 1998).

Tumor board discussion

A multidisciplinary tumor board reviewed this case and reached the following consensus: The immunohistochemical and molecular profile supported the diagnosis of a primary vulvar EGIST, since exclusion of a gastrointestinal primary was confirmed by a negative upper endoscopy and colonoscopy. Despite grossly complete resection, microscopic extension to the deep inked margin was identified. Further radical surgery was deemed to carry unacceptable risk of permanent anorectal dysfunction. Adjuvant imatinib was recommended given presence of KIT exon 11 mutation and positive deep margin, with structured surveillance planned as described below (Agaimy et al., 2007, Demetri et al., 2002, Verweij et al., 2004, Heinrich et al., 2006, DeMatteo et al., 2009).

Outcome

The patient tolerated surgery without complications and was discharged on postoperative day 1. The incision demonstrated appropriate healing with preserved labial contour from flap reconstruction, and there was no clinical evidence of rectal injury or distant disease on postoperative staging studies. Adjuvant imatinib, as discussed in the tumor board, was utilized. Imatinib was initiated approximately six weeks postoperatively, after wound healing was confirmed clinically, in keeping with clinical practice on timing of systemic therapy initiation relative to major surgery (Bailey and Parikh, 2018 Jul). The patient is currently on treatment with no evidence of recurrence. She is undergoing physical examination with vulvar inspection every three months, and cross-sectional imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis) every six months, for the first three years, in alignment with NCCN GIST surveillance recommendations.

Discussion

Diagnostic considerations

Extra-gastrointestinal stromal tumor (EGIST) is an extremely rare tumor, accounting for less than 5% of GIST cases (von Mehren et al., 2022). The tumor’s strong immunoreactivity for CD117 (c-KIT) and DOG-1, combined with a KIT exon 11 mutation, are classic features supporting the diagnosis of GIST despite the extra-gastrointestinal site (Miettinen and Lasota, 2006, Fletcher et al., 2002, Medeiros et al., 2004, Hirota et al., 1998). The broad immunohistochemical panel that was negative for desmin, SMA, STAT6, SOX10, S-100, p63, CD10, ER, and cytokeratins, was crucial for excluding smooth muscle tumors, solitary fibrous tumor, malignant melanoma, metastatic breast carcinoma, and sarcomatoid carcinoma (Miettinen and Lasota, 2006, Fletcher et al., 2002, Medeiros et al., 2004). Given the broad differential of vulvar spindle-cell neoplasms, preoperative biopsy should be performed to guide triage for appropriate surgical management, particularly since complete gross resection is the cornerstone of management. Additionally, given the rarity of vulvar EGIST, exhaustive evaluation for a gastrointestinal primary is essential and includes imaging, endoscopy, and colonoscopy, which were negative in this case (Reith et al., 2000, Agaimy et al., 2007).

Surgical management and anatomic constraints

Complete oncologic resection with negative margins is the principal curative modality for GISTs (Joensuu, 2008, DeMatteo et al., 2009, von Mehren et al., 2022). In the pelvic/perineal location, achieving negative deep margins may require resection that risks rectal or sphincteric injury and significant functional morbidity (Heinrich et al., 2003, Agaimy et al., 2007). In our patient, meticulous dissection enabled complete removal of grossly visible disease, but the tumor extended microscopically to the inked deep margin adjacent to the external anal sphincter, necessitating sphincter repair. Balancing oncologic radicality against preservation of anorectal function is a central challenge in vulvar EGISTs (Heinrich et al., 2003, Agaimy et al., 2007).

The importance of preoperative biopsy cannot be overstated: histologic confirmation before resection allows multidisciplinary planning, ensures that colorectal and reconstructive surgery teams are engaged when appropriate, and enables consideration of neoadjuvant imatinib. In selected cases where critical structures are at risk, neoadjuvant imatinib has been employed in gastric and rectal GIST to reduce tumor volume and improve the likelihood of organ-preserving resection. Although data in vulvar EGISTs are limited to case reports, this strategy merits consideration when initial tumor size or proximity to the anorectal complex would preclude an acceptable functional outcome with upfront surgery (Mohamed et al., 2023, Hanayneh et al., 2019, Huatuco et al., 2021).

Prognosis and systemic therapy rationale

Prognosis in GISTs is influenced primarily by tumor size, mitotic index, and anatomic site (Miettinen and Lasota, 2006, Fletcher et al., 2002, Joensuu, 2008). Extra-gastrointestinal tumors may be associated with worse outcomes compared to gastric primaries (Agaimy et al., 2007). Margin positivity further increases recurrence risk (Joensuu, 2008, Miettinen et al., 2001, Demetri et al., 2002). Identification of a c-KIT exon 11 mutation provides both diagnostic confirmation and therapeutic guidance, as such mutations are typically sensitive to imatinib mesylate (Heinrich et al., 2003, Demetri et al., 2002). Given the positive deep margin and the functional limitations of further radical surgery, adjuvant imatinib was recommended (Heinrich et al., 2006, DeMatteo et al., 2009).

Regarding timing of imatinib initiation, available evidence and expert consensus suggest deferring systemic therapy until adequate wound healing is achieved, typically four to eight weeks postoperatively, given the anti-angiogenic properties of imatinib and theoretical concerns about wound healing impairment (Bailey and Parikh, 2018 Jul). In our patient, imatinib was initiated approximately six weeks after surgery, following confirmed wound healing.

Role of radiation therapy

Radiation therapy is not a standard component of GIST management and has not been formally evaluated in vulvar EGISTs. GISTs are generally considered radioresistant. Evidence is limited to small retrospective reports suggesting possible palliative benefit in metastatic or unresectable disease, without randomized data supporting adjuvant use (Cuaron et al., 2013, Gatto et al., 2017). In the vulvar/perineal location, additional risks of radiation morbidity, including fibrosis, wound healing impairment, bowel and bladder dysfunction, further limit applicability.

Surveillance strategy

Surveillance protocols for vulvar EGISTs should be extrapolated from established GIST guidelines. Per NCCN GIST recommendations, patients with high-risk features are advised to undergo cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI of abdomen and pelvis) every three to six months for the first three to five years, then annually. For vulvar primaries, physical examination with vulvar inspection is critical and should be incorporated at each visit (von Mehren et al., 2022). In this patient, we implemented a surveillance protocol consisting of vulvar examination and CT chest/abdomen/pelvis every three months for year one, every six months for years two and three, and annually thereafter.

Literature context and prior reported cases

Published reports of vulvar and pelvic EGISTs remain sparse. Table 1. summarizes the reported cases. Mohamed et al. described a primary vulvar EGIST in a 77-year-old postmenopausal woman, with immunohistochemical confirmation and no reported mutation testing, noting the diagnostic challenge of vulvar spindle-cell neoplasms (Mohamed et al., 2023). Zhang et al. reported a primary EGIST of the rectovaginal septum managed with resection and adjuvant imatinib (Zhang et al., 2009). Hanayneh et al. documented a vaginal EGIST managed with surgical excision followed by systemic therapy (Hanayneh et al., 2019). Collectively, these and other reported cases emphasize the rarity of pelvic EGISTs, the importance of multidisciplinary management, and the role of adjuvant tyrosine-kinase inhibitors when high-risk features or positive margins are present (Mohamed et al., 2023, Hanayneh et al., 2019, Huatuco et al., 2021, Koo et al., 2021 Apr).

Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of vulvar EGISTs. Recognition requires integration of clinical history, histopathology, broad immunohistochemical profiling, molecular testing, and exclusion of a gastrointestinal primary. Complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, though anatomic constraints in the vulvar/perineal region frequently challenge margin clearance. Adjuvant imatinib is indicated when high-risk features are present, with timing of initiation informed by wound healing considerations. Radiation therapy has no established role. Structured surveillance should follow established GIST protocols. Preoperative biopsy and multidisciplinary planning, with early consideration of neoadjuvant imatinib, may optimize outcomes in future cases. Continued reporting of vulvar EGIST cases is essential to advance the evidence base for management of this exceedingly rare entity.
