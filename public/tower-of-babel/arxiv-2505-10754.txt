Conservative velocity mappings for discontinuous Galerkin kinetics
M. Francisquez, P. Cagas, A. Shukla, J. Juno, G. W. Hammett

Abstract
Continuum computational kinetic plasma models evolve the distribution function of a plasma species f s f_{s} on a phase-space grid over time. In many problems of interest the distribution function has limited extent in velocity space; hence, using a uniform, highly refined mesh would be costly and slow. Nonuniform velocity grids can reduce the computational cost by placing more degrees of freedom where f s f_{s} is appreciable and fewer where it is not. In this work we introduce a first-of-its kind discontinuous Galerkin approach to nonuniform velocity-space discretization using mapped velocity coordinates. This new method is presented in the context of a gyrokinetic model used to study magnetized plasmas. We create discretizations of collisionless and collisional terms using mappings in a way that exactly conserves particles and energy. Numerical tests of such properties are presented, and we show that this new discretization can reproduce earlier gyrokinetic simulations using grids with up to 6-60 times fewer cells and 22X-60X speed-ups depending on dimensionality, geometry and plasma parameters.



1
Introduction

Modeling high-temperature plasmas can benefit from the use of a kinetic model by which the distribution function
f_{s}
of a species
s
is prescribed throughout position and velocity, i.e. phase space. A rich collection of kinetic models exists, with the Vlasov or Boltzmann equations
[
39
]
being some of the most well known. Although constrained by physical principles (e.g. Liouville’s theorem), the variation of
f_{s}
over
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})
can be tremendously diverse and complex, exhibiting wildly different profiles from one system to another or from one parameter regime to another. However, something quite ubiquitous is that
f_{s}
tends to be of appreciable magnitude only in restricted regions of phase space. Consider one of the most basic distributions in local thermodynamic equilibrium; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution decays exponentially with the square of the velocity divided by the thermal speed, so for velocities greater than a few thermal speeds (e.g. 3)
f_{s}
is orders of magnitude smaller and possibly negligible.

Despite
f_{s}
being localized in phase space, kinetic models are difficult to solve analytically, and thus a large body of numerical methods and codes exists to approximate their solutions
[
19
,
54
,
53
,
11
]
. Many of these computational approaches are either Lagrangian (e.g. particle-in-cell) or Eulerian, the latter discretizing the kinetic equation on a multidimensional phase space grid in both position and velocity space, and updating
f_{s}
at discrete time steps. The discrete kinetic operator can be constructed with a variety of methods, such as finite volume
[
68
,
67
]
, finite difference
[
57
,
36
]
, semi-Lagrangian
[
73
]
, or spectral
[
70
]
methods, to name a few. These methods typically use a velocity space mesh that extends sufficiently far such that truncation of the discrete
f_{s}
is stable and justified by its smallness there. At the same time, said mesh must be sufficiently fine to reach the desired level of accuracy, resolve sharp gradients, or remain stable. Many continuum kinetic codes meet these constraints with a uniformly spaced velocity grid, effectively employing the same density of degrees of freedom (DOF) throughout. This commonplace strategy is, albeit a simple and acceptable first step, wasteful because one should only need to place a large density of DOF where
f_{s}
is appreciable or where strong gradients arise. Nonuniform grids attempt to do precisely that, using more DOF in those parts of the domain that require them and fewer in others.

Nonuniform velocity space grids have been employed by kinetic codes in a variety of contexts before. An early use of geometric velocity-space grids (where cell length increases by a multiplicative constant) facilitated the solution of the bounce-averaged Fokker-Planck equation
[
49
,
40
]
. Around the same time, a simple grid with two different cell lengths was used to solve a quasilinear Fokker-Planck equation to study the evolution of the distribution function under radio frequency heating
[
32
]
. A similar two-cell length grid was used 30 years later to study kinetic electrostatic electron nonlinear (KEEN) waves
[
2
]
. This technique is still actively developed to, for example, enhance the capabilities of semi-Lagrangian 1D1V kinetic codes
[
9
]
and resolve low energies near the wall in the presence of electron-neutral collisions
[
52
]
. We should mention that there are other approaches to nonuniform velocity discretization which accomplish the same objective. One is to use a quadrature rule (e.g. Gauss-Laguerre) that places ordinates at unevenly spaced locations in order to maximize the accuracy of the underlying integrals (e.g. to compute moments of the distribution)
[
4
,
5
]
. Alternatively, a closely related method is to use a spectral method in velocity space such that, depending on the type and number of basis functions kept, some parts of velocity space are represented with greater accuracy
[
12
,
45
,
25
]
. On the other hand, the number of DOF may be reduced by using sparse grids
[
69
,
42
,
58
]
. Finally, other techniques to reduce the number of DOF and localize them where phase-structures exist include unstructured meshes and adaptive mesh refinement
[
72
,
34
,
71
]
.

This work offers a new perspective on nonuniform velocity grids for Eulerian kinetic simulations employing the discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method. A DG discretization has a number of benefits
[
1
]
, including that it is local so it parallelizes well on distributed computers, has high arithmetic intensity for each byte fetched from memory which improves performance on graphical processing units (GPUs)
[
41
]
, and supports tailored numerical fluxes at cell boundaries (as done in finite volume methods) to preserve certain properties such as positivity and monotonicity
[
74
]
. We thus present a first-of-its-kind DG method to nonuniformly discretize velocity space with the following merits:
a)
it supports user-defined coordinate mappings tailoring the effective grid to each application,
b)
it conserves particles and energy exactly even with curvilinear position-space coordinates, and
c)
it is implemented in a higher-dimensional (5D) GPU-accelerated code. We will describe this novel method in the context of a gyrokinetic model used to study magnetized plasmas (section
2
), as well as sketch its use for a Vlasov-Poisson solver (
A
). The conservative (gyrokinetic) DG algorithm is described in detail (section
3
and
B
), including a proof of its conservative properties (
C
). We conclude with a series of numerical tests of increasing complexity to demonstrate its use in practical applications (section
4
), and with a brief summary of this work and future possibilities (section
5
).

2
Model

We present a nonuniform velocity-space discretization via conservative discontinuous Galerkin (DG) mappings. As a test-bed for this method, we use a long-wavelength, electrostatic, full-
f
gyrokinetic model
[
16
,
63
,
10
]
. The same technique can be used for an Eulerian DG Vlasov solver
[
38
]
, as described in
A
. The gyrokinetic model we focus on is used in several major codes studying magnetized fusion plasmas
[
35
,
14
,
50
,
65
]
; it governs the evolution of the guiding center distribution function
f_{s}(t,\mathbf{R},v_{\parallel},\mu)
of species
s
(with charge
q_{s}
and mass
m_{s}
) in a plasma magnetized by the equilibrium magnetic field
\mathbf{B}=B\,\mathbf{\hat{b}}
. The time-evolution of
f_{s}
as function of guiding center position
\mathbf{R}
, velocity parallel to the magnetic field
v_{\parallel}=\mathbf{\hat{b}}\cdot\mathbf{v}
, and the magnetic moment
\mu=m_{s}v_{\perp}^{2}/(2B)
, is given by

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}f_{s}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathcal{J}\mathbf{\dot{R}}f_{s}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\mathcal{J}\dot{v_{\parallel}}f_{s}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{C}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{D}_{s},

(1)

where
\mathcal{J}=B_{\parallel}^{*}=\mathbf{\hat{b}}\cdot\mathbf{B^{*}}
is the Jacobian of the gyrocenter coordinates and
\mathbf{B^{*}}=\mathbf{B}+(m_{s}v_{\parallel}/q_{s})\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}
is the effective magnetic field. The second and third (collisionless) terms on the left-hand side of equation
1
contain phase-space advection of
f_{s}
by the velocities
\mathbf{\dot{R}}
and
\dot{v_{\parallel}}
(described in
B
). The right-hand side contains the actions of collisions (
\mathcal{C}_{s}
), sources (
\mathcal{S}_{s}
) and diffusion (
\mathcal{D}_{s}
). Sources are typically used to account for plasma entering our domain of study, say due to transport from another region not modeled or due to external fueling. Cross-field transport is self-consistently modeled inside our domain by drifts and turbulence (the latter in 3D only). In the absence of anomalous cross-field transport, as is the case in 2D axisymmetric studies, one may use the diffusive term
\mathcal{D}_{s}
as a model for such transport. Collisions include the effect of elastic collisions between charged species, and inelastic collisions and reactions (e.g. charge exchange, recombination, ionization, radiation).

We wish to solve equation
1
in curvilinear coordinates, specifically field-line following coordinates that have been a workhorse of turbulence modeling in the core of tokamaks
[
6
]
and stellarators
[
48
]
, and can be used to study other laboratory devices such as linear machines
[
60
]
and mirrors
[
22
]
. Such a formulation, together with suitable boundary conditions, enables the solution of these equations in a small volume following a bundle of magnetic field lines (a flux-tube). A flux-tube domain reduces the number of degrees of freedom, supports larger time steps, and thus results in cheaper simulations than, say, global simulations with Cartesian grids. We thus introduce a field-aligned curvilinear coordinate system
\mathbf{x}=\{x^{i}\}
(here
i\in[1,d_{x}]
and
d_{x}
is the number of position-space dimensions) with a mapping to the Cartesian gyrocenter position
\mathbf{R}
given by
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})
. In this new coordinate system we can write equation
1
as
[
47
]

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}f_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\mathcal{J}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}f_{s}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\mathcal{J}\dot{v_{\parallel}}f_{s}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{C}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{D}_{s},

(2)

where
\mathbf{e_{i}}=\partial\mathbf{R}/\partial x^{i}
and
\mathbf{e^{i}}

are the tangent (covariant) and dual (contravariant) basis vectors of the
\mathbf{x}
coordinates. These vectors define the metric tensors
g_{ij}=\mathbf{e_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{e_{j}}
and
g^{ij}=\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{e^{j}}
, and
J_{x}=\mathrm{det}(g_{ij})^{1/2}=\mathrm{det}(g^{ij})^{-1/2}
is the Jacobian
[
17
]
of the
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})
map. Repeated indices imply summation, unless noted otherwise or explicit summation signs are used.

Model with mapped velocity coordinates

We now map the physical velocity coordinates
(v_{\parallel},\mu)
from a new pair of computational coordinates
(\eta,\kappa)
:

\displaystyle v_{\parallel}=v_{\parallel}(\eta)\qquad\mu=\mu(\kappa)

(3)

These transformations can be provided by the user, meeting the specific requirements of their application, and can vary between species. But such mappings must be continuous, monotonically increasing, and univariate. The latter entails that these mappings allow contraction and dilation of each velocity dimension independently, which, while somewhat restrictive, is sufficiently useful in numerous applications. A similar approach was presented for a 1D1V finite difference Vlasov solver
[
33
]
.

When employing the new velocity variables
(\eta,\kappa)
, the gyrokinetic equation
2
becomes

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}f_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\mathcal{J}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}f_{s}+\frac{1}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\mathcal{J}\dot{v_{\parallel}}f_{s}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{C}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{D}_{s},

(4)

where
v_{\parallel}^{\prime}=\partial_{\eta}v_{\parallel}
and
\mu^{\prime}=\partial_{\kappa}\mu
. The advection speeds,
\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}
and
\dot{v_{\parallel}}
, are now computed using (see equation
100
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{B^{*i}}{m_{s}Bv_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial\eta}+\frac{1}{q_{s}B}\frac{\epsilon^{ijk}}{J_{x}}b_{j}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial x^{k}},\qquad\dot{v_{\parallel}}=-\frac{B^{*i}}{m_{s}B}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial x^{i}},

(5)

employing the particle Hamiltonian
H_{s}=m_{s}v_{\parallel}^{2}/2+\mu B+q_{s}\phi
, the Levi-Civitta symbol
\epsilon^{ijk}
, the contravariant components of
\mathbf{B^{*}}
(
B^{*i}
, equation
99
), and the covariant components of
\mathbf{\hat{b}}
(
b_{j}
, equation
98
). We also need the potential
\phi
appearing in the Hamiltonian, which results from solving the quasineutrality equation (see equation
109
)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}g^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\phi=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s},\qquad i,j\in\{1,2\},

(6)

where
\epsilon_{\perp}=\sum_{s}m_{s}n_{s0}/B_{0}^{2}
for some reference density
n_{s0}
and magnetic field magnitude
B_{0}
.

The source and diffusion terms on the right-hand side of equation
4
do not require modification due to the transformation in equation
3
because they do not involve differential operators in
v_{\parallel}
-
\mu
. One only has to be aware that where
v_{\parallel}
-
\mu
appear therein, these must be interpreted as functions of
\eta
-
\kappa
, respectively. The same is true for our ionization, charge-exchange and recombination models in
\mathcal{C}_{s}

[
7
]
. However, the line radiation model and the Dougherty operator for elastic collisions between charged species (equation
89
) must be transformed accordingly. The Dougherty operator is expressed in
\eta
-
\kappa
coordinates as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{el}}=\sum_{r}\nu_{sr}\,\left\{\frac{1}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel sr}\right)+\frac{v_{t,sr}^{2}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\right]+\frac{1}{\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}2\mu\left(1+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\right)\right\}f_{s},

(7)

and the line radiation model is described in a separate publication
[
56
]
.

3
Algorithm

In this section we develop a conservative discontinuous Galerkin (DG) algorithm to discretize the long-wavelength gyrokinetic model presented in section
2
.
Of particular interest is a modal implementation of DG
[
3
,
31
,
46
]
, using relatively sparse orthonormalized basis functions that allow analytic evaluation of integrals, thus obviating the need for numerical quadratures and producing an alias-free method. We introduce the tessellation (mesh)
\mathcal{T}
with cells
K_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
labeled by
j=1,\dots,N
, with
N=N_{v_{\parallel}}N_{\mu}\prod_{i=1}^{d_{x}}N_{i}
meaning the number of cells in a mesh with
(N_{i},N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu})
cells along
(x^{i},\eta,\kappa)
, respectively. In each cell we define a set of logical coordinates
(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda)\in[-1,1]^{(d_{x}+2)}
which are related to the computational coordinates
(\mathbf{x},\eta,\kappa)
via

\displaystyle x^{i}=x^{i}_{j}+\frac{\Delta x^{i}}{2}\chi^{i},\qquad\eta=\eta_{j}+\frac{\Delta\eta}{2}\zeta,\qquad\kappa=\kappa_{j}+\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\lambda,

(8)

where
(\mathbf{x}_{j},\eta_{j},\kappa_{j})
is the cell center of cell
K_{j}
and
(\Delta\mathbf{x},\Delta\eta,\Delta\kappa)
are its cell lengths along each direction. We also introduce a hybrid space
\mathcal{H}_{j}^{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda)
of polynomials
\psi^{(k)}_{j}
in the set of
(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda)
variables, of order up to
p
in
(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\lambda)
coordinates and up to
\mathrm{max}(p,2)
in
\zeta
, with cardinality
\left|\mathcal{H}_{j}^{p}\right|=N_{b}
. Such polynomials have compact support
\mathrm{supp}(\psi^{(k)}_{j}(\mathbf{x},v_{\parallel},\mu))=K_{j}
restricted to each cell (i.e.
\psi^{(k)}_{j}=0
outside
K_{j}
), and are orthogonal and normalized such that for
\psi^{(\ell)}_{m}\in\mathcal{H}^{p}_{m}
one has

\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(k)}_{j}\psi^{(\ell)}_{m}=\delta_{jm}\delta_{k\ell}.

(9)

For example, in one position (
d_{x}=1
) and two velocity dimensions (1D2V) the
p=1
set of polynomials is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\equiv\frac{1}{2^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\left\{1,\sqrt{3}\chi^{1},\sqrt{3}\zeta,\sqrt{3}\lambda,3\chi^{1}\zeta,3\chi^{1}\lambda,3\zeta\lambda,3^{3/2}\chi^{1}\zeta\lambda,3\sqrt{5}\left(\zeta^{2}-1/3\right),\right.

(10)

\displaystyle\left.~3^{1/2}\sqrt{5}\chi^{1}\left(\zeta^{2}-1/3\right),3^{3/2}\sqrt{5}\left(\zeta^{2}-1/3\right)\lambda,9\sqrt{5}\chi^{1}\left(\zeta^{2}-1/3\right)\lambda\right\}.

We then use these polynomials as a basis set to represent our distribution function
f
(we drop the species index
s
for simplicity) in cell
K_{j}
as

\displaystyle f_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}f_{j}^{(k)}\psi^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda).

(11)

Note the compact support of
\psi^{(k)}_{j}
allows us to write the distribution using a global expansion as well:

\displaystyle f=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}f_{j}^{(k)}\psi^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda).

(12)

We also introduce a DG basis set (
\varphi^{(k)}_{j}
) using elements of space
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{p}(q^{i})
of polynomials of order
p
in
q^{i}
variables, whose compact support is cell
K_{j}
. The dimensionality of
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{p}(q^{i})
is the number
q^{i}
variables, and its cardinality is denoted with
\left|\mathcal{V}_{j}^{p}(q^{i})\right|=N_{b,q^{i}}
. Note that if the set
\{q^{i}\}
contains a single variable (i.e. a 1D space), then
N_{b,q^{1}}=p+1
. Let us then use the basis sets
\varphi^{(k)}_{j}(\zeta)\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{p}(\zeta)
and
\varphi^{(k)}_{j}(\lambda)\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{p}(\lambda)
to represent the velocity transformations in equation
3
as:

\displaystyle v_{\parallel j}=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}v_{\parallel j}^{(k)}\varphi^{(k)}_{j}(\zeta),\qquad\mu_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}\mu_{j}^{(k)}\varphi^{(k)}_{j}(\lambda).

(13)

Although these formulas employ a DG basis, the expansion coefficients
v_{\parallel j}^{(k)}
and
\mu_{j}^{(k)}
must be computed such that the representations in equations
13
are
C^{0}
continuous. This continuous discretizations are accomplished by evaluating the
v_{\parallel}(\eta)
and
\mu(\kappa)
maps at Gauss-Lobatto nodes and performing a nodal-to-modal transformation in each cell.

Unlike
v_{\parallel}
, we represent the squared of the parallel velocity using a
p\geq 2
basis,
\vartheta^{(k)}_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{\max(p,2)}(\zeta)
:

v_{\parallel j}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\max(p,2)+1}v_{\parallel j}^{2,(k)}\vartheta^{(k)}_{j}(\zeta).

(14)

Once the discrete representation of
v_{\parallel}
is established, i.e. the
v_{\parallel j}^{(k)}
coefficients in equation
13
are known, one can compute the coefficients of
v_{\parallel j}^{2}
simply by weak multiplication with the
\vartheta^{(k)}_{j}
basis:

v_{\parallel j}^{2,(k)}=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\vartheta^{(k)}_{j}v_{\parallel j}\,v_{\parallel j}.

(15)

Our use of a
p=1
hybrid basis in phase space, i.e. a basis that is linear in the
\mathbf{x}
and
\mu
dimensions but quadratic in
v_{\parallel}
in equations
10
-
12
, and a quadratic basis for
v_{\parallel}^{2}
helps us avoid some of the complexities needed to conserve energy exactly in the discrete collision operator arising from the use of a purely linear basis
[
24
,
30
]
.

Note that for
p=1
the use of a piecewise linear representation of
v_{\parallel}(\eta)
and
\mu(\kappa)
(equation
13
) means that the rate of change in
v_{\parallel}
and
\mu
with respect to their computational coordinates (
v_{\parallel}^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}
), appearing in equations
4
-
7
, are cellwise constants:

\displaystyle v_{\parallel j}^{\prime}=\frac{\partial v_{\parallel j}}{\partial\eta}=\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial v_{\parallel j}}{\partial\zeta}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\Delta\eta}v_{\parallel j}^{(1)},\qquad\mu^{\prime}_{j}=\frac{\partial\mu_{j}}{\partial\kappa}=\frac{2}{\Delta\kappa}\frac{\partial\mu_{j}}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\Delta\kappa}\mu_{j}^{(1)}.

(16)

In principle one can use a
p\geq 2
basis to represent
v_{\parallel}(\eta)
and
\mu(\kappa)
, but in that case
v_{\parallel}^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}
are no longer cellwise constants and the complexity and cost of the algorithm grows. Furthermore, while it may seem that the
p=1
choice for the velocity maps limits our accuracy, we believe that accuracy is dominated by the basis order used to represent
f_{s}
. However we did not quantify such statement and left this task for future work.

Given the definitions thus far, we can proceed to discretize the collisionless and collisional terms in our full-
f
gyrokinetic equation
4
.

3.1
Dicrete collisionless terms

In this section we discretize the collisionless terms in equation
4
and thus temporarily set its right-hand side to zero. The first step is to name the Jacobian of the transformation in equation
3
:

J_{v}=v_{\parallel}^{\prime}\mu^{\prime},

(17)

define a “total” Jacobian

J_{T}=J_{x}J_{v}\mathcal{J},

(18)

and introduce a scaled distribution

F=J_{T}f,

(19)

whose discrete representation in cell
K_{j}
is obtained via weak multiplication:

F_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}F_{j}^{(k)}\psi^{(k)}_{j},\qquad F_{j}^{(k)}=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(k)}_{j}J_{T,j}f_{j}.

(20)

Next, we multiply the (collisionless, sourceless and diffusionless) gyrokinetic equation by
J_{x}J_{v}
to obtain:

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\dot{R}^{i}F+\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\dot{v_{\parallel}}\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}=0,

(21)

where we used the notation
\dot{R}^{i}=\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}
. This way the gyrokinetic equation in field-aligned and mapped velocity coordinates appears in conservative form. The discrete form arises from projecting it onto the phase-space basis
\psi^{(k)}_{j}
in cell
K_{j}
and integrating by parts:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}+\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\left(\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial x^{i}}\dot{R}^{i}+\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\eta}\frac{\dot{v_{\parallel}}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\right)F=0.

(22)

where
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}

is a differential surface element perpendicular to the
i
-th direction (pointing outward from cell
K_{j}
). The superscripts
\pm
denote a quantity at the upper (
+
) or lower (
-
) surface perpendicular to
x^{i}
(in the second term) or to
v_{\parallel}
(in the third term). The quantities
\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}
and
\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}
represent numerical fluxes that depend on the values of their respective arguments on either side of a cell boundary; for example:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{+}=\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{+}\left(\dot{R}^{i+}_{j},F_{j}^{+},F_{j+1}^{-}\right),\qquad\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{+}=\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{+}\left(\dot{v_{\parallel}}^{+}_{j},v_{\parallel}^{\prime+},v_{\parallel}^{\prime-},F_{j}^{+},F_{j+1}^{-}\right).

(23)

These numerical fluxes are constructed such that they are consistent
[
43
]
, conserve total number of particles and total energy, and avoid the evaluation of geometric quantities at cell corners (to avoid geometric singularities such as the magnetic axis or the X-point of a diverted tokamak). Use the relationship between computational and logical coordinates, equation
8
, to rewrite equation
22
in terms of
(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda)
:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}

(24)

\displaystyle-\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\left(\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\dot{R}^{i}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{\dot{v_{\parallel}}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\right)F=0.

We can employ the orthonormality relation, equation
9
, and substitute the expansion of
F
, equation
20
, in the first term to make it explicit that this equation provides a temporal evolution for each expansion coefficient of
F
:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{j}^{(\ell)}}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}-\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\left(\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\dot{R}^{i}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{\dot{v_{\parallel}}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\right)F=0.

(25)

Two ingredients remain in order to fully prescribe the discretization of collisionless terms: we must construct the discrete advection speeds
\dot{R}^{i}
and
\dot{v_{\parallel}}
used in the volume term (i.e. last term in equation
25
), and we must define the numerical fluxes
\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}
and
\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}
employed in the surface terms (i.e. the second and third terms in equation
25
).

The discrete advection speeds
\dot{R}^{i}
and
\dot{v_{\parallel}}
for the volume term of equation
25
are simply calculated by projecting their definition (equation
5
) onto the phase-space basis
\psi^{(k)}_{j}
, using the discrete Hamiltonian in cell
K_{j}

H_{j}=\frac{1}{2}mv_{\parallel j}^{2}+\mu_{j}B_{j}+q\mathcal{P}(\phi)_{j},

(26)

where
\mathcal{P}(A)
is an operator which makes
A

C^{0}
-continuous along
x^{3}
; the continuity of
\phi
is necessary as particle potential energy should be single-valued at cell boundaries, and the continuity at
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
cell boundaries will be enforced by the finite element (FE) solution of the quasineutrality equation
6
. This continuity requirement is analogous to how DG Vlasov-Poisson solvers need a
\phi
continuous in all directions
[
29
]
, a property guaranteed by a FE solution of a
d_{x}
-dimensional Poisson equation. In our case, the (
d_{x}
-
1
)-dimensional quasineutrality equation returns a
\phi
that is continuous only in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
, thus needing an additional operation to enforce continuity in
x^{3}
.

For the purposes of the volume terms, the discrete advection speeds in cell
K_{j}
are

\displaystyle\dot{R}^{i}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}\dot{R}_{j}^{i,(k)}\psi^{(k)}_{j},\qquad\dot{v_{\parallel}}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}\dot{v_{\parallel}}_{j}^{(k)}\psi^{(k)}_{j},

(27)

whose expansion coefficients, using equations
5
,
97
and
99
, and the fact that
v_{\parallel}=(\partial_{\eta}H_{j})/(mv_{\parallel}^{\prime})
, are:

\displaystyle B^{*i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{B^{*}}=B^{3}\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}},

(28)

\displaystyle\dot{R}^{i,(k)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(k)}_{j}\left[\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\frac{\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)v_{\parallel}+\frac{\epsilon^{i\ell p}}{q_{s}}\frac{b_{\ell}}{J_{x}B}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{p}}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial\chi^{p}}\right],

\displaystyle\dot{v_{\parallel}}^{(k)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(k)}_{j}\left[-\frac{1}{m_{s}}\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\frac{\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial H_{j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\right],

where
\delta^{i}_{j}
is the Kronecker delta. We can compute these integrals analytically with computer algebra if we use a discrete (polynomial) representation of the geometric quantities
g_{33}^{-1/2}
and
\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}})/B
. These geometric quantities are discretized by evaluating them at Gauss-Legendre nodes interior to a cell
\mathbf{c}_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}{},\zeta,\lambda)
, and effecting a nodal-to-modal transformation. The choice of Gauss-Legendre nodes is primarily motivated by our desire to avoid evaluation at cell corners, where a magnetic X- or O-point may be located. For this purpose we introduce a nodal basis
\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}{},\zeta,\lambda)
local to cell
K_{j}
(
\mathrm{supp}(\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda))=K_{j}
) that takes a value of 1 at the
k
-th node
\mathbf{c}_{k}
and 0 at the other nodes. Such nodal basis can be used to represent these geometric coefficients in terms of their nodal values; for example

\displaystyle\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\right)_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b,{\scriptsize\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}}}\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\right)_{j}(\mathbf{c}_{k})~\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda),\qquad\text{nodal form},

(29)

where the expansion coefficients are simply
g_{33}^{-1/2}
evaluated at the
k
-th Gauss-Legendre node
\mathbf{c}_{k}
in the
j
-th cell. Equivalently, one can write its modal representation:

\displaystyle\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\right)_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b,{\scriptsize\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}}}\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\right)^{(k)}_{j}~\varphi^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda),\qquad\text{modal form},

(30)

whose DG expansion coefficients result from projecting the nodal form in equation
29
onto the modal basis:

\displaystyle\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\right)^{(k)}_{j}=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\varphi^{(k)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda)~\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{b,{\scriptsize\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}}}\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\right)_{j}(\mathbf{c}_{\ell})~\Lambda^{(\ell)}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda).

(31)

A similar treatment is used to discretize
\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}})/B
, allowing us to perform the integrals in equation
28
analytically, once, with computer algebra, avoiding numerical quadratures and aliasing errors
[
31
]
.

We turn to the calculation of the surface terms of equation
25
. Recall that we wish the numerical fluxes
\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}
and
\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}
to:
a)
be consistent,
b)
conserve particles and energy, and
c)
avoid evaluation of geometric quantities at cell corners. Let us then introduce a space of polynomials
\mathcal{H}^{p}_{j}(\backslash\{z\})
with cardinality
\left|\mathcal{H}_{j}^{p}(\backslash\{z\})\right|=N_{b\backslash\{z\}}
which is a subset of
\mathcal{H}^{p}_{j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$},\zeta,\lambda)
but is independent of
z
, and use it to form the basis set
\xi^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{z\})\in\mathcal{H}^{p}_{j}(\backslash\{z\})
which can represent quantities on the surface (of a cell) perpendicular to
z
. The numerical fluxes on a cell surface are then:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}}}\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm,(k)}\xi^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}),\qquad\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b\backslash\{\zeta\}}}\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm,(k)}\xi^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\zeta\}),\qquad\text{modal form}.

(32)

In order to compute the DG coefficients in these expansions, we evaluate the fluxes at the set of
N_{b\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}}
or
N_{b\backslash\{\zeta\}}
Gauss-Legendre nodes,
\mathbf{g}_{k}(\backslash\{\chi^{i}\})
or
\mathbf{h}_{k}(\backslash\{\zeta\})
, on the cell surface perpendicular to
x^{i}
or
v_{\parallel}
, respectively. These evaluations, denoted
\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{g}_{k})
and
\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})
, can be used to represent the numerical fluxes as an expansion in
a nodal basis
\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{z\})
local to cell
K_{j}
and independent of the variable
z
(e.g. at a
v_{\parallel}
surface
\mathrm{supp}(\Lambda^{(m)}_{k}(\backslash\{\zeta\}))=\left[\mathbf{x}_{k-1/2},\mathbf{x}_{k+1/2}\right]\times\left[\kappa_{k-1/2},\kappa_{k+1/2}\right]
):

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}}}\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{g}_{k})~\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}),\qquad\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b\backslash\{\zeta\}}}\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})~\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\zeta\}),\qquad\text{nodal form}.

(33)

If we have the nodal value of the surface fluxes (coefficients in equation
33
), we can obtain the modal expansion coefficients in equation
32
simply by projecting the nodal representation onto the modal basis:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm,(k)}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\xi^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\chi^{i}\})\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}}}\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{g}_{k})~\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\chi^{i}\}),

(34)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm,(k)}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\xi^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\zeta\})\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b\backslash\{\zeta\}}}\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})~\Lambda^{(k)}_{j}(\backslash\{\zeta\}).

Now we simply need to evaluate the numerical fluxes at Gauss-Legendre nodes on cell surfaces, and we can do so in a Lax-Friedrichs fashion
[
43
]
:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{x^{i},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{g}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{R}^{i\pm}_{j}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\left(F_{j\pm 1}^{\mp}(\mathbf{g}_{k})+F_{j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\right)\mp\frac{1}{2}\left|\dot{R}^{i\pm}_{j}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\right|\left(F_{j\pm 1}^{\mp}(\mathbf{g}_{k})-F_{j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\right),

(35)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{v_{\parallel},j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{v}_{\parallel j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})\left(\frac{F_{j\pm 1}^{\mp}(\mathbf{h}_{k})}{v_{\parallel j\pm 1}^{\prime}}+\frac{F_{j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})}{v_{\parallel j}^{\prime}}\right)\mp\frac{1}{2}\left|\dot{v}_{\parallel j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})\right|\left(\frac{F_{j\pm 1}^{\mp}(\mathbf{h}_{k})}{v_{\parallel j\pm 1}^{\prime}}-\frac{F_{j}^{\pm}(\mathbf{h}_{k})}{v_{\parallel j}^{\prime}}\right).

Lax-Friedrichs fluxes inherently upwind the discontinuous distribution function
F
, introducing some numerical diffusion proportional to the discontinuity at cell boundaries, and helping avoid spurious oscillations. The last ingredient for the calculation of the surface terms in equation
25
is the evaluation of the advection speeds at cell-surface Gauss-Legendre nodes appearing in equation
35
. This simply involves evaluation of the advection speeds in equation
5
at their respective nodes. After using equations
97
and
99
, and the fact that
v_{\parallel}=(\partial_{\eta}H_{j})/(mv_{\parallel})
, these values are:

\displaystyle\dot{R}^{i\pm}_{j}(\mathbf{g}_{k})

\displaystyle=\left[g_{33}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}(\mathbf{g}_{k})}{q_{s}}\left(\frac{\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)(\mathbf{g}_{k})\right]v_{\parallel}(\mathbf{g}_{k})+\frac{\epsilon^{i\ell p}}{q_{s}}\left(\frac{b_{\ell}}{J_{x}B}\right)(\mathbf{g}_{k})~\frac{2}{\Delta x^{p}}\left(\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial\chi^{p}}\right)(\mathbf{g}_{k}),

(36)

\displaystyle\dot{v_{\parallel}}^{\pm}_{j}(\mathbf{h}_{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m_{s}}\left[g_{33}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{h}_{k})\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel j}^{\pm}}{q_{s}}\left(\frac{\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)(\mathbf{h}_{k})\right]\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\left(\frac{\partial H_{j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\right)(\mathbf{h}_{k}).

Equations
26
-
36
provide the means to compute the surface and volume collisionless terms of equation
25
. This recipe, and its consistency and conservative properties, relies on the Halmitonian in equation
26
being
C^{0}
continuous. Continuity of the Hamiltonian, as well as single-valued nodal geometric quantities appearing in the surface advection speeds (equation
36
) ensure that the advection speeds, and the Lax-Friedrichs fluxes in equation
35
, are
C^{0}
continuous, thus ensuring that the same number particles that leave a cell enter its adjacent cell. The continuity of the Hamiltonian is ensured by using a continuous representation of
v_{\parallel}(\eta)
and
v_{\parallel}(\kappa)
(see equation
13
). It is also necessary to use a continuous representation of the magnetic field amplitude
B_{j}
, which we construct by evaluating
B
at Gauss-Lobatto nodes and performing a nodal-to-modal transformation in each cell. Lastly, we must guarantee that the electrostatic potential
\phi
is also continuous, as described next.

3.1.1
Discrete quasineutrality equation

Following past work on DG methods for Hamiltonian systems with continuous stream functions or potentials
[
44
,
61
,
46
]
, we solve equation
6
such that
\phi
is continuous. We start by multiplying the quasineutrality equation by
J_{x}

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\varepsilon^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\phi=\mathcal{P}\left(\varrho\right)=\overline{\varrho},\qquad i,j\in\{1,2\},

(37)

and employing the notation
\varepsilon^{ij}=J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}g^{ij}
for the polarization density weight including geometric factors, and
\varrho=J_{x}\rho=J_{x}\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}
for the charge density including the position-space Jacobian. We have also introduced the continuous-along-
x^{3}
charge density,
\overline{\varrho}
, produced by the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
acting on
\varrho
. We use
\overline{\varrho}
instead of
\varrho
because
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
appeared in the discrete Hamiltonian (equation
26
) in order to have continuous and conservative numerical fluxes through cell surfaces perpendicular to
x^{3}
, and self-adjointness will allow the same operation on
\rho
.

The projection operator
\mathcal{P}\left(g\right)=\bar{g}
takes a DG scalar field
g
and produces a field
\bar{g}
which is continuous in
x^{3}
. There are various ways to define the
\mathcal{P}
operator, and we here choose to use a projection onto a FE basis that is continuous only in the
x^{3}
direction. That is, introduce the space
\mathcal{F}^{p}_{i,j,\Omega_{x^{3}}}\equiv\left\{u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}(\chi^{1},\chi^{2},x^{3})~|~\mathrm{supp}_{x^{1},x^{2}}(u^{(\ell)}_{i,j})=E_{i,j},~C^{0}(x^{3})\right\}
of polynomials
u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}
with compact support along
x^{1}
and
x^{2}
limited to the cell
E_{i,j}\equiv\left[x^{1}_{i-1/2},x^{1}_{i+1/2}\right]\times\left[x^{2}_{j-1/2},x^{1}_{j+1/2}\right]
(here
i\in[1,N_{x}]
and
j\in[1,N_{y}]
), yet continuous and nonlocal (i.e. they span more than 1 cell) along
x^{3}
. We denote the cardinality of this vector space as
\left|\mathcal{F}^{p}_{i,j,\Omega_{x^{3}}}\right|=N_{b,x^{1}x^{2}\overline{x^{3}}}
. We can use these polynomials as a basis set in which to expand a continuous-in-
x^{3}
scalar field
g
in cell
E_{i,j}
and along
x^{3}
:

\bar{g}_{i,j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{b,x^{1}x^{2}\overline{x^{3}}}}\bar{g}_{i,j}^{(\ell)}u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}(\chi^{1},\chi^{2},x^{3}).

(38)

Simply put, this quantity is discontinuous in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
and continuous in
x^{3}
. Our projection operator
\mathcal{P}
that computes this field is thus a projection of the discontinuous field
g
onto the
u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}
basis

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{i,j}=\mathcal{P}(g_{i,j})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\int_{\Omega_{x^{3}}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}\bar{g}_{i,j}=\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\int_{\Omega_{x^{3}}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}g_{i,j}.

(39)

As it is typically done in a FE method, we can split the global integral over
\Omega_{x^{3}}
into local contributions from each of the
N_{z}
cells of
\Omega_{x^{3}}
, employing the restriction of
u^{(\ell)}_{i,j}
to cell
\left[x^{3}_{k-1/2},x^{3}_{k+1/2}\right]
for
k\in[1,N_{z}]
, which we denote
u^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$})
. The discrete form of the projection
\mathcal{P}
operator is thus

\displaystyle\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{z}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~u^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}\bar{g}_{i,j,k}^{(m)}u^{(m)}_{i,j,k}=\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{z}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~u^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}g_{i,j,k}^{(m)}\varphi^{(m)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}).

(40)

where we inserted the DG representation of
g
employing the set of
N_{b,\mathbf{x}}
piecewise discontinuous polynomials
\varphi^{(m)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$})\in\mathcal{V}_{j}^{p}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$})
. Equation
40
can be written in terms of logical coordinates (see equation
8
) as:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\chi^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{z}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{3}~u^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}\bar{g}_{i,j,k}^{(m)}u^{(m)}_{i,j,k}=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\chi^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{z}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{3}~u^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}g_{i,j,k}^{(m)}\varphi^{(m)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}).

(41)

This FE projection along
x^{3}
results in a linear system of equations that can be written in matrix form
Ax=b
and solved with standard direct or iterative methods. Since these systems are small (e.g.
A
is sparse and
\left(N_{b,\mathbf{x}}N_{z}\right)^{2}
elements large), we typically employ a direct sparse solver. Lastly we note that the adjoint of this operator
\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}
, defined by

\displaystyle\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\int_{\Omega_{x^{3}}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~f\mathcal{P}(g)=\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\int_{\Omega_{x^{3}}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}(f)g,

(42)

can be shown to be equal to the operator
\mathcal{P}
itself, i.e.
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}
is self-adjoint. Note that because
\mathcal{P}
is local in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
, we can promote this self-adjointness relationship to a global one by summing over all cells in
x^{1}
and
x^{2}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\int_{\Omega_{x^{3}}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~f\mathcal{P}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\int_{E_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\int_{\Omega_{x^{3}}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}~\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}(f)g,

(43)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}~f\mathcal{P}(g)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}~\mathcal{P}^{\dagger}(f)g.

We can now use the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
to compute a continuous-in-
x^{3}
charge density (
\overline{\varrho}=\mathcal{P}\left(J_{x}\rho\right)
) to solve the quasineutrality equation
37
. We use a discontinuous representation of the geometric quantity
J_{x}g^{ij}
, constructed by evaluating it at Gauss-Legendre nodes interior to a cell and effecting a nodal-to-modal transformation. This approach avoids evaluation of geometric quantities that may blow up at a magnetic O- or X-point (which our meshing ensures only exist at cell corners). Lastly, we will solve the quasineutrality equation locally in
x^{3}
and globally in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
, for which we introduce the function space
\mathcal{G}_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}},k}^{p}\equiv\left\{w^{(m)}_{k}(x^{1},x^{2},\chi^{3})~|~\mathrm{supp}_{x^{3}}(w^{(m)}_{k})=E_{k},~C^{0}(x^{1},x^{2})\right\}
with cardinality
\left|\mathcal{G}_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}},k}^{p}\right|=N_{b,\overline{x^{1}x^{2}}x^{3}}
of polynomials
w^{(m)}_{k}
with compact support along
x^{3}
limited to the
k
-th cell
E_{k}\equiv\left[x^{3}_{k-1/2},x^{3}_{k+1/2}\right]
(where
k\in\left[1,N_{z}\right]
). These polynomials are continuous (
C^{0}(x^{1},x^{2})
) and nonlocal along
x^{1}
and
x^{2}
. We can project the quasineutrality equation onto
w^{(m)}_{k}
in each cell along
x^{3}
:

\displaystyle-\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,w^{(m)}_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{p}}\varepsilon_{k}^{pq}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{q}}\phi_{k}=\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,w^{(m)}_{k}\overline{\varrho}_{k},\qquad p,q\in\{1,2\}

(44)

which after integration by parts becomes

\displaystyle

\displaystyle-\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{\Omega_{x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,w^{(m)}_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{1q}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{q}}\Bigg|_{x^{1}=x^{1}_{\min}}^{x^{1}=x^{1}_{\max}}-\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{\Omega_{x^{1}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,w^{(m)}_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{2q}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{q}}\Bigg|_{x^{2}=x^{2}_{\min}}^{x^{2}=x^{2}_{\max}}

(45)

\displaystyle+\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\frac{\partial w^{(m)}_{k}}{\partial x^{p}}\varepsilon_{k}^{pq}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{q}}=\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,w^{(m)}_{k}\overline{\varrho}_{k},\qquad i,j\in\{p,q\}

As done for the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
, we can write this global (in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
) weak form in terms of local contributions from each cell, using the restriction of the basis function
w^{(m)}(x^{1},x^{2},\chi^{3})
to cell
E_{i,j,k}
denoted as
w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$})
. Then the weak quasineutrality equation
45
becomes

\displaystyle

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{x^{2}_{j-1/2}}^{x^{2}_{j+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{2}~w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}\varepsilon_{i,j,k}^{1q}\frac{\partial\phi_{i,j,k}}{\partial x^{q}}\Bigg|_{x^{1}=x^{1}_{\min}}^{x^{1}=x^{1}_{\max}}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{x^{1}_{i-1/2}}^{x^{1}_{i+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}~w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}\varepsilon_{i,j,k}^{2q}\frac{\partial\phi_{i,j,k}}{\partial x^{q}}\Bigg|_{x^{2}=x^{2}_{\min}}^{x^{2}=x^{2}_{\max}}

(46)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{x},N_{y}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{x^{1}_{i-1/2}}^{x^{1}_{i+1/2}}\int_{x^{2}_{j-1/2}}^{x^{2}_{j+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}~\frac{\partial w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}}{\partial x^{p}}\varepsilon_{i,j,k}^{pq}\frac{\partial\phi_{i,j,k}}{\partial x^{q}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{x},N_{y}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\int_{x^{1}_{i-1/2}}^{x^{1}_{i+1/2}}\int_{x^{2}_{j-1/2}}^{x^{2}_{j+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}~w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}\overline{\varrho}_{i,j,k}.

Furthermore, we effect the computational-to-logical coordinate transformation (equation
8
) to obtain

\displaystyle

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\Delta x^{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{y}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{3}\mathrm{d}\chi^{2}~w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}\varepsilon_{i,j,k}^{1q}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{j}}\frac{\partial\phi_{i,j,k}}{\partial\chi^{q}}\Bigg|_{i=1,~\chi^{1}=-1}^{i=N_{x},~\chi^{1}=1}-\frac{2}{\Delta x^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{3}\mathrm{d}\chi^{1}~w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}\varepsilon_{i,j,k}^{2q}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{q}}\frac{\partial\phi_{i,j,k}}{\partial\chi^{j}}\Bigg|_{j=1,~\chi^{2}=-1}^{j=N_{y},~\chi^{2}=1}

(47)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{x},N_{y}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}~\frac{\partial w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}}{\partial x^{i}}\varepsilon_{i,j,k}^{ij}\frac{\partial\phi_{i,j,k}}{\partial x^{j}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{x},N_{y}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}~w^{(m)}_{i,j,k}\overline{\varrho}_{i,j,k}.

In order to use a typical Galerkin FE method we can represent
\varepsilon^{ij}
and
\overline{\varrho}
in terms of the local restrictions of the FE basis,
w^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}
, such as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{pq}_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}\hat{\varepsilon}^{pq,(\ell)}_{i,j,k}w^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}),\qquad\overline{\varrho}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}\hat{\overline{\varrho}}^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}w^{(\ell)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}),

(48)

whose expansion coefficients are obtained by evaluating their DG representation at the FE nodes. After inserting these and the FE representation of
\phi

\phi_{i,j,k}=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}\hat{\phi}_{i,j,k}^{(n)}w^{(n)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}),

(49)

into equation
47
, a discrete system of equation ensues. We note a subtlety in the last two equations that arises from mixing DG and FE representations: while
\phi
evaluated at the same node using equation
49
from two adjacent cells give equal values, doing so for
\varrho
using equation
48
from two adjacent cells give different contributions to the right-hand side vector of the ensuing matrix form
Ax=b
because
\varrho
is discontinuous along
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
. Finally, the construction of the discrete FE problem is completed by incorporating boundary conditions. Typically we use periodic, Dirichlet (
\phi(x^{i}=x^{i}_{\min,\max})=\phi_{b}
) or Neumann

(\varepsilon^{iq}\partial_{x^{q}}\phi)(x^{i}=x^{i}_{\min,\max})=\phi^{\prime}_{b}

boundary conditions (where
\phi_{b}
and
\phi^{\prime}_{b}
are externally provided values). The final linear system of equations can be solved with standard methods. These linear problems are global in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
and local in
x^{3}
, with a sparse matrix
A
of approximately
N_{b,\overline{x^{1}x^{2}}x^{3}}^{2}
elements, and can be solved cheaply (compared to the 5D gyrokinetic update) with a direct solver.

The solution to equation
47
yields an FE representation of
\phi
in each cell. We can reconstruct the modal representation within a cell by performing a nodal-to-modal transformation exactly using our computer algebra implementation, minimizing aliasing errors. An equivalent way to compute the modal DG coefficients in the
(i,j,k)
-th cell is to perform projections of the nodal representation onto the corresponding modal DG basis. For example, the modal DG coefficients in the
(i,j,k)
cell would be

\displaystyle\phi_{i,j,k}^{(m)}=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\varphi^{(m)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$})\sum_{n=1}^{N_{b,\mathbf{x}}}\hat{\phi}_{i,j,k}^{(n)}w^{(n)}_{i,j,k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}).

(50)

The procedure thus far produces a potential
\phi
that is continuous in
x^{1}
-
x^{2}
, but since it was carried out independently in each
x^{3}
cell it is discontinuous in that direction. Hence we finalize the solution of the quasineutrality equation by applying the parallel FE projection operator to the potential:
\mathcal{P}(\phi)
. The final outcome is a potential that is continuous in all three directions, allowing us to construct a continuous Hamiltonian, advection speeds and fluxes.

3.1.2
Time integration stability constraint

The phase-space discretization of the collisionless terms in section
3.1
is paired with a suitable time integration scheme. We employ an explicit strong stability preserving (SSP) third-order Runge-Kutta scheme
[
28
]
. The size of the time step
\Delta t
is calculated every time step according to the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability constraint

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}\Delta t=f_{\mathrm{CFL}}\sim 1,

(51)

where
f_{\mathrm{CFL}}
is some factor typically set to 1 and
\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}
is the largest frequency produced by our gyrokinetic system
[
21
]
. We use a conservative estimate of this frequency by adding advection speeds divided by cell lengths in each direction:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}=\max_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{(2p+1)\,\dot{R}^{i}}{\Delta x^{i}}+\frac{(2\max(p,2)+1)\,\dot{v_{\parallel}}}{\Delta v_{\parallel}}\right).

(52)

The
p
-dependent factors account for the higher-order discretization within each cell, and the
\max_{\Omega}
operation is done over the entire phase-space domain. However, a technical detail is that
\Delta v_{\parallel}
does not actually appear in our equations or in their implementation given the velocity map in equation
3
, and that two versions of the advection speeds are computed, one for the surface and one for the volume terms in equation
25
. We choose to compute a CFL frequency using a sum of the maxima of the advection speeds at Gauss-Legendre nodes on each cell-surface:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{(2p+1)}{\Delta x^{i}}\left[\max\left(\left|\dot{R}^{i-}_{j}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\right|\right)+\max\left(\left|\dot{R}^{i+}_{j}(\mathbf{g}_{k})\right|\right)\right]+\frac{(2\max(p,2)+1)}{\Delta\eta}\left[\frac{\max\left(\left|\dot{v_{\parallel}}^{-}_{j}(\mathbf{h}_{k})\right|\right)}{\min\left(v_{\parallel j-1}^{\prime},v_{\parallel j}^{\prime}\right)}+\frac{\max\left(\left|\dot{v_{\parallel}}^{+}_{j}(\mathbf{h}_{k})\right|\right)}{\min\left(v_{\parallel j}^{\prime},v_{\parallel j+1}^{\prime}\right)}\right].

(53)

The final CFL frequency is just the maximum of equation
53
over all cells:

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}=\max_{j=1}^{N}\left(\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},j}\right).

(54)

3.2
Discrete collisional terms

The collision terms on the right-hand side of the gyrokinetic equation
4
also need to be adapted to incorporate the velocity maps. For ionization, charge exchange and recombination the changes are trivial as they only require evaluating the
v_{\parallel}
and
\mu
maps
[
7
]
. The radiation operator does involve more substantial changes, presented in a separate publication
[
56
]
. The essence of such changes is captured by the algorithm for the Dougherty operator modeling elastic collisions between charged particles, equation
7
. Multiplying across by
J_{T}
, this operator becomes

\displaystyle J_{T}\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{el}}=\sum_{r}\nu_{sr}\,\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\left(\frac{v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel sr}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}F_{s}+\frac{v_{t,sr}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\frac{F_{s}}{J_{v}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{\prime}}\left(F_{s}+\frac{mv_{t,sr}^{2}J_{v}}{B\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\frac{F_{s}}{J_{v}}\right)\right].

(55)

In order to simplify notation we consider only like-species collisions (
r=s
) and drop the species index; the multi-species case uses previously reported models
[
24
]
, and its algorithm is equal to that presented here. The algorithm for this operator, extending previous works
[
21
,
24
]
, stems from projecting the operator onto our DG basis
\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}
in cell
K_{j}
:

\displaystyle\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}J_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}\nu\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\left(\frac{v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}F+\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\frac{2\mu}{\mu^{\prime}}\left(F+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{B\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right)\right].

(56)

We can use integration by parts to convert this equation into the form

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}J_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}=

(57)

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j\pm}\nu\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)_{\pm}\widehat{\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}}+v_{t}^{2}\frac{J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\eta}\nu\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}+\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right]

\displaystyle+\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j\pm}\nu 2\mu_{\pm}\left(\widehat{\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right)-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\kappa}\nu 2\mu\left(\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right),

and we can integrate the diffusion volume terms by parts a second time to arrive at

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}J_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}=\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\nu\left\{\psi^{(\ell)}_{j\pm}\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)_{\pm}\widehat{\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}}+v_{t}^{2}\frac{J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]-\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\eta}\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right\}

(58)

\displaystyle-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\nu\left[\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\eta}\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\eta^{2}}\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right]

\displaystyle+\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\nu\left[\psi^{(\ell)}_{j\pm}2\mu_{\pm}\left(\widehat{\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right)-\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\kappa}2\mu\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]

\displaystyle-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\nu\left[\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\kappa}2\mu\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}\left(\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\kappa}2\mu\right)\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right].

Following the notation used in equation
22
for the collisionless terms, the
\pm
subscripts refer to a quantity at the upper (
+
) and lower (
-
) surface of a cell perpendicular to
v_{\parallel}
(first term) or to
\mu
(third term). The quantities
\widehat{F/v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}
and
\widehat{F/\mu^{\prime}}
indicate that the quantities
F/v_{\parallel}^{\prime}
and
F/\mu^{\prime}
are to be upwinded according to the sign of their accompanying advection speed. Lastly, the expression
\widetilde{F/J_{v}}
refers to a two-cell recovered representation of
F/J_{v}

[
21
,
30
,
66
]
, needed to produce continuous surface terms that help us guarantee exact conservation.

We can write equation
58
in terms of logical coordinates using equation
8
:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}J_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left\{\psi^{(\ell)}_{j\pm}\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)_{\pm}\widehat{\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}}+v_{t}^{2}\mu^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]-\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right\}

(59)

\displaystyle-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left[\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta}\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}-\left(\frac{2}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}\Delta\eta}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta^{2}}v_{t}^{2}F\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\Delta\kappa}\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\nu\left\{\psi^{(\ell)}_{j\pm}2\mu_{\pm}\left(\widehat{\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}v_{\parallel}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right)-\frac{2}{\Delta\kappa}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}2\mu\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right\}

\displaystyle-\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left[\frac{2}{\Delta\kappa}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}2\mu\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}-\left(\frac{2}{\mu^{\prime}\Delta\kappa}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left(\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}2\mu\right)\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}F\right].

Note that in the second term of each surface term (in curly braces) we have reverted to a derivative of the recovered
F/J_{v}^{\prime}
with respect to
v_{\parallel}
and
\mu
, rather than the computational
\eta
and
\kappa
, respectively. This approach was favored because we opted to perform the recovery of
F/J_{v}^{\prime}
in
v_{\parallel}
- and
\mu
-space at
v_{\parallel}
and
\mu
boundaries, respectively, using a variant of the two-cell recovery previously reported
[
21
,
30
,
66
]
and extended to the case in which cells have different lengths.

The discretization of the Dougherty operator given in equation
59
is accompanied by mixed boundary conditions at the edge of velocity space. We zero out the flux in advection-like discrete terms:

\displaystyle\nu\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)_{\pm}\widehat{\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}}+v_{t}^{2}\mu^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{at}~\eta=\eta_{\min},\,\eta_{\max}

(60)

\displaystyle\nu 2\mu_{\pm}\left(\widehat{\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}v_{\parallel}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{at}~\kappa=\kappa_{\min},\,\kappa_{\max}.

The last surface terms originating from the second integration by parts of the diffusion terms do not use a 2-cell recovery and are evaluated using the
F/J_{v}
in the cell abutting the boundary instead:

\displaystyle\nu\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\nu\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime 2}}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\qquad\text{at}~\eta=\eta_{\min},\,\eta_{\max}

(61)

\displaystyle\nu\frac{2}{\Delta\kappa}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}2\mu\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\nu\frac{2}{\Delta\kappa}\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}2\mu\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}\frac{J_{v}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\qquad\text{at}~\kappa=\kappa_{\min},\,\kappa_{\max}.

The above discretization, and choice of surface terms and boundary conditions, yields an algorithm that conserves particle, momentum and energy density exactly, provided that
u_{\parallel}
and
v_{t}^{2}
satisfy discrete constraints
[
21
,
24
]
.

3.2.1
Time integration stability constraint

The collision operator imposes a restriction on the time step size
\Delta t
, in addition to that from the collisionless terms described in section
3.1.2
. This time the CFL frequency (
\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}
) contains an advection or drag contribution (
\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},\mathrm{drag}}
) and a diffusion contribution (
\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},\mathrm{diff}}
), and the drag portion is evaluated differently compared to the collisionless terms

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},\mathrm{drag},j}=\left|-\frac{2\max(p,2)+1}{\Delta\eta}\nu_{j}\frac{v_{\parallel j}-u_{\parallel j}}{v_{\parallel j}^{\prime}}-\frac{2p+1}{\Delta\kappa}\nu_{j}\frac{2\mu_{j}}{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}\right|_{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}=\zeta=\lambda=0},

(62)

that is, we evaluate the drag at cell centers rather than at cell surface Gauss-Legendre nodes. The diffusion
\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}
is

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},\mathrm{diff},j}=\left|\left[2\frac{\max(p,2)+1}{\Delta\eta}\right]^{2}\nu_{j}\frac{v_{t,j}}{v_{\parallel j}^{\prime 2}}+\left(2\frac{p+1}{\Delta\kappa}\right)^{2}\nu_{j}\frac{m}{B_{j}}\frac{2\mu_{j}v_{t,j}}{\mu_{j}^{\prime 2}}\right|_{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}=\zeta=\lambda=0}.

(63)

The final time step constraint due to the Dougherty operator is then

\omega_{\mathrm{CFL}}=\max_{j=1}^{N}\left(\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},\mathrm{drag},j}+\omega_{\mathrm{CFL},\mathrm{diff},j}\right).

(64)

4.2
Collisional terms

The discrete Dougherty operator in Gkeyll with uniform velocity grid has been shown to conserve particles, momentum and energy to machine precision independently of resolution, for both like-species
[

and unlike-species
[

[
24

]

(66)

on a
(\eta,\kappa)\in[-2^{-1/2},2^{-1/2}]\times\left[0,1\right]
computational grid, with a mapping that is quadratic in
\kappa
and linear in
\eta
for
\left|v_{\parallel}\right|\leq v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}/2
and quadratic above that:

\displaystyle v_{\parallel}=\begin{cases}v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}\eta&\quad\left|\eta\right|\leq 1/2,\\
2v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}\,\mathrm{sign}\left(\eta\right)\eta^{2}&\quad\left|\eta\right|>1/2,\end{cases}\qquad\mu=\mu_{\mathrm{max}}\,\kappa^{2}.

(67)

The velocity-space grid is visualized in figure
4
(a) with
12\times 16
cells. For this test we use the reference temperatures
T_{\parallel e0}=300

v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}=5v_{te0}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}=m_{e}v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}^{2}/(2B)

4.3
Test with collisionless & collision terms

This section presents tests with both collisionless and collisional terms in one, two and three dimensions.

4.3.1
1D HTS mirror

The use of uniform velocity grids can make gyrokinetic modeling of high-temperature superconducting (HTS) mirrors particularly challenging. As described in previous work, the temperature and density can decrease by orders of magnitude between the core of the confined plasma and the expander region on the other side of the mirror coils
[
22
]
. This variation means that the velocity-space width of the appreciable distribution function diminishes rapidly between these two regions, and the uniform velocity-space grid that seems sufficient in the core is too coarse in the expander or viceversa, a grid that seems suitable in the expander is too narrow or over resolved for the core. Previous 1D simulations of an HTS mirror, including the core plasma and those of the expander, and approximating the Wisconsin HTS Axisymmetric Mirror (WHAM)
[
18
]
, employed a grid that compromised between the requirements of the core and the expander, as well as the numerical requirements of the previous version of Gkeyll
[
22
]
(since then, Gkeyll was rewritten, adding support for GPUs and improving the algorithms). We restrict this discussion to Boltzmann electron simulations, where
f_{e}
is not evolved
[
22
]
, since they are sufficient to describe three challenges, and how nonuniform velocity maps can help.
The first of these challenges is that
f_{i}
was only marginally resolved in parts of the domain, such as the mirror throat and the expander wall. Second, despite the Boltzmann electron approximation these simulations still took a long time (days on hundreds of cores to reach
t=8\nu_{ee}^{-1}/3=48~\mu
s, where
\nu_{ee}
is the electron collision frequency). And third, the largest velocity extent we were able to afford was
v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}=3.75v_{ti0}
, but this small
v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}
caused an artificial accumulation of
f_{i}
near the boundary and eventually made simulations unstable.

The combination of GPUs and nonuniform velocity maps lets us tackle all three of these challenges. We thus revisit such HTS mirror simulations, with a position space domain
z\in\left[-L_{z}/2,L_{z}/2\right]
covering a field line of length
L_{z}
across a 5 m mirror. We use a velocity space mapping resulting in a linear
v_{\parallel}
grid up to
4v_{ti0}
and cubic above that, and similarly the
\mu(\kappa)
mapping yields a linear
\mu
grid up to
0.012\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
and a quadratic grid above that. Specifically, the computational velocity space
(\eta,\kappa)=\left[-1/4^{2/3},1/4^{2/3}\right]\times\left[0,1\right]
employed the mapping

\displaystyle v_{\parallel}=\begin{cases}v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}\eta&\quad\left|\eta\right|\leq 1/4,\\
16v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}\,\mathrm{sign}\left(\eta\right)\left|\eta\right|^{3}&\quad\left|\eta\right|>1/4,\end{cases}\qquad\mu=\begin{cases}\frac{g}{w}\,\mu_{\mathrm{max}}\,\kappa,&\quad\kappa\leq w\\
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}\,\left(\kappa^{2}\frac{g-1}{w^{2}-1}+\frac{w^{2}-g}{w^{2}-1}\right),&\quad\kappa>w,\end{cases}

(69)

with
w=0.3
,

,
v_{\parallel\mathrm{max}}=16v_{ti0}
,
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}=m_{i}\left(3v_{ti0}\right)^{2}/(2B_{p})
,
v_{ti0}^{2}=T_{i0}/m_{i}
,
T_{i0}=8.361

This platform has been used for many years to test fluid

5
Summary

This article presents an approach to nonuniform velocity-space discretization in discontinuous Galerkin (DG) continuum kinetic models, using conservative discrete mapped coordinates and demonstrating it in the context of a full-
f
gyrokinetic model for studying magnetized plasmas. The method consists of rewriting the equations in terms of new computational coordinates and specifying a univariate mapping between physical and computational velocity coordinates. The velocity-space mapping is discretized using DG in a way that results in a continuous inter-cell representation. By carefully designing the numerical fluxes we can ensure that the algorithm for collisionless terms conserves the number of particles and total energy to machine precision regardless of resolution in the absence of temporal discretization error. In addition, collision terms conserve particles, momentum and energy exactly without the additional complications incurred by a pure linear basis
[
24
]
thanks to our novel hybrid basis.

Several tests were performed, independently for collisionless and collisional terms, and also including both types of terms in 1D, 2D, and 3D simulations. The tests show the algorithm’s capacity to produce reasonable results with the new nonuniform velocity-space discretization, in a variety of magnetically aligned domains, such as high-temperature superconducting (HTS) mirrors, diverted tokamak scrape-off layers, and turbulent cylindrical plasmas. In each of these cases, simulations with nonuniform velocity discretization reproduce previous results and in some cases, such as in HTS mirror simulations, the number of cells was reduced by as much as a factor of 30. As a result, calculations took 22-60 times less wall-clock time than a uniform velocity grid simulation with equivalent resolution.

This new capability will allow future DG gyrokinetic simulations to use fewer degrees of freedom (and thus fewer computational resources) or better represent parts of phase-space. Future work on the topic of better or more flexible velocity-space representations could include using position-space-dependent mappings
[
64
]
or using block-structured grids in velocity space with different reference temperatures for each block
[
37
]
. A related desirable capability is that, given that the background temperature evolves in time, it could be helpful to have the option to restart with a different reference temperature and thus a different velocity-space grid, requiring interpolation between the two; such option could bring stability and acceleration to long-running full-
f
simulations. Lastly, we presented a demonstration of 1D, 2D and 3D simulations with the new nonuniform velocity discretization but an in-depth validation against experiments is beyond the scope of this manuscript; such comparisons are ongoing and will be presented as separate publications.

Appendix A
Nonuniform velocity space in Vlasov-Poisson

The approach to nonuniform velocity space discretization presented in this work is applicable to other Vlasov systems. We sketch out how using a 1D1V Vlasov-Poisson system as an example; generalization to higher dimensions is straightforward. Assuming a unit charge and mass, the Vlasov-Poisson equation is

\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\dot{x}f_{s}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\dot{v}f_{s}=0

(76)

where
\dot{x}=v
and
\dot{v}=-\partial_{x}\phi
, and
\phi
is given by the Poisson equation (assuming a unit permittivity):

-\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial x^{2}}=\rho=\sum_{s}\int\mathrm{d}v~f_{s}.

(77)

We can assume a velocity map

v=v(\eta),

(78)

such that equation
76
becomes

\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\dot{x}f_{s}+\frac{1}{v^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\dot{v}f_{s}=0,

(79)

and
v^{\prime}=\partial_{\eta}v
. Let us multiply this equation by
v^{\prime}
and introduce the proxy distribution
F_{s}=v^{\prime}f_{s}
. Projecting this equation onto the DG basis
\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}
, integrating by parts, and using the transformation to logical coordinates
(\chi,\zeta)
:

x=x_{j}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\chi,\qquad\eta=\eta_{j}+\frac{\Delta\eta}{2}\zeta,

(80)

we obtain the weak Vlasov-Poisson equation:

\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\chi^{\,}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}\frac{\partial F_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{\Delta x}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}G_{x,j}^{\pm}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{\,}\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}G_{v,j}^{\pm}-\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{\,}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\left(\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial x}\dot{x}+\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial v}\frac{\dot{v}}{v^{\prime}}\right)F_{s}=0.

(81)

Substituting in the DG expansion of
F_{s}
,

F_{s,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}F_{s,j}^{(k)}\psi^{(k)}_{j},

(82)

and exploting the orthonormality of
\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}
, one obtains an evolution equation for the expansion coefficients of
F_{s}
:

\frac{\partial F_{s,j}^{(k)}}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{\Delta x}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}G_{x,j}^{\pm}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{\,}\psi^{(\ell)\pm}_{j}G_{v,j}^{\pm}-\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\chi^{\,}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\left(\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial x}\dot{x}+\frac{\partial\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}}{\partial v}\frac{\dot{v}}{v^{\prime}}\right)F_{s}=0.

(83)

After computing the electrostatic potential
\phi
via equation
77
, one can follow the same procedure used for the gyrokinetic equation. The volume term is computed analytically with computer algebra given that the integrand is just a product of polynomials. The surface terms are calculated by constructing a surface modal expansion of the numerical fluxes,
G_{x,j}^{\pm}
and
G_{v,j}^{\pm}
, obtained by evaluating the fluxes in Flax-Friedrichs form at Gauss-Legendre nodes of the surface, and employing a nodal-to-modal transformation.

Appendix B
Full-
f
gyrokinetic model in field-aligned coordinates

This appendix details the transformations leading from the gyrokinetic equation

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}f_{s}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathcal{J}\mathbf{\dot{R}}f_{s}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\mathcal{J}\dot{v_{\parallel}}f_{s}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{C}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{D}_{s},

(84)

to the model in field-aligned coordinates used in Gkeyll. The advection velocities in equation
84
,
\mathbf{\dot{R}}
and
\dot{v_{\parallel}}
, are defined in terms of the Poisson bracket

\{A,H_{s}\}=\frac{\mathbf{B^{*}}}{m_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\cdot\left(\nabla A\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial v_{\parallel}}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\nabla H_{s}\right)+\frac{\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{q_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\times\nabla H_{s}\cdot\nabla A,

(85)

and the particle Hamiltonian

H_{s}=m_{s}v_{\parallel}^{2}/2+\mu B+q_{s}\phi
as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{R}}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{R},H_{s}\}=\frac{\mathbf{B^{*}}}{m_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial v_{\parallel}}+\frac{\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{q_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\times\nabla H_{s},\qquad\dot{v_{\parallel}}=\{v_{\parallel},H_{s}\}=-\frac{\mathbf{B^{*}}}{m_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\cdot\nabla H_{s},

(86)

using the effective magnetic field

\displaystyle\mathbf{B^{*}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}.

(87)

The electrostatic potential
\phi
is obtained from the long-wavelength limit of the gyrokinetic quasineutrality equation

-\nabla\cdot\epsilon_{\perp}{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{v}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}\,\mathcal{J}f_{s},\qquad\epsilon_{\perp}=\sum_{s}\frac{m_{s}n_{s0}}{B_{0}^{2}}.

(88)

Here we use
\nabla_{\perp}=-\mathbf{\hat{b}}\times\left(\mathbf{\hat{b}}\times\nabla\right)=\nabla-\mathbf{\hat{b}}\left(\mathbf{\hat{b}}\cdot\nabla\right)
, the shorthand
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}=(2\pi/m_{s})\,\mathrm{d}v_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
, and
\Omega_{\mathbf{v}}
for the domain in velocity space:
\left(v_{\parallel},\mu\right)\in\left[-\infty,\infty\right]\times\left[0,\infty\right]
. We have also defined the guiding center density
n_{s}
as the zeroth velocity moment of
\mathcal{J}f_{s}
. The reference values
n_{0s}
and
B_{0}
are typically set to the global average at
t=0
and are independent of time.

The collision term on the right-hand side of equation
84
,

\mathcal{C}_{s}=\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{el}}+\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{iz}}+\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{cx}}+\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{rec}}+\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{rad}},

(89)

includes elastic collisions between charged species (
\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{el}}
) and inelastic collisions and reactions (with neutrals), which at the moment encompass ionization (
\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{iz}}
), charge exchange (
\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{cx}}
), recombination (
\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{rec}}
) and radiation line (
\mathcal{C}_{s}^{\mathrm{rad}}
). Our model of elastic charged-species collisions is typically a Dougherty operator
[
15
,
21
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}_{s}=\sum_{r}\nu_{sr}\,\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel sr}\right)+v_{t,sr}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}2\mu\left(1+\frac{mv_{t,sr}^{2}}{B}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\right)\right\}f_{s},

(90)

where the sum over
r
is over every charged species other than
s
,
\nu_{sr}
is the frequency of collisions between species
s
and species
r
, and
u_{\parallel sr}
and
v_{t,sr}
are the cross-species mean flow and thermal speeds that ensure momentum and energy conservation
[
24
]
. Models of interactions with neutral species and line radiation are described in separate publications
[
7
,
56
]
. Our source model usually has a Maxwellian or bi-Maxwellian form

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{s}=\frac{\dot{n}_{s,\mathrm{src}}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}v_{t\parallel,\mathrm{src}}v_{t\perp,\mathrm{src}}^{2}}\left[-\frac{\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel,\mathrm{src}}\right)^{2}}{2v_{t\parallel,\mathrm{src}}^{2}}-\frac{2\mu B/m_{s}}{2v_{t\perp,\mathrm{src}}^{2}}\right],

(91)

given a particle source rate (
\dot{n}_{s,\mathrm{src}}
), the source mean flow speed (
u_{\parallel\mathrm{src},s}
) and temperatures parallel (
T_{\parallel\mathrm{src},s}=m_{s}v_{t\parallel,\mathrm{src}}^{2}
) and perpendicular (
T_{\perp\mathrm{src},s}=m_{s}v_{t\perp,\mathrm{src}}^{2}
) to the background magnetic field. Lastly, the diffusion model used in the absence of self-consistent anomalous cross-field transport (only present in 3D) is simply

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{s}=\nabla\cdot\left(D_{s}\cdot\nabla f_{s}\right),

(92)

where
D_{s}
is a rank-2 tensor. Incorporating initial (ICs) and boundary conditions (BCs), equations
84
-
92
represents a closed system that can be integrated with a suitable numerical method.

Model in field-aligned coordinates

A field-aligned curvilinear coordinate system
[
6
]

\mathbf{x}=\{x^{i}\}
(here
i\in[1,d_{x}]
and
d_{x}
is the number of position-space dimensions) with a mapping to the Cartesian gyrocenter position
\mathbf{R}
given by
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})
transforms equation
84
to
[
47
]

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}f_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\mathcal{J}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}f_{s}+\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\mathcal{J}\dot{v_{\parallel}}f_{s}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{C}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}_{s}+\mathcal{J}\mathcal{D}_{s},

(93)

where
\mathbf{e_{i}}=\partial\mathbf{R}/\partial x^{i}
and
\mathbf{e^{i}}

are the tangent (covariant) and dual (contravariant) basis vectors of the
\mathbf{x}
coordinates, defining the metric tensors
g_{ij}=\mathbf{e_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{e_{j}}
and
g^{ij}=\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{e^{j}}
, and the Jacobian
J_{x}=\mathrm{det}(g_{ij})^{1/2}=\mathrm{det}(g^{ij})^{-1/2}

[
17
]
. Repeated indices imply summation unless noted otherwise or where explicit summation appears. The
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})
mapping is field aligned because the coordinates
x^{i}
are chosen so that the magnetic field can be written as

\mathbf{B}=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})~\ \mathbf{e^{1}}\times\mathbf{e^{2}},

(94)

meaning
\mathbf{B}
is aligned with the third tangent vector
\mathbf{e_{3}}
. The scalar function
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})
is constrained by the requirement that
\mathbf{B}
must be divergence-free:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\nabla\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{e^{1}}\times\mathbf{e^{2}}+\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\cdot\mathbf{e^{1}}\times\mathbf{e^{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla x^{1}\times\nabla x^{2}\right)=\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})

(95)

since
\mathbf{e^{1}}\times\mathbf{e^{2}}=\nabla x^{1}\times\nabla x^{2}=\nabla\times\left(x^{1}\nabla x^{2}\right)-x^{1}\nabla\times\nabla x^{2}
and we use the fact that
\nabla\times\nabla A
and
\nabla\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{A}
are both zero (for some scalar
A
and vector
\mathbf{A}
fields). Therefore, as long as
\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{C}=0
, the magnetic field remains divergence free. Given that
\mathbf{e^{1}}\times\mathbf{e^{2}}=\mathbf{e_{3}}/J_{x}
, the magnetic field is

\mathbf{B}=\begin{cases}B^{i}\mathbf{e_{i}}=B^{3}\mathbf{e_{3}}=\mathcal{C}J_{x}^{-1}\mathbf{e_{3}}\qquad&\text{contravariant form},\\
B_{j}\mathbf{e^{j}}=\mathcal{C}J_{x}^{-1}g_{3j}\mathbf{e^{j}}\qquad&\text{covariant form},\end{cases}

(96)

and the magnetic field amplitude is

\displaystyle B=\sqrt{g_{ij}B^{i}B^{j}}=\sqrt{g^{ij}B_{i}B_{j}}=\sqrt{B_{i}B^{j}}=\sqrt{g_{33}}\frac{\mathcal{C}}{J_{x}}=B^{3}\sqrt{g_{33}},

(97)

which allows us to write the magnetic field unit vector as

\mathbf{\hat{b}}=\frac{\mathbf{B}}{B}=\begin{cases}b^{i}\mathbf{e_{i}}=g_{33}^{-1/2}\mathbf{e_{3}}\qquad&\text{contravariant form},\\
b_{j}\mathbf{e^{j}}=g_{3j}g_{33}^{-1/2}\mathbf{e^{j}}=[g_{3j}\mathcal{C}/(J_{x}B)]\mathbf{e^{j}}\qquad&\text{covariant form}.\end{cases}

(98)

In order to compute the contravariant components of
\mathbf{\dot{R}}
(i.e.
\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}
) appearing in equation
93
, in light of equation
86
, it is necessary to express the effective magnetic field in terms of its contravariant components:

\displaystyle B^{*i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{B^{*}}=B^{3}\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}},

(99)

where
\delta^{k}_{j}
being the Kronecker delta, and we used the contravariant form for
\mathbf{B}
from equation
96
. We can now express the advection speeds (equation
86
) in terms of curvilinear coordinates as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{B^{*i}}{m_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial v_{\parallel}}+\frac{1}{q_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\frac{\epsilon^{ijk}}{J_{x}}b_{j}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial x^{k}},\qquad\dot{v_{\parallel}}=-\frac{B^{*i}}{m_{s}B_{\parallel}^{*}}\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial x^{i}}.

(100)

Note that the parallel component of
\mathbf{B^{*}}
can be expressed in terms of its contravariant components as

\displaystyle B_{\parallel}^{*}=\mathbf{\hat{b}}\cdot\mathbf{B^{*}}=b_{i}B^{*i}=b_{3}\frac{\mathcal{C}}{J_{x}}+b_{k}\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\frac{\epsilon^{ijk}}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial b_{j}}{\partial x^{i}}=B+b_{k}\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\frac{\epsilon^{ijk}}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial b_{j}}{\partial x^{i}}.

(101)

The collisionless advection terms also require the electrostatic potential
\phi
, for which we must also express the gyrokinetic quasineutrality equation (
88
) in field-aligned coordinates. First, writing out the outer divergence on the left-hand side of equation

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}\,\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}.

(102)

Note that the inner product on the left side, given that
{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{}=\nabla-\mathbf{\hat{b}}(\mathbf{\hat{b}}\cdot\nabla)
, is

\displaystyle\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{}

\displaystyle=g^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}-\left(b^{i}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},

(103)

and given the contravariant components of
\mathbf{\hat{b}}
in equation

, the expression for
\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{}
becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{}=g^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}-\delta^{i}_{3}\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}}{J_{x}B}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{3}}.

(104)

Therefore the gyrokinetic quasineutrality equation in field-aligned curvilinear coordinates is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}\left[g^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}-\delta^{i}_{3}\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}}{J_{x}B}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{3}}\right]\phi=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s}.

(105)

Lastly, the collision and source terms on the right-hand side of equation

do not involve differential operators in position-space and are thus relatively unaffected by the introduction of field-aligned curvilinear coordinates. The diffusive model of anomalous transport, equation
92
, does need to be adjusted. However, rather than using straightforward algebra in curvilinear spaces
[

]
for a diffusion tensor
D_{s}
expressed in terms of Cartesian basis vectors, we first recognize that this operator is typically used to introduce

Thus when we write the diffusion operator in terms of curvilinear coordinates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\left(D_{s}\cdot\nabla f_{s}\right)=\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\left(D_{s,jk}\mathbf{e^{j}}\mathbf{e^{k}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial x^{\ell}}\mathbf{e^{\ell}}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\left(g^{ij}D_{s,jk}g^{k\ell}\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial x^{\ell}}\right)=\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\left(g^{ij}{D_{s,j}}^{k}\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial x^{k}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\left(g^{ij}D_{s,j}\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial x^{j}}\right).

(107)

Axisymmetric, small

99
) vanish, and the parallel component of the effective magnetic field is now

\displaystyle B_{\parallel}^{*}=B+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}J_{x}}\left[-b_{1}\frac{\partial b_{2}}{\partial x^{3}}+b_{2}\left(\frac{\partial b_{1}}{\partial x^{3}}-\frac{\partial b_{3}}{\partial x^{1}}\right)+b_{3}\frac{\partial b_{2}}{\partial x^{1}}\right].

(108)

Note that if
v_{\parallel}\sim\mathcal{O}\left(v_{t}\right)
and
\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}\sim\mathcal{O}\left(\left|\nabla B\right|/B=1/L_{B}\right)
then the magnitude of the second term in equation
108
relative to the first is
(m_{s}v_{\parallel}/q_{s})\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}/B\sim m_{s}v_{t}/(q_{s}BL_{B})\sim\rho_{s}/L_{B}
. In many experiments of interest (e.g. modern day tokamaks with small
\rho^{*}=\rho_{s}/a
, where
a
is the minor radius), the magnetic field scale length
L_{B}
is of the order of the major radius
R>a
, hence
(m_{s}v_{\parallel}/q_{s})\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}/B\sim\rho_{s}/R\ll 1
and we can approximate
B_{\parallel}^{*}\approx B
.

An additional simplification we will make pertains the gyrokinetic quasineutrality equation (
105
), where the differential operator includes derivatives along
x^{3}
, i.e. along the magnetic field. However, in the gyrokinetic ordering
k_{\parallel}\ll k_{\perp}
(here
k_{\parallel}
and
k_{\perp}
are the wavenumbers parallel and perpendicular to
\mathbf{B}
) and we can simplify this field equation by dropping the derivatives along
x^{3}
. In this case the field-aligned quasineutrality equation becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}g^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\phi=\sum_{s}q_{s}n_{s},\qquad i,j\in\{1,2\}.

(109)

Lastly, for perfectly axisymmetric simulations we can leverage the same
k_{\parallel}\ll k_{\perp}
ordering to remove the field-aligned gradients from the diffusion operator in equation
107
, allowing for the simpler diffusion model:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J_{x}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{1}}J_{x}\left(g^{11}D_{s,1}\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial x^{1}}\right).

(110)

Appendix C
Conservation properties

C.1
Collisionless terms

In this section we prove that the weak form of the gyrokinetic equation
25
, with surface and volume terms computed as described in section
3.1
, and the calculation of the electrostatic potential according to section
3.1.1
, conserves particles and energy exactly in the limit of infinite temporal resolution.

Particle conservation

In order to check that the total number of particles

\mathcal{N}=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}\,J_{x}\mathcal{J}f=\frac{2\pi}{m}\frac{\Delta\mathbf{x}\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{2^{d_{p}}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,F_{j}=\mathbb{V}\sum_{j=1}^{N}F_{j}^{(0)}

(111)

is conserved (
\mathbb{V}=2\pi\Delta\mathbf{x}\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa/(2^{d_{p}}m)
is a dimensional and normalization factor, and
d_{p}=d_{x}+2
), we can set
\psi^{(\ell)}=1
in equation
25
and sum it over all
N=N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}N_{v_{\parallel}}N_{\mu}
cells. Moving the resulting surface terms to the right-hand side we obtain:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{N}}{dt}=\mathbb{V}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial F_{j}^{(0)}}{\partial t}=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}.

(112)

Since the fluxes
\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}
and
\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}
were constructed to be single-valued at cell surfaces, contributions to each of these terms from adjacent cells cancel each other. Only the contributions from cell surfaces on domain boundaries remain. We employ zero-flux BCs in velocity space so the domain-boundary contribution to the second term in equation
112
is zero. We are hence left with

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{N}}{dt}=\mathbb{V}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial F_{j}^{(0)}}{\partial t}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\perp x^{i}_{\max}}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}\Big|_{\chi^{i}=1}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\perp x^{i}_{\min}}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}\Big|_{\chi^{i}=-1}\right),

(113)

where
N_{\perp x^{i}_{\min}}
and
N_{\perp x^{i}_{\max}}
is the number of cells at the first and last cell along
x^{i}
, respectively. If one uses periodic boundary conditions the contributions from the two terms in equation
113
cancel each other, or if one uses zero-flux BCs both of these terms are set to zero. In these cases
\dot{\mathcal{N}}=0
and particles are conserved exactly.

Energy conservation

We show that the total, discrete energy of the system
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{H}-\mathcal{E}_{\phi}
, composed of the particle energy

\mathcal{E}_{H}=\sum_{s}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}\,H_{s}\mathcal{J}f_{s}=\sum_{s}\frac{2\pi}{m_{s}}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,H_{s}F_{s}

(114)

and the field energy

\mathcal{E}_{\phi}=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{R}\,\frac{\epsilon_{\perp}}{2}\left|{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}\right|^{2}=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,J_{x}\frac{\epsilon_{\perp}}{2}\left|{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}\right|^{2}

(115)

is conserved. The time rate of change of the total energy is

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{s}\frac{2\pi}{m_{s}}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,H_{s}\frac{\partial F_{s}}{\partial t}}_{\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s}\frac{2\pi}{m_{s}}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial t}F_{s}-\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}\left({{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}}{\partial t}}_{\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{2}}.

(116)

The first of these terms (
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
) can be obtained by substituting
\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}=(2\pi/m_{s})H_{s,j}
into our weak scheme, equation
24
, and summing over all species and cells:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1}=\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j}\frac{\partial F_{s,j}}{\partial t}=-\underbrace{\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j\pm}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}}_{\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\dot{R}^{i}_{\pm}}}

(117)

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j\pm}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}}_{\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\dot{v}_{\parallel}^{\pm}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\left(\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\dot{R}^{i}+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{\dot{v_{\parallel}}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\right)F_{s,j}}_{\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\mathrm{vol}}}.

The volume term (last term in equation
117
) cancels out due to the antisymmetry of the Poisson bracket. To see this substitute the definitions of
\dot{R}^{i}
and
\dot{v_{\parallel}}
in equation
27
:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\mathrm{vol}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\left\{\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\left[\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\frac{\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)v_{\parallel}+\frac{\epsilon^{i\ell p}}{q_{s}}\frac{b_{\ell}}{J_{x}B}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{p}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{p}}\right]\right.

(118)

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\frac{2}{\Delta\eta}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\zeta}\frac{1}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\left[-\frac{1}{m_{s}}\left(g_{33}^{-1/2}\delta^{i}_{3}+\frac{m_{s}v_{\parallel}}{q_{s}}\frac{\mathbf{e^{i}}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{\hat{b}}}{B}\right)\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\right]\right\}F_{s,j},

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\left(\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\frac{\epsilon^{i\ell p}}{q_{s}}\frac{b_{\ell}}{J_{x}B}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{p}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{p}}\right)F_{s,j},

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\frac{1}{qB_{\parallel}^{*}J_{x}}\left[\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{i}}\left(\epsilon^{\ell i1}b_{\ell}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{1}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{1}}+\epsilon^{\ell i2}b_{\ell}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{2}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{2}}+\epsilon^{\ell i3}b_{\ell}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{3}}\frac{\partial H_{s,j}}{\partial\chi^{3}}\right)\right]F_{s,j},

\displaystyle=0,

where we used
v_{\parallel}=(\partial_{\eta}H)/(mv_{\parallel}^{\prime})=2(\partial_{\zeta}H)/(mv_{\parallel}^{\prime}\Delta\eta)
. The two surface terms in equation
117
(
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\dot{R}^{i}_{\pm}}
and
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\dot{v}_{\parallel}^{\pm}}
) make use of the
C^{0}
-continuity of the Hamiltonian (
H_{s,j\pm}
) and single-valued numerical surface fluxes
\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}
and
\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{v_{\parallel},j}
, to cancel contributions from adjacent cells at common boundaries. We also make use of zero-flux BCs at
v_{\parallel}
boundaries, such that the
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1,\dot{v}_{\parallel}^{\pm}}
term vanishes and equation
117
becomes

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{1}=-\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\perp x^{i}_{\max}}}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j\pm}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}\Big|_{\chi^{i}=1}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\perp x^{i}_{\min}}}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j\pm}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}\Big|_{\chi^{i}=-1}\right).

(119)

Physically speaking, these terms are the surface integral of the particle energy flux through position-space boundaries.

The second and third terms in equation
116
(
\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
) involve exchange of energy between particles and fields. Since
\partial_{t}H_{s}=q_{s}\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}\left(\phi\right)
is independent of velocity space, these terms can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}q_{s}\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\frac{\partial\left(\mathcal{P}\left(\phi\right)\right)}{\partial t}\left(\frac{2\pi}{m_{s}}\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{v}}}\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,F_{s}\right)-\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}\left({{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}}{\partial t},

(120)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\left(\sum_{s}q_{s}J_{x}n_{s}\right)\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right)-\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}\left({{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}}{\partial t}.

At this point we use the self-adjoint property of
\mathcal{P}
(equation
42
) to transfer it from
\dot{\phi}
to the charge density:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\mathcal{P}\left(\sum_{s}q_{s}J_{x}n_{s}\right)-\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,J_{x}\epsilon_{\perp}\left({{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial{{\nabla}_{\perp}}{\phi}}{\partial t},

(121)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\overline{\varrho}-\int_{\Omega_{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\,\varepsilon^{ij}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{i}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\right),\qquad i,j\in\{1,2\}.

We can employ the quasineutrality equation to cancel these two terms. First, we break up the
x^{3}
integrals into cell-wise contributions:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{z}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\left[\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\overline{\varrho}_{k}-\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\right)\varepsilon^{ij}_{k}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{i}}\right].

(122)

Then we employ the weak quasineutrality equation
45
in the first term by replacing
w^{(m)}_{k}=\partial_{t}\phi_{k}
(since
\phi_{k}
is in the space spanned by the
w^{(m)}_{k}
basis set) to obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{E}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{z}}\int_{x^{3}_{k-1/2}}^{x^{3}_{k+1/2}}\mathrm{d}x^{3}\left[-\int_{\Omega_{x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\varepsilon_{k}^{1q}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{q}}\Bigg|_{x^{1}=x^{1}_{\min}}^{x^{1}=x^{1}_{\max}}-\int_{\Omega_{x^{1}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\varepsilon_{k}^{2q}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{q}}\Bigg|_{x^{2}=x^{2}_{\min}}^{x^{2}=x^{2}_{\max}}\right.

(123)

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{p}}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\right)\varepsilon_{k}^{pq}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{q}}-\int_{\Omega_{x^{1},x^{2}}}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial t}\right)\varepsilon^{ij}_{k}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial x^{i}}\right].

For time-independent homogeneous BCs, the first two terms vanish; the last two terms cancel each other.

We can therefore conclude that the discrete energy change of our DG gyrokinetic system is

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s}\mathbb{V}_{s}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{x}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\perp x^{i}_{\max}}}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j\pm}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}\Big|_{\chi^{i}=1}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\perp x^{i}_{\min}}}\frac{2}{\Delta x^{i}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,H_{s,j\pm}\widehat{G}^{\pm}_{x^{i},j}\Big|_{\chi^{i}=-1}\right),

(124)

that is, the only energy change in our system is due to energy fluxes through our domain boundaries.

C.2
Collisional terms

The discrete Dougherty operator described in section
3.2
can conserve particle, momentum and energy density exactly (independently of phase-space resolution) provided that the primitive moments
u_{\parallel}
and
v_{t}^{2}
satisfy discrete constraints described in this section. This approach to computing
u_{\parallel}
and
v_{t}^{2}
was described for like-species
[
21
]
and multi-species
[
24
]
collisions in earlier publications, and only involves minor modifications to accommodate the mapped velocity coordinates introduced in this work.

Particle conservation

In order to show that the change in particle number density (times the Jacobian of
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})
)

\displaystyle J_{x}M_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\int_{v_{\parallel\min}}^{v_{\parallel\max}}\int_{0}^{\mu_{\max}}\mathrm{d}v_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,J_{x}\mathcal{J}f=\frac{2\pi}{m}\int_{\eta_{\min}}^{\eta_{\max}}\int_{\kappa_{\min}}^{\kappa_{\max}}\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,F

(125)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\int_{\eta_{i-1/2}}^{\eta_{i+1/2}}\int_{\kappa_{j-1/2}}^{\kappa_{j+1/2}}\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,F=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,F_{i,j}

over time due to the discrete Dougherty operator

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(J_{x}M_{0}\right)}{\partial t}\Bigg|_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{4}\int_{K_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,J_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}

(126)

is zero in each position-space cell, replace
\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}=(2\pi/m)\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa/4
in equation
59
and sum over all velocity space cells (we omit the position-space index since the collision operator only involves advection and diffusion in velocity-space):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(J_{x}M_{0}\right)}{\partial t}\Bigg|_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\oint_{\partial K_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left\{\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)_{\pm}\widehat{\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}}+v_{t}^{2}\mu^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]\right\}

(127)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\pi}{m}\frac{\Delta\eta}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\oint_{\partial K_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\nu\left[2\mu_{\pm}\left(\widehat{\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}v_{\parallel}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right)\right]=0.

The final equality is justified by our
C^{0}
-continuous advection or drag fluxes, and the use of a recovered
F/J_{v}
with continuous derivatives at cell boundaries, such that the surface terms pairwise cancel when summed over velocity-space cells. The surface terms at the boundaries of velocity space vanish due to our zero-flux BCs (equation
60
).

Momentum conservation

The particle momentum density (multiplied by
J_{x}/m
)

\displaystyle J_{x}M_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\int_{v_{\parallel\min}}^{v_{\parallel\max}}\int_{0}^{\mu_{\max}}\mathrm{d}v_{\parallel}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,v_{\parallel}J_{x}\mathcal{J}f=\frac{2\pi}{m}\int_{\eta_{\min}}^{\eta_{\max}}\int_{\kappa_{\min}}^{\kappa_{\max}}\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,v_{\parallel}F

(128)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\int_{\eta_{i-1/2}}^{\eta_{i+1/2}}\int_{\kappa_{j-1/2}}^{\kappa_{j+1/2}}\mathrm{d}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\kappa\,v_{\parallel i}F=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,v_{\parallel i}F_{i,j}

can also be shown to have no temporal change due to the Dougherty operator

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(J_{x}M_{1}\right)}{\partial t}\Bigg|_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{4}\int_{K_{i,j}}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,v_{\parallel i}J_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}

(129)

by setting
\psi^{(\ell)}_{j}=(2\pi/m)\left(\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa/4\right)v_{\parallel}
in equation
59
and summing over all velocity-space cells:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(J_{x}M_{1}\right)}{\partial t}\Bigg|_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}}=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left\{v_{\parallel j\pm}\left[\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)_{\pm}\widehat{\frac{F}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}}+v_{t}^{2}\mu^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{\parallel}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right]-\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right\}

(130)

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{4}\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)F+\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta}{2}\oint_{\partial K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\nu v_{\parallel j\pm}2\mu_{\pm}\left[\widehat{\frac{F}{\mu^{\prime}}}+\frac{mv_{t}^{2}}{B}v_{\parallel}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\widetilde{\frac{F}{J_{v}}}\Bigg|_{\pm}\right].

Inside the velocity-space domain, the surface flux terms cancel due to continuity and pairwise cancellation of fluxes on either side of an inter-cell surface. At the velocity-space domain boundary zero-flux BCs set the surface terms in square brackets to zero. Therefore, we are left with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left(J_{x}M_{1}\right)}{\partial t}\Bigg|_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{el}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left(-\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right)_{i=N_{v_{\parallel}},\zeta=1}-\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left(-\frac{v_{t}^{2}J_{v}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}\frac{F}{J_{v}}\right)_{i=1,\zeta=-1}

(131)

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N_{v_{\parallel}},N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\eta\Delta\kappa}{4}\int_{K_{j}}\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left(v_{\parallel}-u_{\parallel}\right)F.

We can ensure that equation
131
vanishes by enforcing the constraint

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left(\frac{v_{t}^{2}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}F\right)_{i=N_{v_{\parallel}},\zeta+}-\frac{2\pi}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mu}}\frac{\Delta\kappa}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\nu\left(\frac{v_{t}^{2}}{v_{\parallel}^{\prime}}F\right)_{i=1,\zeta-}+\nu\left(J_{x}M_{1}-u_{\parallel}J_{x}M_{0}\right)=0.

(132)

Energy conservation

We can show that the particle kinetic energy density multiplied by
J_{x}
and divided by
m/2

\displaystyle J_{x}M_{2}
