Primary cardiac angiosarcoma initially presenting with sick sinus syndrome and pericardial effusion: a case report
Authors: Wenjing Zhang, Yue Li, Jin Wang, Xueyang Bai, Gangqiong Liu, Rita Pavasini, Ahmet Taha Sahin, Ahmad Jomaa, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary cardiac angiosarcoma is a rare and highly aggressive malignancy whose clinical presentation is primarily driven by local invasion and metastatic dissemination. Its rapid progression and frequent diagnostic delay contribute to a markedly poor prognosis.Case summaryWe report a 64-year-old man who initially presented with pericardial effusion and symptomatic sick sinus syndrome, for which he underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation. A right atrial mass closely related to the pacing lead was subsequently detected and repeatedly interpreted as thrombus-like on transthoracic echocardiography, with markedly elevated D-dimer levels. Despite anticoagulation, the mass enlarged and multiplied, ultimately causing superior vena cava obstruction. Concurrent pulmonary nodules and ground-glass opacities developed. Surgical exploration and debulking revealed extensive tumour infiltration of the right atrium and venae cavae. Histopathology confirmed cardiac angiosarcoma. The patient remained haemodynamically unstable after surgery and was considered unsuitable for systemic antitumour therapy. He died 13 days after surgery.DiscussionThis case highlights the diagnostic difficulty of primary cardiac angiosarcoma presenting with pericardial effusion and sick sinus syndrome, particularly when a right atrial mass is spatially related to a pacing lead and repeatedly appears thrombus-like on echocardiography. Progressive enlargement despite anticoagulation should prompt early reconsideration of malignancy and multidisciplinary evaluation.Keywords: Case report, Cardiac angiosarcoma, Pericardial effusion, Sick sinus syndrome, Pulmonary nodules

Introduction

Cardiac angiosarcoma is the most common primary malignant cardiac tumour and is characterized by aggressive local invasion, frequent right atrial origin, and early metastatic spread.1 Histopathological examination remains the diagnostic gold standard. This report describes a 64-year-old man with primary cardiac angiosarcoma initially presenting with pericardial effusion and sick sinus syndrome, emphasizing the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges posed by a rapidly progressive right atrial mass after pacemaker implantation.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old man presented with exertional chest tightness, transient visual blackout, and syncope. Coronary and cerebrovascular evaluations were unremarkable. Holter monitoring documented more than 40 daytime sinus pauses lasting >3.0 s; the longest was 4.07 s and corresponded to symptoms (Figure 1). Initial transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%, no right-sided chamber enlargement or dysfunction, no right atrial mass, a small pericardial effusion, and mild tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 2A–C). No reversible cause of sinus node dysfunction was identified, and a dual-chamber pacemaker was implanted. Fever developed on postoperative Day 2. Echocardiography showed no vegetation or valvular involvement, and chest computed tomography (CT) showed pulmonary inflammatory changes (Figure 3E) and persistent small pericardial effusion (Figure 3A). Blood cultures and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging were recommended but declined by the family. The patient became afebrile after antibiotic therapy and was discharged on low-dose oral diuretics.

Two weeks later, the patient developed bilateral lower-extremity swelling and purpura. The D-dimer level was 11.89 mg/L (upper reference limit: 0.55 mg/L), the platelet count was normal, and ultrasonography confirmed bilateral intermuscular venous thrombosis. Following specialist assessment, allergic purpura was considered, and anticoagulation and corticosteroids were initiated for thrombosis and purpura, respectively. One week later, echocardiography demonstrated reduced pericardial effusion but revealed a hypoechoic mass at the right atrial roof near the superior vena cava–right atrial junction, surrounding the pacing lead (Figure 2D). The absence of fever and significant elevation of inflammatory markers favoured lead-associated thrombus rather than infective endocarditis, although microbiological evaluation was not obtained. During subsequent follow-up, the mass enlarged and multiplied (Figure 2D–F; Figure 3B–D) and caused superior vena cava obstruction, presenting as facial oedema. CT demonstrated pulmonary nodules and ground-glass opacities, some with halo signs (Figure 3F and G). Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) showed heterogeneous fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the right atrial lesion (maximum standardized uptake value, ∼ 15.9), including a focal low-metabolic component interpreted as possible thrombus, and mild uptake in the pulmonary lesions (Figure 3H). Progression despite anticoagulation prompted reconsideration of malignancy. Surgical exploration demonstrated masses almost occluding the caval–right atrial pathway with atrial-wall and pericardial invasion. Palliative debulking was performed, and histopathology confirmed cardiac angiosarcoma (Figure 4). Postoperative haemodynamic instability required transfusions and vasopressors. Following oncology consultation, systemic therapy was not pursued because of the patient’s poor condition. He died 13 days after surgery.

Discussion

Primary cardiac angiosarcoma usually arises in the right atrium, infiltrates locally, and metastasizes early. Current cardio-oncology guidance supports multidisciplinary assessment and histopathological confirmation; complete resection may improve survival when feasible.2,3 In a 20-year series, median overall survival was 13 months, falling to 6 months in metastatic disease, and resection was associated with longer survival than leaving the tumour in situ (17 vs. 5 months).1 Evidence for systemic therapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and transplantation remains limited.2,4,5 In this patient, superior vena cava obstruction, pulmonary lesions, and extensive atrial and caval involvement indicated advanced disease. Surgery therefore served diagnostic and decompressive rather than curative purposes. Persistent postoperative haemodynamic instability precluded systemic treatment despite oncology consultation.

This case illustrates the diagnostic ambiguity of a right atrial angiosarcoma mimicking lead-associated thrombus. Thrombus-like echogenicity, marked D-dimer elevation, and the close spatial relationship between the mass and the pacing lead initially supported this diagnosis and reinforced diagnostic anchoring. However, serial enlargement despite anticoagulation and heterogeneous FDG uptake were inconsistent with thrombus and should prompt reconsideration of malignancy. Contrast-enhanced echocardiography might have helped differentiate an avascular thrombus from a vascular tumour,6 while cardiac magnetic resonance could have provided better tissue characterization and assessment of myocardial and pericardial involvement.2 These examinations were not performed, representing limitations of the diagnostic work-up. Pericardiocentesis was not undertaken because the effusion was small and lacked tamponade physiology; consequently, its nature remained undetermined. Although tumour-related bradyarrhythmia, angiosarcoma diagnosed after pacemaker implantation, and intracardiac thrombus mimicry have been reported,7–9 the educational value of this case lies in the evolving diagnostic process rather than a novel presentation. The pacing lead acted as a diagnostic confounder. A right atrial mass that progresses despite anticoagulation warrants prompt reassessment through serial multimodality imaging to facilitate earlier diagnosis and multidisciplinary management.
