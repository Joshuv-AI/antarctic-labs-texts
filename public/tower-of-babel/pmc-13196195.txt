Downregulation of THRB drives immune microenvironment suppression and poor outcomes, acting as a dual biomarker for clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Suzhen Peng, Xiao Liu, Weizi Sun, Jiegang Hu, Zhiwei Tang, Shitong Wang, Zhongshi He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) frequently resists targeted and immune therapies, necessitating predictive biomarkers. Thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRB), a nuclear transcription factor at 3p21-25, shows tumor-suppressive roles in other cancers but its immunomodulatory function in ccRCC remains unclear.MethodsUsing TCGA-KIRC data (535 tumors, 72 normals) and validation cohorts (GSE46699/53757), we analyzed THRB expression, clinical correlations, and immune associations via differential expression (limma), survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier/Cox regression), immune infiltration (CIBERSORT/ESTIMATE), functional enrichment (GO/KEGG/GSEA), and drug sensitivity (pRRophetic).ResultsTHRB was significantly downregulated in ccRCCand 11 other cancers (P < 0.05), correlating with advanced stage/grade (P < 0.001) and poorer survival (OS HR = 2.33, DSS HR = 3.43; P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis confirmed its independent prognostic value (HR = 0.69, P = 0.007). Functionally, THRB associated with immune regulation (T-cell activation, PD-1/PD-L1 pathways) and distinct immune infiltration patterns. High-THRB tumors were enriched for resting immune cells and M2 macrophages, while low-THRB tumors showed higher fractions of CD8⁺ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), along with elevated expression of T-cell exhaustion markers (all P < 0.01). This suggests a complex role for THRB in shaping the immune landscape. THRB negatively correlated with TMB (r=-0.26, P < 0.001) and inhibitory checkpoints (LGALS9, CD70), but positively with VTCN1. In silico analysis predicted that low-THRB patients might exhibit reduced sensitivity to sunitinib/pazopanib (higher IC50, P < 0.001).ConclusionTHRB downregulation is associated with poor prognosis, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and resistance to targeted therapies in ccRCC. These findings suggest its potential as a dual biomarker, implying that THRB loss may contribute to immune dysregulation and therapy resistance. Restoring THRB-mediated pathways may offer novel therapeutic strategies, though its predictive value for immunotherapy requires future clinic validation.Keywords: Thyroid hormone receptor β (THRB), Clear cell renal cell carcinoma(ccRCC), Immune microenvironment, Biomarker

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) ranks as the third most common genitourinary malignancy, with clear cell RCC (ccRCC) representing 75% of cases and demonstrating marked heterogeneity and frequent treatment resistance [1]. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy since 2005, variable treatment responses persist due to tumor heterogeneity [2]. Recent trials (COSMIC-313, CheckMate-9ER) demonstrate promise for cabozantinib-based combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors [3, 4], yet a substantial number of patients remain non-responders, underscoring the critical need for predictive biomarkers.

The thyroid hormone receptor (THR) family, particularly the beta subtype (THRB) located at 3p21-25 [5], encodes nuclear transcription factors regulating metabolism, differentiation, and proliferation. Growing oncogenomic evidence reveals frequent THR dysregulation across malignancies - somatic mutations are documented in breast cancer [6], hepatocellular carcinoma [7], pituitary tumors [8, 9], and thyroid cancer [10], while their anti-tumor/anti-metastatic properties are increasingly recognized [11]. In ccRCC specifically, THRB demonstrated tumor suppressor and immunomodulatory roles through macrophage/T-cell regulation and immune checkpoint modulation, showing significant downregulation versus normal tissue. However, its functional impact on tumor immunity remains unclear—a key knowledge gap given immunotherapy’s growing role in RCC management.

Through comprehensive TCGA multi-omics analysis, we investigated: (1) pan-cancer THRB expression patterns, (2) clinical correlations with progression/survival, (3) associations with tumor mutational burden/immune checkpoints, and (4) immune infiltration relationships. Our comprehensive analysis establishes THRB as a robust prognostic biomarker and reveals its significant association with key immune-modulatory features in the ccRCC microenvironment. these findings suggest its potential relevance for predicting responses to both targeted and immunotherapies, laying a foundation for future clinical investigation.

Materials and methods

Data collection and processing

The Level 3 RNA-sequencing data (HTSeq-FPKM format) and corresponding clinicopathological information for patients with ccRCC from the TCGA-KIRC cohort were downloaded from the UCSC Xena browser (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). For cross-sample comparability, FPKM values were converted to transcripts per million (TPM) using the formula: TPM = (FPKM × 10⁶) / Σ(FPKM). The pre-calculated CIBERSORT immune cell fractions were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://gdc.cancer.gov/).

Differential expression analysis

THRB expression levels between tumor and adjacent normal tissues were compared using the limma package (v4.1.3) in R, with statistical significance defined as false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Visualization was performed using ggpubr We validated differential THRB expression (v0.4.0) to generate scatter plots with paired sample connectors. We validated differential THRB expression in pan-cancer using the TIMER2.0 GEPIA2 database (http://GEPIA2.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) [12].

Prognosis analysis and clinical correlation

To comprehensively evaluate the prognostic significance of THRB in renal cell carcinoma, we analyzed four key survival endpoints using the TCGA-KIRC cohort: overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-free interval (DFI). Survival curves were generated through Kaplan-Meier analysis implemented in the survival (v3.2-13) and survminer R packages, with statistical significance determined by log-rank tests. For external validation, we utilized the KM-Plotter online platform to confirm the association between THRB expression and patient outcomes in independent datasets. Clinical correlation studies were performed using a two-tiered analytical approach: first, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to assess individual prognostic factors; subsequently, multivariate Cox models were constructed incorporating THRB expression levels alongside established clinical variables including patient age, gender, and TNM staging. All regression results were visually summarized using forest plots generated with the forestplot package to display hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals, enabling intuitive interpretation of the relative prognostic weights.

Gene function analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between THRB-high and THRB-low groups (defined by median expression) were identified using the limma package with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Subsequent functional enrichment analysis was performed through two complementary approaches: (1) Gene Ontology (GO) annotation of biological processes to identify overarching functional themes, and (2) KEGG pathway analysis to map molecular interactions and signaling cascades. All enrichment results were computationally processed and visualized as interpretable bubble charts using the clusterProfiler package (v4.0) [13], with bubble size representing gene counts and color intensity indicating statistical significance (-log10(p-value)).

Evaluation of immune score and stromal score

The ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to calculate ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore for each tumor sample [14]. Score distributions between THRB expression groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with results displayed as violin plots (ggpubr package).

Immune infiltration analysis

The correlation between THRB expression and each immune cell fraction was calculated using Spearman’s rank test. To account for multiple comparisons across the 22 immune cell types [15], the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and results were considered significant at FDR < 0.05.

Immune checkpoint analysis and tumor mutation burden analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between THRB expression and 12 immune checkpoint genes (including PDCD1, CTLA4, and LAG3). P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (FDR < 0.05). For the tumor mutation burden (TMB) analysis, complete somatic mutation data required for TMB calculation were available for 332 of the 535 KIRC samples in the TCGA cohort [16]. The relationship between THRB expression and TMB within this subset was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, with P-value adjustment as noted above .

Drug sensitivity analysis

The pRRophetic package (v0.5) was employed to predict therapeutic response based on gene expression profiles [17]. This algorithm provides in silico estimates of drug sensitivity. The predicted half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for 24 targeted drugs were compared between THRB expression groups using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Cell viability and migration assays

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the manufacturer’s instructions(Vazyme A311-02).

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration was evaluated using 8-µm pore Transwell chambers (Falcon). Briefly, 786-O cells (2 × 10⁵) in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, with 10% FBS medium as chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After 24 h, non-migrated cells were removed, and migrated cells were fixed (4% PFA), stained (0.1% crystal violet), and quantified using ImageJ.

Cell culture and siRNA Transfection

786-O cells were maintained in RPMI-1640/10% FBS at 37 °C/5% CO₂. At 90% confluency, cells were transfected with THRB-targeting siRNAs (siTHRB-1: TGCCCAGACCTTCCAAACGG; siTHRB-2: GACGAGCTCTGTGTAGTGTG) or control siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000. Knockdown efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR after 48 h.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using Vazyme kits. THRB mRNA levels were normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences:

THRB: Forward 5′-GGCGCAGCACGTTGAAAAAT-3′, Reverse 5′-CACATCATCATGGTCCAGATGG-3′.

THRB: Forward 5′-GGCGCAGCACGTTGAAAAAT-3′, Reverse 5′-CACATCATCATGGTCCAGATGG-3′.

GAPDH: Forward 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, Reverse 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′.

GAPDH: Forward 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, Reverse 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′.

Statistical analysis

Data represent mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Significance (P < 0.05) was determined by Student’s t-test (GraphPad Prism 9.0).

Results

THRB is downregulated across multiple human cancers

To investigate the potential role of THRB in tumorigenesis, we first analyzed its pan-cancer mRNA expression using the GEPIA2 database. THRB expression was significantly downregulated in 12 cancer types, including adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), brain lower-grade glioma (LGG), rectal adenocarcinoma (READ), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) (Fig. 1A).

THRB expression is downregulated in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC). A Pan-cancer analysis of THRB mRNA expression across human malignancies using GEPIA2. B Comparison of THRB expression between KIRC tumors (n = 535) and normal kidney tissues (n = 72) from the TCGA dataset. C Paired analysis of THRB expression in ccRCC tumors and adjacent normal tissues (n = 72 pairs; paired t-test). D, E Validation of THRB downregulation in KIRC using independent datasets GSE46699 (D) and GSE53757 (E) (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). (P < 0.05, *P < 0.01, ** P < 0.001)

Next, we focused on ccRCC using TCGA RNA-seq data. THRB mRNA levels were significantly lower in 535 tumor tissues compared to 72 normal controls (Fig. 1B). This finding was further validated in 72 paired tumor-normal samples, where THRB expression was consistently reduced in tumors (paired t-test, P < 0.05; Fig. 1C). To strengthen our observations, we analyzed two independent datasets (GSE46699 and GSE53757), both confirming significant THRB downregulation in ccRCC (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05; Fig. 1D and E).

Association between THRB expression and clinicopathological features in ccRCC (TCGA-KIRC)

The clinicopathological characteristics of ccRCC (TCGA-KIRC) patients with THRB expression data from the TCGA cohort are summarized in Table 1. Chi-square tests were performed to evaluate potential associations between THRB expression levels and clinicopathological parameters. Notably, lower THRB expression was significantly correlated with advanced pathological T stage (Fig. 2A), pathological M stage (Fig. 2B), higher tumor stage (Fig. 2C), and poorer histologic grade (Fig. 2D), suggesting that reduced THRB expression may serve as a marker of aggressive disease. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between THRB expression and age, gender, tumor laterality, or N stage (P > 0.05).

Reduced THRB expression predicts poor survival outcomes in ccRCC

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that low THRB expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS; HR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.72–3.13, P = 0.001; Fig. 3A), progression-free interval (PFI; HR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.57–2.93, P < 0.001; Fig. 3B), and disease-specific survival (DSS; HR = 3.43, 95% CI: 2.35–5.00, P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). In contrast, no significant difference in disease-free interval (DFI) was observed between groups (HR = 1.33, 95% CI: 0.48–3.68, P = 0.576; Fig. 3D). Consistent findings were validated in the GEPIA2 database, where low THRB expression correlated with poorer OS and DSS (Fig. 3E and F).

G–T Forest plot of subgroup analyses for OS, stratified by T stage (T1, T2, T3), N stage (N0), M stage (M0, M1), histologic grade (G3), tumor laterality (left, right), sex (male, female), and age (≤ 65 vs. >65 years)

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that high THRB expression predicted longer OS across multiple clinicopathological strata, including: T stage: T1 (HR = 1.90, P = 0.022), T2 (HR = 3.36, P = 0.020), T3 (HR = 1.65, P = 0.028); N/M stage: N0 (HR = 1.80, P = 0.008), M0 (HR = 1.87, P = 0.001), M1 (HR = 1.82, P = 0.033); Tumor characteristics: Grade G3 (HR = 1.90, P = 0.010), left-sided (HR = 2.56, P < 0.001), right-sided (HR = 1.81, P = 0.010); Demographics: Male (HR = 1.69, P = 0.007), female (HR = 3.51, P < 0.001), age ≤ 65 years (HR = 2.35, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3G and T).

Downregulation of THRB expression is an independent predictor of poor OS in ccRCC patients

Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses revealed THRB mRNA expression, age, tumor stage, and grade as independent prognostic factors in ccRCC patients, with advanced age (HR: 1.03, 95% CI [1.01–1.04]; P < 0.001), higher clinical stage (HR: 1.65, 95% CI [1.421–1.923]; P < 0.001), and elevated pathological grade (HR: 1.40, 95% CI [1.107–1.765]; P = 0.005) associated with worse OS, while increased THRB expression (HR: 0.69, 95% CI [0.526–0.904]; P = 0.007) predicted improved survival (Fig. 4A, B).

To facilitate clinical translation, we developed a nomogram integrating THRB expression with key clinical variables (age, gender, pathological stage) to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities. In this model, higher total points corresponded to poorer prognostic outcomes (Fig. 4C). The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed survival rates (Fig. 4D).

THRB modulates the immune microenvironment in ccRCC patients

Our comprehensive bioinformatics analysis identified THRB as a potential regulator of the immune microenvironment in ccRCC. Using the LinkedOmics dataset, we found 2548 genes significantly co-expressed with THRB (P < 0.01, |r| > 0.3), with the top 50 positively and negatively correlated genes displayed in Fig. 5A and B. Functional enrichment analyses demonstrated THRB’s strong association with immune regulation. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in immune-related processes including regulation of cell-cell adhesion, mononuclear cell differentiation, T cell activation, and immune system modulation (Fig. 5C, Table S2). KEGG pathway analysis further showed enrichment in viral infection responses (HIV-1, EBV, HTLV-1) and immune cell functions such as natural killer cell cytotoxicity and T cell receptor signaling (Fig. 5D, Table S3).

Correlation of THRB expression with TME score and immune cell infiltration in ccRCC

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in tumor progression, with its cellular composition increasingly recognized as a determinant of clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses. We evaluated the association between THRB expression and TME characteristics in KIRC. TMEscore Analysis The low-THRB group exhibited significantly higher TME scores compared to the high-THRB group (Fig. 6A), a difference driven primarily by immune scores rather than stromal scores.

Association of THRB expression with tumor microenvironment characteristics in ccRCC(TCGA-KIRC). A Correlation between THRB expression and TME scores (immune and stromal components). B Spearman’s correlation analysis of THRB expression with immune cell infiltration levels. C Differential immune cell infiltration patterns between tumors with high versus low THRB expression. Significance levels: * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001

CIBERSORT analysis revealed that THRB expression showed significant positive correlations with resting mast cells (P < 0.001), resting CD4 + memory T cells (P < 0.001), M2 macrophages (P < 0.001), naïve B cells (P = 0.003), resting NK cells (P = 0.007), and naïve CD4 + T cells (P = 0.009) (Fig. 6B). Conversely, negative correlations were observed with follicular helper T cells (P < 0.001), regulatory T cells (Tregs, P < 0.001), CD8 + T cells (P < 0.001), activated NK cells (P = 0.003), and plasma cells (P = 0.007).

Differential Immune Infiltration Comparative analysis revealed elevated infiltration of immunoregulatory cell types—including CD56bright NK cells, mast cells, monocytes, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and central memory CD8 + T cells—in the high-THRB group (Fig. 6C). In contrast, the low-THRB group showed higher levels of activated immune subsets (CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, macrophages) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). These results demonstrate significant associations between THRB expression and distinct patterns of immune cell infiltration in KIRC, suggesting a potential role for THRB in shaping the immune landscape.

Association between THRB and tumor mutation burden/immune checkpoint genes in ccRCC

We assessed the relationship between THRB expression and tumor mutation burden (TMB) in 332 ccRCC(TCGA-KIRC subset)samples. The high-THRB group exhibited significantly lower TMB than the low-THRB group (P < 0.001; Fig. 7A), supported by a negative Spearman correlation (r = − 0.261, P < 0.001; Fig. 7B), indicating an association between high THRB expression and lower mutational load in ccRCC tumors.

Association of THRB expression with tumor mutation burden and immune checkpoint markers in ccRCC(TCGA-KIRC). A Distribution of tumor mutation burden (TMB) in low- versus high-THRB expression groups. B Negative correlation between THRB expression levels and TMB (Spearman’s rank correlation). C Heatmap of Spearman correlation coefficients between THRB and 15 immune-related genes. Significance levels: * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001

Given the prognostic relevance of immune checkpoint genes (ICGs), we evaluated their correlation with THRB (Fig. 7C). THRB showed negative associations with immunomodulatory genes, including the checkpoint ligand LGALS9, TNF superfamily members (TNFSF9, TNFSF14), and co-stimulatory molecules (CD27, CD70; all P < 0.05). Conversely, THRB positively correlated with the checkpoint molecule VTCN1 (P < 0.05). These findings imply that high THRB expression correlates with both reduced TMB a genomic feature often associated with immunotherapy response—and a distinct immune checkpoint profile. Collectively, these associations suggest that THRB may be linked to tumor immune phenotypes, warranting future investigation into its specific role in modulating response to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Association of THRB expression with targeted therapy response in ccRCC

Given that targeted therapies (particularly sunitinib and pazopanib) serve as first-line treatments for advanced ccRCC, we examined the relationship between THRB expression and drug sensitivity. Using the computational pRRophetic algorithm, we predicted the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for commonly used targeted therapies. The analysis predicted significantly higher IC50 values for both sunitinib (P < 0.001; Fig. 8A) and pazopanib (P < 0.001; Fig. 8B) in the low-THRB group compared to the high-THRB group. These in silico results suggest a potential association between low THRB expression and reduced predicted sensitivity to these tyrosine kinase inhibitors, positioning THRB as a candidate biomarker worthy of further experimental investigation for predicting targeted therapy response.

Silencing THRB expression significantly enhanced the proliferative and migratory capacities of 786-O cells

The effectiveness of THRB silencing by siRNA was validated through RT-qPCR analysis, demonstrating a substantial downregulation of THRB expression (**P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001;Figure 9A). Subsequent CCK-8 assays indicated that THRB knockdown notably enhanced the proliferation of 786-O cells (*P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01; Fig. 9B). Moreover, THRB-deficient cells displayed significantly greater migratory potential (*P < 0.05, **** P < 0.0001;Figure 9C). Taken together, these findings support the hypothesis that THRB functions as a tumor suppressor in ccRCC.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that THRB acts as a pivotal regulator of ccRCC progression. The THRB gene, located on chromosome 3p21-25 [5], functions as a ligand-activated transcription factor regulating cell cycle, metabolism, and differentiation. While recognized as a tumor suppressor in some contexts, its role in immune evasion and metabolic dysregulation in ccRCC has been unclear.

We first confirmed significant THRB downregulation in ccRCC, consistent with prior reports of its post-transcriptional repression by miRNAs like miR-155 and miR-425 [5]. Pan-cancer analysis extended this finding to 11 other cancers, with the most pronounced decreases in KIRC and KIRP.

Most importantly, this downregulation carries profound clinical implications. Low THRB expression was significantly correlated with advanced pathological stages, higher tumor grade, and markedly worse overall, disease-specific, and progression-free survival. Importantly, multivariate Cox regression confirmed THRB as an independent prognostic factor. To enhance clinical applicability, we developed a nomogram integrating THRB expression, which demonstrated strong predictive accuracy for patient survival (C-index: 0.766), solidifying its role as a robust prognostic biomarker for ccRCC.

To uncover the underlying mechanisms, we explored THRB’s impact on the tumor immune microenvironment. Functional enrichment analyses provided initial clues, linking THRB to T cell activation alongside immune-suppressive functions. Pathway analysis further connected THRB to viral infection pathways and immune checkpoint regulation, including PD-1/PD-L1, suggesting its involvement in immune evasion.

Subsequent investigation into specific immune checkpoints revealed a distinctive pattern: THRB expression showed a significant negative correlation with multiple inhibitory molecules, including LGALS9, TNFSF9, and CD70. In contrast, a positive correlation was observed with VTCN1. Given that LGALS9 [18], TNFSF9 [19], and CD70 [20] are established promoters of T-cell exhaustion and immunosuppression, their negative correlation with THRB suggests that loss of THRB may drive the establishment of an immune-resistant microenvironment, a hallmark commonly observed in patients with poor response to immunotherapy. This THRB-low/immune-suppressive phenotype aligns closely with the recently defined “immune-exhausted” multi-omics subtype (MoS1) of ccRCC, which is characterized by a similar immunosuppressive microenvironment and portends the poorest prognosis among all subtypes [21]. This convergence suggests that THRB downregulation may be a key molecular driver underlying this aggressive disease subclass.

We also found that high THRB expression correlated with low tumor mutational burden (TMB), suggesting a link to genomic stability. Although the mechanism remains unclear, THRB may influence processes such as cell cycle regulation or stress responses. Previous studies have indicated that THRB may influence cellular stress responses and damage repair through signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, providing clues to its potential role [22]. However, it must be emphasized that the current associative data do not support a direct regulatory role of THRB in DNA repair. The study also found a negative correlation between low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and high expression of immune checkpoints(e.g.,LGALS9) in ccRCC, which may further support the notion that THRB regulates immune evasion through a dual “TMB-checkpoint” mechanism. However, the current research stage cannot confirm a direct causal link between them. Future studies are still needed to conduct functional experiments to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

High expression of THRB is associated with favorable tumor prognosis, characterized by the enrichment of resting immune cells and M2-type macrophages. Mechanistically, THRB may promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype by inhibiting the NF-κB/STAT1 signaling pathway [23, 24], thereby limiting the full activation of T cells and NK cells. M2 macrophages and resting CD4⁺ memory T cells secrete mediators such as IL-10 and TGF-β [23], forming a positive feedback network that collectively maintains a controlled immune-quiescent microenvironment. Additionally, THRB may reduce tumor cell immunogenicity or alter antigen presentation, thereby diminishing persistent immune stimulation and helping to maintain T and NK cells in a functionally resting state, avoiding excessive activation-induced tissue damage and immunosuppressive feedback. Furthermore, the reduction in follicular helper T cells (Tfh) suggests that THRB may also suppress excessive B cell activation [25]. In summary, THRB signaling shapes an immune-quiescent phenotype conducive to tumor microenvironment stability and tissue normalization through multiple mechanisms. Notably, this THRB-high/immune-quiescent state shares features with the more indolent ccRCC subtypes identified in multi-omics frameworks [21]. However, these conclusions are primarily based on correlative observations, and the precise causal mechanisms require further validation and elucidation through functional experiments.

Conversely, the low-THRB subgroup presented a paradox: a higher global immune score coupled with profound functional impairment. Here, we observed a dominant immunosuppressive network orchestrated by Tfh and Tregs via the IL-21/TGF-β axis [26, 27]. Despite phenotypic activation, CD8 + T cells predominantly cells predominantly exhibited a PD-1 + TIM-3 + terminally exhausted phenotype. This dysfunction was rooted in mitochondrial failure, characterized by defective ATP synthesis and ROS accumulation [28]. Underlying this dysfunctional state is a fundamental metabolic reprogramming driven by THRB loss via PPARγ/RXR signaling [29]. This shift manifested as aberrant glycolytic activation coupled with suppressed fatty acid oxidation, collectively driving the TME toward a pro-tumorigenic state. Additionally, our in silico prediction that low THRB expression correlates with reduced sensitivity to sunitinib/pazopanib aligns with recent multi-omics subtyping, which identified an immune-exhausted ccRCC subtype (MoS1) with poor prognosis and likely inferior response to such therapies [21]. This suggests THRB may serve as a practical biomarker to identify this therapy-resistant phenotype.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the present study. First, our findings rely on retrospective TCGA-KIRC data, which may introduce biases from sample processing variability and incomplete clinical details (e.g., treatments). Second, while we propose a “TMB-checkpoint” mechanism and PPARγ/RXR-mediated metabolic reprogramming, these hypotheses require experimental validation (e.g., THRB modulation in cell lines).Third, the prognostic nomogram (C-index: 0.766) lacks external cohort validation, limiting clinical applicability. Fourth, All conclusions on the tumor immune microenvironment and immune cell states are based on computational analysis of bulk sequencing data and require validation through direct experimental approaches such as flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, or single-cell RNA sequencing. Fifth, the functional validation of THRB was limited to intrinsic tumor properties (proliferation and migration) in one cell line (786-O). Its proposed immunomodulatory role should be directly investigated using immune co-culture or in vivo models. Sixth, the association between THRB expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB) is merely correlative, and our data do not establish a causal or mechanistic link involving DNA repair. Seventh, the drug sensitivity analysis for sunitinib and pazopanib is based on in silico prediction (pRRophetic algorithm). These results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation with in vitro or ex vivo drug response assays.

Conclusion

In summary, our study delineates THRB as a master regulator bridging immune and metabolic circuits in ccRCC. The loss of THRB initiates a cascade of synergistic defects—driving T cells toward exhaustion while imposing a glycolytic straitjacket on the microenvironment. This integrated view not only explains the strong association between low THRB expression and poor patient prognosis. Therefore, we hypothesize that THRB deficiency could represents not only a powerful prognostic indicator but also a candidate marker indicative of a tumor-intrinsic immunometabolic vulnerability. This hypothesis, however, awaits validation in cohorts of patients treated with immunotherapy. Future therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring THRB-driven pathways or cotargeting the resultant immune and metabolic defects hold significant promise for innovative combination therapies in KIRC.
