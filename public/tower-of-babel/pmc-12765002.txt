Generalized Galton’s boards explain social phenomena via statistical physics
Authors: Gennaro Auricchio, Massimiliano Ghiotto, Stefano Gualandi, Giuseppe Toscani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany aspects of our society are shaped by the repeated occurrence of small, random events. For instance, our personal wealth evolves based on how we spend and exchange money, while our opinions are constantly changing as a result of repeated interactions with both others and social media. This idea that the persistent repetition of random events can profoundly impact individuals was insightfully captured by Sir Francis Galton. He illustrated this concept using the device that now bears his name: Galton’s board. In this paper, we revisit the mathematical principles underlying Galton’s board and frame them within the broader context of statistical mechanics. We illustrate how the mechanisms behind the board can be generalized to model a wide range of social phenomena and to capture, in a new and simple way, the universal profile emerging from the details of the repeated elementary interactions.Keywords: Mathematical modelling, Kinetic model, Social scienceSubject terms: Evolution, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Foreseeing how a society evolves throughout time and finding ways to sway its direction is the defining problem of most social sciences. This is certainly the case in economics, where outlining regulations on transactions affects how the wealth of a population is distributed in the long term. If, given the wealth distribution at an initial time step, we were able to foresee how the global population wealth is distributed after many transactions, it would be possible to decide whether introducing a different regulamentation (i.e., to modify D and/or R) would be beneficial to the population. This same scenario occurs in many other fields of life, ranging from opinion formation to disease spreading: in all of these cases, the global evolution of the phenomenon is determined by a binary and random interaction between agents3.

However, even knowing the rules of a single interaction, describing the long-term evolution of the population through classic means is impossible because of three main reasons:

An elegant solution to this problem is offered by statistical physics, a branch of mathematics initially born to describe the evolution of gases4, and then extended to various frameworks, such as opinion formation5,6, econophysics7,8, flock behaviours9,10, and urban evolution11. Indeed, gases are composed by a large number of molecules (around ), the number of interactions (i.e., collisions) between molecules is also very large, and the outcome of each binary collision between molecules is not deterministic. To complete the analogy with the society model, each collision between molecules is easy to describe as it obeys classic physical rules, such as the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy4.

The key idea behind statistical physics is to describe a gas using a probability distribution over its phase space, i.e., the set of all possible positions and velocities of its molecules. By doing so, instead of tracking each individual molecule, one can study the probability of finding a molecule with a given velocity at a specific location at any given time. Although in this formalism it is impossible to keep track of every single molecule, it allows to analyse the gas through observable quantities such as the mean or variance of the particle positions. Indeed, a physicist cannot monitor the energy of every single molecule in a gas, but can instead measure macroscopic quantities such as density, temperature, pressure, and volume.

Moreover, this framework extends beyond gases and can be applied to any system where multiple entities undergo binary interactions, as in (1). In particular, it is well-suited for the study of social systems1,12,13, as it captures the interaction dynamics that drive the macroscopic evolution of multi-agent systems, such as opinion formation14,15, body weight distribution16, or wealth dynamics17.

In this paper, we demonstrate that the foundational idea underlying statistical physics had already been understood by Sir Francis Galton, as his eponymous invention, the Galton Board, exemplifies18. Moreover, we showcase how modelling the evolution of a phenomenon through a sequence of repeated and non-deterministic interactions at a microscopic level leads to the emergence of an equilibrium configuration at a macroscopic level, and the equilibrium profile is uniquely determined by the details of the elementary interactions. To underpin the significance of statistical physics-based models, we highlight three relevant cases in the fields of social sciences, economics, and medicine, where this approach has been fruitfully adopted to explain, replicate, and even foresee observable data.

Galton’s legacy

Historically, Sir Francis Galton was the first to notice a connection between statistical phenomena and human nature. In one of its experiments, Sir Galton showed that if the individuals composing a society randomly select an action when posed with a decision, the overall population will not move far from its initial condition18. To exemplify this effect, he built a machinery that still bears the name of its inventor, the Galton Board (this machinery is also known as quincunx, bean machine, or pachinko game)19,20.

A Galton Board, see Fig. 1, is a device consisting of a rectangular box with rows of interleaved pegs and a hole at the top. To operate the machine, beads are poured into the hole at the top, one at a time. When the device is properly levelled, the beads fall through the rows of pegs, inevitably colliding with them. These collisions cause the beads to move randomly to the left or to the right as they descend until they reach the bottom of the box. There, the beads are collected in bins and sorted by their final positions. The aim of Sir Galton was to give a visual idea of the role society has on individuals. Indeed, beads represent a group of uninformed individuals, while pegs are decisions that individuals are forced to take, i.e., going left or right. By pouring enough beads, it is possible to observe that bins that are located right below the hole contain most of the beads, rightly pointing out that people taking random choices will not move far from their initial social state. Nowadays, the effect captured by the Galton Board has a name and a proof: it is the Central Limit Theorem21, which ensures that given enough beads and enough peg rows, the final beads distribution will shape as a Gaussian Distribution.

From a human perspective, this reflects the influence of governmental or societal forces on a population. Indeed, while in the Galton Board every bead was equally likely to go left or right at every interaction, in this case the bead has a preferred direction. If we go back to the analogy with the evolution of a population, this means that during every decision the individual is pushed one way over the other, leading the agents toward a preselected point. This example shows the agency the government has over society by guiding individuals toward more favourable outcomes. More in general, the effect captured by the evolution in (9) extends to various social phenomena in which interaction dynamics exhibit skewed asymmetry–such as the distribution of service times in call centres26, body weight16, and the timing of first childbirth25. These findings consistently show a larger fraction of the population clustering toward the direction of the skewed interaction, as observed in the cited examples.

Other important distributions

Aside from the two probability distributions discussed so far, there are other distributions that emerge as the limits of microscopic laws governing large numbers of agents interacting one with the other or with the background. Indeed, the Gaussian and Lognormal distributions are among the most commonly observed in everyday life, as the interactions leading to them are relatively simple to occur and straightforward to model.

In this section, we briefly introduce the reader to some less conventional interactions that give rise to distributions which, although less known, possess a remarkable ability to capture phenomena in fields such as economics, social science, and medicine.

From immunogenesis to Luria-Delbrück’s revelation

The Luria-Delbrück model shed light on how our body defends itself against viral infections, fundamentally changing our understanding of the immune response. For a long time, scientists believed that the human body responded to viral attacks by creating a specific type of antibody tailored to combat the disease. However, the work of Luria and Delbrück demonstrated that our body does not directly respond to infections in this targeted way. Instead, it continuously generates random mutations of the cells at our disposal until some of them will be capable of neutralizing the virus. In other words, Luria and Delbrück proved that the immune system’s ability to fight off infections comes from its natural propensity to create a diverse range of antibodies through constant mutation41. Since this breakthrough, several models have been proposed to describe how these cells mutate and evolve over time42. While Luria and Delbrück posited the axioms of this new theory and the empirical data were available, the challenge of identifying a model capable of coherently explaining these findings soon became a central focus of a research that still fascinates the community today43. This led to the development of several mathematical frameworks aimed at modelling mutation rates, firstly introduced by Lea and Coulson44, Kendall45, and Bartlett46, and then improved over time47,48. All these frameworks build upon the model introduced by Luria and Delbrück, but incorporate simplifications or additional assumptions to capture specific aspects of the mutation process49.

Notice that the model proposed by Luria and Delbrück rightly fits the criteria to be described through a kinetic model50. Indeed, given a population of normal cells, the evolution of these types of cells depends on how the cells naturally reproduce, plus a term that accounts for other cells randomly mutating into this specific cell type. Conclusion

In this work, we examined the classic Galton Board from the perspectives of kinetic theory and statistical physics. This approach highlights the board’s relevance as a model for understanding various social, economic, and biological phenomena. We demonstrated how macroscopic distributions, such as the Gaussian, lognormal, Beta, and Pareto laws, naturally emerge from repeated microscopic dynamics by framing individual decisions and interactions as elementary stochastic events. The kinetic framework presented in this work operates under certain limits. The most critical constraint is the requirement of finite variance throughout all derivations. As demonstrated in our analysis, the introduction of the shrinking parameter is essential to ensure that the variance remains uniformly bounded, as otherwise the variance would grow indefinitely, preventing the identification of a limit distribution. This constraint necessarily excludes heavy-tailed phenomena with infinite variance, which require alternative frameworks based on stable distributions or extreme value theory. Additionally, the restriction to linear elementary interactions of the form described in Eq. (15) limits the applicability to phenomena exhibiting non-linear dynamics or higher-order multi-agent interactions.

Despite these limitations, our proposed framework provides a unified and intuitive approach to describing phenomena ranging from wealth exchange to opinion formation and mutation processes. This emphasises the effectiveness of statistical mechanics methods in modelling multi-agent systems. Although the technical section is mathematically rigorous, the overall approach remains broadly accessible and aims to bridge the gap between theoretical modelling and empirical insight.

Ultimately, this work not only pays homage to Galton’s legacy but also demonstrates how foundational ideas from classical probability and physics can be extended and adapted to modern societal questions, offering new tools and perspectives for interdisciplinary research. Future developments could explore the application of this framework to other social traits or other evolutionary models51,52. Indeed, we believe that the approach Sir Galton first perceived and that was lately formalized by statistical physics can be applied to a wide variety of topics. For example, it could be fruitfully used to study ecosystems, where the different living species interact with each other53, to study the evolution of language structures, i.e.how different languages mix and generate new words54–56, and even to explain machine learning-based models, since the training of neural networks happens by feeding the system several pieces of information57,58.
