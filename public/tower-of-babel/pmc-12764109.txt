Volumetric blood cutoff for outcome prediction in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage using semiautomated quantification
Authors: Gema Bravo-Garrido, Antonio José Vargas-Lopez, Miriam Fernández-Gómez, Mario Gomar-Alba, Gaizka Urreta-Juárez, Laura Amaya-Pascasio, Patricia Martínez-Sánchez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHemorrhage volume is a major prognostic factor in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). This study investigates the association between semiautomatically measured hemorrhage volumes and functional outcomes at discharge.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted including patients with aSAH admitted to a tertiary hospital between 2016 and 2021. Hemorrhage volumes—including subarachnoid (SAH), intraventricular (IVH), intraparenchymal (IPH), and total hemorrhage (TH)—were quantified from admission CT scans using semiautomated segmentation software (AW Server). Functional outcome was assessed with the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) at discharge, dichotomized as favorable (GOS 4–5) or unfavorable (GOS ≤ 3). ROC curves were used to identify optimal volume thresholds, and multivariate logistic regression assessed independent associations.ResultsA total of 170 patients were included (65.3% women, mean age 58.5 years). Unfavorable outcomes were observed in 80 patients (47.1%). ROC analysis identified a TH volume > 29 cm3 as the optimal threshold for predicting poor outcomes, with an AUC of 0.791 (95% CI 0.721–0.861), sensitivity of 75.0%, and specificity of 73.3%. Other volume parameters (SAH, IVH, IPH) showed lower discriminatory power. In multivariate analysis, TH > 29 cm3 remained independently associated with unfavorable outcomes (OR: 3.872; 95% CI 1.704–8.799; p = 0.001), after adjusting for potential confounders.ConclusionsSemiautomated volumetric analysis of hemorrhage in aSAH identifies TH volume as a key predictor of unfavorable outcomes. TH volume could serve as a useful metric for early risk stratification and support clinical decision-making. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and assess their broader applicability.Keywords: Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, Hemorrhage volume, Glasgow outcome scale, Prognosis, Semiautomated measurement

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) is one of the most devastating forms of hemorrhagic stroke, with early mortality rates ranging from 30 to 40% [1–5]. Among survivors, functional outcomes remain a major clinical challenge, as secondary complications, particularly delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), substantially increase morbidity and long-term disability [5–10]. Despite the widespread use of grading scales like the Fisher scale, their qualitative nature and interobserver variability limit predictive accuracy [11–14].

Recent evidence supports a direct and quantifiable relationship between hemorrhage volume and neurological outcomes in aSAH [3, 4, 6, 9–11]. Larger hemorrhage volumes may worsen secondary brain injury by increasing intracranial pressure, promoting vasospasm, and disrupting microvascular autoregulation [3, 6, 9, 12]. Despite this, precise volumetric thresholds that predict functional outcomes are still not well established. Semiautomated volumetric analysis of aSAH has shown promising correlations with neurological outcomes and mortality rates [15–27]. These tools utilize Hounsfield unit thresholds and three-dimensional segmentation algorithms to delineate blood from surrounding parenchyma across multiple compartments including subarachnoid, intraventricular, and intraparenchymal spaces [15–17, 20, 21, 24, 26, 27]. Compared to visual grading, semiautomated methods are more reproducible and may better capture the total hemorrhage burden, which has been associated with increased risk of seizures, hydrocephalus, and DCI [22, 27].

Despite these advances, there is no consensus on the prognostic thresholds for hemorrhage volume in aSAH. This study hypothesizes that larger hemorrhage volumes across all intracranial compartments are associated with poorer functional outcomes, as assessed by the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) at discharge [28]. We aim to identify volumetric cutoffs, particularly for total hemorrhage (TH) volume, that predict unfavorable functional outcomes (GOS ≤ 3), with the goal of supporting early risk stratification and improving clinical decision-making.

Methods

Patient population

A retrospective cohort study was conducted including patients with aSAH admitted to a tertiary referral university hospital (from January 2016 to December 2021) serving a population of 761,379 inhabitants [26]. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, aneurysmal hemorrhage confirmed by brain computed tomography (CT) angiography or digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Exclusion criteria included non-aneurysmal hemorrhages, incomplete imaging data, or missing GOS assessments at discharge.

Medical assessment and variables

All patients were managed according to current international guidelines [5] as previously described [26]. On admission to the emergency department, neurological status was assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grading system, and the Fisher scale. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure, as well as blood glucose levels, were documented at presentation. Additional baseline data included demographics and medical history: age, sex, arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and substance use (alcohol and smoking). The Charlson Comorbidity Index and length of hospital stay (in days) were also recorded. Treatment-related variables included endovascular coiling, microsurgical clipping, external ventricular drainage (EVD), ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunting, and decompressive craniectomy.

The primary outcome was functional status at discharge, assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) [8]. This five-point scale classifies outcome from best to worst as follows: 5—good recovery; 4—moderate disability; 3—severe disability; 2—persistent vegetative state; and 1—death. In accordance with international recommendations [29], GOS was dichotomized into favorable (scores 4–5) and unfavorable (scores ≤ 3) outcomes.

Neuroimaging analysis

The presence, size, quantity, and location of the aneurysm (anterior and posterior circulation) were determined using admission CT angiography (General Electric, Siemens, or Philips) or digital subtraction angiography (DSA) (Artis Zee biplane, Siemens) conducted within the first 72 h after symptom onset (Fig. 1). In patients with multiple CT scans within this time window, the earliest scan was used for the primary volumetric analysis. Furthermore, the bicaudate index (BCI), was measured on the admission brain-CT on the axial plane [26]. Intracranial blood volumes were analyzed on admission brain CT using the Advantage Workstation (AW) Server 3.2 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), which enabled semiautomated supervised measurements, as previously described [26]. Briefly, DICOM-format CT images were loaded into the software, which used image tools to outline and quantify hemorrhage dimensions and density. The method involved selecting a region of interest (ROI) within the hemorrhage; adjacent pixels with similar intensity were automatically included. The observer adjusted thresholds and used contour tools to refine the ROI on each slice. In anatomically complex areas—such as near the skull base—windowing and slice-by-slice criteria adjustment improved delineation of subarachnoid hemorrhage. The software then summed the segmented regions across slices to calculate final volumes for subarachnoid (SAH), intraventricular (IVH), intraparenchymal (IPH), and total hemorrhage (TH) (Fig. 2a–f). Image analysis was performed by a blinded author. Interobserver agreement was high, as previously reported [26].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Patients were stratified by discharge outcome (favorable vs. unfavorable). Between-group comparisons used the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data and the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data.

Associations between hemorrhage volumes—subarachnoid (SAH), intraventricular (IVH), intraparenchymal (IPH), and total hemorrhage (TH)—and outcome were evaluated in three steps. First, ROC curves assessed discriminative ability (AUC) and optimal thresholds based on the Youden index. Model performance was also measured using the SPSS “Overall Model Quality” statistic (analogous to pseudo-R2).

Second, multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of unfavorable outcomes. Variables with p ≤ 0.20 and ≥ 10 cases per group were included. A backward stepwise approach was applied, with likelihood ratio tests to assess model fit. Variables whose exclusion altered the OR by ≥ 15% were treated as confounders.

Third, a forward stepwise regression (likelihood ratio method) confirmed model robustness. Results are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The study protocol was approved by the Almería Provincial Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 87/2022). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of fully anonymized data.

Results

A total of 170 patients were included (65.3% women, mean age 58.5 years). Unfavorable functional outcomes were observed in 80 patients (47.1%) (Fig. 3). Baseline characteristics and treatment-related variables are shown in Table 1. Patients with unfavorable outcomes were older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension. They also presented with lower GCS scores and higher WFNS and Fisher grades on admission. Endovascular treatment was more frequent in the favorable outcome group, whereas decompressive craniectomy, external ventricular drainage (EVD), and ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunting were more common in patients with unfavorable outcomes.

A total hemorrhage (TH) volume > 29 cm3 was identified as the optimal cutoff, with a sensitivity of 73.8% and a specificity of 73.3% for predicting unfavorable functional outcomes. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 71.1%, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 75.9%. In comparison, subarachnoid (SAH), intraparenchymal (IPH), and intraventricular (IVH) hemorrhage volumes yielded lower AUCs of 0.646 (95% CI 0.561–0.731; p = 0.001), 0.617 (95% CI 0.532–0.703; p = 0.008), and 0.677 (95% CI 0.596–0.759; p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 4a).

Discussion

In this study, we identified a TH volume threshold > 29 cm3 that demonstrated good sensitivity and specificity in predicting short-term unfavorable outcomes in patients with aSAH. This quantitative marker may serve as a practical metric for early risk stratification in clinical settings. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report a specific TH threshold associated with early outcome using a standardized semiautomated segmentation method integrated into routine clinical software. This enhances both the external validity and the translational applicability of our findings [26].

Our results are consistent with previous volumetric studies. Lagares et al. identified a TH cutoff > 20 cm3 as an independent predictor of poor functional outcome and mortality in a prospective cohort [21]. Kramer et al. showed that IVH volume > 20 cm3 was associated with increased risk of complications and poor prognosis [19]. More recently, Yuan et al. reported that total bleeding volume assessed by automated methods correlated with delayed cerebral ischemia and unfavorable outcomes, with an optimal cutoff near 26 cm3 [20]. The threshold identified in our study aligns with these prior values and further supports the clinical utility of volumetric quantification in aSAH.

Semiautomated volumetric tools offer a reproducible and time-efficient alternative to manual segmentation. While manual methods remain the gold standard in terms of accuracy, they are resource-intensive and impractical in emergency contexts [15, 16]. In contrast, semiautomated methods reduce interobserver variability and enable rapid volumetric estimation with minimal operator input [22, 26]. Traditional grading scales such as the Fisher and Hijdra scores are widely used in clinical practice but are limited by their qualitative nature, interrater variability, and inability to capture complex spatial blood distribution [11, 17]. Recent comparative studies confirm that volumetric methods provide superior prognostic performance, especially when assessing risk of complications such as delayed cerebral ischemia, hydrocephalus, and poor neurological outcome [18, 22, 23].

In our cohort, semi-automated quantification of TH volume showed strong prognostic accuracy for unfavorable outcome (AUC 0.791, 95% CI 0.721–0.861), with higher discriminative ability than conventional qualitative grading. In line with this, Eagles et al. reported modest AUCs for the modified Fisher and modified Graeb scores in predicting delayed cerebral ischemia (0.608 and 0.618, respectively), with only a slight improvement when both were combined (0.647) [30]. Furthermore, when assessed against functional outcome, the modified Graeb score achieved AUCs of 0.65–0.68 for early poor outcome [31]. Overall, these findings suggest that semi-automated volumetry may provide improved prognostic discrimination compared with qualitative scales and could be considered for gradual integration into clinical workflows as tools become more widely available.

Although the prognostic role of SAH and IVH volumes has been separately explored [19, 21], our findings support the integration of all bleeding compartments into a single volumetric metric. This unified TH measurement may better reflect the global burden of early brain injury, including effects related to raised intracranial pressure, parenchymal compression, and cerebrospinal fluid circulation impairment [6, 7, 10]. Previous work by our group also demonstrated an association between TH volume and shunt-dependent hydrocephalus, further supporting its clinical relevance [26].

Importantly, volumetric thresholds such as the one proposed in our study could contribute to standardized classification systems for aSAH and be incorporated into predictive models for outcome or decision-support tools [20, 23, 24]. Early identification of high-volume bleeds could help tailor monitoring intensity and inform discussions with patients and families about prognosis [5, 8, 9].

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective single-center analysis, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, treatment modality was not controlled for in the analysis. Third, although segmentation was performed using a standardized method, some degree of observer-dependent variation may persist. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings, assess long-term predictive value, and evaluate the performance of volumetric analysis against emerging deep learning–based approaches [23].

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that semiautomated volumetric quantification of aSAH using clinically integrated software is a feasible and objective method to assess hemorrhage burden. Our findings support the role of total intracranial blood volume as an early prognostic biomarker. A total hemorrhage volume above 29 cm3 was independently associated with unfavorable outcome, suggesting that this threshold may assist in early risk stratification and clinical decision-making.
