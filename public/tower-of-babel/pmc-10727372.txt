Effects of Long‐Term Home Exercise in Participants With Peripheral Artery Disease
Authors: Andrew W Gardner, Polly S Montgomery, Ming Wang, Menglu Liang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis randomized controlled trial compared long‐term changes in peak walking time (PWT) and exercise time‐to‐minimum calf muscle oxygen saturation (StO2) in symptomatic participants with peripheral artery disease following a long‐term home exercise program (HEP), a short‐term supervised exercise therapy (SET) program that transitioned to a long‐term HEP (SET/HEP), and a control intervention.Methods and ResultsFor the first 3 months, HEP and SET/HEP groups performed intermittent walking to mild‐to‐moderate claudication pain, whereas the control group performed light resistance training. For the subsequent 15 months, the HEP group continued their exercise program, the SET/HEP group transitioned from SET to the HEP program, and the control group transitioned to only receive walking advice. PWT increased significantly from baseline to month 18 in the HEP group (408±279 meters to 814±393 meters, P<0.001) and in the SET/HEP group (457±288 meters to 818±313 meters, P<0.001). Exercise time‐to‐minimum calf muscle StO2 increased significantly from baseline to month 18 in the HEP group (238±241 seconds to 497±485 seconds, P<0.05) and in the SET/HEP group (296±289 seconds to 620±450 seconds, P<0.001). These changes in PWT and exercise time‐to‐minimum calf muscle StO2 were greater than in the control group (P<0.001 and P<0.01, respectively). Additionally, the change in exercise time‐to‐minimum calf muscle StO2 was correlated with the change in PWT in both exercise groups combined (r=0.601, P=0.0015).ConclusionsLong‐term HEP and SET/HEP were efficacious in improving PWT and exercise time‐to‐minimum calf muscle StO2 in symptomatic participants with peripheral artery disease, and these changes were correlated with each other.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT00618670.Keywords: claudication, exercise, peripheral vascular diseaseSubject Categories: Peripheral Vascular Disease, Exercise

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

In symptomatic participants with peripheral artery disease, structured long‐term home exercise was performed with low attrition and high adherence, and was efficacious in improving peak walking time and calf muscle oxygen saturation.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Structured long‐term home exercise is a feasible intervention to improve both claudication and microvascular function of the calf muscle in symptomatic participants with peripheral artery disease.

Structured long‐term home exercise is a feasible intervention to improve both claudication and microvascular function of the calf muscle in symptomatic participants with peripheral artery disease.

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is highly prevalent worldwide affecting >237 million people. 1 A common symptom of PAD is claudication, 2 which impairs walking, 3 physical function, 4 physical activity, 5 and health‐related quality of life. 6

Supervised exercise therapy (SET) is a class IA recommendation for the treatment of symptomatic PAD. 7 However, SET is underused because of several limitations that include being rarely prescribed by clinicians, 8 patients having little time and great travel difficulties to exercise facilities, 9 having relatively few programs available, 10 and nearly 70% of eligible patients are either not interested in participating or report that SET is too inconvenient. 11 , 12 Of the relatively few patients who enroll in SET, 25% of patients drop out primarily due to their own decision to withdraw. 11 Given that the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services provides coverage for SET to patients with PAD, which includes 36 sessions during a 12‐week period, with an additional 36 sessions covered throughout their lifetime, 13 , 14 SET is by its nature a highly efficacious but short‐term experience for patients. Few SET programs have evaluated the long‐term efficacy, and the challenges described for short‐term SET programs become magnified in a long‐term setting.

To examine a viable alternative to SET, we developed a structured home exercise program (HEP) that used a step activity monitor to provide a similar exercise prescription to SET with fewer barriers. 15 , 16 We previously found HEP to be efficacious in these 2 short‐term trials, 17 , 18 and a recent meta‐analysis supports the efficacy of HEP, 17 particularly if objective activity monitoring devices were used. 18 , 19 Although HEPs may be similar 15 or inferior 18 , 20 to SET programs in increasing peak walking time (PWT) in the short‐term, they can elicit walking improvements that are clinically meaningful. 21 Furthermore, HEPs offer some potential advantages in a long‐term setting, which is part of the rationale for the strategy to transition patients with PAD from a short‐term SET to a long‐term HEP. 14 Limited data are available on the long‐term effects of a structured, monitored HEP, and on the transition from a SET to a long‐term HEP. Therefore, this study was designed to address these knowledge gaps.

This prospective, randomized controlled clinical trial compared the long‐term changes in the primary outcome measures of PWT and exercise time to minimum calf muscle oxygen saturation (StO2) in symptomatic participants with PAD following a long‐term HEP, a short‐term SET that transitioned to a long‐term HEP, and a control group.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Participants

Approval and Informed Consent

The institutional review board at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center and the Research and Development Committee at the Oklahoma City Veterans Affairs Medical Center approved the procedures of this study. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant at the beginning of the investigation.

Recruitment

Participants underwent baseline evaluations in the study from October 2006 to July 2012. Participants were recruited from vascular laboratories and vascular clinics from the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center and the Oklahoma City Veterans Affairs Medical Center.

Medical Screening Through History and Physical Examination

Participants were evaluated in the morning at the Clinical Research Center, at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center. Participants arrived fasted, but were permitted to take their usual medications. To begin the study visit, participants were evaluated with a medical history and physical examination in which demographic information, height, weight, waist circumference, cardiovascular risk factors, comorbid conditions, claudication history, ankle/brachial index (ABI), blood samples, and a list of current medications were obtained.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants with symptomatic PAD were included in this study if they met the following criteria: (1) a history of ambulatory leg pain, (2) ambulatory leg pain confirmed by treadmill exercise, 3 and (3) an ABI ≤0.90 2 at rest or ≤0.73 after exercise. 22 Participants were excluded for the following conditions: (1) absence of PAD (ABI >0.90 at rest and ABI >0.73 after exercise), (2) noncompressible vessels (ABI ≥1.40), (3) asymptomatic PAD, (4) use of medications indicated for the treatment of claudication (cilostazol or pentoxifylline) initiated within 3 months before investigation, (5) exercise limited by other diseases or conditions, (6) active cancer, (7) end stage renal disease defined as stage 5 chronic kidney disease, (8) abnormal liver function, and (9) failure to complete the baseline run‐in phase within 3 weeks, as described previously. 18

Interventions

HEP Group

We used a step activity monitor (StepWatch3; Orthoinnovations, Oklahoma City, OK) to accurately record the duration and cadence of ambulation during exercise training sessions. 15 The HEP consisted of 3 months of intermittent walking to mild‐to‐moderate claudication pain at least 3 days per week at a self‐selected pace, as previously described. 15 , 16 At the completion of the 3‐month HEP and posttests, participants were offered the opportunity to continue in HEP for an additional 15 months. Participants who agreed then returned their step activity monitor and logbook for a 15‐ to 30‐minute meeting with the research staff at the end of month 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18. Similar to the first 3 months of HEP, data were downloaded from the step activity monitor, results were reviewed, feedback was provided, and strategies were discussed to overcome any potential exercise barriers for the upcoming 3 months of training. Adherence to exercise was confirmed by recording the step data from each exercise session.

SET/HEP Group

Exercise sessions in our supervised exercise program were performed while wearing a step activity monitor, as previously described. 15 The SET program consisted of 3 months of intermittent treadmill walking to mild‐to‐moderate claudication pain 3 days per week, as previously described. 15 , 16 , 23 Exercise sessions progressively increased during the program from 15 to 40 minutes. 16 At the completion of the 3‐month SET and posttests, participants were offered the opportunity to continue exercising by transitioning to HEP for an additional 15 months. Participants who agreed then returned their step activity monitor and logbook for a 15‐ to 30‐minute meeting with the research staff at the end of month 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18. Data were downloaded from the step activity monitor, results were reviewed, feedback was provided, and strategies were discussed to overcome any potential exercise barriers for the upcoming 3 months of training. Adherence to exercise was confirmed by recording the step data from each exercise session.

Control Group

Attention‐control light resistance training was performed in a supervised program 3 times per week for 3 months, without any walking exercise. 16 This was done to match attention received by the control and SET groups from research staff during the first 3 months in an attempt to rule out attention as a possible explanation for group differences in change scores of outcome measures. At the completion of 3 months of light resistance training, participants were given walking advice without further supervision and were asked if they would be willing to be contacted in 15 months to repeat the research testing. Participants who agreed were then contacted and scheduled for the 18‐month follow‐up testing.

Tests and Measurements

Graded Maximal Treadmill Test

Participants performed a graded treadmill test to determine study eligibility, and then repeated the test on a following visit within 1 week. The primary exercise outcome of PWT and a secondary outcome of claudication onset time (COT) were obtained on the repeat test as previously described. 3 Calf muscle StO2 was continuously measured over the medial gastrocnemius muscle of the more symptomatic leg before and during the treadmill test using continuous‐wave, near‐infrared spectroscopy (InSpectra model 325; Hutchinson Technology, Hutchinson, MN). 24 A resting baseline value of calf muscle StO2 was measured following standing on the treadmill for 2 minutes preceding the exercise test, and the primary vascular outcome of exercise time taken to reach the minimum calf StO2 value was recorded. 24

Six‐Minute Walk Test

Participants performed a 6‐minute walk test, and the pain‐free and total walking distances were recorded, as previously described. 25

Walking Impairment Questionnaire

Self‐reported ambulatory ability was obtained using a validated questionnaire for PAD participants that assesses ability to walk at various speeds and distances, and to climb stairs. 26

Medical Outcomes Study Short‐Form 36

The self‐reported physical function subscale of health‐related quality of life was assessed from the Medical Outcomes Study Short‐Form 36 instrument. 27

Physical Activity Monitoring

Daily walking was measured using a step activity monitor during 7 consecutive days, as we have previously described. 5 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 The total number of steps taken each day and the number of minutes spent walking each day was quantified. Additionally, the number of steps taken each day and the amount of time spent in moderate intensity activity in the community setting were determined, defined as exceeding a cadence of 60 steps per minute by participants with claudication. 5

Circulating Inflammatory and Vascular Biomarkers

Blood samples obtained during the medical screening were sent to the clinical laboratory at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center for the measurement of high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein. Additionally, samples were assayed for the measurement of serum vascular endothelial growth factor‐A, as previously described. 32

Randomization and Minimization of Bias

At the completion of all tests to assess the baseline primary and secondary outcome measures, each participant was randomized to 1 of the 3 groups described above. Participants were randomized using the Number Cruncher Statistical System random number program with blocking to assure that group sizes never differed by >2 at any time during allocation. To reduce selection bias, the allocation sequence was generated by statistical personnel at the Clinical Research Center, and the randomization sequence was concealed from the investigative team until the moment of assignment. Although it was not possible to blind participants to their group assignment, we minimized performance bias by blinding the group assignment to nurses and physicians in the Clinical Research Center and study personnel who conducted the outcome assessments. Exercise technicians administered the interventions but were blinded to the outcome assessments to reduce detection bias. To minimize reporting bias, all data obtained in this trial as primary and secondary outcome measurements are reported in the tables. To minimize attrition bias, all participants were contacted to schedule research tests at month 3 and month 18 regardless of adherence during the interventions. Finally, all participants received their allocated exercise treatment or control condition.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized for measurable variables (eg, baseline, demographic, and clinical characteristics, and exercise intervention and performance measures) by groups (control, SET/HEP, HEP) or by time points (baseline, 3 months, 18 months) as means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. For exercise intervention measures, the changes from baseline to 3 months and those from 3 months to 18 months were the metrics used for measuring impact of exercise program. For exercise performance measures, daily ambulatory activity measures, and vascular measures, the changes from baseline at 3 months and 18 months were considered as metrics for comparisons. For continuous measures, the 1‐way ANOVA or Kruskal‐Wallis tests were used for 3‐group comparisons, and 2‐sample t tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests for 2‐group comparisons, as appropriate; for categorical variables, χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests were adopted for group comparison, as appropriate. Specifically, for the primary and secondary outcomes of interest, we used the Dunnett test to further assess the 2 experimental treatment groups against the control group by incorporating the dependencies between these comparisons. Correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between outcomes by calculating Pearson correlation coefficient with P values based on t tests to conclude if it was significantly different from 0. Furthermore, we followed the intent‐to‐treat analysis principle for analysis by using mixed‐effects models with random intercepts under the assumption of missing at random. The baseline measures as well as potential confounding variables were adjusted to evaluate overall group comparison. Bonferroni correction was used for the P value adjustment of multiple comparisons of multivariate outcomes from each domain. Significance for all analyses was determined using 2‐sided tests with the significance level of 0.05. The data analysis was performed in SAS version 9.4.

Discussion

There are several novel findings in this long‐term randomized controlled trial focused on home exercise. Thirty‐eight out of 105 participants (36%) who completed the 3‐month HEP and SET exercise interventions agreed to participate in the long‐term HEP phase of the study. In these 38 participants, there was low attrition and high adherence to long‐term HEP. Secondly, both long‐term HEP and SET/HEP were efficacious in improving the primary outcome measures of PWT and exercise time to minimum calf muscle StO2, as well as secondary outcomes of COT, 6‐minute walk distance, and the number of steps taken and time spent in moderate intensity daily activity. Finally, the change in exercise time to minimum calf muscle StO2 was correlated with the change in PWT with long‐term exercise.

Exercise Adherence and Attrition

Participants who agreed to continue in the optional long‐term phase of the study had high adherence during the short‐term HEP and SET programs, with both exceeding 90% of the prescribed 36 sessions. During the subsequent 15‐month long‐term phase, the participants who continued in the HEP completed an average of 2.5 exercise sessions per week, whereas the participants who transitioned from the SET program to HEP completed an average of >3 exercise sessions per week. Although 4 other studies have examined HEP for 9 33 to 12 34 , 35 , 36 months, only 2 measured the exercise frequency, and found that participants completed an average of 3.5 weekly exercise sessions for 9 months, 33 and 2.8 and 3.5 weekly exercise sessions for high‐ and low‐intensity groups for 12 months, respectively. 34 The current study supports this observation, and extends the finding by showing that exercise adherence remains high at month 18 of HEP. Furthermore, there was low attrition with long‐term HEP, because only 4 of 38 participants in both exercise groups did not complete the program.

Exercise Performance

A major finding was that PWT and COT were increased from month 3 to month 18 during the long‐term HEP phase of this study. This was evident in both exercise groups, indicating that improvements in PWT and COT occurred regardless of whether participants initially performed short‐term HEP and then continued HEP for an additional 15 months, or whether they performed short‐term SET and then transitioned to long‐term HEP. These improvements in PWT and COT following long‐term HEP supports the efficacy of short‐term HEP in our previous studies 15 , 16 and those of other HEP interventions that used activity monitoring, 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 or cognitive behavioral therapy. 40 , 41 Furthermore, the current study extends these observations by demonstrating that not only are improvements in PWT and COT maintained with long‐term HEP, but that additional gains beyond short‐term changes are possible as well. It is important to recognize that the increases in COT during the long‐term phase in both exercise groups between month 3 and month 18 were clinically important as well as statistically significant, because they exceeded the anchor‐based large minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 101 seconds. 21 The accompanying long‐term increases in PWT were also clinically important, because they exceeded the anchor‐based moderate MCID of 141 seconds, and they approached the large MCID threshold of 241 seconds. 21

A second key result related to exercise performance following long‐term HEP was that the pattern of change in 6‐minute walk distance was similar to the changes observed with PWT and COT. The 6‐minute walk distance increased during the long‐term phase from month 3 to month 18 and accounted for most of the change from baseline. This finding was evident in both exercise groups, indicating that improvements in 6‐minute walk distance occurred with long‐term HEP after a preceding 3‐month exercise intervention of either HEP or SET. The improvements in 6‐minute walk distance following long‐term HEP supports the efficacy of most shorter‐term HEP interventions 17 but not all. 33 Specifically, our results support a recent meta‐analysis that found 6‐minute walk distance increased in participants with PAD by an average of 0.28 standard deviations following structured HEPs compared with control groups. In a recent trial that found no change in 6‐minute walk distance following a 9‐month HEP, a likely explanation was that periodic onsite visits with research staff were not included throughout the HEP. In contrast, the current study required participants to visit the research center and meet with staff every 3 months. The 6‐minute walk distance increased by >40 meters during the long‐term exercise phase in the exercise groups, which was clinically important, because this exceeded the anchor‐based large MCID of 34 meters. 21

Daily Ambulatory Activity

The steps taken and time spent in moderate intensity daily walking activity increased in both exercise groups from baseline to month 18. The increases in daily steps and daily time spent in moderate intensity activity occurred during the short‐term phase of the HEP and SET/HEP programs, and they were maintained during the long‐term phase of the study. Our data indicate that participants with PAD who complete a short‐term HEP or SET program increase their daily walking at moderate intensity, and this was maintained during long‐term exercise. It is important to note that the total daily steps and the total daily activity time were not changed with either the HEP or SET/HEP interventions, which supports previous randomized controlled trials by us 15 , 16 and others. 33 , 37 , 39 These observations indicate that the daily steps and time spent in moderate intensity are more responsive to exercise interventions than total daily steps and time spent in activity, and therefore may be the key outcome measures to obtain in future trials.

The clinical importance of these daily activity findings is that after 3 months and 18 months of exercise intervention, both exercise groups on average met the physical activity time‐intensity guidelines of accumulating >150 minutes of moderate intensity physical activity in a week (>22 min/d). 42 We previously estimated that >1660 daily steps taken at moderate cadence was an optimal threshold for meeting the physical activity time‐intensity guidelines, 43 and both exercise groups far exceeded this clinical cut point after 3 months and 18 months of exercise. Thus, participants not only improve their walking ability with a short‐term and long‐term exercise program, but they also may experience several cardiovascular and overall health benefits associated with meeting the physical activity time‐intensity guidelines. 43 Finally, the increases from baseline in daily time spent in moderate intensity activity following exercise intervention were clinically important, because they exceeded the anchor‐based large MCID of 11 minu/d. 44

Vascular Function

The main finding related to vascular function was that the exercise time to minimum calf StO2 increased from baseline to month 18 in both exercise groups, and that there was an increase during the long‐term HEP phase of this study. This indicates that an improvement in the microcirculation of the calf muscle occurred following exercise intervention, because it took longer to desaturate to the minimum calf StO2 value during the treadmill walking test. The improvement in exercise time to minimum calf StO2 was achieved in participants in both long‐term exercise groups. The increased exercise time to minimum calf StO2 following long‐term HEP supports the efficacy of our previous short‐term HEP intervention, 16 and extends these observations by demonstrating that additional improvements in the microcirculation of the calf muscle occur beyond the short‐term changes. Furthermore, the finding that the changes in exercise time to minimum calf StO2 were correlated with changes in PWT following long‐term exercise suggests that improvements in local oxygen saturation of the calf during exercise partially explains the increase in PWT. The clinical significance of these observations is that improvements in the microcirculation of the calf muscle during exercise is a possible physiologic mechanism for the improvement in PWT.

Limitations

Although this trial supports the long‐term efficacy of HEP for PAD participants, several limitations exist. One limitation is that participants volunteered to participate in this study. Thus, a self‐selection bias may exist because they may represent those who had greater interest in participating, better access to transportation to the research center, and better health than PAD participants who did not volunteer. A second limitation is that the participants who completed the 3‐month short‐term study who agreed to continue in the long‐term phase of the study represent the most motivated participants. However, in post hoc analyses, the participants who continued in the long‐term phase of the study were not different either at baseline or at 3‐month follow‐up on PWT, COT, 6‐minute walk distance, and Walking Impairment Questionnaire scores than those who only participated in the short‐term phase. Furthermore, no differences were found for the prevalence of comorbid conditions. Another limitation is that the results of this study are only generalizable to participants with symptomatic PAD, and may not be applicable to participants with less severe or more severe PAD. Another limitation is that although participants were randomized into 1 of the groups before intervention, it is possible that the motivation to participate in the trial was different among the groups. There is some attrition bias associated with the data, because 4 out of the 72 participants who agreed to participate in the long‐term phase of the study did not complete the month 18 evaluations. Additionally, as is true with exercise studies, blinding of the participants to the exercise interventions was not possible, leading to some performance bias in the data. Finally, other bias may exist in the study that cannot be accounted.

Conclusions and Clinical Implications

Long‐term HEP and SET/HEP were efficacious in improving PWT and exercise time to minimum calf muscle StO2 in symptomatic participants with PAD, and these changes were correlated with each other. The clinical significance is that long‐term HEP and SET/HEP interventions that use activity monitoring were efficacious to improve both claudication and microvascular function of the calf muscle in symptomatic participants with PAD.
