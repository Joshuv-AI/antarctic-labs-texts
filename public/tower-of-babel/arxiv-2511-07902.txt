The Influence of Stable Photon Sphere Advent on Orbital Precession in moving towards the Extremality: Periapsis Shift as a Gateway to the Weak Gravity Conjecture
Mohammad Ali S. Afshar, Jafar Sadeghi

Abstract
While the effects of solar mass change can be neglected in studies of solar periapsis shifts—given the relevant timescales and magnitude of change—the influence of a black hole’s dynamic and chaotic mass variation on the Periapsis Shift of test particles in its surrounding spacetime demands a detailed and meticulous investigation. Recognizing that black hole mass variation is inherently a continuous and dynamic process, but the extended timescales required for such variations allow us, to employ a static, frame-by-frame approach. We’re assuming constant mass within individual frames, while permitting inter-frame mass evolution to prob the effects of mass loss on orbital dynamics. Using this method, we investigate whether the Periapsis Shift in the extremal limit can serve as evidence for the Weak Gravity Conjecture (WGC), addressing the conjecture’s role in preserving black hole integrity during evaporation. Subsequently, we analyze the Periapsis Shift under Aschenbach-like conditions, where a stable photon sphere generates non-monotonic orbital velocity profiles, to assess its dynamical impact on relativistic precession. Finally, we synthesize the combined effects of extremality and the presence of stable photon sphere, revealing profound modifications to the Periapsis Shift profile, including prograde-to-retrograde transitions and radial dependencies. Our results demonstrate that the Periapsis Shift—not merely its magnitude but its qualitative orbital behavior—allows a meaningful experimental probe for the WGC in strong-field regimes.

Periapsis:

The anomalous precession of Mercury’s perihelion, which reveals a fundamental limitation of Newtonian gravity in strong gravitational fields, stands as one of the most pivotal confirmations of Einstein’s general theory of relativity.

In a purely Keplerian orbit—governed by Newtonian gravity under the assumption of a single point mass—the orbital path should form a closed ellipse. The point in a planet’s orbit where it is closest to the Sun is termed the perihelion. Newtonian studies and calculations indicated that this point is not fixed, but shifts slightly forward with each orbital revolution (in the direction of motion)

With the advent of Einstein’s general theory of relativity, gravity is no longer described as a force, but as the curvature of spacetime induced by mass and energy. This curvature enables the prediction of an additional effective force through geodesic motion, compensating for the shortcomings of the Newtonian framework and rendering the previously unexplained precession fully consistent with observations. In other words, the Sun’s mass curves spacetime, altering Mercury’s orbital geometry. Near the Sun, spacetime curvature becomes significant, causing Mercury’s orbit to deviate from a perfect ellipse—this non-Euclidean geometry accounts for the observed precession [1,2].

Aschenbach-Like Effect:

The Aschenbach effect represents a direct prediction of general relativity in the strong gravitational field regime, and its observation confirms that spacetime near black holes behaves precisely as Einstein’s equations forecast. The origins of the Aschenbach effect can be traced to pioneering studies investigating the motion of relativistic particles around rotating (Kerr) black holes. While frame-dragging—a hallmark prediction of general relativity—had already been well-established, systematic analyses of orbital stability near Kerr black holes gained momentum toward the end of the twentieth century
22
. Aschenbach, through analytical and numerical simulations, discovered an anomalous reduction in the orbital velocity of test particles extremely close to the event horizon just prior to infall. This non-monotonic behavior challenges the assumptions of conventional Keplerian disk models, which are frequently employed in simplified accretion disk theories. Rigorous derivations from Einstein’s field equations reveal that this unpredicted deviation in the angular velocity profile near the event horizon, which starkly contradicts Newtonian expectations, fundamentally stems from the interaction between extreme gravitational curvature and frame-dragging due to rotation. It has been extensively studied for rotating black holes across all three major asymptotic geometries: flat, anti-de Sitter (AdS), and de Sitter (dS) spacetimes
23
.

However, this raises a fundamental question: Is this behavior exclusive to rotating black holes, or can static black holes—devoid of intrinsic rotation and therefore frame-dragging—also reproduce similar non-monotonic features, albeit through an alternative mechanism?

To answer, it should be stated that although static black holes lack rotational effects such as frame-dragging, they retain strong gravitational curvature. Consequently, the emergence of a non-monotonic angular velocity profile depends on identifying an alternative mechanism capable of emulating the dynamical role of frame-dragging.

Finally, although observational manifestations may appear similar, the governing physics differs significantly. This distinction necessitates referring to the phenomenon in static black holes as the Aschenbach-like effect, rather than the Aschenbach effect itself.

II.1
Null and Time-like geodesics

In any black hole model, the form of the effective potential derived from the action and the black hole metric—representing the nature of field interactions—fundamentally determines the behavior and geodesic structure of the surrounding spacetime. These geodesics may exhibit distinct turning points, leading to either open or closed trajectories, depending on the underlying geometry.

For null geodesics (photon trajectories or massless particles), closed trajectories are referred to as photon rings or photon spheres, whereas for timelike geodesics (massive particle trajectories), such closed loops are termed timelike circular orbits (TCOs).

Both types of orbits—photon spheres and TCOs—may be either stable or unstable, contingent upon the curvature and parametric structure of the black hole spacetime.

The stability and existence of these orbits are rigorously determined through the analysis of the effective potential’s critical points, specifically by evaluating the second derivative of the potential at extremal radii. Stable orbits correspond to local minima of the potential, while unstable orbits arise at local maxima. In subsequent sections, we will elaborate on the precise mathematical equations employed to compute and analyze these orbital configurations.

VI
Discussion about evaporation and the WGC evidence

The Sun, as a relatively young star, exhibits negligible mass loss on human timescales, rendering any observable changes in Mercury’s perihelion due to solar mass variations so minute that the assumption of constant solar mass is both scientifically acceptable and approximately consistent with theoretical expectations.

Conversely, while black hole mass loss may appear imperceptible on human timescales, it becomes significant when measured against the Sun’s own evolutionary timeline. Thus, if we adopt the Sun’s structural mass evolution rate as a reference timescale, the dynamic mass evolution of black holes emerges as a non-negligible phenomenon capable of profoundly influencing the orbital precession of particles around these objects. It is important to emphasize that while a truly accurate understanding would require continuous dynamical analysis, valuable insights into black hole evaporation and the emergence of conditions relevant to the WGC can still be obtained through frame-by-frame analysis—assuming constant black hole mass within each individual frame while allowing mass to vary between frames.

Within the framework of general relativity, the geometric structure and spacetime dynamics of ultracompact gravitational objects are predominantly governed by their mass. As illustrated in Fig. (
2
) and Fig. (
19
), the event horizon’s geometric location (in terms of radial coordinate
r
) recedes incrementally as the black hole’s charge-to-mass ratio (
q/M
) increases—a process effectively equivalent to gradual mass loss.

This recession, as observed in our previous studies, is clearly reflected in the behavior and location of the photon sphere. Consequently, the progressive radial recession of the photon sphere can serve as a key indicator of the system’s transition toward the extremal limit.

As an interesting corollary, since the U-PS typically defines the boundary of the black hole shadow, analogous structural changes in the shadow can be expected throughout this transition.

However, black hole periapsis shift analysis, specially with the existence of S-PS beyond the horizon, appears to provide a stronger observational framework for investigating the WGC compared to photon sphere studies alone. While the latter relies solely on geometric positional changes, black hole periapsis shift analysis captures both spatial and dynamical information:

- The emergence location and magnitude of the periapsis shift for particle orbits—similar to the event horizon and photon sphere—evolve as
q/M
increases.

- More importantly, the orbital dynamics undergo qualitative transformations, particularly transitions from prograde to retrograde motion (and back to prograde) in the presence of a stable photon sphere (Fig. (
18
)). These transitions, driven by modifications to the effective potential near the stable photon sphere, result in measurable changes to orbital energy profiles. Such variations directly influence the redshift or blueshift of emitted radiation, potentially will be yielding detectable observational signatures.

In summary:

- The radial recession of the event horizon, the photon sphere and the evolving periapsis shift profile provide a compelling empirical signature for distinguishing evaporative dominance over accretion during black hole evolution in moving toward the extremal limit, Which will ultimately lead to a final and permissible limit radius for the emergence of each one.

- The persistence of black hole-like dynamics at extremality (
q/M\geq 1
)—including the presence of photon spheres, consistent periapsis shift behavior, and observable prograde/retrograde transitions in specific parametric configurations(Fig. (
18
)—serves as significant evidence for the formation of black holes containing superextremal charges. This further supports the realization of conditions necessary for the emission of such charges and the practical fulfillment of the WGC.

Finally, it bears repeating that these processes operate on timescales far exceeding human observational capabilities—potentially billions of years—necessitating indirect observational strategies rather than direct temporal monitoring.

VII
Conclusion

As confirmed in prior studies
4
, the periapsis shift in black hole spacetimes exhibits significant sensitivity to the model’s mass parameter. Consequently, the inherently dynamic and turbulent nature of black hole mass evolution cannot be neglected in analyses of orbital periapsis shift.

Building upon this foundation, our study pursued several interconnected objectives with careful attention to theoretical consistency and physical applicability:

1.

periapsis shift in the Extremal Limit and WGC realization:

Since evaporation via Hawking radiation constitutes a central aspect of black hole evolution, it is well showed that black holes tend toward extremality under evaporation.

In this limit, two phenomena emerge:

(i) the increasing likelihood of super-extremal charged particle emission,

(ii) the vanishing of Hawking temperature at the horizon.

Together, these effects strongly suggest that black holes may self-sustain through super-extremal emission while preserving causality and relativity, a scenario consistent with the WGC.

Since, finding as many examples as possible that can still satisfy and imitate the black hole behavior pattern in the extreme limit can serve as a basis for strengthening of the conjecture. We therefore examined whether the characteristic behavior pattern of the periapsis shift persists in the extremal limit. Our analysis revealed that for ModMax black holes in both asymptotically flat and AdS backgrounds, the periapsis shift pattern indeed survives at extremality.

Consequently, in addition to previously established extremal indicators:

- preservation of the event horizon,

- existence of unstable photon spheres beyond the horizon,

- manifestation of the Aschenbach-like effect

we now add the preservation of the periapsis shift behavior as further evidence supporting WGC realization.

2.

Influence of a minimum potential structure (S‑PS):

The emergence of an S‑PS beyond the event horizon, though not a common phenomenon, can significantly alter dynamical profiles, particularly the orbital velocity distribution near the potential minimum, producing the Aschenbach-like effect. In this work and in the first step, we demonstrated the existence of such a feature for AdS black holes in Born–Infeld massive gravity and then investigated its impact on the periapsis shift. Our findings show that the presence of the potential minimum modifies the periapsis shift, leading to an increasing its value near the S‑PS region.

3.

Combined extremal and S‑PS conditions.

Finally, we considered the simultaneous presence of an S‑PS beyond the horizon and the extremal limit. This regime yielded particularly intriguing results: not only were WGC conditions still satisfied, but the periapsis shift exhibited a richer and more diverse structure.

Specifically, the slope of periapsis shift variations near the potential minimum became sharper and more pronounced compared to the pervious case, while the orbital directionality itself underwent structural changes. In the extremal limit, the spacetime was effectively partitioned into three distinct dynamical regions, an inner prograde zone, a middle retrograde zone, and an outer prograde zone (Fig. (
18
)), in contrast to the usual one-directional (Fig. (
6
)) or two-region (prograde/retrograde) division observed without an S‑PS, Fig. (
11
).

This variation in motion behavior in different periapsis shift regions of case 3 can be proposed as an interesting experimental indicator, firstly for the possibility of investigating the existence of a black hole in an extremal form and secondly as evidence for the possibility of realizing WGC in strong field regimes.
