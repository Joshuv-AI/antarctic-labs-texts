Immune-epigenetic convergence in biomaterial-guided tissue regeneration
Authors: Wantong Wen, Zhen Wang, Margaret Armintrout, Annabella Duong, Le Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biomaterials-driven immunomodulation and epigenetic regulation are increasingly recognized as key determinants of cell behavior and regenerative outcomes, yet they have largely been studied independently. Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNAs, regulate gene expression, cellular plasticity, and lineage commitment in response to biomaterial cues. Meanwhile, immune responses, particularly macrophage polarization and the foreign body response, critically influence the inflammatory and regenerative microenvironment after biomaterial implantation. However, regenerative outcomes are rarely governed by either mechanism in isolation. Epigenetic regulation and immune responses are tightly coupled during host-material interactions, so controlling one axis without addressing the other may limit the ability to achieve long-term success. Emerging evidence indicates that these processes are mutually reinforcing, forming a coupled regulatory system in which inflammatory signals reshape the epigenome, whereas epigenetic states regulate how immune cells perceive and respond to environmental cues. Notably, key biomaterial properties such as stiffness, topography, wettability, biochemical composition, and degradability can act as convergent upstream regulators that simultaneously regulate epigenetic states and immune responses, yet this intersection remains largely unexplored. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in biomaterials-driven epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation, emphasizing the emerging immune-epigenetic convergence in tissue regeneration. By integrating current knowledge with proposed design strategies, we establish a framework for engineering biomaterials that coordinate immune and epigenetic regulation to achieve more predictable and durable regenerative outcomes.

Introduction

Tissue engineering has emerged as a transformative field in regenerative medicine, aiming to restore or replace tissues and organs. The widely recognized concept of tissue engineering was introduced by Langer and Vacanti [1] in 1993. It is described as an interdisciplinary approach that applies the principles of engineering and life science to develop biological substitutes for the restoration, maintenance, and improvement of tissue functions. Biomaterials have played a vital role in advancing tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [2]. Historically, biomaterials have evolved from passive structures that primarily provided physical support to designs that actively interact with biological environments to better mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM) and promote tissue regeneration. However, precisely controlling cell fate within engineered environments remains a persistent challenge. Even with sophisticated scaffold designs, existing approaches often fall short in presenting biochemical and biophysical signals with the spatiotemporal precision needed to mimic the native cell niche. There is an urgent need to develop smart biomaterials that can actively and dynamically interact with cells to direct their fate decisions, thereby overcoming the current barriers in tissue engineering.

The intersection of materials science and cell biology has opened new avenues for engineering more effective, precisely targeted therapies in regenerative medicine. In this context, biomaterials-driven epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation represent two complementary strategies that harness the intrinsic and extrinsic properties of materials to direct cell fate and promote tissue regeneration. Epigenetics refers to heritable and reversible modifications of gene expression that occur without altering the underlying DNA sequence, governing fundamental aspects of cell behavior, from stem cell differentiation to somatic cell reprogramming, through mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNAs. Researchers have begun exploiting biomaterial properties to tune the epigenetic state of cells, thereby altering their proliferation and differentiation patterns to facilitate tissue regeneration [3]. In parallel, the body's immune system has long been recognized as an equally critical orchestrator of tissue regeneration [4]. The sequence and intensity of immune responses following injury often determine whether healing progresses successfully or shifts toward fibrosis and chronic inflammation [5]. Neutrophils, macrophages, T cells, and other immune cells not only contribute to the clearance of debris and pathogens but also secrete cytokines and growth factors that direct tissue reconstruction. An imbalance in the immune response, whether excessive inflammation or inadequate immune activation, can severely impair tissue repair. Consequently, a new era of immunomodulatory biomaterials has emerged. Rather than attempting to evade immune responses, immunomodulatory biomaterials are designed to actively modulate the immune environment to promote tissue regeneration.

While epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation have each advanced tissue engineering in important ways, there is a growing recognition that these two fields are closely linked, and their integration holds great potential to open new frontiers in regenerative medicine. The interplay between the immune system and the epigenome is evident in mechanisms such as inflammation-induced epigenetic alterations and, reciprocally, epigenetically trained innate immune memory, termed trained immunity. Inflammatory signals can trigger widespread chromatin remodeling in local cells, thereby altering gene expression profiles that either aid or impair tissue regeneration. Recent research suggests that appropriately regulated inflammation can increase chromatin accessibility and cellular plasticity, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration [6]. This phenomenon, known as “transflammation”, describes how transient inflammatory cues loosen chromatin structure to enable cell fate changes. However, when the inflammatory stimulus is too mild or becomes chronic, these beneficial epigenetic effects are dysregulated, contributing to impaired healing outcomes. Conversely, epigenetic modifications are key drivers of immune cell behavior. Immune cells rely heavily on epigenetic programming to swiftly adapt to environmental cues. For example, macrophages can switch between pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes by activating different gene networks via epigenetic remodeling of their chromatin. These epigenetic changes endow macrophages with a heritable “memory” of past stimulation, enabling them to rapidly modify their cytokine profile upon restimulation. This bidirectional interplay suggests that simultaneous modulation of epigenetic states and immune responses may yield synergistic effects for enhancing tissue regeneration. Notably, many biochemical and biophysical cues can influence both epigenetic programming and immune responses, providing a mechanistic basis for the design of integrated biomaterial strategies.

Yet, to date, most studies and biomaterial designs have focused on either epigenetic regulation or immunomodulation in isolation. As a result, the literature lacks a comprehensive framework that integrates these two approaches into a unified biomaterial design paradigm. Specifically, limited efforts have explored how instructive biomaterials can be engineered to simultaneously regulate inflammation and deliver epigenetic signals in a coordinated manner to promote tissue regeneration. This review aims to bridge this gap by comparing and unifying diverse biomaterial cues that govern epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation. In doing so, we propose a conceptual framework that serves as a roadmap for designing next-generation multifunctional biomaterials capable of instructing cellular behavior and actively driving tissue repair.

Epigenetic regulation and biomaterials

Introduction to epigenetics

The concept of epigenetics was first introduced in 1942 and has since been redefined multiple times [7]. It is now widely accepted that epigenetics refers to the study of molecules and mechanisms that can perpetuate alternative gene activity states in the context of the same DNA sequence. Expanding on these fundamental concepts, the primary mechanisms of epigenetic regulation include DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNA-mediated processes [8]. DNA methylation was one of the earliest mechanisms recognized in epigenetics. Subsequent studies have found that chromatin proteins and non-coding RNAs also play essential roles in epigenetic regulation. For example, histone variants and post-translational histone modifications have a significant impact on local chromatin structure, either directly or indirectly. In addition to classic concepts, increasing studies have recently shown that 3D chromatin organization, RNA modifications, and metabolic regulation of chromatin are also integral components of epigenetic regulation [[9], [10], [11]]. Unlike genetic changes, epigenetic modifications can be dynamically modulated by external stimuli such as the physical and chemical environment. These modifications play a crucial role in regulating cellular functions and are essential for proper development, differentiation, and environmental adaptation across the entire lifespan.

Notably, methylation at the C5 position of cytosine nucleotides, resulting in 5-methylcytosine (5-mC), is a hallmark of DNA methylation. It commonly occurs in cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) islands and is regulated by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and ten-eleven translocation (TET) methylcytosine dioxygenases (TETs) [12]. DNA methylation was first identified in bacteria and later recognized in mammals. It is now considered a conserved epigenetic regulatory mechanism involved in many biological processes, including genome stability, transcriptional regulation, development, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, transposon repression, aging, and disease. In mammalian genomes, DNA methylation mainly occurs at CpG dinucleotides. Methylation at promoter CpG islands is generally associated with transcriptional repression, while methylation in gene bodies or repetitive regions may have more context-dependent functions. DNA methylation patterns are mainly established by the de novo DNA methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT3B, and are maintained after DNA replication by DNMT1. These methylation marks can be recognized by methyl-CpG-binding proteins, which further recruit repressive chromatin regulators, such as histone deacetylases and heterochromatin-associated factors. In contrast, DNA methylation can be removed passively through DNA replication or actively through TET-mediated oxidation, indicating that DNA methylation is a dynamic epigenetic modification [13].

Histone modifications, including methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and sumoylation, occur primarily on the histone tails and play critical roles in modulating chromatin structure and gene accessibility [14,15]. These modifications are heritable yet reversible, and are dynamically regulated by specialized enzyme groups known as "writers" (enzymes that add modifications), "erasers" (enzymes that remove modifications), and "readers" (proteins that recognize specific epigenetic marks and subsequently recruit transcriptional regulators) [[16], [17], [18]]. Specifically, writers are enzymes that deposit epigenetic marks, including DNMTs and histone acetyltransferases (HATs), which introduce DNA methylation or histone acetylation to activate or suppress gene expression; erasers, such as histone deacetylases (HDACs) and histone demethylases, remove these chemical modifications, enabling dynamic reversibility of epigenetic states; readers are specialized domains or proteins (e.g., bromodomains, chromodomains, PHD fingers) that recognize and bind specific histone marks, thereby recruiting transcription factors, chromatin remodelers, or other cofactors to execute downstream regulatory functions. Together, these writer–eraser–reader systems create a highly dynamic, multilayered epigenetic landscape that orchestrates cell fate decisions and tissue-specific gene expression programs [19]. Histone modifications are important epigenetic mechanisms that regulate chromatin structure and genome function. In eukaryotic cells, genomic DNA is wrapped around histone proteins to form nucleosomes. The histone proteins, especially their N-terminal tails, can be modified by different post-translational modifications. These modifications can affect chromatin accessibility and further regulate many DNA-related processes, including gene transcription, DNA replication, DNA damage repair, and three-dimensional genome organization. For example, H3K4me3 is mainly enriched at active promoters, H3K36me3 is associated with actively transcribed gene bodies and DNA repair, while H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 are generally related to transcriptional repression and heterochromatin formation. Histone acetylation usually relaxes chromatin and promotes gene activation, whereas histone methylation can either activate or repress gene expression depending on the modified residue. Therefore, histone modifications not only regulate individual genomic processes, but also coordinate the crosstalk among transcription, replication, and DNA repair [20].

Chromatin remodeling is a fundamental regulatory process that alters the positioning, composition, or accessibility of nucleosomes to control how DNA is packaged within the nucleus. By changing chromatin structure, remodeling complexes govern the ability of transcription factors and other regulatory proteins to access genomic DNA, thereby shaping gene expression programs. Through this dynamic control of chromatin accessibility, chromatin remodeling plays a central role in cellular identity, environmental responsiveness, and adaptive gene regulation. Chromatin remodelers are ATP-dependent enzymes that contain a conserved Snf2-type ATPase motor. By using the energy from ATP hydrolysis, these remodelers can translocate along DNA and change nucleosome organization, including nucleosome sliding, nucleosome assembly or disassembly, and histone variant exchange. Therefore, chromatin remodeling regulates gene transcription, DNA replication, DNA repair, genome stability, development, and cell identity. The major chromatin remodeling regulators can be divided into several families, including SWI/SNF, ISWI, CHD, and INO80 family remodelers. These remodelers share a conserved ATPase motor but contain different accessory domains and subunits, which help determine their specific genomic localization and remodeling outcomes. For example, SWI/SNF-related complexes often disrupt or reposition nucleosomes to promote regulatory factor binding, while ISWI and CHD remodelers are important for nucleosome spacing and chromatin organization. INO80 family remodelers are also involved in nucleosome sliding and histone exchange, especially H2A variant replacement. Mechanistically, chromatin remodelers move nucleosomal DNA through ATPase-driven DNA translocation. Recent studies suggest that remodelers may reposition nucleosomes by inducing small changes in DNA twist, called twist defects, rather than only moving large DNA loops around the histone core. Therefore, chromatin remodeling provides a dynamic way to regulate chromatin accessibility and coordinate epigenetic regulation with genome function [21].

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are important regulatory molecules in epigenetic regulation. Although they do not encode proteins, ncRNAs can regulate gene expression at multiple levels and participate in many biological processes, including development, cell fate determination, stress response, and disease progression. The major types of ncRNAs include microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), endogenous siRNAs, tRNA-derived fragments, and classical ncRNAs such as rRNAs and tRNAs. Among them, miRNAs mainly regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding target mRNAs and reducing their stability or translation. lncRNAs usually have more diverse functions. They can regulate transcription, recruit chromatin-modifying complexes, affect DNA methylation, or serve as scaffolds for protein complexes. For example, some lncRNAs can guide Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) to specific genomic loci and regulate H3K27 methylation, while others can influence SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling or DNA demethylation-related pathways. circRNAs and some lncRNAs can also function as competitive endogenous RNAs by sequestering miRNAs or RNA-binding proteins, thereby indirectly regulating downstream gene expression. In addition, ncRNA networks often form feedback loops and feedforward loops, which help fine-tune signaling pathways and cellular responses. Therefore, ncRNAs should not be considered simple transcriptional byproducts. Instead, they are key components of epigenetic and gene regulatory networks, linking chromatin regulation, transcription, RNA stability, translation, and signal transduction. Dysregulation of ncRNA expression or interactions can disrupt normal cellular programs and contribute to diseases such as cancer [22].

Overall, epigenetic regulation profoundly influences cellular identity and function, playing essential roles in cell fate determination processes, such as stem cell maintenance and lineage commitment. Alterations in these epigenetic patterns can enable cellular reprogramming, whereas precise epigenetic regulation ensures coordinated gene expression programs, allowing cells to appropriately respond to different intra- and extracellular signals. Different epigenetic regulatory pathways do not work independently but communicate with each other to coordinate gene expression. DNA methylation and H3K9 methylation often cooperate to establish and maintain transcriptionally silent chromatin, and specific reader domains help recruit DNA methyltransferases or histone methyltransferases to the correct genomic regions [23]. Chromatin remodeling enzymes and histone modifications can also influence DNA methylation patterns by changing nucleosome positioning and chromatin accessibility. In addition, histone modifications can regulate each other, because some histone “writers” can also read pre-existing marks and further reinforce active or repressive chromatin states [24]. Non-coding RNAs provide another layer of regulation by interacting with chromatin-modifying enzymes or SWI/SNF remodeling complexes to guide these regulators to specific loci [25,26]. Therefore, DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and ncRNAs form interconnected regulatory networks that fine-tune gene expression in development and disease. Biomaterials provide extracellular signals that are sensed and transduced into intracellular signals, ultimately altering the intrinsic epigenetic state through the aforementioned mechanisms. These epigenetic changes influence key cellular behaviors and functions, including nuclear deformation, proliferation, differentiation, and reprogramming.

Linking biomaterial properties to epigenetic regulation

Emerging evidence has revealed an intrinsic relationship between material properties, epigenetic states, and tissue repair. Various physical and biochemical properties of biomaterials can influence cell behavior and function in different ways. As illustrated in Fig. 1, biomaterial properties, including mechanical properties, topography, geometry, degradability, material composition, surface chemistry, ECM ligands, and bioactive cargos, are sensed through mechanotransduction pathways such as integrin–FAK–RhoA/actomyosin, YAP/TAZ, and TRPV4/Piezo1 signaling. These signals converge on the nucleus to modulate DNA methylation, histone methylation/acetylation, chromatin accessibility, non-coding RNAs, and DNA damage-associated epigenetic responses. Depending on cue type, magnitude, duration, and spatiotemporal presentation, these epigenetic changes can promote regenerative outcomes, including proliferation, stemness, differentiation, immunomodulation, and tissue repair, or maladaptive responses such as inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, senescence, apoptosis, genotoxicity, and fibrosis. In the following sections, we review the evidence on how the physical and biochemical properties, as well as the nanoscale characteristics of biomaterials, alter epigenetic states in host cells and highlight related key mechanisms, aiming to provide a clear and comprehensive overview of how biomaterial design can epigenetically regulate cell behavior to enhance tissue regeneration. Biophysical properties affecting epigenetic regulation

The physical properties of biomaterials, including viscoelasticity, stiffness, rigidity, surface topography, surface roughness, charge, degradability, and wettability, serve as potent regulators of cellular epigenetics through mechanosensitive pathways known as mechanotransduction. As illustrated in Fig. 2, mechanotransduction is a highly coordinated process that involves both direct mechanical force transmission and indirect biochemical signaling [27]. Cells adhere to biomaterials via integrins and other receptors, forming structural linkages that connect the extracellular matrix to the cytoskeleton and ultimately to the nucleus. Mechanical forces generated at the cell-material interface are transmitted along actin filaments and cytoskeletal networks, leading to nuclear deformation and reorganization of chromatin architecture, thereby directly influencing gene accessibility and epigenetic states (Fig. 2a) [27]. In parallel, extracellular biophysical cues are sensed by membrane-associated structures, including integrins, cadherins, G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and mechanosensitive ion channels such as Piezo1 and TPRV4, and transduced into intracellular signaling cascades such as FAK, MAPK, and RhoA pathways, as well as the nuclear translocation of transcriptional regulators such as YAP/TAZ (Fig. 2b) [27]. Mechanical properties

Substrate stiffness is one of the most widely studied physical cues known to regulate cell microenvironment and modulate cellular epigenetic states. Mechanical cues transmitted through the cytoskeleton and the nucleus can reprogram DNA and histone modifications, as well as higher-order chromatin architecture, thereby influencing cellular behavior in vitro and in vivo. Thus, a deep understanding of how stiffness modulates epigenetic states is beneficial for designing bio-instructive biomaterials more effectively. Among various biomaterials, hydrogels are one of the primary platforms used to study cellular behaviors in response to changes in matrix stiffness in vitro, because their mechanical properties can be tuned over a wide range of elastic moduli, from very soft to highly rigid, by varying the polymer composition or crosslinking conditions [28]. For example, Taufalele et al. [29] used collagen-coated polyacrylamide (PAM) gels with tunable stiffnesses to investigate the impact of matrix stiffness on DNA methylation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). They found that stiffer substrates reduced global DNA methylation, particularly in the promoter regions of key regulatory genes. Similar stiffness-mediated effects have been observed in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), where exposure to stiff microenvironments induced time-dependent epigenetic remolding, including changes in histone acetylation and chromatin organization, leading to persistent mechanical memory that influences subsequent cell differentiation [30]. Additionally, miRNA expression is responsive to substrate stiffness. In liver cancer cells, for instance, increased matrix stiffness led to upregulation of miR-17-5p, which attenuated the effects of metformin by targeting the PTEN/PI3K/Akt pathway, demonstrating that stiffness-induced epigenetic reprogramming may contribute to chemoresistance [31]. Furthermore, researchers developed a biomimetic hydrogel with tunable physicochemical and mechanical properties that can facilitate bone regeneration by enhancing stem cell osteogenesis via epigenetic modulation of the Tet2-dependent WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway (Fig. 3a) [32]. In another study, a stiffened matrix activated the TRPV4/microRNA-6740/endothelin-1 mechanotransduction pathway in cardiac cells, thereby increasing inflammatory signaling and leading to vascular endothelial dysfunction [33]. Also, soft matrix stiffness directs stem cell fate by mechanotransduction, maintaining stemness and enhancing the reprogramming potential of human mesenchymal stem cells (Fig. 3b) [34]. Topography

The concept of contact guidance was introduced in 1945 to recognize how topographical features can direct cell behavior by modulating biophysical signaling pathways [36]. Topographical cues have been shown to influence cellular reprogramming and differentiation by altering histone methylation and acetylation patterns. These effects are typically mediated through changes in cytoskeletal morphology, nuclear shape, cytoskeletal tension, and focal adhesion dynamics. Such structural adaptations activate mechanotransduction pathways that regulate chromatin organization and gene expression. For instance, microgrooved substrates enhanced the efficiency of genetic reprogramming of fibroblasts into cardiomyocytes by modulating epigenetic states, including decreased HDAC activity and increased histone H3 acetylation and H3K4 trimethylation via upregulation of the methyltransferase complex component WDR5, thereby promoting cardiomyogenic gene expression [37,38]. Similarly, nanogrooved substrates significantly facilitated direct lineage reprogramming of fibroblasts into induced dopaminergic neurons through epigenetic mechanisms, as evidenced by increased H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and upregulation of mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET)-related gene expression during the conversion process [39]. Moreover, microgrooved substrates have been reported to regulate neural development and Notch signaling pathways by altering cellular epigenetic states, demonstrated by increased levels of key histone modification markers, including histone H3 acetylation at K9/K14, histone H4 acetylation at K5/K8/K12/K16 (AcH4), and H3K9 trimethylation (H3K9me3), emphasizing the critical role of physical microenvironments in neuronal differentiation processes [40]. Specifically, AcH3 and AcH4 refer to acetylated histones H3 and H4, respectively, which are epigenetic marks associated with transcriptionally active chromatin through increased chromatin accessibility [41]. Recent studies have further demonstrated the importance of topography in bone and cartilage regeneration. Biomimetic microgrooved titanium surfaces promoted osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) via H3K4 trimethylation, revealing a specific epigenetic mechanism underlying topographical influence [42]. Likewise, micro- and nanotopographies have been shown to critically determine chondrogenic differentiation of hMSCs, facilitating the formation of a specific cartilage phenotype by modulating histone acetylation and methylation [43]. Furthermore, nanotopographical titanium surfaces regulate osteoblast–osteoclast interactions by modulating histone methylation, thereby alleviating osteoclast-mediated suppression of osteogenesis (Fig. 3c) [35]. Additionally, microtopography-based micropillar substrates have been shown to epigenetically reprogram chromatin organization by inducing nuclear constriction, altering lamin A/C multimerization, and enhancing 3D chromatin accessibility, thereby promoting robust osteogenic differentiation in vitro and bone regeneration in vivo [44]. Overall, these studies underscore the importance of biomaterial topography as a potent biophysical regulator of epigenetic states, offering novel strategies to enhance tissue regeneration. However, the optimal pattern designs and feature sizes remain unclear and are likely to depend on many other material properties discussed throughout this review.

Degradability

Biodegradable materials are those that can be broken down in vivo and degraded into small compounds commonly found in nature via mechanisms such as hydrolysis, oxidative degradation, and enzymatic degradation. This degradation not only releases small molecules but also physically remodels the microenvironment by creating space for cell stretching, migration, proliferation, remodeling the ECM, and altering the mechanotransductive signals to the nucleus that govern chromatin state, thereby critically influencing stem cell survival and differentiation [45]. For instance, a highly degradable 3D hydrogel has been shown to maintain the stem-like state of neural progenitor cells (NPCs), characterized by enlarged nuclei enriched with the stemness-associated activating histone mark H3K4me3, sustained Sox2 expression, and high proliferation. In comparison, NPCs cultured in low-degradability hydrogels exhibited more compact, rounded nuclei with non-proliferative and peripherally accumulated heterochromatin [46]. In another study with osteoarthritis (OA) chondrocytes, increasing local matrix compliance using a “sliding” hydrogel (mimicking a more degradable, stress-relaxing matrix) inhibited inflammatory signaling and induced chromatin reorganization, correlating with reduced expression of catabolic genes [47]. Together, these studies demonstrate that biomaterial degradability is an effective modulator of epigenetic regulation, capable of directing cell fate through changes in histone modification patterns, chromatin accessibility, and the activity of histone-modifying enzymes, such as HDACs and methyltransferases/demethylases.

In addition, biomaterial degradation plays an important role in the controlled and sustained release of biochemical cues, such as ions, growth factors, epigenetic drugs, and other molecules, which can further alter the epigenome and the cellular microenvironment, thereby influencing tissue regeneration [45,[48], [49], [50], [51]]. Nevertheless, the epigenetic landscape resulting from degradation-induced release of biomaterials has not been fully understood, as these changes likely result from a combination of mechanical, biochemical, and temporal cues that act in concert to shape cellular fate.

Wettability

Although direct evidence linking the wettability, i.e., the hydrophilic or hydrophobic nature, of biomaterials to epigenetic modifications remains limited, it is widely accepted that surface wettability significantly influences cellular functions by affecting protein adsorption and cell adhesion. Studies have shown that human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) cultured on polyethylene surfaces with wettability gradients exhibit notable differences in adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, highlighting the role of surface wettability in modulating cellular behavior [52]. Likewise, hydrophilic polymer surfaces demonstrated significantly extended neurite outgrowth of PC-12 cells compared to those on hydrophobic surfaces, suggesting that surface wettability impacts neuronal differentiation [53]. It has also been demonstrated that hydrophilic surfaces are more favorable at the early stages of osteointegration, as they can promote the spread of MSCs and facilitate protein adsorption [54]. Collectively, these examples underscore the important roles of biomaterial wettability in regulating cell behaviors by affecting cytoskeletal dynamics and signal transduction, potentially via mechanotransduction. However, the specific epigenetic alterations mediated by wettability have not yet been fully demonstrated, offering a potential future opportunity to better understand the mechanisms of biomaterial-driven epigenetic regulation in tissue repair.

Biochemical properties affecting epigenetic regulation

While biophysical properties primarily influence cellular behavior through interfacial physical interactions, biochemical cues offer more direct pathways to modulate signaling networks and epigenetic regulation. Biochemical properties refer to the characteristics of biomaterials that arise from molecular interactions between material components and biological systems. In the context of epigenetic regulation, biochemical cues primarily activate specific signaling pathways or gene expression networks that govern cellular behaviors. These cues are largely dictated by the chemical composition of biomaterials, such as surface chemistry, degradation kinetics and byproducts, and the release profiles of embedded biomolecules. Herein, we classify and summarize epigenetic cues based on key biochemical properties of biomaterials, including the incorporation of ECM components and the controlled delivery of growth factors, transcription factors, and other small molecules.

ECM components

Beyond serving as structural supports, ECM components act as potent regulators of cell fate through epigenetic mechanisms. By triggering integrin-mediated activation and downstream signaling pathways, ECM-derived cues are transmitted through the cytoskeleton to the nucleus, where they affect nuclear architecture, chromatin organization, and the activity of epigenetic enzymes such as histone deacetylases and methyltransferases [55]. Specifically, ECM-based biomaterials engage cell surface receptors (e.g., integrins) and sequester or present growth factors, thereby activating intracellular mechanotransductive signaling pathways that regulate cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and downstream epigenetic regulation of gene expression [56,57]. ECM and its derivatives, such as collagen, laminin, fibronectin, and Matrigel, are commonly used components in the fabrication of hydrogels, bioactive coatings, and 3D scaffolds to recapitulate the biochemical and mechanical cues of the native tissue microenvironment [58]. Among these, collagen, the main structural protein in the body, has been widely used in regenerative medicine. Numerous collagen-based scaffolds have been established in the past few decades to repair cartilage and bone defects, and remarkable progress has been made [56,59,60]. For instance, collagen-based materials have been used as osteoconductive, biomimetic scaffolds that structurally and compositionally resemble native bone [48]. These materials activate integrin–FAK and Runx2 signaling, transmitting mechanical and biochemical cues to the nucleus, where they modulate chromatin accessibility and osteogenic gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms such as histone modifications associated with lineage-specific commitment [[61], [62], [63]]. Similarly, in cartilage repair, injectable self-crosslinking collagen and collagen–hyaluronic acid (ColHA) hydrogels have demonstrated the ability to recruit endogenous MSCs, promote their chondrogenic differentiation into hyaline cartilage, and preserve cartilage phenotype and homeostasis without the need for exogenous growth factors [64]. Though it may not be explicitly linked to epigenetic changes, such ECM-driven processes are likely mediated by epigenetic regulation of chondrogenic genes and chromatin state. In the context of nerve regeneration, ECM components have been integrated into nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) or hydrogels to promote Schwann cell adhesion and migration, axonal regrowth, and remyelination by engaging pro-regenerative signaling pathways. For example, a porcine nerve-derived, nerve-specific ECM hydrogel used as a luminal filler within silicone conduits in a rat sciatic nerve gap model significantly improved electrophysiological recovery and increased axon counts compared to empty conduits, demonstrating its capacity to recreate nerve-specific biochemical cues and support functional nerve regeneration [65]. Notably, a recent study investigating ECM remodeling following spared nerve injury (SNI) of the sciatic nerve demonstrated a direct link between ECM modulation and epigenetic regulation [66]. It was found that inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) using GM6001 reduced glial activation and modulated HDAC1 and HDAC2 expression, demonstrating that targeting ECM dynamics can impact epigenetic states and glial behavior, underscoring the potential of ECM-focused strategies to guide neural repair through epigenetic mechanisms.

Growth factors

Growth factors (GFs) are important biochemical cues that have been extensively investigated for their potential to enhance or induce cell reprogramming and cell differentiation for tissue regeneration through epigenetic mechanisms. In tissue engineering, GFs are often incorporated into biomaterials to direct spatially controlled regeneration. For example, scaffolds loaded with bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), particularly BMP-2, have shown strong osteoinductive properties, enhancing osteogenic differentiation of MSCs and accelerating bone and osteochondral repair [67,68]. Beyond their well-known regenerative functions, many growth factors also regulate epigenetic states to direct cell fate decisions. In the nervous system, fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) was found to prime neural progenitors for astrocyte differentiation by reshaping chromatin at the GFAP promoter, increasing H3K4 methylation and reducing H3K9 methylation, thereby enabling CNTF-responsive transcription [69]. In endothelial cells, vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) induced dynamic bivalent histone modifications at angiogenic transcription factor loci, creating a poised chromatin state essential for rapid transcriptional activation during angiogenic sprouting [70]. Likewise, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) has been shown to drive fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation by altering histone modifications, including increased histone lactylation and associated acetylation programs, which reprogram profibrotic gene expression [66]. Together, these examples illustrate that GFs not only guide tissue-specific differentiation but also exert potent epigenetic control over lineage commitment and regeneration-relevant cellular behaviors.

At the same time, biomaterials-based platforms offer precise control over the spatiotemporal delivery of GFs, particularly when coupled with externally triggered release systems. These approaches have been shown to improve repair outcomes across various tissues, including neural, vascular, and musculoskeletal systems [71]. By enabling localized and sustained presentation of GFs, these delivery strategies help maintain active epigenetic states and reinforce stable cell fate decisions within engineered microenvironments, underscoring the importance of integrating GFs with biomaterial design to advance epigenetically informed regenerative therapies.

Transcription factors

A landmark development of cellular reprogramming through epigenetic regulation is the Nobel Prize—winning discovery of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) by Dr. Shinya Yamanaka. By introducing defined transcription factors (TFs), such as Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc, differentiated somatic cells can be epigenetically reprogrammed into a pluripotent state, underscoring the profound impact of precise biochemical cues on cellular fate determination and regenerative capability [72]. Similarly, lineage-specific combinations of TFs have enabled direct conversion of one somatic cell type into another, such as the reprogramming of fibroblasts into cardiomyocytes using GATA4, Mef2c, and Tbx5, or into neurons using Ascl1, Brn2, and Myt1l, further highlighting the versatility and effectiveness of TF-based reprogramming approaches [73,74]. However, direct in vivo delivery of TFs to promote tissue regeneration remains challenging due to the need for biocompatible delivery carriers that can efficiently bind TFs, target specific somatic cells, and facilitate endosomal escape, a key step that prevents internalized TFs from being degraded and allows them to reach their intracellular targets. Recent advancements in novel biomaterials have enabled the possibility of overcoming these limitations [75]. For instance, by using self-assembled supramolecular nanoparticles (SNPs) as a nanoscale vector, Liu et al. [76] successfully delivered intact TFs (GAL4-VP16) to the cell nuclei in a highly efficient manner. Similarly, Lee et al. [77] developed a multifunctional oligonucleotide containing a TF-binding sequence to deliver Nrf2 to the liver, thereby rescuing mice from acetaminophen-induced liver injury. Despite the limited number of studies involving TF-mediated epigenetic mechanisms for tissue regeneration, these pilot studies underscore the high potential of combining TF-based regulation with biomaterial platforms to achieve precise epigenetic control, paving the way for next-generation epigenetic biomaterials.

Other small molecules

In addition to GFs and TFs, various other bioactive molecules such as cytokines, antigens, mineral ions, and reprogramming factors have been shown to enhance or induce cell reprogramming and influence epigenetic states [78]. Several small molecules have been identified to boost the efficiency of iPSC generation. For example, the histone deacetylase inhibitor, valproic acid (VPA), was reported to increase reprogramming efficiency by ∼100-fold compared with TFs alone [79]. Moreover, a seven-compound small-molecule cocktail (VPA, CHIR99021, Repsox, Forskolin, SP600125, Go6983, and Y-27632) has been successfully used to directly convert mouse fibroblasts into functional neurons without genetic manipulation, highlighting the potential of non-genetic chemical approaches for safe and efficient cellular reprogramming strategies [80]. Also, Folic acid has been reported to promote neurogenesis and functional neuronal development, in part by enhancing global DNA methylation [81]. Additionally, exogenous citrate has been shown to support and reinforce epigenetic regulation by serving as a critical substrate for protein acetyltransferases, influencing histone acetylation and gene expression [82,83].

Collectively, these biochemical cues, ranging from GFs, TFs, and ECM components to small molecules and metabolic cofactors, demonstrate the diverse ways in which biomaterials can be designed or leveraged to influence epigenetic states and direct cell fate. As summarized in Table 1, both biophysical and biochemical properties, such as stiffness, surface topography, degradation behavior, biochemical composition, and geometry, can significantly influence cellular behavior through epigenetic mechanisms. While the material properties discussed so far largely reflect bulk-scale biomaterial systems, emerging research highlights nanoscale materials as a distinct class of material capable of exerting profound epigenetic effects. These effects may arise from their intrinsic size- and shape-dependent properties or from their interactions with nuclear and chromatin-associated structures. The effect of nanomaterials on epigenetics

Shape and size are fundamental properties of biomaterials that critically influence how cells interpret and respond to external stimuli, including at the epigenetic level. Nanomaterials, typically defined as materials with dimensions in the 1–100 nm range, confer unique size- and shape-dependent physical and chemical properties that facilitate efficient cellular uptake and, in some cases, enable direct interaction with nuclear components. Through these interactions, nanomaterials can modulate epigenetic states, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and microRNA expression [111]. Particularly, nanomaterials composed of silver (Ag), gold (Au), titanium dioxide (TiO2), zinc oxide (ZnO), copper oxide (CuO), silica (SiO2), as well as carbon-based nanoparticles (NPs) and quantum dots, as summarized in Table 2, are highlighted because these classes have been most extensively investigated for their direct epigenetic effects through mechanisms including nuclear penetration, oxidative stress, and mechanotransduction. Importantly, much of the evidence discussed below originates from nanotoxicology rather than regenerative biomaterials research. Therefore, the detection of a change in DNA methylation, histone modification, or non-coding RNA expression should not, by itself, be interpreted as evidence that a nanomaterial exerts controlled or bona fide epigenetic regulation. Such changes may reflect adaptive chromatin remodeling, but they may also arise secondarily from oxidative stress, ion release, DNA damage, cell-cycle disruption, or apoptosis. We therefore evaluate these studies according to four considerations: the physiological relevance of the exposure conditions, the presence of cytotoxic or genotoxic stress, the strength of evidence Summary of material properties of nanoparticles and their impact on epigenetic mechanisms.

Metal-based NPs

Metal-based NPs constitute an important class of nanomaterials capable of modulating epigenetic regulation. For example, AgNPs and AuNPs, due to their small size and spherical geometry, can penetrate cellular membranes and disrupt nuclear processes, leading to measurable changes in DNA and chromatin marks. In lung epithelial cells (A549), AgNPs have been shown to increase global DNA methylation and promote global histone H3 deacetylation, consistent with transcriptionally repressive chromatin remodeling, whereas in erythroid MEL cells, they caused significantly reduced global H3 methylation levels, specifically H3K4 and H3K79 methylation, suggesting broad suppression of transcriptional activity through loss of active chromatin signatures [113,119]. AuNPs have also been demonstrated to remove repressive chromatin marks in fibroblasts and upregulate microRNA-155 (miR-155), leading to aberrant overexpression of the PROS1 gene, which is linked to lung injury, without altering its DNA methylation status [123].

Oxide-based NPs

Oxide-based NPs, particularly transition-metal oxides such as TiO2, ZnO, and CuO, primarily influence epigenetics through nanoscale oxidative stress mechanisms. Exposure to ZnO NPs elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, resulting in inhibition of DNMT and upregulation of TET hydroxylases, thereby causing global DNA hypomethylation and increased DNA hydroxymethylation in human cells [131]. ZnO NPs were also found to induce histone modifications alterations such as increased repressive H3K9 methylation and decreased H4 acetylation in skin keratinocytes, which are epigenetic modifications associated with G2/M cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis [139]. Similarly, TiO2 NPs can disrupt DNA methylation homeostasis. For instance, anatase TiO2 has been shown to hypermethylate the promoter of the DNA repair gene PARP1 via ROS-driven pathways [132]. In another study, chronic TiO2 exposure in fibroblasts downregulated DNMT1 and DNMT3a/b, leading to locus-specific demethylation of repetitive elements [134]. CuO NPs have been reported to inhibit multiple histone deacetylases, leading to increased global histone acetylation and reactivation of silenced tumor-suppressor genes in lung cells [143]. SiO2 NPs, typically 50–100 nm in diameter, have been linked to both DNA methylation and mRNA expression changes. On one hand, several studies have shown that SiO2 NPs induce global DNA hypomethylation [124,145,147]. On the other hand, emerging evidence suggests that long-term, low-dose SiO2 exposure can trigger gene-specific DNA hypermethylation, such as promoter CpG methylation of CREB3L1 and the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 in human bronchial epithelial cells. These epigenetic changes suppress PI3K/Akt survival signaling, ultimately inducing mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis [148].

Carbon-based nanomaterials

Carbon-based nanomaterials further underscore the critical role of shape and size in epigenetic regulation. In particular, high-aspect-ratio carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which are elongated fiber-like nanotubular structures, elicited markedly different epigenetic responses compared to spherical or sheet-like carbon nanomaterials. Multi-walled CNTs can accumulate in the lungs and induce chronic inflammation accompanied by global DNA hypomethylation in exposed lung cells. In contrast, thinner single-walled CNTs, which possess smaller diameters and distinct cellular distributions, often do not alter global methylation levels but instead induce gene-specific DNA methylation alterations in pathways related to cell cycle regulation and immune signaling [159]. Even nearly atom-sized carbon dots demonstrate that subtle nanoscale properties can alter epigenetic outcomes. For example, simply switching the surface charge of 5 nm carbon dots from negative to positive has been shown to cause significant DNA demethylation, reflected by a loss of 5-mc, and upregulate oxidative stress–response genes. In contrast, negatively charged carbon dots induced minimal epigenetic effect under similar conditions [165].

Other nanomaterials

Beyond the aforementioned nanomaterials, several additional classes of nanoscale materials have been shown to exert pronounced effects on epigenetic regulation, either through direct nuclear interactions or through biomimetic modulation of cell fate. Semiconductor quantum dots (QDs), which are nanocrystals only a few nanometers in size, represent one such class. Due to their ultrasmall dimensions, QDs can directly interact with nuclear proteins and ncRNAs. For example, cadmium telluride (CdTe) QDs have been found to associate with core histones and DNA, provoking global histone hypoacetylation and chromatin condensation in breast cancer cells along with genomic instability [151]. QD exposure also dysregulates ncRNA profiles; for instance, QDs widely altered cellular miRNA expression patterns in fibroblasts, correlating with an apoptosis-like gene response in exposed cells [153]. Polymeric nanomaterials, including polymeric NPs, colloidal assemblies, and electrospun nanofibers, can influence cell behavior through nanoscale topography, mechanics, degradation products, and controlled cargo delivery. Emerging studies indicate that polymer-containing nanostructured environments can alter histone modifications and mechanotransduction-associated stem-cell differentiation [169]. Nevertheless, many studies measure gene expression or lineage markers without directly examining chromatin accessibility or epigenetic modifications. Thus, polymeric nanomaterials represent a promising but comparatively less mechanistically defined area of regenerative epigenetics. Lastly, bioceramic nanomaterials or biomimetic nanomaterials, such as nano-hydroxyapatite (nano-HA), illustrate how nanoscale morphology can be harnessed to epigenetically direct cell fate. Nano-HA crystals, which closely resemble native bone mineral in size and shape, have been shown to stimulate DNA methylation at osteogenic gene loci in MSCs, thereby tipping the balance of gene expression toward early osteoblast lineage commitment [166]. However, the response was strongly dependent on differentiation stage and included suppression of several osteogenic markers, indicating that nano-hydroxyapatite-induced epigenetic changes should not be interpreted as uniformly pro-regenerative. In contrast, bioactive glass nanoparticles have demonstrated ion-dependent effects on osteogenesis, angiogenesis, oxidative stress, and macrophage behavior, but direct evidence linking these outcomes to defined DNA methylation, histone, or chromatin changes remains limited [170]. Collectively, these examples further demonstrate that the size, shape, and surface chemistry of nanomaterials are key regulators of epigenetic mechanisms, ranging from DNA methylation landscapes to histone modification patterns and miRNA networks, ultimately dictating how mammalian cells interpret their genome in the presence of these nanoscale materials and influence outcomes.

Epigenetic mechanisms induced by nanomaterials

Among the various epigenetic responses induced by nanomaterials, one of the most consistently reported and mechanistically relevant modifications is the phosphorylation of histone H2AX at serine-139, known as γ-H2AX [[171], [172], [173], [174]]. This modification serves as a well-established early marker of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and plays a central role in initiating the DNA damage response cascade. Upon sensing DNA damage, particularly DSBs, cellular kinases such as ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) rapidly phosphorylate H2AX, leading to the formation of γ-H2AX foci at break sites [175,176]. These foci act as scaffolds to recruit DNA repair proteins and coordinate chromatin remodeling, effectively bridging damage recognition and repair processes [177]. In the context of nanomaterial exposure, γ-H2AX accumulation has emerged as a sensitive and quantifiable indicator of genotoxic stress, enabling rapid assessment of the DNA-damaging potential of various nanomaterials [174,176,178]. As a result, γ-H2AX not only reflects a pivotal mechanistic link between nanomaterial exposure and epigenetic signaling but also offers a practical biomarker for evaluating the biological safety of engineered nanomaterials. For instance, γ-H2AX activation level has been used to evaluate the genotoxicity of TiO2-NPs in human alveolar epithelial cells [172,179,180]. Similarly, it has been used as an essential marker to assess genotoxicity in human intestinal Caco-2 cells treated with SiO2 [181], as well as in various other human cells exposed to NPs, including Au-NPs, CuO-NPs, ZnO-NPs, CeO2-NPs, and As2O3-NPs [128,[182], [183], [184], [185], [186]].

Importantly, while many of the studies discussed in this section have focused on nanomaterial-induced genotoxicity and epigenetic dysregulation, these findings also provide valuable mechanistic insights relevant to regenerative medicine. Epigenetic responses such as γ-H2AX formation, chromatin remodeling, and activation of DNA damage response pathways are not exclusive markers of toxicity but are also integral to cellular plasticity, senescence regulation, and tissue remodeling. In regenerative contexts, controlled activation of DNA damage-associated epigenetic signaling has been implicated in processes such as stem cell fate decisions, wound repair, and resolution of chronic inflammation. For example, increasing evidence indicates that increased generation of γ-H2AX is associated with oxidative stress and ATM activation in cells [172]. It has also been demonstrated that γ-H2AX accumulates in senescent cells undergoing genomic instability, underscoring its role beyond acute DNA damage [187]. In parallel, the tumor suppressor p53 enhances DNA repair and suppresses the formation of cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs). This action limits activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and its proinflammatory downstream signaling cascade, thereby attenuating chronic inflammation during cellular senescence [188]. Collectively, these responses may influence the balance between productive regeneration and pathological senescence by contributing to broader epigenetic–inflammatory crosstalk with implications for long-term tissue homeostasis and regeneration. However, these effects are frequently observed in studies focused on nanotoxicity rather than controlled biological modulation, and the relationship between nanomaterial properties and epigenetic outcomes in regenerative medicine remains incompletely understood. Looking forward, future studies should aim to systematically elucidate how physicochemical parameters, such as size, shape, surface chemistry, and nanomaterial dosage, modulate epigenetic states in a controlled manner, minimizing deleterious effects while promoting regenerative outcomes. Integrating nanomaterial design with epigenetic profiling, spatiotemporal control, and regenerative models will be crucial for translating nanoscale epigenetic modulation from a toxicity paradigm to therapeutic and bio-instructive strategies for tissue regeneration.

Interpreting current evidence in regenerative nanomaterials

Collectively, current evidence confirms that nanomaterials can alter DNA methylation, histone marks, ncRNA expression, and γ-H2AX signaling, but it does not yet establish broadly applicable design rules for regeneration. Outcomes depend strongly on particle composition, aggregation or dissolution, surface coating, exposure duration, delivered dose, and cell model. In particular, nominal culture-medium concentration may differ substantially from the dose that reaches cells, limiting direct comparisons among studies [[189], [190], [191]]. For instance, the contrasting effects of AgNPs in A549 and MEL cells [113,119], together with reports of both global hypomethylation and locus-specific hypermethylation after SiO2 exposure [124,145,147], illustrate this context dependence. Epigenetic alterations accompanied by oxidative stress, apoptosis, or γ-H2AX accumulation should also be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect secondary toxic or genotoxic responses rather than instructive epigenetic remodeling [192].

These limitations indicate that the current nanomaterial literature is stronger in demonstrating that the epigenome is sensitive to nanoscale exposure than in establishing that nanomaterials can be predictably engineered to produce beneficial epigenetic states. By contrast, regeneration-relevant findings, such as nano-HA-associated osteogenic methylation, remain largely correlational [166]. Future studies should therefore use noncytotoxic, physiologically relevant exposures, characterize the delivered dose and particle state, and combine time-resolved epigenomic profiling with mechanistic perturbation and functional regenerative endpoints. Encouragingly, recent studies are beginning to move in this direction. For example, a very recent study by Xu et al. [193] engineered a biomimetic Prussian White nanomaterial that promoted peripheral nerve regeneration by integrating immunometabolic remodeling with epigenetic regulation. Rather than merely correlating nanoparticle exposure with epigenetic changes, the authors demonstrated a mechanistic pathway in which NPs-mediated metabolic rewiring increased intracellular α-ketoglutarate, activated Kdm4a/b-dependent H3K9me3 demethylation, and drove macrophage polarization toward a pro-regenerative phenotype that enhanced Schwann cell-mediated nerve repair. This work exemplifies a new generation of biomaterial studies that establish causal links between nanomaterial properties, metabolic and epigenetic regulation, and functional regenerative outcomes, providing a more rigorous framework for future biomaterial design.

Translating epigenetic modulators: progress in epi-drugs and integration with biomaterials

Introduction to epi-drugs

Epigenetic regulation offers exciting therapeutic opportunities, and the development of epi-drugs has emerged as a promising strategy across various biomedical applications, including cancer, inflammation, and tissue regeneration. A growing number of epi-drugs, including pharmacological histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis), DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (DNMTis), histone methyltransferase inhibitors (HMTis), and ncRNAs-based therapeutics, have been approved for clinical use or are undergoing active clinical trials. As an emerging field, ncRNA-based therapies hold considerable promise for targeted epigenetic regulation and present numerous opportunities for further research exploration and therapeutic innovation.

Integration with biomaterials for regenerative medicine

While epi-drugs were initially developed for the treatment of cancer and immune disorders, researchers are increasingly exploring their application in regenerative medicine by combining them with biomaterial platforms [[205], [206], [207]]. Biomaterials can act as carriers for epi-drugs to improve their pharmacokinetics by enhancing their stability, targeting efficiency, and controlled release. Over the past two decades, significant progress has been made in designing biocompatible and tunable materials that can deliver epigenetic modulators to specific tissues for targeted regenerative effects. For example, a recent study by Wang et al. [204] demonstrated the use of an electrospun poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-based patch (Lnc-GEP) for the localized delivery of Lnc-H19, an epigenetic regulator (lncRNA) implicated in tendon adhesion. By encapsulating lnc-H19 in nanogels, the patch enabled efficient cellular uptake and targeted reprogramming of scar-associated mesenchymal cells. In a mouse tendon injury model, this approach significantly reduced fibrosis and improved tissue regeneration, highlighting the potential of biomaterial-assisted delivery of ncRNAs to modulate epigenetic states and promote scar-free healing. Another study demonstrated that incorporating DNMT inhibitor 5-Azacytidine (5-Aza) into a decellularized skeletal muscle-derived hydrogel enhances muscle regeneration [208]. This biomaterial preserved native ECM architecture while delivering 5-Aza to muscle-derived stem cells (MDSCs), promoting cell viability and improving tissue repair in a rat myectomy model. These results support the potential of biomaterial-based delivery of epigenetic drugs for localized reprogramming and regenerative therapy.

For effective application in regenerative medicine, biomaterials must be rationally designed with consideration of their biophysical and biochemical properties, the targeted tissue environment, and the release profile of epi-drugs. As illustrated by recent examples, biomaterial-assisted delivery enables localized and sustained epigenetic modulation, offering a powerful strategy to enhance cellular reprogramming, lineage commitment, and tissue repair. More broadly, epigenetic mechanisms serve as a critical interface between external environmental stimuli and gene transcriptional control, offering a powerful design lever for next-generation regenerative biomaterials. Looking forward, a key opportunity lies in developing biomaterials capable of spatiotemporal control over epigenetic cues, enabling adaptive regulation of cell fate during different stages of tissue repair, including hemostasis, inflammation, repair, and remodeling [209]. Importantly, biomaterial-driven epigenetic regulation does not occur in isolation; many of the same material cues that reshape the epigenome also engage the immune system, influencing inflammatory signaling, immune cell activation, and resolution pathways. Therefore, harnessing this convergence will be essential for designing bio-instructive materials that coordinate epigenetic and immune responses to achieve durable and functional tissue regeneration. Accordingly, the following sections focus on how engineered materials regulate immune responses and how immune signaling, in turn, intersects with epigenetic mechanisms to shape tissue regeneration, highlighting these processes as complementary pillars.

Biomaterials and immunomodulation

Biomaterials have revolutionized tissue engineering by serving as scaffolds, carriers, and bioactive interfaces for repairing damaged tissues and restoring organ functions. Notably, the long-term success of biomaterial implantation depends not only on their structural or biochemical performance, but also on their ability to interact appropriately with the host immune system. Consequently, the immune system, the body's primary defense mechanism, is a pivotal component of tissue repair and regeneration [210]. Increasing evidence indicates that the interplay between biomaterials and the immune system plays a decisive role in determining the outcomes of tissue regeneration. Upon implantation, biomaterials are immediately exposed to the host immune system, triggering a cascade of inflammatory events that can either promote healing or drive pathological responses such as chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and a foreign body response (FBR) [211,212].

Traditionally, biomaterials have been designed primarily to achieve biocompatibility by minimizing immune recognition. However, recent advances in immunology and materials science have shifted this paradigm toward proactively modulating immune responses, aiming to suppress detrimental inflammation while harnessing immune-mediated mechanisms that support tissue regeneration. This shift reflects the dual role of the immune system, which acts both as a protective barrier and as a repair facilitator that requires precise and delicate regulation. Importantly, this dual role also highlights that immune responses to biomaterials are orchestrated through a coordinated and time-dependent progression [213], evolving over time from initial neutrophil recruitment to later involvement of monocytes and macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes, as shown in Fig. 4a. Indiscriminate suppression of inflammation may impair host defense or tissue remodeling, whereas biomaterials that trigger overreacted immune responses can cause autoimmune-like tissue damage [214]. Consequently, a central question driving current research is: how can biomaterials be engineered to dynamically and precisely interact with the immune system across different phases of healing? Immunogenicity of biomaterials and host responses post-implantation

The immune system protects the host and maintains tissue homeostasis through a highly coordinated inflammatory cascade that comprises sequential and overlapping processes. Following tissue injury, canonical wound healing proceeds through four phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling, each tightly regulated by diverse networks of immune and tissue-resident stromal cells to ensure proper tissue restoration (Fig. 4b) [215]. Although commonly described as distinct phases, these processes overlap substantially, and their timing and cellular composition vary across tissue and injury contexts. The implantation of foreign biomaterials inevitably triggers host immune responses that parallel aspects of wound healing, as they are recognized as foreign bodies. In the meantime, biomaterials present a range of biophysical and biochemical cues that can be rapidly sensed by these immune cells, thereby influencing immune responses and, ultimately, regenerative outcomes [211]. Accordingly, biomaterial-induced immune responses should be understood as dynamic processes whose biological consequences depend not only on their magnitude and phenotype, but also on their timing, duration, and resolution.

Immune responses to biomaterials are strongly influenced by degradation-related factors: (1) the release of degradation byproducts and (2) dynamic surface transformations that occur as materials degrade over time. Additionally, the local tissue environment surrounding the implant critically shapes the magnitude and nature of immune activation. When uncontrolled, the functional performance of biomaterials may be compromised or overshadowed by FBR, characterized by chronic inflammation, immune rejection, and impaired tissue integration. The FBR represents the host's canonical response to implanted materials and includes both acute and chronic inflammatory phases. Its progression follows a well-defined pathological sequence: initial protein adsorption, recruitment of inflammatory cells (particularly monocytes and macrophages), formation of foreign body giant cells (FBGCs), and ultimately fibrous encapsulation (Fig. 5a) [212]. Fibrous encapsulation isolates the biomaterial from surrounding host tissues by forming a dense, collagen-rich scar, thereby disrupting biomaterial-tissue interactions. In addition, this fibrotic barrier also creates a hypoxic microenvironment that exacerbates chronic inflammatory responses, further compromising the long-term efficacy of implanted biomaterials [214]. Within seconds after biomaterial implantation, blood from damaged vessels surrounds the implant, initiating host-biomaterial interaction. Within minutes, plasma components, including proteins (e.g., fibrinogen, fibronectin, albumin, vitronectin, and Igs), lipids, sugars, and ions, spontaneously adsorb onto the biomaterial surface. Over subsequent hours to days, material properties such as surface energy, chemistry, stiffness, topography, roughness, wettability, and surface charge dictate the composition, quantity, and conformational changes of adsorbed proteins, which, in turn, regulate the recruitment, adhesion, and activation of immune cells [216].

The hemorrhagic exudate formed post-implantation contains plasma proteins, platelets, and coagulation factors (e.g., prothrombin, factor V), which collectively initiate clot formation at the biomaterial interface. Platelet adhesion and activation lead to the release of α-granules and amplification of the coagulation process. Thrombin converts fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, forming a provisional blood clot that creates a matrix to temporarily stabilize the implant site [217]. Following the formation of a temporary fibrin matrix, acute and chronic inflammation occur, with intensity determined by the extent of implantation trauma and the physicochemical properties of the biomaterials. In parallel, the complement system, primarily activated through the alternative pathway, further enhances platelet activation and amplifies the inflammatory response. Complement activation products such as C3a and C5a act as potent chemoattractants, enhancing platelet activation and aggregation while simultaneously recruiting immune cells to the implant site, leading to intense local inflammation [218]. Additionally, ECM adhesion proteins, such as fibronectin and vitronectin, are also rapidly deposited onto the biomaterial surface. These proteins engage integrin receptors (e.g., α5β1 and αvβ3) on immune cells, promoting the adhesion and activation of monocytes and macrophages [219]. Beyond their adhesive functions, ECM adhesion proteins also actively modulate inflammatory signaling and play an essential role in macrophage fusion into FBGCs [220].

Molecular danger signals released from injured or stressed cells, including damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on immune cells, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), cytoplasmic PRRs, and NOD-like receptors (NLRs). PAMPs are conserved molecular structures derived from microorganisms that are recognized by PRRs of the host immune system to trigger innate immune responses. DAMPs are endogenous molecules released by damaged or stressed cells. Engagement of these PRRs initiates a cascade of intracellular signaling pathways that activate innate immune responses, leading to the production and secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [216].

Collectively, biomaterial implantation triggers a series of blood- and immune-mediated events, including protein adsorption, platelet activation, coagulation, complement activation, and the recruitment and activation of immune cells, which together establish a complex and dynamic biomaterial-host interface. The evolution of these interfacial interactions determines whether implanted biomaterials integrate successfully or instead provoke adverse responses, such as chronic inflammation and fibrosis, that ultimately compromise biomaterial function. A comprehensive understanding of both early and late immune processes is therefore essential for the rational design of bio-instructive biomaterials that actively guide host responses toward constructive tissue integration and regeneration. To this end, we next examine the roles of innate and adaptive immune systems and how their coordinated cellular and molecular responses underpin biomaterial-host interactions.

Innate and adaptive immunity in biomaterial-host interactions

Innate immunity

The immune system protects the host against invading pathogens, foreign bodies, and tissue damage through a series of well-orchestrated and sequential inflammatory processes, including threat detection, danger clearance, and restoration of tissue homeostasis [221,222]. Innate immunity constitutes the body's first line of defense, providing rapid, non-specific protection that does not require prior sensitization or generate immunological memory. The innate immune system comprises physical barriers (such as the skin and mucosal surfaces), chemical barriers (including gastric acid, lysozymes, and antimicrobial peptides), and a diverse array of cellular components [223]. Key innate immune cells include neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and mast cells. Upon recognition of PAMPs and DAMPs through PRRs, innate immune cells initiate immediate responses, including phagocytosis, cytokine and chemokine secretion, degranulation, and recruitment of additional immune cells [216]. Although innate immune responses lack antigen specificity and long-term immune memory, they play a vital role in the early stage of infection, injury, and other stimulations such as biomaterial implantation. Moreover, innate immunity serves as a critical bridge to adaptive immunity by shaping the activation and direction of T and B cell responses through antigen presentation and cytokine-mediated signaling [224]. A thorough understanding of the components and functions of innate immunity is therefore fundamental not only to immunology but also to the rational design of biomaterials that interact with or modulate host immune responses. In the following sections, we discuss key innate immune cell populations relevant to biomaterial-host interactions.

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are the most abundant circulating white blood cells and serve as key defense cells in the innate immune system [225]. As first responder cells, neutrophils rapidly migrate to sites of injury or infection in response to chemotactic signals such as interleukin-8 (IL-8) and complement components such as C5a [226]. Upon recruitment, neutrophils play multifaceted roles in host defense and the initiation of broader immune responses. Notably, they are among the earliest participants in acute inflammation, and IL-8 is one of the first signals produced by various cell types following inflammatory stimulation [225]. One of the outstanding features of neutrophils is their powerful phagocytic capacity. They can efficiently recognize, phagocytize, and degrade pathogens through phagosome-lysosome fusion, ROS production, and the action of antimicrobial proteases. Moreover, neutrophils release antimicrobial agents such as myeloperoxidase, elastase, and defensins from cytoplasmic granules to further eliminate invading microbes [227]. Another important mechanism employed by neutrophils is the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are web-like DNA structures coated with histones and antimicrobial proteins that immobilize and kill pathogens [228]. Beyond their direct antimicrobial functions, neutrophils actively modulate adaptive immunity through interactions with dendritic cells and lymphocytes. They secrete a variety of cytokines and chemokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), which amplify inflammatory signaling and facilitate the recruitment and activation of additional immune cells such as monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells [229]. Given their short lifespan (6–12 h in circulation) and rapid turnover, neutrophils are tightly regulated to prevent excessive inflammation and collateral tissue damage [230].

In the context of biomaterial implantation, neutrophils are typically the first leukocytes to arrive at the implantation site, where they rapidly sense biomaterial cues and adopt distinct activation states to shape subsequent immune responses and tissue regeneration outcomes [231]. For instance, biomaterial surface properties such as topography and hydrophobicity can influence neutrophil activation and NETosis. It has been reported that hydrophilic titanium surfaces attenuated neutrophil inflammatory responses and reduced NET formation, thereby favoring anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization and improving implant integration [232]. Similarly, matrix mechanics has been shown to modulate neutrophil behavior. Increased substrate stiffness in polymeric materials has been demonstrated to promote NET formation and modify cytoskeletal organization, underscoring a mechanosensitive regulation of neutrophil effector functions [233]. A recent review comprehensively summarized how diverse biomaterial parameters, including surface chemistry, degradability, and nano- and micro-architecture, shape neutrophil lifespan, polarization, and the balance between pro-healing and pro-inflammatory programs during biomaterial-mediated tissue repair [231]. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of precisely and timely modulating neutrophil responses to optimize biomaterial-guided immunomodulation and enhance regenerative outcomes.

Monocytes and macrophages

Monocytes are circulating white blood cells that constitute approximately 5-10% of peripheral blood leukocytes. They serve as precursors for macrophages and dendritic cells in tissues. Upon recruitment to the activation sites through chemotactic signals such as CCL2, monocytes differentiate into macrophages in response to local cytokines and microenvironmental cues. Macrophages play essential roles in detecting, engulfing, and degrading pathogens, apoptotic cells, and cellular debris. Importantly, they are highly plastic and can adopt distinct phenotypes in response to microenvironmental stimuli and signals [234]. It is widely accepted that this plasticity can be categorized into two distinct polarization states: classically activated (M1) and alternatively activated (M2) macrophages, as shown in Fig. 5b.

M1 macrophages are induced by pro-inflammatory signals such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). They are characterized by robust production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α, as well as ROS and nitric oxide (NO). M1 macrophages exhibit enhanced microbicidal and tumoricidal activity, promote Th1 immune responses, and primarily mediate pathogen clearance and acute inflammation. They are commonly observed in the early stages of infection or in inflammatory tissues [235]. In contrast, M2 macrophages are induced by Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) and anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β [235]. They express high levels of scavenger receptors (CD163, CD206) and produce anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β), growth factors (VEGF, PDGF), and arginase-1, thereby contributing to tissue remodeling, angiogenesis, inflammation resolution, and immunosuppression.

The dynamic and spatiotemporal balance between M1 and M2 polarization is therefore critical for maintaining immune hemostasis and successful tissue regeneration [234]. While M1 macrophages are essential during the early stage of healing for pathogen clearance and debris removal, excessive or prolonged M1 activation can lead to chronic inflammation and secondary fibrotic responses due to sustained tissue damage. Conversely, M2 macrophages support tissue repair by promoting angiogenesis and inflammation resolution, but prolonged or excessive M2 activation may drive pathological remodeling and contribute to fibrosis and tumor progression through sustained secretion of pro-fibrotic mediators such as TGF-β. Thus, successful tissue regeneration depends not on a static macrophage phenotype but on a timely transition between macrophage polarization, underscoring the importance of delicate spatiotemporal immunomodulatory control. Recent advances in biomaterials have enabled precise modulation of macrophage polarization, thereby promoting tissue regeneration and wound healing. Hydrogel-based constructs, recognized for their excellent biocompatibility, tunable physical characteristics, and controlled delivery capabilities, have emerged as promising platforms for immunomodulatory tissue repair [236]. For instance, photothermal hydrogels encapsulating bacteria-capturing Bio-MOFs significantly promoted infectious wound healing by inducing macrophage polarization towards an M2 phenotype, thus reducing inflammation and facilitating tissue regeneration [237]. Similarly, bimetal-organic framework/GOx-based hydrogel dressings have been demonstrated to accelerate wound healing by exerting dual antibacterial and immunomodulatory effects, particularly by promoting the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages [238]. Overall, these studies highlight the pivotal role of biomaterials-driven macrophage polarization in directing tissue regeneration and functional restoration. Accordingly, a critical challenge and opportunity in biomaterial design lies in the spatiotemporal regulation of macrophage phenotype, ensuring that appropriate immune programs are activated at each stage of the regenerative processes.

Dendritic cells

Dendritic cells (DCs) are professional antigen-presenting cells that play a crucial role in bridging innate and adaptive immunity. They are strategically distributed throughout peripheral tissues and lymphoid organs, where they continuously monitor the local environment for pathogens, foreign materials, and cellular debris. DCs originate from both myeloid and lymphoid hematopoietic precursors and differentiate into functionally distinct subsets, including conventional DCs (cDCs), plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), and monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs) [239]. The primary function of DCs is to capture, process, and present antigens. Immature DCs residing in peripheral tissues exhibit high phagocytic capacity and express a wide range of PRRs, enabling efficient detection of PAMPs and DAMPs. Upon antigen encounter and PRRs-mediated activation, such as through TLRs, DCs undergo maturation. This process is characterized by upregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II, increased expression of costimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86, CD40), and secretion of immune-related cytokines [240]. Mature DCs migrate to secondary lymphoid organs, where they present processed antigens to naive T cells and initiate antigen-specific adaptive immune responses [241]. By differentially producing cytokines and chemokines, DCs play a critical role in shaping T cell fate, promoting the differentiation of Th1, Th2, Th17, and regulatory T cells (Tregs). For instance, IL-12 promotes the differentiation of Th1 cells, whereas IL-4 drives the differentiation of Th2 cells. Additionally, DCs contribute to immune tolerance by inducing Tregs and facilitating the deletion of self-reactive T cells in both the thymus and the periphery.

Notably, DCs act as critical sensors and regulators of host immune response to biomaterials. A comprehensive review by Zhu et al. [242] highlighted how biomaterial properties, including surface topography, stiffness, hydrophilicity, and degradation products, affect DC recruitment, activation, maturation, and phenotype commitment. Biomaterials that facilitate DC maturation typically enhance the expression of costimulatory molecules, MHC-II, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-12 and TNF-α), thereby increasing T cell activation and the risk of immune rejection. In contrast, biomaterials that induce a tolerogenic DC phenotype, characterized by reduced expression of activation markers and elevated secretion of IL-10 or TGF-β, can suppress excessive immune activation, support immune tolerance, and improve tissue integration. Mechanistically, biomaterial cues have been shown to activate various intracellular signaling pathways in DCs, including NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K/Akt. These insights highlight the critical role of DCs as both detectors and regulators of biomaterial-induced immunity, emphasizing the significance of customizing material design to influence DC phenotype, which may potentially improve implant biocompatibility and ultimately lead to better regenerative outcomes.

Adaptive immunity

Adaptive immunity is a highly specialized branch of the immune system that provides antigen-specific responses following exposure to distinct foreign antigens. In contrast to innate immunity, which provides immediate but non-specific defense, adaptive immunity is characterized by high specificity, immunological memory, and the ability to mount a stronger, faster response upon re-exposure to the same antigen [243]. This complex defense mechanism develops through three fundamental processes: antigen recognition by unique receptors, T cell receptors (TCRs) and B cell receptors (BCRs), followed by clonal selection and expansion of antigen-specific lymphocytes, and the establishment of immunological memory. Adaptive immunity operates through two complementary pathways: cell-mediated immunity, mediated by T lymphocytes, and humoral immunity, mediated by B lymphocytes. Additionally, one distinct feature of adaptive immunity is MHC-restricted antigen presentation. In this process, DCs and other antigen-presenting cells (APCs) process antigens and present peptide fragments on MHC-I and MHC-II molecules, enabling precise activation of various T cells and contributing to subsequent immune responses [241]. Importantly, dysregulation of adaptive immunity can lead to autoimmune diseases, immunodeficiencies, chronic inflammatory disorders, and impaired tissue repair, underscoring the importance of balanced adaptive immune activation.

T cells: orchestrators of cell-mediated immunity

T cells are central components of adaptive immunity, serving as key regulators in immune surveillance, pathogen clearance, and immune hemostasis. T cells originate from thymic precursors and express specific TCRs that recognize antigenic peptides presented by MHC molecules. Based on surface markers and function, T cells are broadly classified into CD4+ helper T (Th) cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T (Tc) cells, each fulfilling distinct immunological roles [244]. CD4+ Th cells differentiate into multiple functional subsets, including Th1, Th2, Th17, T follicular helper (Tfh), and Tregs, that modulate immune responses mainly through the secretion of cytokines. Specifically, Th1 cells enhance macrophage activation and defense against intracellular pathogens by secreting IFN-γ and TNF-α, while Th2 cells promote antibody production and eosinophil responses by secreting IL-4 and IL-5. In contrast, CD8+ Tc cells directly eliminate virus-infected or malignant cells by releasing perforin and granzyme or via Fas/FasL-induced apoptosis. Importantly, Tregs play a critical role in maintaining immune tolerance by suppressing autoreactive lymphocytes and preventing excessive immune activation [245]. The activation of T cells requires three coordinated signals: TCR engagement, costimulation (e.g., CD28-B7 interaction), and cytokine-mediated signaling. Following activation, subsets of T cells differentiate into memory T cells (central and effector subsets), enabling rapid and robust immune responses upon re-exposure. Overall, dysregulated T cell responses can lead to chronic infections, autoimmune diseases, and impaired tissue remodeling, reflecting their ability in both immune defense and tissue hemostasis.

Increasing evidence underscores the crucial role of T cells, particularly Tregs, in tissue repair, where they actively shape the immune microenvironment [245]. Tregs not only suppress excessive, detrimental inflammation but also directly promote regeneration by inducing pro-healing macrophage polarization and secreting essential growth factors that activate resident progenitor and stem cells. Consequently, biomaterials are being strategically engineered as sophisticated immuno-instructive scaffolds or delivery platforms, where properties such as chemical composition, mechanical stiffness, surface topography, and degradation kinetics are leveraged as immunomodulatory signals to bias T cell differentiation away from pro-fibrotic or highly inflammatory phenotypes (e.g., Th1/Th17) and toward pro-regenerative phenotypes (e.g., Tregs), thereby optimizing the local environment to support successful tissue remodeling and functional recovery. For example, the polarization profiles of T cells play a crucial role in determining regenerative outcomes of nerve tissue. A transition from Th1- to Th2-dominated immunity correlates with enhanced angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and functional recovery, as demonstrated in nerve injury models where Th2 responses facilitate remyelination and axonal regeneration [246]. Besides the nervous system, Tregs have been shown to play a key role in promoting repair or regeneration in multiple tissues, including the heart [247,248], muscle [249,250], the central nervous system [251], the lungs [252], and the skin [253]. However, in settings of severe or persistent inflammation, endogenous Tregs may be insufficient to restore immune balance. To therapeutically improve Treg function and enhance regenerative outcomes, MSCs are frequently employed due to their potent immunoregulatory capacity. As reported by Li et al. [254], MSCs can serve as powerful regulators that boost Treg activity through both direct cell-cell interactions and paracrine cytokine signaling. Emerging strategies further explored MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) as effective cell-free carriers for delivering immunoregulatory factors to optimize the local Treg response and promote tissue regeneration [255,256]. Together, these findings underscore that biomaterial strategies designed to amplify Tregs-mediated immune regulation represent a potent and emerging approach for improving healing efficiency, limiting fibrosis, and achieving functional integration in regenerative medicine.

B cells: primary mediators of humoral immunity

B cells are the principal mediators of humoral immunity, contributing to host defense through the production of antigen-specific immunoglobulins (Igs), known as antibodies, which function through neutralization (blocking pathogens), opsonization (enhancing phagocytosis), and complement activation (triggering pathogen lysis). B cells originate and mature in the bone marrow, where they undergo V(D)J recombination to generate BCRs on their surface. Each BCR recognizes a specific antigenic epitope, providing the molecular basis for antibody specificity and diversity. Upon encountering antigens, B cells are activated in secondary lymphoid organs, where they interact with CD4+ Th cells through MHC-II-mediated antigen presentation and CD40−CD40L signaling. Following activation, B cells differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete large amounts of antibodies, or into memory B cells, which persist for a long time to support rapid recall responses and contribute to long-term immune memory and protection. Antibodies produced by plasma cells, including IgM, IgG, IgA, and IgE, play well-defined roles in humoral immunity. While IgD is primarily membrane-bound, it may also be secreted in small quantities by plasma cells in mucosal tissues, though its function remains unclear. Beyond antibody production, B cells also contribute to immune regulation by secreting cytokines such as IL-10 and IL-35 and by functioning as antigen-presenting cells, thereby influencing T cell activation and immune homeostasis.

Biomaterials have been shown to elicit material-specific B cell responses, which may, in turn, influence the immune microenvironment and tissue regeneration. For example, natural ECM-derived scaffolds have been demonstrated to promote B cell differentiation toward antibody-secreting phenotypes, whereas certain synthetic polymers, such as polycaprolactone (PCL), delay B cell influx and favor antigen-presenting phenotypes, thereby shaping downstream immune responses and ultimate regenerative outcomes [257]. Despite these findings, the precise roles of B cells in biomaterial-mediated immune regulation and their responses to different biomaterial properties remain poorly defined [258]. More broadly, these findings highlight a critical knowledge gap: how distinct biophysical and biochemical properties of biomaterials instruct immune cell behavior across multiple immune lineages. To address this challenge, the following sections examine how specific material properties shape immune responses, aiming to provide design principles for engineering biomaterials that actively direct immune regulation toward regenerative outcomes.

Biomaterial property-driven immunomodulation for tissue regeneration

Immune responses to implanted biomaterials are strongly influenced by the physicochemical properties of materials, particularly those associated with the material surface. These properties govern the nature and magnitude of interactions between biomaterials and the host immune system, shaping responses ranging from immediate innate immune activation to long-term adaptive immune regulation. In particular, biomaterial properties such as material origin, surface topography (including roughness and patterning), charge, chemistry, functional moieties, wettability, size and shape, mechanical properties (e.g., rigidity and stiffness), degradability, and crosslinking density have all been shown to influence immune response and ultimately determine whether inflammatory or pro-healing outcomes prevail. In this section, we examine how these material properties modulate immune cell behaviors, including macrophage polarization, cytokine and chemokine production, neutrophil and dendritic cell activity, immune cell recruitment, and the FBR, thereby shaping the immune microenvironment and critically impacting tissue regeneration. It should be noted that the following relationships should be interpreted as context-dependent associations rather than universal design rules. Material properties rarely vary independently: modifying stiffness may also alter ligand density, porosity, or degradation; changing topography can alter wettability and protein adsorption; and introducing charged functional groups can change both surface hydration and protein-corona composition. Consequently, an immune phenotype attributed to one nominal property may reflect the combined effects of several covarying parameters.

Material origin

Biomaterials can be broadly classified as natural or synthetic, which differ fundamentally in how they engage host immune responses. Natural biomaterials such as decellularized ECM (dECM), alginate, chitosan, collagen, dextran, silk fibroin, and hyaluronic acid are commonly employed as implant materials in tissue engineering. These materials inherently resemble native tissue components and often contain ligands that can interact with cell receptors in specific ways. As a result, they can actively modulate immune cell behavior by engaging pathways associated with normal wound healing and tissue repair processes. In many cases, they degrade into non-toxic natural byproducts and support constructive remodeling without causing severe chronic inflammation. For example, collagen-based scaffolds typically do not provoke a classic FBR; instead, they primarily engage macrophages within the normal repair cascade, facilitating a more constructive and timely transition from an inflammatory to a pro-regenerative phenotype that favors tissue integration and remodeling [259,260]. Despite these advantages, natural biomaterials can still introduce immunological risks. For example, insufficient decellularization or the presence of xenogeneic materials may result in residual antigenic components that pose a risk of residual antigenicity [261]. This potential immunogenicity must be carefully considered during biomaterial selection and processing.

In contrast, synthetic materials such as PCL, PLGA, polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) are widely used due to their reproducibility and tunable properties. However, unlike natural biomaterials, many synthetic biomaterials lack intrinsic bioactive ligands and are therefore initially perceived by the host through a layer of adsorbed plasma proteins and DAMPs rather than defined biological motifs. As a result, poorly degradable synthetic implants often elicit a canonical FBR. Since most synthetic polymers lack intrinsic peptide epitopes that can be processed and presented to TCRs via MHC pathways, their FBR formation response is primarily mediated by innate immunity and reflects the inability of the host to enzymatically degrade or metabolically integrate these materials, leading to their isolation rather than remodeling within host tissue [214,262]. However, adaptive immune responses can still arise and be secondary under specific conditions, such as when synthetic materials degrade into reactive ions or small molecules that covalently modify self-proteins (hapten formation) or when they are used to deliver protein or peptide antigens [214,263,264]. Through appropriate surface modification strategies (e.g., PEGylation), synthetic polymers can effectively modulate protein adsorption on their surfaces, thereby attenuating or changing immune recognition [265]. For instance, PEGylated PLGA NPs containing auto-antigen peptide MOG35-55 without any tolerogenic drugs have been shown to sufficiently induce antigen-specific immune tolerance [266].

Overall, material origin critically influences whether the immune response will be an orchestrated regenerative process or a protective FBR. An emerging strategy lies in the rational design of composite biomaterials that integrate the biological advantages of natural matrices with the tunability and structural precision of synthetic materials. For example, materials such as gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) combine bioactive motifs derived from natural collagen with controllable photo-crosslinking density and mechanical properties, enabling more precise modulation of immune cell recruitment, macrophage polarization, and degradation kinetics [267]. Similarly, dECM-synthetic polymer composites and functionalized hydrogel systems offer opportunities to fine-tune protein adsorption, ligand presentation, and spatiotemporal immune signaling [268,269]. By integrating biological recognition with engineered control, such composite systems may provide a more effective means of shaping the trajectory of immune responses toward regenerative integration rather than fibrotic isolation. Moving forward, we believe the design of natural-synthetic hybrid biomaterials represents a promising direction for harmonizing immune compatibility with structural performance in regenerative medicine.

Topography

As we discussed above, topography refers to the physical characteristics of a biomaterial's surface, including roughness, texture, and the presence of micro- and nanoscale features. These surface features, including grooves, ridges, fibers, pillars, pores, and pattern architecture, all affect roughness and morphology detectable by adherent cells, profoundly altering cell morphology, orientation, and cytoskeletal organization. These cues regulate focal adhesion and integrin clustering, thereby altering cytoskeletal tension and intracellular signaling cascades that shape downstream immune responses, including macrophage polarization [270]. Surface roughness is typically quantified using parameters such as Ra (2D roughness) and Sa (3D areal roughness), and it is a key topographical property that influences immune responses [271]. Studies have shown that biomaterials with moderately rough surfaces can facilitate polarization of M2 macrophages and the production of IL-10 and TGF-β, whereas smooth biomaterials may favor M1 polarization and enhance TNF-α and IL-6 production [272]. However, the immunological effects of roughness are scale-dependent and context-specific [273]. In general, increasing roughness within an optimal micro-scale range can improve tissue integration by supporting constructive immune remodeling, but excessive roughness may exacerbate acute inflammatory reactions, particularly those driven by neutrophils and macrophages, leading to mixed or pro-inflammatory phenotypes [216].

Beyond roughness, engineered micro- and nanoscale topographies have emerged as potent immunomodulatory tools. For example, well-defined fibrous architectures have been shown to regulate macrophage polarization and orchestrate sequential immune responses that promote accelerated and scarless tissue regeneration [274]. Recent evidence further demonstrates that nano- and micro-structured surfaces can modulate macrophage polarization via mechanosensitive PIEZO1 channels, creating an anti-inflammatory microenvironment that promotes osteogenic differentiation and tissue regeneration [275]. In addition to macrophages, engineered nanotopographies also influence dendritic cell behavior; For instance, vertically aligned nanoscale pillars were shown to regulate DC plasma membrane deformation and cytoskeletal organization, thereby altering receptor clustering and downstream signaling pathways, including Syk and ERK [276]. These findings reinforce the notion that surface topographies function as primary biophysical regulators of immune cell morphology, cytoskeletal tension, and signaling pathways, rather than merely passive structural features that influence cell attachment. By influencing integrin organization, membrane curvature, and mechanosensitive pathways, topographical cues can bias immune cell activation and polarization in a context-dependent manner. This suggests that rational control of micro- and nanoscale architecture offers a powerful strategy to steer immune responses toward pro-regenerative phenotypes, highlighting surface design as a critical lever in immunomodulatory biomaterial engineering.

Wettability

Surface wettability is a critical determinant of host-material interactions, primarily by influencing protein adsorption and immune recognition. Hydrophobic surfaces, characterized by higher water contact angles, tend to adsorb greater amounts of plasma proteins and promote conformational changes that expose epitopes, thereby engaging pattern recognition receptors on immune cells [277]. In contrast, relatively hydrophilic surfaces generally demonstrate reduced non-specific protein adsorption and limited protein denaturation, which is often associated with attenuated FBR [278]. At the cellular level, neutrophils cultured on hydrophobic surfaces have been shown to secrete higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), release granular enzymes, and exhibit enhanced NET formation compared to their responses on hydrophilic surfaces [216]. Such early neutrophil activation may subsequently amplify macrophage recruitment and polarization, influencing the overall trajectory of the host response. Although no single contact angle value universally predicts immune outcomes, a recurring concept in surface science is the “Berg limit” (∼65°), which has been repeatedly observed as a threshold above which biomatter adhesion and biofouling-related attraction become more favorable in aqueous environments [279]. Consistently, protein adsorption capacity generally decreases with increasing hydrophilicity due to the energetic penalty of surface dehydration, linking wettability to the formation and evolution of the protein interphase [279,280]. Thus, wettability shapes not only the quantity of adsorbed proteins but also the composition and structural evolution of the protein interphase that mediates downstream immune signaling. Nevertheless, these findings highlight that immunomodulation by wettability is not solely governed by contact angle magnitude, but also by surface chemistry, charge, and chemical moieties, as discussed in the following section. From a biomaterial design perspective, enhancing surface hydrophilicity by plasma treatment or surface modification with hydrophilic polymers such as PEG, polyethylene oxide (PEO), poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (PHEMA), and PAM is a widely adopted strategy to reduce protein fouling and mitigate excessive immune activation.

Surface charge and chemical moieties

Surface charge is a critical regulator of biomaterial-immune interactions, primarily by influencing protein adsorption, electrostatic interactions, and membrane engagement. Charged surfaces readily adsorb host proteins (e.g., Igs, opsonins) and interact with cell membranes, thereby mediating downstream immune recognition [281]. For instance, it was found that the chemical functionality of peptide hydrogels, particularly surface charge, governs early host immune responses by modulating immune cell infiltration, inflammation, and tissue remodeling outcomes (Fig. 6a) [282]. In general, cationic (positively charged) surfaces tend to trigger stronger acute inflammatory responses than neutral or negatively charged biomaterial surfaces [283]. This can be partially attributed to enhanced membrane disruption, increased cellular uptake, and activation of the inflammasome, a protein complex that activates a highly pro-inflammatory signaling cascade of the innate immune system. In contrast, anionic (negatively charged) surfaces may reduce certain immune responses by altering protein adsorption patterns and preventing antigen-presenting cells from taking up biomaterials [284,285]. Beyond net surface charge, specific chemical functional groups on biomaterial surfaces play an important role in shaping immune outcomes. Distinct chemical moieties such as methyl (–CH3), hydroxyl (–OH), amine (–NH2), and carboxyl (–COOH) groups, each create different interfacial chemistries that profoundly influence hydration layers, protein binding profiles, and immune cell adhesion and polarization [214]. For example, in an in vivo rodent air-pouch model, self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) presenting different terminal groups (–OH, –COOH, and –CH3) elicited distinct acute inflammatory profiles. Surfaces terminated with –OH and –CH3 induced greater recruitment of inflammatory cells, with –CH3 surfaces provoking the strongest neutrophil-dominated response. Interestingly, while CH3-coated implants attracted the greatest number of inflammatory cells into the surrounding tissue, they exhibited relatively low leukocyte adhesion directly on the implant surface [287]. In another study, polystyrene surfaces functionalized with –COOH groups have been associated with enhanced tissue integration and promoted anti-inflammatory macrophage responses by altering protein adsorption patterns that enhance integrin signaling [288]. This is supported by a study showing that negatively charged carboxylated surfaces can mitigate inflammatory responses and alter the migratory behavior and function of circulating macrophages [284], thereby suppressing macrophage-mediated inflammatory activity and promoting a regulatory T-cell phenotype [289]. However, the relationship between specific functional groups and macrophage polarization is not universally fixed. For instance, carboxylated nanorods have also been conversely reported to promote M1 polarization, whereas amino-terminated nanorods have been shown to induce an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [290]. These discrepancies highlight that immune responses are not dictated solely by surface chemistry but are influenced by material geometry, protein corona composition, charge density, and the surrounding biological milieu. Consistent with this complexity, analysis of the protein layer adsorbed onto the polymer from the culture medium indicated that thicker or more denatured protein interphases may be associated with M1 macrophage phenotypes, whereas more stable or selectively adsorbed protein layers can favor M2 macrophage responses [291]. Thus, surface chemistry functions as part of an integrated physicochemical framework, where electrostatic interactions, hydration energetics, and protein interphase evolution collectively determine immune activation states.

Geometry

The geometry of a biomaterial, primarily its size and shape, is a critical determinant of immune recognition and host responses across formats ranging from bulk implants and scaffold pores to microspheres and nanoscale drug delivery systems. Notably, size-dependent immune behavior is not linear and may exhibit threshold-like effects. For example, spherical implants with diameters ≥1.5 mm have been reported to markedly reduce FBR and fibrotic encapsulation compared with smaller spheres across multiple material systems, suggesting a geometry-dependent threshold effect in vivo [292]. Increasing sphere size has been associated with reduced recruitment of innate immune cells in surrounding tissue, which may reflect geometric constraints that objects beyond a certain size cannot be fully engaged or internalized by phagocytic cells, and larger implants have relatively lower surface area per volume relative to smaller particles, potentially reducing cellular interaction density [221,292]. In contrast, micron- and submicron-sized materials strongly engage phagocytic cells. The classic paradigm is that particles below ∼10 μm are readily internalized by macrophages via phagocytosis [293]. The pore size of scaffolds also alters macrophage behaviors and immune responses in vivo. For example, a larger pore size has been associated with enhanced expression of M2-related markers and decreased expression level of M1 phenotypes [294].

Beyond size, the shape of a biomaterial also plays an important role in affecting immune cell interaction. Phagocytic cells like macrophages and neutrophils are sensitive to the shape of biomaterials; the ease of phagocytosis and subsequent immune activation can vary dramatically with particle shape, even when the total volume is held constant. Spherical particles are generally the most easily phagocytosed shape, as they present a consistent curvature that the macrophages can wrap their membrane around symmetrically. In contrast, elongated or high aspect ratio shapes, such as fibers, filaments, and rods, are more difficult to engulf [295], which may ultimately lead to impaired phagocytosis and exacerbated inflammation, as macrophages remain in an activated state and release inflammatory mediators and ROS that are highly related to the FBGCs and fiber encapsulation [296]. For instance, an injectable composite combining sphere-shaped microspheres and short rod-like microfibers has been shown to significant difference in regulating macrophage recruitment, polarization, and the balance between scar formation and pro-regenerative tissue remodeling [297]. Together, these observations highlight that biomaterial geometry exerts a powerful immunomodulatory effect and provides an additional design axis for steering immune responses toward constructive regeneration.

Mechanical properties

Mechanical properties, particularly structural rigidity (an extrinsic property) and substrate stiffness (an intrinsic property), are key parameters that influence biomaterial-immune interactions. The mechanical mismatch between implant and host tissue can significantly amplify immune activation [298]. Rigid biomaterials that resist deformation under cellular forces can lead to sustained mechanical frustration for adherent immune cells. For example, macrophages attempting to phagocytose a rigid particle or to exert traction on a rigid non-deformable surface will experience high cytoskeletal tension, which activates mechanosensitive signaling pathways involving integrins, focal adhesion kinase, and mechanosensitive ion channels, ultimately promoting pro-inflammatory responses and increasing ROS production [299]. In contrast, more flexible biomaterials such as silicone or soft hydrogels can deform to accommodate local deformation imposed by surrounding tissues, thereby reducing mechanical stress and mitigating severe FBR [300]. For example, the inflammatory responses to compliant silicone breast implants are generally milder than those observed with comparably sized rigid metal implants, partly because the silicone elastomer can deform slightly in response to cellular and tissue forces [301].

At the cellular level, immune cells are mechanosensitive and can sense substrate stiffness via focal adhesions and actin cytoskeleton tension. As a result, immune cells respond to stiffness by activating mechanotransduction pathways, including YAP/TAZ transcriptional signaling and mechanosensitive ion channels such as Piezo1 [302]. Specifically, studies have shown that stiff substrates can enhance YAP nuclear localization and Piezo1 activation, which is associated with inflammatory macrophage signaling, whereas softer matrices reduce these signals and are frequently associated with more pro-regenerative phenotypes (Fig. 6b) [286,303]. Overall, mechanical compliance represents an important design parameter in immunomodulatory biomaterials. Materials with stiffness closer to that of soft tissues (kPa range) generally promote more balanced immune responses, while stiff substrates (MPa to GPa range) can trigger sustained inflammatory activation through increased cytoskeletal tension and mechanotransductive pathways. Therefore, rational control of biomaterial mechanics provides a potent strategy to modulate immune responses and develop immuno-instructive biomaterials.

Degradability

The degradability of biomaterials is a key property that mediates host immune responses, facilitates tissue remodeling, and prevents chronic inflammation. Non-degradable or persistent materials remain as long-term foreign bodies and often provoke sustained FBR. In such cases, macrophages and FBGCs accumulate at the material interface and promote the formation of a collagenous capsule that isolates the implant from surrounding tissue [216]. In contrast, biodegradable materials can create space for ECM deposition, vascular infiltration, and tissue ingrowth [304]. However, the rate and nature of degradation of biodegradable materials are key determinants of immune outcomes, as their degradation byproducts can directly interact with surrounding immune cells, which shape the temporal trajectory of the host immune response. Rapid degradation may produce excessive debris or acidic byproducts that accumulate locally and trigger inflammatory signaling, whereas extremely slow degradation is similar to non-degradable biomaterials that may lead to cysts and chronic FBRs [304]. At the cellular level, macrophages and neutrophils play a central role in orchestrating the clearance of biomaterials. In addition to directly phagocytosing small degradation fragments, they also release enzymes such as collagenases and esterases, as well as ROS, to break down larger material structures [293]. The chemical nature of the degradation byproducts also plays an important role in the immune responses. For instance, acidification induced by certain polymer degradation products such as lactic acid can activate inflammatory pathways such as the NLRP3 inflammasome and recruit additional immune cells [305]. A comparable study demonstrated that nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) fabricated from poly(glycerol sebacate) (PGS) promoted in vivo nerve regeneration with reduced inflammation, fibrosis, and surrounding tissue swelling compared to those made from PLGA [306]. This may be attributed to the more acidic degradation products of PLGA, which can elevate host immune responses [307,308]. Conversely, some degradation byproducts such as citric acid and folic acid may exert anti-inflammatory or immunoregulatory effects [83,309,310], highlighting opportunities to engineer immune-instructive biomaterials with degradation profiles that actively guide immune responses. Ultimately, optimal biomaterial performance requires matching the degradation rate of the material to the rate of tissue regeneration and precisely controlling the degradation products. If the scaffold degrades significantly faster than new tissue can form, the resulting structural gaps and debris accumulation may sustain inflammatory macrophage activation. In contrast, materials that degrade too slowly may become totally encapsulated and persist as chronic foreign bodies [212].

From individual material properties to integrated biomaterial design

While the studies discussed above collectively demonstrate that biomaterial properties can modulate immune-cell behavior, the strength of the evidence varies considerably across different material systems and experimental models. Many reported relationships have been established using simplified in vitro macrophage monocultures or immortalized cell lines, which do not fully recapitulate the dynamic multicellular immune microenvironment surrounding implanted biomaterials. In addition, most studies assess immune responses at a single time point using a limited panel of inflammatory markers, making it difficult to distinguish transient inflammatory activation from long-term immune remodeling. Temporal differences may also contribute to apparently conflicting findings among studies. A material that increases inflammatory markers shortly after implantation may still support constructive regeneration if the response subsequently resolves, whereas an apparently reparative immune phenotype observed at a single time point may become maladaptive if profibrotic or matrix-remodeling signals persist. Single-time-point measurements therefore provide only a limited representation of biomaterial–immune interactions. Longitudinal analyses of immune-cell recruitment, phenotypic transitions, cytokine production, fibrosis, vascularization, and tissue recovery are needed to distinguish productive inflammation from unresolved or maladaptive immune activation.

Moreover, apparently conflicting observations reported for individual material properties should not necessarily be viewed as contradictory. Differences in material composition, fabrication methods, protein adsorption, species, cell source, and culture conditions can all influence immune outcomes. For example, material stiffness or surface topography has been reported to promote either pro-inflammatory or pro-regenerative macrophage phenotypes depending on the surrounding biochemical environment and the duration of exposure. Therefore, current evidence supports the concept that biomaterial-driven immune regulation is highly context dependent rather than governed by universal one-property-one-phenotype relationships. Future studies combining standardized material characterization, multicellular models, longitudinal analyses, and mechanistic perturbation experiments will be essential for establishing robust design principles for immune-instructive biomaterials.

Recognizing these limitations does not diminish the importance of the reported findings. Rather, it highlights that the immune response should be interpreted as an emergent property arising from multiple interacting biomaterial cues rather than isolated material parameters. Taken together, we summarized how core biomaterial features, including material origin, size, shape, mechanical properties, surface topography, degradability, wettability, surface charge, and chemical moieties, influence macrophage polarization and cytokine secretion profiles, thereby modulating the immune response to implants. Instead, multiple physicochemical cues often interact cooperatively or competitively to shape immune outcomes. For example, a recent study systematically decoupled pore size and stiffness in 3D gelatin scaffolds and demonstrated that smaller pores, coupled with softer matrices, were associated with elevated pro-inflammatory markers, whereas larger pores in stiffer scaffolds were associated with reduced inflammatory activation and increased expression of reparative markers [294]. In another study, He et al. [273] designed a series of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) implants with different stiffnesses at ∼ MPa and surface topologies at tens of micrometers to investigate the regulatory rules governing these two material cues on inflammatory responses. They found that stiffness and surface topology do not independently regulate macrophage activation; rather, they interact competitively (Fig. 6c). Surface topography suppressed M1-associated inflammatory responses in softer implants, yet this effect was abolished in stiffer materials. In the absence of topographical features, unexpectedly, lower stiffness elicited stronger inflammatory responses. Mechanistically, increased stiffness enhanced focal adhesion formation and activated the MAPK/NF-κB signaling axis, thereby overriding the modulatory influence of surface topology. These findings highlight that material cues can partner or compete to produce synthesized immune effects, underscoring the necessity of considering material properties as an integrated system rather than isolated variables when designing immunomodulatory biomaterials. Such a design framework moves beyond traditional biocompatibility concepts toward immune-instructive biomaterials, in which coordinated control of multiple material cues enables precise regulation of host responses to support durable tissue regeneration. In this context, emerging approaches that integrate artificial intelligence (AI) with biomaterials engineering offer new opportunities to navigate the complex, multidimensional design space. By leveraging machine learning to analyze relationships among material properties, protein corona formation, and immune responses, it may become possible to predict and optimize biomaterial configurations that promote desirable immunological outcomes. Such data-driven strategies could accelerate the rational design of next-generation biomaterials with precisely tuned, context-dependent immunomodulatory functions.

Integrated epigenetic and immunomodulatory biomaterials

A bidirectional mechanistic framework for immune-epigenetic coupling

Although epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation have traditionally been discussed as separate biological processes, they are functionally interconnected through a bidirectional regulatory circuit. In this circuit, biomaterial- and injury-derived signals reshape chromatin states in immune and tissue-resident cells, while the resulting epigenetic landscape determines the magnitude, duration, and memory of subsequent immune responses. Immune-cell-derived cytokines, metabolites, ROS, and matrix-remodeling factors further modify the local microenvironment and generate secondary signals that feed back to the epigenetic machinery. Immune-epigenetic coupling should therefore not be viewed as a linear sequence in which one process simply occurs upstream of the other, but as a dynamic and self-modifying system [311,312]. This coupling can be organized into five interconnected functional layers: extracellular cue generation and sensing, intracellular signal integration, chromatin encoding, immune functional output, and feedback or memory formation. These layers provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how biomaterials coordinate epigenetic and immunological responses [22,311,312].

At the first layer, physicochemical properties of biomaterials, including stiffness, topography, porosity, wettability, surface chemistry, and degradation behavior, are detected by cellular receptors and mechanosensors [22,281,313,314]. Integrins, pattern-recognition receptors, scavenger receptors, TRPV4 channels, Piezo channels, and cytoskeleton-associated complexes sense material-derived mechanical and biochemical information [27,286,302,303,[315], [316], [317]]. In parallel, implantation-associated tissue damage generates damage-associated molecular patterns, complement activation products, extracellular nucleic acids, inflammatory cytokines, and changes in oxygen and nutrient availability [212,218,222,223]. Together, these material- and tissue-derived cues initiate the host response.

At the second layer, extracellular signals are integrated through inflammatory, mechanical, and metabolic pathways. Pattern-recognition receptors and cytokine receptors activate NF-κB, MAPK-AP-1, JAK-STAT, IRF, and SMAD signaling, whereas integrin engagement, ion-channel activation, and cytoskeletal tension regulate focal adhesion signaling, calcium dynamics, YAP/TAZ activity, nuclear mechanics, and ROS production [234,235,286,302,303,318]. These pathways do not merely induce transient cytoplasmic signaling. Signal-responsive transcription factors enter the nucleus and interact with chromatin regulators, thereby directly connecting immune activation to epigenetic remodeling [311,312,[319], [320], [321], [322]].

The third layer involves chromatin encoding, through which inflammatory and material-derived signals are converted into locus-specific changes in gene accessibility [311,312,319]. For example, TNF-α, microbial ligands, and damage-associated signals activate NF-κB, which binds inflammatory promoters and enhancers and recruits co-activators such as p300/CBP and ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complexes. These events increase histone acetylation, remodel nucleosomes, and facilitate transcription of cytokines, chemokines, and other immune-response genes [[319], [320], [321], [322]]. Interferon signaling similarly activates STAT and IRF proteins, which cooperate with chromatin regulators to establish antiviral and inflammatory gene programs [312]. By contrast, anti-inflammatory and reparative cytokines can activate transcription factors such as STAT6 or SMADs, which recruit distinct co-regulatory complexes and promote chromatin states associated with inflammatory resolution, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and tissue repair [234,235,312,318].

Immune signaling can also influence chromatin indirectly through metabolic reprogramming. Activation of macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes alters glycolysis, mitochondrial metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid utilization [311,323,324]. These metabolic changes modify the intracellular availability of acetyl-CoA, S-adenosylmethionine, α-ketoglutarate, succinate, fumarate, and NAD+, which function as substrates, cofactors, or regulators of histone acetyltransferases, methyltransferases, demethylases, and deacetylases [11,57,311,323,324]. Consequently, inflammatory signaling can reshape chromatin through both transcription-factor-directed recruitment of epigenetic enzymes and metabolism-dependent regulation of their catalytic activity [11,311,312,323,324].

Importantly, the relationship also operates in the opposite direction. The pre-existing epigenetic landscape determines which immune genes are accessible before stimulation and establishes the threshold for cellular activation [311,312]. Accessible or primed enhancers permit rapid recruitment of NF-κB, STATs, IRFs, and AP-1, whereas DNA methylation, histone deacetylation, repressive histone modifications, or compact chromatin can restrict transcription-factor binding [312,319,320]. Epigenetic remodeling therefore determines whether the same biomaterial-derived stimulus induces strong inflammation, immune tolerance, reparative activation, or minimal cellular response. It also controls the ability of immune cells to transition between functional states after the initial stimulus has been removed [312] [[325], [326], [327], [328]].

The fourth layer is the transcriptional and functional immune output. Depending on the chromatin context, biomaterial sensing may induce cytokine production, inflammasome activation, phagocytosis, antigen presentation, antimicrobial defense, immune-cell recruitment, inflammatory resolution, angiogenesis, or matrix remodeling [4,5,209,234,235,301,318,329,330]. These outputs cannot be explained solely by the activation of individual signaling pathways, as signaling intensity and duration are filtered by the chromatin state of each responding cell [311,312]. Epigenetic regulation thus provides a molecular basis for cell-type-specific and context-dependent responses to the same biomaterial [3,242,257,258,312,313].

The fifth layer consists of feedback regulation and memory formation. Cytokines, chemokines, ROS, extracellular metabolites, proteases, and matrix-remodeling enzymes released by activated immune cells alter the biochemical and mechanical properties of the local microenvironment [[56], [57], [58],234,235,318]. These changes influence material degradation, extracellular-matrix stiffness, nutrient and oxygen availability, and the recruitment or activation of additional cell populations [[56], [57], [58],212,298,300,314,331]. The modified microenvironment then generates new signals that further alter transcription-factor activity, metabolism, and chromatin states in immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, stem cells, and progenitor cells [3,30,37,44,57,311,312].

This feedback can produce different outcomes. Negative-feedback mechanisms, including the recruitment of transcriptional repressors, histone deacetylases, and other inhibitory complexes, can close inflammatory chromatin and promote resolution [312]. Positive-feedback mechanisms can stabilize inflammatory enhancers and sustain cytokine production, thereby contributing to chronic inflammation, fibrosis, or foreign body responses [212,222,298,301,312,314]. When permissive chromatin states persist after the initial signal has disappeared, they may establish trained immunity and enhance responses to subsequent stimulation [[325], [326], [327], [328],[332], [333], [334], [335]]. Conversely, persistent repressive remodeling may generate immune tolerance or exhaustion [325,[335], [336], [337], [338]].

Accordingly, immune–epigenetic coupling can be summarized as a reciprocal sequence: biomaterial and tissue cues activate immune and mechanotransductive pathways; these pathways remodel chromatin through transcription-factor recruitment and metabolic regulation; chromatin states determine immune-cell responsiveness and function; and immune outputs reshape the microenvironment to reinforce, redirect, or terminate the original response [311,312]. This framework provides the conceptual basis for the following sections, which examine the molecular routes of bidirectional coupling, its persistence as innate immune memory, and its implications for biomaterial design.

Molecular routes of bidirectional immune-epigenetic regulation

The bidirectional framework described above is implemented through several interconnected molecular routes. Among them, transcription factor-chromatin coupling, metabolic-epigenetic coupling, and mechanotransduction-dependent chromatin remodeling are particularly important for translating biomaterial-derived and inflammatory signals into immune-cell phenotypes [3,11,27,311,312,323].

Inflammatory signaling-chromatin coupling

Inflammatory signaling affects chromatin primarily through signal-responsive transcription factors. Following activation of pattern-recognition receptors or cytokine receptors, NF-κB, AP-1, STATs, IRFs, and other transcription factors bind selected promoters and enhancers [311,312,[319], [320], [321], [322]]. Their binding is influenced by pre-existing chromatin accessibility and by lineage-determining transcription factors that establish cell-type-specific enhancer repertoires [311,312,320]. Thus, an identical inflammatory stimulus may produce different transcriptional outputs in macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes, or tissue-resident stromal cells [234,235,239,242,243,312,318].

Once bound to chromatin, signal-responsive transcription factors recruit histone acetyltransferases, methyltransferases, demethylases, HDACs, and ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complexes [311,312,[319], [320], [321], [322]]. These regulators alter nucleosome positioning and histone modifications and can either increase or restrict transcriptional accessibility [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19],311,312]. NF-κB-dependent recruitment of p300/CBP, for example, promotes histone acetylation at inflammatory genes, whereas recruitment of repressive complexes during the resolution phase contributes to the termination of cytokine expression [311,312,[319], [320], [321], [322]]. In this manner, immune signals are encoded into chromatin as activating, repressive, or poised regulatory states [311,312].

Chromatin states also determine the interpretation of subsequent immune signals. Promoters and enhancers that remain accessible after initial activation can respond rapidly to a second stimulus, whereas loci that acquire repressive modifications become less responsive [312,325,336]. Epigenetic regulation therefore acts both downstream of inflammatory signaling and upstream of future immune responsiveness [311,312,336].

Macrophages provide a representative example of this reciprocity. Pro-inflammatory signals activate enhancers associated with cytokine production, antimicrobial defense, and inflammasome-related functions [234,235,311,312,318]. Anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair signals establish different enhancer and transcriptional programs associated with efferocytosis, angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, and resolution [209,234,235,298,311,312,318,329]. These states are not fixed binary phenotypes. Rather, macrophages occupy a continuum of states that are continuously reshaped by cytokines, metabolites, mechanical forces, and local chromatin accessibility [234,235,[270], [271], [272], [273],286,[298], [299], [300],302,303,311,312,318,[339], [340], [341]].

Similar principles apply to dendritic cells and lymphocytes. In dendritic cells, chromatin remodeling regulates antigen presentation, co-stimulatory molecule expression, and cytokine production [[239], [240], [241], [242],312]. In T cells, lineage-specific transcription factors cooperate with DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin-remodeling complexes to stabilize effector, regulatory, memory, or exhausted states [244,245,252,312,323,336,342]. Therefore, the immune effects of biomaterials are strongly influenced by whether material-derived signals induce transient transcription-factor activity or more persistent epigenetic reprogramming [3,222,223,242,258,262,263,281,286,[312], [313], [314]].

Metabolic-epigenetic coupling

Immune activation is accompanied by extensive metabolic reprogramming, making cellular metabolism a major intermediary between inflammatory signaling and chromatin regulation [311,323,340,341]. Rapid glycolysis can support cytokine production and generate metabolic intermediates required for biosynthesis, whereas mitochondrial metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid utilization contribute to other immune and reparative states [311,323,340,341,343].

Metabolic changes regulate epigenetic enzymes by modifying the availability of their substrates and cofactors [11,311,323]. Acetyl-CoA supports histone acetylation; S-adenosylmethionine provides methyl groups for histone and DNA methylation; α-ketoglutarate supports dioxygenase-dependent histone and DNA demethylation; NAD + regulates sirtuin activity; and accumulated succinate or fumarate can inhibit α-ketoglutarate-dependent enzymes [11,311,323,340,341]. Changes in ROS and redox balance may additionally influence epigenetic enzyme activity and chromatin organization [132,172,179,311,[344], [345], [346]].

This route is inherently bidirectional. Inflammatory signaling changes metabolism and thereby modifies chromatin, while epigenetic regulation controls the expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters that determine immune-cell metabolic identity [11,311,312,323,324]. A stable inflammatory or reparative state may therefore be maintained through a reinforcing metabolism–chromatin circuit [311,312,323,340,341]. Biomaterials that alter oxygen diffusion, nutrient availability, local pH, ion concentrations, ROS production, or mitochondrial activity may influence immune-cell function through this metabolic–epigenetic interface [57,305,309,311,331,344,347].

Mechanotransduction-chromatin coupling

Biomaterial mechanics provide another route connecting extracellular conditions with immune and epigenetic regulation. Material stiffness, topography, porosity, and surface architecture influence integrin clustering, focal adhesion formation, cytoskeletal tension, ion-channel activity, and nuclear deformation [27,29,30,37,44,294,302,313,317,348]. Mechanical forces can be transmitted through the cytoskeleton and nuclear lamina to alter nuclear organization, nucleosome accessibility, and the spatial distribution of chromatin [27,30,37,44,[91], [92], [93],96,99,101].

Mechanotransductive pathways also intersect with inflammatory signaling. Integrins, TRPV4, Piezo channels, focal adhesion kinases, Rho-family GTPases, and YAP/TAZ can influence NF-κB activity, calcium-dependent signaling, ROS generation, and immune-cell polarization [286,302,303,317,348]. Consequently, a mechanical cue may simultaneously alter inflammatory signaling and chromatin structure rather than acting through two independent branches [27,57,91,93,286,302,303].

The biological outcome depends on signal magnitude, duration, and cellular context. A given stiffness or topography may produce different chromatin and immune responses in macrophages, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and stem cells [29,30,37,96,99,101,[270], [271], [272], [273],286,313]. Moreover, inflammatory remodeling of the extracellular matrix can change tissue stiffness and generate a feedback loop in which immune activation modifies the mechanical environment, which subsequently reinforces or redirects chromatin regulation [[56], [57], [58],[234], [235], [236],300,311,312].

Collectively, these molecular routes demonstrate that epigenetic remodeling is neither a passive consequence of immune activation nor an independent regulator acting in parallel [311,312]. Inflammatory signaling, metabolism, mechanics, and chromatin form an interconnected network that determines immune-cell plasticity [3,11,27,311,312,323]. This perspective also suggests that biomaterial-induced immune responses should be evaluated beyond short-term cytokine levels or surface markers [3,281,313,314]. Chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, DNA methylation, metabolic states, and the reversibility of cellular phenotypes may provide more mechanistic indicators of whether a material induces transient activation, stable repair, immune tolerance, or chronic inflammatory dysfunction [3,11,29,30,311,312,325,336,344].

Epigenetic regulation of immune cells

Mechanistically, epigenetic regulation of immune cells is achieved through DNA methylation, histone modifications, and dynamic chromatin remodeling, which translate environmental and biomaterial-derived cues into stable changes in immune cell identity and function. For instance, epigenetic remodeling driven by metabolic cues is fundamental in shaping T-cell differentiation, activation, and effector functions [323]. Upon activation, metabolic reprogramming alters the availability of key metabolites that serve as substrates for epigenetic enzymes, thereby modulating histone modifications and DNA methylation and controlling lineage-specific gene expression programs. Therefore, distinct metabolic states drive the differentiation of effector and regulatory T cell subsets, illustrating how immune cell function is dynamically governed by epigenetic mechanisms [323]. As a result, epigenetic regulation of immune cells has shown great promise in disease treatment in recent years, particularly in cancer and immune-mediated disorders. For example, metabolites produced by cancer cells can induce epigenetic reprogramming of immune cells, thereby impairing antitumor immunity [273]. Targeting epigenetic factors such as DNMTs and HDACs can restore immune cell function and enhance therapeutic efficacy [349]. Similarly, in the treatment of autoimmune diseases, epigenetic modulation can suppress excessive immune responses and alleviate disease symptoms [350]. Epigenetic regulation also plays a central role in the establishment and maintenance of immune memory, influencing the stability and responsiveness of memory T cells and thereby improving vaccine effectiveness [336,342]. Beyond pharmacological interventions, advances in immune cell engineering further highlight the importance of epigenetic control. For example, in chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, adjusting the epigenetic profile of T-cells can improve their persistence and antitumor potency [337,338].

Collectively, these studies underscore that epigenetic regulation is not merely a downstream consequence of immune activation but a central determinant of immune cell behavior and therapeutic potential. Despite its promise, the clinical translation of epigenetic immunomodulation in disease therapy faces notable challenges. The complexity of epigenetic networks, the heterogeneity among immune cell populations, and the difficulty of achieving cell-type-specific, temporally controlled modulation pose significant barriers. These challenges are particularly relevant when biomaterials are involved, where immune responses are highly dynamic and influenced by multiple competitive or cooperative environmental cues. Meanwhile, this perspective positions epigenetic regulation as a key mechanistic link through which biomaterials can actively instruct immune responses. Therefore, the sensitivity of immune cells to epigenetic regulation offers a promising opportunity for biomaterial design, as discussed in the following sections.

Trained immunity: epigenetic memory in innate immune responses

Epigenetic regulation not only governs immediate immune cell function but can also induce long-lasting changes in immune responsiveness, a phenomenon known as trained immunity, first proposed by Mihai Netea in 2011 [325,326]. Trained immunity describes a form of innate immune memory in which cells such as monocytes, macrophages, and NK cells undergo long-term functional reprogramming following an initial inflammatory or metabolic stimulus. This reprogramming leads to enhanced and non-specific responses upon secondary stimulation. Unlike adaptive immune memory, trained immunity does not rely on antigen specificity or clonal expansion, but instead arises from epigenetic modifications, including histone alterations (e.g., H3K4me1 and H3K27ac) and metabolic rewiring that alter the transcriptional responsiveness of innate immune cells [325,327]. Notably, unlike transflammation, which only establishes a short-lived chromatin relaxation associated with acute inflammatory signaling, trained immunity provides a durable, epigenetically encoded long-lasting memory state in innate immune cells.

These reversible but persistent modifications prime innate immune cells for heightened responsiveness to subsequent exposures, even when the secondary stimulus differs from the initial trigger. This reveals that innate immunity is far more plastic and adaptable than previously recognized, offering new opportunities for therapeutic manipulation in infection control, vaccine design, cancer immunotherapy, and tissue repair. For instance, trained immunity can enhance resistance to reinfection by diverse pathogens, confer broad cross-protection with vaccines such as Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) and measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), and trigger antimicrobial defense via β-glucan and other microbial ligands. As summarized in Fig. 7a, trained immunity is established through coordinated metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming that increases cellular responsiveness and can produce either beneficial or detrimental outcomes depending on the biological context [328]. However, trained immunity also represents a double-edged sword. For instance, Fig. 7b illustrates how NLRP3-driven trained immunity in microglia can promote chronic neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration following cortical microinfarcts, highlighting the pathological consequences of persistent innate immune memory [332]. More broadly, dysregulated or prolonged immune training can exacerbate chronic inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, and Alzheimer's disease. Beyond infectious and inflammatory diseases, trained immunity is emerging as a designable biological phenomenon in biomaterials research. Recent studies show that certain nanoparticles can function as immune-training inducers. For example, iron oxide NPs have been shown to induce M1 macrophage polarization in tumor microenvironments, thereby inhibiting tumor growth by activating local immune responses [352]. As a result, metallic NPs have been proposed as potential inducers of trained immunity in breast cancer by reprogramming innate immune memory through coordinated epigenetic and metabolic remodeling [353]. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that biomaterials can be engineered to modulate trained immunity to improve regenerative outcomes. For example, a ROS-responsive nanofiber scaffold functionalized with immunoengineered exosomes was shown to reprogram maladaptive trained immunity by restoring balanced macrophage activation and promoting regulatory T cell responses, thereby coupling angiogenesis and osteogenesis for effective bone defect repair [354].

In the context of implanted biomaterials, the training effect can be conceptualized as a sequential cascade: the implantation first triggers inflammatory signaling, which subsequently initiates chromatin remodeling characterized by the enrichment of histone modifications such as H3K4me1 and H3K27ac. These epigenetic changes establish trained immunity, enabling innate immune cells to exhibit an enhanced, accelerated reparative response upon subsequent stimuli. This process represents a naturally occurring model of integrated immune–epigenetic regulation, which may be harnessed or mimicked through rational biomaterial design. Trained immunity thus provides a powerful conceptual and mechanistic bridge connecting immune responses with epigenetic regulation. Its incorporation into biomaterial strategies may yield next-generation regenerative implants capable of long-term, context-adaptive biological control.

Biomaterials as integrators of epigenetic and immune regulation

Dual modulation by biomaterial physicochemical properties

As discussed earlier, inflammation can alter gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms, while epigenetic programming shapes the magnitude, duration, and memory of inflammatory responses. This bidirectional relationship suggests that simultaneous modulation of both systems via a single biomaterial platform represents a potent strategy to improve outcomes in chronic wound repair, immune-related diseases, and tissue regeneration. Rather than treating immunomodulation and epigenetic regulation as independent or sequential processes, biomaterials can be designed to coordinate these pathways, thereby enabling more precise control over the tissue microenvironment. For instance, Xia et al. [355] showed that mesoporous silica extract reduced RFX-1 in CD4+ T cells, which may lead to IL-17A overexpression through increased histone H3 acetylation and decreased DNA methylation and H3K9 trimethylation, and in vivo this material enhanced vascularized bone regeneration with increased IL-17A and Th17 cells, demonstrating how biomaterial properties can simultaneously influence epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation in a coordinated regenerative process. Additionally, Kurian et al. [356] reported a sprayable nanozyme hydrogel that simultaneously modulates epigenetic regulation and immune responses in diabetic wound healing (Fig. 8). The hydrogel induced epigenetic modifications in macrophages by remodeling chromatin accessibility, which shifted gene expression from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory profile. In rat diabetic wound models, this coordinated action of epigenetic reprogramming and macrophage immunomodulation actively reshaped the wound microenvironment to accelerate tissue regeneration, highlighting that neither epigenetic nor immune regulation alone is sufficient, but their convergence is the key driver of effective wound healing. A further example was recently reported for peripheral nerve regeneration. Xu et al. [193] developed biomimetic Prussian White nanoparticles (PW) that exhibited prolonged local retention following sciatic nerve injury and reprogrammed macrophages through a coordinated immunometabolic–epigenetic mechanism (Fig. 9). The PW nanoparticle induced epigenetic activation of an S100a4+ macrophage substate through α-ketoglutarate accumulation and H3K9me3 demethylation, and these reprogrammed macrophages secreted itaconate to support Schwann cell proliferation. In rodent and canine nerve injury models, this synergistic action of epigenetic reprogramming and immunomodulation markedly enhanced functional and structural recovery, demonstrating that the convergence of these two regulatory processes is essential for successful nerve regeneration. Taken together, these examples, spanning bone, skin, and nerve tissues, reveal a commonly significant strategy that biomaterials orchestrate tissue regeneration through the synergistic modulation of epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation. In each case, the biomaterial platform does not simply deliver a therapeutic agent or passively support cell growth, rather, it actively reprograms the immune cell phenotype via epigenetic mechanisms, such as histone acetylation and DNA demethylation in T cells, chromatin accessibility remodeling in macrophages, and H3K9me3 demethylation in a specific macrophage subset. The specific remodeling thereby dictates the immune cell's functional output, shifting the local microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory state toward a pro-regenerative state, finally facilitating the proliferation and function of parenchymal cells such as osteoblasts, keratinocytes, and Schwann cells. Although these representative studies employ distinct material systems and target different tissues, they share a common design principle: biomaterials serve not merely as passive scaffolds or delivery vehicles but as active regulators that integrate immunomodulation with epigenetic reprogramming. Importantly, the regenerative outcomes observed in these studies arise not from independent regulation of either pathway, but from their coordinated interaction, in which epigenetic remodeling determines immune cell identity and function, while immune responses further reinforce epigenetic adaptation within the regenerative microenvironment. This emerging paradigm highlights that the therapeutic efficacy of next-generation biomaterials increasingly depends on their ability to simultaneously manipulate both regulatory networks in a temporally and spatially coordinated manner.

Building upon this concept, biomaterial physicochemical properties emerge as powerful shared upstream regulators capable of simultaneously shaping immune responses and epigenetic states through parallel, sequential, or reciprocal mechanisms (Fig. 10). Specifically, properties such as stiffness, wettability, degradability, and topography can activate a spectrum of mechanotransductive pathways that regulate both immune cell behavior and nuclear gene expression programs. For example, these cues influence macrophage adhesion, activation, cytokine secretion, and polarization states while simultaneously modulating chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation. Mechanistically, this dual regulation arises from shared common upstream mechanisms that link extracellular cues to intracellular responses. Mechanosensors such as integrins, TRPV4 channels, Piezo channels, and cytoskeletal tension regulators transmit external biomaterial cues directly into the nuclear membrane and chromatin [317,348]. These signals can directly alter histone modifications, DNA methylation dynamics, ncRNA profiles, and transcription factor accessibility, while also shaping innate immune activation, inflammatory resolution, and FBRs. This convergence provides a mechanistic explanation for why a single material parameter can produce coordinated epigenetic and immunological effects.Fig. 10Biomaterials as integrators of epigenetic and immune regulation via physicochemical cues. Often times, however, observing immune and epigenetic changes in the same biomaterial system does not establish whether they arise independently, through a shared upstream pathway, or sequentially through immune-epigenetic crosstalk. Decoupling the immune and epigenetic contributions of biomaterial properties requires complementary material- and biology-centered approaches. At the material level, orthogonal platforms should be used to vary one parameter while maintaining composition, ligand density, porosity, degradation, and other covarying features as constant as possible. Factorial designs and matched controls for protein adsorption, released products, and soluble cargos can further identify interactions among properties. For example, hydrogel network dynamics have been independently varied while maintaining nearly identical static stiffness, illustrating how individual mechanical variables can be isolated experimentally [357]. At the biological level, time-resolved analyses can determine whether chromatin remodeling precedes immune-state changes or occurs downstream of them. Monoculture, conditioned-medium, transwell, and direct co-culture experiments can distinguish cell-autonomous material effects from immune-mediated paracrine signaling. Selective inhibition or genetic disruption of mechanosensors, inflammatory pathways, cytokines, or epigenetic enzymes can then test pathway necessity. For example, FAK inhibition in mechanically tunable hydrogels has been used to examine how a shared mechanotransductive pathway coordinates stromal and immune responses, while macrophage-ablation models have demonstrated when implant-surface effects on downstream cell recruitment and adaptive immunity are macrophage dependent [358,359].

Design strategies for dual modulatory biomaterials

To translate the above-mentioned mechanistic insights into practical applications, biomaterial design must move beyond single-parameter optimization toward coordinated, multi-dimensional control of immune and epigenetic responses. This convergence presents both an opportunity and a challenge: material-derived cues should be engineered to guide immune activation and resolution while simultaneously shaping chromatin states and transcriptional programs in immune and tissue-resident cells. Accordingly, dual modulatory design frameworks should treat physicochemical features and delivered factors as an integrated toolkit that collectively regulates protein interphases, cell signaling, metabolism, and chromatin remodeling. Below, we outline several design strategies for dual epigenetic-immune modulatory biomaterials.

One key approach is the co-delivery of epigenetic and immune modulators, including cytokines, metabolites, nucleic acids, and other small molecules. For example, materials can be loaded with epigenetic regulators (e.g., HDAC inhibitors such as TSA or VPA) together with immunomodulatory factors (e.g., IL-10 or other pro-resolving cues) to influence chromatin states while simultaneously modulating immune cell behavior. As illustrated in Fig. 11, such strategies can be implemented through a wide range of biomaterial delivery platforms, such as liposomes, NPs, hydrogels, and microspheres, which enable precise control over dosage, localization, and release kinetics. Notably, growing evidence also indicates that certain endogenous metabolites and small molecules possess intrinsic dual epigenetic and immunomodulatory functions, creating an opportunity for “single-cue” dual modulation. For example, folic acid regulates DNA methylation through one-carbon metabolism while also influencing immune cell proliferation and function [81,362]; citrate contributes to acetyl-CoA availability that supports histone acetylation and can modulate inflammatory macrophage activation [83]; α-ketoglutarate and succinate regulate dioxygenase-dependent chromatin modifications (e.g., TET and JmjC enzymes) while influencing macrophage polarization and inflammatory signaling [340,341]; and lactate can promote histone lactylation while reshaping immune responses [343,363]. Importantly, the epigenetic and immunological effects of these molecules have often been investigated in parallel rather than in concert, leaving a gap in understanding when and how these dual functions converge or diverge in vivo. Biomaterial platforms are well-suited to close this gap by controlling dose, localization, and timing, enabling coordinated immune–epigenetic reprogramming through a minimal and potentially more design-efficient set of molecular inputs.Fig. 11Biomaterials as platforms for co-delivery and single-cue strategies for dual epigenetic-immunomodulation. A second strategy is the implementation of spatiotemporal regulation of material properties, enabling biomaterials to guide immune trajectories and epigenetic reprogramming across different stages of healing. Because both immune responses and epigenetic programs evolve during tissue repair, effective dual-modulatory biomaterials should coordinate these processes over time rather than continuously imposing a single biological state. During the early response to injury, biomaterials may need to permit controlled innate immune activation, debris clearance, and recruitment of reparative cells. As regeneration progresses, material-derived signals should promote inflammatory resolution while supporting epigenetic programs associated with cell proliferation, angiogenesis, lineage commitment, and matrix formation. During subsequent remodeling, these signals should diminish or further adapt to restore immune homeostasis, stabilize tissue-specific gene expression patterns, and avoid persistent inflammatory, senescent, or profibrotic states. Such temporal coordination may be achieved through sequential cargo delivery, staged ligand presentation, controlled degradation, time-dependent changes in material mechanics, or systems responsive to local ROS, pH, proteases, and cytokines. Because the sequence and duration of repair differ among tissues, the objective is not to impose a universal wound-healing timeline, but to match biomaterial behavior to the evolving immune and epigenetic requirements of each regenerative context.

Moreover, advances in biomaterial-enabled cell programming provide opportunities to engineer immune cells with predefined functional and epigenetic states. For example, incorporating trained-immunity inducers such as β-glucan coatings or PAMP-mimetic architectures may imprint innate immune cells with memory-like responsiveness through epigenetic and metabolic remodeling. In regenerative settings, this concept could be harnessed to promote faster or more robust repair-associated programs upon secondary challenge. However, given the double-edged nature of trained immunity, such approaches will require careful tuning to avoid persistent inflammatory priming. More broadly, biomaterials that control local cytokine milieus, antigen presentation, or metabolic conditions can guide macrophage polarization and T cell differentiation while simultaneously establishing supportive chromatin programs that stabilize desired phenotypes.

Finally, to make dual modulation actionable and reproducible, dual modulatory biomaterials should be developed using paired immune and epigenetic design endpoints rather than evaluating these outcomes separately. In practice, this may involve screening materials using coupled readouts. For example, macrophage polarization together with chromatin accessibility or DNA methylation profiling in immune and stromal cells. However, given the complexity and interdependence of immune and epigenetic responses, such a material library and its readouts could be substantial. As a result, integrating computational and data-driven approaches such as machine learning, could accelerate the identification of design rules to achieve desired regenerative outcomes.

Current challenges and future perspectives in designing dual modulatory biomaterials

Herein, we have summarized and discussed how biomaterial properties can concurrently influence the epigenome and immune responses, providing a conceptual framework for designing dual modulatory biomaterials to shape regenerative outcomes. A central implication is that material physicochemical properties are not merely structural variables, but upstream regulators that can jointly tune epigenetic states and immune behaviors. Parameters such as stiffness/rigidity, wettability, topography, and degradation profiles can converge on shared signaling and metabolic pathways that reach the nucleus and remodel chromatin while also steering innate and adaptive immune systems (Table 3 and Fig. 10). This dual regulatory capacity lays the foundation for a new generation of biomaterials that do more than deliver cues, they actively program the regenerative microenvironment to support constructive remodeling, integration, and functional restoration. However, several major challenges must be addressed before dual modulatory biomaterials can be broadly translated into practical regenerative strategies.

The relationships among different material properties, epigenetic states, and immune responses are inherently complex, and further studies are needed to define the governing principles and interdependencies among these systems. For instance, although wettability has been associated with both changes in cellular behavior and immune activation, direct evidence systemically linking wettability to specific epigenetic modifications remains limited. Similarly, the mechanisms by which nanomaterials alter cytosine DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin organization remain incompletely understood. Accumulating evidence suggests that multiple overlapping pathways, including mechanotransduction, metabolic perturbation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, may simultaneously contribute to these epigenetic changes, underscoring the intricate and context-dependent nature of biomaterial-epigenome interactions. Notably, oxidative stress and inflammation are recurring responses following NP exposure [344], and both have been shown to disrupt chromatin integrity and alter DNA methylation as well as histone modifications [345,346]. Therefore, distinguishing causal mechanisms from secondary stress responses remains a critical challenge for translating nanomaterial-associated epigenetic effects into rational regenerative biomaterial design.

Moreover, epigenetic and immunomodulation rarely arise from a single material parameter acting in isolation. Instead, these responses often result from the interplay of multiple, interdependent material properties, making it difficult to attribute specific epigenetic effects or immunological activities to a single property. For example, although we classified degradability as a biophysical property and correlated it with the changes of epigenetic status and immune responses, the degradation rate of a biomaterial is itself governed by a combination of factors, including morphology (e.g., porosity and micro- or nanoscale architecture), wettability, material origin, and surface chemistry. These determinants span both biophysical and biochemical domains, illustrating that even seemingly well-defined properties represent emergent behaviors of interconnected material features. Failure to consider these interdependencies in an integrated manner may therefore lead to inconsistent or contradictory experimental outcomes.

Additionally, several translational challenges remain. Epigenetic drugs often lack cell-type specificity and may induce unintended chromatin changes in non-target cells, raising concerns about off-target gene regulation and long-term safety. Immunomodulation also demands a precise spatiotemporal balance, as insufficient activation may impair host defense and remodeling, whereas excessive activation can drive fibrosis, chronic inflammation, or pathological immune responses. Moreover, the field still lacks clear roadmaps linking specific material inputs to defined immune trajectories, measurable epigenetic remodeling, and durable regenerative outcomes. This gap is further complicated by the dynamic nature of healing, in which desirable immune and epigenetic states may differ across the inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases (Fig. 4b). Patient-to-patient variability, tissue-specific microenvironments, disease status, aging, and differences in implantation sites also challenge the development of universal biomaterial design principles. Finally, translating dual modulatory biomaterials into clinical use will require improved standardization of immune and epigenetic readouts, scalable manufacturing, and long-term safety evaluation.

Overcoming these challenges will require close interdisciplinary collaboration, integrated evaluation paradigms, and technologies capable of resolving immune-epigenetic interactions across space and time. Building on the design strategies outlined in Section 4.5.2, including integrated dual modulation strategies, spatiotemporal programming of material cues, and the embrace of emerging concepts such as epi-drugs and trained immunity in material design, future efforts should be made to create standardized dual modulation readouts that evaluate immune activity and epigenetic state within the same experimental framework, which may be achieved by discovering new biomarkers or developing new biodevices. Beyond biological design, however, clinical translation will require reproducible manufacturing and rigorous regulatory evaluation. Critical considerations include control of material composition, cargo loading, release kinetics, mechanical properties, and degradation behavior, as well as raw-material quality, batch-to-batch consistency, scalable fabrication, sterilization compatibility, storage stability, and validated potency assays that assess both immune and epigenetic activity. Preclinical studies should further characterize biodistribution, degradation products, immunotoxicity, genotoxicity, and the persistence and reversibility of induced epigenetic states. For platforms that combine material, drug, or biologically active components, the function and safety of each component, as well as those of the integrated product, will need to be established.

Single-cell epigenomics (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics (e.g., spatial RNA-seq), and novel in vivo imaging methods such as photoacoustic imaging, intravital microscopy, and multiphoton imaging, can reveal how immune-epigenetic states emerge, interact, and resolve around implanted materials. These approaches will also enable causal investigation of reciprocal immune-epigenetic regulation within the native tissue microenvironment [364]. Integrating such multidimensional datasets with artificial intelligence and machine-learning-guided biomaterial design may reveal predictive relationships among material properties, epigenetic remodeling, immune phenotypes, and regenerative outcomes, thereby supporting the development of programmable biomaterials that adapt to different stages of tissue repair [339]. In particular, feedback-responsive systems could sense local biological cues, such as cytokine levels, pH, ROS, hypoxia, or metabolic state, and dynamically adjust their epigenetic and immunological outputs, reducing the risk of over-suppressing host defense during early inflammation or sustaining excessive inflammation during later stages of tissue remodeling, while improving precision across heterogeneous microenvironments. Patient-derived models, multi-omics profiling, biomarker-guided stratification, and shared datasets may further enable cross-study validation and biomaterial designs tailored to individual and tissue-specific contexts. Together, these approaches may ultimately establish quantitative design principles for next-generation immune-epigenetic biomaterials toward precision regenerative medicine [365].

Conclusion

By examining the roles of biomaterials in epigenetic regulation and immunomodulation independently and convergently, this review highlights a central principle in regenerative biomaterials: successful tissue regeneration is not dictated solely by scaffold architecture or bioactive payloads, but by the coordinated control of epigenetic and immune processes. Framed in this way, biomaterials evolve from passive structural supports toward bio-instructive regenerative systems capable of actively orchestrating both cell fate and immune balance in a tissue- and stage-dependent manner. Importantly, this perspective underscores a paradigm shift from tuning isolated material properties toward engineering integrated regulatory systems that couple extracellular cues with intracellular gene control without altering the genes. Such an approach enables more precise modulation of inflammation, cellular plasticity, and long-term tissue remodeling. Overall, the future of regenerative biomaterials lies in adaptive, intelligent, and integrated systems capable of sensing, responding, and guiding both immune and epigenetic processes.
