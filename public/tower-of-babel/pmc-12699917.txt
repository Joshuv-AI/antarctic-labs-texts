In vitro anti-diabetic activity and molecular docking studies of Amaranthus cruentus, Amaranthus hybridus and isolated compounds
Authors: Nolitha Nkobole, Oluwakemi Ebenezer, Gerhard Prinsloo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmaranthus species are traditional leafy vegetable also reputed for their potential in managing metabolic disorders, including diabetes. In previous investigations conducted by our group, extracts from Amaranthus cruentus and Amaranthus hybridus, along with isolated compounds, exhibited strong α-glucosidase enzyme and moderate α-amylase inhibition in vitro.MethodsThe present study evaluated the antidiabetic effects of extracts and isolated compounds on C3A hepatocytes and L6 myoblasts. In addition, to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms behind the observed enzymatic inhibition, we conducted docking studies of the isolated compounds against α-glucosidase and α-amylase. The finely powdered leaf samples were mixed with methanol and extracted using a cold maceration process. Three compounds were obtained from the aerial sections of the plant material using silica gel chromatography. Glucose metabolism of the extracts and the isolated compounds was carried out using L6 myoblasts and C3A cells.ResultsThe plant extracts of A. cruentus and A. hybridus and isolated compounds; palmitic acid and α-spinasterol, were found to stimulate glucose uptake in L6 myoblasts and C3A cells at 31.25 µg/mL, A. cruentus and A. hybridus stimulated glucose uptake by 15.67% and 15.95%, respectively, while insulin (10 µg/mL) boosted glucose uptake by 27.1% in L6 myoblasts, although their mechanisms of action remained unclear. Palmitic acid and α-spinasterol enhanced glucose uptake in L6 myoblasts by 28.88% and 21.61%, respectively. After 48 h of pre-treatment, only α-spinasterol showed a significant increase in glucose absorption (56.82%) in L6 myoblasts at a treatment dose of 62.5 µg/mL, while insulin showed a glucose uptake of 78.02%. A. hybridus demonstrated significant glucose uptake (20.86%) in C3A hepatocytes after 24 h of pre-treatment at a dosage of 15.63 µg/mL, in contrast to insulin, which enhanced glucose uptake by 34.8% at 10 µg/mL. Compared to acarbose with a binding affinity of -7.7 Kcal/mol, palmitic acid, α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester showed the binding affinities of -5.7 Kcal/mol, -7.6 Kcal/mol and − 7.8 Kcal/mol respectively, on α-glucosidase. Acarbose showed a comparable binding affinity of -6 Kcal/mol to those of pheophorbide A-methyl ester and α-spinasterol, which showed the binding scores of -6.7 Kcal/mol and − 6.8 Kcal/mol, respectively.ConclusionThis study validates A. cruentus and A. hybridus’s traditional use for the treatment of diabetes mellitus. Future studies will investigate the underlying mechanisms by which the extracts and compounds enhance glucose uptake in L6 myoblasts and C3A hepatocytes. Additionally, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the antidiabetic potential of the Amaranthus extracts and compounds, in vivo experiments using appropriate animal models will be conducted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05169-2.Keywords: Amaranthus species, Glucose uptake, Glucose utilisation, L6 myoblasts, C3A hepatocytes

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major public health problem in both industrialised and developing countries, with its prevalence expected to increase to 643 million by 2030 [1]. It is a class of metabolic disorders distinguished by hyperglycaemia. Hyperglycaemia causes long-term damage, dysfunction and failure of multiple organs, including the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and blood vessels [1]. The available antidiabetic drugs have different mechanism of actions. As an example, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4I) drugs treat diabetes by exhibiting glucose-dependent insulin stimulation [2], whereas sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2I) inhibit the absorption of sodium and glucose in the proximal renal tubules [3]. Sulfonylureas and biguanides increase the removal of glucose from the blood [4] and by increasing insulin release from the pancreas by binding to 140 kDa high-affinity sulfonylurea receptor [5]. The mechanism of action for another set of available antidiabetic drugs, thiazolidinediones, is to decrease insulin resistance in muscle and adipose tissue [6]. Finally, α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibitors inhibit α-glucosidase and α-amylase enzymes that are responsible for increasing the blood glucose levels [7, 8].

A. cruentus L and A. hybridus L, popularly referred to as pigweed, are extensively consumed in South America, Asia and Africa. The leaves of A. hybridus L are used in folk medicine for the treatment of various ailments. In Cameroon, the leaves of A. hybridus are used to enhance fertility [9]. The leaves of A. hybridus are used for the treatment of dysentery, diarrhoea, ulcers and intestinal haemorrhage due to its astringent quality in traditional medicine [10]. A. cruentus has been reported to treat diabetes in many parts of Zambia [11]. Consumption of A. cruentus products is indicated for people with celiac disease, and hence also for diabetics [12]. Amaranthus contains a variety of alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, phenolic acids, steroids, amino acids, terpenoids, lipids, saponin, betalain, b-sitosterol, stigmasterol, linoleic acid, rutin, catechuic tannins and carotenoids [13].

This study extends the antidiabetic activity of these species of our previous study that showed that extracts and isolated compounds from A. cruentus and A. hybridus showed potent α-glucosidase inhibition and mild α-amylase inhibition [12]. The study by Nkobole et al. [12] did not do the in silico analysis to establish the mode of action of how the isolated compounds interacted with α-glucosidase α-amylase enzymes, hence the motivation to carry out the present research. The leaves of A. cruentus are simple in shape from oval to rhombic-ovate. Numerous green unisexual flowers are arranged in finger-like spikes, with a tall and dense terminal panicle and axillary spikes below [13]. The inflorescence, which has a wide colour range, is about 50 cm long and yields 50 000 round or lenticular seeds that are 1 to 1.5 mm in diameter, glossy and dark brown [14]. A. hybridus is an erect annual herbaceous flowering plant from the Amaranthaceae family [15]. The stems are thick and grooved, while the leaves are simple and alternating, with narrow tips [16]. The crop is characterised by the inflorescence that is thick, measuring 50 to 150 mm in length [17].

Computational studies, such as molecular docking, play a crucial role in elucidating the potential binding interactions between bioactive compounds and important therapeutic targets [18]. Furthermore, molecular docking studies predict the interactions between small molecules (ligands) and biological macromolecules such as proteins, DNA, or RNA, thereby supporting the identification of potential drug candidates or hits [19]. Since drug discovery is both time consuming and a very expensive exercise, molecular docking offers a realistic and cost-effective alternative since it reduces the number of compounds requiring experimental validation [20].

Materials and methods

Reagents

All reagents and chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless stated otherwise. HepG2 (C3A) hepatocytes (catalogue number: HB-8065) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and L6 myocytes (catalogue number: JCRB9081) were purchased from the Japanese Cancer Research Resources Bank (JCRB) in Japan. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with low glucose (catalogue number; SH30021.01), Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) (catalogue number: SH30024.FS), RPMI-1640 medium (catalogue number: SH30096.02), Foetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (catalogue number: SH30118.04) and PBS with and without Ca2+ and Mg2+ (catalogue number: SH30256.01) were purchased from Cytiva (Marlborough, MA, USA).

Collection of plant material

A publication by Nkobole et al.,(2021) [12] details the collection of A. cruentus and A. hybridus. The seeds of the cultivated samples of A. cruentus and A. hybridus were donated by the gene bank of the Agricultural Research Council-Vegetables Industrial and Medicinal Plants (ARC-VIMP) where they are maintained to ensure that the material used in the study was “true-to-type”. The leaves of the wild A. hybridus were purchased from the Esikhawini informal market in April 2018. Esikhawini (28.8677° S, 31.9160° E) is a peri-urban settlement in the district of Richards Bay in KwaZulu-Natal. The leaves of A. cruentus were purchased from Stanger (29.3388° S, 31.2913° E), which is in KwaDukuza. Wild Amaranth plants collected from KwaZulu-Natal were confirmed by a botanist at the University of Zululand, Prof. Alfred Zobolo, and compared to the voucher specimens that are deposited at HGWJ Schweickerdt Herbarium (voucher numbers: PRU0012985 for A. cruentus and PRU126045 for A. hybridus). In addition, the identification was also supported by the reported dominance of A. hybridus in Esikhawini, Zululand and A. cruentus in the Stanger area.

Sample preparation

Sample preparation and isolation of compounds are described in a publication by Nkobole et al. [12]. Briefly, one kilogram of the aerial parts of the plant material were mixed and extracted using an 80:20 methanol-water combination (5 L) at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h. The methanolic water extract was filtered and evaporated under reduced pressure at 40 °C, yielding 63.9 g (6.39%). The whole extract (63.9 g) was placed to a silica gel column (10 × 20 cm) and eluted using a gradient of hexane (Hex) and ethyl acetate (EtOAc) with increasing polarity: 36 fractions (500 mL each) were collected and merged based on their TLC characteristics, yielding 13 fractions. The first of 13 combined fractions yielded palmitic acid, α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester, a chlorophyll derivative. The structures of the isolated compounds are included as supplementary material (Figure S1). Due to the insufficient quantity of pheophorbide A-methyl ester, the compound was not tested for the glucose uptake experiments; however, its mechanism of action on α-glucosidase and α-amylase proteins was reported in this paper.

Molecular docking

The docking simulations between the isolated compounds namely, α-spinasterol, palmitic acid and pheophorbide A-methyl ester from wild A. cruentus, and α-glucosidase and α-amylase were performed using Auto-dock Vina 1.0.3 software. The crystal structures of the targeted protein α-glucosidase (PDB: 5NN8) and α-amylase (PDB: 2QV4) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (10.1038/s41467-017-01263-3, 10.1021/bi701652t). PyRx software with Autodock Vina scoring function was used to estimate the binding affinities between the ligands and the proteins (10.1007/978-1-4939-2269-7_19). The dimensions of the grid box were fitted to enclose the binding site pocket of α-glucosidase. The spatial centres for α-glucosidase were X = − 12.41, Y = − 32.43 and Z = 95.39; and the size was 28 × 15 × 26 Å. Meanwhile, the dimensions of grid box for α-amylase for the spatial centres were X = 12.00, Y = 47.79 and Z = 21.78; and the size was 19 × 30 × 27 Å. The binding sites of the ligands were visualised and interpreted using PyMOL and Protein-Ligand interaction Profiler (PLIP) (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.0 Schrödinger, LLC, plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index).

Cell line maintenance

L6 rat myoblast cells were acquired from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (JCRB Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan). As described by Sagbo et al. [10. Sagbo et al., 2018], cells were routinely maintained in 10 cm culture dishes with complete media (low glucose DMEM, 10% FBS and 1% Pen-Strep) and kept at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Human hepatoma-derived C3A hepatocytes were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). Cells were grown in 10 cm culture dishes in full media (MEM with 1% NEAA, 10% FBS and 1% Pen-Strep) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

The glucose utilisation and uptake in C3A cells and L6 myoblasts

The glucose utilisation and uptake are two different experiments. Glucose utilisation measures the amount of glucose that the cells used over the entire incubation period (24–48 h). Glucose uptake measures the amount of glucose that the cells use over a four-hour incubation period after the initial 24- or 48-hour incubation (the cells are given a specific concentration of glucose and incubated with that for an additional four hours). The glucose utilisation in C3A cells and L6 myoblasts was assessed using the method published by Van de Venter et al. in 2008 [21] and modified by Sagbo et al. (2018) [22] and is summarised below.

Glucose uptake

Cells were seeded in 96 well plates (2 × 104 cells per well, 100 µL aliquots) and allowed to attach overnight. Different concentrations of the extract (15.63, 31.25, 62.5 and 125 µg/mL) and compounds (7.81, 15.63, 31.25 and 62.5 µg/mL) were prepared in complete media, added to cells, and incubated for 24 h. Following 24 h of incubation, the treatment was withdrawn, and the cells were transplanted to new 96-well plates. After aspirating the residual medium, cells were rinsed with 100 µL PBS before adding 25 µL of incubation buffer (RPMI-1640 diluted with PBS containing 0.1% BSA to a final glucose content of 8 mM) and incubating for four hours. Plates were treated with 200 µL of glucose oxidase reagent and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a Multiscan MS microtitre plate reader (VarioSkan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vantaa, Finland). Cell-free wells containing incubation buffer and full culture media served as glucose standards. Glucose absorption was calculated as a function of the remaining glucose concentration (mM) and expressed as the difference between the standard and test samples’ means.

Glucose utilisation

The preparation of the cells and the concentrations of extracts and compounds are described above (2.6.1). After 24 h of incubation, cells were transferred to new 96-well plates and 200 µL glucose oxidase (prepared from a mixture of 1 mU/mL Aspergillus niger glucose oxidase with 0.4 mM 4-aminoantipyrine, 2.5 U/mL horseradish peroxidase, 3 mM phenol, 0.25 mM EDTA, and 0.5 M PBS (pH 7.0)) was added. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 15 min before reading at 510 nm with a Multiscan MS microtitre plate reader (VarioSkan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vantaa, Finland). The glucose standards in the wells included culture media and incubation buffer, but no cells. The mean difference (in mM) of the concentrations of glucose left after the reaction, between glucose standards and the samples, was used to determine glucose consumption.

Cytotoxicity

The cytotoxicity effect of the extracts and isolated compounds on HepG2 cells and L6 myoblasts was carried out using the MTT assay. The extracts were tested at 15.63–125 µg/mL. The compounds were tested from 7.8 to 62.5 µg/mL. To determine cell viability, the extracts or compounds were incubated with the cells for one hour at 37 °C. The cytotoxicity of the plant extract and compounds was expressed as percentage of the control (medium only), which was taken as zero, and the percentages were calculated using the formula below:

If cell viability is between 80% and 95%, the extract or compound being tested is considered non-toxic.

Statistical analysis

For the cell culture experiments, statistical analysis was carried out with a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the difference between samples was determined by Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test using Stata (version 17). The data is expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and values are considered significant at p < 0.05. All the analyses were carried out in triplicate and the results are expressed in mean ± SD. All of the data analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel 2010. Moreover, IC50 for the extract was calculated by plotting extract concentration against % inhibition.

Results

Molecular Docking

The native compound was first docked into the respective proteins to give pictures of the interacting residues in the binding pockets of α-glucosidase and α-amylase in the binding pockets 5NN8 and 2QV4 (Fig. 1A A). The results of acarbose ligand- α-glucosidase protein depicted that most of the interactions were through H-bonding. Most of the hydroxyl functional groups on the acarbose (Binding energy = − 7.7 Kcal/mol) were H-bonded with the residues such as Asp282 (carboxylic oxygen, bond distance (b.d) = 1.92 Å)), Trp418 (amino group, b.d = 2.84 Å), Asn524 (carbonyl oxygen, b.d = 2.31 Å), Phe525 (carboxylic oxygen, b.d = 2.24 Å), Arg600 (amino group, b.d = 2.30 Å) and Asp616 (carboxylic oxygen, b.d = 2.36 Å). The hydrogen bonds were accompanied by two hydrophobic interactions; that is, the methyl group on the acarbose hydrophobically interacted with the phenyl group of the amino acid Phe649 (b.d = 5.33 Å, respectively).

Docked poses of (A) acarbose, B palmitic acid, C pheophorbide A-methyl ester and (D) α-spinasterol (stick figure) with human lysosomal acid α-glucosidase (GAA) residues (rainbow colour; PDB ID: 5NN8). PyMOL, Protein Ligand Interaction Profiler was used for the visualisation and detailed analysis

The docked analysis of palmitic acid (B.E = − 5.7 Kcal/mol) in the active site of α-glucosidase (Fig. 1B) shows that four carbon atoms on the palmitic acid form hydrophobic interactions with the indole ring of Trp376 and Trp481 (b.d = 5.49 Å, 5.13 Å, 5.20 Å and 5.16 Å), phenyl group of Phe 649 (b.d = 5.50 Å), non-polar side chain of Leu678 (b.d = 4.79 Å). These were accompanied by four hydrogen bonds formed between the carbon atoms of palmitic acid and carbonyl oxygen atom of the carboxylic acid of Arg600, Trp613, and imidazole ring of His674, with bond distance of 2.63 Å, 3.28 Å, and 2.97 Å, respectively.

The docked complex of pheophorbide A-methyl ester (B.E = − 7.8 Kcal/mol) also formed hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions in the binding pocket of α-glucosidase. The methyl group on the tetrahydrocyclopenta[b]pyrrole-5-carboxylate ring of pheophorbide A-methyl ester displayed hydrophobic interactions with the indole ring of Trp376 with a bond distance of 5.03 Å and Leu678 (b.d = 3.94 Å) (Fig. 1C). The pheophorbide A-methyl ester docked complex was further stabilised by stacking and cation interactions. The methyl-3-vinyl-2,5-dihydro-1 H-pyrrole ring in the pheophorbide A-methyl ester moiety formed interactions with the phenyl group of residues Phe525, and indole ring of Trp481. Meanwhile, the oxygen atom of carbonyl on the methylenepyrrolidine propanoate was H-bonded with Arg600 (b.d = 2.89 Å). In contrast, the dihydro-1 H-pyrrole ring in the pheophorbide A-methyl ester moiety was electrostatically bonded with the amino functional group of Arg411.

The complex of α-spinasterol (B.E = − 7.6 Kcal/mol) was stabilised by hydrophobic interactions with residues Trp376, Trp481, Phe649, and Leu678, with bond distance between 4.45 Å and 5.50 Å (Fig. 1D). In comparison with acarbose, palmitic acid and pheophorbide A-methyl ester which their ligand-protein landscape was similar to α-spinasterol, lack of hydrogen and electrostatic interactions in the complex of α-spinasterol were depicted.

Further, the docking results of acarbose (Fig. 2A) in the α-amylase binding site were analysed and detailed. The analysis of acarbose showed that the two-hydroxyl functional group attached to aminocyclohex-4-ene-1,2,3-triol ring displayed formation of hydrogen bonding with the amino group (NH2) of Ala106 (b.d = 3.17 Å), as well as the two amino functional groups on Asn105 (b.d = 3.15 Å). Meanwhile, the third hydroxyl group and the nitrogen atom on aminocyclohex-4-ene-1,2,3-triol ring were H-bonded with the methylene and the hydroxyl oxygen atom of on Thr163 (b.d = 2.81/2.89 Å) amino acid. The complex was further stabilised by H-bonding between the two-hydroxyl methyl on the tetrahydro-2 H-pyran-2,3,4-triol ring and the carboxylic oxygen atoms of Asp197 (b.d = 3.13 Å).

Docked poses of (A) acarbose (B) palmitic acid, C pheophorbide A-methyl ester and (D) α-spinasterol (stick figure) with α-amylase residues (rainbow colour; PDB ID: 2QV4). Protein Ligand Interaction Profiler was used for the visualisation and Discovery Studio Visualiser was used for detailed analysis

The results of the docked analysis of palmitic acid in the active site of α-amylase protein displayed different interactions involving six residues, namely, Trp59, Leu165, Ala198 and Gln63 (Fig. 2B). For example, Trp59, Leu165 and Ala198 formed a hydrophobic interaction with the carbon atoms 6, 10, 13 and 16 of the palmitic acid, while Trp59 (b.d = 2.48 Å) and Gln63 (b.d = 2.47 Å) showed hydrogen bonds with the hydrogen atom of the carboxylic group.

The docked complexes of α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester and palmitic acid – with binding energies of − 6.8 Kcal/mol and − 6.7 Kcal/mol respectively – demonstrated good fitting within the active site of α-amylase, outperforming acarbose (− 6.0 kcal/mol) in binding affinity. Moreover, pheophorbide A-methyl ester formed hydrophobic interactions. The methyl attached to the carboxyl group on the tetrahydrocyclopenta[b]pyrrole-5-carboxylate ring hydrophobically interacted with imidazole ring of His201, and the methine carbon atom of Ile235 amino acid. Besides, the methyl-3-vinyl-2,5-dihydro-1 H-pyrrole ring, methyl and the methylene carbon atom of the vinyl attached to ring formed hydrophobic interactions with the methine carbon atom of Leu165 (b.d = 4.34/5.06/5.18 Å) and the phenyl ring of Trp59 (b.d = 4.79 Å). In addition, a hydrogen bond was observed between methyl-3-vinyl-2,5-dihydro-1 H-pyrrole ring and Tyr151 with distance of 3.28 Å. Also, π-cation (His305, 4.32 Å), and salt bridges (Arg195 and His299, 4.43 Å and 4.01 Å) interactions with the pyrrole and the pyrrole-5-carboxylate ring (Fig. 2C).

At the same time, α-spinasterol displayed hydrophobic interactions with Ile51, Trp58, Trp59, Tyr62 and Val107, respectively (Fig. 2D). The presence of the long alkene chain enhanced the interaction of α-spinasterol in the binding pocket of the α-amylase protein. The carbon atoms interacted with Val107 (methine carbon atom, 4.94/5.38 Å), Trp59 (phenyl ring, 5.10 Å) and Ile51(methine carbon atom, 4.91 Å) via hydrophobic interactions. Molecular docking revealed favourable binding interactions between all tested compounds (palmitic acid, α-spinosterol, and pheophorbide A-methyl ester) and the active site of α-glucosidase, supporting the in vitro results.

Cell culture

Glucose uptake in L6 myoblasts

Insulin was chosen as a positive control due to its known effect on glucose uptake and metabolism in hepatic cells. The results revealed a small but statistically significant increase in glucose utilisation in L6 myoblasts after 24 h of treatment with all four samples at all the tested concentrations (Fig. 3). Specifically, following a 24-hour incubation period at 31.25, 62.5 and 125 µg/mL concentrations, A. cruentus significantly enhanced glucose utilisation by 2-, 1.84- and 2.7-fold compared to that of the control. Compared with the glucose utilisation in the control, A. hybridus increased glucose utilisation by 1.72-fold and 2.51-fold at 31.25 and 62.5 µg/mL, respectively. The compound, α-spinasterol, significantly increased glucose uptake by 3.73-fold and 1.88-fold at concentrations of 15.63 and 31.25 µg/mL, whereas palmitic acid at concentrations of 15.63 and 62.5 µg/mL (not stated in Fig. 3) enhanced glucose utilisation by 3.76-fold and 4.91-fold compared to that of the control (Fig. 3).

As shown in Fig. 4, similar results and trends were observed for glucose uptake after four hours following a 24-hour treatment period. At 31.25 µg/mL, A. cruentus and A. hybridus stimulated glucose uptake by 15.67% and 15.95% compared to an increased glucose uptake of 27.1% that was exhibited by insulin (10 µg/mL). The compound α-spinasterol showed significant cytotoxicity in C3A cells at 24 and 48 h of treatment, respectively, as indicated in Figs. 9 and 10 at 62.5 µg/mL.

Discussion

Notably, the docked complex of α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester displayed superior and similar binding energy of − 7.6 Kcal/mol and − 7.8 Kcal/mol that were comparable to the binding energy of acarbose (-7.7 Kcal/mol), respectively, against the α-glucosidase enzyme. In contracts, palmitic acid showed lower binding energy of − 5.7 Kcal/mol. The favourable interaction of α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester with the lysosomal acid α-glucosidase might be responsible for their potency. As mentioned earlier, an extensive hydrogen bond interaction network facilitates potent inhibition of the α-glucosidase enzyme. Despite the fact that palmitic acid exhibited greater potency in inhibiting α-glucosidase in vitro compared to α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester [12], the docking analysis revealed that palmitic acid possessed a lower binding energy (− 5.7 Kcal/mol) in contrast to the binding energies of − 7.6 Kcal/mol and − 7.8 Kcal/mol exhibited by α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester, respectively. Another study found that palmitic acid mostly binds to the human intestinal α-glucosidase through hydrophobic interactions [22]. We therefore purport that palmitic acid inhibits α-glucosidase through hydrophobic interactions. The docked complex of α-spinasterol, pheophorbide A-methyl ester and palmitic acid (binding energy of − 6.8 Kcal/mol and − 6.7 Kcal/mol and − 4.6 Kcal/mol, respectively) showed that the compounds fit well into the active site of α-amylase when compared to acarbose (-6 Kcal/mol). However, the compounds displayed poor inhibitory activity against α-amylase.

To integrate traditional medicine into public health efforts, it is critical to first identify toxicity [23]. In this study, A. hybridus did not show toxicity on C3A hepatocytes and L6 myoblasts, and A. cruentus was only toxic at the highest concentration tested (125 µg/mL) on C3A hepatocytes. As a result, the comparatively low degree of toxicity demonstrated by the extracts and compounds suggests that they may be possibly safe for consumers. Diabetes is a complex metabolic disorder [24] that requires multiple treatment approaches. The present study has thus employed a cell-based and biochemical approach to identify the potential mechanisms of the probable antidiabetic actions of A. cruentus, A. hybridus, and isolated compounds. The liver and skeletal muscle are two of the most important tissues for maintaining the body’s glucose homeostasis. Skeletal muscle is the principal peripheral tissue that regulates blood glucose levels, accounting for more than 70% of insulin-mediated glucose absorption [25]. Since muscle plays an important role in glucose utilisation in the body, increasing glucose absorption in muscle tissues is an effective glycaemic control technique [26]. The L6 cells employed in this work are an excellent model for glucose uptake since they have been extensively used to clarify the mechanisms of glucose uptake in muscle [27]. C3A cells are known to exhibit a variety of liver-specific characteristics which makes it the best model for this study [26]. In response to insulin stimulation, the liver participates in glucose uptake, production and processing. In addition, insulin stimulates glycogen production in the liver while inhibiting de novo glucose synthesis (gluconeogenesis) [28]. Improved muscle and liver glucose absorption are regarded important treatment objectives for diabetes management [29].

Although no research on glucose uptake or utilisation in A. cruentus or A. hybridus were found, rutin which was isolated from A. tristis caused significant glucose uptake with moderate toxicity in 3T3-L1 cell lines [30]. In addition to extracts that showed significant glucose uptake, α-spinasterol and a fatty acid, palmitic acid, which were isolated from A. cruentus showed potent glucose uptake in both the L6 myoblasts and the C3A hepatocytes. Molecular docking revealed favorable binding interactions of α-spinasterol which was comparable to acarbose, supporting its strong glucose uptake activity. Contrary to this, even though palmitic acid showed lower binding energy than the rest of the compounds, its potent glucose uptake demonstrates that compounds use multiple pathways to elicit biological activities. A study by Lee et al. (2022) [31] showed that α-spinasterol which was isolated from Aster pseudoglehnii enhanced glucose uptake with no toxic effects on C2C12 cells. In line with our study, Pu et al. (2011) [32] reported that palmitic acid acutely enhanced glucose absorption in skeletal muscle cells via activating Akt and ERK1/2. Furthermore, the same study reported that palmitic acid can serve two contradictory functions in skeletal muscle. On the one hand, chronic administration inhibits insulin-induced glucose uptake by inhibiting Akt phosphorylation, whereas short-term exposure to palmitic acid increases glucose absorption by activating Akt [32]. Results from previous studies on adipocytes found that short-term treatment with palmitic acid increased basal glucose absorption [33, 34]. These previously published studies supported the glucose uptake findings from the current study.

Conclusion

A. hybridus has been shown in this study to have no apparent toxicity to myoblasts or hepatocytes. This suggests that either extract can be used in herbal treatments with minimal cytotoxicity to the liver and muscle cells. At high concentrations, palmitic acid showed toxicity in L6 myoblasts. Moreover, when tested at the highest concentrations, A. cruentus and α-spinasterol showed toxicity in C3A myoblasts. This is the first work to document the glucose uptake of L6 myoblasts and C3A hepatocytes by A. cruentus and A. hybridus. Although pheophorbide A-methyl ester exhibited strong binding affinities comparable to acarbose on both α-glucosidase and α-amylase enzymes, a limitation of the study is the lack of evaluation of its effects on glucose uptake and utilisation due to the insufficient quantity of the compound available. This omission hinders a comprehensive assessment of its potential antidiabetic activity. The mechanism of action on how the extracts and compounds enhanced glucose uptake on L6 hepatocytes and C3A myoblasts needs to be investigated further. Furthermore, a molecular docking study identified α-spinasterol and pheophorbide A-methyl ester as having the most significant impact on α-glucosidase inhibition.
