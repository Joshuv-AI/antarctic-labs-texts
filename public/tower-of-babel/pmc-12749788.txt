Association between the serum albumin-to-creatinine ratio and prognosis and risk of severe disturbance of consciousness in intracerebral hemorrhage patients
Authors: Yuying Huang, Shan Jing, Yuyao Zhang, Jingge Li, Zixi Wang, Wang Zheng, Yupeng Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The relationship between serum albumin-to-creatinine ratio (sACR) and in-hospital mortality (IHM), long-term mortality (LTM), and severe disorder of consciousness (SDOC) in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) remains unclear. This study, based on the MIMIC-IV database, aimed to investigate the association between sACR and the risks of IHM, LTM, and SDOC. A total of 1,233 patients with ICH were encompassed. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves demonstrated significant differences in the occurrence of SDOC and in-hospital and long-term survival among sACR strata. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that sACR exhibited a strong predictive performance for IHM, LTM, and SDOC. Cox proportional hazards analysis further indicated that higher sACR levels were associated with reduced risks of both IHM and LTM. Moreover, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, after full adjustment for potential confounders, revealed a linear inverse relationship between elevated sACR and the risks of SDOC and IHM, while no significant nonlinear association was observed with LTM. The protective effect of sACR continued to strengthen when sACR exceeded 4, but tended to plateau when sACR reached or surpassed 5. Subgroup analysis demonstrated that sACR exhibited an interaction effect with venous thromboembolism (VTE) on IHM, and also showed an interaction effect with a history of cancer on LTM. These findings suggest that higher sACR levels are significantly associated with a reduced risk of IHM and LTM among patients with severe ICH. Nonetheless, further prospective studies are needed to confirm these causal relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29526-w.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a severe and acute cerebrovascular disorder, with a global annual incidence ranging from approximately 6.5% to 19.6%1. The primary etiological factors include hypertension in conjunction with cerebral small vessel disease, particularly arteriolosclerosis. ICH is linked to a high case fatality rate, with roughly 40% of patients dying within one month and 54% within one year; merely 12–39% of survivors attain lasting functional independence2. Although timely surgical intervention may reduce mortality by up to one-third3, a substantial proportion of survivors develop chronic disorders of consciousness (DOC) due to extensive cerebral injury, resulting in markedly diminished long-term quality of life and survival. Therefore, early identification of the critically ill ICH population is important for the timely formulation of clinical management strategies, improvement of prognostic outcomes, and reduction of death.

Albumin (ALB), a crucial plasma protein, exerts multiple physiological functions, including immunomodulation, maintenance of endothelial integrity, and antioxidative activity4. It is also a recognized essential biomarker of nutrition and systemic inflammatory burden, both of which are pivotal mediators of adverse outcomes following stroke5. Serum ALB levels have been established as robust and independent prognostic indicators of long-term mortality (LTM) and poor outcomes in the acute ischemic stroke (AIS) population6. In addition, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is a well-recognized poor outcome risk factor following stroke. This association may be attributed to the development of acute kidney injury (AKI) after stroke, which can exacerbate disease severity and thereby increase the risk of mortality and disability7,8. Recent studies have demonstrated that eGFR computed using both serum creatinine (CRE) and cystatin C (eGFRCr + CysC) is independently related to elevated risks of all-cause mortality (ACM) and post-stroke disability9,10. The serum albumin-to-creatinine ratio (sACR)11, which integrates both ALB and CRE levels, offers a more comprehensive reflection of a patient’s nutrition, inflammatory response, as well as kidney function. It is a prognostic marker in many diseases, including cardiovascular disease12,13, Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea14, and sepsis15,16. Prior studies have reported significant relations of malnutrition and renal dysfunction to elevated ACM risk within the critically ill AIS population17. Notably, a recent investigation identified the serum creatinine-to-albumin ratio (CAR) as an independent 28-day ACM predictor among the critically ill ICH population18. Nevertheless, no study to date has elucidated the association between sACR and the development of severe disturbance of consciousness (SDOC), in-hospital mortality (IHM), or LTM following ICH. Therefore, our study endeavors to explore the predictive value of sACR for SDOC, IHM, and LTM among ICH individuals in the intensive care unit (ICU). This may aid clinicians in the early identification of the high-risk population and in timely treatment.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study leveraged data from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database19 (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/).

MIMIC-IV is a large, publicly accessible, open-source resource of the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). It has comprehensive clinical data on people entering the ICU and emergency departments of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, USA, from 2008 to 2022. It provides valuable support for research on critical care outcomes, predictive modelling, and clinical decision-making. The data collection and research resource development were reviewed and approved by the BIDMC Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were: ICU patients with non-traumatic ICH diagnosed as per ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes. Exclusion criteria were: (a) age ≤ 18; (b) patients not admitted to the ICU; (c) patients lacking CRE and ALB data within the first 72 h of admission; and (d) patients with severe pre-existing consciousness disturbances (Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] score ≤ 8). In cases of multiple admissions, only data from the first ICU entry were encompassed.

Variable extraction

Pertinent clinical data were extracted from MIMIC-IV via SQL queries within the PostgreSQL environment in five domains: (1) Demographic information: age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI); (2) Comorbidities: identified using ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes, including tobacco use, alcohol abuse, diabetes, hypertension, atrial fibrillation (AF), heart failure (HF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary embolism (PE), venous thromboembolism (VTE), peripheral vascular disease (PVD), sepsis, paraplegia, and history of cancer; (3) Laboratory indicators: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum albumin (SALB), as well as CRE. Laboratory measurements were obtained from the first results recorded within 72 h of admission. If SDOC occurred within 72 h, the respective measurements were considered invalid; (4) Severity of illness: assessed via GCS; (5) Treatment measures: including use of vasopressors and vasoactive agents, antiplatelet drugs, anticoagulants, statins, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), mechanical ventilation (MV), as well as neurosurgical procedures. As the MIMIC-IV database does not provide information on medications administered before hospital admission, only medication data recorded during hospitalization were included in this study.

To minimize bias due to missing data, multiple imputation was conducted via “mice” in R for variables with ≤ 20% missing values. For variables with > 20% missing data, missingness was treated as a separate category and encompassed in the analysis.

Exposure variable

The primary exposure variable was sACR, computed using the first available measurements within 72 h of admission. The formula was: sACR = SALB (g/dL)/CRE (mg/dL).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of interest was LTM in critically ill patients with ICH. Secondary outcomes encompassed IHM and SDOC, defined as a GCS score ≤ 8. The follow-up interval was the time from ICU entry to the development of the given outcome.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were shown in means ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed ones were in medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Intergroup differences were examined via t-tests, one-way ANOVA, non-parametric, Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests.

Patients were split into tertiles by sACR: T1 (< 3.272), T2 (3.272–4.714), and T3 (≥ 4.714). The occurrence of SDOC, IHM, and LTM across sACR groups was compared via Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to compare the predictive performance of CRE, albumin (ALB), and sACR. The link of sACR to the clinical outcomes was investigated utilizing Cox proportional hazards models, with sequential adjustment for confounders: Model 1: crude; Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, and race; Model 3: further adjusted for BMI, tobacco use, alcohol abuse, diabetes, hypertension, AF, HF, COPD, PE, VTE, PVD, sepsis, paraplegia, history of stroke, history of cancer, ALT, AST, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) score, use of vasopressors and vasoactive agents, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, statins, CRRT, MV, neurosurgical intervention, and GCS score at admission. To avoid multicollinearity, variables with variance inflation factors (VIF) > 5 were removed from the multivariate models. The possible non-linear relation of sACR to clinical outcomes was explored through restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. Finally, the robustness of associations across patient strata was rated via subgroup analyses. Our statistical analyses were enabled by R 4.5.0, and a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Results

1,233 patients were encompassed as per the predefined eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). The pattern of missing data is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using datasets before and after multiple imputation, and the results indicated no significant differences between the two (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S2). Their median age was 67, with a predominance of male (53%) and White (58%) individuals. Among them, 295 patients (24%) with ICH experienced IHM, and the LTM rate among ICH patients was 47%. 366 patients (29.7%) in the cohort developed SDOC, which was linked to significantly increased risks of both IHM and LTM. Furthermore, ICH patients who died during hospitalization exhibited significantly higher levels of ALT, AST, CRE, and SIRS scores, in comparison to those who survived hospitalization (all P < 0.001). Conversely, SALB levels were notably lower in the SDOC cohort than in the non-SDOC (NSDOC) group (P < 0.001). The occurrence of sepsis and HF was markedly higher among ICH patients who died in the hospital in comparison to survivors (both P < 0.001). The use rates of MV and CRRT were also significantly elevated in the IHM group. In contrast, the administration rates of statins, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, vasopressors, and vasoactive agents were markedly lower in the SDOC cohort than in the NSDOC group (all P < 0.001) (Table 1).

The area under the curve (AUC) values for predicting IHM were as follows: ALB (AUC = 0.637, 95% CI: 0.600–0.675), CRE (AUC = 0.602, 95% CI: 0.562–0.642), and sACR (AUC = 0.645, 95% CI: 0.607–0.683) (Fig. 3A).

For LTM, the AUC values were: ALB (AUC = 0.626, 95% CI: 0.595–0.657), CRE (AUC = 0.586, 95% CI: 0.554–0.618), and sACR (AUC = 0.631, 95% CI: 0.600–0.663) (Fig. 3B). Cox proportional hazards analysis

Our Cox proportional hazards modelling demonstrated that the high sACR cohort exhibited a markedly lowered likelihood of LTM in comparison to those in the low sACR group. Specifically, for every unit of rise in sACR, the LTM risk in ICH individuals decreased by 11%. Patients with higher sACR levels showed notably lower LTM risk in comparison to those with the lowest sACR levels. This inverse association was further supported by the test for trend (P for trend < 0.001).

When sACR was a continuous variable, a negative relation was noted between sACR and both LTM (Model 1: HR 0.89 [95% CI: 0.850–0.932]; Model 2: HR 0.879 [95% CI: 0.838–0.923]; Model 3: HR 0.916 [95% CI 0.869–0.966]) and IHM (Model 1: HR 0.828 [95% CI: 0.775–0.884]; Model 2: HR 0.812 [95% CI: 0.758–0.870]; Model 3: HR 0.875 [95% CI 0.807–0.948]), with all p-values ≤ 0.001. Regarding SDOC, sACR was inversely linked to the risk of SDOC in Model 1 (HR 0.91 [95% CI: 0.859–0.963]) and Model 2 (HR 0.907 [95% CI: 0.854–0.963]). However, this association was no longer significant in Model 3 (HR 0.98 [95% CI 0.917–1.048], p = 0.561).

When sACR was analyzed as a categorical variable, the highest tertile of sACR (T3) had significantly lower risks of LTM and IHM in comparison to the lowest tertile (T1), and these associations remained robust after adjustment for potential confounders (all p < 0.01).

Concerning SDOC, an inverse trend was observed in the unadjusted and partially adjusted models, wherein higher sACR levels correlated with a decreased likelihood of SDOC (all p < 0.05), but this trend was not retained in Model 3 (p = 0.983) (Table 3).

Non-linear association between sACR and clinical outcomes

This study employed RCS models to investigate the potential non-linear relation of sACR to various clinical outcomes. After all confounders were adjusted, increasing sACR levels were linked to an approximately linear decline in the likelihood of SDOC (P for non-linearity = 0.39) and IHM (P for non-linearity = 0.778). These trends were consistent across all models, indicating an overall linear pattern (P for non-linearity > 0.05). When the outcome of interest was LTM, a significant non-linear relation of sACR to mortality risk was observed in Model 1(P for non-linearity = 0.036) and Model 2 (P for non-linearity = 0.013). However, after full adjustment for confounding factors, the nonlinear association was no longer statistically significant (P for nonlinear = 0.054). Moreover, when sACR exceeded 4, further increases in sACR were potentially linked to reduced risks of SDOC, IHM, and LT. Nevertheless, once sACR reached a certain threshold (approximately 5), higher albumin or lower creatinine levels no longer resulted in a further decrease in HR, indicating that the protective effect did not continue to increase (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses revealed that sACR was significantly linked to the risk of IHM across multiple strata, including age (> 60, ≤ 60), sex (male, female), absence of sepsis, diabetes (absent, present), hypertension (absent, present), HF (absent, present), AF (absent, present), VTE (absent, present), absence of prior stroke, and absence of history of cancer. In all these subgroups, the hazard ratios (HRs) were less than 1, indicating a protective association. However, a significant sACR-VTE interaction was noted (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A).

Similarly, sACR was significantly linked to LTM across subgroups defined by age (> 60, ≤ 60), sex (male, female), absence of sepsis, DM (absent, present), hypertension (absent, present), HF (absent, present), AF (absent, present), VTE (absent, present), prior stroke (absent, present), and absence of history of cancer. A marked interaction was identified between sACR and history of cancer (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B).

Furthermore, in subgroup analyses of sACR and SDOC outcomes, sACR was significantly linked to reduced risk of SDOC (P < 0.05) in the following subgroups: age ≤ 60, sex (male, female), absence of sepsis, DM (absent), hypertension (absent), HF (absent), AF (present), VTE (absent), prior stroke (absent), and history of cancer (absent), with all corresponding HRs < 1. No significant interactions were observed between sACR and any of these subgroups (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis based on the ICH population from MIMIC-IV. It is the first investigation to reveal the prognostic significance of sACR in individuals with ICH. The findings suggest that higher sACR levels are associated with a decreased risk of both IHM and LTM in this patient population. This association persisted even after potential confounders were adjusted. Subsequent analyses demonstrated a generally linear relation of sACR to the risks of IHM, SDOC, and LTM after accounting for all confounders.

ALB, synthesized in the liver, is among the most abundant plasma proteins and performs many physiological functions, including the maintenance of plasma oncotic pressure, antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. These properties suggest a potential beneficial role for ALB in the pathophysiological processes of ICH. The underlying mechanisms may include: (1) increasing plasma colloid osmotic pressure, thereby alleviating cerebral edema and reducing intracranial pressure20; (2) downregulating proinflammatory cytokines and inflammatory signaling pathways (like the VCAM-1 pathway), thereby attenuating inflammatory responses21; (3) binding free radicals and acting as an effective antioxidant to mitigate oxidative stress injury22; (4) inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, thus reducing apoptosis and preserving neuronal integrity23; (5) binding nitric oxide (NO) to form S-nitrosothiols, exerting antithrombotic and antiplatelet effects24; and (6) the association between hypoalbuminemia and increased risk of SIRS and infections, which collectively hinder recovery in the ICH population25,26. Hypoalbuminemia is common among patients with ICH and is strongly linked to poor prognosis. A meta-analysis demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia independently predicts LTM in the AIS population6. Another study identified a significant link between hypoalbuminemia and 90-day ACM in critically ill ICH individuals, as well as an increased risk of pneumonia and sepsis27. However, the prognostic utility of ALB alone is limited due to interference from factors like hepatic dysfunction, enhanced catabolism, and extravascular leakage.

CRE, a terminal metabolite of muscle metabolism, is primarily excreted via glomerular filtration. Elevated serum CRE levels typically indicate impaired renal function. Recent studies have identified the clinical relevance of CRE level fluctuations in ICH patients. Higher admission CRE levels are significantly linked to increased short-term mortality in this population28 and may also suggest a heightened likelihood of AKI28. Furthermore, variations in CRE levels may reflect systemic inflammatory responses following ICH, thereby influencing patient outcomes18. From a mechanistic standpoint, post-ICH renal dysfunction may arise through a dual pathway involving neuroendocrine dysregulation, particularly excessive renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, and heightened sympathetic nervous system activity, causing reduced renal perfusion29. Additionally, ICH can trigger extensive inflammatory mediator release and microthrombus formation, causing renal ischemia and functional impairment, thereby elevating CRE levels. Nonetheless, CRE concentrations are also susceptible to multiple confounding influences, like physiological stress and medication use, and are often incapable of distinguishing chronic renal impairment. Therefore, the prognostic value of CRE as a standalone biomarker is limited and must be interpreted in conjunction with a comprehensive clinical evaluation.

sACR, a novel composite biomarker integrating CRE and ALB levels, is a promising prognostic indicator across clinical domains, such as cardiovascular diseases, acute pancreatitis, and refractory Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea12,14,16,30. Erdems et al.31 reported that reduced sACR levels were linked to an elevated likelihood of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Shen et al.30 identified sACR as a reliable prognostic marker in heart transplant recipients. In the study by Lin Yang et al.16, sACR was independently linked to IHM among patients with severe acute pancreatitis (SAP). Similarly, Nseir et al.14 demonstrated that sACR levels could predict short-term ACM in people with refractory C. difficile infection. Recent evidence has also suggested that CAR is significantly and positively linked to 28-day mortality in the ICH population, indicating that CAR is an independent prognostic factor with superior predictive accuracy in comparison to traditional single biomarkers like CRE and ALB18. While previous studies in the ICH population have suggested associations between combined indicators of CRE and ALB with short-term mortality, the impact of such markers on long-term prognosis has not yet been thoroughly evaluated. This retrospective cohort study, based on ICU-admitted ICH patients from MIMIC-IV, revealed a significant inverse relation of sACR to both IHM and LTM in the severe ICH population, even after all possible confounders were adjusted. These findings underscore the prognostic value of sACR in forecasting short- and long-term outcomes in this patient population.

Furthermore, the RCS analysis in this study revealed a plateau phenomenon in the protective effects of sACR across all outcomes when sACR exceeded 5, suggesting the presence of a potential biological threshold beyond which incremental benefits diminish. This may be attributable to abnormally reduced creatinine levels (as in sarcopenia or frailty)32,33 or to the saturation of the neuroprotective effects of albumin33. The borderline nonlinearity p-value for LTM (0.054) may reflect limited statistical power, rendering it insufficient to fully characterize this complex relationship34. This plateau effect warrants further investigation, as it implies the existence of an optimal range of sACR values rather than a simple monotonic benefit.

Finally, subgroup analyses demonstrated significant interactions between sACR and VTE in influencing IHM, as well as between sACR and cancer history in determining LTM. These findings indicate that the protective effect of sACR may be either amplified or attenuated under specific clinical conditions. In patients with ICH complicated by VTE, elevated sACR may enhance survival benefits through a “coagulation–inflammation dual pathway.” (1) Coagulation pathway: high albumin levels can exert direct antithrombotic effects by mediating NO-binding platelet inhibition, counteracting the hypercoagulable state associated with VTE35–37; (2) Inflammatory pathway: albumin may scavenge reactive oxygen species and neutralize proinflammatory cytokines, thereby attenuating endothelial injury38,39. In contrast, renal insufficiency may lead to the accumulation of uremic toxins, platelet dysfunction, and aberrant vWF release, further exacerbating thrombosis risk. Hence, sACR encapsulates two pivotal pathophysiological axes of VTE, coagulation imbalance and inflammatory dysregulation, and higher sACR levels confer greater protection against IHM40. In the context of malignancy, low sACR levels may reflect the combined impact of cachexia–inflammation–antioxidant depletion and cancer-related renal injury (e.g., cisplatin nephrotoxicity or paraneoplastic glomerulopathy)41–43. This state not only signifies impaired nutritional reserves, limiting neurological recovery after ICH, but may also accelerate tumor progression through renal dysfunction44, collectively amplifying the risk of LTM. In summary, as a prognostic marker, sACR offers clinicians valuable insights into both renal function and nutrition, thereby facilitating a more comprehensive and individualized assessment of clinical prognosis in the severe ICH population.

Moreover, critically ill patients often develop capillary leak syndrome, leading to transvascular albumin loss into the interstitial space and further reduction of serum albumin levels. Concurrently, acute kidney injury, frequently observed in patients with ICH, may elevate CRE concentrations25.

Although the findings of this study suggest that sACR may serve as a protective biomarker, the possibility of reverse causation must be carefully considered. As previously noted, severe ICH can trigger systemic inflammatory responses, metabolic stress, and hepatic dysfunction45, all of which may suppress albumin synthesis and accelerate its catabolism. Moreover, critically ill patients are often accompanied by capillary leak syndrome20, leading to the translocation of albumin from the intravascular compartment to the interstitial space, thereby further reducing serum albumin levels. Meanwhile, patients with ICH frequently develop acute kidney injury, which may result in elevated CRE levels29,46. Therefore, the decline in sACR levels may primarily stem from the severity of the illness itself rather than an independent effect of sACR on clinical outcomes. This bidirectional relationship increases the complexity of inferring causality from observational studies. Although adjustments were made in the present analysis for inflammatory markers (such as systemic inflammatory response syndrome) and relevant comorbidities, residual confounding attributable to disease severity cannot be entirely excluded. Future prospective studies are warranted to systematically assess the temporal relationship between dynamic changes in sACR and clinical deterioration, thereby clarifying its potential causal association.

Strengths and limitations

This study first highlights the significant impact of sACR on long-term outcomes in the severe ICH population. Although the study design was optimized, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the MIMIC-IV database is derived predominantly from a Caucasian population in America, which possibly limits the application of the findings to other populations. Second, as a retrospective observational study, causal inferences between sACR levels and prognosis in severe ICH cannot be established. Third, although multivariable adjustments and subgroup analyses were employed to minimize confounding, certain relevant variables, like hematoma location and volume, as well as motor function, were not captured in the database, leaving room for residual confounding. Fourth, this study only evaluated the relation of baseline sACR to outcomes, without assessing the prognostic implications of temporal changes in sACR. Lastly, the relatively small sample size necessitates further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Conclusion

sACR was significantly and inversely associated with IHM and LTM in patients with severe ICH. However, this association may primarily reflect the severity of the disease rather than indicate a direct causal relationship. Although sACR shows considerable promise as a novel biomarker for risk stratification, its clinical utility warrants further validation through large-scale, multicenter prospective studies.
