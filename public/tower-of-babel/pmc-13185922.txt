Effects of different pretreatment methods on the dough properties, bread flavor and in vitro digestion of black highland barley
Authors: Yang Liu, Lifen Li, Hanbing Xiao, Huijuan Wang, Can Zhao, Wenhui Zhang, Qisen Xiang, Yingting Zhao, Yuting Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHighland barley is rich in nutrients, however, its application in bread products is limited due to its poor processing quality. Therefore, physical modification of highland barley is required to improve its processing properties. This study investigated the effects of four pretreatment methods—traditional stir-frying, steam explosion, microwave, and far-infrared baking—on the dough properties and final bread quality of black highland barley. The results indicated that all four pretreatments decreased free water content in the dough and improved its rheological properties. Meanwhile, the protein structure and aggregation state were altered, and treatment-specific changes in free sulfhydryl groups and intermolecular interactions were observed. Microstructural observations revealed that the treated dough samples exhibited looser and more fragmented gluten network structures, with steam explosion treatment notably preserving network continuity and starch encapsulation. Regarding bread quality, pretreatments significantly modified textural properties and enhanced flavor compound diversity, with steam explosion treatment showing the most pronounced flavor enhancement effects. In terms of digestibility, pretreatments reduced the maximum starch digestibility and the content of rapidly digestible starch while increasing the resistant starch content, which consequently lowered the estimated glycemic index of all pretreated breads. Overall, this research provides a theoretical foundation for utilizing pretreatments to improve dough properties and reduce bread digestibility, offering scientific support for developing nutritionally enhanced functional products.Keywords: Highland barley, Bread, Dough rheology, In vitro starch digestibility, Estimated glycemic index

Introduction

Highland barley (HB), a characteristic functional grain from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, is exceptionally rich in β-glucan (approximately 3.66–8.62%, the highest among cereals) (Li et al., 2024), dietary fiber (about 8.16–21.46%) (Lyu et al., 2022), and γ-aminobutyric acid (32–45 mg/100 g) (Guo et al., 2020), it exhibits significant benefits for blood glucose regulation and gut health promotion (Zargarchi & Saremnezhad, 2019).

Black HB, a nutrient-dense traditional cereal, is particularly notable for its slow digestion rate, which helps control postprandial blood glucose fluctuations—an advantage over wheat-based products (Hu et al., 2022). Nevertheless, several critical technical bottlenecks restrict the application of HB in dough processing and bread production. The dense structure of HB starch and high β-glucan content impair dough extensibility and protein cross-linking (Li et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2023). Additionally, HB proteins (primarily hordein and glutelin) lack the cross-linking ability of glutenin, failing to form an effective gel network (Bao et al., 2024). These defects directly led to quality deterioration of HB bread, which was characterized by low specific volume (1.3 mL/g vs. 3.12 mL/g for wheat bread) (He et al., 2022), high hardness (up to 1631.30 g) (Tian et al., 2023), and bland flavor due to insufficient Maillard reaction products. Consequently, HB flour incorporation in traditional bread is limited to 10%–15%, with higher levels worsening quality (Fan et al., 2022).

To break through these constraints, physical modification technology has emerged as a pivotal approach for enhancing the processing performance of HB. Its core strategy lies in improving dough functional properties through structural reorganization (Wang et al., 2025). Currently, traditional stir-frying (TS), steam explosion (SE), microwave (MW), and far-infrared baking (FIB) are among the most common grain processing techniques. TS, widely used in HB processing, modifies starch and protein properties. Wang et al. (2023) found TS altered oat protein secondary structure, increased disulfide bonds, reduced digestibility, and improved dough-related functional properties (water-holding, foaming, emulsifying capacities). SE pretreatment (0.3 MPa, 5 min) on whole wheat flour reduced rapidly digestible starch (RDS), increased slowly digestible starch (SDS), and enhanced gel strength—addressing HB dough's loose texture and poor tolerance (Kong et al., 2022). Qu et al. (2017) observed that short-term MW heating (700 W, ≤20 s) enhanced steamed bread quality, while prolonged treatment degraded quality due to gluten denaturation. Infrared processing induces starch-protein/starch-starch coalescence, starch chain rearrangement, and irreversible oxidation of free sulfhydryl groups (-SH) to disulfide bonds (-S-S-), impacting starch gelatinization (Mahmood et al., 2024). These effects may enhance the interactions between HB dough components and compensate for the deficiencies of the gluten network. We therefore hypothesize that FIB exerts a similar effect. Consequently, different energy-transfer modes exert divergent impacts on the multiscale structures of HB.

Current research predominantly focuses on conventional cereals like wheat, with studies on the pre-gelatinization of distinctive grains such as HB remaining relatively scarce. Existing HB investigations examine individual pre-gelatinization methods in isolation, lacking systematic comparisons, and insufficiently integrate effects on protein conformation, rheological behavior, and nutritional functionality. This study processes black HB using four pre-gelatinization methods—TS, SE, MW, and FIB. Through an integrated analytical approach combining scanning electron microscopy (SEM), confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), and in vitro digestion, we elucidate the differential impacts of these pretreatments on dough properties and bread quality. The study aims to overcome HB's bread-making technical constraints and establish a scientific foundation for developing nutritionally enhanced functional HB products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Longzi black HB sourced from Tibetan Academy of Agricultural and Animal Husbandry Sciences (Xizang province, China) were washed, the impurities such as grass seeds and stones were removed, and the samples were dried in an electric blast drying oven (DJG-9053 A, Yiheng, Changzhou, China) at 40 °C for 24 h. The samples were sealed, and the samples were stored at 25 °C.

Pepsin (P7000, porcine gastric mucosa) and pancreatin (P7545, porcine pancreas) 98 were supplied by Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Chromatographic grade methanol was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Sugar, butter and yeast are purchased from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Preparation of barley flour with different pretreatments

HB grains were first subjected to different thermal pretreatments prior to milling. The degree of starch gelatinization was used as the processing control parameter and was determined using the iodometric method described by Y. Liu et al. (2025). Through fine-tuning of treatment time, the starch gelatinization degree of all pretreated samples was controlled at 50% ± 3.70% (refer to Supplementary material), resulting in four pretreatment modalities. Specifically, SE treatment was performed at a moisture content of 30% under 0.8 MPa for 90 s, followed by drying at 40 °C for 24 h. TS treatment was conducted at a moisture content of 10% by stir-frying at 200 °C for 50 s. MW treatment was carried out at 700 W for 100 s with a moisture content of 10%. FIB treatment was performed at 210 °C for 3 min at a moisture content of 10%. Untreated HB (CK) and treated HB were crushed and screened (80 mesh) using a crusher (RT02, Yili, Yongkang, China) and stored at 4 °C.

Barley dough making and bread baking

The dough was prepared by mixing 100 g of HB flour, 25 g of vital wheat gluten, and 125 g of water to form a yeast-free dough. This mixture was kneaded for 10 min using a stand mixer (model PE4633, Petrus Electric Co., Ltd., China) to obtain the HB dough. The dough was then washed manually by hand three times to extract wet gluten. Both the dough and wet gluten were frozen at −80 °C for 24 h, followed by freeze-drying for 48 h to produce lyophilized dough and gluten protein. Finally, the dough and gluten protein were ground into powder, sieved through an 80-mesh sieve, and stored at −20 °C for further use.

The recipe for HB bread is as follows: 100 g HB flour, 25 g vital wheat gluten, 125 g water, 1.8 g yeast, 10 g sugar, 6 g butter, and 1 g salt. The fermentation method employed the optimized low-temperature overnight fermentation technique determined through preliminary experiments to yield the best-quality bread. The dough preparation process involves thoroughly mixing 50% of the main dough ingredients (excluding sugar and butter), refrigerating the mixture at 4 °C for 12 h, allowing it to return to room temperature, combining it with the remaining main dough ingredients, fermenting at 38 °C for 90 min, rounding and shaping the dough, bench proofing at 38 °C for 15 min, baking in an oven (Model T7-L328E, Midea Group Co., Ltd., China) at 180 °C for 12 min, and cooling to obtain the finished bread, which is then cooled at room temperature for 1 h before subsequent analysis and testing.

Low-field NMR (LF-NMR) and water activity measurements

The moisture state of HB dough was determined using a Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Analyzer (NMI20-060H-I, Magnetic resonance imaging analyzer, Suzhou Newmai Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd., China). Measurements were conducted with a 25 mm diameter NMR tube at 25 ± 0.1 °C. After calibration, approximately 2 g of each dough sample was placed in a 10 mm NMR sealed tube. The acquisition parameters were set as follows: repetition time (TW) = 5500 ms, echo time (TE) = 0.8 ms, with 12,000 echoes (NECH) averaged over 4 scans (NS).

Moisture activity (Aw) was determined at 25 °C using a temperature-controlled Aw analyzer (AW1000T, Moisture Activity Tester, Changer Tek Industrial (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal properties of starch in the dough were analyzed using a differential scanning calorimeter (Model TA DSC25, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). Approximately 3 mg of freeze-dried dough powder was sealed in an aluminum pan with twice its weight of deionized water. Prior to testing, the sealed pan was equilibrated at 4 °C overnight. The sample was then heated from 25 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min. To、Tp and Te referred to the onset, peak, and end end temperatures, respectively. ΔH was the enthalpy of gelatinization.

Rheological properties

The rheological measurement was performed according to a modified method based on Zhang et al. (2022), where the fresh dough sample was placed on the rheometer stage (MCR301, Anton Paar, Austria), lowered to a 2 mm gap using a 40 mm parallel plate, trimmed of excess material, coated with silicone oil to prevent evaporation, and subjected to frequency sweep analysis at 25 °C with 0.02% strain over a 0.1–100 Hz range, following a 5 min equilibration period prior to the frequency sweep test conducted at 0.1% stress and 2 mm gap distance from 0.1 to 10 Hz.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed on the freeze-dried dough samples using a Tensor 27 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) based on the modified method established by He et al. (2026). Briefly, 2 mg of the sample was ground with pre-dried KBr, dried, and compressed into a pellet. The spectrum was collected in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 64 accumulations. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The spectral data were processed using Omnic and PeakFit software (v4.12), and the characteristic bands were assigned as follows: β-sheets at 1600–1640 cm−1, α-helix at 1650–1660 cm−1, β-turns at 1660–1700 cm−1, and random coils at 1640–1650 cm−1.

Determination of free sulfhydryl groups

The free sulfhydryl (SHf) group content was determined using a modified method based on Meng et al. (2023), involving preparation of the following solutions: (1) Tris-Glycine-EDTA buffer (TGE): dissolving 10.4 g Tris, 6.9 g Gly, and 12 g EDTA followed by dilution to 1 L (pH 8.0); (2) Urea-Guanidine hydrochloride solution: dissolving 48.0 g urea and 47.77 g guanidine hydrochloride in TGE with dilution to 100 mL; (3) Urea solution: dissolving 48.0 g urea in TGE diluted to 100 mL; and (4) Ellman's reagent: dissolving 25 mg 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) in TGE diluted to 100 mL, followed by dispersing 75 mg of the freeze-dried dough samples in 10 mL TGE, adding 4.7 g guanidine hydrochloride to form a mixture, reacting 1 mL of this mixture with 4 mL urea-guanidine hydrochloride solution, adding 0.05 mL Ellman's reagent, and measuring absorbance at 412 nm, with SHf content calculated according to the referenced formula (1).

Intermolecular interaction forces

The intermolecular interaction forces of proteins in the gluten samples were quantified using a modified method reported by Meng et al. (2023). Briefly, 1.0 g of the freeze-dried gluten protein was accurately weighed and sequentially mixed with 5 mL of the following solutions: 0.05 mol/L NaCl (S0), 0.6 mol/L NaCl (S1), 0.6 mol/L NaCl +1.5 mol/L urea (S2), 0.6 mol/L NaCl +8 mol/L urea (S3), and 0.6 mol/L NaCl +8 mol/L urea +0.5 mol/L β-mercaptoethanol (S4), homogenized at 10,000 r/min for 1 min, equilibrated at 4 °C in a chromatography cabinet for 1 h, centrifuged at 10,000 r/min and 4 °C for 10 min, with the supernatant protein content determined by the Bradford method, wherein ionic bond content was calculated as the difference in dissolved protein between S1 and S0, hydrogen bonding as S2 minus S1, hydrophobic interactions as S3 minus S2, and disulfide bonds as S4 minus S3. All results were expressed as the mass of dissolved protein per milliliter of solution (mg/mL).

SEM and CLSM

The evaluation of dough microstructure was conducted according to a modified method based on He et al. (2023), using a scanning electron microscope (Sigma 300, ZEISS, Germany), where dry yeast was excluded from the dough formulation to prevent structural changes induced by fermentation, followed by freeze-drying the dough samples, cutting them into 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm cubes, sputter-coating with gold, and observing under the SEM chamber at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

The structural visualization of HB dough was performed by sectioning samples into 0.2 mm slices using a cryostat microtome, mounting the slices on glass slides, incubating them for 10 min in a solution containing fluorescein isothiocyanate (1% w/v) and rhodamine B (0.25% w/v) in 50% acetone, rinsing with distilled water, and observing under a laser scanning confocal microscope (Model FV3000, Olympus, Japan) with excitation/emission wavelengths set at 488 nm and 543 nm respectively.

Referring to the modified method of Huang et al. (Huang et al., 2022), the analysis of volatile compounds in HB bread was performed using GC–MS (SHIMADZU QP2010 SE, Japan). After pre-heating at 60 °C for 10 min, volatile compounds were extracted for 30 min, followed by 5 min desorption in the GC injection port prior to GC–MS analysis, with each sample processed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Results and discussion

HB dough water mobility and water activity

The moisture distribution within dough was characterized using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), revealing that dough formation primarily involves the synergistic combination of gluten protein, starch, and water. Dough water exists in three distinct states: monolayer bound water (tightly associated with starch/protein), multi-molecular bound water (an intermediate layer bridging bound and free water, interacting with macromolecules), and free water (exhibiting the highest mobility) (Bao et al., 2024). The proton signal peaks corresponding to the relaxation times of dough, arranged in descending order of molecular binding strength, are classified into three regions: T21 (0.1 ms ∼ 1 ms), T22 (1 ms ∼ 50 ms), and T23 (50 ms ∼ 200 ms), with their relative peak areas denoted as A21, A22, and A23, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1A, the T22 relaxation time of treated HB dough exhibited a leftward shift, indicating that the gluten matrix in treated groups possessed enhanced water retention capacity compared to untreated samples. The increase in A21 of Table 1 (bound water content) observed in SE, MW, and FIB groups can be attributed to the optimized water-holding structure of the starch-protein-β-glucan composite system (Xie et al., 2021). The elevated A22 (weakly bound water) in TS and MW groups suggests these treatments improved water availability through enhanced starch-gluten interactions, while the decreased A23 (free water) across all treated groups demonstrates that pretreatments restricted free water mobility, promoting its conversion to bound and weakly bound states, thereby strengthening water-binding capacity. Under equilibrium conditions, the different pretreatment methods showed no significant effect on the water activity of HB dough. This result could be attributed to the fact that the starch gelatinization degree of all treatments was approximately 50%. The extent of water “entrapment” primarily achieved by starch via hydrogen bonding appeared to be similar across all treatments (Joseph et al., 2026). Furthermore, during dough preparation, the added water content was kept consistent, resulting in no significant difference in water activity.

HB dough DSC

The thermodynamic parameters of HB dough subjected to different pretreatment methods were analyzed through DSC curves, with results presented in Table 2. The gelatinization temperatures (To, Tp and Te) of treated dough either remained unchanged or slightly increased compared with the control. This trend suggested enhanced thermal stability of starch granules after pretreatment, which could be attributed to enhanced interactions among amylose-amylose, amylose-amylopectin, and amylose-lipid complexes during heat treatment, thereby stabilizing the starch structure. Additionally, the reduced free water content observed in the treated dough, as indicated by LF-NMR analysis, might also contribute to the increased gelatinization temperature by limiting starch granule swelling during heating. The gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH), which correlates with the ratio of ordered to disordered structures in starch (Moreira et al., 2015), varied between 1.05 and 0.29 J/g across samples. The decreased ΔH values in all treated groups indicated that increased pre-gelatinization disrupted most semi-crystalline structures and the most ordered crystalline regions within starch granules, leading to reduced ΔH. The crystalline structure of HB starch treated with TS was partially disrupted, but it still retained a considerable amount of ordered structures that require energy for dissociation. Thus, its ΔH was significantly lower than that of the untreated group, but higher than that of other more efficient processing methods. SE treatment, which combined high-temperature steam and rapid pressure release, resulted in a ΔH value comparable to that of the TS group, suggesting a similar overall extent of crystalline disruption, despite differences in processing mechanisms. The MW treatment group retained far fewer intact crystalline structures than those subjected to stir-frying and steam explosion, leading to a significantly reduced ΔH value. Notably, the FIB group exhibited the highest To, Tp and Te values alongside the lowest ΔH (0.29 J/g). This combination indicated enhanced thermal resistance but a reduced proportion of energy-demanding crystalline domains. Previous studies have reported that FIB treatment promoted the formation of V-type crystalline structures while simultaneously decreasing the relative crystallinity of starch due to surface-selective disruption and chain rearrangement (De Pilli & Alessandrino, 2020; Liu et al., 2025a). Therefore, the DSC results were consistent with the formation of more thermally stable but less crystalline starch domains after FIB treatment. This structure exhibited exceptional mechanical properties, characterized by high strength, elasticity, and resistance to deformation (high G' and low tan δ).

HB dough rheological properties

Rheological analysis can effectively evaluate the frequency-dependent viscoelastic behavior of dough, reflecting the impact of different heat treatments on interactions between dough components. The G' and G" respectively characterize the elastic and viscous properties of dough, while tan δ represents the relative contribution of viscosity to elasticity (Bao et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 1B-D, compared to the CK group, all pretreated HB dough samples exhibited higher G' and G" values. Additionally, the tan δ values of all samples were below 1, indicating solid-like viscoelastic behavior consistent with weak gel rheological characteristics. With increasing frequency, tan δ initially decreased rapidly before rising, suggesting system instability. The overall trend of tan δ variation across frequencies was similar for all samples, with pretreated groups showing lower tan δ values than the control, demonstrating that pretreatment effectively enhanced intermolecular interactions and improved dough viscoelasticity (Gao et al., 2022). Notably, the FIB group displayed the highest G' and G" values along with the lowest tan δ, indicating significantly reinforced gel properties in the dough. This phenomenon might result from FIB treatment stimulating disulfide bond crosslinking density and inducing bending vibrations of water molecules (H-O-H at 6.2 μm), thereby facilitating the conversion of free water to bound water, which was consistent with the decrease in A23 observed in Table 1. Microwave treatment induced rapid internal heating, leading to instantaneous vaporization of moisture within the sample. This could promote the swelling and disruption of starch granules from the interior, thereby enhancing the entanglement between starch components. A relatively strong gel structure was thus formed. However, owing to possible uneven heating, the uniformity of the network structure was lower than that of FIB. The SE process, involving high-pressure steam and instantaneous pressure release, physically tears apart the cellular structure of HB. While it also gelatinized and caused starch to adhere, the resulting structure was fragmented and discontinuous—more like an accumulation of “gelatinized starch clumps” rather than a continuous gel network. Consequently, its overall stiffness was lower than the homogeneous networks formed by FIB and microwave treatment. The uneven heating during TS might cause partial caramelization and Maillard reactions in sugars, producing thickening gummy substances or macromolecular degradation products. These substances significantly increased the viscous resistance of the system but did not contribute to the formation of an elastic network (hence the low G' but high G"). Fig. 2B shows the deconvolution results of the amide I band. The β-sheet conformation is recognized as the most stable protein structure facilitating network formation and serves as the primary component influencing the elastic properties of gluten proteins (Alvarez-Ramirez et al., 2019). Fig. 2B reveals that β-sheet structures predominated in gluten proteins, with their reduced proportion in treated samples indicating that pretreatments induced the unfolding of rigid β-sheet aggregates and the rearrangement of gluten secondary configurations. This structural rearrangement led to decreased irreversible protein aggregation and a moderate enhancement of disorder in the protein backbone, manifested as increased random coils and α-helix formations. Notably, MW and FIB treatments exhibited elevated β-turn content, likely because partially unfolded HB proteins preferentially formed β-turns rather than reverting to rigid β-sheet structures. β-turns act as flexible linkers between rigid secondary structural motifs (α-helix and β-sheet), and the combined increase in moderate backbone disorder, α-helix content, and β-turn content endowed the gluten network with improved structural flexibility and stretchability (rather than reducing elasticity). This structural characteristic of the gluten protein directly contributed to the higher storage modulus (G') and mechanical strength observed in the treated doughs. However, the protein network structure became excessively rigid and aggregated, disrupting the continuity and synergistic interactions between the starch and protein phases in the dough. As a result, it failed to effectively encapsulate and retain gas, ultimately manifesting as large voids under SEM and poor fermentation performance. While the modified gluten secondary structure enhanced the intrinsic elasticity of the protein network, FTIR data only reflect the structural characteristics of HB gluten proteins and not the synergistic interactions between the gluten network, starch, and other dough components. The increased α-helix proportion in treated doughs (exceeding the CK group's 12.88%) demonstrates that pretreatments promoted the formation of ordered α-helix structures through hydrophobic interactions (He et al., 2023), a finding consistent with the results presented in Fig. 2C.

HB dough SHf content

The changes in -SHf content directly reflect the formation or cleavage of intra−/intermolecular disulfide bonds in proteins, which is crucial for the development of gluten network structure. This could be seen from Table 2, the ranking of -SHf content was: MW > CK > FIB > SE > TS. During TS processing, gradual protein denaturation brought adjacent sulfhydryl groups into spatial proximity, promoting oxidative crosslinking (Xu et al., 2024). During the high-pressure infiltration phase, steam (0.8 MPa) disrupted hydrogen bonds. This disruption exposed buried -SH groups. Subsequently, rapid depressurization increased the dissolved oxygen suddenly. The newly exposed -SH groups were immediately oxidized into -S-S- bonds. This oxidation resulted in network fragmentation and reorganization, which partially entrapped -SH groups within new aggregates (Briviba et al., 2016). FIB treatment consumed -SHf groups through Maillard reactions (binding with reducing sugars) while simultaneously enhancing protein polymerization via thermal crosslinking (Mohan et al., 2015). The MW electromagnetic field directly perturbed protein electrostatic interactions, exposing buried -SHf groups; however, the short processing time limited oxygen diffusion, preventing timely recombination of broken -SHf bonds and consequently increasing free -SHf content while inducing protein structural disorder (Han et al., 2018), a phenomenon also observable in SEM images.

HB dough intermolecular interaction forces

The strength of the gluten network in dough is fundamentally determined by the content of ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bonds, with these intermolecular forces exhibiting synergistic and competitive correlations rather than acting as independent additive factors. The various intermolecular forces in the composite protein network formed by mixing processed highland barley flour with unprocessed exogenous wheat gluten were characterized via the determination of differential protein solubility using specific chemical reagents: β-mercaptoethanol for cleaving disulfide bonds, urea for disrupting hydrogen bonds, and SDS for breaking both hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds (Liao et al., 2011). The results in Fig. 2C revealed that all pretreated samples except MW exhibited decreased ionic bond content, which correlated with free amino acid levels. MW induced intense motion and partial unfolding of HB protein molecules through its unique instantaneous and internal volumetric heating mechanism. This exposed charged amino acid residues originally hidden within the molecular structure, while potentially causing localized changes in ionic strength within the system. Consequently, it promoted electrostatic attraction between positively and negatively charged groups, facilitating the formation of ionic bonds—a change that may have competitively affected the formation of weak hydrogen bonds in the local protein microdomain. Notably, FIB group showed the lowest ionic bond content, aligning with our previous findings of fewer free amino acids in FIB-treated samples (Liu et al., 2025b). Conventional dough development involves progressive protein hydration, which reduces hydrophobic interactions as gluten aggregates into compact networks (Kuang et al., 2023). Paradoxically, all pretreated doughs exhibited enhanced hydrophobic interactions. This might be attributed to the unfolding of the protein complex formed by pretreated HB protein and wheat gluten, thereby exposing hydrophobic residues inside the protein molecule. The exposure of hydrophobic groups might inhibit the formation of hydrogen bonds between protein chains, and this ultimately resulted in a detectable increase in hydrophobic interactions. The exposure of these hydrophobic groups might have suppressed the formation of interchain hydrogen bonds in the gluten network, leading to the measurable elevation of hydrophobic interactions (He et al., 2023).

The disulfide content followed the order: SE > TS > FIB > CK > MW. SE exerted strong mechanical unfolding and created a high-temperature and high-pressure oxidative environment, which favored disulfide formation and rearrangement. Thus, SE showed the highest disulfide content. Prolonged heating in TS promoted gradual protein denaturation and oxidation, leading to an increased disulfide content. FIB employed mild but penetrating heating method, causing moderate protein unfolding and cross-linking. With lower intensity than SE and shorter duration than TS, FIB exhibited a moderate disulfide content, which helped balance disulfide cross-linking and non-covalent interactions (ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions). MW heating induced rapid internal denaturation and aggregation, which might trap thiol groups inside the protein and inhibit their exposure and oxidation. Consequently, its disulfide content was even lower than the control. The CK group maintained a native structure with only inherent disulfide bonds.

HB dough SEM and CLSM

The microstructural changes in dough samples are illustrated in Fig. 3, where untreated HB dough exhibits a relatively uniform gluten network structure with both large (approximately 20 μm diameter) and small (around 4 μm diameter) starch granules evenly embedded within the intact gluten matrix, showing plump starch surfaces (indicated by green circles). In contrast, pretreated dough samples display fractured and dented starch granules (marked by yellow squares) along with loosened gluten networks featuring visible breaks (denoted by red arrows), demonstrating that thermal treatments generally weaken gluten network integrity (Bao et al., 2024). This structural deterioration appears most pronounced in the MW-treated group, which shows numerous large voids and extensive gluten network fragmentation, correlating well with its highest free sulfhydryl (-SHf) content among all samples.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) serves as an effective optical tool for visualizing the morphological distribution of starch and protein macromolecules in HB dough, with starch stained green, protein red, and their composite structures appearing yellow (Jia et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. 4, the untreated dough forms a homogeneous, continuous gluten network that uniformly encapsulates starch granules, creating an integrated gluten-starch matrix. In pretreated doughs, however, swollen starch granules occupy significant volume while denatured proteins form a continuous phase between starch particles and elongated protein chains, though with evident gluten aggregation - particularly in MW samples showing substantial voids and irregularity. Notably, SE and FIB treatments maintain relatively complete and uniform protein coating around starch granules, suggesting their milder structural impact compared to MW treatment. This was because the uneven heating characteristic of microwave “preserves” more intact particles that have not burst, while the uniform heating characteristic of far-infrared “compromises” the surface integrity of nearly all particles.

HB bread specific volume

The specific volume of bread, which reflects the volume-to-mass ratio, serves as a crucial quantitative indicator of gas retention capacity and expansion ability during baking (Chockchaisawasdee et al., 2023). As shown in the Table 3, the SE-treated group exhibited the highest specific volume at 1.96 ± 0.11 (mL/g), suggesting that a synergistic interaction between processed highland barley flour and wheat gluten results in strong yet extensible gluten films, likely due to the competitive denaturation and molecular interactions induced by instantaneous pressure release during SE (Huang et al., 2023). The interactions between gelatinized starch and proteins formed a complex network, wherein starch and proteins exhibited both competitive and synergistic effects on the overall structure (Wang, Tang, Geng, Asiamah and Cheng, 2025). However, experimental results demonstrated that the FIB and MW groups developed excessively rigid gelatinized starch gels, as evidenced by their elevated G' values in rheological measurements. Compared with the moderate gelatinization in SE, both FIB and MW caused excessive starch gelatinization and aggregation, which directly increased the stiffness of the paste system. Meanwhile, the TS group showed reduced specific volume compared to SE, likely due to the competitive effect of starch caramelization during pretreatment, which impaired the gel's ability to hold water. FIB treatment formed the hardest and most stable elastic network (highest G′). This network restricted dough expansion during baking, resulting in limited oven spring (low specific volume). Moreover, this highly stable network led to a denser and harder texture in the final bread product.

HB bread texture

Texture profile analysis (TPA) is an instrumental quantification of a food's response to applied forces, providing an objective and standardized measure of bread quality characteristics. As shown in Table 3, breads made from preprocessed HB flour showed significantly increased hardness, springiness, and chewiness (P < 0.05), with the FIB-treated group exhibiting the greatest increases. In contrast, the SE-treated group maintained moderate values (hardness: 6.39 N, chewiness: 3.59 N), which were significantly lower than those of FIB and MW groups but higher than the CK and TS groups, indicating a more balanced texture profile. While the elevated hardness contributed to a desirable firm texture and improved structural stability of the bread crumb, an excessive increase might compromise the oral softness and palatability that were critical for consumer acceptance. Notably, the FIB and MW groups showed the lowest cohesiveness (0.49 and 0.54, respectively) and resilience (0.15 and 0.19, respectively), which further indicated that the overly rigid network formed under these treatments impaired the bread's ability to recover its structure after deformation, in contrast to the CK, TS, and SE groups which maintained higher cohesiveness and resilience. This texture variation was likely due to increased damaged starch content, enhanced gelatinization degree, and stronger intermolecular adhesion forces within the starch following heat treatment. Specifically, FIB and MW treatments induced more severe starch gelatinization and protein denaturation, leading to a highly cross-linked and rigid gel network, whereas SE treatment, through instantaneous pressure release, promoted a more open and extensible network structure. The increased water absorption capacity of the heat-treated HB flour might also have contributed to these effects (González et al., 2021). This was because the gelatinized starch and denatured proteins formed a dense, highly cross-linked three-dimensional gel network that strongly bound and immobilized water; this enhanced water absorption capacity but reduced water mobility within the matrix, ultimately resulting in increased hardness of the final bread. The TS group, on the other hand, showed intermediate texture properties, which can be attributed to the partial starch caramelization that occurred during stir-frying, modifying the network structure differently from the other thermal treatments.

Alcohols (ethanol, isobutanol, isoamyl alcohol, hexanol as main components) are key bread flavor compounds from yeast fermentation (sugar decarboxylation and amino acid deamination), imparting sweet, fruity or whiskey-like aromas with rich mellow notes (Ripari et al., 2016). The numbers of alcohol compounds detected in CK, TS, SE, MW, and FIB groups were 17, 15, 14, 18, and 12, respectively (MW highest, FIB lowest). MW elevated alcohols by exciting starch dipole rotation via electromagnetic fields, enhancing enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation (López-Linares et al., 2019). FIB reduced alcohols due to starch hydrogen bond reorganization forming a barrier that inhibited hydrolysis and fermentation (H. Guo et al., 2024), while lower alcohol content highlighted pyrazine flavors for a balanced sensory profile.

Aldehydes (hexanal, nonanal, benzaldehyde as main components) are oxidation products of unsaturated fatty acids by endogenous peroxidase and lipoxygenase, with low sensory thresholds imparting fruity, grassy, rosy or cherry-like notes (Pétel et al., 2017). The numbers of aldehyde compounds detected in the CK, TS, SE, MW, and FIB groups were 14, 13, 17, 13, and 12, respectively. The SE group exhibited the highest number of aldehydes. This was attributed to SE's high-pressure instantaneous decompression causing lipid system disruption/oxidative activation and cavitation effect activating the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway, with intensity exceeding other pretreatments (Guyon et al., 2016).

Esters (ethyl octanoate, ethyl hexanoate as main components) form via esterification of fatty acids with alcohols or fermentation, imparting winy, fruity or sweet aromas (Pétel et al., 2017). The numbers of ester compounds detected in the CK, TS, SE, MW, and FIB groups were 6, 6, 9, 8, and 4, respectively. SE increased esters by elevating free fatty acids for enhanced enzymatic esterification; FIB inhibited esterification via irreversible esterase denaturation (Zhang et al., 2017).

Ketones (acetoin, 7-octen-2-one, methylheptenone as main components) derive from lipid oxidation and the Maillard reaction, imparting butter, cream or fruit notes (Pacyński et al., 2015). The numbers of ketone compounds detected in the CK, TS, SE, MW, and FIB groups were 6, 6, 7, 7, and 5, respectively. This pattern of change in ketones was similar to that observed for esters. This was due to the factors affecting lipid oxidation or yeast activity (raw material composition, fermentation time, baking temperature) causing synchronous proportional changes in both compounds.

Acids (isobutyric acid, hexanoic acid, 3-methylvaleric acid as main components) form via dough microbial fermentation, imparting butter and cheese-like odors. The numbers of acid compounds detected in the CK, TS, SE, MW, and FIB groups were 6, 7, 7, 7, and 9, respectively. Elevated acids in processed groups resulted from physical field treatments disrupting protective barriers in HB kernels (Bai et al., 2021); these acids impart a refreshing aroma but inhibit yeast activity, consistent with the reduced dough expansion rate in fermentation characteristics.

The numbers of heterocyclic compounds detected in the CK, TS, SE, MW, and FIB groups were 5, 3, 4, 3, and 2, respectively. Furans were the predominant class, while pyrazines, pyrroles, and pyridines were present in much lower quantities. The detected pyrazines primarily included 2,6-dimethylpyrazine, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethylpyrazine. Major furans were 2-pentylfuran and 2-acetylfuran, a key pyrrole was 2-acetylpyrrole, and a notable thiazole was 2-acetylthiazole. They form via the Maillard reaction, caramelization and Strecker degradation (Zhang et al., 2021b), with pyrazines and furans imparting nutty, fatty and cocoa-like aromas. 2-Pentylfuran (roasted aroma, from unsaturated fatty acid oxidative degradation) was detected in all samples, and its reduced levels in processed groups were due to lipoxygenase inactivation during pre-gelatinization. TS had the lowest content (109.66 μg/kg) because low-moisture rapid gelatinization strongly inhibited the lipid oxidation pathway (Zhang et al., 2021a).

Hydrocarbons form via decarboxylation and cleavage of long-chain fatty acid carbon‑carbon chains, with high sensory thresholds and minimal flavor contribution. However, (+)-limonene (a terpenoid with fruity and citrus notes) made a discernible contribution to HB bread flavor.

To further analyze the impact of different pretreatment methods on the flavor of Longzi black HB bread, an OPLS-DA (Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures Discriminant Analysis) was performed on the compounds detected by GC–MS (Fig. 5B-C). As shown in the figure, the contribution rates of the first principal component (PC1) and the second principal component (PC2) were 66.3% and 19%, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 85.3%. This indicated that the model captures the majority of the sample information. The plot clearly showed significant differences in aroma profiles among the samples. CK samples were in the lower-left quadrant, while the pretreated samples were distributed in the upper region, shifting progressively to the right along PC1. Fig. 3C revealed that compounds such as isoamyl alcohol, nonanal, ethanol, hexanal, and furfural (2-furaldehyde) exhibit high VIP (Variable Importance in Projection) values, and their levels vary across samples. These compounds were likely responsible for the flavor differences observed in breads made from differently pretreated HB. The FIB and MW groups showed minimal separation, suggesting similar flavor profiles, likely due to comparable starch gelatinization degrees and rapid uniform heating without surface scorching, leading to analogous volatile compound profiles.

Starch digestibility of HB bread

In vitro digestion

The in vitro starch digestion curves for breads made from HB flour with different pretreatments are shown in Fig. 6A. All hydrolysis curves exhibited a typical exponential growth shape: starch was rapidly hydrolyzed within the first 30 min, reaching a plateau slowly after 120 min. This indicates that bread digestion follows first-order kinetics. The maximum starch hydrolysis extent for standard white bread (SWB) was approximately 95%, likely due to the gelatinization of starch granules during baking, which disrupts all levels of starch supramolecular structure, including crystalline and lamellar structures (Wang et al., 2021). In contrast to SWB, all HB breads exhibited an overall reduction in starch hydrolysis extent (ranging from approximately 45% to 68%), with the order being CK > TS > MW > FIB > SE. As expected, the CK group showed the highest digestibility, as the native intact starch structure was most susceptible to enzymatic hydrolysis. This effect is associated with the inhibition of starch-digesting enzyme activity by β-glucan and dietary fiber present in the HB (Ren et al., 2022). Notably, the SE group exhibited the lowest maximum hydrolysis extent (45.36%). SE maximizes the release of β-glucan, which forms a denser gel network within the bread. This gel effectively encapsulates starch granules, hindering enzymatic access. TS was second only to the blank group in starch digestibility. The high-temperature dehydration effect during TS might cause surface hardening of starch granules, creating a physical barrier. However, although TS involves high temperatures, its brief duration may not alter the starch structure as profoundly as SE. MW treatment is distinct. Its rapid internal heating generates “hot spots,” leading to localized over-gelatinization of starch. Upon cooling, these over-gelatinized regions can recrystallize, forming more resistant starch that is harder to digest. Nevertheless, the non-uniform nature of MW heating places its effectiveness between TS and FIB. FIB radiation has strong penetrability enabling uniform starch gelatinization; the ordered structures formed upon cooling increase resistant starch content, with lower effectiveness than SE possibly due to the lack of high pressure for effective cellulose disruption.

Further presentation and discussion focused on the RDS, SDS, and RS contents (Table 5). The SWB exhibited the highest RDS content at 67.18%. In contrast, all HB bread groups showed significantly reduced RDS levels, with the MW group having the lowest at 29.98%. Regarding SDS, all HB bread groups except the SE group showed increased SDS levels. The TS group contained the highest SDS content at 36.37%. Analysis of RS revealed that SWB contained very little resistant starch (only 6.19%), indicating its rapid digestibility. Conversely, the HB groups displayed a marked increase in RS content, ranging from 20.01% to 41.78%. The FIB group had the highest RS content at 41.78%. These results were consistent with previous research that indigestible insoluble dietary fiber restricts starch swelling and gelatinization, thus hindering in vitro starch digestion and glucose release (Huang et al., 2024). In the CK group, the starch undergoes completed gelatinization during the baking process, so most of the starch became digestible starch (RDS + SDS), while the RS primarily came from the inherent resistant starch naturally present in HB. The TS group showed an increase in SDS, indicating that during pretreatment, the gelatinization of some starch granules led to the unraveling of molecular chains. In the subsequent cooling and bread-making processes, these unraveled molecular chains had more opportunities to form orderly but less densely packed crystalline structures. These structures cause enzymes to act slowly on them, resulting in the highest proportion of SDS. The physical disruption caused by SE provided ample space for the rearrangement of starch molecules (especially amylose), forming a large number of digestion-resistant crystalline structures (RS). Intense MW energy disrupted the original structure of the starch granules, causing a massive leaching of amylose. During subsequent rapid cooling and storage process, massive amylose leached; the amylose intertwined to form dense, stable RS upon rapid cooling, which resisted enzymatic hydrolysis. Similarly, the thorough and uniform gelatinization of HB starch by FIB created ideal conditions for the formation of extensive, uniform, and dense resistant starch during the subsequent cooling process. Additionally, the lower specific volume of MW and FIB breads might be linked to reduce in vitro digestibility, as the denser crumb structure impeded α-amylase access and action.

LOS plots and the number of digestible fractions and CPS kinetics fittings

To further quantify the in vitro digestibility differences among breads made from differently pretreated HB flour, the LOS and CPS kinetics models were applied to fit the digestion curves. In the LOS curve (Fig. 6B-E), the components are termed rapidly digestible fraction (RDF) and slowly digestible fraction (SDF). The CPS kinetics model revealed that RDF and SDF digestion do not commence simultaneously but exhibit a distinct overlapping region. This indicates that the digestion of RDF and SDF follows a combined parallel and sequential pattern (Li & Hu, 2020). It could be seen in Table 5, the rate constant for RDF (k₁) is approximately 4 times higher than that for SDF (k₂). The maximum digestibility extent (C1∞) for RDF exceeded that for SDF (C2∞) in both the SWB and the SE group. Conversely, in the remaining groups (CK, TS, MW, FIB), the maximum digestibility extent for RDF (C1∞) was lower than that for SDF (C2∞). hile the SDF digestion rate constant (k₂) showed no significant trend. This suggests that bread made with HB flour primarily alters the digestion rate of the rapidly digestible fraction. Furthermore, the maximum digestibility extents for both RDF (C1∞) and SDF(C2∞) were higher in the SWB compared to the HB groups. Additionally, the maximum digestibility of RDF (C1∞) and SDF (C2∞) in the untreated group was higher than that in the other treatment groups. This indicates that appropriate pretreatment of HB can effectively slow down the in vitro starch digestion rate (Cao et al., 2022).

eGI of HB bread

The results show that the eGI of untreated HB bread was 75.41, classifying it as a high-GI food. Among the four pretreated groups (Table 5), the eGI values ranked from high to low as follows: TS > SE > MW > FIB. The slow in vitro starch hydrolysis of HB was associated with intrinsic components (proteins, lipids, β-glucan), which formed complex matrices with starch and altered in vitro starch digestibility. Notably, the breads from the SE, MW, and FIB treatment groups achieved medium-GI status, with the FIB group exhibiting the lowest eGI at 63.48. In the CK group, the intact starch granules (possessing an A-type crystalline structure) provide dense sites for enzymatic cleavage. TS generated a large amount of rapidly digestible starch, while the dicarbonyl compounds formed via the Maillard reaction covalently bound to α-amylase amino acid residues and inhibiting enzyme activity and thus reducing the in vitro starch hydrolysis rate (Wu et al., 2023). SE promoted β-glucan release, which forms a physical barrier to delay in vitro starch hydrolysis (Liu et al., 2025a). Furthermore, the resistant starch generated by MW treatment likely exerts a stronger influence on blood glucose than the amylose-lipid complexes formed during FIB processing.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the comprehensive effects of four pretreatment methods (TS, SE, MW and FIB), all controlled at a fixed starch gelatinization endpoint of 50%, on the rheological properties, protein secondary structure, and microstructure of HB dough, as well as the volatile flavor profile, starch digestibility characteristics, and in vitro eGI of the resulting bread. The results indicated that all four pretreatments differentially influenced dough properties by modifying water mobility and altering thermodynamic and rheological behaviors: the MW group exhibited the strongest water-binding capacity (A23 = 1.30), while the FIB group showed the highest G' and G" with the lowest tan δ value. These rheological changes might be attributed to the formation of a more compact starch structure in the FIB-treated dough, which was accompanied by the highest RS content (up to 41.78%) and the lowest gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH = 0.29 J/g). Regarding protein secondary structure, the treatments promoted a reduction in β-sheets and an increase in α-helix in gluten proteins, enhancing hydrophobic interactions, with elevated β-turn proportions in MW and FIB groups indicating reduced protein aggregation due to flexible side-chain rearrangement after unfolding. Microstructural observations confirmed these changes, revealing a loosened gluten network (particularly evident as fissures in the MW group), though SE and FIB treatments maintained relatively uniform protein-starch encapsulation. In terms of bread quality, all pretreatments improved the hardness, elasticity, and chewiness, although the magnitude of these changes varied among methods. The FIB treatment exhibited relatively favorable overall textural performance under the conditions studied. In addition, SE treatment significantly enhanced the characteristic bread flavor compounds, particularly alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones. Regarding starch digestibility, pretreatments effectively reduced RDS content and increased RS content. Notably, SE treatment achieved a 34% reduction in the maximum starch hydrolysis degree, and kinetic modeling revealed that the starch digestion of all HB breads followed a dual-component parallel-sequential digestion pattern. The eGI values of SE, MW, and FIB groups were reduced to medium-GI levels, confirming their potential for developing diabetes-friendly food products. This study provides a solid theoretical foundations and practical processing pathways for the targeted development of functional HB bread.
