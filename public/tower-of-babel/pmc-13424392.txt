Heterogeneous longitudinal trajectories of sense of coherence and associated characteristics among newly diagnosed HIV-positive men who have sex with men: A cohort study
Authors: Mimi Zhai, Yamin Li, Jianhai Long, Yunxia Li, Xianyang Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify heterogeneous longitudinal trajectories of sense of coherence (SOC) among newly diagnosed HIV-positive men who have sex with men (MSM) and examine associated characteristics and factors across different trajectories.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted by recruiting newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM from a tertiary hospital and a social work service center in Changsha, China. Follow-up surveys were administered at baseline (T1), 3 months (T2), and 6 months (T3). Data were collected on SOC, HIV stigma, perceived stress, depression, internalized homophobia, antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence, comfort in accessing healthcare services, coping strategies, social support, and sociodemographic and sexual behavior characteristics. A latent class mixed model (LCMM) was fitted in R to identify SOC trajectories. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine predictors of trajectory class membership, and psychosocial correlates of SOC were analyzed within each trajectory class.ResultsA total of 313 participants were included. Overall SOC declined significantly over the 6-month follow-up (F = 38.78, P < 0.001). LCMM identified four SOC trajectories: Continuously Low (13.10 %), Slow Decline (48.24 %), Rapid Decline (25.24 %), and Active Transition (13.42 %). Significant between-trajectory differences were observed in household registration (P = 0.016), educational level (P = 0.025), monthly income (P = 0.021), age at first sexual activity (H = 7.11, P = 0.041), and condom use during anal intercourse (P = 0.008). Using the Active Transition group as the reference, rural household registration increased the likelihood of belonging to the Slow Decline group (Coef = 1.02, P = 0.001). A lower educational level increased the likelihood of belonging to the Continuously Low group (Coef = −0.84, P = 0.026). Earlier age at first sexual activity (Coef = −0.24, P = 0.025) and a greater number of male partners (Coef = 0.37, P = 0.028) were associated with the Continuously Low group. Across trajectories, HIV stigma, perceived stress, and depression were negatively associated with SOC, with perceived stress showing a relatively stronger negative association. Social support was positively associated with SOC in the Continuously Low group (Coef = 0.78, P = 0.013). In the Slow Decline group, ART adherence (Coef = 0.76, P < 0.001) and comfort in accessing healthcare services (Coef = 0.88, P < 0.001) were positively associated with SOC.ConclusionSOC among newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM declined significantly during the early post-diagnosis period and exhibited marked trajectory heterogeneity. Early trajectory-stratified interventions are warranted, particularly for the Continuously Low and Rapid Decline groups, to help sustain psychological resources and improve health outcomes.Keywords: Human immunodeficiency virus, Latent class mixed model, Men who have sex with men, Nursing, Prospective cohort study, Sense of coherence

Introduction

Despite progress in global human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) prevention and control, HIV infection remains a major challenge to public health [1]. According to Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), in 2024, approximately 40.8 million people were living with HIV worldwide [2], and men who have sex with men (MSM), as a key population, face about 28-fold higher risk of HIV infection than the general male population [3]. In China, the proportion of newly diagnosed HIV cases attributed to MSM has increased from 10.5 % in 2011 to 24.0 % in 2024, underscoring the urgent need to focus on this population in current prevention and control efforts [4].

Newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM face not only the health threats posed by the disease itself but also multiple stressors related to HIV diagnosis and sexual minority status [5,6]. These psychological burdens are particularly severe in the early stages following diagnosis. They may trigger serious mental health problems such as anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation, which directly affect treatment adherence and long-term outcomes [[7], [8], [9]]. Therefore, it is of great importance to explore the developmental trajectories of mental health in this group and identify influencing factors to guide precision interventions and promote long-term health.

To address these challenges, research perspectives should shift from the traditional pathogenesis model to the Salutogenesis Theory, which emphasizes the origins and maintenance of health. The core concept of this framework is the sense of coherence (SOC). This global orientation reflects individuals’ capacity to recognize and integrate available resources to maintain health in the face of stressors. SOC consists of three dimensions: comprehensibility (the tendency to perceive internal and external stimuli as ordered and predictable), manageability (the belief that sufficient resources are available to meet demands), and meaningfulness (the motivational perspective that life’s challenges are worth investing energy in) [10]. Evidence shows that higher levels of SOC serve as an important psychological protective factor, associated with better psychological adaptation, greater treatment adherence, and more positive long-term outcomes [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. However, most existing studies on SOC among people living with HIV have adopted cross-sectional designs, overlooking the complexity of psychological adaptation and individual differences. There is a lack of systematic evidence on the longitudinal changes and heterogeneity of SOC in newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM. Longitudinal research is urgently needed to clarify SOC trajectories and identify distinct subgroups.

This study introduces the Latent Class Mixed Model (LCMM) to newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM, aiming to identify heterogeneous longitudinal trajectories of SOC. Unlike traditional analyses, LCMM allows the identification of latent subgroups within longitudinal follow-up data, revealing different developmental patterns and more accurately reflecting individual differences and population heterogeneity. This approach enables early detection of psychologically vulnerable subgroups and supports the design of tailored, precision interventions. Therefore, in a six-month prospective cohort study, we applied LCMM to identify heterogeneous SOC trajectories among newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM and to examine the associated sociodemographic, behavioral, and psychosocial characteristics, as well as factors influencing different trajectory groups, providing empirical evidence for the establishment of stratified, evidence-based psychological intervention systems.

Methods

Study design

This prospective cohort study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [15].

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted at a tertiary hospital and a social work service center in Changsha, China. Participants were recruited from HIV-related clinical and community service settings. Inclusion criteria were: 1) age ≥18 years; 2) male; 3) having engaged in sexual activity with men in the past 6 months; 4) confirmed HIV diagnosis by the local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention within the past 12 months; and 5) having a stable place of residence. Exclusion criteria were: 1) prior diagnosis of severe psychiatric or psychological disorders; 2) refusal to participate; 3) no access to a mobile phone or the internet; and 4) inability to complete study assessments for personal reasons. Sample size was estimated using pilot data from a preliminary survey conducted by our research team, together with parameters reported in prior studies [16,17]. In this pilot survey, mean SOC scores at baseline (T1), 3 months (T2), and 6 months (T3) were 55.20 ± 15.66, 52.60 ± 15.31, and 48.50 ± 17.68, respectively, with an inter-measurement correlation coefficient of 0.70. With α = 0.05 and power (1−β) = 0.80, PASS 15.0.5 estimated a required sample size of 243. In addition, simulation evidence suggests that at least 200 participants are generally required for stable class enumeration in latent class/growth mixture models [18]. Therefore, the larger estimate (n = 243) was used. Allowing for 20 % attrition, the target sample size was set at 304.

Data collection, follow-up, and quality control

At enrollment (baseline, T1), eligible participants provided written informed consent after receiving full information about the study purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and risks. Participants then completed assessments including sociodemographic/clinical variables, the primary outcome, and secondary psychosocial/behavioral measures.

Follow-up time points were selected to capture early psychological adaptation after HIV diagnosis, informed by prior literature [5,19]. Assessments were conducted at 3 months (T2) and 6 months (T3) after enrollment. Data were collected either on-site or online, depending on participants’ circumstances. Investigators received standardized training and signed confidentiality agreements. Questionnaires were checked for completeness upon submission, and participants were contacted for clarification if necessary. Two researchers independently performed double data entry, and discrepancies were resolved by referring to the original records. A questionnaire was considered valid if the primary outcome (SOC) and pre-specified key covariates were completed and passed completeness and internal logic checks. The final dataset was verified before analysis.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical data

Data for the study were collected from December 2022 to July 2023. A structured questionnaire was used to collect age, household registration, marital status, education level, employment status, monthly income, health insurance type, sexual orientation, and disclosure of sexual identity. At each assessment, trained research staff guided participants to retrieve their most recent CD4+ T-cell count via the hospital’s official mobile portal (the “report inquiry” function within the WeChat public account) and recorded the value on site; assistance was provided when necessary. When immediate access was not feasible, the value was recorded as self-reported.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was SOC, assessed using the 13-item Sense of Coherence Scale (SOC-13) [20]. The SOC-13 measures three core dimensions: comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 13 to 91, with higher scores indicating a stronger SOC. The Chinese version demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.76 [21].

Secondary outcomes

The selection of secondary outcomes was guided by the Minority Stress Model and Salutogenesis Theory, together with evidence from previous research [20,22]. All study instruments were internationally recognized scales or validated item sets widely used in non-commercial academic research, and were administered in accordance with the guidelines of their original developers.

(1)Minority stress-related factors included HIV stigma, internalized homophobia, and comfort in accessing healthcare services. HIV stigma was assessed using the 12-item short version of the HIV Stigma Scale [23], covering personalized stigma, disclosure concerns, negative self-image, and concerns with public attitudes. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater stigma. The Chinese version reported construct validity (KMO = 0.81) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) in MSM living with HIV [24]. Internalized homophobia was measured using the Internalized Homophobia Scale developed for gay Chinese men [25]. The scale includes 11 items (with reverse-scored items) across three dimensions, rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher internalized homophobia. In Chinese applications, the scale demonstrated internal consistency with Cronbach’s α = 0.82 [26]. Comfort in accessing healthcare services was assessed using the Comfort in Accessing Health Care Services Scale [27]. In Chinese studies, the scale was reduced to 6 items and rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4), with higher total scores indicating greater comfort. The Chinese version demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.85 [24].

Statistical analysis

Data were managed in Excel 2021 and analyzed using R (v4.3.0) and Stata (v17.0). Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test [37]. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD when approximately normally distributed and as median (P25, P75) otherwise. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. For between-group comparisons, independent-samples t tests or one-way ANOVA were used for approximately normally distributed variables; Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis H tests were used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared using χ2 tests or exact tests when expected cell counts were small. Selective attrition bias was evaluated by comparing baseline characteristics between participants retained and those lost to follow-up. For missing non-primary repeated-measure variables (excluding SOC), linear interpolation was applied when adjacent time-point data were available; otherwise, multiple imputation was used. Missing baseline covariates were handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption [38,39]. Longitudinal SOC trajectories were identified using latent class mixed modeling (LCMM) implemented in the lcmm package in R [40]. Model selection considered Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), log-likelihood, and entropy (with values closer to 1 indicating clearer separation), together with interpretability and avoidance of very small latent classes [41,42]. Missing SOC outcome data were not imputed before modeling; instead, they were handled within the LCMM framework using maximum likelihood estimation under the missing-at-random assumption [40]. Predictors of trajectory class membership were examined using multinomial logistic regression [43]. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Nursing and Behavioral Medicine at the School of Nursing, Central South University (E2022212).

Results

Participant characteristics

The baseline characteristics of 313 newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM were presented in Appendix A Tables S1 and S2. The median age was 24 (22, 28) years. Among the participants, 53.04 % were from rural areas, 61.66 % had a college or undergraduate education, and 19.17 % held an education level higher than undergraduate. The majority of participants were unmarried. Regarding sexual behavior, 73.80 % identified as homosexual, 26.52 % had disclosed their sexual orientation, and 41.81 % reported consistent condom use during anal intercourse. The median age at first sexual activity was 18 (18, 20) years , and 52.26 % of the participants had a regular partner. Regarding the medical payment method, Uninsured self-pay accounted for the largest share (23.00 %), followed by insured self-pay (22.68 %). Comparison of key baseline study variables between participants who completed the follow-up and those who were lost to follow-up revealed no statistically significant differences (Appendix A Table S3).

Dynamic changes in sense of coherence and psychosocial indicators

SOC and its dimensions—comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness—declined significantly over time (Table 1). The mean SOC score decreased from 56.17 ± 14.30 at baseline (T1) to 52.89 ± 12.40 at 3 months (T2), and further to 47.05 ± 12.59 at 6 months (T3) (F = 38.78, P < 0.001). Specifically, the comprehensibility score dropped from 21.01 ± 5.93 to 17.71 ± 4.96 (F = 32.08, P < 0.001), the manageability score from 21.70 ± 6.21 to 17.20 ± 5.83 (F = 45.57, P < 0.001), and the meaningfulness score from 13.46 ± 3.52 to 12.14 ± 3.10 (F = 13.90, P < 0.001).

Psychosocial indicators exhibited varying trends over time (Table 1). The median HIV stigma score increased from 34.00 (31.00, 38.00) at T1 to 39.00 (34.00, 42.00) at T3 (P < 0.001). Both stress and internalized homophobia also increased, with the mean stress score rising from 18.24 ± 6.20 to 19.72 ± 3.91 (P = 0.003) and the internalized homophobia score increasing from 34.59 ± 8.18 to 38.20 ± 7.11 (P < 0.001). Additionally, the median depression score increased from 9.00 (5.00, 13.00) to 13.00 (8.00, 17.00) (P < 0.001). In contrast, antiretroviral therapy adherence declined from a median of 16.00 (13.00, 16.00) to 10.00 (8.00, 14.00) (P < 0.001), and self-identity decreased from 78.59 ± 15.53 to 71.97 ± 17.00 (P < 0.001). Social support remained stable (P = 0.161).

The sense of coherence sub-trajectories and their characteristics

Using LCMM, four heterogeneous trajectories of sense of coherence were identified (Appendix A Table S4; Appendix B Fig. S1): Continuously Low, Slow Decline, Rapid Decline, and Active Transition. Among these, the Slow Decline group (48.24 %) was the largest subgroup and was characterized by a gradual decline in SOC, whereas the Rapid Decline group (25.24 %) showed the greatest mean decrease and a descriptively unfavorable change pattern (Appendix A Table S7; Appendix B Fig. S2). Continuously Low (13.10 %) remained at a low level throughout, with minimal change over time. Active Transition (13.42 %) initially increased at T2, then dipped slightly at T3, but overall remained at a relatively high level. Significant differences in baseline sociodemographic and sexual behavior characteristics were observed among the different sub-trajectories (Appendix A Table S5–S6). For instance, the Rapid Decline group had an urban household registration rate of 49.37 %, while higher education levels were more prevalent in the Slow Decline (63.57 %) and Active Transition (66.67 %) groups. Additionally, the distribution of condom use during anal intercourse differed significantly among the four groups (P = 0.008). Differences in psychosocial indicators across trajectory groups at T1, T2, and T3 are presented in Appendix A Table S8–S10.

Factors influencing the sub-trajectories

With the Active Transition group used as the reference, the results indicated that rural household registration (Coef = 1.02, P = 0.001) and lower educational level (Coef = −0.84, P = 0.026) increased the likelihood of participants belonging to the Slow Decline group and the Continuously Low group, respectively (Table 2). Sexual behavior-related factors, such as an earlier age at first sexual activity (Coef = −0.24, P = 0.025) and a higher number of male partners (Coef = 0.37, P = 0.028), were associated with the Continuously Low group. Moreover, psychosocial factors (Table 3) indicated that social support (Coef = 0.78, P = 0.013) had a positive impact on SOC in the Continuously Low group. In contrast, HIV stigma, stress, and depression adversely affected SOC across all groups. In the Slow Decline group, ART adherence (Coef = 0.76, P < 0.001) and comfort in accessing healthcare services (Coef = 0.88, P < 0.001) were positive predictors.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the few to apply LCMM to characterize SOC trajectories in newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM, identifying four heterogeneous trajectories: Continuously Low, Slow Decline, Rapid Decline, and Active Transition. This approach overcomes the limitations of traditional mean-based approaches, which obscure individual variability, and empirically demonstrates significant SOC heterogeneity within 6 months post-diagnosis. It provides a theoretical foundation for precisely identifying high-risk groups and developing personalized, stratified interventions.

The baseline SOC level among newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM was low, lower than the SOC means of elderly adults [44], nurses [45], patients with permanent colostomies [46], and patients with malignant tumors [47], and continues to decline over six months. This may stem from the “dual stigma” associated with sexual minority status and HIV infection, discomfort caused by treatment side effects, uncertainty about disease prognosis, and a lack of social support during the early post-diagnosis phase. Compared with previous studies in people living with HIV [48,49], newly diagnosed MSM appeared to have lower SOC, possibly owing to acute post-diagnosis stress and sexual minority-related pressures. Notably, 22.68 % of all participants had health insurance but opted for self-funded treatment, possibly because of concerns about identity disclosure, which may have exacerbated their psychological burden. The study also identified a knowledge-behavior discrepancy: although the participants were primarily young and highly educated, and 93.59 % reported avoiding drug use before sexual activity, sexual behaviors associated with a higher risk of HIV transmission remained prevalent among newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM. As a comprehensive indicator integrating “comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness,” SOC effectively captures the multifaceted social, psychological, and behavioral challenges faced by this population, providing a scientific tool for comprehensively assessing their mental health.

The LCMM analysis revealed heterogeneity in SOC trajectories and their associated factors. The Rapid Decline group had the highest baseline SOC but experienced the largest decrease, possibly due to post-diagnosis disruptions in life or insufficient coping resources, leading to heightened vulnerability. The Continuously Low group maintained persistently low SOC with minimal change, associated with lower educational attainment and lower condom-use rates, reflecting the cumulative impact of long-term resource scarcity and high-risk behaviors. The Slow Decline group exhibited a gradual decrease in SOC from a moderate level, indicating limited coping capacity. The Active Transition group showed an increase in SOC at three months—especially in manageability and comprehensibility—which may reflect a short-term salutogenic adaptation after diagnosis: SOC is conceptually defined as a global orientation that shapes stressor appraisal and supports the mobilization of generalized resistance resources (e.g., information, professional support, and social support) [13]. Specifically, early linkage to care and counseling may increase access to informational and relational resources, thereby strengthening perceived manageability and comprehensibility. However, as initial external supports attenuate and chronic stressors (e.g., stigma and financial strain) persist, sustaining these resources may become difficult, which could explain the slight decline by six months. Significant baseline differences were observed across trajectories: higher education levels were more prevalent in the Active Transition and Slow Decline groups, and household registration also differed across groups. About sexual behavior, condom use during anal intercourse differed significantly across SOC trajectories, indicating behavioral heterogeneity among the four groups. Female sexual partners were more common in the Continuously Low and Rapid Decline groups, potentially exacerbating psychological burden because of the pressure to conceal sexual orientation.

Multiple associated factors, including sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behaviors, and psychosocial factors such as HIV stigma, social support, stress, and depression, influenced SOC trajectory classification. Higher levels of education are associated with the Active Transition group, likely due to greater access to psychological resources or more effective coping strategies. Fewer male sexual partners and consistent condom use are also linked to the Active Transition group, possibly because MSM engaging in safer sexual behaviors typically exhibit higher self-efficacy [50], which is positively correlated with SOC [51]. Antiretroviral therapy adherence, comfort with healthcare services, and social support were significantly associated with higher SOC in some trajectory groups.

Conversely, HIV stigma, stress, and depression were negatively associated with SOC across all trajectory groups, whereas internalized homophobia and negative coping strategies were negatively associated with SOC in some groups. These findings suggest that education and social support serve as protective factors, while stigma and stress are pervasive barriers to psychological resilience. Accordingly, evidence-based psychosocial protocols tested in HIV care (e.g., manualized cognitive–behavioral therapy for adherence and depression) and structured peer-support interventions may be prioritized for vulnerable trajectories (especially the Rapid Decline group) to strengthen coping resources and reduce stigma-related barriers [52,53].

This study also observed a phenomenon in medical decision-making: 22.68 % of all participants had health insurance but chose self-funded treatment. This phenomenon likely stemmed from privacy concerns, as participants may have attempted to avoid disclosing their HIV status through insurance reimbursement. Such behavior may increase psychological burden, as it reflects internalized stigma and fear of discrimination, which may intensify stress responses and negatively affect SOC. This impact may be particularly pronounced in vulnerable trajectory groups such as the Rapid Decline group. Future interventions should address this issue by strengthening privacy protection measures within the healthcare system, thereby enhancing the psychological resilience and sustained care capacity of people living with HIV.

This study has important implications for clinical practice by highlighting the heterogeneity of SOC trajectories and their associated characteristics. Early screening should prioritize the Rapid Decline and Continuously Low groups to implement interventions that alleviate psychological distress. Based on our findings, we recommend stratified interventions for newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM. For the Continuously Low and Rapid Decline groups, early structured psychological treatment may be considered. For example, cognitive–behavioral therapy for adherence and depression (CBT-AD) is a manualized protocol tested in HIV care and is typically delivered weekly over ∼10–12 sessions, integrating behavioral activation, cognitive restructuring, and problem-solving around adherence and stress management [54]. For the Active Transition group, the early improvement phase may represent an actionable window; thus, consolidation strategies could include tapering support and a planned booster session after the core intervention (e.g., a booster delivered several weeks after completion in problem-solving therapy–based adherence/depression programs), alongside continued linkage to peer-support resources [55]. These are evidence-informed options drawn from HIV psychosocial trials and should be evaluated in future trajectory-stratified studies. Given that MSM are generally younger and have higher levels of education, digital follow-ups, anonymous psychological service platforms, and peer support mechanisms can further enhance the accessibility and effectiveness of interventions. At the policy level, strengthening privacy protections in health insurance and improving the healthcare experience are essential to reducing stigma and optimizing access to mental healthcare.

The strengths of this study lie in its application of the LCMM in HIV mental health research, its systematic characterization of the dynamic heterogeneity of SOC, and its overcoming of the limitations of traditional averaged analyses. Additionally, it captures the dynamic changes in SOC among newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM, revealing psychological stress characteristics in the early diagnostic phase and providing a foundation for precise interventions. Finally, by integrating sociodemographic, sexual behavior, and psychosocial factors, the study constructs a refined, actionable framework for stratified interventions, enhancing the practical value of the findings. However, this study has several limitations. First, convenience sampling may limit the representativeness of the findings, making it difficult to reflect SOC trajectories in the broader MSM population fully. Second, reliance on self-reported data may introduce subjective bias. Third, although the study was conducted at a tertiary hospital and a social work service center in Changsha—facilitating standardized data collection and follow-up—differences across regions and healthcare settings may limit generalizability. Fourth, although our multinomial logistic regression controlled for several key sociodemographic and other variables, certain clinical confounders were not included in the primary model, potentially introducing bias. Lastly, the 6-month follow-up period limits the ability to observe long-term SOC trends. These limitations suggest that future research should adopt multi-center designs with longer follow-up, incorporate objective clinical data, and implement more comprehensive confounder adjustment to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, SOC among newly diagnosed HIV-positive MSM showed a significant decline and marked heterogeneity in the early stages following HIV diagnosis, influenced by the interplay of associated sociodemographic, sexual behavior, and psychosocial characteristics. The trajectory stratification driven by the LCMM provides a theoretical foundation for precise screening and personalized interventions.
