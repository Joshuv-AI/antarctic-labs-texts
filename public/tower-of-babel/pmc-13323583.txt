Minimal Fibrinolysis After Acute Intracerebral Hemorrhage Undercuts the Mechanism of Tranexamic Acid
Authors: Juliana Silva Pinheiro do Nascimento, Kaleigh Copenhaver, Rajeev Garg, Fernando Goldenberg, Tiffany Chang, Harish Shownkeen, Brett Faine, Paul F. Lindholm, James Grotta, Andrew M. Naidech
Published: 2026

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a severe form of stroke associated with high mortality and long‐term disability. Hematoma expansion, growth of the hematoma on repeated computed tomography scans, is a preventable cause of poor outcome. Tranexamic acid (TXA), an antifibrinolytic agent, has been tested in clinical trials as a potential strategy to reduce hematoma expansion based on its success in trauma settings. 1

Several prospective, randomized trials of TXA have been conducted in patients with acute ICH. However, the efficacy of TXA has been less than hoped. While TXA was associated with reduced odds of hematoma expansion in meta‐analysis of patients at high risk for hematoma expansion attributable to a positive spot sign, 2 individual trials have had mixed results for reducing hematoma expansion and no evidence of improved functional outcomes at follow‐up.

We conducted a prospective, multicenter observational study of coagulation status, including thromboelastography, in patients with acute ICH. As part of that study, we provide a potential mechanistic explanation for the attenuated efficacy of TXA in patients with acute ICH.

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Participants were enrolled in 6 medical centers in the United States: University of Texas at Houston, University of Iowa in Iowa City, and 4 hospitals in greater Chicago—Northwestern Memorial Hospital, Northwestern Medicine Central DuPage Hospital, Rush University Medical Center, and University of Chicago. Eligible patients were adults aged 21 years or older who presented with radiologically confirmed spontaneous ICH. Exclusion criteria included a history of malignancy, vascular malformation, or sepsis, as well as prior pharmacologic interventions affecting coagulation (eg, desmopressin administration or platelet transfusion) before sample collection. The study protocol was approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient or a legally authorized representative (eg, spouse).

Whole blood samples were collected and analyzed with thromboelastography (TEG 6s, Haemonetics Co., Braintree, MA). TEG 6s is a point‐of‐care assay that assesses the contributions of coagulation factors, fibrinogen, platelets, and fibrinolysis to clot formation and stability. The assay uses resonance frequency technology to detect changes in clot viscoelasticity within a microfluidic cartridge. Fibrinolysis was measured using LY30, defined as the percentage of clot lysis 30 minutes after maximum clot strength, with values >8% considered hyperfibrinolytic.

Neuroimaging was performed according to standard clinical protocols at each participating site, including a baseline head computed tomography scan at presentation and follow‐up computed tomography imaging per routine clinical care. Hematoma volumes were measured using a semi‐automated voxel measurement tool with the AnalyzeDirect software (version 14.0; AnalyzeDirect, Inc., Overland Park, KS). Hematoma expansion was calculated as the absolute difference in hematoma volume between baseline and first available follow‐up computed tomography imaging.

Hyperfibrinolysis was rare in the cohort. The median LY30 value was 0.1% [interquartile range, 0.0%–0.6%]. Overall, 107 patients (97.27%) had LY30 values <3%, and 87 patients (79.09%) had LY30 values <1%. Only 1 patient (0.9%) exhibited hyperfibrinolysis (LY30=16%), and this patient took rivaroxaban; the only other patient on an anticoagulant pre‐ICH had LY30=0.2%.

In a multicenter, prospectively enrolled cohort, we found that patients with acute ICH had little fibrinolysis to pharmacologically inhibit. Other hemostatic treatments are associated with improvement in hemostatic biomarkers (eg, prothrombin complex concentrates normalized the International Normalized Ratio, Andexanet alpha normalizes Factor X activity), so normal hemostatic biomarkers of fibrinolysis suggest there will be a limit on the potential efficacy of TXA. 3 , 4 Accordingly, TXA may have attenuated efficacy in acute ICH because fibrinolysis is rare.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of the studied population. Patients in this cohort largely had mild to moderate hemorrhage severity, relatively modest hematoma volumes, and TEG was performed at a median of approximately 6 hours after symptom onset. There were 2 patients on anticoagulants, and 1 of them accounted for an outlier of fibrinolysis. As such, the results may not readily generalize beyond the clinical phenotypes represented in this cohort.

Other groups have suggested active fibrinolysis values warrant the use of anti‐fibrinolytic medication in patients with trauma. However, patients who present with trauma have a higher propensity for fibrinolysis. TXA may be more appropriate for trauma than spontaneous ICH.

Despite promising results in surgery and trauma, reducing fibrinolysis seems unlikely to be a successful path forward for the treatment of acute ICH. For patients who do not require reversal of a specific anticoagulant taken before ICH, a treatment that more broadly activates coagulation or targets a specific, deficient hemostatic mechanism (eg, platelet activity) might be more likely to be effective. 5
