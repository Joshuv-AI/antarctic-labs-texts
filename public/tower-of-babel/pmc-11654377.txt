Cost-effectiveness analysis of AI-based image quality control for perinatal ultrasound screening
Authors: Yihan Tan, Yulin Peng, Liangyu Guo, Dongmei Liu, Yingchun Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to compare the cost-effectiveness of AI-based approaches with manual approaches in ultrasound image quality control (QC).MethodsEligible ultrasonographers and pregnant volunteers were prospectively recruited from the Hunan Maternal and Child Health Hospital in May 2023. The ultrasonographers were randomly and evenly assigned to either the AI or Manual QC groups with baseline scores determined in June-July. From August to October, these groups received real-time AI or post-scan manual QC with post-interventional scores recorded monthly. We applied the repeated measures analysis of variance to analyze the between-subject and within-subject effectiveness and time trends in effectiveness (QC score improvement) assessment. An extra 50 pregnant volunteers underwent real-time manual QC, with their screening images utilized for post-scan AI and manual QC. The time cost of real-time AI QC was zero since it only required trainees’ involvement. We used Friedman’s M and Quade tests to compare multiple independent medians in cost assessment.ResultsThis study recruited 14 ultrasonographers, equally divided into the AI and Manual QC groups. No significant difference existed between the groups concerning age, service year in perinatal diagnosis, male proportion, and QC frequency. The simple effect of the group revealed that the AI QC method outperformed the Manual QC method at least once (F = 13.113, P = 0.004, η2 = 0.522). The simple effect of the month in the AI QC groups indicated an improvement in the mean QC scores (F = 9.827, P = 0.003, η2 = 0.747) while that of manual QC groups suggested no improvement (F = 0.144, P = 0.931, η2 = 0.041). Baseline scores were equal in June-July (F = 0.031, P = 0.864, η2 = 0.003). However, the AI QC group surpassed the Manual QC group in August (F = 14.579, P = 0.002, η2 = 0.549), September (F = 28.590, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.704), and October (F = 35.411, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.747). Within the Manual QC group, no significant differences were found in scores between June-July and August, September, or October (all P values of 1.000, nominal significance level of 0.0083). In contrast, the AI QC group showed significantly higher scores in August, September, and October compared to June-July (all P values of 0.001, nominal significance level of 0.0083). The time costs of real-time AI QC, post-scan AI QC, post-scan manual QC, and real-time manual QC were 0 s, 13.76 s (interquartile range, IQR: 4.79–46.79 s), 1239.50 s (IQR: 1141.00–1311.25 s), and 1541.00 s (IQR: 1453.50–1635.25 s), with significant differences in both overall and multiple comparisons.ConclusionsThe AI QC method, more cost-effective than the manual method, shows great potential for application in image QC scenarios. The AI QC can enhance operators’ skills in perinatal ultrasound screening, while the manual method can only maintain the existing level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06477-w.Keywords: Ultrasonography, prenatal; Artificial intelligence; Education, medical, continuing; Cost-benefit analysis; Quality control

Introduction

Ultrasound prenatal screening (UPS), an integral component of the tertiary prevention strategy for birth defects [1], plays a crucial role in effectively identifying severe fetal congenital abnormalities [2]. It alleviates the societal and familial burden associated with such conditions, underscoring its pivotal clinical and socioeconomic significance [3]. Experts have developed multiple editions of UPS guidelines [4–7] to improve the reliability and validity of UPS [8] and further actively promote corresponding training programs [3].

Current UPS training primarily focuses on beginners but neglects the importance of image quality control (QC), specifically the continuing education of experienced ultrasonographers [9–11]. Traditional methods for prenatal ultrasound image QC mainly include real-time and post-scan manual QC. The former provides immediate feedback on trainees’ errors, theoretically yielding better results. However, it requires qualified ultrasonographers with QC expertise to supervise trainees, which thus reduces efficiency. Consequently, this method is more suitable for training beginners rather than as a routine QC approach. The post-scan manual QC maximizes human resource utilization by not occupying experienced ultrasound physicians’ time for patient diagnoses. Random sampling effectively lightens the QC workload. This method is currently widely used in QC practice although low-quality cases might bypass the post-scan manual QC. In addition, manual QC faces challenges such as variability in results and time constraints.

In recent years, many significant breakthroughs associated with artificial intelligence (AI) have been made in ultrasound medicine [12], particularly obstetric ultrasound [13–18]. AI is adept at handling extensive repetitive work at a low cost [19], and image QC falls under this category. These make AI an ideal solution for prenatal ultrasound image QC.

Additionally, previous evaluations of ultrasound training methods have predominantly focused on effectiveness assessment, with limited research comparing different methods from a health economics perspective, such as cost-effectiveness [11].

This study aimed to develop and evaluate a prenatal ultrasound QC scoring scale based on Chinese guidelines and expert consensus. The scale was then employed in an effectiveness assessment to compare real-time AI QC and post-scan manual QC. Additional time consumption was also measured to assess the time cost of the real-time AI QC, post-scan AI QC, real-time manual QC, and post-scan manual QC.

Methods and materials

The design and implementation of this study adhered to the following principles: the Declaration of Helsinki, principles for repeated measures analysis [20], and principles governing scale development and evaluation. The research protocol and procedures were approval by the Ethics Committee of the Hunan Provincial Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (2023-S048). All pregnant volunteers duly signed informed consent forms.

Tools and ultrasound screening

Based on the " Practice Guidelines for Performance of Ultrasound Prenatal Screening” [7] and “Chinese Expert Consensus on Assessment Standards of Obstetric Ultrasound Training Program 2022 Edition” [3], our team selected appropriate domains (plains) and items to develop a scale for the evaluation of prenatal ultrasound image QC (STable 1). This scale was then utilized in the subsequent parts of this study (Fig. 1a). The scale was validated by content validity (STable 2) [21, 22], Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (STable 3 and STable 4) [23], and test-retest and inter-scorer reliability [24] (STable 5).

As shown in SFigure 1, the AI QC system utilized in this study was the AiriScan QC V1.0 (Airigin, Guangzhou, China) [14], which operated with two Voluson E10 ultrasound machines equipped with C1-6-D (4–6 MHz) probes (General Electric, Boston, United States). One machine was designated for the AI QC group, while the other was assigned to the manual QC group. Before the experiment, all devices were calibrated, and all participants received training in applying the AI system.

As illustrated in Fig. 1b, pregnant volunteers were recruited based on the following criteria: a woman with singleton pregnancy, a BMI ranging from 18.5 to 23.9 kg/m2, a height over 150 cm, and no abnormalities detected by ultrasound scans. Exclusion criteria included: ① pregnant women requiring prolonged screening time due to inappropriate fetal position, and ② those unwilling to participate in the study. The ultrasound examination process adhered to the ALARA principles [25].

Cost-effectiveness analysis on different methods for ultrasound image QC

In May 2023, a prospective, parallel-designed effectiveness (QC score improvement) assessment was conducted at the Department of Ultrasonography in the Hunan Provincial Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (Fig. 1c). The study spanned four months and recruited attending ultrasonographers able to independently perform ultrasound prenatal screening for fetal abnormalities. Exclusion criteria included ultrasonographers with less than five years of experience in ultrasound prenatal screening or those who had previously participated in AI-based ultrasound image QC studies. The random envelope method without stratification was adopted to evenly allocate enrolled subjects into two groups: the AI QC group (real-time AI QC group) and the Manual QC group (post-scan manual QC group). All subjects were assigned anonymous identifiers throughout the research process to ensure that data collectors remained unaware of personal information and grouping details.

Both groups received an initial assessment by the AI QC system to establish each subject’s baseline score, by the mean score of all QC assessments during June and July. From August to October, the AI QC group used the real-time AI QC, while the Manual QC group used the post-scan manual QC. In the real-time AI QC group, subjects submitted images they deemed satisfactory to the AI QC system for scoring and received prompt feedback on any mistakes. Similarly, members of the Manual QC group submitted self-assessed satisfactory images to the AI QC system for scoring and received weekly feedback from post-scan manual QC. The mean of a subject’s monthly scores in August, September, and October served as his evaluation scores for the corresponding months, excluding scores from the first week of August.

As depicted in Fig. 1d, an additional 50 eligible pregnant women were randomly selected as volunteers to evaluate the time cost of image QC. Each volunteer received a standard fetal ultrasound screening and real-time manual QC. Subsequently, post-scan manual QC and post-scan AI QC were performed on the obtained images. The time spent by trainers on the QC was recorded as the time cost. Notably, the real-time AI QC incurred no time cost (0 s), as it required no involvement apart from that of trainees.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institue Inc., North Carolina, USA). Normality was assessed through Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normal continuous data were described by means and standard deviations, while non-normal continuous data were presented by medians and interquartile ranges [24]. Qualitative data were described by absolute counts and proportions. The two-sample t test was used to compare the means of two independent continuous samples in normal distributions. The Friedman M test and the follow-up Quade test were used for the comparison and multiple comparisons of the medians among numerous non-normal continuous independent samples in a randomized block design [13]. The Pearson chi-squared test would be employed to compare the proportions of two independent samples if the total number of cases and all theoretical frequencies () were at least 40 and five, respectively. The Fisher exact test would be applied if or P values approximated the significant level.

We evaluated the effectiveness of different QC methods through the repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) after confirming the approximate normality of each outcome and its change score [26]. A minimum sample size of six subjects (three per group) was determined via PASS, grounded in the preliminary trial (, , , , , , , , , Group allocation: equal, Test: Pillai-Bartlett). We thus decided to recruit at least 12 ultrasonographers (six per group). Time was treated as a categorical variable (month) to avoid multicollinearity [8]. A subject’s mean QC score in a particular month was regarded as the evaluation score for that month to avoid autocorrelation at a specific time point [27, 28]. The group served as the between-subject factor, and the evaluation scores of June-July, August, September, and October served as the within-subject variables. The Pillai trace would be utilized to analyze this repeated measures data if the infringement of sphericity occurred [20]. Focus was placed on simple effects and interactions instead of main effects when interactions existed between the within-subject variables and between-subject factors [29]. The within-subject effect (multiple comparisons between time points in different groups), between-subject effect (multiple comparisons between the AI and Manual QC groups), and time trend (multiple comparisons between time points in different groups) were carefully analyzed. All statistical analyses were two-tailed at a significance level of 0.05, except for nominal significance levels modified by the Bonferroni method, namely dividing 0.05 into the number of tests.

Results

General information

A total of 14 eligible ultrasonographers were recruited to participate in this prospective parallel effectiveness assessment. Their mean age and service time for ultrasound prenatal screening were 42.6 ± 1.2 years old and 18.5 ± 1.3 annual, respectively. All subjects held MD degrees and belonged to the ethnic Han group, with females accounting for 85.7% of the sample size. Random assignment allocated half of the fourteen subjects to the AI QC group and the other half to the Manual QC group. The two groups had no statistically significant differences in age, service time for ultrasound prenatal screening, or sex composition (Table 1). All subjects received the expected interventions and analyses without any exclusions or loss to follow-up after randomization, which made the intention-to-treat analysis unnecessary. From August to October, the AI and Manual QC groups had mean QC frequencies per person of 25.2 ± 2.3 times and 25.0 ± 2.8 times, respectively. Statistically, the two mean QC frequencies were identical (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this prospective study, we initially have experts select appropriate dimensions (planes) and items for the QC scale following existing guidelines and expert consensus. We then evaluated the scale according to the evaluation criteria of scales. Subsequently, we contrasted the AI and manual QC methods from a novel perspective: cost-effectiveness analysis, which had never been utilized for perinatal ultrasound QC previously. The effectiveness analyses were designed based on a parallel test with a completely randomized repeated measures design, while the cost analyses were ground in a block randomization-designed test. The objective results disclosed the remarkable performance of our scale for perinatal ultrasound QC and highlighted the overwhelming superiority of the AI QC approach over the manual method in terms of cost-effectiveness analysis.

The absence of a widely accepted scale for ultrasound prenatal screening QC prompted us to develop our own from scratch. We meticulously referred to guidelines and selected 16 domains (planes) with a total of 135 items. To ensure the content validity of our scale, we applied expert ratings via indices such as item level-CVI (I-CVI), S-CVI/Ave ((sum of I-CVI scores)/ (number of items)), justified Kapp. The findings, presented in Table 2, demonstrate the high content validity achieved by our scale. All items achieved acceptable I-CVI and excellent justified Kappa. Moreover, the S-CVI/Ave confirmed an excellent overall content validity [21, 22]. After establishing our scale, we executed a pilot study with a small sample size to assess its reliability. The Cronbach’s Alpha for all domains reached 0.735 and exceeded most of Cronbach’s Alpha if any domain was deleted, which implies good reliability between domains (STables 3 and 4) [23]. The intra-viewer intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for QC was 0.869 (95% CI: 0.771–0.925), which measures the measurement errors from the observed viewer. The inter-viewer ICC was 0.859 (95% CI: 0.765–0.917), which measures the measurement errors from both viewers. Both intra-viewer and inter-viewer ICCs indicated good reliability [8].

In the effectiveness analysis, we employed the RM-ANOVA, considering the simple scenario of repeated measures at multiple time points with a continuous outcome and no loss to follow-up. Additionally, the RM-ANOVA is effective yet simpler than the mixed-effects model or generalized estimating Eqs. [26, 30]. Pillai’s trace was utilized for data analyses due to the violation of the sphericity assumption [20]. We primarily concentrated on the simple effects and interactions of the group and month owing to their interaction (F = 4.609, P = 0.028, η2 = 0.580) [29]. As shown in Table 2; Fig. 3, the simple effect of the group suggested that the mean QC scores differed significantly between the AI and Manual QC groups at different time points (F = 13.113, P = 0.004, η2 = 0.522). The simple effect of the month within the AI QC group showed that the mean QC scores differed across months (F = 9.827, P = 0.003, η2 = 0.747). However, the simple effect of the month within the Manual QC group confirmed that the variations in mean QC scores across months were not statistically significant (F = 0.144, P = 0.931, η2= ​​​​​​​0 0.041).

The nominal significance level adjusted by the Bonferroni method remained at 0.05, owing to only two levels in the group for between-subject effect analysis. Table 3 reveals no significant differences between the mean QC scores of the two groups prior to the intervention (F = 0.031, P = 0.864, η2 = 0.003). In contrast, the AI QC group outperformed the Manual QC group in August (F = 14.579, P = 0.002, η2 = 0.549), September (F = 28.590, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.704), and October (F = 35.411, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.747) after the intervention. These findings indicate that the real-time AI QC surpasses the post-scan manual QC.

In the within-subject effect analysis (Table 4), the means QC scores of the Manual QC group in June-July, August, September, and October exhibited no statistically significant differences. This implies that the post-scan manual QC can only sustain the current QC level without improvement. Conversely, the mean QC scores of the AI QC group in August, September, and October significantly exceeded those in June-July, suggesting that the real-time AI QC effectively enhanced the QC level.

The linear trends of the month (F = 14.684, P = 0.002, η2 = 0.550) and month * group (F = 14.752, P = 0.002, η2 = 0.551) exhibited statistical significance, with the biggest SS and partial η2 values among all trends. These findings have two vital implications. First, both groups’ scores are more suitable for linear functions when we fit variational patterns over time. Second, the AI QC group displays a steeper slope than the Manual QC group.

The effectiveness evaluation results validated our hypothesis that real-time AI QC is superior to post-scan manual QC. Although no other studies support our findings, they are theoretically grounded. Perinatal ultrasound QC is a pedagogical behavior enabling trainees to achieve higher proficiency levels through learning. From a physiological perspective, this behavior falls into operant conditioning, a form of associative learning [31, 32]. This learning process involves repeated cycles of “image collection → feedback (QC) → more standard image,” which ultimately leads to long-term memory formation. Since short-term memory requires timely and repetitive application and consolidation to be converted into long-term memory [33], prompt feedback (QC in this research) can yield superior learning outcomes. From an educational standpoint, timely evaluation of learning outcomes (QC in this research) allows learners to comprehend better their proficiency levels and shortcomings, stimulating their motivation for active learning and consequently achieving improved proficiency [34]. These two rationales effectively elucidate why real-time AI QC outperforms post-scan manual QC.

As per cost evaluation, real-time AI QC incurred no additional time costs apart from those of the trainees, so its time cost was recorded as 0 s per patient. The time cost of the post-scan AI QC (time spent by a reviewer operating the AI QC system) and the post-scan manual QC (time spent by a human reviewer screening the images) were 13.76 s (interquartile range, IQR: 4.79–46.79 s) and 1239.50 s (IQR: 1141.00–1311.25 s) per set of images, respectively. The real-time manual QC took a human reviewer 1541.00 s (IQR: 1453.50–1635.25 s) to screen images and instruct the trainee per patient. Significant differences are observed among these time costs in both overall and pairwise multiple comparisons, as shown in Table 6; Fig. 4. The real-time AI QC boasted the highest time utilization rate. The high-quality scale-based manual QC was extremely time-consuming compared to AI QC approaches. The IQRs of post-scan and real-time manual QC are large since their time costs and the corresponding variation are remarkably greater than those of AI.

The main reason why we selected time costs rather than other economic costs as the leading indicator for cost evaluation is that the scarcity of qualified senior ultrasonographers, which poses a significant bottleneck in ultrasound prenatal screening QC. The availability of senior ultrasonographers who can perform image QC is consistently limited across various countries and regions. Doctors’ salaries are extremely high in developed countries, making it even more challenging to afford doctors qualified for perinatal ultrasound QC [35]. Few institutions can financially sustain employing these senior doctors as full-time image QC reviewers over an extended period. Similarly, developing countries face a shortage of qualified ultrasonographers [9]. Even if institutions were affordable, there would still be insufficient ultrasonographers available. Moreover, enforcing image QC on scarce senior ultrasonographers instead of prenatal diagnosis is not feasible.

There are two main limitations in this study. First, we did not execute exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses in developing our scale because of the concrete nature of our domains (planes). These analyses are commonly applied in establishing psychological scales where abstract and conceptual psychological issues need clustering and dimension reduction [36]. Second, blinding all subjects or instructors in the Manual QC group was unfeasible because of the intervention (QC) implemented in different groups. Therefore, we used AI with solid reliability and robustness as the QC reviewer and applied blinding to data analysts to minimize information bias [37]. Additionally, the healthcare settings and operator skill levels may affect the generalizability of our findings since this is single-center research.

Conclusion

This prospective study provides a comprehensive scale for the QC of perinatal ultrasound screening and validates its impressive reliability and content validity. Moreover, it highlights the undeniable superiority of the AI QC method over the manual approach in educational effectiveness. Specifically, the AI QC approach enhances the operation skill in perinatal ultrasound screening, while the manual method merely sustains existing skill levels. The AI QC also demonstrates a significantly higher time utilization ratio and lower time cost than the manual QC. Given its exceptional cost-effectiveness, the AI QC method holds immense potential for widespread applications across various image QC scenarios. Although AI QC offers promising time savings and consistency, human oversight is still valuable, especially for complex scenarios where AI may not yet match experienced ultrasonographes’ judgment. Furthermore, our findings shall be verified in multi-center research with a larger sample size.
