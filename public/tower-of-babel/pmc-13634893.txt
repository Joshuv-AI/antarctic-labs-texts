From withdrawal to motivation: The feasibility, acceptability and early clinical benefits of adapted physical activity during inpatient alcohol withdrawal
Authors: Aude Barraud, Alice Laniepce, Julien Cabe, Shailendra Segobin, Anne-Lise Pitel, Nicolas Cabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPhysical activity (PA) may improve physical and mental health in alcohol use disorder (AUD), yet poor adherence limits its clinical impact. Inpatient alcohol withdrawal represents a critical window for initiating health behavior change, although patients are often encouraged to rest.ObjectivesWe evaluated whether supervised adapted physical activity (APA) can be feasibly implemented during inpatient withdrawal care and whether it influences short-term health and exercise engagement and motivation compared with free access to a PA room (FPA).MethodsIn this real-world study, 64 participants with severe AUD (26.5% women; mean age = 45.8 ± 11 years) completed pre- and post-assessments during a three-week inpatient withdrawal program. Participants were sequentially allocated to supervised APA or FPA with general advice. Feasibility (attendance and adverse events), session satisfaction, health outcomes (anxiety, depressive symptoms, self-esteem, craving, sleep quality, and physical functional capacity), and PA motivation (amotivation, extrinsic, and intrinsic motivation) were assessed after acute withdrawal at hospital discharge (post-test).ResultsAPA participants engaged in significantly more PA (p < 0.05), with high levels of session satisfaction (median = 8.86 and 8.83/10), and an attendance rate of 83%. Health outcomes and amotivation improved over time in both groups (p < 0.001), without group-by-time interaction. Motivation showed a significant group and interaction effect (p < 0.05): APA participants scored higher post-test, while FPA participants decreased in extrinsic motivation (p = 0.013). Intention-to-treat analyses revealed consistent findings.ConclusionsImplementing supervised APA during early withdrawal treatment is feasible and acceptable. While short-term clinical improvements were similar across groups, APA strengthened motivational processes relevant to PA engagement during recovery.

Introduction

Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) is a chronic, relapsing, and multifaceted mental disorder marked by a loss of control and persistent alcohol consumption despite negative consequences, as well as craving (American Psychiatric Association & Task, 2013; Koob and Le Moal, 2008; Koob and Volkow, 2016). Among patients seeking treatment for AUD, up to 79% present with at least one physical health problem and almost 60% have multiple physical alterations (Gossop et al., 2007). Physical fitness is markedly reduced in adults with AUD (Hallgren et al., 2021; Vancampfort et al., 2019). Altered physical fitness and physical disabilities in AUD are related to low levels of physical activity (PA) (Vancampfort et al., 2015a; Vancampfort et al., 2019) frequently observed in this population (Hallgren et al., 2021; Vancampfort et al., 2019). Insufficient PA is associated with greater AUD severity (Ternay et al., 2022), and higher levels of depressive, stress, and anxiety symptoms, nicotine dependence, and comorbid opioid use (Kelly et al., 2024; Ternay et al., 2022; Vancampfort et al., 2015a). Physically inactive individuals with AUD are more likely to experience poor sleep quality and insomnia (Bolstad et al., 2023; Duquet et al., 2025). In this context, interventions promoting PA among people with AUD are promising to counteract alcohol-related physical and psychological alterations exacerbated by inactivity (Cabé et al., 2021).

Most studies converge in showing the beneficial effect of PA on physical fitness among adults with AUD (Brown et al., 2014; Giménez-Meseguer et al., 2023; Piché et al., 2023; Sinyor et al., 1982). Sleep quality improvements have also been reported, although it remains unclear whether these changes are attributable to exercise or to its combined effects with alcohol drinking cessation (Duquet et al., 2025; Gunillasdotter et al., 2022). Three meta-analyses reported reductions in depressive symptoms (Hallgren et al., 2017a; Li et al., 2024; Piché et al., 2023) with greater effects observed in longer interventions involving more PA sessions (Hallgren et al., 2017a), but the impact of PA on mental health appears heterogeneous in AUD. Two meta-analyses found no effect on anxiety (Gür and Can Gür, 2020; Hallgren et al., 2017a) while two others reported improvements (Li et al., 2024; Piché et al., 2023).

The effect of PA on alcohol-related outcomes remains unclear. Some meta-analyses found no significant effects on risky or hazardous alcohol consumption (Gür and Can Gür, 2020; Li et al., 2024; Thompson et al., 2020). Other authors found small but significant reductions in weekly alcohol intake (Hallgren et al., 2017a; Lardier et al., 2021). A 3-week aerobic exercise intervention had a short-term effect with lower alcohol craving and fewer drinks consumed per week (Pennington et al., 2022).

PA is thus a promising and acceptable adjunctive treatment for AUD (Cabé et al., 2021; Stoutenberg et al., 2015), yet participation remains low (Giesen et al., 2016; Hallgren et al., 2017a) with a substantially higher dropout rate of 40,3% than that observed for other severe mental illnesses (Stubbs et al., 2016; Vancampfort et al., 2016) or chronic conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes (Bullard et al., 2019). Adherence to PA interventions is commonly observed to be low among people with AUD and tends to decrease over time (Brown et al., 2009; Welford et al., 2023).

Motivation, in particular autonomous motivation, is a central aspect for sustaining PA engagement (Blevins et al., 2017; Weinstock, 2010), making it essential to embed motivational strategies into PA intervention design (Roessler et al., 2017; Vancampfort et al., 2015b; Vancampfort et al., 2025). The Self-Determination Theory (Deci and Ryan, 2000; Deci & Ryan, 2008) frames motivation as a continuum ranging from amotivation to intrinsic motivation (Fig. 1). Between these extremes, extrinsic motivation comprises four types of regulation that differ in their degree of internalization: external regulation, introjected regulation, identified regulation, and integrated regulation. Autonomous motivation appears to sustain PA by fostering intrinsic interest, personal endorsement, and satisfaction of the basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2008).Fig. 1The self-determination continuum according to Deci et Ryan,2000.Fig. 1

Adapted PA (APA) is a research, theory, and service delivery directed toward persons of all ages underserved by the general sport sciences, disadvantaged in resources, or lacking power to access equal PA opportunities and rights (Hutzler and Sherrill, 2007; International Federation of Adapted Physical Activity, 2025). The tailored and supervised nature of APA intervention makes it suitable for much earlier integration into standard care than previously assumed, including during alcohol withdrawal and among inpatients with severe forms of AUD (sAUD). APA appears to be a relevant approach to enhance recovery and well-being in individuals with AUD, notably through enhanced exercise motivation (Cabé et al., 2021; Hallgren et al., 2017b; Horrell et al., 2020). Furthermore, alcohol withdrawal is a critical period for establishing new health-related behaviors (National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2012), but also a time when many barriers to PA can be overcome. Nevertheless, PA is still rarely offered in that context, with patients often advised to observe near-complete rest (Cabé et al., 2021; Horrell et al., 2020).

The present real-world interventional study explored early PA implementation during alcohol withdrawal hospitalization in adult inpatients with sAUD who require complete hospitalization. The first objective was to evaluate the feasibility and session satisfaction of an APA intervention for inpatients with sAUD undergoing alcohol withdrawal treatment. The secondary objective was to compare the short-term effect of supervised APA and free access to a PA room (FPA) on anxiety and depressive symptoms, self-esteem, alcohol craving, subjective sleep quality, physical functional capacity and exercise motivation. The hypothesis was that the APA condition would yield higher levels of satisfaction, greater adherence, and improvements in overall health and exercise motivation compared with the FPA condition.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a real-world, embedded clinical study and conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki with an ethic approval from the Local Ethical Committee for Health Research, of the University Hospital of Caen (N°99). Sequential allocation was used to fit logistical constraints related to the presence of an APA instructor and minimize contamination between groups within a shared inpatient environment: APA (April–May 2018, 2019, and 2021) and FPA (June 2019–January 2020). Clinical outcomes are reported as exploratory, while feasibility outcomes are primary.

Participants

Eighty-seven inpatients aged 18 or over, weighing less than 130 kg, and diagnosed with sAUD according to the DSM-5 criteria (American Psychiatric Association & Task, 2013) were recruited in the Addiction Department at the University Hospital of Caen. The complete inclusion criteria and the flow chart for study recruitment are shown in Fig. 2. Experimental design

The usual inpatient treatment program consisted of a three-week hospitalization, beginning with a medically supervised acute alcohol detoxification. Alcohol withdrawal syndrome was managed pharmacologically when necessary (Laniepce et al., 2021). During the first week of usual care, which is dedicated to detoxification and assessment, patients remained in their rooms most of the time. This was followed by two weeks of individual and group therapy, with motivational enhancement therapy provided throughout the entire stay.

As for neuropsychological practice assessments, the pre-test was conducted once inpatients were considered detoxified by the medical team, as indicated by a low and stable Cushman score of two or less (Cushman Jr et al., 1985) and discontinuation of benzodiazepines. This was done to minimize the bias of acute alcohol withdrawal, but before initiating any moderate-intensity PA. Post-test was performed on the day of hospital discharge, replicating the initial evaluation battery (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Experimental design oftheAPAddicto protocol.APA = Adapted Physical Activity; PA = Physical Activity; FPA = Free Physical Activity.Fig. 3

Interventions

Adapted physical activity (APA)

According to recommendations for PA adherence in people suffering from an AUD and French health recommendations in APA (HAS, 2022; Horrell et al., 2020; Roessler et al., 2017; Thompson et al., 2020), APA instructors tailored each session according to treatment constraints, as well as individual needs and abilities. During the alcohol detoxification phase, two 45–60-min familiarization sessions of low-intensity PA occurred in the PA room, combining breath exercises, stretching, and self-massage techniques. Over the following two weeks, APA instructors supervised a total of five 60-min group sessions of moderate-intensity PA. Each session began with a 10-min warm-up and ended with a 5–10-min cool-down period involving stretching and discussion. Sessions included aerobic exercise, as well as resistance and balance exercises with gradual intensity progression. In addition, during the second weekend, APA instructors encouraged the inpatients to exercise independently. Inpatients could choose aerobic or resistance exercise sessions, which were listed on a sheet for the weekend. The APA teacher recorded inpatient attendance for each PA session.

Free physical activity (FPA)

Upon their inclusion, participants assigned to the FPA group received advice on the benefits of regular PA. The information sheet explicitly encouraged them to engage in PA after completing the detoxification phase, provided they had the necessary medical authorization giving them unrestricted access to the PA room. This room was equipped with four stationary bikes, a rowing machine, and some strength-training equipment such as weights and elastic bands, to allow aerobic or resistance exercise. Participants who used the PA room were asked to report on the duration of each exercise session.

Assessments

We collected sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by reviewing medical records (Table 2). Pre-test was conducted after completion of the acute detoxification phase and before any moderate-intensity PA sessions. The two low-intensity PA sessions were designed for safety and engagement and were not intended as the therapeutic exposure to be assessed on clinical outcomes. Pre-test evaluated alcohol misuse and PA level using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) (Gache et al., 2005) and Ricci & Gagnon's (Laureyns and Séné, 2011) questionnaires. These scientifically validated assessment tools for adults, along with the health and motivation outcome assessment tools used during the pre- and post-tests, are described in Table 1. The pre-post-test tools included the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (Zigmond and Snaith, 1983), the Rosenberg scale (Rosenberg, 1965; Vallieres and Vallerand, 1990), the Obsessive-Compulsive Drinking Scale (OCDS) (Ansseau et al., 2000; Anton et al., 1995), the 6-Minute walking test (6MWT) (ATS Statement, 2002), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (Buysse et al., 1989). To evaluate the feasibility of the APA intervention, PA adherence was quantified based on the number of sessions, mean session duration and total PA duration performed weeks two and three (when both groups practiced). The safety of PA was assessed by the occurrence of PA-related adverse events during and after PA sessions.

Session satisfaction of the APA intervention was evaluated using two self-reported Likert scales, each ranging 0 to 10, which measured perceived enjoyment and well-being at the end of each session. Inpatients answered the following questions: “How much did you enjoy exercising?” and “ How would you rate your well-being?”

Data analysis and statistics

Descriptive statistics describe participant characteristics, with the sample median imputed for missing education data. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were compared between groups at pre-test using Wilcoxon or chi-square tests, when appropriate.

Between-group comparisons of PA adherence were conducted using Wilcoxon tests. Session satisfaction of APA sessions was presented using descriptive statistics. Health and motivation outcomes were analyzed using general linear mixed models (GLMMs), including group, time, and their interaction as fixed effects, with participants as random effects and age, sex, and education as covariates. For the 6MWT, height and weight were additionally controlled. We finally conducted within- and between-group planned comparisons (pre- versus post-test for each group, FPA versus APA at each assessment time).

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (Posit Software, PBC., 2023) with an alpha level of 0.05.

Intervention adherence

Thirty-five sAUD inpatients initiated the FPA intervention, of whom 24 completed the program. Similarly, among the 52 sAUD inpatients included in the APA group, 40 completed the program. There was no significant difference in group dropout (Χ2 = 0.382, p = 0.536), without any dropout reason specifically related to PA intervention. Subsequent statistical analyses were therefore conducted on the 64 subjects who completed both assessments (Fig. 2).

Session satisfaction and feasibility

We reported no undesirable effects or adverse events related to PA. The APA group completed more PA sessions (p = 0.027, r = 0.277 (small)) and spent more time exercising per session (p < 0.001, r = 0.854 (large)) than the FPA group, with a significantly higher total PA duration during hospitalization (p < 0.001, r = 0.484 (moderate)). Within the FPA group, two distinct subgroups emerged: 13 inpatients reported no PA during hospitalization (FPA-) and 11 engaged in at least one exercise session (FPA+).

In the APA group, the attendance rate, enjoyment and well-being scores were consistently high. The median attendance rate was 83%, and mean ratings ranged from 7.67 to 10. Amotivation

There was no group effect, but a significant effect of time (p = 0.008), reflecting a decrease in amotivation from pre- to post-test. No significant group-by-time interaction was found (p > 0.05, marginal R2 = 0.068).

Extrinsic motivation

We found a significant group effect (APA > FPA, 0.001), no significant effect of time, and a significant group-by-time interaction (marginal R2 = 0.154, p = 0.004). Planned comparisons revealed a decline in extrinsic motivation during FPA intervention (estimate = −0.204, 95% CI [−0.365; −0.044], p = 0.013) and higher post-test scores in APA compared with FPA (estimate = 0.316, 95% CI [0.121; 0.511], p = 0.001).

Intrinsic motivation

The GLMM revealed a significant group effect (APA > FPA, p < 0.001), but no significant effect of time. However, a significant group-by-time interaction was observed (marginal R2 = 0.197, p = 0.037). Planned comparisons indicated no significant difference between the groups at pre-test, but higher intrinsic motivation at post-test in APA compared with FPA (estimate = 0.376, 95% CI [0.185; 0.566], p < 0.001).

Exploratory analyses

Intention-to-treat analyses

Exploratory intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses, using the full dataset (n = 87), with last observation carried forward imputation for missing post-test data, confirmed the primary findings, with minor variations. A significant group effect on self-esteem persisted (p = 0.015), with APA exhibiting higher Rosenberg scores than FPA at both assessments. Amotivation significantly decreased only in the APA group (p = 0.010). The group-by-time interaction remained significant for intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (p = 0.024 and p = 0.010, respectively). The APA group demonstrated higher levels of intrinsic motivation at both assessment times (pre: p = 0.011 and post: p < 0.001), while its decline in FPA was non-significant in ITT (p = 0.173). These nuances did not alter the overall interpretation of the results.

Subscale analyses

Exploratory analyses of the SMS subscales confirmed the overall pattern for the extrinsic and intrinsic composite scores (Supplementary Materials, S1). The significant interaction effects were primarily driven by an increased tendency for motivation in APA and/or decrease tendency in the FPA group over time. Planned comparisons showed that both groups had a similar level of motivation at pre-test, except for intrinsic motivation linked to knowledge. However, post-test, APA inpatients demonstrated higher levels of motivation across all subscales, particularly concerning the introjected, identified, and intrinsic motivation.

Discussion

This study is the first to implement an APA intervention from the earliest stage of alcohol withdrawal hospitalization. Our findings suggest that detoxification treatment could be a key factor in improving the short-term health outcomes in both groups, while supervised APA can be safely integrated into withdrawal care and has a specific added value in strengthening inpatients' motivation and adherence to PA compared with unsupervised access to PA facilities. Thus, these findings imply that early supervised and structured APA is a promising intervention to change behavior by increasing motivation for exercise rather than a clinical treatment with free access to a PA room during withdrawal hospitalization.

Feasibility and satisfaction of APA during inpatient alcohol withdrawal

The APA program was safe and readily implemented, even when introduced early after alcohol cessation, as reflected by the high enjoyment and well-being scores reported by APA inpatients and the absence of dropout and adverse events related to PA. The delivery of the program within the ward was smooth and caused no operational difficulties. While most inpatients report believing that PA can be initiated during alcohol treatment (Stoutenberg et al., 2015), only half of FPA inpatients initially intending to exercise at the start of the study finally engaged in PA during hospitalization. In contrast, APA inpatients exercised at least 2 sessions, indicating that APA intervention integrated into usual care is associated with higher engagement for sAUD inpatients compared to spontaneous exercise (Stoutenberg et al., 2015). Such programs and sessions include goal setting, positive feedback, enjoyment and social support through supervision and/or group-based activities, all of which can enhance acceptability, self-efficacy, and motivation (Hallgren et al., 2017b; Weinstock, 2010). By its personalized and motivational approach, APA appears as a promising approach.

Health-related outcomes

Although PA adherence was significantly higher in the APA group, physical and mental health outcomes improved similarly in both groups. This suggests that these changes could reflect common factors in hospital care, such as stopping drinking alcohol and following the entire therapeutic program. The early weeks of abstinence are typically associated with significant physical and psychological recovery, including reduction in anxiety, depressive symptoms and craving (Manning et al., 2008; Petit et al., 2017).

The overall therapeutic effects of alcohol withdrawal and inpatient stabilization may have overshadowed the specific contribution of PA intervention. Moreover, the PA intervention was designed to fit the actual duration of alcohol withdrawal hospitalization. However, three weeks were likely too short to induce measurable health changes. Previous studies and guidelines and clinical guidelines suggest that exercise intervention should last at least 12 weeks to observe a significant effect on clinical outcomes (Gür and Can Gür, 2020; Piché et al., 2023). Therefore, the absence of significant effects in our study may be partly explained by the short intervention duration. While previous studies have reported greater improvements in psychoaffective outcomes and craving with longer interventions, suggesting a possible dose-response relationship (Abrantes et al., 2017; Roessler et al., 2013), our study was not designed to evaluate such an effect. Further studies with longer intervention periods are needed to determine whether dose-response relation exists in this population. However, follow-up studies reported that PA practice diminished after the end of intervention (Fagan et al., 2021; Patten et al., 2003; Weinstock et al., 2020), highlighting the importance of promoting long-term PA adherence after hospital discharge.

Motivation and self-determination processes

Even though a 3-week program was likely too short to elicit specific health benefits, significant between-group differences emerged regarding changes in exercise motivation.

The decrease in amotivation observed at the end of the intervention in both groups may reflect the beneficial effects of alcohol withdrawal treatment on patients' general clinical condition. Improvements in withdrawal symptoms and overall functioning during inpatient care may contribute to greater perceived capacity and readiness to consider health-promoting behaviors, including PA. The absence of a specific effect of PA intervention on amotivation is consistent with previous findings in individuals with AUD, where amotivation was not associated with any stage of change for PA according to the Transtheoretical Model of Prochaska and DiClemente (Vancampfort et al., 2018). Similarly, the relationship between amotivation and PA appears to be complex and may depend on the level of engagement in exercise. Indeed, previous research reported no association between amotivation and exercise outcomes in samples including both exercisers and non-exercisers, whereas negative associations were observed among regular exercisers only (Teixeira et al., 2012). Although no significant difference in amotivation was observed between FPA(+) and FPA(−) groups, future studies should investigate whether sustained participation and adherence to PA programs may influence motivational regulation over time. This question may be particularly relevant in clinical populations, as previous research in individuals with severe mental illness has reported a negative association between amotivation and PA engagement (Farholm et al., 2017; Vancampfort et al., 2015b).

Furthermore, among FPA inpatients, particularly non-adherent ones (FPA-), extrinsic motivation decreased significantly during the intervention, suggesting that providing access to the PA room without program and supervision may be insufficient for patients lacking the necessary resources to exercise independently. Moreover, the APA group showed higher extrinsic and intrinsic motivation than the FPA group at post-test, primarily due to the low motivation of the FPA(−) group. PA practice seems to favor both types of motivation for exercise, regardless of the modality of PA (Farholm et al., 2017; Vancampfort et al., 2015b). These observations reinforce the relevance of supervised and structured APA programs and suggest that simply providing free access to a PA room may even be insufficient for the least active inpatients, who showed increased amotivation and decreased extrinsic motivation.

Concerning extrinsic motivation, APA inpatients showed significantly higher external and introjected regulation at post-test. Although such controlled motivation leads to the initiation of PA behavior, it is not sustainable in the long-term (Teixeira et al., 2012). Introjected regulation may help to initiate PA but APA interventions must ideally promote autonomous motivation (Vancampfort et al., 2015b; Vancampfort et al., 2025), which was associated with 31% higher moderate-to-vigorous PA in people with mental illness (Chapman et al., 2024). A higher level of identified regulation in the APA group could be linked to the exchange of knowledge about PA with a qualified APA supervisor and the acute effect of exercise, which may help participants to recognize and identify the benefits of PA within just three weeks. Previous research suggests that identified regulation is a strong predictor of exercise adoption, while intrinsic motivation plays a critical role in sustaining exercise over time (Teixeira et al., 2012). The APA supervisor likely contributes to these gains by providing information during practice, fostering enjoyment and support psychological basic needs of autonomy, competence and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2008; Farholm et al., 2017). As far as we know, only one clinical trial has examined exercise motivation based on the Self-Determination Theory in people with AUD. This study showed that a 12-week lifestyle PA intervention increased autonomous motivation (Blevins et al., 2017), which is consistent with our results. However, autonomous motivation was not related with PA duration but rather with a greater use of PA as a coping strategy for alcohol craving and negative affect (Blevins et al., 2017).

Limitations and future directions

Even though there was no between-group difference in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, the small proportion of women remains a limitation that may prevent the generalizability of our finding for inpatient populations with a higher representation of women. Additionally, sequential design remains a limitation, and the use of self-reported measures could result in desirability and recall bias. Furthermore, the relatively small and unequal sample sizes may have reduced statistical power. Nonetheless, randomization could have been considered as a limitation given the inevitable interaction between APA and FPA participants in the Addiction department. Thus, the naturalistic and ecological setting of this study ensures external validity. In addition, we were unable to distinguish the respective effect of supervision and greater exposure to PA, as supervision was considered an integral component of the APA intervention. To better examine how an APA approach that supports basic psychological needs fosters self-determined motivation, future researches should use a more comprehensive measure of exercise motivation across the continuum of self-determination. Additionally, the dynamic relationships with PA adherence, craving and risk of relapse should be examined over a longer follow-up period.

Conclusion

This real-world study shows that APA can be safely and effectively integrated into inpatient alcohol withdrawal care, with excellent session satisfaction and greater adherence than unsupervised exercise. While short-term clinical improvements were comparable between groups, APA was associated with more favorable changes in exercise motivation. These findings highlight the early alcohol withdrawal period as a pivotal moment for re-engagement in health-promoting activities and a window for PA. By providing structured, individualized guidance and meaningful social support, supervised APA may address psychological needs, relevant for sustained engagement. Integrating tailored and supervised exercise programs from standard withdrawal care and for several weeks could therefore serve as a promising clinical lever to boost motivation, enhance recovery trajectories.
