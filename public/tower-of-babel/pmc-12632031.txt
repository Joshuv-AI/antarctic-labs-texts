Improving succinic acid production by tuning polysulfides metabolism in Yarrowia lipolytica
Authors: Xiaokun Zhang, Zhiyong Cui, Yongzhen Xia, Qingsheng Qi, Luying Xun, Huaiwei Liu, Qingda Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroudSuccinic acid (SA) is a significant C4-dicarboxylic acid with broad applications in the food, chemical, and pharmaceutical industries. In microbial SA production, Yarrowia lipolytica shows great potential. Polysulfides are vital for maintaining redox balance and cellular health in yeast.ResultsIn this study, we changed the polysulfides metabolism of Y. lipolytica to enhance SA production. The 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST) and rhodanese (RHOD) encoding genes were disrupted in Y. lipolytica PGC01003, which led to increased biomass and SA production. In a 3-L scale bioreactor, the mutant strain produced 64.5 g/L SA, representing a 37.8% increase compared with PGC01003. Further investigations indicated that the number of mitochondria was decreased, but the ATP production and oxygen consumption rate were increased in the mutant strain. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that apoptosis genes were downregulated, and cell cycle related genes were upregulated.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that polysulfides affected the overall growth and metabolism of Y. lipolytica. The same strategy may have the potential to be applied in improving other cell factories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02870-2.

Introduction

Using microorganisms for green and sustainable production is more and more attractive nowadays [1, 2]. Accompanied with the development of synthetic biology, constructing highly efficient and intelligent microbial cell factories has become a hot research focus [3]. Y lipolytica, the oleaginous non-conventional yeast, has garnered increasing attention due to its unique characteristics, including the broad substrate adaptability, high cell density during fermentation, and tolerance to harsh conditions. The potential applications in creating various metabolites enhance its prominence as a significant cell factory model [4, 5].

Succinic acid (SA), a natural carboxylic acid with unique physiological and biochemical properties, has broad applications in the food, light manufacturing, and pharmaceutical industries [6–8]. It has been ranked as one of the 12 most valuable biochemicals [9, 10]. The high flux of the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) makes Y. lipolytica well-suited for SA production [11]. Several investigations have targeted the enhancement of SA production in Y. lipolytica by adjusting its carbon metabolism, especially the oxidative and reductive branches of the TCA cycle, as well as the glyoxylate pathway. For instance, the activity of succinate dehydrogenase complex (SDH) was interfered by deletion of Ylsdh5 gene in strain Y. lipolytica PGC01003 to enhance the oxidative branch of the TCA cycle [12].

Polysulfides are zero-valent sulfur (S⁰) containing compounds that exist in various forms within cells. Based on the group attached to the S⁰ atom, polysulfides can be classified into inorganic polysulfides (such as HSnH, n ≥ 2 and S8) and organic polysulfides (RSnR, n ≥ 3, and RSnH, n ≥ 2, where R is small thiols including cysteine and glutathione) [13–15]. Polysulfides play important physiological functions such as maintaining redox homeostasis, mitochondrial health, and energy metabolism [15–17]. Several polysulfide-producing enzymes have been identified, including cysteinyl-tRNA synthetase (CRS), sulfide: quinone oxidoreductase (SQR), 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST), and rhodanese (RHOD) [18, 19]. Previous studies indicated that decreasing the expression of these genes reduced intracellular polysulfides, thereby affecting cell growth and physiology [20, 21]. For instance, knocking out SQR encoding gene in fission yeast affected the TCA cycle, leading to a 6.5-fold upregulation of SA production [22] Therefore, low amount of intracellular polysulfides may benefit SA production in yeasts.

Y. lipolytica contains 3-MST and RHOD but no SQR. In this study, we knocked out 3-MST and RHOD encoding genes (3-mst and rhod) in Y. lipolytica PGC01003 and observed a sharp increase in SA production. Further investigations indicated that intracellular polysulfide content was decreased and reactive oxygen species (ROS) was increased in the mutant strain. Mitochondrial physiological characteristics including ATP production and oxygen consumption rate were increased. Thus, manipulating polysulfides metabolism is a promising approach in Y. lipolytica cell factory construction.

Materials and methods

Strains and cultivation media

Y. lipolytica PGC01003 was derived from Y. lipolytica Po1f (Leu−, Ura−) [12]. For screening yeast transformants, the YNBD medium was used, consisting of 0.67% (w/v) yeast nitrogen base (lacking amino acids and containing ammonium sulfate, solarbo), 0.2% casamino acids, and 2% (w/v) glucose. The yeast strains were grown in YPG or YPD medium which includes 2% glycerol (for YPG) or glucose (for YPD), 1% yeast extract and 2% peptone. The fermentation medium in 3-L scale bioreactor was modified YPG or YPD medium containing 50 ~ 100 g/L of glycerol (or 20 ~ 60 g/L glucose), 1% yeast extract and 2% peptone. Plasmid construction utilized E. coli DH5α, cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth with ampicillin (50 mg/L) supplementation as needed.

Gene cloning and strain construction

Table 1 provides a list of the strains, vectors, and primers utilized in this study. In order to knock out the genes encoding 3-MST (mst, accession number: CR382132.1) and RHOD (rhod, accession number: CR382128.1) of Y. lipolytica, we inserted hyg and leu2 by homologous recombination using PLT cassette or PhygT cassette as reported previously [23]. The PLT or PhygT deletion cassette comprising upper homologous fragment, marker, and down homologous fragment was constructed using the MultiF Seamless Assembly Mix (ABclonal). Gene fragments of mst/rhod-up (~ 2000 bp) and mst/rhod-down (~ 2000 bp) were amplified from the genomic DNA of Y. lipolytica W29. The hyg fragment was rescued from from pUB4-Cre and the leu2 fragment was rescued from JMP114. Then the assembled cassettes were transformed into the Po1f and PGC01003 respectively by LiAc method [24]. The hyg+ transformant selected on YPG-hygromycin (400 mg/L) and the leu+ transformant screened on YNBD medium (lacking leucine) were verified using colony PCR. Ten colonies were chosen for additional assessment due to their potential for increased SA production. Strains exhibiting outstanding phenotypes were picked for further repeated validation.

Shaking flask cultivation

YPG or YPD medium (60 mL) was added to a 300-mL scale flask. Following inoculation, Y. lipolytica were cultured at 28 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The initial inoculum was adjusted to 0.05 of OD600. Glycerol (1 ~ 2 g) was supplemented at 24 and 72 h post-inoculation. Samples were collected to determine optical density, residual glycerol concentration, and organic acid levels.

Fed‑batch fermentation

A single colony of Y. lipolytica cell was inoculated into 5 mL YPG or YPD medium and cultivated at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 24 h. After cultivation, the culture was transferred into a 300 mL shaking flask containing 60 mL of fresh YPG or YPD medium, adjusted to an initial OD600 of 0.1, and incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h. Then, 60 mL of seed culture was inoculated into 1.7 L of fermentation medium within a 3-L bench-top bioreactor (FS-02-D03P, Winpact, USA) for the purpose of fed-batch fermentation. The fermentation process involved maintaining a pH of 6.0, an agitation speed of 600 rpm, and an aeration rate of 2.0 L/min. For fed-batch fermentation, YPG medium containing 100 g/L of pure glycerol or YPD medium containing 60 g/L of pure glucose served as the initial batch medium. Glycerol or glucose was added to bring the concentration back to its initial level when the remaining carbon source concentration fell below 15 g/L. Antifoam A (Sigma, Germany) was used as necessary to control foaming.

SA analytical techniques

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with an Aminex HPX-87 H column (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and a refractive index detector were used to determine the concentrations of organic acids and residual glycerol. For the analysis, 5 mM H2SO4 served as the mobile phase with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, and the column was kept at a temperature of 60 °C. Samples were filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters before analysis.

Detection of intracellular polysulfides

Intracellular polysulfides detection was performed using a HPLC-based method as reported previously [25]. Briefly, harvested Y. lipolytica cells were resuspended in a reaction buffer composed of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.5), 1% Triton X-100, 50 µM DTPA, and 50 mM sodium sulfite. The mixture was heated at 95 °C for 10 min to facilitate the transformation of intracellular polysulfides into thiosulfate. The thiosulfate produced was then reacted with monobromobimane (mBBr) and measured using HPLC.

Mitochondrial membrane potential analysis

The JC-1 assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2006) was used to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. Y. lipolytica cells were collected, rinsed twice with buffer, and then resuspended in the JC-1 working solution. The JC-1 probe was used to incubate the cells, and the fluorescence intensities of its monomeric (FL1) and aggregated (FL2) forms were assessed with a BD Accuri™ C6 flow cytometer. At least 50,000 cells were examined for each sample. The FL2/FL1 fluorescence ratio was determined using CFlow software from BD Biosciences. Wild-type cells exposed to carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) acted as the negative control.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Y. lipolytica cells were grown until they reached an OD600 between 0.8 and 1.5. The total RNA was then extracted using the E.Z.N.A. Yeast RNA Kit (Omega, R6870) following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, 500 ng of total RNA was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit and gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A). The SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (TaKaRa, DRR081A) was utilized for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche). To ensure consistency, gene expression levels were compared to the act1 mRNA as a reference.

MtDNA number analysis

The relative copy number of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) was assessed using quantitative PCR (qPCR). The E.Z.N.A.™ Yeast DNA Kit (Omega, D3370) was used to extract genomic DNA (gDNA) from Y. lipolytica cells, and 100 ng of this gDNA served as the template for each reaction. The cox1 gene acted as a marker for mitochondrial DNA, whereas act1 was used as a reference for nuclear DNA. The cycle threshold (CT) values were derived from three parallel samples, and the ΔCT was calculated by interpolating CTCOX1 and CTACT1, with 2ΔCT representing the relative mtDNA quantity.

ROS analysis

The level of cellular ROS was assessed using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033S). Y. lipolytica was grown in YPG medium at 30 °C with a shaking speed of 220 rpm. Cells were collected at each time interval by spinning them at 6000 rpm for 5 min, rinsed twice with 50 mM PBS buffer (pH 7.4), and then resuspended to an OD600 of 1.0 in PBS with 10 µM DCFH-DA. The cell suspensions were kept in the dark at 30 °C for 20 min, then washed three times with PBS to eliminate any excess probe. A BD Accuri™ C6 flow cytometer was used to detect fluorescence, analyzing at least 10,000 cells per sample. Wild-type cells exposed to Rosup (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033S), which increases intracellular ROS, served as a positive control.

Oxygen consumption analysis

Y. lipolytica strains were cultured in YPG medium until reaching OD600 of 0.8 ~ 1.0, and then harvested and rinsed twice with 50 mM PBS buffer at pH 7.4. The OD600 was then set to 5 in PBS buffer. 1% glucose (w/v) was added. An Orion RDO meter was used to monitor the concentration of dissolved oxygen (O2) in the cell suspension.

Transcriptomic analysis

Y. lipolytica strains were cultured in YPG medium until OD600 reached 0.8, and the cultivation was expanded for 12 h. Cells were harvested for the omics analysis, which were performed at Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) [20]. The library was sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The raw data were processed to remove reads with adapters, poly-N sequences, and low-quality reads, resulting in clean data. These clean reads were then aligned to the ASM294v2 Ensemble genome using HISAT2. The FPKM value for each gene expression in every sample was calculated using the feature Counts software. Genes with a p-value less than 0.05 and a fold change greater than 2 were identified as significantly differentially expressed.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Cells were collected for targeted metabolomics analysis and immediately frozen using liquid nitrogen. After being ground with liquid nitrogen, 60 mg of cells were combined with 1 mL of a methanol, acetonitrile, and water solution (2:2:1, v/v) and vortexed for 60 s. The cells were disrupted using ultrasound, then stored at -20 °C for 1 h before being centrifuged at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The collected supernatant underwent freeze-drying followed by LC-MS analysis [20].

Within the sample group, one QC sample was included every six experimental repetitions to assess the system’s stability and repeatability. The AB 5500 QqQ mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA) was utilized for mass spectrometry analysis [20]. Levels of metabolites were considered significantly different when the p-value was below 0.05 and the fold change was above 1.5.

Y. lipolytica’s adaptive evolution in glucose-rich YPD medium

A set of experiments involving adaptive evolution in cell fermentation was carried out in 250 mL shake flasks with 60 mL of YPD media, using different starting glucose concentrations and shaking at 220 rpm and 28 °C. To enhance cell biomass, the zero generation YPD medium was supplemented with 10 g/L each of glycerol and glucose. After cultivating for 48 h, 2 ml of the culture was transferred to a fresh YPD medium with 10 g/L glycerol and 20 g/L glucose, designated as the first generation. The second generation was cultured in the YPD medium with 3 g/L of glycerol and 20 g/L of glucose. Afterward, it was introduced into a YPD medium with 20 g/L of glucose, referred to as the third generation. When the OD600 reached a range of 5.0 to 7.0 within 48 h, the culture was transferred to YPD medium with a higher glucose concentration, gradually increasing the initial glucose concentration from 27.5, 35, 42.5, 50, to 80 g/L. In shaking flask fermentation, the initial OD600 of Y. lipolytica PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod was set at 0.2. Following 10 generations of adaptive evolution, 0.2 mL of the cell cultures were spread on YPD medium with 80 g/L glucose, and individual colony was chosen. The original strain PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod served as a control.

Result

Knocking out 3-mst and rhod genes led to increase of SA production by Y. lipolytica PGC01003 in YPG medium

We first knocked out 3-mst and rhod genes individually in the wild-type strain Y. lipolytica Po1f. The knockout strains (Po1fΔmst and Po1fΔrhod) were cultured in YPG medium, which contains 2% (w/v) glycerol as the carbon source. The results of shaking flask scale fermentation showed that Po1fΔmst showed no growth difference compared with wide type strain (Po1f wt), but its SA production was slightly increased with the highest titer reached 1.3 g/L, about 42.7% higher than that of Po1f wt (Fig. 1A, B). The Po1fΔrhod showed a slight biomass increase compared with Po1f wt, but its SA production was slightly decreased. We then constructed the third strain, Po1fΔmstΔrhod, in which both genes were knocked out. The strain Po1fΔmstΔrhod showed obvious biomass increase but its SA production was sharply decreased.

Considering that Po1f wt performs not well in SA production, we switched to its derivative strain Y. lipolytica PGC01003, whose SA production was increased by deleting the gene sdh5 [12]. Three mutants of PGC01003 (PGC01003Δmst, PGC01003Δrhod, and PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod) were constructed and subjected to shaking flask fermentation test. Compared with PGC01003, PGC01003Δmst and PGC01003Δrhod showed no obvious difference in terms of biomass and SA production (Fig. 1C, D). However, PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod had great increase in both biomass and SA production. The cell density increased by 3.27 times and SA production reached 17.38 g/L, which was 3.09 times higher than that of PGC01003 (Fig. 1C, D).

Performance of PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod in fed-batch fermentation

To further test the performance of PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod, a fed-batch fermentation experiment was performed in a 3-L scale bioreactor. Glycerol began at a concentration of 100 g/L. When it decreased to less than 15 g/L, it needed to be replenished back to the original concentration. Following 168 h of fermentation, the dry cell weight (DCW) of PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod reached 36.2 g/L, and the SA titer was up to 64.5 g/L. In comparison, DCW of PGC01003 was 26.3 g/L and SA titer was 46.8 g/L (Fig. 2B). These results confirmed that PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod performed better than PGC01003 in both biomass and SA production.

It is noteworthy that PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod showed different growth curve compared with PGC01003. The former continued fast growing after 48 h cultivation while the later decreased its growth rate obviously. In addition, PGC01003 accumulated high acetate during 48 h to 144 h (the maximum content reacted 14.38 g/L); whereas, PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod had no obvious acetate accumulation. The maximum content was only 4.71 g/L.

Knocking out 3-mst and rhod genes changed intracellular redox balance

To investigate why knocking out 3-mst and rhod genes influences biomass and SA production of Y. liolytica PGC01003, we first analyzed the change of intracellular polysulfides of PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod. Compared with PGC01003 wt, PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod had significant reduction in intracellular content of polysulfides (Fig. 3A). Since polysulfides have ROS quenching function, the content reduction may lead to ROS increase. We detected the ROS content of PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod using a fluorescent based ROS detection kit. Indeed, ROS content was significantly increased in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod (Fig. 3B). Considering that GSH/GSSG ratio is closely related with ROS content, we then analyzed GSSG and GSH contents in PGC01003 and PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod. Both GSH content and GSH/GSSG ratio were lower in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod than that in PGC01003 (Fig. 3C, D). Analysis of NAD+ and NADH contents indicated that NAD+ content was higher in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod than that in PGC01003, leading to a decreased NADH/NAD+ ratio in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod. (Fig. 3E, F). Surprisingly, analysis of NADP+ and NADPH contents indicated that NADP+ content was lower and NADPH content was higher in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod than that in PGC01003, leading to a sharply increased NADPH/NADP+ ratio in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod (Fig. 3G, H).

PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod displayed changed mitochondrial characteristics

Mitochondrial characteristics of PGC01003 and PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod were investigated. First, morphology of mitochondria was examined using MitoTracker and laser scanning confocal microscopy. The number of mitochondria was obviously lower in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod than that in PGC01003. In addition, mitochondria in PGC01003 were closely arranged and displayed, mainly in filamentous or granular shapes. In comparison, mitochondria in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod showed irregular shape, mainly were scattered dots (Fig. 4A). For confirmation, we analyzed the ratio of mitochondrial DNA number to genomic DNA number (mit-DNA/genomic DNA) using qRT-PCR, which reflects the number of mitochondria. Mitochondrion number in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod was significantly lower than that in PGC01003 in the logarithmic stage, and slightly lower in the stationary stage (Fig. 4B).

Mitochondria is the most important organelle for SA production. Theoretically, its impairment will decrease cell capability of producing SA. However, we observed that SA titer was increased in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod culture, most probably because PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod exhibited a substantial increase in biomass, which means PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod has more cells to convert carbon source to SA and ultimately shows higher SA titer.

Mitochondrial membrane potential is closed related with the oxidative phosphorylation activity. We examined mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 assay kit. PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod showed lower mitochondrial membrane potential than PGC01003. (Fig. 4C), suggesting that PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod has lower oxidative phosphorylation activity. We then quantified the intracellular ATP content. However, PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod contained more ATP than did PGC01003 (Fig. 4D). Its oxygen consumption rate also was higher than that of PGC01003, which was quite unexpected (Fig. 4E). A previous study indicated that knocking out thiosulfate transferase (TST, which has similar function as RHOD) in cerebral cortex increased the ATP level by regulating oxidative phosphorylation activity [26]. To see whether this is the same scenario, we analyzed the transcription levels of critical genes of complex I ~ Ⅳ and ATP synthase using qRT-PCR. Only transcription of cox10, gene of complex IV was sharply increased in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod. (Fig. 4F).

Systematic investigation of the transcription and metabolism changes in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod

Transcriptomics and metabolomics analyses were conducted to explore the worldwide alterations resulting from mst and rhod knockout. In PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod compared to PGC01003, a total of 692 genes showed increased expression, while 950 genes showed decreased expression (fold change >2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Approximately 20.3% of the entire genome consisted of genes with altered transcription. In particular, 17 genes essential for apoptosis were reduced in expression (Fig. 5B), including cypd and atg15, these genes can promote cell apoptosis [27, 28], while 18 genes related to cell cycle control were increased (Fig. 5C), including cdc6, clb3/4, mcm5, apc6, and hsl1, which accelerate cell cycle progression [29–32]; cdc5 and cdc14 contribute to the stability of the cell division cycle [33]. This should be the reason why PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod showed enhanced biomass production.

For metabolomics analysis, we focused on metabolites in TCA cycle of central and energetic metabolism. Within this group, 31 compounds were identified, with 18 demonstrating significant abundance changes in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod (fold change > 1.5, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5D). For specification:

Metabolomic data revealed a significant accumulation of citrate and oxaloacetate, accompanied by a 0.78-fold reduction of SA in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod. This metabolic shift may be attributed to mitochondrial dysfunction, which impedes the progression of the TCA cycle at the citrate and oxaloacetate steps, thereby reducing succinate formation. The accumulated citrate and oxaloacetate are likely channeled into macromolecule biosynthesis, potentially contributing to the biomass increase.

Knocking out 3-mst and Rhod led to increase of SA production of Y. lipolytica PGC01003 in YPD medium

For microbial cell factories, glucose is used as a carbon source more widely than glycerol [34]. Gao et al.., reported that PGC01003 produced 5.5 g/L SA from glycerol, a 13-fold increase compared to the parental strain [12]. However, PGC01003 exhibited impaired growth on glucose and accumulated acetic acid. We also tested the SA production of Y. lipolytica in YPD medium. Knocking out 3-mst or rhod, or both of them did not increase but decreased SA production of Y. lipolytica Po1f in glucose medium (Fig. 6A, B). Knocking out 3-mst or rhod did not obviously change SA production and growth of Y. lipolytica PGC01003, but knocking out both 3-mst and rhod significantly increased SA production and growth in glucose medium (Fig. 6C, D). Between glycerol and glucose, PGC01003 ΔmstΔrhod preferred the former for both growth and SA production, evidenced by 17.38 g/L SA and 7.12 g/L DCW in glucose medium but only 1.86 g/L SA and 1.26 g/L DCW in glycerol medium after 120 h of shaking flask-scale fermentation.

Adaptive evolution of PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod in glucose

Adaptive evolution is a powerful method to improve the performance of microbial cell factories [35, 36]. We applied this method to increase the growth and SA production of Y. lipolytica PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod in glucose. This strain was first cultivated in the medium containing 10 g/L glycerol and 10 g/L glucose for 48 h. The obtained culture was denoted as generation 0 (named PAS00). After the cultivation, 1 mL PAS00 culture was transferred into fresh medium containing 10 g/L of glycerol and 20 g/L of glucose. After 48 h cultivation, the obtained culture was denoted as generation 1 (named PAS01). In the following transfers, glycerol content was gradually decreased to 0 and glucose content was gradually increased to 60 g/L. For each generation, we tested the growth, glucose consumption, and SA production (Fig. 7A-C). The transfer was performed 10 times and PAS09 showed the best performance. Shaking flask scale experiments demonstrated that compared with the starting strain, PAS09 had a 2.77-fold increase in growth and 5.03-fold increase in SA production (Fig. 7D, E). Fed-batch fermentation with a 3-L scale bioreactor experiment showed that PAS09 produced 32.58 g/L SA from glucose in 96 h with a yield of 0.53 g/g (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

Y. lipolytica is a commonly used cell factory for SA production and it has been engineered in different ways according to previous reports. However, the previous engineering approaches mainly focused on carbon or coenzyme metabolism modifications, aiming to provide more NADH direct or carbon flux to SA. For instance, coupling the oxidative and reductive TCA cycles provides more NADH for SA synthesis, and the engineered strain produced 111.9 g/L SA within 62 h in a 50-L pilot-scale bioreactor [37]. In this study, we applied to tuning the sulfur metabolism, polysulfides generation pathway, for specification, to improve its performance in SA production. The parent strain used in our study is PGC01003, in which sdh5 gene is deleted. In a previous report, its SA reached as high as 209.7 g/L when testing in a in situ fibrous bed bioreactor [38]. The strain constructed in this study, PGC01003 ΔmstΔrhod exhibited substantial increases in SA production. In a 3-L scale bioreactor, the mutant strain produced 64.5 g/L SA, representing a 37.8% increase compared with PGC01003.

Compared with PGC01003, PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod had greatly increased SA production. However, the SA/OD600 index was not obviously changed, evidenced by the 0.894 g SA/OD600 for PGC01003 and 0.885 g SA/OD600 for PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod in the 3-L scale fermenter. Metabolomics analysis also indicated that the intracellular SA amount was lower in PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod than that in PGC01003 (the ratio was 0.78, Fig. 5). Therefore, the increased SA titer was mainly due to the increased biomass. Analysis of mitochondrial characteristics indicated that 3-mst and rhod knockout led to decreased the number of mitochondria, which should be the reason why PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod showed lower growth rate during logarithmic phase compared with PGC01003 (Fig. 2). On the other hand, 3-mst and rhod knockout also led to increased oxygen consumption rate and ATP production of mitochondria, which confers the strain an extended logarithmic growth. In addition, high amount of acetate is toxic and inhibits cell growth, but 3-mst and rhod knockout dramatically decreased acetate production. For the reasons mentioned above, PGC01003ΔmstΔrhod displayed a relatively slower growth rate but longer growth phase and finally accumulated more SA than did PGC01003.

Polysulfides are critical to mitochondrial health [39, 40]. An important reason is polysulfides can quench ROS that generated in mitochondria [41, 42]. Therefore, knocking out polysulfides producing enzymes usually results in ROS increase. We observed the same scenario in Y. lipolytica. ROS have multiple physiological signaling functions in cells depending on their concentration [43]. Appropriate ROS level can stimulate cell growth and proliferation [44, 45], which may affect biomass and SA production. Although the deep underlying mechanisms still require further investigation, this study provided a new strategy in developing more efficient cell factories.

Conclusions

Polysulfides play a key role in yeast cells through maintaining redox homeostasis and regulating metabolic processes. This study demonstrated that disrupting the polysulfides metabolism of Y. lipolytica by knocking out the sulfurtransferase genes (mst and rhod) enhanced SA production. The mutant strain showed a 37.8% increase in SA titer (64.5 g/L in a 3-L bioreactor) and an increase in biomass compared with strain PGC01003. Further investigation revealed that the mutant strain exhibits disrupted redox homeostasis and mitochondrial dysfunction. The mutant strain showed a decrease in the number of mitochondria but an increase in ATP production and oxygen consumption rates. Transcriptomic analysis revealed downregulation of apoptotic genes and upregulation of cell cycle-associated genes, potentially contributing to the increased biomass of the mutant strain. This study demonstrates that manipulating polysulfide metabolism constitutes an effective strategy for engineering growth and metabolic phenotypes in Y. lipolytica.
