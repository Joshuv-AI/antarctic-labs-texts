Coaches’ perspectives on the implementation of injury prevention training in youth handball: a qualitative study
Authors: Karolina Lucander, Arlind Reuter, Alex Donaldson, Jenny Älmqvist Nae, Eva Ageberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInjury prevention programmes can reduce injuries in youth team sports, including handball. However, real-world effectiveness and impact of such programmes are limited due to poor implementation. To address this in Swedish youth handball, the ‘Implementing injury Prevention training ROutines in TEams and Clubs in youth Team handball’ (I-PROTECT) programme was co-created with end-users, including coaches. Despite tailoring the programme and its strategies to the context and needs of end-users, weekly adherence and programme fidelity remain low. Understanding coaches’ experiences of I-PROTECT may provide insights to revise existing and inform new strategies to support the implementation of injury prevention training in youth handball. The aim of this qualitative study was to explore coaches’ perceptions of the implementation and usability of I-PROTECT. Six coaches (two women), purposively recruited, participated in a 1.5-hour online workshop, including two group activities. They provided feedback on using I-PROTECT and identified challenges and driving conditions for its implementation. Data were collected via an online whiteboard, and audio and video were recorded and transcribed verbatim. The data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, guided by self-determination theory (SDT). Three themes were developed: (1) Navigating choices and chores, (2) Learning by doing and (3) Sharing is caring. Coaches responded positively to I-PROTECT and suggested strategies to enhance motivation for using injury prevention training, primarily focusing on app improvements, education and organisational support. Strategies that support coaches’ autonomy, competence and relatedness could be applied in future studies to support the implementation of injury prevention training in youth sports.Keywords: Adolescent, Prevention, Qualitative Research, Sports & exercise medicine, Implementation

Introduction

Handball is a physically demanding team sport.1 Among youth players, both acute and overuse injuries are common, with the majority affecting the lower and upper limbs.2 3 Injuries may result in long-term physical,4 psychological and social consequences5 for the individual. These effects may reduce participation and limit the health benefits of physical activity,5 highlighting the importance of injury prevention. Exercise-based injury prevention programmes can reduce musculoskeletal injuries among youth athletes,6 7 including handball players.8 However, their real-world effectiveness and impact are often limited by adoption and implementation challenges.9 10 To support long-term use of injury prevention training in settings like community sports, it is important to consider the sport context,11,13 ensure organisational support12 14 and recognise coaches’ key role in delivering injury prevention training.12 14

Barriers to implementing injury prevention training for coaches include limited confidence or knowledge,1014,16 low motivation9 10 and perceived increased responsibility.9 Additional challenges can involve insufficient organisational support,9 10 14 16 high staff turnover, poor timing of implementation17 and programme-related issues like being too time-consuming, inflexible9 10 14 or not sport specific.9 Implementation facilitators include stakeholder buy-in,9 awareness of programme efficacy, access to educational materials, integration into existing routines10 and top-down implementation support.14

Previous studies have evaluated the implementation of injury prevention programmes in youth team sports, such as field hockey,17 soccer/football,18 floorball19 and handball.20 21 The ‘Implementing injury Prevention training ROutines in TEams and Clubs in youth Team handball’ (I-PROTECT) programme was co-created with end-users to promote injury prevention in community youth handball.22 It includes handball-specific, evidence-based physical and psychological exercises, delivered via the I-PROTECT GO app,22 23 which includes separate modules for coaches, players, parents and club administrators. The goal is to integrate injury prevention into regular handball training24 using tailored implementation strategies.23 In this study, ‘I-PROTECT’ refers to the programme, app and implementation strategies, including online education.

The implementation of I-PROTECT was evaluated in youth teams (aged 12–16) across 18 Swedish handball clubs during the 2023/2024 season.20 Coaches were generally positive towards injury prevention training and I-PROTECT, with most coaches downloading the app (77%), over half attending the education (57%) and adopting the training (66%). However, only 22% used I-PROTECT as intended (≥2 or more times/week) and programme fidelity was low. Coaches found I-PROTECT easy to use, and more than 70% intended to use it next season. These findings suggest that, while reach and attitudes were promising, weekly adherence and programme fidelity were low.20 This highlights the need to further adapt I-PROTECT to become part of regular handball training.

To gain a deeper understanding of coaches’ perspectives on the implementation challenges of I-PROTECT, we adopted a qualitative approach. Insights from this study could revise existing and inform new strategies to support the implementation of injury prevention training in youth handball. The aim was to explore coaches’ perceptions of the implementation and usability of I-PROTECT, guided by two research questions:

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study, grounded in a constructivist paradigm, acknowledged the diverse experiences of participants and viewed knowledge as co-constructed through researcher-participant interaction, and shaped by the social and cultural context.25 The overall I-PROTECT project was methodologically framed by the principles of participatory action research,22 emphasising collaboration between researchers and stakeholders. A workshop format was selected for this study based on its flexibility and suitability as a platform to evaluate I-PROTECT. Workshops are commonly used in Human-Computer Interaction and Participatory Design to enable co-design processes, evaluate usability of digital applications and create alignment between stakeholders.26 This was especially important in relation to I-PROTECT, which was developed collaboratively with the Swedish handball community. This study was part of the I-PROTECT project,22 24 described in detail elsewhere,23 27 with a summary available in online supplemental material 1. The study adheres to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)28 (online supplemental file 2).

Participants

Purposive sampling29 was used, recruiting participants through a survey conducted as part of the I-PROTECT randomised controlled trial (RCT).20 Coaches who reported using the exercises during the RCT (n=29) were invited to a workshop to evaluate I-PROTECT. Ten agreed to participate, and one more coach was recruited via a follow-up email. Four coaches later withdrew due to impediment or illness, and one left the workshop after the introduction round for unknown reasons. Overall, six coaches participated in the workshop (table 1).

Patient and public involvement

Researchers and end-users (coaches, players, club administrators) co-created I-PROTECT.23 27 This study’s design and workshop activities were developed collaboratively with key representatives from the Swedish Handball Federation (SHF), leveraging on their expertise in engaging coaches and ensuring practical relevance and applicability to community youth handball. The results will be shared in a popular science report to improve accessibility for end-users.

Data collection

Coaches attended a 1.5-hour workshop over Zoom (through Lund University) in June 2024 and used a shared online whiteboard (Collaboard). Audio and video were recorded to support transcription and analysis. The workshop format allowed for interactive, hands-on activities and collaborative thinking, making it suitable for exploring perceptions, generating ideas and evaluating digital applications,26 while the online delivery enabled participation regardless of geographical location. The workshop was developed by researchers with sports sciences and human-computer interaction expertise, facilitated by KL and supported by two co-authors (EA, JÄN). The shared online whiteboard was used to enhance interaction between coaches, and two activities aligned with the research questions were included. A similar format and design were successfully used in a previous study on concussion prevention in handball.30 Details of the data collection, workshop activities and online whiteboard are provided in online supplemental file 3.

In the first activity, ‘Traffic light’, coaches provided feedback on three app features (sports medicine exercises, sport psychology exercises, and information), and the online education. We used an intuitive colour-coding system (table 2) adapted from the ‘Rose, bud, thorn’ technique.31 The activity involved brainstorming, adding feedback using sticky notes on the whiteboard and group discussion. This process was repeated for each app feature and the online education.

In the second activity, ‘Sailboat’, coaches shared their experiences of challenges and driving conditions for implementing I-PROTECT. The activity was inspired by previous work32 and adapted to better suit an online format. The coaches brainstormed challenges, added feedback on sticky notes on the whiteboard and engaged in group discussions. This process was repeated to capture coaches’ experiences of driving conditions. After the workshop, coaches had the opportunity to add information on the whiteboard for 5 days or contact researchers by phone or email. However, no one used these options.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, following the six-step approach of Braun and Clarke.33 Reflexive thematic analysis is a theoretically flexible approach to the qualitative analysis of data and well-suited for sports and exercise research.34 The method recognises the researcher’s active role and subjectivity as strengths, making it particularly appropriate for this study, given I-PROTECT was co-created with end-users.24

Step 1: familiarisation

The first author (KL) reviewed the audio recording, noting key observations such as strong statements and contradictions. The recording was transcribed verbatim using AI software (Open AI’s Whisper) and verified for accuracy by KL. Feedback from sticky notes was compiled and organised by KL and incorporated as anonymous quotes during coding and analysis.

Step 2: coding

KL conducted initial coding in NVivo (V.14) using an inductive approach to capture all relevant findings, using both latent and semantic codes. Co-authors (AR, EA, JÄN) provided input and review to ensure methodological rigour and to support the interpretation of deeper meanings of participants’ statements.

Step 3: generating themes

A deductive orientation was applied to the interpretation of data, guided by the framework of self-determination theory (SDT).35 The three domains of SDT (autonomy, competence and relatedness) served as a structure for developing, reviewing and revising themes throughout the analysis. This enabled a deeper understanding of participants’ experiences of I-PROTECT in relation to motivation, offering insights that may inform future clinical implications focused on motivational factors. The codes were examined and preliminary organised under the SDT domains by KL, while remaining open to patterns not aligning with SDT.

Step 4: reviewing themes

Co-authors (KL, AR, EA, JÄN) reviewed the structure of themes, organisation of codes and interpretation of the quotes. The researchers’ expertise in clinical physiotherapy and sport, and research experience in physiotherapy, sports science, health promotion and interaction design, enabled analysis from multiple perspectives, promoting reflection and interpretation. During this process, co-authors discussed and collaboratively refined the potential themes, ensuring they were grounded in the data and relevant to the research questions.

Step 5: revising themes

KL defined and named the themes, with input from EA and JÄN, to ensure clarity and contextual relevance. Themes and preliminary findings were also discussed with colleagues in a research group in sport sciences, to confirm that the terminology was meaningful within research, clinical physiotherapy and the sport community.

Step 6: writing a report

The first author (KL) selected representative quotes to illustrate each theme, with input from all co-authors. The names of coaches, teams and clubs were pseudonymised to ensure anonymity. Quotes were translated from Swedish to English using Microsoft Word’s ‘Translate’ feature and reviewed for accuracy.

Results

Drawing on the narrative of lessons learnt from I-PROTECT, three themes were developed, aligned with the SDT domains (autonomy, competence, relatedness): (1) Navigating choices and chores, (2) Learning by doing and (3) Sharing is caring.

Theme 1. Navigating choices and chores

This theme highlights the role of autonomy in coaches’ experiences with I-PROTECT. Key findings include the need for a simpler introduction, greater variety and structure in app content and introducing organisational requirements. These insights underscore the importance of creating conditions that support coaches’ autonomy to enhance the implementation of injury prevention training.

Simple start supports self-directed use

…There was no interest in using the app. … It’s way too new and it’s too complicated so I don’t bother with it. …Then you get the [information] email… ‘I’ve just worked a whole day and I can’t be bothered with this.’ … (C1) (Sailboat)

Coaches faced difficulties adopting I-PROTECT and the app due to organisational and technical issues, as well as the voluntary nature of their role. On the organisational side, a poor initial communication and lack of support from club administrators were considered challenges to the implementation of I-PROTECT, which may have reduced coaches’ motivation (Sailboat). On the technical side, the start-up process with the app was expressed as a challenge and considered to be complicated and overwhelming (Sailboat, Traffic light). The coaches’ onboarding process, including online education and gaining access codes, along with app crashes, contributed to the perception that the app was more of a research project than a practical tool (Traffic light). Coaches were familiar with using apps and other educational materials to support their injury prevention efforts, which made them expect that I-PROTECT should be just as intuitive, self-explanatory and easy to use. Previous use of training apps could theoretically facilitate the adoption of an app-based programme. However, unmet expectations led to frustration and preference for competitive apps. In Swedish youth handball, coaching roles are often filled by parents and unpaid volunteers, who have limited time to dedicate to additional responsibilities and tasks. In this context, it becomes crucial to provide coaches with simple and easy access to evidence-based injury prevention training, rather than having them rely on generic training apps or media accounts. To support self-directed use and minimise the reliance on external support, a simple introduction and easy access to the app may further support coaches’ autonomy and motivation to implement I-PROTECT.

Variety and structure encourage informed decisions

… It’s good [the app], but it needs to be better and have more variety. …You need to modify it a little based on what you want to do. … (C6) (Sailboat)

Coaches were generally positive about the exercises in the app (Traffic light). However, a greater variety of exercise options was identified as a driving factor for successful implementation (Sailboat). In addition, coaches suggested adding searchable ‘tags’ or categories, for example, ‘rubber band exercises’, so that they could easily find and select exercises they prefer to use. They also requested the ability to create and modify their own training programmes, as well as having training programmes with randomly selected exercises, for example, a ‘scramble function’ (Traffic light). Since coaches have limited time to prepare for handball training sessions, predefined programmes could serve as a simple and preferred option. However, the discussion made it clear that coaches seek to actively take part in decisions regarding injury prevention training and show preference for certain exercises over others. Such preferences are likely influenced by the coaches’ confidence in using specific exercises or a perception of what suits their team. These exercises are more likely to be implemented in practice and sustained over time. Improving the app’s interface to offer a clearer overview and easier navigation could improve the usability of the app and empower coaches to make their own decisions in the selection of exercises. These additions could support both structured training planning and spontaneous sessions, which may foster a sense of ownership of the training, and increase the motivation to use the app.

External requirements promote uptake

…I think it’s more important that all clubs have some kind of plan. … Sometimes we are so happy that a mom or dad comes in to help so we hardly dare to say that they have to know anything. By setting some requirements…we provide coach education and we actually require you to attend it… (C3) (Sailboat)

At the sport system level, coaches suggested integrating injury prevention into club policies and making it mandatory, similar to licensing requirement for coaching in youth championships (Sailboat). They also wanted external requirements for injury prevention training, issued by regional divisions and the SHF, to ensure it is being used. This indicates that coaches view injury prevention training as a structural responsibility shared by the broader handball community, rather than an individual obligation. Coaches further highlighted the need for educational requirements for new or parent coaches as an important condition for implementing I-PROTECT (Sailboat). Since many coaches are volunteers with varying levels of experience, establishing clear expectations and requirements is important to ensure a consistent level of injury prevention knowledge across teams. Coaches’ request for external requirements likely reflects a need for organisational support and leverage, rather than a willingness to give up personal choice. However, making injury prevention education mandatory could reduce coaches’ sense of autonomy, potentially discouraging participation. While such requirements may promote adoption and short-term adherence to I-PROTECT, they risk undermining motivation. Therefore, future implementation efforts of I-PROTECT involving SHF must consider framing the programme in a way that supports the coaches’ autonomy.

Creating a user-friendly app that gives coaches freedom in decision-making seems crucial for adopting injury prevention training in youth handball. Providing more choice of content and improving the interface could help the coaches to feel ownership of the training. On the other hand, while the introduction of requirements may support the uptake of injury prevention training, it could be perceived as a chore and potentially reduce coaches’ motivation. Striking a balance between perceptions of choice and chore is therefore challenging and risks undermining motivation for injury prevention training rather than enhancing it.

Theme 2. Learning by doing

This theme offers insights into coaches’ perceived competence of using I-PROTECT. Key findings show that the app supports skill development, while coaches express a need for varied educational formats and strategies to engage players. This underscores the importance of tailoring both theoretical and practical education to strengthen coaches’ competence with I-PROTECT.

App content builds confidence

The app shows simple exercises that I as a coach can understand, whether I have been here for a day or have completed all the coaching education and played handball myself. It’s super easy to follow… (C3) (Traffic light)

Coaches voiced that the app was a helpful pedagogical tool, particularly because it included pictures and videos that facilitated learning and supported instructing others during training (Traffic light). The app’s content supported them to learn new skills and try out new aspects of injury prevention training, such as psychological exercises, giving them a mandate to work with previously overlooked topics. This indicates that coaches seem to recognise the value of psychological training for injury prevention and are positive about learning the skills needed to apply it during handball practice. Despite technical issues, coaches viewed the app as a modern tool for delivering injury prevention training, suitable for different coaching styles and youth players (Traffic light). Given the common high turnover of coaches in many clubs, it was considered a valuable resource for new coaches to quickly acquire injury prevention knowledge and skills. This could shorten the onboarding period, support continuous learning and promote long-term maintenance. Moreover, the app’s applicability across all experience levels underscores its value for the entire coaching community. While some coaches found the section of general information on injury prevention supportive, easy to read and useful, it was rarely accessed (Traffic light). This suggests that exercise features are essential for coaches, while background information is helpful but not critical. These insights could guide priorities for future implementation efforts. Some coaches believed the app could serve as a ‘one-stop-shop’ for information, education and injury prevention training. By combining these resources, the app can improve coaches’ sense of competence and motivation to adopt I-PROTECT.

Educational options support behaviour change

… As experienced coaches, we have our plans, our thoughts, our idea banks…Then we will implement this [I-PROTECT] in these, and then it can be difficult. Like you have to rethink and change your routines a bit. … (C1) (Sailboat)

Coaches found it difficult to generate interest in I-PROTECT among the coaching team and to maintain consistency in training routines. Experienced coaches were often less inclined to engage in I-PROTECT (Sailboat), possibly because they relied on established routines or believed they already had sufficient competence to deliver injury prevention training. In contrast, inexperienced coaches, often parents, were perceived as more receptive to adopting I-PROTECT. Although some coaches are well informed, they still need support to integrate injury prevention training into regular routines. Therefore, future implementation strategies should focus on promoting app adoption and be tailored to different levels of coaching experience. The online education was considered a familiar learning method and easy to integrate into the SHF educational platform (Traffic light). However, some coaches were unaware that it was part of I-PROTECT, and others were unsure whether they had completed it. Those who had completed it found it valuable and suggested repeating it to reinforce their skills. Coaches identified that opportunity and willingness to participate in coach education vary widely. To maximise impact, they suggested integrating I-PROTECT into existing coach education, benefiting those on formal education pathways, such as head coaches. In contrast, shorter stand-alone courses were considered suitable for parent or young coaches who may not attend formal coach education. Offering diverse education options for I-PROTECT, varying in length and depth, could enhance participation and support coaches to develop their competence for injury prevention.

Framing and hands-on guidance help players

It’s really very simple…It’s just to say that ‘Now it’s handball training and this is as much handball training, that it is physical [physical training], as it is that we’re passing a ball’… (C1) (Sailboat)

Coaches reflected on how to communicate the importance of injury prevention training to players. They recognised the link between injury prevention and team performance and suggested framing it as a way to enhance performance and stay injury free throughout the season (Sailboat). One coach suggested presenting the exercises as a standard part of handball training, rather than labelling them as injury prevention training. This perception highlights the need to position injury prevention training as an integral part of handball practice, rather than a separate activity. While coaches may feel competent using exercises during practice, they seem to lack skills or strategies to involve players in using the app. They reported that the online education lacked practical guidance on using the app (Traffic light). Differences between the player and coach modules likely contributed to coaches feeling less capable of assisting players, both technically and in exercise content. This underscores the importance of supporting coaches’ confidence to assist players effectively. Although the online format offers flexibility and sustainability in injury prevention education, it lacks the hands-on practice with the app. To build coaches’ sense of competence in guiding players in I-PROTECT, more concrete and practical training opportunities with the app are needed.

Using a digital format to learn about injury prevention training appears to be valuable for coaches in youth handball. Suggesting educational options for knowledge and practical skills acquisition reflects coaches’ wish to feel competent and confident in their injury prevention efforts. Going forward, focusing on the practical aspect of injury prevention, the actual doing, may enhance coaches’ motivation to use and promote injury prevention training.

Theme 3. Sharing is caring

This theme highlights the need for greater collaboration among coaches to support the use of I-PROTECT. Key findings include sharing app content, promoting peer support and strengthening organisational support. These insights emphasise the importance of social connection and collaboration for implementing I-PROTECT and fostering a safe sport environment.

Open access promotes teamwork

I got my players to download the app. But when we realised that we…didn’t have the same things [content] then it became very difficult for me… So, it broke down pretty quickly so they didn’t use the app…. (C2) (Traffic light)

Coaches found the app’s exercises inspirational, sports related and encouraging team spirit (Traffic light). However, differences in exercise content and interface between the coach and player modules led to confusion and reduced motivation. Coaches believed that access to the player module and exercise sharing through the app could improve collaboration with players (Sailboat). They also felt that players needed more hands-on support to start using the app (Sailboat). Other challenges included navigation and login issues, too much text and a preference for other apps (eg, TikTok, Snapchat) (Traffic light). Some coaches considered the app unnecessary for certain players, either due to their younger age or because they played recreationally, and therefore chose not to promote it (Traffic light). This view reflects the nature of youth handball, where participation often emphasises enjoyment and camaraderie over elite performance, raising questions about the relevance of the app for younger players. Nevertheless, creating better opportunities for collaboration through shared content could strengthen coaches’ sense of relatedness and motivation to actively promote the app to players.

Peer support enhances motivation

… To sit together and look at things is a little easier than just having it dumped in your lap. …It has been one of the benefits from TU, I think, is the dialogue between us coaches. … (C3) (Sailboat)

Coaches expressed a desire for greater collaboration and mutual support in injury prevention training, both in educational settings and practical application (Traffic light, Sailboat). They suggested using collaborative and peer learning approaches for I-PROTECT, similar to methods used in formal coach education (Tränarutbildning (TU) in Swedish). Coaches emphasised practical training ideas, including sharing personal experiences, providing hands-on advice for player involvement and how to effectively integrate exercises into handball practice. They also wanted to share exercises and collaborate across teams (Traffic light), indicating a desire for support from the broader coaching community within the club, particularly from those with similar views or responsibilities related to injury prevention training. This suggests that coaches trust one another and value their peers’ opinions, insights that can be further developed in future implementation efforts.

In some teams, the app facilitated collaboration on training plans and shared responsibility for I-PROTECT, serving as a platform for cooperation. In youth handball, coach turnover is common; therefore, sharing responsibility helps mitigate the risk of losing valuable knowledge when coaches leave or switch teams. The findings suggest that creating opportunities to exchange ideas and practical advice on implementing exercises during training could further enhance motivation and increase coaches’ willingness to use I-PROTECT.

Organisational support empowers coaches

The goal is super important that we have healthy, strong youths and later seniors [players], and citizens for that matter who can actually take care of themselves… (C1) (Sailboat)

Coaches recognised the high risk of injury in handball and emphasised their responsibility for players’ well-being both in sport and personal lives (Traffic light). This highlights the importance of injury prevention training from a holistic perspective, extending beyond players’ handball careers to their development as valuable members of society. Reflecting the realities of community handball, coaches often assumed responsibilities beyond their traditional role, such as equipment repairs, financial management and providing psychological support to players. By doing so, they create safe spaces for young people to express emotions and discuss problems, building trust and a sense of community. However, coaches acknowledged that adding injury prevention training duties could be overwhelming for some (Sailboat). Moreover, many felt on their own in their efforts to implement I-PROTECT in their teams, facing little interest and support from other coaches and club organisations (Sailboat). This underscores that coaches play a key role in creating a safe sport environment; however, their efforts require support from other stakeholders, such as club organisations and parents. Future implementation of I-PROTECT could focus on strengthening organisational support for coaches and signalling that their contributions are valued. Such recognition may enhance their motivation to use injury prevention training.

Overall, sharing information, experiences and responsibilities appears to be important for coaches to successfully implement injury prevention training in youth handball. Opportunities for coach collaboration may reflect the inherent sense of teamwork found in handball and the basic need to feel connected to others. Making coaches feel cared for, valued and part of a coaching community may increase motivation to create a safe sport environment in youth handball, where injury prevention training is an integral component.

Discussion

This study provides in-depth insights into coaches’ experiences with I-PROTECT and identifies strategies to support its implementation in youth community handball. Coaches viewed I-PROTECT positively and considered the app a valuable pedagogical tool, relevant to their coaching style and youth players. However, a poor introduction, technical issues and separate modules for players and coaches hindered its use. Coaches highlighted the need for app improvements, coach education options and stronger organisational support in future implementation strategies.

Autonomy-supportive strategies

Coaches wanted a simpler introduction to I-PROTECT and easier access to the app. Despite perceiving the process of accessing the app as complicated, 78% of coaches in the RCT downloaded it.20 This is a substantially higher proportion than reported in other implementation studies of app-based programmes for ankle sprains (2.6%)36 and lower limb injuries in field hockey (7%).17 However, these previous studies involved nationwide dissemination,17 36 which may explain the lower app uptake compared with our RCT. However, app downloads do not guarantee adherence,20 36 and coaches in this study, like in previous research,17 cited technical issues as a barrier to adopting I-PROTECT. According to diffusion of innovations theory, the adoption rate of a new innovation is influenced by several aspects, including relative advantage and compatibility.37 Preventive innovations often experience slower adoption rates due to the delay in potential advantage.37 In the case of injury prevention, this advantage is the reduced rate of injuries. Also, if an innovation, such as an app, is compatible with users’ values or needs, it is more likely to be adopted.37 When applied to I-PROTECT, this suggests slow adoption is expected and highlights the importance of emphasising more immediate advantages of adoption (eg, improvements in athletic performance), and ensuring the app is user-friendly and supports coaches’ needs.

Coaches wanted more freedom to choose exercises and training programmes in the app. This finding is supported by multiple previous implementation studies across various sports and programmes that have reported coaches’ preference for flexible and adaptable injury prevention training programmes.17 19 23 38 Although the app offered 68 exercises and adjustable programmes,23 coaches may have been unaware of the range of options, possibly due to the app’s current interface. An improved app interface with a clear overview, structure and greater autonomy and flexibility in choosing exercise options may enhance motivation to engage in injury prevention training and should be considered when developing future app-based programmes.

Appointing a club administrator as the first point of contact was a deliberate implementation strategy, chosen in collaboration with SHF to reflect typical conditions in Swedish community handball.27 However, high staff turnover is common in community sports, which makes relying on one person a potential weakness for implementation. Coaches suggested clubs develop formal strategies and plans to prioritise injury prevention and facilitate its integration within the club, reducing the risks linked to staff turnover. According to SDT,35 external demands, such as official guidelines, can enhance motivation if coaches or clubs have autonomy in how they apply such guidelines. Therefore, future implementation efforts in community handball should consider making injury prevention training a club-level requirement and framing it in a way that supports the coaches’ autonomy.

Competence-supportive strategies

The app served as a valuable resource, offering video-based learning, which coaches considered pedagogically sound, and a modern tool with potential for building competence. This positive view of app-based delivery of injury prevention training aligns with findings in football39 and field hockey.17 Coaches in this study valued the online education but found it insufficient for learning how to use the app and unclear that it was part of I-PROTECT. A similar outcome was reported among coaches in youth and senior football, where up to 19% accessed the digital material for the injury prevention programme Knee Control+.18 This highlights the need to clearly communicate the purpose and value of online resources for enhancing knowledge. While end-users co-created the app content, they were not involved in developing the online education. Involving them in this process could be a valuable strategy for future implementation.

Coaches identified three types of handball coaches—new, parent/young and experienced—and recommended tailored educational strategies for each. For new coaches on the SHF formal education pathway, they suggested integrating injury prevention training into the existing coach education. This proposal aligns with suggestions from a recent systematic review14 and prior research in handball,40 field hockey,17 soccer39 41 and Australian football.42 Integrating injury prevention into coach education has the potential to establish it as a fundamental skill, highlighting its importance alongside other core coaching competencies. However, the responsibility for initiating such integration lies with sport federations, which must actively develop and implement it. For parent or young coaches, shorter evening or weekend injury prevention courses were suggested to engage those with limited time or interest in formal coach education. A recent scoping review reported that 90% of injury prevention programmes for youth coaches consist of a single session or workshop, lasting between 30 min and 6 hours,15 indicating that shorter courses are commonly used to educate coaches. In Danish youth handball, adding on-site education to online training did not improve adherence to injury prevention training or reduce players’ injury risk.21 However, online education remains a cost-effective21 and feasible20 option for educating parent/young coaches. Experienced coaches were identified as less motivated to adopt injury prevention training due to established coaching habits. This aligns with a previous study in which ‘know-it-all’ coaches were identified as barriers to the implementation of injury prevention training.17 This coaching group may require different educational strategies to effectively motivate them to implement injury prevention training, rather than simply attending more courses. Future studies could explore strategies like mentorship or role modelling by high-profile coaches or teams, as relatable role models may motivate coaches to implement injury prevention training.43

Coaches expressed a need to better communicate the benefits of injury prevention training to players. Knowledge of injury risk and awareness of the preventive effects and performance benefits are commonly reported as facilitating factors to athlete adoption of injury prevention programmes.9 10 14 In contrast, youth handball coaches have suggested that promoting injury prevention training should focus on participation and having fun, rather than enhancing performance.40 In this study, coaches suggested framing injury prevention training simply as ‘handball training’ to establish it as a core part of handball practice. This aligns with the overall goal of I-PROTECT,44 and future implementation strategies should have greater SHF involvement to facilitate this integration.

Relatedness-supportive strategies

Coaches expressed that the app’s exercises encouraged teamwork. However, end-user-specific modules, an unsatisfactory interface and login challenges hindered player engagement. To our knowledge, this is the first injury prevention app with tailored content for players, coaches, parents and club administrators.23 One implementation strategy recommended in I-PROTECT was for coaches and players to download the app together as a team.20 However, this strategy was either insufficient or not implemented as intended. To enhance coaches’ sense of relatedness and support collaboration with players and fellow coaches, future app-based programmes should consider providing coaches with access to player content and unrestricted sharing of exercises between coaches. Additionally, future implementation strategies should include practical tips and guidance on how coaches can support players to use the app and exercises.

Coaches highlighted a need for peer support, exchanging ideas and sharing experiences about the practical application of I-PROTECT in their training routine. This aligns with previous research in female Australian football, where coaches identified peer-to-peer learning and exchanging experiences as valuable strategies for enhancing their competence in injury prevention training.42 A systematic review reported that coaches prefer informal learning methods, such as learning by doing, self-reflection and interactions with other coaches or mentors, highlighting the importance of social processes in skill development.45 One example of this train-the-trainer approach was used in a concussion prevention initiative in American football, where master trainers educated ‘player safety coaches’, who then promoted safety practices at the youth level.46 A scoping review also reported that while some injury prevention programmes included peer coaching, hands-on practice and group discussions, they often lacked follow-up support, underscoring the need for ongoing feedback to strengthen coaches’ competencies.15 While ongoing support from researchers or health professionals may not be feasible in a community setting, peer coaching or support may offer a more sustainable alternative, especially in youth handball. Although online education can provide basic knowledge, it may not provide the practical training coaches need to implement injury prevention and peer-coaching skills. On-site workshops may help motivate coaches by fostering collaborative learning and a sense of relatedness.

While coaches play a central role in creating a safe sports environment, their efforts rely on support from stakeholders such as parents, clubs and federations. This aligns with previous research in team ball sports, where coaches identified organisational and federation support as facilitators to implementing injury prevention training.19 21 A recent systematic review proposed a top-down approach to be most effective for implementing injury prevention in youth sports, as organisations have the reach, resources and authority to influence clubs and coaches.14 Organisational support has proven crucial in country-wide implementation efforts in elite Australian football47 and amateur soccer.41 Although I-PROTECT targeted multiple end-users, including parents and club administrators, these components of the implementation strategy may have been underused, possibly due to insufficient communication or low perceived relevance. Coaches’ motivation to use injury prevention training could be strengthened through a shared commitment to player safety across all levels of the sports delivery system.12 Future youth handball research should explore club-level strategies and continue collaboration with SHF to support this vision.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this qualitative study is the development of potential context-specific strategies to enhance the implementation of injury prevention training in youth handball, drawing from the experiences and insights of coaches. There are some limitations to acknowledge, along with methodological strengths and considerations. First, while online data collection enabled participation regardless of location and minimised personal time and effort, it may have deterred some coaches due to technical requirements. Second, one researcher (EA) involved in the data collection served as principal investigator of the I-PROTECT project and appeared in the online coach education and written materials. It cannot be excluded that this may have enhanced the social desirability bias of participants’ responses. However, participants were encouraged to give both negative and positive feedback. Third, the small sample size may be a limitation. Time constraints and the voluntary nature of community coaching made it difficult to recruit more participants or schedule additional workshops. However, the sample included diverse demographics (gender, age, educational level) and coaches of both boys’ and girls’ teams. Small samples are common in qualitative research, which aims for saturation and transferability of findings, rather than generalisability through numbers. A small, information-rich sample can be sufficient when the study aim is narrow, participants are specific to the aim and theoretical perspectives are applied.48 This concept of ‘information power’ indicates fewer participants are needed when the sample is highly relevant. Indeed, for remote co-design/user experience workshops, between 5 and 10 participants are recommended as a sensible baseline to get valid usability results.49 Several strategies were used to enhance the study’s trustworthiness.50 To strengthen credibility, we applied data triangulation by including both audio transcription of the workshop and sticky notes in the analysis. Member checking was omitted to reduce the burden on participants. To support transferability and dependability, we provided rich descriptions of the participants, their context and the methods used for data collection.

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored coaches’ perspectives on I-PROTECT, and challenges and driving conditions influencing its implementation. Coaches responded positively towards the app and online education, indicating they found I-PROTECT relevant to their roles, contexts and youth players. However, implementation was hindered by challenges such as technical issues, a suboptimal user interface and insufficient organisational support. Coaches suggested app developments, the inclusion of collaborative educational formats and greater involvement from clubs and the federation to enhance adoption. These insights should inform future studies aiming to increase motivation for injury prevention training and support implementation in youth handball.
