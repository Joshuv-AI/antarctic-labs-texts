Detecting relativistic black hole collisions near a massive black hole
Yirong Fang, Changfu Shi, Jianwei Mei

Abstract
Relativistic black hole collisions are one of the most dramatic astrophysical events that can be imagined. They could provide the ideal condition for searching for possible new physics beyond general relativity. However, such events are presumably rare and difficult to occur under normal conditions. Black holes in a triple system can be accelerated to the relativistic limit and may harbor the chance for a relativistic collision. In this paper, we study the relativistic black hole collisions in a massive black hole background and the capabilities of several current and future gravitational wave detectors in detecting such signals.

II
Initial boost

The key difference between the collision of black holes and particles in a
MBH
background is that the black holes experience two stages of acceleration:

•

First stage: The acceleration is caused by the
MBH
when the two small black holes reach the collision region.
In this stage, the small black holes can be treated as point particles (The exact mass of the
MBH
is not needed here but we always assume that it is many orders greater than those of the small black holes).

•

Second stage: The acceleration is caused by the mutual gravitational attraction of the two small black holes when they are close enough to each other.

In this section, we study the initial boost that the two small black holes can acquire during these two stages of acceleration.

For the first stage, there has been much study on the motion of particles in a black hole background; see, e.g.
42
;
71
;
51
;
58
.
As a proof of principle study, we assume that the two small black holes move in the equatorial plane of the
MBH
with opposite orbital angular momenta, and we further assume that the collision occurs at the periastron of one of the small black holes.
In the following, we recall some basic results on the motion of a test body in the Kerr background, mostly following

;

In the usual Boyer-Lindquist coordinates, the Kerr metric is given by

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big(1-\frac{2Mr}{\Sigma}\Big)dt^{2}-\frac{4Mar\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}dtd\phi

(1)

\displaystyle+\Big(r^{2}+a^{2}+\frac{2Ma^{2}r\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}\Big)\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}dr^{2}+\Sigma d\theta^{2}\,,

The radial equation for a unit mass test body on an equatorial orbit is:

\displaystyle r^{2}\frac{dr}{d\tau}=\pm V^{1/2}\,,

\displaystyle E=-g_{\mu\nu}(\partial_{t})^{\mu}\frac{dx^{\nu}}{d\tau}\,,\quad L=g_{\mu\nu}(\partial_{\phi})^{\mu}\frac{dx^{\nu}}{d\tau}\,,

In this section, we always assume

So, prograde orbits correspond to

From (

The potential can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{V}=(E^{2}-1)(r-r_{a})(r-r_{p})(r-r_{3})\,,

In this paper, we do not expect that an unbound black hole has a significant velocity at spatial infinity.

For an elliptical bound orbit, we can assume

\displaystyle r_{3}=\frac{2px^{2}}{p^{2}-x^{2}(1-\epsilon^{2})}\,.

\displaystyle E=\Big\{1-\frac{M(1-\epsilon^{2})}{p}\Big[1-\frac{x^{2}}{p^{2}}(1-\epsilon^{2})\Big]\Big\}^{1/2}\,.

To quantify the boost, we need to find the momentum of each of the small black holes observed by a local static observer at the collision location.
This can be done by splitting the four momentum of each of the small black holes as follows.

\displaystyle P^{\mu}\equiv m_{0}\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau}={\cal E}T^{\mu}+P_{0}S^{\mu}\,,

where

For the cases we are focusing on,

\displaystyle T^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2M/r_{p}}}(\partial_{t})^{\mu}\,,

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad{\cal E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{0}E}{\sqrt{1-2M/r_{p}}}\,.

\displaystyle\frac{P_{0}}{m_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{E^{2}}{1-2M/r_{p}}-1}\,.

For the marginally stable orbits needed to reach

, corresponding to a speed

.

So, limited to the symmetric collision described above, the maximal possible boost ratio from the first stage is
P_{0}/m_{0}=1
.

The acceleration of the first stage ends when the two small black holes reach the region where their mutual attraction starts to dominate their acceleration along the collision axis.
Because the
MBH
is assumed to be many orders more massive than the small black holes, one can approximate the background spacetime as flat for the second stage of acceleration.
We further assume that the two small black holes are approximately infinitely apart when they first enter the mutual attraction region.

Because of the large speed of the small black holes at the beginning of the second stage, there is no reliable analytical method to estimate the additional boost that the small black holes can acquire.
For example, if one were to use the

\displaystyle+{\cal O}\Big(\frac{1}{c^{6}}\Big)\Big\}\,.

The second stage ends when an encompassing apparent horizon forms around the two small black holes.
For different momentum, the encompassing horizon forms when the two small black holes are at different proper distances apart,

\displaystyle\frac{L_{P}}{2m_{0}}\approx 1.88+0.48\Big(\frac{P}{2m_{0}}\Big)+0.27\Big(\frac{P}{2m_{0}}\Big)^{2}\,,

Waveform

Due to the symmetry of the collision, the amplitude of
GW
is zero along the axis of collision and is maximal on the transverse plane that intersects the axis at the collision point.
For simplicity, we assume that the collision is located on the line connecting the center of
MBH
and the detector, so that the detector is in the plane where the amplitude of
GW
is maximal (See Fig.
3

An illustration of the detection scenario considered in this paper.

Apart from giving the small black holes the needed initial boost, the background
MBH
also affects the propagation of the

Since the background
MBH
is assumed to be many orders of magnitude more massive than the small black holes, the geometric optics approximation can be used for the radiated
GW

56
.
In the leading order, the amplitude of
GW
is not affected by the
MBH
, but there is a gravitational redshift to the

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}\Big(1-\frac{2M}{r_{p}}\Big)^{1/2}(1+z)^{-1}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{0}E}{(1+z)\sqrt{1+(P/m_{0})^{2}}}\,,

where the
MBH
is assumed to be located at redshift

,
f_{0}
is the frequency observed by a local static observer at the collision point, and

is the

frequency observed by a distant observer at redshift
0
.

With this in mind, we take the following steps to construct the waveform:

•

Step 1: The initial waveform of the collision is calculated at the location of the collision.
Since the size of the collision region is many orders of magnitude smaller than the radius of the
MBH
, we approximate the background as a flat spacetime for this step.

•

Step 2: The waveform is redshiftted as in (
19
) to give raise to the signal that reaches the detector.

In the rest of the section, we explain in more detail how step 1 is achieved.

To obtain the waveforms at the collision location, we use the
CLAP
(

.
The method is based on the observation that, after a common horizon forms around the small black holes, the spacetime outside the horizon evolves as a perturbed single black hole.
The method has been shown to make predictions that match the numerical relativity results quite well

;
9
;
10
.
It has also been generalized to describe the collision of black holes that have initial momenta
8
;
16
;
69
.
However, when comparing the radiated energy predicted by

(e.g.

) with that by more recent numerical relativity simulations (e.g.

For example, when

If both calculations are correct, it indicates that a significant part of the radiated energy, some of which is possibly generated before the formation of the common horizon, is not taken into account by

Therefore, when using the waveform calculated using

III.1
The Zerilli equation

After the formation of a common horizon, the evolution of spacetime outside the horizon is described by the perturbation of a single black hole, which in this paper is a Schwarzschild black hole with the metric,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-X(r)dt^{2}+\frac{1}{X(r)}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,

(20)

where
X(r)=1-2m/r
, and
m
is the ADM mass of the black hole.
The metric can be split as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=g_{ab}dx^{a}dx^{b}+r^{2}\Omega_{AB}dx^{A}dx^{B}\,,

(21)

where
x^{a}=(t,r)
and
x^{A}=(\theta,\phi)\,
.

\Omega_{AB}
is the metric of a unit two-sphere.
The covariant derivatives associated with
g_{ab}
and
\Omega_{AB}
will be denoted as
\nabla_{a}
and
\nabla_{A}
, respectively.

Due to the symmetry of the collision, we only need to consider the even-parity perturbation of the metric, which is given by

\displaystyle h_{ab}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{lm}h_{ab}^{lm}Y^{lm}\,,\quad h_{aB}=\sum_{lm}j_{a}^{lm}Y_{B}^{lm}\,,

\displaystyle h_{AB}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\sum_{lm}\Big(K^{lm}\Omega_{AB}Y^{lm}+G^{lm}Y_{AB}^{lm}\Big)\,.

(22)

Here,
h_{ab}^{lm}
,
j_{a}^{lm}
,
K^{lm}
, and
G^{lm}
are functions of
x^{a}
.

Y^{lm}(\theta,\phi)
are sphereical harmonics, from which one can construct the even-parity vector harmonics,
Y^{lm}_{A}=\nabla_{A}Y^{lm}
, and the even-parity tensor harmonics,

\displaystyle Y^{lm}_{AB}=\Big[\nabla_{A}\nabla_{B}+\frac{1}{2}l(l+1)\Omega_{AB}\Big]Y^{lm}\,.

(23)

The perturbation is governed by the Zerilli equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi^{lm}}{\partial r_{*}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi^{lm}}{\partial t^{2}}-V_{eff}\Psi^{lm}=0\,,

(24)

where
65
;
76

\displaystyle\Psi^{lm}(t,r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2r}{l(l+1)}\Big[\tilde{K}^{lm}+\frac{1}{n+3m/r}

(25)

\displaystyle\times\Big(r^{a}r^{b}\tilde{h}_{ab}^{lm}-rr^{a}\nabla_{a}\tilde{K}^{lm}\Big)\Big]\,,

\displaystyle V_{eff}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3r^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{2m}{r}\Big)\Big(n+\frac{3m}{r}\Big)

(26)

\displaystyle\times\Big[1+\frac{n^{2}(3+2n)}{(n+3m/r)^{3}}\Big]\,.

Here,
r^{a}=g^{ab}\partial_{b}r
and

,
\tilde{K}^{lm}
and

are gauge invariant combinations of

,

,

, and

, see

Once

V
Summary

In this paper, we have studied the intriguing scenario of
RBHC
taking place in a
MBH
background.
The two small black holes are expected to undergo two stages of acceleration before collision: the first caused by the background
MBH
and the second caused by their own mutual attraction.

Focusing on the special cases that the two small black holes move in the equitorial plane of the
MBH
and that the collision occurs at the periastron of one of the small black holes, we find that the largest boost can be achieved when the background
MBH
is not rotating.
When the two small black holes are on a common circular orbit but move in opposite directions, the largest boost is about
P/m_{0}\approx 0.94
when the two small black holes are on the
ISCO
of the
MBH
.
When the two small black holes are on a common eccentric orbit but move in opposite directions, the largest boost is about
P/m_{0}\approx 1.42
when the eccentricity of the orbit approaches 1.
One can get the same largest boost
P/m_{0}\approx 1.42
if the two small black holes are on the same lowest scattering trajectory (assuming
E=1
) of the
MBH
but move in opposite directions.

We use
CLAP
to calculate the waveform of the collision.
We find that the difference caused by different initial boosts is most noticeable near the peaks and troughs of the wavefroms.
In addition, we find that for
P/m_{0}\lesssim 1
, the radiated energy in the (4,0) and (6,0) modes is several orders of magnitude smaller than that in the (2,0) mode.
It will be important to find out if more realistic collision scenarios, such as unequal masses, non-zero spins, and a non-zero impact parameter for the small black holes, can help significantly increase the relative contribution of the higher modes.

We have studied the capability of several current and future detectors in detecting the
RBHC
signals, including TianQin, LISA (Taiji),
CE
,
ET
and LIGO.
For future space-based detectors, the detection horizon can reach redshift
z\approx 8
(TianQin) and
z\approx 14
(LISA).
For future ground-based detectors, the detection horizon can reach the redshift
z\approx 5
.
For LIGO (assuming O4 sensitivity), the detection horizon can reach the redshift
z\approx 0.5
.
For TianQin, the expected relative precision of parameter estimation can reach the order
{\cal O}(10^{-2})\,
,
{\cal O}(10^{-2})\,
,
{\cal O}(10^{-3})\,
for
(m_{0},P,D_{L})
, respectively.
With favorable source parameters, LISA can achieve the relative precision of parameter estimation at the order
{\cal O}(10^{-3})\,
,
{\cal O}(10^{-2})\,
,
{\cal O}(10^{-4})\,
for
(m_{0},P,D_{L})
, respectively.
For future ground-based detectors, the expected relative precision of parameter estimation can reach the order
{\cal O}(10^{-4})\,
,
{\cal O}(10^{-3})\,
,
{\cal O}(10^{-4})\,
for
(m_{0},P,D_{L})
, respectively.
LIGO is about 2 orders of magnitude worse than
CE
or
ET
.
