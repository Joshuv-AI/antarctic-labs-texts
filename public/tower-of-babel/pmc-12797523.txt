The correlation between serum ECM1 and cardiac fibrosis in heart failure patients
Authors: Yanqian Xu, Zhongyuan Han, Qi Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic heart failure (HF) is characterized by progressive myocardial injury and remodeling. Extracellular matrix protein 1 (ECM1) and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) have been implicated in fibrosis; however, their clinical significance in HF remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between circulating ECM1 and LRP1 levels and cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis markers in patients with chronic HF.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 93 patients with chronic HF (LVEF ≤ 50%) and 80 age- and sex-matched controls with stable cardiovascular disease (LVEF > 50%). Clinical data, routine biochemical parameters, and echocardiography images were obtained. Serum ECM1, LRP1, TGF-β1, CTGF, and PICP levels were measured using ELISA. Statistical analyses included group comparisons, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and Spearman’s correlation with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple testing.ResultsPatients with HF had significantly higher serum ECM1 (157.3 ± 28.4 vs. 92.8 ± 14.7 pg/mL) and LRP1 (14.3 ± 2.6 vs. 8.7 ± 1.3 µg/mL) levels than controls (both P < 0.001). ROC analysis showed that ECM1 discriminated HF from non-HF patients with an AUC of 0.985, sensitivity of 91.4%, and specificity of 98.7%. Both ECM1 and LRP1 levels increased with worsening NYHA class. ECM1 was negatively correlated with LVEF and positively correlated with CK-MB, NT-proBNP, TGF-β1, CTGF, PICP, and LRP1. LRP1 expression was also correlated with LVEF, CK-MB, NT-proBNP, and fibrosis marker levels.ConclusionsCirculating ECM1 and LRP1 levels are elevated in chronic HF and are closely associated with cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis. These findings suggest that ECM1 and LRP1 could serve as novel biomarkers for myocardial remodeling, with potential utility in the risk stratification and monitoring of patients with HF.Keywords: ECM1, Cardiac fibrosis, Heart failure, Biomarkers, Extracellular matrix

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with cardiac fibrosis serving as a pivotal contributor to its pathogenesis and progression [1]. Cardiac fibrosis is characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins during pathological tissue remodeling. This process results in myocardial stiffness and impairs the cardiac structure and function [2–5]. Clinically, fibrosis can be evaluated using histological assessment of myocardial tissue or noninvasively via imaging modalities such as cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) with late gadolinium enhancement [6]. Although fibrosis is a hallmark of aging and myocardial infarction, the mechanisms driving its development vary depending on the specific disease [7].

Two commonly used models in HF research are pressure overload-induced cardiomyopathy and myocardial infarction (MI) [8, 9]. Pressure-overload cardiomyopathy models are employed to study fibrosis resulting from sustained hemodynamic stress and left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy [10]. MI models, on the other hand, are used to investigate fibrosis that develops following an intense inflammatory response and subsequent extracellular matrix remodeling in both infarcted and surrounding myocardial tissue [5, 11, 12]. The post-MI response is generally characterized by an early ‘inflammatory phase’ (approximately 0–3 days), followed by a ‘proliferative phase’ (approximately 2–7 days) involving fibrotic tissue deposition and angiogenesis, and a ‘maturation phase’ (beyond 7 days) marked by ECM crosslinking and vascular maturation, although the precise timing of these phases may vary among patients [13]. However, identifying biomarkers associated with cardiac fibrosis could improve early diagnosis, risk stratification, and targeted therapies for HF patients.

The molecular mechanisms underlying ECM remodeling remain incompletely elucidated, thereby constraining the development of effective therapeutic interventions for cardiac fibrosis. Extracellular matrix protein 1 (ECM1) is an 85-kDa glycoprotein encoded by the ECM1 gene located on human chromosome 1q21 [14]. This protein exhibits multifunctionality and interacts with a majority of other ECM proteins, thus enhancing their binding and facilitating various processes across diverse tissues. These processes include maintenance of the integrity and barrier function of the dermal-epidermal ECM [15, 16], regulation of T helper lymphocyte type 2 (Th2) cell migration [17], influence on inflammatory bowel disease [18], contribution to endochondral bone formation [19], promotion of angiogenesis and tumor progression [20, 21], and mediation of resistance to trastuzumab in breast cancer [22]. Although specific cell types expressing ECM1 have not been identified, they were initially discovered in the murine osteogenic stromal cell line MN7 and subsequently in TH1 and TH2 lymphocytes [17].

While ECM1 has been extensively characterized in dermatological and oncological contexts for its involvement in ECM organization, angiogenesis, and fibroblast regulation [15–22], these same mechanisms are also fundamental to cardiac tissue remodeling. Given that fibroblast activation and ECM deposition are central drivers of myocardial fibrosis, dysregulation of ECM1 expression may contribute to the pathological remodeling processes observed in HF. This mechanistic overlap suggests that ECM1 may play a previously unrecognized role in the initiation and progression of cardiac fibrosis.

Importantly, ECM1 interacts with low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), a multifunctional endocytic receptor involved in ECM turnover, lipid metabolism, and cellular signaling [23, 24]. LRP1 plays a pivotal role in maintaining cardiac tissue homeostasis by modulating fibroblast activation, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling, and inflammation [25–27]. Dysregulation of LRP1 has been implicated in the progression of cardiac fibrosis and HF [28]. Given the potential interaction between ECM1 and LRP1 in ECM organization and tissue remodeling, investigating their relationship may provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying fibrosis in HF.

Although several established biomarkers, such as galectin-3 and soluble suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (sST2), have been used to evaluate cardiac fibrosis and inflammation in HF, these markers primarily reflect downstream inflammatory and fibrotic activity rather than the upstream regulatory mechanisms driving ECM remodeling [29, 30]. In contrast, ECM1 and LRP1 are directly involved in ECM organization and fibroblast activation, suggesting a more proximal role in fibrotic remodeling. Despite their potential biological significance, the clinical relevance of ECM1 and LRP1 as biomarkers of HF has not been systematically investigated.

Therefore, this research aimed to evaluate the relationship between serum ECM1 levels and the severity of cardiac fibrosis in patients with HF and to explore the potential involvement of LRP1 in this context. Understanding these associations may contribute to establishing ECM1 and LRP1 as potential biomarkers or modulators of fibrotic remodeling in HF.

Subjects and methods

Study population

All eligible patients admitted to the Department of Cardiology at the Second People’s Hospital of Nantong between January 2022 and December 2023 were consecutively enrolled according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize selection bias. After screening, 93 patients with chronic HF (HF group) and 80 patients with stable cardiovascular diseases other than HF (control group) were included. The control group comprised patients with conditions such as hypertension, coronary artery disease without systolic dysfunction, or arrhythmia, all of whom were confirmed to have a normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF >50%) and no symptoms or signs of HF. The HF group included patients diagnosed with echocardiography within 48 h of hospital admission, defined as LVEF ≤ 50% [31]. The control group included 43 male and 37 female, and the HF group included 51 male and 42 female. The two groups were matched for age and sex. According to clinical manifestations, patients with HF were classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II in 38 cases, class III in 34 cases, and class IV in 21 cases.

All patients in the HF group received standard guideline-directed medical therapy according to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines [32], including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), β-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), and diuretics, as clinically indicated. Some patients also received sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors based on their clinical condition. Patients in the control group received routine therapy for their respective cardiovascular diseases (such as antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, or antiplatelet agents) but did not receive HF-specific treatment.

Inclusion criteria

(1) HF Group: (a) The patients exhibit symptoms and/or signs of HF, including paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, jugular vein engorgement, pulmonary rales, nocturnal cough, and post activity dyspnea; (b)The patients were classified according to the NYHA as II-IV; (c) LVEF ≤ 50%. (2) Control group: No symptoms or signs of HF, and their age and gender were matched with HF group, LVEF > 50%.

Exclusion criteria

(i) Patients with severe liver and kidney dysfunction; (ii) Patients with a history of acute myocardial infarction; (iii) Patients with acute HF; (iv) Patients with cerebral infarction or cerebral hemorrhage; (v) Patients with high fever, acute and chronic infectious diseases, malignant tumors, or immune system diseases.

Sample size calculation

GPower 3.1 software was used to calculate the sample size. The significance level (alpha error) was set to 0.05, statistical power (1-β) was set to 0.9, and effect size (Cohen’s d) was determined to have a median effect of 0.5 (serum ECM1 in the HF group was 50% higher than that in the control group). A two-sided test was chosen, with a 1:1 sample size ratio between the two groups. The calculation results showed that each group required at least 28 samples.

Collection of general and clinical information of patients

Upon admission, demographic and clinical data were obtained through patient interviews and review of electronic medical records, including age, sex, smoking or drinking history, comorbidities (hypertension and diabetes), and heart rate at admission. Height and weight were measured to calculate the body mass index (BMI).

On the morning of the second day after admission, 10 mL of fasting venous blood was drawn from each patient before the administration of morning medications to minimize metabolic and pharmacologic variability. Routine biochemical parameters, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), serum creatinine (Scr), creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB), and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), were measured. Echocardiography was performed within 48 h of admission to determine the LVEF.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from both groups in the morning using anticoagulant-containing vacuum tubes. The samples were immediately centrifuged at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 20 min. The upper serum was separated and stored at −20 °C until analysis. To minimize bias, all serum samples were labeled with anonymized codes, and the personnel performing the ELISA assays were blinded to the patients’ clinical information and group assignments (HF or control). All ELISA measurements were performed in duplicate, and mean values were used for analysis. Outlier results, defined as values deviating more than three standard deviations (± 3 SD) from the group mean, were rechecked and, if attributed to technical or handling errors, reanalyzed using stored serum samples to ensure data reliability. Serum levels of ECM1, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and procollagen type I C-terminal propeptide (PICP) were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (ECM1: ab246524, Abcam; LRP1: ab278128, Abcam; TGF-β1: DY240, R&D Systems; CTGF: DY9190-05, R&D Systems; PICP: ab272198, Abcam).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 software. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical data as frequency and percentage. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc tests. The least significant difference (LSD) test was used due to the limited number of group comparisons, providing higher sensitivity while maintaining acceptable Type I error, and Bonferroni correction was applied in select analyses to verify robustness. For data that were non-normally distributed or had heterogeneous variance, the Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (three groups) was applied. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, and for correlation analyses involving multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error, with only correlations remaining significant after adjustment interpreted as statistically meaningful. The ROC curve was used to determine the optimal cutoff values for serum ECM1 and other indices that distinguished HF patients from non-HF controls. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics and laboratory findings of the study population

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the HF group (n = 93, aged 62.45 ± 9.71 years) and the control group (n = 80, aged 62.08 ± 9.87 years) in terms of age, sex distribution, BMI, smoking or drinking status, or prevalence of hypertension and diabetes (P > 0.05 for all). However, compared with the control group, patients with HF had a significantly higher heart rate (86.01 ± 13.48 vs. 75.09 ± 9.41 bpm, P < 0.001), elevated blood glucose (5.85 ± 0.92 vs. 5.49 ± 0.81 mmol/L, P = 0.007), and increased Sc levels (81.55 ± 17.73 vs. 71.43 ± 13.54 µmol/L, P < 0.001). As expected, LVEF was markedly reduced in patients with HF than in controls (44.02 ± 4.32% vs. 62.06 ± 5.03%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, circulating cardiac biomarkers, including CK-MB and NT-proBNP, were significantly elevated in the HF group (both P < 0.001). Similarly, fibrosis-related factors, such as TGF-β1, CTGF, and PICP, were markedly higher in patients with HF than in controls (all P < 0.001). Importantly, both serum ECM1 and LRP1 levels were significantly increased in the HF group (ECM1:157.25 ± 28.35 vs. 92.77 ± 14.67 pg/mL; LRP1:14.28 ± 2.59 vs. 8.67 ± 1.31 µg/mL; both P < 0.001). These findings indicate that patients with HF exhibit not only impaired cardiac function but also elevated circulating markers associated with myocardial fibrosis and remodeling.

Comparison of serum ECM1 and LRP1 levels between the control and heart failure groups

Serum ECM1 and LRP1 levels were quantified using ELISA in patients with HF (n = 93) and control subjects (n = 80). As shown in Fig. 2A, serum ECM1 concentrations were significantly higher in the HF group than in the control group (P < 0.001). Similarly, serum LRP1 levels were markedly elevated in patients with HF compared to those in the control group (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001). To assess the diagnostic performance of ECM1 in distinguishing patients with HF from those without, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed (Fig. 2C). The optimal cutoff value for serum ECM1 was 122.9 pg/mL, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.985, with a sensitivity of 91.4% and specificity of 98.7%. These findings indicate that ECM1 demonstrates excellent discriminative ability comparable to that of established cardiac biomarkers, such as NT-proBNP, CK-MB, and LVEF.

The levels of serum ECM1 and LRP1 in HF patients with different cardiac functions

In order to explore whether serum ECM1 and LRP1 levels are related to the severity of the disease, all HF patients were divided into 38 cases of NYHA class II, 34 cases of NYHA class III, and 21 cases of NYHA class IV based on signs and symptoms. The differences in serum ECM1 and LRP1 concentrations among three levels of cardiac function were compared. The results showed significant differences in serum ECM1 and LRP1 concentrations between different groups. Using LSD for pairwise comparison, the serum ECM1 and LRP1 concentrations in grade IV patients were significantly higher than those in grade II and III patients, and the serum ECM1 and LRP1 concentrations in grade III patients were significantly higher than those in grade II patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A and B).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that circulating ECM1 and LRP1 levels are significantly elevated in patients with chronic HF and are closely associated with cardiac dysfunction (LVEF, CK-MB, and NT-proBNP), myocardial fibrosis markers (TGF-β1, CTGF, and PICP), and NYHA functional class. These findings suggest that ECM1 and LRP1 may serve as noninvasive biomarkers for assessing the severity of cardiac dysfunction and myocardial fibrosis, potentially aiding in risk stratification, monitoring disease progression, and evaluating treatment responses in patients with HF.

While ECM1 has been studied primarily in non‑cardiac fibrotic settings (e.g., hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis), emerging evidence supports the role of ECM1 in the heart. For example, experiments in aging and post-MI hearts have shown ECM1 expression in fibrotic, inflamed, and perivascular regions [16, 33]. Mechanistically, ECM1 may act as a matricellular protein, influencing fibroblast behavior, collagen deposition, and ECM remodeling [33, 34]. LRP1 has been studied less in HF but is known to modulate TGF‑β signaling and ECM turnover in other tissues, suggesting its potential involvement in myocardial fibrosis. Regarding PICP, a large study (n >1,000) in patients with HF demonstrated that baseline PICP predicted left ventricular reverse remodeling and clinical outcomes [35]. Our results, showing associations between ECM1, LRP1, PICP, and other fibrosis markers, extend this literature by identifying additional circulating proteins that may reflect fibrotic activity.

The strong positive correlations of ECM1 and LRP1 with fibrosis-related biomarkers (TGF‑β1, CTGF, and PICP) and their progressive increase across NYHA classes support the hypothesis that these proteins reflect the myocardial fibrotic burden. However, alternative explanations could be considered. ECM1 (and possibly LRP1) may not be specific to cardiac fibrosis but could represent systemic fibrotic and inflammatory processes. For example, studies on hepatic fibrosis have demonstrated that ECM1 modulates latent TGF‑β1 activation and is broadly implicated in fibrogenesis across tissues [34, 36]. Therefore, elevated ECM1 levels in HF may partially reflect extracardiac fibrosis (e.g., liver, lung, and dermal) or systemic ECM remodeling rather than being strictly cardiac-specific [37, 38], mitigating potential confirmation bias.

Importantly, this was a cross-sectional observational study and correlations do not imply causation. Elevated ECM1 and LRP1 levels may be consequences rather than drivers of myocardial injury or fibrosis; for instance, a failing heart could release ECM1 or upregulate LRP1 in response to injury. This limitation highlights the need for longitudinal and mechanistic studies to clarify these causal relationships.

Although it is tempting to speculate that targeting ECM1- or LRP1-mediated pathways may be therapeutic, such hypotheses remain speculative. Antifibrotic therapies, such as pirfenidone, are under investigation for cardiac remodeling; however, no mechanistic data currently support the direct modulation of ECM1 or LRP1 in HF. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than immediately translational studies.

The present study had some additional limitations. First, the modest sample size and single-center design warrant validation in larger multicenter cohorts. Second, mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether ECM1 and LRP1 directly contribute to myocardial fibrosis or serve as secondary markers. Third, multivariate regression analyses were not performed because of sample size limitations, which restrict the assessment of independent associations while controlling for confounders.

Despite these limitations, our findings indicate that serum ECM1 and LRP1 levels are promising biomarkers that correlate with cardiac dysfunction, myocardial injury, and fibrosis severity. Clinically, these proteins may complement established markers such as NT-proBNP, CK-MB, and PICP, enhancing risk stratification, disease monitoring, and individualized HF management.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that circulating ECM1 and LRP1 levels are elevated in chronic HF and are associated with worse cardiac function, higher myocardial injury, and greater fibrotic marker levels. These findings support the idea that ECM1 and LRP1 may serve as biomarkers of myocardial remodeling and fibrosis (Fig. 6). Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design, the possibility of systemic rather than cardiac specificity, and the absence of mechanistic data, the interpretation must remain cautious. Future longitudinal, mechanistic, and interventional studies are needed to clarify whether ECM1 and LRP1 are causally involved in HF pathophysiology, whether they are myocardial-specific markers, and whether their modulation could translate into clinical benefits.
