Humoral and cellular responses to SARS‐CoV‐2 variants after ancestral COVID‐19 vaccines in people with HIV and lung transplant recipients
Authors: David WJ Griffin, Paul A Gill, Irene Boo, Shir Sun, Raffi Gugasyan, Alina Wang, Scott J Bornheimer, Stuart Turville, Anupriya Aggarwal, Greg I Snell, Glen P Westall, Emily SJ Edwards, Anna Coldham, Menno C van Zelm, Heidi E Drummer, James H McMahon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesImmunocompromised hosts have reduced immune responses to COVID‐19 vaccination, and more severe disease. Antibody responses correlate with protection but markers of immunity vary across a spectrum of immunocompromise. We compared serologic and cellular responses following Ancestral COVID‐19 vaccines in healthy controls (HC), people with HIV (PWH) and lung transplant (LTx) recipients.MethodsAnti‐spike receptor binding domain (RBD) IgG, neutralising antibodies (nAb) and T‐cell responses were assessed one‐month post‐dose 2 and dose 3 of Ancestral COVID‐19 vaccination in HC, PWH and LTx. NAb responses to Ancestral, Delta and Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 variants were assessed.ResultsTwenty‐nine HC, 21 PWH and 12 LTx recipients were included. PWH demonstrated lower anti‐RBD‐IgG responses (median post‐dose 3: 80.3 μg mL−1 vs 43.3 μg mL−1, P = 0.03) to mRNA COVID‐19 vaccination than HC, while LTx recipients displayed diminished responses following any vaccine (15.3 μg mL−1 vs 74.0 μg mL−1, P = 0.01). Dose 3 increased anti‐RBD‐IgG concentrations and nAb responses in HC and PWH, though Omicron variant neutralisation was attenuated. LTx recipients mounted limited nAb responses. PWH and HC had no difference in nAb responses for Ancestral (median 1738 vs 486.2, P > 0.99) or BA.5 variants (median 34.0 vs 67.9, P > 0.99). Compared with HC, PWH and LTx demonstrated reduced frequencies of SARS‐CoV‐2‐specific memory T cells and a reduced functional memory T‐cell response in LTx.ConclusionAlthough Dose 3 was beneficial, LTx recipients demonstrated lower serological responses than HC, while reductions were modest in PWH. Immunocompromised groups had reduced but detectable SARS‐CoV‐2‐specific T‐cell responses, demonstrating the utility of COVID‐19 vaccination despite poorer serological responses.

Introduction

Immunocompromised hosts, including people with HIV (PWH) and solid organ transplant recipients (SOTR), have reduced immunological responses following vaccination, and poorer clinical outcomes following several vaccine‐preventable infections. 1 , 2 , 3 Early studies in the COVID‐19 pandemic indicated that the same was true following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, with PWH and SOTR experiencing attenuated serological responses following a primary schedule of COVID‐19 vaccination, and a higher risk of mortality following infection. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 These serological responses assessed by quantifying SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies specific to the spike protein by binding or neutralisation assays are the most widely reported correlate of clinical protection. 8 , 9 Previous work by this group highlights that PWH have a small but significant reduction in seroconversion and neutralisation following primary COVID‐19 vaccination, compared with healthy controls (HC), which was most pronounced in those with CD4 T‐cell counts of < 350 cells μL−1. 10 Similarly, kidney transplant recipients had reduced serological responses and a higher risk of breakthrough infection than their dialysis counterparts. 10 , 11

More contemporary evidence reveals improving outcomes for PWH and SOTR, associated with improved treatment, including the use of early and effective antivirals, and uptake of COVID‐19 vaccination. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 However, despite advances in the prevention and management of COVID‐19 infection, immune compromised hosts such as PWH and SOTR remain at higher risk of adverse outcomes, which is particularly so in more immune compromised SOTR such as lung transplant (LTx) recipients. 12 Compared with the general population, PWH experience higher mortality, chronic inflammation, burden of chronic comorbidities associated with severe COVID‐19 and a fourfold increased risk of long COVID‐19, which are reduced but not eliminated by COVID‐19 vaccination. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 In contrast, LTx recipients demonstrate baseline immune dysregulation, characterised by elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines, decreased expression of human leukocyte antigens on antigen‐presenting cells and diminished cytokine responses, leading to blunted innate, and humoral and cellular adaptive immune responses to COVID‐19 immunisation. 20 , 21 Consequently, LTx recipients experience a more than fourfold increased risk of severe COVID‐19, which can predispose to secondary bacterial and fungal infections. 12 , 22 Characterising cellular immune responses in these higher risk populations can give greater insights into protective immunity beyond the most commonly reported antibody correlates of protection. 23 , 24

These observations reflect a spectrum of immune function from HC to more milder states of immune compromise such as PWH on antiretroviral therapy and the more severe iatrogenic immune compromise of LTx recipients. To complement immune deficient states, monoclonal antibodies targeting SARS‐CoV‐2 have been manufactured but their activity has been limited by loss of activity as new SARS‐CoV‐2 variants emerge, leading to immune escape. 25 , 26 Hence, understanding and optimising endogenous immunity through vaccination remains a critical COVID‐19 prevention strategy in people with immune compromise.

Unlike many parts of the world, extensive public health measures and vaccine implementation in Australia meant that the community incidence of COVID‐19 remained exceptionally low or even zero until late 2021, after the widespread uptake of a primary schedule of two Ancestral COVID‐19 vaccines in early 2021, followed by a third dose later that year. 27 , 28 This presented a unique opportunity to understand the immunological outcomes of vaccination in different states of immunocompromise. Therefore, we sought to describe both humoral and cellular immune responses following doses two and three of ancestral COVID‐19 vaccination in a cohort of relatively COVID‐19‐naive participants representing a spectrum of immune states: HC, PWH and LTx recipients.

Results

Participants and clinical details

Thirty‐two participants, including 20 PWH, 11 LTx recipients and one person with both HIV and LTx, were included. Twenty‐nine HC were included for comparison. Key demographics at baseline are summarised in Table 1. Briefly, PWH were predominantly male, all were on antiretroviral therapy, four participants (19%) had CD4+ T‐cell counts < 350 cells μL−1, and one had VL > 200 copies mL−1. LTx recipients were older and more frequently female than PWH, and all remained on immunosuppression; most were prescribed tacrolimus, and all remained on prednisolone. Primary indications for LTx included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (3, 14.3%), cystic fibrosis (1, 8.3%), interstitial lung disease (5, 41.6%) and pulmonary arterial hypertension (2, 16.7%). One participant had combined heart and lung transplantation for Eisenmenger's syndrome.

Prior to study enrolment, no participants reported experiencing COVID‐19 infection. Two LTx recipients had received transplants within 6 months of baseline, but none had documented allograft rejection in the preceding 6 months.

All participants received three doses of COVID‐19 vaccination, with SOTR prioritised earlier in the Australian vaccine roll out than PWH. Participants received either homologous mRNA vaccination (two BNT162b2 followed by a third BNT162b2 or mRNA‐1273 vaccine), or heterologous vaccination (two ChAdOx‐1 viral vector vaccines, followed by either a BNT162b2 or mRNA‐1273 vaccine). Among PWH, specimens were collected a median of 29.5 (IQR 23.8–33.0) and 32 (IQR 28.0–37.0) days after Doses 2 and 3 of vaccination. The median times for LTx recipients were 29 (IQR 26–34.8) and 34 (IQR 33.3–40.5) days post‐dose 2 and 3, respectively. This was similar to that of healthy controls, with the median duration between specimen collection and Doses 2 and 3 of vaccinations 28 (IQR 27.5–29) and 29 (IQR 28–31.5) days, respectively.

Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2‐IgG responses following Doses 2 and 3 of COVID‐19 vaccination

Spike receptor binding domain (RBD) IgG concentrations post the second dose of COVID‐19 vaccine were higher following mRNA vaccines (med. 48.9 μg mL−1, IQR 28.9–91.8) (Figure 1a) than adenoviral vector vaccines (med. 8.7 ug mL−1, IQR 2.5–17.8) (Figure 1b) (P < 0.0001). PWH had significantly lower median concentrations of anti‐RBD IgG than HC following doses 2 (39.9 μg mL−1, IQR 11.8–47.3 vs. 90.0 μg mL−1, IQR 48.9–118.0, P = 0.01) and 3 (80.3 μg mL−1, IQR 56.1–111.1 vs 43.3 μg mL−1, 0–58.1, P = 0.03) of mRNA vaccination (Figure 1a). Vaccination Dose 3 increased the concentrations of anti‐RBD IgG in all groups, though LTx recipients had reduced responses compared with HC following both Doses 2 (0.4 μg mL−1, IQR 0.2–5.0 vs 31.7 μg mL−1, IQR 11.5–90.0, P = 0.01) and 3 (15.3 μg mL−1, IQR 1.6–55.2 vs 74.0 μg mL−1 57.9–117.0, P = 0.01) of either vaccine (Figure 1b and c). The fold change in RBD‐specific IgG between Doses 2 and 3 among PWH and LTx recipients by priming vaccine is shown in Supplementary figure 2.

Neutralisation responses against SARS‐CoV‐2 is reduced in LTx recipients

Neutralising antibody responses to Ancestral, Delta and Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 variants were assessed post‐dose 2 and 3 of Ancestral vaccine in PWH and LTx recipients (Figure 2, Supplementary figure 3). Among PWH, 18/21 (85.7%) and 16/21 (76.2%) had detectable neutralisation responses to Ancestral and Delta SARS‐CoV‐2 variants, respectively, following Dose 2, while 21/21 (100%) participants had demonstrable and higher median neutralisation titres following Dose 3 (Figure 2a). Although dose three increased the magnitude of neutralisation responses to BA.2 and BA.5 Omicron variants (Figure 2a), neutralisation responses to both Omicron variants were significantly lower than for Ancestral virus (Figure 2a). Fold change in neutralising antibody response between Doses 2 and 3 was significantly higher for ancestral virus than the BA.5 Omicron variant, but similar between other variants (Supplementary figure 3A). Following Dose 3, 4/14 (28.5%) and 6/14 (42.9%) of PWH had no detectable neutralisation response to BA.2 and BA.5 variants. Neutralisation responses in PWH were similar to those observed in HC (Figure 2c).

In contrast, only 1/12 (8.3%) LTx recipients, who also had detectable anti‐NCP IgG (Supplementary figure 1), was able to neutralise Ancestral, Delta and both Omicron variants following Dose 2 vaccination (Figure 2b). Following Dose 3, 5/8 (60%) had detectable neutralisation to Ancestral, 4/8 (50%) to Delta, 3/8 (37.5%) BA.2 variants, but 1/8 (12.5%) had detectable neutralisation to BA.5. Neutralisation responses to Omicron Variants appeared lower than for Ancestral and Delta Variants, but this did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2b). Neutralisation of the BA.5 variant was significantly lower in LTx compared with HC following Dose 3 (Figure 2d). However, the increase in neutralising antibiotic response to all variants was limited in LTx recipients following dose three (Supplementary figure 3B).

CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell responses to SARS‐CoV‐2 spike peptides in PWH and LTx recipients

Total T cells were evaluated in all donors following delineation of CD4+ and CD8+ lineages into naïve (Tnaive), central memory (Tcm), effector memory RO (TemRO), effector memory RA (TemRA) and follicular helper cells (Tfh) (Supplementary figure 4, Supplementary table 2). Notably, PWH had reduced proportions of CD4+ T cells and CD4+ Tn cells, but a higher proportion of CD4+ TemRA cells than HC (Supplementary figure 5). In contrast, LTx recipients appeared to have reduced proportions of CD4+ Tn cells and circulating Tfh cells, though these changes did not reach statistical significance. Conversely, PWH had higher proportions of CD8+ T cells than LTx recipients following Dose 2 vaccination, which normalised following Dose 3 and was comparable to HC. However, both PWH and Lung Tx recipients had reduced proportions of CD8+ Tnaive cells post‐dose 2 and 3 of vaccination, but higher proportions of CD8+ TemRO and TemRA cells (Supplementary figure 6).

To examine T‐cell responses to ancestral COVID‐19 vaccines, donor PBMCs were stimulated with pooled Spike peptides, enabling evaluation of SARS‐CoV‐2 spike‐specific memory CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell responses through detection of activation‐induced markers (AIM) CD69 and CD137 (Figures 3a and 4a). PWH had significantly reduced proportions of spike‐specific (CD69+ CD137+) CD4+ memory T‐cells compared with HC following both Doses 2 and 3, while LTx recipients had the lowest proportion of AIM specific CD4+ T‐cells post‐dose 2, which normalised post‐dose 3 (Figure 3b). Further sub‐group analysis highlighted that mRNA primed HC had significantly higher spike‐specific CD4+ T cells than adenoviral primed HC after Dose 2, while no significant differences were observed between PWH and LTx recipients who received mRNA vs adenoviral primary vaccination (Supplementary figure 7A). The composition of the spike‐specific CD4+ memory T‐cell compartment was also different. When compared with HC, both PWH and LTx recipients had lower proportions of spike‐specific CD4+ TemRO cells following Dose 3, while LTx recipients also had a greater proportion of spike‐specific CD4+ Tcm following Dose 3 (Figure 3d). LTx recipients showed impaired cytokine production within the spike‐specific CD4+ memory T‐cell compartment compared with HC (Figure 3f, Supplementary figure 9A), with a significantly reduced proportion of TNFα−IFNγ+‐expressing cells after Dose 3. PWH demonstrated cytokine responses to the same level as HC despite the reduction in overall numbers of CD4+ memory T cells (Figure 3f). The proportions of spike‐specific activated Tfh cells also remained similar across patients and controls after vaccination (Figure 3g and h).

Within CD8+ cells, PWH had a reduced proportion of spike‐specific memory T cells (Figure 4a) compared with HC following Doses 2 and 3 vaccination (Figure 4b). In contrast, LTx recipients had a low proportion of spike‐specific CD69+ CD137+ memory CD8+ T‐cells post‐dose 2, which recovered post‐dose 3 (Figure 4b). No significant differences in spike‐specific CD8+ memory T cells were seen between mRNA or adenoviral primed HC, PWH or LTx recipients (Supplementary figure 7B), though LTx recipients demonstrated a significantly higher fold change in the spike‐specific CD8+ memory T cells compared with the other groups (Supplementary figure 8). The composition of the CD8+ activated memory compartment (Figure 4c) was found to be similar between HC, PWH and LTx after vaccination, although the proportion of T central memory had a small but significant (P = 0.05) increase after Dose 3 in LTx when compared to HC (Figure 4d). The proportion of cytokine expressing (Figure 4e) spike‐specific memory CD8+ T cells was found to significantly increase in HC after Dose 3 when compared to Dose 2 (Supplementary figure 9B), predominantly because of an expansion of TNFα−IFNγ+ cells (Figure 4f). However, the expansion in cytokine production after Dose 3 appeared smaller in PWH and LTx groups and was significantly lower in LTx after Dose 3 when compared to HC (Supplementary figure 9B). The proportion of spike‐specific activated CD8+ Tfh following Doses 2 and 3 of vaccination in PWH and LTx recipients was all comparable to HC (Figure 4g and h).

Discussion

In a predominantly COVID‐19‐naive cohort of individuals spanning a non‐immune compromised population to PWH and LTx recipients, we demonstrated distinct immune profiles to SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination. The PWH and LTx groups experienced reduced concentrations of anti‐RBD IgG compared with HC, but the administration of a third dose enhanced antibody titres, particularly after a two‐dose course of adenoviral vector vaccination. Neutralising antibody responses were variable, with PWH demonstrating higher neutralisation of Ancestral and Delta variants than either BA.2 or BA.5 Omicron variant, which was enhanced following a third dose of vaccination. In contrast, LTx recipients showed limited neutralisation of all variants, despite three doses of vaccine, though dose three did enhance neutralisation of Ancestral and Delta variants. T‐cell analyses demonstrated quantitative and qualitative differences between PWH, LTx recipients and HC, with impaired activation of spike‐specific CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cells observed in both immunocompromised groups, which normalised with Dose 3 in LTx recipients but not PWH. Despite the reduction in these activated T‐cell memory populations, there was a consistently detectable functional response in all memory T cells with expression of antiviral cytokines, albeit with higher proportions seen in CD4+ as compared to CD8+ T‐cells.

The finding of impaired humoral responses in PWH and LTx is in keeping with previous studies. 10 , 29 , 30 All PWH and most LTx recipients developed detectable anti‐RBD IgG following Dose 3 of ancestral vaccination, but this did not translate to detectable neutralising antibody responses, especially in LTx recipients and to Omicron variants. A systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated that PWH have a small but significant reduction in seroconversion and neutralisation responses following two doses of SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination when compared with HC, 10 with a subsequent meta‐analysis demonstrating no difference in immune response between PWH and HC following Dose 3. 31 However, both studies were limited by substantial methodological and outcome heterogeneity. Studies have consistently illustrated poor humoral responses in SOTR, with LTx recipients demonstrating the lowest serological responses, which are not completely restored by a third dose of vaccination. 32 , 33 Others have also demonstrated the limited cross‐neutralisation of Omicron variants, following immunisation, seen in our study. 34 , 35 The ability to generate neutralising antibody responses is important, as neutralising antibody titres have been demonstrated to strongly predict protection from infection and severe COVID‐19 disease. 8 , 36 However, more recent data indicate that the importance of neutralising antibodies is variant specific, and while boosting may be beneficial, protection from SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is likely mediated by other components of the immune system, including mucosal IgA, memory B cells and T‐cell responses. 37 , 38 , 39

T‐cell responses in PWH and LTx recipients showed distinct alterations in activation and cytokine production following vaccination in our study. PWH and LTx recipients demonstrated reductions in the numbers of activated memory CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell populations compared with HC. However, there was a consistently detectable functional response despite this reduction in overall SARS‐CoV‐2‐specific T cells, particularly in CD4+ T cells in PWH, which were similar to controls. Memory CD8+ T cells for HIV and LTx groups and CD4+ T cells for LTx recipients showed reductions in this functional response compared to HC but still clearly demonstrated these functional T‐cell populations. Other data indicate the importance of T‐cell responses in protection from severe COVID‐19, hospitalisation and death, 40 , 41 and that T‐cell responses may still be detectable in the absence of humoral responses in a substantial proportion of SOTR. 29 , 42 Data from our study support this, as the reduction in binding and neutralising antibody responses particularly in participants with greater immune compromise and to the more recent Omicron variants contrasts with easily detectable functional memory T‐cell responses to pooled spike peptides. These cellular responses from ancestral vaccination could play an important role in protecting from clinical disease on exposure to SARS‐CoV‐2, including more recent variants where neutralising antibody responses are likely to be poor. 24 Moreover, existing T‐cell immunity may support B‐cell collaboration and isotype switching.

HIV infection is known to lead to chronic immune activation, persistent inflammation, with progressive loss of naïve s, with an increased proportion of terminally differentiated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (CD28−/CD27−) and increased markers of immunosenescence (CD57+). 43 Recent data indicate that this immunosenescence is moderated by antiretroviral therapy. 44 , 45 The loss of CD28 on CD4+ T cells reduces capacity for B‐cell activation, proliferation and antibody responses. In keeping with this, markers of T‐cell‐related dysfunction (i.e. low CD4:CD8 ratio and CD4+ CD127 percentage, and high CD8+ CD38+ CD45RO+ percentage) have been associated by lower magnitude of antibody responses and more rapid decline in antibody responses among PWH following SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination and boosting, by others. 46 , 47 We described of increased maturation in both the CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell compartments for PWH, evidenced by reduced Tnaive and increased effector memory (TemRO and TemRA) proportions. Therefore, immunosenescence and the reduced CD4+ T‐cell count in PWH may in part explain the moderate reduction in serologic response to vaccination compared with HC.

Conversely, several factors have been demonstrated to negatively impact T‐cell responses following LTx, such as recency of transplantation and immunosuppressive drugs, such as mycophenolate. 48 , 49 LTx recipients appear to have elevated proinflammatory cytokines at baseline, compared with HC, which wane with time after transplantation but are associated with decreased T‐cell and memory B‐cell responses. 20 In keeping with our results, the authors identified that LTx recipients demonstrated attenuated but detectable IFNγ and other cytokines produced in spike‐specific CD4+ T cells, following a booster dose of SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination, when compared with HC and correlated with nAb responses. 20 Similarly, Gao et al. observed diminished spike‐specific T‐cell functionality in SOTR compared with both PWH and HC at both 35 days and 6 months post vaccination. 42 Our finding that LTx recipients had a small but significant increase in the proportion of both activated central memory T‐cells post‐dose 3 of vaccination is an interesting one, not previously described in the context of SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination. We speculate that this could result from either the inflammatory cytokine milieu 50 or modulation of T‐cell receptor signalling by calcineurin inhibitors, such as tacrolimus, which may blunt T‐cell activation and skew differentiation towards Tcm, rather than Tem phenotype. 51 , 52 , 53 Indeed, haematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients have also been observed to have fewer TemRO cells after SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination. 54 While reducing peripheral memory T‐cell populations may be ideal in limiting T‐cell‐mediated organ rejection, it may limit effector memory function against pathogens such as SARS‐CoV‐2, including co‐stimulation of T cells, activation of B cells and antibody responses that are essential for host‐defences against infection. Indeed, spike‐specific T cells from LTx recipients had reduced IFN‐γ and TNF‐α expression. Tcm may generate a relatively delayed effector response on antigen re‐exposure, and reduced location‐specific protection, given their typical residence in secondary lymphoid tissues. 55

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size in both immunocompromised groups is small, and some participants were unable to provide blood samples after both vaccine doses, which limited the power to detect statistically significant differences between groups. Additionally, it limited our ability to explore predictors of impaired immunological responses within these groups, including the role of CD4+ T‐cell count, HIV viral load in PWH and individual anti‐rejection agents in LTx recipients. Furthermore, it also limited sub‐group analysis to compare the impact of mRNA and adenoviral priming on the serological and cellular response observed in healthy controls. 56 Although HIV and LTx participants differed in age and gender, work by others indicates that this is unlikely to have affected outcomes of this study. Studies describing differences in humoral responses between those over 65 years versus younger participants after Dose 2 of mRNA vaccine were ameliorated by a third dose of vaccine, 57 with similar results observed against the Omicron variant. 58 Moreover, female sex, reflecting two‐thirds of lung transplant recipients in our study, has been associated with stronger immune responses following vaccination, though data following COVID‐19 vaccination are less clear. 59 While the use of a mostly naive group of participants allows the study of vaccine responses in isolation, making this study more unique, it may be less generalisable to the current era, where there is a high prevalence of hybrid immunity in the population. Certainly, previous studies have identified more robust humoral and cellular responses, following infection than with immunisation alone in both influenza and COVID‐19 in transplant recipients that were comparable with HC. 60 , 61 Similarly, although this study demonstrates the potential benefit of additional doses of ancestral vaccines against Omicron variants, we did not explore the utility of bivalent or monovalent Omicron variant vaccines, which are now available. Furthermore, this study did not investigate cellular responses to variant spike epitopes, though work by others suggests that these are preserved, but at lower magnitudes. 62

PWH and LTx demonstrate impaired serological responses to SARS‐CoV‐2 following Doses 2 and 3 of Ancestral COVID‐19 vaccination compared with HC. While the third vaccine dose enhanced antibody titres and neutralisation responses to all variants, LTx recipients showed limited neutralisation capacity, and the benefit of Dose 3 was notably insufficient against Omicron variants in both cohorts. However, PWH and LTx recipients showed some reduction in memory T‐cell responses, yet consistently detectable expression of cytokines that are crucial in providing an effective immune response to viral infections. This study supports the need for better understanding of vaccine responses in different immunocompromised hosts and supports the benefit of initial vaccination in these populations even in settings of an evolving viral pathogen. Longitudinal study of humoral and cellular immune responses and the development of tailored vaccination strategies, including the use of booster doses can help optimise protection against SARS‐CoV‐2 for vulnerable populations.

Methods

Participant recruitment

We enrolled PWH, LTx recipients and HC without immune compromise at the Alfred Hospital in Melbourne, Australia. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with HIV and/or a history of LTx planning to initiate COVID‐19 vaccination, and able to provide informed consent. PWH and LTx participants were recruited from ambulatory specialty clinics and by promotion within the Alfred Hospital between 1 March and 31 August 2021. HC without conditions causing immune compromise or receiving immunosuppressing medications were recruited in parallel from February to April 2021, as described previously. 56 , 63 All participants were followed until 1 month after their third dose of SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination, during a period in Australia where the incidence of COVID‐19 infection was low. Those unwilling or unable to consent, or with contraindications to SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination were excluded.

Baseline clinical information was collected from participant electronic medical records, including age, sex and administered COVID‐19 vaccine type. Additional data for PWH included date of diagnosis, CD4 nadir, antiretroviral therapy and most recent HIV viral load and CD4 count. In contrast, LTx recipient data included date of transplantation and baseline immunosuppression, including tacrolimus level.

This study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Alfred Health human research ethics committee, project numbers 182/20 and 72794.

Specimen collection and processing

Participants underwent venepuncture for collection of up to 36 mL of blood for plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) at baseline, prior to SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination, and 28 ± 7 days following Doses 2 and 3 of vaccination. Whole blood was centrifuged at 1000× g for 10 min at 22°C–24°C then plasma was removed within 5 mm of the buffy coat. Plasma aliquots were transferred to 2‐mL cryovials for long‐term storage at −80°C. Plasma was separated and utilised for the measurement of anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 nucleocapsid (NCP) and spike receptor binding domain (RBD) IgG, and viral neutralisation assays. PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll–Paque density centrifugation and cryopreservation, and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 receptor binding domain and nucleocapsid IgG

SARS‐CoV‐2 Ancestral (WH1) spike RBD and NCP proteins were recombinantly produced as described previously, 37 , 56 , 64 , 65 and used for ELISAs for quantitation of virus‐specific IgG. 96‐well EIA‐RIA plates (Corning Incorporated, Costar, St Louis, MO) were coated with 2 μg mL−1 recombinant protein overnight at 4°C. Separate wells were serially diluted with recombinant human IgG (in‐house made human IgG1 Rituximab), to generate a standard curve for IgG level quantitation. Plates were then blocked with 3% BSA in PBS and subsequently incubated with plasma samples diluted 1:300 for quantitation of RBD‐ or NCP‐specific IgG post‐dose 2 and post‐dose 3 of COVID‐19 vaccination. Antigen‐specific IgG was detected using polyclonal rabbit anti‐human IgG HRP (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). ELISA plates were developed using TMB solution (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and the reaction was stopped with 1 m HCl. Absorbance (OD450 nm) was measured using a Multiskan Microplate Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HC SARS‐CoV‐2 binding IgG data is previously published. 56 , 64 , 65

Measurement of plasma ancestral and variant SARS‐CoV‐2 neutralisation

Neutralising antibodies against Ancestral, Delta and Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 in plasma from PWH and LTx recipients at baseline, and 1 month following Doses 2 and 3 of ancestral SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination were assessed. Neutralisation was measured in plasma from LTx recipients using SARS‐CoV‐2 retroviral pseudotyped viruses and 293 T‐ACE2 cell line as described previously. 64 , 66 , 67 In PWH, given the observed inhibition of the retroviral pseudovirus assays as a result of the presence of antiretrovirals in plasma samples from PWH described by others, 68 neutralisation was assessed using a live SARS‐CoV‐2 microneutralisation assay as described. 69 , 70 The percentage virus entry was calculated as described previously and plotted against reciprocal plasma dilution using the GraphPad Prism 10 Software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) and curves fitted with a one‐site specific binding Hill plot. 64 , 66 Summary estimates of neutralisation for an individual were expressed as the reciprocal of the dilution of plasma required to inhibit 50% viral entry (ID50), calculated from the nonlinear regression line. The lowest neutralisation titre detected by the assay was 20, with neutralisation titres < 20 arbitrarily expressed as 10.

T‐cell activation‐induced markers assay

Spike‐specific T cells were enumerated and characterised based on a previously reported protocol. 63 , 71 Thawed PBMCs were seeded at 1.5 × 106 cells per well and cultured under three different conditions in a 96‐well U‐bottom plate: (1) Plate‐bound Anti‐CD3 (clone OKT3) and soluble Anti‐CD28 (clone CD28.2) monoclonal antibodies (mAb; both eBioscience; at 1 μg mL−1); (2) PepTivator® peptide (SARS‐CoV‐2 Protein_S Complete), research grade (3 μL of a 30 nmoL mL−1 stock; Miltenyi Biotec) and (3) Media alone (RPMI supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% Pen/Strep and 2‐Mercaptoenthanol). CD3 mAb was immobilised by coating wells for 2 h at 37°C and washed away before addition of cells. Three wells were used for peptide‐specific stimulation and pooled subsequently to acquire sufficient numbers of peptide‐specific T cells. Assay plates were incubated for 4 h at 37°C, and then for a further 16 h in the presence of Brefeldin A (3 μg mL−1, Golgi Plug®).

Following stimulation, cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 0.1% sodium azide and 0.2% BSA) containing brilliant stain buffer and Fc receptor block (BD Biosciences) to minimise background staining. After incubation for 10 min at RT, the cell viability dye (FVS780) was added followed by an antibody cocktail containing CD3 HorV500, CD4 BV786, CD8α R718, CCR7 BV605, CD45RA BV711 and CXCR5 BV421 (BD Biosciences) (Supplementary table 1). Samples were incubated for 20 min in the dark at RT and then washed in FACS buffer. Supernatants were removed and cell pellets resuspended in 200 μL of BD cytofix/cytoperm (BD Biosciences) to permeabilise cell membranes. Samples were incubated for a further 20 min under the same conditions and then centrifuged. Pelleted cells were resuspended in 200 μL of Phosphoflow Perm/Wash Buffer I (BD Biosciences), centrifuged and resuspended with the antibody mix containing CD134 PE, CD137 APC, CD69 PE‐Cy7, IFNγ FITC and TNFα PerCP Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences) (Supplementary table 2). After a 20‐min incubation at RT in the dark, samples were washed twice in the presence of Phosphoflow Perm/Wash Buffer I. The final cell pellets were resuspended in 200–400 μL of chilled PBS and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences) into FACS tubes. Samples were stored at 4°C prior to acquisition on a BD FACSLyric™ analyzer. Unstimulated and Anti‐CD3 samples had 1 million events acquired, while 2–3 million events were acquired for peptide‐stimulated samples.
