Summary

Qualitative and quantitative analyses showed that superresolution US enabled noninvasive evaluation of lymph node microvasculature in rabbits and that it can serve as a potential diagnostic modality for metastatic lymph nodes.

Introduction

Lymph node (LN) metastases in patients with cancer are associated with advanced tumor stage and poorer prognosis; the benefit of preoperative identification of LN metastases by imaging is that it allows clinicians to decide on appropriate treatment (1,2). Imaging findings on primary modalities, including US and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, that suggest metastatic LNs are marked enlargement and gross abnormalities in morphologic features, such as round shape and cortical thickening. However, most findings are nonspecific, which can be associated with hyperplastic LNs (3).

Primary tumors actively prime regional LNs by inducing a proangiogenic premetastatic niche before metastasis (4,5). Upon metastatic colonization, intranodal tumor growth triggers a robust angiogenic response, wherein rapid endothelial proliferation substantially outpaces the recruitment of supporting pericytes (6,7). Therefore, this tumor-induced neovasculature exhibits severe structural abnormalities, including high vessel density, tortuosity, and loss of normal hierarchy (8,9). Visualizing these specific microvascular abnormalities could aid in the characterization of metastatic LNs (10). Although some progress has been made in the clinical assessment of LN vasculature, limitations remain. Color Doppler flow imaging is sensitive to fast blood flow only in vessels larger than 0.2 mm in diameter (11,12). Contrast-enhanced US (CEUS) makes it possible to assess the tissue and tumor perfusion by means of real-time perfusion imaging (13). The effectiveness of the enhancement pattern at CEUS for the noninvasive assessment of LN metastasis has been demonstrated (14,15). However, CEUS is restricted to visualizing LN microvasculature abnormalities by its relatively low spatial resolution at submillimeter scale owing to the acoustic diffraction limit (16). Furthermore, CEUS primarily provides semiquantitative parameters, constraining the accurate quantification of microvascular hemodynamics (17,18). These limitations highlight a clinical need for a high-spatial-resolution imaging technique for the noninvasive evaluation of LN microvasculature.

Superresolution US (SRUS), also known as US localization microscopy, breaks through the acoustic diffraction limit by precisely locating and tracking intravascular microbubbles, thereby enabling in vivo high-resolution microvascular imaging at the micron scale (16,19–21). By accumulating microbubble localization tracks over time, SRUS generates high-resolution maps of microvascular intensity, velocity, and direction, providing a powerful tool for quantifying microvascular hemodynamics (22). Recent pilot studies have explored the initial feasibility of SRUS for visualizing hyperplastic and metastatic LN microvasculature (23,24). However, different quantitative parameters are defined across studies, with substantial value variations, which may limit the characterization of LN status. Another issue is that in previous studies, core biopsy was used as the reference standard, making it difficult to establish a clear correspondence between an area of microvascular abnormality and a metastatic region within an individual LN (25–27). Furthermore, based on SRUS microvasculature maps, visual assessment (qualitative analysis) of nodal microvascular abnormalities, such as loss of hierarchical branching and tortuous microvessels, could directly indicate metastatic LN involvement. To our knowledge, however, previous studies have not confirmed this.

In this study, we hypothesized that qualitative and quantitative assessment of LN microvasculature using SRUS would help to differentiate LN metastasis from hyperplasia. The aim of our study was to compare the diagnostic performance of SRUS for detecting LN metastasis in rabbits with that of US and CEUS.

Materials and Methods

Animal Model

All experimental procedures in this prospective study involving animals followed the guidelines of the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments imaging protocol (28) and were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University (SYSU-IACUC-2024-000945). Female New Zealand white rabbits (age, 3.5–4 weeks; weight, 2.5–3 kg; n = 32) were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Animals were housed at a constant room temperature (20–26°C) and humidity (40%–70%) with a 10-hour light, 14-hour dark cycle and were fed a standard diet. They were randomly assigned to a hyperplastic LN group (n = 16) or a metastatic LN group (n = 16). For hyperplastic LN modeling, each rabbit received subcutaneous 1.0-mL injections of 10% egg yolk into the right second mammary gland (29,30). Metastatic LN models were established by administering subcutaneous 1.0-mL injections of VX2 tumor suspension (JNO-Q0030-1; Jennio) into the right second mammary gland (31–34). Rabbits without palpable solid masses at the injection site 2 weeks after implantation were excluded because of delayed tumor growth or implantation failure. LNs were palpable 3–4 weeks after modeling and prepared for imaging. See Appendix S1 for detailed procedures of model establishment.

Imaging Protocol

Each rabbit was imaged under anesthesia (maintained with 0.1 mL/kg/hr Zoletil50 solution; Virbac) (Appendix S1) using a clinical scanner (Resona A20; Mindray) equipped with an LM18-5WU linear array transducer for US and an SL10-3U linear array transducer for CEUS and SRUS; the latter lower-frequency transducer was used to better match the resonance frequency of perfluorobutane microbubbles (Sonazoid; GE HealthCare) and enhance signal detection (35). US was initially performed to localize the target palpable LN and determine the maximal cross-section, and US and color Doppler flow images were recorded. CEUS was performed using perfluorobutane microbubbles (0.04 mL/kg), and a continuous cine loop was acquired for approximately 60 seconds after injection (frame rate, 12 Hz; mechanical index, 0.129). After the initial 60 seconds, the SRUS protocol was immediately executed to acquire 5 seconds of raw data (frame rate, 500 Hz; mechanical index, 0.113). SRUS images, including density, velocity, and direction maps, were derived from SRUS raw data with SRCEUS Platform software (version 3.0; Mindray) (Fig 1B). Protocol details are presented in Appendix S2.

Qualitative Analysis

On US images, five qualitative features of each LN were reviewed (36,37): (a) shape (oval or round), (b) echogenicity (homogeneous or heterogeneous), (c) lymphatic hilum (present or absent), (d) cortical thickening (no thickening, eccentric thickening, or diffuse uniform thickening), and (e) color Doppler flow imaging vascular pattern (hilar, peripheral, mixed, or indeterminate). On CEUS images, three qualitative features of LNs were reviewed (38): (a) perfusion pattern (centrifugal, integral, centripetal, or asynchronous), (b) arterial phase enhancing pattern (homogeneous or heterogeneous), and (c) perfusion defect (absent or present). On SRUS images, two SRUS features were reviewed (8,37,39): (a) SRUS vascular pattern (hilar, peripheral, mixed, or indeterminate) and (b) chaotic microvasculature (absent, focal, or integral). Hilar vascular pattern was characterized by one or a few vessels entering through the LN hilum and branching radially into the parenchyma in a centrifugal direction, whereas peripheral vascular pattern was identified by vessels originating from the periphery of the LN and extending toward the hilum in a centripetal direction. If both were present, a mixed vascular pattern was established (Fig 2A). LNs exhibiting microvessel distortion with loss of hierarchical branching on SRUS images were defined as having chaotic microvasculature. If present, this pattern was further categorized as focal when distortion affected only a portion of the microvasculature or integral when observed throughout the entire microvessel network (Fig 2B).

Two expert radiologists (Q.L. and C.P., with 13 and 12 years of experience in US, respectively) blinded to group assignment independently reviewed all US, CEUS and SRUS images and assessed qualitative features (Fig 1C). Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third expert radiologist (J.Z., with 24 years of experience in US). The major qualitative features associated with metastatic LNs were identified, as described later in the Statistical Analysis section.

Quantitative Analysis

A radiologist (Y.H., with 6 years of experience in US), also blinded to group assignment, manually delineated the regions of interest of each LN on SRUS maps three times. These regions of interest were then analyzed by SRCEUS Platform software to generate SRUS quantitative parameters. For each LN, quantitative parameters derived from triplicate region-of-interest delineations were averaged for subsequent analyses.

Eight SRUS quantitative parameters were analyzed (22): (a) fractal dimension (FD), defined as vascular morphologic complexity calculated by a box-counting algorithm; (b) vessel density (VD), expressed as the ratio of blood vessel count to the LN total area; (c) flow-weighted vessel density (FWVD), representing perfused blood volume; (d) direction entropy, quantified as information entropy of flow direction distribution; (e) mean velocity; (f) peak velocity; (g) velocity variance, measuring variance of flow velocities; and (h) perfusion index, calculated as VD × mean velocity to reflect microcirculatory perfusion. See Appendix S3 for detailed definitions and calculations of these quantitative parameters. The relevant SRUS quantitative parameters associated with metastatic LNs were identified, as described later in the Statistical Analysis section.

Reference Standard

After imaging, rabbits were euthanized by intravenous overdose of anesthesia (0.8 mL/kg Zoletil50 solution). LNs were then resected under US guidance. The dissected LNs were marked with histocompatible ink to visually indicate the orientation of imaging; this orientation served as a reference for sectioning alignment with the maximal cross-section of imaging plane during tissue processing (Fig 1D). Dissected LNs were then fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sliced along the ink marking. A set of five consecutive slices were acquired per node, with a slice thickness of 2–3 μm. Definitive pathology reference standards were established on the basis of hematoxylin-eosin–stained slices, reviewed by a pathologist (P.L., 10 years of experience in LN pathology) blinded to group assignment. Metastatic involvement was defined as the presence of macroscopic tumor deposits (>2 mm) according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th Edition Staging Manual (40).

Immunostaining of vascular endothelial cells in LN was performed with CD31 antibody (1:200, bsm-4366M; Bioss) as a marker of microvasculature reference for calculating microvascular density (MVD), followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000, ab205718; Abcam). MVD was assessed using CaseViewer software (version 2.4; 3DHistech) in accordance with the Weidner method (41). For each LN section, five regions of high MVD (hot spots) were initially identified by the same pathologist (P.L.) at low magnification (×20). Subsequently, vessel counts were performed within each hot spot at high magnification (×40), and vessel counts derived from five hot spots were averaged as the MVD value (Fig 1D).

Statistical Analysis

Sample size was calculated using software (PASS 2023; NCSS). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies with percentages. Continuous variables are reported as means ± SDs or medians with IQRs, as appropriate, and were compared between groups using the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test. Interreader agreement of individual qualitative features between the two independent radiologists was assessed using κ statistics with 95% CIs, and intrareader reproducibility of three measurements of quantitative parameters was calculated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% CIs. κ coefficients were categorized according to Landis and Koch using the following criteria (42): 0.00–0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, good agreement; and 0.81–1.00, excellent agreement. ICCs were interpreted according to Koo and Li as follows (43): less than 0.50, poor; 0.50–0.75, moderate; 0.76–0.90, good; and greater than 0.90, excellent. Major qualitative features and relevant SRUS quantitative parameters were identified using univariable binary logistic regression analysis, with the odds ratios (ORs) corrected using the Firth method and internally validated using 1000 bootstrap samples. The diagnostic performance of major qualitative features and relevant SRUS quantitative parameters for differentiating metastatic from benign LNs was evaluated, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) serving as the overall performance measure. The optimal cutoff values of relevant SRUS quantitative parameters were determined according to the Youden index. The 95% CIs for sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and AUCs were computed and compared using the bootstrap method with 1000 resamples. Correlations between major qualitative features and relevant SRUS quantitative parameters, and correlations of major features and relevant parameters with MVD were analyzed with the Spearman correlation coefficient (ρ) according to Schober as follows (44): 0.00–0.09, negligible; 0.10–0.39, weak; 0.40–0.69, moderate; 0.70–0.89, strong; and 0.90–1.00, very strong correlation. P values were adjusted with the false discovery rate method for multiple comparisons. A two-tailed P value less than .05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (version 31.0; IBM), and Python (version 3.12.7; Python Software Foundation) by two authors (H.Y. and X.L.). For more details about the statistical analysis plan, see Appendix S4.

Results

Animals and Target LNs

In this study, a total of 30 rabbits with 30 palpable LNs (16 hyperplastic and 14 metastatic LNs; long axis, 1.05 cm [IQR, 0.94–1.26 cm]; short axis, 0.38 cm [IQR, 0.30–0.53 cm]) were included in the final analysis after the exclusion of two rabbits with tumor implantation failure. Hyperplastic LN sizes were similar to those of metastatic LNs (long axis, 1.02 cm [IQR, 0.94–1.11 cm] vs 1.17 cm [IQR, 0.94–1.29 cm]; P = .43; short axis, 0.36 cm [IQR, 0.30–0.48 cm] vs 0.41 cm [IQR, 0.33–0.58 cm]; P = .29).

Discussion

In this in vivo imaging study of 30 rabbits with palpable LNs, qualitative and quantitative analyses of SRUS showed that chaotic microvasculature (AUC, 0.92) and FD (AUC, 0.92) were the highest-performing qualitative feature and quantitative parameter, respectively, for the diagnosis of metastatic LNs. Chaotic microvasculature and FD were strongly correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.80; P < .001), with both reflecting vessel tortuosity. CD31 immunostaining also reflected the relationship between SRUS and MVD pathology. These findings suggested that SRUS-based qualitative and quantitative analyses may provide a noninvasive method of evaluating LN microvasculature to identify features associated with metastatic LNs in rabbits.

We explored the utility of SRUS in characterizing the metastatic status of LNs. SRUS enables in vivo micron-scale high-resolution imaging of microvasculature through the location and tracking of intravascular microbubbles (16,19–21). Tumors promote LN angiogenesis through increased vascular endothelial growth factor secretion and the remodeling of high endothelial venules into capillary-type vessels (16). Abnormal microvasculature, including loss of hierarchy, a chaotic network of tortuous microvessels, and high vascular density, could be observed during this angiogenetic process (10,45). This explains the underlying mechanism by which SRUS emerges as a potential imaging modality for LN metastasis and provides the theoretical foundation of our study.

In our study, qualitative analysis showed that chaotic microvasculature visually assessed at SRUS achieved the highest AUC (0.92) and moderate interreader agreement (κ = 0.60) for the diagnosis of metastatic LNs in rabbits when compared with those of conventional imaging features at US and CEUS. These results suggest that SRUS-derived qualitative features may facilitate translation of this technology into clinical practice. It could be anticipated that high-resolution SRUS adds to a radiologist’s diagnostic toolbox, enabling more accurate and reliable identification of LN metastasis through the evaluation of LN microvasculature. Given the technique’s complexity, SRUS may help characterize challenging cases or guide biopsy of LNs with focal intranodal neoplastic invasion to reduce the false-negative rate.

Regarding the results of SRUS-based quantitative analysis, FD yielded the optimal AUC (0.92) for the diagnosis of metastatic LNs, followed by VD (0.70), FWVD (0.70), and direction entropy (0.71), indicating increased vessel density, perfusion, and tortuosity. These results are generally consistent with those of a previous study (23) that suggested that the metastatic LN microvasculature has higher MVD and flow direction variance. However, results derived from algorithm-dependent quantitative analysis raise concerns regarding cross-platform reproducibility. The establishment of a unified multicenter international consensus protocol may resolve this issue, but continuous efforts are still required.

Several previous studies have explored the feasibility of applying SRUS to evaluate LN metastasis in humans (23,24). A major difference between these previous studies and our study was the manner in which SRUS and histopathologic data were correlated. In our study, we performed US-guided resection of imaged LNs to obtain whole-mount sections and aligned the tissue cutting plane with the imaging plane using ink marking, thereby ensuring spatial correlation between SRUS and histopathologic data. The other major difference was that we performed SRUS-based qualitative analyses and MVD analysis in our study. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt at the visual interpretation of SRUS microvasculature maps, the results of which were consistent with those of the quantitative analysis (Spearman ρ = 0.80; P < .001) and MVD (Spearman ρ = 0.82; P < .001).

Our study had several limitations. First, the sample size was limited to 30 rabbits, resulting in relatively wide CIs for the point estimates examined. To mitigate this limitation and increase the robustness of our statistical estimates, we used bootstrap resampling techniques. Second, VX2 tumor–bearing metastatic LNs and egg yolk–induced hyperplastic LNs in rabbits may not fully reflect the actual pathologic features of human metastatic and hyperplastic LNs. Therefore, the findings of our study cannot be directly applied to clinical settings. A small-scale human validation study is ongoing. Third, the design of our study limits the assessment of vascular changes at different metastatic stages. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to characterize microvascular dynamics during metastasis. Finally, clinical application of SRUS is further limited by its high susceptibility to physiologic motion artifacts, coupled with its strict reliance on contrast agents and computationally intensive postprocessing procedures.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that SRUS via qualitative and quantitative analyses enabled noninvasive evaluation of LN microvasculature in rabbits, demonstrating its potential as an imaging modality to identify metastatic LNs. However, further studies are needed to validate the diagnostic performance of SRUS for differentiating metastatic from benign hyperplastic LNs in humans.
