A feasibility study on physical therapist interventions in the Japanese integrated health services and preventive care program for older adults
Authors: Yuta Mori, Ryota Watanabe, Koichi Saito, Masashi Mitani, Masahide Yamamoto, Masako Okabe, Takuya Itou, Mitsuaki Minamide
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] This study examined the feasibility of including physical therapists in the Health Services and Preventive Care program at community gatherings. [Participants and Methods] The participants were 56 older adults from five community gatherings who did not have a long-term care insurance service. As part of the Health Services and Preventive Care program, interventions by physical therapists were conducted for 90 minutes once a month for 6 months at five community gatherings, which involved exercises, and home-based exercises. The feasibility outcomes were dropout rate, number of times people participated in the program, and understanding of frailty. The effectiveness outcomes were frailty, grip strength, walking speed, and five-times-sit-to-stand test. [Results] The feasibility study results showed a dropout rate of 4.3%, 4.7 ± 0.4 times participation, and a 93.2% frailty understanding. Effectiveness outcomes revealed no increase in frailty. The physical indices showed that all participants statistically improved in the five-times-sit-to-stand test (Pre: 7.3 ± 2.3 times, Post: 6.5 ± 7.6 times). [Conclusion] The results of the feasibility study indicate that the program is feasible because of the high rates of completion and frailty prevention understanding.Keywords: Kayoinoba, Frailty, Physical therapist, Feasibility study

INTRODUCTION

In Japan, “community gathering places” are used to give long-term senior citizens advice about care prevention1). Community gathering places are venues at which senior citizens can engage in health promotion activities, such as exercise and free-time activities. The Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare (MHLW) provides local community gathering places, generally within walking distance, where older adults in the neighborhood can drop by to receive a range of care prevention services. In 2019, community gathering places were introduced in 95.9% (1670/1741) of Japanese municipalities, at which around 6.7% of the older population participated2). It has been found that participation in community gathering places has halved the need for senior citizen support and care3), reduced the risks of developing dementia by 30%4), and delayed the onset of functional disability by 15.6%5). There are also reports of increased social participation aimed at preventing care and improving health outside these places6).

The “Integrated Implementation Project of Health Services and Preventive Care for Older Adults (Health Services and Preventive Care) program” was introduced as an initiative for older adults7). This program integrates care, disease, and serious illness prevention programs to support the health of the older people in the community. The answers from a frailty questionnaire that sought information on health checkups, medical care, and nursing care were extracted from the National Health Insurance Database System to identify the programs needed by older people and the number of medical institution visits they generally made. The program uses community gathering places to disseminate knowledge and provide programs to local older populations about frailty prevention, the most popular of which are exercise classes (52.8%), tea meetings (19.0%), and free-time activities (16.9%)8). These programs are provided by a range of healthcare professionals, such as public health nurses, physical therapists, dietitians, and dental hygienists.

The involvement of physical therapists at community gathering places has also been recommended as part of the Health Services and Preventive Care programs. Preventing frailty is a key goal of the Health Services and Preventive Care project; therefore, physical therapists who specialize in exercise could use the frailty assessment and health checkup data to develop specific frailty-focused exercise programs. A previous study found that 48.3% of 34 prefrailty participants working with physical therapists at a community gathering place had health improvements after a year9).

However, there have been few feasibility studies using a set framework that have analyzed and evaluated the Health Services and Preventive Care program implementation. Each municipality is granted a funding for this new long-term care prevention project that enables it to provide funding for collaborative programs that involve a range of paid nonvolunteer healthcare professionals, such as dietitians, dental hygienists, physical therapists, and other rehabilitation professionals as required7).

This study, therefore, examined the feasibility of the involvement of physical therapists in the implementation of the Health Services and Preventive Care program. We also identified areas for improvement when new community gathering places are established in other neighborhoods in the future.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

We used a feasibility study framework with before and after comparative data10). The feasibility study framework examines the feasibility of implementing similar projects in the future based on various evaluations. The feasibility of the newly implemented program is being examined, and plans are being made to expand the program to new community gathering places in other neighborhoods. Note that no prior research protocols were registered for this study.

The study was conducted at five community gathering places in Matsusaka City, Mie Prefecture, all of which are model Health Services and Preventive Care sites. The sites were selected based on three criteria: (1) the community gathering places are resident-led organizations managed mainly by volunteers; (2) exercise programs are conducted once or twice a month for 90–120 minutes; and (3) the administrative public health nurse requires professionals such as physical therapists. The exclusion criteria were (1) dropouts who were judged to be unable to continue participating in the project in the future due to poor health or instructional reasons during the project implementation; and (2) participants who were eligible for long-term care.

The intervention program is shown in Table 1. Participants were instructed to attend the community gathering places once a month for 90 minutes over 6 months to receive interventions from a physical therapist or another Health Services and Preventive Care professional. While the main interventions were conducted by the physical therapists, dental hygienists, and dietitians were also partially involved. As in a previous study9), in addition to the frailty prevention health lectures, stretching and resistance training using tubing were also conducted (Table 2). Each participant set their individual goals, and to encourage home-based activities, the physical therapists and other professionals developed home programs based on the results of each participant’s initial physical assessments. To determine the participants’ exercise routines, each of them was given a monthly check calendar to record how many times they chose to exercise. During the monthly visits to the community gathering places by the physical therapists and other professionals, the participants’ home-based exercise calendars were checked and they were advised on their exercise loads.

Participant characteristics at baseline

The feasibility outcomes were the number of participants, the dropout rate, the understanding of frailty, programs that needed to be incorporated after the project, and areas that needed to be improved by the project participants, leaders, professionals, and administrative health workers. The dropout rate was defined as the percentage of participants who dropped out before the end of the intervention. The frailty prevention understanding, which was partially modified from a previous study11), was determined using a self-administered form that asked; Did you understand that diet, exercise, and oral health are the three necessary components for preventing frailty?; which required them to answer either “yes”, “no”, or “neither”. The programs to be incorporated after the project’s completion were exercises, tube exercises, physical assessments, and home-based exercises. Possible project improvements were addressed in the free descriptions by the project participants, leaders, professionals, and administrative health workers.

The effectiveness outcomes were assessed based on previous studies9) using the Frailty, Physical and Fitness Assessment, the Kihon checklist (KCL), and the Risk Assessment Scale for needing long-term care12). The KCL, which is an indicator developed by the MHLW to identify those at high risk of needing long-term care13), was used to determine frailty and is a self-administered questionnaire with 25 multiple-choice questions for which 1 point is allocated for each applicable question for a total score of 25 points: 0–3 points: “robust”, 4–7 points: “prefrailty”, 8 points or more: “frailty”14). The physical and physical fitness assessments used previous studies as a reference and measured the participants’ 5 m comfortable walking speed9), grip strength9), five-times-sit-to-stand test (CS-5)15), a Timed Up & Go Test (TUG)9), and one-leg standing time9). The measurement section in the 5 m comfortable walking speed, which is a walking ability index, was 5 m. The speed was converted by measuring the time required on a walking path with 3 m of spare path in front and behind. Grip strength, an index of muscle strength, was measured in a standing position with the dominant hand using a Smedley grip strength meter (Takei Kiki Kogyo, Niigata, Japan, T.K.K. 5401). The CS-5 was measured by repeating five consecutive standing and sitting movements as quickly as possible from a seated position in a chair. The TUG test, which was timed, required participants to stand up, walk around a landmark 3 m away, and walk back to their chairs. The one-leg standing time test required participants to put both hands on their hips, and at the cue “raise one leg”, assume a one-leg standing posture and hold it for 60 seconds. The KCL subitems identified the mean scores for each of the seven domains: five for daily living instrumental activities, six for the Physical domain, two for the Nutrition domain, three for the Eating domain, two for the House boundedness domain, three for the Memory domain, and five for the Mood domain. A systematic review has confirmed the predictive validity of the Basic Checklist in identifying support and care needs16). The risk assessment for requiring long-term care has 10 items, such as Instrumental Activities of Daily Living, motor function, nutritional status, and frequency of outings, all of which are included in KCL, as well as gender (0–1 point) and age (0–24 points). A score is calculated for each applicable situation, with the total score (0–48 points) being a measure that can predict the probability of needing long-term care within around three years regardless of the population density of the city or town12).

The main objective of this study was to determine the feasibility of the program. For this reason, no sample size calculations were performed. The number of participants was within the range of previous feasibility studies17).

The feasibility outcomes were verified using descriptive statistics and free-text descriptions, and the effectiveness outcomes were validated using pre and postintervention comparison tests. Corresponding t-tests were employed for the continuous variables and McNemar tests were employed for the categorical variables. As the robust and prefrailty participant characteristics differed from a previous study, the analysis was stratified using overall/robust/prefrailty or frailty9). All statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 4.3.0 (Vienna, Austria).

This study was conducted after receiving approval (05006) from the Ethical Review Committee of Hana-no-Oka Hospital of Shoutoku-kai Medical Corporation. After an oral explanation of the study to the participants, consent to conduct the study was obtained in writing.

RESULTS

There were 58 participants across the five community gathering places registered with the Health Services and Preventive Care program. Two participants dropped out as they were unable to continue due to poor health. No participants required support or nursing care. Therefore, the final analysis of the 6-month intervention included 56 participants; however, as 12 did not participate in the final evaluation, the analysis was based on responses from 44 participants.

Table 3 shows the basic participant characteristics at the baseline. The mean age was 78.8 ± 6.3 years, 31 were considered robust and 25 were assessed as prefrailty and fraility. Table 4 shows the results of the feasibility outcomes: there were two dropouts (dropout rate 4.3%) due to poor health or instructional reasons, and participation was on average 4.7 ± 0.4 times out of six. As stated, the 12 participants who did not complete the final evaluation were excluded from the analysis. More than 90% of the remaining respondents showed a good understanding of frailty prevention, both overall and by frailty tier.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the continuity and development of the Health Services and Preventive Care program and examined the feasibility of implementing a frailty prevention program involving physical therapists and other health care professionals. The physical therapists visited the community gathering places once a month for 90 minutes over 6 months to give lectures, exercises, and home-based exercise guidance to prevent older adult frailty. There was a dropout rate of about 4%, a project participation rate of about 80%, and a level of understanding regarding frailty of over 90%. Over the six months, there was no increase in frailty in any of the participants, and the CS-5 tests revealed a statistical improvement in overall, prefrailty and frailty. In a previous study, a combination of physical activity, food variety, and social interaction was found to be associated with a reduction in the risk of developing long-term care18), which suggested that multifaceted approaches should be considered.

The feasibility outcomes showed that most participants completed the six-month, once a month program and had a good understanding of frailty prevention. The program that the respondents most wanted to incorporate after the project was completed was exercise, and the second most popular program was physical fitness testing. Previous studies found that the frailty awareness of older people was only about 20% and that those who had good social activities, regular exercise, and good nutritional habits were more likely to be aware of their frailty11). The participants in our study had a higher understanding of frailty, which may have been because they had greater access to health information19). Around 90% of other healthcare professionals believed that the role of the physical therapists at the community gathering places was to conduct evaluations and teach and conduct exercises20). Therefore, our study supported the results from previous studies.

The effectiveness outcome results showed that there had been no increase in frailty throughout the program and the CS-5 indicated that there have been overall, prefrailty and frailty improvements. In a previous study, a home exercise program for participants from community gatherings places also prevented the onset of frailty and significantly improved the CS-521), and another study found that physical therapist involvement in community gatherings places with physical prefrailty participants showed significant CS-30 improvements22). Therefore, stand up performance improvements are expected in the Health Services and Preventive Care program.

Overall, our feasibility study showed that there was high participation in the project as there was only a 4% dropout rate and a significant increase in frailty understanding. However, the free description results revealed that some had difficulty continuing the exercises at home, and only 30% wanted to incorporate load-bearing tube exercises in the program after the project was completed. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a program that allows management to continue the exercise project. If this can be realized, the number of community gatherings places should be expanded to other neighborhoods.

There are three possible strengths of this study. First, this is the first study to examine the feasibility of the Health Services and Preventive Care program. Second, the implementation feasibility was verified from quantitative and qualitative aspects. Third, the physical, psychological, and social outcomes were verified. However, there are also five possible study limitations. First, the study was not preregistered as an intervention study. Second, the home-based exercise compliance rates were not verified; however, the feasibility of implementing a home-based exercise qualitative study was confirmed. Third, exercise retention and frailty development immediately after the intervention and six months and one year later were not tested. Fourth, while the frailty understanding after the project ended was more than 90%, the frailty understanding at the beginning of the project was not evaluated, which meant that any changes could not be confirmed. Fifth, our study was not able to evaluate the effectiveness of the study with a control group, and as 12 participants did not complete the final evaluation, the results may have been overestimated.

To evaluate the continuity and development of the project, we examined the effectiveness of involving physical therapists in a frailty prevention program in Matsusaka City as part of the Health Services and Preventive Care program. There was no increase in frailty before and after the project and the CS-5 showed overall, prefrailty and frailty improvements. The results of feasibility outcomes indicate that the program may be feasible due to the high rates of completion and frailty prevention understanding. Modifications to the content, frequency, and quantity of the home-based exercises and community gathering place exercises could contribute to the continuation of the Health Services and Preventive Care program.
