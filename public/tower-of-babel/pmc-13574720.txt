Efficacy of an Adhesive Hydrocolloid Bandage on Wound Healing: Findings of a 28‐Day, Single‐Centre, Randomised, Controlled Study
Authors: Alana S. Levine, Jenny Du‐Soriano, Madeline M. Gleason, Latrisha Petersen, Robin Mathew, Eleanor Small, Robert S. Kirsner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis single‐centre, randomised, controlled study assessed the wound healing efficacy of adhesive bandages in eligible healthy adults (N = 36) aged 25–55 with Fitzpatrick skin types II and III using a model of laser‐induced wounds. Here, we report data for wounds that were randomised to treatment with either a hydrocolloid bandage for multi‐day use (BAND‐AID Adhesive Bandage Hydroseal), standard of care (SoC) (BAND‐AID Adhesive Bandage Tru‐Stay Sheer) or an uncovered control. Primary endpoints included the time to complete healing and a composite wound healing score. Hydrocolloid bandage‐treated wounds healed twice as fast as the uncovered control or SoC‐treated wounds. Median time (days) to complete healing was significantly faster for the hydrocolloid bandage‐treated wounds (6.9) versus SoC (11.9) and uncovered control (11.8). Hydrocolloid bandage‐treated wounds had significantly better composite wound healing scores versus SoC and versus uncovered control over 2 weeks. At Day 28, cosmesis was better with hydrocolloid bandage treatment (0.3) versus SoC (0.6) and uncovered control (2.1) (change from baseline in a composite scar score; higher score = worse outcome). Multi‐day wound occlusion with a hydrocolloid bandage promoted faster healing and improved cosmesis compared with daily SoC dressings or uncovered control.

Introduction

For minor acute wounds, a key objective is to prevent further damage to the wound and facilitate healing through an occlusive, moist wound healing environment that speeds healing, limits infection and inflammation and improves cosmesis with less scarring [1, 2]. Appropriate wound dressings can facilitate the complex healing process by augmenting a highly orchestrated multistage repair process without disruption [2, 3]. In addition, dressings can promote restoration of skin barrier function [1] and reduce inflammation and scarring [2]. Furthermore, wounds treated with occlusive dressings can improve the quality of healing and minimise scarring, resulting in a more cosmetically acceptable appearance than those left uncovered [4].

An important factor in wound healing is maintaining a restorative, moist wound environment. Winter et al. reported that keeping wounds moist helps them heal faster compared with dry wounds, contrary to the often practised approach of allowing wounds to dry out and form scabs [5]. When wounds are left uncovered and exposed to air, normal wound exudate dries, leading to scab formation with resultant reduction in pro‐healing cytokines and growth factors, diminished electrical gradient and more difficult epithelial migration [4, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Despite scab formation, there is still significant water loss from the wound, as the scab does not form an occlusive barrier. Uncovered wounds are then at risk of bacterial invasion from the environment and are more susceptible to infection [10]. In occluded acute wounds, however, wound fluid helps to facilitate the healing process by supplying moisture and allowing for a variety of bioactive mediators (e.g., enzymes, growth factors and nutrients) [9] and matrix proteins to be more readily available. This also facilitates the electrical gradient for healing [7] and reduces inflammation by providing various immune cells within the environment needed for the elimination of debris, pathogens and foreign bodies.

Occlusion influences both the epidermis and dermis, enhancing epithelial cell migration and promoting dermal collagen synthesis and deposition, respectively [11, 12, 13]. By maintaining a moist wound environment, occlusive dressings support restoration of the epidermal barrier and facilitate optimal wound healing [4, 6, 8, 9]. Hydrocolloid dressings are designed to provide sustained occlusion over multiple days while improving ease of use by reducing the need for frequent dressing changes. This prolonged wear feature also minimises dressing removal and replacement, which can otherwise disrupt the healing process and contribute to discomfort [14].

Over several decades, the science supporting moist wound healing and occlusive dressings has evolved into an accepted best practice for wound management. However, even the most effective clinical interventions must align with patient preferences, behaviours and daily lifestyles to achieve meaningful real‐world outcomes. Patient adherence is critical to realising the therapeutic benefits of occlusive hydrocolloid dressings; without adequate compliance, the clinical advantages demonstrated under controlled conditions may not be fully achieved in practice.

Despite the established efficacy of occlusive wound care, there is limited research evaluating consumer preferences and behaviours related to the management of minor wounds, including preferred dressing wear time, wound cleansing practices and compatibility with everyday activities. Therefore, in this study, we substantiated the benefits of occlusive hydrocolloid in a ‘real‐world’ wound model, while assessing whether a multiple‐day wear regimen aligns with consumer preferences, promotes adherence and supports effective healing outcomes [15, 16].

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This randomised, comparator‐controlled, proof‐of‐principle clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04040933) was conducted in a single centre in the United States. The study protocol and related materials were approved by the IntegReview Institutional Review Board (Austin, TX, USA) before enrolment started, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant before study initiation.

Key Eligibility Criteria

Eligible individuals were 25‐ to 55‐year‐old men and women with Fitzpatrick skin types II and III and uniform skin colour on both volar forearms, who were generally in good health and able to read/write/speak/understand English. Potential participants were required to attend all study visits and follow study instructions, including wearing long sleeves when going outdoors during the study (to protect the test areas from exposure to sunlight) and refraining from the following for the study duration: immersing the bandages in water; showering within 2 h before each study visit; and using any daily cleanser/wash‐off products on their forearms, other than the auxiliary cleanser provided at screening.

The key exclusion criteria were as follows: known allergies/hypersensitivity/adverse reactions to any of the products used in the study; presence of excessive hair, acne scars, pigmentation or friable skin on either volar forearm that could have interfered with the study procedures or assessments, history of keloid formation, hypertrophic scars, cracked or excoriated skin, infected skin lesions or any other skin or health conditions that could have put the subject at risk, interfered with the assessments or confounded the study results; use of any oral/topical medications or products that could have masked an adverse event (AE), confounded the study results or altered/compromised the bleeding/healing process, including the use of vitamin A derivatives (oral or topical) within 1 year before the screening visit; and use of topical leave‐on products on the volar forearms within 1 week before the screening visit.

Randomisation

For eligible participants, eight different treatments were each randomly allocated to each wound site (one study treatment for each of the wound sites), based on a randomisation scheme devised by the sponsor's biostatistics group, using assigned treatment codes. For the purposes of this study, we compared only three of the treatment groups as follows: uncovered control, covered control (standard of care [SoC]: BAND‐AID Adhesive Bandage Tru‐Stay Sheer) and hydrocolloid bandage (BAND‐AID Adhesive Bandage Hydroseal).

Study Interventions, Visits and Assessments

Study visits were scheduled at screening (3–7 days before baseline), Day 0 (baseline), Days 1–16 and 28. At screening, prospective participants were reviewed for eligibility, completed the informed consent process and were provided with the auxiliary product (Johnson's Head‐to‐Toe Wash & Shampoo, Kenvue Brands LLC) to be used on their forearms and body during the study (instead of their regular body cleanser). Demographic details, medical history, and details of concomitant medications were also collected. The study physician confirmed the eligibility of each subject prior to baseline. See Figure S1 for a summary of the schedule of events.

At baseline (Day 0), participants returned to the clinic where clinic personnel recorded concomitant medications and questioned candidates regarding changes in their health. Daily diaries were collected, reviewed for compliance and retained. Participants who still met the eligibility criteria were enrolled in the study, and each participant had wound sites marked on their volar forearms. After completion of the baseline evaluations (clinical grading of wound healing parameters described below), a thick layer of anhydrous numbing benzocaine‐lidocaine‐tetracaine cream (20%/8%/8%) was applied on each designated wound site to cover an area of ~2 cm × 2 cm. Approximately 30 min later, the forearms of each subject were cleaned using alcohol wipes and a certified laser specialist created uniform minor wounds at the designated wound sites using an Erbium:Yttrium Aluminium Garnet (YAG) 2940‐nm laser (Sciton Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) at an energy setting of 12.2 J/cm2 and three passes of ~50 μm depth each, resulting in a total depth of ~150 μm. Each created wound was circular, 6 mm in diameter and placed at least 4 cm away from adjacent wounds. This protocol and rationale for using laser‐induced wounds have been previously reported [17], but, briefly, it is thought to more accurately represent minor wounds encountered in real life as opposed to scalpel or other induced wound models and allows for consistency between wounds.

Wounds were photographed using a Nikon D7000 digital single‐lens reflex camera (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with a Sigma DG 70 mm f/2.8 macro lens or equivalent under standard lighting conditions. They were also assessed (using the 0–4 scales described in Table S1) for clinical grading of wound healing parameters, including erythema, oedema, epithelial confluence, crusting/scabbing, smoothness and general wound appearance [17].

After the assessments, a trained, gloved clinic staff member applied fresh product on the wound sites (Days 1–15 for SoC; Days 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 and 15 for hydrocolloid bandage). Subjects were also provided with a unit of cloth tape (BAND‐AID Brand Adhesive Bandage All‐Purpose Cloth Tape, Kenvue Brands LLC) to be used at home as needed to secure the investigational products if they became loose during the study. Using a computer or phone, subjects completed a self‐assessment questionnaire from Days 0 to 4: subjects were asked to rate each wound (not the surrounding skin that might also be covered by a bandage) for pain/soreness in a resting position and in normal motion and for itchiness, using a scale where 0 = no pain/itch and 9 = as bad as it could be (secondary endpoint). All wound sites were left uncovered from Days 16 to 28. Each wound site was also assessed on Day 0 using a validated scar scale (The Manchester Scar Scale, secondary endpoint) [18]. Subjects returned for a further assessment on Day 28, and final photographs were taken (see Table S2 for details of scar scale scoring). If applicable, AEs were recorded at each visit per the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) AE classification system.

The wound evaluator was blinded to the treatment groups and did not have access to the randomisation schedule. Subjects were instructed not to disclose information about their assigned study treatments to the wound evaluator or to discuss any other information that may reveal the treatment assignment. While patients were not blinded, they were not aware of the goals of the study.

Endpoints and Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint was ‘time to complete healing’, which measured how many days elapsed from the time of wound creation to when the composite wound healing score of the wound was ≥ 8 (described below). If a composite wound healing score did not meet the complete healing criterion by Day 16, the time to complete healing was censored at the last day on which the composite wound healing score was available.

A previously utilised composite wound healing score [17] was used as a measure of healing to compare the different treatments. The composite wound healing score was calculated from the clinical grading of wound healing parameters as follows: the sum of scores for general wound appearance, smoothness and epithelial confluence, minus the sum of scores for erythema, oedema and crusting/scabbing. The total scale ranged from −12 to +12; the higher the composite wound healing score, the greater the extent of wound healing. Time to complete healing was analysed using a survival analysis method. The survival function (cumulative percentage of wounds healed at each time point) was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method for each treatment separately. The median time to complete healing was derived from the estimated survival functions and compared using the bootstrap resampling method. The between‐treatment comparison was based on the changes from baseline at each post‐baseline visit using a mixed‐effect analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model that included the treatment as a factor, the baseline value as the covariate and the subject as a random effect (to incorporate the within‐subject correlation).

Secondary endpoints included erythema, oedema, subject pain self‐assessment on irritation parameters and the scar scale [18]. All secondary endpoints were analysed within‐treatment and between‐treatment. The within‐treatment comparison was performed by comparing the post‐baseline scores with the baseline score within each treatment using the paired t‐test. The between‐treatment comparison was performed by comparing the change from baseline between treatments using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). The analysis model included the treatment as a factor and baseline score as a covariate.

Analyses were based on the intent‐to‐treat (ITT) population (i.e., all participants who received laser‐induced wounds and started the study treatments were included in the analyses). The number (%) of participants with AEs was analysed in the safety population (i.e., all individuals enrolled with ≥ 1 application of the study product). SAS software version 9.4 was used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was based on p ≤ 0.05.

Sample Size

A total of 36 subjects were screened and enrolled to ensure completion of the study by 30 subjects.

Secondary Endpoints

Wounds treated with the hydrocolloid bandage had statistically significantly lower mean crusting/scabbing scores on Days 3–11 versus the uncovered control and on Days 7–13 versus SoC, indicating improved healing in the hydrocolloid bandage‐treated wounds (Table S3). Epithelial confluence, smoothness and general wound appearance scores were greater for the hydrocolloid bandage versus both the uncovered control and SoC on most days from Day 3, indicating greater healing of hydrocolloid bandage‐treated wounds (Table S3). Wounds that were treated with the hydrocolloid bandage had statistically significantly lower erythema scores on Days 3–16 versus uncovered control and on Days 3–15 versus SoC (Table S4). Oedema scores were generally zero through to Day 16 (where 0 = no oedema and 4 = severe oedema) and no higher than 0.1 at any other timepoint (Table S4).

Representative wound images of each treatment group (Figure 2) are consistent with the wound healing scoring measurements, showing improved wound healing for wounds treated with the hydrocolloid bandage compared to those treated with SoC and uncovered control.

Subject Self‐Assessment

There were no meaningful differences reported between the treatment groups for subject self‐assessment parameters (pain/soreness in a resting position and in normal motion, and for itchiness). All subjects reported very low scores of ~1 or less (on a scale where 0 = no pain/itch and 9 = as bad as it could be) on each of the 4 days when self‐assessments were recorded.

Scar Scale

Wounds were evaluated based on colour, finish, contour, distortion and texture using a standardised and validated Scar Scale (The Manchester Scar Scale) [18] at baseline before wounding (Day 0) and on Day 28. Higher scores indicate worse outcomes and worse cosmesis. Both the SoC and the hydrocolloid bandage resulted in lower scarring than the uncovered control. At Day 28, the mean change from baseline in the composite scar score was 2.1 for the uncovered control versus 0.6 for SoC and 0.3 for the hydrocolloid bandage (p < 0.001 for the hydrocolloid bandage versus the uncovered control and p < 0.001 for SoC vs. the uncovered control) (Figure 3).

Safety

Treatment‐emergent AEs occurred in most subjects (94.4%). Those occurring in more than 5% of subjects included erythema (n = 28, 77.8%), rash (n = 11, 30.6%) and pruritus (n = 2, 5.6%). No serious AEs were reported.

Discussion

This study utilised a laser‐induced wound model to compare healing outcomes for wounds left uncovered, treated with a multi‐day hydrocolloid bandage or treated with the daily replacement SoC bandage. Results demonstrated that wounds covered with the hydrocolloid bandage—replaced every other day after Day 3 for the purposes of collecting study measurements—healed twice as fast as wounds left uncovered or treated with the SoC bandage, which required daily replacement. Furthermore, the mean change from baseline in composite wound healing scores was significantly greater for hydrocolloid‐treated wounds compared with uncovered controls and those treated with the SoC bandage. It is also important to note that covering the wounds with either the SoC or the hydrocolloid bandage significantly reduced scarring by Day 28 compared to the uncovered control. These findings provide strong clinical evidence for the importance of maintaining wound occlusion for multiple days (compared with not covering a wound) to optimise wound healing and to improve cosmesis and reduce scarring. Furthermore, not all wound dressings hold the same efficacy, and the clinical evidence showcases that using an innovative hydrocolloid bandage (without the need for daily replacement) promotes superior healing compared with daily SoC dressing changes for acute wounds. We hypothesise that the product design and technology of the hydrocolloid bandage are largely responsible for the superior wound healing properties compared to the SoC dressing, specifically its ability to balance moisture and provide a more occlusive barrier due to its superior adhesive properties.

While the clinical benefit of covering a wound to improve healing is evident, a shift in patient behaviour and compliant bandage use may be needed. Although guidelines recommend keeping wounds covered until complete healing (typically 7–10 days for minor wounds) [19], there are data to show that approximately 60% of patients fail to keep minor wounds covered for more than 3 days (data on file). This early discontinuation is often triggered by bandages becoming wet or soggy, along with patient concerns about healing progress (data on file), leading to inconsistent replacement and gaps in coverage. As a result, wounds may remain unprotected long before complete healing, prolonging the healing time and increasing the risk of wound infection and scarring. These findings highlight the need for innovative solutions that provide waterproof, multi‐day wear and maintain continuous coverage to maximise healing. Encouragingly, 62% of patients indicate willingness to try a bandage designed for multiple days of wear (data on file), eliminating the need for daily replacement and minimising periods of non‐coverage. This patient's openness is vital because a treatment solution is only effective if a patient is willing to comply with its use, in this case, an occlusive hydrocolloid bandage.

The hydrocolloid bandage evaluated in this study is designed to provide wound coverage for up to 5 days, helping to reduce infection risk, prevent premature discontinuation of wear and support optimal healing outcomes, as demonstrated in this research. Its user‐friendly format enhances patient comfort and willingness to adhere to treatment, potentially improving compliance and promoting overall wound care effectiveness.

The hydrocolloid bandage was well tolerated, without unexpected AEs during the study period. As with many studies, the clinical trial held several study limitations, including only enrolling individuals with generally good health. In addition, the wounds created were approximately 6 mm in diameter and may not be generalisable to larger wounds, although this model has been validated for the investigation of wound healing [17]. That said, the study intervention was not evaluated in a wider variety of wounds, including postoperative biopsies, surgical wounds or ulcers. Further, other limitations include not having a larger sample size or a heterogeneous group of wounds; therefore, caution exists with broader generalisations to a more diverse population. Future research to evaluate the efficacy and compliance of the intervention hydrocolloid bandage, may include investigating the healing efficacy in paediatric patients or in patients with compromised health, such as those with diabetes mellitus or poor circulation, to better understand diverse patient populations in real‐world settings.
