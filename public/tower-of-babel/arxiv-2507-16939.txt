Exact distinguishability between real-valued and complex-valued Haar random quantum states
Tristan Nemoz, Romain Alléaume, Peter Brown

Abstract
Haar random states are fundamental objects in quantum information theory and quantum computing. We study the density matrix resulting from sampling t t copies of a d d -dimensional quantum state according to the Haar measure on the orthogonal group. In particular, we analytically compute its spectral decomposition. This allows us to compute exactly the trace distance between t t -copies of a real Haar random state and t t -copies of a complex Haar random state. Using this we show a lower-bound on the approximation parameter of real-valued state t t -designs and improve the lower-bound on the number of copies required for imaginarity testing.



1
Introduction

Haar random unitaries and states are important tools in the study of quantum information and quantum computing
[
Mel24
]
. They have found prolific applications to problems such as the development of shadow tomography
[
HKP20
]
, demonstrating quantum advantage
[
Nei+18
]
and benchmarking quantum computers
[
Cro+19
]
. However, sampling a state from the Haar measure requires quantum circuits of exponential size
[
HP07
]
, which is impractical. Therefore, the consideration of practicality necessitates a search for ensembles of efficiently-preparable quantum states that, when sampled, closely resemble a Haar random state.

Ensembles of random states that mimic Haar random states are more formally known as approximate state
t
-designs
[
AE07
]
. Roughly, an ensemble of states is an
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design if when given
t
-copies of either a sample from the ensemble or a Haar random state, the probability we correctly distinguish the two is bounded above by
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
. In other words, there is only an
\varepsilon
advantage over randomly guessing. As such, any task requiring to sample
t
copies of a state according to the Haar measure on the unitary group can be performed up to precision
\varepsilon
by using an
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design instead.

Approximate state
t
-designs have recently found significant applications to quantum cryptography in the context of Asymptotically Random State generators (ARS)
[
BS19
]
, which are a particular cryptographic notion of a random state generator – akin to a random number generator in classical cryptography. Loosely, an ARS is a sequence of
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-designs (indexed by the dimension of the states) with
t
growing polynomially in the logarithm of the dimension and
\varepsilon
remaining negligible. An ARS is thus a family of approximate state
t
-designs with
t
growing modestly with the dimension. ARS have been critical in the development of constructions of pseudorandom states (PRS)
[
JLS18
,
BS19
]
, which are a quantum analogue of pseudorandom generators. For PRS, the information theoretic indistinguishability of ARS with Haar random states is replaced with a computational indistinguishability. That is, the ensembles cannot be distinguished from Haar random states by any computationally bounded agent. PRS are in turn widely studied in the quantum cryptography field as they not only represent a new approach for performing quantum cryptography, namely adding computational assumptions to surpass what’s classically achievable, but because they also are a potentially weaker assumption than quantum-secure one-way functions
[
Kre21
,
Kre+23
]
. This has led to many cryptographic primitives being built out of PRS in a black-box way, such as quantum bit commitments
[
AQY22
]
or multi-party computation
[
MY22
]
amongst others
[
Sat24
]
.

Interestingly, many ARS are constructed solely from real-valued quantum states
[
BS19
,
Aar+24
,
GB23
,
JMW24
,
Cui+25
]
. These constitute ensembles of real-valued quantum states for which sampling a polynomial number of copies of a state approximates sampling the same number of copies of a state from the Haar measure on the unitary group. Beyond cryptographic applications, such constructions have also been used to show that there is no efficient procedure to test whether a generic state requires complex numbers to be expressed in the standard formalism
[
HBK25
]
, this is quantified by the imaginarity of the state and has been studied in the context of quantum resource theories
[
HG18
,
Wu+21
,
Xue+21
]
.

In order for an
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design to capture well a Haar random sample, its approximation parameter
\varepsilon
should be as small as possible. Now, whilst real-valued quantum theory can sometimes be as powerful as its complex counterpart, for instance in computation
[
Shi03
]
, it has been shown that it is insufficient to describe all the phenomena we observe
[
Ren+21
]
. We may thus wonder

Are there fundamental limitations on the performance of real-valued state
t
-designs?

If answered positively, this would also imply fundamental limitations on their various applications such as the performance of real-valued ARS.

1.1
Contributions

In this work we derive a fundamental lower bound on the approximation parameter
\varepsilon
of any real-valued
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design. To achieve this result we analytically study the spectral decomposition of the density matrix resulting from sampling
t
copies of a
d
-dimensional real-valued quantum state according to the Haar distribution on the orthogonal group. This forms the main technical result of our work, based on representation theory of the orthogonal group and connections to Harmonic polynomials. We expect the proof technique and result (which are presented in
Section

3
) to find further applications beyond this work.

Theorem 1
(Spectral decomposition)

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers. Let
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
be the density matrix associated to sampling
t
copies of a real-valued state according to the Haar distribution of the orthogonal group, that is

\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}=\int_{O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}}\left(O\left|0\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle 0\right|O^{\top}\right)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(O)\,.

(1)

Then for
k\in\{0,1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor-1\}
, the eigenvalues of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
are

\lambda_{k}=\frac{t!(d-2)!!}{(2k)!!(d+2t-2k-2)!!}

(2)

with multiplicity

\alpha_{k}=\binom{d+t-2k-1}{d-1}-\binom{d+t-2k-3}{d-1}\,.

(3)

■

Using this spectral decomposition, we are able to provide an exact analytical formula for the distinguishing advantage between real-valued Haar random states and complex-valued Haar random states. This completes a recently derived upper bound
[
Sch24
]
.

Corollary 1
(Informal)

Let
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
be the density matrices associated with sampling
t
-copies of a real and complex Haar random state respectively. Then the maximal probability with which one can distinguish these states, assuming they are both chosen with probability
\frac{1}{2}
, is given by
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}
and

\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\left|\lambda_{k}-\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}\right|

(4)

where
\alpha_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
are given in the previous theorem.

■

In
Section

4
we describe several applications of this result. Firstly, by a data processing inequality for the trace-norm, we obtain a lower bound on the distinguishability between sampling
t
-copies of any real-valued ensemble of states and
t
-copies of a complex-valued Haar random state. This lower bound places a fundamental limitation on the performance of real-valued state
t
-designs. In turn, we also derive limitations on the performance of real-valued ARS, providing a bound on how
t
scales in terms of
d
, limiting their performance. This is captured in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

There exists a projective measurement that can distinguish any ensemble of real-valued states from the complex-valued Haar random ensemble with probability
p
if given
t\sim\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1}{2(1-p)}\right)d}
copies.

■

Finally, we can, using the result on the trace distance, generalize
[
HBK25
]
’s result on the number of copies required for testing for imaginarity
[
HBK25
]
. We refine their lower-bound from
\Omega\!\left(\sqrt{2^{n}}\right)
to
\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)2^{n}}+o\!\left(\sqrt{2^{n}}\right)
by exactly computing the trace distance between the binary phase density matrix and the Haar random one, thus improving upon the works of
[
BS19
,
Ana+22
]
. We then show that this bound can be improved to
\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)d}+o\!\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
for an arbitrary dimension
d
. This not only generalizes their result to arbitrary dimensions, but improve the lower-bound by a factor
\sqrt{2}
. Along the way, we also derive the distribution of imaginarity for Haar random states, showing they follow a power law. This is informally stated in the following Proposition.

Proposition 2
(Informal)

Any algorithm testing whether a quantum state has any amount of imaginarity requires at least
\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)d}+o\!\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
copies of that quantum state to succeed with probability at least
\frac{2}{3}
.

\mathcal{O}_{d}

\mathcal{U}_{d}

d\times d

\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{S}^{t}
the set of objects from
\mathcal{S}

t

\mathcal{S}^{t}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left\{x^{\otimes t}\middle|x\in\mathcal{S}\right\}\,.

(5)

Two particularly important sets in this work are the set of real and complex-valued states, which we define as

\mathcal{R}_{d}=\left\{O\left|0\right\rangle\middle|O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{C}_{d}=\left\{U\left|0\right\rangle\middle|U\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\right\}\,,

(6)

respectively and to which we implicitly attach their respective Haar measures. We will also make extensive use of the notation

\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{R}

(7)

We can then define the symmetric subspace as the subspace whose elements are invariant under these actions, i.e.

\vee^{t}\mathbb{K}^{d}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left\{\left|v\right\rangle\in\left(\mathbb{K}^{d}\right)^{\otimes t}\,\middle|\,P_{\pi}\left|v\right\rangle=\left|v\right\rangle\,\,\forall\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}\right\}\,.

For a subspace

, with

3
Eigenvalues of real Haar random states

The goal of this section is to compute, for
d
and
t
being two natural numbers, the eigenvalues and associated multiplicities of the operator
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
(see Theorem
1
), where

\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}}\left(O\left|0\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle 0\right|O^{\top}\right)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(O)\,.

(9)

To begin, we will briefly review how the eigenvalues and multiplicities can be computed in the complex-case, i.e. for the operator

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(U\left|0\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle 0\right|U^{\dagger}\right)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(U)\,.

(10)

We note that this case is well-studied and arguably much simpler. We refer the interested reader to
[
Har13
]
where many of the concepts in this introductory discussion can be found in significantly more detail.

We begin by recalling two well-known facts about
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
. Firstly, the support of
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
is contained entirely within the symmetric subspace
\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}
and so for any
\left|v\right\rangle\in\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}
we have
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\left|v\right\rangle\in\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Secondly, for any
U\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we have that

U^{\otimes t}\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}=\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}U^{\otimes t}\,.

(11)

Using these two facts, we can apply some representation theory to diagonalize
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
. We provide a brief introduction to necessary concepts in
Appendix

A
. In particular, since
U\mapsto U^{\otimes t}
is an irreducible representation of
\mathcal{U}_{d}
in
\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}

[
Har13
, Theorem 5]
, it follows by
Corollary

2
, which is derived from
Lemma 5
(Schur)
, that
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
must be proportional to the identity operator on
\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}
, namely
\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}}
. That is, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}=\lambda\,\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}}
. Finally, we know that
\trace\left[\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right]=1
and
\trace\left[\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}}\right]=\dim(\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d})=\binom{d+t-1}{t}
and so

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}=\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}\,\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}}\,.

(12)

As
\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}}
is a projector we immediately obtain the spectral decomposition of
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
.

We now proceed to try to obtain a similar result for the case of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
. We begin with two lemmas whose proofs can be found in
Appendix

C
.

Lemma 1

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers. The support of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
viewed as an endomorphism of
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{\otimes t}
is contained within the real symmetric subspace
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

■

Lemma 2

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers. For any
O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}
we have

O^{\otimes t}\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}=\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}O^{\otimes t}\,.

(13)

■

Note at this point that the real-valued case appears to mirror the complex-valued case exactly. As such, we would now look to apply
Corollary

2
as we did previously. Unfortunately this is not immediately possible as
O\mapsto O^{\otimes t}
is not an irreducible representation of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
in
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
t>1
. Indeed, let us consider the case
t=d=2
. We have

\vee^{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}=\SpanOp_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\left\{\left|01\right\rangle+\left|10\right\rangle,\left|00\right\rangle-\left|11\right\rangle,\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle\right\}\right)\,.

(14)

However, a quick calculation shows that for any
O\in\mathcal{O}_{2}
we have
O^{\otimes 2}(\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle)=\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle
. Hence
\SpanOp_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\{\left|00\right\rangle+\left|11\right\rangle\}\right)
is a one-dimensional
\mathcal{O}_{2}
-invariant subspace and hence this representation is not irreducible.
Thus, in order to apply
Corollary

2
, we first need to decompose
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
into subspaces in which the representation
O\mapsto O^{\otimes t}
of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
is irreducible.

In order to construct such a decomposition, it will be useful to demonstrate an isomorphism between
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the space of homogeneous polynomials with
d
variables of degree
t
which we denote as

\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\SpanOp_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\left\{X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\middle|\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}=t\right\}\right)\,.

(15)

The isomorphism can be shown to exist by noting that the two vector spaces are over the same field and have the same dimension, namely
\binom{d+t-1}{t}
. However, it will prove useful to exhibit an explicit isomorphism between the two spaces. Consider the standard basis for
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes t}
which we may write as
\left|x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\right\rangle
for
x_{i}\in[d]
. To each basis vector we may associate a
d
-dimensional vector
a
taking values in
[t]
which counts the number of times each value in
[d]
appears in the basis vector, i.e.
a_{k}=\left|\left\{i\in[t]\,\middle|\,x_{i}=k\right\}\right|
. To construct a basis for
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
we can apply the map
\left|v\right\rangle\mapsto\sum\limits_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}}P_{\pi}\left|v\right\rangle
to each basis vector in the standard basis – the remaining unique vectors form the basis of
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We note that two vectors are the same under this mapping if and only if their associated
a
vectors are the same. In particular, this implies that the basis for
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
may be succinctly described by the unique
a
vectors. We can then map these
a
vectors to
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
by interpreting the
a
vector as encoding the the degrees of the different variables.

To illustrate this isomorphism, let us take
d=t=2
once again. An orthonormal basis for
\vee^{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}
is

\left\{\left|00\right\rangle,\left|11\right\rangle,\frac{\left|01\right\rangle+\left|10\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}\,,

(16)

whose elements have the respective
a
vectors
\{(2,0),(0,2),(1,1)\}
. The associated orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{P}_{2}^{2}
would then be

\left\{X_{0}^{2},X_{1}^{2},\sqrt{2}\,X_{0}X_{1}\right\}

(17)

where the norm of a monomial is given by the Bombieri norm
[
Bea+90
]
, which is defined as

\left\|\prod_{i}X_{i}^{a_{i}}\right\|\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{i}a_{i}!}{(\sum_{i}a_{i})!}}\,.

(18)

More formally, the isomorphism is given by

X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\mapsto\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle

(19)

with
\sum_{i}a_{i}=t
. In particular, this isomorphism described maps polynomial with unit Bombieri norm to quantum states with unit norm, as shown in
Lemma

6
.

Now for
k\leq t
, consider the subspace of homogeneous harmonic polynomials defined as

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{k}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{k}\middle|\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial X_{i}^{2}}=0\right\}\,.

(20)

A classical result from representation theory of the orthogonal group
[
CW68
, Theorem 2.12]
tells us that

\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}=\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t}\oplus q\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2}\oplus q^{2}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-4}\oplus\cdots

(21)

where

q\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}X_{i}^{2}\,.

(22)

Moreover, this result also states that there exists a certain representation of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
that acts irreducibly on each of these
q^{k}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2k}
subspaces. We show in
Lemma

8
that this representation corresponds exactly to
\mathcal{O}_{d}\ni O\mapsto O^{\otimes t}
when mapped back to
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
using the aforementioned isomorphism. As such, we can freely apply
Corollary

2
on each of these subspaces. We further know from
[
ABR13
, Proposition 5.8]
that for all
k\in\left[1+\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor\right]
, we have

\dim\left(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2k}\right)=\binom{d+t-2k-1}{d-1}-\binom{d+t-2k-3}{d-1}\,.

(23)

Note that in the case where
d=2
and
t
is even, we use the convention
\binom{-1}{1}=0
, so that
\dim\left(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{0}\right)=1
. Thus, we know the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and are only left with determining their values. In order to do so, we will explicitly construct corresponding eigenvectors.

To achieve this we begin by constructing a more explicit expression for
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
. For
x,y\in[d]^{t}
and for
i\in[d]
, we define
n_{i}(x,y)
to be the number of times
i
appears in the concatenation of
x
and
y
:

n_{i}(x,y)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left|\left\{z\in x|z=i\right\}\right|+\left|\left\{z\in y|z=i\right\}\right|\,.

(24)

This allows us to define the following equivalence relation on
[d]^{t}
:

x\sim y\iff\forall i\in[d],\,\,n_{i}(x,y)=0\!\!\!\!\pmod{2}\,.

(25)

That is,
x
and
y
are equivalent if and only if no element of
[d]
appears an odd number of times in the concatenation of
x
and
y
. Using this relation we are able to give a more explicit form of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
.

Lemma 3

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers. We have

\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}=\frac{(d-2)!!}{(d+2t-2)!!}\sum_{x\sim y}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[n_{i}(x,y)-1\right]!!\left|x\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle y\right|

(26)

with the convention
-1!!=0!!=1
.

■

As the proof is mostly computational, we defer it to
Section

C.3
.

With this explicit form we can use the isomorphism to generate eigenvectors
\left|v_{k}\right\rangle
and compute analytically
\lambda
such that
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\left|v_{k}\right\rangle=\lambda\left|v_{k}\right\rangle
. Overall, we arrive at the following theorem whose proof is given in
Section

C.4
.

Theorem 1

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers and let
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
be the eigenvalues of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and let
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k}
be their respective multiplicities. Then we have

\lambda_{k}=\frac{t!(d-2)!!}{(2k)!!(d+2t-2k-2)!!}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{k}=\binom{d+t-2k-1}{d-1}-\binom{d+t-2k-3}{d-1}

(27)

where
k\in\left[1+\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor\right]
.

■

As
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
commute, computing the trace distance
\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\|_{1}
is now straightforward and we find that

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\rfloor}\alpha_{k}\left|\lambda_{k}-\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}\right|

(28)

where
\lambda_{k}
and
\alpha_{k}
are given in
Theorem

1
. Through the operational interpretation of the trace distance in terms of distinguishability, this gives us precisely the advantage in distinguishing real-valued and complex-valued Haar random states when given access to
t
-copies. In certain cases we can also find a closed form expression for the trace distance. To begin, we note that
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
have the same rank and trace. Therefore, as
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
always has more than one eigenvalue (when
d,t\geq 2
) we necessarily have that
\lambda_{0}<\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}
. The above trace distance simplifies in the case when we have that
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}
which we describe in the following result.

Corollary 1

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers such that

\frac{t!(d-2)!!}{2(d+2t-4)!!}\geqslant\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}\,.

(29)

Then the trace distance between
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
is given by

4.1
Real-valued approximate state
t
-designs and ARS

Let us start by formally defining an
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design.

Definition 1
(
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design, adapted from
[
BS19
]
)

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. A set of quantum states
\mathcal{S}
equipped with a probability measure
\lambda
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate
t
-design if

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}}(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\leqslant\varepsilon\,.

(36)

■

By extension, we’ll generally call an ensemble of states an
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design when
\varepsilon
is the smallest number satisfying this definition, in which case we’ll call
\varepsilon
the
approximation parameter
of the approximate state design.

Note that the definition usually restricts the approximate design to be a finite ensemble of quantum states. Here we allow for arbitrary ensembles. Furthermore, though this definition defines
\varepsilon
-approximate states
t
-designs using the trace norm, we could instead require closeness in spectral norm, as originally done in
[
AE07
]
. In all cases, the approximation parameter
\varepsilon
translates how well does the design reproduces the first
t
moments of the Haar measure. It is thus natural to ask whether there are fundamental lower-bounds on this approximation parameter given certain constraints.

Following
[
BS19
]
’s footsteps, we can then use state
t
-designs to define Asymptotically Random States (ARS).

Definition 2
(Asymptotically Random State, adapted from
[
BS19
]
)

An Asymptotically Random State (ARS) is a sequence of
\Negl(\log(d))
-approximate state
\log(d)^{\omega(1)}
-designs, with
d
being the dimension of the quantum states and
\Negl
is a function such that for any polynomial
P
,
\Negl(x)<\frac{1}{P(x)}
for
x
being large enough.

■

Whilst this definition is somewhat technical it can be readily unpacked with an example.
Consider the case of qubit systems, where the dimension is
d=2^{n}
, with
n
being the number of qubits. Using this definition, an ARS is a sequence indexed by the number of qubits such that, when increasing the number of qubits by 1, one increases the number of copies (by more than
1
) that one can send to an adversary while retaining the same security.

When analysing constructions of ARS, it is common to fix
t
and to compute the optimal approximation parameter as a function of the dimension
d
and the number of copies
t
. As an example, let us consider the first ARS construction, introduced and studied in
[
JLS18
]
. For a fixed
t
and dimension
d
, one considers the set of states

\mathcal{S}_{\text{Fourier}}=\left\{\left|\psi_{f}^{\text{Fourier}}\right\rangle\middle|f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\left[2^{n}\right]\right\}

(37)

with
\left|\psi_{f}^{\text{Fourier}}\right\rangle
being defined as

\left|\psi_{f}^{\text{Fourier}}\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\mathrm{i}\pi f(x)}{2^{n}}}\,\left|x\right\rangle\,.

(38)

To make up an ensemble of states, we equip
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Fourier}}
with the uniform measure on the set of functions from the
n
-bit bitstrings
\{0,1\}^{n}
to
\left[2^{n}\right]
. That is, sampling a state in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Fourier}}
is done by assigning to the phase of each component
\left|x\right\rangle
of the statevector a randomly chosen power of the
2^{n}
-th root of unity
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2\mathrm{i}\pi}{2^{n}}}
.
Let us denote
\rho_{\mathcal{S}^{t}_{\text{Fourier}}}
the density matrix resulting from sampling
t
copies of a quantum state in
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Fourier}}
uniformly at random. The trace distance between
\rho_{\mathcal{S}^{t}_{\text{Fourier}}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
has been exactly computed in
[
JLS18
, Lemma 1]
. If we compute the asymptotics of this exact expression for the trace distance, we find that we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}^{t}_{\text{Fourier}}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}\left(2^{n}-1\right)\cdots\left(2^{n}-t+1\right)}{2^{nt}}-\frac{2^{n}\left(2^{n}-1\right)\cdots\left(2^{n}-t+1\right)}{\left(2^{n}+t-1\right)\cdots 2^{n}}

(39a)

\displaystyle=\frac{t(t-1)}{2^{n+1}}+\Theta\!\left(\frac{t^{4}}{2^{2n}}\right)\,.

(39b)

Thus,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{Fourier}}
equipped with the uniform measure forms a
\Theta\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2^{n}}\right)
-approximate state
t
-design for all
t
and
n
. Loosely speaking, increasing
n
by
1
allows to send
\sqrt{2}
more copies while keeping the same approximation parameter.

As
t
grows, it becomes more difficult to reproduce the first
t
moments of the Haar measure with a good precision, meaning that the optimal
\varepsilon
will at least grow with
t
. For the purposes of applications, we are interested in understanding the speed of this growth. For example, consider the private-key quantum money scheme described in
[
JLS18
, Theorem 6]
. For the sake of the argument, we will ignore all considerations relating to the complexity of producing the quantum states here. In this scheme, users can deposit a fixed amount of money in the bank, in exchange of which they receive a state
\left|\psi_{f_{\text{Bank}}}^{\text{Fourier}}\right\rangle
, where
f_{\text{Bank}}
is a secret function, known only to the bank and identical for all users. Intuitively, the security of this scheme relies on the fact that users can’t clone this quantum state, which means that the number of states that the bank can issue is limited, as it would otherwise risk the users colluding and perform tomography on this state. What the approximation parameter tells us is that if
n
is large enough, then as long as the bank issues less than
\approx\sqrt{2^{n}}
quantum states, the security of the system is ensured. Indeed, in that case, this quantum state cannot be readily distinguished from a Haar random state, even with
\Omega(\sqrt{2^{n}})
copies.

Whilst this is not a formal security proof, morally the approximation parameter limits the performance of the ARS in practical applications. For instance, had the bank used an ARS with a smaller approximation parameter, such as
O\!\left(\frac{t}{2^{n}}\right)
for the construction in
[
BS20
]
, then it could issue significantly more quantum states whilst remaining secure.

It is thus of interest to understand any fundamental limitations that exist on the approximation parameter of state
t
-designs and in turn ARS. To this end, let us consider the following lemma, which shows such a limitation.

Lemma 4
(Distinguishing advantage lower bound)

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be integers, let
p\in[1,\infty]
and let
\mathcal{S}_{d}
be any given set of
d
-dimensional real-valued pure quantum states equipped with a probability measure
\lambda
. Let

\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\,,

(40)

then we have

\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{p}\geqslant\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{p}\,.

(41)

■

Proof

Let us define
\Phi
to be the orthogonal group twirling channel:

\Phi\overset{\text{def}}{=}\rho\mapsto\int_{O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}}O^{\otimes t}\rho\left(O^{\top}\right)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(O)\,.

(42)

We have

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\right)

(43a)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\Phi\left(\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)

(43b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)

(43c)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}

(43d)

where on the third line we used the fact that we have we have
\Phi\left(\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\right)=\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
for any real-valued state
\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
. Similarly, we have
\Phi\left(\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right)=\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
as
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
is invariant under
t
-copy unitary conjugation. Now, since
\Phi
is a unital map, it is contractive under the
p
-norm
[
Pér+06
, Theorem II.4]
, which yields

\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{p}\geqslant\left\|\Phi\left(\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\right)-\Phi\left(\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right)\right\|_{p}=\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{p}

(44)

which concludes the proof.

■

The above lemma shows that
any
real-valued approximate state
t
-design is intrinsically limited in how well in can approximate the first
t
-moments of the Haar distribution on the whole space. It also formally shows, what one would intuitively expect, real Haar random states are the real-valued state
t
-design with the smallest approximation parameter.

Whilst the actual probability of distinguishing a real-valued ensemble of states and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
will likely be much higher than the lower bound implied by
Lemma

4
, it is interesting to note that an explicit projective measurement achieving the lower bound can easily by defined. If we let
\{\Pi_{+},\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{+}\}
be the optimal measurement for distinguishing
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
then it turns out that

\frac{1}{2}\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\trace\left[(\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{+})\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\|_{1}\,.

(45)

That is, the measurement
\{\Pi_{+},\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{+}\}
achieves precisely this lower bound as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers and and let
\mathcal{S}_{d}
be any given set of
d
-dimensional real-valued pure quantum states equipped with a probability measure
\lambda
. Let us denote

\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\,.

(46)

Let
\Pi_{+}
be the projector onto the eigenspaces of
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
associated to an eigenvalue larger than
\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}
. We then have

\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\right]=\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\right]\,.

(47)

■

Proof

Note that we have by linearity

\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\right]=\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\,.

(48)

Now, recall that by construction,
\Pi_{+}
can be written as

\Pi_{+}=\sum_{k}\Pi_{q^{k}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2k}}

(49)

where
\Pi_{q^{k}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2k}}
is the projector onto the subspace of
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
isomorphic to
q^{k}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2k}
, and where
k
indexes the eigenspaces where
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
has eigenvalues larger than
\binom{d+t-1}{t}^{-1}
. Since the representation
\mathcal{O}_{d}\ni O\mapsto O^{\otimes t}
is irreducible on these spaces, it means that
\Pi_{q^{k}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{t-2k}}
and
O^{\otimes t}
commute for all
O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}
and all indexes
k
. As a consequence,
\Pi_{+}
commutes with every
O^{\otimes t}
for
O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}
. This means that for all
\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
we have

\displaystyle\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\psi\right|^{\otimes t}\Pi_{+}\left|\psi\right\rangle^{\otimes t}

(50a)

\displaystyle=\left\langle 0\right|\left(O^{\top}\right)^{\otimes t}\Pi_{+}O^{\otimes t}\left|0\right\rangle

(50b)

\displaystyle=\left\langle 0\right|\Pi_{+}\left|0\right\rangle

(50c)

where
O
is any orthogonal matrix such that
O\left|0\right\rangle=\left|\psi\right\rangle
, and where on the third line we used the fact that
O^{\otimes t}
and
\Pi_{+}
commute. In particular, this quantity is independent of the input state. By linearity, we thus get that for all
\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
, we have

\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\left(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|\right)^{\otimes t}\right]=\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\right]

(51)

which implies by linearity and the fact that
\lambda
is a probability measure that

\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{S}_{d}^{t}}\right]=\trace\left[\Pi_{+}\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\right]\,.

(52)

■

Let us try to give some intuition as to why this result holds. In order to achieve the same probability of success, an adversary can apply the orthogonal twirling channel to the state they were given, and then apply the
\left\{\Pi_{+},\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{+}\right\}
measurement. However, there is an additional property, as shown in the proof, that this measurement is invariant under orthogonal conjugation and hence the distinguishing probability remains the same whether or not the twirling channel is applied.

4.2
Testing the imaginarity of a state

Consider the following task: a tester
\mathcal{A}
is given
t
copies of a pure state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
and must say whether the
imaginarity
of
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is larger than a certain threshold
\delta
with constant probability. Following
[
HBK25
, Equation 3]
, we define the imaginarity of a pure state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
as

\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\overset{\text{def}}{=}1-\left|\left\langle\psi\middle|\overline{\psi}\right\rangle\right|^{2}.

(53)

A formal definition of such a tester is given below.

Definition 3
(Imaginarity tester, adapted from
[
HBK25
, Definition 2]
)

Let
\delta>\beta>0
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is called a
(\beta,\delta,t)
-imaginarity tester if for all states
\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
it satisfies the following.

1.

(Completeness) If
\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\leqslant\beta
, then
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left|\psi\right\rangle^{\otimes t}\right)=1\right]\geqslant\frac{2}{3}
.

2.

(Soundness) If
\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\geqslant\delta
, then
\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{A}\left(\left|\psi\right\rangle^{\otimes t}\right)=1\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{3}
.

■

It was shown in
[
HBK25
, Lemma 2]
that Haar random states have high imaginarity on average, namely
1-\frac{2}{d+1}
, but distinguishing them from sets of real-valued states with non-negligible probability may still require
\Omega\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
copies. Since computing the imaginarity of a state is one way an adversary may try to distinguish sampling from a set of real-valued states and sampling from the Haar measure, it follows that computing the imaginarity on average must require at least as many copies as required to distinguish these two ensembles.

To see this more explicitly, let us consider an imaginarity tester
\mathcal{A}
using
t
copies and let us consider a real-valued
O\!\left(\frac{t^{x}}{d}\right)
-approximate state
t
-design
\left\{\left|\psi_{f}\right\rangle\right\}_{f}
, with
x>0
. We can use
\mathcal{A}
as a potential strategy for distinguishing between this approximate design and Haar random states. Namely, we use
\mathcal{A}
to test for the imaginarity of the states they’ve been given and if they have imaginarity larger than
\delta
, then they will conclude that they were given a Haar random state, and will otherwise conclude that they were given a state from the approximate design family. However, since the approximate design has an approximation parameter of
O\!\left(\frac{t^{x}}{d}\right)
,
\mathcal{A}
must necessarily have
\Omega\left(d^{\frac{1}{x}}\right)
copies to distinguish them with constant probability, thus proving that testing for imaginarity requires at least
\Omega\left(d^{\frac{1}{x}}\right)
copies. In particular, the smaller
x
, the larger the number of copies.

An interesting question is then what is the smallest
x
such that there exists a real-valued
O\!\left(\frac{t^{x}}{d}\right)
-approximate
t
-design as this will give us the best bound on the number of copies required to test for imaginarity using this technique. In
[
HBK25
]
the authors used the
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design which consists of the states

\left|\psi_{f,S}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{x\in S}(-1)^{f(x)}\,\left|x\right\rangle

(54)

equipped with the uniform probability measure and with
|S|=K
being a fixed parameter and
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, with
n
being the number of qubits. This construction was introduced and studied in
[
Aar+24
]
, where it was shown that
\varepsilon=O\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)
. In order to have the smallest
\varepsilon
possible,
[
HBK25
]
used
K=2^{n}
, effectively reducing to the binary phase state approximate design which is comprised of states of the form

\left|\psi_{f}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{x}(-1)^{f(x)}\,\left|x\right\rangle

(55)

also equipped with a uniform probability measure, which was introduced in
[
JLS18
]
and studied in
[
BS19
,
Ana+22
]
. In particular, it was proved in
[
Ana+22
]
that this construction yields an
\varepsilon
-approximate state
t
-design with
\varepsilon=\frac{t(3t-1)}{2^{n+1}}+O\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2^{2n}}\right)
. However, by focusing on a particular construction,
[
HBK25
]
left open the possibility for better bounds. Using
Corollary

1
, we can now close this door and affirm that no better asymptotic lower-bound can be found using this proof technique. In fact, using
Equation

33
, we can improve this lower-bound.

As we show in
Section

C.6
, if
t\sim\alpha\sqrt{d}
then the trace distance between the density matrix resulting from sampling from the family of binary phase states and the one resulting from sampling from the Haar distribution on the whole space converges to
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha^{2}}
. In particular, in order for
\mathcal{A}
to satisfy the completeness property, they should be able to distinguish these two states with probability at least
\frac{2}{3}
. Since we know that the optimal probability of distinguishing these two states is
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}T
, where
T
is the aforementioned trace distance, it follows that one must have
T\geqslant\frac{1}{3}
, which gives us
\alpha\geqslant\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)d}
.

But now, we can instead use the family of states defined by the Haar measure on the orthogonal group. As per
Equation

33
, we know that if
t\sim\alpha\sqrt{d}
with
\alpha\leqslant\sqrt{2}
, then the trace distance between the two relevant density matrices converges to
1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}}
. By a similar argument, we can then conclude that the number of copies required by
\mathcal{A}
is at least
t=\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)d}+o\!\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
. This thus improves upon
[
HBK25
]
’s bound by a
\sqrt{2}
factor. Furthermore, we know that this lower-bound is asymptotically optimal as
\delta
goes to 0, as our goal in this case is to distinguish between
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
, which we know from
Corollary

1
can be done using
t\sim\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)d}
copies. Note that
\ln\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)<1
, which ensures that we work in the
\alpha\leqslant\sqrt{2}
regime.

Finally, note the previous reasoning works in the limit of
\delta
being close to
0
, since we want in that case to distinguish an ensemble of states that is close to being real-valued and the Haar random ensemble.
[
HBK25
]
proved that it was in fact valid for all
\delta
lower than
1-\frac{n^{2}}{2^{\frac{n}{2}}}
(for
n\geqslant 80
). By studying the distribution of imaginarity of Haar random states, we can increase the regime of
\delta
in which this result holds, be more precise in how
\delta
affects the number of copies and simplify
[
HBK25
]
’s proof, as we don’t require the use of Lévy’s lemma anymore.

All of this is summarised and formally proved in the following theorem.

Proposition 2
(Generalization of
[
HBK25
, Theorem 2]
)

Any imaginarity tester
\mathcal{A}
of a quantum state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
requires at least
t\sim\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2}{3}+\delta^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\right)d}
copies of
\left|\psi\right\rangle
for any
\delta<3^{-\frac{2}{d+1}}
and
\beta<\delta
.

■

Proof

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}
be the ensemble of states having an imaginarity larger than or equal to
\delta
and let

\rho_{\mathcal{E}^{t}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\mu\left(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}\right)}\int_{\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}}(\left|\psi\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi\right|)^{\otimes t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\left|\psi\right\rangle)

(56)

be the average density matrix of this ensemble, with
\mu
being the Haar measure on
\mathcal{C}_{d}
. It was shown in
[
HBK25
, Equation (S17)]
that we have

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{E}^{t}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\leqslant\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)<\delta]\,.

(57)

We then get by triangle inequality

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\leqslant\delta\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\,.

(58)

Now, we know that an algorithm that is able to test for imaginarity must be able to distinguish
\rho_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}^{t}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}
with probability larger than
\frac{2}{3}
. But we also know that such an algorithm can only do so with probability
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}
. As such, in order for such an algorithm to exist, one must necessarily have

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{I}\geqslant\delta}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\,.

(59)

This in turn implies

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\leqslant\delta\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\,.

(60)

Now, we know from
Lemma

14
that

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\leqslant\delta\right]=\delta^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\,.

(61)

In particular, if we pick
\delta<\frac{1}{3^{\frac{2}{d-1}}}
, this ensures

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\leqslant\delta\right]<\frac{1}{3}\,.

(62)

One thus requires at least as many copies to test for imaginarity with such a
\delta
as one requires to have

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{R}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}-\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\leqslant\delta\right]\,.

(63)

We then know from the asymptotics derived in
Equation

33
that if one has

t\sim\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2}{3}+\delta^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\right)d}

(64)

then
Equation

63
is satisfied, which provides a lower-bound on the number of copies required to test for imaginarity. Indeed, note that
Equation

33
holds since

\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2}{3}+\delta^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\right)}\leqslant\sqrt{2}

(65)

holds for all positive
\delta
. This can be seen by the fact that the left-hand side term is a strictly decreasing function of
\delta
, which is thus maximized at
\delta=0
.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we proved a fundamental lower-bound on the approximation parameter of any real-valued approximate state
t
-design. We showed that this bound was tight and attained by the design resulting from sampling from the Haar measure on
\mathcal{O}_{d}
.
A notable implication of our work is a bound on the asymptotic approximation parameters of ARS generators, showing that having non-zero imaginarity is a requirement to have approximation parameters scaling linearly with the number of copies. As a second application, we also generalized a previous result of
[
HBK25
]
on imaginarity testing to arbitrary dimensions and improved upon the number of copies required for imaginarity testing.

On a technical level, our main result was the derivation of the complete spectrum of
t
-copies of a real Haar random state. We derived this through a classical result on a representation of the orthogonal group on the space of homogeneous polynomials. We believe that this isomorphism between the symmetric subspace and homogeneous polynomials could prove useful in other contexts when exploring applications of real-valued Haar random states and we leave such an exploration to future work.

However, our proofs prompt several further questions. Firstly, though we gave an explicit measurement that can be used to distinguish any real-valued approximate state design from a Haar random state, it is unclear if this measurement can be efficiently implemented. Possible directions to showing this would be to explore techniques like the Schur transform
[
Kro19
]
. If the measurement is efficiently implementable, this implies that our results for ARS will also extend to pseudorandom quantum states
[
JLS18
]
, as we will have derived distinguishing lower bounds that are accessible to computationally bounded adversaries. In particular, this will also imply fundamental limitations on the performance of real-valued constructions of pseudorandom states.

Finally, of particular interest, would be a study of further necessary conditions and sufficient conditions for approximate state
t
-designs to have an approximation parameter scaling linearly in
t
. Though we’ve proved in this work that having complex amplitudes is necessary, we do not know how much imaginarity an approximate state design requires to have such an approximation parameter or what other properties are also necessary. A comprehensive understanding of such conditions would pave the way towards designing efficient and practical ARS.

Appendix A
A very quick introduction to representation theory

In this section, we introduce the representation theory concepts used in the main text. Let
G
be a group and let
V
be a vector space of finite dimension
d
. A
representation
of
G
in
V
is a group homomorphism
\tau:G\to\generallinear\left(V\right)
, with
\generallinear\left(V\right)
being the general linear group associated to
V
. That is, the mapping preserves the group operation, i.e.,

\tau(gh)=\tau(g)\tau(h)

(66)

for all
g,h\in G
. A nontrivial subspace
6
6

6

A subspace
W\subseteq V
is called nontrivial if
W\neq\{0\}
and
W\neq V
.

W\subset V
is called
G
-invariant if for all
\left|w\right\rangle\in W
and all
g\in G
we have
\tau(g)\left|w\right\rangle\in W
. If no nontrivial
G
-invariant subspaces exist, then the representation is called
irreducible
. A fundamental result in representation theory is Schur’s lemma, which we now state. Note that this is an edulcorated version of the original lemma that we’ve adapted to our needs.

Lemma 5
(Schur)

Let
G
be a group,
V
be a finite dimensional vector space and
\tau:G\to\generallinear\left(V\right)
be an irreducible representation of
G
in
V
. Let
\phi
be an endomorphism of
V
. If
\phi
and
\tau(g)
commute for all
g\in G
, then
\phi
is either an automorphism or nil.

■

Schur’s lemma is commonly used in the quantum information field by the means of the following corollary, that allows to characterize a density matrix invariant by a group representation.

Corollary 2

Let
G
be a group,
V
be a finite dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and
\tau:G\to\generallinear\left(V\right)
be an irreducible representation of
G
in
V
. Let
\rho\in\Hermitian\left(V\right)
be such that
\rho
commutes with
\tau(g)
for all
g\in G
. Then there exists a
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho=\lambda\,\mathbb{I}_{V}
.

■

Proof

Since
\rho
is Hermitian it admits at least one real eigenvalue
\lambda
. Now, note that
\rho-\lambda\,\mathbb{I}_{V}
commutes with
\tau(g)
for all
g\in G
. By
Lemma 5
(Schur)
,
\rho-\lambda\,\mathbb{I}_{V}
is either an automorphism or is nil. Since it cannot be an automorphism by definition of
\lambda
, it follows that
\rho-\lambda\,\mathbb{I}_{V}
is nil, which concludes the proof.

Appendix B
On the isomorphism between
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}

The goal of this section is to translate the result of
[
CW68
, Theorem 2.12]
from the space of homogeneous polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
to the symmetric subspace
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. That is, we want to show that the representation of the orthogonal group on
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
as considered in
[
CW68
]
maps back to
\mathcal{O}_{d}\ni O\mapsto O^{\otimes t}
when using the isomomorphism that we consider between these two spaces.

Recall the isomorphism between
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by

X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\mapsto\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle\,.

(67)

The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\text{Bombieri}}
on
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
is defined for two monomials
M,N
as

\langle M,N\rangle_{\text{Bombieri}}=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if }M\neq N\\
\frac{1}{t!}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}!&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(68)

where
M=X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}
. For the remainder of this section, if
P\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
then
\left|P\right\rangle\in\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes its image by the isomorphism, the notation is motivated by the fact that this isomorphism preserves the inner product, as shown in the following Lemma.

Lemma 6

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers.
Let
P
and
Q
be two elements of
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
, then we have

\left\langle P,Q\right\rangle_{\text{Bombieri}}=\left\langle P\middle|Q\right\rangle\,.

(69)

That is, the isomorphism described in
Equation

19
preserves the inner product.

■

Proof

By linearity it is sufficient to show that the inner products are equal when
P
and
Q
are monomials. Then let
P=X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}
and
Q=X_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{b_{d-1}}
. Then

\left|P\right\rangle=\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}}A_{\sigma}\left|0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle

(70)

and

\left|Q\right\rangle=\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}}A_{\pi}\left|0^{b_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle

(71)

where we’ve denoted

A_{\pi}(\left|v_{1}\right\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes\left|v_{t}\right\rangle)=\left|v_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\right\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes\left|v_{\pi^{-1}(t)}\right\rangle

(72)

for any permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}
and any basis state
\left|v_{1},\cdots,v_{t}\right\rangle
of
\left(\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)^{\otimes t}
. We thus have

\left\langle P\middle|Q\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(t!)^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}}\left\langle 0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\middle|A_{\sigma}A_{\pi}\middle|0^{b_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\,.

(73)

Note that whenever there is an index
i
such that
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
, then the expectation value in the summation vanishes, as it can be seen as the inner product between two different computational basis states. This is consistent with the definition of the Bombieri inner product, which should be
0
whenever the two monomials are not equal.

Let us thus assume that
a_{i}=b_{i}
for all indices
i
. The expectation value in the sums in now either equal to
0
or
1
, depending on whether
\sigma
and
\pi
send
\left(0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right)
to the same tuple. Let us fix
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}
and let us count the number of
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}
such that
\sigma\left(0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right)=\pi\left(0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right)
. To perform this counting, note that for such a permutation, we can swap the placements of the different
0
elements and still end up with a permutation having this property. There are
a_{0}!
such permutations of the
0
elements. Similarly, there are
a_{1}!
permutations of the
1
elements. Choosing a valid
\pi
is equivalent to starting from
\sigma
and choosing a permutation of the
i
elements that leaves the result invariant for all
i
elements. All in all, this amounts to
\prod_{i}a_{i}!
permutations. As such, we have

\left\langle P\middle|Q\right\rangle=\frac{1}{(t!)^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}}\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}!=\frac{1}{t!}\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}a_{i}!

(74)

which is consistent with the Bombieri inner product definition.

■

Since the symmetric subspace is intrinsically linked to the tensor product, it is natural to wonder how the tensor product is translated through the isomorphism between
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To that end, we start by showing the following Lemma that relates together projctors onto symmetric subspaces of different sizes.

Lemma 7

Let
d\geqslant 2
,
t\geqslant 1
and
k\geqslant 1
be three natural numbers. Then

\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\otimes\Pi_{\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)=\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,.

(75)

■

Proof

Note that
\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\otimes\Pi_{\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is the projector onto
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As such, if we show that
\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subspace of
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the result would follow.

Note that
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the space of states that are invariant under operators of the form
P_{\pi}\otimes P_{\sigma}
, with
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
. As a recall, if
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
for some natural number
n
, then
P_{\tau}
is defined on the basis states of
\mathbb{C}^{\otimes n}
as

P_{\tau}\left|x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\right\rangle=\left|x_{\tau^{-1}(1)},\cdots,x_{\tau^{-1}(n)}\right\rangle\,.

(76)

But now, note that we can see applying a permutation
\pi
on the first
t
registers and a permutation
\sigma
on the last
k
ones as a permutation on the whole
t+k
registers. In particular, every state of
\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
is also left invariant by all operators of the form
P_{\pi}\otimes P_{\sigma}
for
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{t}
and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, which shows that

\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}\subseteq\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(77)

In particular, the projectors onto these spaces satisfy the desired relation.

■

We can now leverage this Lemma to prove that, up to an additional symmetrization using the projector onto the symmetric subspace, the tensor product of states in
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}
relates to the product of polynomials in
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
.

Corollary 3

Let
d\geqslant 2
,
t\geqslant 1
and
k\geqslant 1
be three natural numbers. Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{k}
. The image of
PQ
by the isomorphism described in
Equation

19
is
\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\left|P\right\rangle\otimes\left|Q\right\rangle)
.

■

Proof

Let
\left\{P_{i}\right\}_{i}
and
\left\{Q_{j}\right\}_{j}
be two orthogonal bases of
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{k}
respectively, and let us write

P=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\,P_{i}

(78a)

and

Q=\sum_{j}\mu_{j}\,Q_{j}\,.

(78b)

We have on the one hand

PQ=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,P_{i}Q_{j}

(79)

and on the other hand

\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\left|P\right\rangle\otimes\left|Q\right\rangle)=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}\,\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|P_{i},Q_{j}\right\rangle\,.

(80)

We thus only have to show the result for monomials. Let
X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
X_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{b_{d-1}}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{k}
be two monomials. We first have by the definition of the isomorphism between the symmetric subspace and the space of homogeneous polynomials

\begin{split}&\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\left|X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle\otimes\left|X_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\right)\\
={}&\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle\otimes\Pi_{\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|0^{b_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\right)\,.\end{split}

(81)

We can now factor out the different tensor products, which gives us

\begin{split}&\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\left|X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle\otimes\left|X_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\right)\\
={}&\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\otimes\Pi_{\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)\left|0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}},0^{b_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\,.\end{split}

(82)

We can now use
Lemma

7
to remove the projector into
\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\vee^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which gives us

\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\left|X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle\otimes\left|X_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\right)=\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|0^{a_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}},0^{b_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\,.

(83)

Finally, since
\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is invariant under all permutations of registers, we can shift around the different registers to get

\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\left|X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\right\rangle\otimes\left|X_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{b_{d-1}}\right\rangle\right)=\Pi_{\vee^{t+k}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|0^{a_{0}+b_{0}},\cdots,(d-1)^{a_{d-1}+b_{d-1}}\right\rangle

(84)

which is equal to
\left|X_{0}^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}+b_{d-1}}\right\rangle
by definition.

■

We will now slightly generalize this statement to products of polynomials with more than two terms.

\Pi_{\vee^{K-k_{N+1}}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigotimes_{i=0}^{N-1}\left|P_{i}\right\rangle

3
, we thus have that the image of
\prod_{i=0}^{N}P_{i}

\mathcal{O}_{d}
in

used in

As a recall, a representation

Lemma 8

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers. Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
and
O\in\mathcal{O}_{d}
. The image of
P\left(O^{\top}X\right)
by the isomorphism described in
Equation

19
is
O^{\otimes t}\left|P\right\rangle
.

■

Proof

Let
\left\{P_{i}\right\}_{i}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
. Let us write
P
in this basis, which gives us

P=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\,P_{i}\,.

(90)

We then have

P\left(O^{\top}X\right)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\,P_{i}\left(O^{\top}X\right)\,.

(91)

We thus only have to show the result on monomials. Let
Q=X_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots X_{d-1}^{a_{d-1}}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{t}
be a monomial. We have by definition

Q\left(O^{\top}X\right)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left\langle i\middle|O\middle|0\right\rangle\,X_{i}\right)^{a_{0}}\cdots\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left\langle i\middle|O\middle|d-1\right\rangle\,X_{i}\right)^{a_{d-1}}=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left\langle i\middle|O\middle|j\right\rangle\,X_{i}\right)^{a_{j}}\,.

(92)

Since this is a product of polynomials, we can use
Corollary

4
to assert that

\left|Q\left(O^{\top}X\right)\right\rangle=\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigotimes_{j=0}^{d-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left\langle i\middle|O\middle|j\right\rangle\,\left|i\right\rangle\right)^{\otimes a_{j}}\,.

(93)

Indeed, the image of any
P^{a}
by the isomorphism is
\left|P\right\rangle^{\otimes a}
, which can also be seen as a consequence of
Corollary

4
. Now, note that we have

\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left\langle i\middle|O\middle|j\right\rangle\,\left|i\right\rangle=O\left|j\right\rangle

(94)

for any indices
i
and
j
in
[d]
. This gives us

\left|Q\left(O^{\top}X\right)\right\rangle=\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigotimes_{j=0}^{d-1}\left(O\left|j\right\rangle\right)^{\otimes a_{j}}\,.

(95)

We can now factor the orthogonal matrices out of the sum like so

\left|Q\left(O^{\top}X\right)\right\rangle=\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}O^{\otimes t}\bigotimes_{j=0}^{d-1}\left|j\right\rangle^{\otimes a_{j}}\,.

(96)

Now, recall that
\Pi_{\vee^{R}\mathbb{R}^{d}}=\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}}
commute with every unitary matrices tensored
t
times. As such we have

\left|Q\left(O^{\top}X\right)\right\rangle=O^{\otimes t}\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigotimes_{j=0}^{d-1}\left|j\right\rangle^{\otimes a_{j}}\,.

(97)

Finally, by definition of the isomorphism, we have

\Pi_{\vee^{t}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigotimes_{j=0}^{d-1}\left|j\right\rangle^{\otimes a_{j}}=\left|Q\right\rangle

(98)

which concludes the proof.

C.6
Exactly trace distance between binary phase states and Haar random states

In order to give a more precise asymptotics of
[
HBK25
]
’s argument on the number of copies required to test for the imaginarity of a state, we need to know for which regime does the trace distance between the density matrix resulting from sampling
t
copies of a binary phase state and the one resulting from sampling
t
copies of a Haar random state converges to a given value. In order to do so, we will compute exactly the trace distance between these two density matrices.

Note that our result only holds whenever
t\leqslant d
. Fortunately, as we’ll see, this covers every interesting regime for pseudorandomness, since this trace distance converges to
1
whenever
t=\omega\!\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
. Extending this result to
t>d
using the same technique is certainly possible, but this assumption greatly simplifies the analysis of the problem.

Lemma 10

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\leqslant d
be two natural numbers. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of functions from
[d]
to
\{0,1\}
. Let
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
be the density matrix resulting from sampling
t
copies of a binary phase state, that is

\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}(\left|\psi_{f}\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle\psi_{f}\right|)^{\otimes t}

(157)

where
\left|\psi_{f}\right\rangle
is defined as

\left|\psi_{f}\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{x}(-1)^{f(x)}\left|x\right\rangle

(158)

for
f
being a binary function on
[d]
. We have

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}=1-\frac{1}{\binom{d+t-1}{t}}\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor}\binom{d}{t-2k}\,.

(159)

■

Proof

By a similar argument to the one that was used in
[
BS19
, Subsection 4.2.3]
, we have

\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}=\frac{1}{d^{t}}\sum_{x\sim y}\left|x\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle y\right|

(160)

with
\sim
being an equivalence relation on
[d]^{t}
which is defined as
x\sim y
if and only if no element
z\in[d]
appears an odd number of times in the concatenation
x\parallel y
of
x
and
y
. We thus know via
Lemma

13
that we can find the eigenvalues of
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
and their multiplicities by studying the equivalence classes of
\sim
. Indeed, in this case, this Lemma asserts that the eigenvalue of
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
are exactly the sizes of the equivalence classes of
\sim
divided by
\frac{1}{d^{t}}
. Let us thus characterize these equivalence classes.

Suppose an element
z\in[d]
appears an odd number of times in
x\in[d]^{t}
. Then in order for
y\in[d]^{t}
to be in relation with
x
,
y
must also contain
z
an odd number of times. Putting things differently, two elements
x
and
y
are in relation if and only if they have in common their elements that appear an odd number of times in them. Thus, an equivalence class is uniquely defined by a set
\left\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{k}\right\}\in[d]^{k}
which defines the elements of
[d]
that appear an odd number of times in the
t
-tuple. Note that
k
necessarily has the same parity as
t
. Indeed, if
t
is odd and
k
is even, then there will necessarily be another element
z_{k+1}
that must appear in
x
and
y
an odd number of times to complete the
t
-tuple. Similarly, if
t
is even, then so must be
k
.

Let us write
t
as
t=2T+b
with
b\in\{0,1\}
being its parity and let us consider an equivalence class uniquely defined by a set of
2k+b
elements of
[d]
. We are then interested in the size of this equivalence class. Note that this size does not depend on the individual values of
z_{1},\cdots,z_{2k+b}
but only on
k

2k+b
have the same size. It means that if we denote
\lambda_{k}
the associated eigenvalue, it has multiplicity at least
\binom{d}{2k+b}
, since there are
\binom{d}{2k+b}
ways of choosing the
2k+b
elements of
[d]
that must have an odd cardinality.

Furthermore, note that the larger
k
and the lower the size of the equivalence class. Indeed, consider an equivalence class defined by a set containing
2k+2+b
elements
\left\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{2k+2+b}\right\}
. Consider a
t
-tuple within this equivalence class and replace each
z_{2k+1+b}
and
z_{2k+2+b}
by any element
z^{\prime}\in[d]
. Note that this results in an element of the equivalence class defined by
\left\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{2k+b}\right\}
. Indeed, since both
z_{2k+1+b}
and
z_{2k+2+b}
appear an odd number of times, the sum of their cardinality is necessarily even. As such, for each element in an equivalence class defined by
2k+b+2

\left\{z_{1},\cdots,z_{2k+b}\right\}
. In particular, the smallest equivalence class is the one defined by having the maximum number of odd cardinality elements, namely
t
(recall that we assumed
t\leqslant d
). The size of this equivalence class is then the number of permutations of these elements, namely
t!
. As such, the smallest positive eigenvalue of
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
is
\frac{t!}{d^{t}}
.

Now, note that we have

\rank\left(\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor}\binom{d}{t-2k}

(161)

which we find by summing the multiplicities of the eigenvalues. In particular, when seen as an operator on
\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}

\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
is not full-rank. As such, the lowest eigenvalue of
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}

\frac{t!}{d^{t}}
. But now, note that we have

\frac{(d+t-1)!}{(d-1)!}\geqslant d^{t}

(162)

which can be seen by rewriting the left-hand side term as a product of

\frac{1}{\binom{d+t-1}{t}}\leqslant\frac{t!}{d^{t}}\,.

(163)

Since the second lowest eigenvalue of
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
is always larger than the unique eigenvalue of
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}
, their trace distance admits a simple form, namely

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}=\frac{\binom{d+t-1}{t}-\rank\left(\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}\right)}{\binom{d+t-1}{t}}

(164)

with
\binom{d+t-1}{t}-\rank\left(\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}\right)
being the multiplicity of the 0 eigenvalue of
\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}
viewed as an operator on
\vee^{t}\mathbb{C}^{d}

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}=\frac{t(t-1)}{d}+\Theta\!\left(\frac{t^{2}}{d^{4}}\right)\,.

(165)

\frac{1}{2}\left\|\rho_{\mathcal{B}_{d}^{t}}-\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{t}}\right\|_{1}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha^{2}}+o\!\left(1\right)\,.

Lemma 11

Let
d\geqslant 2
be a natural number. Let
n_{0},\cdots,n_{d-1}
be natural numbers and let us denote their sum by
N
. Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be distributed according to a standard centered multivariate normal law
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)
. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{W_{i}^{n_{i}}}{\|W\|^{n_{i}}}\right]=\begin{cases}\frac{(d-2)!!}{(d+N-2)!!}\prod\limits_{i=0}^{d-1}\left(n_{i}-1\right)!!&\text{ if all }n_{i}\text{ are even,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(167)

■

Proof

We’ll use the well-known fact that the magnitude
\|W\|
and the direction
\frac{W}{\|W\|}
of a gaussian vector are independent random variables. We then have on the one hand

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}W_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]

(168)

since the
\left\{W_{i}\right\}_{i}
are independent. We then have that if one of the
n_{i}
is odd, this expectation is equal to
0
. Otherwise we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}W_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\left(n_{i}-1\right)!!\,.

(169)

On the other hand, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}W_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|W\|^{N}\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{W_{i}^{n_{i}}}{\|W\|^{n}_{i}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|W\|^{N}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{W_{i}^{n_{i}}}{\|W\|^{n}_{i}}\right]\,.

(170)

Thus, we know that the expectation that we want to compute will be nil if at least one of the
n_{i}
is odd. Let us thus assume that all the
n_{i}
are even. This means that
N
is also even, which means that
\|W\|^{N}
follows a
\chi^{2}\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)
law. This gives us

\mathbb{E}\left[\|W\|^{N}\right]=\frac{\left(d+N-2\right)!!}{\left(d-2\right)!!}

(171)

from which the result follows.

Lemma 12

Let
(n,p,q)\in\mathbb{N}
. We have

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2n-2k-1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!=\frac{(p-1)!!(q-1)!!}{(p+q)!!}(2n+p+q)!!\,.

(172)

■

Proof

We prove this by induction on
n
. We denote
u_{n,p,q}
this sum. First, note that this is true for
n=0
. Let us thus assume that this assertion is true for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and let us show that it also holds for
n+1
and all
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
. We have

u_{n+1,p,q}=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{k}(2n-2k+1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!\,.

(173)

Let us first remove the first and last terms out of the sum to get

u_{n+1,p,q}=(2n+1+q)!!(p-1)!!+(q-1)!!(2n+1+p)!!+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k}(2n-2k+1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!\,.

(174)

We can then split
\binom{n+1}{k}
into
\binom{n}{k}+\binom{n}{k-1}
to get

\begin{split}u_{n+1,p,q}={}&(2n+1+q)!!(p-1)!!+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2n-2k+1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!+{}\\
&(q-1)!!(2n+1+p)!!+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k-1}(2n-2k+1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!\,.\end{split}

(175)

We then change the index of the second sum to get

\begin{split}u_{n+1,p,q}={}&(2n+1+q)!!(p-1)!!+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2n-2k+1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!+{}\\
&(q-1)!!(2n+1+p)!!+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}(2n-2k-1+q)!!(2k+1+p)!!\,.\end{split}

(176)

We are now able to merge into these two sums the terms that we previously left out to get

u_{n+1,p,q}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2n-2k+1+q)!!(2k-1+p)!!+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(2n-2k-1+q)!!(2k+1+p)!!

(177)

which we identify as

u_{n+1,p,q}=u_{n,p,q+2}+u_{n,p+2,q}\,.

(178)

We thus have by our induction assumption

u_{n+1,p,q}=\frac{(p-1)!!(q+1)!!}{(p+q+2)!!}(2n+p+q+2)!!+\frac{(p+1)!!(q-1)!!}{(p+q+2)!!}(2n+p+q+2)!!\,.

(179)

We then factorize this expression to get

u_{n+1,p,q}=\frac{(2n+p+q+2)!!}{(p+q+2)!!}(p-1)!!(q-1)!!(p+1+q+1)

(180)

which we can finally simplify to

u_{n+1,p,q}=\frac{(2(n+1)+p+q)!!}{(p+q)!!}(p-1)!!(q-1)!!

(181)

which concludes the proof.

Corollary 5

Let
d\geqslant 2
,
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
be four natural numbers. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{d-1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant k_{1}=0}^{k_{0}}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{d-2}\binom{k_{j}}{k_{j+1}}\left(2k_{j}-2k_{j+1}+q-1\right)!!\right]\left(2k_{d-1}+p-1\right)!!

(182)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\left(2k_{0}+p+(d-1)(q+1)-1\right)!!(p-1)!!\left[(q-1)!!\right]^{d-1}}{(p+(d-1)(q+1)-1)!!}\,.

■

Proof

We prove this by induction on
d
. The case
d=2
reduces to the previous lemma and is thus true. Let us assume that this proposition is true for some
d
, and let’s prove that it’s also true for
d+1
. Let us denote

u_{d,k_{0},p,q}=\sum_{k_{d-1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant k_{1}=0}^{k_{0}}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{d-2}\binom{k_{j}}{k_{j+1}}\left(2k_{j}-2k_{j+1}+q-1\right)!!\right]\left(2k_{d-1}+p-1\right)!!

(183)

for all
d\geqslant 2
,
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, we have

u_{d+1,k_{0},p,q}=\sum_{k_{d}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant k_{1}=0}^{k_{0}}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\binom{k_{j}}{k_{j+1}}\left(2k_{j}-2k_{j+1}+q-1\right)!!\right]\left(2k_{d}+p-1\right)!!\,.

(184)

By summing over the terms that depend only on the
k_{d}
index, we find that we have

u_{d+1,k_{0},p,q}=\sum_{k_{d-1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant k_{1}=0}^{k_{0}}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{d-2}\binom{k_{j}}{k_{j+1}}\left(2k_{j}-2k_{j+1}+q-1\right)!!\right]u_{2,k_{d-1},p,q}\,.

(185)

We then have by
Lemma

12

u_{d+1,k_{0},p,q}=\frac{(p-1)!!(q-1)!!}{(p+q)!!}\sum_{k_{d-1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant k_{1}=0}^{k_{0}}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{d-2}\binom{k_{j}}{k_{j+1}}\left(2k_{j}-2k_{j+1}+q-1\right)!!\right]\left(2k_{d-1}+p+q\right)!!

(186)

which we can identify as

u_{d+1,k_{0},p,q}=\frac{(p-1)!!(q-1)!!}{(p+q)!!}u_{d,k_{0},p+q+1,q}\,.

(187)

We thus have by our induction assumption

u_{d+1,k_{0},p,q}=\frac{(p-1)!!(q-1)!!\left[2k_{0}+p+q+1+(d-1)(q+1)-1\right]!!(p+q)!!\left[(q-1)!!\right]^{d-1}}{(p+q)!![p+q+1+(d-1)(q+1)-1]!!}

(188)

which we can finally simplify to

u_{d+1,k_{0},p,q}=\frac{(p-1)!!\left[2k_{0}+p+d(q+1)-1\right]!!\left[(q-1)!!\right]^{d}}{[p+d(q+1)-1]!!}

(189)

which concludes the proof.

Lemma 13

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
t\geqslant 1
be two natural numbers. Let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
[d]^{t}
. For an element
x
of
[d]^{t}
, we define
\mathcal{C}(x)
to be its equivalence class with respect to
\sim
and we define:

\left|\mathcal{C}(x)\right\rangle=\sum_{y\sim x}\left|y\right\rangle\,.

(190)

Let
\rho
be a density matrix that can be written as:

\rho=\sum_{x\in[d]^{t}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[d]^{t}\\
y\sim x\end{subarray}}\left|x\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle y\right|

(191)

with
\left\{\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(x)}\right\}_{x\in[d]^{t}}
being arbitrary positive numbers that are constant across equivalence classes.
We then have:

\rho=\sum_{z\in[d]^{t}_{/\sim}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(z)}|\mathcal{C}(z)|\,\Pi_{\mathcal{C}(z)}\,.

(192)

In particular, since
\left\langle\mathcal{C}(x)\middle|\mathcal{C}(y)\right\rangle\neq 0\iff\mathcal{C}(x)=\mathcal{C}(y)
, this is the spectral decomposition of
\rho
.

■

Proof

Let
z\in[d]^{t}
. Let us show that
\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle
s an eigenvector of
\rho
. We have by definition of the different terms

\rho\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle=\sum_{x\in[d]^{t}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[d]^{t}\\
y\sim x\end{subarray}}\left|x\middle\rangle\!\middle\langle y\right|\sum_{w\sim z}\left|w\right\rangle\,.

(193)

Since
\left\langle y\middle|w\right\rangle=\delta_{y,w}
, this simplifies to

\rho\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle=\sum_{x\in[d]^{t}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[d]^{t}\\
y\sim x\\
y\sim z\end{subarray}}\left|x\right\rangle\,.

(194)

Now, note that the summand is independent of the summation index
y
, that is

\rho\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[d]^{t}\\
x\sim z\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(x)}\,\left|x\right\rangle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in[d]^{t}\\
y\sim x\end{subarray}}1\,.

(195)

The rightmost term simply counts the number of elements that are in relation with a given
x\in[d]^{t}
, which is the size of
\mathcal{C}(x)
by definition. We thus have

\rho\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[d]^{t}\\
x\sim z\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(x)}|\mathcal{C}(x)|\,\left|x\right\rangle\,.

(196)

Now, note that by assumption the coefficients in the summand are constant across a given equivalence class, which means that we have

\rho\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle=\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(z)}|\mathcal{C}(z)|\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[d]^{t}\\
x\sim z\end{subarray}}\left|x\right\rangle\,.

(197)

Finally, note that the rightmost sum is now
\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle
by definition, which finally gives us

\rho\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle=\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(z)}|\mathcal{C}(z)|\,\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle\,.

(198)

Thus, for any
z
,
\left|\mathcal{C}(z)\right\rangle
is an eigenvector of
\rho
with its eigenvalue being
\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(z)}|\mathcal{C}(z)|
. In order to consider a single element of each equivalence class, we can quotient
[d]^{t}
by
\sim
. Finally, note that there is no other eigenvectors associated to a non-zero eigenvalue, since:

\displaystyle\trace\left[\rho\right]=\sum_{z\in[d]^{t}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(z)}=\sum_{z\in[d]^{t}_{/\sim}}\alpha_{\mathcal{C}(z)}|\mathcal{C}(z)|\,.

(199a)

■

Before introducing the next Lemma, we recall the definition and some properties of the Beta distribution.

A random variable
X
follows a
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
distribution with
\alpha
and
\beta
being two positive numbers if its density function is given by

f_{\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}x^{\alpha-1}\,(1-x)^{\beta-1}&\text{if }x\in(0,1)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(200)

with
\Gamma
being the Gamma function. In particular, note that we have

\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}

(201)

for all positive numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
. If
X
is distributed according to a
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
law, then its moments are given by

\mathbb{E}\left[X^{n}\right]=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+k}

(202)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finally, we’ll make use of the fact that if
A
follows a
\chi^{2}(p)
law and
B
follows a
\chi^{2}(q)
law and
A
and
B
are independent, then
\frac{A}{A+B}
follows a
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{p}{2},\frac{q}{2}\right)
law.

We are now ready to state the following Lemma on the distribution of imaginarity of Haar random states.

Lemma 14

Let
d\geqslant 2
be a natural number. Recall that the imaginarity of a pure state
\left|\psi\right\rangle\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
is given by

\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)=1-\left|\left\langle\overline{\psi}\middle|\psi\right\rangle\right|^{2}\,.

(203)

If
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is distributed according to the Haar measure on
\mathcal{C}_{d}
, then
\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)
follows a
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d-1}{2},1\right)
law.

■

Proof

Since
\left|\psi\right\rangle
is Haar random, we can write it as

\left|\psi\right\rangle=\frac{A+\mathrm{i}\,B}{\sqrt{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}}

(204)

with
A
and
B
being independently distributed according to a central standard normal law
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)
, as per
[
Mez07
, Theorem 1]
.
We have

\left\langle\overline{\psi}\middle|\psi\right\rangle=\frac{\|A\|^{2}-\|B\|^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\left\langle A\middle|B\right\rangle}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\,.

(205)

This gives us

\left|\left\langle\overline{\psi}\middle|\psi\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{\left(\|A\|^{2}-\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}+4\left\langle A\middle|B\right\rangle^{2}}{\left(\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}}

(206)

which finally gives us

\displaystyle 1-\left|\left\langle\overline{\psi}\middle|\psi\right\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\|A\|^{2}-\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}-4\left\langle A\middle|B\right\rangle^{2}}{\left(\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}}

(207a)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\|A\|^{2}\|B\|^{2}-4\left\langle A\middle|B\right\rangle^{2}}{\left(\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}}

(207b)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\|A\|^{2}\|B\|^{2}}{\left(\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}\right)^{2}}\left(1-\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|\frac{B}{\|B\|}\right\rangle^{2}\right)\,.

(207c)

We can rewrite this expression as

\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)=4\left(\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\left(1-\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|\frac{B}{\|B\|}\right\rangle^{2}\right)\,.

(208)

Since
A
is a standard normal gaussian variable, we have that
\|A\|
and
\frac{A}{\|A\|}
are independent, and similarly for
B
. As such, we have that
\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}
and
\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|\frac{B}{\|B\|}\right\rangle^{2}
are independent.

Furthermore, on the one hand, by the rotational invariance of the gaussian distribution, we have that
\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|\frac{B}{\|B\|}\right\rangle^{2}
has the same distribution as
\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|0\right\rangle^{2}
, which we can write as
\frac{A_{0}^{2}}{A_{0}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}A_{i}^{2}}
. Since
A_{0}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(1)
and
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}A_{i}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(d-1)
are independent, we have that this variable follows a
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
law. We thus have that
1-\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|\frac{B}{\|B\|}\right\rangle^{2}
follows a
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
law. In particular, its moments are given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\left\langle\frac{A}{\|A\|}\middle|\frac{B}{\|B\|}\right\rangle^{2}\right)^{n}\right]=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\frac{d-1}{2}+k}{\frac{d}{2}+k}=\frac{(d+2n-3)!!(d-2)!!}{(d-3)!!(d+2n-2)!!}

(209)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
as per
Equation

202
.

On the other hand, by a similar argument, we have that
\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}
follows a
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{d}{2},\frac{d}{2}\right)
law. We thus have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(d)}{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}(1-x)^{n}x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}(1-x)^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\,\mathrm{d}x

(210)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This simplifies to

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(d)\Gamma^{2}\left(n+\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\Gamma(2n+d)}

(211)

as per
Equation

201
. Using the
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
identity, we then find

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}\right)\right)^{n}\right]=\frac{(d-1)![(2n+d-2)!!]^{2}}{4^{n}(2n+d-1)![(d-2)!!]^{2}}\,.

(212)

Now, since the two variables we computed the moments of are independent, the moments of their product are given by the product of their moments. That is, we have for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{I}^{n}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)\right]=\frac{(d-1)!(2n+d-2)!!(d+2n-3)!!}{(2n+d-1)!(d-2)!!(d-3)!!}=\frac{(d-1)!(2n+d-2)!}{(d-2)!(2n+d-1)!}=\frac{d-1}{2n+d-1}\,.

(213)

Thus, the moments of
\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)
match with the moments of the
\beta\left(\frac{d-1}{2},1\right)
law.

Since
\mathcal{I}(\left|\psi\right\rangle)
is defined over the bounded interval
[0,1]
, its moments completely define its distribution as per the Hausdorff moment problem, which concludes the proof.
