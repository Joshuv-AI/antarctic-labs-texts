Molecular mechanisms, therapeutic strategies, and inter-organ fibrosis crosstalk of Gla proteins in cardiovascular disease
Authors: Shuai Zhao, Siena Bush, Ivan O Rosas, Svetlana Reilly, Xander H T Wehrens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGla proteins are a vitamin K–dependent family long studied for their roles in blood clotting and vascular calcification. This review examines their broader involvement in cardiovascular disease, particularly cardiac fibrosis. We highlight that their functions are context-dependent, varying with disease stage and cellular environment, and that several occur independently of γ-carboxylation, challenging the view that this modification is universally required for their function. By comparing fibrosis across the heart, lung, liver, and kidney, we distinguish shared from organ-specific mechanisms. Finally, we discuss prospective therapies, from vitamin K supplementation to receptor-targeted approaches, and the questions that remain before clinical translation.Subject terms: Mechanisms of disease, Cardiovascular biology, Post-translational modifications

Introduction

γ-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla) proteins are widely expressed in various tissues in secreted, membrane-bound, or circulating forms, contributing to physiological homeostasis and various pathological processes1. These proteins are traditionally classified as vitamin K-dependent because of the post-translational y-carboxylation of glutamic acid residues (Glu) within their Gla domains1. This modification gives them a high-affinity for calcium, enabling their involvement in essential processes like coagulation, biomineralization, tissue remodeling, and cellular signaling1. Nonetheless, mounting evidence suggests that certain functions of Gla proteins, especially in cancer, chronic inflammation, and fibrosis, may occur independently of γ-carboxylation2–5. These findings expand upon the established belief in vitamin K dependence and suggest that other factors, such as expression levels, subcellular localization, and interactions with tissue-specific microenvironments, significantly influence their functions. Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for nearly 20 million deaths annually6. Despite diverse origins, cardiac fibrosis represents a common pathological hallmark in CVD. This process is characterized by excessive extracellular matrix deposition that disrupts myocardial architecture, compromises contractile and electrical function, and correlates with ventricular dysfunction, reduced compliance, and increased arrhythmia risk7,8. Fibrotic remodeling is likewise a key driver in disease progression and adverse clinical outcomes in heart failure and atrial fibrillation9,10. Collectively, these observations place cardiac fibrosis at the core of CVD pathogenesis and highlight it as a key therapeutic target.

In the context of cardiovascular remodeling and fibrosis, accumulating evidence further indicates that the roles of Gla proteins are highly heterogeneous and, in some cases, extend beyond γ-carboxylation. Rather than functioning as a unified mechanistic class, individual Gla proteins exhibit context-specific activities that reflect differences in structural features, receptor interactions, and tissue environment. This functional diversity, while presenting interpretational challenges, also highlights an opportunity to better define protein-specific and context-specific roles in cardiovascular disease and fibrosis.

Over recent decades, the biological roles of Gla proteins have garnered increasing attention in CVDs, particularly in regulating vascular calcification, arterial stiffness, and thrombosis11. Key members of the Gla protein family, including matrix Gla protein (MGP) and Gla-rich protein (GRP), serve as principal inhibitors of cardiovascular calcification12,13. Osteocalcin (OC/BGLAP), another well-characterized Gla protein, is one of the most abundant non-collagenous proteins in bone and has increasingly recognized roles as an endocrine factor14. Other members, such as growth arrest-specific protein 6 (Gas6), promote platelet activation and thrombus stabilization via interactions with the TYRO3-AXL-MERTK (TAM) receptor family15. Despite the established contributions of Gla proteins, their roles in cardiac fibrosis remain largely unexplored. Given that multiple Gla proteins are highly expressed in the heart and interact with the ECM16, investigating their impact on cardiac ECM remodeling holds substantial promise for developing innovative anti-fibrotic therapies to address unmet needs in CVDs.

To provide a structured framework for this complexity, this review first provides a foundational overview of the Gla protein family, emphasizing members with well-defined biological functions. It then examines their established roles in CVD, focusing on their contributions to vascular calcification, thrombosis, and structural remodeling. Finally, it explores emerging insights into Gla proteins in cardiac fibrosis, drawing parallels with fibrosis in other organs to highlight shared and distinct mechanisms and to propose potential therapeutic strategies for cardiac applications.

General aspects of Gla protein biology

Nineteen members of the Gla protein family have been identified so far, and a full list of these proteins is summarized in Table 1, which provides a concise overview of their sources and primary biological functions. Gla proteins exhibit functional diversity and span a broad range of physiological systems. For example, OC/BGLAP, MGP, GRP, and Gas6 have been widely implicated in processes such as vascular calcification inhibition, bone turnover, metabolism, and the cell cycle11,17. Seven Gla family members (protein C, protein S, protein Z, factor II/thrombin, factor VII, factor IX, and factor X) are produced by hepatocytes and regulate blood clot formation, contributing to the coagulation cascade18. This group also includes four integral membrane Gla proteins, which are proline-rich Gla protein (PRGP) 1, PRGP2, transmembrane Gla protein (TMG) 3, and TMG4; however, their functional characterization remains largely underexplored19,20. Additionally, the inclusion of periostin (POSTN) and TGFβ-induced protein (TGFBI) as bona fide Gla proteins remains debated due to conflicting data concerning the presence and function of Gla domains in their structures21,22. In the following sections, we focus on the distribution, cellular sources, biological roles, and PTM of selected, well-documented Gla proteins with particular relevance to cardiovascular biology.

The vitamin K cycle and γ-carboxylation machinery

The post-translational conversion of glutamate (Glu) to γ-carboxyglutamate (Gla) is orchestrated by a tightly coupled enzymatic cycle localized to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane (Fig. 1). γ-Glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX) catalyzes the carboxylation reaction itself, recognizing a propeptide sequence on its substrates and using reduced vitamin K hydroquinone, molecular oxygen, and carbon dioxide to introduce a second carboxyl group onto target Glu residues23. This reaction generates inactive vitamin K 2,3-epoxide as an obligate by-product, which must be reductively recycled to its active hydroquinone form to sustain continued γ-carboxylation. Two paralogous enzymes shoulder this regeneration: vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 (VKORC1), the dominant and warfarin-sensitive isoform predominantly expressed in the liver, and its paralog VKORC1-Like 1 (VKORC1L1), which contributes particularly to extrahepatic tissues and partially compensates during warfarin inhibition24,25.

This reaction generates vitamin K 2,3-epoxide (KO), which is reduced back to vitamin K quinone (K) and then to KH₂ by VKORC1, with VKORC1L1 contributing within extrahepatic tissues. Warfarin primarily inhibits VKORC1, blocking regeneration of KH₂ and thereby suppressing γ-carboxylation of Gla protein substrates. The cardiovascular relevance of this machinery is brought into sharp focus by inherited deficiencies. Biallelic GGCX mutations cause vitamin K-dependent coagulation factor deficiency type 1 (VKCFD1), whereas VKORC1 mutations underlie VKCFD226,27. Beyond bleeding phenotypes, affected individuals exhibit extrahepatic manifestations including pseudoxanthoma elasticum-like vascular calcification, congenital heart defects, and skeletal abnormalities, which are largely attributed to insufficient carboxylation of MGP, GRP, and other non-coagulation substrates28. Common VKORC1 polymorphisms have likewise been linked in meta-analyses to elevated risk of ischemic stroke, coronary heart disease, and aortic dissection, further supporting the cardiovascular relevance of impaired Gla protein activation29,30. Yet, as the following sections will lay out, the carboxylation status of individual Gla proteins affects their biological output in protein-specific and often context-dependent ways: sometimes indispensable, sometimes dispensable, and sometimes uncoupling distinct functions of the same protein. The carboxylation machinery sets the stage but does not dictate every act.

Osteocalcin

Osteocalcin (OC), encoded by the BGLAP gene, is a small (5.8 kDa), non-collagenous extracellular bone matrix protein produced primarily by osteoblasts31. Originally characterized as a bone mineralization protein and marker of bone formation, the minimal bone phenotype in OC-deficient mice led to the discovery of its endocrine functions31. Specifically, OC has been proposed to function as a hormone that regulates energy metabolism and systemic homeostasis32. These effects rely critically on its post-translational carboxylation status, which is mediated by GGCX32. As a result, fully carboxylated OC (cOC) remains sequestered in the bone matrix due to its high affinity for hydroxyapatite, whereas undercarboxylated OC (ucOC) has reduced bone-binding capacity and thus circulates in the bloodstream to exert endocrine effects33. Important species differences further complicate interpretation of OC biology. In mice, osteocalcin is encoded by three paralogous genes: Bglap1, Bglap2, and Bglap3, whereas humans and rats possess a single BGLAP gene34–36.

Recently, researchers have discovered connections linking OC levels and its carboxylation status to CVDs37. Although both positive and inverse relationships between ucOC or cOC levels and cardiovascular risk factors have been reported, the evidence remains inconsistent38. Such heterogeneity in reported correlations may arise from differences in measurement approaches used to quantify OC carboxylation, confounding variables, and different experimental or clinical models employed. Moreover, the genetic differences among species likely adds another dimension of variability, complicating direct comparison between rodent and human findings.

MGP

MGP is a small (~14 KDa) non-structural, secreted extracellular matrix protein primarily expressed in bone, cartilage, heart, kidney, and various soft tissues39. Chondrocytes and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) are the major cellular contributors of MGP, conferring on MGP its widely acknowledged function in tissue calcification inhibition40. In humans, loss-of-function mutations in the MGP gene lead to a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder called Keutel syndrome, characterized by abnormal cartilage calcification41. The pivotal role of MGP in preserving vascular and cartilage integrity is further highlighted by the rapid vascular calcification and premature death in homozygous MGP knockout mice12. Two post-translational modifications—vitamin K–dependent γ-glutamate carboxylation and serine phosphorylation—are imperative for MGP to manifest its role as a local inhibitor of calcification subsequent to its secretion into the extracellular space39,40. MGP exerts anti-calcification functions by directly chelating calcium ions and blocking pro-calcific signaling pathways by acting as a BMP antagonist that inhibits BMP-2, -4, and -742–44. However, whether BMP inhibition is the primary mechanism underlying MGP’s protective effects in vascular calcification remains debated. In Mgp-deficient mice, arterial calcification initiates within elastic laminae and is not preceded by upregulation of chondrogenic or osteogenic markers, and elastin haploinsufficiency markedly attenuates this phenotype, suggesting that alterations of the elastin-rich extracellular matrix may contribute to the initiation of calcification. These observations support a model in which BMP-dependent signaling and matrix-mediated protection act as complementary mechanisms by which MGP inhibits vascular calcification45. Accordingly, the circulating uncarboxylated and dephosphorylated form (dp-ucMGP) has become a clinical biomarker for vascular calcification and cardiovascular risk46. However, the functional role of MGP appears to be specific to both cell type and organ. For instance, in dermal fibroblasts, MGP inhibits ectopic calcification locally47, whereas in hepatic stellate cells, it participates in ECM remodeling during liver fibrosis5. In endothelial cells, MGP has been shown to affect myofibroblast activation and ECM deposition in lung fibrosis4. Notably, recent research indicates that MGP may also coordinate intracellular processes despite its traditional classification as a secreted extracellular protein. For example, nuclear MGP has been shown to act as a transcriptional co-activator in cancer cells3. Interestingly, MGP PTMs have not been demonstrated as a prerequisite for its biological activities in these non-calcific contexts. These findings point to the possibility that MGP may function independently of PTMs in certain pathological settings.

Gas6

Gas6 is a secreted glycoprotein of approximately 75 kDa with an N-terminal Gla domain that binds anionic phospholipids on activated or apoptotic cell membranes in a calcium-dependent manner48. At the C-terminus, Gas6 has a sex hormone–binding globulin-like domain responsible for binding TAM receptor tyrosine kinases (TYRO3, AXL, and MER), with AXL serving as the preferred receptor49. Interestingly, Gas6 mutants that lack the Gla domain retain receptor activation capacity, indicating that TAM signaling can occur independently of γ-carboxylation50.

Gas6 is broadly expressed across tissues and cell types, including the heart, lung, kidney, bone marrow, vascular smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and monocytes48,51. Gas6 participates in a range of biological processes, such as cellular homeostasis, inflammation, apoptosis, ECM remodeling, and oncogenesis, through its interaction with the TAM family of receptors51. In the cardiovascular system, Gas6 has been reported to promote cardiomyocyte proliferation during neonatal heart regeneration52, preserve mitochondrial integrity following myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury53, support survival of protective immune cells to promote cardiac repair, and increase platelet activation to affect thrombosis54. Overall, Gas6 is a diverse and context-dependent mediator in the heart, with its influence shaped by cell type, receptor distribution, and the surrounding microenvironment.

Coagulation factors

Another major branch of the Gla protein family, the coagulation factors, include both procoagulant (prothrombin/Factor II, Factor VII, Factor IX, and Factor X) and anticoagulant proteins (Protein C, Protein S, and Protein Z) that work together to maintain hemostatic balance55. The primary cellular source for synthesizing these proteins is the hepatocyte55; however, endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and osteoblasts have also been documented as extrahepatic production sites56.

γ-carboxylation of Glu residues within the N-terminal Gla domains allows calcium-dependent membrane binding and proper localization of these factors during coagulation23,57. Disruption of this finely tuned process directly impairs hemostatic balance and contributes to cardiovascular pathology. For example, elevated Factor VII levels are associated with an increased risk of coronary artery disease58, while deficiencies in Protein C or Protein S predispose individuals to venous and arterial thromboembolism59. Moreover, loss-of-function variants in Protein Z have also been tied to a higher risk of ischemic stroke60, underscoring the broader cardiovascular significance of these proteins beyond their roles in clot formation.

POSTN

POSTN is a 93 kDa secreted matricellular protein involved in ECM remodeling that is predominantly produced by stromal cells and induced substantially in response to injury61. POSTN structurally consists of a cysteine-rich EMI domain and four fasciclin-like (FAS1) domains that mediate interactions with ECM components and promote myofibroblast differentiation during remodeling62.

Although recent studies have suggested that POSTN is a potential novel Gla protein, its γ-carboxylation status and classification within the Gla protein family remain a topic of debate. A study that first identified POSTN as the most abundant Gla-containing protein secreted by bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells provided initial evidence for its γ-carboxylation, including identification of consensus γ-carboxylase recognition sites and warfarin sensitivity21. However, a later study cast doubt on these findings, reporting no detectable γ-carboxylation of POSTN in fibrotic lung tissue or in recombinant POSTN produced in vitamin K–optimized HEK293 cells by mass spectrometry22. These conflicting findings call for a re-evaluation of POSTN carboxylation, which may require tissue-specific cellular environments, cofactors, or carboxylation machinery that may not be consistently replicated across experimental systems. It is important to note, however, that the warfarin inhibition observed in the first study provides compelling evidence for vitamin K dependence, while the contradictory findings from the subsequent study may be due to technical limitations, given that Gla residues are notoriously difficult to detect by MS due to their inherent instability during ionization63. Taken together, unlike classical Gla proteins, whether γ-carboxylation is required for POSTN function remains unresolved.

POSTN has been implicated in cardiovascular disorders, where its influence on tissue remodeling varies depending on the biological context. After acute myocardial infarction, POSTN appears to promote reparative processes, including fibroblast-mediated structural repair and cardiomyocyte renewal64,65. In contrast, sustained POSTN expression is associated with maladaptive ventricular remodeling and pro-atherogenic vascular changes during chronic disease66,67. Despite this body of work, no studies have examined whether POSTN γ-carboxylation shapes its cardiovascular actions; filling this gap will be necessary to clarify whether targeting vitamin K availability or carboxylation efficiency is a plausible therapeutic strategy.

Osteocalcin in CVDs

The relationship between OC and CVD remains complex across species and disease stages. In controlled studies using male rats of the same strain and diet, ucOC levels demonstrated significant (p < 0.05) inverse correlation with cardiovascular dysfunction and diabetes68. Yet in human cohorts, the pattern blurs and contradictions emerge. For instance, some population-based studies have reported inverse correlations between total serum OC levels and coronary heart disease risk69, while others have observed positive correlations between OC or ucOC levels and CVD risk factors70,71. These observed discrepancies may arise from several sources. Human populations, in particular, show wide variability in factors that influence OC biology, including vitamin K status, diet, ethnicity, and renal function. Among these factors, renal dysfunction is particularly noteworthy. In moderate to severe CKD, total circulating OC concentrations are elevated, accompanied by increased proportions of fragmented forms and consistent with altered renal handling of OC72. This pattern suggests that changes in OC metabolism, rather than alterations in biologically active OC, may contribute to elevated total OC levels, complicating the interpretation of total OC measurements in populations with cardiovascular risk. Meanwhile, CKD independently increases cardiovascular risk through separate mechanisms73. This dual consideration may help explain why OC often appears elevated in individuals with cardiovascular risk factors, yet does not consistently predict cardiovascular outcomes in epidemiological studies73. Another possibility is that the OC–CVD relationship is dynamic rather than linear, evolving with disease progression, which means OC levels might be elevated during early disease stages as a compensatory response to cardiovascular stress, then fall as cardiac function worsens and this adaptive mechanism becomes exhausted. This hypothesis, however, remains to be validated through longitudinal studies that monitor OC levels throughout disease progression.

Despite inconsistencies in epidemiological findings, growing mechanistic evidence suggests that ucOC may help guard against cardiovascular injury. Studies into OC’s cardiovascular effects began with in vitro models, where Jung et al. demonstrated that ucOC protects human aortic endothelial cells from fatty acid-induced apoptosis and endothelial dysfunction by activating the PI3K-Akt-eNOS pathway, thereby enhancing nitric oxide bioavailability74. Because nitric oxide is a key endothelial-derived vasodilator with both anti-atherogenic and anti-inflammatory properties75, these data suggest that ucOC may protect vascular health through endothelial function enhancement. Subsequently, more sophisticated in vivo models further unveiled multiple cardioprotective actions of ucOC. In one study, Gamal et al. used prophylactic warfarin treatment to raise ucOC levels in diabetic rats induced by a high-fat diet and streptozotocin injections68. Elevated ucOC preserved cardiac function, accompanied by increased cardiac GPRC6A expression, reduced oxidative stress and apoptosis, and improved insulin sensitivity via adiponectin signaling68. Using the same model, Sadek et al. showed that ucOC elevation following warfarin treatment also improved microvascular insufficiency by increasing VEGF expression, enhancing capillary density, and promoting cardiac neovascularization, while reducing myocardial fibrosis at the same time76. Taken together, these studies support a unified model of ucOC-mediated cardioprotection in which ucOC coordinates metabolic and endothelial homeostasis, promotes cytoprotection through GPRC6A signaling, and preserves microvascular integrity via VEGF-mediated angiogenesis.

However, these encouraging findings should be interpreted cautiously, as causality cannot be definitively assigned to ucOC in these models. First, warfarin is known as a broad inhibitor of vitamin K-dependent γ-carboxylation of multiple Gla proteins, including MGP. Thus, these studies do not allow attribution of the observed cardiovascular effects specifically to ucOC, as the simultaneous rise in undercarboxylated MGP could impair vascular calcification control and potentially offset any benefit through accelerated arterial calcification77. This trade-off apparently limits the suitability of warfarin as a means to elevate ucOC. A second caveat involves the receptor question. The role of GPRC6A as the receptor for OC remains controversial. Newly generated Gprc6a-deficient mice exhibit variable or absent metabolic phenotypes depending on genetic background and environmental conditions36,78. In addition, a prevalent GPRC6A polymorphism found in humans, which differs from the ancestral rodent allele, may alter receptor functions36,78, further challenging the direct translation of animal findings to human physiology. Meanwhile, the species differences in OC genetics add another layer of uncertainty. Looking forward, future therapeutic strategies will need to take a more selective path, favoring direct ucOC supplementation or tissue-targeted modulation of vitamin K metabolism over systemic warfarin exposure. In the broader view, ucOC is suggested as a plausible physiological protector against cardiovascular pathology, but its value as a clinical biomarker will depend on the development of standardized assays, careful adjustment for renal function and vitamin K status, and validation through longitudinal studies.

MGP in CVDs

MGP exists in multiple forms due to different post-translational modifications: phosphorylated and carboxylated (p-cMGP), dephosphorylated and carboxylated (dp-cMGP), phosphorylated and uncarboxylated (p-ucMGP), and dephosphorylated and uncarboxylated (dp-ucMGP)79. The p-cMGP represents the fully active form with maximal calcification-inhibiting capacity, while dp-ucMGP is the fully inactive form, reflecting poor vitamin K status. The levels of these MGP species in circulation, especially dp-ucMGP, have emerged as biomarkers for assessing cardiovascular risk.

In patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), higher dp-ucMGP levels were found in patients with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) compared to ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)80. Additionally, elevated dp-ucMGP levels were associated with a higher risk of in-hospital mortality among NSTEMI patients. These increased levels may indicate a greater calcification of atherosclerotic plaques in NSTEMI, potentially explaining the increased mortality risk80. The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) found that elevated dp-ucMGP levels predicted incident cardiovascular events and mortality, but notably only in younger adults aged 45–53 years81. Longitudinal MESA data further confirmed that elevated baseline dp-ucMGP predicted accelerated vascular calcification progression across coronary and aortic sites over 10 years82. A straightforward interpretation of circulating inactive MGP as merely reflecting calcification burden is complicated by the intriguing finding of a recent study that patients with higher coronary arterial calcification scores tended to have lower levels of ucMGP compared to those with moderate calcification83. However, this study acknowledged technical limitations in distinguishing phosphorylated from dephosphorylated forms of inactive MGP83. This may explain the conflicting findings since p-ucMGP may act differently than dp-ucMGP at the biological level in vascular tissues. Elevated dp-ucMGP is also linked to systolic and diastolic left ventricular dysfunction in addition to vascular effects84,85. Histology studies showed that diseased myocardium had higher levels of uncarboxylated MGP than healthy hearts85. This suggests that local MGP alteration may contribute to cardiac remodeling and impaired heart function. A zebrafish model deficient in vitamin K showed less carboxylated MGP, leading to both ectopic calcification and cardiac fibrosis, thus supporting this mechanistic link86. These pathological changes were alleviated by vitamin K supplementation, which also restored the levels of cardiac carboxylated MGP86. However, considering the simple heart geometry of zebrafish, this leaves the question of whether MGP dysfunction preferentially affects regions most vulnerable in human heart disease unanswered. Moreover, compared to humans, zebrafish vitamin K metabolism diverges substantially due to distinct lipid absorption pathways and specific xenobiotics metabolism87, potentially overestimating the clinical efficacy of vitamin K supplementation. This concern is supported by clinical evidence, where biochemical improvement has not translated into favorable cardiovascular outcomes88. Randomized trials of vitamin K supplementation consistently reduce circulating dp-ucMGP levels without corresponding reductions in vascular calcification or clear cardiovascular benefit89–92. This discrepancy may expose limitations of circulating dp-ucMGP as a biomarker, which may not faithfully represent tissue-level MGP activity, as well as the inadequacy of systemic vitamin K supplementation to confer cell-specific regulation in complex pathological settings. Together, these observations suggest that more targeted or context-specific therapeutic approaches may be required.

Gas6 in CVDs

Recent clinical studies highlight convoluted and condition-dependent associations between Gas6 and cardiovascular disease outcomes. In chronic coronary heart disease, only male patients demonstrated significantly lower serum Gas6 levels compared to healthy controls, suggesting a possible sex-specific association93. Additionally, Gas6 levels exhibited a positive correlation with testosterone, indicating hormonal regulation in the potential cardioprotective role of Gas693. This protective paradigm was further reinforced by the inverse association between circulating Gas6 levels and carotid plaque burden in patients undergoing coronary angiography, suggesting that Gas6 may limit atherosclerotic plaque development94. The trajectory of Gas6 alters, however, when cardiovascular disease transitions to acute clinical presentations. In a clinical trial with more than 200 STEMI patients and controls, Gas6 levels were lower in STEMI patients, likely implying consumption or redistribution of Gas6 as a protective response during acute myocardial injury and repair processes95. This acute depletion contrasts with findings from a recent large clinical cohort involving 1039 patients with acute heart failure, where elevated plasma Gas6 levels independently predicted both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality over nearly 2.5 years96. Due to the minimal information on patient comorbidities96, it is reasonable to speculate that the persistently elevated Gas6 in heart failure results from ongoing chronic inflammation and sustained activation of the Gas6-Axl axis under prolonged cardiac stress97.

Seemingly opposing clinical observations are now being unraveled by preclinical studies, uncovering the dual roles of Gas6 in cardiovascular disease pathogenesis. In a mouse model of ischemia-reperfusion injury, the activation of Gas6 through either AAV9-mediated overexpression or the small molecule catechin both led to an improvement in cardiac function through inhibition of cardiac apoptosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial injury53. These observed myocardial benefits were mediated through the activation of the Gas6/AXL-SIRT1 signaling axis53, which represents a potentially druggable pathway, particularly because SIRT1 activators such as SRT2104 have already entered clinical trials for metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes (NCT00937326, registration date 07/09/2009; NCT01018017, registration date 11/19/2009; and NCT00937872: registration date 07/09/2009). However, clinical trials targeting this pathway for cardiovascular indications remain glaringly absent, underscoring a substantial translation gap that warrants investigation. The protective effects of Gas6 also extend to other forms of experimental cardiovascular conditions. One example is sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy: in a mouse model of sepsis induced by cecal ligation and puncture, AAV9-mediated Gas6 overexpression was able to inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome in cardiomyocytes, further affording cardioprotection through attenuation of mitochondrial injury and endoplasmic reticulum stress98. Pharmacological activation of Gas6 receptors with puerarin further underscored its therapeutic potential by preserving coronary microvascular integrity via regulation of mitophagy in septic cardiomyopathy99. These protective mechanisms, however, represent only one edge of Gas6’s double-edged role in CVDs. Zhao et al.100 reported elevated myocardial Gas6 expression in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and in mice subjected to pressure overload100. In these mice, genetic knockout of Gas6 led to reduced cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis compared to wild-type (WT) controls, whereas selective overexpression of cardiomyocyte Gas6 worsened cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis and dysfunction through activation of the MEK1/2-ERK1/2 signaling pathway100. Intriguingly, Gas6-induced cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction could be reversed via pharmacological MEK1/2 inhibition with U0126, pointing to its therapeutic relevance100. Collectively, these findings suggest that Gas6 functions in dual roles that provide directions for future therapeutic strategies. Therapeutically Gas6 may operate within a narrow window: brief Gas6 elevation promotes cardiac recovery from injury, while prolonged activation may eventually tip the balance, with beneficial repair mechanisms giving way to pathological remodeling processes. Future studies should aim at temporally controlled modulation of Gas6 activity, such as through a controlled-release delivery system101, which may open new avenues for tailored cardiovascular therapeutics.

POSTN in CVDs

POSTN expression has been repeatedly reported as being elevated in a variety of CVDs, from acute injury to chronic conditions, with levels detectable in patient plasma/serum, myocardial tissue, and atrial appendage biopsies102–106. Serum POSTN levels in STEMI patients demonstrated a temporal pattern, progressively increasing from Day 1 to Month 4 post-infarction, and associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality106. This temporal elevation suggests that POSTN is potentially involved in maladaptive remodeling processes that continue long after the acute injury phase has passed. In patients with end-stage DCM, echoing the ongoing remodeling pattern, the elevated levels of plasma POSTN also showed a positive correlation with the severity of diastolic dysfunction103. Regardless of subtype, proteomic profiling of myocardial tissue from patients with cardiomyopathy identified POSTN as the most significantly upregulated protein, being elevated in both DCM and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)104. The upregulated levels correlated with contractile dysfunction and altered calcium signaling104. Further examination in advanced HCM patients who underwent septal myectomy revealed a strong positive correlation between myocardial POSTN expression and tissue fibrosis (r = 0.82)105. In addition, spatial co-localization of POSTN with fibrotic regions corroborated the direct involvement of POSTN in fibrotic remodeling processes105. Extending beyond ventricular pathology, a study using right atrial appendages from healthy controls, sinus rhythm (SR), paroxysmal AF (paAF) and persistent AF (peAF) reported increased POSTN expression in AF patients compared to healthy and SR cohorts, with even higher expression in patients with peAF than in those with paAF102. This observation further supports the concept that POSTN is a key player in the progressive chronic remodeling process. Moreover, increased TGFβ levels were found to coincide with upregulation of POSTN, and both markers positively correlated with worsening atrial fibrosis severity, implying a TGFβ/POSTN-mediated fibrotic pathway underlying atrial remodeling in AF pathogenesis102.

AF develops through several upstream factors, one of which is sinus node dysfunction (SND)34. In patients, up to 20% with AF also have SND, and this is often accompanied by greater fibrosis in the right atrium107. In a rabbit SND model, SN damage induced remarkable molecular changes in right atrial tissue within 7 days, including TGFβ activation and drastic POSTN upregulation, which resulted in enhanced fibrosis in the right atria even in the absence of overt AF108. This finding indicates that the TGFβ/POSTN pathway represents a primary driver of structural remodeling rather than a secondary consequence of pathological remodeling. Although the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the TGFβ/POSTN axis in atrial fibrosis and its further influence on AF remain incompletely understood, insights can be gleaned from mechanistic studies in other CVDs, given that cardiac fibrosis represents a featured hallmark in nearly all forms of CVD. Among the key cardiac fibrosis drivers is lysyl oxidase (LOX)-mediated collagen crosslinking109. In a rat MI model, POSTN and LOX co-localized in collagen-rich scar tissue at the infarct zone and their upregulated levels correlated with reduced cardiac function110. Mechanistically, in TGFβ-treated cardiac fibroblasts, POSTN activated ERK1/2 MAPK signaling to transcriptionally upregulate LOX expression through serum response factor110. Nevertheless, direct evidence measuring collagen crosslinking activity and matrix stiffness was missing from the study, leaving the question of whether increased LOX expression actually causes pathological structural remodeling unresolved. Additional studies in preclinical cardiomyopathy models also showed POSTN as a nodal mediator of cardiac fibrosis, acting downstream of TGFβ signaling111,112. When POSTN was removed genetically or blocked pharmacologically, mice were protected from cardiac dysfunction and fibrotic remodeling111,112. Taken together, these findings cement the concept that POSTN is a convergent and targetable driver of cardiac fibrosis across CVDs. Of note, we must recognize that existing studies have focused on POSTN expression while overlooking the potential impact of the carboxylation status of POSTN on its pathological consequences. Whether vitamin K deficiency or impaired γ-carboxylation contributes to pathological activities of POSTN in the heart is an area demanding deeper exploration.

Other Gla proteins in CVDs

Osteocalcin, MGP, Gas6, and POSTN have commanded the stage in discussions of CVDs. Recent data suggest that several other Gla proteins may contribute to cardiovascular pathogenesis, providing compelling insights into novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. For instance, GRP, also known as unique cartilage matrix-associated protein (UCMA), represents an emerging calcification inhibitor that works through carboxylation-dependent mechanisms13,113. Histological analysis of calcified aortic valve disease tissues (CAVD) from patients who underwent aortic valve replacement revealed differential localization between uncarboxylated GRP (ucGRP) and carboxylated GRP (cGRP)13. ucGRP mainly localized intracellularly within valvular interstitial cells, suggesting impaired vitamin K status or deficient carboxylation; while cGRP mainly accumulated in the extracellular matrix near mineral deposits, indicating active attempts at inhibiting mineral deposition13. Accordingly, functional studies validated that only cGRP was able to significantly reduce calcium accumulation in an ex vivo aortic ring model13. Using a GRP total knockout mouse model, the mechanistic pathways through which GRP inhibits calcification have become clear113. GRP was found to directly bind BMP-2 and suppress SMAD1/5/8 signaling, which prevents vascular calcification113. The functional efficacy of GRP’s calcification inhibitory effects depends largely on its carboxylation status, as only cGRP demonstrates high-affinity BMP-2 binding, hence reinforcing the potential therapeutic value of vitamin K supplementation113. Beyond its role in calcification, another aspect of GRP that should capture the attention of cardiovascular researchers is its connection with inflammatory responses. Specifically, in lipopolysaccharide- or hydroxyapatite-stimulated macrophages, GRP treatment downregulated key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, IL-1β, and NFκB114. Strikingly, these anti-inflammatory effects of GRP occurred independently of its carboxylation status114. Because macrophages serve as central immune cells integral to the progression of various CVDs, including heart failure and atrial fibrillation, through regulation of cell-cell communication, inflammation, and fibrotic remodeling115,116, future studies exploring the anti-inflammatory properties of GRP in cardiovascular contexts could yield new therapeutic ideas. The fact that its anti-inflammatory and anti-calcific functions rely on distinct carboxylation requirements is particularly worthy of attention because it implies separable mechanisms of action and opens possibilities for selectively targeting specific protective pathways.

As discussed earlier, coagulation factors form another key branch in the Gla family. They have long been recognized for driving coagulation cascades and maintaining hemostasis. Increasingly, however, they are emerging as multifaceted players in different cardiovascular disorders, particularly in the case of the anticoagulant Protein S. Clinical evidence indicates that a physiologic decline in Protein S during pregnancy contributes to hypercoagulability, predisposing women with prosthetic heart valves to thrombosis and adverse maternal complications117. The picture grows more complex when considering different patient populations. In a large prospective study involving thousands of healthy middle-aged men (aged 50–61 years), higher baseline free Protein S levels were highly correlated with an increased risk of coronary heart disease events during follow-up118. This finding may reflect a maladaptive response to vascular inflammation, suggesting that elevated Protein S could possibly serve as a marker of systemic hemostatic imbalance. By contrast, a retrospective study of hereditary Protein S deficiency cohorts revealed that genetic deficiency increases the risk of arterial thromboembolism, particularly in individuals under 55 years of age59. Viewed jointly, these studies clearly suggest that Protein S is not a simple “more is better, less is worse” factor. Future work should shift from measuring its concentration to assessing its functional activity. In addition, at present, another unanswered question is whether elevated Protein S contributes directly to cardiovascular events or simply reflects underlying vascular pathology. Until mechanistic studies clarify its direct effects, therapeutic modulation remains a premature prospect.

Having examined the roles of Gla proteins in cardiovascular disease, we next focus on fibrosis, a critical shared pathological endpoint across many cardiovascular conditions. Examining Gla proteins through this lens reveals shared mechanisms across organs that are not apparent when fibrosis is considered in isolation, with implications for cardiac therapeutic development.

Major Gla proteins in cardiac fibrosis

Fibrosis can affect any organ, therefore contributing substantially to the global disease burden. Fibrosis develops when tissue repair mechanisms become aberrantly sustained following initial injury, thereby transforming beneficial healing responses into destructive cycles of ECM accumulation (Fig. 2). Although fibrotic processes exhibit fundamental similarities across anatomical sites, they exhibit organ-specific variations due to their unique cellular compositions, mechanical environments, and regulatory networks that are present (Fig. 2). Among fibrotic conditions, cardiac fibrosis poses particularly complex therapeutic challenges due to the heart’s distinct contractile demands and limited regenerative capacity119. In this section, we will discuss how Gla proteins integrate themselves into fibrotic remodeling and discuss what their implications for therapeutic opportunities, beginning with cardiac fibrosis. Because our current understanding of Gla proteins in cardiac fibrosis remains fragmentary, we aim to build a more integrated framework for mechanistic discovery. To do so, we draw on characterized pathways involving Gla proteins in fibrosis in other organs. By integrating these lessons into the cardiovascular context, we aim to derive insights that can be translated into the cardiac setting for cardioprotective strategies grounded in the biology of Gla proteins. Roles of major Gla proteins in organ fibrosis

ATII alveolar type II, CKD chronic kidney disease, EMT epithelial-mesenchymal transition, HCM hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, HSC hepatic stellate cells, IPF idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, LVEF left ventricular ejection fraction, MI myocardial infarction, NASH nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, STZ streptozotocin, UUO unilateral ureteral obstruction.

Cardiac fibrosis manifests through two main patterns: replacement (reparative) and reactive fibrosis. Replacement fibrosis develops when dead cardiomyocytes are replaced by collagen-rich scar tissue120. This type of fibrosis is necessary to prevent cardiac rupture after a myocardial infarction (MI). However, its persistence reduces the heart’s contractility, leading to systolic dysfunction, and creates electrically inactive regions120. In contrast, reactive fibrosis develops more subtly in the absence of cardiomyocyte loss, as collagen accumulates between individual cardiomyocytes or cardiac muscle bundles in response to prolonged fibrotic stimuli such as hypertension or stenosis121. This process perturbs electrical impulse propagation, potentially giving rise to re-entrant circuits, and is now recognized as the major type of fibrosis contributing to the development of arrhythmias such as AF10.

These two patterns are increasingly recognized as spatially and functionally heterogeneous rather than uniform processes122. After an MI, dense collagen deposition within the infarct core is vital for maintaining structural integrity, whereas fibrosis in the peri-infarct or border zone is often more heterogeneous in composition and organization and can contribute to maladaptive remodeling, impaired electrical conduction, and arrhythmogenic substrate formation122. Persistent interstitial fibrosis may further expand into remote non-infarcted myocardium during chronic remodeling, effectively blurring the boundary between reparative and reactive processes123. Additionally, distinct fibroblast populations and ECM signaling pathways have been implicated in the regional specialization of these fibrotic responses123. However, the crosstalk between replacement and reactive fibrosis, and its impact on the progression of CVDs, remains poorly delineated. Understanding how molecular mediators, including Gla proteins, differentially participate in these distinct yet interconnected fibrotic processes represents a critical gap in cardiac research.

Gas6 represents a good example of this complexity. In aging mice (18-month-old), decreased serum testosterone and Gas6 levels coincided with aggravated cardiac fibrosis124. Exogenous testosterone administration increased serum Gas6 and mitigated fibrosis in the heart of WT aging mice, but failed to provide cardioprotection in aging mice deficient in AXL124. These findings strongly suggest a partial protective effect of Gas6 against cardiac fibrosis through the Gas6-AXL axis. Notably, testosterone treatment also reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis in WT aging mice, yet the study did not clarify whether Gas6 acts predominantly on replacement fibrosis or on reactive fibrosis124. This blind spot is consequential because understanding the differential effects of Gas6 on fibrosis subtypes could reveal whether its benefit stems from preventing initial cardiomyocyte loss, limiting subsequent scar formation, or a combination of both, which has very different therapeutic implications for targeting cardiac remodeling. Returning to Gas6’s double-edged nature mentioned earlier, other studies point to its pro-fibrotic actions. A recent single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) study of engineered heart tissues modeling cardiomyopathy identified cardiomyocyte-secreted Gas6 was closely associated with myocardial inflammation and fibrotic gene signatures125. Another in vitro study demonstrated pro-proliferative effects of recombinant Gas6 on primary cardiac fibroblasts from genetic Gas6-deficient mice via Axl signaling, which required γ-carboxylation126. However, we must acknowledge the limitations of these studies that temper their implications. The snRNA-seq study provides correlative evidence without experimental validation of Gas6 in fibrosis125. Likewise, the in vitro findings, while mechanistically informative, were obtained under artificial conditions where cardiac fibroblasts lack cell-cell interactions and the complex cytokine milieu present in the intact heart126. There is clearly a dire need for rigorous in vivo studies to unravel the context-dependent effects of Gas6 in cardiac fibrogenesis and to determine whether and how Gas6 ultimately tips the balance between repair and maladaptation.

Insights from extracardiac fibrosis further illustrate Gas6’s duality and offer translational clues for the cardiovascular system. For example, in pulmonary fibrosis, endothelial cell-secreted Gas6 promotes fibrotic signaling via Axl on fibroblasts in response to environmental pollutants or bleomycin injury. However, pre-treatment with recombinant Gas6 can mitigate fibrosis by priming protective mechanisms, highlighting temporal dependency that mirrors observations in the heart52,127,128. In liver fibrosis, Gas6 correlates with disease severity and drives hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation via AXL-AKT signaling, but it also protects hepatocytes from lipotoxicity through MerTK, suggesting receptor-specific effects that could explain its protective role in cardiac aging models129–133. Similarly, in renal fibrosis, Gas6 exhibits a biphasic pattern, protecting against acute injury via anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways while amplifying TGFβ-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in chronic phases; this stage-dependent role implies that modulating Gas6 timing or receptor engagement (e.g., selective Axl inhibition) might optimize cardioprotective strategies without worsening maladaptation134,135. Collectively, these cross-organ comparisons underscore that Gas6’s impact in the heart likely depends on temporal windows and receptor selectivity, informing therapies that could switch its actions toward repair.

In addition to Gas6, other Gla proteins, including POSTN and MGP, have shown growing but still incompletely characterized roles in cardiac fibrosis. POSTN shows a consistent pattern in cardiac fibrosis. POSTN was barely detectable in healthy human hearts but increased drastically in failing myocardium, with expression levels positively correlating with fibrotic burden in both end-stage heart failure and HCM patients105,136. Genetic studies in mice have shown that POSTN deletion increases the risk of early ventricular rupture after MI, while surviving mice develop less fibrosis and demonstrate better ventricular function137. On the other hand, cardiac-specific inducible overexpression of POSTN prevents rupture but induces age-related hypertrophy137. A follow-up study mapped the temporal dynamics of POSTN upregulation after infarction in mice, showing that its expression rises between days 2 and 7 and peaks at day 7 across mouse strains138. Spatial transcriptomic studies of the same temporal window have further shown that POSTN expression is spatially enriched rather than uniformly distributed across the injured heart139. POSTN-high fibroblasts preferentially colocalize with a thin layer of morphologically distorted border-zone cardiomyocytes at the infarct–border zone interface, where matricellular signaling is concentrated, a pattern that also appears conserved in human ischemic myocardium139. Taken together, these findings suggest that POSTN serves as both a scaffold for early scar integrity and a driver of chronic fibrotic expansion, pointing to therapeutic strategies that hinge not only on how but also on when and where POSTN is targeted. Extracardiac studies reinforce POSTN’s predominant pro-fibrotic profile. In liver fibrosis, POSTN activates HSCs via αv integrins and enhanced ECM crosslinking through LOX/LOXL upregulation, consistent with its chronic pathogenic effects in the heart140,141. In renal fibrosis, POSTN correlates with disease progression, drives EMT and ECM deposition via FAK, p38-MAPK, and ERK signaling pathways, and remains detrimental even in acute injury models. This is in contrast with POSTN’s acute protective role post-MI, and emphasizes common matricellular pathways (e.g., integrin and MAPK signaling) that could enable unified anti-fibrotic targeting across organs, including in cardiovascular disease142–146.

To date, the direct participation of MGP in cardiac fibrosis has received limited attention. The most pertinent evidence comes from a zebrafish genetic model of pseudoxanthoma elasticum, where abcc6a deficiency disrupts vitamin K–dependent mineralization and drives fibrotic gene upregulation, resulting in cardiac fibrosis86. These mutants exhibited reduced vitamin K levels and cMGP, while vitamin K supplementation restored cMGP levels and mitigated cardiac fibrosis86. Although suggestive of possible anti-fibrotic effects of MGP in the heart, this single study in a non-mammalian disease model provides only indirect, mechanistically unclear evidence. The paucity of cardiac-specific data makes it necessary to draw insights from the role of MGP in other organ fibrosis to anticipate its cardioprotective mechanisms and open new directions for cardiovascular applications. For example, in pulmonary fibrosis, MGP exerts anti-fibrotic effects by binding and inhibiting BMP1, preventing TGFβ maturation and myofibroblast differentiation from EC-like precursors; this pathway intersects with cardiac biology, as atrial cardiomyocyte-secreted calcitonin upregulates MGP147, raising the possibility that MGP contributes to calcitonin-mediated BMP1 inhibition, thereby dampening fibrotic remodeling and reducing AF susceptibility. This, in turn, suggests that MGP could mediate similar anti-fibrotic effects in the heart via BMP1-TGFβ inhibition4,147. However, in liver fibrosis, MGP promotes fibrogenesis by enhancing TGFβ signaling in HSCs, correlating with the severity in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, though methodological difference (e.g., systemic vs. cell-specific knockouts) may explain this organ-specific divergence5. Similarly, in kidney fibrosis, MGP promotes fibrotic remodeling in pericytes and myofibroblasts, partly via Notch signaling, with Mgp deletion attenuating renal fibrosis, suggesting that cell-type-specific expression and injury microenvironment may further influence its functional role148. These contrasts highlight the need for cardiac-focused studies to clarify MGP’s role, particularly given warfarin’s acceleration of extracardiac fibrosis via impaired γ-carboxylation149,150, which could inform vitamin K-based cardioprotective approaches.

Therapeutic implications

Based on the discussions from the preceding sections, it is clear that the major Gla proteins exhibit highly context-dependent functions that vary according to disease stage, cellular environment, and tissue type. This complexity has impeded the translation of mechanistic findings into effective therapies and restricted clinical validation of Gla protein-targeted interventions. Despite this complexity, various therapeutic approaches targeting Gla proteins are being investigated. Vitamin K supplementation represents the most clinically advanced approach, building on the fundamental requirement for γ-carboxylation in Gla protein function. Early European clinical studies in chronic hemodialysis patients showed that menaquinone-7 (vitamin K2) supplementation dose-dependently reduced circulating dp-ucMGP levels by up to ~60% after 6–8 weeks of treatment151,152. These findings were confirmed by a more recent prospective study in Lebanese hemodialysis patients (NCT02876354). Daily supplementation of 360 µg menaquinone-7 for 4 weeks led to an average 86% reduction in dp-ucMGP; importantly, in this cohort, baseline dp-ucMGP levels also correlated significantly with vascular calcification scores153. While these studies demonstrate that vitamin K supplementation reliably improves dp-ucMGP as a biochemical marker, evidence from randomized trials assessing cardiovascular or clinical outcomes has not consistently shown cardiovascular benefit, highlighting the limitations of this biomarker and the need for more targeted therapeutic strategies153. In addition, these studies primarily address biochemical responses and cross-sectional correlations with dp-ucMGP, rather than longitudinal effects on calcification itself. Most recently, another clinical trial (NCT05060809) investigating intravenous vitamin K1 supplementation in hemodialysis patients has been completed, with results pending publication. This trial was designed to assess both dp-ucMGP levels and structural outcomes, including aortic calcification scores and valve lesion severity. However, established vascular calcification involves hydroxyapatite deposition within the vessel wall and is not readily reversed by enhanced MGP carboxylation alone, because regression of established calcification likely requires active mineral resorption and tissue remodeling mechanisms beyond MGP’s inhibitory function154,155. Vitamin K supplementation may therefore be more effective in limiting progression than reversing calcified lesions. Beyond this limitation, vitamin K supplementation also lacks pathway specificity, as it broadly affects the γ-carboxylation of multiple Gla proteins, thereby limiting its utility in conditions where different Gla proteins exert opposing or context-dependent effects on cardiac remodeling.

A more direct targeting approach to modulate Gla proteins may offer greater precision in the cardiac setting. This is particularly relevant for those Gla proteins, whose pathophysiological functions may be carboxylation-independent, thus rendering vitamin K repletion ineffective and necessitating direct intervention. The Gas6/AXL pathway is a leading candidate for this direct intervention. As an AXL inhibitor, Bemcentinib (R428/BGB324) is currently in clinical trials, although primarily for cancer applications (NCT02488408, NCT03184571, NCT06516887). However, a plethora of preclinical evidence suggests its potential use in cardiovascular contexts, particularly given the context-dependent roles of Gas6 in cardiac pathology: protective during acute cardiac injury but contributing to pathological remodeling with chronic activation. To bring this pathway into the cardiovascular arena, future clinical efforts must establish appropriate dosing, define safety profiles, and determine efficacy endpoints that are specific to cardiac remodeling. Similarly, careful calibration of therapeutic timing and patient selection will require close attention to capture protective effects while avoiding disruption of beneficial signaling.

Neutralizing antibodies targeting POSTN represents another potential strategy for direct targeting. In a rat MI model, a POSTN neutralizing antibody targeting exon 17 selectively blocked the pathogenic isoform of POSTN, leading to reduced cardiac fibrosis and prevention of adverse ventricular remodeling156. In parallel, directly targeting POSTN could also be achieved through microRNA regulatory pathways. In a mouse MI model, cardiac-specific miR-150 deletion aggravated fibrosis and dysfunction, while POSTN ablation rescued these effects67. In adult human cardiac fibroblasts, miR-150 was found to repress POSTN, with carvedilol enhancing this axis by upregulating miR-150 and downregulating POSTN67. The miR-150/POSTN pathway thus emerges as a promising therapeutic target in post-MI remodeling. Nevertheless, when considering these therapeutic strategies clinically, a key challenge will be achieving cardiac-specific delivery157. As an example, antibody approaches may require targeting moieties such as targeted nanoparticle systems or cardiac-homing peptides158,159, while miRNA therapies will rely on platforms like lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors, or chemically modified oligonucleotides designed with cardiac specificity in mind160.

More broadly, the therapeutic considerations discussed above, including timing-aware intervention, controlled-release modulation, and cardiac-specific delivery, converge on a common translational principle. For Gla proteins whose roles shift between acute protection and chronic maladaptation, clinical success will likely depend on integrating biomarker-guided patient selection with stage-restricted dosing and temporally controlled delivery, allowing intervention to be matched to the relevant phase of disease.

Conclusions

The discussions in this review lead to some fundamental conclusions and realistic expectations. The first conclusion is that Gla proteins do not function as a unified mechanistic class in cardiovascular disease and fibrosis. While γ-carboxylation remains central to the activity of certain members, such as MGP, other Gla proteins exhibit context-dependent functions that may be partially or substantially independent of this modification, varying instead with disease stage, cellular environment, and tissue type. This heterogeneity also helps explain a current clinical gap: despite the growing mechanistic understanding of Gla proteins, no Gla protein-targeted therapy has reached clinical practice for cardiovascular interventions. Current clinical investigations have remained focused on vitamin K supplementation, which broadly enhances γ-carboxylation of multiple vitamin K–dependent proteins rather than enabling more selective targeting strategies. Given the mechanistic complexity and context-dependent functions of individual Gla proteins, future clinical efforts may benefit from moving beyond systemic vitamin K supplementation toward direct protein-specific or receptor-targeted approaches. Another conclusion is that the context-dependent nature of Gla proteins, such as Gas6 and POSTN presents both opportunities and challenges for therapeutic development. Clinical success will depend on biomarker-guided patient selection, careful control of therapeutic timing, and strategies for delivery to achieve cardiac-specific targeting. The final reality is that translation will require the cardiovascular research community to embrace interdisciplinary collaboration. Partnerships with clinical researchers, bioengineers, and pharmaceutical developers are not an imposition but a necessity if mechanistic discoveries are to be converted into meaningful therapies for patients.
