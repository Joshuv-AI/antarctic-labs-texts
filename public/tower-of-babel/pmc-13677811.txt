Estimated alcohol‐attributable health burden in New Zealand: A comparative risk assessment
Authors: Tim Chambers, Anja Mizdrak, Anna Davies, Amanda Jones, Adam Sherk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo estimate the alcohol‐attributable morbidity and mortality in New Zealand (NZ) as measured by deaths, cancer registrations, hospitalisations and Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) injury claims.DesignComparative risk assessment methodology.Setting and participantsThe 2018 NZ population aged > 14 years, by ethnicity (Māori/non‐Māori), age and sex.MeasurementsAlcohol‐attributable fractions (AAF) for 26 alcohol‐related conditions were generated in the International Model of Alcohol Harms and Policies (v3.0) using 2016 Global Burden of Disease Study relative risk functions and 2018 NZ alcohol exposure statistics. AAFs were applied to enumerated outcomes, with age–sex standardised rates computed using the 2018 estimated resident population. Primary results present net outcomes (allowing potential protective effects at low consumption where supported by risk curves); gross outcomes are presented in sensitivity analyses. Māori (Indigenous population in NZ) experienced a disproportionately high health burden: 4.7% of all deaths, 5.2% of all cancers, 3.4% of hospitalisations and 9.9% of ACC claims among Māori were attributable to alcohol, compared with 2.5%, 4.7%, 2.7% and 8.7% for non‐Māori, respectively. Age–sex standardised rates of alcohol‐attributable (AA) mortality, cancer registrations and hospitalisations were ~20–100% higher for Māori than non‐Māori. Males accounted for the majority of health harms (e.g. 83% of AA deaths and 64% of AA hospitalisations). Māori bore a markedly higher age–sex standardised burden than non‐Māori.Keywords: alcohol, alcohol attributable fractions, burden of disease, comparative risk assessment, New Zealand, policy

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol is a component cause for numerous disease and injury conditions [1]. Alcohol use is associated with cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including ischaemic heart disease, hypertensive heart disease, atrial fibrillation and flutter, cardiomyopathies and stroke [1]. Alcohol is also classified as a group 1 carcinogen [2] and is causally associated with numerous types of cancer including: pharyngeal, laryngeal, oesophageal, colorectal, liver and breast cancers [1]. Alcohol contributes to intentional and unintentional injuries, poor mental health [3] and fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) [4].

Numerous studies have used comparative risk methodologies to estimate the alcohol‐attributable health burden in the United States [5], Canada [6], Australia [7], Switzerland [8], France [9], Finland [9], Germany [10, 11], the United Kingdom [10, 11], Denmark [10, 11], Italy [10, 11], Finland [12], Sweden [13], Ireland [14] and globally [15]. Estimates of the health harms attributable to alcohol are important for understanding the extent of the alcohol‐caused burden of disease, for identifying and addressing health inequities and for policy development and prioritisation.

In New Zealand (NZ), there have been two previous locally derived estimates of the burden of mortality and morbidity [16, 17]. These reports estimated the burden to be 3.9% of all deaths in 2000 and 5.4% of deaths in those under 80 years of age in 2007. Overall, the alcohol‐attributable mortality rate was two and a half times higher for Māori than for non‐Māori in the 2007 assessment [17]. In addition, New Zealand is included the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, in which, the 2023 analysis estimated alcohol was attributable for 1.9% of all deaths. However, GBD estimates do not produce ethnicity‐specific results nor capture the full scope of alcohol‐related outcomes available through data stored in New Zealand's national collections (e.g. hospitalisations).

This study builds on our analysis commissioned by Health New Zealand‐Te Whatu Ora to provide new, locally derived, estimates of the alcohol‐attributable health burden for New Zealand in 2018, updating previous NZ estimates from 2000 and 2007 [16, 17]. The current study used updated: alcohol consumption figures (2018); relative risk (RR) estimates for 26 alcohol‐attributable disease and injury conditions (produced in 2016); and additional outcomes of mortality and morbidity including cancer registrations, hospitalisations, Accident Compensation Corporation (ACC) injury claims and deaths.

METHOD

Study design

This study used a comparative risk assessment methodology to estimate the health loss because of alcohol consumption in New Zealand in 2018 for Māori and non‐Māori, by sex and by seven age groups (15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74 and 75+).

We used the International Model of Alcohol Harms and Policies (InterMAHP, version 3.0), an open access alcohol harms and policy scenario model developed by researchers at the Canadian Institute for Substance Use Research at the University of Victoria Canada, along with several other experts [18, 19]. InterMAHP has been used to estimate the alcohol‐caused burden of disease in projects in Canada [6], the United States [5], Finland [12], Sweden [13] and Australia [7]. The model combines population‐level alcohol consumption data with published RR relationships between alcohol use and specific health conditions to estimate alcohol‐attributable fractions (AAFs). An AAF is interpreted as the proportion of each health condition that can be attributed to alcohol use. InterMAHP automates the estimation of AAFs by integrating, by population subgroup, a γ distribution‐based [20] continuous prevalence distribution of daily alcohol use against RR functions identified for each health condition, as described in more detail in the publication set formalising the InterMAHP methodology [19, 21, 22].

Our analysis included 26 health conditions causally related to individuals' alcohol use, including four injuries categories, eight cancer categories, six CVD types, two communicable diseases and six other conditions (see Appendix S1–S4 for full documentation of included conditions and their mapping to outcome data). The InterMAHP‐generated AAFs' estimates with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) are presented in the Data S1.

We centred our analysis on 2018 data, because this was the most recent year for which complete, routinely collected administrative data were available across all datasets, largely aligned with the 2018 Census. At the time of this study, the 2016 GBD RR library was implemented in InterMAHP.

Alcohol use statistics

Our analysis combined multiple data sources to calculate per capita alcohol consumption for the NZ population, for each ethnicity, age group and sex. We calculated per capita consumption by combining information from Statistics New Zealand's alcohol available for consumption [23] and NZ‐specific estimates of unrecorded and tourist alcohol consumption from the World Health Organisation (WHO) Global Information System on Alcohol and Health (GISAH) [24]. We, then, apportioned this total per capita estimate into demographic groups using self‐reported alcohol use data collected in the New Zealand Health Survey 2018/2019 (this represents a single year of data from July 2018 to June 2019) [25].

Per capita alcohol consumption inputs used in InterMAHP were disaggregated by sex, age group and ethnicity (Māori/non‐Māori). For a comprehensive overview of the calculation and values for alcohol consumption used in this study, see the Table S1. The prevalence of past‐year alcohol consumption, former consumption and lifetime abstention, as well as relative alcohol consumption quantities, were calculated for each ethnicity, age group and sex from the New Zealand Health Survey 2018/2019.

Health loss data

We applied estimated AAFs to four measures of health loss: deaths, cancer registrations, publicly funded hospitalisations and ACC injury claims (Table 1). For each measure of health loss, we obtained condition‐level data by age, sex and ethnicity where available (see Appendix S1–S4 for further detail on how health loss measures were extracted).

Statistical analysis

Using InterMAHP, a single AAF estimate was calculated for each ethnicity, sex and age group, health condition and outcome severity (morbidity or mortality). For each population group based on ethnicity, age group and sex, estimates of condition‐specific health loss were calculated by multiplying the AAF by the health loss count (e.g. number of deaths). The analysis was conducted in R (version 4.3.1) and the corresponding code is available on request.

Differences in population composition by age and sex (i.e. the distribution of individuals across age groups and between males and females) between Māori and non‐Māori were accounted for using age‐sex standardisation (‘standardised rate’ from here on). The standardised rates were calculated based on the 2018 NZ estimated resident population (using the estimated resident Māori population as the reference group) for consistency with the population of interest in this work [26]. The analysis plan was not pre‐registered on a publicly available platform, so the results should be considered exploratory.

Sensitivity analyses

Unless specified, the results presented throughout this report are the ‘net’ alcohol‐attributable outcomes, that is, where the outcome encompasses both positive and negative effects of alcohol consumption. We use the term ‘gross’ to specify results where we have assumed there are no potential protective effects of alcohol consumption. Specifically, gross outcomes are the result of removing identifiable potential protective effects from net outcomes (by setting the protective RRs to 1). The decision to present gross outcomes was to ensure compatibility with previous NZ assessments, which prioritised gross outcomes.

The GBD RR relationships used by InterMAHP suggest a protective effect between low levels of alcohol consumption and ischaemic heart disease (IHD), diabetes, hypertension and ischaemic stroke [27]. Particularly for IHD, the protective effect observed within the RR curve is contested with increasing evidence showing that there is no benefit at low levels of alcohol consumption [28] or that any possible risk reduction is mitigated by binge drinking [18]. Therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis whereby any AAFs that suggested a potential protective effect (e.g. AAF <0) were set to zero to facilitate comparability between studies using a gross estimate and to acknowledge the contested nature of these potential protective effects.

RESULTS

Overall alcohol‐attributable health loss

In 2018, an estimated 901 deaths (95% UI = 681–1104, 2.7% of deaths from all causes); 1250 cancer registrations (95% UI = 1084−1383, 4.8% of all cancer registrations), 29 282 hospitalisations (95% UI = 25 713–32 318, 2.8% of all hospitalisations) and 128 963 ACC injury claims (95% UI = 114 324−140 681, 8.9% of all claims) were attributable to alcohol (Table 2). When removing the protective effects of alcohol consumption, the alcohol‐attributable health burden increased substantially for deaths (up to 1379, a 53% increase from the net estimate), while there was a slight increase in hospitalisations (up to 31 484, an 8% increase on the net estimate) and no change to cancer registrations or ACC claims.

Alcohol‐attributable health loss by disease and injury categories

Injuries were a major contributor to overall health loss attributable to alcohol making up 33% of all deaths, 44% of all hospitalisations and 100% of ACC claims (Table 2). Cancers contributed the largest proportion of mortality with an estimated 376 deaths (95% UI = 323–420) or 42% of all alcohol‐attributable deaths, but smaller percentages of hospitalisations (5%) than injuries. The ‘other’ category (alcohol use disorders, alcoholic gastritis, epilepsy, liver cirrhosis and pancreatitis) also contributed a substantial health burden contributing 24% of deaths and 40% of hospitalisations attributable to alcohol. Despite including a substantial proportion of overall health gains associated with low levels of alcohol consumption, alcohol had a net negative effect on cardiovascular conditions contributing to 21 deaths and 1707 hospitalisations.

Alcohol‐attributable health loss by age‐sex‐ethnicity stratification

Males contributed 84% of deaths, 64% of hospitalisations and 63% of all ACC claims attributable to alcohol. Females were slightly over‐represented in alcohol‐attributable cancers with 660 cancer registrations (95% UI = 563–744), which represents 53% of all alcohol‐attributable cancer registrations. The greatest alcohol‐attributable health burden was experienced in those 35 to 64 years old with 422 deaths (95% UI = 365–467), 642 cancers (95% UI = 566–700), 13 335 hospitalisations (95% UI = 11 946–14 432) and 64 260 ACC injuries (95% UI = 57 387–69 593).

Māori had a standardised rate of alcohol‐attributable mortality that was twice that of non‐Māori (0.31 compared to 0.15 deaths per 1000 people in 2018) (Table 3). Māori also had an age‐standardised rate of alcohol‐attributable cancers 21% higher than non‐Māori (0.26 compared to 0.21 cancer registrations per 1000 people) and 50% higher for alcohol‐attributable hospitalisations (9.29 compared to 6.21 hospitalisations per 1000 people).

Our analysis shows that a large amount of the alcohol‐attributable mortality for Māori is because of injuries and CVD, compared to cancers for non‐Māori, across both sexes. Age‐sex‐ethnicity stratification of alcohol‐attributable health burden by health outcome and disease and injury condition.

Our analysis shows that the majority of the cancer burden for females is because of breast cancer, whereas the colorectal and liver cancers contribute the greatest number of cancer registrations for men (Figure 1). A greater burden of alcohol‐attributable cancers was observed earlier for women (from age 35 onward) compared to men (from age 45 onward). The sex‐specific distribution of cancer registrations by age likely means women experience a greater health burden from alcohol‐attributable cancers as a result of additional years of life lost and/or years lived in disability. For women, the inclusion of breast cancer, one of the most common alcohol‐attributable cancers and occurring almost exclusively in females, results in a greater overall proportion of alcohol‐attributable cancers despite lower overall alcohol consumption.

DISCUSSION

The estimated burden of alcohol‐attributable disease and injury in this study was large and consistent with previous analyses. Two previous NZ‐based analyses of alcohol‐attributable burden of disease have been conducted in 2000 [16] and 2007 [17]. Each of these analyses estimated relatively similar alcohol‐attributable mortality of 1040 and 802 deaths representing 4.0% (of all ages) and 5.4% (of those under age 80) of all deaths. However, both estimates reflect gross deaths (assuming no potential protective effects), which are comparable to our gross estimate of 1379 deaths representing 4.2% of all deaths in 2018. The reported net deaths as a proportion of all deaths were 0.2% in 2000, 3.0% in 2007 (of all deaths of those under age 80) and 2.7% in this current study.

For Māori, net alcohol‐attributable disease and injuries accounted for 4.7% of all deaths, 5.2% of all cancers, 3.4% of all hospitalisations and 9.9% of all ACC claims, compared to 2.5%, 4.7%, 2.7% and 8.7% for non‐Māori, respectively. Standardised rates of alcohol‐attributable mortality, cancer registrations and hospitalisations were between 20% and 100% higher for Māori than for non‐Māori. These inequities in alcohol‐attributable disease and injury are consistent with previous AAF estimates in New Zealand [16, 17], a Waitangi Tribunal claim (Health Services and Kaupapa Inquiry, Wai 2575) [29] and a specific Waitangi Tribunal report on tobacco, alcohol and other substance abuse [30].

The implications of alcohol‐attributable health burden

In addition to substantial social impacts of losing family to an alcohol‐attributable disease or living with an alcohol‐attributable disability, there are wider system pressures because of the alcohol‐attributable health burden. The health system is under significant pressure across multiple areas including staffing, resourcing and treatment availability. In 2018, 45 000 people were on a waitlist for planned care (which has increased to 75 000 in 2023) because of system capacity issues [31, 32]. We estimated that 29 282 hospitalisations (2.8% of all hospitalisations) in 2018 were attributable to alcohol, which would have detracted from the provision of core services. For example, central region hospitals (Wellington Regional Hospital, Hutt Valley Hospital and Kenepuru Hospital) in 2018 completed 67 000 hospital discharges [33], meaning that nation‐wide, the equivalent of half of the Wellington region's hospital capacity was dedicated to alcohol‐attributable admissions. National cancer treatment performance metrics were below their targets for the year 2022 to 2023, with lower performance for Māori and Pacific compared to overall non‐Māori, non‐Pacific [34], suggesting cancer treatment services are under pressure. Alcohol‐attributable cancer mortality is estimated at approximately 3.5% of all cancer deaths. However, for some prevalent cancers, alcohol is a substantial contributor (11.9% for colorectal cancer; 12.6% for breast), while for some rarer cancers, alcohol is one of the main contributors (42% for lip and oral cancers; 45% for nasopharyngeal cancer).

For ACC claims, using publicly available estimates of the average 2018‐inflation adjusted claim cost within each injury category (unintentional injury = $1883 [35]; traffic injuries $10 563 [35]; self‐harm and interpersonal violence = $56 706 [36]), the estimated annual 2018 inflation‐adjusted cost of alcohol‐attributable claims to ACC would have been: $287 million (m) for unintentional injuries; $54 m for traffic injuries; and $56 m for self‐harm and interpersonal violence injuries. Note these crude estimates only include the cost to ACC for that year for new claims and do not include the lifetime costs of ongoing support. A 2021 analysis by the Helen Clark Foundation of ACC alcohol‐attributable crash data from 2016 to 2020 revealed the lifetime cost of alcohol‐related crash injuries was on average $233 m per year [37], substantially higher than the crude estimate above of $54 m. Collectively, this crude analysis suggests alcohol‐attributable injuries could be costing ACC an estimated approximately $397–$576 m each year (depending on the figure used for traffic injuries), which is equivalent to the yearly cost of all motor vehicle ACC injury claims ($474 m). Motor vehicle injury claims have justified their own separate account within ACC and a dedicated levy imposed on drivers (via vehicle registrations) and on petrol to cost‐recover for that account. Based on cost‐recovery principles, there may be scope to recover the costs incurred by ACC because of alcohol‐attributable claims from those who contributed to those claims (e.g. drinkers and/or the alcohol industry) via a specific levy on alcohol (note this is the opinion of the authors and does not reflect ACC policy).

Strengths and limitations

The study had several strengths that include using more recent RR estimates for 26 alcohol‐attributable disease and injury conditions than used in previous NZ analyses. The use of GBD risk estimates improves comparability with AAF studies from other jurisdictions and potentially over time in New Zealand. As such, this approach can be replicated relatively easily as the evidence base is updated (both in relation to RRs and domestic alcohol consumption), which lays the groundwork for monitoring alcohol‐attributable harm over time. The current study also leveraged a number of routinely collected data sources to examine a range of different health‐related outcomes (e.g. deaths, cancer registrations, hospitalisations and ACC claims), which better captures the full extent of alcohol‐related health harms at a population level, but also facilitates cost attribution of these harms (e.g. to the health sector or ACC).

Our analysis was limited to those health harms with appropriate dose–response relationships identified in the GBD study. Consequently, our results do not consider the health consequences of FASD or other social harms (e.g. relationship and workplace problems). For example, it is estimated that in New Zealand in the 2018/2019 year, FASD prevalence was 1.3% [95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.9–1.9] and that 776 (95% CI = 492–1112) children are born with FASD each year [4]. Similarly, we do not consider the wider harms of alcohol‐attributable crime, lost productivity or harms to others, which can also impact health. Our analysis also does not include other measures of health loss such as utilisation of secondary and community mental health and addiction treatments.

Another limitation is related to the comparative risk methodology in general. The results of this analysis reflect the evidence at one point in time (2016 for RRs in this study) from a single series of meta‐analyses (GBD RRs). As a result, we have included the contested evidence of reduced risk of lower alcohol consumption on diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and ischaemic stroke. A 2017 meta‐analysis observed no statistically significant protective effect of lower levels of alcohol use for ischaemic heart disease [38]. In addition, annual per capita alcohol consumption estimates for 2019 to 2025 were, on average, approximately 5% lower than 2018 levels, indicating a modest overall decline over time. However, without re‐running the analysis using updated epidemiological and alcohol consumption data, it is difficult to determine the extent to which these changes would affect the estimates, particularly as the distribution of the decline in alcohol consumption across population subgroups is unknown.

Options for reducing the alcohol‐attributable health burden

The roadmap to reduced alcohol‐related harm is clear. The WHO SAFER initiative identifies highly effective strategies for governments to adopt [39]. New Zealand modelling suggests substantial potential health gains (>700 000 health‐adjusted life years) are possible from a package of interventions on alcohol tax, availability and marketing [40]. Based on available evidence, each one of the modelled interventions could each bring relatively similar amounts of health gain (~200 000 health‐adjusted life years) [40]. The 2010 Law Commission [41] and subsequent reviews [42, 43] recommended increasing tax by at least 50% and phasing out alcohol sponsorship of sport. Given the substantial economic burden of alcohol‐related harm on government services [e.g. ACC ~$400 m (author derived estimate) and health >$400 m alone] [44], there is scope for recovering these costs. Section 101 of the Pae Ora (Healthy Futures) Act 2022 permits Manatū Hauora to recover the costs it incurs in addressing alcohol‐related harm and in its other alcohol‐related activities via a levy [45]. Similarly, an amendment to the Accident Compensation Act 2001 could enable the ACC to cost recover for alcohol‐attributable ACC claims.

Strategic coordination is required to address alcohol‐related harms. The last National Alcohol Strategy was published by the Ministry of Health in 2003. Consequently, strategic government action on alcohol‐related harm has largely relied on a collection of broader policies from different government agencies (e.g. Ministry of Social Development strategy for youth offending or Ministry of Justice strategy for reducing violence) rather than a dedicated alcohol strategy [46]. The strength of a dedicated national alcohol strategy is that it would bring together different partners, identify their contributions and responsibilities under shared objectives and goals and ultimately hold the Government to account by benchmarking performance against the strategy.

CONCLUSION

In summary, alcohol caused a substantial preventable health burden in 2018 via a range of disease and injury conditions. The health burden from alcohol was disproportionately borne by Māori and males. Cancers, injuries and wholly attributable alcohol conditions (e.g. alcohol use disorders) contribute the majority of alcohol‐attributable mortality and morbidity.
