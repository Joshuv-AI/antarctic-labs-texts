Development and Validation of the Medication Adherence Scale for Iraqi Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis (MASIPRA): A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Lamyaa Abdullah Habeeb, Ehab Mudher Mikhael, Mohammed Hadi Alosami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdherence to disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) is essential to ensure optimum benefit for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients. However, adherence to DMARDs was suboptimal worldwide, especially in developing countries like Iraq. Many adherence scales are available to help healthcare providers assess RA patients’ adherence to their prescribed therapies; however, such tools are often generic, lengthy, unavailable in Arabic, or not freely accessible.ObjectiveTo develop and validate the Medication Adherence Scale for Iraqi Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis (MASIPRA) and examine its psychometric properties.MethodsThe Medication Adherence Scale for Iraqi Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis was developed based on previously validated scales with cultural adaptation. It consists of ten items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Content validity of MASIPRA was done by a panel of seven experts. Clarity of the scale items was established through testing on ten RA patients. Reliability of MASIPRA was confirmed through testing internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) and test–retest reliability. Convergent validity was established by correlating the total MASIPRA score with an already validated scale, the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale-4 (MMAS-4). Concurrent validity was confirmed by correlating the MASIPRA score with the RA clinical disease activity index (CDAI).ResultsMASIPRA showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.804) and test–retest reliability (r=0.804, p< 0.001). It was strongly and significantly correlated with MMAS-4 (r=0.863, p< 0.001) and significantly but inversely correlated with CDAI (r = –0.278, p=0.005). Compared with the MMAS-4, the MASIPRA showed higher sensitivity and specificity in discriminating RA patients according to their disease activity.ConclusionThe MASIPRA is a brief, reliable, and valid instrument for assessing adherence to DMARDs among Iraqi patients with RA.Keywords: rheumatoid arthritis, medication adherence, development, validation, Iraq

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic, inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by persistent joint inflammation leading to joint deformity and functional disability.1,2 It can therefore result in patients experiencing pain, fatigue, emotional distress, and social limitations, all of which significantly impair their physical, psychological, and social well-being.3,4 Fortunately, numerous disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) are available that effectively control rheumatoid arthritis (RA) symptoms, slow its progression, and consequently improve patients’ quality of life.5,6 Meanwhile, these therapies have a gradual and slow onset of action, and require consistent ongoing use to maintain their effectiveness.7 Therefore, patients should fully adhere to these therapies to ensure optimum benefit.8 Nonetheless, adherence to these therapies is suboptimal worldwide9–12 and especially in developing countries like Iraq.13,14 Consequently, healthcare providers must assess patients’ adherence to their DMARDs at every visit to the clinic.15 Unfortunately, there is no universally accepted standard method for measuring medication adherence in routine care;16 however, many scales are available to do so, such as the four- and eight-item Morisky medication adherence scale (MMAS), Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5), Adherence Starts with Knowledge-20 (ASK-20), Compliance-Questionnaire-Rheumatology (CQR)-19, and the shortened CQR (CQR-5),17–22 but they have limitations, including not being specific for RA,17–20 being lengthy,20,21 lacking comprehensive coverage of non-adherence causes,22 or not being freely accessible.17

To address these gaps, there is a pressing need for a comprehensive, culturally sensitive, brief, and hence practical assessment tool specifically to assess medication adherence among Iraqi RA patients. Therefore, the current study aims to develop and validate a new RA-specific medication adherence questionnaire tailored to the Iraqi RA population.

Methods

Development of the Medication Adherence Scale for Iraqi Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis (MASIPRA)

A comprehensive review of the literature on self-reported medication adherence scales for patients with rheumatoid arthritis was conducted, using various sets of suitable keywords. Although numerous scales were identified, only those featuring validated questionnaires—whether generic or specific to RA—and with questionnaire items readily accessible within the articles without the need for further contact with the authors were considered suitable for detailed examination. One RA-specific medication adherence scale, CQR-1921 and two general medication adherence scales, MARS-519 and ASK-20,20 were used to generate a pool of items that have already been tried and validated among RA patients. The researchers carefully assessed the pool of items and selected those suitable for evaluating adherence among Iraqi RA patients, after taking into account reasons for non-adherence reported among such patients in the literature.13,14 Nine items were relevant to the Iraqi patients with RA and thus were adapted from the aforementioned scales. However, the original items were not used verbatim, as they were obtained from different questionnaires. Instead, they were thoughtfully reworded to ensure consistency among the selected items. These items were translated into Arabic by the primary study author and subsequently reviewed and revised by the second author (supervisor). Some items were modified after translation to enhance cultural appropriateness and ease of understanding, while still preserving the original concepts; for all modified items in Arabic, the corresponding English items were also adjusted to ensure consistency between both versions.

Additionally, one new item (item no. 4) was added to the Medication Adherence Scale for Iraqi patients with rheumatoid arthritis (MASIPRA) to increase its sensitivity and specificity in predicting non-adherence among patients with RA. The added item was intended to assess the effect of hand joint pain on medication adherence.23

In total, the MASIPRA (Appendix 1) consists of ten items with 5-point Likert scale responses: (1) always (daily), (2) often, (3) sometimes, (4) rarely, and (5) never. Items 1 through 5 evaluate routine adherence behaviors and recent access barriers occurring over the past month. Conversely, Items 6 through 10 omit a fixed recall period, allowing all respondents to reflect on low-frequency actions triggered by individual health beliefs and treatment perceptions.

The first item is designed to assess the overall level of medication adherence among patients undergoing treatment for rheumatoid arthritis. Item 2 addresses unintentional medication non-adherence. Items 4 and 5 assess barriers to adherence stemming from difficulties in accessing medications. Items 3, 6 through 10 specifically evaluate the extent of intentional non-adherence to prescribed medications.

Scoring of all items ranged from 0 to 4, 0 for a non-adherent answer and 4 for total adherence. All items, except item 1, are inversely calculated. The total score of the MASIPRA ranged from 0 to 40. The cut-off point for MASIPRA was determined based on previous relevant literature among RA patients, with 80% of the maximum score used as the threshold.9,24 Accordingly, patients scoring less than 32 are considered poorly adherent, while those with a score of 32 or higher are regarded as having good adherence.

Validation of the MASIPRA

Content Validity of the MASIPRA

To assess and establish the content validity of MASIPRA, a panel of seven experts (two rheumatologists, four clinical pharmacists experienced in rheumatological diseases, and one psychiatrist) was given the Arabic and the English versions of the MASIPRA.

All experts were asked to assess the relevance and clarity of each item, as well as the overall representativeness of the questionnaire representativeness, using dichotomous responses. Experts were encouraged to write their feedback comments, if any, at the end of the assessment form. All of the above parameters were assessed using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR). Items with CVR = 1 were retained, whereas items with CVR < 1 were removed if irrelevant or modified if unclear to improve readability and comprehension.25

All experts considered the MASIPRA to be comprehensive and all of its items to be relevant; however, two experts provided minor linguistic comments on some words in 5 items. Therefore, these five items were revised to enhance clarity. The revised questionnaire was then shared with the experts, who reached a consensus on its clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness.

The revised version of MASIPRA, after validating its content by the expert panel, was given to ten Iraqi patients with RA, during which these patients were asked to read aloud the Arabic version of the MASIPRA. The main researchers observed the patients to identify any misinterpretations or hesitations in reading and responding to the questions. Additionally, participants were asked about any difficulties they encountered, such as confusing or ambiguous wording and cultural relevance issues. Based on the feedback received and the researcher’s observations, necessary modifications were made to the wording of one item to prevent any confusion and enhance overall clarity and ease of reading for the target population.

Additional validations (concurrent and convergent) were done only for the Arabic version through pilot testing with RA patients.

Pilot Study

A cross-sectional study was conducted at the Rheumatology outpatient clinic at the Baghdad Teaching Hospital, Baghdad, Iraq, from November 2024 to April 2026. This clinic represents the primary tertiary referral hub and largest rheumatology center in Iraq, which manages patients from all Iraqi governorates.

The current study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the ethical committee at the College of Pharmacy – University of Baghdad (Ethical approval no. RECAUBCP0625196R in December 2025).

Due to cultural considerations and potential reluctance surrounding signed documentation in Iraqi society,26,27 the Ethics Committee granted a waiver of written informed consent and approved obtaining verbal informed consent from all participants prior to study inclusion.

Sample Size

There is some ambiguity regarding the ideal sample size for pilot studies aimed at validating new questionnaires. Generally, a rule of thumb suggests that 5–10 participants per item are adequate. However, increasing the sample size can enhance the accuracy of the results.28,29 Since MASIPRA consists of ten items, the sample size for this study was set at 100 to ensure 10 participants per item. Additionally, the target sample size was increased to 105 patients to account for an estimated 5% non-response rate.

Patient Selection

Arab patients aged 18 years and older with a rheumatologist confirmed diagnosis of RA, who were receiving any class of DMARDs for a minimum of 3 months, irrespective of their disease activity status were included in this study.

However, patients who had renal impairment, cognitive impairment, depression, and pregnant women were excluded.

The principal investigator (the first author, a master’s candidate in clinical pharmacy) employed a convenience sampling strategy in the outpatient waiting area to identify and assess patients for study eligibility.

Prior to enrollment, all eligible candidates were screened using a validated two-question assessment to identify and exclude individuals with suspected, undiagnosed depression.30 Patients with abnormal responses were referred to a psychiatrist for further evaluation. The principal investigator then explained the study’s purpose and protocol to eligible participants, emphasizing that participation was entirely voluntary and that declining would carry no negative consequences or affect their standard clinical care. Only those who provided verbal informed consent were invited to complete two self-administered questionnaires (MASIPRA and MMAS-4).

For test–retest reliability, only patients who were maintained on the same treatment (the same medication and same dose) were asked to return after 2 weeks to fill in the MASIPRA again.

Convergent Validity

To assess the convergent validity of the MASIPRA, it should be compared with a questionnaire that is already validated to assess medication adherence among RA patients. To date, there is no validated instrument specifically designed to measure medication adherence among Iraqi RA patients for use as a comparator. Therefore, the 4-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-4) was selected as a comparator because it has already been validated for assessing adherence among patients with RA,31 is available in Arabic,32 and is characterized by its brevity, ease of use, and validation in patients with low literacy levels.16 Although the MMAS-4 is freely available in many publications, a request was sent via Email to the corresponding author (Morisky) to obtain ethical approval for its use. Direct permission was granted by the corresponding author.

The participants need approximately 5–7 min to complete the two questionnaires. All patients who were maintained on the same treatment and same doses (n=72) were asked to return after 2 weeks to fill in the MASIPRA.

Concurrent Validity

For assessing concurrent validity, the clinical disease activity index (CDAI) was chosen as a comparator to the MASIPRA because it better correlates with the patient’s response to treatment;33 besides, CDAI is easy to calculate34 and routinely done by physicians during a patient’s visit to the rheumatology center.

CDAI scores were obtained directly from patients’ records completed on the day of their visit to the center, which was the same day the MASIPRA was administered.

The Diagnostic Accuracy of MASIPRA

Patients with CDAI values of ≤ 10 were considered to have well-controlled RA disease activity.35 The correlation between disease activity and medication adherence was employed to categorize the results as follows: true positive (TP) for patients who are non-adherent to their DMARDs and exhibit high disease activity; false positive (FP) for patients with high disease activity despite adhering well to their medication; true negative (TN) for patients with low disease activity (or remission) and good adherence to DMARDs; and false negative (FN) for patients with low disease activity or remission despite being non-adherent to their DMARDs.

The sensitivity of the MASIPRA reflects its capability to correctly predict high RA disease activity among non-adherent patients, while the specificity of the MASIPRA reflects its capability to predict low RA disease activity among adherent patients to their DMARDs.36

Statistical Analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 17. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. The normality of data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Spearman’s ρ correlation was employed to examine the relationship between continuous variables that were not normally distributed. Item redundancy was evaluated using an inter-item Spearman correlation matrix. Item pairs exhibiting a strong correlation (≥0.80) were reviewed for conceptual overlap, and redundant items were considered for removal to optimize scale brevity without sacrificing internal consistency.

The internal consistency of the MASIPRA was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, with a value of ≥0.7 deemed acceptable. A corrected item-total correlation exceeding 0.2 was considered sufficient for item validity.37 For test–retest reliability, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare scores for each item, and the Spearman correlation coefficient assessed the correlation of the MASIPRA total score before and after retesting.

Excluding item 9 from the MASIPRA modestly increased the Cronbach’s alpha to 0.809, while removing item 4 from the MMAS-4 raised its Cronbach’s alpha to 0.601. Discussion

This study revealed that the MASIPRA has optimum internal consistency, stable reliability, and good concurrent validity, sensitivity, and specificity.

The psychometric properties of the MASIPRA were better than those of the validated MMAS-4. In this regard, the MASIPRA achieved Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.804 with Corrected item–total correlations for all items of more than 0.2, while it was 0.584 for MMAS-4. This result was very close to a previously reported study, where a low Cronbach’s alpha (0.61) was achieved for the MMAS-4 among patients with rheumatic disease.38 Based on these results, only MASIPRA has good internal consistency because most references regarded Cronbach’s alpha values greater than 0.7 as good and those below 0.6 to be poor.39

Despite this good internal consistency for MASIPRA, its Cronbach’s alpha value could be further increased if item 9 is removed. However, this item was kept in the MASIPRA since it was recognized as an essential question; besides, the improvement in Cronbach’s alpha by removing this item is marginal and non-significant. A similar action was taken during the development and validation of different scales for Iraqi patients.40

Statistically, all MASIPRA items showed a non-significant difference at test-retest. In addition, the MASIPRA total score between test-retest showed good correlation. These results indicate a stable reliability for the MASIPRA, which is mandatory especially for patients on chronic treatment, such as those with RA.

A strong correlation was observed between the MASIPRA and the MMAS-4. Additionally, there was no significant difference between the MASIPRA and the MMAS-4 in estimating the prevalence of medication non-adherence, confirming the convergent validity of the MASIPRA.

This study showed a significant inverse correlation between total MASIPRA scores and CDAI values, besides two of the CDAI components: the VAS for the patient and the number of tender joints. This finding confirmed the concurrent validity of the MASIPRA, and it was consistent with other studies that showed a negative correlation between medication adherence and disease activity.10,41

Meanwhile, the correlation between MASIPRA and CDAI is stronger among patients on mono DMARD and slightly weaker among those using dual DMARDs; however, significance was only observed in the dual DMARD group, which may be attributed to the smaller sample of patients on mono DMARDs. The current result further confirms the concurrent validity of the MASIPRA among all RA patients.

Moreover, all subscales of MASIPRA had a significant inverse correlation with CDAI except for unintentional non-adherence (Question 2), which had a non-significant correlation. The lack of association between MASIPRA and unintentional non-adherence could not be considered as a weakness in the scale, but it may be attributed to the limited effect of forgetfulness of a few doses of DMARDs on disease activity, taking into account the chronic nature of RA, the long duration of action for DMARDs, and the differences in pharmacokinetic properties of DMARDs.42–44

This study revealed that the MASIPRA had very high sensitivity but relatively low specificity; however, both values are higher than those with MMAS-4. The relatively low MASIPRA specificity is not an indication of weakness for the currently developed scale since such an issue was commonly seen across other self-reporting patient assessment tools.16,36

Furthermore, this finding may be attributed to the application of a fixed cut-off threshold (80%), whereas a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis could yield a cut-off value that better balances sensitivity and specificity.

Meanwhile, high sensitivity is prioritized in clinical practice for adherence screening to ensure that non-adherent patients with active disease (TP) are not missed, even at the expense of lower specificity.

Compared to the adherence scales from which MASIPRA was developed, it offers distinct methodological and clinical advantages for assessing medication adherence in Iraqi patients with RA. In this regard, validated general tools such as the MARS-519 and ASK-2020 are not suitable for capturing disease-specific functional limitations unique to RA, specifically hand joint pain and dexterity barriers that directly impair medicine administration (evaluated in MASIPRA Item 4). Other general tools, namely the MMAS-4, are restricted by licensing fees, whereas MASIPRA provides a brief, freely accessible, and culturally adapted alternative with superior psychometric stability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.804 vs 0.584), as well as higher specificity and sensitivity. Furthermore, MASIPRA demonstrates clear clinical utility compared to the RA-specific CQR-19.21 In this context, lengthy questionnaires such as the 19-item CQR-19 require extended administration time and trigger higher respondent fatigue, intensifying the operational burden on both patients and treating rheumatologists in busy outpatient settings and ultimately lowering response rates.45 MASIPRA overcomes these challenges through a brief 10-item design that streamlines completion, mitigates patient fatigue, and enhances overall response compliance. Consequently, this concise tool (MASIPRA) offers a clinical advantage over CQR-19 for Iraqi and Arab patients—who often struggle with lengthy self-reported questionnaires40,46—and offers high feasibility for clinicians in crowded outpatient centers throughout Iraq47 and similar developing healthcare systems.

Finally, MASIPRA captures both routine adherence behaviors and intermittent, event-driven decision-making by integrating a fixed recall timeframe with a flexible one, making it superior to the aforementioned scales that rely exclusively on either a fixed or unrestricted recall period.

On the other hand, MASIPRA has many cons. First, MASIPRA demonstrated relatively low specificity, which highlights the need for cautious interpretation when using it as a standalone diagnostic tool. Second, because the scale was tailored specifically to the cultural and linguistic nuances of Iraqi patients with RA, its cross-cultural validity outside Iraq remains to be established in future multi-center studies.

The current study has several limitations. The first is the recruitment of patients from one rheumatology center; however, this center is the largest center in Iraq and patients from all governorates visited it. In addition, the full validation of MASIPRA was in the Arabic language, and hence only Arabic Iraqi patients could be enrolled in the current study. Therefore, it is suggested to do other studies to translate and validate the MASIPRA in other common languages among Iraqis, such as Kurdish, Assyrian, and Turkmen. However, such ethnic groups that use these languages are a minority in Iraq.

Conclusion

MASIPRA is a reliable, validated instrument for evaluating DMARD adherence among Iraqi patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Its brief 10-item architecture minimizes administration time and respondent fatigue, thereby mitigating operational strain for both patients and attending rheumatologists in busy outpatient clinics. Moreover, as a disease-specific and open-access tool, MASIPRA overcomes the clinical constraints of general adherence scales and the financial barriers of licensed tools, ensuring high feasibility across Iraq and comparable resource-constrained healthcare systems.
