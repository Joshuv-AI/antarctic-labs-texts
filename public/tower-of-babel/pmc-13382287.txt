Visualizing dermatology clinic routines using narrative networks
Authors: Julie Ryan Wolf, Amanda Rodriguez Orengo, Gayin Lee, Alice P. Pentland, Brian T. Pentland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthcare routines consist of interdependent events essential to patient care, and understanding each role’s contribution is critical for effective processes. Aggregating these interdependent events and perspectives into patterns has been recognized as a central problem in empirical research on routines. The problem is harder when participants have different understandings of an organizational routine. We combine qualitative structured interviews and narrative event networks using ThreadNet, an R-based software, to visualize the clinic process in dermatology. Structured interviews captured role-specific clinic visit activities from 36 dermatology clinic staff members. Narrative event networks illustrated the role-specific activities. Physicians had the highest cross-role awareness, whereas outpatient access specialists and residents were the least informed. Physicians reported 54% of actions, reflecting only half of the total clinic activity. Licensed practical nurse, residents, and outpatient access specialists showed less understanding of the complete visit process (37, 27, and 25%, respectively). The application of narrative networks to sequence data (ie, structured interviews, electronic health record) can aggregate multiple fragmented interdependent patterns to enhance our understanding of clinic processes and routines.

Introduction

Healthcare routines are a series of events and interdependent elements needed for patient care. Understanding clinic visit processes requires knowledge of all the roles involved and all the tasks completed during the visit. In business, comprehension of how routines support work activities is essential for enhancing efficiency and improving performance. Similarly, in healthcare clinics, many actors and their actions contribute to patient care and to the broader functioning of the healthcare organization. The process of these actions is not always identical or predictable. Each individual involved in a clinic visit has detailed knowledge of their own actions but also has an understanding of the larger whole process (Feldman and Pentland, 2003).

Processes, routines, and other patterns of action are difficult to observe in full. Our narrow window of perception is framed in time and space, so at best, we see one action at a time (Feldman and Pentland, 2022). Aggregating fragments into patterns has been recognized as a central problem in empirical research on routines (Pentland and Feldman, 2007). The problem is harder when participants have different understandings of an organizational routine. They are familiar with their own work, but the work of others may be less visible, may be less understood, and may also be perceived as less important. When different participants see and understand a routine differently, it contributes to the multiplicity of the routine and decreases efficiency (Feldman et al, 2016). For this reason, routines have multiple ostensive aspects. By ostensive aspect, we mean the typical or abstract pattern of the routine rather than the actual, specific performances of the routine (Feldman and Pentland, 2003).

Process multiplicity refers to the number of possible paths that can exist within a pattern of action. van Mierlo et al (2019) demonstrated that in-depth interviews captured differing points of view (POVs) on the best hiring routines from human resource professionals and multilevel managers in a corporate setting. Owing to these different perspectives, a new and more efficient company-wide hiring routine was established that encompassed these differing perspectives. Although too many differing POVs may break down routines (Deken et al, 2016), increased knowledge of the whole process can facilitate modifications to the process with minimal disruption. Operational resilience redesign is the process of restructuring an organization’s systems, processes, and capabilities to withstand, adapt to, and recover from disruptions (Emami et al, 2024; Ignatowicz et al, 2023). Healthcare organizations need to sustain operations under expected and unexpected conditions, which require the ability to anticipate, monitor, learn, and respond to any disruption (Emami et al, 2024; Ignatowicz et al, 2023). Standardizing workflows and communications can ensure flexibility and resilience in processes; however, in some scenarios, a full redesign of the process must occur to alleviate future disruption and optimize efficiency (Emami et al, 2024; Ignatowicz et al, 2023). Understanding “who does what” is helpful for process change and improvement.

In this paper, we combine qualitative structured interviews and narrative event networks to visualize how dermatology clinical staff perceive their own and others’ contributions to the clinic process. Using the technique outlined by Pentland and Liu (2017), we interviewed individuals in each clinical role (office staff, technician, licensed practical nurse [LPN], registered nurse, resident, physician) in each clinic. The interviews provided us with structured narratives that describe typical clinical visits (and typical variations) from the POV of each role in the clinic. These structured network interviews can be interpreted as the ostensive aspects of the routines in each clinic because they describe typified understandings. We visualized the routines as narrative networks using ThreadNet (Pentland and Liu, 2017) to aggregate perspectives on the clinic visit processes from the POV of the interviewed clinic staff. This methodology allows us to compare explicitly the ostensive aspects of routines from different roles in an organization. Through this visualization, we identified gaps in shared understanding and visibility across roles. Moving forward, this methodology may assist in enhancing comprehension of healthcare routines and the interdependence of healthcare roles.

Materials and Methods

Structured interviews

We conducted qualitative structured interviews in dermatology to understand how different roles describe the activities involved in a dermatology clinic visit. The interview protocol was approved by the Subjects Review Board with a waiver of Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and an alteration of the consent process. All interview participants provided verbal consent. The role (ie, physician, LPN, resident, etc) was obtained for each participant without any identifiers. To maintain uniformity, each interview was conducted using the same protocol. The interview protocol (Supplementary Material S1) requested that each individual break down the clinic visit process into a sequence of steps and actions that they themselves perform as well as the steps and actions that they know occur but do not directly involve them. To capture the activities in all visit types in all clinics, interviewees were asked to provide as many different sequences for a clinic visit as possible. A total of 36 staff members in dermatology clinics were interviewed, consisting of 8 clinical technicians (Clinical Tech), 7 outpatient access specialists [OASs], 6 LPNs, 7 residents, and 8 attending physicians.

The interviews provided structured narratives for clinic visits from check-in to checkout as a sequence of steps or actions and from each role or actor (ie, physician, LPN, resident, etc). Different clinic visit scenarios (ie, follow-up, new patient, surgery, biopsy) were described as different sequences of actions. Each different sequence of actions from check-in to checkout was designated as a “clinic visit.” Our approach did not examine individual patient visits in a specific clinic but instead focused on typical activities for dermatology clinic visits.

Coding interviews

Table 1 contains a glossary of terminology that may be helpful for understanding our methodology. The sequence of actions generated by the interviews was transformed into a matrix consisting of rows representing sequence order and columns representing variables in the clinic visit process (ie, visit identification [ID], sequence ID, POV, actor, and action) (Table 2). The visit ID designated a block of rows as part of the same “clinic visit.” A different visit ID (“clinic visit”) indicated a different description of action–actor sequences for a clinic visit. The sequence ID designated the order of the actions within the clinic visit. The “POV” indicated the role and perspective of the interviewee (ie, physician, resident, etc). The role of the individual who performed the specified action in the clinic visit was the “actor.” For example, “Patient checks-in at front desk” was performed by “OAS” (ie, the actor), whereas “Puts patient in exam room” was performed by the “Clinical Tech” (ie, the actor). Coding allowed the interviews to indicate the role of the person being interviewed (ie, POV) as well as the role of the person performing the actions (ie, actor). Each branch in a sequence of actions created a new clinic visit. Similar actions were coded and documented by the interviewer in the same manner to ensure consistent terminology for all visits. The resulting narrative interview data indicated the role of the interviewee (ie, POV), the role of the person performing the actions (ie, actor), the actions performed by the “actor,” and the sequence of those actions. Narrative network visualization

ThreadNet is an R-coded, graph-theoretic application that provides simple visualizations of sequence data (Pentland and Liu, 2017). ThreadNet is available on GitHub (https://github.com/ThreadNet/ThreadNet) with installation instructions at http://routines.broad.msu.edu/ThreadNet/. We used ThreadNet to convert the narrative interview data into event networks summarizing the clinic visit routine from the POV of each participant. The ThreadNet algorithm takes coded sequences of action and converts them into an event network (Pentland and Feldman, 2007). This methodology enables visualization of clinic visit activities on the basis of how the clinical staff described each other’s work. Each unique action by a particular role (ie, actor) becomes a node in the network (eg, “Puts patient in exam room – Clinical Tech”). Thus, each node is an action–actor combination (eg, “Puts patient in exam room – Clinical Tech” and “Puts patient in exam room – LPN” are 2 different nodes). Each sequentially adjacent pair of action–actor combinations becomes an edge in the network. Table 2 provides examples of action-actor sequences for clinic visits. For example, the action–role combination “Puts patient in exam room - Clinical Tech” followed by “Takes BP, height, weight in exam room - Clinical Tech” or “Asks Chief Complaint – Clinical Tech” would create 2 edges from the node for “Puts patient in exam room - Clinical Tech” to the nodes for the other 2 action–actor combinations. The network summarizes the sequential relations between those action–actor combinations. A graph with more nodes indicates a larger number of action–actor combinations. A more densely connected network, with more edges, indicates a larger number of action–actor sequences (ie, more edges = more action–actor sequences). Multiple edges on a node show that the action–actor is sequentially related to multiple other action–actors. The length of the edge does not have meaning and is a result of the layout algorithm. The weight (ie, thickness) of the edge reflects the frequency of the sequentially adjacent pairs of action–role combinations (ie, thicker line = greater frequency). Networks were constructed from each role’s POV (interviewees) for the whole clinic process, their own activities, and the activities of other roles. Each network represents a pooled perspective by the specified role POV (ie, data from 7 residents are depicted in the resident POV networks). A network for the complete clinic visit process combines all interview data to plot all sequential relations between action–actor combinations. Results

How clinical staff perceive their work

This methodology allows us to visualize how the clinical staff see each other’s work. Figure 2 shows 4 narrative networks, each constructed from a different POV using ThreadNet. The boxed numbers in the upper right corners of each network graph indicate the number of nodes (ie, action–actor combinations) in the network. These networks represent portions of role routines in the same clinics. Each network is a partial view of the overall clinical routines. When using the software, these images are interactive; action names appear when the mouse hovers over a node. The networks in Figure 2 show the difference between a role’s perception of their own clinical routine compared with how another role perceives their work. These networks suggest that clinical staff are not fully aware of all activities in the clinic. OASs and residents are largely unaware of each other’s role-specific activities. This is not surprising, because they work in physically separate areas of the clinic. OASs also typically turn over in their employment every few years, which may reduce the attention paid to OAS activities by other roles. In contrast, LPNs had a clear view of residents’ work, whereas residents minimized the work of the LPNs. LPNs and residents do work in the same physical space and often coordinate clinic visits together; therefore, they should be more aware of each other’s work. As expected, all roles knew the most about their own activities during a clinic visit (Figure 2). Physicians provided the most thorough detail about actor-specific actions across roles. Visualizing whole patterns

This evidence suggests that it is difficult for individuals to see whole processes, whole routines, or all patterns of action. Narrative networks are a way to aggregate multiple POVs to assemble a more complete picture of clinical work. Figure 3 shows 5 different network graphs: the complete clinic process (ie, all role-specific activities combined) in the center and the complete clinic process from 4 POVs (OAS, LPN, resident, physician) in the 4 corners. The node color indicates the actor for the action–actor combination. Figure 3 also contains the percentage of the overall network that each role described in the interviews on each graph. The percentages show that no role knows the complete clinic process. The LPNs have a more complete view than the OASs or residents, but physicians have the best overall perspective. Discussion and Potential Applications

Clinical settings bring together a diverse team of professionals with different training, goals, and working styles, all working toward a common objective: delivering high-quality patient care (Baker et al, 2006; Hayes et al, 2011). Although individuals in different clinical roles may share this overarching goal, each has their own perspective on how best to achieve it. Narrative networks provide a dynamic framework for visualizing clinical routines by mapping the complex, interwoven activities of healthcare providers to offer valuable insights into how individuals understand their own roles and those of others within the clinical environment (Hayes et al, 2011).

Narrative networks using ThreadNet are an exploratory method to facilitate inquiry and to visualize patterns you would not usually see. The emphasis is on comparison over time to visualize change, alternative POVs, and granularity without confirmatory statistics. Because ThreadNet is open source for R software, other R scripts for network metrics and additional analytical modeling can be incorporated. Future work could harness the customizability of ThreadNet to include more complex network analytics with narrative network visualizations.

Our findings support the notion that routines possess multiple ostensive aspects because each clinical role perceives and interprets the workflow differently (Feldman and Pentland, 2003). Individuals are most aware of their own responsibilities, which can vary substantially among team members, yet the interdependent tasks of each role are very essential to the clinic’s overall function. The diversity of the perspectives we observed is interesting, given the rather small physical space in the clinic. These individuals work in close proximity to each other; however, the different roles have different responsibilities. To work as an effective healthcare team, knowledge of their own and teammates’ task responsibilities is crucial (Baker et al, 2006). A lack of shared role understanding is a major barrier to effective teamwork and may contribute to inefficiencies and errors in clinical settings (O’Daniel and Rosenstein, 2008). Consequently, increased role awareness may improve both clinic efficiency and the quality of care.

Successful coordination and performance of complex routines require knowing who knows what and who can perform each action (Emami et al, 2024; Ignatowicz et al, 2023). If a patient has an insurance billing question, it is important to know how to direct the patient and who can answer their question (ie, send the patient to the OAS). It is also necessary to know who can perform tasks if a role is absent. For process resiliency, it is important to have multiple roles with the ability to perform the same tasks, allowing for task coverage. Staff shortages during the COVID-19 pandemic caused many roles to take on responsibilities and activities that were usually assigned to other roles. Physicians and residents performed rooming procedures because LPNs and Clinical Techs were not in the clinics. Our findings demonstrate resiliency in clinic visit activities, with multiple roles performing the same task (ie, both LPNs and Clinical Techs room patients). Although not demonstrated in this paper, ThreadNet can apply clustering to show how many different roles perform the same task. In addition, our approach illustrates patterns of actions for each role and handoffs between roles, providing a visualization of the organization and responsibilities of each role. Our methodology could help clinical departments identify routines that require full redesign and those that just need adjustment to optimize efficiency.

Coordination in teams has been extensively studied in organizational psychology (Argote and Guo, 2016; Kost et al, 2026; Lewis, 2003). That research shows that teams have transactive memory systems that enable team members to keep track of “who knows what” and “who can do what.” For a clinical team to perform successfully, ongoing communication and exchange of information about responsibilities and tasks across multiple roles are required for the contribution of each role and optimal performance (Kost et al, 2026). Our methodology provides a list of activities and handoffs by each role involved in a dermatology clinic visit. This information could be used to formulate educational interventions to reinforce that the whole clinic process is the integration of different roles performing different activities to provide patient care. Full comprehension of all role responsibilities and activities would facilitate information exchange, coordination, and implementation of new processes. Furthermore, the application of interview-based narrative network methodology across specialties could reveal variations and consistencies in typical clinic visit processes, routine resiliency, and role interactions.

Our results have obvious implications for research on routines or processes where data are collected by interview. Even if an individual is very well-informed, such as the physicians in dermatology clinics, they are unlikely to be able to map out the entirety of a routine. The same is true for data collected through observation or participant observation. Being present in the flow of action does not allow observers to escape the inherent limitations of time and space (Jarzabkowski et al, 2016; Langley et al, 2013). We can see the here and now, but everything else must be reconstructed.

Our network visualization methodology can be applied to other sequence data, such as electronic health record data, to illustrate, compare, and understand healthcare routines and processes (Pentland et al, 2023, 2021, 2017; Ryan et al, 2016; Wolf et al, 2023). Electronic health record audit trail data are digital trace data for clinical documentation of clinical encounters, which contain roles (ie, actors), actions, time stamps of the actions, and workstations (ie, computers). We previously used ThreadNet to examine longitudinal alterations in healthcare routines using electronic health record audit trail data (Pentland et al, 2021; Ryan et al, 2016; Wolf et al, 2023). We examined patterns of actions and handoffs in individual patient visits instead of typical average visits. In one study, we identified the start and end of flu season on the basis of changes in dermatology action patterns over a 2-year period, and in another study, we showed changes and recovery of action patterns during different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic (Pentland et al, 2021; Wolf et al, 2023). This work visualized, measured, and compared the similarity of dermatology action patterns over time and across different healthcare specialties.

Our methodology is not without its limitations. Although the work presented in the paper was conducted at a single institution, the methodology and overall concept are generalizable, even if a reader is not familiar with every step of the process. Although we attempted to elicit comparable information in each interview, the interview protocol captures what people say about each other’s work, not what they know about each other’s work. The gap between saying and knowing is an inherent limitation of our method. Individuals often differ in their tendency to talk about themselves versus others; therefore, they might say less than they actually know about others. To mitigate this risk, we interviewed several individuals within each role. The differences observed in actions may have been consistent with a shared understanding concerning higher-level goals (eg, patient care, confidentiality, etc), but we did not collect data on goals. Although we interviewed individuals, we performed the analyses by role. This aggregation of individual responses mitigated the risk and noise introduced by individual bias, strengthening role generalizability. Realistically, the conduct and coding of interviews were time consuming and labor intensive, which limit the implementation of the interview-based methodology in healthcare practice. However, the interviews could be a component of a Unit-based Program Performance or a quality improvement initiative focused on clinic process education for clinical staff. Overall, our findings support the possibility of sharing general goals and, at the same time, having different understandings about how to accomplish those goals.

This paper highlights the application of structured interviews and narrative networks to visualize healthcare routines in dermatology. Our methodology supports the idea of multiple ostensive aspects (ie, abstract patterns) of a routine, the resiliency of clinic processes, and the necessary information exchange among complex teams. Processes, routines, and other patterns of action are difficult to observe in full. Our methodology allows the aggregation of clinic processes from different perspectives to capture the whole process. In the future, this methodology may be applied to electronic health record data and sequences to compare and contrast longitudinal patterns of routines and processes.
