Outcome variation in the use and duration of antibiotic therapy in patients presenting with acute diverticulitis
Authors: Olivia Ziegler, Eric W Schaefer, Alicia C Greene, Eric D Moyer, Zachary Delgado, Patricio B Lynn, Jeffrey S Scow, Micheal J Deutsch, A S Kulaylat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal antibiotic duration for diverticulitis managed without procedural source control is unknown. While antibiotic-sparing approaches in select patients are supported by high quality data, up to 24% experience treatment failure. Here we assess outcomes stratified by antibiotic duration in diverticulitis patients treated on an outpatient basis after an emergency department (ED) visit.MethodsEmploying the MarketScan® database (2016–2022), adult patients with a diverticulitis-associated ED visit treated on an outpatient basis were identified. Claims for diverticulitis-directed antibiotics filled within three days of ED visit were captured.Antibiotic receipt/duration were compared for 90-day outcomes, including repeat diverticulitis-related ED visit, additional antibiotic prescriptions, and surgery. Outcomes were assessed using chi square, unadjusted and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsSeventy-three thousand eight hundred ten patients were included; 54.6% were female, median age was 52 years. 11,311 (15.3%) patients did not fill an antibiotic prescription. Of those who filled one, median duration of prescription was 10.0 days.In unadjusted analysis, those who did not fill a prescription had comparable rates of surgery, but lower rates of subsequent antibiotic use (24.2% vs. 29.9%, p < 0.001) and repeat diverticulitis-related ED visits (4.9% vs. 6.0%, p < 0.001) than those with prescriptions. Background

Diverticulitis is an inflammatory condition of pouch-like protrusions of the inner layer of the bowel, most commonly in the sigmoid colon [1]. In 2021, diverticulitis was associated with nine billion dollars in health care costs in the United States (US), and nearly 300,000 emergency department (ED) visits [2]. While the incidence of diverticulitis is increasing [3], management is in the midst of a paradigm shift with an increasing emphasis on conservative, outpatient management of uncomplicated disease [4, 5].

Related to this change in management is a gradual adoption of an antibiotic-sparing approach for uncomplicated disease in select patients [6]. This approach may be acceptable to patients and has the potential to significantly reduce the associated health care costs and the overuse of antibiotics [7, 8]. While this antibiotic-sparing approach is supported by data from randomized control trials [9] and has been incorporated into guidelines from the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons [10], up to 24% of individuals experience treatment failure [4]. These same guidelines caution that data are limited to uncomplicated disease in immunocompetent patients without significant comorbidities, such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and hypertension, which dramatically limits the generalizability of this management strategy [11]. Further, a recent Cochrane review cautions that the overall body of evidence regarding this practice remains limited in part due to wide confidence intervals and associated uncertainty regarding the potential benefits of antibiosis [12].

Overall, the widespread adoption of antibiotic-sparing approaches has not occurred [1], and prognosticating which patients may benefit from antibiotics remains understudied. Finally, optimal duration of treatment in patients who are offered antibiotic management without procedural source control is unknown. Given this complex and evolving landscape, exploring the patterns of antibiotic use and their associated outcomes in diverticulitis is warranted. Here, we employ a large commercial insurance claims database to examine the relationship between antibiotic use/duration and outcomes, including: subsequent antibiotic use, repeat ED visits, and surgery.

Methods

Data and population

Data were collected from the MarketScan Commercial Claims and Encounters (Truven MarketScan, Health Analytics Inc., Ann Arbor, MI) database from 2016 to 2022. This was done to coincide with implementation of the 10th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), which saw changes in the classification of diverticulitis. MarketScan consists of reimbursed insurance claims for patients with private, employer-based insurance in the US. Patients who experience lapses in coverage or who are enrolled in Medicare, Medicaid, or other government-issued insurance are not captured in these data. This study was evaluated by the Penn State Milton S. Hershey Institutional Review Board and deemed not human subject research and thus exempt from further review (STUDY00024381, 2/9/24).

This database was queried to identify patients over 18 years of age who visited an emergency department with an associated ICD-10 code indicating diverticulitis (K57.20, K57.21, K57.32, K57.33, K57.40, K57.41, K57.52, K57.53, K57.80, K57.81, K57.92, K57.93). Patients were required to be enrolled in commercial insurance for the six months prior to, and at least three months following, the index ED visit. Patients who were admitted as an inpatient or who had International Classification of Disease (ICD) -10 or current procedural technology (CPT) codes associated with diverticular fistula, or who underwent surgery or a percutaneous drainage procedure associated with their index ED visit, were excluded (Supplemental Table 1). Only patients who were managed on an outpatient basis were included. This was defined as no inpatient admission associated with the ED visit, but did include those under observation within the emergency department. Patients with observation stays over 2 days, however, were excluded. Pharmaceutical claims for diverticulitis-related antibiotics filled within three days of ED visit were captured.

Outcomes & statistical analysis

Patients were stratified by antibiotic use and duration of prescription and assessed for the following outcomes: surgery for diverticulitis, need for percutaneous drainage procedures, diverticulitis-related repeat ED visits, and subsequent antibiotic prescription, all occurring within 90 days of index ED visit. Drainage procedures were identified by the CPT codes 48,000, 49,021, 49,041, 49,061, 49,062, 49,406, 50,021, 51,080, 75,984, 75,989, 76,942, 77,002, 77,012, and 77,021. Logistic regression was used to model each outcome. Two models were fit, one which included a binary indicator for receipt of antibiotics, and another indicating the duration of antibiotic prescription. For the latter case, duration was stratified into 1–6 days, 7 days, 8–9 days, 10 days, and 11 or more days. These categories were chosen after examining frequency of antibiotic duration which were heavily weighted toward exactly 7 or exactly 10 days. Seven days was chosen as the reference group. Adjusted analysis was then performed on each model to account for sex, age, year of index event, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (Supplemental Table 2). Pre-selected alpha was set at 0.05.

Results

The sample included 73,810 patients who met all inclusion/exclusion criteria; 54.6% (n = 40,283) were female with median age 52.0 years (Table 1). The vast majority were in the ED for 0 days (87.3%), followed by 10.7% who were in the ED for one day. Among the cohort, 11,311 (15.3%) patients did not fill an antibiotic prescription within three days of the index ED visit. Examining 90-day outcomes in all patients, 1.7% (n = 1,242) had a surgery for diverticulitis, and 5.8% (n = 4,278) had another diverticulitis-related ED visit. The most frequent outcome was subsequent antibiotic prescription, with 29.0% (n = 21,390) of the cohort experiencing this outcome.

Antibiotic use vs. non-use

Examining these same outcomes on unadjusted analysis, stratified by antibiotic use vs. non-use, we observed comparable rates of surgery (1.6% vs. 1.7%, p = 0.731) and need for percutaneous drainage (1.5%, 1.6%, p = 0.669). Rates of repeat diverticulitis-related ED visits were significantly lower in those without antibiotic use (4.9% vs. 6.0%, p < 0.001). Finally, subsequent antibiotic prescriptions were significantly lower in those who did not use antibiotics compared to those who did (24.1% vs. 29.9%, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Antibiotic duration

Of the 84.7% (n = 62,499) of individuals in the cohort who filled a prescription within three days of ED index visit, 62.4% (n = 38,999) were for a duration of ten days, while 27.5% (n = 17,179) were for seven days (Fig. 1). The most common prescription was for ciprofloxacin and metronidazole (54.8%) followed by amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (23.0%) (Table 3).

Unadjusted comparisons of outcomes by antibiotic durations revealed significant differences (Fig. 2). Those receiving antibiotic courses of 11 + days experienced the highest rates of surgery (3.2%, vs. 1.4% for 7-days and 1.6% for no antibiotics, p < 0.001). Again, those who had 11 + day prescription durations had the highest rates of diverticulitis-related ED visits, while those with no initial prescription or only 7 days prescription had lower rates of this outcome (6.4% vs. 4.9% and 5.5%, respectively, p < 0.001). Additional antibiotic prescriptions were again different between groups, with the lowest rates observed with no antibiotic use (24.1%, p < 0.001 between all groups). No differences between groups were identified regarding need for percutaneous drainage within 90 days of index ED visit. (Fig. 2).

Proportions of each outcome of interest by initial antibiotic prescription duration

In an adjusted model, those with prescriptions of 10 days or 11 + days had higher odds of surgery than those with shorter courses (10 days, OR = 1.17 (95% CI = 1.01–1.36) p = 0.038); 11 + days, OR = 2.20 (95% CI = 1.77–2.74), p < 0.001; reference 7 days). Each 10-year increase in age was associated with 1.16-fold increased odds of requiring subsequent surgery (95%CI = 1.09–1.23, p < 0.001) (Table 4a).

Individuals without antibiotic prescriptions had significantly lower rates of diverticulitis-related ED visits (OR = 0.89 (95% CI = 0.79–0.99) p = 0.027) compared to those with 7-day prescriptions, while those with courses of 10 (OR = 1.13 (95% CI = 1.04–1.22) p = 0.003), or 11 + days (OR = 1.17 (95% CI = 1.01–1.35) p = 0.034) had significantly higher odds of a diverticulitis-related ED visit. Increasing CCI was associated with increased odds of this outcome (OR = 1.09 (95% CI = 1.06–1.13) p < 0.001) (Table 4b).

Finally, examining odds of repeat antibiotic prescriptions compared to 7-day prescriptions, those without prescriptions had significantly reduced odds of subsequent antibiotic use (OR = 0.70 (95% CI = 0.66–0.74) p < 0.001), although so did those with 10-day courses (OR = 0.90 (95% CI = 0.86–0.93) p < 0.001) This finding was not observed in patients with 11 + day courses (OR 0.95 (95% CI 0.88–1.02), p = 0.15). Once again, increasing CCI (OR = 1.13 (95% CI = 01.11–1.15) p < 0.001) and age (OR = 1.10 (95% CI = 1.08–1.12) p < 0.001) were each associated with increased odds of a repeat antibiotic prescription (Table 4c).

Discussion

In this large, retrospective claims-based cohort, we find favorable outcomes in individuals managed with antibiotic-sparing approaches, recapitulating evidence that has been incorporated into society guidelines [9, 10, 13]. Despite this evidence base, we report that it is still a minority of individuals who are being managed with an antibiotic-sparing approach – only about 15% in these data. The rationale behind this is likely multifaceted and driven by both patient and provider factors. In a mixed methods survey attempting to identify barriers to trial enrollment for antibiotic-sparing approaches to diverticulitis, a major theme that emerged was hesitancy to undergo a treatment that did not involve antibiotics, based on a belief that antibiotics were “necessary for recovery;” conversely, some patients espoused a hesitancy to take antibiotics, or medications in general [14]. Regarding provider factors, in a survey of Canadian providers, including both general surgeons and ED providers, 42% reported that they “never” omit antibiotics for uncomplicated diverticulitis, citing discomfort with this modality and/or a lack of awareness of updated guidelines [15]. Overall, changes in both patient and provider attitudes are necessary if adoption of antibiotic-sparing approaches is to become widespread.

Despite enthusiasm in the literature for antibiotic-sparing approaches, available data have rather limited generalizability amongst the US population, with its high prevalence of obesity and comorbidity burden [12]. Specifically, individuals with more comorbidities appear to have higher rates of treatment failure with antibiotic-sparing approaches [11]. This is concerning given the high comorbidity burden in the US population, especially as diverticulitis incidence is evolving toward a younger, but more comorbid population [16]. Further, while obesity has been strongly linked both to development of diverticulitis and to worse diverticulitis-related outcomes, data regarding antibiotic-sparing management has been limited in obese individuals, with the median body mass index in randomized trials being 26 kg/m2 [11, 17]. Further, results from a recent Cochrane review suggest that there may be benefits to antibiosis that have not been captured with existing literature, given the wide confidence intervals in reported data [12].

While we are limited in our ability to assess the impact of obesity with the available MarketScan data, we were able to assess comorbidities. In our adjusted model, with the exception of surgery, all outcomes of interest had significantly higher odds of occurring with increased CCI. While this is not unexpected, it highlights that patient selection is particularly important when considering antibiotic-sparing approaches to diverticulitis, and further cautions this strategy in comorbid individuals. Of note, the same pattern is appreciated with age: odds of each outcome of interest (except for repeat diverticulitis-related ED visits) increased significantly with increasing age, even after adjusting for comorbidities. Despite the high prevalence of diverticulitis in older adults, studies examining antibiotic sparing approaches are limited in this population [18, 19]. The association of age with adverse outcomes further cautions the use of antibiotic sparing approaches in this population and suggests further examination of this strategy in older, comorbid populations. Overall, the generalizability of antibiotic-sparing approaches appears to be somewhat limited.

While optimal timing of antibiotic management in individuals who have undergone source control has been established, duration of antibiotic use in those with uncomplicated disease remains obscure [20]. Here, we report that longer durations of antibiotic use are generally not associated with improved outcomes in terms of subsequent diverticulitis related ED visits, admissions, or subsequent antibiotic use, compared to shorter durations. These findings are likely multifactorial. Although we lack objective data regarding severity of disease, those with longer courses may represent individuals who providers deemed to have more severe disease, or were otherwise at higher risk. However, the fact that all patients were managed on an outpatient basis suggests a limited disease severity, which we incorporated into our inclusion criteria. While this is a limitation of the study, if this selection bias is present then it still supports the notion that for patients who do not appear to have severe disease, shorter regimens of antibiotics are likely adequate (if needed at all). This is particularly supported by the successes observed within the no-antibiotic group. Moreover, it may be surmised that many patients do not complete their full courses of antibiotics, which would then bias the results towards the null. A prospective study designed to look at this hypothesis within a more consistent cohort is warranted.

Alternatively, it may be the case that longer durations of antibiotics are actually related to worse outcomes. Our observation that shorter courses of antibiotics in diverticulitis are associated with acceptable outcomes, when compared to longer courses, is of significant potential benefit. Longer antibiotic durations have been significantly associated with higher risk of Clostridioides difficile infections [21], increased microbiota disruption [22], and the development of antibiotic resistance [23]. Each of these represent significant potential for disease burden on patients and dramatic increases in health-care expenditure. If reducing antibiotic duration may mitigate these deleterious outcomes, without increasing adverse diverticulitis-related outcomes, there is a valid need to pursue this management strategy. This also supports a general trend towards shorter duration of antibiotic therapy across disciplines [24]. This, combined with a growing appreciation that diverticulitis may have a significant inflammatory component, versus an infectious one alone [25], again argues for antibiotic avoidance or shorter courses of antibiotics in appropriately selected individuals.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Despite its large size, it is retrospective and non-randomized, and therefore subject to limitations inherent in studies of this nature. Further, in this cohort we are unable to assess why antibiotics were or were not prescribed, and whether this was the result of provider judgement or other factors. As such, antibiotic prescriptions, or longer durations of prescriptions, might represent a surrogate marker for more severe disease that we are unable to account for, which may influence our findings. As this database captured only reimbursed prescriptions, a proportion of patients who did not receive antibiotics may have been prescribed them, but for unknown reasons did not fill this prescription. Further, data may be subject to a diluting effect as we are unable to assess if individuals who filled a prescription actually completed any or all of their prescribed course.

While we report significant differences between groups for repeat ED-visits as well as subsequent antibiotic prescriptions, the absolute difference between groups is rather small: 1.9% and 5.8% respectively. Though these findings are statistically significant, the corresponding clinical impact may be small. Further prospective work may be needed to delineate the clinical meaning of findings with greater certainty.

Additionally, while we have attempted to control for baseline differences between groups with adjusted analysis, unaccounted for differences may partially explain observed results, including aforementioned differences in disease severity. Finally, we are limited to patients within the US who have commercial insurance, and unable to assess those patients who are uninsured or using government-sponsored insurance, limiting generalizability both within the US as well as to other countries that utilize single-payer systems.

Conclusions

With some institutions adopting a no-antibiotics approach to patients with acute diverticulitis, our data suggest comparable or even improved outcomes in patients who are selected for this approach. Among those who receive antibiotics, shorter- vs. longer-durations of treatment do not seem to reduce episodes of recurrent disease. Indeed, those given longer courses were more likely to require eventual surgery than those with shorter durations. These data support institutional consideration for antibiotic-sparing protocols in appropriately selected patients with diverticulitis. Further, they suggest that longer courses of antibiotics are associated with worse outcomes, including higher rates of subsequent antibiotic use and surgery. Finally, there is a persistent need for investigation into obese, older, and comorbid individuals and the viability of antibiotic sparing approaches in these cohorts that increasingly represent the US population.
