Causal role of immune cells in muscle atrophy: mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xing Yu, Xiaojun Chen, Yunyun Su, Huibin Tang, Liangdi Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune system and inflammation had a great influence on the progression of muscle atrophy. However, the causal relationship with specific immune cell traits remained uncertain. The aim of this study was to elucidate the genetic influences on these associations, providing insights into the underlying mechanisms of muscle atrophy. A bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted to investigate the causal relationship between immune cell phenotypes and muscle atrophy. Data on immune cell phenotypes were obtained from a research cohort containing data on 731 immune cell phenotypes and data on muscle atrophy were sourced from a Finnish database. MR analysis was performed using the MR-Egger method, weighted median, inverse variance weighting, heterogeneity testing, sensitivity analysis, and multiplicity analysis, with results subjected to false discovery rate(FDR) correction. Additionally, the UK Biobank cohort was utilized as an external validation. A total of 31 immune phenotypes with causal relationships with muscle atrophy were identified, including various phenotypes of conventional dendritic cells, myeloid cells, T cells/B cells/natural killer cells, regulatory cells, and T cell maturation stages. Among them, 12 immune phenotypes were identified as exhibiting a positive causal relationship with muscle atrophy, while 19 immune phenotypes were demonstrated to have a negative causal association, highlighting the complex interactions between immune cells and muscle health. The results of the reverse MR analysis indicated that a negative correlation between muscle atrophy and CD28 on secreting Treg (OR = 0.9038, 95%CI:0.8308 ~ 0.9832, P = 0.0186). A significant positive correlation was revealed by external datasets between the CD25 on IgD + CD38- immune phenotype and the risk of muscle atrophy, which was consistent with the trend observed in the training group (OR = 1.1041, 95% CI: 1.1005–1.1076, P = 0.0263). No evidence of pleiotropy was observed, and the reliability of these findings was demonstrated by the leave-one-out analysis. The findings highlight significant correlations between certain immune cell features and muscle atrophy, providing potential targets for further investigation of immunological mechanisms and therapeutic interventions for this condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76828-6.Keywords: Muscle atrophy, Immune Cell features, Mendelian randomization, Genetic analysis, GWAS, ImmunologySubject terms: Genetic association study, Inflammasome

Introduction

Muscle atrophy, characterized by significant loss of muscle mass and strength, which was a natural result of aging and a common feature of various chronic diseases1,2. The pathophysiology of muscle atrophy was complex, mainly involving the regulation of protein synthesis, degradation, and the maintained of muscle homeostasis3. Exercise was the most effective treatment, but it was only suitable for some patients. Several compounds targeting muscle atrophy underwent clinical trials, but they were not commercialized. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the relevant mechanisms of muscle atrophy was very important for innovative treatment strategies4.

Previous studies revealed the role of the immune system and inflammation in driving muscle atrophy. Muscle atrophy and immune aging (including the accumulation of aging T cells, impaired function of natural killer (NK) cells, macrophages and neutrophils, and defective functional response of lymphocytes) may be related/interacted through skeletal muscle5. Inflammatory cytokines contributed to muscle atrophy by interacting with the immune system, promoting a pro-inflammatory environment, and disrupting the balance of oxidative stress6. In China patient cohort, high levels of the inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-like weak inducer of apoptosis and TNF-α were associated with muscle atrophy7. Moreover, elevated levels of TNF-α, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-6 were observed in muscle atrophy patients, which subsequently declined after nutrition support and exercise interventions8. However, the relationship between immune inflammation and muscle atrophy was inconsistent in the current research, which may be due to potential confounding factors9,10. Therefore, it was important to understand the complex relationship between the functioning of immune system and muscle atrophy.

Mendelian randomization (MR) was a powerful technique in epidemiology that employed genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationship, which avoided the problems of reverse causality and confounding factors in observational studies11,12. MR analysis was also utilized to assess the causal relationship between modifiable exposures/biomarkers and muscle atrophy. For instance, a MR analysis found an L-shaped association between serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration and the risk of muscle atrophy13. Another bi-directional MR study suggested a significant mutual causal effect between type 2 diabetes mellitus and muscle atrophy14. In our study, a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis and external validation were employed to comprehensively investigate the causal relationship between immune cell phenotypes, including different cell types and immune markers, and the risk of muscle atrophy. Our primary objective was to gain a better understanding of the intricate interplay between muscle atrophy and the immune system, and to identify potential immunotherapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Research design

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) reporting guidelines. The causal relationship between 731 immune cell traits (across 7 groups) and muscle atrophy were assessed using a two-sample MR analysis. Additionally, genome-wide association study (GWAS) data was employed for heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis, and external validation. In this analysis, genetic variants were employed as IVs to represent risk factors, necessitating that effective IVs in causal inference satisfy three key assumptions15: (1) the exposure is directly related to genetic variation; (2) there is no genetically-linked confounding factor between exposure and outcome; and (3) genetic variation influences the outcome only through the exposure factor, excluding other pathways. The overall design is shown in Fig. 1.

Overview of the overall design. Assumption 1, direct association between genetic variants and the exposure; Assumption 2, no relationship between genetic variants and confounders between the exposure and the outcome; Assumption 3, no influence of genetic variants on the outcome through pathways other than the exposure. *Abbreviations: IVs, instrumental variables; SNPs, single nucleotide polymorphisms; MR, Mendelian randomization; IVW, inverse variance weighting; LOO, leave-one-out; MR-PRESSO, MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier.

Data sources

The comprehensive GWAS summary data for each immune feature were publicly accessible from the GWAS catalog under registration numbers GCST90001391 to GCST9000212116. This dataset included a total of 731 immune phenotypes, which consisted of absolute cell counts (AC) (n = 118), median fluorescence intensity reflecting surface antigen levels (MFI) (n = 389), relative cell counts (RC) (n = 192), and morphological parameters (MP) (n = 32). Specifically, the AC, MFI, and RC features encompass B cells, conventional dendritic cells (cDCs), mature T cells, monocytes, bone marrow cells, T cells/B cells/NK cells (TBNK), and regulatory (Treg) cells, while MP features include cDCs and TBNK. The initial GWAS for immune features utilized data from 3,757 individuals of European descent with no overlapping data. Approximately 22 million high-density array genotypes of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were computed using a reference panel based on the Sardinian population sequence, and correlation was examined after adjusting for covariates (i.e., gender, age, and age)17.

Data for muscle atrophy were obtained from the Finnish database, which consisted of a GWAS study involving 275,375 individuals of European descent. For external validation, appendicular lean mass (ALM) was selected as an indicator of muscle mass, recognized as a key component of muscle atrophy18. Genetic instruments for ALM (n = 450,243) were derived from the largest GWASs of the UK Biobank, where participants were aged between 48 and 73 years at recruitment. ALM was quantified as the sum of fat-free mass of arms and legs measured by BIA19.

Instrumental variables selection

The significance level for IVs for each immune feature was set at 1 × 10−5, considering the direct correlation between genetic variants and exposure. To obtain independent IVs at distinct loci, the TwoSampleMR package data was utilized, with a linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold set at R2 < 0.001 and an aggregation distance of 10,000 kb20. For muscle atrophy, the significance level was adjusted to 1 × 10−5, which is commonly used to denote genome-wide significance in GWAS. The LD threshold remained at R2 < 0.001, and the aggregation distance was set to 10,000 kb21.

Statistical analysis

Causal relationship between immune cell traits and muscle atrophy risk were assessed using R software (version 4.2.1) with the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7) and MRPRESSO package (version 1.0). The inverse variance weighting (IVW) method was primarily employed to establish causal relationship, with additional utilization of the methods such as weighted median and MR-Egger to ensure the stability of the results. The IVW method combined Wald estimates from multiple genetic variants, weighted by the inverse variance of each SNP outcome association, providing strong ability in determining causality22. If the results from weighted median and MR‐Egger were not statistically significant(P > 0.05), but the IVW results were significant and consistent with other methods, they were considered indicative of significance23. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to validate the robustness of the results. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, while horizontal pleiotropy was examined through MR‐Egger regression and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO). The influence of individual SNPs was evaluated via leave‐one‐out analysis24. Furthermore, a reverse MR analysis was conducted to determine the direction of causality. In addition, taking into account the issue of multiple testing, A false discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed using the online tool Bioladder. An FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant causal relationship. Finally, funnel plots and scatter plots were utilized. The scatter plots were shown to demonstrate minimal influence of outliers on the data, while the funnel plots were exhibited to show high correlation and an absence of heterogeneity.

Results

Exploration of causal relationship between immune phenotypes and muscle atrophy risk

Two-sample MR analysis was employed to investigate the causal relationship between immune phenotypes and muscle atrophy. The primary analytical approach employed was IVW method, which was complemented by other methods. At a significance level of 0.05, a total of 31 immune phenotypes were identified to have causal relationship with the risk of muscle atrophy. These included 10 from the B cell group, 8 from the Treg cell group, 4 from the TBNK cell group, 4 from the Myeloid cell group, 3 from the Maturation stages of T cell group, and 2 from the cDC cell group (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S1).

During the validation phase in the UK Biobank cohort, the MR-IVW results indicated (Table 1) a significant positive correlation between CD25on IgD + CD38-(OR = 1.1041, 95% CI: 1.1005–1.1076, P = 0.0263) and the risk of muscle atrophy. Scatter plots and forest plots for the validation group were plotted, revealing a similar trend of influence on muscle atrophy by immune phenotypes compared to the training group, further supporting our findings (Fig. 3). Additionally, a “leave-one-out” sensitivity analysis was conducted on both the training and validation groups to assess the impact of each SNP on the overall causal relationship (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figure S2). The results revealed no significant differences when excluding any single SNP (Supplementary Figure S2). MR-Egger intercept ruled out the possibility of horizontal pleiotropy, and the Cochran Q test demonstrated no heterogeneity across all results in both the training and validation groups (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, the stability of our findings was illustrated by scatter plots and funnel plots (Supplementary Figure S3 and Figure S4).

Discussion

In this study, we employed a two-sample MR analysis and examined the causal relationship between 731 distinct immune cell phenotypes and the risk of muscle atrophy. Notably, our study was the first to utilize MR analysis to identify the causal relationship between 31 immune phenotypes and muscle atrophy. The observed results revealed both positive and negative causal relationship between specific immune phenotypes and the development of muscle atrophy. These novel findings shed new perspectives on the targeting of immune pathways to mitigate muscle atrophy, indicating that immunomodulation was poised to emerge as a therapeutic strategy against muscle atrophy.

Firstly, our study provided evidence of the correlation between B cell phenotypes and the risk of muscle atrophy. We observed a significant positive correlation between CD25 on IgD + CD38- and the risk of muscle atrophy during the validation phase, a finding that had not been previously reported and warranted further investigation. In addition, our results indicated that an increased percentage of IgD-CD27- %lymphocyte was associated with an elevated risk of muscle atrophy. Previous research showed that IgD- CD27- double negative B cells was related to low-grade chronic inflammatory disorders, including aging25. This can be explained by the involvement of B cells in muscle inflammation and injury responses through cytokine secretion and antigen presentation26. In particular, memory B cell produced elevated levels of the IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, contributing to the inflammatory environment associated with atrophy in the elderly27. Additionally, specific subpopulations of B cell activated proteolytic pathways in muscle, leading to atrophy. Dysregulated B cell activation also disrupted muscle regeneration28. Our findings aligned with a recent RNA-seq analysis of the immune-related transcriptional regulatory network, which highlighted the involvement of B cells in the PAX5-SERPINB5-PI3K/Akt signal transduction29. Overall, these findings underscored the involvement of B cell pathways in the pathogenesis of muscle atrophy, while further research was needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of different B cell subpopulations.

Next, we identified various Treg phenotypes associated with the risk of muscle atrophy, and CD39 + activated Tregs showed protection against muscle atrophy. This genetic evidence reinforced the effects of Tregs and unveiled exciting possibilities for Tregs therapies in alleviating muscle atrophy. This inference was supported by the well-established functions of Tregs, whereby they acted as potent regulators of inflammation, effectively suppressing muscle inflammation, and promoting tissue regeneration30. Previous studies also indicated that the modulation of Tregs function ameliorated muscle atrophy. For instance, Tregs function depended on IL-33 signaling, which was impaired in older animals. However, IL-33 injection rescued Tregs function and improved muscle regeneration31. Our findings prompted interventions targeting Tregs as potential therapeutics for muscle atrophy. However, further research was warranted to elucidate the diverse causal relationship between various Treg phenotypes and muscle atrophy.

In addition, our study discovered a positive association between naive CD8 + T cell phenotypes and the risk of muscle atrophy. The findings provided population-level genetic evidence supporting the crucial role of CD8 + T cells in muscle atrophy. Previous studies had implicated cytotoxic CD8 + T cells in muscle damage, particularly in conditions like polymyositis/inclusion body myositis32. In those conditions, CD8 + T cells directly attacked muscle fibers that expressed MHC class I-presenting antigens, resulting in the release of cytotoxic molecules, tissue destruction, and the release of autoantigens33. Furthermore, CD8 + T cells contributed to muscle atrophy by secreting pro-inflammatory and catabolic factors such as IFN-γ, perforin-1, and granzyme B34. Additionally, studies found a correlation between the infiltration of CD8 + T cells and muscle atrophy in colorectal cancer. Importantly, the adoptive transfer of CD8 + T cells isolated from colorectal cancer mice had led to muscle wasting in the recipients35. These findings highlighted the importance of CD8 + T cells in muscle atrophy, with targeting CD8 + T cells emerging as a promising treatment strategy. Further research was needed to gain a better understanding of the specific role of CD8 + T cell subpopulations in muscle atrophy.

In this study, we also conducted external validation using the UK Biobank database. The results showed that only one phenotype, CD25 + CD38- on IgD, was replicated in the UK Biobank validation cohort. While this may appear limited, replication in MR studies can be challenging. We further analyzed the potential reasons for the limited reproducibility: (1) Differences in population and cohort characteristics: The main study and validation cohort were drawn from different populations with distinct genetic backgrounds. The main study utilized a Finnish database, while the UK Biobank consists of a broader UK-based population. These differences in population structure, environmental factors, and genetic variability can influence immune cell phenotypes and muscle atrophy risk, thereby affecting replication success. (2) Sample size and statistical power: The sample size for the immune cell phenotypes in the UK Biobank was smaller compared to the original research cohort, reducing statistical power and the likelihood of detecting weaker causal relationships. It is possible that some immune phenotypes identified in the main study may require larger validation datasets to achieve statistical significance.3) Phenotype definition and heterogeneity: Immune cell phenotypes are complex traits that may have been defined or measured slightly differently across the two cohorts, introducing potential heterogeneity in the results. Despite this, we applied rigorous statistical approaches, including sensitivity analyses and FDR correction, to mitigate these issues as much as possible. While the replication of only one immune phenotype, CD25 on IgD + CD38-, may seem limited, it was worth noting that this phenotype showed a robust and significant causal association with muscle atrophy in both cohorts. This consistent finding strengthened its biological relevance and highlighted its potential as a key immune regulator of muscle atrophy. We also acknowledge that the limited replication of other phenotypes underscores the complexity of immune-mediation in muscle health, and we believe that further studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate these findings comprehensively.

We also need to pay attention to the five pitfalls of MR analysis36. Our research mitigated these potential challenges by taking the following measures. Firstly, we focused on investigating the causal role of immune cell phenotypes in muscle atrophy, a clearly defined research question that was highly relevant in the field of immunology and muscle degeneration. By utilizing publicly available GWAS datasets and conducting a bidirectional MR analysis, we have ensured that the causal relationships between these traits were examined in a well-grounded biological context. The involvement of immune cells in muscle atrophy was supported by previous mechanistic studies5, validating the appropriateness of our research question. Secondly, in line with Burgess et al.’s recommendation36, we carefully selected instrumental variables based on strict genome-wide significance (p < 1 × 10⁻⁵) and linkage disequilibrium thresholds (R² < 0.001). These IVs were chosen to ensure robust association with the exposures (immune cell phenotypes) while minimizing pleiotropy. These measures helped guarantee that the IVs used in our analysis had a robust and direct relationship with the exposure. Moreover, we applied multiple MR approaches, including IVW, MR-Egger, and weighted median, to account for potential biases introduced by instrument selection. The consistency of results across these methods, provided strong evidence that the genetic variants influenced muscle atrophy risk primarily through immune cell phenotypes, without acting through alternative pathways. Additionally, the absence of significant outliers in leave-one-out analysis further supported the validity of this assumption. Thirdly, to interrogate the robustness of our findings, we performed a range of sensitivity analyses, including heterogeneity tests (Cochran’s Q), pleiotropy checks (MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO), and leave-one-out analyses. These approaches allowed us to confirm the consistency and reliability of our results, addressing the concern raised by Burgess et al. about inadequate validation of MR findings36. The absence of directional pleiotropy in our analysis, as evidenced by non-significant MR-Egger intercepts and the robustness of MR-PRESSO results, indicated that the genetic variants used were not associated with confounders that could bias the causal inference. Fourthly, we have been cautious in interpreting our results. For example, we distinguished between causal relationships and correlations, carefully considering the biological mechanisms that may underlie our findings. Our study also included a bidirectional MR analysis to test for reverse causality, ensuring that we did not overstate the directionality of the observed associations. Additionally, we have been mindful of the statistical significance thresholds and corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR, as recommended in MR literature. Fifthly, our study was built on the foundations laid by prior research, including the work by Burgess et al.36. We have cited relevant literature extensively, ensuring that our research is framed within the broader context of Mendelian randomization and immune-related muscle atrophy. Moreover, by incorporating external validation cohorts, such as the UK Biobank, we demonstrated our commitment to cross-referencing and engaged with available data sources, which strengthened the credibility of our findings.

In summary, we have taken careful measures to address the pitfalls highlighted by Burgess et al.36. Our study had a clear and appropriate research question, robust instrumental variable selection, thorough sensitivity analyses, cautious interpretation, and strong engagement with previous work. Additionally, from a clinical perspective, our results were of great significance. Immunological analysis can better identify individuals at risk of muscular atrophy and implement targeted preventive strategies to optimize immune function7,37,38. However, there were some limitations in this study. The GWAS samples were predominantly of European descent, necessitating confirmation in diverse populations39. In addition, MR could not account for all confounders, and further experimental validation was necessary to substantiate the biological underpinnings11. Overall, our research provided strong genetic evidence linking immune phenotypes to the risk of muscle atrophy.

Conclusions

In short, through comprehensive two-sample MR analysis, we revealed the strong causal relationship between specific immunophenotype and muscle atrophy. This discovery deepened our understanding of the intricate interplay between the immune system and muscle atrophy. Furthermore, our research provided a foundation for further investigating the underlying immune mechanisms driving muscle atrophy, offering promising way for developing targeted preventive and early intervention strategies.
