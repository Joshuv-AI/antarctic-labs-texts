Prevalence and Risk Factors of Heat-Related Illness Among Pilgrims During the Hajj Season 2024: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Elbagir A Elfaki, Hatem A Sembawa, Ahmed Hassan Badrek Alamodi, Nahla H Hariri, Majed S Obaid, Amar Mohammad A Alkhotani, Ismail A Alghamdi, Muhammad irfanullah Siddiqui, Heba M Adly, Mohammed A Garout, Adeeb A Bulkhi, Hani M Almoallim, Altaf Ahmed Abdulkhaliq, Saleh A K Saleh, Aous S Alhazmi, Abdulrahman T Taeyb, Mohammed J Basnawi, Ammar A AbuSeer, Hassan H Abu Rokbah, Jaffar A Al-Tawfiq, Maher N Alandiyjany, Rania Abubaker Bagabir, Nizar S Bawahab, Ghadah Alsaleh, Hala Aljishi, Fahad A Alamri, Anas A Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeat-related illnesses (HRIs) remain a significant health concern during mass gatherings (MGs), where high temperatures and physical exertion exacerbate physiological stress. This study examines the clinical spectrum, management practices, and treatment outcomes of HRIs among pilgrims during the 2024 Hajj season, to inform public health responses and mitigation strategies.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted across nine healthcare facilities in Makkah and the Mashaer region during the 2024 Hajj. Data were retrospectively extracted from medical health care facilities registries using a standardized form to capture demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, management interventions, and in-hospital outcomes of patients diagnosed with heat-related illness.ResultsOf the 2,785 screened patients, 2,551 had complete clinical records, and 1,576 met the diagnostic criteria for heat exhaustion or heatstroke, with outcome data available. Most of the patients were male (1,171; 74.3%), with nearly half aged 60 years or older (776; 49.2%) and a large proportion aged 39–59 years (693; 44.0%). The majority were non-Saudi pilgrims (1,419; 90%), primarily from Arab countries, with 793 (50.3%) from these regions. A total of 1,094 patients (69.4%) had no prior history of HRIs. Common symptoms included altered mental status in 635 (40.3%) patients, dizziness in 486 (30.8%), and management interventions included intravenous fluid administration in 569 cases (36.1%) and the application of cooling measures in emergency settings for 1,105 patients (70.1%). The overall mortality rate was 77 (4.9%) cases. Intensive care unit (ICU) admission and elevated lactic acid levels were significantly associated with poor outcomes.ConclusionHeat-related illness during the Hajj remains a major public health concern, with a substantial clinical burden and potentially severe outcomes. Early recognition, timely intervention, and targeted education are essential for high-risk groups. Strengthening frontline preparedness and resource deployment is crucial to reducing its potential impact.Keywords: Heat-Related Illnesses, Heatstroke, Pilgrimage Health, Mass Gatherings, Hajj

Introduction

Heat-related illnesses (HRIs) represent a significant global health challenge, particularly as climate change continues to drive temperatures higher. Between 2000 and 2019, an estimated 489,000 heat-related deaths occurred each year, with extreme events like the 2003 European heatwave accounting for over 70,000 fatalities [1]. In 2022, Europe reported nearly 62,000 heat-related deaths an increase of 30% compared to two decades earlier [2]. Likewise, the United States experienced a 63% rise in heat-related deaths between 1999 and 2023 [3].

Mass gatherings (MGs), particularly those held in hot climates, such as the annual Hajj pilgrimage to Makkah, Saudi Arabia, increase these risks due to the convergence of large, diverse populations in environments with heightened environmental stressors [4, 5]. Hajj, the most significant, consistent, and longest-standing annual Muslim pilgrimage in the world, draws around 2 million pilgrims from over 186 countries [6, 7]. These individuals, with diverse ethnicities, medical backgrounds, and social circumstances, travel to Makkah to perform their rituals. Hajj takes place from the 8th to the 13th of Dhul-Hijjah, the final month of the Islamic lunar calendar. Since the lunar calendar shifts each year, Hajj progressively occurs during the hotter months of the Gregorian calendar, typically between June and September in Makkah, when ambient temperatures are at their highest, thereby heightening the risk of heat-related illnesses (HRIs). The city’s harsh desert climate presents significant challenges, particularly for pilgrims who are not accustomed to extreme heat conditions [8, 9].

Global climate change has significant impacts on major international events, including the Olympic Games and the FIFA World Cup [2, 3]. Recent evidence indicates that global climate change, with its associated rise in ambient temperatures and altered weather patterns, is likely to heighten the risk of HRIs during MGs [10, 11]. Experience gained from managing HRIs during the Hajj offers important guidance for developing preventive strategies and response plans in comparable events worldwide.

During the Hajj of 2023, Saudi authorities reported 1,301 deaths during a period of intense heat waves [12]. Despite Saudi Arabia’s implementation of extensive preventive measures, this unprecedented number of fatalities underscored the severity of the issue and the need for enhanced strategies to protect pilgrims. Saudi Arabia has implemented multiple preventive strategies to mitigate heat-related risks during Hajj. These include coordinated public health awareness campaigns, real-time heat advisories, expansion of shaded infrastructure in ritual sites, provision of water distribution points and cooling mist systems, structured crowd flow management, and deployment of temporary healthcare facilities with rapid response teams. The Ministry of Health also conducts pre-Hajj health planning and surveillance initiatives aimed at reducing heat-related morbidity and mortality during peak ritual days [12–14]. Hajj presents a unique opportunity to assess heat-related mortality risks in transient populations compared to year-round residents of Makkah [13, 14]. Although heat-related illness has been reported during previous Hajj seasons, many available studies were limited to single-center analyses or descriptive reports. The 2024 Hajj coincided with documented extreme environmental heat exposure during peak ritual days, providing an opportunity to examine multicenter facility-based data under high thermal stress conditions. By incorporating data from nine healthcare facilities and evaluating clinical predictors of in-hospital outcomes, the present study contributes to strengthening surveillance and risk stratification efforts in mass gathering settings [12–14].

HRIs encompass a spectrum of conditions resulting from thermoregulatory failure due to excessive exposure to environmental heat. These range from mild symptoms, such as heat rash and muscle cramps, to moderate heat exhaustion, and severe and life-threatening conditions of heatstroke [15, 16]. Although the Saudi Ministry of Health (MoH) issued updated guidelines for managing HRIs [17], substantial research gaps persist. Most available reports are descriptive and single-center in nature, with limited multicenter data integrating clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, management practices, and patient outcomes. In addition, few studies have examined predictors of in-hospital mortality or evaluated how surveillance data can inform risk stratification during mass gatherings. Broader global discussions on climate-related health risks at mass gatherings have also emphasized the need for integrated preparedness frameworks linking meteorological monitoring, clinical triage, and early intervention strategies [17]. These gaps underscore the importance of systematic, facility-based analyses to strengthen evidence-informed planning and response.

The Hajj serves as a valuable case study for addressing heat-related health risks. It highlights the need for strong public health approaches and innovative strategies to mitigate risks associated with extreme heat. This study examines the frequency, clinical features, and outcomes of heat-related illness among pilgrims during the 2024 Hajj season and identifies predictors of morbidity and mortality to inform prevention and clinical management strategies.

Methodology

Study Setting

This study targeted the 2024 Hajj season, capturing patients from June 14 to June 18, across nine healthcare facilities located in Mena and Arafat (Ritual Sites). These sites provided care for pilgrims presenting with HRIs, specifically heat exhaustion and heatstroke. Meteorological conditions during the study period (June 14–18, 2024) were characterized by high ambient temperatures and low relative humidity. According to forecasts issued by the Saudi National Center for Meteorology (NCM) [18], the pilgrimage season in Makkah and adjacent sacred sites was expected to experience above-normal temperatures, with average daily maximums of approximately 44 °C, relative humidity near 25%, and minimal rainfall [19]. During the peak ritual days, local meteorological reporting indicated forecasted maximum temperatures reaching up to 49 °C in Makkah and the holy sites [20]. These environmental conditions are consistent with hazardous heat exposure and are known risk factors for heat-related illness in outdoor mass gatherings.

Study Design

A cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate demographic characteristics, clinical features, and management practices associated with heat-related illness.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria encompassed all patients presenting with heat-related illness during the study period, including pilgrims who sought medical care at participating health facilities of any age and sex who were diagnosed with heat exhaustion or heatstroke based on standard clinical definitions. Heat exhaustion was defined by symptoms such as weakness or dizziness, while body temperature was < 40 °C. Heatstroke was characterized by a core body temperature exceeding 40 °C accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction.

Data Collection

A structured data collection form was used to systematically extract relevant data from patient registries during Hajj 2024 (June 14–19, 2024, corresponding to 8–13 Dhul Hijjah 1445 AH, (Hegirae, Islamic calendar year). The data collectors, who were trained physicians and health professionals, underwent targeted orientation sessions to ensure standardized data abstraction. The collected variables spanned five domains: demographic characteristics (age, sex, and camp cluster), clinical presentation (pre-existing conditions, presenting symptoms, and vital signs), therapeutic interventions (airway management, fluid therapy, and cooling techniques), and outcomes.

Core temperature was measured at presentation as part of routine clinical assessment and was incorporated into physician-based classification of heat exhaustion and heatstroke. However, temperature values were not consistently transcribed into the structured registry dataset for all patients, resulting in incomplete documentation in 662 cases (42.0%), particularly during periods of high clinical workload.

The dataset reflects only individuals who were evaluated and documented within these participating healthcare facilities. Cases managed exclusively at peripheral medical posts, those who did not seek medical care, or patients who died prior to hospital arrival would not have been captured within the current registry. Accordingly, the dataset represents facility-based case ascertainment rather than complete population-level surveillance.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee at Umm Al-Qura University (Approval No. HAPO-02-K-012-2025-09-2860). This study was based on a retrospective review of data obtained from the patient registry. No direct patient contact was involved, and informed consent was not required. All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the data: numeric variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Inferential statistical analyses included t-tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical data, as appropriate. To identify predictors of mortality, univariate logistic regression analysis was performed using in-hospital death (yes/no) as the dependent variable. Clinically relevant variables reflecting age and organ dysfunction (age ≥ 60 years, creatinine > 130 µmol/L, lactic acid > 2.5 mmol/L, ICU admission, and ALT > 200 U/L) were evaluated individually. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Due to limitations in the available dataset and incomplete covariate structure, multivariable logistic regression modeling was not conducted. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a p-value ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. Assumptions for each test were checked before analysis, and any violations were addressed appropriately.

ICU admission was the strongest predictor of mortality (Adjusted OR = 4.5; 95% CI: 3.1–6.8; p < 0.001), followed by elevated lactic acid (> 2.5 mmol/L) (Adjusted OR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.4–3.7; p = 0.001). Overall, older age, ICU admission, and elevated laboratory markers including lactic acid and creatinine were the strongest predictors of mortality among pilgrims diagnosed with heat-related illnesses.

Discussion

We found that heat-related illness among pilgrims during the Hajj season 2024 in Makkah, Saudi Arabia was associated with several clinical predictors of poor outcomes. Older age (≥ 60 years), elevated lactic acid levels, elevated creatinine, and ICU admission were significantly associated with increased mortality. The analysis included 1,576 documented cases across nine healthcare facilities located in the Hajj ritual sites, providing an overview of the clinical presentation and outcomes of heat-related illness during the pilgrimage. Most patients were aged 39 years or older, representing more than 80% of the study population. This age distribution and the observed proportion of female patients differ from earlier observations reported by Alkassas et al. [21], which may reflect changing demographic patterns among Hajj pilgrims or possible sex-related differences in thermoregulation and health-seeking behavior during extreme heat exposure [22].

Older pilgrims were the most affected group, which is consistent with previous studies identifying age as an important risk factor for heat-related illness during mass gatherings [23]. Interestingly, most patients in this cohort had no documented chronic medical conditions, indicating that severe heat illness can also occur in otherwise healthy individuals when exposed to extreme environmental temperatures [21, 24]. The recurrence of heat-related illness in this setting appears higher than that reported in military or athletic populations [23], highlighting the importance of improved pre-travel health education and risk awareness among pilgrims.

Altered mental status was a strong clinical indicator of poor outcome, consistent with previous reports linking central nervous system dysfunction with severe heat-related illness [24, 25]. Among laboratory parameters, elevated lactate levels showed the strongest association with mortality. This finding aligns with earlier studies demonstrating that hyperlactatemia reflects systemic hypoperfusion and is associated with worse prognosis in heatstroke patients [24–31].

Chest radiography was commonly performed but had limited diagnostic value in the absence of respiratory symptoms. Electrocardiography was performed in a relatively small proportion of cases, and ST-segment elevation was the most frequently documented abnormality, similar to findings previously reported by Mimish [30]. In contrast, sinus tachycardia, often described as a common clinical feature of heat-related illness [27], was observed less frequently in this cohort. These findings suggest that ECG evaluation may be particularly useful in older patients or in those presenting with cardiovascular symptoms [28–30].

Early initiation of cooling before hospital arrival remains a key factor in improving survival in patients with severe heat illness. Although 70.1% of patients in this study received cooling measures after hospital arrival, delays in initiating cooling may have contributed to adverse outcomes. Nevertheless, most patients recovered and were discharged (87.9%), and the observed mortality rate of 4.9% was lower than classical mortality estimates reported for untreated heatstroke, which may reach 63% [31–34].

Cooling practices varied across facilities. Water spraying (33.3%), ice packs (21.6%), and wet towels (19.3%) were the most frequently reported cooling approaches. Traditional Ihram clothing, sometimes believed to provide thermal protection, did not appear to significantly reduce the risk of heat illness in vulnerable individuals. This observation is consistent with thermophysiological studies suggesting that cultural clothing may not provide sufficient protection during extreme heat exposure [31, 35]. Because documentation of cooling methods was inconsistent, the available dataset did not allow reliable regression analysis of cooling modality in relation to mortality.

ICU admission and altered mental status were the strongest predictors of mortality in this cohort, consistent with findings from both military and mass-gathering settings [33, 36]. Elevated lactic acid (> 2.5 mmol/L) and creatinine (> 130 µmol/L) were also significantly associated with mortality, indicating systemic metabolic dysfunction. Interestingly, no significant difference in outcomes was observed between patients diagnosed with heat exhaustion and those diagnosed with heatstroke (p = 0.137). This finding suggests that laboratory parameters and clinical indicators may provide better prognostic value than traditional diagnostic classifications.

The relatively low rate of rhabdomyolysis (1.1%) compared with rates reported in exertional cohorts [23, 33] may reflect the different physical demands associated with Hajj rituals, particularly among older pilgrims. However, strong religious motivation may lead some pilgrims to continue physical activity despite early symptoms of heat stress, a behavioral pattern also described in military personnel and competitive athletes [23, 34].

The mortality rate of 4.9% represents outcomes among patients with documented follow-up in participating healthcare facilities and therefore reflects an in-hospital case fatality proportion. This estimate does not include deaths that may have occurred before hospital arrival or cases treated outside the participating facilities.

Public health communication remains essential in this context. Engagement of religious leaders and community health educators may help improve awareness of early symptoms and encourage preventive behaviors among pilgrims [35]. Because pilgrims come from diverse linguistic and socioeconomic backgrounds, communication challenges may occur during clinical encounters. During Hajj, these challenges are partly addressed through multilingual healthcare staff, volunteer interpreters, and structured triage systems coordinated by the Ministry of Health.

Our findings are consistent with global evidence highlighting the increasing health impact of climate change and extreme heat in vulnerable populations living in arid environments [37, 38]. In this context, structured risk assessment tools, such as the Jeddah Tool for mass gatherings, may strengthen preparedness and operational planning [39]. Emerging evidence also supports the use of immersive cooling techniques such as cold or temperate water immersion [40], structured triage systems [41], and rapid first-aid cooling strategies for exertional hyperthermia [41, 42].

Timely cooling remains a critical determinant of survival in heatstroke. International recommendations emphasize that cooling should begin immediately once heatstroke is suspected rather than waiting for hospital transfer. A “cool first, transport second” approach has been associated with improved neurological outcomes and lower mortality. Cold-water immersion remains the most effective method when feasible, while evaporative cooling combined with ice packs may be more practical in high-volume clinical settings [43]. During Hajj, where patient numbers may surge rapidly, strengthening early recognition and rapid cooling capacity at triage points and emergency facilities remains essential.

Although uncommon, severe complications such as disseminated intravascular coagulation have been reported in heatstroke [44]. Behavioral factors, including a strong motivation to complete pilgrimage rituals despite symptoms, may also contribute to delayed medical care and increased risk of severe heat illness [45]. These findings highlight the importance of preventive strategies addressing physiological, behavioral, and environmental risk factors.

International consensus guidelines continue to emphasize rapid cooling and appropriate fluid resuscitation as the cornerstone of heatstroke management [46]. During Hajj, participating healthcare facilities operate under national emergency care protocols aligned with these recommendations. Differences observed in documented cooling practices likely reflect variability in documentation, triage priorities, and clinical severity rather than deviation from recommended management standards [47, 48].

The results of this multicenter analysis have implications for other large gatherings held in hot climates, including international sporting events and outdoor festivals. Identification of high-risk individuals, particularly older adults and those with early laboratory indicators of organ dysfunction, may help guide preventive strategies. Integrating meteorological monitoring with clinical surveillance systems may allow earlier activation of heat-risk mitigation measures during extreme temperature conditions.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to cases documented in nine participating healthcare facilities and therefore represents facility-based surveillance rather than population-level case ascertainment. Individuals who did not seek medical care, were treated in peripheral clinics, or died before hospital arrival were not included in the dataset. Second, missing values occurred in several variables because documentation was sometimes incomplete during periods of high patient volume. In some cases, detailed clinical information could not be obtained because patients presented with altered consciousness. Excluding all incomplete records would have substantially reduced the sample size and introduced potential bias; therefore, analyses were conducted using available data and regression-based imputation was applied where appropriate. Despite these adjustments, residual information bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This study provides a large multicenter assessment of heat-related illness among pilgrims during the Hajj season 2024. Older age, ICU admission, and laboratory indicators of organ dysfunction were significant predictors of mortality. Although most patients recovered with appropriate treatment, the findings highlight the importance of early recognition, rapid cooling, and targeted prevention strategies for vulnerable pilgrims. The findings underscore the urgent need to enhance heat preparedness strategies, including public education, pre-travel screening, and minimizing sunlight exposure during extreme heat. High-risk indicators such as altered mental status, ICU admission, and elevated lactic acid levels must be prioritized in triage and clinical management. Moreover, greater emphasis on preventive measures, especially among the elderly and vulnerable pilgrims, is critical to reducing HRI-related morbidity and mortality. These results should inform future policy, resource allocation, and international guidelines for managing heat risk in large-scale religious and cultural gatherings.
