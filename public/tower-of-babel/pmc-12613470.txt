Integrated transcriptomic and phenotypic analysis reveals tissue-specific regulatory effects of guanidinoacetic acid in cattle
Authors: Yating Wei, Ye Bu, Yushan Li, Fuwen Wang, Halima Jafari, Li Liu, Li Luo, Xinxin Sui, Chuzhao Lei, Fang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuanidinoacetic acid (GAA) is an essential precursor of creatine and plays a vital role in maintaining energy metabolism and muscle function in cattle. However, the mechanisms by which GAA exerts its effects remain unclear. This study used transcriptomic profiling combined with phenotypic analysis to evaluate the impact of dietary GAA supplementation at levels of 0.04%, 0.08% and 0.12% on metabolic pathways in the liver and pectoral muscles of Holstein castrated cattle, linking gene expression changes to key physiological and biochemical traits.ResultsDietary supplementation with GAA activated the AMPK signaling pathway in liver tissue, enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation, and moderately suppressed immune-related pathways (e.g., NF-κB and IL-17), suggesting a balance between energy efficiency and immune regulation. In muscle, GAA promoted creatine biosynthesis, facilitates intramuscular fat deposition via PLIN2, induced muscle fiber remodeling, and triggered a metabolic shift toward glycolytic metabolism. Furthermore, the 0.08% GAA level achieved a coordinated balance between liver energy metabolism and fat deposition in the pectoral muscles. These molecular changes were consistent with phenotypic outcomes, as intramuscular fat content exhibited a significant quadratic increase (P = 0.0049), peaking at 0.08% GAA, and plasma glucose levels increased linearly with supplementation (P < 0.01), indicating improved systemic energy availability.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that GAA exerts dose-dependent and tissue-specific effects on bovine energy metabolism and muscle development, with 0.08% appearing to be the optimal supplementation level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12214-4.Keywords: Guanidinoacetic acid, Transcriptome, Energy metabolism, Fatty acid metabolism, Creatine, Muscle structure

Introduction

GAA is a naturally occurring amino acid derivative and a direct precursor of creatine (CRE) [1]. In mammals, GAA is primarily synthesized in the kidneys and transported via the bloodstream to the liver, where it is converted into CRE [2]. A substantial amount of CRE is stored in skeletal muscle as phosphocreatine, while smaller amounts are found in the brain, liver, and kidneys. Phosphocreatine functions as an energy reservoir, enabling rapid regeneration of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) during short bursts of muscular activity [3]. Animals need a continuous intake of CRE, approximately 67% of which can be synthesized endogenously from glycine and arginine, with the remaining 33% obtained through the diet [4]. CRE is primarily found in animal-derived feed ingredients such as fish meal, poultry by-product meal, and meat and bone meal, whereas plant-based feeds contain little to no CRE [5]. Reliance on animal by-products as a CRE source poses challenges due to fluctuating supply and high costs [6]. In contrast, GAA offers advantages such as greater stability and lower cost, making it a widely used alternative to direct CRE supplementation in livestock and poultry diets [7].

Recent studies have shown that GAA not only participates in CRE synthesis to regulate energy metabolism but is also vital to animal nutrition. Supplementing with GAA can reduce the consumption of glycine and arginine, enabling these amino acids to be used in protein synthesis [8]. Beyond its role in energy metabolism, GAA exerts a broad spectrum of biological effects, including modulation of hormonal signaling, neural function, and arginine metabolism, as well as enhancement of antioxidant capacity [9]. In livestock and poultry, dietary GAA supplementation has been shown to significantly improve growth performance, particularly in species such as poultry [10], cattle [9], and swine [11], highlighting its potential to promote skeletal muscle development and optimize energy utilization.

Although the physiological functions of GAA as a feed additive have been extensively characterized, its optimal supplementation level, tissue- and species-specific effects, and molecular regulatory mechanisms in ruminants remain inadequately defined. To fill these knowledge gaps and provide a scientific basis for the rational use of GAA in animal production, we conducted a multi-dimensional study in Holstein castrated cattle supplemented with graded GAA levels (0, 0.04%, 0.08%, 0.12%). Plasma biochemical parameters (e.g., total protein, glucose, cholesterol) and slaughter performance were analyzed to evaluate metabolic and phenotypic responses. Liver and pectoral muscle transcriptomes were sequenced, including differential gene expression, gene expression pattern clustering, GO/KEGG pathway enrichment, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. By correlating transcriptional signatures with phenotypic data, this study aims to unravel GAA’s regulatory roles in fatty acid metabolism, energy homeostasis, and muscle development, providing a theoretical foundation for optimizing dietary strategies in livestock.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and treatments

This experiment was conducted following strict animal welfare guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Thirty-two healthy Holstein castrated cattle (average body weight: 562.75 ± 29.5 kg) were randomly assigned to four groups of eight animals each: control (CON), 0.04% GAA (basal diet + 0.04% GAA), 0.08% GAA (basal diet + 0.08% GAA), and 0.12% GAA (basal diet + 0.12% GAA). GAA used in this study was supplied by Liaoning Yaho Nutrition Technology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). The experiment lasted 139 days, consisting of a 19-day pre-experimental adaptation phase and a 120-day formal experimental phase. During the adaptation phase, cattle were acclimated to the basal diet and housing conditions before the formal trial. GAA was premixed with a portion of the concentrate supplement, blended with the remaining concentrate ingredients to prepare the final concentrate, and then mixed with steam-flaked corn and rice straw to formulate a total mixed ration (TMR). All animals were housed individually in pens and managed under identical conditions; they were offered feed twice daily (06:00 and 17:00) in amounts allowing ad libitum intake and had free access to fresh water. Body weights were recorded and performance data were collected on the 19th of each month throughout the trial. Slaughter protocol and tissue sampling

After the trial, all cattle were transported to a commercial slaughterhouse (Kerchin Beef Industry Co., Ltd., Inner Mongolia, China) for slaughter. Live body weights were recorded prior to slaughter. Carcass weight was measured post-evisceration, and slaughter percentage was calculated as (carcass weight/live weight) ×100%. Organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidneys) and adipose tissues (perirenal, omental, mesenteric fat) were dissected and weighed, with relative organ weights expressed as percentages of live body weight.

Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein into EDTA-coated vacuum tubes (EDTA-K3, Jiangsu Kangjian Medical Apparatus Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) immediately before slaughter. Plasma was separated by centrifugation (3,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) using a refrigerated centrifuge (Model 5810 R, Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany) and stored at − 80 °C for biochemical parameter analysis (total protein, albumin, glucose, cholesterol, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)) using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600 − 210, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Longissimus dorsi muscle samples were excised from the left carcass, homogenized after fat trimming, and intramuscular fat content was quantified via near-infrared spectroscopy (FoodScan™ 2, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerod, Denmark). For transcriptomic analysis, liver and pectoral muscle tissues were collected within 30 min postmortem, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

RNA library construction

Total RNA was extracted from liver and muscle tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) and with a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7 were retained for sequencing. Qualified RNA samples were used to construct libraries through poly(A) tail-based mRNA enrichment, followed by paired-end sequencing (PE150) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform [12, 13].

Data quality control

Data were sequenced and quality controlled with fastq software v0.20.1 [14]. Fastq was used to perform quality checks, including adapter removal and filtering of low-quality reads (Phred score < 20 and sequences containing >10% unknown nucleotides, N). The clean data was then further processed by removing short, adapter-contaminated, and low-quality reads. This process ensured that only high-quality data remained for subsequent analysis. The following step was to remove any remaining adapter sequences and low-quality regions using Trimmomatic v0.39, resulting in high-quality clean reads that could be aligned to the Bos taurus reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2) and quantified downstream [12, 15].

Genome alignment and expression quantification

Data alignment was performed by downloading reference genomes and annotation files from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The clean data were aligned to the Bos taurus reference genome(ARS-UCD1.2) using STAR v2.7.3a with default parameters to ensure accurate and efficient mapping [16]. Alignment results were generated in BAM format, containing location information for each read. Kallisto v0.46.1 was used for transcript quantification using high-speed algorithms based on the pseudoalignment principle. Data were normalized using TPM (Transcript Per Million), a method that accounts for both transcript length and sequencing depth, enabling accurate comparisons between samples [17].

Differential gene expression and functional annotation

We analyzed differential expression between treatment groups using DESeq2 v1.32.1 [18] and edgeR v3.32.1 [19], with thresholds set at FDR < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1. This threshold ensured the selection of genes that exhibited both statistical significance and substantial expression changes. Both DESeq2 v1.32.1 and edgeR v3.32.1, multiple testing issues are handled with negative binomial distribution-based statistical models, providing reliable differential expression results. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs were conducted using the clusterProfiler package v4.0.5 [20]. GO and KEGG enrichment utilized human databases (c3.GO.bp, c2.cp.kegg), which were mapped to cattle orthologs. While this strategy addresses limited bovine annotations, species-specific pathway variations may exist. GO analysis identified the potential roles of DEGs by categorizing them into various functional groups, such as cellular components and molecular functions. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified metabolic and signaling pathways associated with DEGs, providing biological insights into the underlying mechanisms [21]. Significant pathways (p.adjust < 0.05) were determined using the enrichGO and enrichKEGG functions. To ensure complete objectivity and avoid any subjective bias in pathway selection, we implemented a standardized procedure where all enriched pathways were ranked by their corrected p-value (FDR), and only the top 30 most statistically significant non-redundant pathways were selected for visualization in each analysis.

Gene expression pattern clustering

To characterize the dynamic transcriptional responses to varying levels of guanidinoacetic acid (GAA) supplementation, fuzzy c-means clustering was conducted using the Mfuzz package v2.50.0 in R [22]. This unsupervised clustering algorithm identifies genes with similar expression trajectories across multiple conditions by assigning membership values, thereby accommodating gradual transitions in gene expression patterns. Prior to clustering, gene expression data were standardized to minimize technical variability. The optimal cluster number was found using the elbow method, which quantifies within cluster dispersion as the sum of squares within the cluster. The inflection point of the resulting curve, where the marginal gain in clustering cohesion diminishes, was identified as the “elbow” and selected as the optimal cluster number. For each cluster, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify potential molecular mechanisms involved in specific transcriptional responses to GAA supplementation.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

We applied the GSEA v4.1.0 to further validate the biological pathways associated with differentially expressed genes. GSEA is a method for analyzing pathway-level enrichment based on predefined gene sets. It was performed with the clusterProfiler package v4.7.1.001 using GO and KEGG databases as background gene sets. Our analysis identified pathways with significant enrichment (P-adjusted < 0.05) and calculated a normalized enrichment score (NES) for each gene set [23].

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and core gene identification

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was used to construct a protein-protein interaction network to identify key genes associated with GAA supplementation. The interaction confidence threshold of 0.4 indicates moderate confidence in protein-protein interactions. The PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.8.2 [13], and central genes within the network were identified through analysis of network structure and centrality. By integrating DEG enrichment pathways, clustering analysis, and GSEA results, gene intersection analysis was conducted to identify genes that were commonly enriched across all methods, which were then considered potential core genes involved in GAA regulation. The expression patterns of these core genes across different GAA concentrations were visualized using heatmaps.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables meeting normality assumptions were analyzed using Proc Mixed with the model Yi = µ + Ti + Ei, where Yi is the dependent variable, µ the overall mean, Ti the fixed effect of dietary treatment, and Ei the residual error. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were applied to evaluate linear and quadratic effects of GAA, and pairwise comparisons among treatment groups were conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) method. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, with P < 0.05 considered significant and 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 indicating a trend.

Results

Determination of slaughter performance

As shown in Table 2, no significant differences (P >0.05) were observed in carcass weight, slaughter percentage, heart, liver, kidney, perirenal fat, mesentery, or visceral fat among the treatment groups. However, spleen weight exhibited a significant quadratic response (P < 0.05), and omental fat showed a linear trend toward increase with GAA supplementation (P = 0.07). Importantly, beef fat content significantly increased in a quadratic manner (P = 0.0049), with the highest value observed in the 0.08% GAA group, indicating a non-linear relationship between GAA level and intramuscular fat deposition. The remaining parameters showed no significant linear or quadratic trends (P >0.05). Our results are consistent with those observed in Red Angus beef castrated cattle supplemented with GAA, indicating that GAA supplementation does not affect overall growth performance or carcass traits [24].

RNA-seq data quality and alignment summary

A total of 63 RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced, comprising 31 liver samples and 32 pectoral muscle samples. Each library generated approximately 20 to 65 million raw reads. Quality control was followed by alignment of all samples to the bovine reference genome, resulting in high mapping efficiency. The average proportion of uniquely mapped reads in liver samples was 93.77%, with a mean mismatch rate of 0.21% (Supplementary Table 1). Similarly, pectoral muscle samples exhibited an average uniquely mapped rate of 93.00% and a mismatch rate of 0.17% (Supplementary Table 2). The average mapped read length was consistent between tissues, approximating 296 bp. Additionally, the proportions of multi-mapped and unmapped reads were relatively low in both datasets. These results collectively indicate high-quality sequencing data, supporting the reliability of subsequent transcriptomic analyses.

Differentially expressed genes

In liver tissue, the transcriptomic response to GAA supplementation was markedly evident, as demonstrated by the volcano plots and principal component analysis (PCA). In comparison to the control group, the 0.04% GAA treatment identified 579 DEGs, the 0.08% GAA group exhibited 1064 DEGs, and the 0.12% GAA group showed 577 DEGs (Fig. 1A-C). The PCA plot shows the separation among samples with different addition levels (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Venn diagram analysis further elucidated the overlap of DEGs across the different treatment groups (Figure 1G), revealing 12 DEGs commonly regulated across all three GAA treatment levels. Additionally, 36 DEGs were shared between the 0.04% and 0.08% GAA groups, 36 between the 0.08% and 0.12% GAA groups, and 13 between the 0.04% and 0.12% GAA groups. These results indicate that there are differences in gene expression among different addition levels. In contrast, GAA supplementation elicited a more modest transcriptomic response in pectoral muscle tissue, as reflected in the volcano plots (Fig. 1D–F) and PCA results (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Only limited overlap was observed, with 11 DEGs shared between the 0.04% and 0.08% GAA groups, and 5 DEGs shared between the 0.08% and 0.12% GAA groups (Fig. 1D). These results underscore a tissue-specific response to GAA, with a more robust and consistent transcriptional modulation in liver tissue compared to pectoral muscle tissue.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

In this study, transcriptome analysis was used to investigate the effects of different amounts of guanidinoacetic acid supplementation (0.04%, 0.08%, and 0.12%) on liver and pectoral muscle tissues in Holstein castrated cattle. To elucidate the potential biological significance of GAA-responsive genes, DEGs were enriched for pathways in the KEGG and functionally annotated using GO. In liver tissue, GO terms were related to cellular response to low-density lipoprotein and cytokine-mediated signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2A), while KEGG analysis showed that 0.04% GAA group was enriched in arachidonic acid metabolism and metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2B), suggesting roles in lipid metabolism and immune regulation. At 0.08% GAA, GO enrichment analysis indicated involvement in innate immune responses and the regulation of cytokine signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2C), while KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the MAPK, phospholipase D, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2D). In the 0.12% GAA group, enriched pathways included T cell differentiation, complement activation, NF-κB signaling, and processes such as ERK1/2 cascade and cell cycle regulation (Supplementary Fig. 2E-F), indicating stronger impacts on immune and metabolic functions.

In pectoral muscle tissue, the effects of GAA supplementation were primarily reflected in pathways related to oxidative stress, energy metabolism, and signal transduction. In the 0.04% GAA group, GO analysis revealed enrichment in positive regulation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, and innate immunity(Supplementary Fig. 3A). The KEGG analysis showed significant enrichment in the PI3K-Akt, MAPK, Rap1, and Ras signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In the 0.08% GAA group, GO terms included extracellular matrix organization, innate immune response, hydrogen peroxide catabolic process, and peroxidase activity (Supplementary Fig. 3C). KEGG enriched in pathways related to choline metabolism in cancer, arginine and proline metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, as well as PI3K-Akt, Ras, Rap1, MAPK, and IL-17 signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3D). In contrast, only 12 DEGs were identified in the 0.12% GAA group. KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in cholesterol metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 3E), indicating a limited transcriptional response at this supplementation level.

Clustering of gene expression patterns in liver tissue

Based on expression trends, six clusters were grouped into three response types (Fig. 2A). Type I (Cluster 4) showed a continuous increase across the GAA gradient, indicating a stable, dose-dependent response. Type II (Cluster 1) was markedly upregulated at 0.08% and further enhanced at 0.12%, suggesting greater sensitivity at higher GAA levels. Type III (Clusters 3, 5, and 6) responded specifically to 0.08% GAA, with Clusters 3 and 5 downregulated and Cluster 6 upregulated, reflecting divergent regulatory mechanisms. Notably, all three types showed pronounced changes at 0.08%, suggesting this level may serve as a regulatory threshold (Fig. 2C). To visualize gene expression patterns, a sample-wise heatmap was generated using Z-score normalized values, with red and blue representing upregulation and downregulation, respectively. Genes were classified into six clusters (C1–C6), consistent with the Mfuzz results. The heatmap revealed clear expression shifts across GAA levels. Clusters 1 and 4 showed gradual upregulation, while Clusters 3 and 5 were downregulated at 0.08% but partially recovered at 0.12%. Cluster 6 showed strong upregulation at both 0.08% and 0.12%, suggesting roles in energy metabolism or stress response (Fig. 2B). KEGG pathway analysis was subsequently performed to explore the underlying biological mechanisms (Supplementary Table 3).

Genes in Type I (Cluster 4) exhibited sustained upregulation with increasing GAA levels and were enriched in immune-related and signaling pathways, including NF-κB, Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation, chemokine, and PI3K-Akt signaling, indicating roles in immune regulation and metabolic adaptation (Supplementary Table 3). GSEA consistently showed downregulation of NF-κB, Th cell differentiation, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions, alongside progressive upregulation of protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (0.08% >0.12% >0.04%), suggesting supported cellular homeostasis (Fig. 3A). Integration of GSEA, KEGG, and DEG analysis identified 24 overlapping genes, with PPI network analysis highlighting SYK, BTK, CD4, LCK, and VAV1 as potential hub regulators (Fig. 3B–C). Heatmap analysis revealed significant expression changes, particularly at 0.08% and 0.12%, with strong upregulation of CCR1, BTK, and SYK, implicating their roles in immune modulation and protein processing (Fig. 3D).

Type II clusters in liver

Type II (Cluster 1) genes showed similar expression in the control and 0.04% GAA groups, with marked upregulation at 0.08% and 0.12%. KEGG analysis indicated enrichment in MAPK, mTOR, TNF, and Ras signaling pathways, suggesting activation of processes related to cell proliferation, inflammation, and signal transduction at higher GAA levels (Supplementary Table 3). GSEA further revealed upregulation of steroid hormone biosynthesis and fatty acid degradation, particularly at 0.12%, while Toll-like receptor signaling was downregulated (Fig. 4A–C). These results suggest that higher GAA supplementation enhances lipid metabolism and steroid synthesis while attenuating inflammatory signaling, contributing to improved metabolic and immune regulation. Further investigation of fatty acid degradation genes at 0.08% and 0.12% will help determine the optimal GAA dose.

Type III clusters in liver

KEGG enrichment analysis of Type III clusters (3, 5, and 6) revealed involvement in energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, and signal transduction. Clusters 3 and 5 were upregulated at 0.08% GAA and enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation, the TCA cycle, glycolysis, AMPK, mTOR, MAPK, FoxO signaling, and BCAA degradation (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, Cluster 6 genes were downregulated and enriched in MAPK, PI3K-Akt, Toll-like receptor, and FoxO pathways. GSEA supported these findings, showing upregulation of fatty acid degradation, BCAA degradation, pyruvate metabolism, the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and peroxisome pathways, alongside downregulation of NF-κB and IL-17 signaling at 0.08% GAA (Fig. 5A). These results suggest GAA promotes metabolic efficiency while attenuating inflammation. Integration of GSEA, cluster analysis, and DEG enrichment identified 189 overlapping genes (Fig. 5B), from which 17 key genes were selected (Fig. 5C). Downregulated genes were associated with the TCA cycle (SUCLG1, MDH2, SDHA), oxidative phosphorylation (ATP5F1C, ATP5F1A), and fatty acid synthesis (ACACA). Upregulated genes included ACSL6 and CBR3 (fatty acid oxidation), HSPA2 (protein folding), and several related to signaling and immune modulation (FAS, DUSP2, RASGRP2/4, IL18, PIK3CD) (Fig. 5C). Notably, ACSL6 and CBR3 were significantly upregulated, while ACACA was suppressed. Despite SUCLG1 downregulation, overall TCA cycle activity increased, indicating that pathway-level regulation may not align with individual gene expression.

Clustering of gene expression patterns in pectoral muscle tissue

Using the Elbow Method, gene expression profiles from the pectoralis muscle were clustered into six distinct gene clusters with Cluster 1, 2, and 4 showing significant upregulation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 0.12%, 0.08%, and 0.04% levels, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that Cluster 1 was enriched in pathways associated with chemokine signaling, PI3K-Akt, MAPK, Ras, calcium signaling, and NF-κB, suggesting roles in inflammation regulation and cell proliferation; Cluster 2 was primarily linked to oxidative phosphorylation, IL-17 signaling, PPAR signaling, and fatty acid degradation, implicating its involvement in energy metabolism and lipid mobilization; while Cluster 4 was characterized by enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation, PPAR signaling, and amino acid metabolism, emphasizing metabolic homeostasis regulation (Supplementary Table 4).

Further GSEA comparing the 0.04% GAA-treated group with controls identified upregulated immune-related pathways (NF-κB, cAMP, B cell receptor signaling), PI3K-Akt, lipolysis regulation, FoxO, and calcium signaling, alongside downregulated steroid biosynthesis and cholesterol metabolism pathways (Fig. 6A-B), whereas the 0.08% GAA-treated group exhibited activation of Wnt and FoxO pathways but suppression of oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 6C). Notably, PPAR signaling was exclusively enriched in the pectoralis muscle, and given its established role in regulating lipid synthesis, storage, and mobilization [25], combined with phenotypic data showing quadratic growth of intramuscular fat content (peak at 0.08% GAA, P = 0.0049), we propose that PPAR signaling is a central driver of GAA-mediated fat deposition. Mechanistic validation through a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of PPAR-related genes identified PLIN2 as a core regulatory node (Fig. 6D), which mediates fatty acid uptake and triglyceride storage, thereby corroborating the non-linear relationship between GAA levels and intramuscular lipid accumulation.

Tissue-specific expression patterns of metabolic genes

To explore the tissue-specific effects of GAA on gene expression, we analyzed 23 key metabolic genes in the liver and pectoral muscle of Holstein castrated cattle under three GAA supplementation levels (0.04%, 0.08%, and 0.12%) (Fig. 7). Significant differences in the expression levels of genes were observed among tissues. In pectoral muscle, lipid uptake and storage genes(FABP3, FABP5, PLIN2, ACSL6) were upregulated across all GAA groups (Fig. 7), suggesting enhanced intramuscular lipid deposition. NR1H3, a master regulator of cholesterol efflux and lipogenesis [26], and SYK, a kinase mediating immune-metabolic crosstalk [27], were also significantly up regulated, potentially coordinating lipid storage and tissue remodeling. In contrast, the liver exhibited increased expression of ACSL6—a rate-limiting enzyme in mitochondrial β-oxidation—at all three GAA levels (0.04%, 0.08%, and 0.12%) (Fig. 7), indicating a tissue-specific divergence in lipid handling: the muscle prioritized lipid storage via upregulated transporters (FABP3/FABP5) and lipid droplet regulators (PLIN2), whereas the liver enhanced fatty acid breakdown to supply energy through β-oxidation.

Discussion

GAA driven liver energy metabolism reprogramming via AMPK

The AMPK signaling pathway, significantly enriched in liver Cluster III and pectoral muscle Cluster II, likely serves as a key mediator in this process. AMPK activation promotes lipid catabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization, while also inhibiting NF-κB signaling via downstream targets such as AKT and GSK3-β [28, 29]. As GAA functions as a precursor of phosphocreatine and modulator of cellular ATP levels [30], its supplementation may activate AMPK-centered regulatory networks that coordinate metabolic and immune responses [30–32]. These pathway results suggest that 0.08% GAA may promote metabolic benefits and immune balance through AMPK-mediated signal transduction, supporting an adaptive mechanism that sustains energy and immune homeostasis under altered nutritional inputs [29, 33].

We observed mechanisms in the liver in which branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and fatty acids (FAs) serve not only as important energy substrates but also as metabolic intermediates involved in key biological processes, including mitochondrial function, ATP synthesis, and energy homeostasis [34, 35]. In this study, transcriptomic analysis combined with mechanistic mapping revealed that GAA supplementation activates the energy-sensing AMPK pathway and reprograms mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux by modulating BCAA and fatty acid metabolism, thereby optimizing the balance between energy supply and demand (Fig. 8). BCAA catabolic pathways contribute intermediates such as acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA to the TCA cycle, supporting mitochondrial energy production and metabolic adaptation [36]. In terms of fatty acid metabolism, both PPI network and GSEA analyses demonstrated upregulation of ACSL6 (acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 6), which activates long-chain fatty acids, enabling their transport into mitochondria for β-oxidation. Concurrently, the elevated p-AMPK/AMPK ratio indicates significant activation of AMPK, a key energy sensor. AMPK negatively regulates ACACA, reducing fatty acid synthesis and enhancing lipid oxidation [37, 38], while also coordinating glucose utilization and mitochondrial biogenesis under energy-deficient conditions [39]. Furthermore, the upregulation of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway observed in this study indicates enhanced activity of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, accelerated reoxidation of NADH and FADH₂, and increased ATP synthesis [40]. These findings align with established mechanisms of energy metabolism regulation [41]. The results suggest that GAA may modulate growth performance by activating AMPK, promoting fatty acid oxidation and TCA cycle flux, enhancing oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and mitochondrial energy output, and ultimately optimizing the organism’s energy homeostasis.

Effects of GAA on muscle fiber development and metabolism

Skeletal muscle is the primary storage site of creatine (CRE) in animals, where it is converted into phosphocreatine to enhance muscle performance and maintain energy reserves [42]. GAA, a natural precursor of CRE, has been shown to improve growth performance in various livestock species—including poultry [5, 7], pigs [3], cattle [43] and sheep [42]—mainly by enhancing intracellular energy metabolism. This study employed transcriptome sequencing combined with differential gene functional enrichment analysis (KEGG/GO) and expression pattern clustering to investigate the regulatory effects of different GAA dosages on pectoral muscle tissue. The results demonstrated that at 0.04% and 0.08% GAA supplementation levels, multiple KEGG signaling pathways associated with myofiber development were significantly enriched. Notably, GSEA revealed up regulation trends for the PI3K-AKT and FoxO signaling pathways under both supplementation regimes. Previous studies have established that the PI3K/AKT/FOXO axis plays a pivotal role in maintaining myofiber type composition, while activation of the Akt/mTOR pathway regulates myofiber hypertrophy and suppresses muscle atrophy [44]. Additionally, studies in pigs [3] and sheep [45] have demonstrated that GAA influences myofiber diameter and myofibril density, suggesting conserved effects of GAA on muscle structural characteristics across species.​.

Lipid droplets (LDs) are highly dynamic and evolutionarily conserved organelles widely distributed in muscle cells. They play a central role in lipid homeostasis by storing neutral lipids such as triacylglycerols (TAGs), thereby regulating fat accumulation [46]. The PLIN protein family, particularly PLIN1, is a key regulator of lipid storage, contributing to LD stabilization and promoting fat deposition [47]. In our study, meat quality assessments revealed that dietary supplementation with 0.08% GAA significantly enhanced intramuscular fat deposition (P = 0.0034), with both linear (P = 0.0036) and quadratic (P = 0.0049) dose-dependent trends. These findings suggest that GAA may mimic the biological functions of ACSL3 and PLIN1 by promoting fatty acid esterification into TAGs and enhancing neutral lipid storage within LDs [48]. Transcriptomic analysis of the Pectoral Muscle showed that expression of the PLIN2 gene was significantly upregulated at 0.04%, 0.08%, and 0.12% GAA levels compared to the control, indicating that GAA-induced fat deposition may involve modulation of PLIN family proteins, particularly PLIN2. Similar associations between PLIN2 and meat quality traits have been reported in pigs [49]. Beyond serving as lipid reservoirs, LDs also function as platforms for the release of inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins and leukotrienes, thereby participating in immune regulation [50]. PLIN proteins such as PLIN2 have been implicated in the coupling of LD formation and inflammatory cytokine secretion in macrophages. Moreover, the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway has been shown to influence immune cell function by regulating transcription factors such as NF-κB [51]. This study’s GSEA results further revealed significant upregulation of immune-related pathways, including NF-κB and B cell receptor signaling, in the pectoralis major following GAA supplementation. This suggests that GAA-induced PLIN2 expression may activate the AKT/mTOR pathway—similar to its reported role in inhibiting autophagy and promoting proliferation in non-small cell lung cancer [52]—and thereby indirectly enhance NF-κB activity. Collectively, these findings support the existence of a “metabolism-immune interaction network,” in which PLIN2 not only facilitates lipid storage by stabilizing LDs but also modulates the immune microenvironment to favor intramuscular fat deposition [53, 54].

This study suggests that supplementation with 0.08% GAA may induce metabolic reprogramming in Holstein castrated cattle pectoral muscle tissue, potentially characterized by enhanced glycolytic activity and reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). GSEA analysis revealed significant enrichment of Wnt signaling pathways, whereas the OXPHOS pathway was markedly suppressed. Such a metabolic pattern could reflect an adaptive tendency of fast-twitch (type II) muscle fibers toward meeting increased demands for rapid energy supply. Wu et al. [55] used transcriptomic analysis to reveal significant differences between glycolytic (OEA) and oxidative (BF) muscle types in yak at the levels of mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA. The differentially expressed genes were primarily enriched in key energy metabolism pathways such as PPAR signaling, the TCA cycle, and calcium signaling, providing important insights for understanding the metabolic characteristics of bovine muscle fibers. Based on these findings, we propose that the Wnt signaling activation and OXPHOS suppression observed in pectoral muscle following GAA supplementation in our study may reflect a metabolic reprogramming process resembling that of fast-twitch fibers. However, this inference is derived solely from transcriptomic associations and warrants validation through functional experiments, such as pathway-specific inhibition or activation of Wnt or PI3K-Akt signaling.

The long-term implications of this metabolic switch under normal conditions remain unclear. In fast-twitch fibers, enhanced glycolytic flux can rapidly supply ATP, offering potential advantages during acute stress or high-intensity energy demand by improving energy efficiency and resilience to transient hypoxia [56]. However, sustained reliance on glycolysis at the expense of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation may increase lactate accumulation, disrupt redox balance, elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS), and place additional metabolic stress on buffering systems [57, 58]. In ruminants, prolonged glycolytic dominance could impair mitochondrial function or reduce metabolic efficiency under sustained workloads [55]. Thus, while GAA-induced reprogramming may represent a short-term adaptive benefit, its long-term effects under typical husbandry conditions remain uncertain, warranting further investigation through extended feeding trials and oxidative stress assessments.

Tissue specificity and dose dependence

Our study found that dietary supplementation with GAA leads to distinct regulatory mechanisms in the liver and pectoral muscle, with differential responses observed across varying dosage levels. The liver exhibited downregulation of immune-related pathways (e.g., NF-κB, NOD-like receptor signaling) and enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation, suggesting improved energy metabolism and reduced inflammatory tone. In contrast, the pectoral muscles exhibited upregulation of immune-related pathways, and increased expression of lipid droplet-associated genes (such as PLIN2), suggesting enhanced intramuscular fat deposition. GAA appears to attenuate the inflammatory response, as evidenced by the downregulation of immune-related pathways, including NF-κB and Th17 signaling. Consistent with this observation, GAA supplementation has been shown to effectively alleviate stress in chickens [59], lambs [60], and beef cattle [61]. In future studies, functional analyses such as measuring oxidative stress and β-oxidation should be performed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its effects. Moreover, GAA appeared to modulate the AMPK signaling pathway in the liver, thereby enhancing fatty acid oxidation. Previous studies have shown that creatine and its precursors can activate AMPK and promote oxidative metabolism, providing mechanistic support for this effect [62]. Although enhanced fatty acid oxidation is commonly associated with increased energy supply, it may also reflect a protective adaptation to prevent excessive lipid accumulation and maintain hepatic lipid homeostasis [63]. In muscle tissue, moderate intramuscular fat (IMF) deposition can improve meat quality, whereas excessive lipid accumulation in non-adipose tissues has been linked to lipotoxicity [64, 65]. In ruminants, GAA supplementation has been associated with improved antioxidant capacity and energy metabolism, but most evidence derives from short-term or stress-related studies, leaving its long-term safety uncertain. Thus, GAA-induced metabolic shifts may confer benefits yet al.so entail risks, necessitating further functional validation.

Among all supplementation levels, the 0.08% GAA group exhibited the highest number of DEGs compared to the control. In addition, beef fat content increased significantly in a quadratic pattern (P = 0.0049), with the peak value observed at the 0.08% GAA level, suggesting that supplementation with 0.08% GAA was more effective than 0.04% or 0.12%. However, only 12 differentially expressed genes were identified in the pectoral muscle of the 0.12% GAA group compared to the control, and these genes were mainly enriched in cholesterol metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways. This phenomenon may reflect a metabolic saturation effect induced by high-dose GAA supplementation. Previous studies have shown that excessive GAA intake can saturate GAA transporters in the intestine and liver, thereby limiting its absorption and biotransformation [66–68]. Although the optimal GAA dosage may vary depending on animal species and experimental conditions, multiple studies have reported beneficial effects within the range of 0.05% to 0.1%. For instance, supplementation with 0.6–0.9 g/kg dry matter (DM) of GAA in Angus bulls showed significant improvements in growth, digestion, and fermentation [69]. Similarly, Jin H et al. [45] found that 750 mg/kg (0.075%) GAA was optimal for promoting growth and improving rumen fermentation in lake sheep. Li [70] reported that supplementation with 0.05% to 0.1% GAA improved average daily gain (ADG), blood parameters, and antioxidant capacity in Jinjiang bulls, while even higher levels (0.1% to 0.4%) continued to yield positive outcomes in growth and feed efficiency. Our study revealed that dietary supplementation with GAA in Holstein castrated cattle induced upregulation of steroid hormone biosynthesis in the liver, while a downregulation trend was observed in the pectoral muscles. Previous studies have demonstrated a correlation between creatine levels and sex hormone secretion. As GAA is a precursor of creatine, it may indirectly affect the secretion of sex hormones by elevating creatine levels, thereby modulating lipid metabolism [71, 72]. Therefore, future research should further investigate whether GAA is associated with sex-specific differences in creatine synthesis or metabolism to comprehensively understand its potential role in sex hormone regulation and energy metabolism.

Conclusion

This study conducted an integrated analysis using transcriptomic data from multiple perspectives. Dietary GAA supplementation improves energy metabolism and meat quality in cattle through tissue-specific mechanisms. In the liver, GAA appeared to modulate the AMPK signaling pathway and genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, while branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) catabolism may contribute intermediates to the TCA cycle, thereby supporting mitochondrial function. In muscle tissue, the regulation of genes such as PLIN2 suggests a potential role in promoting intramuscular fat deposition. At the 0.08% supplementation level, a balance between immune response and metabolism was observed, and phenotypic evaluation further indicated that multiple indicators at this level outperformed those at other concentrations. Future studies combining transcriptomic analyses with measurements of creatine metabolism and production traits will further strengthen the applicability of these findings.
