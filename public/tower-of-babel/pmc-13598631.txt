Fatal Cutaneous Mucormycosis Initially Misdiagnosed as Necrotizing Fasciitis in an Uncontrolled Diabetic Patient: A Case Report Highlighting Diagnostic Delay
Authors: Yovita Hartantri, Stephanie Victoria Gunadi, Ferdy Ferdian, Indra Wijaya, Risa Miliawati Nurul Hidayah, Nada Nisrina Aisyah, Naufal Fakhri Nugraha, Nisa Fauziah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMucormycosis is a rare yet highly aggressive invasive fungal infection associated with significant mortality, particularly among patients with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus. Early diagnosis remains challenging because its clinical presentation may closely mimic bacterial necrotizing soft tissue infections, particularly in critically ill patients, while conventional diagnostic tests such as potassium hydroxide (KOH) smear have limited sensitivity.Case PresentationWe report a 64-year-old male with undiagnosed diabetes who presented with progressive decline in consciousness and extensive necrotic lesions over the occipital and periorbital regions. On admission, he was in septic shock with complications including acute kidney injury, arrhythmia, and multi-organ dysfunction. The initial working diagnosis was bacterial necrotizing fasciitis, supported by an initial false-negative KOH smear, resulting in empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy and surgical debridement. Subsequently, wound cultures grew carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), further reinforcing the presumed bacterial etiology and complicating clinical management. Necrotic tissue culture ultimately confirmed mucormycosis, and amphotericin B was initiated only after fungal culture confirmation. Despite combined medical and surgical interventions, the patient’s condition deteriorated, and he died on the twelfth day of hospitalization. This case illustrates how diagnostic anchoring, limited sensitivity of initial mycological investigations, and bacterial co-infection contributed to delayed recognition of invasive mucormycosis and postponed appropriate antifungal therapy.ConclusionMucormycosis in uncontrolled diabetes carries a poor prognosis when diagnosis and treatment are delayed. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for mucormycosis in diabetic patients presenting with rapidly progressive necrotic soft tissue infections despite negative initial fungal microscopy or the isolation of bacterial pathogens. Early deep tissue biopsy, repeat mycological investigations, and timely empirical antifungal therapy should be considered when clinical suspicion remains high.

Introduction

Invasive mold infections (IMIs) represent a group of opportunistic fungal diseases that emerge in individuals with impaired immunity, with diabetes mellitus being one of the most common predisposing conditions.1,2 Hyperglycemia and metabolic acidosis in diabetes diminish the effectiveness of innate immune defenses, thereby facilitating fungal invasion and growth.3 Once established, IMIs tend to involve blood vessels, deep tissue compartments, and vital organs, most often after inhalation of fungal spores.4 Although infrequent, these infections are clinically significant because they are rapidly progressive, life-threatening, and most commonly caused by fungi of the genera Mucorales or Aspergilllus. Of these, mucormycosis is particularly notorious for its high mortality and aggressive clinical course.5

Mucormycosis is caused by filamentous fungi of the order Mucorales. Spores may enter the host via the respiratory tract, the gastrointestinal system through contaminated food, or directly through disrupted cutaneous barriers.5 Epidemiological patterns differ between regions: in high-income countries, Mucormycosis usually affects patients with hematologic malignancies or profound immunosuppression,6 while in many low- and middle-income settings, uncontrolled diabetes is one of the leading risk factors.7 The disease is characterized by its angioinvasive nature, leading to vascular thrombosis, tissue necrosis, and infarction, which collectively contribute to its poor prognosis.8

One of the greatest clinical challenges is that cutaneous mucormycosis may initially mimic bacterial necrotizing soft tissue infections, particularly necrotizing fasciitis. Both conditions can present with rapidly progressive soft tissue necrosis, severe sepsis, and multiorgan dysfunction, making early differentiation difficult, especially in critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit.9 The diagnostic challenge is further compounded by the limited sensitivity of initial mycological investigations, such as potassium hydroxide (KOH) smear, and by concomitant bacterial infections that may reinforce an initial bacterial diagnosis. Consequently, definitive diagnosis often depends on fungal culture or histopathology, which may delay the initiation of appropriate antifungal therapy.10

Over the past two decades, reported cases of Mucormycosis have increased worldwide. Multiple risk factors have been identified, including uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hematological malignancies with neutropenia, organ transplantation, autoimmune disease treated with immunosuppressants, HIV infection, extensive burns, peritoneal dialysis, trauma including surgical procedures, malnutrition, and prior exposure to voriconazole.7 Based on the anatomical site of involvement, six clinical forms are commonly recognized: rhino-orbito-cerebral (ROCM), pulmonary, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, disseminated, and less common atypical presentations.2 At present, the cornerstone of therapy remains lipid formulations of Amphotericin B, often in combination with prompt and extensive surgical debridement.11 Despite these interventions, survival remains limited, underscoring the importance of early recognition and rapid initiation of treatment.

We report a fatal case of cutaneous mucormycosis in a patient with previously undiagnosed diabetes mellitus that was initially misdiagnosed as bacterial necrotizing fasciitis. The diagnosis was further obscured by an initial false-negative KOH smear and concurrent carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) wound infection, resulting in delayed initiation of amphotericin B therapy. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for invasive fungal infection in diabetic patients with rapidly progressive necrotic soft tissue lesions despite negative initial mycological findings or the isolation of bacterial pathogens.

Case Report

A 64-year-old male was admitted with a five-day history of progressive worsening level of consciousness. According to his family, the patient initially became incoherent during conversation, gradually became more somnolent, and was increasingly difficult to arouse. One week prior to admission, he developed a dark lesion at the occipital region, initially presenting as a pustule that ruptured, turned black, and progressively expanded to involve the posterior scalp and upper cervical area. Facial swelling was noted, particularly around the left eyelid, accompanied by necrotic discoloration of the periorbital skin (Figure 1). The patient also had intermittent fever and persistent headache, but denied facial pain, nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, or visual disturbances. His medical history included hypertension for five years, managed irregularly with amlodipine 5 mg once daily without consistent follow-up. Although undiagnosed, his family reported a one-year history of polyphagia, polydipsia, and polyuria, especially nocturia, suggestive of diabetes mellitus. There was no known history of cardiac, renal, or other chronic diseases.

On arrival at the emergency department of Dr. Hasan Sadikin General Hospital (April 3, 2023), the patient was somnolent and in critical condition. Vital signs revealed blood pressure of 77/54 mmHg, irregular pulse of 130–170 bpm, respiratory rate 30 breaths/min (shallow and rapid), temperature 37.2°C, and oxygen saturation of 95% with a non-rebreathing mask at 15 L/min. Physical examination showed facial edema, most prominent at the left eyelid, with surrounding erythema and necrotic lesions. A large necrotic ulcer measuring 10×8 cm with irregular borders was present in the occipital, parietal, and upper cervical regions (Figure 1). Jugular venous pressure was 5 + 2 cmH2O without lymphadenopathy. Chest examination revealed symmetrical expansion and normal vesicular breath sounds without rales or wheezing. Cardiac assessment demonstrated an irregular rhythm with the apex beat at the fifth intercostal space, midclavicular line, without heave or thrill. Abdominal examination was unremarkable, with normal bowel sounds, no hepatosplenomegaly and no shifting dullness. The extremities were warm without edema or lesions.

Contrast-enhanced head computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a cerebral infarction involving the subcortical cortex of the left parieto-occipital lobe, accompanied by soft tissue swelling in the right frontotemporal region, thickening of the left upper eyelid soft tissue, and left ethmoidal sinusitis. The initial working diagnoses included septic shock secondary to necrotizing fasciitis involving the occipital and periorbital regions, with multiple abscesses (occipital, bilateral temporal, submandibular, and cervical) complicated by multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). The patient was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) and underwent endotracheal intubation for airway protection. On day 4 of hospitalization, surgical necrotizing debridement was performed by the plastic surgery team. Two days later, similar necrotic lesions appeared over the right eyelid. Renal function deteriorated with rising creatinine, accompanied by anuria. Hemodialysis was initiated on day 6. By day 7, the patient developed jaundice with markedly elevated bilirubin levels but without elevation of transaminases or alkaline phosphatase. Persistent tachycardia and hypotension raised suspicion of myocarditis, although troponin I remained within normal limits (0.01 ng/mL on day 10).

Initial mycological evaluation using potassium hydroxide (KOH) examination of the necrotic tissue was negative for fungal elements; therefore, empirical intravenous meropenem therapy was initiated for presumed necrotizing fasciitis. Pus culture subsequently grew carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CR-Ab), prompting modification of antimicrobial therapy to intravenous amikacin. Histopathological examination of tissue biopsy later demonstrated broad, thick-walled, coenocytic fungal hyphae consistent with mucormycosis (Figure 3). Fungal culture on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) yielded rapidly growing fluffy, cottony white colonies (Figure 4). Microscopic examination revealed broad, ribbon-like, aseptate (coenocytic), non-pigmented hyphae with irregular branching, characteristic of fungi belonging to the order Mucorales (Figure 5). Confirmation was further obtained through slide culture performed on day 14, which demonstrated dense cottony mycelial growth on SDA and Lactophenol Cotton Blue (LPCB) staining showing broad aseptate hyphae with sporangiophores and immature sporangia, while wet mount preparation revealed hyaline, non-septate hyphae with right-angle branching, findings consistent with Mucor species (Figure 6). Despite these interventions, the patient deteriorated further. On day 12, he developed hypotension, bradycardia, and worsening anuria with hyperkalemia and subsequently suffered cardiac arrest. Advanced resuscitation was performed according to protocol, but no return of spontaneous circulation was achieved. The patient was pronounced dead on the twelfth day of hospitalization.

Discussion

Mucormycosis is a highly aggressive fungal infection caused by members of the orders Mucorales with mortality rates that remain unacceptably high despite advances in critical care.12 These fungi are ubiquitous in the environment and commonly found in soil, decaying vegetation, bread, and organic debris. Infection occurs when spores are inhaled into the respiratory tract, ingested through contaminated food, or directly inoculated through disrupted skin.13 The true global prevalence and incidence of Mucormycosis remain unknown, largely due to underdiagnosis and the difficulties inherent in obtaining adequate tissue samples, as well as the low sensitivity of conventional diagnostic tests. A global review by Jeong et al, which analyzed cases reported between January 2000 and January 2017, identified 851 cases worldwide, with the majority reported from Europe (34%), Asia (31%), and the Americas (28%), with smaller proportions from Africa (3%) and Oceania (3%).14

The principal educational value of this case lies in the diagnostic pitfalls that delayed appropriate treatment. The patient initially fulfilled the clinical picture of bacterial necrotizing fasciitis, with septic shock, extensive necrotic soft tissue lesions, and multiple abscesses. Consequently, empirical broad-spectrum antibacterial therapy and surgical debridement were appropriately initiated. However, the continued progression of necrosis despite treatment should have prompted earlier reconsideration of invasive fungal infection, particularly in the presence of previously unrecognized diabetes mellitus.

Risk factors for Mucormycosis include uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hematological malignancies with neutropenia, hematopoietic stem cell or solid organ transplantation, prolonged immunosuppressive or chemotherapeutic regimens, autoimmune or rheumatologic disease, HIV infection, chronic ambulatory peritoneal dialysis, extensive burns, and prior exposure to voriconazole.7 In this case, uncontrolled diabetes was the most prominent risk factor, consistent with reports from Asia where diabetes accounts for approximately 27–52% of cases. Hyperglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis impair innate immune defenses, particularly neutrophil chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and macrophage activity, predisposing patients to invasive fungal disease. Patients with diabetic ketoacidosis are especially prone to rhino-orbito-cerebral Mucormycosis (ROCM).15

Another important diagnostic pitfall was the initial false-negative KOH smear. Although direct microscopic examination is widely used because it provides rapid preliminary results, its diagnostic performance depends greatly on specimen quality and fungal burden. Superficial wound specimens may fail to detect Mucorales because the organism preferentially invades blood vessels and deep tissues. Therefore, a negative KOH smear should not exclude mucormycosis when clinical suspicion remains high. In such situations, repeat sampling, early deep tissue biopsy, histopathological examination, and fungal culture are essential to establish the diagnosis.

Diagnosis relies on histopathological demonstration of broad, aseptate hyphae with tissue angioinvasion, and/or positive fungal culture. Mucorales characteristically invade blood vessels, leading to thrombosis, ischemia, and extensive tissue necrosis. In pulmonary disease, fine needle aspiration biopsy may reveal diagnostic findings.16 Diagnosis of mucormycosis is often difficult, as cultures may be negative despite histopathological evidence, partly due to inadequate sampling or tissue handling. Blood cultures are rarely positive, and overall culture sensitivity is only about 50%. Current recommendations highlight direct microscopic examination and culture as the mainstays, with special stains and species-level identification important for guiding epidemiological knowledge. Molecular tools such as sequencing offer greater accuracy, but their use remains limited by lack of standardization and variable sensitivity. Histopathology remains essential, while immunohistochemistry and newer molecular methods require specialized facilities.17 In this case, the diagnosis was confirmed through fungal culture from necrotic cutaneous tissue.

The subsequent isolation of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) further reinforced the presumed bacterial diagnosis and contributed to diagnostic anchoring. Importantly, bacterial co-infection does not exclude invasive fungal infection. Instead, polymicrobial infection should prompt clinicians to reconsider the diagnosis when tissue necrosis progresses despite appropriate antibacterial therapy. This case illustrates the danger of diagnostic fixation on a single etiology, particularly in patients with multiple predisposing factors for mucormycosis.

One major limitation in managing Mucormycosis is the frequent diagnostic delay, as culture confirmation is often required before initiating antifungal therapy. This delay substantially worsens prognosis.18 Current international guidelines emphasize that treatment should be initiated based on clinical suspicion rather than awaiting definitive microbiological confirmation whenever mucormycosis is strongly suspected. The World Health Organization (WHO) recently listed Mucorales among priority fungal pathogens due to their high morbidity and mortality.19 In high-risk patients with uncontrolled diabetes, rapidly progressive necrotic lesions, black eschar, or failure to respond to broad-spectrum antibiotics, empirical liposomal amphotericin B should be considered while definitive diagnostic investigations are ongoing. In our patient, amphotericin B was initiated only after fungal culture confirmation on hospital day 11, by which time irreversible multiorgan dysfunction had already developed.

Anatomically, Mucormycosis can present in six main forms: rhino-orbito-cerebral (44–49%), cutaneous (10–19%), pulmonary (10–11%), disseminated (6–11%), gastrointestinal (2–11%), and other atypical sites.20 In this patient, the predominant presentation was cutaneous involvement of the scalp and periorbital tissues. However, concomitant ROCM could not be excluded, given the periorbital swelling, although nasal and palatal lesions were not observed and no cranial imaging was performed. Neurological decline might have been multifactorial, including sepsis-associated encephalopathy and concurrent ischemic stroke, though cerebral invasion by Mucorales remains possible. Pulmonary and gastrointestinal involvement were not evident on imaging or clinical examination, although gastrointestinal disease—though rare—is associated with poor prognosis. Cardiac involvement has also been described in Mucormycosis through myocarditis or endocarditis, but this was unlikely in this patient, given normal troponin I levels.

The fatality rate of invasive mucormycosis remains considerable, generally exceeding 30–50%, and can reach up to 90% in cases of disseminated infection.21 The unfavorable outcome in this case most likely reflects the combined effects of uncontrolled diabetes, delayed diagnosis, delayed antifungal therapy, septic shock, multiorgan failure, and concurrent CRAB infection rather than cutaneous disease alone.

Timely recognition of mucormycosis and prompt initiation of therapy are critical for improving outcomes. Optimal management generally includes correction of predisposing factors when feasible, administration of antifungal agents, and surgical intervention.7 Therapeutic options for Mucormycosis remain limited.22 Randomized controlled trials are scarce due to the rarity of the disease. Since members of the Mucorales group exhibit resistance to many antifungal agents, lipid formulations of amphotericin B—especially the liposomal form—are regarded as the cornerstone of therapy and should be started as soon as the disease is suspected. International guidelines consistently highlight liposomal amphotericin B as the drug of choice, with stronger evidence supporting its use compared with other agents. Clinical studies suggest that a daily dose of 5 mg/kg provides better outcomes in terms of response and survival, while higher doses (10 mg/kg/day for one month) in combination with surgery achieved survival rates of up to 62% at 12 weeks, although renal impairment occurred in a substantial proportion of patients. In individuals with hematological malignancies, therapy often combines surgical removal of infected tissue with liposomal amphotericin B, alongside measures such as reversing neutropenia and reducing immunosuppression when possible. Other triazoles, including posaconazole and isavuconazole, have shown encouraging efficacy and tolerability in observational studies and are generally reserved for patients who cannot continue amphotericin B. In more challenging cases, combination regimens—for example liposomal amphotericin B with posaconazole—have been attempted, with reports of clinical improvement in over half of treated patients, although firm evidence from trials is still lacking. Surgical management, especially aggressive debridement of necrotic lesions, continues to play an essential role in care, particularly for cutaneous and rhino-orbito-cerebral presentations, and is strongly linked with improved survival.11 Early aggressive surgical debridement combined with prompt liposomal amphotericin B remains the cornerstone of management. However, this case demonstrates that treatment success depends not only on the availability of effective antifungal agents but also on early clinical recognition before irreversible tissue invasion and multiorgan dysfunction occur.

Conclusion

Mucormycosis remains a devastating infection characterized by rapid progression, angioinvasive, and high mortality. Early diagnosis and prompt initiation of antifungal therapy are essential to improving patient outcomes. This case demonstrates how diagnostic anchoring to bacterial necrotizing fasciitis, an initial false-negative KOH smear, and concurrent carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) infection contributed to delayed recognition of invasive mucormycosis and postponed appropriate antifungal treatment.

In high-risk patients, particularly those with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus presenting with rapidly progressive necrotic soft tissue lesions, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for mucormycosis despite negative initial fungal microscopy or the isolation of bacterial pathogens. Early deep tissue biopsy and repeat mycological investigations should be pursued without delay.

When clinical suspicion for mucormycosis is high, empiric liposomal amphotericin B should be considered alongside broad-spectrum antibiotics and aggressive surgical debridement while awaiting definitive fungal culture or histopathological confirmation. Such an approach may reduce diagnostic delay and improve survival in this rapidly fatal infection.
