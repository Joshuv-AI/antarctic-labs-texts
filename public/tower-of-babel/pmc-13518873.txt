TNF-α-responsive YAP1 promotes CCL20 expression in colorectal inflammation and cancer
Authors: Libin Chen, Feiyan Ai, Wentao Yu, Bo Xiang, Shourong Shen, Jian Ma, Xiayu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) exemplifies how chronic intestinal inflammation can be converted into sustained epithelial programs and a permissive tumor microenvironment, yet the molecular linkage between inflammatory cytokine cues and chemokine outputs remains incompletely defined. Here, we identify a TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program in colorectal inflammation and cancer. Across public cohorts and clinical specimens, YAP1 was elevated in ulcerative colitis and colorectal cancer and positively correlated with TNF-α expression. TNF-α increased YAP1 mRNA in both NCM460 and HCT116 cells. In HCT116 cells, TNF-α also increased YAP1 protein abundance and promoted its nuclear accumulation, whereas infliximab attenuated TNF-α-induced YAP1 mRNA and protein upregulation. Transcriptomic profiling following YAP1 silencing highlighted CCL20 among the most markedly downregulated genes. YAP1 gain- and loss-of-function consistently regulated CCL20 expression and secretion, and mechanistic assays demonstrated that YAP1/TEAD activity directly transactivated the CCL20 promoter. Functionally, TNF-α enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer cells, effects that were blunted by YAP1 knockdown; CCL20 overexpression partially rescued the suppressed malignant phenotypes caused by YAP1 silencing. In vivo, verteporfin and/or infliximab ameliorated DSS-induced colitis, accompanied by reduced colonic Yap1 and Ccl20 expression, lower serum CCL20, and diminished mucosal accumulation of CD3⁺ T cells, CD19⁺ B cells, CD11c⁺ dendritic cells, and FOXP3⁺ regulatory T cells. Moreover, combined YAP pathway inhibition and CCL20 knockdown produced the most pronounced suppression of xenograft tumor growth. Together, these findings position CCL20 as a mechanistically defined downstream effector of YAP1 and suggest that a TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program contributes to inflammatory chemokine remodeling in colitis and to malignant phenotypes of colorectal cancer cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-66199-5.Keywords: TNF-α, YAP1, CCL20, Ulcerative colitis, Colorectal cancerSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Gastroenterology, Immunology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide1. A subset of CRC arises on the background of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), where long-standing mucosal injury and regeneration create conditions permissive for malignant transformation2,3. Although the absolute proportion of IBD-associated CRC is relatively small, the clinical impact is substantial because affected patients often develop cancer at a younger age and may present with multifocal lesions4. Despite advances in surveillance and anti-inflammatory therapies, the molecular events that connect chronic intestinal inflammation to epithelial transformation and tumor progression remain incompletely defined.

Among inflammatory mediators enriched in colitis, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is a central cytokine with established relevance to IBD pathogenesis and therapeutic targeting5,6. Beyond shaping immune circuits, TNF-α can directly influence epithelial programs7,8. However, how TNF-α–rich inflammatory milieus are converted into durable transcriptional outputs that support epithelial remodeling and tumor-promoting microenvironments is still not fully understood.

Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1), a Hippo pathway–regulated transcriptional co-activator, is a compelling candidate to bridge injury responses and tumor biology in the intestine9–11. YAP1 is largely restrained during homeostasis but becomes engaged during epithelial repair12,13, and sustained YAP1 activity has been linked to tumor-promoting programs and adverse outcomes in CRC14. Emerging work also suggests that YAP1 interfaces with inflammatory signaling and can regulate immune-related gene expression15–17. These observations raise an important question: does TNF-α promote YAP1 accumulation and nuclear localization in the inflamed colon, and what downstream effector program couples YAP1 signaling to inflammation-associated tumor phenotypes?

Here, we define a TNF-α–responsive program in which YAP1 drives expression of the chemokine CCL20, a CCR6 ligand implicated in immune cell trafficking in intestinal inflammation18,19. Guided by transcriptomic profiling after YAP1 silencing, we identify CCL20 as a prominent YAP1-dependent output and demonstrate that YAP1 directly transactivates the CCL20 promoter. Functionally, the TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program contributes to CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. In vivo, verteporfin treatment in DSS colitis was accompanied by reduced CCL20 levels and decreased mucosal immune cell accumulation, and combined YAP pathway inhibition and CCL20 knockdown limited xenograft growth. Together, these findings provide a mechanistic link between inflammatory cytokine exposure and chemokine output in colorectal inflammation and cancer, and suggest that the TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program may represent a candidate pathway for further investigation.

Materials and methods

Human tissue specimens

Colonic tissue specimens were collected at the Third Xiangya Hospital of Central South University from patients with ulcerative colitis (UC, n = 11) and colorectal cancer (CRC, n = 13). For CRC cases, paired adjacent non-tumor tissues were obtained from the same patients. CRC diagnosis was confirmed histologically and staged according to the AJCC/UICC TNM classification. All sections were independently reviewed by two board-certified pathologists. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of The Third Xiangya Hospital (K-26090) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture

Human NCM460 (normal colonic epithelial cell line) and HCT116 (CRC cell line) were obtained from the Institute of Tumor Research, Central South University. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Plasmid and transfection

HCT116 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The pGL3-Basic-CCL20 promoter plasmid and pGL3-Basic-MUT-CCL20 site mutation plasmid were purchased from Hunan Exploration Biotechnology Co. pLV-hef1a-mNeongreen-P2A-Puro-WPRE-CMV-YAP11 overexpression vector was purchased from Beijing Hesheng Biotechnology Co. Ltd. pCDH-CMV-CCL20(human)−2-EF1a-CopGFP-T2A-Puro was purchased from Wuhan Miaoling Biotechnology Co. sh-CCL20 lentiviral plasmid was purchased from Hanheng Biotechnology Co. siRNA was purchased from Sangong Bioengineering (Shanghai) Co.

Experimental animals

BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) and male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old, 20–22 g) were purchased from Hunan Slake Jingda experimental animal Co., Ltd. Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Central South University (Approval No. 20220318) and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines. No anesthesia was used for subcutaneous tumor cell inoculation. Mice were euthanized by gradual-fill carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death.

DSS-induced colitis

After one week of acclimatization, C57BL/6J mice were randomized into five groups (n = 6 per group): Control, DSS+vehicle (DMSO), DSS+infliximab, DSS+verteporfin, and DSS+infliximab+verteporfin. Experimental colitis was induced by administering 2.5% DSS in drinking water for 7 days, with DSS solution refreshed every 2 days. Infliximab (30 mg/kg) and verteporfin (30 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally once daily throughout DSS exposure; control mice received the corresponding vehicle.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

Colon tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 5-µm slices for histopathological analysis using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Histological scoring was conducted based on the degree of inflammation, lymphocytic infiltration, crypt damage, epithelial erosion, and structural abnormalities in the colonic wall.

Clinical score assessment

Mice were monitored daily for body weight changes, diarrhea, and fecal blood. Hemoccult testing was performed using blood detection kits (Built Biological Engineering Research Institute, Nanjing, China). The disease activity index (DAI) was calculated based on weight loss, stool consistency, and the presence of blood in feces.

Xenograft tumor model

To evaluate the in vivo effects of combined YAP1 inhibition and CCL20 suppression, HCT116 cells stably expressing shCCL20 or control vector were used to establish subcutaneous xenografts. Cells were resuspended in serum-free RPMI-1640 at 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL, and 2 × 10⁶ cells in 200 µL were injected subcutaneously into the axillary region of nude mice. Mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5 per group): vehicle, verteporfin, vehicle+shCCL20, and verteporfin+shCCL20. Verteporfin was administered intraperitoneally at 30 mg/kg/day starting on day 5 post-inoculation. Tumor size was measured every 2–3 days with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as V = (length × width²)/2. On day 35, mice were euthanized by gradual-fill CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, and tumors were excised and weighed.

HCT116 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min. HCT116 cells were co-transfected with CCL20 promoter luciferase reporter plasmids (WT or MUT) and the Renilla luciferase control plasmid. Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Total RNA from cells and tissues was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen, CA). cDNA was synthesized using 2 µg of total RNA and a cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific). Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by SDS–PAGE (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). After transfer, the membranes were cut horizontally according to the positions of the molecular-weight markers and before incubation with the primary antibodies. The upper membrane segment containing the expected molecular-weight region of YAP1 was probed with the anti-YAP1 antibody, whereas the lower membrane segment was probed with the anti-GAPDH antibody. Consequently, full-length images of intact membranes containing both YAP1 and GAPDH were not generated. Original images of the complete membrane segments from all independent experiments are provided in the Supplementary Information. The membrane segments were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C. Protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and captured using a ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad).

ELISA

Human CCL20 in cell-culture supernatants was quantified using a Human CCL20/MIP-3α ELISA kit (4 A Biotech), whereas mouse serum CCL20 was measured using a mouse CCL20 ELISA kit (Bioswamp), according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay at indicated time points. For wound-healing assays, confluent HCT116 monolayers were scratched with a sterile pipette tip, and wound closure was imaged at 0 h and 72 h; migration was quantified as the percentage of wound closure. For invasion assays, HCT116 cells (1.5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded into Matrigel-coated Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size; Corning) in medium containing 1% FBS. The lower chamber contained complete medium with 10% FBS as chemoattractant. After 48 h, cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained, and counted in five random high-power fields (100×).

Bioinformatics correlation analysis

Gene expression data were obtained from TCGA and GEO datasets GSE206285, GSE75214, and GSE73661. The JASPAR database was used to predict potential transcription-factor-binding sites within the CCL20 promoter.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated. Two-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test. Multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with appropriate multiple-comparison correction. Body weight and DAI over time were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TNF-α induces YAP1 upregulation in colitis and colorectal cancer

To evaluate YAP1 dysregulation across colorectal inflammation and malignancy, we analyzed public transcriptomic datasets. YAP1 expression was significantly increased in ulcerative colitis (UC) compared with healthy controls in GSE206285, and was also elevated in colorectal cancer (CRC) relative to normal tissues in TCGA (Fig. 1 A). We next explored inflammatory cues that might account for this increase. In HCT116 cells, TNF-α markedly elevated YAP1 mRNA, whereas LPS did not produce a comparable effect under these conditions (Fig. 1 C). Consistently, in GSE73661, YAP1 expression showed a downward trend in UC patients receiving infliximab therapy, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1B). We then treated HCT116 cells with increasing concentrations of TNF-α for 48 h. TNF-α increased YAP1 protein abundance (Fig. 1D) and induced a dose-dependent rise in YAP1 mRNA (Fig. 1E). A dose-dependent increase in YAP1 mRNA was also observed in NCM460 cells following TNF-α treatment (Fig. 1G). Moreover, infliximab attenuated TNF-α–induced YAP1 mRNA expression in HCT116 cells (Fig. 1 F) and reduced YAP1 protein levels (Fig. 1H), supporting TNF-α–dependent regulation of YAP1. In patient cohorts, TNF-α and YAP1 showed a modest positive association in both UC (GSE206285) and CRC (TCGA) datasets (Fig. 1I). Immunofluorescence analysis in HCT116 cells further indicated increased nuclear accumulation of YAP1 following TNF-α stimulation (Fig. 1 J). Finally, immunohistochemistry confirmed higher TNF-α and YAP1 staining in UC and CRC tissues than in normal controls, and quantification of staining intensity supported these differences (Fig. 1 K).

YAP1 activates CCL20 transcription in colorectal cancer cells

To identify YAP1-dependent genes in colorectal cancer, we performed RNA-seq in HCT116 cells after YAP1 knockdown. CCL20 emerged as one of the most strongly downregulated transcripts (Fig. 2 A). We then manipulated YAP1 levels in HCT116 cells and quantified CCL20 expression by RT–qPCR. siRNA-mediated YAP1 silencing reduced CCL20 mRNA, whereas forced expression of YAP1 increased CCL20 mRNA (Fig. 2C–D), indicating that YAP1 positively regulates CCL20 in CRC cells. We next examined whether CCL20 is upregulated in intestinal inflammation and colorectal cancer. In public datasets, CCL20 expression was higher in ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease than in controls (GSE75214), and was also increased in CRC compared with normal tissues (TCGA) (Fig. 2B). Consistently, immunohistochemistry showed stronger CCL20 staining in UC and CRC specimens than in normal controls, and quantification confirmed an overall increase in staining intensity in disease groups (Fig. 2E). Because YAP1 is a transcriptional co-activator, we tested whether YAP1 directly promotes CCL20 transcription through the CCL20 promoter. Motif analysis (JASPAR) identified two candidate TEAD-binding regions (P1 and P2) within the CCL20 promoter (Fig. 2F–G). ChIP–qPCR showed significant YAP1 occupancy at P2 but not P1 (Fig. 2H), consistent with recruitment of YAP1 to a TEAD motif–containing promoter element. In a dual-luciferase reporter assay, YAP1 overexpression increased the activity of the wild-type CCL20 promoter, whereas mutation of the P2 site abolished this induction (Fig. 2I). Together, these results support a model in which YAP1 drives CCL20 transcription by occupying the CCL20 promoter in CRC cells.

TNF-α induces CCL20 in a YAP1-dependent manner in CRC

To compare the basal transcript levels of YAP1 and CCL20 between non-tumor and CRC epithelial cell models, we analyzed NCM460 and HCT116 cells under the same culture conditions. RT–qPCR analysis showed that HCT116 cells exhibited significantly higher basal mRNA levels of both YAP1 and CCL20 than NCM460 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings demonstrate differences in basal transcript abundance between the two cell lines but do not establish greater pathway activity or increased TNF-α sensitivity in HCT116 cells. We next examined whether TNF-α induces CCL20 expression in colorectal epithelial and cancer cells. After treatment with increasing concentrations of TNF-α for 48 h, CCL20 mRNA levels increased in a dose-dependent manner in both HCT116 (Fig. 3A) and NCM460 cells (Fig. 3B). Consistently, ELISA confirmed a parallel increase in secreted CCL20 protein in HCT116 cell supernatants (Fig. 3C). To further support the clinical relevance of this observation, we analyzed GSE73661 and found that CCL20 expression was reduced in UC patients after infliximab treatment compared with pre-treatment levels (Fig. 3D). We then tested whether YAP1 is required for TNF-α-induced CCL20 expression. In HCT116 cells, TNF-α stimulation increased CCL20 expression, whereas YAP1 knockdown markedly reduced basal CCL20 levels and blunted TNF-α-mediated CCL20 induction at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3E–F).

TNF-α promotes colorectal cancer proliferation, migration and invasion via YAP1

We assessed whether YAP1 mediates TNF-α–driven malignant phenotypes in colorectal cancer cells using HCT116 cells. Following TNF-α stimulation, YAP1 was silenced by siRNA and cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were evaluated. In the Transwell assay, TNF-α markedly increased the number of invading HCT116 cells. YAP1 knockdown substantially reduced invasion and largely abolished the TNF-α–induced increase in invading cells (Fig. 4A–B). In the wound-healing assay, TNF-α accelerated wound closure, indicating enhanced migratory capacity. This effect was attenuated when YAP1 was silenced (Fig. 4C–D). Consistently, CCK-8 assays showed that TNF-α promoted HCT116 cell growth over time, whereas YAP1 knockdown significantly suppressed proliferation and blunted the proliferative response to TNF-α (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results indicate that TNF-α promotes HCT116 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and that YAP1 is required for maximal TNF-α–induced malignant phenotypes.

YAP1 promotes colorectal cancer proliferation, migration and invasion through regulation of CCL20

To determine whether CCL20 mediates YAP1-driven malignant phenotypes, we performed rescue experiments in HCT116 cells. Cells were transfected with control (NC+Vector), siYAP1 (siYAP1 + Vector), or siYAP1 together with CCL20 overexpression (siYAP1 + oe-CCL20). RT–qPCR confirmed efficient YAP1 knockdown and restoration of CCL20 expression upon CCL20 overexpression (Fig. 5A). Functionally, YAP1 silencing significantly reduced HCT116 cell proliferation measured by CCK-8, whereas CCL20 overexpression partially restored cell growth (Fig. 5B). In wound-healing assays, YAP1 knockdown slowed wound closure, and CCL20 overexpression increased migratory activity compared with siYAP1 alone (Fig. 5C–D). In Matrigel-coated Transwell assays, YAP1 knockdown markedly decreased invasive capacity, while CCL20 overexpression rescued the reduced invasion phenotype (Fig. 5E–F). Collectively, these findings support that CCL20 acts downstream of YAP1 and contributes to CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

YAP1 pathway inhibition alleviates DSS-induced colitis and is associated with reduced CCL20 expression

We established DSS-induced colitis (2.5% DSS) and treated mice (n = 6 per group) with infliximab (30 mg/kg, i.p.), verteporfin (30 mg/kg, i.p.), or the combination. DSS caused progressive weight loss and increased disease activity index (DAI) scores compared with controls. Verteporfin, alone or combined with infliximab, significantly mitigated weight loss and reduced DAI scores, whereas infliximab alone showed a modest improvement trend (Fig. 6C–D). On day 7, mice were euthanized and colons were harvested for macroscopic and histological analyses. DSS induced marked colon shortening, which was partially reversed by infliximab or verteporfin and most evident in the combination group (Fig. 6A–B). H&E staining showed pronounced epithelial injury, crypt distortion, and inflammatory infiltration in DSS colitis, accompanied by elevated histopathology scores; these changes were attenuated by verteporfin and by the combination treatment (Fig. 6E–F). We next assessed whether these improvements were accompanied by changes in Yap1 and Ccl20 expression. Colonic Yap1 mRNA was increased after DSS and reduced in mice receiving verteporfin and/or infliximab (Fig. 6I). Consistently, serum CCL20 levels and colonic Ccl20 mRNA were lower in treated groups than in DSS alone (Fig. 6G–H). Immunofluorescence further showed prominent accumulation of CD3⁺ T cells, CD19⁺ B cells, CD11c⁺ dendritic cells, and FOXP3⁺ regulatory T cells in DSS colitis, with reduced mucosal accumulation in treated groups (Fig. 6 J). Collectively, verteporfin treatment alleviated DSS-induced colitis and was associated with reduced Ccl20 expression and diminished inflammatory cell accumulation.

Combined YAP pathway inhibition and CCL20 knockdown suppress HCT116 xenograft growth

To evaluate the in vivo relevance of combined YAP pathway inhibition and CCL20 suppression, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using HCT116 cells with stable CCL20 knockdown (shCCL20). Nude mice were allocated into four groups (n = 5): control (NC), verteporfin, shCCL20, and verteporfin plus shCCL20. Verteporfin (30 mg/kg/day, i.p.) or vehicle was administered starting on day 5 after inoculation. Compared with controls, verteporfin treatment or CCL20 knockdown alone slowed tumor growth, as reflected by reduced tumor volumes throughout the observation period (Fig. 7C). Notably, the combination group exhibited the strongest suppression of xenograft expansion. On day 35, tumors were excised for macroscopic assessment and weighing. Tumors from the combination group were visibly smaller (Fig. 7A–B) and had the lowest tumor weights among groups (Fig. 7D). Together, these results indicate that combined YAP pathway inhibition and CCL20 knockdown produce an enhanced antitumor effect in this xenograft model.

Discussion

Chronic colonic inflammation is a recognized driver of colorectal carcinogenesis, yet the molecular steps that translate inflammatory cytokine cues into durable epithelial programs and microenvironmental remodeling are still being defined20–22. In this study, we identified a TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program in colorectal inflammation and cancer. Our data show that TNF-α increases YAP1 mRNA in both NCM460 and HCT116 cells and increases YAP1 protein abundance and nuclear accumulation in HCT116 cells. YAP1 directly promotes CCL20 transcription through a TEAD motif-containing promoter region, and CCL20 contributes, at least in part, to CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. In vivo, YAP pathway inhibition and/or anti-TNF treatment alleviated DSS-induced colitis and was accompanied by reduced YAP1/CCL20 expression and decreased mucosal immune cell accumulation, whereas combined YAP pathway inhibition and CCL20 knockdown suppressed xenograft tumor growth. These findings support a role for the TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program in inflammatory chemokine remodeling and CRC cell-associated malignant phenotypes.

YAP1 has been positioned as a “repair-associated” regulator in the intestine23: it is largely restrained during homeostasis but becomes engaged after injury to support regenerative epithelial states and crypt expansion24,25. This regenerative function provides a biological rationale for why inflammatory milieus may engage YAP126,27, but it also highlights a key tension in colitis-associated cancer—programs that promote repair can become maladaptive when chronically sustained. Consistent with this concept, TNF-α increased YAP1 mRNA in both NCM460 and HCT116 cells and increased YAP1 protein abundance and nuclear accumulation in HCT116 cells, whereas infliximab attenuated TNF-α-induced YAP1 mRNA and protein upregulation in HCT116 cells. These findings suggest that TNF-rich inflammatory conditions may engage YAP1 as part of an epithelial stress- and repair-associated response.

Although YAP1 regulates broad transcriptional programs, our RNA-seq analysis identified CCL20 as one of the most prominently downregulated transcripts after YAP1 silencing. CCL20 was prioritized because it is consistently induced in inflamed intestinal mucosa and, as a CCR6 ligand, provides a plausible mechanism by which epithelial transcriptional changes may influence immune cell trafficking. In parallel, CCL20 has been implicated in CRC progression and tumor cell motility28,29. Our mechanistic assays support direct transcriptional regulation of CCL20 by YAP1. Bidirectional manipulation of YAP1 altered CCL20 mRNA and secreted protein levels, ChIP–qPCR showed YAP1 enrichment at the CCL20 promoter, and mutation of the predicted TEAD-binding element abolished YAP1-dependent promoter activation. Because YAP1 lacks a DNA-binding domain, these findings are consistent with YAP1 acting through TEAD-containing transcriptional complexes to regulate CCL20 expression.

The TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program should be interpreted within the broader inflammatory signaling network rather than as an exclusive linear pathway. TNF-α is a canonical activator of NF-κB signaling, and NF-κB is known to regulate multiple inflammatory chemokines, including CCL2030,31. Previous studies have shown that TNF-α-induced CCL20 expression can involve TNFR1-dependent EGFR/p38, JNK, and NF-κB-related pathways32–34. Therefore, the YAP1-dependent mechanism identified here may cooperate with, converge with, or act in parallel to canonical TNF-α–NF-κB signaling. Our current data demonstrate that YAP1 contributes to TNF-α-induced CCL20 expression, but they do not exclude the contribution of NF-κB or other inflammatory transcription factors. Future studies using NF-κB inhibition, p65 ChIP–qPCR, and combined perturbation of YAP1 and NF-κB signaling will be required to define the relative contribution and potential crosstalk between these pathways.

Functionally, our data support CCL20 as an important downstream effector of YAP1 in CRC cells. TNF-α promoted HCT116 cell proliferation, migration, and Matrigel invasion, and these effects were attenuated by YAP1 knockdown. Moreover, CCL20 overexpression partially rescued the impaired growth and motility caused by YAP1 silencing, indicating that CCL20 is not merely a marker of YAP1 activity but contributes to YAP1-associated malignant phenotypes. The partial rescue is also informative, as YAP1 likely regulates additional downstream genes that cooperate with CCL20 to shape CRC cell behavior.

Our data support observations in two distinct experimental contexts but do not establish differential regulation or function of the YAP1–CCL20 pathway between colitis and CRC. In the DSS-induced colitis model, increased colonic YAP1 and CCL20 expression was accompanied by mucosal accumulation of multiple immune-cell populations, whereas verteporfin and/or infliximab treatment reduced CCL20 expression and decreased the accumulation of CD3 + T cells, CD19 + B cells, CD11c+ dendritic cells, and FOXP3 + regulatory T cells. Because CCR6 was not quantified in the current staining panel, these findings should be interpreted as being compatible with reduced chemokine-associated immune-cell recruitment rather than as definitive evidence of CCR6-dependent recruitment. In HCT116 cells, YAP1-dependent CCL20 expression contributed to proliferation, migration, invasion, and xenograft tumor growth. However, matched YAP1 and CCL20 perturbation experiments were not performed in non-tumor or UC-derived epithelial cells. Therefore, the present study cannot determine whether the YAP1–CCL20 pathway is functionally distinct or selectively utilized in CRC cells compared with inflamed non-malignant epithelium.

The xenograft experiment further supports the functional relevance of the YAP1–CCL20 pathway in an established CRC model. Verteporfin treatment or CCL20 knockdown alone reduced tumor growth, whereas the combined intervention produced the greatest reduction in tumor volume and weight. We interpret this pattern as an enhanced antitumor effect rather than formal pharmacological synergy, which would require dedicated interaction analyses. Thus, the xenograft findings support the contribution of YAP-dependent transcriptional output and CCL20 to tumor growth in this model, without establishing whether the relative dependence on this pathway differs between malignant and non-malignant epithelial cells.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the DSS-induced colitis model primarily reflects epithelial injury, barrier disruption, and acute inflammatory responses, but it does not recapitulate the full process of colitis-associated colorectal tumorigenesis. Therefore, our DSS data should not be interpreted as direct evidence that the TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program drives CRC initiation. AOM/DSS or genetically engineered colitis-associated cancer models will be required to determine whether this pathway contributes directly to inflammation-driven tumor initiation or progression. Second, although our data show that TNF-α increases YAP1 mRNA in both NCM460 and HCT116 cells and increases YAP1 protein abundance and nuclear accumulation in HCT116 cells, the precise upstream mechanism linking TNF-α stimulation to YAP1 activation remains incompletely resolved. We did not systematically examine YAP1 phosphorylation, upstream Hippo pathway components, YAP1 protein stability, or TNF-α-induced changes in YAP1 chromatin occupancy. Thus, our findings support a TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program rather than a fully defined linear signaling cascade from TNF-α to YAP1. Third, although CCL20 restoration partially rescued the impaired proliferation, migration, and invasion caused by YAP1 silencing, we did not systematically profile cytokine or chemokine secretion in oeCCL20 cells. Therefore, our data do not establish a self-amplifying inflammatory feedback loop. Fourth, NCM460 cells were included as a non-tumor colonic epithelial comparator rather than as a cellular model of UC. Although TNF-α induced YAP1 and CCL20 mRNA expression in NCM460 cells, protein-level responses, infliximab sensitivity, and matched YAP1-silencing and CCL20-overexpression experiments were not assessed in NCM460 or UC-derived epithelial models. Therefore, the present study was not designed to establish whether differences in basal YAP1 expression translate into distinct functional responses between non-tumor and CRC epithelial cells. Future validation in additional CRC cell lines, UC-derived epithelial models, patient-derived organoids, and clinical cohorts would further extend the generalizability of these findings.

In summary, our data support a model in which TNF-α induces YAP1 mRNA expression in both NCM460 and HCT116 cells and promotes YAP1 protein accumulation and nuclear localization in HCT116 cells. YAP1 directly induces CCL20 transcription, and this chemokine program contributes to CRC cell malignant phenotypes while accompanying inflammatory immune-cell enrichment in colitis. These findings identify a TNF-α-responsive YAP1–CCL20 program linking inflammatory cytokine exposure to chemokine-associated epithelial and microenvironmental remodeling. Further studies are required to define the upstream mechanisms of TNF-α-mediated YAP1 activation and to determine whether this pathway directly contributes to colitis-associated colorectal tumor initiation or progression.
