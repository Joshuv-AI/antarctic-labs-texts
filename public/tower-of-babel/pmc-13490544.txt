Will participating in entrepreneurship competitions enhance college students’ employability? — evidence from Sichuan Province, China
Authors: Liping Wang, Ruying Xu, Ke Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
China’s economy has been downward since the pandemic. At the same time, the scale of college students’ graduation has reached its peak, and the contradiction between employment supply and demand has become increasingly prominent. Since implementing the dual-creation strategy, significant universities have generally emphasized the implementation of entrepreneurship education, and entrepreneurship competitions have become an essential part of quality education for college students. Against this background, the article explores whether participating in entrepreneurship competitions positively impacts the employability of Chinese college students, with employability operationalized as self-perceived employability. The article is based on 3,595 micro college students’ research data in Sichuan Province, using Propensity Score Matching to conduct a quantitative study. The study finds that participation in entrepreneurship competitions has a significant positive effect on improving the employability of university students. The higher the level of the school, the less critical the impact of entrepreneurship competitions on enhancing employability. Participation in entrepreneurship competitions has a more significant effect on promoting employability in the female student population. Colleges and universities should actively guide college students to participate in entrepreneurship competitions to enhance their employability, encourage the construction of platforms for entrepreneurship competitions, ensure the quality and quantity of entrepreneurship competition projects, and strengthen differentiated support.

Introduction

The popularization of higher education has exacerbated the imbalance between labor supply and demand1, making college students’ employment difficulty a prominent social concern2. Since China implemented the “college expansion” policy in 1999, the rapid expansion of higher education has led to an overall decline in educational quality and even academic credential depreciation3. According to the latest data from China’s Ministry of Education4, the number of 2024 graduates from national general colleges and universities is expected to reach 11.79 million, an increase of 210,000 year-on-year. In the post-COVID-19 era, amid economic recession and escalating international disputes, “post-90s” and “post-00s” college graduates are entering or preparing to enter the labor market during a critical structural transformation of China’s economy—shifting from high-speed to moderate growth. This context has further heightened the uncertainty surrounding university graduates’ employment5. Chinese college students’ employment intentions have shown a diversified trend. Phenomena such as delayed employment and new-type dependency on parents have emerged, alongside prominent characteristics of “further education involution” and “institutional involution.” Against this backdrop, enhancing college students’ employability and alleviating their employment predicament have become particularly crucial.

Employability is a core competency that should be present on the supply side of the labor market6 and is often understood as the skills and attributes that make it possible for graduates to find a job and succeed7. In research on undergraduate employability, the focus has been on perceived employability8. Gamboa, et al.9 define perceived employability as “an individual’s perception that they have access to a job opportunity of their choosing “. The personal resource perspective views employability as including skills, competencies, and self-perceptions, which are resources that contribute to the acquisition of employability10. The dilemma of employability development lies in the lack of student participation in employability-building activities11 and the mismatch between the skills acquired in major-based undergraduate programs and the needs of society12. Entrepreneurship competition programs combine traditional theoretical and practical approaches, and participation in entrepreneurship competitions promotes the development of university students at both the internal (entrepreneurial skills and spirit, self-directed learning ability, self-efficacy) and external levels (social relationships). From the employer’s point of view, it enhances the productivity and attractiveness of the students in the labor market, which in turn makes them more employable.

Entrepreneurship competitions are one of the most widely practiced forms of entrepreneurship education. It first originated in the United States13. The inception of entrepreneurship competitions in China can be traced back to the inaugural “Challenge Cup” business plan contest held by Tsinghua University in 199714. The main competitions include the Business Plan Competition15, and the “Internet+” Competition16, among others. A highly impactful competition is the “Internet+” Innovation and Entrepreneurship Competition for College Students in China. The competition has a broad global appeal, with 9.43 million teams and 39.83 million university students from more than 100 countries/regions at home and abroad17.

As a highly contextualized and practice-driven educational vehicle, entrepreneurship competitions transcend the scope of knowledge transmission in traditional classrooms by simulating real-world entrepreneurial pressures and decision-making environments, thereby providing students with an irreplaceable platform for skill development18 This model achieves more effective outcomes compared to conventional education that emphasizes theoretical knowledge transmission. Previous research on entrepreneurship education has predominantly focused on dedicated entrepreneurship courses19. In contrast, this study concentrates on the specific context of entrepreneurship competitions, which are not merely a subset of general entrepreneurship education but rather a practice-oriented mechanism with distinct attributes, higher intensity, and more focused objectives. Functioning as a targeted educational intervention, entrepreneurship competitions are characterized by project-based experiential learning, real-time feedback from industry experts and investors, and competitive incentives for innovation and problem-solving. These features position competitions as a “bridge” to real labor market demands, making them a unique and effective intervention for enhancing employability. In recent years, many scholars have conducted more in-depth discussions and empirical analyses of entrepreneurship competitions and found that the role of entrepreneurship competitions is reflected in the enhancement of entrepreneurial knowledge and skills20, entrepreneurial spirit21, and entrepreneurial resources22, but their role in enhancing employability has not been widely validated.

Sichuan Province, located in the west of China, has always been the country’s strategic hinterland. It is a province with a large population, science and education, and economy. Based on the statistical results in 202323, Sichuan Province ranks fifth in China with a total GDP of 6.01 trillion yuan and ranks first among all provinces and regions in central and western China. The permanent population is about 83.68 million, and the registered population is 90.714 million. Sichuan Province is actively responding to the call of the state by organizing various kinds of innovation and entrepreneurship competitions to provide a platform for college students to show themselves and exercise their abilities and, at the same time, promote the in-depth integration of industry and academia. In recent years, the number of participating schools, students, and projects of the “Challenge Cup” Sichuan Student Entrepreneurship Program Competition has grown significantly, and the 2024 competition attracted students from 126 colleges, universities, and vocational colleges across the province to participate, with 1,440 projects submitted. These results and experiences are important inspirations and promotions for reforming innovation and entrepreneurship education in colleges and economic and social development in other regions of China.

This study makes three primary contributions. First, while existing research on the role of entrepreneurship competitions has primarily focused on their impact on participants’ entrepreneurial learning and capabilities24,25, this study empirically demonstrates that such competitions also significantly enhance university students’ employability, thereby highlighting their value in non-entrepreneurial career pathways. Second, in terms of research subjects, prior studies have often sampled students from elite universities26, which may limit the generalizability of findings due to their inherent employment advantages and fail to reflect the broader landscape of China’s higher education system. This study expands the sample to include students from ordinary undergraduate institutions and vocational colleges—groups that constitute the majority of China’s higher education enrollment and often face greater employment challenges. The findings thus offer broader relevance and better align with the stratified reality of China’s higher education structure. Third, regarding research scope and mechanisms, previous studies have typically examined entrepreneurship competitions within the broader framework of “entrepreneurship education”27, making it difficult to isolate their effects from other educational activities such as courses and seminars. By focusing specifically on entrepreneurship competitions as a distinct intervention, this study attempts to identify their “net effect” on employability enhancement. Furthermore, it explores heterogeneity in effects across gender, institution type, and academic background, providing an empirical basis and policy insights for universities to design and manage competitions in a more differentiated and targeted manner.

Theories and hypothesis development

This study develops an integrated analytical framework to systematically examine the impact of entrepreneurship competition participation on university students’ employability. Grounded in human capital theory, social capital theory, and the theoretical construct of psychological capital, we propose that entrepreneurship competitions constitute a critical experiential mechanism through which students acquire, develop, and consolidate three complementary forms of capital essential for labor market success. Specifically, we posit that such competitions simultaneously enhance students’ knowledge and skill portfolios (human capital), foster adaptive psychological resources (psychological capital), and expand their relational networks (social capital). These three dimensions operate synergistically, creating a compounding effect on employability development. The subsequent sections delineate the theoretical pathways through which each form of capital contributes to employability, culminating in our principal research hypothesis.

The mechanism of human capital

Entrepreneurship competitions are a valuable means to augment entrepreneurial knowledge and skills and furnish a realm for nurturing students’ entrepreneurial spirit28. Students obtain the requisite skills and knowledge for initiating and evolving new ventures by participating in the competition29 by developing, repeatedly revising, and refining their proposals. These skills can be used in a work environment30. Participating in competitions cultivates an entrepreneurial mindset through hands-on experience, enhancing their ability to leapfrog challenges and achieve ambitious goals31. Competitors are immersed in authentic industry scenarios, experiencing the entire entrepreneurial process. Each phase of the competition challenges their practical abilities. These invaluable hands-on experiences are precisely what traditional classroom-based entrepreneurship education struggles to provide.

Entrepreneurship competitions foster self-directed learning. As an experiential, participatory learning tool, distinct from the traditional entrepreneurship education courses in which students passively accept theoretical knowledge, the entrepreneurship competition enables students to learn relevant entrepreneurial knowledge and policies actively. Competitions afford students the chance to acquire perceptions from their counterparts20 and stimulate them to boost their capabilities. Team members apply their knowledge and skills to develop a viable solution to a well-defined problem while participating in a competition. This can enhance the participants’ problem-solving proficiencies, concurrently nurturing teamwork and experiential learning32.

Human capital typically refers to the knowledge, skills, and work proficiency possessed by an individual33. Existing research indicates that university students’ human capital significantly and positively influences their employability34. From the perspective of employee development, innovation capability is regarded as a core skill in lifelong learning (European Commission, 2013), and university students’ employment competitiveness is closely tied to their learning and innovation abilities35. In this context, entrepreneurship competitions enhance participants’ employability by stimulating innovative thinking and strengthening self-directed learning capabilities. From the employer’s perspective, amid the Fourth Industrial Revolution, enterprises increasingly require talent with entrepreneurial skills36. Entrepreneurship competitions serve as an effective platform to cultivate these skills, and graduates equipped with such competencies are more favored by employers compared to those lacking them37. Therefore, it can be argued that entrepreneurship competitions substantially enhance university students’ overall human capital, which is further reflected in their long-term capacity to secure sustainable wages throughout their careers.

The mechanism of psychological capital

Psychological capital consists of four core components: hope, self-efficacy, resilience, and optimism. These factors are particularly influential for university students, who are at a critical stage of personal and professional development38.

Competitions serve as highly competitive, practice-oriented platforms. Participants must manage setbacks from their projects while also handling pressure from peers and rivals. This environment challenges students to persevere under stress, thereby strengthening their resilience—a key capacity not only for coping with adversity but also for enhancing long-term employability39.

Entrepreneurship competitions also help participants build practical skills and confidence in areas such as public speaking, networking, and business planning, which reinforces their self-efficacy25. According to social learning theory, prior experience in such competitions is directly linked to the development of self-efficacy40. Self-efficacy reflects an individual’s ability to assess their own capabilities realistically and maintain confidence in their competence41. Students with higher self-efficacy tend to transition more successfully from university to the workplace and generally outperform their peers, indicating its strong predictive role in employability42.

The mechanism of social capital

Entrepreneurship competitions are a collaborative and competitive educational mechanism that facilitates the favorable interchange of knowledge and the attainment of resources40,43 and provides a space for participants to learn from each other and connect with other stakeholders, developing their social networks44. The most common way to evaluate a business plan or model is by a jury of external experts45. These experts represent experienced entrepreneurs, venture capitalists, bankers, financiers, academics, university staff46, and entrepreneurial or business consultants47. Beyond conventional academic exposure, entrepreneurship competitions facilitate direct engagement with industry experts, enabling students to gain industry insights, broaden their professional outlook, and refine career decision-making. Furthermore, participants may obtain both material rewards and expanded access to social resources through competition outcomes, thereby enhancing both informational and relational capital22. Certain exceptional entrepreneurial initiatives in competitions can secure funding from businesses, governmental bodies, and venture capitalists48. University students engage more with social enterprises in financing, thereby accessing social resources and transforming them for utilization. Entrepreneurship competitions provide students with a valuable opportunity to expand their networks, thereby enhancing their social capital (Fig. 1).

Enhanced social capital significantly boosts employability34, primarily through two key mechanisms: First, it helps establish an extensive and reliable information network, enabling students to access high-quality job opportunities in a more timely manner. Second, it can materially influence the recruitment process—often referred to as “using connections” or “backdoor access”—which has become an informal yet widely recognized practice in China’s relationship-driven social context49.

In the labor market, human capital serves as the essential hard competence that qualifies graduates for selection; psychological capital acts as the internal engine that empowers them to actively choose and persevere; and social capital opens the gateway to information and opportunities, enhancing their visibility and access to selection. The synergy of these three dimensions enables graduates to stand out in the competitive employment landscape. Based on the above three aspects, this paper puts forward the following hypotheses:

H: Participation in entrepreneurship competitions can enhance the employability of university students.

Study area and data source

Study area

Sichuan Province is located in the southwest of China and ranks fifth in China in population52. The Sichuan government attaches great importance to education, and its educational resources are high nationwide. According to the Sichuan Provincial Department of Finance, it plans to invest 36.05 billion yuan in education in 202453. Sichuan Province has a total of 139 ordinary higher education institutions, including 85 higher vocational schools and 54 undergraduate colleges, of which there are five “985&211” program colleges54. The “985 and 211 Project” aims to build several world-class universities and disciplines with strong research capabilities and outstanding faculty, representing the top level of China’s higher education. The college graduates in 2024 in Sichuan Province totaled 647,000, an increase of 5.56% year-on-year, of which 49,000 were postgraduates, 294,000 were undergraduates, and 304,000 were specialists55. The proportion of graduates intending to be employed in state-owned enterprises is the highest, accounting for more than 30%, followed by party and government organizations. Therefore, it is typical and representative to take Sichuan Province as the source region of the data sample. It should be noted, however, that as this study is situated in the specific regional context of Western China, the conclusions drawn from this sample may not be directly transferable to regions with considerably different economic environments—such as the more developed coastal hubs, where labor market dynamics may diverge substantially.

Questionnaire design and implementation

Considering the diverse composition of China’s higher education system, this study covers three types of institutions—985/211 universities, general undergraduate colleges, and vocational colleges—to ensure the sample reflects the characteristics of institutions at different educational levels and with varied training objectives. Priority was given to institutions in Sichuan Province with complete qualifications, standardized management systems, and no major teaching incidents in the past three years. Additionally, through preliminary communication with student affairs offices of various institutions in Sichuan, their willingness to cooperate was confirmed, ensuring institutional support during the survey. The sample distribution across schools followed the principle of “matching institution scale and aligning with group characteristics.” The sample included 713 students from 3 institutions classified as 985/211 universities, such as Sichuan University; 1,141 students from 11 general undergraduate colleges, such as Southwest Minzu University; and 1,741 students from 11 vocational colleges, such as Sichuan Changjiang Vocational College. A simple random sampling method was adopted to select respondents, ensuring adequate coverage across all groups.

The survey utilized an electronic questionnaire via the Wenjuanxing platform, distributed by institutional contacts to student class groups for voluntary online completion. The process included: questionnaire design and institution selection (Nov 1–20, 2023); a pilot survey with 30 students from three institutions to refine the questionnaire (Nov 20–30); and official distribution and data collection (Dec 1–30).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) full-time enrolled students, (2) voluntary participation with informed consent, and (3) complete responses to core variables. Exclusion criteria were applied to remove: (1) submissions with implausibly short completion times (< total items × 2 s), and (2) questionnaires with missing key information. Based on these criteria, 129 invalid responses were excluded, resulting in the final analytical sample of 3,595.

Statistical power and sample size adequacy statement

To ensure adequate statistical power, we conducted an a priori sample size calculation. Assuming a small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.2), α = 0.05, and power = 0.80 for a two-group comparison, the “power twomeans” command in Stata indicated that approximately 394 participants per group (788 in total) were required. Our final sample of 3,595 (808 participants and 2,787 non-participants) far exceeds this threshold, confirming that the study is well‑powered to detect the expected effect.

Variable measurement and descriptive statistical analysis

Explained variable

The paper takes college students’ employability as an explained variable. Drawing on the measures of employability by Knight and Yorke56 and Fugate, et al.57, this paper measures employability with the results of factor analysis on self-management ability, professional application ability, innovative thinking ability, teamwork ability, communication and collaboration ability, and active learning ability. The results were measured by “good at balancing study, work and life,” “able to effectively apply selected solutions to practice,” “often participate in professional, innovative activities and experience innovative products or services,” “an understanding of the team’s goals and is willing to contribute to the team,” " skilled in engaging with others and achieving agreement,” “I can plan my own learning goals” to obtain the data, which was assessed using a five-point Likert scale (Likert scale). Descriptive statistical analysis

Specifically, Nearly 50% of students have attended innovation and entrepreneurship lectures, and over 70% are aware of the competition policies related to the recommendation for graduate studies.; By grade, first-year students accounted for the majority, with 2,308 students, sophomores and juniors, with 760 and 698 students, respectively, and a small number of students were seniors and above. In terms of school categories, there are 713 students in 985 and 211 project colleges and universities, accounting for 19.83%; 1141 students in general undergraduate universities, accounting for 31.74%; and 1,741 students in higher vocational colleges and universities, accounting for 48.43%. Few college students have party membership, only 103, accounting for 2.87% of the total. In terms of school performance, the number of students in the top 10% of the grade is the lowest, the number of students in the top 40% to 60% of the grade is the highest, and the proportion of students in the middle and upper reaches is 54.10%. Regarding family matters, 94.60% of the students had a family size of 4 or less.Most students come from families with an annual income below 150,000 yuan, accounting for 84.76%.

Estimation of decision equations for participation in entrepreneurship competitions

To match college students who participate in competitions with those who do not, this paper utilizes a Logit model to estimate the probability of college students participating in competitions. Table 6 shows the estimation results of the decision model for participating in entrepreneurship competitions, and the results indicate that the probability of college students participating in competitions is significantly related to their characteristics, family characteristics, and social capital. Regarding the personal characteristics of university students, college students’ school performance significantly positively impacts participation in competitions. Being a student leader means that college students are more capable in all aspects and have positive self-perception60. During the period of being a student leader, college students can learn more about entrepreneurship competitions and come into contact with excellent competition teams so that college students as student leaders will be more inclined to participate in the competitions. Grade level has a significant positive effect on college students’ participation in competitions, which may be because college students in higher grade levels have higher entrepreneurial self-efficacy61. It is interesting to note that school level significantly negatively affects college students’ participation in competitions, possibly because students in higher vocational schools have shorter school terms and enter the workforce as soon as they graduate, and focusing on career development is crucial for them62. They lack competitive advantages regarding academic qualifications, so they tend to participate in competitions more often to improve their quality. In addition, social capital and household income have a significant positive effect on college students’ participation in competitions. Individuals with social capital and political capital have advantages in resource allocation63. College students have high-level business practitioners and government officials in their relationship network, and the more they are exposed to entrepreneurial competition policies, resources, and technologies, the more likely they are to participate in the competition.

Fit test for PSM modeling

Common support domain test

The common support domain is a fundamental test to examine the effectiveness of propensity score matching. Different matching methods have different common support domains and sample loss amounts; an increased range of the common support domain corresponds to a reduced likelihood of sample loss during the matching process. The paper uses K-Nearest Neighbor matching(K = 3), Radius matching, and Kernel matching, which are three methods to show the matching effect. Using 1:3 nearest neighbor matching (n = 3) with a caliper of 0.05, we restrict the maximum propensity score difference between matched samples to reduce bias. Only observations within the common support region are retained, and those outside it without valid matches are excluded. As can be seen from Fig. 2, In the common support domain of K-Nearest Neighbor matching, only a very small number of observations fall outside the common range of values. In contrast, in the common support domains of Radius matching and Kernel matching, no observations fall outside the common range of values. This suggests that the level of sample loss during the matching process is minimal, resulting in an effective matching outcome.

Balance test

The balance test functions as a methodology for evaluating whether the variables attain equilibrium between the control and treatment groups subsequent to the matching procedure. Table 7 shows notable reductions post-matching: Pseudo R2 falls sharply (0.185 to 0.004 ~ 0.008), LR statistic declines significantly (708.34 to 9.18 ~ 17.73), significance test changes from highly to non-significant, and both mean and median deviations narrow substantially (26.6% to 3.4% and 26.1% to 2.6% respectively). The test outcomes manifest that after the matching operation, the total sample bias is notably mitigated, the traits of samples among groups are commensurable, and the results of the balance test are gratifying. Table 7a–c further present the distribution of covariates and changes in standardized differences between the treatment and control groups before and after matching. Prior to matching, most covariates showed significant differences between the two groups; after matching, the standardized bias of all covariates decreased substantially. Except for the variable political_profile, the t-test results of other covariates became insignificant.

Heterogeneity analysis

Grouping by school level

The student sample data of the paper involves 985/211 students, general undergraduate universities, and students of higher vocational schools, and different college student groups not only have significant differences in individual and family characteristics but also have noticeable differences in the education, information, and policies of related competitions and the degree of feedback they have been exposed to. To more deeply analyze the impact of participating in entrepreneurship competitions on enhancing the employability of college students, the paper divided the sample college students into two groups of 985/211 and non-985/211 students according to the characteristics of the college student groups, and the sample sizes of each group were 713 and 2882, which accounted for 19.83% and 80.17% of the total sample, respectively. Table 9 presents the proportions of the experimental and control groups in enhancing employability and the ATT obtained from the two college student groups using K-Nearest Neighbor matching, Radius matching, and Kernel matching, respectively. The results show that the ATT values obtained by the 985/211 college student group based on the three types of matching are not significant; the ATT values obtained by the non-985/211 college student group based on the three types of matching are similar, and all of them meet the criteria at the 1% significance level, suggesting that the matching results are fairly robust.

In terms of the ATTof each group match, the ATT values of 985/211 and non-985/211 college students are between 0.115 ~ 0.141 and 0.178 ~ 0.182, respectively, i.e., compared with those who did not participate in the entrepreneurship competition, the proportion of improving employability of non-985/211 college students who participated in the entrepreneurship competition increased by 17.8%~18.2%, indicating that participation in entrepreneurship competitions can effectively promote non-985/211 college students to enhance their employability. However, participation in entrepreneurship competitions does not have a substantial impact on the employability of students from 985/211 universities. The reason may be that relative to 985/211 school college students, non-985/211 college students are often explicit about the lack of competitive advantage in academic qualifications, and their awareness of acquiring resources and skills through participating in entrepreneurship competitions is stronger, with more robust subjective initiative. Therefore, their participation in entrepreneurship competitions has a greater possibility of enhancing employability. In addition, 985/211 college students with high comprehensive quality and good resources have high employability themselves, which may make the effect of participating in entrepreneurship competitions to enhance their employability less obvious. This finding also supports Decker-Lange, et al27. argument that university ranking is a key social factor that weakens the link between entrepreneurship education and employability.

Grouping by gender

In addition, this paper explores the influence of gender. Noddings64 explains the unique caring value of women and theorizes that women are more inclined to create social value than men. The university students were categorized into male and female, with sample sizes of 1215 and 2380, representing 33.80% and 66.20% of the total sample, respectively. Table 10 reveals that participation in entrepreneurship competitions exerts a more pronounced effect on enhancing female college students’ employability. A comparison of matching results across genders shows that this promotional effect is slightly stronger for female students than for male counterparts. In terms of ATT values, females’ ATT exceeds males’ by 0.004–0.049, indicating that entrepreneurship competitions increase female students’ employability improvement rate by an additional 0.4%–4.9% relative to male students. The possible reason for this is that female university students are more conscientious in participating in competitions, which allows them to access more resources and learn more skills, making the effect of participating in entrepreneurship competitions on their employability enhancement more pronounced. Nowiński, et al.65 also noted that female students benefit more from entrepreneurship education than male students.

Instrumental variables approach

Endogeneity mainly stems from two-way causality and omitted variables. To address this issue, this study employs the heteroskedasticity-based instrumental variable method to construct instrumental variables (Lewbel, 2012), with results presented in Table 12. The p-value of the underidentification test is less than 0.001, rejecting the null hypothesis of “underidentified instrumental variables” at the 1% significance level. The F-statistic of the weak instrumental variable test is 80.116, which exceeds the critical value of 21.10 at the 5% bias level, indicating the reliability of the instrumental variables. As shown in Table 12, after addressing endogeneity via the instrumental variable method, the impact of entrepreneurship competition participation on students’ employability remains significantly positive, confirming the robustness of the study’s findings. It should be noted that heteroskedasticity-based instruments are considered “internal” instruments that rely on the assumption of heteroskedasticity in the first-stage errors.

Discussion

Based on the human capital perspective, this study investigates whether participation in entrepreneurship competitions is conducive to improving college students’ employability. The empirical results of large-sample propensity score matching are consistent with previous findings that entrepreneurship competitions positively impact students43,66, which validates the efficacy of entrepreneurship competitions as a pedagogical tool40. Some scholars believe that one of the disadvantages of entrepreneurship competitions is the lack of authenticity67; however, in fact, entrepreneurship competitions provide students with the chance to engage in entrepreneurial activities, which conserves numerous resources in comparison with fully genuine entrepreneurship68. and achieves the purpose of enhancing the employability of college students. This key finding echoes relevant studies in Europe. An interview-based study of 45 entrepreneurship education practitioners in UK higher education institutions27 explicitly highlights a significant link between entrepreneurship education and graduate employability. Meanwhile, a longitudinal qualitative study on university business plan competitions25 tracked participants across multiple stages (start, end, and six months post-competition) and found that participants developed core competencies such as pitching, public speaking, networking, and business plan writing—key dimensions of employability—confirming the fundamental value of entrepreneurship competitions in fostering student capabilities. Our analytical findings reveal a 19.2% improvement in the comprehensive employability scores of entrepreneurship competition participants. This enhancement represents a significant intermediate outcome, indicating notable strengthening in participants’ skills, preparedness, and confidence, which lays a solid foundation for achieving smoother employment opportunities and higher income potential in the future.

The results show that the higher the school level, the less significant the effect of entrepreneurship competitions on employability enhancement. The researchers claim that the college itself or the ranking affects the perceived employability of college students69. In China, students of 985/211 colleges and universities are recognized to have higher employability compared to students of ordinary undergraduate and higher vocational schools. Therefore, the role of participating in entrepreneurship competitions will be more effective for non-985/211 students who have relatively low employability in the first place, and entrepreneurship competitions will be more critical for them. This result is essentially a derivative effect of the structural stratification of higher education in China. Students from elite universities, leveraging institutional brand value, priority internship opportunities, and formal skills training, gain limited human capital improvement from entrepreneurship competitions. In contrast, students from non-“985/211” universities face greater barriers to accessing high-quality employment resources, making entrepreneurship competitions a key compensatory tool to fill their practical skills gap. Scholars’ analysis of fiscal resources in colleges and universities of the United States and Canada shows that both the market-oriented U.S. higher education system and the public-dominated, equality-focused Canadian system have witnessed a growing trend of structural stratification in higher education. The structural disparities in resource allocation among universities give rise to a hierarchical landscape, which not only shapes the status differences between institutions but also translates into unequal development opportunities for students from different universities through mechanisms such as “student screening” and “advantage conferral"70.

Gender differences in employability have been extensively explored in previous literature, such as Chung, et al.71 and Baek72, which showed that gender has a moderating role in higher education affecting employability. In contrast, Kwon73 did not find a moderating effect of gender on employability. The conclusions of this paper support the idea that gender differences can have an impact on employability. Gender norms influence employers’ evaluation of students’ skills. Female undergraduates often face implicit biases in the labor market, yet the practical experience gained from entrepreneurship competitions can serve as credible proof of competence, effectively mitigating such biases. In contrast, employers tend to presuppose male undergraduates’ employability based on gender stereotypes, resulting in a lower marginal value of competition participation for them. This study reveals that entrepreneurship competitions play a context-specific role in alleviating gender disparities in the labor market.

Notably, the data of this study were collected in the post-pandemic era, when the employment landscape has grown highly complex and college graduates are required to adapt to new employment environments and challenges. Against this backdrop, the Chinese government has attached great importance to employment issues and introduced a series of measures to strengthen the employment-first policy and promote high-quality employment.From the perspective of participation, the restrictions on offline practical activities during the pandemic drove the shift of university entrepreneurship competitions to online formats. Part of this model has persisted in the post-pandemic era, lowering the geographical and resource barriers to participation. This may have created more opportunities for students from non-“985/211” universities and female students, which partially explains the more significant effect of competition participation observed among these groups in this study.Furthermore, the pandemic has intensified competition pressures in the labor market, with particularly acute challenges for vulnerable groups such as low-skilled workers and women. Rising employment anxiety among college students has shifted their motivation for participating in entrepreneurship competitions from “interest exploration” to “competence enhancement”, further strengthening the correlation between competition experience and employability.

Based on this study, the following policy recommendations are proposed. First, universities should prioritize the provision of structured guidance and offer tiered training modules such as business plan workshops, roadshow coaching, and industry expert consultations. They should implement a collaborative mentoring model of “Mentor + Project + Team”, build interdisciplinary and cross-major innovation and entrepreneurship teams around high-quality competition projects, and form mentoring panels consisting of both internal and external mentors to provide systematic guidance covering technology, management, and marketing dimensions. Meanwhile, universities should integrate competitions closely with career services, linking competition performance directly to internship placements and corporate recruitment channels. In addition, they can collaborate with local key enterprises to design competition topics based on real technical challenges faced by these enterprises, enabling students to tackle practical industry issues head-on.Second, education authorities should strive to address the structural imbalance in entrepreneurship education resources and ensure that students from different types of institutions have equal access to high-quality competition opportunities. On the one hand, they should tilt entrepreneurship competition resources toward non-“985/211” universities and allocate special funds to support regular undergraduate and vocational colleges in developing competition infrastructure, such as full-time mentor teams and university-enterprise cooperation platforms.

On the other hand, they should guide “985/211” universities to focus on high-value, innovation-oriented competitions (e.g., technology entrepreneurship competitions aligned with national strategic industries) to complement their existing resources. Furthermore, authorities should promote gender-inclusive competition design by establishing special tracks for women’s entrepreneurship, earmarking special funds to support female participation in entrepreneurship competitions, and inviting experienced female entrepreneurs or experts to provide personalized guidance for participants.

Conclusions

This study is grounded on the survey data of 3,595 college students from 25 universities in Sichuan Province. It empirically investigates whether participation in entrepreneurship competitions can boost the employability of college students through the application of the propensity score matching method, with employability measured as self-perceived employability. Furthermore, it analyses the impact of participating in the competitions on diverse groups from the perspective of heterogeneity among college students. The main conclusions are as follows:

Implications

Based on existing research, this paper empirically examines the impact of participating in entrepreneurship competitions on employability to explore the opportunity to promote college students’ high-quality employment in the context of entrepreneurship competition education. Firstly, unlike existing studies that focus more on entrepreneurship education as a whole, this paper takes entrepreneurship competitions as an entry point to expand entrepreneurship competitions into the factors influencing employability in colleges and universities, to provide references for global colleges and universities to enhance the employability of college students to carry out relevant activities. Secondly, while previous studies mainly focus on entrepreneurial ability, this paper measures the impact on college students’ employability and further segments the student population. It can enrich and expand the existing research. Third, unlike most other empirical evidence that adopts regression models, this study proficiently resolves the endogeneity problem that emerges due to sample selection bias through the PSM. It simultaneously adopts three different matching methods, namely, nearest-neighbor matching, radius matching, and kernel matching, for matching to ensure the robustness of the model.

Limitations and further research

The research in this paper also has certain shortcomings. First, this paper only discusses whether participation in entrepreneurship competition has an effect on enhancing the employability of college students, and lacks a more in-depth mechanism and path test. Second, while PSM addresses endogeneity arising from sample selection bias, it fails to incorporate unobservable variables such as personality traits (e.g., risk-taking propensity, conscientiousness) and individual motivations (e.g., innovation willingness). These factors may jointly affect both core explanatory and explained variables, thereby introducing potential biases to causal identification results. Furthermore, this study uses cross-sectional data, which only reveals the correlation between variables but cannot dynamically capture the long-term evolution of core effects. Therefore, further verifying the impact mechanism of entrepreneurship competition participation on college students’ employability—using richer datasets and more rigorous, comprehensive methodologies—will be a key direction for future research. Specifically, subsequent studies can expand in two dimensions:

Adopt longitudinal tracking data for long-term follow-ups to dynamically observe the evolution of core variables, thus more accurately identifying the temporal characteristics and long-term stability of causal effects.Combine qualitative methods such as semi-structured interviews, select samples with heterogeneous characteristics to explore the mechanism of unobservable factors (e.g., personality traits, individual motivations), clarify the specific transmission paths of core effects, and thereby enhance the theoretical explanatory power and practical guidance value of research findings.
