AI-assisted preoperative surgical planning for dental implant
Authors: Fanxuan Chen, Haoman Chen, Darong Hai, Yilei Yang, Peng Xu, Dongren Yang, Ruoyun Wang, Zixuan Bi, Chang Yuan, Yijun Wang, Chufan Ren, Li Zeng, Zefei Mo, Yan Zhang, Jingye Pan, Gen Yang, Qi Zhao, Mei Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn accurate preoperative assessment of alveolar bone morphology is essential to the success of dental implant surgery. Although Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) provides high-resolution volumetric imaging for this purpose, interpreting it requires substantial expertise and remains inherently subjective. Integrating artificial intelligence (AI), specifically three-dimensional convolutional neural networks (3D-CNNs), could automate and standardize CBCT interpretation. However, there has been no systematic development of AI-driven preoperative tools capable of objectively predicting the necessity of adjunctive procedures, such as guided bone regeneration or maxillary sinus elevation.MethodsWe retrospectively collect 285 CBCT datasets from a single institution from patients undergoing dental implant surgery. Then, we construct a 3D-CNN–based deep learning model to automatically predict the need for adjunctive surgical intervention prior to implant placement. To optimize the model’s performance, we design a four-stage optimization framework that incorporates multimodal data augmentation, learning rate decay scheduling, optimizer selection, and convolutional channel configuration. We comprehensively evaluate model performance using accuracy (ACC), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and F1-score across training, validation, and test sets. We employ Grad-CAM visualization to reveal spatial attention patterns and apply LASSO regression to extract key latent features from the model’s fully connected layers. These features are then used to create a quantitative nomogram to improve clinical interpretability.ResultsThe optimized 3D-CNN achieves an accuracy of 0.81, an AUC of 0.79, and an F1-score of 0.82 on the validation and test sets, demonstrating strong discriminative and generalizability capabilities. Grad-CAM heatmaps shows that the model focuses on the edentulous ridge and the adjacent maxillary sinus regions, which are areas that align with expert clinical reasoning. LASSO regression identifies 14 high-contribution features for constructing an interpretable clinical nomogram (AUC = 0.855). Decision curve analysis indicates a positive net clinical benefit across multiple threshold ranges.ConclusionsThis study presents a 3D-CNN-based CBCT interpretation model that can objectively predict the need for bone augmentation procedures before implant surgery. Integrating multimodal data augmentation and standardized Hounsfield unit (HU) normalization significantly improve the model’s robustness and generalization. By combining deep learning with clinical decision-making processes, this study provides an interpretable, quantitative artificial intelligence (AI) framework for preoperative implant assessment. As the current model was developed, optimized, and tested on a single-center dataset, prospective multicenter external validation across diverse patient populations, varied CBCT acquisition protocols, and different clinical practice settings is essential to establish its generalizability and clinical utility before widespread deployment. This framework offers a feasible paradigm for future intelligent, standardized surgical planning in dental implantology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07584-y.Keywords: Dental implantology, CBCT, 3D-CNN, Bone augmentation, Model interpretability

Introduction

Dental implantation, as a vital method for restoring missing teeth, significantly enhances patients’ quality of life. Its long-term success largely depends on precise and meticulous preoperative planning [1]. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) has become the gold standard for preoperative assessment due to its capability of providing high-resolution three-dimensional volumetric data [2]. It provides a more precise depiction of jawbone anatomy compared to traditional two-dimensional radiographs [3]. Studies showed that approximately 33.8% of dental implants require adjunctive surgical procedures for bone augmentation prior to implantation. These procedures include guided bone regeneration (GBR) and maxillary sinus elevation [4]. Clinicians and specialists utilize CBCT to precisely assess alveolar ridge height and width, thereby determining the necessity for adjunctive surgical procedures. However, interpreting complex CBCT data demands specialized expertise and experience, while also being time-consuming and inherently subjective [5].

Particularly when assessing whether sufficient bone volume exists to support implants, current evaluation methods often rely on the clinician’s subjective judgment. This can lead to diagnostic discrepancies and assessment difficulties, ultimately impacting treatment outcomes [6].

Clinical evidence demonstrates several practical challenges in conventional CBCT interpretation for implant planning. Manual CBCT assessment demands substantial time investment from clinicians, creating workflow inefficiencies in busy clinical environments and potentially delaying treatment initiation. Research on inter-observer agreement in CBCT-based diagnostic tasks has revealed considerable variability in clinical decision-making. For instance, Setzer et al. developed a U-Net-based model for tooth, alveolar bone, and periapical lesion segmentation, achieving a DICE coefficient of only 0.52 for lesion labels, highlighting the challenges in consistent lesion identification [7]. Lim et al. compared manual and automated segmentation of the inferior alveolar nerve canal and found that manual segmentation achieved higher accuracy than automatic segmentation (DICE coefficient of 0.58 for the automated approach), suggesting that even experienced clinicians face challenges in achieving optimal segmentation consistency [8]. Additionally, diagnostic proficiency varies considerably across different experience levels, demonstrating the expertise-dependent nature of CBCT interpretation in implant planning. Therefore, there is an urgent need for more objective and standardized assessment tools to assist clinicians in making more precise decisions.

In recent years, AI, particularly deep learning technologies represented by three-dimensional convolutional neural networks (3D CNNs), has demonstrated significant potential in analyzing three-dimensional medical images such as CT scans [9, 10]. AI-based automated interpretation offers several potential advantages in addressing these clinical challenges. AI systems provide rapid computational processing, substantially accelerating the diagnostic workflow compared to traditional manual assessment methods. Roongruangsilp et al. developed an AI-based implant planning method using the Faster R-CNN algorithm for alveolar bone quantity assessment, demonstrating the feasibility of automated analysis [11]. The Diagnocat system, evaluated by Orhan et al. for periapical lesion detection, showed high reliability (92.8%) in correctly detecting periapical lesions, indicating that AI systems can apply standardized computational criteria consistently across cases [12]. Sorkhabi et al. proposed a 3D deep convolutional neural network for qualitative assessment of alveolar bone that outperformed existing methods, demonstrating AI’s potential to extend specialist-level analytical capabilities to clinical settings with limited access to experienced implant specialists [13]. AI has been successfully applied to various tasks in dental image analysis, including anatomical structure recognition and treatment planning assistance [14]. In implant dentistry, the integration of CBCT data with AI has become a hot topic. Applications include implant design optimization (shape, size, brand) [15–17], implant site planning and assessment (bone density analysis, neural canal localization) [18–20], surgical navigation and real-time guidance [21–23], as well as postoperative outcome prediction and complication forecasting [24–27]. However, a critical gap remains: few studies have explored AI-based prediction of whether adjunctive surgical procedures are needed prior to implant placement. This represents a fundamental clinical decision that directly impacts surgical planning, patient counseling, and treatment outcomes. 3D CNNs can directly process volumetric data like CT scans using three-dimensional convolutional kernels, capturing spatial dependencies and contextual information across slices. This makes them particularly well-suited for tasks requiring comprehensive assessment of three-dimensional bone structures to determine the need for assisted surgery [13].

Addressing this gap, This study aims to develop and systematically evaluate a deep learning model based on a 3D CNNs. It utilizes preoperative CBCT images to automatically predict whether specific implant sites require adjunctive surgical intervention prior to implant placement. Through end-to-end image feature learning, this model provides an objective, consistent, and efficient decision support method for preoperative planning. Furthermore, we systematically optimized key modules in model training and architecture, identifying optimal strategy combinations to maximize the model’s generalization performance and discriminative capability. By integrating AI with clinical preoperative assessment, this study provides clinicians with a quantifiable and interpretable support tool for critical decision-making. It facilitates a more appropriate risk-benefit balance while reducing patient wait times for consultation and decision-making, ultimately enhancing the predictability and safety of implant treatment.

Materials and methods

Ethics and quality standards

This study has received approval from the Medical Ethics Review Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University for the use of patient cases in the research (No. WYKQ2023010). Our methods and reporting strictly adhere to the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) guidelines, meeting the ethical and quality standards for this quality improvement study.

Dental CBCT data and medical record collection

The collected dental CBCT and medical records data are obtained from 309 patients who underwent dental implant surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between August 12, 2022, and September 1, 2023. After rigorous screening, 6 patients are excluded due to missing CBCT data, and 18 patients are excluded because they received multiple implants at different sites requiring either augmentative surgery or no augmentative surgery. Ultimately, 285 cases meeting the study criteria are included. The included case data contains outpatient records, surgical records, follow-up records, and imaging materials such as CBCT. The medical records contained multiple patient-specific details, categorized as quantitative data, categorical data, and other information: Quantitative data included preoperative available bone height and width, as well as postoperative implant diameter and length (Fig. 1b). Categorical data comprised patient cementum classification, gender, implant location, and implant brand (Fig. 1c and d). Other data includes initial consultation date, follow-up date, etc.

Dental CBCT data contains CT images acquired for each patient prior to implant surgery. CBCT scans for all cases were obtained using either the Alphard-3030 (Asahi Roentgen, Kyoto, Japan; current: 10 mA; voltage: 85 kV; exposure time: 17 s; voxel resolution: 0.2 mm) or NewTom VGi evo (QR s.r.l., Verona, Italy; current: 11 mA; voltage: 90 kV; exposure time: 18 s; voxel resolution: 0.125 mm). All acquired images are stored in DCM format with 12-bit grayscale depth, matrix sizes ranging from 512 × 512 to 640 × 640, and slice counts between 400 and 832, averaging 466 slices (Fig. 1e). Every CBCT image was captured and reviewed by a certified radiologist to ensure the scan coverage encompassed the intended implant site and excluded artifacts.

Dataset cleaning and preprocessing

The data cleaning process primarily consists of two major components: key textual information extraction and CBCT preprocessing.

Key Textual Information Extraction: (1) Manually pre-screen patient records highly relevant to dental implants from the hospital’s medical record system; (2) Manually extract key information related to implant surgery from patient records stored as PDFs, removing sensitive personally identifiable health information (PIH).

CBCT Preprocessing: (1) Firstly, exclude patients without CBCT scans and confirm that the CBCT images are preoperative radiographic data. (2) Consolidate multiple dcm files into individual nii file formats; (3) Apply threshold normalization to CBCT data within the range [-500, 2500] HU. (4) Image enhancement: Randomly rotate and flip images around the z-axis (50% probability) (Fig. 2a). After rigorous information extraction and data preprocessing, we ultimately include 285 cases meeting the study criteria, providing a solid foundation for subsequent model training.

Annotation of the dataset was conducted from September 1, 2023, to January 24, 2024. We set a binary label based on whether patients required auxiliary surgery prior to dental implant surgery. Patients not requiring surgical procedures prior to implant placement are labeled 0, while those requiring such procedures are labeled 1. These procedures include GBR and maxillary sinus elevation, both commonly employed to provide sufficient three-dimensional bone volume for implants in cases of horizontal or vertical bone deficiency.

Data cleaning and labeling were done by five qualified personnel with specific training backgrounds: all five were graduate-level medical students (three from the School of Stomatology and two from the School of Clinical Medicine at Wenzhou Medical University) who had completed formal courses in oral radiology and dental implantology. Each annotator had at least 2 years of clinical rotation experience in departments of oral and maxillofacial surgery or prosthodontics. Prior to data annotation, all five personnel underwent a standardized training protocol that included: (1) a 10-hour didactic session on CBCT interpretation for implant planning, delivered by board-certified oral radiologists; (2) hands-on practice with 30 representative cases under expert supervision; and (3) an inter-rater reliability assessment achieving Cohen’s kappa > 0.85 for labeling consistency. Ambiguous cases were adjudicated through consensus review with senior clinicians to ensure annotation quality.

Dataset division and external verification

To ensure the scientific rigor of the study and the reliability of model training results, we randomly partition the dental dataset into three segments in a 6:2:2 ratio to guarantee no patient overlap between subsets: the training set comprises 60% of the data, containing 171 paired sets of EMR and CBCT information; the validation set constitutes 20%, containing 57 paired sets of EMR and CBCT information; and the test set comprised 20%, containing 57 paired EMR and CBCT cases. The test set is isolated in a separate section and maintained independently by professionals.

Framework construction and model optimization

To fully leverage the spatial characteristics and inter-slice continuity of CBCT images, our study builds a deep learning framework based on a 3D-CNN for predicting whether patients require auxiliary surgery prior to dental implant procedures. Inspired by the research of Hasib Zunair et al. [28], we developed a 3D-CNN model with module-by-module updates to process voxel-level CBCT image information. Key components of the network are systematically screened and progressively optimized.

The 3D-CNN framework comprises two major modules: feature extraction and classification. The feature extraction module accepts 3D image inputs and sequentially processes them through four submodules—each consisting of a 3D convolutional layer, a max pooling layer, and a batch normalization layer—to extract spatial and hierarchical features across multiple levels. The first two 3D convolutional layers contain 64 filters, while the subsequent two layers contain 128 and 256 filters, respectively. All convolutions employ 3 × 3 × 3 kernels, followed by ReLU activation functions. Each convolutional layer connects to a max pooling layer, after which the final output undergoes batch normalization. The classification module is able to feed the extracted features into a fully connected layer with 512 neurons for feature integration. Subsequently, the features enter a fully connected layer with 2 neurons and generate a binary classification prediction result through the softmax activation function (Fig. 2b).

Based on this framework, we systematically optimize the model’s training process and structural parameters, covering aspects such as data augmentation, learning rate scheduling, optimizer selection, and convolutional layer depth configuration.

We sequentially select and combine four key modules within the original 3D-CNN framework: (1) Data augmentation module: Three progressive augmentation schemes are designed. Strategy A includes random rotation along the z-axis (–15° to 15°); Strategy B adds multi-axis rotation (x, y, z axes) and random scaling (0.8–1.2x) to this base; Strategy C further incorporates multimodal perturbations, including triaxial rotation, z-axis flipping, random Gaussian noise, contrast adjustment (0.7–1.35), and brightness adjustment (–0.2 to 0.2) to maximize simulation of clinical image heterogeneity. (2) Hyperparameter Module: Tests two learning rate update rules. Strategy A uses an initial learning rate of 0.001, decaying by 0.96 every 200 steps. Strategy B employs periodic dynamic adjustment based on a cosine function. (3) Optimizer Module: Compares Adam and AdamW optimizers. (4) Convolution Module: Two channel configurations were tested in the feature extraction layer, Strategy A: (64, 64, 128, 256) and Strategy B: (64, 64, 128, 512), to investigate the impact of deeper convolutional channel designs on performance (Fig. 2c).

Throughout all module selection processes, we use the F1 score on the validation set as the core ranking metric. We sequentially select the best-performing strategy and combine them into the final optimization scheme. During training, the batch size is set to 2, the initial learning rate is 0.0001, and iterative optimization is performed based on the accuracy (ACC) on the validation set. The model will be trained for 400 epochs, with validation set metrics—accuracy (ACC), area under the curve (AUC), and F1 score—evaluated after each epoch to enable continuous performance monitoring and dynamic optimization.

Indicator setting and model evaluation

During training, we systematically record commonly used model evaluation metrics, loss, accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and F1 score, to dynamically monitor model learning and generalization. To further compare predictive performance, we also count and analyze confusion matrix elements (True Positives TP, True Negatives TN, False Positives FP, False Negatives FN) and the total number of correctly predicted cases (TN + TP).

Model diagnostic capability is quantified using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and its AUC value. The ROC curve visually illustrates the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, while the AUC provides a clearer scalar assessment of classification performance. To address sample imbalance, we plot precision-recall curves (PR curves) to sensitively reflect the model’s discrimination capability for minority class identification. Concurrently, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) is employed to evaluate the potential clinical net benefit of the model across different risk thresholds.

Explainability analysis

Building upon this foundation, to further reveal the spatial attention patterns of neural networks during image classification, we adopt the Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Map (Grad-CAM) method. Grad-CAM is a visualization technique that produces visual explanations for decisions from CNN-based models by highlighting the regions in the input image that are most influential for the prediction [29]. The method combines the gradient information flowing into the final convolutional layer with the activation maps to generate a coarse localization map indicating the important regions for predicting specific classes.

In our implementation, Grad-CAM computes the gradient of the target class score with respect to the feature maps of the final convolutional layer. These gradients are global-average-pooled to obtain the importance weights for each feature map, which indicate how much each feature map contributes to the final classification decision. The weighted combination of these feature maps produces a heatmap that highlights the discriminative regions relevant to the target class. A rectified linear unit (ReLU) function is applied to retain only features that have a positive influence on the class of interest, ensuring that the visualization focuses on supportive rather than suppressive evidence.

In our study, attention heatmaps are generated based on the final convolutional feature map (12*12*4*256, without normalization or down-sampling). These heatmaps are then upsampled to match the original input dimensions and overlaid onto the original CBCT images, visually demonstrating the weighting of each region in the final prediction. The intensity of heat values reflects the importance of that region to the model output, with warmer colors (red/yellow) indicating regions of high importance and cooler colors (blue) indicating regions of low importance. This thereby helps us understand the key anatomical structures or pathological signals relied upon by the neural network during decision-making, such as alveolar bone height, bone density variations, or proximity to critical anatomical landmarks like the maxillary sinus or inferior alveolar nerve canal. By systematically analyzing these attention patterns across multiple cases, we can validate whether the model’s decision-making process aligns with clinical diagnostic criteria and identify potential biases or spurious correlations that may affect model reliability.

Clinically available simple assessment tools

Furthermore, to facilitate actionable quantitative interpretation during preoperative assessment for clinicians, we design a personalized risk prediction tool, the interpretable feature histogram. Specifically, we extract 256-dimensional high-dimensional feature vectors from the GlobalAveragePooling3D layer, which are considered to represent the model’s abstract expression of underlying structures and patterns within the images during the learning process. To reduce dimensionality and identify the most predictive features, we conduct regularized modeling using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. This effectively suppresses multicollinearity while retaining a small set of key features. Subsequently, these selected core features are incorporated into a logistic regression model to quantitatively assess their independent contribution to predicting outcomes.

Results

Dataset quality control and baseline analysis

This study includes 285 patients, of whom 177 required no auxiliary surgery (label 0) and 108 required auxiliary surgery prior to implantation (label 1). The dataset is divided into a training set (60%), internal validation set (20%), and external test set (20%) at a ratio of 6:2:2.

Patient baseline information consists of three types of features: (1) CT data: Images are stored in DCM format, with individual cases ranging from 400 to 832 slices and an average of 466 slices, ensuring complete three-dimensional maxillary bone structure information; (2) Quantitative features: These include preoperative available bone height, bone width, and postoperative implant diameter and length. The mean and standard deviation distributions of these parameters are similar across groups, with no significant differences observed; (3) Categorical features: these includes cementum type, gender, receipt of implant surgery, and preoperative auxiliary procedures.

Model construction and optimization

Based on the aforementioned 3D-CNN framework, we systematically select and combine four key modules, data augmentation, hyperparameter tuning, optimizer, and convolutional, to enhance the model’s discriminative performance and convergence stability in CBCT image analysis. The entire selection process primarily evaluates the validation set F1-score, supplemented by comprehensive assessments of loss curve trends and convergence speed.

In the comparison of data augmentation strategies, Strategy C demonstrates significant advantages over Strategies A and B. By incorporating multi-modal augmentations, including three-axis rotation, z-axis flipping, random Gaussian noise, and contrast/brightness perturbations, Strategy C achieves a higher validation set F1-score of 0.79 compared to Strategies A and B. This result indicates that multimodal perturbation-based data augmentation enhances the model’s robustness to image heterogeneity, effectively improving its generalization performance on CBCT data.

In the hyperparameter module, the exponential decay learning rate scheduling strategy (Strategy A) outperforms the cosine annealing-based dynamic adjustment strategy (Strategy B) in both convergence speed and final performance. After adopting this scheme, the model reaches a stable convergence range by the 200th epoch, with the validation set F1-score improving to 0.80. Strategy A’s loss curve rapidly decreases from 0.62 to 0.20 between the 100th and 300th epochs, followed by steady, minor oscillations. Strategy B’s loss curve rapidly decreases from 0.58 to 0.32 between epochs 176 and 276. After significant oscillations, the loss rapidly decreases again from 0.46 to 0.20 between epochs 300 and 380. This demonstrates that the learning rate decay strategy more effectively balances gradient update speed with convergence stability.

Comparative results from the optimizer module show that the Adam optimizer outperforms AdamW in both training stability and generalization performance. Adam exhibited rapid, small oscillations between epochs 100 and 300, decreasing from 0.62 to 0.17 before converging swiftly. AdamW’s loss curve exhibits rapid, large fluctuations between epoch 110 and 330, dropping from 0.58 to 0.16 before converging with minor oscillations. The validation set F1-score further improves to 0.81, demonstrating superior generalization capability and stability. This demonstrates that Adam’s adaptive gradient update mechanism enables a smoother loss decline during training without noticeable oscillations.

In the convolutional module, different channel configurations yield varying impacts on feature extraction performance and model stability. Compared to deeper convolutional structures (64, 64, 128, 512) (where loss decreased from 0.58 to 0.16 between approximately 110 and 330 epochs), shallower channel designs (64, 64, 128, 256) achieves faster convergence in the early stages (approximately 100–300 epochs, with loss decreasing from 0.62 to 0.16) while maintaining high feature expressiveness, ultimately yielding a validation set F1-score of 0.82. We hypothesize this phenomenon may stem from shallower convolutional structures mitigating overfitting risks under limited sample conditions. These structures form more discriminative intermediate representations in the feature space, enabling smoother gradient propagation and faster convergence during early training phases (Fig. 3a).

Optimal module combinations and model evaluation

Based on the above module-by-module screening results, we ultimately combine them to form the optimized 3D-CNN model. This model adopts Strategy C in the data augmentation module (Step1), which enhances and expands the dataset by applying operations such as three-axis rotation, z-axis flipping, random Gaussian noise, contrast adjustment (0.7–1.35), and brightness adjustment (–0.2 to 0.2) to CBCT images; Strategy A is selected for the learning rate rule module (Step2), using an initial learning rate of 0.001 with a decay of 0.96 every 200 steps; Strategy A is chosen for the optimizer module (Step3), employing the Adam optimizer; Strategy A is adopted for the convolutional module (Step4), with the feature extraction layer set to (64, 64, 128, 256). During model optimization, the ACC changes on the test set for Base, Step1, Step2, Step3, and Step4 were 0.68, 0.72, 0.74, 0.72, and 0.74, respectively. AUC changes were 0.64, 0.70, 0.69, 0.62, 0.75, respectively; F1 Score changes were 0.77, 0.80, 0.80, 0.81, 0.81 (Fig. 3b).

The final model achieves full convergence within 150 epochs on the training set, with accuracy on the internal validation set improving to 0.81, AUC rising to 0.79, and precision, recall, and F1-score reaching 0.79, 0.94, and 0.86 respectively. On the test set, Accuracy, AUC, and F1-score reaches 0.73, 0.75, and 0.82 respectively. To further compare sensitivity and specificity changes during model training, we also calculated and analyzed confusion matrix elements (TP, TN, FP, FN) and the number of correctly predicted cases (TN + TP). The number of correct predictions (TN + TP) in the training set reaches 171/171; in the validation set, it reaches 44/57; and in the test set, it reaches 19/57 (Fig. 3c and d).

Nomogram for clinical guidance

To enhance model interpretability, we conduct LASSO regression analysis on the fully connected layer output features of the optimized 3D-CNN model. LASSO applies L1 regularization (λ‖β‖₁) to the loss function, imposing sparsity constraints during feature selection. This shrinks non-essential coefficients to zero, identifying features most linearly correlated with the target variable (need for bone augmentation surgery).

Ultimately, we filter out 14 features with significant predictive value (Feature_11, Feature_45, Feature_68, Feature_75, Feature_87, Feature_148, Feature_149, Feature_182, Feature_187, Feature_197, Feature_212, Feature_215, Feature_221, Feature_234) for plotting in a nomogram (Fig. 4b). The regression coefficients, standard errors, Z-scores, and significance levels (P-values) of these features within the model are systematically documented in Table S1. Among them, Feature_182 exhibits the largest absolute coefficient (|Coefficient| = 9.529), corresponding to a Z-score of -5.107 and a P-value of 3.27 × 10⁻⁷. This indicates its greatest contribution in predicting the need for auxiliary surgery, making it the model’s most critical image feature. Other features such as Feature_149, Feature_148, and Feature_212 also exhibit significant positive or negative correlations (|Coefficient| > 4, P < 0.01), demonstrating their importance in the comprehensive assessment.

To validate clinical interpretability, we investigated the correspondence between these features and the spatial anatomical structures of oral implants. We found that features with large absolute coefficients and high contributions were often visualized as concentrated in the maxillary sinus floor region, which is a critical structure directly impacting preoperative bone volume assessment and surgical decision-making for implant placement. Features with low absolute values and low contributions were automatically eliminated by the LASSO regression penalty term. Convolutional image features selected via LASSO provide a more concise and focused representation of regions of interest to the model, thereby offering faster and more effective guidance and recommendations for clinical practice.

To quantify its practical discriminatory power, we plot a ROC curve. The AUC value of this receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.855, indicating excellent discrimination performance (Fig. 4c). Considering the imbalanced patient numbers between the two groups included in the study, we plot a PR curve. The results show that the calibrated curve is close to the ideal curve, suggesting that the predicted results are generally consistent with the actual outcomes. The DCA curve for the nomogram is shown in Fig. 4c. The high-risk threshold probability represents the likelihood of severe prediction bias when clinicians use the nomogram for diagnosis and decision-making. In this study, the DCA curve indicates that the nomogram demonstrates a favorable net clinical benefit.

Discussion and conclusion

The integration of CBCT data with AI has significantly advanced the field of dental implantology. CBCT provides detailed three-dimensional imaging of the oral cavity, and when combined with AI, enhances the accuracy of implant placement while minimizing complications [30–32]. AI algorithms analyze this data for precise measurements and outcome prediction, enabling more personalized treatment planning and shorter recovery times [33–36].

Currently, preoperative predictions in implant dentistry primarily focus on bone quality and density assessment [37], implant success rates [38], and optimization of implant parameters and design [39]. No studies have evaluated the necessity of supplementary surgical procedures before implant placement. Clinicians typically rely on CBCT scans to assess bone quality and quantity in the implant site, a process heavily dependent on the practitioner’s expertise [40, 41]. Given the varying capabilities of dental clinics, this AI-based preliminary assessment method can provide clinicians with valuable guidance and facilitate the establishment of standardized protocols, thereby optimizing resource allocation. Our research design features an optimized 3D CNN model that can be directly accessed via a web client (Fig. 4d). This model assists dentists in determining the necessity of surgical intervention. Patients can also choose to upload their CBCT image data directly to obtain recommendations for surgical assistance. It significantly streamlines the workflow and optimizes the allocation of medical resources, helping alleviate the pressure on doctors’ outpatient clinics in areas experiencing shortages or imbalances in healthcare resources (Fig. 4c).

A comprehensive comparative analysis of the four optimization modules reveals distinct strengths and limitations that collectively contribute to model performance.

In the data augmentation module, Strategy C’s multimodal perturbations (triaxial rotation, z-axis flipping, Gaussian noise, and contrast/brightness adjustments) demonstrated superior generalization capability (F1-score: 0.79) compared to simpler strategies (A and B), effectively simulating clinical CBCT heterogeneity across different scanning protocols and patient positioning. However, excessive augmentation may introduce unrealistic artifacts that deviate from actual clinical scenarios, necessitating careful calibration.

The learning rate scheduling module comparison showed that exponential decay (Strategy A) achieved faster convergence (stable by epoch 200) and smoother optimization trajectories than cosine annealing (Strategy B), which exhibited substantial oscillations between epochs 176–276. This suggests that for moderate-sized medical imaging datasets, monotonic decay schedules provide more predictable training dynamics, though they may sacrifice the potential benefits of cyclical learning rates in escaping local minima.

In the optimizer module, Adam outperformed AdamW despite the latter’s theoretical advantages in weight decay regularization. Adam’s loss curve exhibited rapid, small oscillations (0.62 to 0.17 between epochs 100 and 300) versus AdamW’s larger fluctuations (0.58 to 0.16 between epochs 110 and 330), suggesting that for our specific architecture and dataset scale, aggressive weight decay may have hindered feature learning from limited samples.

The convolutional channel configuration comparison revealed that shallower designs (64,64,128,256) achieved better performance (F1-score: 0.82) than deeper structures (64,64,128,512), likely because limited sample sizes (285 cases) made deeper networks prone to overfitting, while shallower architectures maintained sufficient representational capacity without excessive parameterization. This finding underscores the importance of balancing model complexity with dataset scale in medical AI applications. Collectively, these module-specific insights demonstrate that optimal performance emerges not from universally “superior” strategies, but from careful alignment of augmentation intensity, optimization dynamics, regularization strength, and architectural depth with dataset characteristics and clinical task requirements.

In this study, the phenomenon where the baseline model achieves near-perfect performance on the training set but exhibits significant degradation on the validation/test sets demonstrates that deep networks are susceptible to learning task-irrelevant noise or dataset-specific representations when trained on limited and potentially biased data. In contrast, data augmentation reduces the model’s dependence on a single data distribution by artificially expanding the morphological and imaging variability of samples; HU interval clipping-normalization enhances tissue contrast consistency by suppressing clinically irrelevant grayscale artifacts (e.g., metal artifacts or background noise), thereby improving cross-sample representation stability. To further address the model’s insufficient generalization ability, several countermeasures warrant consideration for future iterations of this framework.

Expansion of the training dataset through collaborative multi-institutional data sharing represents a fundamental approach to increasing sample diversity and reducing overfitting to site-specific imaging characteristics. Implementation of more aggressive regularization techniques, such as increased dropout rates, L2 weight decay, or early stopping based on validation loss plateauing, could prevent the model from memorizing training-specific patterns rather than learning generalizable features [42]. Adoption of domain adaptation or transfer learning strategies, where the model is pretrained on large-scale generic medical imaging datasets before fine-tuning on implant-specific CBCT data, may enhance feature representation robustness across diverse acquisition protocols [43].

Ensemble methods that combine predictions from multiple model architectures or training initializations could reduce prediction variance and improve test-set stability, albeit at the cost of increased computational complexity. Adversarial training approaches, wherein synthetic adversarial perturbations are introduced during the training phase, may enhance model resilience to imaging artifacts and protocol variations commonly encountered in clinical practice [44]. Ultimately, prospective external validation on completely independent datasets from different geographic regions, patient populations, and equipment vendors remains the definitive benchmark for assessing true generalization capability [45]. These complementary strategies, when systematically integrated into the model development pipeline, can mitigate overfitting risks and establish a more clinically reliable and robust AI framework for preoperative implant assessment.

Beyond the current retrospective validation, translation of this AI framework into prospective clinical deployment necessitates systematic external validation and workflow integration strategies. A multicenter validation cohort encompassing diverse patient demographics (age, ethnicity, systemic comorbidities), heterogeneous CBCT acquisition protocols (manufacturers, voxel resolutions, scanning parameters), and varied clinical practice settings (tertiary academic centers versus community-based clinics) represents the cornerstone for establishing model generalizability and identifying potential domain shift vulnerabilities [46]. Prospective randomized controlled trials comparing AI-assisted preoperative planning against conventional expert-driven workflows should be conducted with rigorously defined primary endpoints, including diagnostic concordance with multidisciplinary consensus panels, reduction in preoperative consultation duration, intraoperative and postoperative complication rates, and patient-centered outcomes such as treatment satisfaction and decision-making anxiety. Seamless integration into existing digital dental ecosystems, including CAD/CAM implant guide design platforms, CBCT visualization software, and electronic health record systems, would facilitate clinical adoption without disrupting established workflows, while enabling real-time decision support at the point of care [47–49].

Implementation of continuous learning pipelines, whereby the model undergoes periodic retraining on accumulating clinical data with rigorous version control and performance monitoring, would enhance long-term accuracy and adapt to evolving surgical techniques and imaging technologies. Furthermore, incorporation of uncertainty quantification frameworks, such as Bayesian deep learning or Monte Carlo dropout inference, would enable the model to assign confidence scores to predictions and automatically flag ambiguous cases requiring mandatory expert review, thereby maintaining clinical safety standards and medicolegal compliance [50, 51].

Looking forward, future research directions should explore multimodal data integration by combining CBCT imaging with complementary clinical information sources. Integration of patient-reported outcomes, medical history data (such as diabetes, smoking status, osteoporosis, or immunosuppressive medication use), panoramic radiographs, intraoral photographs, and digital dental models could provide a more holistic assessment framework that captures both anatomical and systemic risk factors influencing surgical necessity. Incorporation of laboratory biomarkers (bone turnover markers, vitamin D levels) and genetic predisposition data may further refine risk stratification models. Additionally, integration with real-time intraoperative navigation systems and surgical simulation platforms could enable dynamic treatment plan adjustments based on actual surgical findings. Furthermore, longitudinal tracking of postoperative outcomes linked to preoperative AI predictions would facilitate continuous model refinement and evidence-based validation of prediction accuracy. Exploration of explainable AI techniques, such as attention heatmaps and feature attribution methods, would enhance clinical interpretability by highlighting specific anatomical regions or imaging features driving the model’s recommendations. Finally, extending the framework to predict not only the necessity but also the optimal type and timing of adjunctive procedures (guided bone regeneration, block grafts, sinus augmentation approaches) represents a natural progression toward comprehensive AI-assisted surgical planning.

However, this study has several limitations: the data source is relatively single, and the limited data scale may weaken the model’s generalization ability to heterogeneous populations and equipment; furthermore, the model analysis is primarily based on internal segmentation, requiring more prospective and multicenter external validation in the future.

In summary, our study demonstrates that CBCT-based 3D-CNNs can automatically identify potential need for adjunctive surgical interventions at implant sites preoperatively. It also highlights that data augmentation and image grayscale standardization (HU clipping/normalization) play a critical role in enhancing model generalization for medical imaging deep learning tasks. Although the results are encouraging, larger-scale, multicenter validation, rigorous statistical evaluation, and deeper analysis of interpretability and uncertainty are required before this tool can be applied for clinical decision support.
