From pulp to pomace: Distribution and bioactivity of free and bound phenolics in Rosa roxburghii
Authors: JinTao Yang, Xiuping Wan, Juyan Sun, Qiuqiu Zhang, Qingshan Yang, Yan Hu, Yong Li, Renshuai Huang, Guangjing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe functional properties of Rosa roxburghii phenolics are governed by their distribution among free (FP), ester-bound (EP), glycoside-bound (GP), and insoluble-bound forms (IBP). In this study, these four fractions were systematically profiled in pulp (RTP), seed (RTS), seedless pomace (SRTP), and seeded pomace (RTPS) using total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC), UHPLC–QTOF–MS with targeted MS/MS confirmation, antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP), α-glucosidase inhibition assays, and chemometric modelling. Free phenolics were found to predominate across all tissue types, exhibiting the highest TPC, TFC, and antioxidant capacities, with pulp and pomace outperforming seeds. Strong in vitro α-glucosidase inhibition was observed in RTP-EP and RTPS-FP, both of which demonstrated lower IC50 values than acarbose and exhibited mixed-type inhibition kinetics. Metabolomic analysis annotated 59 phenolic compounds, with catechin, epicatechin, and isoquercitrin enriched in FP fractions. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong associations between antioxidant activity and these specific flavonoids. These findings establish pomace as a rich source of bioactive phenolics and provide a mechanistic and compositional basis for the targeted recovery and application of R. roxburghii phenolics in functional food and nutraceutical formulations.Keywords: Rosa roxburghii, Free and bound phenolics, UHPLC–Q–TOF–MS, Antioxidant activity, Α-Glucosidase inhibition, Chemometrics

Introduction

Polyphenols—including flavonoids, tannins, and phenolic acids—are abundant in fruits and vegetables and widely recognized as broad-spectrum natural antioxidants (Mithul Aravind, Wichienchot, Tsao, Ramakrishnan, & Chakkaravarthi, 2021). These compounds exhibit a wide array of biological effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, anti-atherosclerotic, and antineoplastic activities. Accordingly, polyphenol-rich diets have been recommended for the prevention of chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, liver dysfunction, obesity, and diabetes (Villegas-Aguilar et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). Recent studies have examined the anti-inflammatory properties of polyphenols from various Chinese olive cultivars (He et al., 2024), as well as and the enzyme-inhibitory activities of pecan-derived polyphenols, particularly against α-amylase and α-glucosidase (Feng & Kong, 2022). Polyphenols are present in both free and bound forms. Bound phenolics may exist in esterified, glycosylated, or insoluble-bound states, each characterized by unique structural properties that affect both their bioavailability and biological functions (Machado et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2022). Free polyphenols typically accumulate in vacuoles, whereas soluble-bound forms are covalently attached to sugars or low-molecular-weight co-metabolites through C—C or C—O bonds. Bound polyphenols may also be esterified, meaning they are linked to sugars, fatty acids, or glucuronic acid via ester bonds; glycosylated, through sugar conjugation; or tightly incorporated into insoluble matrices such as cell wall structures (Oracz, Nebesny, & Zyzelewicz, 2019; Shahidi & Hossain, 2023). Insoluble-bound polyphenols are often embedded within cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and proteins, making their extraction and digestion more challenging (Xu, Rao, & Chen, 2020). As living standards improve and public awareness of nutritional health grows, there is increasing demand for natural functional foods and scientifically supported dietary supplements. This has driven extensive research into the extraction, characterization, and utilization of polyphenols from fruit sources. These compounds are now recognized as key bioactive ingredients in functional food formulations, offering promising avenues for chronic disease prevention and overall health enhancement.

Rosa roxburghii Tratt (RT), a Rosaceae shrub endemic to southwest and south-central China, particularly Guizhou Province, flourishes at elevations of 1000–1600 m (Chen & Kan, 2018). By 2020, approximately 133,300 ha of RT were cultivated in Guizhou, yielding about 100,000 tons of fresh fruit and 26,200 tons of processed products (Huang et al., 2022). Cultivars such as ‘AL11,’ ‘GP6,’ ‘Guinong 1,’ ‘Guinong 2,’ and ‘Guinong 5–7’ have been developed for commercial production (Lu, An, & Wang, 2017). The medicinal use of RT was first recorded in 1698 in the Guizhou Chorography and later documented in the 1765 edition of the Compendium of Materia Medica, where it was primarily used to support digestive health (Li, Fang, Wang, & Chen, 2022). The fruit, often referred to as the “King of Vitamin C,” contains 1300–3500 mg/100 g fresh weight—more than most other fruits (C. Chen et al., 2022), and exhibits the highest superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity among commonly consumed fruits (Xu, Vidyarthi, Bai, & Pan, 2019). Its high levels of polysaccharides, polyphenols, flavonoids, and organic acids have driven increasing interest in its use in both the food and pharmaceutical industries (Huang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021).

Previous studies have identified major free polyphenols in RT, such as gallic acid, ellagic acid, catechin, procyanidin B1, and isoquercitrin (L. Xu et al., 2023). These compounds contribute significantly to its antioxidant capacity, as demonstrated by ABTS, DPPH, and FRAP assays (Zhou, Qin, Wang, Wang, & Qin, 2024). As interest in RT's bioactive profile grows, research into its functional applications continues to expand. Although RT products are typically derived from juice or whole fruit, processing generates substantial pomace, which is often discarded despite its rich phenolic content. RT pomace contains abundant bound phenolics, particularly gallocatechin, epigallocatechin, catechin, and epicatechin, along with smaller quantities of free phenolics, such as gallic acid and ellagic acid. Total phenolic and flavonoid contents in RT pomace have been reported to reach 580.8 mg/g and 861 mg/g, respectively (Huang et al., 2022). In other fruit systems (e.g., passion fruit), retaining seeds during processing has been shown to increase phenolic acid release and enhance bioactivity (Zou, Niu, Yi, & Zhou, 2024). This suggests that seed removal during RT processing may significantly affect phenolic composition and function. Comparative profiling of phenolic compounds across different RT tissues—including pulp, seeds, seeded pomace, and seedless pomace—is therefore needed. Although most studies focus on whole fruits or seed-containing residues, few have systematically examined phenolic distribution among individual plant components. Moreover, prior research has largely concentrated on free (FP) and esterified phenolics (EP), with limited investigation into glycosylated (GP) and insoluble-bound phenolics (IBP), which are likely abundant and biologically relevant. This represents a significant research gap (Wang et al., 2022).

To address this gap and establish a direct link between chemical composition and functional properties, this study was designed around three interrelated hypotheses. First, it is hypothesized that tissue type and phenolic state (FP, EP, GP, IBP) yield distinct, quantifiable distributions—with FP expected to predominate in pulp, and bound phenolics enriched in pomace due to cell wall association. Second, it is proposed that these distributions predict functional outcomes: FP-rich matrices and specific subclasses (e.g., catechin, epicatechin, isoquercitrin) are expected to drive antioxidant activity, while FP/EP pools are anticipated to exert strong α-glucosidase inhibition with characteristic kinetic profiles. Third, mapping the composition–activity relationship is expected to reveal opportunities for valorizing processing by-products (seeded vs. seedless pomace), enabling targeted recovery of high-value fractions for use in functional foods or nutraceuticals.

Accordingly, the objectives of this study are: (i) to characterize tissue-specific phenolic distributions across pulp (RTP), seeds (RTS), seedless pomace (SRTP), and seeded pomace (RTPS) at fraction-level resolution (FP/EP/GP/IBP); (ii) to evaluate functional bioactivities (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP, and α-glucosidase inhibition with mode-of-inhibition analysis) and link them to chemical markers through UHPLC–Q-TOF-MS and HPLC-MS/MS, principal component analysis (PCA), and Pearson correlations; and (iii) to develop valorization strategies by identifying tissue–fraction combinations best suited for functional ingredient development. This hypothesis-driven framework establishes a clear trajectory from chemical composition to functional potential, providing a scientific foundation for the sustainable use of RT processing by-products in food systems.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Trolox (6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid), ABTS (2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt), and DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) were obtained from TO-KYO Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Japan). 2,4,6-Tri(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ), α-glucosidase (yeast-derived, 50 units/mg protein), acarbose, and p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) were sourced from Yuanye Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). HPLC-grade methanol and dimethyl sulfoxide (≥ 99.9 %) were provided by Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Reference standards (purity ≥98 %) for 37 individual phenolics are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Unless otherwise specified, all reagents were of analytical grade and used as received.

Sample collection and preparation

Fruits of Rosa roxburghii Tratt (cv. Guinong No. 5) at commercial maturity were harvested in September 2023 from Gujiaozhen, Longli County, Guizhou Province, China (26.34°N, 106.56°E). To minimize metabolic alterations post-harvest, the fruits were immediately placed in insulated containers and transported to the laboratory within 4 h. The fruits were randomly divided into three batches (approximately 450–500 g each) to serve as biological replicates. As shown in Fig. 1, the samples were processed to obtain four anatomical components. Seeds and pulp were manually separated; the pulp was pressed to yield seedless pomace, while whole fruits were pressed to obtain seeded pomace. The resulting samples were labeled as RTP (RT pulp), RTS (RT seed), SRTP (seedless RT pomace), and RTPS (RT pomace with seeds). All samples were freeze-dried, ground, sieved through a 60-mesh screen, and stored at −80 °C for no longer than one week before phenolic extraction.

Schematic workflow for the extraction and classification of polyphenol subclasses—free (FP), esterified (EP), glycoside-conjugated (GP), and insoluble-bound phenolics (IBP)—from four anatomical parts of Rosa roxburghii Tratt: pulp (RTP), seeds (RTS), seedless pomace (SRTP), and seeded pomace (RTPS). The process includes sequential methanolic extraction, acid/base hydrolysis, and ethyl acetate partitioning, with each step designed to selectively release specific phenolic forms.

Extraction of phenolic fractions

Phenolic subclasses—including FP, EP, and GP, and IBP—were sequentially isolated following a modified protocol based on Arruda, Pereira, de Morais, Eberlin, and Pastore (2018), with minor refinements (Fig. 1). Powdered material (2.0 g) was dispersed in 40 mL of 70 % aqueous methanol (1:20 w/v) and sonicated for 30 min in an ice bath under a nitrogen atmosphere to enhance extraction efficiency while minimizing oxidative degradation. The mixture was centrifuged at 6000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The remaining pellet was extracted twice more under the same conditions. Supernatants were pooled to yield the soluble extract, while the residual pellet was retained for subsequent isolation of IBP.

Extraction of FP

Methanol was removed from the soluble extract under reduced pressure at 35 °C. The resulting aqueous phase was acidified to pH 2.0 using ascorbic acid (AsA) to chelate trace metals and prevent oxidative degradation. Free aglycones were partitioned into 6 M HCl containing 10 mM EDTA-Na2 and 10 g/L ethyl acetate over five consecutive extractions (1:1, v/v). The combined organic layers were evaporated via rotary evaporation under reduced pressure, redissolved in 10 mL HPLC-grade methanol and lyophilized, affording the FP fraction.

Extraction of EP

The acidified aqueous phase remaining after FP extraction was subjected to alkaline hydrolysis with 4 M NaOH containing 10 mM EDTA-Na2 and 10 g/L AsA (1:2, v/v). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 4 h at room temperature in the dark under nitrogen to minimize oxidative losses. The hydrolysate was then acidified to pH 2.0 and extracted with ethyl acetate (5×). The organic extracts were combined, evaporated under reduced pressure, and lyophilized to generate the EP sub-fraction.

Extraction of GP

The aqueous phase remaining from Section 2.3.2 was refluxed with 6 M HCl containing 10 mM EDTA-Na2 and 10 g/L AsA (1:3, v/v), under reflux at 80 °C for 2 h in a nitrogen atmosphere to cleave glycosidic bonds. After cooling to ambient temperature, liberated aglycones were extracted with ethyl acetate (5×), concentrated under vacuum, and lyophilized to obtain the GP sub-fraction.

Extraction of IBP

The methanol-insoluble pellet was suspended in 4 M NaOH containing 10 mM EDTA-Na2 and 10 g/L AsA at a 1:20 (w/v) solid-to-solvent ratio. Alkaline hydrolysis was carried out for 4 h at room temperature in the dark under a nitrogen atmosphere to release phenolics covalently bound to cell wall components. After acidification to pH 2.0, released phenolics were extracted with ethyl acetate (5×). The pooled organic extracts were concentrated and lyophilized to yield the IBP sub-fraction.

Determination of total phenolic and flavonoid contents

The total phenolic content (TPC) in FP, EP, GP, and IBP sub-fractions of the four anatomical parts of RT was quantified using a microscale Folin–Ciocalteu assay (Zahid et al., 2022). In brief, 100 μL of each extract was mixed with 200 μL of 25 % (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 2.0 mL of Milli-Q water in a 96-well microplate. Following homogenization, 900 μL of 20 % (w/v) Na2CO3 was added, and the mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 2 h in darkness with intermittent agitation. Absorbance was recorded at 765 nm. Quantification was performed using gallic acid as an external standard, and results were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry weight (mg GAE/g DW). Total flavonoid content (TFC) was measured using a colorimetric aluminum chloride complexation method (Arruda et al., 2018). A 1.0 mL aliquot of extract was reacted with 0.5 mL of 5 % (m/v) NaNO2 and allowed to stand for 6 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 10 % (w/v) AlCl3•6H2O was introduced, followed by incubation for 5 min. Thereafter, 4.0 mL of 4 % (m/v) NaOH and 3.0 mL of distilled water were added. After 15 min of incubation, absorbance was measured at 510 nm. Catechin served as the calibration standard, and results were expressed as milligrams of catechin equivalents per gram of dry weight (mg CE/g DW).

Comprehensive profiling of phenolic metabolites

Equal volumes of the FP, EP, GP, and IBP sub-fractions were combined in a 1:1:1:1 ratio to generate a pooled extract representative of the total phenolic metabolome. The mixture was subjected to ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC–Q-TOF–MS; 1290 Infinity/iFunnel 6550, Agilent Technologies, USA), equipped with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Waters BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) using a binary solvent system comprising eluent A (0.1 % formic acid in water) and eluent B (methanol), delivered at 0.30 mL/min. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–14 min, 10 % B; 14–16 min, 60 % B; 16–18 min, 95 % B; 18–22 min, re-equilibration at 10 % B. The injection volume was 5 μL. All data acquisition and processing were conducted using MassHunter B.09.00 software (Agilent Technologies), and spectral deconvolution was performed via MassHunter Qualitative Analysis. Compound identification was carried out based on accurate mass determination, isotope pattern fitting, and tandem MS fragmentation analysis. Identification relied on spectral comparison against curated metabolite databases, including MassBank, HMDB, and METLIN, as well as corroborating information from primary literature. Due to the absence of authentic reference standards, compound identifications were reported following the confidence levels defined by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI). A Level 2 confidence was assigned when a compound's MS/MS spectrum matched a library entry for the same adduct or molecular formula within the defined mass error tolerance and reproduced at least three characteristic fragment ions, including the base peak or a high-abundance ion. Level 3 identifications were assigned when the compound's accurate mass and key class-specific fragments supported structural classification in agreement with literature reports, even in the absence of a direct spectral match. Any features that could not be confidently resolved or characterized were reported as Level 4, denoting unknown compounds.

Targeted quantification of abundant phenolics

Phenolic compounds exhibiting high ion-current intensities in the untargeted Q-TOF survey were quantitatively determined using targeted UHPLC–MS/MS. Separation was performed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II system equipped with a Poroshell SB-Aq C18 column (100 mm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1 % formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1 % formic acid in methanol). The following gradient was employed: 0–2 min, 3 % B; 2–7 min, 40 % B; 7–11 min, 95 % B; 11–11.5 min, 3 % B; 11.5–15 min, 10 % B. The column temperature was maintained at 35 °C, with a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min and an injection volume of 5 μL. Mass detection was carried out on an API 4000+ triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA) operated in scheduled multiple-reaction-monitoring (MRM) mode. The ESI source was maintained at 500 °C, with ionisation voltages of +5.5 kV (positive mode) and − 4.5 kV (negative mode). Nebulizing and auxiliary gas pressures were set at 35 and 10 arbitrary units, respectively. Data acquisition and quantitation were performed using Analyst 1.6.3 software. Calibration was established using authentic reference standards via external standard curves (R2 > 0.995). Quantification was based on concordance in both retention time and MRM transition profiles with those of the respective reference compounds.

In vitro antioxidant activity assays

The antioxidant potential of phenolic sub-fractions—FP, EP, GP, and IBP—isolated from four anatomical regions of RT was evaluated using three orthogonal spectrophotometric assays: DPPH• radical scavenging, ABTS•+ cation decolorization, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP).

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The capacity of phenolic sub-fractions to scavenge DPPH• radicals was assessed following the method of Wu et al. (2023), with minor modifications. Sample aliquots (200 μL; 5–100 μg/mL) were mixed with 1.60 mL of freshly prepared 4 % (w/v) DPPH in ethanol and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 30 min. The decrease in absorbance at 517 nm, relative to a reagent blank, was measured. Quantification was performed using Trolox calibration (8–80 μM; R2 = 0.9991), and results were expressed as milligrams of Trolox equivalents per gram dry weight (mg TE/g DW).

ABTS radical scavenging assay

The ABTS•+ radical cation was generated by incubating a solution of ABTS (19.2 mg) and potassium persulfate (3.3 mg) in 5 mL distilled water for 24 h in the dark at room temperature (Yang, Kim, & Shin, 2019). The resulting stock solution was diluted with ethanol to achieve an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm prior to use. For analysis, 80 μL of extract (5–100 μg/mL) was combined with 3.92 mL of the working ABTS•+ solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Absorbance was recorded at 734 nm. Antioxidant capacity was calculated against a Trolox standard curve and expressed as mg TE/g DW.

FRAP assay

FRAP was determined based on the method of Rumpf, Burger, and Schulze (2023). The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared by mixing acetate buffer (300 mM, pH 3.6), TPTZ (10 mM in 40 mM HCl), and FeCl3 (20 mM) in a 10:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio, followed by equilibration at 37 °C for 10 min. Sample aliquots (200 μL; 5–100 μg/mL) were added to 2.0 mL of reagent and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The formation of the Fe2+–TPTZ complex was monitored at 593 nm. Quantification was achieved using a FeSO4 calibration curve (100–1000 μM; R2 = 0.9991), and results were reported as milligrams of FeSO4 equivalents per gram dry weight (mg FE/g DW).

In vitro hypoglycemic activity assays

The ability of phenolic sub-fractions (FP, EP, GP, IBP) isolated from four anatomical regions of RT to inhibit intestinal α-glucosidase was assessed using standardized colorimetric assays described by Zheng et al. (2020), with minor adjustments. Unless otherwise stated, all solutions were prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.9), which was also employed for extract dilution and enzyme preparation. Each experimental condition was conducted in triplicate, and absorbance values were corrected against matched blanks and controls to ensure analytical validity.

Α-Glucosidase inhibition assay

In each reaction, 100 μL of phenolic extract (5–100 μg/mL) was mixed with 100 μL of α-glucosidase solution (0.5 U/mL) and pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to allow enzyme–inhibitor interaction. The reaction was initiated by adding 100 μL of pNPG (5 mM) and allowed to proceed for 20 min at 37 °C. Enzymatic activity was terminated by the addition of 1.0 mL of 1 M Na2CO3, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Acarbose was used as a positive control. The percentage inhibition was calculated using Eq. (1), where A represents the absorbance of the test reaction, A1 denotes the enzyme-omitted control (buffer in place of enzyme), and A2 corresponds to the extract-omitted blank (buffer in place of sample). Concentration–response curves were fitted using non-linear regression to determine IC50 values (μg/mL).

Kinetic analysis and mode of inhibition

The mode of inhibition was elucidated through Lineweaver–Burk kinetic analysis, following the procedure of Yu, Fan, and Duan (2019). Each reaction consisted of 100 μL α-glucosidase (0.5 U/mL), 100 μL pNPG (1–4 mM), and 100 μL phenolic extract at final concentrations of 0, 1.50, 6.25, or 12.50 μg/mL. Reactions were carried out at 37 °C for 20 min, after which absorbance at 405 nm was recorded and initial velocities were computed. Double-reciprocal plots of 1/v versus 1/[S] were constructed to determine the inhibition type, based on the pattern of line intersections at different inhibitor concentrations. The apparent Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) and maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) were extracted from linear fits using Eq. (2). Secondary replots were generated to estimate inhibition constants. The slopes of Lineweaver–Burk plots were plotted against inhibitor concentrations to obtain Ki (equilibrium constant for the enzyme–inhibitor complex, EI), while the y-intercepts were plotted to determine Kis (for the enzyme–substrate–inhibitor complex, ESI), based on Eqs. (3) and (4) (Chen, Zhang, Yang, Zhang, & Chen, 2024). These parameters served as quantitative descriptors of inhibitory strength and binding specificity.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) based on three biological replicates, each comprising two technical replicates for mass spectrometric quantification (duplicate injections). Instrumental variability, assessed as relative standard deviation (RSD), remained below 5 %. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA), and group means were compared by Duncan's multiple-range test. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05. To evaluate inter-variable relationships and underlying data structure, Pearson product–moment correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) were conducted using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corp., USA). Prior to parametric analyses, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed to ensure statistical validity. Pearson correlations were computed using mean-centered and unit-variance–scaled variables.

Results and discussion

Yields, total phenolic (TPCs) and flavonoid (TFCs) contents

The extraction yields of FP, EP, GP, and IBP from four R. roxburghii tissue types—pulp (RTP), seeds (RTS), seedless pomace (SRTP), and seeded pomace (RTPS)—are summarized in Fig. 2A. Among all phenolic forms, FP consistently exhibited the highest yield, particularly in RTP (4.75 %), SRTP (3.83 %), and RTPS (3.34 %), whereas a notably lower yield was observed in RTS (0.85 %). In contrast, the yields of EP, GP, and IBP were relatively lower, ranging from 0.16 to 0.47 %, 0.56–0.64 %, and 0.25–0.79 %, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2B, FP represented the dominant subclass across most tissues, comprising 76.61 %, 43.19 %, and 63.27 % of TPCs in RTP, RTS, and RTPS, respectively. The remaining contributions in these tissues were attributed to GP (15.29 %, 34.32 %, 14.46 %), IBP (4.65 %, 13.46 %, 12.49 %), and EP (3.44 %, 9.03 %, 9.78 %). In SRTP, FP also prevailed (66.26 %), followed by IBP (14.50 %), GP (13.63 %), and EP (5.61 %). On an absolute scale for TPCs, the highest FP concentration was found in the pulp (47.34 ± 3.61 mg GAE/g DW), followed by SRTP (39.22 ± 1.02), RTPS (34.86 ± 1.43), and RTS (9.63 ± 0.10). For other subclasses, the hierarchy varied: EP content ranked RTPS > SRTP > RTP > RTS; GP content followed RTP > SRTP > RTPS > RTS; and IBP content was highest in SRTP, followed by RTPS, RTS, and RTP. Thus, FP was most abundant in the pulp, EP in seeded pomace, and GP and IBP in seed-free pomace. The enrichment of bound phenolics in pomace is likely attributable to its high dietary fiber content (> 60 %), predominantly insoluble (> 55 %), which offers abundant ester- and ether-binding sites within cell wall matrices (Huang et al., 2022). The predominance of FP aligns with previous reports highlighting the prevalence of free phenolics in most fruits and vegetables, with bound phenolics more closely associated with fibrous residues (Chan, Huang, Wong, Li, & Zhao, 2023; Yang, Li, Xian, Huang, & Yang, 2022). Notably, FP levels in RT pomace exceeded those reported previously (8.5–26.45 mg GAE/g DW) (Zhou, Zhong, Huang, & Cheng, 2023), a discrepancy potentially stemming from cultivar-specific traits, solvent polarity, pH, extraction conditions, or pre-analytical processing parameters (Arruda, Pereira, & Pastore, 2016). Collectively, these findings suggest that phenolics in RT are primarily present in the free form, with the pulp serving as the richest source of FP and seeds contributing the least.

All values are expressed on a dry-weight basis and presented as mean ± SD (n = 3).

A distinct distribution pattern was observed for TFCs, although FP again emerged as the predominant subclass (Fig. 2C). In RTP, SRTP, and RTPS, FP accounted for 92.60 %, 74.55 %, and 71.38 % of TFCs, respectively, while IBP (3.49 %, 13.90 %, 15.53 %), EP (3.00 %, 6.56 %, 7.67 %), and GP (0.89 %, 5.00 %, 5.43 %) comprised the remainder. In RTS, FP still dominated (62.97 %), followed by GP (15.24 %), IBP (14.85 %), and EP (6.93 %). Absolute FP concentrations decreased in the order: RTP (24.00 ± 0.08 mg CE/g DW) > SRTP (18.31 ± 0.50) > RTPS (15.36 ± 0.53) > RTS (3.18 ± 0.03). EP was most abundant in RTPS, while the highest GP and IBP concentrations were observed in SRTP. The resulting subclass-specific rankings were: EP: RTPS > SRTP > RTP > RTS; GP: SRTP > RTPS > RTS > RTP; and IBP: SRTP > RTPS > RTP > RTS. These results indicate that TFCs was highest in the pulp for FP, in RTPS for EP, and in SRTP for GP and IBP—broadly mirroring TPCs distribution trends. Across all tissues, TFCs values were significantly lower than corresponding TPCs values (p < 0.05), supporting the view that non-flavonoid phenolics constitute a major proportion of RT polyphenols. Previously, FP-type TFCs in RT pomace was reported at approximately 5.9 mg CE/g DW (Zhou et al., 2023). In the present study, SRTP and RTPS exhibited markedly higher values, exceeding even those recorded for high-flavonoid matrices such as kiwifruit peel (114.82 mg CE/g DW) (Zhang et al., 2021). Overall, these results confirm that flavonoids in RT occur primarily in the free form, with the highest concentrations found in the pulp and the lowest in seeds.

Qualitative identification of phenolic and flavonoid compounds

A comprehensive qualitative survey of phenolic constituents in the four phenolic fractions from each RT tissue was conducted using UHPLC–QTOF–MS. Peak detection and deconvolution were carried out using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis software. Compound annotation was achieved through integration of accurate mass measurement, isotope distribution pattern analysis, and MS2 fragmentation data, which were matched against curated spectral libraries (MassBank, HMDB, and METLIN) and supported by literature references (Huang et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024). In total, 59 compounds were annotated, comprising 14 phenolic acids, 33 flavonoids, 3 procyanidins, 3 fatty acids, and 6 additional metabolites (Table 1). Phenolic acids

Fourteen phenolic acids were identified, encompassing hydroxybenzoic acids, hydroxycinnamic acids, and related derivatives. These included malic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, protocatechuic acid, cinnamic acid, ellagic acid, p-coumaric acid, chlorogenic acid, sinapic acid, ferulic acid, gallic acid, 6-gingerol, brevifolincarboxylic acid, quinic acid, and syringic acid. For instance, ellagic acid (Compd 10) was recognized by [M–H]− at m/z 301.0008 with a prominent fragment at m/z 257.0118 consistent with CO2 loss. Gallic acid (Compd 33) showed [M–H]− at m/z 169.0144 and a characteristic CO2-loss fragment at m/z 125.0229 (Huang et al., 2022). The identity of compound 3 was confirmed as p-hydroxybenzoic acid based on its precursor ion at m/z 137.0251. Compounds 4 (m/z 153.0201 [M − H]−), 6 (m/z 147.0459 [M − H]−), 10 (m/z 301.0008 [M − H]−), 11 (m/z 197.0459 [M − H]−), 13 (m/z 163.041 [M − H]−), 16 (m/z 353.0884 [M − H]−), 21 (m/z 223.0614 [M − H]−), 24 (m/z 193.0506 [M − H]−), 54 (m/z 293.1765 [M − H]−), 58 (m/z 291.0146 [M − H]−), and 59 (m/z 191.0549 [M − H]−) were identified as protocatechuic acid, cinnamic acid, ellagic acid, syringic acid, coumaric acid, chlorogenic acid, sinapic acid, ferulic acid, 6-gingerol, brevifolincarboxylic acid, and quinic acid. The observed exact masses and fragment ions agreed with literature precedents. All identifications were supported by accurate mass data and characteristic MS/MS fragmentation patterns consistent with previously reported values.

Flavonoids

A total of 33 flavonoids were identified in this study. For instance, compounds 7 ([M − H]− m/z 289.0722) and 9 ([M + H]+ m/z 291.0863) were likely isomers, identified as epicatechin and catechin, respectively, based on their similar fragmentation patterns. Compound 28 was identified as (−)-epigallocatechin, with a precursor ion at m/z 305.0678 [M − H]−. Its MS2 analysis revealed a fragment at m/z 274.0481, indicating the loss of CH3OH. Compound 40, identified as quercetin-4-O-glycoside, displayed fragment ions at m/z 447.0739 (loss of H2O) and m/z 375.0455 (loss of -C3H6O3). Quercetin ([M − H]− m/z 301.0356) and isoquercitrin ([M − H]− m/z 463.0889), corresponding to compounds 31 and 15, respectively, were identified. These flavonoids include genistein (17, [M + H]+ m/z 271.0599), naringin (18, [M − H]− m/z 579.1741), taxifolin (19, [M − H]− m/z 303.0493), xanthoxylin (20, [M + H]+ m/z 197.0802), osajin (22, [M + H]+ m/z 405.1733), isookanin-7-O-glucoside (23, [M − H]− m/z 449.1068), aromadendrin (25, [M − H]− m/z 287.0566), esculetin (26, [M − H]− m/z 177.0201), naringenin (27, [M − H]− m/z 271.0602), rutin (29, [M − H]− m/z 610.153), tiliroside (30, [M − H]− m/z 593.1324), nobiletin (32, [M − H]− m/z 401.1261), hesperetin (36, [M − H]− m/z 301.0718), apigenin-8-C-glucoside (38, [M − H]− m/z 373.0921), quercetin-3-O-rutinoside (39, [M + H]+ m/z 611.1606), reynoutrin (41, [M + H]+ m/z 435.0926), plantaginin (42, [M + H]+ m/z 449.1083), puerarin (43, [M + H]+ m/z 417.1156), acacetin (44, [M + H]+ m/z 285.0764), casticin (45, [M + H]+ m/z 375.1056), luteolin (46, [M + H]+ m/z 287.055), kaempferol-7-O-hexoside (47, [M + H]+ m/z 449.104), isorhamnetin (48, [M + H]+ m/z 317.0656), kaempferol (49, [M + H]+ m/z 287.0552), eriodictyol (51, [M + H]+ m/z 289.0712), and phloretin (52, [M + H]+ m/z 275.0915).

Procyanidins

Three procyanidins—B1, B2, and C1—were identified in the four parts of RT. Compound 2 ([M − H]− m/z 577.1357) was identified as procyanidin B1, with MS2 ions at m/z 559.1132, indicating the loss of H₂O. Additional MS2 ions at m/z 407.0761 likely originated from the loss of -C8H8O3 (Yang et al., 2020). Compound 5 ([M − H]− m/z 865.1976) and compound 12 ([M + H]+ m/z 579.1497) were identified as procyanidin C1 and B2, respectively.

Other compounds

Beyond phenolics, several additional metabolites were observed. 1-Hydroxyeuscaphic acid (Compd 50, m/z 503.3382 [M–H]−) produced MS2 ions at m/z 485.3241 (–H₂O) and m/z 441.3334 (–CO₂). Fatty acids included species assigned as linoleic and linolenic acids (Compds 53 and 55; m/z 277.2173 and m/z 279.2338 [M–H]−, respectively) and palmitic acid (Compd 57, m/z 255.2339 [M–H]−); a fragment at m/z 237.3 from Compd 37 indicated H2O loss. Additional constituents comprised aesculetin (Compd 8, m/z 179.034 [M + H]+), naringenin-7-O-glucoside (Compd 14, m/z 433.1117 [M–H]−), scopoletin (Compd 35, m/z 191.0348 [M–H]−) and myristic acid (Compd 56, m/z 227.2022 [M–H]−).

Across tissues, 42, 42, 39 and 59 compounds were recorded for RTP, SRTP, RTS and RTPS, respectively. Thus, RTPS exhibited the highest diversity of polyphenols and flavonoids, whereas RTS showed the least.RTP and SRTP displayed broadly similar phenolic/flavonoid patterns, while RTS and RTPS contained distinctive species, including cinnamic acid, naringenin-7-O-glucoside, taxifolin, sinapic acid, isookanin-7-O-glucoside, ferulic acid, aromadendrin, esculetin, naringenin, nobiletin, hesperetin and apigenin-8-C-glucoside. By contrast, the RTS fraction lacked procyanidin B1, procyanidin B2, esculetin, genistein, naringin, xanthoxylin, osajin, naringenin, quercetin-3-O-rutinoside, quercetin-4-O-glucoside, reynoutrin, plantaginin, puerarin, acacetin, casticin, luteolin, isorhamnetin, 1-hydroxyeuscaphic acid, eriodictyol and phloretin, pointing to a comparatively restricted seed metabolome. Compared with a previous study that catalogued 42 compounds in RTPS (Huang et al., 2022), 59 were detected here while the principal constituents remained aligned. For whole RT fruit, Xu et al. (2023) reported 38 phenolics and first noted tiliroside, sinapic acid, taxifolin and phloretin. In the present work, 42 compounds were found in RTP, whereas caprylic acid and taxifolin were not observed. Such differences are reasonably attributed to variation in plant source and maturity, extraction chemistry and ionisation/fragmentation settings, which are known to shift detectability across phenolic subclasses without changing the underlying qualitative landscape.

Comparison of major phenolic composition and content

Targeted HPLC–MS/MS analysis was conducted to quantify selected phenolic compounds in the FP, EP, GP, and IBP fractions across the four RT matrices. Nineteen marker compounds were reliably quantified, while others either remained undetectable or were excluded due to low signal intensity. Key compound-level values are summarized in Table 2. In RTP-FP, malic acid (2405.00 μg/g DW), epicatechin (1083.25 μg/g), and catechin (1022.54 μg/g) were particularly abundant, accompanied by elevated levels of ellagic acid (252.88 μg/g), isoquercitrin (150.84 μg/g), quinic acid (138.51 μg/g), and gallic acid (125.48 μg/g). Lower levels were observed in RTS-FP, although epicatechin (320.04 μg/g) and catechin (259.14 μg/g) remained dominant. SRTP-FP showed substantial enrichment in malic acid (1656.99 μg/g), epicatechin (975.17 μg/g), catechin (890.58 μg/g), and gallic acid (182.53 μg/g), with the latter being the highest FP-gallic acid value recorded. RTPS-FP presented a comparable profile. Notably, ellagic acid was detected in RTP-FP but not in the other tissues, and luteolin, naringin, and kaempferol-7-O-glucopyranoside were absent in RTS-FP.

In EP fractions, clear tissue-specific differences were observed. RTP-EP primarily contained malic acid (301.20 μg/g), gallic acid (177.48 μg/g), ellagic acid (80.26 μg/g), and protocatechuic acid (69.11 μg/g). RTS-EP exhibited slightly higher levels of malic acid (315.38 μg/g), gallic acid (206.04 μg/g), and ellagic acid (90.94 μg/g), along with protocatechuic acid (39.35 μg/g), catechin (36.23 μg/g), and epicatechin (29.05 μg/g). SRTP-EP was rich in malic acid (473.64 μg/g), gallic acid (194.88 μg/g), ellagic acid (89.35 μg/g), and protocatechuic acid (67.82 μg/g). RTPS-EP contained the highest levels of all major phenolics, including malic acid (474.02 μg/g), gallic acid (383.11 μg/g), ellagic acid (161.05 μg/g), and protocatechuic acid (82.41 μg/g), as well as catechin (30.61 μg/g) and epicatechin (27.51 μg/g), which were undetected in RTP-EP and SRTP-EP. Overall, RTPS-EP showed the highest values for malic, gallic, ellagic, and protocatechuic acids. Moderate levels were found in SRTP-EP, followed by RTS-EP and RTP-EP. Catechin and epicatechin were uniquely detected in RTS-EP and RTPS-EP. In contrast, RTP-EP ranked lowest in nearly all measured analytes within this fraction.

In the GP fractions, RTP-GP contained malic acid (184.05 μg/g), ellagic acid (61.97 μg/g), and gallic acid (54.49 μg/g). RTS-GP had higher ellagic (77.10 μg/g) and gallic acid (75.20 μg/g) contents but lower malic acid (151.06 μg/g). SRTP-GP exhibited the highest levels across all three phenolics—malic acid (294.40 μg/g), ellagic acid (133.30 μg/g), and gallic acid (108.12 μg/g). RTPS-GP also showed elevated levels, with malic acid at 278.15 μg/g, ellagic acid at 105.15 μg/g, and gallic acid at 99.28 μg/g. Overall, SRTP-GP yielded the highest malic, ellagic, and gallic concentrations. RTPS-GP followed closely. RTS-GP and RTP-GP contained lower levels, with RTS-GP having the lowest malic acid and RTP-GP the lowest ellagic and gallic acids. Thus, SRTP emerged as the richest GP source.

In the IBP fractions, the dominant phenolics in RTP-IBP were malic acid (150.96 μg/g), epicatechin and catechin (117.63 and 81.78 μg/g), gallic acid (83.67 μg/g), protocatechuic acid (82.32 μg/g), p-hydroxybenzoic acid (39.88 μg/g), and ellagic acid (33.95 μg/g). In RTS-IBP, gallic acid (168.60 μg/g) and ellagic acid (77.77 μg/g) were most abundant, followed by malic acid (58.67 μg/g), protocatechuic acid (53.87 μg/g), and p-hydroxybenzoic acid (26.79 μg/g). SRTP-IBP contained elevated levels of gallic acid (371.96 μg/g), malic acid (314.59 μg/g), epicatechin (102.30 μg/g), catechin (74.26 μg/g), ellagic acid (67.26 μg/g), kaempferol-7-O-glucopyranoside (56.21 μg/g), p-coumaric acid (45.03 μg/g), and isoquercitrin (42.33 μg/g). RTPS-IBP exhibited the highest concentrations of nearly all phenolics, including gallic acid (539.49 μg/g), malic acid (345.08 μg/g), epicatechin (232.22 μg/g), catechin (226.42 μg/g), ellagic acid (109.26 μg/g), p-coumaric acid (129.70 μg/g), kaempferol-7-O-glucopyranoside (75.90 μg/g), and isoquercitrin (43.64 μg/g). IBP phenolics were heavily concentrated in pomace fractions, particularly RTPS-IBP. This fraction exhibited the highest levels for nearly all quantified analytes: gallic acid (539.49 μg/g), malic acid (345.08 μg/g), epicatechin (232.22 μg/g), catechin (226.42 μg/g), ellagic acid (109.26 μg/g), p-coumaric acid (129.70 μg/g), kaempferol-7-O-glucopyranoside (75.90 μg/g), and isoquercitrin (43.64 μg/g). Overall, IBP phenolics were heavily concentrated in pomace fractions, particularly RTPS-IBP. This fraction exhibited the highest levels for nearly all quantified analytes: gallic acid, malic acid, epicatechin, catechin, ellagic acid, p-coumaric acid, kaempferol-7-O-glucopyranoside, and isoquercitrin. By contrast, RTP-IBP and RTS-IBP exhibited markedly lower values, with catechin and epicatechin undetected in RTS-IBP. Certain flavonoids such as quercetin and ferulic acid were also absent from RTP-IBP and SRTP-IBP.

Consistent distribution patterns were observed when comparing phenolic fractions within each RT tissue. In the pulp (RTP), most analytes were concentrated in the FP fraction, with the exception of p-hydroxybenzoic acid, protocatechuic acid, p-coumaric acid, and kaempferol-7-O-glucopyranoside, which were more abundant in the bound fractions. In seeds (RTS), gallic and malic acids were predominant in the EP fraction, while epicatechin and catechin were mainly found in FP fraction, and gallic acid was the dominant constituent in IBP fraction. For both SRTP and RTPS, FP fractions were dominated by malic acid, epicatechin, and catechin; EP fractions were likewise led by malic acid, while IBP fractions featured gallic acid as the principal compound.

At the matrix–fraction level, several standout maxima were observed: RTP-FP exhibited the highest concentrations of catechin, epicatechin, ellagic acid, malic acid, and quinic acid; SRTP-IBP showed the greatest protocatechuic acid content; RTPS-IBP recorded the highest gallic acid level; and SRTP-FP contained the most isoquercitrin. These phenolic compounds are associated with a broad range of bioactivities. Catechin, abundant in tea, fruits, and cocoa, is associated with anti-cancer, anti-obesity, anti-diabetic, cardioprotective, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective effects (Isemura, 2019). Gallic acid is well known for its potent antioxidant and anti-apoptotic properties (Al Zahrani, El-Shishtawy, & Asiri, 2020). Isoquercitrin, a flavonol glycoside widely distributed in fruits and vegetables, offers protective effects against oxidative stress, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, and allergic responses (Valentová, Vrba, Bancířová, Ulrichová, & Křen, 2014). Quinic acid, a ubiquitous plant metabolite, exhibits notable anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity (Nguyen, Bhosale, & Bennet, 2024). These findings suggest that the tissue- and fraction-specific distribution of phenolics may play a pivotal role in shaping the antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities of RT extracts.

In vitro antioxidant activity

The DPPH assay, based on the reduction of a stable nitrogen-centered radical by electron- or hydrogen-donating antioxidants, is commonly used to evaluate radical-scavenging capacity (Yangthong, Hutadilok-Towatana, & Phromkunthong, 2009). As illustrated in Fig. 2D, FP fractions exhibited markedly higher activities than any single bound fraction and surpassed the cumulative activity of all three bound pools within each tissue. Among FP extracts, the antioxidant ranking was: RTP-FP (37.77 ± 0.06 mg TE/g DW) > SRTP-FP (37.02 ± 0.15) > RTPS-FP (35.07 ± 0.59) > RTS-FP (27.03 ± 0.58), with RTP-FP yielding the highest and RTS-EP the lowest activity overall. This hierarchy paralleled total phenolic and flavonoid content (TPC, TFC), reinforcing the strong quantitative association between polyphenol levels and radical-quenching efficiency. This is mechanistically consistent with the known electron-donating abilities of hydroxyl-rich phenolics such as gallic acid, catechin, and epicatechin (Huang et al., 2022). The ABTS assay offers a broader assessment of antioxidant potential, as the ABTS•+ radical cation reacts with both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants and tolerates highly pigmented extracts (Saleem et al., 2023). Sample differentiation was more pronounced than in the DPPH• assay (Fig. 2E), reflecting the broader substrate reactivity of ABTS•+. Nonetheless, a consistent within-tissue trend was observed: FP > EP ≈ GP > IBP. RTP-FP again exhibited the strongest scavenging activity (209.84 ± 4.72 mg TE/g DW), while RTS-EP remained the weakest (29.20 ± 1.40 mg/TE g). These results corroborate the central role of polyphenol abundance and composition in determining antioxidant potential across tissues and fractions. Reducing power, indicative of the electron-donating ability of antioxidants to terminate oxidative chain reactions, was assessed using the FRAP assay based on Fe3+–TPTZ reduction (Lyu, Agar, Barrow, Dunshea, & Suleria, 2023). As shown in Fig. 2F, reducing capacity followed a similar trend to the DPPH and ABTS assays: RTP-FP exhibited the highest activity (102.03 ± 1.95 mg FE/g), while RTS-EP showed the lowest (5.36 ± 0.12 mg FE/g).

Collectively, RTP-FP demonstrated the most robust antioxidant performance across all three assays, followed in descending order by SRTP-FP, RTPS-FP, and RTS-FP. This tissue- and fraction-specific variation reflects not only total polyphenol content but also differences in compound structure—such as hydroxylation degree, ring substitutions, and glycosylation patterns—which critically influence redox behavior (Hazrati, Govahi, Sedaghat, & Beyraghdar Kashkooli, 2020; Wang, Lin, Tu, & Zhang, 2020). Given that antioxidant activity often arises from synergistic interactions among diverse phenolic species, the heterogeneous profiles observed in RT—especially in free phenolic pools—may yield functional synergy and enhanced biological efficacy (Song et al., 2022). These results underscore the potential of RT extracts as potent natural antioxidants and offer mechanistic insight into their tissue-specific bioactivities.

Α-glucosidase inhibition activity assay

α-Glucosidase is a key brush-border enzyme of the small intestine that catalyzes the final hydrolysis of dietary oligo- and disaccharides into d-glucose. Inhibiting this enzyme delays carbohydrate digestion and glucose absorption, thereby moderating postprandial glycemic excursions (Wu et al., 2020). As illustrated in Fig. 3, all RT fractions exhibited significant, dose-dependent inhibition of α-glucosidase activity, achieving nearly 90 % inhibition at 0.10 mg/mL. The positive control acarbose yielded an IC50 of 0.645 ± 0.005 μg/mL. Several RT fractions displayed even greater inhibitory potency (i.e., lower IC50 values), notably: RTP-EP: 0.241 ± 0.024 μg/mL; RTP-GP: 0.313 ± 0.032 μg/mL; RTPS-FP: 0.372 ± 0.046 μg/mL; SRTP-FP: 0.559 ± 0.008 μg/mL; and RTS-EP: 0.582 ± 0.043 μg/mL. Although RTS-GP exhibited the highest IC50 (4.518 ± 0.274 μg/mL) among the RT fractions, it still outperformed previously reported values for RT leaf polyphenols (39.72 ± 0.59 to 4.524 ± 0.30 μg/mL) (Yang et al., 2022). Collectively, these results highlight the strong α-glucosidase inhibitory potential of polyphenols from all RT anatomical parts. The variation in inhibitory potency can be attributed to known structure–activity relationships. The inhibitory capacity of polyphenols has been linked to the number and position of hydroxyl groups, unsaturation in the aromatic rings, and the stereochemistry of the heterocyclic C-ring (Aleixandre, Gil, Sineiro, & Rosell, 2022). Rutin, a well-established α-glucosidase inhibitor, was notably absent from the GP fractions, which may partly account for their relatively weaker activity. Other active components such as gallic acid, quercetin, and isoquercitrin are also known to contribute significantly to α-glucosidase inhibition (Ji et al., 2024) Therefore, the strong inhibition observed across RT fractions likely reflects synergistic effects among multiple bioactive phenolics.

Inhibition kinetics against α-glucosidase

Mechanistic evaluation of inhibition was conducted via Lineweaver–Burk plots. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the intersection of the lines in the second or third quadrants is indicative of mixed-type inhibition. This type of inhibition involves binding to both the free enzyme (E) and the enzyme–substrate (ES) complex. Specifically, the inhibitor binds to the free enzyme to form an EI complex, which reduces substrate affinity (reflected by an altered Km), and to the ES complex to form an inactive enzyme–substrate–inhibitor (ESI) ternary complex, thereby suppressing catalytic activity by reducing Vmax (Qin, Chen, & Li, 2024; Zheng et al., 2022). These observations suggest that RT polyphenols interact with both E and ES, forming ESI complexes and likely inducing allosteric conformational changes that inhibit enzymatic function. Secondary replots of the Lineweaver–Burk data—specifically the slopes and intercepts versus inhibitor concentrations—yielded linear relationships, indicating the presence of a single, or at least dominant, class of binding sites (Gong, Li, Li, Wang, & Fan, 2023). As detailed in Table 3, the inhibition constants (Ki for E and Kis for ES) varied by sample: Fractions such as RTP-EP, RTS-IBP, SRTP-FP, and SRTP-EP exhibited Ki < Kis, suggesting stronger affinity for the free enzyme. In contrast, other fractions showed Kis < Ki, indicating preferential stabilization of the ES complex. These mechanistic distinctions imply that while all RT polyphenol fractions effectively inhibit α-glucosidase, they differ in their predominant mode of action—ranging from more competitive to more non-competitive types. These differences align with the distinct phenolic compositions observed across RT tissues and extraction fractions, as discussed earlier.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To explore compositional–functional relationships, a multivariate dataset comprising TPC, TFC, 13 marker phenolics, and three antioxidant indices (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP) was subjected to PCA following mean-centering and unit variance scaling. As illustrated by the scree plot (Fig. S1), a distinct inflection point was observed at PC2, with subsequent eigenvalues declining sharply. Only the first two principal components met the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue >1) and were thus retained, collectively explaining 80.7 % of the total variance (PC1: 62.1 %; PC2: 18.6 %). In the PC1–PC2 score plot (Fig. 5A), fractions enriched in free and esterified phenolics—particularly those derived from pulp and pomace tissues (e.g., RTP-FP, RTPS-FP, RTPS-EP)—were distributed positively along PC1. In contrast, seed-derived fractions and those containing glycosylated or insoluble-bound phenolics were positioned closer to the origin or on the negative side of PC1, reflecting weaker covariance with antioxidant-associated variables. Notably, GP sub-fractions formed a compact cluster, indicating higher internal consistency, whereas FP and EP fractions exhibited greater separation by tissue, consistent with reported tissue–fraction specificity. The corresponding loading biplot (Fig. 5B; values detailed in Table S2) identified the major chemical contributors driving this separation. TPC, TFC, and the three antioxidant indices projected as long, co-linear vectors along the positive PC1 axis, with loadings ranging from 0.2786 to 0.2956, confirming that PC1 represents an “antioxidant-intensity” gradient. High positive PC1 loadings were also observed for catechin (0.2947), epicatechin (0.2933), isoquercitrin (0.2894), quinic acid (0.2931), and malic acid (0.2855), implicating these compounds as primary contributors to antioxidant activity. In contrast, simple hydroxybenzoic acids—including p-hydroxybenzoic (−0.239), protocatechuic (−0.143), and gallic acid (−0.053)—exhibited weak or negative PC1 loadings but projected positively on PC2 (0.213–0.482), suggesting minimal or inverse contribution to PC1 and a distinct functional axis captured by PC2. Additional compounds—kaempferol-3-O-glucopyranoside (PC2: 0.480), rutin (0.298), and brevifolin carboxylic acid (PC2: 0.367)—contributed predominantly to PC2, whereas quercetin exhibited a negative PC2 loading (−0.153), indicating functional divergence from its glycosylated form, isoquercitrin. Collectively, PC1 reflects a gradient in antioxidant potential dominated by FP/EP-enriched flavonoids, while PC2 delineates structural and functional differences among hydroxybenzoic acids and selected flavonol glycosides. Fractions located on the positive PC1 axis, such as RTP-FP and RTPS-FP/EP, were associated with higher DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP values, reinforcing the link between phenolic composition and antioxidant activity. In contrast, GP, IBP, and seed-related fractions were resolved primarily along PC2, reflecting compositional differences with lower direct antioxidant correlation.

Pearson correlation analysis

To further elucidate composition–activity relationships, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between phenolic variables (TPC, TFC, and 13 marker compounds) and antioxidant indices (DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP). All data were mean-centered and scaled to unit variance (Fig. 5C). Two distinct correlation domains were identified. The first domain, representing a positive “antioxidant–intensity” axis, was defined by robust correlations between TPC and TFC and all three antioxidant assays (p < 0.01), supporting the notion that phenolic enrichment underlies antioxidant performance in RT-derived matrices. These results are consistent with the electron transfer and hydrogen atom donation mechanisms of the employed assays and corroborate prior findings that ABTS and FRAP responses are highly concordant with TPC across multiple systems (Rumpf et al., 2023; Xiao, Xu, Lu, & Liu, 2020). At the compound level, the flavan-3-ols catechin and epicatechin exhibited strong positive correlations with DPPH and FRAP (catechin-DPPH, r = 0.95, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.91–0.97]; catechin-FRAP, r = 0.94, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.89–0.97]; epicatechin-DPPH, r = 0.96, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.92–0.98]; epicatechin-FRAP, r = 0.92, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.86–0.96]). Similarly, isoquercitrin was positively correlated with all three antioxidant indices (isoquercitrin-DPPH, r = 0.93, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.87–0.96]; isoquercitrin-ABTS, r = 0.87, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.76–0.92]; isoquercitrin-FRAP, r = 0.92, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.86–0.96]). These associations align with well-established structure–activity relationships, in which the presence of a catechol-type B-ring and a 2,3-double bond conjugated to a 4-oxo moiety enhances electron donation and stabilizes phenoxyl radicals (Hassanpour & Doroudi, 2023; Rice-Evans, Miller, & Paganga, 1996). Notably, isoquercitrin, in contrast to its aglycone quercetin—mapped to an orthogonal axis in PCA—displayed strong positive associations with antioxidant assays. This observation aligns with reports indicating that glycosylation at the 3-O or 6-O position modulates solubility and redox behavior, with isoquercitrin frequently outperforming other quercetin glycosides in both chemical and cellular contexts (Li, Jiang, Wang, Liu, & Chen, 2016). Furthermore, strong positive correlations were observed between quinic and malic acids and FRAP/ABTS indices (e.g., quinic–FRAP, r = 0.98, p < 0.01, 95 % CI [0.97–0.99]), despite their inherently low antioxidant capacity. These relationships may reflect co-accumulation patterns with phenolic esters (e.g., caffeoyl- or coumaroyl-quinates) and possible matrix effects that modulate reducing power in single electron transfer (SET)-based assays (Baroni et al., 2018; Spórna-Kucab et al., 2022).

In contrast, the second domain was characterized by significant inverse correlations between antioxidant activity and benzoic acid derivatives, including p-hydroxybenzoic, protocatechuic, and gallic acids (e.g., p-hydroxybenzoic–DPPH/ABTS/FRAP, r = −0.87/−0.65/−0.73; 95 % CI [−0.92, −0.77]/[−0.79, −0.42]/[−0.84, −0.55]; all p < 0.01). These results should not be interpreted as evidence of pro-oxidant behavior. Rather, they are attributed to the relatively lower radical-scavenging activity of simple hydroxybenzoic acids compared to flavonoids (Moazzen, Oztinen, Ak-Sakalli, & Kosar, 2022). Differences in assay sensitivities and mechanisms further contribute to these patterns, as DPPH is known to respond variably to structural and solvent conditions compared to ABTS and FRAP (Chaves, Santiago, & Carlos Alias, 2020). The observed negative correlations likely reflect inverse covariance with flavonoid-rich fractions that dominate antioxidant activity, rather than direct antagonistic effects. Additionally, non-linear mixture interactions—including synergism or antagonism—have been reported even in simple binary phenolic systems (Silva et al., 2024), complicating direct interpretation of pairwise correlations. High concordance among antioxidant assays was also observed, with DPPH and ABTS displaying a strong positive correlation (r = 0.81, p < 0.01; 95 % CI [0.68–0.89]), further supporting the reliability of redox-based readouts and their association with phenolic abundance (Apak, Ozyurek, Guclu, & Capanoglu, 2016). Together with PCA results, which revealed a dominant PC1 accounting for 62.1 % of total variance, these findings underscore the role of TPC-, TFC-, and flavonoid-enriched profiles in governing antioxidant capacity, while PC2 captures structural variation contributed by hydroxybenzoic acids and selected glycosides. In practical terms, phenolic fingerprinting at the fraction level—rather than reliance on individual markers—provides a more robust explanation for the observed variation in antioxidant potential across RT tissues.

This study offers a detailed tissue–fraction–function map of RT phenolics, highlighting the strong antioxidant and α-glucosidase inhibitory potential of flavonoid-rich free and esterified fractions, particularly those derived from pulp and seedless pomace. Multivariate analyses consistently pointed to a shared antioxidant-intensity axis defined by TPC, TFC, and key contributors such as catechin, epicatechin, isoquercitrin, quinic acid, and malic acid. Seed-derived and glycoside-bound fractions, while less impactful in chemical assays, may still contribute meaningfully through delayed or systemic mechanisms given their higher bioavailability. The observed enzyme inhibition patterns further support this compositional basis, with RTP-EP and RTPS-FP outperforming acarbose in IC50-based assays and displaying mixed-type inhibition—findings that align well with the enrichment of flavan-3-ols and quercetin glycosides in these fractions. However, a few limitations should be acknowledged. The use of pNPG as substrate and yeast-derived α-glucosidase introduces some uncertainty when translating these findings to human systems. In vitro inhibition also does not fully account for physiological complexities such as intestinal absorption, hepatic metabolism, and microbial transformations in the colon. These factors could significantly modulate in vivo efficacy and merit further investigation. Moreover, while chemical antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP) provide valuable first-pass insights, they capture only a portion of the biological picture. Future work should incorporate cellular antioxidant assays (e.g., CAA), lipid peroxidation models, and oxidative stress-responsive systems to better approximate in vivo relevance. For glycemic regulation, validation using human α-glucosidase (sucrase-isomaltase), alongside simulated digestion and Caco-2 transport models, would help bridge the gap between bench and biology. In vivo studies—such as starch challenge tests in rodent models or postprandial glycemia trials in humans—would further clarify functional impacts. Finally, given the complex and often non-linear interactions among polyphenols, we believe it is essential to explore possible synergistic or antagonistic effects using fraction recombination and isobologram-based designs. Such approaches could clarify whether the observed activities are driven by dominant individual compounds or emergent properties of the phenolic matrix. In sum, our results underscore the potential of RT-derived pomace—particularly its FP and EP fractions—as high-value bioactive resources for functional food development. Their favorable redox and enzyme-inhibition profiles position them as promising candidates for further formulation and translational research.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive phenolic atlas of Rosa roxburghii, systematically resolving free, ester-bound, glycoside-bound, and insoluble-bound forms across four anatomical and processing matrices: pulp, seeds, seedless pomace, and seeded pomace. A total of 59 compounds were annotated via untargeted UHPLC–Q–TOF–MS and targeted UHPLC–MS/MS, revealing tissue-specific fingerprints that explain the observed distribution of bioactivity. Free phenolics dominated in both total phenolic/flavonoid content (TPC/TFC) and antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, FRAP), whereas bound phenolics were enriched in pomace fractions—particularly those retaining seeds—highlighting pomace as an underutilized yet potent reservoir of bioactive. All phenolic fractions demonstrated strong α-glucosidase inhibitory activity. Kinetic analysis confirmed mixed-type inhibition via interactions with both the free enzyme and enzyme–substrate complex, supporting broad efficacy across structurally diverse extracts. Multivariate modelling (PCA and Pearson correlation) established that redox and hypoglycemic capacities are governed by composite phenolic signatures rather than single dominant compounds. Key contributors—catechin, epicatechin, and isoquercitrin—were consistently aligned with antioxidant potency and enzymatic inhibition. Collectively, these findings (i) identify the bioactive loci of phenolic diversity along the R. roxburghii processing continuum, (ii) prioritize pomace as a sustainable and valuable source for functional ingredient recovery, and (iii) provide a preliminary composition–activity framework to inform cultivar selection, phenolic fractionation, and targeted formulation strategies aimed at managing oxidative stress and postprandial glycemia. Future studies should integrate this framework with in vivo efficacy, gut microbiota modulation, and techno-economic assessments to support translational development of pomace-derived functional foods and nutraceuticals.
