Social isolation and social media addiction: the serial mediation roles of social anxiety and FoMO among Chinese university students
Authors: Xian Zhao, Tongfeng Xu, Xiaosheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the direct and indirect relationships between social isolation and social media addiction among Chinese university students, with social anxiety and fear of missing out as serial mediators in the digital era. A convenience sample of students from eight universities in China was recruited. A total of 1,000 self report questionnaires were distributed via digital platforms including email, WeChat, and QQ between December 1 and 6, 2025. In total, 732 valid responses were obtained (female 454, male 278). Hypotheses were tested in SPSS and SmartPLS through three stages: (1) descriptive analysis, (2) confirmatory factor analysis, and (3) structural model evaluation. SI is not directly associated with SMA but shows an indirect association with SMA through FoMO, including a significant sequential association via SA and FoMO. SA is not directly associated with SMA and does not function as a simple mediator between SI and SMA. SI is positively associated with SA and FoMO, SA is positively associated with FoMO, and FoMO is positively associated with SMA. By examining potential intra-psychological associations between social isolation and social media addiction, this study advances the compensatory internet use theory and the cognitive activation theory of stress. The findings may contribute to a deeper understanding of this relationship and could guide preventive strategies and educational initiatives that foster healthier engagement with social media among university students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33176-3.

Introduction

In today’s digital age, social media generally refers to third-party internet-based platforms that mainly focus on social interactions, community-based inputs, and content sharing among its community of users and only feature content created by their users and not that licensed from third parties. Social networking sites such as Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok are prominent examples of social media that allow people to stay connected in an online world regardless of geographical distance or other obstacles1–5.

Even though social media has some benefits, excessive use of it can lead to addiction and increase feelings of depression, and loneliness6. Addiction is a global healthcare burden, and behavioral addiction is an emerging activity that has evolved beyond substance abuse. Social media addiction, a type of behavioral addiction related to the compulsive use of social media and associated with adverse outcomes, has been discussed by scholars and practitioners alike7. This form of addiction involves persistent and uncontrollable use of social media platforms, often accompanied by withdrawal symptoms, mood-regulation behaviors, and a strong compulsion to remain online, particularly among university students. The incidence rate of internet disorder (often encompassing social media addiction) among Chinese university students is as high as 11%8. For example, a 2024 study involving 4,101 Chinese university students found a negative correlation. between social media addiction and sleep quality, highlighting how addiction displaces time for rest and exacerbates mental health issues9. Additionally, manifestations of social media addiction—including compulsive engagement, adverse social consequences, and significant time displacement—are associated with declines in key academic performance indicators such as GPA, and course completion rates10. University students are in a psychologically and socially sensitive period characterized by identity formation, career planning, and value development. As noted in recent studies, this group tends to have high cognitive capacity but lower self-regulation, making them especially vulnerable to the addictive features of social media11,12. Given these risks, it is critical to examine the underlying causes of social media addiction in this population in order to develop effective interventions and mitigate its long-term consequences.

The compensatory internet use theory (CIU) highlights the importance of interpreting social media addiction from a compensatory perspective13. When individuals experience difficulties in the real world, they may turn to social media as a means of escaping negative emotions. Those with heightened anxiety may engage in social media use for social interaction, online entertainment to alleviate their distress, which could in turn increase their risk of developing social media addiction14. CIU theory suggest that smartphone addiction in college students is a positive response to stressful events (e.g., isolation) in an attempt to meet psychological needs, although it may lead to negative outcomes (e.g., addiction). Several studies have shown that social media addiction among university students is driven by factors such as the need for social validation15 and psychological states associated with campus isolation13. Consistently, empirical evidence indicates that social isolation is a significant antecedent of social media addiction. Social isolation includes both subjective feelings of not belonging and objective reductions in social contact16,17. It is prevalent among university students; for instance, a recent survey reported that 64% self-isolated most or all of the time18, partly due to excessive academic pressure, dormitory interpersonal detachment, and low participation in student organizations19,20. Isolation restricts students’ opportunities for offline interaction, leading them to rely on social media to fulfill unmet social needs21. This compensatory use increases the likelihood of excessive engagement, thereby making social isolation a significant antecedent of social media addiction.

The Cognitive Activation Theory of Stress (CATS), proposed by Ursin and Eriksen, defines stress as a cognitive activation response triggered by a perceived mismatch between expected and actual outcomes, leading to a general alarm reaction that is adaptive unless sustained over time22. The theory identifies three response outcome expectancies, namely coping, helplessness, and hopelessness, which determine whether stress leads to adaptation or pathology23. Hung, Hung24 reported that anxiety fully mediated the relationship between peer pressure and smartphone addiction. Fu, Li25 indicated that stress perception was positively linked to problematic social network use and FoMO, with FoMO mediating this relationship and showing how stress perception can increase social network dependence. Cheng, Liu26 found that COVID-19 lockdown stress significantly predicted problematic social networking site use, with fear of missing out and anxiety mediating the relationship between lockdown stress and excessive social media engagement. Within this framework, social isolation generates negative expectancies and sustained physiological activation, which in turn foster social anxiety or fear of missing out. These factors act as mediators that reinforce maladaptive stress patterns. Consequently, social media use functions as a compensatory coping mechanism, whereby intermittent relief from anxiety strengthens psychological dependence and promotes addictive behavioral cycles. Previous studies have separately examined the effects of social isolation27, anxiety28, and fear of missing out29 on social media addiction. However, these factors have rarely been considered simultaneously, leaving it unclear whether social isolation, social anxiety, and fear of missing out may exert combined or interactive effects on social media addiction.

The present study attempts to fill these research gaps by constructing a serial mediation model grounded in CIU and CATS theory, incorporating social anxiety and FoMO as mediating variables to reveal the underlying associations in the relationship between social isolation and social media addiction among Chinese university students. By clarifying these associations, this study improves understanding of the psychological correlates of social media addiction among Chinese university students, a growing concern for well-being and academic performance. It also highlights the need for supportive online and campus environments that may help reduce social isolation and the risk of social media addiction.

Social isolation and social media addiction

Theoretical background and hypotheses

Social isolation (SI) is an apparent (objective) or perceived (subjective) lack of contact between an individual and society30. It is defined by characteristics such as physical isolation, a reduced size or diversity of one’s social network, and less frequent contact with family and friends31. Donovan and Blazer31 proposed that the level of social isolation or integration is determined by four domains: marital status; the frequency of contact with others; participation in religious activities; and participation in other community groups. As a stressor, social isolation severely impacts long-term quality of life through its association with various health and mental conditions32. In the same vein, Shannon, Bush33 reported that social isolation is consistently associated with higher levels of problematic social media use among adolescents and young adults, since lonely individuals may rely more on online interactions to compensate for deficits in offline social relationships. Social media addiction is defined as a form of addiction that harms the social, physical and psychological functionality of a person through excessive and increased frequency of social media use over time34. suggested that the term “problematic use of social media” (or “problematic social media use”) is more appropriate for describing maladaptive or dysregulated social media engagement. According to Salari, Zarei35, the pooled global prevalence of social network addiction among university students was 18.4%, with the highest prevalence (22.8%) found in Asian studies. Excessive use and lack of control are main reasons for social media addiction or behavioral addiction disorder, which leads to social overload, similar to compulsive buying behaviors.

From a psychological perspective, CIU theory suggests that individuals engage in online activities to escape real-life problems or regulate dysphoric moods, as proposed by Kardefelt-Winther36, and that such compensatory use may lead to negative outcomes, including addiction. In same vein, Li, Zhan37 found that psychosocial problems increase the likelihood of using virtual networks or mobile phones to escape negative feelings, which can in turn lead to problematic or addictive use. Grounded in Compensatory Internet Use (CIU) theory, recent research has consistently shown a positive association between social isolation or loneliness and problematic internet or social media use (PIU/SMA)38,39. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H1. Social isolation may positively relate to university students’ social media addiction.

The mediating role of social anxiety

Social anxiety

Social anxiety is a condition characterized by a debilitating fear of, and avoidance of, various social situations40. Behavioral manifestations of social anxiety may include inadequate assertiveness, rigid body posture, trembling, speaking in an excessively soft voice, mumbling, stuttering, nail-biting, heightened self-consciousness, and withdrawal from social groups or unfamiliar settings. Researchers have postulated that individual differences in the development of social anxiety stem from a multidimensional relationship between genetic factors and environmental factors, such as parenting, peer relationships41. A growing body of research offers divergent views on the impact of social exclusion during adolescence. Studies show that exclusion in this period can result in negative psychological outcomes, such as increased anxiety, depression42. In addition, a hierarchical regression analysis showed that university students ‘social appearance anxiety predicted their social media addiction levels positively43. Zeng, Zhang44 highlighted that anxiety mediates the relationship between social isolation and smartphone addiction. Wang and Ma13 posited that the isolation resulting from campus closures, gathering prohibitions, and social distancing requirements intensified students’ anxiety, thereby increasing their risk of developing addictions, such as smartphone addiction. Based on the above, we hypothesize that:

H2a. Social isolation may positively relate to university students’ social anxiety.

H2b. Social anxiety may positively relate to university students’ social media addiction.

H2c. University students’ social anxiety may mediate the relationship between social isolation and social media addiction.

The mediating role of fear of missing out

Fear of missing out

Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) is defined as a pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent. It is characterized by a compulsive desire to constantly stay connected with and updated about the activities of others45. Within the Chinese context, FoMO can be categorized into two dimensions: fear of missing novel information and fear of missing social opportunities46. Zhen, Liu47 proposed that negative life events diminish the satisfaction of psychological needs and increase FoMO. Social isolation, particularly among university students distanced from their support systems, can trigger feelings of inferiority that heighten this fear. Specifically, students experiencing FoMO may feel a strong urge to stay updated on others’ activities, prompting them to use social media for the latest news and information48. Hu, Zhu49 reported that COVID-19 related stress significantly and positively influenced social network addiction, with FoMO serving as a complementary mediator. Additionally, a substantial body of studies indicated that FoMO acted as a mediator in explaining the link between specific negative life events and problematic social media use50. Hence, we hypothesize that:

H3a. Social isolation may positively relate to fear of missing out among university students.

H3b. Fear of missing out may positively relate to university students’ social media addiction.

H3c. Fear of missing out may mediate the relationship between social isolation and social media addiction.

The multiple mediation model

The Cognitive Activation Theory of Stress (CATS) provides a theoretical framework for interpreting the multiple mediation model, in which social isolation may be associated with social media addiction via its correlations with social anxiety and fear of missing out as sequential mediators. According to CATS, social isolation activates the stress response system, leading to social anxiety (as cognitive activation) and fear of missing out (as sustained activation), and individuals may engage in social media addiction as a maladaptive coping strategy to alleviate this chronic stress. Specifically, individuals who experience social isolation may perceive reduced social support and increased threat of exclusion, which triggers heightened social anxiety. This anxiety then fosters persistent cognitive activation manifested as fear of missing out, ultimately motivating excessive social media use as an attempt to regain social connection and reduce stress, yet paradoxically reinforcing addictive behaviors. Lyngdoh, El-Manstrly51 mentioned that social anxiety can be primarily driven by perceived social isolation, which is defined as the perceived absence of satisfying social relationships accompanied by symptoms of psychological distress. Elhai, Yang52 reported in PsyCh Journal that more severe latent profiles, characterized by higher levels of FoMO, were associated with greater anxiety. Tanrikulu and Mouratidis53 suggested that adolescents with higher social anxiety may be more prone to FoMO, which, in turn, disrupts their school engagement. Gong, Guo54 pointed out that socially anxious students struggle to integrate into academic environments because they fear not being valued in interpersonal relationships. Chan, Van Solt55 also found that people with higher levels of social anxiety or a greater need for social connection are more prone to experiencing FOMO. These individuals tend to worry about the social costs of missing events, leading to compulsive behaviors, such as constantly checking social media for updates. For example, those driven by a strong desire for social connection frequently use social media but experience FoMO when seeing posts about others’ exclusive experiences. To cope, they increase social media use, which may temporarily alleviate feelings of exclusion but perpetuates FoMO, reinforcing habitual checking behaviors over time. Thus, students’ social anxiety and fear of missing out function sequentially as mediators that explain how social isolation ultimately increases social media addiction through a chain of psychological processes. Then we hypothesize that:

H4a. University students’ social anxiety may positively relate to their fear of missing out.

H4b. Both university students’ social anxiety and fear of missing out may sequentially mediate the relationship between their social isolation and social media addiction.

Methods

Research design

We chose a cross-sectional survey design to test the hypothesized research model. Using a convenient sampling procedure, a survey was administered in eight different conveniently accessible universities in China. The inclusion criteria comprised: (a) current enrollment as a university student in China (i.e., not yet graduated); (b) routine use of social media as part of daily life and academic study for at least 12 months; and (c) the provision of electronic informed consent indicating voluntary participation in the study. As noted by Stritmatter56, correlational research evaluates the strength and direction of relationships among variables. Consistent with Amoozegar, Elsiddig57, no variables are manipulated in this approach, and the emphasis lies on identifying associations rather than inferring causation. In line with this design, the present study employed a survey method as an appropriate means of assessing these relationships.

Online surveys offer advantages in accessibility and efficiency, and the increased sense of privacy may facilitate more accurate self-reporting compared with traditional survey approaches58. With the assistance of faculty contacts at eight universities across diverse geographical regions in China (including Hubei, Henan, and Beijing), the questionnaire was distributed through digital channels such as email, WeChat (Chinese Whatsapp), and QQ. The sample encompassed a wide range of academic disciplines, including engineering, medicine, business, and education. Due to confidentiality concerns, the article does not mention the names of the university involved. The data collection period lasted from December 1 to December 6, 2025. Electronic informed consent was secured from all participants, emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw, and the confidentiality of their data. A total of 751 university students completed the survey, resulting in a response rate of 75.1% from the 1000 students approached. After excluding 19 invalid responses, a total of 732 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 73.2%. Among the valid respondents, 38% were male and 62% were female. Data analysis was carried out using SPSS and SmartPLS 4. The analytical process included1 conducting descriptive analyses2, performing confirmatory factor analysis to assess the measurement model, and3 evaluating the structural model.

Measures

This study designed a questionnaire with a total of 21 items. The questionnaire was divided into five sections: demographic background, social isolation scale, social anxiety scale, fear of missing out scale, and Bergen social media addiction scale (BSMAS). The demographic background information collected pertained to gender, age, and length of enrollment (years). To ensure the reliability and validity of the measurement instruments, all scales employed in this study were drawn from leading international journals and had previously been validated in the Chinese context. A double-blind translation procedure was implemented, involving multiple researchers and student reviewers. After two rounds of evaluation and discussion, the final versions of the scales were confirmed. Participants responded to all items on a 5-point Likert scale. The Social Isolation Scale, Social Anxiety Scale, and Fear of Missing Out Scale were rated from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), whereas the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) was rated from 1 (“very rarely”) to 5 (“very often”).

Social isolation

The social isolation manipulation check was assessed using the original, unmodified 5-item Social Isolation Scale developed by Lyngdoh, El-Manstrly52, a well-established instrument with demonstrated psychometric reliability and validity. An example item is “I do not have anyone to socialize with.” In the present study, the scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.896).

Social anxiety

Five items assessing social anxiety were adapted from the original scale developed by Lyngdoh, El-Manstrly51, including statements such as “I have difficulty talking with other people.” The Cronbach’s α for this scale was 0.893.

Fear of missing out

We adapted five items from the validated and originally unmodified FoMO scale developed by Jabeen, Tandon59, which was itself adapted from Tandon, Dhir60, to assess fear of missing out with a primary focus on social-media–related experiences. An example item is: ‘I fear my friends have more rewarding experiences than I do on social media.’ This scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.890.

Social media addiction

We assessed social media addiction among university students using the six-item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS). This scale was adapted from Eichenberg, Schneider61, and its original version was developed by Andreassen, Billieux62. The instrument measures six behavioral components-salience, tolerance, mood modification, relapse, withdrawal, and conflict-with reference to the preceding 12 months. Each item begins with the common stem, “How often during the last year have you…”, illustrated by statements such as “…spent a lot of time thinking about social media…” (salience). The internal consistency of this scale was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.906).

Bootstrapping with 5,000 subsamples confirmed that all direct paths were statistically significant (p ≦ 0.001). The structural model showed that SI was not statistically associated with SMA (p = 0.716). SI was positively associated with SA (p = 0.000), whereas SA was not associated with SMA (p = 0.580). SI was also positively associated with FoMO (p = 0.000), and FoMO was positively associated with SMA (p = 0.000). SA showed a positive association with FoMO as well (p = 0.000). Regarding the indirect pathways, the SI → SA → SMA association was not significant (p = 0.582). In contrast, the SI → FoMO → SMA pathway showed a significant indirect association (p = 0.000), as did the sequential pathway SI → SA → FoMO → SMA (p = 0.000). The values of the effect sizes (f²) reported in Table 3 are positive and exceed the threshold of 0.02 for most associations, except for the SI–SMA (f² = 0.000) and SA–SMA (f² = 0.001) paths, indicating negligible effect sizes for these two associations. In contrast, the remaining f² values fall within the small to large range, suggesting that these associations contribute meaningfully to the explained variance.

Common method bias (CMB) is usually viewed as a threat to the analysis results when the survey method was self-reported66. According to Kock67, if VIFs in the inner model resulting from a full collinearity test are equal to or lower than 3.3, the model can be considered free of CMB. Our results showed that all VIFs ranged from 1.000 to 2.806, thus, CMB is not an issue in this study. Moreover, the heterotrait–monotrait (HTMT) ratios supported discriminant validity. All values were below the conservative threshold of 0.85, except for the SA–FoMO pair (HTMT = 0.862), which remained below the more liberal cutoff of 0.90 and therefore within an acceptable range68.

The finding that the direct SI - SMA association was nonsignificant, whereas the sequential indirect association through SA and FoMO was significant, aligns with what is typically referred to as full serial mediation. The model showed adequate explanatory power for all endogenous constructs. SA had an R² of 0.434 (adjusted R² = 0.433), FoMO an R² of 0.621 (adjusted R² = 0.620), and SMA an R² of 0.406 (adjusted R² = 0.403). Following Cohen’s (1988) guidelines, these values reflect moderate to substantial explanatory power69. Predictive relevance was assessed using PLSpredict procedures. The Q²predict values derived from the PLSpredict procedure suggested that the model exhibits predictive relevance for all endogenous constructs. Specifically, FoMO (Q²predict = 0.393) and SA (Q²predict = 0.431) demonstrated moderate levels of predictive accuracy, whereas SMA showed a smaller yet still positive value (Q²predict = 0.169), indicating limited but acceptable predictive relevance. Collectively, these results support the model’s ability to generate meaningful out-of-sample predictions70. Discussion

This study examines the relationship between social isolation and social media addiction among Chinese university students, with a particular focus on the serial mediating roles of social anxiety and fear of missing out within the context of digital era. The finding that 62.57% scored above the mean aligns with the sample and context. About 43.44% of students scored above the overall mean level of social media addiction, with comparable elevations across all six BSMAS components. Roughly one third to nearly half of the sample exceeded the mean on each dimension, suggesting that heightened addiction-related tendencies are widespread rather than confined to specific components. These elevated proportions are consistent with broader shifts in digital behavior among university students. By 2025, high-frequency online engagement has become structurally embedded in academic and social routines, blurring distinctions between necessary and compulsive use. Algorithm-driven platforms reinforce repeated checking and emotional reliance, while post-pandemic communication patterns normalize online interaction and may heighten perceived isolation. Online data collection also likely attracted students with stronger digital engagement. Taken together, these contextual factors help explain why substantial shares of the sample scored above the mean on both social isolation and social media addiction measures, reflecting cohort-wide digital norms rather than unusually high individual pathology71,72. It is noteworthy that Fournier, Schimmenti73 argued that problematic social media use should not be straightforwardly conceptualized as an addiction, because traditional addiction criteria may obscure the distinction between normative engagement and genuinely pathological patterns of behavior. Additionally, cross-cultural evidence from Shabahang, Zsila74 showed that mindful social media use reduces impulsive engagement due to increased awareness of users’ feelings and motives. This awareness helps them pause and evaluate their urges, which limits overuse and emotional strain and supports more balanced, healthier platform use.

Consistent with the proposed model, the study supported the associations from social isolation to social anxiety, social isolation to FoMO, from FoMO to social media addiction, and from social anxiety to FoMO. Consistent with previous evidence, greater social isolation during and after the COVID-19 pandemic has been associated with heightened anxiety75, since reduced interpersonal contact limits emotional support and fosters rumination. Low levels of perceived social support were negatively associated with college students’ FoMO, as unmet basic psychological needs resulting from insufficient support can heighten FoMO46. Additional studies further indicate that excessive social media use is strongly correlated with FoMO, because FoMO could be heightened by worries about future belongingness76 and the persistent need to maintain perceived social inclusion77. A recent cross-national body of research conducted among Saudi university students77 found that social anxiety predicts heightened fear of missing out (FoMO) because it activates maladaptive cognitive mechanisms such as rumination, intolerance of uncertainty, and perceived threat to social belonging, thereby transforming offline fear of exclusion into online vigilance toward others’ experiences. These associations may reflect a compensatory tendency in which isolated individuals seek belonging through increased online engagement.

In contrast, the direct associations from social isolation and social anxiety to social media addiction were not supported. The finding is not aligned with Costin, Roman78, who reported that prolonged social isolation during the COVID-19 pandemic was linked to reduced technology use. Yet studies conducted during the same period found that social isolation itself did not significantly predict social media addiction, whereas cognitive cohesion within the family environment emerged as the primary predictor79. Accordingly, college students who face objective isolation, such as having few friends or limited participation in social activities, may not display a stronger inclination to seek connection through social media. Muflih and Amestiasih80 indicated that there was no significant relationship between social media addiction, anxiety, and social health disaster risk variables in adolescents. Among Chinese college students, frequent social media use is a cultural norm rather than a compensatory behavior81. Platforms such as WeChat, QQ, and Weibo are embedded in academic communication, coursework coordination, and daily life. Because usage is driven by social norms and functional needs, individual differences in social anxiety may not meaningfully predict addiction tendencies. It also could be acknowledged that, because the current study is cross-sectional and does not included temporal order, alternative causal directions may be plausible. For instance, the Malaysian study by Kutty, Mahmood82 reported that internet addiction significantly predicted higher levels of social isolation among Malaysian university students. Specifically, students who spent more than three hours per day on social media were 7.4 times more likely to experience social isolation and loneliness compared with those who used social media for three hours or less.

Regarding the simple mediation hypotheses, the indirect association between social isolation and social media addiction through social anxiety was not supported, whereas the indirect association through fear of missing out was significant. This pattern is consistent with prior research. Evidence from research on university students suggests that social isolation is associated with higher social anxiety, partly through reduced perceived social connectedness and possible disruptions in social reward processing83. In this context, isolated students often report more upward social comparison on social media, which is linked to greater social anxiety and compensatory online engagement. However, such engagement is typically characterized by avoidance and self-protective motives, such as monitoring others while limiting self-disclosure, rather than by active reward seeking84. Isolation-related loneliness and reduced campus interactions may therefore be associated with heightened vigilance to negative social evaluation and stronger social fear and avoidance, without necessarily fostering the positive reinforcement cycles that underlie addictive use. As a result, socially anxious students may increase their online monitoring and cautious participation, but may not escalate to compulsive, reward-driven social media addiction85. Notably, when offline belonging is uncertain, students monitor others’ online activities to maintain a perceived sense of participation, forming the basis of FoMO86. Once activated, this FoMO-driven attentional pattern can become reinforced through the algorithmic architecture of social media platforms, where intermittent social rewards such as likes, comments, and mentions are designed to capture and sustain users’ attention, gradually establishing habitual checking behaviors87. Consistently, FoMO has been shown to mediate the relationship between loneliness and social media addiction among university students, as individuals who feel more isolated tend to stay more alert to others’ social experiences and engage more frequently online88. Theoretically, this pattern could indicate that FoMO functions as a perceptual bridge between social isolation and online dependency, aligning with the finding that the indirect association through FoMO was significant, whereas the indirect association through social anxiety was not.

Moreover, a reverse mediation model was identified, in which social anxiety was the first mediator and FoMO was the second mediator. This pattern is consistent with prior research. The association between social isolation and social media addiction does not appear to be a simple direct linkage but rather a complex psychological developmental process. University students in China typically experience dual social stressors: on the one hand, the transition to university life often entails separation from familiar support networks and increased academic competition, potentially fostering perceived social isolation; on the other hand, China’s digital ecosystem, centered on platforms such as WeChat, Douyin, and Weibo, offers constant social engagement that may simultaneously reduce and intensify feelings of isolation89. Within this environment, social isolation could be associated with higher social anxiety, as students may perceive a mismatch between their desired level of social connectedness and their actual offline experiences. This association has been consistently observed among Chinese university populations, particularly during and after COVID 19 lockdowns90. Social anxiety, in turn, may heighten cognitive vigilance toward social information and increase sensitivity to social comparison cues prevalent in online environments, thus corresponding with higher levels of fear of missing out91. Exposure to peers’ curated online lives in China’s competitive digital culture, in which academic success, appearance, and social capital are prominently displayed, could intensify FoMO by increasing students’ reliance on digital validation for social self worth92. Students experiencing greater FoMO may thus engage in more frequent or compulsive social media checking as a means of maintaining perceived social inclusion or monitoring social opportunities93. In the Chinese digital ecosystem, this behavioral pattern may be further reinforced by the algorithmic design of digital platforms, which deliver intermittent social rewards such as likes, comments, and group interactions, creating a perceived sense of belonging and emotional relief94. Therefore, this serial mediation may reflect a culturally embedded psychological pathway: social isolation among Chinese college students is linked with emotional strain (social anxiety), which may correspond to increased cognitive vigilance toward social connection (FoMO), leading to habitual and potentially problematic social media engagement. These findings could also highlighting that the Chinese digital environment may amplify these psychosocial processes by embedding social validation within online interaction norms.

Theoretical and practical implications

Drawing on compensatory internet use theory, this study suggests that social isolation may be associated with higher levels of social anxiety, which in turn could relate to greater FoMO and heavier social media use. The findings may provide a possible framework illustrating how emotional and motivational factors could help explain the associations between social isolation and social media addiction among Chinese university students, thereby offering a nuanced extension of CIU and CATS theories in digital contexts.

These findings may provide useful insights for stakeholders seeking to foster healthier or more adaptive social media use among Chinese university students. Educational policymakers could consider supporting programs that encourage offline social connection and emotional well-being, which may help reduce students’ reliance on digital platforms. Families and teachers may also play a role by offering emotional support and monitoring signs of isolation, potentially helping students manage social anxiety and FoMO. Social media platforms could contribute by incorporating design features, such as focus modes or positive content prompts, that may help users maintain more balanced and intentional engagement.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the study relied on cross-sectional, correlational data; thus, causal inferences cannot be made. Because temporal order was not assessed, alternative causal directions may also be plausible. Future studies may adopt designs that explicitly establish temporal order. Second, the use of convenience sampling, resulting in a sample of 737 participants, limits the generalizability of the findings. This approach may not fully capture the diversity of the broader faculty population, which restricts the external validity of the results and necessitates cautious interpretation95. Future research should employ probability-based sampling techniques to obtain larger and more representative samples. Third, the study conceptualized social media addiction as equivalent to problematic social media use. Although this practice is common, the two constructs have theoretical distinctions that warrant further investigation. Future research may benefit from examining these constructs separately to identify their unique correlates and differential predictive patterns. Fourth, the use of self-reported data may have introduced response distortion96. Future studies could incorporate objective measures, such as behavioral records, third party evaluations, or digital trace data.
