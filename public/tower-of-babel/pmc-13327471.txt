Lipid Interference Flagging and Haemoglobin Correction in Lipemic Samples With a Hematology Analyzer
Authors: Yinhua Fan, Yimin Shen, Dongmei Liu, Yong Wang, Jun Cao, Bo Zhao, Yushuai Ma, Zijie Chen, Shuaishuai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLipemia can interfere with haemoglobin (HGB) levels, which is critical for evaluating anemia and polycythemia. This study aims to develop and evaluate the performance of a lipid flagging and haemoglobin correction system.MethodsLipemic samples were simulated by the addition of a fat emulsion to 79 whole‐blood samples from healthy participants. Additional blood samples were collected from 152 patients with lipemia. Plasma exchange was performed to eliminate lipid interference and establish reference HGB values. A lipid interference flagging method was developed, showing the “Lipid Particles?” flag on the hematology analyzer. Lipid interference was defined as samples showing a percentage change in HGB (ΔHGB%) before and after plasma exchange exceeding 4%. The accuracy of the optical haemoglobin (HGB‐O) value was assessed in the samples with confirmed lipid interference.ResultsA flagging method was developed based on the changes in routine blood parameters and blood cell scattergram. Among the 152 clinical lipemic samples, 27 were identified as lipid interference. While the “Lipid Particles?” flag was triggered for 28 samples, yielding a sensitivity of 92.59%, specificity of 97.60%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 89.29%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 98.39%. Additionally, among the 27 lipid‐interfered samples, the HGB‐O value showed excellent correlation (Pearson's r = 0.935) and an average deviation of 0.72% (95% CI, −1.02% to 2.47%) with the reference value.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the clinical value of the “Lipid Particles?” flag in identifying lipid interference, demonstrating the accuracy of HGB‐O in providing a reliable haemoglobin measurement.Keywords: erythrocyte‐reticulocyte‐platelet (ERP) channel, lipemia, lipid interference, optical measurement method

Introduction

In clinical laboratories, interference is a major source of laboratory error that may affect the clinician's diagnosis. The haemoglobin (HGB) concentration is used mainly to assess anaemia and the red blood cell (RBC) status of patients and is an essential component of the complete blood count (CBC). The colorimetric method is the common measurement method and widely used in the detection systems of hematology analyzers. However, its results are often affected by the interference of plasma turbidity in lipemic samples, which may lead to falsely elevated HGB levels [1, 2].

Lipemia is caused by the accumulation of lipoprotein particles, primarily very low‐density lipoproteins and chylomicrons, both of which are primarily composed of triglyceride (TG) in plasma [3]. These lipoprotein particles increase plasma or serum turbidity and cause light scattering, thereby causing interference during measurements with photometers [4, 5]. This effectively leads to falsely increased HGB concentration, mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) (> 36 g/dL) [1, 6]. The most common causes of lipemia include impaired fasting function, a high‐fat diet, alcohol intake, lipid metabolism disorders, parenteral nutrition, certain drugs, and chronic diseases [7, 8, 9]. Insufficient time for blood sampling after a meal may also result in lipemia or inaccurate parameter measurement [10]. The frequency of lipemic samples in routine laboratory practice has been reported to be 0.5%–2.5%, with the frequency of lipid interference in outpatient samples being significantly higher than that in inpatient samples [11, 12, 13]. Therefore, accurately identifying lipid interference samples remains a major challenge and a necessary task for clinical laboratories to avoid misdiagnosis caused by preanalytical errors in the laboratory.

Visual inspection of blood samples, although subjective and lacking scientific validity, is still widely used to identify lipemic samples. Lipemia can be observed in the upper plasma layer with a TG concentration of greater than 3.4 mmol/L, and in whole blood samples, lipemia can be observed at TG concentrations exceeding 11.3 mmol/L. However, visual inspection is very subjective and lacks scientific validity, leading to poor consistency in visual inspection results between different laboratories [14, 15]. In addition, while the TG concentration is commonly used to describe the degree of lipemia, different types of lipoproteins have different TG ratios. Therefore, the degree of blood sample turbidity and the TG concentration are poorly correlated [16]. Measuring the lipemic index is the optimal method to detect the degree of lipemia on analytical platforms, but plasma turbidity caused by other molecules may result in false positives [17]. In clinical practice, samples showing lipid interference are discovered through abnormally elevated MCHC (typically > 360 g/L), and Hb and Hct failure of one or more of the so‐called “three rules of three”: Hct = Hb × 3 ± 3, Hb = RBC × 3 ± 0.3, RBC = Hb ÷ 3 ± 0.3 [18]. Nonetheless, the “Rules” are inadequate in cases with microcytic or macrocytic RBCs.

Accurately identifying lipid interference samples is not enough; only by accurately detecting HGB values can we truly solve the impact of lipid interference on clinical practice. Lipids can be processed in the laboratory to obtain accurate HGB measurement values, the most common method is to measure the concentration of HGB in plasma and obtain approximately accurate HGB values through formulas, or directly obtain HGB concentration through plasma exchange [19, 20]. Still, conventional correction methods require additional manual procedures, such as centrifugation and replacement of turbid plasma with buffer, which may adversely affect the accuracy of other laboratory tests. In recent years, optical detection technology has been applied in hematology analyzers, improving the accuracy of HBG measurements in lipemic samples. Hence, optical haemoglobin (HGB‐O) detection is recommended to eliminate lipid interference in HGB measurements [21, 22].

In this study, the blood cell detection technology of the Mindray BC‐7800 hematology analyzer was leveraged to develop a method for identifying samples with lipid interference. The ERP channel‐based optical detection method was used to correct the measurements from samples with lipid interference. The ERP channel (E‐Erythrocyte, R‐Reticulocyte, P‐Platelet) uses dilution‐induced cell spherification and fluorescent staining generated by Mindray and applies the Mie scattering principle. This method allows accurate measurements of red blood cell‐related parameters (including HGB‐O), reticulocyte indices, and platelets.

Materials and Methods

Establishment of Lipid Interference Flagging Model Using Simulated Samples With Fat Emulsion

Simulation With Fat Emulsion

A total of 79 blood samples (TG < 1.7 mmol/L and TC < 5.15 mmol/L) from physical examinations were collected. Each sample was divided into a fat emulsion group and a control group. A 20% fat emulsion (Sichuan Kelun Pharmaceutical; TG approximately 450 mmol/L, TC approximately 1.7 mmol/L) was added into the fat emulsion group samples, and an equal volume of dilution solution for the hematology analyzer (DS, Mindray) was added to the control group samples. Specifically, the whole blood sample was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and a volume of plasma equal to the volume of added fat emulsion was discarded. Subsequently, fat emulsions were added into the fresh whole blood samples to simulate lipemic samples with different levels of interference. A total of 15 samples were prepared at 1:10 dilution ratios (fat emulsion: whole blood), 29 samples were prepared at 1:15 dilution ratios, 11 samples were prepared at 1:30 dilution ratios, 16 samples were prepared at 1:60 dilution ratios, and 8 samples were prepared at 1:100 dilution ratios.

Determination of Interference Thresholds

After the lipid particles in the fat emulsion were added to the whole blood, the plasma turbidity increased, resulting in a false elevation of the HGB value detected by the colourimetric method. Adhesion of the lipid particles to the cell's surface affected the position of the blood cell scattergram from the DIFF channel. In addition, many lipid particle bands were detected in the blood shadow region below the white blood cell (WBC)‐nucleated RBC‐basophil (WNB) channel of the hematology analyzer. Therefore, interference thresholds were determined based on these changes in blood cell scattergrams and routine blood parameters before and after the addition of fat emulsion.

Validation of Lipid Interference Flagging Model Using Patient Lipemic Samples

A total of 152 EDTA‐anticoagulated whole blood samples with TG > 3.4 mmol/L were collected. HGB was obtained from routine blood tests via a Mindray BC‐7800 hematology analyzer equipped with lipid interference flagging and HGB correction function. The whole blood sample was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. Plasma replacement was performed with the DS hematology analyzer, and the samples were re‐examined to obtain the true reference value HGB‐DS. A difference in the HGB from the routine blood test before and after plasma replacement (ΔHGB%) > 4% [23] was used as a criterion to determine lipid‐interference samples, to assess the effectiveness of the flagging function of the hematology analyzer for lipid interference samples. Notably, jaundiced samples and RBC agglutinins were excluded from this analysis. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (EC Approval Number: [2022]216).

Correction of Lipid Interference Samples With Hematology Analyzer Parameters

In contrast, the HGB‐O value on the RBC volume‐haemoglobin concentration fluorescence (VHF) scattergram obtained by the erythrocyte‐reticulocyte‐platelet (ERP) channel of the Mindray BC7800 hematology analyzer was not affected by the plasma turbidity. Hence, this method allowed an accurate evaluation of the HGB value. Corrections were made for the samples with lipemic interference, and the difference between the HGB‐O value and the true reference value HGB‐DS after plasma exchange was analysed, compared to the HGB‐O value from the RBC VHF scattergram.

Statistical Analysis

The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were used to assess the effectiveness of the flagging system for lipid interference. For normally distributed test values, the pairwise differences between the test values and the true reference values were analysed with the paired t‐test, and the test values are expressed as the means ± SDs. Variables not conforming to a normal distribution were analysed using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney rank‐sum test, and the test values are expressed as the medians (IQRs). In this study, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Pearson's correlation analyses were used to assess the associations between the HGB‐O values and the true reference values. Relative deviations were assessed to determine the clinical acceptability of the corrected values.

Results

Effects of Fat Emulsion Interference on Routine Blood Parameters and the Blood Cell Scattergram

Changes in Routine Blood Parameters

Owing to interference from the fat emulsion, the MCHC of the fat emulsion group was significantly greater than that of the control group (p < 0.05); moreover, except for samples with dilution ratios of 1:100 and 1:60, the difference in the level of HGB between the fat emulsion group and the control group was statistically significant. ΔHGB% > 4% was used as a criterion to determine samples with lipid interference. Notably, routine blood parameters were affected in samples with lipid interference at a fat emulsion ratio of 1:60 and above, corresponding to TG ≥ 7.5 mmol/L (Figure 1A). In 79 simulated lipemic samples, the routine blood parameters HGB, MCH, and MCHC were significantly greater than those in the control group (p < 0.001) (Figure 1B). The lipid particles in the fat emulsion led to varying degrees of plasma turbidity, which did not satisfy the “rules of three” in the routine blood test (0.3*HGB‐HCT≈0 was not met). In this study, 0.3HGB‐HCT was defined as the plasma turbidity score. Figure 1C shows that the turbidity score of the fat emulsion group was significantly greater than that of the control group (p < 0.001).

The mean relative deviation of the HGB test value from HGB‐O in the fat emulsion group was 17.6% (95% CI, 14.9% to 20.2%), showing significantly higher levels compared to the −0.3% (95% CI, −0.6% to 0.0%) of the control group (Figure 1D).

Abnormal Changes in the Blood Cell Scattergram

After the addition of fat emulsion to the whole blood samples, the morphological characteristics of the blood cell scattergram changed due to the adhesion of the lipid particles to the surface of the blood cells. Conversely, the blood cell scattergram from the DIFF channel revealed a close proximity of monocytes and lymphocytes, showing an unclear boundary (Figure 2A). The MonLym_Peak_Rate of the histogram of monocytes and lymphocytes projected in the SS direction in the scattergram of the fat emulsion group was significantly lower than that of the control group (p < 0.001). Closer relative positions of the cell‐particle clusters in the scattergram indicate a higher potential energy. Therefore, the potential energy MonLym_PE between monocytes and lymphocytes in the fat emulsion group was significantly higher than that in the control group (p < 0.001) (Figure 2C). Moreover, owing to the adhesion of lipid particles on the cells' surface, the fluorescence staining of WBC was reduced, resulting in a decreased WBC fluorescent signal, accompanied by a left shift in the WBC position on the scattergram of the WNB channel in the fluorescence (FL) direction (Figure 2B). The centre of gravity of the WBC position of the WNB channel in the FL direction N_WBC_SFL‐P in the fat emulsion group was significantly lower than that in the control group (p < 0.001) (Figure 2D).

Detection of Lipid Particle Characteristics

Large lipid particles, such as those produced by the fat emulsion injection and chylomicrons in clinical chylomicron samples, have diameters reaching the limit of detection in the DIFF and WNB channels, allowing the detection of these particles. Figure 3A shows the formation of a band in the scattergram from the WNB channel (the blood shadow region). Signal processing was performed on the samples and lipid particle templates (Figure 3B) via a visual artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm, and cross‐correlation analysis was performed between the signals from the samples and a predefined lipid particle template to calculate the lipid particle score. The lipid particle score of the fat emulsion group was significantly greater than that of the control group (p < 0.001), and the value of lipid particles in the fat emulsion group was significantly greater than that in the control group (p < 0.001) (Figure 3C).

Establishment of Lipid Interference Flagging Model

Based on the effects of fat emulsion interference on routine blood parameters and blood cell scattergrams (Figures 1, 2, 3), as well as the previous analysis of clinical lipemic samples, the clinical lipemic samples were divided into four categories. The identification of different categories of lipid interference samples was used to determine the flagging rules (Figure 4). A total of four branch rules were set to cover the identification of different types of lipid interference samples. Category 1: significantly abnormal routine blood parameters and slightly abnormal cell scattergram, suitable for samples with severe lipid interference; Category 2: lipid particle characteristic areas and slightly abnormal cell scattergram, suitable for samples from patients intravenously injected with fat emulsion; Category 3: significantly abnormal cell scattergram and mildly abnormal blood routine parameters, suitable for exogenous and endogenous lipemic samples; and Category 4: large deviation from the RET channel test value of HGB‐O, suitable for samples with detectable reticulocytes, employing the ERP channel.

Classification and flagging rules for lipid interference samples.

Performance of Lipid Interference Flagging Model for Clinical Lipemia Interference Samples

Among the 152 whole blood samples, 27 samples presented with lipid interference, with a median TG concentration of 32.20 mmol/L (IQR, 23.39–38.01 mmol/L), which was significantly greater than the median concentration of the samples with no interference (8.66 mmol/L, IQR, 5.07–14.78 mmol/L; p < 0.001). No significant difference in the TC concentration was observed between the two groups (Figure 5A). The hematology analyzer flagged 28 lipid interference samples, including 3 false positives and 2 false negatives. This method achieved a sensitivity of 92.59%, a specificity of 97.60%, a PPV of 89.29%, and an NPV of 98.39% (Figure 5B).

HGB Level Correction in Clinical Lipid Interference Samples via the HGB‐O Value of the RBC VHF Scattergram From the ERP Channel

In the scattergram from the ERP channel, the lipid particles were distributed in the large SS signal area and could clearly be distinguished from the RBCs and PLTs. Hence, the lipid particles did not affect the calculations of PLT‐O and HGB‐O. The scattered light intensity of a uniform isotropic sphere has a one‐to‐one correspondence with the volume and reflective index, conforming to Mie scattering. Following blood cell spheroidization and lipid particle removal in the ERP channel, HGB‐O can be calculated according to the Mie scattering principle using the formula HGB‐O = ∑i=1NCHi. In this formula, CH refers to the haemoglobin content of a single cell, which is calculated by multiplying the RBC volume VOL and the RBC haemoglobin concentration HC from the RBC VHF scattergram, and N refers to the number of RBCs in 1 L of blood (Figure 6A). Correction was performed for the 27 clinical lipid interference samples, after which the HGB‐O from the RBC VHF scattergram and the reference value HGB‐DS obtained after plasma exchange were compared. The results revealed that the HGB test value of the lipid interference samples was affected by plasma lipids and was significantly greater than the reference value HGB‐DS obtained after plasma exchange (p < 0.01), whereas the value of HGB‐O, corrected via the RBC VHF scattergram from the ERP channel, showed no significant difference from HGB‐DS (p > 0.05) (Figure 6B, Table 1), with a corrected relative deviation of 0.72% (95% CI, −1.02% to 2.47%) (Figure 6C, Table 1). In addition, the corrected HGB‐O and HGB‐DS were highly correlated (Pearson's r = 0.935), with the slope and y‐intercept of HGB‐O and HGB‐DS regression being 0.94 and 8.44, respectively (Figure 6D).

Discussion

Lipemia is characterised by plasma turbidity due to the accumulation of lipid particles, and is one of the most common sources of preanalytical interference in blood sample analysis [24, 25]. Lipemia may interfere with these analyses through a variety of mechanisms. Lipid particles can increase light absorption, which can affect the results of spectrophotometric tests; this mechanism is likely the most common method by which lipemia affects laboratory test results [26]. Lipid particles can also attach to the cell surface and affect the WBC classification scattergrams of a hematology analyzer, thereby affecting the monocyte and lymphocyte counts.

In this study, a flagging method for detecting lipid interference was developed based on the changes in routine blood parameters and blood cell scattergrams between lipid interference samples and control samples. The proposed method demonstrated good performance, yielding a sensitivity of 92.59% and a specificity of 97.60% (Figure 5B), it compensates for the shortcomings of several detection methods of lipemic samples [27], including the triglyceride quantification in serum or plasma samples [16], the lipemic index [17], or mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCHC) concentration in blood samples [18]. The HGB‐O value derived from the ERP channel RBC VHF scattergram of the Mindray BC‐7800 hematology analyzer is not affected by lipemia; therefore, this value was used to correct the routine blood parameter HGB obtained in clinical lipemia interference samples. The corrected relative deviation was 0.72% (95% CI, −1.02% to 2.47%) (Figure 6C), falling within the clinically acceptable range (≤ ±4%), as the study of Radisic Biljak et al. [28], it indicates that Hb‐O showed an acceptable bias compared to the initial reference Hb measurement in the lipemic samples used by Sysmex XN‐1000 hematology analyzer (−0.4%, 95% CI: −1.2% to 0.3%, p = 0.2447). This study successfully corrected HGB from the identification of lipid interference samples, addressing clinical lipemic interference in blood analysis results. Lipemic interference can mask the true severity of anaemia or haemolysis and may lead to diagnostic errors and inappropriate medical interventions.

In the simulated lipemia experiment involving the addition of fat emulsion, samples showing a TG concentration above 7.5 mmol/L were considered to have been affected by lipemic interference. However, the median TG concentration of the clinical lipid interference samples of this study was 32.20 mmol/L (First Quartile: 23.39 mmol/L), which indicates that the plasma turbidity may not interfere with the test results in real clinical high TG concentration samples. Twomey et al. simulated lipemia by adding fat emulsion and confirmed a linear association between the TG concentration and the lipemic index (Deming regression R 2 0.9994). However, when the lipemic index was correlated with triglyceride concentrations in patient samples, the consistency for both visually turbid and non‐turbid samples was much lower (R 2 values are 0.2399 and 0.7795, respectively) [29]. A study of various types of lipemia revealed that only samples with a TG concentration of at least 20 g/L (22.6 mmol/L) might result in interference with the measurement of HGB concentrations, corresponding to type I and some type V lipemia (rich in chylomicrons) and not type IV lipemia (rich in very low‐density lipoprotein) [1]. Thus, in samples with TG concentrations greater than 22.6 mmol/L, the HGB measurement may not be affected by lipemia. These findings are consistent with the statistical results of the TG content of samples not affected by lipids (Figure 5A).

In addition to the false increase in HGB concentration, the adhesion of lipid particles on the cell surface can affect the distributions on blood cell scattergrams, especially those corresponding to monocytes and lymphocytes. This results in incorrect cell counts and flagging for abnormal cells, such as abnormal lymphocytes and blasts. Since the colour of the plasma from a patient with jaundice also interferes with the colourimetric method but does not affect the distributions on the blood cell scattergram, this flagging rule may assist in distinguishing between jaundice and lipemia samples. In addition, monocytes and lymphocytes in hematology samples also showed unclear boundaries on the scattergram. In the presence of lipemia, the flagging method may achieve poor identification performance. In this study, among the 152 clinical high‐TG samples, the blood cell scattergram of the analyzer identified 3 false‐positive samples as having interference (Figure 5B). However, the effect on the HGB before and after plasma exchange was not significant, suggesting the possibility of insufficient plasma exchange.

Similar to the presence of cold agglutinins, anomalies related to lipids are suspected in blood samples showing MCHC > 36 g/dL or a high number of particles of low to moderate size in the elongating PLT region on the WBC scattergram [1]. In this study, characteristic bands of lipid particles were observed at the lower blood shadow and PLT region on the WNB and DIFF channel scattergrams (Figure 3A), reflecting interference in the PLT count. The ERP channel of the BC‐7800 hematology analyzer can obtain the correct PLT count regardless of lipemic interference. However, the method for obtaining the reference value in this study involved plasma exchange, which could result in the loss of some PLTs in the whole blood and thus an incorrect reference PLT. Therefore, the correction effect on the PLT count was not validated in this study. However, optical detection technology is a recommended method for eliminating lipid interference in HGB measurements [21, 22]. The ERP channel combines cell spherification and optical detection technology, presenting a suitable method for eliminating lipid interference and accurately measuring the HGB value and PLT count.

Nevertheless, the limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Lipids were removed from plasma through plasma exchange in this experiment, and the impact of cell lysis during centrifugation on HGB measurement remains undetermined. Furthermore, confirmation of lipid removal relied on visual observation of the upper layer of plasma becoming clear. Considering the potential errors in visual inspection, it may have been more appropriate to include an assessment of the L‐index and/or lipid panel before and after manipulation.
