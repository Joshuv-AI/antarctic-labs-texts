RAS-targeted therapies for pancreatic cancer
Authors: L.L. Chan, T.T. Kwong, J.C.W. Yau, S.L. Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a RAS-driven cancer with poor prognosis. Although tremendous progress has been made in other cancers with the development of targeted therapies and immunotherapy, cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment for PDAC. For decades, the therapeutic development of RAS-targeted therapy stagnated due to the lack of an identifiable binding pocket. However, the recent breakthrough discovery of the switch-II pocket enabled the development of KRASG12C inhibitors, which have demonstrated effective tumor control in many cancers carrying this mutation, including PDAC. Furthermore, a recent clinical trial evaluating the pan-RAS inhibitor, daraxonrasib, demonstrated a survival extension more than double that of conventional chemotherapy in previously treated metastatic PDAC. These encouraging results mark a new dawn of hope for PDAC management. In this review, we provide an updated overview of the therapeutic landscape of RAS inhibitors in PDAC and discuss the future challenges associated with these novel treatments.

Introduction

The rat sarcoma (RAS) gene family, including KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS, is considered one of the most important oncogenes in human cancer. The RAS proteins are key regulators of major signaling pathways including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways, which fundamentally govern cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival.1 Among the three isoforms, KRAS is the most frequently mutated, accounting for 75% of RAS-mutant cancers.2 Notably, RAS mutations exhibit striking tissue specificity, with KRAS mutations affecting 90% of pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC), 40% of colorectal cancer, and 30% of non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC).2

Given its prominence across human cancers, the quest to effectively target RAS has spanned decades of intensive scientific research yet early efforts were long deemed futile. Notably, the KRAS protein was considered ‘featureless’ like a greasy tennis ball, with a nearly spherical structure that lacks a deep hydrophobic pocket and obvious binding sites.3 Consequently, RAS was considered undruggable. This paradigm shifted dramatically after 40 years of intensive research with the discovery of the switch-II pocket.4 This breakthrough enabled the development of small-molecule inhibitors capable of covalently and irreversibly trapping KRASG12C in its inactive guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-binding state, providing the first definitive proof of concept that this elusive family of proteins could be successfully targeted.

Therapeutic progress in PDAC has remained largely stagnant over the past decade, with cytotoxic chemotherapy continuing to serve as the backbone of systemic treatment. This stagnation is directly attributed to the disease’s strict dependence on oncogenic RAS signaling and the historical absence of viable anti-RAS therapies. However, recent years have witnessed a wave of groundbreaking research. Preclinical and clinical investigations have begun to dismantle this formidable therapeutic barrier, resulting in highly promising data with both mutant-selective and pan-RAS inhibitors. In this review, we provide a comprehensive update on the emerging clinical evidence surrounding RAS inhibition in PDAC. We also discuss future opportunities and challenges that lie ahead.

Historical undruggability of RAS

RAS proteins are small GTPases that function as molecular switches controlling key downstream signaling pathways such as rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF)/mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and PI3K/protein kinase. Among these isoforms, KRAS is particularly important in PDAC, in which KRAS mutations are the dominant oncogenic driver. In particular, KRASG12D is highly prevalent accounting for 40% of PDAC5 and has been associated with strong activation of survival pathways such as PI3K/protein kinase.6

Despite its central role in cancer, all attempts to target KRAS mutations in patients, either directly or indirectly through upstream regulators or downstream effectors, proved ineffective or too toxic, leading to the widespread conception that KRAS mutations were undruggable. RAS was historically considered ‘undruggable’ for several reasons. Firstly, RAS binds GDP and guanosine triphosphate (GTP) with exceptionally high affinity, making it difficult for conventional competitive inhibitors to displace these native nucleotides from the active site. Its nucleotide-binding pocket is highly optimized for guanine nucleotides and offers limited opportunity for selective drug-like molecules to bind. As a result, directly competing with GDP or GTP is pharmacologically challenging, particularly given the strong nucleotide affinity of RAS and the high intracellular abundance of GTP.7 Secondly, early structural studies suggested that RAS has a relatively smooth protein surface and lacks deep binding pockets suitable for high-affinity small-molecule engagement. This posed a major challenge, as classical small-molecule inhibitors often rely on pre-existing clefts or grooves to achieve sufficient potency and selectivity.8 Thirdly, the effector-binding interface of RAS, particularly the switch I and II regions, is broad and conformationally dynamic, making it difficult to disrupt interactions with downstream effectors such as RAF kinases using conventional inhibitors.9 Together, the high-affinity binding of RAS to guanine nucleotides, the absence of obvious druggable pockets, and the difficulty of targeting broad protein–protein interaction surfaces contributed to decades of unsuccessful attempts to directly inhibit RAS.

Switch-II pocket and G12C breakthrough

The perception of RAS as undruggable changed substantially with the discovery of a previously unrecognized allosteric pocket beneath the switch-II region of KRASG12C. In 2013, Ostrem et al.10 reported small molecules that selectively target the mutant cysteine residue in KRASG12C protein. These compounds preferentially bind to a cryptic switch-II pocket in the inactive GDP-bound conformation of KRASG12C and allosterically alter nucleotide preference and effector interactions. By stabilizing mutant KRASG12C in its inactive GDP-bound OFF state, these inhibitors prevent reactivation of downstream signaling, leading to reduced RAF/MEK/ERK pathway activity and suppression of tumor growth in KRASG12C-mutant cancers.11 Importantly, this strategy avoided direct competition with GTP/GDP and instead exploited a mutant-selective reactive cysteine residue, providing the first convincing evidence that mutant KRAS could be directly targeted by small molecules.

This discovery led to the clinical development of KRASG12C inhibitors such as sotorasib and adagrasib. Sotorasib became the first US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved direct KRAS inhibitor in 2021 for KRASG12C-mutant NSCLC, representing a landmark in RAS-targeted therapy.12 Adagrasib was subsequently developed as another covalent KRASG12C inhibitor with similar KRAS inactive-state trapping activity.13 Although KRASG12C inhibitors transformed the therapeutic landscape of RAS-driven cancers, their allele-selective mechanism limits their application to only a subset of KRAS-mutant cancers. In pancreatic cancer, KRASG12D and KRASG12V are more prevalent but they lack the reactive cysteine residue required for covalent G12C inhibition.14 This has driven the development of next-generation RAS inhibitors, including mutant-selective KRASG12D inhibitors, pan-RAS/RAS(ON) inhibitors, and emerging degradation-based approaches.

Development and mechanisms of PAN-RAS inhibitors

Unlike first-generation KRASG12C inhibitors, which selectively inhibit a single mutant allele, pan-RAS inhibitors are designed to target multiple RAS isoforms. By suppressing both the oncogenic RAS driver and compensatory wild-type RAS activity, these agents may help limit adaptive pathway reactivation and provide more durable inhibition of downstream signaling. RMC-6236, also known as daraxonrasib, and RMC-7977, a preclinical tool compound, are representative RAS(ON) multiselective inhibitors developed by Revolution Medicines.15,16 These agents are designed to inhibit the active GTP-bound state of multiple RAS isoforms, including mutant and wild-type KRAS, NRAS (NRAS proto-oncogene, GTPase), and HRAS (HRas proto-oncogene, GTPase) variants. Mechanistically, they form a noncovalent tricomplex by recruiting cyclophilin A to active GTP-bound RAS. This complex occupies a composite binding surface involving the switch I and II regions of RAS, thereby interfering with the binding of downstream effectors such as RAF and PI3K.

ES cell expressed Ras (ERAS)-0015 and ERAS-4001 represent additional investigational approaches designed to broaden therapeutic targeting of RAS-driven cancers. ERAS-0015 is an oral pan-RAS molecular glue that selectively targets the active ON state of both mutant and wild-type RAS proteins. In addition to its preclinical activity, early dose-escalation data from the AURORAS-1 phase I trial showed favorable safety and tolerability, linear pharmacokinetics, and preliminary antitumor activity across multiple RAS-mutant solid tumor types.17 ERAS-4001 is designed to selectively target KRAS rather than all RAS isoforms. It has shown potent preclinical efficacy against both GTP-bound active-state and GDP-bound inactive-state KRAS.18

Allele-Selective RAS inhibitors beyond KRASG12C

The success of KRASG12C inhibitors has accelerated the development of inhibitors against other KRAS alleles, particularly highly relevant to PDAC because KRASG12D is more prevalent in pancreatic cancer.5 However, these alleles lack the reactive cysteine residue exploited by covalent KRASG12C inhibitors, requiring distinct drug-design strategies (Figure 1).Figure 1Mechanism of action of RAS inhibitors beyond KRASG12C. MRTX1133 is a potent, selective, noncovalent KRASG12D inhibitor. Interestingly, MRTX1133 can bind to both the inactive and active states of KRASG12D by stabilizing the switch-II region, leaving the switch I region dynamically in an inactive conformation.19 The effects of MRTX1133 extend beyond tumor-intrinsic suppression of KRAS signaling. In KRASG12D-driven PDAC preclinical models, MRTX1133 increased intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration, reduced suppressive myeloid cell infiltration, and reprogrammed cancer-associated fibroblasts.20,21 These findings suggest that KRASG12D inhibition may remodel the immunosuppressive PDAC tumor microenvironment. Although MRTX1133 showed promising preclinical activity, its clinical development was discontinued before phase II initiation, reportedly due to highly variable and suboptimal pharmacokinetics, despite an acceptable safety profile.

More recently, zoldonrasib, also known as RMC-9805, is a first-in-class oral covalent tricomplex RAS(ON) inhibitor designed to selectively target the active GTP-bound state of KRASG12D.22 Early phase I clinical data have shown encouraging antitumor activity and a manageable safety profile. Importantly, RMC-9805 has been reported to synergize with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy and extend the durability of antitumor responses in vivo.23 This strategy is particularly attractive in PDAC, where dense desmoplastic stroma, poor T cell infiltration, and early T cell exhaustion limit the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors when used alone. VS-7375 is a selective dual-state inhibitor that targets both the active GTP-bound ON state and inactive GDP-bound OFF state of KRASG12D.24 Preclinical data suggest that VS-7375 has improved potency and selectivity compared with RMC-9805 and MRTX1133, resulting in stronger suppression of downstream signaling.

Another emerging class of KRAS-targeted therapeutics is represented by targeted protein degraders, which promote the interaction between mutant RAS proteins and E3 ubiquitin ligases, leading to ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of oncogenic RAS proteins. Setidegrasib (ASP3082), which is developed by Astellas, is a KRASG12D-targeted proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) and is among the most advanced KRAS degrader candidates currently under clinical development.25, 26, 27

Clinical activity of KRASG12C inhibitors in PDAC

Although KRASG12C mutation is present in only 1%-2% of patients in PDAC,28 first-in-class inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib showed efficacy in heavily pretreated patients in the CodeBreaK-100 [objective response rate (ORR), 21%; median progression-free survival (mPFS), 4.0 months] and KRYSTAL-1 trial (ORR, 33.3%; mPFS, 5.4 months), respectively.29,30 The modest benefits observed with first-generation KRASG12C inhibitors have driven the development of multiple next-generation agents that exhibit superior target binding affinity and enhanced potency. The largest dataset reported to date derives from a pooled analysis of two phase I/II trials evaluating glecirasib in 32 pretreated PDAC patients.31 In this cohort, the confirmed ORR was 46.9%, with a mPFS of 5.5 months and a median overall survival (mOS) of 10.8 months. Clinical activity of KRASG12D inhibitors in PDAC

The KRASG12D mutation was once considered undruggable due to the lack of susceptibility to covalent drug binding and impaired intrinsic hydrolysis, despite accounting for >40% of KRAS mutations in PDAC.35,36 Novel approaches such as tricomplex inhibition and targeted protein degradation are bringing new hope to overcome these obstacles. Zoldronrasib, a tricomplex covalent RAS-on-state G12D-selective inhibitor, showed preliminary efficacy in a phase I study of 40 pretreated PDAC patients, with an ORR of 30% and disease control rate (DCR) of 80%.37 Among 28 patients with baseline KRASG12D mutations detectable in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), 86% had a >50% decrease and 39% achieved complete clearance. Setidegrasib, a first-in-class PROTAC that selectively degrades mutated KRASG12D protein, has recently published phase I results.27 Among the 21 PDAC patients who received a recommended phase II dose of 600 mg as second- or third-line treatment, the ORR was 24%, mPFS was 3.0 months, and mOS was 10.3 months. ctDNA response was observed in 10 patients with stable disease or partial response, with a median greatest reduction of 91.3% (95% confidence interval 96.7-78.1). In the overall population of 76 patients with NSCLC or PDAC who received setidegrasib 600 mg, treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) of grade 3 or higher occurred in 9% of patients. Infusion-related reactions were the most common TRAE, reported in 80% of patients, with all events being grade 1 or 2. In the treatment-naïve setting, the novel KRASG12D inhibitor HRS-4642 in combination with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel demonstrated a confirmed ORR of 60% in the phase Ib/II trial presented at European Society for Medical Oncology 2025.38 At a median follow-up of 4.4 months, 17 of 18 responses were ongoing. Additional noncovalent KRASG12D inhibitors, including GFH375, INCB161734, and TSN1611, have also reported their safety and preliminary efficacy from their first-in-human studies (Table 1).39, 40, 41 These agents are currently undergoing further evaluation as monotherapy or in combination regimens.

Clinical activity of PAN-RAS inhibitors in PDAC

Aiming at a broader coverage of RAS mutations and overcoming adaptive resistance driven by wild-type RAS activation, the therapeutic landscape of RAS inhibition is expanding from mutant-selective to a pan-RAS approach.42 Daraxonrasib, an oral tricomplex inhibitor targeting both mutant and wild-type RAS proteins in their GTP-bound state, demonstrated efficacy in previously treated PDAC patients in a phase I study at doses ranging from 160 to 300 mg.43 In the KRASG12X group (n = 42), daraxonrasib achieved an ORR of 29%, with a mPFS of 8.5 months and a mOS of 14.5 months. In any RAS-mutant subgroup (n = 57), the ORR was 25%, mPFS was 7.6 months, and mOS was 14.5 months. First-line data were recently presented at the American Association for Cancer Research Annual Meeting 2026; among 38 treated patients, the ORR was 47% and the DCR was 92%, with 6-month progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) rates of 71% and 83%, respectively.44 The promising results observed have accelerated the development of daraxonrasib into phase III evaluation. The RASolute-302 study is a phase III randomized study that evaluated daraxonrasib versus chemotherapy in previously treated metastatic PDAC. The study was presented at the ASCO 2026 Annual Meeting and met its dual primary endpoints (OS and PFS) in the RAS G12 population. Daraxonrasib demonstrated significant improvements in OS (13.2 versus 6.6 months, HR 0.40, P < 0.001) and PFS (7.3 versus 3.5 months, HR 0.49, P < 0.001).45 Based on these unprecedented findings, the FDA swiftly granted an expanded access treatment protocol for daraxonrasib, allowing patients with previously treated metastatic PDAC to access the drug in the United States.46 In parallel, the RASolute-303 trial (NCT07491445) is currently open for enrolment to evaluate daraxonrasib both as monotherapy and in combination with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, compared with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel in the first-line setting. Notably, the selected dose of daraxonrasib is 300 mg for monotherapy and 200 mg for the combination therapy arm, reflecting concerns regarding on-target toxicities associated with RAS inhibition. Other pan-RAS tricomplex inhibitors currently under evaluation in phase I clinical trials include ERAS-4001 (NCT07021898) and AN9025 (NCT07252479).

Mechanisms of resistance

Although recent studies demonstrate high response rates and improved survival with RAS inhibitors in PDAC, these responses are not usually durable. Several resistance mechanisms have been elucidated, drawing on experiences from KRASG12C inhibitor studies in NSCLC and colorectal cancer. For patients with primary resistance, comutations in KEAP1, SMARCA4, and CDKN2A, are associated with worse outcomes and predict poor response to KRASG12C inhibition in NSCLC.47 Acquired mutations are observed in ∼60% of patients at the time of clinical resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors.48 These include adaptive RAS/MAPK feedback activation, which drives wild-type NRAS and HRAS signaling to bypass the original targeted oncogenic pathway.49 Additionally, acquired missense mutations in the KRASG12C drug-binding pocket, or in trans activating mutations in KRAS, such as G12D, G12V, and G13D, can also lead to resistance to KRASG12C inhibitors such as adagrasib or sotorasib.50

The development of pan-RAS inhibitors has been shown to effectively overcome second-hit activating KRAS mutations in preclinical models. However, whether the resistance mechanisms of these pan-RAS inhibitors are similar to those of mutant-selective inhibitors is less studied. In PDAC patients treated with the pan-RAS inhibitor daraxonrasib, acquired genomic alterations were observed in ctDNA at clinical progression in 60% of patients.51 Importantly, recurrent alterations were identified in nearly half the cases. Structural and functional analyses revealed that major resistance mechanisms include the disruption of daraxonrasib binding to RAS (e.g. RAS Y64 mutations) or enhancement of RAS-RAF interactions that favor native RAS-RAF signaling, such as RAS Y71 or hypoactive BRAF (B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase) mutations51,52 (Figure 2). Beyond genomic alterations, epigenetic reprogramming, stromal environment, and transcriptional changes have also been implicated in resistance to RAS inhibitors.53 Preclinical models suggest that epithelial-mesenchymal transition confers resistance to KRAS inhibition54 (Figure 2). Within the tumor stroma, cancer-associated fibroblasts induce downstream fibroblast growth factor receptor signaling, which impairs the downregulation of cMyc during MAPK pathway inhibition and drives resistance.55 Furthermore, KRASG12C inhibition can shift the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling toward the GTP-bound ON-state (Figure 2). Newly synthesized KRASG12C protein can escape inhibition by rapidly transitioning to the GTP-bound ON conformation, preventing OFF-state inhibitors from binding and suppressing the signaling pathway.56

Emerging data also suggest an orthogonal pathway, the Yes-associated protein (YAP), transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ, also known as WWyTR1), and transcriptional enhanced associate domain (TEAD) signaling pathway, is also involved in the adaptive resistance to KRAS inhibition (Figure 2). The YAP/TAZ/TEAD signaling pathway is a central component of the Hippo pathway, an evolutionarily conserved signaling network that mediates cell proliferation, apoptosis, and stem cell self-renewal.57 Upon KRAS inhibition, there is disruption of MAPK signaling-dependent membrane localization of the apical polarity protein Scribble, which enables the nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ and its subsequent interaction with TEAD transcriptional factors.58 Downstream activation of the YAP/TAZ/TEAD pathway drives activation of muscle RAS oncogene homolog, ultimately reactivating the MAPK pathway.58 Collectively, these findings underscore a persistent oncogenic dependency on the RAS/MAPK signaling even in the acquired resistance setting, providing a strong rationale for combination strategies that cotarget parallel and bypass signaling pathways.

Combination strategies to overcome resistance

Insights gained from BRAFV600E melanoma and colorectal cancer demonstrate that targeting a single node of the MAPK pathway often leads to rapid responses but emergence of resistance pathways is inevitable through acquired mutations or feedback activation of upstream wild-type RAS isoforms (e.g. NRAS and HRAS).49 Durable tumor control requires combination strategies capable of tackling distinct resistance mechanisms.59,60 However, the optimal combinatorial partner for RAS inhibitors in PDAC remains to be investigated. Currently, several approaches are being explored, including RAS inhibition with chemotherapy, mutant-selective RAS inhibition in combination with pan-RAS inhibition, RAS inhibition with immunotherapy, and coinhibition with protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) inhibitors.

Combining RAS inhibitors with systemic chemotherapy is a rational approach because it can circumvent tumor plasticity that is difficult to address with single modality treatment. Transcriptomic data indicate that PDAC can be broadly classified into basal-like or classical subtypes, with distinct differentiation and therapeutic sensitivities.61 The classical subtype is associated with a more differentiated epithelial phenotype and correlates with better survival. However, it demonstrates intrinsic resistance to KRAS inhibition due to lower dependence on KRAS signaling relative to basal cells.62 Conversely, the poorly differentiated mesenchymal-like basal subtype possesses stem-like features, confers chemoresistance, and carries a poor prognosis.63 Preclinical models demonstrate that KRAS inhibition selectively depletes the basal population, inducing an adaptive enrichment of classical-subtype cells.62 Importantly, these emergent classical cells that survive KRAS blockade unmask vulnerabilities to cytotoxic chemotherapy, providing a clear mechanistic rationale for this combination approach. In an early-phase study evaluating daraxonrasib in combination with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel in first-line metastatic PDAC, such a combination demonstrated a high ORR of 58%, a DCR of 90%, and a 6-month PFS of 84%.64 Building on these findings, several phase III trials have been initiated in the first-line metastatic setting, including the RASolute-303 (daraxonrasib with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, NCT07491445), RASolute-305 (zoldonrasib with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, NCT07621718), and an ongoing study evaluating setidegrasib combined with mFOLFIRINOX or NALIRIFOX (NCT07409272).

An alternative, appealing approach involves a chemotherapy-free regimen that combines mutant-selective KRAS inhibitors with pan-RAS inhibitors. Because reactivation of the RAS-MAPK pathway is the primary driver of adaptive resistance to mutant-selective KRAS inhibition, vertical pathway blockade is a logical approach. Among the most extensively studied combination therapies of this approach involves epidermal growth factor receptor antibodies, with clinical success demonstrated in colorectal cancer.65,66 Unfortunately, preclinical studies indicate response with this approach in only a minor subset of PDAC models with epithelial differentiation.67 As multiple RTKs are indicated in driving resistance to mutant-selective KRAS inhibitors, instead of using a complex cocktail approach that inhibits each specific RTK, it is more effective to target a common node across RTK signaling pathways (e.g. wild-type RAS).68 Coinhibition with a pan-RAS inhibitor and a mutant-selective inhibitor therefore more potently blocks RTK-driven RAS-MAPK signaling and has demonstrated broad tumor control across diverse cell-state differentiation in preclinical models.67 Recently, a phase I study evaluated the safety and efficacy of combining zoldonrasib and daraxonrasib in patients with previously treated KRASG12D metastatic PDAC.69 In patients (n = 30) who had received one prior line of treatment, the ORR was 50%, the DCR was 97%, the mPFS was 9.6 months (6-month PFS: 71%), and the mOS was not reached (6-month OS: 89%). In patients who received two or more prior lines of treatment, the ORR was 47%, DCR was 90%, the mPFS was 7.6 months (6-month PFS: 59%), and the mOS was 10.5 months (6-month OS: 82%).69 These encouraging results provided the foundation for the phase III RASolute-309 trial, in which patients with untreated KRASG12D metastatic PDAC were randomly assigned to receive zoldonrasib plus daraxonrasib versus gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel.

PDAC is considered to be a ‘graveyard’ for immunotherapy. Multiple trials failed to demonstrate a survival benefit with immunotherapy.70,71 This refractory nature is driven by the heavily immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment composed of regulatory immune populations, dysfunctional effector T cells, and dense stroma that restricts immune cell infiltration.72 Interestingly, emerging evidence indicates that oncogenic KRAS directly orchestrates the establishment and maintenance of this immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.73 Preclinical models show that RAS inhibition can modulate the tumor microenvironment by promoting T cell infiltration and depleting immunosuppressive myeloid cells.74 Combining KRAS inhibition with immunotherapy including anti-PD-1, anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4, and CD40 molecule agonist led to sustained tumor regression and complete response in some mouse models, which was not observed with monotherapy alone.74 These findings provide a mechanistic rationale to support evaluation of immunotherapy in combination with RAS inhibition in PDAC.

Another emerging combination is to combine RAS inhibition with PRMT5 inhibition, which targets patients with homozygous methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) deletion. MTAP deletion is present in ∼20% of PDAC patients.75 MTAP loss provides a therapeutic opportunity for PRMT5 inhibition via synthetic lethality. Homozygous loss of MTAP leads to intracellular accumulation of methylthioadenosine (MTA), which competes with S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) for binding to PRMT5. PRMT5 is responsible for post-translational modifications of various proteins, including components of the spliceosome, transcription factors, and histone proteins, which are essential for the viability of most cells.76 Importantly, PRMT5 requires SAM to carry out its cellular function. Therefore, MTA partially inhibits the enzymatic activity of PRMT5. In early-phase trials, PRMT5 has shown clinical activity in MTAP-loss PDAC and it is under evaluation in a phase II/III study.77 Interestingly, although PRMT5 and KRAS inhibitors tackle distinct pathways, preclinical studies have shown a synergistic effect in suppressing tumor growth when coinhibiting these targets.78 To that end, an early-phase study is currently evaluating the combination of PRMT5 inhibitor (TNG462) with daraxonrasib in metastatic PDAC (NCT06922591).

Future direction

RAS inhibitors have revolutionized the treatment landscape for metastatic PDAC. These drugs provide new hope for a disease long defined by limited treatment options and dismal prognosis. Although the initial clinical success and survival benefits of RAS inhibitors are promising, managing treatment toxicity and understanding resistance mechanisms will be critical to sustain long-term benefits. Preclinical and early-phase studies are currently evaluating different combination strategies, with encouraging preliminary signals justifying continued investigation. Ultimately, there is strong optimism that a long-awaited turning point in PDAC has arrived, reshaping what was once a disease with a universally poor prognosis.
