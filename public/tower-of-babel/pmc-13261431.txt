Influence of ZnO–Decorated Multi-Walled Carbon Nanotubes and Pure-Bore Biopolymers on Shale Chemical Stability and Fluid Loss Control
Authors: Jianlong Wang, Ren Wang, Faisal Noor Afridi, Muhammad Arqam Khan, Qibing Wang, Shaine Mohammadali Lalji, Syed Imran Ali, Jianwei Liu, Han Ren, Bo Liao, Yingying Li, Mei-Chun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present work examines the fluid loss volume (FLV) and shale swelling behavior of water-based mud (WBM) modified with a hydrothermally synthesized Zinc Oxide/Multiwalled Carbon Nanotubes (Zn-COM) nanocomposite and a Bio-Poly­(Pure bore). A central composite design methodology (CCD) was employed to systematically evaluate the influence of these additives’ concentration on the FLV and shale swelling. The most problematic shale formation (Ranikot) was analyzed in this study. A total of 13 experimental runs were investigated in this article. The result of the study reveals that the nanocomposite material, with its high aspect ratio, penetrates and seals both micro- and Nanopores’ spacing, ultimately creating a robust bridging network. A substantial reduction in FLV from 26.0 to 6.5 mL was recorded as the concentration of the nanocomposite material increased to 4.154 g. However, the concentration of Bio-Poly does not have a significant impact on the FLV reduction. The oversaturation of Bio-Poly causes polymer chain entanglement and coagulation, which reduces the uniform dispersion of both entities. For the shale swelling, as the loading of Zn-COM increases in WBM, the shale swelling drastically reduces. The swelling dropped to 5.8% from 17.2% as the weight of the nanocomposite increased to 4.154 g. The nanocomposite material predominantly suppresses the osmotic diffusion and minimizes the water ingress into the shale sample. On the other hand, this suppression was not substantial with Bio-Poly concentration, and high swelling percentages were reflected with high Bio-Poly concentration and low Zn-COM loading. Analysis of Variance supported all the findings, with the nanocomposite demonstrating a strong statistical significance of p < 0.05 for both FLV and shale swelling. This statistical measurement confirms the effectiveness of Zn-COM in sealing the pores and in suppressing the shale hydration.

Introduction

Drilling fluids are one of the most essential constituents of the oil and gas exploration process. These are non-Newtonian in nature and perform multiple critical functions such as sustaining hydrostatic pressure, removing and transporting drilled cuttings from the wellbore, cooling and lubricating the drill string especially the drilling bit, and assisting in wireline logging operations. As the demand for oil and gas continues to increase globally, the design and performance of drilling fluids must be enhanced to ensure effective and reliable drilling in complex reservoir conditions and geologically challenging environments, especially shale formations.

Shale formations are fine-grained clastic sedimentary rocks the primarily consist of clay minerals. This is the most abundant form of sedimentary rock found beneath the Earth’s crust. Moreover, this formation is also utterly responsible for approximately 70% of the wellbore instability problems. The fundamental source of these integrity issues is the high weight percentage of clay minerals inherent in the shale formation. These clay minerals are Kaolinite, Smectite, and Illite, which demonstrate a prominent affinity toward water. When interacted with water in the drilling fluid, these clay minerals experience dispersion and hydration, which eventually compromises the wellbore stability. These clay minerals contain platy morphologies of stacked octahedral and tetrahedral sheets, which are interconnected together with O, Si, and Al atoms within their structure. This configuration gives shale the negative charge and promotes weak interlayer bonding, and different wellbore instability problems. To alleviate these complications, conventional additives, primarily salt, were initially used as drilling fluid additives. However, at elevated concentrations, this additive poses environmental concerns, disintegrates mud into two separate phases, and can also disrupt the accuracy of wireline logging tools. These deficiencies have encouraged the adoption of polymeric materials as alternate shale swelling inhibitors in the drilling fluid industry. However, these materials degrade at high pressure and high temperature conditions. Consequently, the petroleum sector started to focus on SMART drilling fluids, which offer enhanced stability, environmental compatibility, and multifunctionality across diverse stages of drilling activity. The formulation and execution of these SMART fluids are strongly guided by nanomaterials, which provide exceptional opportunities to improve the characteristics and operational performance of the mud system.

Nanomaterials are increasingly being integrated into drilling fluids to improve the rheology, enhance stability, and reduce wellbore instability during drilling operations. Most of these particles are quite vulnerable to ionic strength, temperature, and pH. Conversely, one problem associated with the nanoparticles is the formation of aggregates, which reduces the rheological properties and can greatly affect the performance of a drilling fluid due to poor colloidal stability. Recent literature has shown that nanomaterials such as multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), silica dioxide (SiO2), titanium dioxide (TiO2), copper oxide (CuO), zinc oxide (ZnO), and iron oxide (Fe3O4) can considerably enhance the characteristics of a drilling fluid.

Pure-bore is a high shear thinning, natural polysaccharide microparticle with particle size ranging between 5 and 200 μm. The polymer is classified as a biopolymer because it contains no metallic component in its configuration. This polymer mainly consists of oxygen, carbon, sodium, and chlorine. The polymer exhibits surface-active behavior that enables it to form a protective barrier across shale samples and prevent them from disintegration. Additionally, it provides better filtration control by sealing off the micropores in the mud cake. However, the plugging efficiency of Pure-bore diminishes after a certain threshold due to its micron-scale dimensions. Hence, it is extremely crucial to add appropriately sized particles for better filtration control and shale inhibition. Furthermore, the exceptional physicochemical characteristics of CNTs make them a prominent entity in environmental engineering, and in the oil and gas sector. In this article, MWCNTs, a major class of CNTs, are used to decorate ZnO nanoparticles. The MWCNTs demonstrate unique sorptive, mechanical, and thermal properties and serve as a highly efficient Nanofiller, imparting the essential structural reinforcement and functional performance to the nanocomposite.

ZnO nanoparticles are a prominent class of metal oxide nanomaterials that demonstrate versatile physicochemical characteristics. This particle demonstrates extraordinary chemical stability, high electrochemical coupling, and a high degree of photostability. However, one major limitation associated with this particle is its tendency to form agglomeration due to strong van der Waals forces and high surface energy. A limited number of studies have investigated ZnO performance in the petroleum industry. Moreover, to improve its characteristics, the particle was supported with MWCNTs in the present study. The composite material is termed Zn-COM, and the influence of this particle on fluid loss volume (FLV) and shale swelling is observed in this article.

The study aims to comparatively examine the performance of the Zn-COM nanocomposite and a biopolymer (Pure-bore) in water-based mud (WBM), with particular importance on their effectiveness in controlling the FLV and mitigating Ranikot shale swelling characteristics. The study also focuses on systematically assessing the individual and combined influence of these drilling additives while establishing the relative efficacy of the nanocomposite over the biopolymer. The composite material was then added to WBM along with Bio-Poly­(Pure-bore) at a concentration prescribed by the central composite design methodology. A total of 13 different samples were prepared and investigated for FLV and shale swelling. The performance of particles in minimizing FLV and mitigating shale swelling was observed in this article. All experiments were conducted under standard-temperature and -pressure conditions.

Materials and Methodology

Synthesis of Zinc Oxide and Multiwalled CNTs (Zn-COM) Nanocomposite.

Zn-COM was synthesized using the hydrothermal method. The flowchart in Figure shows the complete experimental procedure for the synthesis process. Initially, a homogeneous solution was prepared using 0.5 g of MWCNTs and 50 mL of deionized water. In parallel a solution consisting of 5 g of Oxalic acid and 5 g of Zinc Acetate was dissolved in 50 mL of water. The two solutions prepared were mixed and 100 mL was transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave, where it was heated at 170 °C for 24 h. After completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature (25 °C) and washed multiple times with deionized water and ethanol to remove any impurities. The final dried product is the Zn-COM nanocomposite powder.

Characterization of the Nanocomposite Material

The morphology of the composite particle was studied using scanning electron microscope imaging (SEM). The image was collected at 100 nm using a ZEISS SEM. The image clearly shows that the ZnO particles easily diffused into the MWCNTs particles. The rods like ZnO particles appear as bright nanomaterials interacting with the tubular MWCNTs in Figure a. The Fourier Transform Infrared spectrum of the composite material is shown in Figure b. This technique demonstrates the chemical structure of the composite material. The strong peak at 455.60 cm–1 confirms the presence of Zn–O stretching. The peaks at 1280.02 cm–1 and 1416.46 cm–1 represent C–O stretching and symmetric stretching, respectively. The broad peak at 1633.89 cm–1 is assigned to the stretching of CC bonds in the MWCNTs. The broad peak confirms that the MWCNTs provide anchoring sites to ZnO nanoparticles. The final peak at 3522.35 cm–1 represents the OH stretching vibrations of OH groups associated with MWCNTs. The XRD pattern of the nanocomposite material is shown in Figure c. The strong intensities at 31.74°, 34.39°, 36.21°, 47.50°, 56.59°, 62.81°, 66.36°, 67.96°, 69.09° represents the diffraction peaks of zinc oxide nanoparticles. On the other hand, the broad graphitic intensities at 25.94° and 43.25° demonstrate the presence of MWCNTs.

Formulation of the Mud System

The study investigates the impact of newly synthesized particles on the FLV and shale swelling. One laboratory barrel (equivalent to 350 cc) of base mud was prepared by using 300 mL of tap water, 0.25 g of Na2CO3, 60 g of Na-bentonite, and 80 g of BaSO4. Each particle was added sequentially under continuous mixing, with a 5 min mixing interval maintained between the addition of each material to ensure proper dispersion and homogeneity of the mud system. During mixing, it was ensured that no lumps were formed in the test systems. The performance of WBM was improved by the addition of Bio-Poly­(sodium alginate) and Zn-COM, a composite synthesized from ZnO and MWCNTs. These particles were added in the WBM according to the concentrations obtained from response surface methodology. The mud was formulated in the Hamilton Beach mixer, and API recommended practices 13B-1 were accurately followed. The mud was formulated under ambient conditions, and each drilling fluid additive was added in a systematic manner to control lump formation.

Results and Discussion

Ranikot Shale Formation

The Ranikot Shale Formation was collected from the Indus Basin (Pakistan), as shown in Figure a. The formation is of the Paleocene age and was primarily deposited in a fluvial environment. The cuttings were obtained from depths ranging between 11,400 and 11,450 ft. Previous studies have reported that this specific shale possesses a relatively high organic carbon content with type III kerogen and elevated thermal maturity. The mineralogical composition of the Ranikot Shale Formation is presented in Figure b. The formation primarily consists of four major lithologies: 51% sandstone, 30% shale, 8% carbonates, and 11% iron ores. A relatively high proportion of smectite (a reactive clay mineral) was identified. This clay mineral exhibits significant swelling behavior upon hydration, which can adversely affect the borehole stability. Figure c demonstrates the physical appearance of the shale sample before and after moisture removal. The cuttings were extremely wet upon recovery, which was clearly demonstrated by their dark appearance. The high moisture content makes this shale challenging during drilling activity. Figure d illustrates the time-dependent wetting test conducted on Ranikot shale. The test illustrates the stability of the shale pellets after their contact with water. Visual observation was recorded after 1 h of immersion. The images show complete degradation of the shale pellet within 1 h of the water contact. The interaction reduces the mechanical strength of the pellet created at 8000 psi, which eventually leads to disintegration.

Fabrication of Shale Cutting Pellets

Ranikot shale cuttings from the Indus Basin (Pakistan) were acquired from an active drilling operation. The cutting samples were oven-dried at 120 °C for 3 h in an oven to remove any residual moisture. The dried shale cuttings were then ground using a Mortar Grinder, and were placed on a Sieve Shaker for 30 min. Approximately 15 g of the fine fraction retained on a 0.212 mm sieve was compacted using a OFITE hydraulic press at 8000 psi for 30 min to produce cylindrical size shale pellets. API Filtration Test

Filtration experiments on the drilling fluid were conducted in strict accordance with the American Petroleum Institute (API) standard procedures. Tests were performed under controlled laboratory conditions by using a filter paper of specified thickness and pore size to ensure consistency and reproducibility. The API filtration testing assembly consists of a 350 cc filtration cell for the drilling mud, a filter paper of known thickness, a nitrogen gas supply to create a differential pressure across the drilling fluid, and a graduated beaker for the collection of filtrate loss from the mud. A differential pressure of 100 psia was supplied using nitrogen gas, and the volume of filtrate loss was recorded at every 2.5 min interval. The standard testing time for this test was 30 min. Once the test was completed, the total volume of filtrate loss from the mud was analyzed, along with the mud cake collected from the cell. The texture, thickness, and compactness of the filter cake were investigated in this study. However, before analysis, the mud cakes were dried under standard conditions. In the drying process, weights were placed along the edges of the filter paper to avoid unnecessary curling. Figure shows the 13 mud cakes collected at the end of the API filter press test. The dark-colored mud cakes reflect the presence of a higher weight percentage of nanocomposite material, whereas a distinctly different behavior was observed in the higher concentration of the biopolymer. The filtration test confirms the effectiveness of the Zn-COM nanocomposite in preventing the fluid loss from the mud. From Table , it can be observed that loading the mud with this nanocomposite material significantly reduces the FLV. The reduction in FLV was mainly due to the formation of a thin, compacted, and low-permeability mud cake. The nanodimensions of ZnO help it to effectively penetrate and seal the spacing present in the filter paper, while MWCNTs act as a mechanical strengthening to the filter cake by bridging the larger pore throat. This effect reduces the filtrate loss and provides necessary stability to the mud cake. On the other hand, Bio-Poly improves the suspension stability of the nanocomposite and contributes heavily to the formation of a hydrated polymeric membrane, which additionally decreases the fluid loss from the mud (Figure ).

Linear Dynamic Swell-Meter Testing

The efficacy of a shale inhibitor can be evaluated using linear dynamic swell meter testing. The test measures the time-dependent swelling of a shale sample after its interaction with the drilling mud. Around 15 g of the fine fraction retained on a 0.212 mm sieve was compacted using an OFITE hydraulic press at 8000 psi for 30 min to produce cylindrical size shale pellets. The sample was then placed in the LDSM cell along with 175 mL of drilling mud. The extent of shale hydration is strongly dependent on the composition of the drilling fluid and the type of clay minerals associated with the shale formation. The instrument records changes in the height of a sample over the entire duration of the experiment, which then transforms into swelling percentage. Interaction Plots

Figure shows Interaction Plots demonstrating the combined influence of Zn-COM and Bio-Poly on the Filtrate Loss Volume from the WBM. A strong interaction is evident from the figure, which clearly shows the significant influence of these particles in minimizing the filtrate loss from the mud system. The interaction matrix is subdivided into two sections. The lower left portion shows the influence of Zn-COM on FLV. This section clearly shows a reduction in FLV as the concentration of the nanocomposite increases. The decrease in FLV is mainly associated with the film strengthening and nanomaterial plugging. The Zn-COM physically blocks the Nano- and micropores present in the filter paper. The rod-like morphology and high aspect ratio of MWNCTs in the composite enable the nanomaterial to form a strong network and ultimately enhance the stability and compactness of the mud cake. Concurrently, zinc oxide nanoparticles serve as secondary filling material, effectively plugging the finer interstitial spaces and developed a compacted filter cake with low permeability. Moreover, the surface hydroxyl group associated with ZnO particles form hydrogen bonding with −OH, –COOH groups of Bio-Poly. This interaction strengthens the interaction between the nanoparticle and the polymeric chain, and eventually exhibits superior sealing ability. On the other hand, as the concentration of Bio-Poly increases, the FLV reduces slightly. This effect is mainly due to the oversaturation of polymeric material. At elevated saturation, the polymers promote hinderance and flocculation. This effect impacts the uniformity of the nanocomposite structure and decreases filtration performance.

The interaction at the top right shows that at lower concentrations of Zn-COM, the Bio-Poly alone decreases the FLV by developing a thin polymeric membrane. According to the figure, the most optimal concentration was 1.925 g. At this loading, optimal dispersion of nanoparticles and bridging of polymer occur, ultimately influencing the filtration control characteristics.

Main Interaction Plot

Figure a shows the impact of both the studied particles on FLV through an interaction plot. The Zn-COM clearly shows a sharp decrease in the FLV as its concentration increases. This pronounced reduction in FLV is mainly due to the synergistic structural reinforcement and nanoplugging in the mud cake due to the composite material. The zinc oxide with its high aspect ratio penetrates and seals both micro- and nanopore spacings, while the MWCNTs plug the larger size pore throats, ultimately creating a strong and robust bridging network. This interaction between the nanoparticles (ZnO) and nanotubes (MWCNTs) reduces the permeability of the mud cake, minimizes the FLV, and increases the compactness of the mud cake. On the other hand, Bio-Poly exhibits a parabolic response, where FLV initially increases but begins to decrease as the concentration of polymer increases. As the Bio-Poly loading increases, the FLV decreases, which is primarily because of the hydrate polymeric film, which keeps a stable dispersion of nanoparticle material in the mud system. The large quantity of polar functional groups associated with Bio-Poly interact with ZnO through hydrogen bonding, thereby reinforcing interfacial adhesion. Moreover, these interactions assist in the formation of a hybrid sealing mechanism, which eventually reduces FLV. However, the concentration of Bio-Poly does not have a significant impact on the FLV reduction. The oversaturation of Bio-Poly causes polymer chain entanglement and coagulation, which reduce the uniform dispersion of both entities. This ultimately deteriorates the sealing capability of the polymeric material and then influences the FLV, which remains almost unchanged.

Main Interaction Plot showing the influence of particle concentrations; (a) FLV from the mud, (b) shale swelling.

Figure b shows the impact of both particles on shale swelling. The nanocomposite material Zn-COM demonstrated a sharp decline in shale swelling as the concentration in WBM increases. This clearly shows that the composite material demonstrated a strong shale inhibition at higher concentrations. The two primary mechanisms that are utterly responsible for this sharp decline are first, ZnO nanoparticles plugging the microcracks and interlayered spacing present within the clay minerals. This phenomenon reduces water ingress and restricts shale hydration. Second, the nanotubes of MWCNTs form a strong physical network that limits the access of free water from the drilling mud into the reactive clay. On the other hand, Bio-Poly exhibited a nonmonotonic effect in shale swelling. This behavior is characterized by an initial increase in shale swelling followed by a subsequent reduction in shale swelling as the concentration of particles increases. At lower concentrations, the Bio-Poly partially adsorbed onto the negative clay surface, which ultimately leaves a significant number of reactive sites exposed to free water. However, at higher concentrations (≥2 wt %), the inhibition of shale becomes prominent. The higher concentration of the polymeric material forms a continuous, highly coordinated polymer film. Moreover, this factor increases the osmotic diffusion of the fluid, which reduces water transportation into the interlayered spacing of the clay mineral.

2D Contour Plots

The contour map in Figure a shows the dependence of FLV on the concentrations of nanocomposite Zn-COM and biopolymer additive Bio-Poly. A minimal FLV of 6.5 mL (red region) was achieved only at a significantly high loading of nanocomposite Zn-COM, while the biopolymer additive provided a secondary influence. The trend indicated in the 2D contour plot emphasizes the dominant role of the nanocomposite in developing a denser and less permeable mud cake. The high aspect ratio of MWCNTs was utterly responsible for developing a strengthening network, while the ZnO plugs the pore opening and reduces pore connectivity. On the other hand, Bio-Poly reduces FLV through dispersion stability. ZnO, because of its amphoteric functional group, undergoes interfacial interactions with Bio-Poly chains. These communications improve nanoparticle dispersion, minimize agglomeration, and enhance strong bonding between the two entities, thereby improving the structural integrity of the mud cake. Additionally, increasing the concentration of Bio-Poly enhances the dispersion stability of the nanocomposite material. However, with a low concentration of nanocomposite material, the Bio-Poly alone as a separate entity is inadequate in effective pore sealing, as evident from (blue region). The majority of pore sealing, microplugging, and strengthening of mud cake is predominantly governed by the Zn-COM.

2D contour map showing the impact of Zn-COM and Bio-Poly on (a) FLV, (b) shale swelling.

Figure b shows the influence of both the particles on shale swelling. The maximum shale swelling (5.8%) occurs when the concentration of the nanocomposite was on the lower side. As the loading of Zn-COM increases in WBM, the shale swelling drastically reduces as evident from the yellow to red region. The nanocomposite material predominantly suppresses the osmotic diffusion and minimizes the water ingress into the shale sample. The ZnO presence in the nanocomposite material improves the shale stability through hydroxyl and adsorption interaction. Hydrogen bonds are established between the – OH functional group in the ZnO and oxygen-bearing functional groups present in the clay minerals. This interaction between the two entities causes an adsorption layer to develop over the clay surface, which prevents the movement of free water molecules into the basal spacing of the clay mineral. On the contrary, MWCNTs act as a hydrophobic material, which acts as a physical barrier in front of the water molecules. The tube-like structure blocks the pore-throats and microcracks and suppresses the expansion of clay basal spacing. On the other hand, this suppression was not substantial with Bio-Poly concentration. Hence, high swelling percentages were reflected with high Bio-Poly concentration and low Zn-COM loading. The Bio-Poly is relatively larger in dimension; therefore, it undergoes polymer chain entanglement, which impacts uniformity of its dispersion and deteriorates the strength of the protective film. The nonuniform nature of this particle leaves some exposed areas in the shale, which eventually allows the movement of water molecules inside and increases shale swelling percentages. This observation is in good agreement with the fact that the polymeric material improves the dispersion stability; however, in the absence and minimum loading of Zn-COM, the Bio-Poly cannot physically and chemically preclude shale hydration.

3D Surface Plots

The 3-dimensional response surface plot in Figure a shows the influence of the studied particles on FLV from the drilling fluid. The surface of this plot demonstrated a nonlinear topology, which emphasizes that the concentration of an additive alone is insignificant in reducing the FLV; instead, minimum FLV occurs with the interaction of both the entities. At a low concentration, both the nanoparticle and polymer exhibit higher FLV, which is mainly due to the highly permeable mud cake. At lower concentrations, the polymeric material forms insufficient cross-linking chains, while the nanoparticle concentrations remain too sparse to develop an effective pore plugging network. As the nanocomposite concentration increases, the FLV decreases drastically. The decrease in FLV is mainly due to the interaction between the hydroxyl group present in the nanocomposite material and the functional groups of Bio-Poly. The communication between the two entities developed a stable network that uniformly sealed off the pore spacing in the mud cake. Moreover, the presence of MWCNTs in the nanocomposite material provides reinforcement, which improves the integrity of the mud cakes. The surface plot also shows an upturn in FLV as the concentration of Bio-Poly increases and goes beyond 2 wt %. The oversaturation of Bio-Poly is mainly responsible for this behavior. The excess concentration disrupts the network of the nanocomposite material and enhances the free-water phase in the drilling mud. Both of these phenomena produce a mud cake that is substantially weak and is incapable of reducing the FLV. Overall, the balance between the two additives contributes heavily in sealing off the pores and forming a dense, low-permeability mud cake.

Figure b is the 3-dimensional plot for shale swelling that shows the influence of the studied particles on the shale swelling. From the figure, it is evident that as the concentration of nanocomposite and Bio-Poly increases, a considerable decrease in shale swelling was observed. Bio-Poly reduces the free water activity that suppresses the hydration of shale. The combined influence of both particles significantly decreases shale swelling and stabilizes the shale behavior. However, at a low concentration, the nanoparticle is unable to form an effective surface barrier, while the biopolymer forms an insufficient network, which cannot sufficiently prevent the movement of H2O molecules.

Collectively, Figures – illustrate that the nanocomposite material (Zn-COM) is the dominant element that controls FLV and shale stability. A significant decrease in FLV and shale swelling was recorded with increasing Zn-COM concentration, confirming its superior performance over Bio-Poly. These characteristics are mainly due to the synergistic physicochemical and mechanical behavior of the nanocomposite. The Zn-COM is a nanocomposite material with nanoscale dimensions, which enables this particle to easily penetrate deep into the pore spaces and effectively seal the nanopores, microfractures, and interlayered clay spacing. This eventually prevents the accessibility of water molecules and reduces osmotic swelling. Simultaneously, MWCNTs in the nanocomposite act as a reinforcement agent due to their structural morphology. This particle further blocks the pore throats and microcracks, thereby preventing water migration. On the other hand, the comparatively lower performance of Bio-Poly is mainly associated with its surface-film-dominated mechanism. This behavior is relatively weak and decreases Bio-Poly performance in sealing the nanopores efficiently. Although the material can improve dispersion stability, chain entanglement at high loading decreases its efficacy and is unable to suppress the water intake into the shale structure.

ANOVA

The ANOVA interpretation in Table shows a highly significant model for the FLV with F = 27.03, p < 0.05, which clearly indicates that the nanocomposite and polymeric material collectively govern the response variable (FLV). However, the controlling factor is the Zn-COM concentration with F = 115.61, p < 0.05. This clearly confirms that the nanocomposite material was extremely effective in sealing the pore spacing in the filter paper and reducing the filtrate loss from the mud. On the other hand, the Bio-Poly concentration was statistically insignificant with F = 2.75, p > 0.05. This observation clearly suggests that the biopolymer alone as a separate entity was insignificant in controlling the FLV from the mud system. Similarly, the Bio-Poly quadratic parameter was also proven to be statistically insignificant with p = 0.141. This analysis indicates that increasing the concentration of Bio-Poly does not have any influence on the FLV reduction from drilling mud. In general, the Bio-Poly usually acts as a rheology modifier rather than a direct pore sealing constituent. Furthermore, the combined impact of both the entities was statistically significant with p < 0.05. This observation shows that the synergic combination of polymer and nanoparticle creates a stable, dispersed, and minimum agglomeration system, which is highly effective in forming an effective filter cake with lower filtration loss characteristics.

Conclusion

The study in this article elucidates the effect of hydrothermally synthesized zinc oxide/multiwalled CNTs (Zn-COM) nanocomposite and a biopoly­(pure bore) on FLV from the drilling mud and shale swelling. Since FLV and shale swelling directly govern wellbore stability, understanding these interactions is imperative for augmenting drilling performance. The experimental results of the study demonstrated that the concentration of Zn-COM exerted a significant impact on FLV and shale swelling. A considerable reduction in FLV was achieved from 26.0 to 6.5 mL, corresponding to 75% improvement in the performance of WBM as the concentration of nanocomposite increases from 0.00 to 4.154 g. Similarly, shale swelling also drastically decreased from 17. Two % to 5.8%, reflecting a 66.3% stronger inhibition behavior of the WBM as the loading of nanocomposite increases. The high aspect ratio of MWCNTs was utterly responsible for developing a strengthening network, while the ZnO plugs the pore opening and reduces pore connectivity. On the other hand, Bio-Poly reduces FLV through dispersion stability. However, the concentration of Bio-Poly does not have a significant impact on the FLV reduction. The oversaturation of Bio-Poly causes polymer chain entanglement and coagulation, which reduces the uniform dispersion of both entities. In addition, in shale, ZnO nanoparticles plug the microcracks and interlayered spacing present within the clay minerals. This phenomenon reduces the water ingress and restricts shale hydration, while the nanotubes of MWCNTs form a strong physical network that limits the access of free water from drilling mud into the reactive clay. Furthermore, this suppression was not substantial with Bio-Poly concentration, and high swelling percentages were reflected with high Bio-Poly concentration and low Zn-COM loading. Overall, the combined experimental results and statistical measurements clearly show that the Zn-COM surpasses Bio-Poly in minimizing FLV and shale swelling and is also effectively maintaining the wellbore integrity.
