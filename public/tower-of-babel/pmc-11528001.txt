Evaluation of herbicidal potential of Siderophores produced by Amycolatopsis lurida strain 407
Authors: Mahsa Eigharlou, Sara Javidpoor, Ali Mohammadi, Fatemeh Khelghatibana, Yousef Nami, Leila Ma’mani, Akram Sadeghi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe urgent need for sustainable agriculture has intensified the search for environmentally friendly alternatives to chemical herbicides. This study investigates the herbicidal potential of siderophores produced by Amycolatopsis lurida strain 407, focusing on its effects on the growth of ryegrass and redroot weeds. Strain 407 exhibited two distinct colony morphologies—red and white—when cultured under varying environmental conditions. The cell-free culture filtrate (CFCF) from both colony types significantly inhibited the growth of ryegrass and redroot. The concentration of siderophore produced in the iron-deficient medium was measured to be 613.4 ppm for 407 red and 388.5 ppm for 407 white, which indicates significant iron chelating activity. This study also showed a direct relationship between the presence of siderophore in plant culture medium and reduced growth. Also, analysis of fractions of the aqueous phase resulting from column chromatography revealed that all fractions from the 407 red reduced ryegrass shoot length by up to 45% and root length by 83–86%, while redroot seedling length decreased by up to 36%. Fractions from 407 white completely inhibited germination or reduced ryegrass root length by up to 94% and redroot seedling length by 52%. Fractions F4 W to F7 W and F2 R to F8 R, which showed iron chelating activity were most effective in reducing plant growth, suggesting that there are metabolites, alone or in company with siderophores, synergistically do herbicidal activity. Introduction

Herbicide resistance in weeds mirrors the growing challenge of antibiotic resistance in pathogens, both arising from organisms’ adaptations to chemical treatments. One promising avenue is the exploration of naturally occurring compounds produced by soil-dwelling fungi and bacteria. The phylum Actinobacteria is one of the most various groups of bacteria but is often recognized by the genus Streptomyces which is the source of more than 70% of all secondary metabolites, especially antibiotics34. As researchers seek new antibiotics to combat resistant microorganisms, they are increasingly turning to less-studied antibiotic-producing bacteria such as Amycolatopsis.21. This same strategy can be applied to identify Amycolatopsis metabolites with herbicidal properties, offering a potential solution to the problem of herbicide-resistant weeds.

For years, researchers have been reporting the production of herbicidal metabolites by bacteria, but the most of these materials have never made it to the market. For example, Actinonin produced by Actinomycetes inhibit deformylase, leading to stunting, bleaching, and necrosis in a wide range of weed species. Besides, Bestatin produced by Actinomycetes is an inhibitor of plant aminopeptidases and could be a candidate to produce bioherbicide. Gostatin targets Asp transaminase and results in a time-dependent plant suicide. Toyocamycin inhibits auxin-responsive gene expression and blocks the auxin-enhanced degradation of the auxin and finally weed disorders. Inhibition of deoxyxylulose-5-phosphate reductase by Fosmidomycin produced by Streptomyces also causes bleaching of green tissues due to a reduction in carotenoid content10.

At the present time two bioherbicides Bialaphos® (released in 2016) and Opportune® (released in 2013), produced based on secondary methabolites of Streptomyces are commercially available32. Opportune®, which contains Thaxtomin A, inhibits cellulose biosynthesis in the meristem of sensitive plant species12. Also, Streptomyces bottropensis Gö-Dra 17, which yields considerable amounts of Thaxtomin A, has recently been introduced by Marrone Bio Innovations company as a promising source for bioherbicide production28.

Undoubtedly, there are many factors for the success or failure of a product made on the basis of bacterial metabolites in the market. In addition to the advantages that natural compounds have over the chemical herbicides, one of the factors that has led to the growing market of microbial herbicides is the lack of resistance of weeds to them so far12.

The rising development of resistance to a chemical herbicide, even those with a completely different and new mode of action, has become a major problem. The speed of discovering a new herbicide in itself is much lower than the speed of developing resistance to it. In addition to these, attention to economic issues, cost-effective production and efficiency of a new herbicide compared to existing types, as well as the need to pay care to its safety, have caused weeds to always be ahead of researchers14.

Fortunately, resistance to biological herbicides has not been reported for any weed so far11. There are three main reasons for this issue. First, the use of biological herbicides is not as old as chemical herbicides. Second, the expansion of the use of chemical and biological herbicides is not the same. To evaluate the impact of the first two reasons, we need time to see if resistance to them is reported over time and with the increase in the use of biological herbicides. The third reason is that the development of genes related to the production of secondary metabolites with herbicidal properties occurs in bacteria during millions of years of evolution. This means that these metabolites are produced by bacteria for important reasons that are related to their survival, so their production requires special genetic complexities that have been victorious in competition with the power of genetic evolution of weeds and have been successfully maintained. Therefore, resistance to the product of these genes, if it occurs, will not be very rapid.

Lolium temulentum L. (ryegrass) is a worldwide invasive annual weed known as one of the “world’s worst weeds” that grows with wheat and due to the production of toxic seeds that are difficult to separate from the wheat causes problems for human health42. Moreover, Amaranthus acanthochiton L., commonly known as hairy amaranth or redroot, has been documented to exhibit resistance to multiple herbicides and is considered a problematic weed in many agricultural systems40.

This research aimed to investigate the herbicidal effects (inhibition of germination and seedling growth) of Amycolatopsis strain 407 isolated from the tomato rhizosphere soil2 on ryegrass a monocotyledonous and redroot a dicotyledonous weed. These two weeds were chosen as representatives of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous species due to their significant damage and global distribution. Since this bacterium showed two different appearances (red and white colonies) in different environmental conditions, both forms were considered. The herbicidal effect of fractions collected from an aqueous extract of white and red colonies liquid culture was investigated in the next step. The results showed a significant difference in the herbicidal properties of white and red culture fractions and their relationship with the siderophore compounds.

Materials and methods

Cultivation, and maintenance of strain 407

Amycolatopsis lurida strain 407 was isolated from tomato rhizosphere soil in our previous study2. The strain 407 was registered in NCBI GenBank under accession number MG995004 and deposited in the Agricultural Biotechnology Research Institute of Iran Culture Collection (ABRIICC) under accession number 20,806.

To prepare liquid culture, 1 mL of a fresh bacterial culture (optical density 1 at 600 nm) was transferred to a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL ISP2 medium (International Streptomyces Project media 2) including 10 g/L malt extract, 4 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L glucose, adjusted to pH 7.2 and incubated in a rotary shaker incubator at 150 rpm at 28 °C.

After 72 h, the culture was centrifuged at 5000× g for 10 min and the supernatant was used as a cell-free culture filtrate (CFCF) for further tests.

Effect of light and temperature on the growth and phenotypic characterization of strain 407

An aliquot of 0.1 mL of a fresh bacterial culture was spread on each plate containing ISP2 (solidified with 18 g/L agar). The plates were divided into two groups; a group of plates was completely covered by aluminum foil so that no light could reach them. This group and the second group, which was not covered, were divided into two parts. One part was kept at 37 °C and the other group was kept at 28 °C. After 7 days, the color of aerial, substrate mycelium and spore chain and soluble colored pigment production were observed and recorded.

Siderophore extraction and measurement

In our previous study2, siderophore production by strain 407 was evaluated on the Chrome Azurol agar (CAS) medium1 and reported. In this research, the CAS assay liquid method was used to check the presence of siderophore in the aqueous phase fractions of 407 red and 407 white. Although CAS assay on agar or in liquid media is a good qualitative method but is inefficient for evaluating siderophore amounts. To measure the amount of siderophore produced by bacteria, the method of22 was used. Actinomycete strains were grown in Erlenmeyer flasks (250 mL) containing 50 mL iron-deficient medium (8 g/L glucose, 1 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 1 g/L K2HPO4, 0.5 g/L KH2PO4, and 1 g/L CaCO3) and incubated at 28 °C and 150 rpm in a rotary shaker incubator. After 2 days, the culture was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min and supernatant was separated. The pH of the supernatant was adjusted to 3 by adding HCl. Total siderophore was extracted by adding an equal volume of ethyl acetate. The water phase was dried by lyophilizer and the resulting powder containing siderophore was purified with methanol and methanol was removed by evaporation. The partially purified siderophore was added to a 2% FeCl3 solution. Then the amount of dissolved Fe3 + in FeCl3 (2%) solution as a control and sample was measured with inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OCE).

The seeds used in this research

The freshly harvested seeds of weeds, including ryegrass (Lolium temulentum L.), and redroot (Amaranthus acanthochiton L.) were used in this study. The weed seeds were kindly provided by the Department of Weed Research, Iranian Research Institute of Plant Protection (IRIPP), Tehran, Iran. First, the germination ability of the seeds was checked before further investigations. More than 80% of the seeds in the control group germinated in all three following experiments (data not shown).

Effect of cell-free culture filtrate (CFCF) on seedling growth

Pots (250 g) were filled with washed sand and eight seeds were planted in each pot. Thirty-five mL of CFCF was used to completely (from the top to the bottom of the pot) wet the sands. Tap water was used as control. After 48 h, the pots were watered with 35 mL of tap water. Irrigation was done every 48 h. Four replicates were considered for each strain, weed, or control, and each replicate consisted of one pot. The pots were kept for 21 days in a greenhouse at a temperature of 23 ± 2°C with day length of 16 h and light intensity in the range of 600–1000 µmol m-2 s−1 (in addition to sunlight, light-emitting diodes (LEDs) were used). At the end of the experiment, the seedlings were completely removed from the pot and washed with tap water. Vegetative traits including root and shoot length, and fresh and dry weight of root and shoot were measured and recorded. Because the seedlings of the redroot were very small, it was not possible to separate the roots and shoots in some experiments, so the length and weight of the whole seedlings were measured and presented.

Fractionation of the aqueous phase of CFCF by Sephadex G-100 column chromatography

The concentrated aqueous phase (500 mL) was poured into a 130 × 10 cm glass chromatography column and filled with 900 g Sephadex G-100 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Then, a plug of glass wool was placed on the column top. The column was pre-wetted with 2 L of distilled water8. Fractions of 35 mL each were collected and assayed for herbicide activity.

Effect of fractions of CFCF aqueous phase on weeds (ryegrass and redroot) germination and growth

This experiment was performed under sterile conditions. One mL of each fraction was added to 44 mL of sterile distilled water and poured into a 250 mL tissue culture glass jar. The pH was adjusted to 5.8 with NaOH and plant agar (final concentration 9 g/L) was added to solidify the medium. The control consisted of 45 mL of sterile distilled water and plant agar. Four seeds were cultured in each jar. Four replications were considered for each bacterial strain, weed, or control, and each replicate consisted of one jar. The jars were placed in a phytotron at a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. At first, the jars were kept for 3 days in the dark and then 5 days in light (16 light/8 dark photoperiods, with a light intensity of 30 µmol m−2 s−1). After 8 days, the germination of the seeds and the length of the root and shoot (ryegrass) or seedlings (redroot) were measured and compared with the control.

Effect of siderophore produced by strains 407 red and 407 white on weeds (ryegrass and redroot) germination and growth

The cultivation conditions of the seeds were according to the previous part. Seeds of ryegrass and redroot were cultured on plant agar containing 1 mL of a 60 mg/L siderophore solution extracted from the CFCF of strains 407 red and 407 white, both grown in iron-deficient media. The concentration of siderophores in these samples, determined by ICP-OES analysis, was 613.4 mg/L for 407 red and 388.5 mg/L for 407 white. Thus, 97 µL and 154 µL of siderophore extract from 407 red and 407 white, respectively, were used to prepare the 60 mg/L siderophore solutions. Sterile distilled water was used as a control. Four seeds were cultured individually in tissue culture glass jars and incubated for 8 days at 25 ± 2 °C.

Prediction of the siderophore gene clusters

antiSMASH webserver (Version 6.0.1) was used to investigate siderophore biosynthetic genes. This webserver has been for discovery of secondary metabolites in complete genome clusters (Blin et al. 4). The default parameters were used for the antiSMASH analysis with relaxed detection strictness. The complete genome sequence of A. lurida DSM 43,134 was retrieved from the GenBank (www.ncbi.nlm.nih. gov) database under accession numbers NZ-FNTA01000004.1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) by Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, USA) and SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL USA) packages. All data shown are the average values of three (in vitro experiments) or four (in vivo experiments) biological replicates ± SE. The significance of differences between treatments and control (separately for each weed) was evaluated using Duncan’s test at the level of P < 0.05. Greenhouse experiments were performed in a completely randomized design (CRD).

Results

Appearance characteristics of strain 407 on ISP2 medium

The first colony of strain 407 that was isolated on the ISP2 medium was white. Over time, two types of colonies appeared depending on the environmental conditions (Fig. 1). The production of colored mycelia and red pigmentation around strain 407 colonies was assessed under different light conditions (light and dark) and temperatures (28 °C and 37 °C). Strain 407 exhibited colored mycelia and red pigments with increasing temperature to 37 °C and at darkness conditions. The colony color characteristic remains unchanged in the dark and at a higher temperature (37 °C) if the conditions are not changed. Also, the white colonies that were formed at a lower temperature, 28 °C and received light did not produce any pigment and remained white if the environmental conditions did not change (Fig. 2). These results suggest that temperature and light exposure influence the pigment production in strain 407.

Production of red color around red and white 407 strain colonies under different light conditions (light or dark) and temperature (28°C or 37°C). The accumulation of red color is indicated by a red arrow or a black circle.

Effect of CFCF of strain 407 on ryegrass and redroot

The cell-free culture filtrate (CFCF) of strain 407 was tested on ryegrass and redroot plants. After 21 days of pre-emerge treatment, ryegrass and redroot treated with CFCF showed stunted growth with reduced shoot and root lengths compared to the control group treated with tap water (Fig. 3). Quantitative analysis showed that ryegrass treated with CFCF had significantly shorter shoot lengths (mean ± SE) of 17.33 ± 0.5 cm compared to the control group at 19.66 ± 0.7 cm (P < 0.05). Similarly, redroot plants showed reduced shoot lengths under CFCF treatment, with averages of 2.5 ± 0.5 cm compared to 4.0 ± 0.3 cm in the control group. Both ryegrass and redroot treated with CFCF exhibited shorter root lengths than the controls, with ryegrass roots measuring 10.83 ± 1.7 cm versus 19.5 ± 1.5 cm in controls and redroot roots measuring 2.0 ± 0.2 cm versus 1.5 ± 0.2 cm (P < 0.05). The fresh weight of ryegrass shoots was considerably lower in the CFCF-treated group, at 550 ± 37 mg, compared to 710 ± 39 mg in controls. Redroot showed similar trends, with fresh shoot weights of 300 ± 46 mg in treated plants compared to 560 ± 27 mg in controls. Due to the small size of redroot seedlings, only ryegrass dry weights were measured, showing a significant reduction in both shoot and root dry weights under CFCF treatment, with shoot dry weights of 52 ± 4.0 mg in treated plants versus 70 ± 5.0 mg in controls, and root dry weights of 67 ± 6.0 mg in treated plants versus 92 ± 7.0 mg in controls (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Fractionation of the aqueous phase of CFCF

The aqueous phase of the liquid culture of both red and white colonies of strain 407 was fractionated using a Sephadex G-100 column (supplemental Fig. S1). Eight fractions were collected from the red colonies (F1 R to F8 R), while seven fractions were obtained from the white colonies (F1 W to F7 W). This fractionation aimed to isolate specific bioactive compounds responsible for the observed biological effects.

Effect of fractions of CFCF aqueous phase on ryegrass and redroot germination and growth

All fractions from the 407 red aqueous phase significantly reduced the shoot length of ryegrass compared to the control (Fig. 5), with the most pronounced effect seen in the fraction F5 R. The shoot length in this fraction was 4.83 ± 0.4 cm compared to 8.8 ± 0.7 cm in the control group (Fig. 6A). Similar trends were observed for root length and all fractions reduced the root length of ryegrass compared to the control (Fig. 6B), with the most pronounced effect seen in fractions F1 R, F5 R and F6 R causing significant reductions in growth, ranging from 1.07 ± 0.6 cm (F5-R) to 0.9 ± 0.2 cm (F1 R and F6 R), compared to 6.5 ± 1.7 cm in the control group (P < 0.05). Also, the seedling length of the redroot was decreased by all fractions, particularly F3 R -F6 R, ranging from 5.6 ± 0.7 cm to 5.8 ± 0.6 cm, compared to 8.8 ± 1.1 cm in control (Figs. 5 and 6C). Fractions F4 W -F7 W collected from 407 white aqueous phase completely prevented the germination of ryegrass and redroot seeds (Fig. 7). Also, fractions F1 W -F3 W decreased the shoot length of ryegrass ranging from 6.0 ± 0.6 cm to 6.5 ± 0.3 cm, compared to 8.1 ± 0.9 cm in control (Fig. 8A) and root length of ryegrass ranging from 2.17 ± 0.2 cm to 0.37 ± 0.1 cm, compared to 6.3 ± 1.2 cm in control (Fig. 8B). Fractions F1 W -F3 W also reduced redroot seedling length ranging from 7.6 ± 0.7 cm to 4.5 ± 0.4 cm, compared to 9.3 ± 0.9 cm in control (Fig. 8C). This negative effect increased significantly from F1 W to F3 W, especially for ryegrass root and redroot seedling length.

Iron-chelating activity of aqueous phase fractions

The iron-chelating activity of fractions from the aqueous phase of 407 red (F1 R to F8 R) 407 and white (F1 W to F7 W) was evaluated using the CAS liquid assay. Fractions F4 W to F7 W and F2 R to F8 R exhibited noticeable color changes to orange, indicating significant iron-chelating activity in these fractions (supplemental Fig. S2).

Discussion

Strains of Amycolatopsis can produce various metabolites, some of these metabolites can be growth promoters7 or plant growth inhibitors16. Our previous results showed that some of Amycolatopsis strains had a negative effect and some positively impacted cucumber germination and growth2. Here, for the first time, the negative impact of A. lurida on the growth and germination of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous weeds is reported. The negative effect of this bacterium on weeds was to prevent the growth of the plant, including reducing the length and fresh and dry weight of the seedlings. The application of cell-free culture filtrate of strain 407 led to a reduction in fresh (22%) and dry (26%) shoot weight of ryegrass as well as a 44% decrease in root length of this plant. Similarly, redroot exhibited a 46% reduction in fresh shoot weight and a 25% decrease in root length. These results indicate that this bacterium has the potential to produce herbicide metabolites. Albino plant production or bleaching activity like that produced by methoxyhygromycin metabolite synthesized by Streptomyces sp. 8E-12 occurs25 or leaf necrosis caused by Streptomyces anulatus strain-3295 was not observed for this bacterium.

Color diversity of vegetative and aerial mycelium and spore chain is common among Actinomycetes, especially Streptomyces. The use of the ISP culture medium series is useful as a key to identifying these bacteria33. Recording the appearance of Actinomycetia colonies after growth on these culture media is usually reported as an indicator for bacterial identification in many articles29,35,38.

To optimize the production of effective substances such as antibiotics and enzymes in liquid fermentation, adjusting cultivation conditions including temperature, light, pH, aeration, and changing the composition of the cultivation medium, including the ratio and type of carbon and nitrogen, is usual13.

However, the study of changes in the appearance of an Actinomycete strain on a solid medium under different temperature and light conditions is less reported. In this study, pigment production and color of mycelia of strain 407 were investigated under the influence of culture conditions, including light and temperature.

No pigment was produced around the colonies at 28 °C and natural light (incubator with a clear glass window). These colonies did not produce red pigment in the liquid medium either. These colonies were named 407 white. At a higher temperature (37 °C) and complete darkness, the production rate of red pigments around the colonies and a spectrum of red color in the mycelia and bacterial cells were established. These colonies were named 407 red. The color of type strain A. lurida (DSM 43134T) on the ISP2 medium at 28 °C was reported as yellow37.

Although the production of soluble colored pigments of Streptomyces18 and Amycolatopsis19 has been reported in different culture conditions, but this is the first report of the change of pigments and mycelium color of A. lurida in different temperature and light conditions. Many colored metabolites with antibiotic properties are produced by Streptomyces.36 Colored pigments, especially red, produced by bacteria can have interesting properties such as antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral, antiparasitic, anticancer or antioxidant effects30. Roseoflavin is a red pigment with antibiotic properties produced by Streptomyces.26 The production of red pigment by A. lurida has not been reported so far. Isolation and purification of pigments produced by strain 407 and investigating its properties in the future is valuable in the sense that it may lead us to metabolites with novel industrial or medicinal properties.

Bacteria synthesize molecules called siderophores, meaning iron carriers with a high affinity for chelating Fe3+. Siderophores are synthesized inside the cell and then excreted into the microorganism environment to capture existing free iron or sequester it from metallic compounds. Based on the affinity for iron, siderophores competitively take iron, turn it back into the cell, and remove it from other organisms’ reach, creating interspecies competition for this vital metal. The inability to actively absorb the siderophore and the iron attached to it (iron chelate), can cause the organism’s death because of iron deficiency15. Plants, especially dicots, obtain most of their iron requirements by absorbing iron chelates, existing in the soil are produced by microorganisms.9 They actively absorb iron chelate. Active transport of microbial iron chelate depends on reductase enzymes and transporter proteins located on root epidermal cells41. If root cells do not have suitable iron transporters, the plant will suffer from severe iron deficiency, leading to reduced growth and eventually death. However, when there is a beneficial symbiotic relationship between a microorganism and a plant, the iron chelates produced by the microorganism can be absorbed by the plant to meet its needs and promote growth. As the plant grows, it secretes more nutrients into the soil around its roots, which in turn feeds the beneficial microorganisms24. The mutually beneficial relationship between crops and siderophore-producing bacteria in the soil has been well described39. It is possible to hypothesize that the effectiveness of microbial siderophores for crops, as opposed to weeds, is an evolutionary matter. This may be due to the limited opportunity for weeds to evolve to use microbial siderophores, given the expansion of agriculture and ongoing efforts to control weeds.

Siderophore production by strain 407 was reported in our previous study2. Our previous results were based on the production of an orange halo around a colony grown on a solid CAS medium. In this study, siderophore production was evaluated for both white and red colonies in solid and liquid CAS media. Here, a liquid iron deficiency medium was used for the quantitative investigation of siderophore production. In this medium, unlike the ISP2, both 407 red and 407 white did not produce water-soluble red pigment. However, the supernatant of both bacteria had an iron-chelating activity based on the color change of the CAS medium. But, the amount of total siderophore extracted for 407 red (613.4 mg/L) was higher than for 407 white (388.5 mg/L). The production of siderophore albachelin22 by A. lurida and the possibility of siderophore mirubactin production by this species (Rajwani et al.31) have been reported previously. According to the results of AntiSMASH analysis, the genes related to the synthesis of albachelin and mirubactin are located in regions 1 and 4 of the A. lurida genome, respectively. The agreement of the results of this program with the results of the laboratory has been reported in other studies6. Future studies of our research team will focus on the isolation, purification and structural identification of the siderophores produced by this little-known bacterium. Also, as with any new herbicide metabolite, to accurately identify the effect of each siderophore, a purified (HPLC-grade) sample must be prepared and used for study in a dose-dependent experiment.

In addition to their role in providing iron to plants, microbial siderophores are also known to stimulate plant health through the stimulation of the induced systemic resistance (ISR). The physiological response of a plant to a siderophore depends on whether it is perceived as a friend or an enemy after being absorbed and circulated within the plant organs. A strong immune response, akin to that seen during attacks by plant pathogens, can lead to damage in the plant. Conversely, a gentle and superficial response can help the plant become more resilient and alert to potential stressors3. Concerning stimulating the immune system, numerous genes are involved in the plant’s response to siderophores, which suggests that, unlike chemical herbicides, the development of weed resistance to microbial siderophores is not easily achievable.

Being safe is one of the characteristics of bio-based products. The compounds that are used as herbicides should do the least harm to other living organisms and the environment17. Also, during the production of these products, there should be minimal pollution to the environment. The use of dangerous chemical solvents is not only harmful to manufacturers in the industry, but their return to the sewage or their leakage into the environment around the factories is a big problem23. In this study, the effect of the organic phase (extracted with chloroform and dissolved in ethanol) of 407 white and 407 red on the germination and growth of the studied weeds was not significant. The use of three times the concentration of organic metabolites also had no significant effect (data not shown). However, the fractions obtained from the aqueous phase (metabolites dissolved in water) of both bacteria had a significant (P < 0.05) effect on the germination and growth of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous weed seedlings compared to the control. In such a way that all fractions from the 407 red reduced ryegrass shoot length by up to 45% and root length by 83–86%, while redroot seedling length decreased by up to 36%. Fractions from 407 white completely inhibited germination or reduced ryegrass root length by up to 94% and redroot seedling length by 52%.

At the industrial level, water as a safe, cheap, and available solvent for the production and synthesis of various metabolites has always been of interest27.

The iron chelating activity of the fractions obtained from the aqueous phase of both 407 white and 407 red was investigated using the CAS liquid assay. The results showed that the first three fractions of 407 white and the first fraction of 407 red did not have iron chelating activity. On the other hand, these fractions reduced the growth of studied weeds. These results show that although there are metabolites other than siderophore in each of the fractions, in the presence of which weed growth is reduced, probably the siderophore present in fractions F4 W-F7 W and F2 R-F8 R also have herbicidal properties. To find out the effect of siderophore obtained from both 407 white and 407 red another experiment was carried out in sterile conditions, and as was done to check the fractions previously. The results showed that although both siderophores have an inhibitory effect on the growth of weeds including a reduction of the length of ryegrass and redroot by 13 and 38%, respectively, this effect is not as intense as the fractions obtained from the aqueous phase.

In the iron-deficient medium, complex food ingredients and organic materials such as nitrogen sources are not available to the bacteria, and due to the absence of iron from the culture medium, the most metabolite the bacteria try to produce is siderophore20. Therefore, unlike the rich environment of ISP2, where bacteria can produce all kinds of secondary metabolites with herbicidal properties in addition to siderophore, in the environment of iron deficiency, such metabolites do not exist or are in small amounts. Based on our literature review, this is the first report on the herbicidal properties of A. lurida. Further studies are being conducted to identify the chemical structure of the siderophores produced by this bacterium and the effective metabolites in the aqueous phase fractions of 407 white and 407 red.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the significant herbicidal potential of A. lurida strain 407, isolated from tomato rhizosphere soil, against ryegrass and redroot weeds. The strain’s ability to produce distinct phenotypes, probably each with different bioactive compounds, underscores the versatility and ecological adaptability of A. lurida. The observed herbicidal activity, especially from the white phenotype, suggests the presence of potent secondary metabolites, likely synergistically act with siderophore and effectively inhibit weed growth. The predicted siderophore gene clusters, albachelin and mirubactin, may be key contributors to these herbicidal effects. The findings provide valuable insights into the potential application of A. lurida in bioherbicide development, presenting a promising alternative to traditional chemical herbicides. Given the rising issue of herbicide resistance, the use of bioherbicide derived from soil bacteria like Amycolatopsis offers a sustainable approach to weed management. Future research should focus on identifying and characterizing the specific compounds responsible for these effects. Optimizing the conditions and culture environment for cost-effective production of bacterial culture and extracting effective metabolites with herbicidal properties from the aqueous phase of culture is an important step in the extraction of herbicide from this bacterium, which should be addressed. Also, evaluating the effectiveness of the resulting herbicide in the field and comparing it with common chemical herbicides is the next step in the development of a bacterial-based biological herbicide should be done.
