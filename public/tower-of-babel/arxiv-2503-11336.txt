Rule-Guided Feedback: Enhancing Reasoning by Enforcing Rule Adherence in Large Language Models
Aïssatou Diallo, Antonis Bikakis, Luke Dickens, Anthony Hunter, Rob Miller

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce Rule-Guided Feedback (RGF), a framework designed to enhance Large Language Model (LLM) performance through structured rule adherence and strategic information seeking. RGF implements a teacher-student paradigm where rule-following is forced through established guidelines. Our framework employs a Teacher model that rigorously evaluates each student output against task-specific rules, providing constructive guidance rather than direct answers when detecting deviations. This iterative feedback loop serves two crucial purposes: maintaining solutions within defined constraints and encouraging proactive information seeking to resolve uncertainties. We evaluate RGF on diverse tasks including Checkmate-in-One puzzles, Sonnet Writing, Penguins-In-a-Table classification, GSM8k, and StrategyQA. Our findings suggest that structured feedback mechanisms can significantly enhance LLMs’ performance across various domains.

Large Language Models (LLMs)
Brown (2020)
;
OpenAI et al. ()
have gained remarkable performance across various tasks due to their potential reasoning ability.
As the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) expand, they are increasingly utilized in real-world settings characterized by uncertainty and ambiguity. In particular, recent work

In this context, rule-following is beneficial as it provides a set of guidelines to enhance rational decision-making and judgments. By following these rules, the agent gets established methods for reasoning and at the same time they have guidance to recognize and avoid reasoning errors.

2
Approach

Problem Formulation

Our framework consists of two agents: a Performer tasked with solving a reasoning problem, and a Teacher who evaluates and guides the Performer’s attempts. Each task is defined by a set of natural language rules, as exemplified in
Appendix

E
. The interaction proceeds in turns, where the Performer proposes a solution and the Teacher evaluates it against the predefined rules, providing specific feedback on rule violations. The Performer may request clarification about the feedback, but the Teacher must avoid revealing the solution directly. This exchange continues until either the Performer reaches the correct solution or the maximum number of allowed turns is exhausted. Success is achieved only when the Performer independently arrives at the correct solution while adhering to all rules.

We set the temperature value at 0, the maximum number of steps allowed is 5 and the threshold determining the window for the Performer to ask clarifying questions is set to 0.7, that is to say that the Performer can ask question within the first and third turn only. Baselines

We compare Rule-Guided Feedback (RGF) with the following prompting methods:
(i)
Standard Prompting (SP)
: the LLM is asked to directly yield a response without any specific guiding input-output exemplars or any additional guiding instructions; (ii)
Zero-shot CoT prompting (CoT)

Kojima et al. (2022)
, inspired by
Wei et al. (2022)
, this zero-shot prompting approach simply appends “Let’s think step by
step” to the input query, encouraging the model to generate "thoughts" before addressing the problem or task at hand; (iii)
Expert Prompting (EP)

Xu et al. (2023)
: functions by first designing an expert identity tailored to the input query and then integrating it into the input to generate a response; (iv)
Tree of Thoughts (ToT)

Yao et al. (2024)
: enables deliberate decisions by considering multiple reasoning paths and self-evaluating choices to determine the next action; (v)
Meta Prompting (MP)

Suzgun and Kalai (2024)
, employs a scaffolding technique where the LLM suggest the ideal prompt, revises it before executing said revised prompt, to help improve outcomes.

Tasks

GSM8k

Cobbe et al. (2021)

and
StrategyQA

Evaluation Metrics

We employ four key metrics to evaluate performance:
(1)
Accuracy
(%): For Penguin-In-A-Table and Checkmate-In-One, we use exact match comparison with ground truth. For Sonnet Writing, we verify functional correctness by checking adherence to all sonnet rules (14 lines, rhyme scheme, meter);
(2)
Mean Conversation Length in Accurate Cases (MCA)
: Average number of exchanges needed to reach a correct solution, indicating efficiency of successful dialogues;
(3)
Mean Conversation Length (MCL)
: Average number of total exchanges across all attempts, reflecting overall interaction complexity;
(4)
Dialogue Density (DD)
: Ratio of cases requiring multiple exchanges to total samples, measuring how often clarification or correction is needed (
DD=N_{m}/N
, where
N_{m}
is the number of multi-turn conversations).
These metrics assess both solution quality and the interaction dynamics of our framework. While accuracy measures correctness, MCA and MCL provide insights into efficiency, and DD quantifies the framework’s tendency to engage in iterative refinement when needed.

4
Ablation Studies

We conduct ablation studies on three key components of our Rule-Guided Feedback framework to understand their contributions.
Figure

5
shows the impact of main components of RGF.

Impact of Clarifying Questions

Removing the Performer’s ability to ask clarifying questions leads to a 15% decrease in accuracy across tasks on average. This decline is most notable in Checkmate-in-One, where understanding piece positions and movement rules is crucial. The ability to seek clarification helps resolve ambiguities early, preventing cascading errors.

Impact of Expert Validation

Without access to explicit rule violation lists, the Teacher’s evaluation accuracy drops by 8.5%, with a 12% increase in undetected rule violations (falsely claiming the Performer is right or wrong). The expert validation step is crucial for catching edge cases, ensuring consistent feedback quality, and providing additional verification for complex tasks.
Figure

4
shows the impact of expert verification.

Impact of Feedback Iteration Limit

We analyze performance across different iteration limits (1, 3, 5, and unlimited). Most successful solutions are reached within 3 iterations, with 5 iterations providing optimal performance (13% improvement over single iteration). Additional iterations beyond 5 yield minimal benefits (<1%) while significantly increasing computational cost. The optimal iteration limit varies by task - Checkmate-in-One shows 22% improvement between 1-3 iterations, while Penguins-in-a-Table typically succeeds within 2-3 iterations.

3
shows the impact of the number of iterations on accuracy.

5
Discussion

While the RGF framework enhances (LLMs) adherence to predefined rules and improves information-seeking efficiency, it is not without limitations. One challenge occurs from the Performer’s tendency to persist with previous answers despite receiving corrective feedback from the Teacher. This issue sometimes happens even if both the Performer and the Teacher have the memory of prior interactions, leading to repeated errors and diminished overall effectiveness of the feedback loop.

Another limitation comes from the Teacher’s ability to provide accurate and constructive feedback. The efficacy of the framework relies on the Teacher’s capacity to thoroughly understand the problem, accurately identify the Performer’s mistakes, and articulate these errors clearly as violated rules. If the Teacher fails to comprehend the issues of the Performer’s responses or misinterprets rule violations, the feedback becomes less effective, hindering the Performer’s "reasoning".

To address these challenges, we have incorporated an expert verification step following each Performer’s response. This addition serves a dual purpose: it verifies the correctness of the Performer’s answer and ensures that any feedback provided highlights specific rule violations in a clear and actionable manner. By requiring the Teacher to rephrase feedback in terms of explicit rule violations, we enhance the clarity and utility of the guidance offered to the Performer. This structured approach not only mitigates the Performer’s inclination to disregard feedback but also improves the Teacher’s role in providing meaningful corrections.

Future work could use a reward system to better align the Performer with the Teacher.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces Rule-Guided Feedback, a framework that improves LLMs’ adherence to rules and enhances their information-seeking capabilities. On three datasets RGF increases accuracy rate by 26.5% on average over direct prompting and 7.9 % over the best baseline which demonstrates substantial improvements in task completion rates. Future work will focus on refining the feedback integration process and enhancing the Teacher’s analytical capabilities to further strengthen the framework’s robustness. Ultimately, RGF represents a step forward in providing reliable and intelligent LLM interactions within rule-based environments.

Limitations

As stated previously, one limitation of this work consists in the fact that the Performer’s occasionally fails to effectively integrate the Teacher’s feedback, leading to repeated adherence to incorrect solutions despite available conversational history. Additionally, the framework’s reliance on the Teacher’s ability to accurately comprehend and identify the Performer’s mistakes depends on the domain-knowledge and reasoning ability of the Teacher model. While the inclusion of expert verification helps mitigate these issues by ensuring the correctness of evaluations, there remains a need for enhancing the Performer’s responsiveness to feedback and further improving the Teacher’s analytical capabilities to consistently deliver precise and actionable guidance. These points are left for future work.

Ethical Statement

Throughout the paper, we utilize anthropomorphic language, such as "reason" and "understand" to enhance the clarity of our discourse on LLMs. This is not intended to imply that LLMs possess human-like cognitive abilities.

The generative models are based on pre-trained language models, which may
generate offensive content if prompted with inappropriate inputs. Additionally, concerns may arise regarding potential bias amplification and misuse as per any prompting method for LLMs. This necessitates careful bias detection and mitigation. Additionally, the rules could exploited to "trick" the model, generating harmful or misleading outputs and feedback. Safeguards like fact-checking and verification become crucial, especially in sensitive domains. Responsible deployment and use are fundamental to ensure LLMs benefits are harnessed ethically and its risks are minimized.

Appendix B
Expert Verification

Expert verification step is used as tool due to the inherent limitation of the LLMs with some challenging tasks. It is a call to a function to programmatically evaluate the performer answer. It is used in SonnetWriting specifically as GPT-4 couldn’t reliably evaluate these points:

•

Check if the sonnet adheres to a given rhyming scheme

•

Check if each line has 10-11 syllables, more precisely, there’s some pronunciation of each line with 10-11 syllables.

The python libraries used for this evaluation are:

•

pronounce
- a well-known pronunciation dict based on from CMU’s pronouncing dictionary

•

syllables
- a Python library for syllable counting

•

pyphen
- a Python wrapper for the hyphenation library

Once the Performer outputs an answer, this is passed to the expert verification function and evaluated. Then. this evaluation and the Performer’s answer are passed to the Teacher.

Appendix C
Related Work

Teacher-student framework

The teacher-student framework has traditionally focused on knowledge transfer from larger teacher models to smaller student models
Gou et al. (2021)
. Early approaches relied on supervised fine-tuning methods
Magister et al. (2022)
;
Shridhar et al. (2022)
, as demonstrated by Rajani et al. (2019) who generated explanations using GPT
Radford (2018)
to enhance BERT models’ commonsense reasoning. Similarly,
Hendrycks et al. (2021)
improved mathematical reasoning through fine-tuning on step-by-step solutions, while
Ho et al. (2022)
leveraged GPT-3
Brown (2020)
to generate Chain-of-Thought reasoning steps for training smaller models. However, these approaches face significant limitations: the fine-tuning process is resource-intensive, collecting task-specific data is challenging, and the resulting models often lack cross-task generalization capabilities.
Recent work has shifted towards prompting-based teacher-student frameworks to address these limitations
Saha et al. (2023)
;
Yu et al. (2023)
. Most relevant to our work is Teaching via Principle Discovery (TPD)
Wang et al. (2024)
, which uses a teacher LLM to generate problem-solving instructions and corrective principles based on student errors. While both TPD and RGF leverage teacher-student interactions, they differ fundamentally in their approach and objectives. TPD focuses on discovering principles from errors to improve instruction and training, with the teacher disengaging after principle generation. In contrast, RGF maintains active teacher involvement during inference, using predefined rules to guide real-time reasoning and information seeking. Furthermore, while TPD aims to transfer knowledge between models through principle-based learning, RGF emphasizes structured reasoning through continuous rule adherence and feedback. These distinctions highlight RGF’s novel contribution to the field, particularly in scenarios requiring strict rule compliance and dynamic information gathering.

Appendix E
Rules

E.1
Checkmate In One

1. All provided chess moves must be written in Standard Algebraic Notation (SAN).

2. The sequence of moves must accurately reflect a valid game progression up to the current position.

3. The next move must result in an immediate checkmate of the opponent’s king.

4. The proposed move must comply with the official rules of chess, including correct piece movement and capturing mechanisms.

5. Only one move should be provided as the solution, representing the checkmating move.

6. The move must be legal in the given position and must not leave the player’s own king in check.

7. If the checkmate involves special moves (such as castling, en passant, or pawn promotion), the notation must correctly represent these moves.

8. Disambiguation in SAN must be used correctly when multiple identical pieces can move to the same square (e.g., Nbd7).

9. The solution must demonstrate that no additional moves are required after the proposed move to achieve checkmate.

10. The performer is allowed to ask one clarifying question, but it cannot be a repetition of the original question.

E.2
Penguins In A Table

1. Each table must have a header row with clearly labeled columns.

2. Each subsequent row represents one penguin with corresponding attribute values.

3. Attribute names in questions must exactly match those in the table headers.

4. Questions must be answerable solely using the information provided in the table(s).

5. Numerical values (age, height, weight) must be interpreted and compared accurately.

6. Units of measurement (e.g., cm, kg) must be correctly used in both questions and answers.

7. When comparing attributes, ensure that the relationships (e.g., taller than) are correctly determined based on the data.

8. If multiple tables are present, clearly reference the correct table when answering questions about specific penguins.

9. All answers must be clear, concise, and directly derived from the table data without introducing external information.

10. The performer is allowed to ask one clarifying question, but it cannot be a repetition of the original question.

E.3
Sonnet Writing

1. The sonnet must consist of exactly 14 lines.

2. Strictly follow the specified rhyme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG scheme throughout all 14 lines.

3. Include all the given words within the sonnet.

4. Adhere to the traditional structure associated with the chosen rhyme scheme (Shakespearean).

5. Ensure that each line contributes coherently to the overall theme or message of the sonnet.

6. Use the given words naturally, without forcing them into the lines.

7. Ensure proper punctuation and capitalization are used to enhance readability and flow.

8. The number of allowed syllables per line is 10 or 11.

9. The performer is allowed to ask one clarifying question, but it cannot be a repetition of the original question.

E.4
GSM8k

1. **Identify the Core Mathematical Operations:** Accurately determine the sequence of arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, percentages) required to solve the problem.
2. **Break Down the Problem into Steps:** Decompose the problem into smaller, more manageable sub-problems.
3. **Extract Relevant Information:** Identify and isolate the crucial numerical values and units from the problem statement.
4. **Perform Calculations Accurately:** Execute the identified arithmetic operations correctly, paying close attention to order of operations (e.g., parentheses, exponents, multiplication/division, addition/subtraction).
5. **Handle Units Correctly:** Ensure proper unit conversions and consistency throughout the calculations.
6. **Address Multi-Step Problems:** For problems involving multiple steps, maintain intermediate results and clearly label each step in the solution.
7. **Check for Reasonable Answers:** After obtaining the final answer, perform a quick sanity check to ensure it aligns with expectations and common sense.
8. **Consider Special Cases:** Be mindful of potential edge cases or unusual scenarios described in the problem (e.g., discounts, taxes, varying speeds).
9. **Document the Solution Clearly:** Present the solution in a structured and organized manner, showing all intermediate steps and calculations.
10. **Utilize Visual Aids (Optional):** If helpful, create simple diagrams or tables to visualize the problem and aid in understanding.

E.5
StrategyQA

1.Identify the Main Question: The response must accurately identify the core sub-question within the given StrategyQA question.
2. Recognize Implicit Strategies: The response must explicitly state the underlying reasoning steps required to answer the main question, even if these steps are not explicitly stated in the original question.
3. Leverage Background Knowledge: The response must demonstrate the use of general knowledge and world facts to understand the context and relationships between concepts within the question.
4. Focus on Relevant Evidence: The response must clearly indicate which evidence paragraphs are most relevant to the sub-question and the identified reasoning steps.
5. Reasoning Chain Construction: The response must present a clear and logical chain of reasoning that connects the sub-question, the identified evidence, and the final answer.
6. Answer Justification: The response must include a justification for the answer using the collected "facts" (justification) provided by the question writers.
7. Identify Ambiguous Questions: If the question exhibits ambiguity, the response must acknowledge this ambiguity and explain how the chosen interpretation is supported by the evidence.
8. Counterfactual Reasoning: The response may include a brief discussion of alternative scenarios and how they would impact the answer based on the provided evidence.
9. Identify Out-of-Scope Information: The response must demonstrate an understanding of which information in the evidence paragraphs is irrelevant to the main question or the reasoning steps.
10. Confidence Calibration: The response must include a brief statement indicating the confidence level in the provided answer and justification, based on the strength and clarity of the supporting evidence.
