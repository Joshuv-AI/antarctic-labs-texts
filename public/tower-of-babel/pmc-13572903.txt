Predictors of Primary Arteriovenous Fistula Failure in Patients With End‐Stage Renal Disease: A Single‐Centre Retrospective Cohort
Authors: Duc Chi Tieu, Hung Pham, Dat Vinh Lieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundArteriovenous fistula (AVF) is the preferred vascular access for haemodialysis, yet primary failure remains common and its determinants in Southeast Asian populations are incompletely characterised.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 151 AVFs created in 125 adults with end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) at a tertiary hospital (2020–2022). The primary outcome was AVF failure, defined as an access that never became usable for haemodialysis (primary non‐function, early occlusion, or non‐maturation).ResultsPrimary failure occurred in 34/151 fistulas (22.5%). Failure rates differed by configuration: 9.3% for brachiocephalic, 26.0% for radiocephalic (wrist), and 50.0% for brachiobasilic fistulas (p = 0.011). Lower BMI was associated with failure (21.3 ± 2.9 vs. 22.9 ± 3.7 kg/m2; p = 0.010). In multivariable analysis, higher BMI was independently protective (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.84 per kg/m2, 95% CI 0.72–0.97; p = 0.016), and a proximal (brachial‐based) anastomosis showed a protective trend (aOR 0.42, 95% CI 0.17–1.05; p = 0.063). Preoperative brachial‐artery and cephalic‐vein diameters, age, sex, diabetes, and heart failure were not associated with failure. The model was well calibrated (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.66) with modest discrimination (AUC 0.69, 95% CI 0.59–0.79).ConclusionsLow BMI was associated with a higher risk of primary AVF failure. The observed differences among AVF configurations should be interpreted with caution because the choice of access type was likely influenced by patient and vascular characteristics. Preoperative vessel diameter, however, was not significantly associated with failure in this cohort.Keywords: arteriovenous fistula, body mass index, end‐stage renal disease, haemodialysis, primary failure, vascular access, Vietnam

Background

Chronic kidney disease is a major global health burden, and a substantial proportion of affected patients progress to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring kidney‐replacement therapy [1, 2, 3]. Haemodialysis remains the most widely used modality, and reliable long‐term vascular access is central to its delivery. Since the description of the wrist arteriovenous fistula (AVF) by Brescia and Cimino in 1966 [4], the autologous AVF has become the recommended first‐choice access because of its lower rates of infection, thrombosis, and long‐term morbidity compared with grafts and catheters [5, 6, 7]. A persistent limitation of the AVF is primary failure—the fistula that never becomes usable for dialysis because of early thrombosis or inadequate maturation. Reported primary‐failure rates range widely, from roughly 20% to 60% [8, 9, 10], and the mechanisms are multifactorial, encompassing patient factors, vessel biology, and surgical configuration [9, 11]. Preoperative duplex ultrasound is widely used to select suitable vessels, and society guidance references minimum artery and vein diameters [5, 12, 13]; however, the incremental predictive value of these thresholds, particularly outside high‐income settings, remains debated [14]. This cohort has previously been described by our group in aggregate, reporting overall procedural outcomes and complication rates [15]. Here we address a distinct question—which patient and anatomical factors predict primary failure—using multivariable modelling. We hypothesised that, in a Vietnamese ESRD population with a high burden of hypertension and diabetes, anastomosis configuration and nutritional status would carry more predictive weight than isolated vessel‐diameter cut‐offs.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of consecutive ESRD patients who underwent vascular‐access surgery at the Department of Thoracic and Vascular Surgery, Gia Dinh People's Hospital, Ho Chi Minh City, between January 2020 and December 2022. The manuscript follows the STROBE recommendations for observational studies.

Participants

Adults diagnosed with ESRD who required haemodialysis and underwent autologous AVF creation were eligible. Patients with incomplete records or who were lost to follow‐up were excluded. Screening and exclusion are summarised in Figure 1. The unit of analysis was the individual fistula; 125 patients contributed 151 fistulas (22 patients had access created at two sites and two at three sites). Access site followed a distal‐first policy: a non‐dominant forearm radiocephalic fistula was attempted first whenever preoperative duplex identified suitable wrist vessels, and brachiocephalic or brachiobasilic configurations were reserved for patients with previously failed radiocephalic access or unsuitable wrist vessels, without a rigid diameter cut‐off. All exclusions occurred before the primary outcome could be ascertained; patients who died or were lost to follow‐up before outcome assessment were therefore not analysed.

Patient selection and exclusion flow diagram.

Variables and Outcome

Preoperative variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart failure), dialysis status at operation, laterality, anastomosis configuration (radiocephalic [wrist], brachiocephalic, and brachiobasilic), and duplex‐derived vessel diameters. The primary outcome was AVF failure, defined as an access that never achieved use for haemodialysis—comprising primary non‐function, occlusion within the early postoperative period, and failure to mature on duplex assessment at 6–8 weeks without subsequent salvage. Maturity was judged on institutional duplex maturation criteria, namely the traditional Rule of 6s (flow greater than 600 mL/min, diameter greater than 6 mm, and depth less than 6 mm) together with clinical readiness for cannulation; the 2019 KDOQI update treats these as prior criteria to be weighed alongside clinical cannulability rather than as absolute thresholds [5]. Primary non‐function was defined as thrombosis or absence of a functional thrill within 72 h of creation; early occlusion as thrombosis occurring before the access had ever been successfully cannulated (up to 3 months); and non‐maturation as a patent fistula that failed to meet these duplex criteria at 6–8 weeks and could not be cannulated. Four fistulas complicated by steal syndrome or distal ischaemia that required ligation or abandonment, so that the access never became usable, were also counted as primary failures; in every case the endpoint required that the fistula never achieved use for haemodialysis. For pre‐dialysis patients, duplex‐confirmed maturation with clinical readiness for cannulation was regarded as successful maturation even if dialysis had not yet commenced. Each failure was further classified by mechanism as immediate thrombosis, early thrombosis, steal syndrome or distal ischaemia, or non‐maturation.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarised as mean ± standard deviation and compared with the Welch t‐test; categorical variables are compared with the Fisher exact test. Predictors of failure were examined by univariable logistic regression, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A multivariable model included age, sex, BMI, diabetes, and a proximal‐vs.‐distal configuration term; hypertension was not entered because of quasi‐complete separation (all failures occurred in hypertensive patients). Model discrimination was quantified by the AUC and calibration by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 (packages foreign and stats::glm). A persistent patient identifier was not present in the final de‐identified analytic dataset, so a mixed‐effects or generalised‐estimating‐equations model could not be fitted on the full fistula‐level data. Repeated accesses were reconstructed from the source records, allowing the earliest fistula for each patient to be identified for a sensitivity analysis restricted to one fistula per patient (n = 125). Given only 34 failure events, the primary model was limited to five prespecified covariates to reduce the risk of overfitting; configuration entered as a proximal‐vs.‐distal term because a three‐level model was unstable with only eight brachiobasilic fistulas, and configuration‐specific effects and an analysis excluding brachiobasilic fistulas are reported separately. Vessel diameter was examined overall, as the configuration‐specific inflow artery (radial for radiocephalic and brachial for brachial‐based fistulas) and target vein (distal cephalic, proximal cephalic, or basilic), within the radiocephalic subgroup, and as an addition to the multivariable model. BMI was analysed continuously and in Asian‐specific categories (below 18.5, 18.5–22.9, and 23 kg/m2 or above), with a likelihood‐ratio test for departure from linearity.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research of Gia Dinh People's Hospital (approval No. 31/NDGĐ‐HĐĐĐ). All procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and to ICH‐GCP.

Results

Of 160 patients who underwent access surgery during the study period, 35 were excluded (lost to follow‐up, 20; died, six; protocol violation or incomplete data, nine), leaving 125 patients contributing 151 fistulas for analysis (Figure 1). Primary failure occurred in 34 of 151 fistulas (22.5%). Failure rates differed significantly by configuration (Figure 2; p = 0.011), lowest for brachiocephalic (9.3%), intermediate for radiocephalic (26.0%), and highest for brachiobasilic (50.0%) accesses. Patients whose fistula failed had a significantly lower BMI than those with a functioning access (21.3 ± 2.9 vs. 22.9 ± 3.7 kg/m2; p = 0.010). Age, sex, eGFR, comorbidities, laterality, and dialysis status did not differ between groups, and neither preoperative brachial‐artery nor cephalic‐vein diameter was associated with failure. In multivariable analysis, higher BMI remained independently protective (aOR 0.84 per kg/m2, 95% CI 0.72–0.97; p = 0.016) and a proximal configuration showed a protective trend (aOR 0.42, 95% CI 0.17–1.05; p = 0.063); the model was well calibrated (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.66) with modest discrimination (AUC 0.69, 95% CI 0.59–0.79) (Table 3, Figure 3).

Relative to radiocephalic fistulas, brachiocephalic access carried lower crude odds of failure (OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.90; p = 0.031), whereas brachiobasilic access did not differ significantly (OR 2.85, 95% CI 0.66–12.21; p = 0.159), the latter resting on eight fistulas and four events. When the eight brachiobasilic fistulas were excluded (n = 143), the lower failure odds for brachiocephalic vs. radiocephalic configuration reached significance (adjusted OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.08–0.76; p = 0.015) and the BMI estimate was unchanged (adjusted OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98; p = 0.025) (Table 5).

In the analysis restricted to the earliest fistula per patient (n = 125; 28 failures, 22.4%), effect estimates lay in the same direction, although the BMI association was less precise and its confidence interval crossed the null (adjusted OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.75–1.01; p = 0.072); proximal configuration was significant (adjusted OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.06–0.83; p = 0.026) (Table 5). Analysed in Asian‐specific categories, failure decreased across BMI groups: six of 14 (42.9%) below 18.5 kg/m2, 21 of 82 (25.6%) at 18.5–22.9 kg/m2, and seven of 55 (12.7%) at 23 kg/m2 or above (Table 5). A likelihood‐ratio test showed no departure from linearity (p = 0.78), and no U‐shaped pattern was apparent across the observed range, although few patients lay at the extremes. Using the configuration‐appropriate inflow artery and target vein, neither the inflow artery (OR 0.80 per mm, 95% CI 0.55–1.18; p = 0.26) nor the target vein (OR 0.89 per mm, 95% CI 0.59–1.33; p = 0.56) predicted failure. The same held within the radiocephalic subgroup, where the radial artery (OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.58–2.27; p = 0.68) and the distal cephalic vein (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.42–1.25; p = 0.25) were non‐predictive. When both diameters were added to the multivariable model, BMI remained significant while neither diameter contributed (Table 5).

Discussion

In this single‐centre cohort of 151 fistulas, primary AVF failure affected roughly one in five accesses (22.5%), consistent with the 20%–60% range reported internationally [8, 9, 10]. Two findings stand out. First, lower BMI was the single independent clinical predictor of failure, remaining significant after adjustment (aOR 0.84 per kg/m2). Second, outcome varied substantially by anastomosis configuration, with the lowest failure rate for brachiocephalic fistulas and the highest for brachiobasilic accesses, and a protective trend for proximal over distal (wrist) anastomoses in the adjusted model.

The association between lower BMI and failure aligns with reports linking undernutrition, smaller‐calibre or less‐distensible vessels, and impaired outward remodelling to poor maturation; both Venkat Ramanan et al. and Bashar et al. found that maturation failure is driven by a constellation of factors rather than any single measurement, and reported inconsistent effects of individual vessel diameters [8, 9, 11, 16]. In a population where malnutrition‐inflammation is common in advanced ESRD, BMI may act as an accessible surrogate for vascular and systemic reserve, suggesting that patients with low BMI warrant closer surveillance. Our configuration findings echo the long‐standing observation that more proximal, larger‐inflow anastomoses mature more reliably than distal wrist fistulas, albeit at the cost of a higher theoretical risk of distal ischaemia and reduced downstream options; this accords with the conclusion of Sultan et al. that patients already on dialysis may be better served by a proximal AVF [17, 18]. The high failure rate of brachiobasilic accesses in our series should be interpreted cautiously given the small number (n = 8) and their selective use after distal options had been exhausted. The overall gradient is consistent with individualised configuration choice guided by the patient's vascular map, although these observational data cannot establish a preferred access sequence [5, 9]. Preoperative brachial‐artery and cephalic‐vein diameters were also not associated with failure, and no diameter threshold discriminated outcome. This does not negate the value of duplex mapping—which remains essential for identifying usable vessels [5, 12]—but it tempers reliance on single‐vessel diameter cut‐offs as stand‐alone predictors, consistent with the heterogeneous performance of such thresholds across cohorts [12, 14]. Vessel quality, distensibility and inflow dynamics, which diameter alone does not capture, may be more decisive. In resource‐limited settings, where duplex expertise varies, over‐weighting a borderline diameter risks denying patients an access that might have matured.

Under a distal‐first policy, brachiocephalic and brachiobasilic fistulas were created selectively in patients with failed or unsuitable wrist vessels, so comparisons across configurations are confounded by indication. The 50% failure of brachiobasilic access rests on four events in eight fistulas and is better read as a marker of exhausted distal options and adverse vessel anatomy than as an intrinsic property of the configuration. Consistent with this, brachiocephalic access, the elective proximal option, was associated with the lowest failure rate, and its lower failure odds relative to radiocephalic access reached significance once the brachiobasilic fistulas were removed. We therefore describe configuration as associated with, rather than causally determining, failure, and we retained a proximal‐vs.‐distal term in the primary model because a three‐level model was unstable with only eight brachiobasilic fistulas.

Our 22.5% primary‐failure rate sits slightly above the 19.3% (95% CI 14.1%–24.5%) pooled estimate from a recent Malaysian meta‐analysis of 12 studies (about 1426 patients), which likewise reported better maturation for upper‐arm than forearm fistulas and identified diabetes and smaller vessel calibre among predictors of poorer outcomes [19]. Because our composite endpoint included early thrombosis, perioperative practice is also relevant: a Malaysian double‐blind randomised trial in radiocephalic fistulas reported that intraoperative systemic heparin approximately halved early thrombosis (9.1% vs. 25.0% at 1 week) and improved 6‐week maturation (86.4% vs. 68.2%) [20], and clopidogrel likewise reduced early thrombosis without improving maturation in a large trial [21]. Antithrombotic and other perioperative measures were not recorded in our retrospective dataset and remain unmeasured determinants of the primary‐failure endpoint.

Limitations

This is a single‐centre, retrospective study, which limits generalisability and precludes control of unmeasured confounders such as surgeon experience, intraoperative flow, and cannulation practice. In particular, medications (e.g., antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents)—which may influence AVF patency—were not recorded and could not be analysed, and comorbidities beyond hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure were captured only as sparse free‐text entries (e.g., atrial fibrillation, n = 6; coronary artery disease; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) that were too few for separate analysis. The fistula—rather than the patient—was the unit of analysis, and 24 patients contributed more than one access; because a persistent patient identifier was unavailable in the analytic dataset, within‐patient clustering could not be formally modelled, but the earliest‐fistula sensitivity analysis (n = 125) gave estimates in the same direction, which mitigates this concern. Patients who died or were lost before the outcome could be ascertained were excluded, which may bias the observed failure rate if early adverse outcomes were preferentially lost to follow‐up. The number of failures (n = 34) constrained the number of covariates and the precision of subgroup estimates, and model discrimination was modest (AUC 0.69). Finally, the primary outcome captures usability for dialysis rather than long‐term patency; late functional outcomes were not the focus here.

Conclusions

Among Vietnamese ESRD patients undergoing autologous AVF creation, low BMI, and a distal (wrist) anastomosis—not isolated preoperative vessel‐diameter thresholds—were the principal correlates of primary failure. These accessible factors could inform access planning and post‐operative surveillance, particularly in resource‐limited settings. Prospective, multicentre studies with patient‐level modelling and long‐term patency endpoints are needed to confirm and extend these findings.
