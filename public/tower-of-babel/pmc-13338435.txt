Experiences of physical activity during pregnancy: a phenomenological study among Sri Lankan primigravid women
Authors: Sepalage Nilanthi Chathurika, Rathnayake Mudiyanselage Chathura Rathnayake, Deepika Indumathie Nanayakkara, Sampath Udaya BandaraThennakoon, Abey Rathnayake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Physical activity (PA) during pregnancy improves maternal and fetal outcomes, yet cultural factors may influence behavior. Despite its advantages, the majority of pregnant women do not follow PA recommendations during pregnancy. Therefore, this study aimed to explore experiences of PA among Sri Lankan pregnant women. A qualitative phenomenological study was conducted with primigravid women attending antenatal clinics at a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted until data saturation was reached (n = 19). Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis framework. Five themes emerged (1) Modification of PA with some restrictions (2) Avoidance of PA other than self-care activities (3) Increasing sedentary period (4) Focusing on psychological relaxation and (5) Time allocation for leisure activities with number of sub themes. Most participants reported a decline in physical activity compared to the pre-pregnancy period, influenced by fear of fetal harm, physical discomfort, family advice, and culturally embedded beliefs emphasizing rest. Physical activity patterns among Sri Lankan primigravid women shift toward restriction and increased sedentary behavior during pregnancy, largely driven by sociocultural influences and safety perceptions. Culturally sensitive antenatal counseling is essential to promote safe and informed engagement in physical activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49950-w.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) during pregnancy has been widely recognized as beneficial for maternal and fetal health1. Regular PA contributes to improved cardiovascular fitness, reduced gestational diabetes risk, and better psychological well being among pregnant women2. Conversely, pregnancy is a period often associated with reduced PA3 and more sedentary lifestyles4. Furthermore, women tend to decline PA level as the pregnancy progresses in later trimesters due to physical discomfort, fatigue, and sociocultural barriers5.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that pregnant women without contraindications engage in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic PA per week6. Similarly, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) advises 20–30 min of moderate exercise on most days of the week7. Despite these recommendations, evidence consistently shows a decline in PA levels as pregnancy progresses, particularly during the second and third trimesters8–10.

In South Asian settings, including Sri Lanka, pregnancy is culturally viewed as a period requiring rest and protection. Family elders and traditional beliefs often influence maternal behavior, sometimes discouraging physical exertion due to perceived fetal risks. In Sri Lanka, cultural beliefs and family norms often influence women’s activity patterns during pregnancy11. Traditional expectations may discourage moderate activity due to perceived risks to the fetus12. However, national antenatal guidelines encourage safe, moderate-intensity exercise similarly to international recommendation13.

Though numerical findings broadly available on PA, the nature of PA changes during pregnancy with its influencing factors yet to be explored through qualitative research. Also, it is important to distinguish & understand the aspect of PA changes (how these changes happen when pregnant) with pregnant women’s views prior to implementing any healthcare intervention.

Although quantitative studies have documented declining physical activity levels during pregnancy, there is limited qualitative evidence exploring how these changes occur and how women interpret and experience them. Understanding these lived experiences is essential to design culturally appropriate antenatal interventions, education and to promote maternal health. Therefore, this study aimed to explore changes in physical activity during pregnancy among Sri Lankan primigravid women and to identify the social and cultural factors influencing these changes.

Materials and methods

A phenomenological qualitative design was used to explore primigravid women’s experiences of PA during pregnancy. The study was conducted at Teaching hospital, Peradeniya, a one of the tertiary hospitals in Sri Lanka. Participants were primigravid women attending antenatal clinics between January to May 2021. Participants were recruited from all three trimesters of pregnancy to capture variation in experiences across gestational stages. Women in the first, second, and third trimesters attending the antenatal clinic during the study period were purposively selected. Gestational age was confirmed using clinic records.

After providing detailed explanations about the study, participants’ written informed consent was obtained prior to the data collection. Socio-demographic data was obtained using pre-designed self-administered data sheets. Qualitative data was collected through in-depth interviews using a pre-designed semi-structured interview guide to explore their experiences on ante-natal PA changes during the pregnancy period.

Interview guide consists of a set of questions mainly focused on how mother’s PA status changes in the pregnancy and leading factors to those PA changes.The semi-structured interview guide was developed based on literature review and expert opinion. Prior to data collection, the guide was pre-tested among two primigravid pregnant women attending a different antenatal clinic. Minor modifications were made to improve clarity, wording, and sequencing of questions. Data from the pre-test were not included in the final analysis. The interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Interviews were conducted with 19 Sinhala-speaking primigravid women using a one-to-one interview approach until data saturation was achieved. Each interview session lasted approximately 45–60 min. All interview sessions were audio-tape recorded. The interviews were conducted by the principal investigator, a female lecturer in nursing with formal training in qualitative research methods and prior experience in maternal health research. The interviewer had no prior relationship with participants.

Field notes were maintained during and immediately after each interview to document contextual details, participants’ emotional expressions, pauses, tone variations, and significant non-verbal behaviors. Non-verbal communications such as facial expressions, body posture, hesitations, emotional reactions, and changes in voice tone were documented in field notes by the principal investigator. These observations were later integrated during data interpretation to enrich understanding of participants’ lived experiences.

Interviews were conducted in a private room within the clinic to ensure confidentiality and minimize interruptions. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. After the 17th interview, no new codes or themes emerged. Two additional interviews were conducted to confirm data saturation, where no new themes emerged, resulting in a total sample of 19 participants.

Interviews were conducted in Sinhala, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English by the principal investigator. An independent bilingual academic reviewed the translations to ensure accuracy, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis14 framework: (1) familiarization with data, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report. Coding was conducted manually without software. Two researchers independently coded transcripts and compared codes to identify similarities and differences. Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was achieved.

To ensure trustworthiness, Lincoln and Guba’s criteria were applied15. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants, independent coding by two researchers, and discussion until consensus was reached. Member checking was performed by clarifying responses during interviews. Dependability was ensured by maintaining an audit trail of decisions made during coding and theme development. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive discussions between researchers to minimize bias. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of study setting, participants, and data collection procedures.The principal investigator maintained reflexive notes throughout the research process to acknowledge potential biases arising from her professional background in nursing. Bracketing was practiced to minimize preconceptions during interviews and analysis.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya (Protocol No.2019/EC/16). Permission was obtained from the Director, Teaching Hospital, Peradeniya (THP/Planning/Approval research/02/2019) and from consultant obstetrician, Ante-natal clinic, Teaching Hospital, Peradeniya before initiating data collection of the study. This study was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards of the responsible institution on human subjects as well as with the Helsinki Declaration.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Table 2 summarizes the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants. The majority of women were Sinhala (84%) and Buddhist (74%), reflecting the dominant ethnic and religious composition of the region. Most participants were aged between 20 and 25 years (58%), while the remainder were between 26 and 30 years. Educational attainment varied, with over one-third having completed Advanced Level education and nearly half possessing diploma or degree qualifications. Approximately two-thirds of the women were unemployed and reported a monthly household income between Rs. 40,000–100,000. Slightly more than half lived in extended family settings, and nearly half resided in semi-urban areas. Based on the qualitative data, predominantly all women in the study sample expressed that their physical activities differ with the pregnancy. In diverse ways their normal PA patterns have been transformed, influenced by wide range of physical, psychological, emotional and spiritual factors. None of them stated about continuing usual day-to-day PA as in the pre-pregnancy period or trying to achieve pregnancy PA recommendation.

Qualitative findings

Five major themes were identified through thematic analysis: (1) Modification of PA with restrictions, (2) Avoidance of PA other than self-care, (3) Increasing sedentary period, (4) Focusing on psychological relaxation, and (5) Time allocation for leisure activities.

Theme 1: modification of PA with some restrictions

Most participants described altering their usual physical activity (PA) patterns after becoming pregnant. Rather than completely discontinuing activity, women reported continuing light household tasks while intentionally avoiding heavy or strenuous work. These modifications were largely influenced by perceived fetal vulnerability, family advice, and cultural expectations.

Sub theme 1: engagement in light household tasks

Many participants (14) stated that they continued routine domestic responsibilities such as cooking, sweeping, and light cleaning, but with reduced intensity. Activities perceived as physically demanding were either minimized or performed with assistance.

One participant explained “I do most of the normal day-to-day activities as previous like cooking, sweeping home and garden, light cleaning activities specially in the kitchen, but prevent doing any of heavy activities, even washing heavy clothes, lifting weighted things from the floor like”. (Woman No.4, first trimester).

Similarly, another woman verbalized “I do my usual work, daily living activities, but I avoid heavy and vigorous activities because it may harm the baby.”(Woman No.10, second trimester).

Sub theme 2: avoidance of heavy and strenuous activities

Several women reported completely stopping activities perceived as physically demanding, such as lifting heavy objects, gardening, or manual labor. These decisions were often reinforced by advice from family members, particularly mothers and husbands.

An one woman expressed “I do usual works in home and office, but difficult to do some bending/stretching activities, when cleaning floor, removing spider nets on roof, then others supports, also prevent from washing huge, weighted bed sheets, but washing light clothes, hmm, prevent from lifting rice bag or other heavy things, because they are not good, may harmful to baby” (Woman No.5, first trimester).

Another participant also described “After I became pregnant, I stopped heavy work like gardening and cutting firewood. My mother and husband told me it is not good for the baby.” (Woman No.12, second trimester).

Avoidance of heavy tasks reflected a strong perception that physical strain could negatively affect fetal development.

Theme 2: avoidance of PA other than self-care activities

Some women described markedly reducing their daily physical activities beyond essential self-care. This reduction was influenced by physical discomfort, fear, fatigue, and perceived bodily vulnerability.

Sub theme 1: difficulties while PA with increased body weight and low back pain

Participants commonly reported back pain, increased body weight, and physical discomfort as barriers to maintaining routine activities.

One of them verbalized “once I became pregnant, most of daily activities were reduced, and now do not engage in any physical activity other than my personal care, because, difficult to do those activities with increased body weight. Actually, ms do not cooking, even washing clothes, sweeping in home. Mother and my sister are there to support me. Also, if I do some activity like cleaning dishes, plates in standing feel back pain. Therefore, trying to be prevent activities always” (Woman No.15, third trimester).

Another participant also stated “I do not do any other activities, I do only my personal care. Standing for long makes my leg and back pain worse.” (Woman No.08, second trimester).

Sub theme 2: Fear of fetal harm

Fear emerged as a powerful determinant of inactivity. Despite receiving advice from healthcare providers, some women avoided activities due to anxiety about miscarriage or fetal harm.

A one woman described “Although the midwife told me to do some activities, I am afraid it may harm my baby, so I avoid most work. Family members doing instead” (Woman No. 03, first trimester).

Also, another mother told “Since this is my first experience, I am very careful when doing any activity. Although in the ante-natal clinic it was said to do some, fell much fear, thinking it causes harm to my baby, so, do not performing even daily activities” (Woman No. 09, second trimester).

Sub theme 3: lack of physical fitness & lethargy

Many participants described lethargy and reduced energy leading them to limit even minor household tasks.

A participant mother explained “Usually I do my works by own, but when I pregnant, it is totally changed, because, it is new and much different experience, specially feel like no physical fitness as previous to do work, although having no any pain yet, therefore, not performing household or other activities. Sometimes, feeling lazy to start tasks” (Woman No.10, second trimester).

Sub theme 4: exhaustion minimizes physical activity

Some of the study participants verbalized that they prevent even daily routines because of tiredness.

One mother expressed, “Even sweeping makes me feel very tired, so I prefer to rest.” (Woman No 03, first trimester).

“It is feeling much exhausted if I do small activity like sweeping home even. So, do not try to do physical activities, always try to be relax and comfortable without any work. Also, difficult to managing with body weight” (Woman No.8, second trimester).

Overall, avoidance of activities beyond self-care reflected a combination of physical discomfort, emotional concerns, and uncertainty regarding safe levels of activity during pregnancy.

Theme 3: increasing sedentary period

A noticeable shift toward sedentary behavior was reported by most participants. Women described spending more time sitting, resting, or sleeping compared to their pre-pregnancy routines.It is well detected that pregnancy induced physical discomfort urged more sedentary PA rather than moderate/light intensity PA as a relief technique. So, some extra PA which not consuming energy were added to their daily routine in order to get adequate break during the work.

Sub theme 1: increased sitting

Most of the expectant mothers described their experiences related to adhering more sedentary behaviors specially sitting. One of them stated that, “most of the times, I need to be seated comfortably, feels more difficulties when standing or walking” (Woman No.11, second trimester).

Another participant also stated “I try to do most of my work while sitting because standing or walking feels difficult.” (Woman No.14, Third trimester).

Sub theme 2: increased bed rest

“After finishing household work, I rest on the bed most of the time like 06 h in day time ” (Woman No 06, first trimester).

mother said, “When I do some activities feeling more tired, so, I used to rest on bed most of the times after doing my household works, specially, after finishing morning workload” (Woman No.5, first trimester).

Sub theme 3: daytime sleeping

Is also recognized as a sub theme, as one of expectant mother mentioned that “After, finishing my household routines, I sleep about 2 h after lunch.it good for me to reduce tiredness” (Woman No.9, second trimester).

Participants described this transition as gradual, often attributing it to pregnancy-related discomfort and the belief that rest promotes fetal well-being.

Theme 4: focusing on psychological relaxation

Basically, pregnant mothers believe that peaceful mindset positively impacts on fetal psychological development other than reducing tension. So, they preferred to allocate some hours to calm their mind as well as to receive blessings for growing fetus by adding selected reassuring spiritual activities.

Subtheme 1: engagement in religious activities

Considerable number of participants (7) also well focused on more religious activities when pregnant.

“I go to the temple in the evening and listen to pirith (Buddhist chanting) before sleeping to keep my mind calm.” (Woman No. 11, second trimester).

and it can be evidenced that, one of them expressed “I often used to go to the temple at evening, also before sleeping everyday hearing pirith, it will make my mind calm and relax” (Woman No.12, second trimester).

sub theme 2: mental relaxation

Few numbers of women (3) and mentioned that they allocate some time duration for make their minds free and reduce stress due to pregnancy related worries. This theme can be evidenced by, one of mother voiced “Mother always advice to do meditation, so I do sometimes, she said it is good for mental development of my baby. I also like to engage this activity, because it offers lot of benefits for mind to free, decrease stress, also helps to sleep better” (Woman No.16, third trimester).

“My mother advised me to meditate because it helps the baby’s mental development and reduces stress.” (Woman No 10 ,second trimester).

These practices reflected a culturally embedded belief that emotional stability and spiritual engagement positively influence fetal development.

Theme 5: time allocation for leisure activities

A smaller group of participants reported allocating additional time for leisure and relaxation activities compared to their pre-pregnancy period.Leisure activities were primarily viewed as strategies for emotional comfort and distraction from pregnancy-related discomfort.

“I read books about pregnancy and listen to music to feel relaxed.” (Woman No. 18, third trimester).

One of them said “Often we two going places for leisure to specially walking path or garden, or musical programs, drama. Husband helps more to enjoy ignoring difficulties” (Woman No.10, second trimester).

Overall, participants described a transition from relatively active lifestyles to more restricted and sedentary routines during pregnancy. Physical discomfort, fear of fetal harm, fatigue, and strong family influence were central factors shaping these changes. Cultural beliefs emphasizing rest, protection, and psychological calmness further reinforced reduced engagement in moderate or strenuous physical activity. Although most women continued essential self-care and light domestic tasks, awareness of recommended levels of physical activity during pregnancy was limited.

Discussion

This study identified five interrelated themes reflecting a transition from relatively active pre-pregnancy routines to more restricted and sedentary lifestyles during pregnancy. Women’s decisions to reduce physical activity were strongly influenced by perceived fetal vulnerability, fear of miscarriage, physical discomfort, and sociocultural expectations emphasizing rest and protection. These findings can be interpreted using the Health Belief Model16, where perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and cues to action, particularly from family members-shaped behavior.

Participants in the present study expressed concerns about miscarriage, abdominal pain, or fetal harm. Similar findings have been reported in other Asian contexts, where cultural restrictions and fear of miscarriage reduce activity levels in China and Bangladesh17,18.

Findings revealed a shift from active to sedentary lifestyles, mainly influenced by cultural beliefs and family expectations. Such cultural orientations differ from Western contexts, where prenatal exercise is strongly promoted as part of healthy pregnancy management19,20. In many Asian societies, rest is traditionally associated with fetal protection, and physical effort is perceived as potentially harmful21. These cultural beliefs often dominate and result in continued sedentary practices, even though evidence supports the safety and benefits of moderate-intensity exercise during pregnancy.

As this study found, pregnant women’s chief concern towards fetal well-being and refrain from risk activities were substantial factors for reducing PA, similar results presented through research which was done in Scotland22. Lack of focusing on PA in primary health care setting may be the reason for this situation.

Furthermore, psychological factors such as fear, uncertainty, and lack of confidence also contributed to reduced PA according to this study. Fear-avoidance behaviors, commonly reported among pregnant women with discomfort or low back pain, appeared to influence decisions to limit some tasks23. Participants’ preference for relaxation practices, including religious activities, indicates an emphasis on psychological well-being rather than physical fitness. This aligns with research showing that pregnant women often prioritize emotional and spiritual coping strategies over physical exertion24,25.

However, unlike Western populations, where exercise is encouraged, Sri Lankan women prioritize rest as a sign of care. Even though Sri Lankan ministry of Health introduced PA national guideline including pregnancy PA recommendations13 which aligns with evidence suggesting that Asian women generally possess limited understanding of exercise recommendations during pregnancy23,26. Poor dissemination of health information within primary health care settings may partly explain this gap. Studies conducted in both developing and developed settings have similarly documented inadequate emphasis on PA counselling during routine antenatal visits23,26.

Findings highlight the need for culturally tailored antenatal PA counselling that directly addresses misconceptions about fetal safety. Nurses and midwives should provide culturally sensitive guidance to promote safe exercise routines. This study provides context-specific insights into Sri Lankan primigravida experiences, based on an understudied group through qualitative approach allowing in-depth exploration of cultural and familial influences.

A key strength of this study is its in-depth exploration of culturally embedded perceptions among primigravid women using rigorous qualitative methodology. This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary care hospital, which may limit transferability of findings to other rural or community settings in Sri Lanka. Second, the sample included only primigravid Sinhala-speaking women, and experiences of multiparous women or other linguistic groups may differ. Third, self-reported experiences may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias. Further studies involving multiparous women, different geographic settings, and quantitative assessments are recommended to strengthen evidence and support national policy development.

Conclusion

Sri Lankan primigravid women exhibit substantial reductions in PA during pregnancy, shaped by cultural norms, safety concerns, inadequate knowledge, and strong influence from family members. Despite national guidelines, awareness of appropriate activity levels remains limited. The findings underscore the importance of addressing culturally rooted safety concerns and misconceptions regarding PA during pregnancy. Integrating culturally sensitive counselling into routine antenatal care may help improve awareness and informed decision-making among primigravid women.
