Effectiveness and safety of umbilical cord milking in premature infants: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yanyan Zhang, Ming Tao, Shaojun Wang, Juan Chen, Qiong Hu, Shuju Luo, Zhonglan Tang, Yongfang Mu, Nian Luo, Qing Wang, Mingsheng Wang, Tao Peng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Both UCM and DCC are used to treat preterm infants, but there is no uniform standard for the length of UCM. The aim of this work was to explore the effectiveness and safety of different umbilical cord milking (UCM) lengths versus delayed cord clamping (DCC).Methods:We enrolled premature infants from the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University between September 2019 and October 2020 with random allocation (1:1:1:1) to the UCM 10 cm, UCM 20 cm, UCM 30 cm, and DCC groups. The primary outcome was hemoglobin at birth.Results:Ultimately, 143 participants completed the trial (UCM 10 cm, n = 35; UCM 20 cm, n = 35; UCM 30 cm, n = 38; DCC, n = 35). The hemoglobin levels were significantly lower at birth in the UCM 10 cm group than in the UCM 20 and 30 cm and DCC groups (182.29 ± 22.15 vs 202.83 ± 21.46, 208.82 ± 20.72, and 198.46 ± 24.92, P = .001, .001, and .003, respectively). The systolic blood pressure and diastolic pressures in the UCM 30 cm group were higher than those in the UCM 10 and 20 cm and DCC groups at birth, postnatal day 3 and postnatal day 7 (P < .05). The occurrence rates of anemia were significantly higher in the UCM 10 cm group than in the UCM 20 and 30 cm and DCC groups (42.9% vs 14.3%, 10.5%, and 14.3%, all P < .0083). There were no significant differences in heart rate or complications among the 4 groups.Conclusions:A UCM of 20 or 30 cm is a safe, effective operation for preterm infants and could improve blood pressure and hemoglobin levels and reduce anemia.

1. Introduction

In preterm infants, anemia is likely to occur.[1] Anemia can easily lead to different degrees of hypoxia in various organs of premature infants, thus prolonging the hospital stay, while in later stages, anemia can affect the growth and development of the premature infant and even cause irreversible damage to the nervous system with impacts on puberty and adulthood.[2] Blood transfusion is the most important effective means to treat neonatal anemia. Approximately 90% of ultralow-birth-weight infants need to be infused with red blood cells to correct severe anemia.[1] While blood transfusion therapy can rapidly improve hemoglobin levels, maintain normal blood volume, and improve circulatory perfusion and hypoxia, it may also induce hemolysis, infection, and even serious complications.[1,3,4]

Placental blood transfusion can increase iron stores and hemoglobin, reduce blood transfusion demand, and improve postnatal hemodynamic stability in premature infants, thus improving postnatal adaptability.[5–8] The methods used for placental blood transfusion include delayed cord clamping (DCC) (i.e., slow and passive transfer of blood from the placenta to the newborn via uterine contraction, namely, through the umbilical cord after the umbilical cord pulsation disappears) and umbilical cord milking (UCM) (i.e., active transmission of blood from part of the umbilical cord to the newborn by milking after the fetus is delivered, with a time requirement of 20 seconds).[9–11]

Although DCC improves anemia in preterm infants and even reduces the risk of death or major disability at 2 years,[12] the disappearance of umbilical cord pulsation requires a waiting time of 1 to 3 minutes, and healthcare workers worry that DCC might delay the timing of premature birth resuscitation.[8,11,13–15] Therefore, many hospitals still use immediate cord clamping (ICC).[16,17] To avoid delaying resuscitation of the premature infant, some authors have suggested that UCM can ensure placental blood transfusion without affecting infant resuscitation.[18–20] Recent studies have shown that UCM is beneficial.[21]

While UCM significantly improves the prognosis of premature infants, different authors suggest different methods for UCM, mainly involving large spanning differences in the length of UCM, ranging from 20 to 40 cm.[18,22–24] Since there is no uniform standard for the length of UCM for premature infants, the clinical importance of current guidance is unclear, and there is a lack of research on the UCM length of premature infants.

Therefore, this trial was designed to explore the effectiveness and safety of different UCM lengths versus DCC in preterm infants.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This triple-blind randomized controlled trial enrolled consecutive preterm newborns from the Obstetrics Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University, Guizhou Province, China, delivered between September 2019 and October 2020. The Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University approved the study (# X LLY-2019-011.). Written consent was obtained from the women who were eligible and willing to participate after admission to the obstetrics department of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: preterm infants of a single birth; born between 28+0 and 36+6 weeks of gestation; and guardians who could conduct daily verbal communication and provide informed consent to voluntarily participate. The exclusion criteria were as follows: umbilical cord length < 40 cm, umbilical cord true knot, or umbilical cord too tight around the neck; sail-shaped or rotary-shaped placenta; mother with hematological diseases (e.g., thalassemia, severe anemia, or polycythemia); diseases affecting fetal bilirubin metabolism and blood (e.g., ABO hemolysis, RH hemolysis, placental abruption, gestational diabetes, or intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy); and neonatal malformation or disease impacting bilirubin metabolism (e.g., congenital anal atresia or biliary atresia).

2.2. Randomization and blinding

This trial included 4 parallel groups (1:1:1:1 ratio) with women randomized to UCM 10 cm, UCM 20 cm, UCM 30 cm, and DCC groups. The randomization was prepared by a statistician using a computer-generated randomized table generated using SPSS 18.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and sealed opaque envelopes. Special attention was given to ensuring that the infants and parents, neonatology staff members (both physicians and nurses) in the delivery room or theater who provided the initial care to the infants, and statisticians who analyzed the data were blinded to the intervention. In addition, the umbilical cord ligation methods were not noted in the infant chart, so this information was not available for the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) staff. In addition, the research assistant and data evaluator were unaware of the specific grouping of the participants when collecting the data.

2.3. Intervention

All premature infants were delivered by experienced and trained surgeons or midwives. An ethylene oxide sterilized ruler was used as the standard for measuring cord length, and the following were ensured: readiness for resuscitation before birth, a moderate temperature (24–26°C) and humidity (50%–60%) in the operating/delivery room, a warm-up of the radiation table 30 minutes in advance, the proper placement of the resuscitation items, instruments, and medicines, and notification for the pediatrician to be present.

UCM 10 cm group: Immediately after delivery, the facial, oral, and nasal secretions of the premature infant were cleaned up, and the infant was placed at or below the level of the placenta. The umbilical cord was immediately fixed with one thumb and index finger at 10 cm from the root of the umbilical cord, and the other end was squeezed 3 times toward the premature infant. The squeezing strength was moderate for 2 seconds each time, and the milking speed was approximately 10 cm/s. Then, the umbilical cord was cut.

UCM 20 cm group: The length of umbilical milking was 20 cm, and the remaining protocol was the same as that for the UCM 10 cm group.

UCM 30 cm group: The length of umbilical milking was 30 cm, and the remaining protocol was the same as that for the UCM 10 cm group.

DCC group: Immediately after delivery, the facial, oral, and nasal secretions of the premature infant were cleaned up, and the infant was placed at or below the level of the placenta. The infant was thoroughly dried, and the umbilical cord was cut off after umbilical cord pulsation ceased.

The subsequent clinical management of the infants was left to neonatologists with more than 5 years of clinical experience and was appropriate to clinical signs and symptoms.[25] Transfusion was used for the treatment of anemia. Phototherapy was used for the treatment of icterus.

2.4. Outcomes and measurement

The primary outcome was the initial hemoglobin level at birth. The secondary outcomes were: (body) temperature at birth and 3 and 7 days after birth, heart rate at birth and 3 and 7 days after birth, blood pressure at birth and 3 and 7 days after birth, hemoglobin at 3 and 7 days after birth, hematocrit values at birth and 3 and 7 days after birth, requirement for red blood cell (RBC) transfusions during the hospital stay, percutaneous bilirubin peak, duration of phototherapy, pathologic jaundice, occurrence of anemia, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), IVH, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and sepsis,[25] hospital stay, hospital cost, and mortality rate. From 6 to 13 secondary outcomes were observed during hospitalization. The outcomes were assessed by the neonatologists and collected by the investigators.

At birth and 3 and 7 days after birth, laboratory staff collected a capillary blood sample (30 μL) from the infants using a disposable blood collection needle (28 G/I Type; Tianjin Huahong Technology Co., Ltd. Tianjin, China) and a disposable microblood collection straw (Baoding Yushan Medical Device Manufacturing Co., Ltd. China). The blood sample was transferred into an anticoagulant-treated vial (K2EDTA; Jiangsu Kangjian Medical Products Co. Ltd. Jiangsu, China). The blood samples were stored at room temperature and analyzed on the same day. A hematology analyzer (xs-500i; Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan) was used for hemoglobin and hematocrit analysis, and the diagnostic criterion for anemia was hemoglobin < 145 g/L. Every morning, the percutaneous bilirubin levels were measured and recorded continuously with a percutaneous bilirubin detector (JH20-1B, Nanjing University of Technology Science and Technology Consulting Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China.) on the forehead of the preterm infant. The maximum value was the peak value of transdermal bilirubin.

2.5. Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated as N = 2[(μα+μβ)σ/δ]2, based on the pretest results, according to the main outcome indicators (initial hemoglobin values), α = 0.05 (bilateral), β = 0.2, and the substitution formula N ≈ 35.24. In total, 35 infants were needed in each group, and after allowing for 30% attrition, we decided to allocate 45 or 46 to each treatment group, for a total of 182 premature infants.

All data were analyzed using SPSS 18 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA). Analysis was based on the intention-to-treat. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and nonnormally distributed continuous variables are described as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and repeated ANOVA were used to compare the continuous data with a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, and multiple pairwise comparisons were carried out using the least-square difference method. Comparisons of nonnormally distributed continuous data were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H-test. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher exact probability method. Two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. When pairwise comparisons among the 4 groups were performed with the Chi-square test, the test level was estimated with the following formula: α’=α/N, where N is the number of tests needed.

3. Results

Ultimately, 182 women were enrolled. After written informed consent was obtained and as birth was imminent, 45 women were randomly assigned to UCM 10 cm, 45 to UCM 20 cm, 46 to UCM 30 cm, and 46 to DCC. Of the 182 included newborns (96 boys [53.0%] and 86 girls [47.0%]; mean gestational age, 34.3 ± 2.1 weeks), 168 (92.3%) received the allocated intervention. A total of 143 participants completed the trial (UCM 10 cm, n = 35; UCM 20 cm, n = 35; UCM 30 cm, n = 38; DCC, n = 35) (Fig. 1). There were no significant differences in the maternal characteristics or neonatal baseline data among the 4 groups (P > .05) (Table 1).

4. Discussion

4.1. Main findings

The results showed that the hemoglobin levels were lower at birth in the UCM 10 cm group than in the UCM 20 and 30 cm and DCC groups, and the occurrence rates of anemia among premature infants were higher in the UCM 10 cm group than in the UCM 20 and 30 cm and DCC groups. There were no significant differences in complications among the 4 groups. The results suggest that a UCM of 20 or 30 cm may be a safe, effective operation for preterm infants that could achieve a similar-level effect as DCC.

4.2. Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is that it was a triple-blind controlled trial. Nevertheless, this study has limitations. First, a control group for immediate cord clamping and longer-term Hb level monitoring is lacking. Second, it lacks partial parameters of complete blood cells, such as red blood cell count, white blood cell count, and platelet count. Last, it involved selection of premature infants from only 1 hospital, the sample size was small, the representativeness of the participants was limited, and the research results may have a certain selection bias. Therefore, large-sample, multicenter, high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to explore the standard operating methods of UCM to provide a more evidence-based foundation for UCM in premature infants.

4.3. Interpretation

After birth, the ambient temperature of premature infants is considerably lower than the intrauterine temperature, and the heat dissipation increases. If the infants are not kept warm enough in time, hypothermia can easily occur, which leads to neonatal hard swelling, important organ damage, and even death;[26,27] therefore, it is extremely important to manage the body temperature of premature infants after birth. This study showed that the temperature of premature infants at birth in the DCC group was lower than that in the 3 UCM groups. This finding may be related to the fact that for the infants in the 3 UCM group, the umbilical cord can be broken within 20 seconds and the infant can be immediately transferred to the preheated radiation stage to be kept warm. In contrast, infants in the DCC group need to wait 1 to 3 minutes on the stage before transfer to the preheated radiation stage for warmth. These results are consistent with the findings of Tarnow-Mordi et al[28] This shows that, compared with DCC, the procedures in all 3 UCM groups were beneficial for keeping premature infants warm at birth.

The neonatal heart conduction system is not perfect, the physiological function is not mature, the cardiopulmonary regulation function is poor, the heart rate is prone to be fast and unstable,[29] the fluctuation range is large, and premature infants are more obvious.[30]The results of the animal study by Bhatt et al[31] showed a 50% decrease in pulmonary blood flow and a 40% decrease in heart rate in preterm lambs receiving ICC, which may be related to the cessation of umbilical vein blood flow in the placenta. Katheria et al[32] showed that premature infants in the UCM group had a higher heart rate during the initial 5 minutes after birth compared to the ICC group. All this evidence indicates that placental transfusion is beneficial for improving the heart rate of preterm infants. The results of this study show that the heart rate comparison between the 4 groups of preterm infants was not statistically significant. This is not consistent with the findings of Katheria et al[32] and may be related to the control group setting of ICC in his study.

Preterm infants are prone to hypotension due to insufficient blood volume or myocardial dysfunction. Therefore, to adapt to these changes, preterm infants must have sufficient blood volume at birth to ensure the maintenance of stable blood pressure levels, oxygen supply, and perfusion of tissues and organs.[5] Dipak et al[33] showed that DCC lasting 60 seconds favors elevated blood pressure in preterm infants and thus contributes to improved hemodynamics and organ perfusion in preterm infants. Katheria et al[34] showed that premature blood pressure in the UCM group was higher in the first 15 hours after birth than in the DCC group. This study showed that systolic and diastolic BP were higher in the UCM 30 cm group than in the UCM 10 cm, UCM 20 cm and DCC groups, which is consistent with the findings of Katheria et al[34] This shows that UCM is more conducive for maintaining BP in preterm infants and that UCM 30 cm plays the most obvious role in BP maintenance in this group of cases.

The present study showed that the initial hemoglobin levels of premature infants in the UCM 10 cm group were significantly lower than those in the 3 other groups, and the hemoglobin levels in the UCM 30 cm group were higher on days 3 and 7 than those in the 3 other groups. Moreover, the results showed that the occurrence rate of anemia in premature infants in the UCM 30 cm group was significantly lower than that in the UCM 10 cm group and that the occurrence rate of RBC transfusions in premature infants was significantly lower in the UCM 30 cm group than in the other groups. These results might be related to the longest length of UCM in the UCM 30 cm group, leading to more placental blood transfusion, and the shortest UCM and less placental blood transfusion and iron reserves in the UCM 10 cm group.

Neonatal jaundice is the most common clinical problem during the neonatal period.[35] Premature infants are more prone to neonatal jaundice than term infants, with earlier, more severe, and longer jaundice.[36] Phototherapy is a simple and effective method to reduce unconjugated serum bilirubin, but phototherapy can also have some side effects, such as fever, diarrhea, rash, bronze baby syndrome, and DNA damage.[37,38] UCM and DCC allow preterm infants to receive more RBCs from the placental circulation, which may theoretically increase bilirubin levels and increase the incidence of neonatal jaundice and the duration of phototherapy. According to the present study, the peak value of percutaneous bilirubin, the number of cases of jaundice, and the duration of phototherapy were not affected by different UCM lengths, which is consistent with previous studies.[9,10,19–21]

In recent years, with the continuous development of perinatal and neonatal medicine and pediatrics, the treatment level of premature infants has greatly improved, and an increasing number of premature infants can survive.[39] Nevertheless, due to immature organ function and poor adaptability to the external environment, premature infants are prone to complications such as IVH, RDS, and BPD after birth, and the long-term prognosis of these cases is not optimistic.[40] IVH is an important factor leading to premature death and late severe neurodevelopmental disorders.[41] Previous studies have shown that the occurrence of IVH is related to cerebral oxygenation, cerebral perfusion insufficiency, and hemodynamic instability.[42] UCM increases blood volume, stabilizes cerebral oxygenation, and stabilizes cerebral perfusion in premature infants, which might be why UCM can reduce IVH in premature infants.[43] Toledo et al[44] showed that UCM significantly reduced the incidence of premature IVH compared with ICC. The present study showed that the occurrence rates of IVH, RDS, BPD, ROP, SIRS, NEC, sepsis, and other complications were not significantly different among the 4 groups of premature infants. This is inconsistent with the results from March et al[45] and may be related to the difference between the study groups and the observation time. Of course, the present study might be underpowered to detect differences in the secondary outcomes.

While UCM significantly improves the prognosis of premature infants, there is no uniform standard for the length of UCM for premature infants.[8,15] The clinical importance of current guidelines is unclear, and there is still a lack of research on the UCM length of premature infants. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial for investigating the effect of UCM at different lengths in preterm infants. The present trial provides a reference for the practice standard of UCM. DCC is often used to transfer placental blood to the infant, but it is based on the natural transfer of the blood until cord pulsations stop, and it can delay subsequent interventions.[13] In the present trial, there were no differences between UCM of 20 and 30 cm and DCC, suggesting that an equivalent volume of blood can be transferred but without waiting 2 to 3 minutes.

5. Conclusion

This study showed that a UCM of 20 or 30 cm is a safe, effective operation for preterm infants. It can increase blood pressure hemoglobin levels and reduce the need for RBC blood transfusions and the anemia rate in premature infants and does not increase bilirubin levels or phototherapy demand.
