Transition to the ultimate turbulent regime in stratified inclined duct flow Preprint: APS/123-QED
Rundong Zhou 1, Adrien Lefauve 2,3, Roberto Verzicco 1,4,5, Detlef Lohse 1,6

Abstract
The stratified inclined duct (SID) flow provides a canonical model for sustained, buoyancy-driven exchange between two reservoirs of different density, and emerges as a new paradigm in geophysical fluid dynamics. Yet, the flow dynamics remain unclear in the highly turbulent regime; laboratory experiments can access this regime but they lack resolution, while direct numerical simulations (DNS) at realistically high Prandtl number Pr = 7 (for heat in water) have not achieved sufficiently high Reynolds numbers Re \mathrm{Re} . We conduct three-dimensional DNS up to Re = 8000 \mathrm{Re}=8000 and observe the transition to the so-called ultimate regime of turbulent convection as evidenced by the Nusselt number scaling Nu ∼ Ra 1 / 2 \mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{1/2} . We further report that the shear Reynolds number, a key parameter characterizing boundary layer (BL) dynamics, exceeds the threshold range of 420 for turbulent kinetic BLs with the emergence of logarithmic velocity profiles. Our work connects SID flow with the broader class of wall-bounded turbulent convection flows and gives insight into mixing in the vigorously turbulent regimes of oceanography and industry.



Introduction.
—Buoyancy-driven exchange flows occur in many natural and built environments when two large bodies of fluid at different densities are connected through a narrow channel.
Such flows cause significant exchange of scalars (e.g., heat, salt, pollutants, nutrients) between the two reservoirs with minimal to no net volume transport
Wood 1970
;
Wilkinson 1986
.
They underpin key mixing and transport processes in oceanography and industrial flows.
For example, the Mediterranean Sea depends on exchanges through the straits of Gibraltar and the Bosphorus
Deacon 1971
, coastal environments depend on estuarine transport processes
Fischer 1976
;
MacCready et al. 2018
, and natural air ventilation in buildings and industrial safety scenarios depend on exchange between stratified air masses
Linden 1999
.
Moreover, these flows are inherently stably stratified shear flows, ideal for studying shear instabilities and sustained stratified turbulence, a research lineage extending back to seminal works by Osborne Reynolds
Reynolds 1883
and G. I. Taylor
Taylor 1931

The stratified inclined duct (SID) experiment was developed to study such buoyancy-driven exchange flows with sustained shear under well-controlled conditions

A key feature of SID flow is that its shear is generated exclusively by buoyancy, distinguishing it from canonical sheared convective flows that are typically driven mechanically

;
Zhou et al. 2017a
;
Zhou et al. 2017b

;

Additionally, SID flow is bounded by solid walls with no-slip conditions.
These characteristics connect SID flow to other extensively studied canonical buoyancy-driven wall-bounded systems such as Rayleigh–Bénard (RB) convection (flow heated from below and cooled from above in an enclosed domain)

;
Lohse and Xia 2010
;

;
Schmidt et al. 2012
;
Pawar and Arakeri 2016
;
Castaing et al. 2017
,
vertical convection (flow between two vertical heated and cooled plates)
Ng et al. 2015
;
Ng et al. 2017
;
Ng et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
,
and Taylor-Couette flow (flow between two coaxial co- or counter-rotating cylinders)
Dubrulle and Hersant 2002
;
Eckhardt et al. 2007
;
Grossmann et al. 2016
.
In particular, for an extreme
\theta=90^{\circ}

\theta

In spite of the recent interest, SID flow characteristics in the strongly turbulent regime have remained out of reach: experiments reached
\mathrm{Re}=\mathcal{O}(10^{4})

Lefauve and Linden 2020
;
Lefauve and Couchman 2024
but the measurements are spatially under-resolved, while numerical simulations resolve full flow fields but have been limited to lower
\mathrm{Re}=\mathcal{O}(10^{3})

Zhu et al. 2023

In this Letter, we overcome previous limitations by directly simulating highly turbulent SID flow up to
\mathrm{Re}=8000

\mathrm{Ra}\approx 1.8\times 10^{9}

Our primary finding is a pronounced enhancement in mass transfer efficiency beyond a threshold of
\mathrm{Ra}\approx 10^{8}
, shifting to a scaling of
\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{1/2}

This transition is concomitant with the development of turbulent boundary layers (BL), suggesting a novel turbulent channel configuration.
The results present the first observation of ultimate regime in SID, and the first 3D numerical evidence of turbulent BLs in purely buoyancy-driven systems.

Conclusions and outlook.
—In this Letter, we presented the first 3D DNS of SID flow in the ultimate regime. The transition at
\mathrm{Ra}\approx 10^{8}
is marked by an enhancement in mass transfer efficiency to the asymptotic ultimate scaling of
\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{1/2}
.
We interpret this as the point where turbulent convection becomes the dominant driving mechanism, sustained by the formation of a streamwise uniform density gradient, analogous to the behavior observed in homogeneous RB convection.
Simultaneously, the shear Reynolds number at the top and bottom walls exceeds
\mathrm{Re}^{*}_{s}\approx 420
, triggering turbulent BLs through the nonlinear-non-normal subcritical route.
The ultimate onset Ra range coincides with the turbulent BL transition, independently of the duct inclination
\theta
, despite clear variations in global flow regimes with
\theta
, suggesting the transition is associated with the BLs.
From a broader fluid dynamics perspective, SID flow offers a novel internal wall shear flow configuration characterized by two extrema in the mean velocity profile, contrasting with canonical wall turbulence having no (plane Couette) or a single (Poiseuille) velocity peak.

Many questions remain open.
Although the asymptotic
Q_{m}=1/2
predicted by inviscid two-layer hydraulics coincides with the scaling
\mathrm{Nu}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{1/2}
, our results show that
Q_{m}<0.5
in the current range of
\mathrm{Ra}\approx 10^{7}
-
10^{9}
.
It remains unclear whether
Q_{m}
will remain below 0.5 indefinitely or eventually approach 0.5 as
\mathrm{Ra}\rightarrow\infty
.
Our current interpretation is unlikely to hold for very small
\theta
or in a horizontal duct (
\theta\approx 0^{\circ}
), where the streamwise convective driving is weak or absent compared to horizontal hydrostatic pressure, even in the turbulent regime. Whether such horizontal SID flows can exhibit the ultimate regime remains unknown.

Our results highlights universalities among various wall-bounded convective flows. Beyond fundamental fluid dynamics, our scaling laws for turbulent transport—including mixing efficiency in the End Matter—help inform high-Re and high-Ra closure models in geophysics and engineering.

Mixing and energy budget.
—In contrast with RB convection, where the relation between
\mathrm{Nu}
and the kinetic energy dissipation
\langle{\epsilon_{u}}\rangle=\nu\langle|\mathbf{\nabla u}|^{2}\rangle
is exact and closed
Lohse and Shishkina 2024
, such a relation exists neither in SID nor in vertical convection
Ng et al. 2015
. The energy budget equation is

\begin{split}\langle\epsilon_{u}\rangle-\Phi_{u}=\frac{\nu^{3}}{H^{4}}\frac{\mathrm{Ra}}{\mathrm{Pr}^{2}}\big[\sin\theta&\>(\mathrm{Nu}-1)-\cos\theta\>(\mathrm{Nu}_{b}-1)\big],\end{split}

(4)

where
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}=\frac{\langle{u_{z}(\rho-\rho_{0})}\rangle}{\kappa\Delta\rho/H}+1
is the buoyancy flux quantifying vertical mixing in the duct’s frame of reference and
\Phi_{u}=(1/V)\langle u_{x}|\bm{u}|^{2}/2+u_{x}p\rangle_{A}\big|_{R-L}
is the net convective momentum flux (
\langle\cdot\rangle_{A}
denoting averaging over the duct cross-section of
H^{2}
and time and
\cdot|_{R-L}
is the difference between the right and left duct ends).
The dimensionless dissipation
\epsilon_{u}^{\prime}=\langle\epsilon_{u}\rangle\frac{H^{3}\mathrm{Pr}^{2}}{\nu^{3}\mathrm{Ra}}
, shown in
fig.

6a
, exhibits a steeper
\mathrm{Ra}
scaling in the ultimate regime, with
\epsilon_{u}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{1/3}
before and
\epsilon_{u}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{1/2}
after the transition, mirroring the
\mathrm{Nu}(\mathrm{Ra})
scalings.
The edge momentum flux
|\Phi_{u}|\ll\langle{\epsilon_{u}}\rangle
is very small according to our numerical results and can thus be neglected in
eq.

4
(see the Supplemental Material).
The buoyancy flux
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}
, shown in
fig.

6b
, exhibits an effective scaling
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}\sim\mathrm{Ra}^{0.70}
.
In contrast to RB convection, where
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}=0
due to homogeneity along the direction normal to the lateral walls, SID stratification leads to a finite buoyancy flux.
The negative sign in front of
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}
in
eq.

4
indicates that the kinetic energy generated by the buoyancy
\mathrm{Nu}
is consumed by both dissipation
\epsilon_{u}
and mixing
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}
in the stratified layer.
Notably, no clear transition is observed in the
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}(\mathrm{Ra})
scaling, consistent with our conjecture that the ultimate transition in SID is associated with the top and bottom wall kinetic BLs, where
|\partial_{z}\rho|\approx 0
and
|u_{z}|\ll 1
, contributing negligibly to
\mathrm{Nu}_{b}
.

In
fig.

6c
, we plot the ratio
\Gamma=\mathrm{Nu}_{b}/\epsilon_{u}^{\prime}
, commonly referred to as the flux parameter, a key quantity in the ocean mixing literature that characterizes mixing efficiency—essentially quantifying the ‘taxation’ of stratification on turbulent dissipation
Caulfield 2020
.
We observe that
\Gamma
asymptotes to 0.1 at large Ra for all three angles
\theta
.
The value aligns with previous stratified turbulence DNS in a triply periodic domain
Feraco et al. 2018
and the estimates from SID experiments
Lefauve and Linden 2022b
despite limitations in the spatial resolution required to fully resolve
\epsilon_{u}
.
Notably, it is significantly lower than the widely adopted
\Gamma\approx 0.2
in the oceanography literature
Osborn 1980
;
Gregg et al. 2018
;
Caulfield 2020
for reasons that remain to be understood.

Turbulent boundary layers.
—A hallmark feature of a turbulent BL is the log-law of the wall. We plot the averaged streamwise velocity profiles near
z=0
(bottom wall) at
y=H/2
(spanwise mid-plane) in
fig.

7
a.
The profiles are shown in the wall-normal units with
u^{+}\equiv\langle{u_{x}}\rangle_{x,t}/u_{\tau}
, where
u_{\tau}=\sqrt{\nu\partial_{z}\langle{u_{x}}\rangle_{x,t}|_{z=0}}
is the friction velocity, and
the spatial coordinates
z^{+}\equiv z/\delta_{\nu}
, with
\delta_{\nu}=\nu/u_{\tau}
being the viscous unit.
The magenta curves mark the profiles near the ultimate transition where the BLs have just turned turbulent.
At large
\mathrm{Ra}
, we notice that the curves converge to the shape of canonical 3D wall turbulence, comprising the viscous linear sublayer and by the short logarithmic region
u^{+}=(1/\kappa_{\nu})\ln z^{+}+B
with
\kappa_{\nu}=0.41
agreeing with the von Kármán constant. The parameter
B
differs slightly for different
\theta
.
We note that the logarithmic regions are not fully developed even at our highest
\mathrm{Ra}
, spanning only about half a decade in
z^{+}
.
This is not surprising: in SID, the logarithmic region is immediately followed by a negative mean velocity gradient in the bulk, arising from the counter-directional nature of the exchange flows, rather than by the free-streaming wake region of canonical turbulent boundary layers. This likely suppresses the log-law from extending further into
z^{+}
.

To further appreciate the features in the BLs in different regimes, we plot snapshots of the streamwise velocity
u_{x}

These streaks are another hallmark of turbulent BLs, well-documented in canonical turbulent BL literature
Smits et al. 2011
.
In the zoom-in
