AR‐assisted surgery: Precision placement of patient specific hip implants based on 3D printed PSIs
Authors: Amaia Iribar‐Zabala, Tanya Fernández‐Fernández, Javier Orozco‐Martínez, José Calvo‐Haro, Rubén Pérez‐Mañanes, Elena Aguilera‐Jiménez, Carla de Gregorio‐Bermejo, Rafael Benito, Alicia Pose‐Díez‐de‐la‐Lastra, Andoni Beristain‐Iraola, Javier Pascau, Mónica García‐Sevilla
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatient‐specific implant placement in the case of pelvic tumour resection is usually a complex procedure, where the planned optimal position of the prosthesis may differ from the final location. This discrepancy arises from incorrect or differently executed bone resection and improper final positioning of the prosthesis. In order to overcome such mismatch, a navigation solution is presented based on an augmented reality application for HoloLens 2 to assist the entire procedure. This involves placing patient‐specific instruments for tumour resection guidance in the supraacetabular, ischial and symphysial regions, performing the osteotomy and assisting within the adequate positioning of the implant. The supraacetabular patient‐specific instrument and the prosthesis included optical markers attached to them to be used as reference for surgical guidance. The proposed application and workflow were validated by two clinicians on six phantoms, designed and fabricated from different cadaver specimens. The accuracy of the solution was evaluated by comparing the final position after navigation with the position defined in the surgical plan. Preliminary assessment shows promising results for the guidance system, with positive clinician feedback.Keywords: augmented reality, biomedical imaging, computer vision, holography, prosthetics, surgery, tracking

INTRODUCTION

Pelvic bone tumours account for 5–15% of primary malignancies and are the third most common region of bone metastases after the spine and ribs [1]. Tumours affecting the acetabulum severely impair hip function, necessitating specialised treatment. En‐bloc resection is a common approach for these tumours, but reconstructing the resulting hemipelvic bone defects with low complication rates remains challenging [2]. The procedure presents two major complexities: bone resection and implant placement.

Periacetabular resection is an invasive process that can lead to complications if adjacent structures are damaged. Thus, it must be performed with precision. Consequently, numerous studies have explored the use of technologies such as surgical navigation based on optical tracking system (OTS) [3, 4, 5], 3D patient‐specific instruments (PSIs) [6, 7], or a combination of both [8, 9], to improve cutting accuracy, thereby minimising errors, enhancing patient safety and potentially improving surgical outcomes. OTS offer high precision and real‐time tracking capabilities. However, they often require a cumbersome setup and a clear line of sight, limiting their application in complex surgical environments. In contrast, PSIs offer a less cumbersome alternative, but according to [6, 7] they are usually large, implying a more invasive procedure as they necessitate more extensive bone cleaning. In [10] they proposed the use of smaller PSIs to mitigate the invasiveness caused by large designs and contribute to shorter procedure times by serving as reference guides.

Recently, solutions based on augmented reality (AR) have been introduced. In [8] it was shown that the use of AR significantly reduced the errors in PSI placement with respect to manual positioning, enabling visualisation of cutting planes within the tumour area and thereby enhancing cutting precision. In another investigation conducted by Hoch et al. [5] they developed an AR application for navigating periacetabular osteotomy (PAO). The procedure was tested on 14 human hip cadavers. This research aimed to assess the viability and accuracy of using AR to perform the four osteotomies and to realign the acetabular fragment based on preoperative 3D plans.

Regarding the concerns related to implant placement, precise positioning of the implant is essential to prevent misalignment relative to the femoral head and potential long‐term loosening of the prosthesis due to inadequate fixation. The current technique relies on preoperative CT for planning the procedure and defining the optimal placement of the prosthesis [11]. Additionally, some methods incorporate computer‐assisted navigation primarily using surgical instrument tracking combined with PSIs to enhance the accuracy [9]. However, all the information concerning the prosthesis pipeline is displayed on 2D monitors, not even being displayed from the surgeon's point of view, which forces them to look away from the patient to visualise the information.

Some research groups have explored the use of smartphone‐based AR for acetabular cup placement in total hip arthoplasty using OTS [12, 13]. These type of devices may be suitable for allowing all the surgeons to visualise the virtual scene overlaid in the patient through the screen of the smartphone. However, they lack manoeuvrability, as the surgeon should be holding them, and may create interference with the direct line of sight to the marker. The introduction of AR navigation systems based on head mounted displays (HMD) could potentially benefit the surgeon avoid all these inconveniences. To the best of our knowledge, no current approach addresses prosthesis implantation through the use of HMD‐based AR, or the entire workflow procedure.

In this study, we evaluate the feasibility of using AR on a HMD to assist the whole workflow, providing guidance for the accurate placement of both, the PSIs for resections and the implant. Our objective is to reduce the invasiveness of the procedure through the use of small PSIs for resection and relying on them for registration. This approach minimises the need to expose a larger bone area, thereby leading to a reduction in surgical time.

The complete study will encompass three principal phases. The initial phase, which constitutes the focus of this article, involves the development of a proof‐of‐concept scenario utilising 3D‐printed models of the pelvis to validate the surgical workflow and enhance any potential shortcomings of the AR application based on clinician feedback. The second and third phases, to be conducted in subsequent investigations, will consist of validations on cadavers and patients, respectively.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A CT scan of 512x matrix and 0.98 mm of pixel size was acquired for 10 cadaveric specimens. For this phase of the study, as the main objective was to validate the complete workflow, we randomly selected six CT scans, specifically focusing on the left hemipelvis of these cases.

Design and fabrication

First, we manually segmented the bone of the hemipelvis using 3D Slicer [14]. From this segmentation, an expert clinician defined three cutting planes in the supraacetabular, ischial, and symphysial regions to resect the acetabulum. These planes were used as reference to design three PSIs indicating the cutting planes in the actual procedure (Figure 1). The design of the PSIs was carried out using 3‐matic software (Materialise, Belgium). Holes were added to each PSI for fixation to the bone using screws. A socket was included in the supraacetabular PSI to allow the placement of an AR marker. As irregular shapes and non‐uniform characteristics help minimise the error in placement, as detailed in [10], this information was leveraged in the design of the supraacetabular PSI, that was used as reference. A personalised implant was designed, also featuring a removable socket for placement of another AR marker. Regarding the AR markers, they were designed with a size of 4 × 4 cm, presenting a unique and characteristic pattern following the quality standards of Vuforia, the library used for marker detection.

Right hemipelvis with three PSIs and their corresponding plane cuts. In green the supraacetabular PSI, in orange the ischial PSI and in pink the symphysial PSI.

All these components were 3D printed using different materials and 3D printers. The healthy portion of the bone was 3D printed in acrylonitrile styrene acrylate (ASA) material, while the AR markers were printed with a double extruder in black and white polylactic acid (PLA) filament, all of them using a Bambu Lab X1E printer. The PSIs and the personalised implant were 3D printed in rigid 10k resin, a radiopaque material, using a Form 2 (Formslab, MA, U.S.A.) printer. The selection of materials for each component was based on facilitating its segmentation in a CT scan after the experiment for posterior analysis.

Navigation

An AR application for HoloLens 2 (Microsoft Corporation, WA, U.S.A) HMD was developed to facilitate the navigation through the entire workflow of implant placement. The development was carried out using Unity engine, integrated with mixed reality toolkit (MRTK) to enhance functionality and user experience. Precise hologram positioning within the application was achieved using the Vuforia library [15].

The application features a hand menu that includes on/off buttons for visualising PSIs relative to the marker placed on the supraacetabular region, and sliders for adjusting the transparency of the objects. The visualisation of the implant can also be activated to show the model in a semi‐transparent white. This allows the user to visualise the target position of the implant and use it for guidance.

Additionally, a second marker placed on the implant enables tracking of its position. As the implant approaches the target, the colour of the marker changes from red to yellow and finally to green upon reaching an acceptable position.

Once the design and fabrication phase was concluded, the actual experiment was performed by two expert clinicians following these steps: (1) manual placement of the supraacetabular PSI; (2) placement of the AR marker; (3) placement of the ischial and symphysial PSIs assisted by the visualisation of the hologram displayed in the HoloLens 2; (4) acetabular osteotomy assisted by the visualisation of the cutting planes displayed in the HoloLens 2; (5) placement of the AR marker on the implant; (6) placement of the implant assisted by the visualisation of the target position as a hologram displayed on the phantom and by the colour displayed on the implant hologram (S1). In Figure 3 it can be observed a clinician looking at the markers placed in the phantom, upon completion of the workflow and the holographic view.

On the left, a clinician wearing the HoloLens 2 HMD for tracking the Vuforia markers and practising on the workflow. On the right, the holographic scene with the holograms overlaid in the phantom: the supraacetabular PSI in dark green, the ischial and symphysial PSIs in blue and the prosthesis in light green.

Finally, a postoperative CT was acquired with all the phantoms. This CT was used for the analysis. All structures, including the PSIs, the phantom, and the implant, were segmented for evaluation. This analysis assessed the positional deviation of the PSIs in terms of plane cuts, the placement of the implant, considering both fit and acetabular orientation, and the error introduced by the final positioning of the AR marker.

The described workflow will be maintained for the following phases of the study (validation in cadavers and validation in patients). However, in the patient's phase, PSIs will be removed upon completion of the surgery and, if a postoperative CT is acquired, the analysis will be performed differently.

Evaluation

Five metrics have been evaluated: the deviation of the planes according to the final placement of the PSIs, the osteotomy accuracy, the prosthesis placement, the error introduced by the AR marker and the time spent on each task.

Osteotomy accuracy

The cutting accuracy was assessed using the postoperative CT scans, where both PSIs and the phantom bone were manually segmented. Following this segmentation, the expected cutting planes, as defined by the attached PSIs, were computed. Prosthesis placement

The evaluation of prosthesis placement considers two main aspects: the fitting in the pelvis through plane comparison and the position and orientation of the acetabular component.

The fitting of the prosthesis in the pelvis was assessed by comparing the planes in the same manner as described in previous subsection.

The position of the acetabular component was calculated by fitting a sphere to the acetabulum and determining its centre. Then this position was compared to the one defined in the planning.

Regarding the orientation of the acetabulum, the horizontality/verticality was measured in the coronal plane (see Figure 5a). This was achieved by calculating the angle between a horizontal line parallel to the axial plane drawn from the inferior corner of the acetabulum and another line intersecting both the superior and inferior edges of the acetabulum. Typically, this angle should range between 40 and 50. Additionally, a slight anteversion, measured with respect to the sagittal plane, is desired within the range of 15 to 20 (see Figure 5b). The error is given in relative values, considering the differences between the planned angles and the obtained ones.

Acetabulum orientation in terms of (a) horizontality, and (b) anteversion.

Task time

The time spent for the different procedure tasks was measured: (1) supraacetabular PSI placement, (2) supraacetabular PSI fixation, (3) symphysial PSI placement, (4) symphysial PSI fixation, (5) ischial PSI placement, (6) ischial PSI fixation, (7) supraacetabular osteotomy, (8) symphysial osteotomy, (9) ischial osteotomy, (10) prosthesis placement, and (11) prosthesis fixation.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

In this work, a novel and less invasive method for the placement of an acetabular prosthesis has been presented, using AR to assist with the two most challenging aspects of the surgery: resection and implant placement. Proper execution of these steps is crucial to minimise the risk of recurrence and ensure optimal biomechanical function for the patient. To assess the feasibility of the proposed solution, the workflow has been tested on phantoms, as an initial step of the entire study. The results obtained demonstrate the viability of the proposed workflow, achieving a high accuracy using the developed AR‐based surgical navigation app. The entire workflow has been designed to align with the standard clinical pipeline for this procedure, incorporating markers that can be sterilised and has already been used in previous studies in the surgical rooms.

Regarding the results obtained related to the PSIs placement in terms of plane cut orientation and translation, it is observed that in general the mean angular and distance errors are low. The supraacetabular PSI generally shows lower angular errors (around 1) and moderate distance errors, with the highest reaching 3.24 mm because it was broken during the osteotomy. The symphysis PSI exhibits a broader range of angular errors, and in this case the distance remains lower, except from one case that reaches 3.26 mm, that was also broken during the osteotomy. The ischial PSI consistently presents higher angular errors peaking at 2,96, with higher maximum distance errors. The errors observed are lower than those reported in [18], which conducted a very similar approach in the PSI placement and obtained an average error value of 2.19 mm and was also validated on phantoms.

Concerning the osteotomy accuracy, this one shows high variability in angular deviations across the different cutting plane regions and phantoms. The supraacetabular region shows a broad range of deviations, from 1.07 to 6.33, indicating inconsistent performance that may need refinement for better accuracy. The symphysial region consistently exhibits larger deviations, ranging from 1.06 to 7.46, suggesting a potential design flaw or procedural issue requiring improvement. In contrast, the ischial region demonstrates more stable deviations, between 2.50 and 3.75, reflecting better consistency in this area. This variability may be explained by an incorrect fixation of the phantom in the setup, which could cause movement and directly impact cutting performance.

In terms of personalised prosthesis placement, the cutting planes and the acetabulum show higher errors than the PSIs in general. From the rest of the results, we infer that this is due to the accumulated errors across the previous steps, as well as, the inherent errors of the printing process and the tolerances of the various materials used. Moreover, it was observed that once the phantom was cut, the material exhibited slight flexibility, allowing for minimal deformation when placing the prosthesis.

Additionally, the horizontality and the anteversion of the acetabulum were analysed. According to the results, there is no significant relative error, as they fall within the average values accepted in clinical practice: 40–50 for horizontality and 15–20 for anteversion. The results are lower than those presented in [13] which uses a smartphone. However, their evaluation was performed in a prospective study with patients, which differs from our validation setup.

The tracking system employed is based on the Vuforia library for HoloLens 2. Although the markers must be specifically designed to meet quality standards, including good contrast for proper detection, they are quite accurate in positioning the hologram without the need for complex registration. Additionally, users do not experience the hologram as constantly trembling, resulting in a smoother experience. Furthermore, since the markers are properly sterilised and removable, their size can be relatively large, up to 4×4 cm.

However, despite the stability provided by Vuforia, it may be beneficial to test other marker tracking libraries, such as [16] where they used ArUco library with HMD‐based AR. According to recent literature [17], where a comparison of four visual marker‐based libraries for HoloLens 2 was done —including, Vuforia, ArUco, ARToolKit, and Mixed Reality Toolkit QRCode— ArUco demonstrated the best results in terms of translation accuracy, while Vuforia provided the greatest stability in orientation. A limitation of Vuforia is that it blocks the photo/video camera of the HoloLens, preventing the transmission of the user's view to a remote computer, which is a valuable characteristic in surgery for allowing the rest of the clinicians to observe the same as the surgeon within the application. In further approaches, the use of other markers may be explored.

It can be noted that in order to address line‐of‐sight challenges, marker‐less methods could also be considered. However, in contrast to phantoms, human tissue is highly heterogeneous, making the distinction of the pelvis bone complex and challenging. The application of deep learning (DL) algorithms for real‐time localisation may help overcome these difficulties, yet currently, no approaches exist that can accurately identify the pelvis bone in real‐time.

With regards to the time elapsed for each task, the mean times range from approximately 5 s for PSI placements to over 132 s for prosthesis fixation. The standard deviations highlight significant variability in these times, especially for prosthesis fixation (TFProst), which has a high deviation of 47.579 s. In contrast, steps that involve the placement and fixation (TPSupra and TFSupra) exhibit relatively lower deviations, suggesting a more consistent performance in these procedures. The high variability in times for certain procedures, such as the ischial osteotomy (TOIsc), suggests that clinicians faced challenges during this step. This procedure was conducted after the other two osteotomies, at a point when the acetabulum had increased mobility, which may have contributed to the difficulties and inconsistencies observed. The mean time spent is approximately 10 ± 4 min, which is minimal considering that these types of surgery are generally quite long.

The primary objective of this research was to validate the initial workflow for subsequent studies in cadaver specimens. To this end, the feedback provided by the expert clinician on the use of the application as a navigation system was collected. Despite an initial learning curve, evidenced by a significant reduction in execution time as the clinician became accustomed to the app, the overall feedback was very positive. It could be observed that as the clinician got used to the interface, they performed the workflow more confindently.

The experiment was successfully conducted. However, in order to enhance the user experience, improvements in the depth perception will be explored. Regarding the colour‐based navigation solution, the feedback provided by the clinicians was very positive in terms of utility and intuitiveness. Even if no formal user study was conducted; clinician feedback was evaluated to gauge the overall experience. The application was tailored to meet their specific needs and address the technological limitations that they report encountering during the procedure. They appreciated the functionality to activate different components in the holographic scene, adjust transparencies to guide osteotomies through cutting planes, placing PSIs, and changing the implant colour. Despite one user having prior experience with the HoloLens and the other having limited experience, both were able to successfully complete the experiments.

In conclusion, a novel approach for AR‐based navigation in pelvic tumour resection and implant placement has been proposed, validating the entire workflow. At the same time, this research has proven highly valuable in identifying areas for refinement in the final application, particularly for its implementation in subsequent study phases. Nevertheless, the results demonstrate the potential usefulness and feasibility of the workflow, highlighting its innovative and less invasive approach that could enhance the precision of the surgical procedure.
