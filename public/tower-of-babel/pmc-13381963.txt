The soil microbiome of the Caatinga drylands in Brazil
Authors: Luísa Mayumi Arake de Tacca, Rayane Nunes Lima, Marco Antônio de Oliveira, Patrícia Verdugo Pascoal, Deborah Bambil, Grácia Maria Soares Rosinha, Diana Signor, Marcelo Freire, Elibio Rech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drylands cover a significant portion of the Earth’s surface and play a key role in maintaining global ecological balance. The Caatinga, with its unique biodiversity adapted to the extreme conditions of this semi-arid region, offers a valuable opportunity to expand our knowledge about these ecosystems. Here, this work reveals the high microbial diversity in the soil and rhizosphere of the Caatinga, with the roots presenting more specialized communities. Bacteria such as Bacilli, Alphaproteobacteria and Firmicutes excelled in critical functions such as nutrient cycling. The Interplant differences suggested the influence of root exudates. Altogether, the metagenomic study of interactions between microorganisms in the rhizosphere of selected plants revealed microbial biodiversity and contributed to our understanding of nutrient cycling, plant growth and resistance to water stress. In addition, they demonstrate biotechnological potential to address global challenges such as desertification and food security.

Introduction

Drylands are a widespread and diverse ecological condition present on all continents except Antarctica1. They cover more than 47% of Earth’s land surface, with approximately 15% of this area located in Latin America2,3. As the largest terrestrial biome, drylands encompass a variety of distinct ecosystems including rangelands, grasslands, woodlands, savannahs, deserts, scrublands, and dry forests4. Globally, drylands are defined by several climatic, ecological, and geographical criteria including water deficit, high spatial and temporal variability in precipitation, and seasonal climatic extremes. Precipitation levels categorize drylands into arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid regions, with annual rainfall ranging from approximately 10 inches (250 mm) to 30 inches (750 mm)4,5. Additionally, drylands are marked by polyextreme conditions such as high evaporation rates, extremely high solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and temperature extremes4,6. These arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid environments face extreme climatic conditions, including irregular rainfall and high evaporation. These factors challenge the adaptive strategies of resident plants and microorganisms7. Drylands globally consist of diverse land types, including forests (18%), barren land (28%), grasslands (25%), croplands (14%), and other wooded lands (10%)8. Drylands are ecologically significant as they contribute to approximately 40% of global net primary productivity (NPP) and host 35% of the world’s biodiversity hotspots9–11.

Furthermore, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, drylands occupy about 1.1 billion hectares, or 27%, of the global forested area8. In South America, drylands predominantly feature arid and semi-arid climates and the primary dryland regions include the Atacama Desert, the Patagonian Desert, the Monte Desert, the Caatinga, and the Chaco region. These areas are critically important for their biodiversity and ecological roles but are also exceptionally vulnerable to climate change and desertification12. The Brazilian semi-arid region (SAB) is within this category, with more than twenty-eight million inhabitants, constituting the most populous semi-arid zone in the world13. The SAB has a high biodiversity, with the presence of the Caatinga biome (with xerophytic vegetation), along with enclaves of Cerrado (semi-deciduous characteristics), and Atlantic Forest (ombrophilous vegetation) regions that create unique ecosystems14 and all are characterized by low rainfall rates of < 800 mm/year15–17. The Caatinga, a unique biome located in the Brazilian semi-arid region, exemplifies the typical characteristics of drylands. With an area of more than 800,000 km², bordered to the east by the Atlantic Ocean and to the west/southwest by the Cerrado and Atlantic Forest Biomes, the Caatinga has a diversity of plants adapted to a climate of low precipitation and high climate variability. This unique identity means that its entire ecosystem, with its unique biodiversity adapted to arid conditions, is contained within national borders, giving Brazil full responsibility for its conservation. Characterized by a semiarid climate, with irregular rainfall and long periods of drought, the Caatinga’s physiognomy is marked by xerophilic, deciduous, and thorny vegetation, which exhibits remarkable resilience strategies to survive and regenerate in the face of water scarcity18,19. This combination of biogeographic endemism and adaptation to dryland conditions makes it an irreplaceable natural laboratory for ecological studies and a biological heritage of global significance. The Caatinga biome is protected and recognized through national legislation from the Ministry of the Environment, such as the Caatinga Protection Law (Law No. 14,785/2023) and the new Forest Code (Law No. 12,651/2012), which establish rules for the conservation, restoration, and sustainable use of the biome. The biome has been internationally recognized as a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve since 2001, which fosters conservation and sustainable development activities within its area. However, this biome is still under-studied compared to other dryland ecosystems, such as those found in Africa or Asia, which limits the understanding of its ecological dynamics, biodiversity and biological interactions. Additionally, drylands in general host 39% of the global population and as such it is of substantial socioeconomic1. Recent studies have shown an increase in areas of desertification, plant species becoming more adapted to drought, and an intensification of socioeconomic problems20,21.

Despite its ecological importance and remarkable plant endemism, the Caatinga remains significantly underrepresented in global microbiological databases22,23. This study addresses a critical knowledge gap by investigating the rhizosphere microbiota of key Caatinga species, a drought-resilient microbial environment that is largely uncharacterized on a global scale22,24. While the Caatinga is not conventionally classified as a microbial biodiversity hotspot25–27, this very status makes it a high-priority system for global microbial sampling initiatives. Our research, by focusing on a threatened and neglected ecosystem19,28,29, directly aligns with the objectives of international efforts like the Earth Microbiome Project; (which is not currently taking more samples)30 and the Soil Biodiversity Observation Network (Soil BON)60,61, which prioritizes filling biogeographic gaps in our understanding of global microbial diversity.

Drylands support a rich diversity of microorganisms, collectively known as dryland microbiomes, which exist either freely or in symbiotic relationships with vascular plants or within biological soil crusts (biocrusts)4,11,25,26,31,32. These microbiomes play vital roles in dryland ecosystems, contributing to crucial functions such as the formation of fertile islands, nutrient cycling, and climate regulation, and so are foundational to the ecological succession of vegetation in these extreme environments4,33. The Caatinga soil, generally dry and poor in nutrients, is a complex environment of interactions between microorganisms, plants and abiotic factors19,24. The rhizosphere, the region of soil surrounding the plant’s roots, is a dynamic ecosystem where microorganisms play a key role in cycling nutrients, promoting plant growth and mitigating environmental stresses34–36. Symbiotic interactions between the roots of Caatinga plants and microorganisms, especially arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), are vital for the adaptation of plants to arid conditions37,38. AMFs, for example, increase the absorption of nutrients and water by plants, essential for their survival in soils with little water availability37. Furthermore, AMFs facilitate the acquisition of the essential nutrient phosphorus in poor soils by forming a network of hyphae that expands the area of soil exploration, connecting plant roots to more distant sources of phosphorus, often inaccessible to the roots alone, increasing availability38. Prokaryotes present in the rhizosphere, such as nitrogen-fixing bacteria, also play a fundamental role in the Caatinga, contributing to nitrogen cycling and improving soil fertility39,40. Furthermore, prokaryotes actively participate in the carbon cycle by decomposing organic matter and releasing essential nutrients required for plant growth39,41. Another important aspect of the Caatinga soil microbiota is the ability of some bacteria and fungi to mediate the production and consumption of methane, a potent greenhouse gas. The microbial communities present in the soil influence the balance between methane production and oxidation, directly impacting emissions of this gas into the atmosphere39,42,43.

Exploring the dryland ecology of microorganisms and their response to climate change and other anthropogenic pressures is nowadays of primary importance7,25,44. In our study, we collected samples from the soil, rhizosphere, and roots of ten prominent Caatinga plant species. The selection of these species was based on a specific set of criteria to ensure they were ecologically and culturally representative of the study area. All ten species are predominant and endemic to the Caatinga biome within the authorized collection area. Beyond their ecological significance as foundational native species critical for the biome’s structure, we prioritized specimens with broad multifunctional importance. These species were chosen due to their traditional use in folk medicine, fuel and timber production, food and animal forage, and apiculture. Most importantly, these plants have a high potential for the recovery of degraded areas within the Caatinga. Therefore, our selection aimed to represent a plant group that is simultaneously ecologically and culturally valuable, and key for future restoration and sustainable use proposals.

The selected species were: Umburana (Commiphora leptophloeos), Sapium (Sapium glandulosum (L.) Morong), Faveleira (Cnidoscolus phyllacanthus), Baraúna (Schinopsis brasiliensis), Jurema-preta (Mimosa tenuiflora), Angico (Anadenanthera colubrina), Sete-cascas (Handroanthus spongiosus), Umbuzeiro (Spondias tuberosa Arruda), Catingueira (Caesalpinia pyramidalis Tul.) and Maniçoba (Manihot pseudoglaziovii Pax & K. Hoffm.). A map of the Caatinga location in relation to Brazil is shown in Fig. 1, as well as a flow diagram with the steps of our study.

The harsh conditions of the Caatinga biome, characterized by extreme drought, high temperatures, and soil degradation, create a unique evolutionary pressure that has driven the adaptation of resilient plant species22. Therefore, the biotechnological potential of the metagenomic biodiversity of Caatinga microorganisms is considerable24. These species have developed mechanisms that help them thrive in challenging environments, and these mechanisms can be harnessed for agricultural benefits, especially through the development of biological products like biopesticides, biofertilizers, and biostimulants. Such biological agents are highly sought due to their low impact on the environment22,45. Although the Caatinga biome has been studied in several aspects, the interaction between soil microbiota, plant roots and climatic conditions is still an unexplored field62. Functional analysis of these microorganisms may uncover new strategies for developing biotechnological products applicable to sustainable agriculture in Brazil and other dryland regions worldwide. The use of native microorganisms represents a promising approach to promote more efficient plant growth in arid soils and to protect against disease in agricultural biotechnology46–49. The Caatinga, although rich in biodiversity, faces major challenges due to soil degradation, desertification and unsustainable agricultural practices50,51. However, exploring microorganism-plant interactions, especially in regions with preserved or recovering native vegetation, can offer new opportunities to restore soil functionality and improve land productivity in the semi-arid region. Based on the ecological distinctiveness and climatic extremity of the Caatinga biome, we hypothesize that (i) the rhizosphere of native Caatinga plant species harbors unique and taxonomically distinct microbial communities compared to the surrounding bulk soil, shaped by host plant identity and the selective pressures of the semi-arid environment; (ii) the microbial community structure differs significantly across the three compartments analyzed (bulk soil, rhizosphere, and root endosphere), reflecting niche-specific filtering processes; and (iii) the functional metagenomic profile of these communities is enriched in genes and pathways associated with drought tolerance, nutrient acquisition under scarcity, and stress response, reflecting microbial adaptation to the polyextreme conditions of the Caatinga. To test these hypotheses, we established the following specific objectives: (1) to characterize and compare the taxonomic composition and diversity of prokaryotic and fungal communities across the bulk soil, rhizosphere, and root endosphere of ten native Caatinga plant species; (2) to evaluate the influence of host plant species identity on the assembly and structure of microbial communities; (3) to identify the core microbiome shared across Caatinga plant species and determine indicator taxa specific to each species and compartment; (4) to perform functional metagenomic profiling to identify metabolic pathways associated with plant growth promotion, stress tolerance, and nutrient cycling; and (5) to contribute baseline metagenomic data from an underrepresented biome to global soil microbial databases, addressing a critical biogeographic gap in our understanding of dryland microbiomes.

Results

Rhizosphere and soil share higher diversity and common microbes

We collected samples from 10 plant species from the Caatinga and separated these into three compartments: bulk soil, rhizosphere, and root endosphere. Total DNA was isolated according to McPherson et al. 52. Amplicon sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene was performed and results were grouped by compartment (hereafter referred to as microbiome sectors.

The rarefaction curves for the three compartments are shown in Fig. 2a. The soil and rhizosphere samples displayed higher rarefaction curves, indicating greater species richness (Chao1 and ACE indices) compared to root samples, which showed consistently lower curves. None of the curves reached a plateau across any compartment.

The Venn diagram of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) between compartments (Fig. 2b) revealed 571 shared OTUs between soil and rhizosphere, 82 between roots and rhizosphere, and 29 between roots and soil.

Taxonomic profiling of the top 10 bacterial classes by total count in each compartment (Fig. 2c–e; Table 1) showed that the soil microbiome had a more evenly distributed composition across classes. In contrast, the rhizosphere was dominated by Bacilli, and the root endosphere was dominated by Alphaproteobacteria and Actinobacteria. Seven of the top 10 classes were shared across all three compartments: Acidobacteriia, Actinobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Bacilli, Bacteroidia, Ktedonobacteria, and Planctomycetacia (Table 1).

Plant alpha diversity metrics differ between soil, rhizosphere and roots

Alpha diversity was analyzed at the plant species level within each compartment (Fig. 3a–c). In soil samples, Schinopsis brasiliensis (Baraúna) showed the highest rarefaction curve, followed by Sapium glandulosum (Sapium) and Commiphora leptophloeos (Umburana). The lowest soil richness was observed in Mimosa tenuiflora (Jurema-preta) and Spondias tuberosa (Umbuzeiro). In the root compartment, Baraúna displayed the lowest rarefaction curve and also exhibited an undersampling pattern (Fig. 3c).

Rhizosphere alpha diversity (Fig. 3b) was highest in Jurema-preta, Handroanthus spongiosus (Sete-cascas), and Umbuzeiro. In the root compartment (Fig. 3c), Umburana showed the highest alpha diversity, while Baraúna and Umbuzeiro showed the lowest.

Shannon index comparisons across compartments for each plant species (Fig. 3d) showed a clear separation between soil, rhizosphere, and root communities for most species. Soil and rhizosphere communities were more similar to each other than to roots across most plant species, with the exception of Sapium, which showed similar Shannon diversity values between roots and rhizosphere.

In the NMDS ordination at the plant level (Fig. 2f), Umburana, Umbuzeiro, and Baraúna were positioned at the edges of the root sample cluster. Umbuzeiro showed the most dissimilar rhizosphere community.

The heatmap of bacterial class abundance across plants and compartments (Fig. 3e) showed high abundance of Bacilli in the rhizosphere across most plant species, high prevalence of Nitrososphaeria in soil, and dominance of Alphaproteobacteria in roots. At the plant species level, Anadenanthera colubrina (Angico), Sapium, Cnidoscolus phyllacanthus (Faveleira), Manihot pseudoglaziovii (Maniçoba), Baraúna, and Umbuzeiro showed the highest rhizosphere abundance of Bacilli. Jurema-preta displayed the highest root abundance of Actinobacteria. Baraúna and Faveleira also showed high Bacilli abundance in roots. Plant species with low Bacilli abundance in roots showed correspondingly high Actinobacteria abundance in the same compartment (Fig. 3e).

Interplant differences are more pronounced in the root’s microbiome

Bray-Curtis NMDS analysis (Fig. 2f) showed that the greatest interplant dissimilarity was observed in the root compartment. Baraúna had fewer than 700 reads in the root compartment after quality filtering; therefore, results for this sample should be interpreted with caution pending resampling and resequencing.

Relative abundance of the top 10 genera was calculated for each plant species within each compartment to account for differences in sequencing depth between roots, soil, and rhizosphere. The shared genera between roots and rhizosphere for each plant are shown in Fig. 4a. For Faveleira and Baraúna, the relative abundances of shared genera were similar between roots and rhizosphere. Caesalpinia pyramidalis (Caatingueira), Umbuzeiro, and Jurema-preta had their shared genera predominantly concentrated in the roots. Sete-cascas, Sapium, Angico, Umburana, and Maniçoba had their shared genera predominantly concentrated in the rhizosphere (Fig. 4a).

A 5,000-read threshold was applied to root samples for deeper taxonomic analysis. Five species passed this filter: Umburana, Maniçoba, Sete-cascas, Sapium, and Jurema-preta. The top 10 genera for each of these species in the root compartment are shown in Fig. 4b:

Jurema-preta: Top 10 genera composed the majority of the root microbiome, dominated by Mycobacterium, Acidothermus, and Streptomyces.

Maniçoba: Evenly distributed among Bacillus (5.6%), Chitinophaga, Bradyrhizobium (4.4%), an unclassified Ktedonobacteraceae member (3%), an unclassified Xanthobacteraceae member (1.9%), an unclassified Chitinophagaceae member (0.4%), and Bryobacter (0.2%).

Sapium

Evenly distributed among Bradyrhizobium (6.8%), Actinophytocola (5.1%), Streptomyces (4.7%), Mycobacterium (4.5%), Pseudonocardia (4.2%), Acidibacter (4%), Bacillus (3.6%), and an unclassified Xanthobacteraceae member (2.6%).

Sete-cascas: Dominated by an unclassified Ktedonobacteraceae member (5.6%), Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia (3.1%), and Acidothermus (2%).

Umburana: Predominantly composed of members from class Bathyarchaeia (4%) and order Acidobacteriales (3.6%) (Fig. 4b).

No genus was shared across all five root samples. The most frequently shared genera were: Acidothermus (present in Sete-cascas and Jurema-preta), Mycobacterium (present in Sapium and Jurema-preta), and Streptomyces (present in Sapium, Umburana, and Jurema-preta; absent only from Sete-cascas). When Jurema-preta was excluded from the analysis, Bacillus was shared among Sapium, Sete-cascas, and Umburana.

In the rhizosphere, Bacillus was present in all 10 plant species (Fig. 4c). All soil samples also contained Bacillus. The genera shared across all samples in both soil and rhizosphere were Bacillus and an unclassified Nitrososphaeraceae member. The Bacillus abundance pattern was consistent across plants: low relative abundance in soil and high relative abundance in rhizosphere. The Nitrososphaeraceae pattern varied by plant species. In most species (Angico, Baraúna, Faveleira, Maniçoba, Sapium, and Umburana), rhizosphere abundance was slightly lower than soil abundance, with both compartments showing similar values. In Caatingueira, both compartments showed higher relative abundance, with rhizosphere exceeding soil. In Sete-cascas, Nitrososphaeraceae showed low soil abundance but higher rhizosphere abundance (Fig. 5a).

Unique taxa per plant species (not discriminating by compartment) were calculated as total counts for each taxon for a given plant divided by all reads from that plant. Only Umburana had no unique genus. Unique taxa never represented more than 5% of any plant’s total taxa (Fig. 5). For Maniçoba and Sapium, unique genera were predominantly found in roots (Fig. 5b and c). For Sete-cascas and Jurema-preta, unique genera were predominantly found in rhizosphere and soil (Fig. 5d and e).

We also checked the unique taxa for each plant. For this calculation, we did not discriminate between the compartments from which the taxa were from. The relative abundance was calculated as total counts for each taxon for a given plant divided by all reads from said plant. Only Umburana did not have any unique genus. Interestingly Sete-cascas and Jurema-preta were also the two plants that had a different pattern for presence of shared taxa in soil and rhizosphere (Fig. 5a).

Functional profiling reveals plant-specific metabolic signatures

The KEGG ortholog (KO) functional profile calculated from 16 S rRNA amplicon data using Tax4Fun2 is shown in Fig. 6a (all compartments combined) and Fig. 6b–d (separated by soil, roots, and rhizosphere respectively). Data were filtered to include root samples with more than 5,000 reads after filtering (Maniçoba, Sete-cascas, Jurema-preta, Sapium, and Umburana).

When all compartments were analyzed together (Fig. 6a), Jurema-preta showed enrichment of KOs involved in wax biosynthesis, long-chain lipid synthesis, polyketide/NRPS synthesis, fatty acid catabolism, and kinase signaling. Umbuzeiro showed a distinct enrichment profile dominated by transposase-related KOs, chemotaxis pathways, porin and transporter genes, and PKS/NRPS pathways.

Discussion

Microbial diversity and compartmentalization

Our investigation into the microbial communities associated with plants from the Caatinga biome highlighted significant distinctions and overlaps among the soil, rhizosphere, and root microbiomes. The rhizosphere and soil compartments exhibited higher microbial diversity and a greater degree of taxonomic overlap compared to the root microbiome, which showcased a more specialized and distinct microbial profile.

The rarefaction analysis revealed that soil and rhizosphere samples harbor richer microbial communities than roots. The absence of a plateau in the rarefaction curves suggests that additional sequencing could uncover more diversity, especially in the root-associated microbiota. This supports the rhizosphere as a microbial hotspot due to its dynamic interface between plant roots and soil.

The progressive decline in microbial diversity from bulk soil to rhizosphere to root endosphere is consistent with the “ecological filtering” model, in which the plant root system acts as a multi-stage selective barrier that progressively narrows microbial community membership34,36. In this model, the bulk soil represents the regional species pool from which rhizosphere communities are recruited through root exudate-mediated chemotaxis35. The further reduction in diversity within the root endosphere reflects a more stringent selection imposed by plant innate immunity and the physicochemical conditions of the apoplastic space, which together permit colonization by only a subset of rhizosphere-competent taxa71.

In the context of the Caatinga, this filtering is likely intensified by the polyextreme conditions of the semi-arid environment, where water scarcity, high UV radiation, and nutrient-poor soils impose additional abiotic filters that constrain the microbial pool available for root colonization72,73. The low and erratic precipitation characteristic of the Caatinga (< 800 mm/year) creates periodic soil desiccation events that favor drought-tolerant taxa such as spore-forming Bacilli and desiccation-resistant Actinobacteria, which may explain their dominance in the rhizosphere and root compartments respectively4,31.

Further analysis using OTU-based Venn diagrams indicated a substantial overlap between soil and rhizosphere, but much less overlap with roots, reinforcing the concept of the rhizosphere as a transitional zone. Taxonomic profiling showed the soil microbiome with an even distribution of microbial classes, while the rhizosphere and root microbiomes were dominated by specific taxa. Bacilli were enriched in the rhizosphere, likely due to their roles in plant growth promotion, whereas Alphaproteobacteria and Actinobacteria dominated the roots, consistent with their symbiotic functions. This pattern of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria dominance in semi-arid soils is not unique to the Caatinga; a similar community structure has been reported in the semi-arid soils of northeastern Nigeria, where these phyla were also the most abundant groups, alongside a surprisingly high diversity of rare species77. This convergence across geographically distant drylands suggests that semi-arid conditions globally select for a common set of dominant stress-tolerant phyla, while simultaneously harboring region-specific rare biosphere components that may reflect local ecological and biogeographic factors.

Core and compartment-specific microbiomes

Seven microbial classes were consistently present across soil, rhizosphere, and roots, suggesting a core microbiome spanning the soil-plant continuum. However, distinctive taxa emerged in each compartment. The Bray-Curtis NMDS analysis visually captured compositional dissimilarities, supporting the concept of microbial filtering with the rhizosphere as a selective interface.

The persistence of seven shared classes across all three compartments suggests a functionally conserved core microbiome that may be essential for maintaining plant-soil feedbacks under the stressful conditions of the Caatinga35. The ecological significance of this core is likely related to fundamental biogeochemical functions: Acidobacteriia are known oligotrophs that thrive in nutrient-poor, acidic soils and contribute to the slow decomposition of recalcitrant organic matter31,74; Bacilli produce endospores that confer exceptional resistance to desiccation and heat, traits that are critically advantageous in the Caatinga’s seasonally extreme environment4; and Actinobacteria are prolific producers of secondary metabolites including antibiotics and siderophores that may protect plants from soil-borne pathogens while facilitating iron acquisition in alkaline dryland soils35,36.

The presence of Oxyphotobacteria exclusively in the root compartment is noteworthy. While typically associated with phototrophic environments, endophytic cyanobacteria have been documented in dryland plants where they may contribute to nitrogen fixation within the root interior, providing a direct source of bioavailable nitrogen to the host plant, a function of particular importance in the nitrogen-limited soils of the Caatinga73. The occurrence of Deltaproteobacteria exclusively in roots may reflect the microaerophilic or anaerobic conditions within the root endosphere, which favor sulfate-reducing and syntrophic bacteria involved in interspecies electron transfer34. These compartment-specific taxa likely represent niche specialists whose presence is determined by the distinct physicochemical gradients (oxygen tension, pH, carbon availability) that exist along the soil-root continuum.

Plant-specific microbial patterns

Microbial diversity and composition were strongly influenced by plant identity. The alpha diversity metrics confirmed compartment-dependent variation among plant species. While soil samples had the highest richness, roots showed the lowest, illustrating a strong filtering effect. The rhizosphere displayed intermediate diversity, with plant-specific variations.

The strong influence of plant species identity on microbial community composition, particularly in the root compartment, likely reflects the combined action of multiple biological drivers. First, root architectural and morphological traits vary considerably among the ten studied species and are known to shape rhizosphere microbial assembly. For example, Spondias tuberosa (Umbuzeiro) possesses specialized water-storing xylopodia, tuberous root structures that create a unique microenvironment with higher moisture content and distinct carbon inputs compared to the fibrous root systems of species like Mimosa tenuiflora18. These structural differences likely generate distinct ecological niches that favor different microbial colonizers. Second, root exudate chemistry, which is genetically determined and species-specific, is a primary driver of rhizosphere microbial recruitment34,35. Leguminous species in our study (Mimosa tenuiflora, Anadenanthera colubrina, Caesalpinia pyramidalis) belong to the Fabaceae and are known to exude flavonoids and isoflavonoids that specifically recruit nitrogen-fixing rhizobia and mycorrhizal fungi36. This may explain the distinct microbial signatures observed in these species compared to non-leguminous taxa. Third, plant phylogenetic relatedness may partially explain the observed patterns, as closely related plant species tend to harbor more similar microbiomes due to conserved root traits, immune system components, and exudate profiles which is a phenomenon termed “phylosymbiosis". The fact that the three Fabaceae species in our study showed partially convergent microbial patterns supports this hypothesis, though a formal phylogenetic analysis would be required to test it rigorously.

Taxonomic composition highlighted Bacilli as most prevalent in the rhizosphere and Actinobacteria in roots and soil. Plant-specific taxonomic signatures emerged, with some plants exhibiting unique microbial profiles. The observation that plants lacking high Bacilli abundance in roots instead showed high Actinobacteria abundance suggests potential competitive exclusion or niche partitioning between these two functional groups within the root endosphere. Both groups produce antimicrobial secondary metabolites and compete for similar carbon sources, and their reciprocal distribution pattern may reflect resource-mediated competition within the spatially constrained root environment31. This pattern has important implications for understanding how plant species in the Caatinga may assemble functionally redundant but taxonomically distinct microbial partnerships to achieve similar ecological outcomes, a form of functional convergence driven by environmental filtering.

Root microbiome specialization

Root microbiomes were highly individualized across plant species, as revealed by NMDS ordination and genus-level analyses. The striking individualization of root microbiomes across the ten Caatinga species, to the point where no single genus was universally shared, is remarkable and distinguishes this system from studies in mesic environments, where core root endophytic taxa are commonly identified across plant species35,75. We propose that this extreme specificity reflects the heightened selective pressures of the semi-arid environment, where the ecological costs of maintaining microbial partnerships are amplified by resource scarcity. Under these conditions, each plant species may have evolved highly specific recruitment strategies to select only those microorganisms that provide the greatest fitness benefit. The dominance of Mycobacterium and Acidothermus in the roots of Jurema-preta is particularly intriguing. Both genera are thermotolerant Actinobacteria known for their ability to degrade complex polymers including cellulose and lignin74. Their enrichment in Jurema-preta, a pioneer species known for rapid colonization of degraded areas, suggests a functional partnership in which these microbes may facilitate nutrient mobilization from recalcitrant organic matter, supporting the plant’s aggressive colonization strategy. Similarly, the prominence of Bradyrhizobium in Sapium and Maniçoba roots, despite these species not being legumes, suggests potential non-canonical associations for nitrogen acquisition that merit further investigation through metatranscriptomic validation. The presence of Streptomyces across multiple root samples (Sapium, Umburana, and Jurema-preta) indicates a potential core beneficial genus in Caatinga root communities. Streptomyces species are well-known producers of antibiotics and antifungal compounds, and their recruitment into the root endosphere may serve a bioprotective function against soil-borne pathogens, a role of heightened importance in dryland environments where plants cannot afford to lose biomass to disease during the limited growing season35,36.

Rhizosphere as a microbial gateway

The rhizosphere acts as a microbial gateway, filtering and enriching taxa. Bacillus, universally present in the rhizosphere, plays a key role in plant-microbe interactions. The variability in shared genera between roots and rhizosphere underscores differences in microbial filtering and recruitment among plants.

The universal enrichment of Bacillus in the rhizosphere across all ten plant species represents a compelling case of convergent microbial recruitment under environmental stress. Bacillus species are well-documented plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) that enhance plant drought tolerance through multiple mechanisms: production of exopolysaccharides that improve soil aggregate stability and water retention; synthesis of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and other phytohormones that stimulate root elongation; and the production of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, which reduces stress-induced ethylene levels in the plant42,76. In the water-limited Caatinga environment, these functions would confer substantial fitness advantages to the host plant, creating strong selective pressure for Bacillus enrichment regardless of plant identity. The differential pattern of Nitrososphaeraceae (ammonia-oxidizing archaea) between compartments is ecologically significant. Their generally higher abundance in bulk soil compared to rhizosphere likely reflects the “rhizosphere priming effect,” whereby enhanced carbon availability from root exudates shifts the microbial community from autotrophic nitrifiers toward heterotrophic competitors34. The exception observed in Sete-cascas, where Nitrososphaeraceae was enriched in the rhizosphere, may indicate species-specific differences in exudate chemistry or root oxygen release patterns that create distinct niches for ammonia-oxidizing archaea. This finding has implications for understanding localized nitrogen cycling dynamics around individual Caatinga plant species and may influence soil nitrogen availability at the scale of individual plant canopies—the “fertile island” effect that is characteristic of dryland ecosystems4.

Functional implications: drought adaptation and ecosystem processes

The functional metagenomic profiles provide direct insight into how microbial communities may support plant survival under the extreme conditions of the Caatinga. The enrichment of wax biosynthesis and long-chain lipid synthesis pathways in the Jurema-preta microbiome is particularly relevant to drought adaptation. Microbial production of lipids and waxes can contribute to biofilm formation on root surfaces, creating a hydrophobic barrier that reduces water loss from the rhizosphere, a critical function during the prolonged dry periods (6–8 months) that characterize the Caatinga climate48. Furthermore, polyketide and non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (PKS/NRPS) pathways enriched in Jurema-preta are associated with the production of antimicrobial compounds and siderophores, suggesting that this pioneer species maintains a microbiome equipped for both chemical defense and enhanced nutrient scavenging in degraded soils35.

The contrasting functional profile of Umbuzeiro, enriched in transposase-related functions and chemotaxis/motility pathways, suggests a fundamentally different microbiome strategy. The high frequency of mobile genetic elements (transposons) may indicate a microbiome under strong selective pressure for genomic flexibility, enabling rapid adaptation to the fluctuating conditions within the xylopodium31. The enrichment of chemotaxis and transporter pathways is consistent with a microbial community optimized for rapid nutrient uptake during the brief pulses of water availability that characterize the Caatinga’s “boom-bust” precipitation regime.

This functional dichotomy between Jurema-preta (defense/conservation strategy) and Umbuzeiro (plasticity/opportunistic strategy) mirrors the broader ecological strategies of these plants (Jurema-preta as a drought-tolerant pioneer and Umbuzeiro as a drought-avoiding species with water-storage organs) suggesting that plant life-history strategy may shape the functional assembly of associated microbiomes. This observation raises the hypothesis that the microbiome functional profile of a Caatinga plant species can be predicted from its ecophysiological strategy, a pattern that, if confirmed across more species, would have significant implications for microbiome-informed restoration planning.

The pattern of nitrogen cycling pathways in the long-read metagenomic data from rhizosphere pools, particularly nitrate assimilatory and dissimilatory reduction, nitrite assimilation, and denitrification, has profound implications for ecosystem-level nutrient dynamics in the Caatinga. Nitrogen is frequently the limiting nutrient in dryland ecosystems, and the capacity for dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) is particularly significant because it retains nitrogen within the soil system rather than losing it as gaseous N₂ through denitrification34. The relatively high abundance of DNRA-related functions in both pools suggests that the Caatinga rhizosphere microbiome may play a critical role in nitrogen conservation, a function of paramount importance in an ecosystem where nitrogen inputs are limited by low rainfall and sparse biological nitrogen fixation43. The presence of urease pathway functions at high abundance further supports the importance of nitrogen recycling, as urea hydrolysis represents a major pathway for ammonium regeneration from organic nitrogen sources in nutrient-poor soils.

Future directions

Collectively, our findings reveal that the Caatinga harbors a highly structured and ecologically coherent microbiome system in which microbial community assembly is governed by the interaction of environmental filtering (semi-arid conditions), compartment-specific selection (soil → rhizosphere → root), and host plant identity. The convergent enrichment of stress-tolerant, plant-beneficial taxa such as Bacillus across all rhizospheres, combined with the highly individualized root microbiomes, suggests a two-tier assembly model: a conserved, environmentally driven “first filter” that selects drought-adapted generalists in the rhizosphere, followed by a plant-specific “second filter” that recruits specialized endophytes into the root interior.

These patterns have direct implications for restoration ecology in the Caatinga, as they suggest that successful reforestation efforts must consider not only plant species selection but also the microbial partnerships upon which these plants depend22. The highly individualized root microbiomes observed here imply that microbial inoculants for restoration should be species-specific rather than generic, tailored to match the native microbial partners of each target plant species.

The biotechnological potential of these findings is considerable: the drought-adapted Bacillus strains universally recruited to the Caatinga rhizosphere represent prime candidates for bioinoculant development for dryland agriculture worldwide. Similarly, the Streptomyces strains found across multiple root endospheres may harbor novel bioactive compounds with applications in crop protection.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. The lack of plateau in rarefaction curves indicates that deeper sequencing would capture additional diversity, particularly in the root compartment. The low read count for Baraúna roots limits inferences for this species. Additionally, the functional predictions from Tax4Fun2 are based on 16 S amplicon data and should be validated through direct shotgun metagenomics or metatranscriptomics of individual samples. Future research integrating metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, and controlled inoculation experiments will be essential to validate the functional roles inferred here and to translate these ecological insights into applied tools for sustainable land management in the world’s expanding drylands. Soil physicochemical characterization (pH, nutrient content, moisture) paired with root trait measurements across species would allow formal testing of the drivers we have proposed, including the relative contributions of soil properties, root traits, and plant phylogeny to microbiome assembly in this unique biome.

Methods

Collection of samples

A total of 10 samples were meticulously collected, with distribution across the Caatinga. Plants for this study were identified in the field by Caatinga researchers Diana Sigmor and Magna Soelma Beserra de Moura. To collect the samples, the upper part of the plant was cut and the soil around the plant was drilled to an approximate depth of 30 cm, cutting and removing the main root, while the secondary and tertiary roots remained with the soil sample. Approximately 200 g of soil was collected and, together with the secondary and tertiary roots, when present, placed in zip lock plastic bags kept on ice and/or refrigerated until shipping. All collection points were photographed, georeferenced by GPS and described based on the characteristics of the local vegetation (Table 1). The collected samples were meticulously divided into soil, roots and rhizosphere components. The soil samples were manually sieved to remove rocks and roots to generate a uniform sample. Then, the rhizosphere was obtained by washing the roots with PBS 1X pH8.3. Finally, the roots were carefully removed, and samples followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min (Table 5).

DNA extraction

The total genomic DNA from the soil and rhizosphere was extracted from 250 mg of each sample using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Plant roots were rinsed in sterile PBS 1x pH 8.3 to remove the rhizosphere and disinfected in a solution of bleach 50% with 0.01% Tween 20 and agitated gently for 3 min to remove remaining contaminants. Then, plant roots were then immersed in 70% ethanol for 2 min, followed by thorough rinsing with sterile water (bleach/ethanol wash was repeated 3x times). Afterwards, the roots were grounded after freezing with liquid nitrogen followed by total genomic DNA extraction using the The DNeasy Plant Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of DNA were determined by measuring the absorbance at 260/280 nm (A260/A280) on the NanoDrop device (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA) and on Invitrogen™ Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer device (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA), respectively. DNA integrity was verified by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. Sample preparation was done according to McPherson et al. (2018)52.

16 S rRNA gene amplicon library and sequence data analysis

For each area, we used the following 16s primer pair to amplify and sequence this region: Primers to amplify the 16 S V4 region:

515 F GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and 806R GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT.

The amplifications and amplicon-sequencing were performed at JCVI.

Shotgun sequencing and data processing

For all areas, samples were normalized and pooled. All root, rhizosphere and soil samples for any given area were merged into a single sample. We ran samples on a gel to check for degradation (Supplemental Fig. 1). 50 ng of each pool was sequenced using Illumina shotgun at Novogene.

For the shotgun sequencing data, samples were processed and cleaned by the sequencing company Novogene. Cleaned sequencing files were paired and a library was made for each biome. These libraries were used to classify the microbes using Kaiju through the standard settings in the KBase platform53.

For the amplicon-sequencing data, the DADA2 pipeline was utilized to process the clean reads54, leading to the formation of operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Taxonomic classification of each representative read and OTU was carried out using the ribosomal database project (RDP) classifier within the SILVA database for bacterial species (with a confidence level of 70%) and the UNITE database for fungal species55,56. The analysis of OTUs included their relative abundance at both the genus and phylum levels. Analysis was done using the Phyloseq package57.

PromethION nanopore sequencing

Rhizosphere samples from the 10 plants were quantified using Qbit. 5 ng of each sample were mixed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and the final volume was quantified again. Metagenomic libraries were prepared using an Oxford Nanopore ligation-based native DNA library preparation workflow compatible with PromethION sequencing. Basecalling was performed using Oxford Nanopore’s Dorado basecaller.

Long-read assembly and binning

Long-read metagenomic reads were processed using the VEBA pipeline, a reproducible framework for end-to-end metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analysis58. Within VEBA, reads were quality controlled and assembled using a long-read assembly workflow, after which contig abundance information was used for genome binning and bin refinement to recover MAGs58,59. Genome quality assessment and downstream annotation were performed using VEBA-integrated tools following the recommended workflow58.
