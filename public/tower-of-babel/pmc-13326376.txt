Nostalgia proneness and acceptance of AI-designed cultural products: the mediating role of perceived authenticity and the moderating role of need for uniqueness
Authors: Hua Jia, Pinmeng Zhou, YongLi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence is increasingly used to design cultural and creative products, yet consumer acceptance varies considerably. Personality traits may play a key role in shaping these responses, but research has not examined how nostalgia proneness — an individual’s dispositional tendency to long for the past — influences attitudes toward AI-designed cultural products. This study investigated whether nostalgia proneness predicts lower acceptance of such products through reduced perceived authenticity, and whether need for uniqueness moderates this indirect pathway A cross-sectional survey was conducted with 291 Chinese university students. Participants viewed images of museum cultural products explicitly labeled as AI-designed and completed measures of nostalgia proneness (Southampton Nostalgia Scale, 7 items), perceived authenticity (8 items), need for uniqueness (8 items), and acceptance of AI-designed cultural products (5 items). A moderated mediation model (PROCESS Model 14) was tested using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals with 5,000 resamples. Nostalgia proneness negatively predicted acceptance of AI-designed cultural products (B = − 0.29, p < 0.001). Perceived authenticity partially mediated this relationship; the indirect effect was significant (ab = − 0.129, 95% CI [− 0.198, − 0.071]), accounting for 44.6% of the total effect. Need for uniqueness moderated the second stage of the mediation path: the positive effect of perceived authenticity on acceptance was stronger among individuals with low need for uniqueness (B = 0.50) than among those with high need for uniqueness (B = 0.22). The index of moderated mediation was significant (index = 0.061, 95% CI [0.013, 0.124]), confirming that the negative indirect effect was attenuated as need for uniqueness increased. Consumers higher in nostalgia proneness are less accepting of AI-designed cultural products, largely because they perceive such products as less authentic. However, this negative pathway is attenuated among consumers with a stronger need for uniqueness, who are less reliant on authenticity judgments when evaluating AI-designed offerings. These findings extend nostalgia psychology to the domain of AI acceptance and provide actionable insights for culturally sensitive AI product marketing strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04683-3.Keywords: Nostalgia proneness, Perceived authenticity, Need for uniqueness, AI-designed products, Cultural products, Consumer acceptance, Moderated mediation

Introduction

Research background and problem statement

Generative artificial intelligence technology is profoundly transforming the design process of cultural and creative products. Cultural products with symbolic significance—such as museum-branded merchandise and derivatives featuring traditional patterns—are increasingly relying on AI tools for design scheme generation and optimization [1]. Meanwhile, consumer attitudes toward such products exhibit significant individual differences [2], which raises a critical question: What psychological factors drive consumers’ divergent responses to AI-designed cultural products?

From a psychological perspective on technology acceptance behavior, De Freitas et al. [2] identified five core barriers: opacity, lack of emotionality, rigidity, threat to autonomy, and group identity attribution. However, this framework focuses on the general attributes of AI tools and has not yet addressed how users’ own personality traits shape their evaluative tendencies. The value of cultural products is closely tied to imagery such as “tradition,” “craftsmanship,” and “historical memory,” and consumer acceptance involves not only functional judgments but also deep emotional identification [3].

Nostalgia proneness reflects the degree of an individual’s emotional attachment to past experiences and traditional things [4]. Dang et al.’s comprehensive program of research revealed dual pathways through which nostalgia influences attitudes toward innovative technology: nostalgia both inhibits technology support through change-skepticism and promotes technology embrace through a sense of social connectedness [5]. The same team further found that nostalgia stimulates AI exploration while concurrently shaping how individuals respond to uncertainty in the AI domain [6, 7]. However, these studies focused on the effects of nostalgic emotional states and did not examine the role of nostalgia proneness as a stable personality trait in the context of AI-designed cultural products.

Lee and Kim experimentally demonstrated that consumers’ negative responses to AI-designed products are driven by a reduction in perceived authenticity [3]. Brüns and Meißner also found that content created by generative AI significantly undermines perceived brand authenticity [8]. This evidence points to a judgment: perceived authenticity may be the key psychological bridge connecting consumer personality traits with AI product acceptance.

Not all consumers hold reservations about AI involvement. Individuals with a higher need for uniqueness are more inclined to adopt novel, unconventional experiences [2]. When AI is used to promote innovative products, consumer resistance is notably reduced [9]. Consumers with a high need for uniqueness may be attracted to the non-traditional designs created by AI, thereby attenuating the negative impact of insufficient perceived authenticity—yet this hypothesis lacks empirical validation.

In summary, the present study focuses on: How does nostalgia proneness influence consumer acceptance of AI-designed cultural products through perceived authenticity? Does need for uniqueness moderate this process?

Literature review and theoretical foundations

Nostalgia proneness and technology attitudes.Nostalgia is a sentimental longing for one’s personally meaningful past, and nostalgia proneness measures the frequency and intensity with which individuals experience nostalgic emotions [4, 6] Psychological research has established nostalgia’s multiple functions: enhancing self-esteem, conferring a sense of meaning in life, strengthening authenticity experiences, and reinforcing social connectedness [10, 11].

Dang et al.‘s [5] dual-pathway model revealed that nostalgia reduces support for innovative technologies such as AI by activating change-skeptical attitudes, while simultaneously promoting open attitudes through social connectedness. In the context of cultural products, AI replacing the human role in creation may be perceived by individuals high in nostalgia proneness as a rupture with cultural traditions. The team’s subsequent research on AI clarified this picture: nostalgia stimulates exploratory engagement with AI while shaping how individuals tolerate and process uncertainty in the AI domain rather than uniformly amplifying uncertainty perception [7], which is particularly noteworthy in the subjective value assessment of cultural products [12].

Existing research has predominantly taken experimental manipulation of nostalgic emotional states as the entry point [5, 7], revealing the causal effects of emotional states but unable to answer a more practically valuable question: Do consumers who are naturally inclined toward nostalgia in daily life systematically exhibit lower acceptance when confronted with AI-designed cultural products? This constitutes the first theoretical gap of the present study.

It is important to acknowledge that state and trait nostalgia are conceptually distinct, and evidence drawn from experimentally induced nostalgia does not automatically generalize to dispositional nostalgia proneness. We treat the link between the two as theoretical rather than equivalent, and ground it in three considerations. First, trait nostalgia is defined as the frequency and intensity with which individuals spontaneously experience nostalgic states [13], so individuals high in this trait are more likely to enter nostalgic states when encountering identity-relevant cues such as cultural products. Second, repeated activation of state nostalgia in daily life can consolidate into stable cognitive schemas, including a heightened sensitivity to authenticity [10]. Third, the developmental trajectory of trait nostalgia further supports this position [14]. Together, these considerations justify the expectation that trait-level nostalgia proneness should produce systematic, but not necessarily identical, differences in attitudes toward AI-designed cultural products. The present study is therefore positioned as an initial trait-level test of mechanisms originally identified at the state level, rather than a confirmatory replication.

Nostalgia proneness should also be distinguished from neighboring constructs that may seem to predict similar reactions to AI-designed cultural products, including traditionalism, essentialist beliefs about authorship, cultural conservatism, and general technology skepticism. Nostalgia proneness is fundamentally an affective disposition, oriented toward the emotional re-experiencing of personally meaningful pasts, whereas traditionalism and essentialism are cognitive endorsements of what culture or art ought to be, and technology skepticism is a domain-general distrust of new tools. These constructs are likely to covary, but they are not interchangeable. The present study focuses on nostalgia proneness because emotional attachment to the past is theoretically the most direct candidate for shaping authenticity perceptions of cultural products, while we acknowledge in the limitations that future research should incorporate these neighboring constructs as covariates to isolate the unique contribution of nostalgia proneness.

Perceived authenticity: A psychological bridge from personality traits to product evaluation. Authenticity is a multifaceted construct that encompasses product genuineness, fidelity to cultural tradition, sincerity of the design process, and broader symbolic legitimacy. The present study focuses primarily on perceived product-cultural authenticity, defined as the integrative judgment of whether a cultural product faithfully embodies its underlying cultural traditions, rather than on brand-level authenticity or designer-process authenticity in isolation. We retain this integrative framing because cultural products are typically evaluated as cultural wholes, but we acknowledge that future research could productively decompose authenticity into its sub-dimensions [15]. Sedikides and Schlegel’s systematic review of the concept of authenticity noted that authenticity involves both the consistency between individual behavior and the “true self” and judgments about the “genuineness” and “credibility” of external objects [16, 17]. Kelley et al.‘s cross-cultural study demonstrated that nostalgic experiences enhance individuals’ sense of authenticity, implying that individuals high in nostalgia proneness may possess heightened sensitivity to authenticity [10].

Lee and Kim confirmed that consumers’ negative responses to AI-designed products are driven by reduced authenticity [3]; Brüns and Meißner found that negative effects can be mitigated when AI is positioned as “assisting humans” rather than “replacing humans” [8]; Kim et al. also showed that the core challenge for AI-designed souvenirs lies in authenticity questioning [18]. Based on this, the present study proposes that consumers with higher nostalgia proneness are more likely to perceive a deficit in authenticity, which in turn inhibits their acceptance. Perceived authenticity is positioned as the mediating variable connecting the two.

Three reasons motivate our focus on perceived authenticity as the central mechanism, while we recognize that other psychological pathways may operate in parallel. The first is theoretical specificity: nostalgia psychology has long linked nostalgic experiences to a heightened sensitivity to authenticity, including the genuineness of objects and connections to meaningful pasts. The second is empirical consolidation: a growing body of work has converged on AI labeling as a systematic depressor of perceived authenticity in branded and creative contexts, providing measurement traditions that translate well to cultural products. The third is judgments of mind. Authenticity perceptions for cultural artifacts are partly anchored in inferences about the creator’s mind — perceived intentionality, sincerity, and human craftsmanship — and AI is widely judged to lack such mind-related properties, which can disproportionately reduce authenticity perceptions among consumers who care about cultural transmission. We therefore situate authenticity at the heart of our model while noting that nostalgia proneness may also relate to AI cultural products through symbolic threat, perceived rupture with heritage, and identity-relevant interpretations of what AI represents [19]. The mind-perception perspective has been articulated in early theoretical work [20], and the role of consumer co-creation in restoring valuation has been demonstrated in the IKEA-effect tradition [21]. We treat these alternative pathways as plausible co-mechanisms whose disentangling is a task for future research.

Need for uniqueness: A boundary condition for the buffering effect. Individuals with a high need for uniqueness prefer non-mainstream products in consumption. De Freitas et al. pointed out that the degree of openness to novelty moderates AI acceptance levels [2]. When AI promotes innovative products, consumers’ negative bias is reduced [9], suggesting that individuals with a high need for uniqueness may more readily accept AI creations. The present study positions need for uniqueness as a boundary condition moderating the relationship between perceived authenticity and AI product acceptance. Two theoretical considerations support placing this moderator on the second-stage path rather than positing a uniform main effect. First, individuals high in need for uniqueness rely more on perceived distinctiveness and self-expressive value when evaluating consumer offerings, which shifts their evaluative criteria away from traditional authenticity toward novelty-laden judgments. Second, this shift is best understood as a reduced reliance on authenticity rather than as an active embrace of AI; the prediction is that authenticity becomes a less binding criterion for these consumers, not that they intrinsically prefer AI-generated designs. This distinction matters because it specifies what the moderator should and should not do empirically. The expected pattern is a flatter authenticity-to-acceptance slope at higher levels of need for uniqueness, rather than a higher intercept overall, and the present study uses this prediction to derive a falsifiable second-stage moderation hypothesis.

Conceptual model

The present study is expected to make contributions in three areas: introducing nostalgia proneness into the AI product acceptance framework [5, 7]; revealing the mediating mechanism of perceived authenticity [3, 16]; and identifying the boundary buffering effect of need for uniqueness [2]. At the practical level, it provides differentiated user strategies for the cultural industry—emphasizing “human-AI collaboration” for consumers with high nostalgia proneness and highlighting “AI-driven innovative design” for consumers with a high need for uniqueness.

Methods

Research design and participants

The present study adopted a cross-sectional survey design and collected data through an online platform. Cross-sectional designs are a commonly used paradigm in consumer psychology and social psychology research for examining relationships between personality traits and attitudinal variables, particularly suitable for testing mediation and moderation effects with continuous variables at their core [22]. The study used nostalgia proneness as the independent variable (continuous variable), perceived authenticity as the mediating variable, need for uniqueness as the moderating variable, and acceptance of AI-designed cultural products as the dependent variable, with gender, age, academic background (design-related/non-design-related), and AI usage experience as control variables. The overall research procedure is shown in Fig. 2.

Research procedure flowchart

Sample size determination was based on Xu et al.‘s simulation study on statistical power analysis and sample size planning for moderated mediation models [23]. That study indicated that for detecting medium-sized moderation and moderated mediation effects (α = 0.05, power = 0.80), a minimum sample of approximately 200 is required when using the bias-corrected bootstrap method. Fossum and Montoya further confirmed through Monte Carlo simulations that the percentile bootstrap method provides robust power estimation for between-subjects mediation under various conditions [24]. Considering that the present study includes both mediation and moderation effect testing and the possibility of invalid questionnaires, the target sample size was ultimately set at 300 to ensure sufficient statistical power.

Participants were undergraduate students enrolled at universities in Hubei Province, China, and questionnaires were distributed and collected through the Credamo online survey platform. The university student population represents an important consumer force for cultural and creative products, possesses basic awareness of AI technology, and has formed relatively stable preference patterns in cultural product consumption [25]. The Credamo platform was selected because it has mature data quality control mechanisms and is widely used in Chinese social science survey research. In the questionnaire design, the present study drew on existing online survey paradigms from research on the interaction between new technology and nostalgic emotions [26]. Each participant who completed the entire questionnaire received appropriate compensation.

Stimulus materials and procedure

A standardized product introduction scenario was presented at the beginning of the questionnaire. All participants read the same scenario description text to ensure consistency of stimulus materials. The core content of the scenario description was: “The following is a newly launched cultural and creative product from a museum. Its design inspiration is derived from traditional Chinese cultural patterns. The product’s design scheme was generated by an AI intelligent design system. Please view the following product images and then answer the questions.” This text was accompanied by actual publicly available images of museum cultural and creative products (sourced from promotional materials of well-known domestic museums that had already been released), enabling participants to respond based on a concrete visual product experience.

The use of an explicit AI labeling strategy was an important design choice in this study. Existing research has shown that after being informed that a product was created by AI, consumers’ evaluations of product authenticity and quality change significantly [27], whereas when AI involvement is not disclosed, consumers may have difficulty distinguishing between AI and human creations [28]. The core concern of this study is to examine psychological response differences among consumers after they are aware of the AI design identity, rather than to compare differences between aware and unaware conditions; therefore, all participants were informed that the product was designed by AI. A schematic of the stimulus presentation interface is shown in Fig. 3.

Stimulus presentation interface

The questionnaire completion order was specifically designed: demographic information and AI usage experience first, followed by the nostalgia proneness scale, perceived authenticity scale, need for uniqueness scale, and AI-designed cultural product acceptance scale, in that sequence. Placing the independent variable scale before the mediator and dependent variable scales is a common practice for reducing cognitive bias regarding the direction of causal inference [29]. Placing control variables at the beginning helps mitigate fatigue effects when participants respond to the core scales. An attention check item was embedded in the questionnaire (“Please select ‘Strongly agree’ for this item”) to screen out samples that were not answered carefully.

Measurement instruments

All scales used in this study were derived from published English-language literature and converted to Chinese versions through a standard translation–back-translation procedure following the guidelines recommended by Cruchinho et al. [30]. The translation work was independently completed by two bilingual researchers with psychology backgrounds for the forward translation and back-translation, and the final wording was confirmed through research team discussion. Prior to formal administration, a pretest was conducted with 30 university students to evaluate the semantic clarity of the items and the reasonableness of the completion time. Summary of measurement instruments

Nostalgia proneness was measured using the Southampton Nostalgia Scale (SNS). Wildschut et al. [13] systematically reviewed and validated four trait nostalgia scales and recommended the SNS as the most psychometrically robust measure; its unidimensional structure has been confirmed through multi-group confirmatory factor analysis across both U.S. and Chinese cultural samples. This scale contains 7 items that ask participants to evaluate the frequency and importance of their nostalgic emotional experiences after reading a definition of nostalgia (“a sentimental longing for the past”) (e.g., “How often do you experience feelings of nostalgia?” “How important is nostalgia to you?“), using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all/very unimportant, 7 = very frequently/very important). The SNS is the most widely used trait nostalgia measurement tool in the psychology of nostalgia [14].

Perceived authenticity was measured using the brand perceived authenticity scale, which contains four dimensions—continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbolism—comprising 8 items in total (e.g., “This product makes me feel authentic and credible,” “This product is faithful to its cultural traditions”). Rodrigues et al. [31] recently validated this multidimensional scale across Japanese, Indian, and Portuguese samples, confirming its cross-cultural robustness. In this study, item wording was adaptively adjusted to fit the cultural product evaluation context. Scoring used a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). This scale is widely cited in brand authenticity and consumer behavior research and demonstrates good cross-context applicability [32]. Three points clarify how the scale was adapted for AI-designed cultural products. First, all eight items retained their original four-dimensional structure (continuity, credibility, integrity, symbolism) and the original response anchors. Second, the referent was redirected from a brand entity to a cultural product, so phrases such as “this brand” were rewritten as “this product”, while preserving the underlying construct meaning of each item. For example, the continuity item “This brand stays true to its origins” was rewritten as “This product stays true to the cultural origins it represents”; the symbolism item “This brand reflects important values” was rewritten as “This product reflects important cultural values”. Third, the adapted set was screened in a 30-participant pretest for semantic clarity and cultural relevance, and the formal sample yielded Cronbach’s α = 0.91, with all standardized factor loadings between 0.65 and 0.86, supporting the construct validity of the adapted measure for product-level cultural authenticity.

Need for uniqueness was measured using the short form of the Consumers’ Need for Uniqueness Scale, containing 8 items that measure individuals’ tendencies to seek differentiation in consumption (e.g., “I often look for unique products or brands to create a distinctive image for myself,” “I like to own things that are different from what others have”), scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Song and Kim [33] recently applied and validated this short-form scale in the context of consumer engagement with social media brand pages, demonstrating its continued psychometric soundness. The reliability and validity of this scale have been confirmed across multiple cross-cultural studies [34]. A note on response-format heterogeneity is in order. The need-for-uniqueness scale retained its original 5-point format, while the remaining instruments retained their original 7-point format, in line with the validated psychometric properties reported by the developers. We elected not to harmonize formats to avoid disturbing established item-response equivalence. We acknowledge that mixing 5-point and 7-point scales within a single questionnaire can in principle introduce minor method variance, and we revisit this issue when discussing the limitations of the present measurement design.

The AI-designed cultural product acceptance scale was adapted from the behavioral intention dimension of the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). Gansser and Reich [35] recently extended the UTAUT framework specifically for artificial intelligence acceptance across multiple application domains, confirming the applicability of its behavioral intention dimension for measuring AI product acceptance. Item wording was adjusted to fit the AI-designed cultural product context, comprising 5 items (e.g., “I am willing to purchase this type of AI-designed cultural and creative product,” “I would recommend AI-designed cultural and creative products to friends”), scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The behavioral intention dimension of the UTAUT model has been adapted by multiple studies for measuring AI product acceptance [36].

Control variables comprised 6 items covering gender (male/female), age (continuous variable), academic background (design-related/non-design-related), whether the participant had used AI creation tools (yes/no), frequency of use (5-point scale), and overall familiarity with AI technology (7-point scale). The complete questionnaire contained approximately 34 items, and pretesting indicated an average completion time of approximately 12 to 15 min, with participant burden within an acceptable range.

Data analysis strategy

The data analysis of this study was conducted in four stages, and the overall analytical framework is shown in Fig. 4.

Data analysis framework

Stage 1: Common method bias testing. Since all variables were collected through the same self-report questionnaire at the same time point, common method bias (CMB) may systematically influence the relationships between variables [37]. As Podsakoff et al. [29] noted in their comprehensive review, CMB remains a pervasive and complex challenge in behavioral research. This study employed Harman’s single-factor test to assess the severity of CMB—all scale items were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis, and if the variance explained by the first factor was below the critical threshold of 40%, common method bias was considered not to constitute a serious threat.

Stage 2: Reliability and validity testing. The internal consistency of each scale was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficients, with α values needing to reach the minimum acceptable level of 0.70 [38]. On this basis, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was employed to test the construct validity of the measurement model. Model fit indices were evaluated according to recommended criteria: χ²/df < 3.00, CFI > 0.90, TLI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.08 [39]. Convergent validity was assessed through composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). CR was calculated as the squared sum of standardized factor loadings divided by the sum of the squared factor loadings and the total measurement error variances, with values required to exceed 0.70. AVE was computed as the mean of the squared standardized factor loadings across all items of a given latent variable, with values required to exceed 0.50, indicating that the latent variable explains more variance in its indicators than measurement error. Discriminant validity was assessed based on best-practice recommendations for structural equation modeling [40]: the square root of the AVE for each latent variable should be greater than the correlation coefficient between that variable and other latent variables. The structure of the CFA measurement model is shown in Fig. 5.

CFA measurement model structure diagram

Stage 3: Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis. The means and standard deviations of all variables were calculated, and a Pearson product-moment correlation matrix was constructed to provide a preliminary examination of the direction and strength of associations among variables. Correlation analysis provides a foundational reference for constructing subsequent regression models.

Stage 4: Hypothesis testing. Hypothesis testing was conducted using the PROCESS macro (v4.2) developed by Hayes [41]. The mediation effect test corresponds to PROCESS Model 4, and the moderated mediation effect test corresponds to PROCESS Model 14.

In Model 4, two regression equations were estimated. The first regressed perceived authenticity (M) on nostalgia proneness (X) to obtain path coefficient a₁. The second regressed AI product acceptance (Y) simultaneously on nostalgia proneness and perceived authenticity, yielding the direct effect c′ and path coefficient b₁. The point estimate of the indirect effect is the product a₁ × b₁, and a 95% confidence interval was constructed using the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples; if the interval does not contain zero, the indirect effect is deemed significant [41].

Results

Common method bias and reliability and validity testing

Sample overview. A total of 312 questionnaires were collected through the Credamo platform. After data cleaning, samples that failed the attention check item (9), samples with completion times under 3 min (7), and samples with extensive repetitive responses (5) were excluded, ultimately yielding 291 valid questionnaires with an effective response rate of 93.3%. The proportion of females in the sample was slightly higher than that of males, a ratio consistent with the gender composition characteristics of the cultural and creative product consumer group. Ages were concentrated in the 21–23 age range, with a mean participant age of 21.4 years (SD = 1.82). Approximately one-quarter of the participants had design-related academic backgrounds, covering areas such as visual communication, environmental design, and product design. Nearly two-thirds of the participants reported having used AI creation tools (e.g., Midjourney, Wenxin Yige), a proportion reflecting the high exposure rate of current university students to AI creation technologies.

Common method bias testing. All 28 core scale items were entered into an unrotated exploratory factor analysis. The results showed that 4 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, consistent with the number of four core constructs involved in this study. The variance explained by the first factor was 23.46%, well below the critical threshold of 40% [29], indicating that common method bias did not constitute a serious threat. The cumulative variance explained by the four factors was 62.37%, suggesting that the four-factor structure adequately captured the information contained in the scale items. Harman’s single-factor test should be regarded as a coarse rather than a definitive diagnostic. The test signals only that no single underlying factor accounts for an overwhelming share of total variance; it does not rule out subtler sources of common method variance such as shared response styles, social desirability, or anchor effects induced by item proximity. We therefore treat the present result as a necessary but not sufficient condition for ruling out CMB, and we revisit the implications of single-source self-report measurement in the limitations section, where we recommend that future studies adopt more diagnostic procedures such as marker variables, unmeasured latent method constructs, or temporally separated data collection.

Confirmatory factor analysis. The overall fit indices for the four-factor measurement model were: χ²/df = 1.78, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.052, SRMR = 0.044. All indices met the criteria for good fit [39]. The standardized factor loadings of each item on its corresponding latent variable ranged from 0.63 to 0.86, all exceeding the minimum acceptable level of 0.50. The mean of AI product acceptance was 4.12 (SD = 1.28), close to the scale midpoint. The mean of need for uniqueness was 3.18 (SD = 0.76), at a moderate level on the 5-point scale.

The correlation analysis results were generally consistent with theoretical expectations. Nostalgia proneness was significantly negatively correlated with perceived authenticity (r = − 0.32, p < 0.001), indicating that participants with stronger nostalgia proneness tended to give lower authenticity evaluations of AI-designed cultural products. Nostalgia proneness was also significantly negatively correlated with AI product acceptance (r = − 0.29, p < 0.001), providing preliminary correlational evidence for Hypothesis H1. Perceived authenticity was significantly positively correlated with AI product acceptance (r = 0.43, p < 0.001), which was the strongest association among the four pairs of variable relationships, suggesting that consumers’ authenticity evaluations of AI products largely influence their acceptance intentions. Need for uniqueness was significantly positively correlated with AI product acceptance (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), indicating that consumers who pursue differentiation exhibited more positive attitudes toward AI-designed products. The correlation coefficients between need for uniqueness and nostalgia proneness (r = − 0.09) and between need for uniqueness and perceived authenticity (r = 0.11) were both not statistically significant, implying that the risk of multicollinearity between need for uniqueness as a moderating variable and the independent and mediating variables was low, which is favorable for subsequent interaction effect testing. This correlation pattern laid the necessary foundation for mediation and moderation effect analyses. The distribution of correlation strengths among variables is presented as a heatmap in Fig. 8.

Synthesizing the above analysis results, all four hypotheses proposed in this study received empirical support from the data. Nostalgia proneness had a significant negative predictive effect on acceptance of AI-designed cultural products (H1 supported); perceived authenticity played a partial mediating role between the two, accounting for approximately 44.6% of the total effect (H2 supported); need for uniqueness significantly attenuated the positive effect of perceived authenticity on AI product acceptance, reducing the degree to which high-need-for-uniqueness consumers’ acceptance depended on authenticity perceptions (H3 supported); and the indirect effect of nostalgia proneness on AI product acceptance through perceived authenticity was significantly moderated by need for uniqueness—the indirect effect decreased from − 0.170 under the low need for uniqueness condition to − 0.075 under the high need for uniqueness condition (H4 supported).

This pattern of results paints a clear psychological picture: nostalgia proneness inhibits acceptance by lowering consumers’ perceptions of AI product authenticity, but the potency of this transmission pathway is not immutable—it varies in strength depending on the level of an individual’s need for uniqueness.

Discussion

Main findings and theoretical interpretation

The present study constructed and tested a moderated mediation model, with all four hypotheses receiving empirical support.

The direct negative effect of nostalgia proneness on AI product acceptance (H1) is consistent with the “skepticism pathway” in Dang et al.‘s [5] dual-pathway model—nostalgia activates skeptical attitudes toward change, which in turn inhibits positive evaluations of innovative technologies. In the context of cultural products, this effect may be further amplified: the value of the products themselves is rooted in “tradition” and “humanistic heritage,” and the intervention of AI as a designer is easily interpreted by individuals high in nostalgia proneness as a replacement of traditional creative methods. Dang et al. [7] further showed that nostalgia shapes how individuals process and tolerate uncertainty in the AI domain, and that such uncertainty processing can translate into a more cautious stance in the value assessment of cultural products.

The partial mediating effect of perceived authenticity (H2) revealed a key psychological mechanism. The indirect effect accounted for 44.6% of the total effect, indicating that perceived authenticity played an important transmitting role. At the individual psychological level, Kelley et al. [10] have demonstrated that nostalgia enhances individuals’ sensitivity to authenticity, and this sensitivity leads individuals high in nostalgia proneness to exhibit lower tolerance for “machine traces” in products. At the product evaluation level, Lee and Kim [3] and Brüns and Meißner [8] have both confirmed that AI labels systematically reduce authenticity perception; the present study further demonstrates that this reduction is particularly pronounced among individuals high in nostalgia proneness. The fact that the direct effect remained significant (c’ = −0.16) means that approximately 55% of the influence cannot be explained by authenticity alone, and potential alternative mediators may include change-skeptical attitudes [5] and insufficient trust in AI [2], among others.

The moderating effect of need for uniqueness (H3) is the most novel finding of this study. Simple slope analysis showed that the perceived authenticity effect for individuals with low need for uniqueness (B = 0.50) was considerably stronger than that for individuals with high need for uniqueness (B = 0.22), indicating that the latter’s acceptance of AI products is driven more by novelty and differentiated experiences rather than being constrained by considerations of “whether it is sufficiently traditional.” De Freitas et al. [2] pointed out that the degree of openness to novelty is an important individual difference factor moderating AI acceptance, and the present study provides specific support for this from the domain of cultural consumption. The moderated mediation effect (H4) further showed that the indirect effect decreased from − 0.170 under the low need for uniqueness condition to − 0.075 under the high need for uniqueness condition. Need for uniqueness effectively buffered the negative transmission, but did not completely eliminate it—even for individuals with high need for uniqueness, the inhibitory effect of nostalgia proneness through authenticity still existed, albeit to a lesser degree. This pattern of results paints a clear psychological picture: nostalgia proneness inhibits acceptance by lowering consumers’ perceptions of AI product authenticity, but the potency of this transmission pathway is not immutable—it varies in strength depending on the level of an individual’s need for uniqueness.

Theoretical contributions

The present study extends existing understanding in three areas. First, it introduces nostalgia proneness as a stable personality trait into the AI product acceptance framework. Existing research has predominantly used experimental manipulation of nostalgic emotional states [5, 7]; the present study focuses on individual-level trait differences, providing initial evidence that consumer responses to AI cultural products are also associated with stable individual differences in nostalgia proneness, complementing rather than displacing prior work on transient nostalgic states. This extends the theoretical reach of nostalgia psychology from the domains of mental health and interpersonal relationships [6, 11] to the domains of technology acceptance and consumer behavior.

Second, it reveals perceived authenticity as a mediating mechanism explaining “who is more likely to reduce authenticity evaluations due to AI labeling.” Existing research has treated perceived authenticity as a direct consequence of AI labeling [3, 8]; the present study opens an individual-difference explanatory channel by positioning nostalgia proneness as its antecedent variable, echoing Sedikides and Schlegel’s [16] elaboration on authenticity as a core judgment framework.

Third, it identifies the boundary buffering effect of need for uniqueness. AI design does not produce equally negative impacts on all consumers with high nostalgia proneness—individuals with high need for uniqueness can partially offset the psychological cost of insufficient authenticity [2], enriching the understanding of the interactive effects of multiple personality factors in shaping attitudes toward AI products.

Conclusion

This study investigated the relationship between nostalgia proneness and consumer acceptance of AI-designed cultural products by constructing a moderated mediation model, with all four hypotheses receiving empirical support. Nostalgia proneness significantly and negatively predicted acceptance of AI-designed cultural products. Perceived authenticity partially mediated this relationship, with the indirect effect accounting for approximately 44.6% of the total effect. Need for uniqueness moderated the second stage of the mediation path, such that the positive effect of perceived authenticity on acceptance was weaker among individuals with higher need for uniqueness. Accordingly, the indirect effect of nostalgia proneness on acceptance through perceived authenticity decreased from − 0.170 under the low need for uniqueness condition to − 0.075 under the high need for uniqueness condition.

Although the present study did not manipulate marketing communications, framing strategies, or customization features, the observed pattern of associations suggests several testable hypotheses for applied research, which we outline below as directions rather than as actionable conclusions. These findings carry direct implications for the cultural and creative industry. For consumer groups with higher nostalgia proneness, firms should avoid overemphasizing AI’s autonomous creative role and instead highlight the concept of “human-AI collaboration.” Brüns and Meißner’s [8] experiments have shown that the narrative of “AI assisting human designers” can restore authenticity perceptions, and Kim et al. [18] also recommend enhancing authenticity evaluations through validating information (e.g., “reviewed by cultural experts”) and scarcity cues (e.g., “limited customization”). For consumer groups with higher need for uniqueness, firms can boldly highlight AI’s innovative design capabilities and unique visual styles. The introduction of customization features is particularly noteworthy—allowing consumers to freely select and combine elements within AI-generated schemes satisfies their pursuit of differentiation while enhancing acceptance through a sense of participation [3]. Overall, the cultural and creative industry needs to establish differentiated communication systems based on user psychological profiles, avoiding the use of a single product narrative for consumers with different personality profiles.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. In terms of research design, the cross-sectional survey cannot provide rigorous causal inference. Future research could employ experimental designs to manipulate nostalgic emotional states [5], complementing the trait-level findings of the present study; longitudinal tracking designs are also worth attempting to observe whether the negative effect of nostalgia proneness diminishes as AI exposure experience accumulates. In terms of the sample, all participants were university students from Hubei Province. Older consumers may exhibit systematic differences in nostalgia proneness levels and expression patterns [4], and cross-cultural comparisons represent another valuable direction—different cultural understandings of “authenticity” may alter the strength of various paths in the model [10]. In terms of the theoretical model, the fact that the direct effect remained significant suggests the possible existence of other mediating mechanisms, such as perceived risk, AI trust, and cultural identity [2]. Furthermore, whether need for uniqueness may also moderate the first half of the path (X→M)—that is, whether individuals with high need for uniqueness are less concerned from the outset about whether AI products are “authentic”—provides a direction for constructing a more complete model.

A more thorough enumeration of these limitations is warranted given the cross-sectional, single-source nature of the design. The mediation language adopted throughout this paper should be read as descriptive of an indirect statistical pathway rather than as a demonstration of a temporally ordered psychological process. Perceived authenticity and acceptance were measured at the same survey occasion and may reflect closely related evaluative judgments, so the present results are best interpreted as consistent with, rather than as proof of, a causal mechanism. Relatedly, all participants viewed products under a single explicit AI-design label, with no human-designed comparison condition and no unlabeled control. This restricts our ability to isolate the AI label effect from product-specific preferences, a limitation we recommend that future work address through factorial designs that orthogonally manipulate AI labeling, human-collaboration framing, and unlabeled baselines.

The measurement strategy also has identifiable limits. Acceptance was operationalized as behavioral intention to purchase or recommend, which captures only a slice of the broader concept of acceptance; aesthetic appreciation, perceived appropriateness of AI in cultural heritage domains, trust in algorithmic legitimacy, and perceived compatibility between AI and tradition were not measured. Common method bias was assessed only with Harman’s single-factor test, which is a coarse diagnostic; more rigorous procedures such as marker variables, an unmeasured latent method construct, or temporally separated data collection should be adopted in subsequent studies. The questionnaire ordering, although designed to reduce causal-inference bias, may have inadvertently primed past-orientation concerns before participants evaluated authenticity. Finally, the perceived authenticity scale was administered as an integrative measure rather than decomposed into the four sub-dimensions of continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbolism; future research could test whether each sub-dimension is differentially affected by AI labeling.

Sampling and cultural specificity merit explicit attention. The sample was restricted to undergraduate students in a single Chinese province within an 18–24 age band, which precludes any examination of how nostalgia proneness, which tends to vary across the lifespan, interacts with AI acceptance among older consumers, museum patrons, or individuals with stronger investments in heritage preservation. Cultural products in this study were grounded in Chinese museum traditions, and the interpretation of authenticity in such contexts may interweave personal memory, collective heritage, and national symbolism in ways that do not generalize to markets and product categories shaped by different cultural logics. Cross-cultural replication, comparison with non-heritage product categories, and inclusion of broader age and socioeconomic strata are therefore important next steps. Beyond these constraints, we did not control for several variables that future research should consider, including pre-existing attitudes toward the specific museum brands or cultural product categories used as stimuli, general technology acceptance propensity and AI familiarity, technology anxiety, valuation of human craftsmanship, and ideological commitments to human-led creativity. Any of these could plausibly contribute to the observed associations and should be incorporated as covariates in subsequent designs [43].

A productive program of future research follows from these limitations. Three lines seem especially valuable. First, factorial experiments that manipulate AI versus human authorship, with and without collaborative framing, would adjudicate between authenticity-driven and identity-threat accounts of the observed pattern. Second, multi-wave or longitudinal designs would better support process-level claims about whether nostalgia proneness shapes authenticity judgments before, after, or simultaneously with acceptance evaluations, and whether repeated AI exposure attenuates the negative pathway over time. Third, broader theoretical engagement with mind perception, identity-relevant interpretations of AI, and past-oriented judgment processes would help test whether perceived authenticity functions alongside, rather than instead of, alternative mechanisms such as symbolic threat, perceived rupture with heritage, and anthropomorphic inferences about creator intent. Together, these directions would help determine whether the pattern documented here is best understood as a stable trait-level effect or as one node in a larger network of past-oriented psychological processes that govern responses to emerging technologies.
