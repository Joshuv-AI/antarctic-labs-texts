Impact of Remote Cholecystectomy on Clinical Outcomes Following Pseudomonas aeruginosa Bloodstream Infection
Authors: Hanna K Bertucci, Lyndsey R Heise, Anne Kurze, Michael P Angarone, Alan R Hauser, Marc H Scheetz, Susheel Reddy, Kelly E R Bachta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMortality associated with Pseudomonas aeruginosa bloodstream infection (PABSI) remains high despite advances in clinical care and therapeutics. In a recent study using a mouse model of PABSI, the gallbladder was identified as a reservoir for bacterial expansion. Furthermore, bile exposure has been linked to increased antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, we asked whether patients with retained gallbladders might experience more antimicrobial-resistant PABSIs, extended culture positivity, and worsened clinical outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults hospitalized over a 5-year period with PABSI. PABSI cases were defined as patients with ≥1 positive P. aeruginosa bacterial culture from the blood. Patients were categorized as those retaining a gallbladder (no cholecystectomy) or not (cholecystectomy). Cholecystectomy was defined as a history of cholecystectomy ≥1 year prior to the index episode of PABSI. Inferential statistics were used to identify associations between remote cholecystectomy and antimicrobial resistance profile, length of blood culture positivity, and in-hospital and 90-day mortality.ResultsThe overall study population included 336 patients: 262 (78%) with a retained gallbladder and 74 (22%) without. Using the entire study population and a matched cohort, we observed no difference in length of culture positivity, 90-day mortality, or in-hospital mortality between groups based on the presence of a gallbladder. Overall, composite 90-day mortality was 30.1%, which was similar to a prior investigation of PABSI outcomes at our institution. While the presence of a gallbladder did not affect the outcome, patients with PABSI and liver disease had significantly higher 90-day mortality than those without liver disease. Furthermore, no robust differences were observed in the antimicrobial resistance profile of P. aeruginosa isolates from the group with or without a gallbladder.ConclusionsIn our study, neither PABSI antimicrobial resistance pattern nor clinical outcomes were affected by remote cholecystectomy. However, we do demonstrate that mortality for patients with PABSI in the modern era remains high despite advances in antipseudomonal therapeutics.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, bloodstream infection, cholecystectomy, Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) , as defined by the ability to culture bacteria from the bloodstream of a patient with signs of systemic infection, are a growing threat to public health. It is estimated that >575 000 cases of BSIs occur annually in North America, leading to >90 000 patient deaths per year [1]. Positive blood cultures in the context of infection have been shown to increase overall mortality risk, and BSIs are a leading cause of sepsis, an often-devastating consequence of infection. Hospitalization due to BSI can increase hospital length of stay, leading to significant economic consequences for the patient and increased resource burden on health care systems [2, 3]. In patients whose BSI progresses to clinical sepsis, the average length of hospitalization increases by 75%, making sepsis the most expensive condition to treat in the hospital [3, 4]. Sepsis is often fatal, and those patients who do survive are more likely to have permanent organ damage and physical or mental impairments [3]. Among all bacterial organisms that cause BSIs, gram-negative organisms are increasingly problematic, as their prevalence is increasing and they are associated with high rates of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

One such gram-negative organism is Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Pa). Pa is an opportunistic environmental bacterium that commonly causes nosocomial or health care–associated infections, including BSI. Pa is the fourth-leading cause of BSIs, representing approximately 4% to 9% of all episodes [5, 6]. When compared with all other bacterial species, BSI secondary to Pa carries a disproportionately high risk of mortality [7, 8], with reported crude mortality rates ranging widely from 20% to 70% [7–11]. A 13-year prospective study that evaluated 2600 patients with BSI and adjusted for AMR patterns and comorbidities found that the mortality for Pa BSI (PABSI) was higher than that of all other bacterial pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus [7]. PABSI is typically health care associated and is acquired in situ via central venous catheters or secondarily following dissemination following pneumonia or urinary tract infection [11, 12]. Risk factors for PABSI include prolonged hospitalization, neutropenia, liver and biliary disease, immunodeficiency, the presence of an indwelling catheter, and prior gastrointestinal (GI) colonization with Pa [10, 13–16]. Factors such as delayed effective antibiotic treatment, neutropenia, underlying pneumonia, and more severe disease at the time of presentation have been associated with poor PABSI outcomes [9, 17]. Multidrug-resistance (MDR) rates for Pa are among the highest of all BSI-causing pathogens, occurring in more than a quarter of all isolates [5], leading to newer classifications of AMR, including a “difficult-to-treat resistance” (DTR) category [18, 19]. Given this rise in antibiotic resistance, Pa is 1 of the top 2 pathogens initially treated with inappropriate antibiotic therapy [20]. Inadequate or delayed appropriate antibiotic therapy has been shown to worsen prognosis for PABSI [17, 20]. Despite the development of newer antimicrobial combinations to combat rising AMR (eg, ceftazidime-avibactam and ceftolozane-tazobactam), limited headway has been made in reducing overall PABSI mortality.

In a recent report, Bachta et al used a mouse infection model to better understand PABSI intrahost infection dynamics [21]. Following PABSI, Pa was found to traffic to the gallbladder, where the bacterial population expanded dramatically and facilitated high levels of excretion of Pa into the GI tract. Remarkably, post infection the gallbladder contained the highest bacterial burden of any organ. Removal of the gallbladder led to dramatic reductions in the fecal excretion of Pa, thereby suggesting that the gallbladder may be a previously underappreciated niche for Pa expansion. Pa was also shown to replicate robustly in bile in vitro. Although the majority of gallbladder infections are caused by enteric bacterial organisms, epidemiologic studies report that Pa is a frequently isolated pathogen in cholecystitis and cholangitis, especially following prior instrumentation [22–27], and preexisting biliary disease has been identified as a risk factor for PABSI in patients with cirrhosis [16].

The gallbladder acts as an important reservoir for bile, a complex mixture of bile acids, phospholipids, cholesterol, bilirubin, inorganic salts, and trace metals [28]. Bile is one of the most inherently bactericidal compounds in the human body, serving to decrease bacterial burden in the GI tract. Despite this, several studies have demonstrated that the gallbladder is an important replicative niche for certain bacterial species. It has been shown to facilitate persistent colonization and enhance transmission of Salmonella typhi, Listeria monocytogenes, and Campylobacter jejuni [29–32]. Gallbladder epithelial cells serve as the niche for in vivo replication of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi [33], and L. monocytogenes replicates within the lumen of the murine gallbladder [34]. As in PABSI, mouse models of BSI demonstrated that L. monocytogenes also traffics to the liver and gallbladder and is subsequently excreted in the GI tract [31, 35, 36].

To promote survival and GI colonization, bacteria have developed mechanisms that subvert the toxic effects of bile through adaptation and tolerance [29], and many utilize bile as a signal to enhance virulence gene expression for efficient infection [29]. L. monocytogenes expresses a bile salt hydrolase that, when deleted, decreases virulence [37]. Exposure to bile promotes germination of Clostridioides difficile, an important cause of infectious colitis in patients. Other mechanisms that promote bile tolerance include induction of biofilm formation and intracellular invasion of gallbladder epithelial cells, a phenomenon thought to promote chronic carriage and GI shedding of Salmonella [38]. Critically, bile exposure has been linked to the promotion of increased AMR. There is mounting evidence in Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pa that bile exposure stimulates efflux pump expression, enhancing the ability of bacteria to survive in harsh environments [29, 39]. For example, bile exposure in E coli increases expression of multidrug efflux pumps, including AcrAB and EmrAB, that promote bile tolerance and increase antibiotic efflux [40, 41]. In Salmonella typhimurium, an AcrAB-like efflux pump contributes to resistance to bile salts and detergents [42]. Bile exposure in Enterococcus faecalis induces genes encoding for EmrB/QacA family drug resistance transporters and increases expression of phospholipid synthetase, which is linked to daptomycin resistance [43]. Thus, increased exposure to bile may be contributing to rising AMR in pathogenic bacteria. However, how these adaptations affect overall survival is poorly understood.

There are limited studies investigating the responses of Pa, a nonenteric pathogen, to bile. Pa replicates robustly in bile, and bile exposure has been shown to stimulate the production of Pa virulence factors, including genes important in biofilm formation, cell envelope biogenesis, quorum sensing, type VI secretion, and antimicrobial tolerance [21, 39, 44–46]. In the lungs of patients with cystic fibrosis and structural lung disease, bile aspiration is associated with the early acquisition of Pa [47, 48]. Once established in the lungs of such patients, Pa is exceedingly difficult to eradicate, as ongoing bile exposure secondary to gastroesophageal reflux potentiates adaptations that allow for persistent colonization [45, 49]. Given that (1) Pa replicates robustly in bile, (2) is cultured relatively frequently from the gallbladder in the setting of cholecystitis and cholangitis, and (3) biliary disease is a risk factor for PABSI, we questioned if the presence of a gallbladder at the time of BSI played a role in PABSI outcome. We hypothesized that the presence of a gallbladder might facilitate greater Pa exposure to bile and promote the development of AMR. Additionally, we hypothesized that remote cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) through elimination of an in vivo replication niche may improve outcomes following PABSI. The purpose of this retrospective analysis was to evaluate the impact of remote cholecystectomy on AMR and infection outcomes in patients with PABSI.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Setting, Design, and Protocol

In this retrospective cohort study, we examined patients with PABSIs hospitalized at Northwestern Memorial Hospital (NMH) and Prentice Women's Hospital from 1 July 2014 to 1 July 2019. NMH is an 894-bed academic medical center with a full range of clinical services, including a robust population of patients who are immunocompromised. As Pa is rarely a contaminant in the bloodstream, patients were considered to have PABSI if ≥1 blood culture was positive for Pa. The first positive isolate per patient was included in this analysis (Figure 1). Exclusion criteria included age <18 years, a cholecystectomy within the last 1 year, HIV/AIDS, and pregnancy. We chose to exclude patients with cholecystectomies within 1 year of their PABSI to ensure that patients were completely healed from surgery and to minimize the likelihood that the bacteremia episode was a direct result of the surgical procedure. Patients with polymicrobial bacteremia were also included in the study. This project was reviewed and approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (project STU00210641). It was deemed an observational study; thus, no ethical approval or informed consent was required in accordance with Northwestern University's Institutional Review Board. All data collection and analyses were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and declarations.

Patient inclusion criteria. Algorithm for identifying patients with Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia with or without a gallbladder based on radiographic or ICD-9 and ICD-10 code–based strategies. A total of 336 unique patients with P. aeruginosa bloodstream infection were screened. ICD-9/ICD-10 or manual chart review was used to determine whether patients had a gallbladder (n = 272) or not (n = 74). From the original sample, cholecystectomy and noncholecystectomy cases were matched 1:1 according to a propensity score greedy match with a specified caliper width of 0.2 and with neutropenia and leukemia/lymphoma as matching criteria. A complete match of all 74 patients with a cholecystectomy was obtained.

Inpatient electronic medical records (NMH Enterprise Data Warehouse) and pharmacy and microbiology databases were reviewed. Patient demographics included gender, age, history of positive culture for Pa, hospitalization within the last 90 days, and severity of illness at the time of PABSI. Critical illness was defined as occupancy in an intensive care unit or need for vasoactive support at the time of positive blood culture. The presence of the following comorbid conditions was documented: neutropenia, localized and metastatic solid tumor, leukemia/lymphoma, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular accident/transient ischemic attack, dementia, hemiplegia, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, and connective tissue disease. Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated at the time of bacteremia to estimate illness severity [50]. Charts were reviewed for evidence of cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) through identification via ICD-9 or ICD-10 code (K91.5, K91.86, T86.4, Z48.23, Z94.4, OFB4, OFT4, OFYOOZO, OFOOZ1) or CPT code (47135, 47143, 47562, 47563, 47564, 47600, 47600, 47605, 47610, 47612, 47620) or through the absence of a gallbladder on abdominal computed tomography scan. For patients with a history of cholecystectomy, the approximate procedure date and reason were recorded, if available. Recorded characteristics of BSIs included the suspected source of infection, whether the infection was polymicrobial, and whether the infection was hospital acquired (nosocomial, onset ≥48 hours after admission). The following aspects of antimicrobial therapy were recorded: time to active therapy, length of blood culture positivity, and culture susceptibility to antibiotics spanning several classes, as detailed later. Length of culture positivity was determined by manual chart review. Outcomes included length of blood culture positivity and 90-day and in-hospital mortality if <90 days. As a secondary end point, we used this dataset to compare outcomes with a similar study published in 2009 [9] to examine changes in PABSI outcomes at our institution over time.

Microbiology

Blood cultures from hospitalized patients were processed by the NMH Microbiology Laboratory using the BacT/ALERT blood culture system (bioMérieux), with each set consisting of aerobic and anaerobic cultures. Genus and species identification of bacteria was performed via mass spectrometry identification via MALDI-TOF (bioMérieux) or manual biochemical assays when necessary. Susceptibility testing was performed by either the Vitek II system to determine minimal inhibitory concentration or Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion on Mueller-Hinton agar plates. Antimicrobial agents tested included ciprofloxacin, cefepime, ceftazidime, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, meropenem, imipenem-cilastatin, aztreonam, amikacin, tobramycin, colistin, and polymyxin B. Minimal inhibitory concentrations and zones of inhibition were categorically interpreted according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (30th edition) [51].

Definitions

Neutropenia was defined as an absolute neutrophil count <500/mm3 [52]. Nosocomial bacteremia was defined as bacteremia acquired ≥48 hours after hospitalization. Polymicrobial BSI was defined as concurrent isolation of Pa plus any additional bacterium from the bloodstream at time of the diagnostic blood culture. Active antimicrobial therapy was defined as therapy with at least 1 agent to which the causative Pa strain was susceptible. Specific doses and duration of antibiotic therapy were not reviewed, but all antibiotics were dosed prospectively by clinical pharmacists to optimize efficacy while minimizing safety concerns. Recurrent PABSI was defined as reisolation of Pa from a patient's bloodstream ≥2 weeks from the incident episode, provided that there was interval clearance (documented negative blood culture) of the bloodstream between episodes. Infections were categorized as MDR if they were nonsusceptible (intermediate or resistant) to ≥1 agent from ≥3 classes of antibiotics; extensively drug resistant (XDR) if they were susceptible to ≤2 classes of antibiotics; and DTR if nonsusceptible to ciprofloxacin, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, ceftazidime, and meropenem or imipenem-cilastatin [18, 19, 53]. To determine length of culture positivity, we recorded the total number of days of positive blood cultures documented. For patients with no blood culture follow-up within 48 hours of index culture, the length of culture positivity was recorded as 1 day. For patients with recurrent PABSI episodes, the longest consecutive duration of documented positive cultures was included.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Results were expressed as mean with standard deviation (SD) or frequency and percentage. For the unmatched cohort, 2-tailed inferential analyses were applied by χ2 methodologies or Fisher exact test for categorical variables and 2-sample t tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom for continuous variables. Data regarding length of culture positivity lacked a parametric distribution; thus, P values were calculated by the 2-sample Wilcon rank sum test (Mann-Whitney). Time-to-event analyses were completed with log-rank tests for assessments of equality. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated in Prism version 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software).

To address identified imbalances within the original cohort, we pursued a propensity-matched cohort analysis. From the original sample of 336 patients, the 74 patients with cholecystectomy were matched 1:1 to noncholecystectomy cases via a propensity score greedy match with a specified caliper width of 0.2 and with neutropenia and leukemia/lymphoma as matching criteria (Figure 1). Inferential analyses on the matched cohort were performed via methodologies for paired data, with paired t tests and Wilcoxon signed rank tests applied to continuous data. Categorical data were evaluated by generalized estimating equation logistic regression models, with cholecystectomy status as the lone independent variable and observations clustered by matched pair. Exact McNemar and McNemar-Bowker tests for symmetry were also applied as needed.

Multivariable models were fit per the matched cohort to evaluate associations between the outcomes and the independent exposures of cholecystectomy status, liver disease, and gender. Patient characteristics selected for inclusion in the models were based on identification of remaining imbalances within the matched cohort. Length of culture positivity was evaluated by a log-normal mixed model with match ID as the random intercept and an unstructured covariance matrix. Generalized estimating equation logistic regression was used to evaluate the mortality outcomes by applying an unstructured working correlation matrix, with observations clustered by matched pair.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

During the study period, 352 episodes of PABSI were recorded in 336 unique patients (Figure 1, Table 1), of which 12 (3.6%) had recurrent PABSI. The mean age of patients in the study was 66.9 years and 64.3% were male. Approximately one-quarter (24.7%) of all episodes were nosocomial and 23.9% were polymicrobial. Among polymicrobial infections, the most common coisolated pathogen was E. coli (22/94, 23.4%), followed by coagulase-negative staphylococci (12/94, 12.8%) and Enterococcus species (12/94, 12.8%; Supplementary Table 1). Approximately half of the patients (158/336, 47.0%) were critically ill at the time of diagnosis, and 64.3% (216/336) had been hospitalized within the antecedent 90 days. Surprisingly, only 22.0% (74/336) of all patients had a history of a culture that was positive for Pa. The likely source of the BSI was identified in 63.1% of all patients (212/336), with urinary tract infection as the leading source (91/336, 27.1%) in individuals with (78/262, 29.8%) and without (13/74, 17.6%) a gallbladder. A clear source was unable to be identified in nearly 40% of all patients (132/336, 39.3%).

Of the individual patients in our entire cohort, 262 (78.0%) had a gallbladder at the time of infection (no cholecystectomy), and 74 (22.0%) had gallbladder removal (cholecystectomy) at least 1 year prior to the PABSI episode. Unsurprisingly, given the predilection of females for gallbladder disease, fewer male patients had prior cholecystectomy (43.2% vs 70.2%, P < .01). Additionally, the mean time to active therapy was statistically different between the groups (1.4 days for patients without a gallbladder and 0.55 days for patients with a gallbladder, P < .01). The majority of patients (217/336, 64.6%) had a CCI ≥5 at the time of their diagnosis, and the average CCI was slightly higher in patients without a gallbladder (6.4) than in those with one (5.6, P = .03). Generally, the presence of most medical comorbidities was not statistically different between cohorts; however, there were some notable differences suggesting imbalance. Among patients without a gallbladder, a smaller proportion of patients were neutropenic (8.1% vs 22.1%, P < .01) or had leukemia or lymphoma (13.5% vs 25.6%, P = .03). Additionally, among patients without a gallbladder, a larger proportion had peripheral vascular disease (20.3% vs 9.2%, P < .01) and liver disease (16.2% vs 5.0%, P < .01).

Demographics and patient characteristics within the matched cohort are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Again, given the predilection of females for gallbladder disease, fewer male patients had prior cholecystectomy (43.2% vs 67.6%, P < .01) in the matched cohort. Additionally, the mean time to active therapy retained a clinically significant difference between the groups (1.4 days for patients without a gallbladder and 0.69 days for patients with a gallbladder) but was no longer statistically significant. Unlike the full cohort, there were no differences in CCI or the presence of peripheral vascular disease within the matched cohort. Finally, among patients without a gallbladder, the respiratory tract was the source for a larger proportion of patients' PABSI (9.5% vs 2.7%, P = .03), and more patients had liver disease (16.2% vs 4.1%, P = .03; Supplementary Table 2).

AMR Profiles of PABSI Isolates

Of the 336 unique episodes of PABSI, we had AMR data for all but 13 (3.9%), which were excluded from this analysis (Table 2). As mentioned, 12 patients (3.6%) had recurrent PABSI during this study period. For patients with recurrence, antibiotic susceptibility data were assessed for each patient's first positive blood culture, yielding a total of 323 unique isolates. Overall, for those antibiotics for which we had complete data (ciprofloxacin, cefepime, ceftazidime, piperacillin-tazobactam, and tobramycin), we saw the highest rates of nonsusceptibility to ciprofloxacin (48/323, 14.9%), followed closely by piperacillin-tazobactam (39/323, 12.1%). Of all isolates for which we had resistance testing, more than one-quarter were nonsusceptible to aztreonam (90/322, 28.0%). Carbapenem susceptibility data were available for 322 of the 323 isolates: 8.2% (23/282) were nonsusceptible to meropenem and 17.5% (7/40) were nonsusceptible to imipenem-cilastatin. Very few isolates underwent testing for ceftazidime/avibactam or ceftolozane/tazobactam, as the study period was prior to extensive clinical use of these antimicrobial agents. A considerable proportion of PABSI episodes were characterized as MDR (12.1%) or XDR, (4.3%), with 5 isolates (1.5%) having DTR. No isolates were pan–drug resistant.

To discern if prior cholecystectomy was associated with decreased resistance within our isolates, we compared the susceptibility profiles between patients with and without a gallbladder. The percentage of nonsusceptible isolates for each of the aforementioned antibiotics was generally similar among patients with and without a gallbladder. Surprisingly, a clinically significant proportion of isolates in patients without a gallbladder were nonsusceptible to piperacillin/tazobactam (17.1% vs 10.7%, P = .14), although these results did not achieve statistical significance. Additionally, there was no statistical difference in MDR, XDR, or DTR phenotype observed between patients without and with a gallbladder (MDR: 12.9% vs 11.9%, P = .84; XDR: 2.9% vs 4.8%, P = .74; DTR: 1.4% vs 1.6%, P > .99, respectively). Overall, across all 7 classes of antibiotics, we did not observe any statistically significant differences in AMR patterns that correlated with the presence of a gallbladder at the time of PABSI diagnosis.

Impact of Prior Cholecystectomy on Outcome of PABSI

Overall, no statistically significant difference was observed between cohorts for 90-day or in-hospital mortality (Table 1), as seen in Kaplan-Maier curves displaying mortality data (P > .5; Figure 2). Using our propensity-matched cohort (n = 148), we examined 3 major outcome measures in our patient population. We performed multivariate analysis investigating the impact of cholecystectomy, liver disease, and gender on the length of culture positivity (Table 3) and 90-day and in-hospital mortality (Table 4). The unadjusted average length of culture positivity for patients without a gallbladder was 1.35 days as compared with 1.36 days in patients with a gallbladder (mean paired difference, 0.01 days; P = .81, adjusted P = .92). Overall, there was no significant difference in length of culture positivity based on the presence of a gallbladder, liver disease (mean paired difference, 0.13; P = .88, adjusted P = .28), or gender (mean paired difference, 0.10; P = .50, adjusted P = .51) in bivariate or multivariate analyses.

DISCUSSION

In this single-institution retrospective cohort study, we assessed the impact of prior remote gallbladder removal on clinical outcomes and AMR profile following PABSI. No previous studies have investigated the relationship among remote cholecystectomy, development of AMR, and PABSI outcomes. Several studies, however, have investigated the impact of cholecystectomy on C. difficile incident infection, recurrence, and mortality [54, 55]. Given that cholecystectomy is associated with increased levels of fecal secondary bile acids that confer resistance to C. difficile infection in animal models, the authors of those studies propose that cholecystectomy might protect against C. difficile incident infection. Patients without a gallbladder did not experience higher rates of C. difficile incident infection or recurrence. Yet, there was a significant increase in mortality among patients following cholecystectomy [55]. The reason for the increase in mortality is poorly understood. Conversely, in our study investigating PABSI, having a prior remote cholecystectomy did not affect clinical outcome, as patients with and without a gallbladder had similar durations of bacteremia and 90-day and in-hospital mortality rates. In the overall cohort, there were baseline differences in the 2 study populations that may have masked outcome differences. Neutropenia, a known risk factor for poor outcomes in PABSI [9, 13, 15, 17], and leukemia/lymphoma were more common in patients with a gallbladder, whereas peripheral vascular disease and liver disease (other characteristics associated with poor PABSI outcomes [15, 16]) were more common in patients without one. Furthermore, patients without a gallbladder experienced a longer delay in the onset of active antimicrobial therapy and averaged higher CCI scores at the time of PABSI diagnosis. Using a 1:1 propensity-matched cohort, we adjusted for neutropenia and leukemia/lymphoma in a multivariable analysis (Supplementary Table 2, Tables 3 and 4). Upon adjustment, there were no longer differences in time to onset of active antimicrobial therapy, CCI, or peripheral vascular disease between patients with and without a gallbladder. Thus, using this matched cohort, we found that there was no association between the presence of a gallbladder and the length of culture positivity or 90-day or in-hospital mortality following PABSI. One notable finding was that the 90-day mortality for patients with liver disease was considerably higher than it was for those without liver disease. Liver disease has been linked with poor outcomes following PABSI [16, 56].

Additionally, we found no association between prior cholecystectomy and increased antibiotic resistance among PABSI isolates, as there were no statistically significant differences in AMR patterns dependent on the presence or absence of a gallbladder at the time of diagnosis. We had theorized that in patients with an intact gallbladder during PABSI, the Pa isolate may have increased bile exposure, a risk factor for the development of AMR [45]. Other pathogens, such as E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and enterococci, have displayed unexpected resistance traits when cultured from the gallbladder, and bile exposure has been shown to increase expression of efflux pumps critical for the development of antibacterial resistance [22, 23, 39, 57]. Therefore, the lack of increased AMR in patients with a retained gallbladder was surprising. However, several factors may explain this observation. First, the quantity of bile exposure in patients following cholecystectomy is difficult to ascertain. Even in the absence of a gallbladder, the liver produces bile, which is subsequently excreted into the small intestine. Furthermore, patients who have undergone cholecystectomy have had at least 1 contact with the health care system, thereby increasing their exposure to health care–associated pathogens, which routinely display higher patterns of AMR [58–61]. Finally, it was apparent that in a majority of our cohort, PABSI arose from sites other than the GI tract (eg, urinary tract, respiratory; Table 1). In these cases, it is unlikely that Pa would have been exposed to bile in either the gallbladder or the GI tract prior to infection. These factors may have obscured any differences in AMR related to the presence or absence of a gallbladder.

Finally, this study was able to place PABSIs at our institution within a historical context, as a similar study completed more than a decade earlier assessed morbidity associated with PABSI. In that study, the average patient was 57 years old, the in-hospital mortality rate was 37% [9], and one-third of all infections were polymicrobial. By comparison, our cohort was older (average age, 66.9 years), the in-hospital mortality was lower (20.8%), and only 23.5% of all BSIs were polymicrobial. We are encouraged by the 16% decline in the in-hospital mortality rate of PABSI over the last 15 years, and we believe that several factors have contributed to this decline. First, we believe that recognition of rising rates of AMR, particularly in Pa infections, has led to more rapid and aggressive initiation of antipseudomonal antibiotics early in the hospital stay in those patients with risk factors for Pa infections. Additionally, during the study period, newer combination antimicrobials for Pa infections were introduced, including ceftazidime-avibactam and ceftolozane-tazobactam (2014 and 2015, respectively). Both have expanded the antibiotic toolkit available to treat MDR and DTR Pa infections. Lastly, there are differences in study populations between the studies. Although our cohort was older than that of the original study, there was a considerable reduction in the rate of polymicrobial BSIs. These factors may offset one another; however, our study is not powered to fully assess the impact of these differences on outcomes. Despite the 15-year interval between the studies, it is important to acknowledge that the overall mortality rate of PABSI remains high.

General estimates suggest that the lifetime prevalence of cholecystectomy is approximately 4.0% in the general population, 5.3% among women, and 2.4% among men [62]. In our cohort, 22.0% of our patients with PABSI had a prior cholecystectomy. Contrary to our hypothesis that cholecystectomy may confer protection against PABSI, the disproportionate number of patients without a gallbladder suggests that this population may in fact be vulnerable to PABSI or have significant risk factors that predispose them to PABSI.

This study has several important limitations. First, the population size was relatively small and included patients at a single center, and generalizability to larger populations and different clinical settings may be limited. Additionally, the data collected reflected a disproportionate ratio of patients with and without a gallbladder (3:1), with overall rates of cholecystectomy that were 5 times higher than expected based on general population estimates. We attempted to adjust for these discrepancies using a matched cohort approach with subsequent adjustment for remaining imbalances via multivariable analysis. Second, this analysis was limited to the first episode of PABSI observed in each patient. As prior infection predisposes patients to Pa colonization and increases the risk of invasive infection, there may have been colonized individuals in the cohort who were more predisposed to invasive infection, as 22% of our overall cohort had a prior culture positive for Pa [15, 63]. This study did not assess whether cholecystectomy affects Pa carriage in the GI tract, an important risk factor for invasive disease. Bachta et al demonstrated that the gallbladder is crucial for fecal excretion of Pa in mice [21]; however, its role in amplification of fecal shedding in humans is unknown. Additionally, most patients received prompt active antimicrobial therapy within 1 day of their first positive blood culture (mean days to active therapy, 0.73 ± 2.1), limiting our ability to assess associations among onset of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, underlying resistance patterns, and outcomes.

While we did not observe an improvement in PABSI outcomes in patients lacking a gallbladder, we were able to observe improved overall survival rates following PABSI as compared with a prior study at our institution. This suggests that recognition and treatment of PABSI may be improving, but there is still much to learn about carriage, colonization, and excretion of Pa. Given the observation that the gallbladder promoted increased GI excretion of Pa in mice, it would be interesting to investigate this question in human patients. A future prospective study comparing fecal carriage of Pa in patients with and without a gallbladder following PABSI would help elucidate whether the gallbladder is important for amplification of Pa in patients. If the presence of a gallbladder provided an important in vivo replication niche and increased excretion of Pa, this may change the way that clinicians approach patients with disseminated Pa infections. Such patients might benefit from increased infection control measures that limit environmental contamination of their rooms. Additionally, prophylactic elective cholecystectomy in patients with recurrent BSI or a lower threshold for emergent cholecystectomy in patients who are critically ill could be entertained. Ultimately, it is critical that we continue to explore the interplay among the gallbladder, bile exposure, and AMR in the context of PABSI to better understand the pathophysiology of BSIs and to enhance our ability to develop new therapeutics.
