Effects of Social Prescribing for Older Adults: An Evidence and Gap Map

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany factors such as declining physical function, low socioeconomic status and unemployment play a role in the complex health and social needs of older adults. While healthcare providers can assist in treating health conditions, non-clinical interventions are most likely needed to meet their social needs and improve wellbeing. Social prescribing has been introduced to help primary care providers and other connectors meet patient’s social needs that contribute to poor health and inequalities. With the increasing recognition of these complex challenges, decision-makers need to know which social prescribing interventions or social prescriptions are effective for older adults.ObjectivesTo map the available evidence on the effectiveness of social prescribing interventions and social prescriptions addressing a non-medical health-related need for older adults in clinical and community settings.Search MethodsWe searched the following databases from inception until November 14, 2023, with no language restrictions: Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, EBM Reviews, Elsevier Scopus, Epistemonikos, CABI CAB Direct, NIHR PROSPERO, WHO Global Index Medicus, Clarivate Web of Science Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI), Conference Proceedings, Clarivate Korean Citation Index (KCI), Clarivate SciELO Citation Index and Google Scholar via Harzing Publish or Perish.Selection CriteriaTitles and abstracts and full text of potentially eligible articles were independently screened in duplicate for systematic reviews and studies with concurrent control groups assessing the effectiveness of social prescribing and social prescription interventions on the wellbeing of older adults. Conflicts were resolved through consensus.Data Collection and AnalysisWe developed and pilot-tested a data collection form in EPPI-Reviewer. Data was extracted and coded individually by reviewers based on an intervention-outcome framework which was also used to define the dimensions of the evidence and gap map. Data extraction was validated by a second reviewer for 20%. We assessed the quality of reviews using the AMSTAR2 tool. The quality of primary studies was not assessed.Main ResultsWe included 662 articles (430 primary studies, 232 reviews) that assessed the effectiveness of social prescribing interventions or social prescriptions targeting non-medical health-related social needs of older adults to improve their health and wellbeing. Over 65% of the articles were published in 2019 or later.AMSTAR2 quality was rated as critically low for 55%, low for 31%, moderate for 6% and high for only 4% of systematic reviews. Of the included primary studies, 48% were randomized controlled trials and 52% were non-randomized studies.Most of the evidence was from high-income countries (78% of reviews and 93% of primary studies), few from upper-middle (25% of reviews and 5% of primary studies), and lower-middle (9% of reviews and 3% of primary studies) countries, and none from low-income countries (0%). The most common intervention types identified were psychosocial (e.g. social and emotional support interventions), with 81% of reviews and 63% of primary studies including these interventions, lifestyle (exercise on prescription) with 45% of reviews and 30% of primary studies including these interventions, and arts-based (museums, galleries, arts & crafts), with 29% of reviews and 20% of primary studies including these interventions. These were followed by material support in 13% of reviews and 7% of primary studies, and nature-based interventions in 10% of reviews and 4% of primary studies. Ninety-eight percent of the reviews and 95% of the primary studies focused on assessing the impact on individual outcomes (e.g. participant wellbeing/quality of life), with 41% of reviews and 47% of primary studies assessing process outcomes (e.g. participant satisfaction), and 27% of both reviews and primary studies looking at health system outcomes (e.g. health/social service use). Eleven percent of reviews and primary studies looked at adverse effects, and no studies assessed health equity as a community outcome.Key characteristics of social prescribing interventions or social prescriptions were described in few included articles; codesign of the interventions in 6% of reviews and 15% of primary studies, while participant empowerment was described in 24% of reviews and 19% of primary studies.Most articles focused on a population at risk of or experiencing inequity (91% of reviews and 96% of primary studies). Less than 10% of both reviews and primary studies conducted any equity analysis to assess differential effects for populations experiencing inequities across factors such as age, gender or ethnicity.Authors’ ConclusionsMost of the available evidence on social prescribing and social prescription was on psychosocial and health system interventions; there is limited research on material support and nature-based interventions. Furthermore, despite the aims of social prescribing or social prescription to empower communities and reduce health inequalities, there are few studies that assess community-level outcomes or adverse effects. There is a need for more research in low-middle-income countries and for high-quality reviews. Background

The Problem, Condition or Issue

With increasing life expectancy, the global population is expected to reach 2 billion people over the age of 60 years by 2050 (WHO, 2020). Low socioeconomic status, declining physical, function, and cognitive capacity occur with age resulting in complex functional, social and health needs, and multimorbidity for many older adults, increasing the demand for health and social services (Elston et al., 2019; Weaver & Burley, 2020; WHO, 2015) to improve their wellbeing and functional ability (Elston et al., 2019; WHO, 2015). Non-medical factors, or social determinants of health, including socioeconomic status, unemployment, education, social support, social exclusion, and transport, affect health outcomes and wellbeing of older adults (Andermann, 2016; Elston et al., 2019; Weaver & Burley, 2020). Addressing these factors may lead to healthy ageing which is the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables wellbeing in older adults (WHO, 2015).

General practice is a central access point of health systems and consultation patterns have shown persistent, frequent attendance of older adults with medical and social problems (Welzel et al., 2017) that affect health, defined as the state of complete physical, mental and social wellbeing and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity (WHO, 2002). Over 20% of visits, according to general practitioners in the UK, are spent addressing patients’ psychosocial issues such as financial concerns, housing, employment, or social relationship and support network problems (Elston et al., 2019; Kiely et al., 2022; Zantinge et al., 2005). Challenges arising from a disadvantaged social environment and lifestyle may result in negative psychological, physical, and social outcomes such as depression or loneliness. Medical treatments for depression and other health issues may be pragmatic, but they can easily become a very demanding option for health systems when the underlying psychosocial problems are not addressed. General practitioners may not routinely address these issues due to consultation time constraints or a lack of knowledge of available resources and partnerships with other sectors that can address them (Andermann, 2016; Pescheny et al., 2020).

One of the interventions introduced to help general practitioners manage these problems is social prescribing. The term originated in the U.K. and is being adopted internationally in high-income countries (Australia, Canada, Germany, Japan, Netherlands, Portugal, Singapore, Spain, USA) especially but also in some middle-income countries such as China, India, Iran, and Malaysia (Khan & Giurca, 2023; Morse et al., 2022). It describes interventions that connect individuals to non-clinical services and activities typically offered by voluntary and community sectors to improve health and wellbeing (Khan & Giurca, 2023; Morse et al., 2022 WHO, 2022). Similar interventions have been developed in other countries but not called social prescribing (Morse et al., 2022), for example, care navigation (Tierney et al., 2019) and community health programs (Khan & Giurca, 2023). There is no commonly accepted definition for social prescribing, and the activities or support services that constitute social prescribing are sometimes recommended as social prescriptions (Khan & Giurca, 2023 Morton et al., 2015; Rempel et al., 2017; Vogelpoel & Jarrold, 2014) and vary depending on the person’s needs and the community and care settings. However, a recent Delphi study, through a global consensus, has established a definition for social prescribing as a means for trusted individuals in clinical and community settings to identify that a person has non-medical, health-related social needs and to subsequently connect them to non-clinical supports and services within the community by co-producing a social prescription – a non-medical prescription, to improve health and wellbeing and to strengthen community connections (Muhl et al., 2023). Models of social prescribing rely on the principles of health promotion and addressing social determinants of health, such as socioeconomic status, social inclusion, housing, and education, to improve health outcomes by connecting patients with non-clinical sources of support (Morse et al., 2022; Mulligan et al., 2020; WHO, 2022).

Given that only 10–20% of the modifiable contributions to population health outcomes are thought to be accounted for by medical care (Hood et al., 2016), several national and international policy organizations such as the National Health Service (NHS) England, National Institute of Health Research UK (Tierney et al., 2020), and the World Health Organization (WHO) (WHO, 2022) advocate for social prescribing as an important avenue for expanding the treatment options. Social prescribing has been proposed as an option for available general practitioners and other community-based practitioners to address psychosocial factors and refocus the healthcare system by moving people away from the medicalization of social needs (Adbowale et al., 2014; WHO, 2022).

Social prescribing can target anyone, but populations with needs rooted in the social and structural determinants of health (such as income security, housing, belonging, or discrimination), and intersecting health equity factors (such as place of residence, race, ethnicity, class, gender, sexuality, disability, or age) benefit most (Fixsen et al., 2022). These populations include older adults who are at risk of social isolation or experience loneliness due to health and social inequality issues such as chronic health conditions, disability, socioeconomic deprivation, digital exclusion, or belonging to marginalized groups (e.g., racialized or ethnic minority groups, LGBTQ2S+) (WHO, 2022). Potential benefits of social prescribing for the ageing population and health system include improving health and wellbeing, reducing social isolation and loneliness, building resilience, delaying/preventing admissions to hospital, and improving systems (Hamilton-West et al., 2020). However, the ageing population is often hard to reach and tends to be under-represented in research (Forsat et al., 2020).

Social prescribing provides a model of health that can mitigate the effects of unmet social needs and address health inequalities (Chatterjee et al., 2018). Addressing social needs has gained greater attention given how the COVID-19 pandemic placed people, especially older adults, at increased risk of social isolation, with physical and mental health impacts, due to public health policies limiting their access to community services and family support (Haines et al., 2020; Hwang et al., 2020). Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, social prescribing was increasingly provided to older adults given their increased likelihood of experiencing depression, increased cognitive impairment, or mobility impairment in the presence of elevated social risk (Del Brutto et al., 2020, 2022; O’Toole et al., 2016). The pandemic has prompted the consideration of social prescribing on national and international levels and demonstrated the value of strong community social support in addressing public health concerns of vulnerable populations, including older adults (Younan et al., 2020).

The Intervention

Social prescribing usually involves screening for non-medical health-related social needs and connecting to non-clinical supports and services that are typically provided by community-based organizations to improve health and wellbeing, and to strengthen community connections (Muhl et al., 2023). It may be operationalised differently across the globe. For instance, the identification of non-medical health-related social needs is not always by a health care provider in clinical settings; it could be conducted by a trusted individual in the community setting. The identifier could do the following: (1) directly connect the person to community supports and services; (2) or refer the person to a connector who connects them to non-clinical community supports and services.

Other terms used in the literature for the connector include link worker, wellbeing coordinator, community connector, community health worker, patient or care navigator, social prescriber, health facilitator, or wellbeing coach (Hamilton-West et al., 2020; Morse et al., 2022). Tierney et al. found 75 different terms used in the literature (Tierney et al., 2019).

Social prescribing creates a trackable pathway for identifiers and connectors to connect people to community-based support and services and follow them up (Mulligan et al., 2020). Although signposting, which is the provision of a list of resources, is considered on the light touch side of the intensiveness spectrum (Howarth & Burns, 2019; Kimberlee, 2015), it involves people contacting the services they need or self-referral which might be challenging for some (White et al., 2022). Therefore, social prescribing is recently thought to be more than signposting and distinct (Morse et al., 2022; White et al., 2022), although both terms are sometimes used interchangeably (Morse et al., 2022). Social prescription has also been used interchangeably with social prescribing (Percival et al., 2022). However, social prescription involves identifying a non-medical health-related social need and providing non-clinical support but does not involve referral to a connector (Morse et al., 2022; Muhl et al., 2023). Care navigation is another term used interchangeably with social prescribing. Care navigation is defined as an avenue to link patients to activities or organizations that can help address non-medical needs affecting health and wellbeing (Tierney et al., 2019). It originated in the USA and Canada to help patients with complex needs or socially disadvantaged groups to access health services (Brunton et al., 2022; Carter et al., 2018) through signposting (Brunton et al., 2022; Tierney et al., 2019). Like social prescribing, care navigation connects people to community-based resources, improves health and wellbeing, and reduces healthcare demand (Brunton et al., 2022).

The community-based resources vary depending on the needs of the patients. Examples of services include art therapy, walking and exercise classes, nature-based activities, volunteering, as well as employment, debt, housing, and legal advice (Bickerdike et al., 2017; Kimberlee, 2013; Morse et al., 2022). Different models for social prescribing, or a continuum, have been described in the literature (Husk et al., 2019; Kimberlee, 2015). Why it is Important to Develop the EGM

Social prescribing is implemented differently in various health systems globally (Khan & Giurca, 2023) and its impact varies depending on the delivery scheme (Pescheny et al., 2020). For example, White et al. (White & Kinsella, 2010) report on the effects of referring patients with severe mental health issues on health outcomes and found limited improvements to patients’ wellbeing. The intervention scheme was made to help people solve problems themselves rather than getting the problem solved. Alternatively, other randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies have demonstrated positive effects of social prescribing on patient’s health and wellbeing (Jensen & Bonde, 2018; Maughan et al., 2016) and reduced healthcare use (Carnes et al., 2017; Loftus et al., 2017). Jensen & Bond assessed the effects of arts and creative activities while Maughan et al., assessed the impact of various services including self-management resources, educational, leisure and recreational facilities and fitness-, health- and exercise-related activities in conjunction with raising awareness of mental health. Carnes et al. assessed the effects of a social prescribing service involving developing an action plan and assigning a volunteer if necessary to help patients achieve their goals. They found reduced general practitioner consultation rates after one year in the intervention group, although there were no differences in general health, depression, anxiety and ‘positive and active engagement in life’ between the intervention and control groups. Loftus et al. found that participation in social prescribing activities including social clubs, men’s shed, counselling, arts programs, craft classes, crochet classes, falls prevention, exercises, befriending, personal development, and computer courses reduced healthcare use compared to controls.

Many of the evaluations of social prescribing schemes are small-scale with poor methodologies and show little evidence of their effectiveness (Bickerdike et al., 2017; Hamilton-West et al., 2020; Kiely et al., 2022; Pescheny et al., 2020; Woodhall et al., 2018). This may be in part due to challenges in evaluating social prescribing interventions because of their complex nature. Even though several studies have been conducted on social prescribing, researchers have drawn attention to the methodological and generalizability limitations of the available evidence for older adults (Hamilton-West et al., 2020). Older adults may have unique preferences or challenges for social prescribing to support connection back to the community. By mapping the existing and emerging research, this approach can group interventions and outcomes, enhancing the discoverability of evidence for interest holders, including policymakers.

The literature on social prescribing is sparse and based on operational elements. Programs for connecting people with non-clinical community services are not always called social prescribing and may sometimes be labelled as community connector programs, community referral, community links, social referral, signposting, or social prescription (Rempel et al., 2017; Thomson et al., 2015).

Emerging work on social prescribing shows global promise as a low-cost, scalable health promotion intervention that links health and social systems; tracks the impacts of social interventions on the wellbeing of participants, staff and volunteers; promotes health and reduces systems costs by moving care upstream; and improves health equity by supporting the capacity of people and communities to make choices and exert more control over the context-specific circumstances of their own health and wellbeing (Carnes et al., 2017; Dowden, 2019; Morse et al., 2022). Social prescribing has been shown to address needs rooted in the social determinants of health, to help reduce healthcare demands and costs (Drinkwater et al., 2019). Social determinants of health are especially crucial for older adults who are classified as a high-risk group for experiencing loneliness with negative effects on mental health and wellbeing (Kim et al., 2021). The COVID-19 pandemic has demonstrated an immediate need for new and better-connected interventions that identify, and address needs for individual social connectedness and collective social cohesion (Ndumbe-Eyoh et al., 2021). Different models of social prescribing improve social connections and community resilience by encouraging mutual support and improving access to community services (Younan et al., 2020). However, these services are usually provided by voluntary and community sectors which are facing challenges with increasing demands and insufficient resources (Khan & Giurca, 2023 Morse et al., 2022; Younan et al., 2020). There is also a need to build a collective capacity that supports the sustainability and wellbeing of a health and social services workforce (Khan & Giurca, 2023; Morse et al., 2022).

Despite rapidly growing interest and several local and regional social prescribing initiatives, social prescribing has not yet emerged as a systems-level intervention in most countries (Morse et al., 2022). For example, the UK has social prescribing schemes embedded in its primary care with general practitioners referring patients to link workers whereas different models exist across Canada and the recently created Canadian Institute for Social Prescribing (CISP) is in the process of developing a national framework for social prescribing (Khan & Giurca, 2023). Social prescribing is being piloted in the health systems of other countries such as Austria, Poland, Portugal, Singapore and South Korea (Khan & Giurca, 2023). There is an immediate need to understand the practical context; map existing and emerging social prescribing initiatives; define common languages, practices, and indicators; link to global scale social prescribing research and policy frameworks; and ensure the full incorporation of health equity and community engagement throughout the growth and development of social prescribing models (Hamilton-West et al., 2020; Woodall et al., 2018).

Given that social prescribing is still novel with a recent global Delphi definition and an emerging evidence base (Khan & Giurca, 2023; Muhl et al., 2023), we will include all approaches that meet the global Delphi definition for social prescribing even if authors do not explicitly name social prescribing. We will include the full continuum of social prescribing from signposting to holistic models and clarify the distinction between social prescribing and social prescriptions. The map will examine up-to-date evidence of effectiveness from systematic reviews as well as primary studies to identify gaps and clusters in interventions and outcomes assessed. It will improve the discoverability of available evidence by interest holders including health and social care providers, the public, policymakers, and relevant national and global social prescribing organizations such as the Canadian Institute of Social Prescribing (CISP), the National Academy of Social Prescribing (NASP), the Global Initiative to End Loneliness, Social Prescribing USA, the International Social Prescribing Collaborative, or the Global Social Prescribing Alliance. This map will inform the prioritization of future research for areas where there are gaps. It will also inform future evaluations and guide decisions about investing in social prescribing.

Existing EGMs and/or Relevant Systematic Reviews

To our knowledge, there is only one evidence gap map on social prescribing assessing improvements in health, wellbeing, and healthcare use in patients of all ages (Price et al., 2017). The evidence and gap map covered studies published up to December 2016 and did not focus on older adults.

Several reviews have assessed the effects of social prescribing (Bickerdike et al., 2017; Chatterjee et al., 2018; Cooper et al., 2022; Napierala et al., 2022; Percival et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021) with mixed findings and only two of them were focused on older adults (Percival et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2019). In 2019, Smith et al., assessed the impact of social prescribing on preventing or delaying frailty in community-dwelling adults and did not find any eligible reports. However, a recent review led by Percival and colleagues (2022) assessing social prescribing on older adults in any setting, included only interventions labelled as social prescribing and identified seven studies with considerable positive effects on physical and psychosocial outcomes, depending on the health resource use, although all studies had a considerable risk of bias.

We aim to develop a more comprehensive and up-to- date map that will include social prescribing, social prescription, signposting and care navigation activities related to social prescribing that are focused on older adults.

Objectives

The aim of this evidence and gap map is to map the available evidence on the effectiveness of social prescribing programs and social prescriptions addressing a non-medical, health-related social need for older adults in clinical and community settings.

Specific objectives are as follows.(1) To identify existing evidence from primary studies and systematic reviews on the effects of social prescribing programs and social prescriptions (community-based interventions) that address non-medical, health-related social needs of older adults to improve their health and wellbeing.(2) To identify research evidence gaps to inform the conduct of future high-quality primary studies and systematic reviews assessing the effects of social prescribing/prescription.(3) To highlight evidence of health equity considerations from included primary studies and systematic reviews assessing the effects of social prescribing/prescription.

Methods

We adhered to the Campbell Collaboration guidance for producing evidence and gap maps (White et al., 2020) and the protocol of this EGM (Ghogomu et al., 2024).

Evidence and Gap Map: Definition and Purpose

Evidence and gap maps are a type of systematic evidence synthesis product that visually present the existing evidence and its quality relevant to a specific research question (Snilstveit et al., 2016; White et al., 2020).

The evidence and gap map typically presents findings in a two-dimensional matrix with interventions as row headings and outcomes as column headings (Snilstveit et al., 2016; White et al., 2020). Each cell shows the studies with evidence corresponding to the intervention and outcome of interest. This map will also highlight areas where evidence is lacking for effects of social prescribing interventions and social prescriptions on older adults.

Framework Development and Scope

The development of the intervention-outcome framework for this EGM was informed by existing strategy documents and research studies, and consultation with key interest holders.

We identified and reviewed existing frameworks and typology of intervention and outcome categories used in the following documents: the WHO toolkit on how to implement social prescribing (WHO, 2022), the social prescribing pathway by Bridgeable (Social Prescribing: A holistic approach to improving the health and wellbeing of Canadians) (Bridgeable, 2023), the Common Understanding of Social Prescribing (CUSP) framework (Muhl et al., 2023), National Academy for Social Prescribing resources, the evidence and gap map on social prescribing by Price (Price et al., 2017), and some research studies about social prescribing (Chatterjee et al., 2018; Hamilton-West et al., 2020; Polley et al., 2020).

Since social prescribing and social prescription are a means to connect patients to a range of non-clinical supports and services in the community to improve health and wellbeing, we defined the intervention categories by the types of non-clinical supports and services provided to meet the needs of older adults. The five main categories include lifestyle support, psychosocial support, material support, arts-based activities, and nature. We also considered others including population-specific or culturally safe interventions which could be multicomponent programs, signposting programs, and care navigation.

We considered process outcomes, individual-level outcomes related to health and psychosocial wellbeing, health system usage and cost, as well as community-level outcomes and adverse effects.

The scope of this EGM is to cover all evidence on the effectiveness of social prescribing interventions and social prescriptions in older adults in clinical and community settings.

Interest Holder Engagement

We consulted a 10-member Canadian interest holder advisory group throughout the development of the evidence and gap map via bimonthly virtual meetings. They helped to define the scope and develop the framework for the map, and we presented the framework to the CISP codesign partners for input. The advisory group members and codesign partners were academics, researchers, decision-makers, healthcare professionals and providers of social prescribing from key organizations including Abilities Center, Canadian Coalition for Seniors Mental Health (CCSMH), Canadian Institute of Social Prescribing (CISP), Canadian Red Cross, Healthy Aging Alberta, United Way British Columbia, United Way Centraide Canada, Victorian Order of Nurses (VON), as well as University of Alberta, University of Toronto, and Queen’s University.

At one of the two virtual meetings with 20 codesign partners we discussed the framework in detail, and they suggested we consider other interventions that were provided as social prescribing such as care navigation and population-specific or culturally safe programs. Since men’s shed is a known social prescribing program with multiple components that fit in different categories, we discussed coding it as population-specific under “other” category since these allowed combinations of the other intervention categories into one.

The Advisory group and codesign partners approved the revised framework, and provided input on preliminary findings, and draft map. We also shared preliminary findings and the draft map by email with other interest holders and users of social prescribing services and programs beyond Canada including some members of the National Academy of Social Prescribing (NASP) and the Global Initiative to End Loneliness, to seek their feedback.

Conceptual Framework

Older adults are susceptible to unmet needs due to functional impairment, declining physical and cognitive health, and chronic illnesses (Abdi et al., 2019; Hamilton-West et al., 2020). These needs require care and support that can be provided through a holistic approach addressing complex physical and psychological health and social challenges that older adults face. While there are community support services and programs, they are not readily available and accessible to older adults (Hamilton-West et al., 2020) leading to increased healthcare utilization and costs (Clements-Cortés & Yip, 2020). Social prescribing, or social prescription, is one of the strategies that complements health care by increasing access to non-clinical community support services and programs to help older adults improve their health and wellbeing, to reduce the burden on the healthcare system (Clements-Cortés & Yip, 2020; Elston et al., 2019; Hamilton-West et al., 2020; Muhl et al., 2023; Percival et al., 2022) and to strengthen community connections (Muhl et al., 2023).

Conceptual Framework

Healthy ageing and wellbeing are determined by health conditions such as frailty, depression, dementia, chronic diseases, and sociodemographic characteristics like age, education, sex, socio-economic status, as well as physical and social environments (Clements-Cortés & Yip, 2020; WHO, 2015). By assessing and understanding the state of wellbeing and needs of older adults, an identifier (health practitioners or community workers or volunteers/trusted individuals in the community) can refer them to appropriate community services and programs which can improve their health and wellbeing or refer them to a connector (community link worker or navigator) who will support them to identify their needs and appropriate services to engage with (Elston et al., 2019; Muhl et al., 2023; Percival et al., 2022).

Several models of social prescribing exist, and the theoretical underpinnings have been described using the self-determination theory of motivation (Bhatti et al., 2021; Hanlon et al., 2021; Morse et al., 2022), where the motivation to maintain health and wellbeing is influenced by social and environmental factors (Morse et al., 2022; Ryan & Deci, 2000). The acceptance, uptake, and adherence to services and programs to improve wellbeing are linked with self-determination or self-motivation and depend on the satisfaction of basic psychological needs including autonomy (the need to feel control over one’s activities and behaviours, or life and decisions), competence (the ability to influence outcomes, be capable, and effective), belonging/relatedness (the need to have close, affectionate relationships), and beneficence (having a positive impact on others) (Bhatti et al., 2021; Hanlon et al., 2021; Husk et al., 2019; Morse et al., 2022). Other factors may influence uptake and adherence to programs and behaviours such as availability and visibility of resources, social norms, cultural expectations, education, health literacy, physical, material, or structural barriers to change (Hanlon et al., 2021; Pescheny et al., 2018). In addition, building a trusted relationship between the identifier/connector and the individual may facilitate the implementation of social prescribing and improve acceptance and uptake (Bhatti et al., 2021; Pescheny et al., 2018; White et al., 2022). However, identifiers and connectors must be skilled to reduce the risk of dependency while establishing trust and building self-esteem and empowerment in their clients (Bhatti et al., 2021; White et al., 2022).

Dimensions

Types of Study Design

We included completed or on-going systematic reviews and primary studies including RCTs and evaluative quasi-experimental studies. We also included systematic and scoping reviews based on their PICO question if they consider effectiveness and explicitly describe adequate search methods used to identify studies, eligibility criteria, methods for critical appraisal and synthesis approaches (Moher et al., 2015).

We included quasi-experimental studies if the participants were allocated through alternate assignment, included threshold on a continuous variable (regression discontinuity) or exogenous variation in the treatment allocation (natural experiments) or other rules including self-selection by investigators or participants, provided data was collected contemporaneously in a comparison group (non-equivalent comparison group design), or before and after a discrete intervention (pre-post analysis design).

We included eligible studies irrespective of the publication status.

We excluded primary studies lacking a comparative analysis over time such as cross-sectional studies. We excluded qualitative research without a pre-post evaluation.

Types of Intervention/Problem

We considered all types of social prescribing models addressing a non-medical, health-related need through referral to a connector and then connection to the community service or program as well as social prescription interventions addressing a non-medical, health-related social need through direct connection to the community service or program without prior referral to a connector. The interventions could be conducted in any clinical and community setting.

We also included interventions that met the definition of social prescribing or social prescription (Muhl et al., 2023) but were not labelled as social prescribing or social prescription. We relied on the characteristics of interventions for eligibility not the name social prescribing or social prescription since other names are used such as community connection program, and care navigation.

We considered six categories including lifestyle support, psychosocial support, material support, arts-based activities, nature, and others (population specific or culturally safe interventions, signposting, and care navigation).

In this EGM, we excluded interventions or programs that were provided strictly from a clinical perspective in clinical settings or community settings as part of routine medical care with no social considerations e.g., substance abuse consultation service for hospitalised patients, routine outpatient palliative care, post-stroke home-based rehabilitation by physiotherapists/occupational therapists. Types of Location/Situation (as Applicable)

We included all country settings as defined by the World Health Organization regions (Africa, Regions of the Americas, South-East Region, European Region, Eastern Mediterranean Region, Western Pacific Region) (WHO, 2019) and the World Bank classification by income: low-income economies, lower-middle income economies, upper-middle income incomes, high-income economies (World Bank Country and Lending Groups, 2022).

We included studies conducted in all types of community settings such as community centres or parks, art museums, community-dwelling (residential or personal home), supportive care institutions (long-term care or nursing home and assisted living facilities) and clinical settings if the interventions address a non-medical, health-related social need.

Equity Analysis

We described population sociodemographics using the PROGRESS-Plus factors (O’Neill et al., 2014).

We captured studies that are focused on populations that are at risk of experiencing barriers to health and social care or health inequities (e.g. caregivers). We used the PROGRESS-Plus acronym to describe factors associated with health inequities (O’Neill et al., 2014). For these studies, we documented how potentially vulnerable older people are defined and identified (e.g., using case finding, outreach, screening). Similarly, we coded for analyses that aim to understand potential differences across any PROGRESS-Plus factors.

Dependency

Multiple reports of the same study were treated as one study. A study consisting of multiple interventions or outcomes was displayed accordingly on the map for each intervention or outcome identified. We coded all relevant categories for interventions with multiple components that fall into multiple categories. We mapped systematic reviews to the interventions and outcomes as defined by the question of the systematic review.

If multiple studies in a review were conducted in the same country, the country was coded only once for the review.

Data Collection and Analysis

Screening and Study Selection

Two reviewers from this pool (ETG, VW, VB, MTM, SD, OD, ME, HS, YL, MR, AA, AAA, WK, TH, EA, BA, KT, IR, AI, FJ, LM, and MY), independently screened each title and abstract and full text of potentially eligible articles using EPPI-Reviewer software, and we resolved disagreements through consensus. We screened systematic reviews based on their population, intervention, comparison, outcomes (PICO) framework.

We used the machine learning text mining function in EPPI-Reviewer software to assist in screening at the title and abstract stage. We deployed a priority screening function that developed a classifier based on the probability of inclusion determined by manually screening approximately 10% of the titles and abstracts. All studies were screened independently by two reviewers at the title and abstract stage and the full text stage following the eligibility criteria described above under Dimensions section. We also screened the reference list of eligible systematic reviews to identify additional studies.

Data Extraction and Management

We developed and pilot tested a data collection form in EPPI-Reviewer using 10 studies (Draft in Appendix 2). After the pilot test, members of the team (ETG, MTM, VB, SD, OD, HS, YL, MR, YP, ME, WK, AA, AAA, EA, BA, KT, and MB) individually extracted and coded the data, and 20% of the data extraction and coding was validated by three reviewers (ETG, VB, SD). Any discrepancies in data extraction and coding were resolved through discussion. We did not use any automatic function for coding. The coding categories include study characteristics (study design, publication status, methodological quality assessment of systematic reviews), population description, intervention categories (lifestyle support, psychosocial support, material support, arts, nature, others) and subcategories, outcome domains and subdomains, setting and location (countries where studies were conducted, and the corresponding World Health Organization regions and World Bank classification by income).

We aimed to capture information describing the population using the PROGRESS-Plus framework.

We did not attempt to contact organizations or authors of studies and systematic reviews for missing information given the size of the map.

Tools for Assessing Risk of Bias of Studies/Quality of Included Reviews

The methodological quality of systematic reviews was assessed independently by two reviewers from this pool (ETG, VB, HS, YL, MTM, ME, MR, SD, EA, BA, KT, and AAA) using the AMSTAR 2 tool (Shea et al., 2017). Disagreements were resolved by discussion. We did not assess the risk of bias of primary studies due to the expected volume of studies. However, following the guidance for developing evidence and gap maps (Snilstveit et al., 2016; White et al., 2020), we coded the study designs as randomized controlled trials and non-randomized studies.

Methods for Mapping

The evidence and gap map was developed using the EPPI-Mapping tool (Digital Solution Foundry and EPPI-Centre, 2022).

Synthesis of Included Studies

We included 662 articles that assessed the effectiveness of non-clinical services and programs or interventions which connect individuals to these services through referral to a connector to address social needs and improve their health and wellbeing. See supporting information and references in the interactive EGM.

The majority were primary studies (n = 430, 65%) while only 35% were reviews (n = 232). Primary studies included RCTs (n = 207, 48%) and non-randomized studies (n = 223, 52%). Reviews included systematic reviews (n = 205, 88%), scoping reviews (n = 20, 9%), and umbrella reviews (n = 7, 3%) that were classified according to the AMSTAR2 tool as high quality (n = 9, 4%), moderate quality (n = 13, 6%), low quality (n = 73, 31%), or critically low-quality (n = 128, 55%) reviews.

While most of the articles included in the EGM had been completed (reviews: n = 615, 96%; primary studies: n = 152, 91%), there were some ongoing articles (reviews: n = 7, 3%; primary studies: n = 37, 9%) and conference abstracts (reviews: n = 2, 1%; primary studies: n = 1, 0.2%) included as well.

Of the 662 included articles, almost half assessed social prescribing models which included a formal referral process through a connector (reviews: n = 96, 41%; primary studies: n = 194, 45%) versus social prescription interventions which evaluates the provision of social supports without the referral to a connector (reviews: n = 128, 59%; primary studies: n = 236, 55%). A few reviews (n = 8, 3%) included both social prescribing and social prescription interventions. Only 79 articles used the terms social prescribing or social prescription when describing their intervention (reviews: n = 30, 13%; primary studies: n = 49, 11%) while in the remaining articles, the interventions met our definition of social prescribing/social prescription without labelling them as such. Interventions

The framework for this EGM considered the broad concept of social prescribing that includes social prescription, signposting and care navigation programs focusing on linking people to various types of support and services to address a non-medical need and improve health and wellbeing.

We classified interventions by the types of support and services or programs. Within each category, a variable number of sub-categories used to code interventions was identified. It should be noted that a complex intervention may have fallen under multiple categories and/or sub-categories and would have been coded for all of those that were applicable. Of the 662 included articles, 274 (reviews: n = 134, 58%; primary studies: n = 140, 33%) had interventions coded in multiple categories and 395 (reviews: n = 169, 73%; primary studies: n = 226, 53%) had interventions coded in multiple sub-categories.

The categories were psychosocial support (reviews: n = 187, 81%; primary studies: n = 273, 63%), lifestyle support (reviews: n = 104, 45%; primary studies: n = 131, 30%), arts based activities (reviews: n = 68, 29%; primary studies: n = 85, 20%), material support (reviews: n = 30, 13%; primary studies: n = 28, 7%), nature (reviews: n = 23, 10%; primary studies: n = 17, 4%), and others (reviews: n = 47, 20%; primary studies: n = 95, 22%) (see Figure 4).

Outcomes

Outcomes were coded into one of five categories including process outcomes (reviews: n = 96, 41%; primary studies: n = 201, 47%), individual outcomes (reviews: 227, 98%; primary studies: n = 407, 95%), health system outcomes (reviews: n = 63, 27%; primary studies: n = 118, 27%), community outcomes (reviews: n = 31, 13%; primary studies: n = 38, 9%), and adverse effects (reviews: n = 26, 11%; primary studies: n = 47, 11%) (Figure 5).

As with the interventions, all outcome categories consisted of varying numbers of sub-categories. The category of process outcomes consisted of the five sub-categories: acceptance, uptake, adherence, feasibility, and participant satisfaction/experience. Participant satisfaction/experience was the most reported process outcome for both reviews and primary studies (reviews: n = 68, 29%; primary studies: n = 127, 30%), followed by adherence (reviews: n = 20, 9%; primary studies: n = 56, 13%), acceptance (reviews: n = 13, 6%; primary studies: n = 48, 11%), and feasibility (reviews: n = 9, 4%; primary studies: n = 43, 10%). Uptake was the least reported (reviews: n = 11, 5%; primary studies: n = 24, 6%) (Figure 6).

Individual outcomes were the most common type of outcomes and included 10 subcategories: wellbeing/quality of life (reviews: n 183, 79%; primary studies: n = 323, 75%), anxiety/depression (reviews: n = 112, 48%; primary studies: n = 163, 38%), social connections (reviews: n = 93, 40%; primary studies: n = 131, 30%), self-esteem or self-management (reviews: n = 78, 34%; primary studies: n = 114, 27%), social support (reviews: n = 65, 28%; primary studies: n = 113, 26%). Other individual outcomes that were less reported were physical activity (reviews: n = 44, 19%; primary studies: n = 94, 22%), social participation (reviews: n = 45, 19%; primary studies: n = 60, 14%), physiological outcomes (reviews: n = 42, 18%; primary studies: n = 63, 15%), empowerment (reviews: n = 25, 11%, primary studies: n = 21, 5%), and trust (reviews: n = 2, 1%; primary studies: n = 7, 2%) (Figure 7).

Health system outcomes included health or social service use (reviews: n = 47, 20%; primary studies: n = 86, 20%), cost-effectiveness (reviews: n = 17, 5%; primary studies: n = 32, 7%), healthcare or social care utilization cost (reviews: n = 12, 7%; primary studies: n = 32, 7%), care team wellbeing or experience (reviews: n = 11, 5%; primary studies: n = 15, 3%) and health system integration (reviews: n = 3, 1%; primary studies: n = 6, 1%) (Figure 8).

Community outcomes were not commonly assessed in general and included five subcategories: community connections (reviews: n = 23, 10%; primary studies: n = 25, 6%), community impact (reviews: n = 7, 3%; primary studies: n = 9, 2%), civic participation (reviews: n = 6, 3%; primary studies: n = 4, 1%), community resilience (reviews: n = 2, 1%; primary studies: n = 4, 1%), and health equity, which was not reported in any included review or study (Figure 9).

Adverse effects were hardly reported and included unintended outcomes (reviews: n = 23, 10%; primary studies: n = 40, 9%) and stigma (reviews: n = 3, 1%; primary studies: n = 7, 2%). Unintended outcomes included adverse effects such as worsening outcomes (e.g. anxiety and depression, loneliness, hypoglycaemia), increased emergency department/GP visits, hospitalizations, falls, injuries, and mortality.

Quality of Included Reviews

We appraised the quality of 223 systematic reviews using AMSTAR2. The results indicated that most (>80%) were critically low quality (n = 128, 57%), or low quality (n = 73, 33%). Only a few (10%) were moderate quality (n = 13, 6%) or high quality (n = 9, 4%). Protocols (n = 7) and conference abstracts (n = 2) were not appraised for quality.

The quality of the reviews was downgraded because of three main critical flaws including lack of justification for excluded studies (n = 189, 81%), absence of an explicit statement that the review methods were established a priori (n = 102, 44%), or lack of considering risk of bias when interpreting results in the review (n = 63, 30%).

All seven umbrella reviews assessed the methodological quality of the included systematic reviews using AMSTAR or the JBI checklist for systematic reviews and only one did not consider the quality when interpreting results. We did not assess item 9 (risk of bias assessment of individual studies included in the review) and item 13 (consideration of risk of bias when interpreting results of the review) for 18 scoping reviews since they are optional requirements (Peters et al., 2021). Intervention Characteristics

In addition, we coded the format in which interventions were provided – as technology-based or non-technology-based. The majority were non-technology based (reviews: n = 175, 75%; primary studies: n = 326, 76%). Some were technology based (reviews: n = 106, 46%; primary studies: n = 121, 28%) and others did not specify the format (reviews: n = 32, 14%; primary studies: n = 32, 7%).

When information about referral pathways was available, we coded the types of connectors involved and the points of connection where referrals were made. Some articles had multiple types of connectors (e.g., healthcare professionals and link workers) or multiple points of connection (e.g., medical facility and community), and all were coded.

Most participants in the articles were referred to the intervention by a healthcare professional (reviews: n = 77, 33%; primary studies: n = 182, 42%). In the reviews, referral by a lay person or non-professional (n = 37, 16%) was the second most common, followed by the researchers (n = 29, 13%), while just over half of the reviews did not specify this information (n = 129, 56%). For the primary studies, referral from the researchers (n = 153, 36%) was the second most common, followed by referral from a lay person (n = 53, 12%), and few studies did not specify the type of connector (n = 73, 17%) (Figure 14).

Point of connection: Connection in the community (reviews: n = 96, 41%; primary studies: n = 238, 55%) was the most common, followed by a medical facility (reviews: n = 55, 24%; primary studies: n = 106, 25%) and then a participant’s home (reviews: n = 41, 18%; primary studies: n = 67, 16%). Over 40% of the reviews did not specify this information (n = 113, 49%), while less than 20% of primary studies did not specify the point of connection (n = 63, 15%) (Figure 15).

Discussion

Summary of Main Results

This evidence and gap map has 622 included articles of which 430 are primary studies and 232 are reviews. Over 60% were published between 2019 and 2023, and the majority were conducted in the UK and USA. More than half of the reviews were critically low quality and only 4% were high quality reviews.

There is a similar pattern of gaps and evidence on interventions and outcomes across the included reviews and primary studies in general. Psychosocial support, lifestyle support and art-based activities were the most common interventions assessed. Although referral of participants was done mostly by healthcare professionals, the most common point of connection was the community as some healthcare professionals also saw patients in the community. Only a few studies indicated that participants were involved in codesigning the interventions or that the intervention was designed to empower participants. The lack of detail on empowerment may be due to the broad scope used in this EGM, and the inclusion of light touch interventions such as signposting and social prescription interventions with no referral to a connector.

Individual outcomes, process outcomes, and health system outcomes were the most common compared to community outcomes and adverse effects. Among the individual outcomes, wellbeing or quality of life were reported the most while participant satisfaction/experience was the most reported process outcome and health/social service use was the most reported health system outcome.

Social and emotional needs were the most addressed non-medical needs of participants, and the most common age range was 60-75 years old. Most of the included articles focused on patients with specific health conditions such as cancer, diabetes, dementia, or depression. Participants were identified through a community-based program or screening in primary care most often. Less than 10% of primary studies conducted equity analyses to understand differences in intervention effect across gender or sex, and age, especially.

Areas of Major Gaps in the Evidence

Major gaps were identified in outcome measures including adverse effects and community outcomes. Only 11% of reviews and 11% of primary studies reported adverse effects such as worsening anxiety and depression, increased emergency/GP visits and falls and injuries. For community outcomes, only 1% of reviews and 1% of primary studies reported community resilience as an outcome and health equity was not reported in any review or primary study. Process outcomes such as uptake and feasibility were the least reported for that category of outcomes. Although individual outcomes were commonly reported, empowerment and trust were hardly reported. Likewise, health system integration, care team wellbeing or experience, and health system cost were not well reported among health system outcomes.

Although all intervention categories were represented, there were few studies and reviews on material support and nature interventions, for example, only one review each assessed legal and citizen advice and support or green gyms, deprescribing for greenhouse gas reductions. Very few studies and reviews described important characteristics of self-determination associated with social prescribing or prescription like codesign and empowerment.

Very few studies and reviews were conducted in low- and middle-income countries. There was very sparse evidence on specific vulnerable populations. For example, we found only three reviews on older adults with disability, two reviews including LGBTQIA2S + communities and no review or primary study focused only on oldest-old participants aged 80 or older.

Potential Biases in the Mapping Process

We followed rigorous methods to limit potential biases. Our search strategy was developed by an experienced information retrieval specialist using common terms, and seed articles, and covered published literature until November 14, 2023. We screened all identified records and references of included reviews and assessed the quality of the reviews in pairs. Since our research question was about the effectiveness of social prescribing or prescription, we included only study designs that used a comparative analysis over time.

Our framework considered the broad concept of social prescribing and included any intervention that connects individuals with non-medical needs to services and activities to improve their health and wellbeing even if the intervention was not designated as social prescribing. We expanded to include social prescription interventions based on consultations with the commissioner and end users who were interested in seeing specifically prescribed interventions on the map.

Although our search strategy was comprehensive for community-based social prescribing and social prescriptions, we may have missed some studies because we did not search for every specific term possible for social prescription such as gardening, volunteering. Our search was based on iterative testing and seed articles, and the performance of the search strategy was validated with the retrieval of all seed articles (MacDonald et al., 2024). However, we may have missed some publications because some databases were searched as a bundle which limits the use of subject headings available in individual databases. We also did forward and backward citation searching to reduce the risk of missing relevant articles but may have missed recent publications since research in this area is growing rapidly. We did not contact organizations or authors for missing information which might have helped to identify unpublished research. We are planning an update of this map and will include terms to improve the search for social prescriptions. We will use terms from the categorizations of community support and services from guidance documents on social prescribing e.g., the WHO toolkit on how to implement social prescribing (WHO, 2022), from broad reviews included in our map and from consultation with interest holders.

We included studies conducted in people aged 18 and over if they have no age restrictions since the studies may also include older adults. Although the focus may not be on an older population, the findings of these studies may be relevant for the emerging evidence base of social prescribing for older adults.

We assessed the quality of the methods used in the included reviews but not the quality of included primary studies. One of the goals of evidence and gap maps is to support evidence-informed decision-making. Evidence from reviews included in evidence and gap maps can support informed decision-making whereas evidence from primary studies can identify gaps in research rather than inform decision-making. Therefore, it is not essential to appraise the quality of primary studies included in evidence and gap maps that have both reviews and primary studies.

Limitations of the EGM

Since the focus of this evidence and gap map is on the effectiveness of social prescribing and prescription interventions, we included only studies with comparative analysis over time. Although studies with no comparative analysis may provide important information about social prescribing and prescription (e.g., contextual and implementation factors like barriers and facilitators), they do not assess effectiveness.

Since social prescribing or prescription involves addressing the needs of individuals, some individuals may require interventions to address complex needs. We set up intervention categories and subcategories to meet knowledge user needs, which means there are some interventions which fit in more than one category.

Interest Holder Engagement Throughout the EGM Process

We engaged interest holders from the Canadian Institute of Social Prescribing as the advisory board and sought global input throughout the EGM process. There were no changes in our interest holder engagement plan throughout the EGM process.

Authors’ Conclusions

Implications for Research, Practice and/or Policy

As the global population continues to age, the challenges faced by an ageing population become more prominent. Not all these challenges are medical and can be treated through visits to healthcare providers. Many are non-medical challenges, such as low access to community support or resources or social relationship and support network problems. This evidence and gap map shows the available evidence regarding social prescribing interventions and social prescriptions addressing non-medical needs for older adults. Of the 232 reviews that were included in this map, over 50% were of critically low quality, while 4% were of high quality. More than 60% of these reviews were published in 2019 or later, correlating with the COVID-19 pandemic which highlighted that many older adults had challenges with small or distant social support networks. Most of the evidence in the map is clustered around psychosocial support interventions, lifestyle support and arts-based activities which focus on individual outcomes. Specifically, most of the included reviews reported a social and emotional support intervention, exercise on prescription, or museums, galleries, and art & crafts. Most of these interventions adopted an individualized approach to reporting outcomes and looked at participant wellbeing or quality of life. Most included articles focused on older adults aged 65-75 years old and vulnerable populations across PROGRESS-Plus factors, especially participants who had a specific health condition, such as cancer, diabetes, dementia, or depression.

Major gaps in the evidence can be seen in research that looks at social prescribing and prescription interventions of nature activities, namely green gyms, deprescribing for greenhouse gas reductions and material support such as legal and citizen advice and support. Other gaps are in research assessing participant adverse effects from interventions, community outcomes, such as health equity and community resilience, process outcomes, such as feasibility, adherence, and uptake, as well as health system outcomes including health system integration, care team wellbeing or experience, and health system cost. If the non-medical needs of older adults are to be met, it is important to not only consider the effectiveness of the interventions, but how well the participants respond to them, as well as if there are any adverse effects experienced.

Though many included articles reported focusing on specific populations experiencing health inequities, very few focused on people from the LGBTQIA2S + community and none focused on the oldest old. Very few articles performed an equity analysis to assess differences in effects across PROGRESS-Plus factors. Furthermore, most of the research was conducted in high-income countries, specifically the UK and USA, while almost no articles came from low- and middle-income countries. Many older adults have complex, multi-morbid health conditions which only serve to exacerbate their non-medical needs. As such, we need whole system solutions rather than those which only target one aspect or area of an older adult’s needs and may only be effective for a certain population/community.

Despite recent increases in systematic reviews in the past few years, most reviews are of low or critically low quality. Most available primary research focused on social and emotional support interventions which aimed to improve participant wellbeing/quality of life. The clustering of evidence emphasizes the need for more high-quality reviews answering more diverse and holistic research questions, with less focus solely on individual outcomes.

This evidence and gap map contributes towards developing an evidence base that can be used to guide future researchers and funders to fill in some of the identified evidence gaps, as well as inform future policy decisions. For example, priority should be given to studies evaluating nature-based and material support interventions. Studies evaluating characteristics of social prescribing interventions such as delivery schemes, codesign and empowerment, the role of various connectors, as well as studies involving under-represented populations such as the oldest old and people in rural settings or low- and middle-income countries. Economic and social impact evaluations would also strengthen the evidence base and enhance relevance and applicability of the evidence for policy and practice.
