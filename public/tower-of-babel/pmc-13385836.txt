Interfacial dipolar interactions drive giant second-harmonic generation in 2D organic–inorganic heterostructures
Authors: Ye-Tao Chen, Shi-Yi Yuan, Xiu-Bin Liu, Xiu-Qi Shi, Xi Xu, Yi-Neng Bao, Keng Chen, Jun-Rong Zheng, Zi-Feng Guo, Zhao-Dong Meng, Kaichao Deng, Kai-Qiang Lin, Hua-Lei Yin, Jun Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interfacial coupling governs the emergent functionality of two-dimensional (2D) organic–inorganic heterostructures, underpinning advances in linear optoelectronics such as photovoltaics. However, extending this control to the nonlinear optical regime remains elusive, as it requires driving the coherent nonlinear polarization of the inorganic lattice using molecular states, which is distinct from the incoherent charge transfer exploited in linear devices. Here we report a giant second-harmonic generation (SHG) response driven purely by interfacial near-field dipole coupling in an α‑perylene organic crystal (α‑Pe)/WS2 heterostructure. We show that the specific crystalline anisotropy of the organic layer creates a polarization-tunable antenna, where anisotropic dipole–dipole coupling efficiently channels excitation energy into the WS2 layer to drive the nonlinear process. This mechanism yields a spatially uniform, giant effective second-order susceptibility \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{\chi }}}}}_{{{\rm{eff}}}}^{(2)}$$\end{document}χeff(2) approaching ~ 20 nm V−1 in monolayers and, notably, induces interfacial symmetry breaking to activate robust SHG in nominally centrosymmetric bilayer WS2. Our results establish organic–inorganic interfacial coupling as a generalizable lever for engineering nonlinear optical responses; given the vast abundance of molecular crystals with distinct dipolar symmetries, this strategy offers a scalable route to compact, chemically designable on-chip nonlinear photonic systems.

Introduction

Hybrid organic–inorganic interfaces have emerged as a powerful paradigm for engineering quantum matter, merging the chemical programmability of molecular solids with the robust electronic bands of atomically thin lattices.1,2 This approach combines the complementary electrical and optical properties of the constituent materials and leverages interfacial interactions to elicit emergent functionalities, thereby extending the capabilities of the individual components.3,4 In recent years, such heterostructures have shown great promise in optoelectronic devices including organic field effect transistors,5 memory,6 photodetectors7 and solar cells,8 with organic-transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) systems attracting particular attention. For instance, functionalizing TMDC with photochromic molecules has enabled optically switchable memory devices where molecular isomerization gates the channel conductivity.9 However, while the incoherent transfer of charge carriers has been widely exploited for linear optoelectronics, extending this interfacial control to the nonlinear optical regime remains a formidable challenge. Nonlinear processes require driving a coherent polarization in the inorganic lattice, which is generally distinct from the population dynamics that govern photovoltaics or sensing.

This gap is particularly critical because monolayer TMDCs are intrinsically potent nonlinear media.10 In particular, their second-order susceptibility (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi }^{(2)}$$\end{document}χ(2)) can exceed that of conventional nonlinear crystals by a wide margin. For example, the typical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi }^{(2)}$$\end{document}χ(2) of monolayer WS2 is approximately 0.45 nm V−1,11 more than two orders of magnitude larger than that of traditional nonlinear crystals, such as beta barium borate (BBO).12 With their remarkably high second-order susceptibility and sub-nanometer thickness, TMDCs are poised as promising platforms for laser frequency conversion,13 Terahertz photonics14 and quantum nonlinear optics.15 Nevertheless, the intrinsically limited interaction length and the absence of phase matching constrain the absolute light–matter interaction strength and hinder efficient device operation.16,17 Structural phase engineering, most notably through 3R-type stacking, has been shown to enhance SHG by lifting inversion symmetry.18 Alongside such intrinsic routes, extrinsic methods based on external electric fields, doping or strain have also been investigated,19,20 although the resulting improvements generally remain below one order of magnitude.21 By contrast, photonic strategies, such as plasmonic nanocavities, waveguides22–24 and metamaterials25 can deliver electromagnetic field enhancements of several orders of magnitude, however, these rely on localized “hotspots” that inherently degrade spatial uniformity. Such inhomogeneity prohibits scalable device integration, leaving a critical need for a strategy that can deliver giant, uniform enhancement without complex nanostructuring.

Organic-inorganic hybrid interfaces offer an effective route to overcome nonuniform SHG enhancement and the constraint of limited interaction length. The central idea is to engineer the interfacial coupling between organic layers and TMDCs to enable precise control over the TMDCs’ optical response. In linear regimes, strong dipole–dipole coupling (e.g., in J-aggregate/TMDCs systems) has been shown to boost excitonic absorption by nearly 100%.26 Yet, whether such interfacial coupling can provide spatially uniform modulation of second‑order nonlinear processes remains unclear, particularly under near‑infrared pump wavelengths typically used for SHG (for example, 800 nm), where TMDCs generally lack resonant excitonic transitions.

Herein, we report a giant, tunable, and spatially uniform enhancement of SHG in an α-perylene (α-Pe) crystal/monolayer (1 L) WS2 van der Waals heterostructure. Pe, as a prototypical photosensitizer, combines robust optical absorption with a fluorescence quantum yield exceeding 99%,27,28 and forms single crystals that display pronounced optical anisotropy and strong light–matter interactions.29–31 Leveraging these properties, we employ α-Pe single crystals as highly efficient interfacial coupling partners with 1 L WS2 and exploit their intrinsic anisotropy to realize a tunable, spatially uniform modulation of the SHG response across large areas. This strategy demonstrates that 2D organic–inorganic32,33 interfacial coupling can augment and control the nonlinear optical properties of TMDCs, bypassing thickness-limited interaction length, while establishing design rules for compact, tunable, and integrable on-chip nonlinear photonic devices.

Results

Uniform SHG enhancement in α-Pe/WS2 heterostructures

We fabricated high-quality heterostructures by integrating mechanically exfoliated 1 L WS2 with solution-grown α-Pe single crystals (Fig. S1). The square-shaped α-Pe, a quasi-2D organic single crystal32,33 with an average lateral size of ~50 μm and a thickness of 250 nm, was prepared through a spatially confined self-assembly method (see Methods and Fig. S1b for details). X‑ray diffraction (Fig. S2) showed diffraction peaks consistent with prior reports,34 confirming phase‑pure α-Pe crystals. Interfacial dipolar energy transfer dominated SHG enhancement

To account for the large‑area, spatially uniform SHG enhancement in α-Pe/WS2, generally, there are three candidate mechanisms involved: (1) optical‑cavity mediated local‑field enhancement35,36 arising from the finite thickness of the α‑Pe crystal; (2) interfacial charge transfer37,38 that further breaks inversion symmetry at the WS2 layer and increases the nonlinear susceptibility; and (3) interfacial energy transfer39,40 between α‑Pe and WS2, in which donor‑to‑acceptor energy funneling enhances the acceptor’s optical response.

We first assessed cavity‑driven local‑field effects. Because the thin‑film cavity resonances depend sensitively on thickness, such a mechanism would modulate the enhancement at both the excitation (800 nm) and second‑harmonic (400 nm) wavelengths. We quantitatively calculated the optical field distribution at the WS2 interface for α-Pe crystals of different thicknesses (Fig. S6) and compared these predictions with thickness-dependent measurements (Fig. S7). Both analyses indicate that cavity‑induced local fields can account for only an approximately threefold increase in SHG intensity, far below the ~650-fold enhancement observed in α-Pe/WS2, demonstrating that optical cavity effects make at most a minor contribution in our system.

To elucidate the origin of the giant SHG enhancement, we first measured the excitation-wavelength dependence of the SHG emission from the pristine 1 L WS2 and α-Pe/WS2 heterostructure (Fig. S9a, b). Notably, the pristine 1 L WS2 reference was taken as the averaged response of three independent samples measured under identical conditions to improve the reliability of the analysis. (Fig. S8). The SHG intensity exhibits a pronounced maximum under ~800 nm excitation, which coincides with a strong absorption band of the α-Pe/WS2 stack, whereas isolated monolayer WS2 shows negligible absorption in this spectral range (Fig. S9c). This correlation indicates that the enhancement is resonantly boosted at 800 nm by the pronounced absorption of the heterostructure. A possible microscopic origin is interfacial charge transfer, which would require good electrical contact between the two components. To test whether interfacial charge transfer is responsible for the SHG enhancement, we introduced a hBN spacer layer (3.4 nm) between α-Pe and WS2 (Fig. 2a). Across various excitation wavelengths (Fig. 2b, Fig. S10), the α-Pe/hBN/WS2 heterostructure exhibits a giant SHG response comparable to that of α-Pe/WS2, indicating that introducing an insulating spacer does not degrade the SHG enhancement. As a control, removing the WS2 layer yields α-Pe/hBN, which produces negligible SHG under the same 800 nm excitation and detection settings (Fig. S11), excluding contributions from hBN itself or from the α-Pe/hBN interface. Polarization‑resolved SHG provides an independent signature of interfacial coupling. In pristine 1 L WS2, the polar plot of the SHG intensity follows the expected six‑lobed pattern set by the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{3{{{\rm{v}}}}}$$\end{document}C3v crystal symmetry. By contrast, the α-Pe/WS2 heterostructure displays a pronounced figure‑eight, two‑lobe pattern (Fig. S12), with the lobe orientation aligned with one of the molecular dipole axis of α‑Pe. This symmetry reduction and dipolar character are also observed in the α-Pe/hBN/WS2 sample (Fig. S13), which indicates that molecular dipoles of α-Pe might couple to and reorient the effective nonlinear response of WS2.

Such dipolar coupling is conceptually aligned with prior observations by Xiong and co‑workers,40 who reported interfacial energy transfer induced SHG enhancement in CdSe/ZnS quantum dot/MoS2 heterostructures, where donors excited via two‑photon absorption funneled energy into the 2D layer and boosted its SHG by nearly two orders of magnitude. Motivated by this framework, we measured SHG and two-photon–excited photoluminescence (TPL) from pristine 1 L WS2, α-Pe crystals, and the α-Pe/WS2 heterostructure under 800 nm femtosecond excitation (Fig. 2c). Both 1 L WS2 and α-Pe/WS2 exhibit a sharp 400 nm peak corresponding to the SHG, while α-Pe shows a broad band centered at ~575 nm assigned to TPL (Fig. S14).41 The Pristine 1 L WS2 displays negligible TPL under identical conditions. Strikingly, in the heterostructure the giant SHG enhancement is accompanied by pronounced quenching of α-Pe TPL. Statistical measurements on 20 independently fabricated α-Pe/WS2 heterostructures show a robust anticorrelation between SHG and TPL (Fig. 2d). Across the dataset, strong SHG is consistently correlated with weak TPL in the α-Pe/WS2, which indicates depletion of the excited-state population when interfaced with WS2 and is consistent with interfacial energy transfer that increases the effective nonlinear susceptibility of the hybrid, thereby amplifying the SHG.

Since the interfacial energy‑transfer efficiency is highly sensitive to donor–acceptor separation distance, we modulated the interaction distance by inserting hBN spacer layers of precisely controlled thickness at the α-Pe/WS2 interface, defining d as the interlayer separation (Fig. S15). To verify the reliability of this thickness-dependent behavior, we present nine representative samples, chosen from the full set of 16 heterostructures, together with their AFM images, corresponding height profiles, and SHG/TPL spectra (Fig. S16). From a photophysical perspective, this structural anisotropy should dictate distinct excitonic behavior.46 The co-facial stacking along the a-axis corresponds to H-aggregate formation, where positive dipole coupling yields blue-shifted, optically dark states that suppress far-field radiation. We speculate that these H-type excitons, unable to decay radiatively, delocalize coherently along the stacking direction, effectively behaving as the 1D polarization line sources required for near-field coupling. Conversely, the b-axis stacking promotes J-aggregate-like behavior, creating bright radiative states that couple efficiently to the far field (Fig. S18) but contribute little to the localized near field.

To validate this hypothesis and confirm the H-aggregate origin of the 1D behavior, we performed polarization-resolved spectroscopy on the α-Pe/WS2 heterostructure. The linear absorption spectra is quite similar to that of α-Pe crystal (Fig. 3c, Fig. S19), which reveals alternating dominance of b‑ and a‑axis dipoles with analyzer angle: b‑axis features dominate at 0°/180°, while a‑axis features dominate at 90°/270°. It provides the first line of evidence that the a-axis resonance is blue-shifted by ~114 meV relative to the b-axis, a canonical signature of H-aggregation. However, the nonlinear response reveals the most interesting verification (Fig. 3d). Despite comparable linear absorption strengths near the 800 nm pump, the two axes yield diametrically opposite outcomes. When excitation aligns with the radiative b-axis (J-type), the system exhibits strong TPL but negligible SHG enhancement. In sharp contrast, aligning with the non-radiative a-axis (H-type) quenches the TPL while triggering a giant SHG response from the WS2, confirming that the near-field drive is exclusively mediated by the a-axis dipoles.

To enable direct comparison with the monolayer case, we further measured the excitation-polarization dependence of the SHG from the α-Pe/2 L WS2 heterostructure over a full 0–360° range. As shown in Fig. S22, the SHG exhibits a pronounced two-lobed pattern with maxima at 90° and 270° and minima at 0° and 180°. This behavior is consistent with that of the monolayer heterostructure, and the strongest response occurs when the excitation polarization is parallel to the α-Pe a-axis. Guided by this result, we performed SHG mapping at the representative minimum and maximum polarization conditions. As shown in Fig. 4b, the SHG signal is strongly suppressed at 0° excitation, whereas it becomes markedly enhanced and spatially uniform across the flake at 90°, where the excitation is aligned with the α-Pe a-axis, suggesting that the SHG activation in bilayer WS2 is associated with symmetry perturbation induced by interfacial coupling with α-Pe. Under identical measurement conditions, a direct comparison between pristine 2 L WS2 and the α‑Pe/2 L WS2 heterostructure (Fig. 4c) reveals a distinct contrast. Remarkably, although the inversion‑symmetry could be slightly broken by the substrate, the bare 2 L WS2 exhibits almost negligible SHG, whereas the heterostructure shows a pronounced enhanced SHG signal due to the interfacial energy transfer.

Discussion

We demonstrate an interface-driven strategy to engineer giant second-order nonlinearity in van der Waals heterostructures by exploiting the coherent coupling between organic molecular crystals and inorganic 2D lattices. Using α‑Pe/WS2 as model system, we achieved a spatially uniform, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}~650-fold enhancement of SHG in monolayers, yielding an effective \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{\chi }}}}}_{{{\rm{eff}}}}^{(2)}$$\end{document}χeff(2) approaching ~20 nm V−1, and crucially, overcame intrinsic symmetry constraints to activate robust SHG in centrosymmetric bilayer. We identify the crystalline anisotropy of the organic layer as a functional control knob, enabling polarization-tunable gating of the interfacial energy transfer. This mechanism is rigorously corroborated by spacer-dependent spectroscopy, SHG–TPL anticorrelation, and a characteristic distance scaling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\propto {d}^{-3}$$\end{document}∝d−3) consistent with reduced-dimensional dipole–dipole interactions. Collectively, these findings provide a blueprint for overcoming the interaction-length limitations of atomically thin materials, positioning interfacial dipole engineering as a versatile, scalable platform for realizing low-power, chip-integrated nonlinear photonics.

Methods

Preparation of the two-dimensional organic crystals of α-perylene

Perylene (99%+) and TBAB (tetrabutylammonium bromide) were purchased from Innochem and used without further purification. Two-dimensional square-shaped perylene crystalline nanosheets were obtained by a spatially confined self-assembly method.47 In a typical procedure, 50 μL of a 0.5 mg/mL perylene solution in chlorobenzene was gently dropped onto the surface of a 1 mg/mL TBAB aqueous solution. After complete evaporation of chlorobenzene, two-dimensional square perylene nanosheets with an approximate thickness of 250 nm formed at the water–air interface and were transferred onto Si/SiO2 substrates.

Fabrication of α‑Pe /WS2 hybrids

Monolayer WS2 flakes were prepared by mechanical exfoliation. Bulk WS2 crystals were first exfoliated using clean blue tape, and the exfoliated flakes were transferred onto a Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp. The flakes on PDMS were then repeatedly picked up and re-exfoliated with blue tape to progressively reduce the thickness, yielding monolayer WS2 flakes. Subsequently, the monolayer WS₂ was transferred onto α‑Pe crystals pre-deposited on Si/SiO2 substrates by a dry-transfer process at 55 °C, thereby forming the α‑Pe/WS2 heterojunction.

Sample characterization

The arrangement of α‑Pe was determined by single-crystal diffraction (XRD, Oxford Diffraction Gemini, Oxford Diffraction Company) with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 30 Ma). In addition, the crystal form of α‑Pe was further identified through X-ray Powder Diffractometer (Ultima-IV, Rigaku). Raman spectra were gained by a Microscopic Raman spectrometer of Xplora Plus from HORIBA. All Raman spectra are recorded at room temperature, through the excitation of 532 nm laser focused using a Nikon objective (×100, NA = 0.95), with the power of 1.5 mW and the grating of 600 gr·mm−1. Additionally, for the absorption spectrum measurement of the heterojunction formed by crystalline α‑Pe and 1 L WS2, white light was focused onto the sample using a 100× Mitutoyo objective lens (M Plan Apo-NIR, 100×/0.5 NA), and the signal was collected via a spectrometer (iHR550, HORIBA).

Second-harmonic generation measurements

The SHG measurements were performed using the optical setup shown in Fig. S3. A laser beam (800 nm, pulse width 140 fs, repetition rate 80 MHz) was polarized (film linear polarizer, OPP-WG20, JCOTIX) and filtered (680 long-pass filter, LBTEK) before being directed by a beam-splitter (UV fused Silica broadband plate beam-splitter, THORLABS) onto the sample through a half-wave plate (super-achromatic half-wave plate, Bernhard Halle Nachfl) and objective lens (M Plan Apo-NIR, 100×/0.5 NA, Mitutoyo). The generated SHG signal was collected in reflection, passed through the same half-wave plate, a short-pass filter (FF01-680/SP-25, Semrock) and analyzer (film linear polarizer, OPP-WG20, JCOTIX), and finally recorded by a spectrometer (iHR550, HORIBA) equipped with a UV enhanced CCD detector (Horiba SynapsePlus-BIUV). Based on this system, polarization-dependent SHG measurements were obtained by rotating the half-wave plate in a step, and the detected intensity was normalized by the laser power. Similarly, the same system was employed for polarization-dependent PL measurements. In this configuration, the excitation source was replaced with a 405 nm continuous-wave laser, and a 410 nm long-pass filter was inserted in the collection path to suppress the residual excitation light. The polarization of both excitation and emission was controlled by a half-wave plate and analyzer, respectively, and the PL spectra were collected by the spectrometer.

Second-harmonic generation mapping measurements

An XYZ three-dimensional nano-positioning stage (P11.XYZ100S, COREMORROW) was integrated into the system, enabling SHG mapping measurements of heterostructure samples (Fig. 1c). The mapping was performed in a point-by-point scanning mode. At each position within the target area, an SHG spectrum was acquired with a predefined step size. The collected spectra were fitted with a Gaussian function, and the peak intensity was extracted to represent the SHG signal at each point. The spatially resolved SHG map was then constructed by arranging these intensity values according to their corresponding coordinates. An excitation power of 1 mW was used for SHG mapping of 1 L WS2. For the mapping of heterostructure, a lower excitation power of 0.3 mW was used to minimize potential laser-induced damage of α-Pe crystal during the prolonged mapping process.
