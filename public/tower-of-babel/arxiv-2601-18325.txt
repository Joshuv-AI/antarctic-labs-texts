Discrete spectrum from local perturbations of leaky curves
Pavel Exner

Abstract
. We discuss spectrum of a class of singular Schrödinger operator models known as leaky curves and show that if the interaction support has a periodic shape, its local perturbations can give rise to a discrete spectrum below the continuum threshold even if they are of ‘zero mean’.

1.
Introduction

Being aware that main interests of the man we are celebrating are focused on many-body systems, I choose the topic of this paper with the hope that he would agree with the phrase I heard many years ago from the great physicist Mahito Kohmoto: ‘Surprisingly, there are still interesting effects in one-body quantum mechanics’. The system I am going to discuss is simple indeed; its Hamiltonian is a singular Schrödinger operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
formally given by

(1.1)

H_{\alpha,\Gamma}=-\Delta-\alpha\delta(x-\Gamma),\quad\alpha>0,

where
\Gamma
is a sufficiently regular curve. It is well known that the geometry of
\Gamma
induces an effective interaction. For instance, if the curve is straight asymptotically but not globally, operator (
1.1
) typically has at least one eigenvalue below the continuum starting at
-\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}

[
EI01
]
.

In the present paper we deal with local perturbations of
periodic
curves. We prove sufficient conditions under which they give rise to eigenvalues at the bottom of the spectrum. Not surprisingly, it happens if the perturbation represents a local contraction, but we are going to show that this is possible even in the ‘critical’ case when the perturbation is of zero mean.

2.
Preliminaries

Let us now describe the model in more precise terms. We suppose that the curve
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is Lipschitz and piecewise
C^{1}
; with an abuse of notation we use the same symbol for the map and its image. The formal expression (
1.1
) can be then naturally given meaning using the quadratic form

(2.1)

q_{\alpha,\Gamma}:q_{\alpha,\Gamma}[\psi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla\psi(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(\Gamma(s))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where
s
is the arc length of
\Gamma
. The domain of (
2.1
) is
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and the form is closed and bounded from below, cf.
[
BEKŠ94
, Sec. 2]
or
[
Ex08
, Sec. 2.1]
, being thus uniquely associated with a self-adjoint operator for which we use again the symbol
H_{\alpha,\Gamma}
.

The unperturbed system will correspond to a periodic curve. For simplicity we restrict ourselves to the case when
\Gamma
is a graph of a function; without loss of generality we may fix the Cartesian coordinates and assume that

(i)

\Gamma_{0}(s)=(x_{1},x_{2}=\gamma(x_{1}))
with a Lipschitz function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, piecewise
C^{1}
and such that
\gamma(x_{1}+a)=\gamma(x_{1})
for some
a>0
, where
x_{1}=x_{1}(s)
is the inverse function to
s:\>s(x_{1})=\int^{x_{1}}_{0}\sqrt{1+\gamma^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi
. We exclude the trivial case when
\gamma
is a constant function.

Proposition 2.1
.

Under the assumption (
i
), the spectrum of operator
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}
is purely essential,

\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)\supset\mathbb{R}_{+}

with
\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)<0
; the negative part is absolutely continuous.

Proof.

In view of the singular potential periodicity one can use Floquet decomposition: with an abuse of notation we write

(2.2)

H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}=\frac{a}{2\pi}\int_{|\pi\theta|\leq a}^{\oplus}H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

where the right-hand side has to be understood as
UH_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}U^{-1}
with the unitary
U:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(S_{a})\times[-\frac{\pi}{a},\frac{\pi}{a}]
defined on
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

(U\psi)(x,\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{-im\theta}\psi\Big(x_{1}+ma,x_{2}\Big)

and extended by continuity; the elementary cell here is the strip

(2.3)

S_{a}=[-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}a,\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}a]\times\mathbb{R}.

The fiber operators on
L^{2}(S_{a})
in (
2.2
) act as

(2.4)

H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)=-\Delta-\alpha\delta(x-\Gamma(s)),

their domains consisting of functions from
H^{2}(S_{a})
satisfying the conditions

(2.5)

\partial_{1}^{j}\psi\big(\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}a,x_{2}\big)=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta a}\,\partial_{1}^{j}\psi\big(-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}a,x_{2}\big),\quad j=0,1\,;

the associated quadratic form

(2.6)

q_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}[\psi,\theta]=\|\nabla\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(S_{a})}-\alpha\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}\psi\big(\Gamma(s(\xi))\big)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

is defined on functions from
H^{1}(S_{a})
satisfying the condition (
2.5
) with
j=0
. Alternatively, one can consider the fiber operators

(2.7)

\tilde{H}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)=(-i\partial_{1}+\theta)^{2}-\partial_{2}^{2}-\alpha\delta(x-\Gamma(s)),

with quasiperiodic conditions (
2.5
) replaced by periodic ones, unitarily equivalent to (
2.4
) by means of the operators
V:\,(V\psi)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta x_{1}}\psi(x)
, and similarly for the associated quadratic forms. The advantage of operators (
2.7
) is that their domain is independent of
\theta
so they form a type A analytic family in the sense of
[
Ka76
, Sec. VII.2.1]
; their spectral properties following from this fact translate in a straightforward way to those of operators (
2.4
).

It is useful to introduce the orthonormal basis associated with the eigenvalues of the transverse part of operator
H_{0,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)
, namely

\mu_{m}(\theta)=\Big(\frac{2\pi m}{a}+\theta\Big)^{2},\quad\eta_{m}^{\theta}(x_{1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\,\mathrm{e}^{i(2\pi m+\theta a)x_{1}/a}

(or alternatively
\tilde{\eta}_{m}^{\theta}(x_{1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi imx_{1}/a}
referring to
\tilde{\mu}_{m}(\theta)=\mu_{m}(\theta)
and periodic boundary conditions), allowing us to understand
H_{0,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)
as a matrix differential operator with the entries
(\eta_{n}^{\theta},H_{0,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)\eta_{m}^{\theta})=\big(-\partial_{2}^{2}+\mu_{m}(\theta)\big)\,\delta_{nm}
; for its resolvent kernel we have

(\eta_{n}^{\theta},\big(H_{0,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)+\kappa^{2}\big)^{-1}\eta_{m}^{\theta})(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{1})=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa|x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1}|}}{2\kappa}\,\delta_{nm}.

Since the part of
\Gamma_{0}
in
S_{a}
is finite, one can check in the same way as in
[
BEKŠ94
, Thm. 4.2]
that the singular term in (
2.7
) is relatively compact with respect to
(-i\partial_{1}+\theta)^{2}-\partial_{2}^{2}
, and consequently,

(2.8)

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)\big)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{0,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)\big)=[\theta^{2},\infty),\quad\theta\in\mathcal{B}_{a}:=\big[-\textstyle{\frac{\pi}{a}},\textstyle{\frac{\pi}{a}}\big].

At the same time, we have
\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)\big)<\theta^{2}
. Indeed, let us choose

\psi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\eta_{0}^{\theta}(x_{1})\,g(\varepsilon x_{2}),

where the function
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
equals one in the vicinity of zero. Plugging it into (
2.6
), we get

q_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}[\psi_{\varepsilon}]=\theta^{2}+\varepsilon\|g^{\prime}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{a}\,\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}|g(\varepsilon\Gamma_{0}(s(x_{1}))|^{2}\,\sqrt{1+\gamma^{\prime}(x_{1})^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the second term on the right-hand side tends to zero, while the third one approaches
-\alpha L(\Gamma_{0}\!\!\upharpoonright_{S_{a}})<-\alpha a<0
, where
L(\Gamma_{0}\!\!\upharpoonright_{S_{a}})
is the length of the segment of the curve
\Gamma_{0}
in the strip (
2.3
), hence
q_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}[\psi_{\varepsilon}]<\theta^{2}
holds for all
\varepsilon
small enough.

The spectrum of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)
below
\theta^{2}
is thus nonempty and in view of (
2.8
) it consists of eigenvalues which are real-analytic as functions of the quasimomentum
\theta
. Moreover, since we have excluded the trivial case of a constant
\gamma
, none of the dispersion curves is constant. To see that, assume that a dispersion curve satisfies
\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta)=0
in
\mathcal{B}_{a}
. By Feynman-Hellmann argument we get for the corresponding eigenfunction
(\tilde{\psi}(\theta),2(-i\partial_{1}+\theta)\tilde{\psi}(\theta))=0
, however,
\tilde{\psi}(\theta)
is nonzero a.e., so that the eigenfunction must satisfy

(-i\partial_{1}+\theta)\tilde{\psi}(x_{1},x_{2};\theta)=0

for a.a.
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
which can happen only if
\tilde{\psi}(\cdot,x_{2};\theta)
is a multiple of
\tilde{\eta}_{0}^{\theta}(\cdot)
. The interaction term in the operator (
2.7
), though, leaves the subspace of functions independent of
x_{1}
invariant only if
\gamma^{\prime}=0
, which contradicts to the assumption we have made.

Finally, the spectrum of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}
is by (
2.2
) union of the fiber operator (
2.4
) spectra as
\theta
runs through
\mathcal{B}_{a}
which concludes the proof.

is replaced by the curve

\Gamma_{\tau}(s)=(x_{1}+\tau(x_{1}),\gamma(x_{1}))

assuming that

(ii)

3.
The singular version of Birman-Schwinger principle

The core element of spectral analysis of a self-adjoint operator, in our case
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}
, is the investigation of its resolvent. An efficient tool to identify the discrete spectrum of Schrödinger operators is the Birman-Schwinger principle; we refer to
[
BEG22
]
for an extensive bibliography on this subject. If the involved potentials are singular, of measure type, a modification is needed.

Let us recall that the result comes from comparison of the resolvent,
R^{k}_{\alpha,\Gamma}=\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma}-k^{2})^{-1}
for
k^{2}\in\rho\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma}\big)
, with the free one,
R_{0}^{k}
, which in the two-dimensional case is an integral operator with the kernel

G_{k}(x-y)=\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k|x-y|).

Given positive Radon measures
\mu,\nu
with
\mu(x)=\nu(x)=0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define by
R^{k}_{\nu,\mu}
the integral operator
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mu)=:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(\nu)
acting as
R^{k}_{\nu,\mu}\psi=G_{k}*\psi\mu
for all
\psi\in D(R^{k}_{\nu,\mu})\subset L^{2}(\mu)
. Specifically, we deal with combinations of the measure
m_{\Gamma}:\,m_{\Gamma}(M)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{M\cap\Gamma}(x_{1})\,\sqrt{1+\gamma^{\prime}(x_{1})^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}
for any Borel
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}x
. The generalized Birman-Schwinger principle is then expressed as follows
[
Ex08
, Prop. 2.3]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

(i) Fix a complex
k
with
\mathrm{Im}\,k>0
. If
\,I-\alpha R^{k}_{m_{\Gamma},m_{\Gamma}}
is invertible and the operator

R^{k}:=R^{k}+\alpha R^{k}_{\mathrm{d}x,m_{\Gamma}}\big(I-\alpha R^{k}_{m_{\Gamma},m_{\Gamma}}\big)^{-1}R^{k}_{m_{\Gamma},\mathrm{d}x}

on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is everywhere defined, then
k^{2}\in\rho\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma}\big)
and
R^{k}=R^{k}_{\alpha,\Gamma}
.

(ii)
\dim\ker\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma}-k^{2})=\dim\ker\big(I-\alpha R^{k}_{m_{\Gamma},m_{\Gamma}}\big)
for any such
k
.

(iii) An eigenfunction
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma}
with eigenvalue
k^{2}
is associated with an appropriate eigenfunction
\phi\in L^{2}(m_{\Gamma})\backsimeq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of
R^{k}_{m_{\Gamma},m_{\Gamma}}
with eigenvalue one, being related by
\psi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}R^{k}_{\mathrm{d}x,m_{\Gamma}}(x,s)\,\phi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
, and on the other hand,
\phi(s)=\psi(\Gamma(s))
.

Instead of the Laplacian resolvent sandwiched between square roots of the potential we have in the singular case an integral operator, the kernel of which is the (
\alpha
multiple of the) trace of the free resolvent kernel at the points of the interaction support. To be concrete, we are interested in the negative spectrum of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma}
corresponding to
k=i\kappa
with
\kappa>0
, thus we seek the values of
\kappa
for which operator
\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with the kernel

(3.1)

\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma}(s,s^{\prime})=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}K_{0}\big(\kappa|\Gamma(s)-\Gamma(s^{\prime})|\big),

where
K_{0}
is the Macdonald function, has eigenvalue one.

Proposition 3.2
.

The operator
\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma}
is positive and decreasing with respect to the spectral parameter,
\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma}\geq\mathcal{R}^{\kappa^{\prime}}_{\alpha,\Gamma}
for
\kappa^{\prime}>\kappa
, and
\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\|\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma}\|=0
. Moreover,
\sup\sigma_{ess}(\mathcal{R}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma})=1
holds for
-\kappa_{0}^{2}=\inf\sup\sigma_{ess}(H_{\alpha,\Gamma})
.

Proof.

The positivity follows from positivity of
(-\Delta+\kappa^{2})^{-1}
, the monotonicity and vanishing as
\kappa\to\infty
follow from the properties of
K_{0}(\cdot)
. The last claim is a consequence of the monotonicity in combination with part (ii) of the previous proposition

4.
The perturbed essential spectrum

Our aim to find what is the effect of the perturbation (
2.9
) on the spectrum, in particular, below
\epsilon_{0}=\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)
. Since the unperturbed spectrum is purely essential by Proposition
2.1
, we have first to check that this spectral component cannot spread below
\epsilon_{0}
under the hypotheses made so far; recall that assumption (
i
) about the unperturbed curve was used in the indicated proposition and (
ii
) concerns the perturbation (
2.9
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Assuming (
i
) and (
ii
), we have
\inf\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)=\epsilon_{0}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that the essential spectrum threshold cannot move up,
\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)\leq\epsilon_{0}
. To this aim, it is sufficient to find an appropriate Weyl sequence, for instance, the generalized eigenfunction at the threshold with a suitable mollifier,

(4.1)

f_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\psi_{0}(x)\,g\big(\textstyle{\frac{x_{1}-b_{m}}{m}}\big),

where
g
is a fixed function from
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Choosing the shifts
b_{m}
in such a way that the supports of
g
and
\tau^{\prime}
are disjoint, and therefore
\Gamma_{\tau}=\Gamma_{0}
holds on
\mathrm{supp}\,f_{m}
(modulo a possible longitudinal shift), we can express

(4.2)

\displaystyle(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}f_{m}-\epsilon_{0}f_{m})(x)

\displaystyle\quad=-2m^{-3/2}\,(\partial_{1}\psi_{0})(x)\,g^{\prime}\big(\textstyle{\frac{x_{1}-b_{m}}{m}}\big)-m^{-5/2}\psi_{0}(x)g^{\prime\prime}\big(\textstyle{\frac{x_{1}-b_{m}}{m}}\big).

Next we observe that by Remarks
2.2
(a)
\psi_{0}
is a periodic extension of a Lipschitz function from
L^{2}(S_{a})
which is smooth away from
\Gamma_{0}
and that its derivative normal to the curve has a jump equal to
\alpha
; it follows that both
\psi_{0}
and
\partial_{1}\psi_{0}
have a uniform bound, and consequently, the norms of the two terms at the right-hand side of (
4.2
) are
\mathcal{O}(m^{-1})
and
\mathcal{O}(m^{-2})
, respectively, as
m\to\infty
. Moreover, one can choose the sequence
\{b_{m}\}
is such a way that the functions
f_{m}
have disjoint supports in which case
f_{m}\to 0
weakly as
m\to\infty
, and from that
\epsilon_{0}\in\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)
follows.

The opposite inequality can be obtained by Neumann bracketing
[
RS78
, Sec. XIII.15]
; it is enough to find an operator obtained from
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}
by imposing additional Neumann condition such that the threshold of its essential spectrum is
\epsilon_{0}
. To begin with, we divide the plane into union (modulo the boundaries of codimension one) of five disjoint regions, namely
\Omega_{1}^{(\pm)}:=\{x:\,\pm x_{1}>(n+\frac{1}{2})a,\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
,
\Omega_{2}^{(\pm)}:=\{x:\,|x_{1}|<(n+\frac{1}{2})a,\pm x_{2}>\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\}
, and
\Omega_{0}:=\{x:\,|x_{1}|<(n+\frac{1}{2})a,|x_{2}|<\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\}
; the number
n
is chosen in such a way that
\mathrm{supp}\,\tau\subset\big(-(n+\frac{1}{2})a,(n+\frac{1}{2})a\big)
in which case
\Gamma_{\tau}
coincides with
\Gamma_{0}
in
\Omega_{1}^{(\pm)}
up to a possible shift. Neumann conditions at the common boundaries of the domains allow us to estimate or operator from below,

(4.3)

H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\geq H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}=H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,+)}\oplus H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,-)}\oplus H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,-)}\oplus H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(2,+)}\oplus H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(2,-)}\oplus H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(0)}.

The last three terms of this decomposition are irrelevant as far as the essential spectrum threshold is concerned, since
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(2,\pm)}\geq 0
and we know that
\epsilon_{0}<0
, and
\Omega_{0}
is bounded so that the spectrum of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(0)}
is purely discrete.

The estimate (
4.3
) yields the result if the function
\gamma
is mirror-symmetric; without loss of generality we may suppose that the symmetry point is at the origin,
\gamma(x_{1})=\gamma(-x_{1})
. Indeed, in that case we have
\partial_{1}\psi_{0}(0,x_{2})=0
for any
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)=\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}^{\mathrm{N}}\big)
, where the last operator has an additional Neumann condition imposed at the line
x_{1}=0
, however, the said operator is unitarily equivalent to
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,+)}\oplus H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,-)}
.

In the absence of the symmetry this argument no longer works and have to take a closer look at the spectral threshold of the operators
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,\pm)}
. For definiteness, we consider the plus sign, the argument for the other is analogous. In the same spirit as above,

H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,+)}\geq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\!\!\raisebox{6.45831pt}{{\scriptsize$\oplus$}}H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,m)},

where the orthogonal sum terms refer to restrictions of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}^{(1,m)}
to the regions
\Omega_{1,m}:=\{x:\,(n+m(2j+1)+\frac{1}{2})a<x_{1}<(n+(m+1)(2j+1)+\frac{1}{2})a,\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
with Neuman boundary conditions. They are all unitarily equivalent by shifts on multiples of
(2j+1)a
in the
x_{1}
direction, hence we can consider one of them only, and this is in turn unitarily equivalent to the Neumann restriction
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}^{[j]}
of
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}
to
\tilde{\Omega}_{j}:=\{x:\,|x_{1}|<(j+\frac{1}{2})a,\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
for any fixed
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Using Neumann bracketing inductively, on checks easily that
\epsilon[j]:=\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}^{[j]}\big)
is nondecreasing with respect to
j
and bounded from above by
\epsilon_{0}
, hence the limit
\epsilon_{\infty}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\epsilon[j]
exists and
\epsilon_{\infty}<\epsilon_{0}
. Consider next the family of quadratic forms with the common domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
defined by

q^{(j)}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}[\psi]=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}|\nabla\psi(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(\Gamma_{0}(s))|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

clearly associated with the orthogonal sum of operators,
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}^{[j]}\oplus(-\alpha I)
on
L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega}_{j})\oplus L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{j})
. In view of the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral we have
\lim_{j\to\infty}q^{(j)}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}[\psi]=q_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}[\psi]
for any
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and this in view of the form family monotonicity implies that
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}^{[j]}\oplus(-\alpha I)\to H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}
in the strong resolvent sense, cf.
[
Ka76
, Sec. VIII.3.4]
or
[
Si18
, Thm. 10.12]
. In this way, we arrive at
\epsilon_{\infty}=\epsilon_{0}
which concludes the proof.

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tau^{\prime}(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}=0

Proposition 4.2
.

Under (

)–(
iii
), we have
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)\supset\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)
using suitable Weyl sequences. For the nonnegative
k^{2}\in\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)
its elements can be plane waves of momentum
k
with compactly supported mollifiers, for the energies
0>\epsilon_{0}+\kappa^{2}\in\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)
we repeat the argument from the previous proof replacing
\psi_{0}
in (
4.1
) by the periodic extension of the function
\psi_{\kappa}
satisfying
H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}(\theta)\psi_{\kappa}=(\epsilon_{0}+\kappa^{2})\psi_{\kappa}
.

To prove the opposite inclusion, we can use Weyl’s criterion again; as before we may consider the negative part of
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)
only. To any element
\epsilon_{0}+\kappa^{2}
of it, there is a sequence
\{\psi_{m}\}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\psi_{m}\to 0
weakly and

5.
Discrete spectrum due to local perturbations

To find whether the perturbation can create a spectrum below the threshold of the essential one, we are going to compare Birman-Schwinger operators referring to
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{\tau}
. We follow the idea proposed in
[
EI01
]
: by Proposition
3.2
both
\sup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma}\big)
and the possible eigenvalues above it decrease continuously to zero as

. We have thus to check the inequality

(5.1)

\sup\sigma\big(\mathcal{R}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)>\sup\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(\mathcal{R}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)

for
\kappa_{0}:=\big(\!-\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)\big)^{1/2}
, since if it holds, there is at least one eigenvalue
\mu(\kappa)
of
\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}
above the essential spectrum threshold which crosses the value one
to the right
of the point
\kappa_{0}
, and consequently, the discrete spectrum of the operator is by Proposition
3.1
(ii) nonempty.

As one might expect, this happens if the perturbation leads to a local contraction of the interaction support:

Theorem 5.1
.

Under (
i
) and (
ii
),
\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}\big)\neq\emptyset
holds if
\tau^{\prime}\leq 0

Proof.

To verify inequality (
5.1
), it is sufficient to find a suitable trial function. The natural starting point is the generalized eigenfunction
\psi_{0}
corresponding to
\inf\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)
; by Remark

(a) it is simple and one choose it positive and by Proposition
3.1
(iii) the corresponding generalized eigenfunction of
\mathcal{R}^{\kappa}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}
is
\phi_{0}(s)=\psi_{0}(\Gamma_{0}(s))
, or alternatively
\tilde{\phi}_{0}=U\phi_{0}
referring to the operator with the kernel (

Such a function does not belong to

\big(g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0},\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0}\big)-\|g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})

so that

M_{n}=\big(g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0},\big[\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}},g_{n}\big]\tilde{\phi}_{0}\big),

or explicitly

\displaystyle M_{n}=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g_{n}(x_{1})\tilde{\phi}_{0}(x_{1})\,K_{0}\big(\kappa_{0}|\Gamma_{0}(s(x_{1}))-\Gamma_{0}(s(x^{\prime}_{1}))|\big)

(5.3)

\displaystyle\times(g_{n}(x^{\prime}_{1})-g_{n}(x_{1}))\,\tilde{\phi}_{0}(x^{\prime}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{1}.

To estimate the right-hand side of (

and
u=x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle M_{n}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\,n^{2}\,\|\tilde{\phi}_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|u||v|}{\big(n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(u+v)^{2}\big)^{2}\big(n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(u-v)^{2}\big)}\,K_{0}(\kappa_{0}|u|)\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}\,n^{2}\,\|\tilde{\phi}_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2}v^{2}}{\big(n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(u+v)^{2}\big)^{2}\big(n^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(u-v)^{2}\big)^{2}}\,K_{0}(\kappa_{0}|u|)\,\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

and since the denominator is bounded from below by

for any

is bounded and exponentially decaying as

5.2

To prove that (
5.1
) holds under the assumptions of the theorem, we have to check that

\big(g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0},\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0}\big)-\|g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0}\|^{2}>0

holds for some

to the left-hand side we see that this will happen provided

(5.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big[\big(g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0},\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{\tau}}g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0}\big)-\big(g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0},\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}g_{n}\tilde{\phi}_{0}\big)\big]>0

because then the difference will for all large enough

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{\phi}_{0}(x_{1})\,

\displaystyle\big[K_{0}\big(\kappa_{0}|\Gamma_{\tau}(s(x_{1}))-\Gamma_{\tau}(s(x^{\prime}_{1}))|\big)

(5.6)

\displaystyle-K_{0}\big(\kappa_{0}|\Gamma_{0}(s(x_{1}))-\Gamma_{0}(s(x^{\prime}_{1}))|\big)\big]\,\tilde{\phi}_{0}(x^{\prime}_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{1}>0.

If

is sign-changing, the situation is more involved, nevertheless, one can get an existence result in the situation where both the undulation of

The argument of the previous proof applies naturally again, hence one has to check whether

Remark 5.4
.

These results are certainly far from optimal. One reason is the rough estimate we used in (

6.
The perturbation may not be gentle

Before coming to the message of this section announced in the title we will present an auxiliary result which could be of independent interest. Let us first describe the setting. We consider a positive, compactly supported function
V\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathrm{supp}\,V\subset\Sigma_{b}:=[-\frac{1}{2}b,\frac{1}{2}b]
for some
b>0
. Furthermore, let
X_{0}=\{na\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a periodic array with
a>b
and
X_{\delta}=\{na+\delta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence obtained by shifting a
finite
number of the array points; we denote
x_{n}=na+\delta_{n}
. We do not impose restrictions on the
individual

\delta_{n}
’s but we require that
\delta_{n+1}-\delta_{n}>-b
, so that the perturbation does not change the order of the points and, in addition, we have
x_{n+1}-x_{n}>a-b
. The object of our interest is the one-dimensional Schrödinger operator

H_{V,X_{\delta}}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}-V_{\delta}(x),\quad V_{\delta}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}V(x-x_{n}),

describing motion in the array of potential wells which do not overlap by assumption; it is a self-adjoint operator bounded from below with the domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
because the potential belongs to Kato class
K_{1}

[
Si82
, Thm. A.2.7]
. It is well known that the spectrum of
H_{V,X_{0}}
is purely essential and the compactly supported perturbation
V_{\delta}-V_{0}
does not change its essential component, and it gives rise to
at most
finite number of eigenvalues in each spectral gap of
\sigma\big(H_{V,X_{0}}\big)

[
RB73
,
GS93
]
. Our question is whether such an eigenvalue does indeed exist in the lowest gap, i.e. below the spectral threshold
\epsilon_{0}
of
H_{V,X_{0}}
; the answer is affirmative:

Theorem 6.1
.

In the described situation,
\inf\sigma\big(H_{V,X_{\delta}}\big)<\epsilon_{0}
unless
\delta_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, in other words, any nontrivial perturbation of the described type creates at least one eigenvalue below
\inf\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{V,X_{\delta}}\big)

Proof.

The argument follows the same line as in the multidimensional case
[
ES25

but to make the paper self-contained, we present it skipping just a few details. The question is by Birman-Schwinger principle translated into analysis of the operator

\kappa

. Since the potential well supports do not overlap, we may regard

as an infinite-matrix integral operator the entries of which map of

with the kernels

where

. As in the proof of Theorem

-\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}
, the operator (
6.4
) is of the type considered in Theorem
6.1
above; note the potential modification there is compactly supported which is a requirement equivalent to assumption (
iii
). Moreover, the curvature-induced potential is easily computed,

-\frac{1}{4}k^{2}(s)=-\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x)^{2}}{4(1+\gamma^{\prime}(x)^{2})^{3}},

so that it is
C^{4}
satisfying thus the regularity property needed for the bracketing method reviewed in
[
Ex08
]
. This allows us to conclude that the discrete spectrum
H_{\alpha,\Gamma}
below the threshold of
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma}\big)
is in this case nonempty for all coupling strengths
\alpha
large enough.

7.
Concluding remarks

As is usually the case, a resolved problem opens further questions, and for sure not only technical ones like replacing the compactly supported perturbations by those with an appropriate decay. For instance,
\sigma\big(H_{\alpha,\Gamma_{0}}\big)
may have gaps – in fact, this happens for all sufficiently large
\alpha
, cf.
[
Yo98
]
– and one is interested in the perturbation-induced discrete spectrum in them.

Other natural question concern sufficient conditions for the absence of the discrete spectrum – at the bottom this may be a perturbation opposite to that of Theorem
5.1
, a local expansion of the curve – or the three-dimensional analogue of the present problem in which the interaction term is no longer of additive type
[
Ex08
, Sec. 2.4]
. For the moment, however, we stop and leave these and other questions to a future research.
