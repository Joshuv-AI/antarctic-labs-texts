Beta Thalassemia and Chronic Myeloid Leukemia: Dual Diagnosis Under the Microscope
Authors: Paige Muir, Mohammad Bahmanyar, Hamish Nicolson, Hayley Merkeley
Published: 2026

A 28‐year‐old male with non‐transfusion‐dependent hemoglobin E‐beta thalassemia (baseline hemoglobin 100 g/L) and enlarged spleen (17.8 cm) was admitted to hospital for investigation of progressive leukocytosis, splenomegaly and right hip pain. He was admitted 2 weeks prior for pneumonia in the context of respiratory symptoms and infiltrates on chest imaging, with a progressive anemia (hemoglobin 37 g/L) in the absence of bleeding. He was noted to have a markedly elevated white blood cell count of 180 × 109/L with 33% neutrophils, 37% immature granulocytes, no initial eosinophilia or basophilia and 2.7% myeloblasts on peripheral blood flow cytometry, from a normal baseline the year prior (Table 1). He was treated with a course of antibiotics and 6 units of packed red blood cells. Given the improvement in his cell count indices at discharge (Table 1), his clinical picture was thought to represent an acute reactive extramedullary hematopoietic process in the setting of pneumonia. However, a neoplastic process, specifically chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), was on the differential given the degree of left‐shift and basophilia at the time of discharge. Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) for BCR::ABL1 fusion was sent and was positive with a baseline International Scale (IS) burden 190% (log burden 0.28), confirming a diagnosis of CML. There were no other abnormalities identified on FISH including JAK2, FGFR1, or PDGFRA/B, and the karyotype was 46,XY,t(9;22)(q34;q11.2). His Sokal index was 1.1 points (intermediate risk) and EUTOS long term survival (ELTS) score was 2.5 (high risk).

He was readmitted for bone marrow investigation and treatment initiation. His blood film demonstrated characteristic features of beta thalassemia (microcytic anemia with codocytes and dacrocytes, coarse basophilic stippling, polychromasia and nucleated red cells) and CML (left shifted neutrophilic leukocytosis with predominance of myelocytes and metamyelocytes, monocytosis, basophilia, eosinophilia and circulating blasts) (Figure 1A). Bone marrow aspirate was a dry tap. Trephine biopsy revealed 100% cellularity (Figure 1B) with left shifted granulocytic hyperplasia, increased eosinophils and basophils, paratrabecular expansion of immature granulocytes, sparse erythroid islands and scattered small megakaryocytes with hyposegmented nuclei (Figure 1C,D). Reticulin stain showed grade 2/3 myelofibrosis based on the World Health Organization 5th edition classification. These findings reflect a composite of underlying hemoglobinopathy‐related changes and superimposed leukemic involvement from CML. In thalassemia, chronic ineffective hematopoiesis leads to increased hematopoietic drive and marrow hyperplasia, resulting in hypercellularity and rarely marrow fibrosis, as seen in this case [1]. These changes overlap morphologically with those of CML, which is also associated with myeloid predominance. Furthermore, myeloproliferation seen with CML may further exacerbate stromal activation and fibrosis [2]. Although thalassemia is often accompanied by increased erythroid islands, this feature was not evident in the current case, likely secondary to the patient's concomitant CML. The patient was ultimately diagnosed with chronic phase CML (blasts < 5%) and started on imatinib mesylate. He was also found to have avascular necrosis (AVN) of the right hip in the context of his underlying chronic anemia, thalassemia and CML. He did not have other risk factors for AVN including recent steroid use, significant alcohol intake, prior trauma or evidence of underlying endocrinopathy. This was managed conservatively.

This case highlights the development of CML in a patient with underlying hemoglobin E‐beta thalassemia, the histopathologic features of both conditions, and the importance of heightened clinical suspicion to avoid diagnostic delay. Patients with hemoglobinopathies are at increased risk of developing hematologic malignancies due to chronic hemolysis, ineffective hematopoiesis, hematopoietic stress and marrow hyperplasia [3]. In this patient, progressive anemia and splenomegaly could easily have been attributed solely to their underlying hemoglobinopathy; however, these are also common manifestations of CML and should prompt further evaluation. Likewise, rising leukocytosis with a marked left shift should not be dismissed as merely reactive in the setting of acute inflammation, as it may instead indicate an underlying hematologic malignancy. Heightened clinical vigilance and a thorough diagnostic workup in this patient allowed for prompt diagnosis and timely initiation of therapy.
