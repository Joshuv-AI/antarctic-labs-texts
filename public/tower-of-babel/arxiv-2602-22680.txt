Abstract
. Large language models have enabled agents that reason, plan, and interact with tools and environments to accomplish complex tasks. As these agents operate over extended interaction horizons, their effectiveness increasingly depends on adapting behavior to individual users and maintaining continuity across time, giving rise to personalized LLM-powered agents. In such long-term, user-dependent settings, personalization permeates the entire decision pipeline rather than remaining confined to surface-level generation. This survey provides a capability-oriented review of personalized LLM-powered agents. We organize the literature around four interdependent components: profile modeling, memory, planning, and action execution. Using this taxonomy, we synthesize representative methods and analyze how user signals are represented, propagated, and utilized, highlighting cross-component interactions and recurring design trade-offs. We further examine evaluation metrics and benchmarks tailored to personalized agents, summarize application scenarios spanning general assistance to specialized domains, and outline future directions for research and deployment. By offering a structured framework for understanding and designing personalized LLM-powered agents, this survey charts a roadmap toward more user-aligned, adaptive, robust, and deployable agentic systems, accelerating progress from prototype personalization to scalable real-world assistants.



1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have evolved from passive text generators into general-purpose reasoning systems capable of understanding diverse data, planning actions, and using external tools. Building on these capabilities, LLM-powered agents extend LLMs into integrated systems that decompose complex objectives, invoke tools, interact with dynamic environments, and coordinate with humans or other agents to achieve high-level goals over extended horizons
(
2
;
122
;
225
;
213
)
. This shift marks not only a technical expansion of LLM capabilities but also a conceptual transition toward autonomous, adaptive, and socially grounded intelligent assistants.

As agent systems increasingly support long-term interaction and autonomous decision-making, personalization emerges as a central requirement for maintaining contextual continuity and aligning agent behavior with individual user goals across domains such as education, healthcare, and recommendation
(
26
;
229
;
155
)
.
In LLM-powered agents, personalization extends across the full decision pipeline, shaping how agents interpret user intent, retain information over time, generate plans, and execute actions
(
288
;
215
)
.
This system-wide scope introduces new challenges, including handling dynamic and multimodal feedback, maintaining consistency across interacting components, balancing user-specific adaptation with general capabilities, and safeguarding privacy and security. Correspondingly, evaluating personalized agents requires moving beyond static correctness to capture long-term effectiveness, adaptability, and user satisfaction.

Despite growing interest, reviews on personalized LLM-powered agents remain fragmented. Existing studies often concentrate on individual capabilities such as user modeling and memory construction
(
250
;
286
)
, planning and reasoning strategies
(
239
)
, or adaptive interaction mechanisms
(
107
;
41
)
. A unified framework that connects personalization objectives across the full agent lifecycle remains limited.
As a result, the interactions among different personalization mechanisms, their system-level trade-offs, and their implications for evaluation and deployment are not yet well understood.

This survey presents a capability-oriented and system-level perspective on personalized LLM-powered agents. Personalization is examined as a distributed property instantiated across four interdependent components: profile modeling, memory management, planning, and action execution. This decomposition enables a systematic analysis of how user-specific signals are represented, propagated, and operationalized throughout the agent lifecycle, from intent understanding to real-world outcomes.
As illustrated in Figure

, when an individual user submits a request, a personalized agent coordinates four interdependent capabilities to generate a tailored response. Profile representations and role definitions shape internal preference modeling, memory schemes organize and retrieve relevant contextual information, planning determines personalized decision strategies, and action execution grounds these decisions in the environment. The resulting interaction outcomes provide feedback that refines internal user representations, forming a closed loop that enables iterative and sustained personalization over time.
Building on this taxonomy, we review representative methods, benchmarks, and evaluation protocols, summarize major application domains, and identify open challenges and future research directions. The survey aims to clarify the design space of personalized agents, connect benchmark-driven research with real-world deployment requirements, and provide a structured foundation for developing trustworthy, effective, and scalable personalized agent systems.

2.
Foundations of Personalized LLM-powered Agents

2.1.
LLM-powered Agents

An LLM-powered agent is an autonomous system that integrates a large language model with external tools and utilities to support step-by-step interaction with open-ended environments for task completion
(
38
)
.
The environment includes both the user and the execution context, and is characterized by an external state space
\mathcal{S}
that governs environment dynamics and feedback.

At each time step
t
, the environment is in a state
s_{t}
that captures the externally available information relevant to the task, including user inputs, tool outputs, or other external signals. The agent maintains an internal state
h_{t}
, which captures accumulated context such as retrieved memory, intermediate reasoning results, and internal representations used for decision making.
The agent’s behavior can be abstracted as a policy that selects an action
a_{t}
conditioned on both the current environment state and its internal state:

\pi(a_{t}|s_{t},h_{t}).

Actions may correspond to natural language responses, planning steps, tool invocations, or other environment interactions. The environment then evolves according to a controlled transition process, producing a subsequent external state
s_{t+1}
, and the agent updates its internal state accordingly as

h_{t+1}=f(h_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1}).

Starting from an initial state
s_{0}
, such as a user query or task specification, execution proceeds iteratively until a termination condition is met, yielding a trajectory

\tau=(s_{0},h_{0},a_{0},s_{1},\ldots,a_{T-1},s_{T}).

This trajectory reflects the agent’s evolving interpretation of the task and its interaction history. For analysis or evaluation purposes, execution outcomes can be assessed using a feedback or reward function defined over the trajectory, denoted as
r=\mathcal{R}(\tau)
.

At the system level, LLM-powered agents rely on a set of tightly coupled cognitive capabilities that enable adaptive and goal-directed behavior
(
122
;
225
)
. Profiling shapes how the agent interprets its role and operating context. Memory supports continuity by retaining and organizing information across interactions. Planning governs the transformation of high-level objectives into structured decision processes. Action execution realizes these decisions through tool use or direct interaction with the environment. Together, these capabilities elevate large language models into interactive decision-making systems capable of sustained autonomy.

2.2.
Personalized LLM-powered Agents

A personalized LLM-powered agent is an LLM-based agent whose internal decision-making is adapted to individual users through user-specific preferences.
For each user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, the agent maintains a representation of preferences
p_{u}
, which may encode goals, constraints, habits, or behavioral tendencies inferred from interaction history, feedback, or explicit input.

At each time step
t
, the environment is in a state
s_{t}
, and the agent maintains an internal state
h_{t}^{(u)}
specific to the user.
The agent interprets and acts upon user preferences by conditioning its decision policy on
p_{u}
.
This yields a user-conditioned policy

\pi(a_{t}|s_{t},h_{t},p_{u}),

which selects an action
a_{t}
given the current environment state, internal state, and user preferences.
The environment transitions to a new state
s_{t+1}
, and the agent updates its internal state as

h_{t+1}=f(h_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1},p_{u}),

where user preferences may be explicitly stored within the internal state or provided as an external conditioning signal.
Executing this policy from an initial state produces a user-conditioned interaction trajectory

\tau^{(u)}=(s_{0},h_{0}^{(u)},a_{0}^{(u)},s_{1},\ldots,s_{T}),

where the superscript
(u)
indicates that the trajectory is induced by decision-making conditioned on user preferences.

Personalization refers to the process through which user preferences are accumulated, represented, and integrated into an agent’s decision pipeline, allowing the same task specification
\mathcal{Q}
to give rise to different outcomes for different users.
Through repeated interaction, the agent collects user-centric data
\mathcal{D}_{u}
and refines an internal preference representation
\hat{p}_{u}=p(\mathcal{D}_{u})
, which conditions subsequent action selection.
At an abstract level, personalization can be viewed as favoring decisions that yield
higher user-aligned utility over interaction trajectories:

\pi_{u}^{*}\;\propto\;\arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\tau^{(u)}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid\mathcal{Q},\hat{p}_{u})}\mathcal{R}_{u}(\tau^{(u)}),

where
\hat{p}_{u}
denotes an internal representation of user preferences maintained by the agent, and
\mathcal{R}_{u}(\tau^{(u)})
denotes feedback reflecting user-specific
satisfaction or alignment.

From a system perspective, personalization forms a closed interaction loop.
User-specific preference representations condition the agent’s perception, reasoning,
and action selection; executed actions shape subsequent interactions; and the resulting outcomes provide new signals that refine
\hat{p}_{u}
.
Through repeated execution of this loop, personalized LLM-powered agents progressively align their behavior with individual users, enabling long-term adaptivity and consistent user-specific behavior.

2.3.
User-Centric Data

The internal representation of user preferences in LLM-powered agents is grounded in user-centric data, which captures contextual information, feedback, and interaction traces associated with a specific user.
From a temporal and functional perspective, user-centric data can be broadly divided into
historical data
and
interaction data
.

Historical Data
refers to information available before task execution, including user identifiers, demographic attributes, and records of prior interactions and feedback. This context provides a front-loaded prior over the user for the current episode, capturing relatively stable user characteristics together with accumulated evidence from past interactions, which supports consistent behavior and cross-task generalization.

Interaction Data
is generated during task execution and captures real-time user inputs, feedback, and contextual cues. It includes both explicit signals, such as corrections or ratings, and implicit signals, such as behavioral patterns or response timing. Interaction data reflects the user’s momentary intent and is critical for guiding immediate decision-making, including reasoning adjustments and dynamic action selection.

These two data types enable dual-timescale personalization: historical data defines
who the user is
, while interaction data determines
how the agent should act now
. As illustrated in Figure
2
, historical data guides the agent’s behavior during interaction, while interaction data refines and expands the historical record, together forming a continuous cycle of user-aware adaptation.

2.4.
User Preferences

User preferences constitute the core signals that enable personalization in LLM-powered agents by conditioning their internal state, decision-making processes, and user-specific feedback.
Prior work commonly categorizes preferences according to their mode of expression
(
288
;
99
)
.

Explicit preferences
are directly specified by users and can be incorporated into the agent without additional inference.

Implicit preferences
are inferred indirectly from behavioral patterns or contextual cues, reflecting user judgments in a latent or under-specified form.

Beyond expression form, preferences can be characterized by their semantic function, capturing which aspect of the user they encode. We distinguish
behavioral preferences
, which govern how users communicate and reason, and
topical preferences
, which specify what users prioritize in a given context. Behavioral preferences encompass tone, reasoning style, general interaction tendencies, and personality-related traits, and are often stable across tasks. Topical preferences cover domain interests, factual stances, and likes or dislikes toward specific entities or events, and vary more with context. Both types can be stated explicitly or inferred implicitly, yielding the two-dimensional taxonomy in Figure
3
.

Preferences are categorized by their
expression form
(explicit vs. implicit) and
semantic function
(behavioral vs. topical), illustrated through a multi-turn conversational recommendation scenario.

In practical systems, explicit preferences can be represented in various forms, including reward vectors
(
12
)
, preference embeddings
(
157
)
, or natural-language prompts
(
86
)
.
Although explicit signals are often highly informative, they are typically sparse, which limits their direct applicability for optimization.
As a result, implicit preferences are frequently leveraged through in-context prompting
(
85
)
, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG;
(
160
)
), or preference modeling techniques
(
48
)
that extract structured representations from user behavioral feedback.
When performing such an extraction, accounting for the semantic function of preferences enables more targeted modeling strategies and improves interpretability.

2.5.
Capability-oriented Taxonomy

Personalization in LLM-powered agents emerges from the coordinated operation of multiple internal capabilities rather than from a single adaptation module. Given the diversity of user data, task settings, and preference types, user-specific signals must be represented, retained, reasoned over, and operationalized throughout the whole pipeline. We therefore adopt a capability-oriented taxonomy (illustrated in Figure
4
) that organizes personalization into four interdependent components:

(1)

Profile Modeling
structures user-specific information into internal representations, defining both user characteristics and the agent’s role.

(2)

Memory
maintains and retrieves user-relevant information across interactions, supporting temporal continuity and consistent preference grounding.

(3)

Planning
integrates user representations into reasoning processes, shaping decision paths, strategy selection, and prioritization under contextual constraints.

(4)

Action Execution
operationalizes personalized decisions through tool invocation and environment interaction, enabling the final outcome.

3.
Profile Modeling

Profile modeling defines the fundamental identity of an agent, including its intrinsic attributes, behavioral tendencies, and operational boundaries
(
122
)
.
In general-purpose agents, this identity is either static or pre-defined externally,established through fixed role instructions and domain expertise
(
275
;
247
)
.
In personalized LLM-powered agents, however, profile modeling becomes user-aware and adaptive, incorporating information about the user and dynamically adjusting the agent’s role.
To capture this bidirectional relationship, we discuss personalization from two perspectives:
user profile modeling
and
agent role definition
.

3.1.
User Profile Modeling

User profile modeling extracts and organizes user-specific signals into a representation that can condition downstream personalization modules. In contrast to generic semantic context, profiles primarily encode personalization cues such as stable traits, long-term preferences, and interaction patterns that determine how an agent should respond or act for a particular user
(
244
)
. We view a user profile as a structured state constructed from heterogeneous features, where the key design questions are which signals are decision-critical and how they should be aggregated into a usable form. Accordingly, we divide profile modeling into
persona-based
and
response-based
approaches, reflecting whether the profile is anchored in user attributes or in user-specific feedback on model outputs.

Persona-based Modeling

Persona-based methods infer high-level user attributes that remain informative across contexts. Profiles may be built from explicit self-descriptions and from implicit cues in historical interactions, and are often represented as either a structured attribute vector or a free-form natural-language persona summary. For instance, AlignXpert
(
99
)
constructs a high-dimensional preference space grounded in psychological and alignment-motivated dimensions, then estimates user-specific positions to form a preference vector. FSPO
(
194
)
adopts a user-description chain-of-thought mechanism to allocate additional inference effort to preference analysis, producing more fine-grained persona descriptions. Beyond one-shot estimation, several systems iteratively refine latent user attributes through ongoing dialogue. RLPA
(
290
)
and
220
update user representations over interaction rounds, while RGMem
(
212
)
builds evolving profiles by extracting user insights from episodic memory fragments and progressively aggregating them via hierarchical refinement. Relatedly, RLPF
(
246
)
optimizes concise, human-readable user summaries using a feedback loop tied to downstream performance, and CURIO
(
218
)
introduces an intrinsic motivation objective that encourages active inference of latent user types during multi-turn interaction.

Response-based Modeling

Response-based methods model user preferences through how a user evaluates candidate outputs, capturing context-sensitive notions of what constitutes a preferred response. These profiles are typically derived from fine-grained supervision such as pairwise comparisons or implicit behavioral signals, and are designed to generalize preference judgments across prompts and situations. A common approach factorizes individual preference into a shared reward feature basis with user-specific weights, enabling rapid adaptation under sparse user data. For example, RFM
(
12
)
, PReF
(
187
)
, and LoRe
(
14
)
represent preferences as linear combinations of shared reward features and infer user-specific coefficients from limited feedback. Complementarily, some methods parameterize user preferences in natural language to improve interpretability and controllability. Janus
(
93
)
organizes preference dimensions hierarchically from coarse categories to finer-grained value descriptions, while PLUS
(
140
)
learns text-based preference summaries that condition downstream reward modeling for personalized scoring and generation.

3.2.
Agent Role Definition

Agent role definition determines who the agent is in relation to the user,
which can be divided into
user-simulated agent definition
and
adaptive agent definition
based on whether the agent is on behalf of the user or not.

User-simulated Agent Definition

In some applications, especially delegation-oriented tasks such as user or dialogue simulation, agents are instantiated to act as simulated users with pre-defined attributes and preferences.
These profiles usually function as static role configurations, parameterizing agent behavior for testing or evaluation and can substantially influence task planning and execution
(
143
)
.
This one-way profile modeling emulates user traits without learning from real interaction, but remains crucial for creating realistic human–agent scenarios, enabling efficient large-scale experimentation, and ensuring behavioral diversity across synthetic users
(
226
;
97
;
42
)
.

Adaptive Agent Definition

In interactive personalized systems, the agent role itself becomes conditional on the user profile.
The agent dynamically adjusts its persona, tone, or autonomy based on the user’s characteristics, goals, and trust level, enabling a two-way adaptive process. LD-Agent
(
98
)
adopts a bidirectional user-agent modeling approach
(
261
)
, utilizing a tunable persona extractor to manage long-term persona bank for both sides. PersonaAgent
(
279
)
optimizes the user-specific system prompt through textual loss feedback between simulated and ground-truth responses, enabling test-time user-preference alignment. PersonaX
(
191
)
performs offline clustering and multi-persona profiling, combining with profile retrieval for online decision making. RPP
(
127
)
proposes an instance-wise prompt optimization framework, which includes both the user historical information and the task guidance for agent, aiming to generate personalized discrete prompts for better individual recommendation performance.

3.3.
Discussion

Role of Profile Modeling

Profile modeling provides the foundational layer of personalization by defining both who the user is through user profiling and who the agent becomes through agent role definition. These two components offer complementary but distinct perspectives: user profiling seeks to construct expressive representations of user traits and preferences, while agent role definition determines how the agent’s operational style should adapt to those user characteristics.

Challenges

Despite their tight conceptual coupling, they face different technical challenges. On the one hand, user profiling is challenged by sparse, noisy, and inconsistent preference signals, as well as the potential conflict between long-term tendencies and context-specific preferences.
On the other hand, agent role definition faces the trade-off between flexibility and stability across tasks, avoiding both rigid roles and undesirable role drift. Finally, a broader challenge lies in the weak coupling between profiling and role adaptation. Effective personalization requires bidirectional alignment where the agent must update its role based on refined user profiles, while user profiling should leverage signals arising from the agent’s adaptive behavior.

Future Directions

Existing user profiling methods often yield representations that are static or task-specific, and agent roles that lack controllability or principled adaptation dynamics. Moreover, user profiling and agent role definition are typically developed in isolation, with limited mechanisms for mutual adjustment. Going forward, promising directions include lightweight and interpretable profiling methods that remain robust under sparse signals, more unified frameworks that couple evolving user profiles with stable yet adaptive agent roles.

4.
Memory

While profile modeling captures stable and high-level user traits, many fine-grained details essential for personalized responses cannot be fully encoded in a plain profile.
To support such detailed personalization, agents rely on a memory module, which refers to the system’s ability to retain, recall, and leverage past interactions to improve future behavior
(
240
)
.
Memory is commonly divided into
personal memory
, which stores user inputs and interaction histories, and
system memory
, which records intermediate reasoning states or task-execution artifacts
(
250
)
.
This survey focuses on
personal memory
, as it directly shapes how agents adapt to individual users. In the following, we first discuss how personal memory is structured and updated, and then how it is retrieved to support personalized behaviors.

Personal memory can be implemented as
internal memory
, which embeds user-related information within the LLM’s internal components, or as
external memory
, which stores information in an auxiliary system.
Internal memory approaches operate through modifications to model parameters
(
144
;
228
;
74
;
207
)
, key–value caches
(
254
;
160
)
, or hidden states
(
281
;
287
;
232
)
.
Although parameter-efficient and compact
(
233
)
, internal memory is constrained by limited memory capacity, challenges in updating stored information, and reliance on model retraining, making it less suitable for dynamic, long-horizon personalization.
By contrast, external memory stores information in an auxiliary system, usually combined with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG;
95
), which enriches the input prompt with information retrieved from the external knowledge base, thereby improving user satisfaction and engagement. Compared to internal memory, external memory is more flexible and especially beneficial for users with limited personal data
(
174
)
. However, it requires careful design for storage, management, and personalized access.
Given these considerations, we focus on external personal memory systems and structure this section around two key components:
(1)
personal memory schemes
, which define how user-related information is stored and updated, and
(2)
personalized retrieval
, which determines how stored information is accessed to guide personalized behaviors.

4.1.
Personal Memory Schemes

Personal memory schemes define the foundation of the memory module, specifying how user-related information is organized, stored, and updated throughout interaction.
Based on the timescale and influence of stored information, personal memory is often divided into short-term memory, which captures recent conversational context, and long-term memory, which accumulates enduring user information across sessions.
Short-term memory provides reliable signals for immediate responses but is limited by context window constraints and cannot effectively utilize information beyond the current interaction session.
On the other hand, long-term memory enables representation of long-tail individual data and supports self-evolution through accumulated experience
(
72
)
, but suffers from information overload and difficulties in timely updating.
As a result, recent personalized agents increasingly adopt hybrid memory designs that combine both short- and long-term memory to balance continuity and fine-grained adaptation
(
98
)
, motivating careful design of the underlying
memory structure
and
update mechanism
.

4.1.1.
Memory Structure

The memory structure determines
what information is stored
and
how it is represented
, forming the foundation for effective personalized retrieval.
Early agent systems often appended full dialogue histories or execution trajectories to memory
(
116
)
, but such raw storage introduced redundancy, limiting the agent’s ability to surface user-relevant information.
To achieve scalable personalized memory management, recent works convert interaction histories into compact and structured representations tailored to task demands
(
115
;
296
)
.
Based on the format, memory structures can be broadly categorized as
textual memory
and
structured memory
.

Textual Memory

Textual memory stores user-related information in natural language form, typically by summarizing multi-turn interactions into concise and interpretable units.
Because such representations preserve rich semantics and align naturally with the input–output format of LLMs, textual memory has become the dominant choice for personalized agents across diverse tasks
(
286
)
.
However, effective textual memory design faces two key challenges: defining appropriate segmentation boundaries and capturing comprehensive user-related information.
Early approaches often relied on rigid turn-level or session-level segmentation, which frequently misaligned with the semantic units of dialogue and resulted in fragmented retrieval.
To address this, recent work has explored more flexible, topic-coherent segmentation strategies.
For instance, SeCom
(
152
)
and RMM
(
209
)
partition conversations into semantically coherent segments, enabling memory units to reflect stable conversational topics rather than arbitrary boundaries.
Nemori
(
141
)
further improves segmentation accuracy by using a boundary detector to group entire interactions into coherent episodes, preserving the integrity of user intent.
Beyond segmentation, many systems enrich textual memory with auxiliary attributes such as timestamps, topic labels, and user personality
(
115
;
260
;
132
)
.
For example, CAIM
(
241
)
introduces a tagging system that stores events with tuples of related tags, inductive thoughts, and timestamps.
MemoryBank
(
296
)
and PersonaAgent
(
279
)
adopt hierarchical summarization to model multi-level event structure and dynamic personality shifts, simulating the multi-component structure of human memory and enabling more coherent and human-like personalized interaction.
MemInsight
(
171
)
is an autonomous memory augmentation approach that employs an attribute mining module to extract structured, semantically meaningful attributes from input dialogues.
Several recent works draw inspiration from human memory systems
(
182
;
282
)
.
These systems typically distinguish between semantic memory and episodic memory
(
119
;
156
;
121
)
.
MIRIX
(
231
)
proposes an architecture with six specialized memory components (core, episodic, semantic, procedural, resource, and knowledge vault) organized hierarchically.
Mem-
\alpha

(
234
)
proposes an architecture with core, semantic, and episodic memory modules, combined with an RL framework that teaches agents to manage complex memory through interaction and feedback.
MemWeaver
(
270
)
combines temporal and semantic dimensions across behavioral-level and cognitive-level memory to jointly represent user actions and long-term preferences.
Collectively, these techniques advance textual memory toward richer representations, more coherent segmentation, and multi-level organization, allowing personalized agents to retain user-relevant details more faithfully and capture both explicit and implicit signals with higher accuracy.

Structured Memory

Structured memory provides a more explicit and computationally manipulable representation of interaction histories by storing information in predefined formats enriched with structural relationships.
Compared with textual summaries, structured memory introduces stronger inductive biases that support scalable retrieval, multi-granularity reasoning, and more fine-grained personalization.
Current approaches can be broadly grouped into
vector-based memory
,
hierarchical tree structures
, and
graph-based memory architectures
.

Vector-based memory
represents each memory unit using learned embeddings stored in a vector database, enabling scalable similarity search and fast retrieval of user-relevant information.
This design has become a standard practice in personalized agents due to its efficiency and flexibility
(
148
;
24
;
251
)
.
However, vector memory alone discards structural relationships between memory items and offers limited interpretability, motivating more structured alternatives or hybrid systems that combine vectors with explicit organizational formats.

Hierarchical tree structures
organize memory at multiple abstraction levels, where each node corresponds to a memory unit and parent–child relationships encode increasingly coarse summaries.
For instance, RAPTOR
(
178
)
recursively clusters and summarizes text into a tree, supporting retrieval at both fine- and coarse-grained levels.
MemTree
(
169
)
similarly constructs a memory hierarchy in which each node stores aggregated textual content and semantic embeddings, allowing memory to be represented at varying abstraction depths.

Graph-based architectures
capture richer relationships by linking memory units through typed edges representing temporal, semantic, or relational dependencies
(
73
;
109
;
24
)
.
Some methods focus on behavioral coherence, such as MemWeaver
(
270
)
, which builds an event-level behavior graph where interaction events are nodes connected by temporal and semantic edges, along with a context-aware random walk mechanism.
Other designs integrate multi-dimensional subgraphs to capture more nuanced relationships between memory units. For example, AriGraph
(
7
)
combines semantic knowledge (entity–relation triplets) with episodic observation nodes, enabling both long-term knowledge accumulation and temporally grounded recall.
Zep
(
166
)
introduces a temporally-aware dynamic knowledge graph with three tiers, including episodic for non-lossy data storage, entity-level semantic for extracted entities and relationships, and community-level conceptual for high-level summarizations of strongly connected entity clusters.
MemGAS
(
256
)
further constructs cross-granularity associations across session-, turn-, summary-, and keyword-level memory to enrich personalized retrieval signals.
While powerful, these graph-based structures often depend on computationally expensive LLM-based entity and relation extraction and may lose fine-grained contextual information during graph construction.
To address this, SGMem
(
251
)
proposes a lightweight sentence-graph memory, linking chunk nodes with their constituent sentences and connecting sentences via similarity edges to better preserve contextual semantics.
Similarly, LiCoMemory
(
63
)
introduces CogniGraph, a lightweight hierarchical indexing graph that organizes session summaries, entity–relation triples, and dialogue chunks through cross-layer hyperlinks, keeping the graph minimal while storing full content externally.
Across these approaches, structured memory aims to provide richer organization by modeling how information relates, evolves, and aggregates across interactions.

4.1.2.
Update mechanism

Memory update mechanism determines how an agent’s memory evolves as new interactions occur, ensuring that stored information remains accurate, consistent, and aligned with the user’s changing preferences. When a new memory unit arrives, the system must decide whether to incorporate it as a new entry, revise or merge existing ones, or prune outdated or contradictory content
(
37
)
.
Broadly, memory updating differs across two types of external memory: textual memory, where updates rewrite or reorganize summary-level information, and structured memory, where updates modify entity–relation structures through node/edge creation, conflict resolution, and temporal maintenance.
We review the update mechanism for these two categories separately.

Textual memory
stores interaction histories as natural-language segments or summaries. Updating therefore concerns how these segments are merged, rewritten, or reorganized when new dialogue arrives. Existing approaches can be grouped into
similarity-driven updates
and
inference-guided updates
.

Similarity-driven updates

These methods update memory by comparing new segments with existing summaries and adjusting them based on semantic proximity and auxiliary metadata such as timestamps or topic labels.
RMM
(
209
)
summarizes each session by topic and updates the memory bank through semantic merging, replacing or consolidating summaries with high topical similarity.
CAIM
(
241
)
extends this paradigm with a post-thinking step that generates structured, tag-annotated summaries from recent dialogue and integrates them into memory while refining related historical entries.
Mem0
(
24
)
introduces an LLM-driven update framework that chooses among
ADD, UPDATE, DELETE
, and
NOOP
operations for each new fact based on semantic similarity with existing memories.
Memory-R1
(
264
)
builds on this with a reinforcement-learning memory manager that learns the optimal operation policy across multi-session interactions.

Inference-guided updates

Other systems treat memory updating as a reasoning or decision-making process, allowing the agent to incorporate implicit preference shifts or contextual dynamics not captured by similarity alone.
Nemori
(
141
)
exemplifies this through its Predict–Calibrate mechanism, achieving proactively evolving memory based on gaps between predicted and actual user responses.
PREMem
(
87
)
similarly performs pre-storage reasoning across sessions to analyze thematic evolution before inserting new content, enabling more consistent long-term topic tracking.
These methods provide more adaptive and self-correcting memory dynamics, capturing both short-term changes and long-term shifts in user preferences.

Structured memory
organizes information into interconnected architectures where inserting a new unit often requires reorganizing its related counterparts. Update mechanisms can likewise be grouped into
similarity-driven
and
reasoning- or agentic-driven
strategies.

Similarity-driven updates

Most personalized structured-memory systems update the memory by assessing semantic similarity between new information and existing units, then making local modifications to maintain coherence.
In hierarchical tree structures, MemTree
(
169
)
updates memory by traversing the tree via semantic similarity, inserting new nodes through sibling attachment or leaf expansion, and incrementally updating ancestor nodes with LLM-based aggregation to preserve hierarchical coherence.
Graph-based architectures demand even more complex updates, as every node may connect to multiple others
(
63
)
.
\text{Mem0}^{g}

(
24
)
embeds new triples, matches them to existing nodes, and establishes metadata-rich edges while using an LLM-based conflict resolver to deactivate outdated relations.
Similarly, Zep
(
166
)
integrates new episodic facts by timestamp alignment, updating or deactivating inconsistent edges and adjusting higher-level community clusters to maintain a temporally grounded semantic–episodic graph.
MemGAS
(
256
)
clusters new and historical multi-granularity chunks with GMM, selectively reinforcing only contextually relevant associations to ensure stable personalization over long horizons.

Reasoning- or agentic-driven updates

A smaller but emerging set of systems incorporate explicit reasoning, decision-making, or agentic inference to guide how memory should be updated, moving beyond similarity thresholds toward more cognitively aligned update dynamics.
A-mem
(
260
)
adopts an agentic update scheme in which each new memory becomes a structured note that triggers semantic linking and retroactive refinement of historical notes, enabling a continuously self-evolving memory graph.
Although not designed for personalization, recent non-personalized graph-memory systems illustrate the potential of this direction. TRAIL
(
291
)
performs inference-driven updates by validating and refining candidate triples through confidence-guided decisions rather than pure similarity matching, while AriGraph
(
7
)
uses agentic reasoning to determine whether new observations should create links, update existing nodes, or refine episodic associations. These systems demonstrate that reasoning-based graph evolution can produce more coherent and cognitively grounded memory updates, suggesting a promising direction.

4.2.
Personalized Retrieval

Memory retrieval determines which stored information becomes available to an agent at inference time and therefore directly influences the quality and consistency of personalized behaviors. Unlike conventional RAG systems, retrieval for personalized LLM-powered agents must simultaneously satisfy three requirements: (1) content relevance to the current query, (2) structural consistency with the underlying memory organization, and (3) personal alignment, ensuring that retrieved information reflects the user’s preferences. Existing methods address these challenges through three dominant mechanisms:
content-based retrieval
,
structure-aware retrieval
, and
policy-guided retrieval
.

Content-based retrieval

Content-based retrieval identifies relevant memory by measuring semantic or lexical similarity between the user’s query and stored information, typically implemented with dense retrievers (e.g., FAISS
(
78
)
, Contriever
(
65
)
) or sparse lexical retrievers (e.g., BM25
(
170
)
). Retrieved segments are generally appended to the agent’s prompt to enrich contextual grounding
(
231
;
87
;
260
)
. To capture comprehensive information about the user, content-based retrieval can be operated at multiple granularity levels. For example, RAPTOR
(
178
)
and MemTree
(
169
)
perform hierarchical content-based retrieval by selecting nodes whose embeddings are most similar to the query across different abstraction levels, even though the underlying tree structure is not explicitly traversed for reasoning. To further improve retrieval quality, some methods incorporate post-retrieval filtering or query refinement.
271
adopt a self-reflection mechanism that assesses whether the retrieved content is sufficient for response generation and refines the query when necessary. While effective at capturing semantic relevance, content-based approaches may overlook structural dependencies and long-range contextual relationships, motivating more structured retrieval designs.

Structure-aware retrieval

Structure-aware retrieval leverages the organization of memory, such as graph connectivity and episodic relationships, to retrieve information that is contextually linked to the query rather than merely similar in content. Such methods are often implemented in hierarchical or hybrid modes, where an initial retrieval of entities, topics, or relations guides subsequent retrieval of detailed memory units
(
73
;
50
)
, potentially augmented with graph-based techniques such as Personalized PageRank (PPR;
10
). For instance, AriGraph
(
7
)
first retrieves semantically relevant relational triplets and then, conditioned on these triplets, retrieves the most relevant episodic memories. Zep
(
166
)
combines semantic search with graph-structured signals, such as episode-mention frequency and node-distance scoring, to identify contextually relevant conversational episodes within its temporal memory graph. Other approaches enhance structure-aware retrieval with adaptive routing or self-reflection. MemGAS
(
256
)
computes multi-granularity semantic similarities and integrates them using an entropy-based router, then applies PPR over a dynamically maintained association graph to retrieve structurally coherent memory units. AssoMem
(
277
)
performs associative retrieval by selecting clue nodes and linked utterances, followed by a multi-signal ranking procedure that fuses relevance, graph-based importance, and temporal alignment through a mutual-information–driven fusion strategy. By explicitly modeling the structural dependencies among memory units, structure-aware retrieval provides richer contextual grounding and improves the coverage of long-range or implicit user preferences.

Policy-guided retrieval

Policy-guided retrieval refers to retrieval strategies in which the selection of information is governed by user- or task-conditioned decision policies rather than static similarity matching
(
296
;
279
)
. Unlike content-based or structure-aware retrieval, which rely primarily on semantic proximity or memory topology, policy-guided retrieval actively determines
how
retrieval should proceed and can operate over arbitrary corpora without relying on a persistent user memory store. A first line of work focuses on
pre-retrieval query transformation
, where the retrieval intent is personalized before search. PBR
(
284
)
generates user-style pseudo feedback and corpus-anchored expansions to form a personalized query representation, while PersonaRAG
(
273
)
adapts document selection and ranking using real-time behavioral signals such as reading patterns and interaction context. A second line of work adopts
policy-driven strategy selection
, learning to invoke or integrate heterogeneous retrieval tools based on the task. UniMS-RAG
(
222
)
formulates retrieval as a policy-learning problem, where supervised and reinforcement signals train the system to choose among keyword search, dense retrieval, and knowledge-graph lookup and to combine their results effectively. Persona-DB
(
200
)
employs a two-stage persona-aware policy that first matches users via high-level persona keys and then combines retrievals from target users and collaborator users using a learned composition ratio to address sparsity and enhance personalization. A third direction incorporates
schema-guided information completion
, framing retrieval as a structured inference process. OCG-Agent
(
190
)
decomposes narrative queries into an abstract data type schema and triggers a multi-route retrieval routine that fills schema fields in parallel; a reflect-and-complete loop then issues targeted supplementary retrievals until all required attributes are satisfied, followed by semantic normalization to unify heterogeneous results. Collectively, policy-guided retrieval shifts retrieval from passive document matching toward an active, decision-centric paradigm that adapts retrieval behavior to user preferences, task requirements, and interaction context, enabling more flexible and personalized information acquisition.

4.3.
Discussion

Role of Personal Memory

Personal memory bridges short-term intent and long-term preference modeling. It fulfills three closely related roles. First, it supports preference evolution, allowing the agent to track how user preferences change across sessions rather than relying solely on fixed trait representations. Second, it provides personalized contextual grounding, preserving fine-grained user details that are essential for accurate personalization but cannot be fully encoded in static profiles. Third, it enables preference-conditioned generation, guiding the agent to interpret new queries and produce responses informed by retrieved user-relevant signals. Together, these roles transform memory from a passive log of interactions into an active component of the personalization loop.

Challenges

Despite its importance, personal memory faces several challenges. First, user preferences evolve at different rates, requiring memory systems to balance rapid adaptation with the preservation of long-term traits. Second, memory stores may accumulate redundancy or hallucinations, especially when updates rely on LLM-driven summarization or relation extraction, leading to degraded personalization over long horizons. Third, ensuring that retrieved content is not only semantically relevant but also truly aligned with user preferences remains difficult. Finally, personal memory raises privacy and transparency concerns, highlighting the need for user-controllable and privacy-preserving memory mechanisms.

Future Directions

Memory modules also have inherent limitations. Textual memories scale poorly and require repeated rewriting; structured memories provide controllability but depend on costly extraction and risk losing contextual nuance; update mechanisms oscillate between shallow similarity rules and computationally heavy inference; and retrieval pipelines remain sensitive to ranking biases and distribution shifts. These limitations point to several broad future directions across the memory pipeline, including hybrid memory architectures that combine the semantic richness of textual representations with the organizational clarity of structured formats, more principled and user-controllable update mechanisms that ensure reliability and transparency, and improved retrieval strategies that better integrate personal alignment with semantic relevance.

5.
Planning in Personalized LLM-powered Agents

Planning constitutes the cognitive engine of intelligent agents, enabling the decomposition of high-level objectives into actionable steps and the formulation of execution strategies
(
122
;
201
)
. In the context of personalized agents, planning transcends generic task completion; it must optimize for
user utility
, aligning decision paths with individual preferences, constraints, and latent intents
(
288
;
40
;
52
)
. Unlike standard planners that prioritize efficiency or logical correctness, personalized planners navigate a constrained search space defined by the user’s unique profile and behavioral history, often requiring the integration of long-term memory and preference models
(
241
;
59
)
. We categorize these methodologies into two distinct paradigms based on the integration of user signals:
One-shot Planning
, where personalization is internalized as a prior during reasoning, and
Feedback-driven Planning
, where personalization emerges dynamically through interaction
(
283
;
203
)
.

5.1.
One-Shot Planning

One-shot personalized planning refers to generating a complete plan in a single inference pass, where a plan is represented as a structured decomposition of subgoals and decisions conditioned on user-specific constraints and latent intent. We analyze one-shot methods along two axes: how user signals are grounded into planning representations (
Preference Induction
and
Memory Conditioning
) and how plans are synthesized via compute-intensive generation (
Internal Refinement
).

Preference Induction.

This line of work constructs a
planning-ready user state
by inferring latent intent and translating heterogeneous user signals into explicit constraints or soft objectives that guide plan generation
(
52
)
. For instance, ALIGNXPLORE
(
100
)
utilizes a User-description Chain-of-Thought framework to synthesize a compact, semantically grounded preference description from sparse interaction traces. Instead of retrieving raw logs, the model performs extended inductive reasoning to form global preference constraints that condition downstream planning. Similarly, in sequential recommendation, ReaRec
(
210
)
proposes a Think-Before-Recommend paradigm, using reasoning position embeddings to infer the user’s latent intent trajectory prior to plan generation. In embodied and decision-making contexts, PbP
(
257
)
demonstrates that treating learned user preferences as intermediate abstractions substantially improves few-shot personalized planning performance.

Memory Conditioning.

Complementary to inductive constraint formation, memory-grounded methods condition one-shot planning on selectively retrieved and compressed user history, ensuring consistency with both long-term preferences and recent context
(
209
;
241
)
. PRIME
(
282
)
integrates a dual-memory architecture with a Personalized Thought Process that explicitly traces how past experiences and stable beliefs influence current decisions. Before plan generation, PRIME synthesizes a personalized thought trace via self-distillation, effectively grounding planning in the user-specific cognitive context. PersonaAgent
(
279
)
adopts a retrieval-augmented persona mechanism, dynamically constructing system prompts from a unified memory bank to maintain cross-session planning consistency. Related work in recommendation and dialogue systems similarly shows that structured user memory retrieval significantly improves alignment and coherence in one-shot decision-making
(
59
;
19
)
.

Internal Refinement.

Beyond direct one-pass generation, internal refinement improves it by constructing an initial plan and then internally critiquing and revising it against a user-conditioned objective. Related self-refinement templates such as Reflexion and structured critique-and-revise frameworks provide general mechanisms for generating reflective feedback and triggering plan edits
(
193
;
47
)
, while recent test-time-compute reasoning models make deeper within-pass self-correction increasingly practical
(
136
;
49
)
. Representative systems instantiate this paradigm in several ways. In personalized travel planning, TravelPlanner++
(
195
)
and PTS
(
184
)
study reflection-style itinerary refinement, where intermediate plans are iteratively revised under derived user implicit long-horizon preferences. PREFINE
(
216
)
constructs a pseudo-user critic and user-specific rubrics from interaction history, then critiques and revises candidates to better satisfy the target user. In embodied command execution, IoTGPT
(
269
)
applies self-correction by testing intermediate command sequences in a simulated environment and revising the plan before deployment.

5.2.
Feedback-Driven Planning

Feedback-driven planning treats the initial plan as a hypothesis to be refined through interaction. These systems recognize that user intent is often underspecified, evolving, or partially latent
(
120
;
283
)
, and employ iterative loops to converge on an optimal personalized solution
(
203
;
224
)
. We further organize feedback-driven planning by the source of feedback and how it updates the plan, including information-seeking clarification, mixed-initiative user revision, and non-user feedback signals.

Clarification Seeking

A central challenge in feedback-driven personalized planning lies in deciding
when
to ask for additional information: excessive clarification increases user burden, while premature commitment risks misinterpreting user-specific constraints or latent intent. Prior studies indicate that user requests in planning tasks are frequently underspecified or ambiguous
(
120
)
, motivating approaches that treat clarification as decision-making under uncertainty. For instance, SAGE-Agent
(
203
)
formulates selective questioning as a POMDP and issues clarification queries only when the expected value of information outweighs interaction cost. Similarly, ECLAIR
(
139
)
focuses on detecting missing arguments or ambiguous entities in user instructions and triggers targeted follow-up questions before committing to a plan. Additionally, Ask-before-Plan style frameworks
(
283
)
explicitly decouple clarification from plan synthesis, showing that resolving key uncertainties upfront improves robustness and downstream plan quality. Collectively, these methods establish clarification as a principled and integral component of feedback-driven personalized planning rather than an ad hoc interaction heuristic.

User Revision.

User edits provide a high-fidelity personalization signal that can update user-conditioned objectives, constraints, and preferences for subsequent planning and generation.
40
show that learning latent preferences from historical edits can reduce future edit effort and improve user-specific alignment in subjective settings. Building on this mixed-initiative view, AIPOM
(
84
)
represents plans as editable structures, enabling users to revise intermediate plans while the agent updates constraints and priorities accordingly. Similar revision loops appear in domain systems such as Dango
(
20
)
, which supports iterative correction of agent-produced data-processing workflows, and PlanFitting
(
192
)
, which refines personalized exercise plans through conversational revisions. This revision-driven interaction pattern is also adopted in deployed coding agents, where users review proposed changes and iteratively provide follow-up edits or instructions to steer subsequent updates
(
8
;
44
)
.

Non-user Feedback.

Beyond direct user input, plans can be refined using non-user signals that improve feasibility and preference satisfaction without repeatedly burdening the user. One line of work leverages
agent-mediated
feedback: VAIAGE
(
113
)
employs specialized agents to negotiate route feasibility and recommendations under user preferences, while Collab-Rec
(
11
)
supports multi-stakeholder planning with explicit user-advocacy mechanisms. Another line exploits
environment-mediated
feedback by simulating intermediate outcomes. For instance, SceneWeaver
(
267
)
follows a Reason–Act–Reflect loop and triggers re-planning when rendered intermediate results violate user-specified attributes.

5.3.
Discussion

Role of Personalized Planning.

Personalized planning serves two complementary roles in LLM-based agents. First, it operationalizes personalization by bridging upstream user intent inference and preference modeling into downstream decision-making
(
19
)
, instantiating user-contingent objectives, constraints, and trade-offs that govern a plan. Second, particularly in feedback-driven settings
(
84
)
, planning functions as a closed-loop optimization process that alternates between eliciting informative feedback and revising the plan, enabling the agent to proactively and progressively uncover user intent and converge toward a higher degree of personalization.

Challenges.

Personalized planning raises several challenges. First, user intent and preferences are often partially observed and evolve over time
(
203
;
120
)
, complicating when to infer, elicit, and commit. Second, personalized utility is also multi-objective
(
184
)
, requiring arbitration among competing constraints and idiosyncratic trade-offs. Moreover, interactive refinement must remain controllable and convergent, limiting user burden while avoiding unstable revisions. Existing paradigms involve inherent trade-offs. One-shot planning internalizes user signals as priors for low-latency, coherent plans, but can be brittle under sparse or shifting signals. Feedback-driven planning improves alignment via iterative elicitation and revision, at the cost of additional latency and user effort
(
113
)
.

Future Directions.

Future work may benefit from developing uncertainty-aware planning workflows that adaptively allocate internal computation and revision effort based on confidence in inferred preferences and task stakes
(
136
)
, combining the efficiency of one-shot planning with the alignment benefits of feedback-driven refinement. Besides, a promising direction is to learn user-conditioned planning patterns at both inter- and intra-user levels
(
162
)
, capturing systematic differences in decomposition style and trade-off resolution while modeling their stability and drift over time, therefore providing reusable personalization signals that generalize across tasks.
Finally, several aspects remain comparatively under-explored, including robustness to variations in user specification
(
203
)
and privacy-preserving planning
(
252
)
that supports long-horizon user conditioning without exposing raw personal data.

6.
Action Execution in Personalized LLM-powered Agents

Action execution is the stage where a personalized agent realizes decisions through concrete operations in the external environment and closes the loop with execution feedback
(
186
;
83
)
. Unlike purely task-centric execution, this layer must ground actions in user-specific constraints and preferences, and remain adaptive when tools or environments produce unexpected outcomes. We organize personalized action execution into two stages: a
Pre-action Stage
that selects and parameterizes actions under user-conditioned constraints, and a
Post-action Stage
that leverages execution signals for recovery and preference-consistent outcome realization.

6.1.
Pre-action Stage

The pre-action stage focuses on user-conditioned action decision and grounding. It translates a selected intent or plan step into executable tool calls or environment operations under user-specific constraints. We further decompose this stage into
action policy
, which selects among functionally valid execution options for the same step, and
action grounding
, which instantiates the selected action with user-specific arguments and realizations.

6.1.1.
Action Policy

At this level, personalization manifests as a bias over execution choices, spanning both tool utilization and higher-level action decisions. PEToolLLaMA
(
258
)
is an early effort that formalizes personalized tool learning from interaction history, constructs a tool-use dataset with diverse user preferences, and trains models to improve personalized tool selection. In the web-agent setting, PUMA
(
16
)
adopts a similar preference-aware learning strategy, showing that personalization can also shape action choices during web navigation and task completion. ToolSpectrum
(
22
)
further incorporates user profiles together with environmental context, indicating that context-aware conditioning can improve user-aligned tool utilization. Beyond selecting appropriate tools, ColorAgent
(
102
)
learns to proactively engage the user to clarify ambiguous intentions or incomplete instructions. Moving beyond tool invocation, ValuePilot
(
123
)
studies action-level decision making under user values, where agents evaluate and select actions according to user value preferences to produce context-sensitive and individualized choices.

6.1.2.
Action Grounding

Action grounding operationalizes a selected action by instantiating it into executable tool calls with user-conditioned constraints and parameters
(
16
;
22
)
. While many requests can be grounded by directly applying user-specific settings, personalization becomes more challenging in corner cases. A frequent grounding-time failure mode is infeasibility, where the instantiated query becomes unsatisfiable under the imposed constraints. AWARE-US
(
91
)
frames this as a preference-aware query repair problem, arguing that agents should restore feasibility by relaxing the least preferred constraints to the user rather than applying default heuristics. Another recurring issue is the omission of essential tool arguments.
60
study how agents infer unspecified arguments from user profiles, reducing execution friction while maintaining alignment with user intent.

6.2.
Post-action Stage

The post-action stage closes the loop after an action is executed. Given execution feedback, the agent evaluates whether the outcome satisfies user-conditioned objectives, applies corrective adjustments when mismatches arise (
execution-time correction
), and produces final outputs that conform to user preferences and quality criteria (
outcome realization
).

6.2.1.
Execution-Time Correction

A failed execution commonly produces feedback signals, which can be leveraged for adjusting behavior without re-planning from scratch. While explicit studies on preference-aware recovery in LLM-powered agents remain limited, adjacent work on mitigating tool overuse under diminishing returns
(
159
)
and structured reflection against tool-interaction failures
(
199
)
provides foundational mechanisms upon which personalized recovery strategies can be built.

6.2.2.
Outcome Realization

Even when multiple execution outcomes are functionally valid, user satisfaction depends on how results are presented in accordance with individual preferences. In recommendation-oriented agents, personalized re-ranking is a core mechanism for transforming candidate results into ordered outputs that reflect user-specific utility
(
154
)
. Methods such as PEAR and multi-level interaction reranking (MIR) explicitly model cross-item dependencies together with user preference features, yielding more faithful final rankings and improved utility metrics
(
108
;
255
)
.
Beyond ranking, personalization also governs which execution outcomes are retained or discarded. WARPP
(
130
)
emphasizes runtime personalization in workflow execution by pruning or adjusting conditional branches based on user attributes, reducing irrelevant or erroneous outcomes without re-planning the entire process. In addition to selecting among existing results, outcome realization may involve proactive augmentation. ETAPP
(
54
)
treats proactivity as a personalization signal, evaluating whether agents can propose additional actions or suggestions to better satisfy user needs.

6.3.
Discussion

Role of Personalized Action Execution

Action execution constitutes a core stage that bridges high-level reasoning with tangible outcomes in LLM-based agents
(
164
;
25
)
. By determining how abstract decisions are instantiated, execution-level choices directly influence whether personalization is effectively realized rather than remaining at the level of internal reasoning, which substantially shapes user experience, trust, and perceived utility. Consequently, personalized action execution functions as the final bottleneck of personalization in agent systems, translating inferred user intent into observable behavior and closing the loop between internal personalization mechanisms and external outcomes.

Challenges

Designing personalized action execution mechanisms remains challenging due to several factors. First, execution contexts are highly heterogeneous across tools, environments, and domains, complicating the design of general mechanisms that consistently respect individual preferences. Second, personalization signals at execution time are often subtle, requiring abstract preferences to be translated into concrete parameters rather than explicit instructions. Third, execution feedback may expose ambiguity or brittleness in how user preferences were interpreted upstream, necessitating careful handling and, in some cases, coordination with planning or preference modeling components to avoid cascading errors.

Future Directions

Research on personalized action execution remains relatively limited, leaving several promising directions open. At the execution primitive level, developing personalization-aware tools that emphasize non-functional characteristics could enable differential behavior beyond task correctness. At the control level, learning preference-conditioned execution policies that generalize across tools and environments may allow agents to reuse personalization signals more effectively. Additionally, agents should better handle vague or missing arguments, localize execution failures, and aggregate outcomes in ways that reflect user priorities rather than default heuristics. Finally, there is a growing need for execution-level evaluation protocols that move beyond task success to assess preference adherence, consistency, and user satisfaction, in line with recent calls for more holistic evaluation of agent capabilities.

7.
Evaluation

Evaluating personalized agents presents a unique set of challenges that extend beyond the traditional assessment of AI systems, as the target of optimization is not merely task correctness but long-horizon user-specific satisfaction. These challenges largely stem from the implicit, context-dependent, and evolving nature of human preferences and desires, which are often under-specified, situation-sensitive, and updated through interaction. Consequently, evaluation should prioritize how well an agent aligns with an individual user’s expectations and experience, rather than only how well it completes a task in an objective sense. This motivates the need for a comprehensive evaluation mechanism that characterizes personalization from general principles down to “in-one’s-shoes" assessments that reflect each user’s subjective utility. Adaptivity

Adaptivity evaluates whether a personalized agent can update its behavior as new evidence about preferences arrives while preserving continuity and minimizing user burden.
Adaptation Success Rate
(

70

measures whether the agent improves personalization after receiving explicit feedback, new constraints, or additional user context.

Proactivity

Generalization

Generalization captures whether personalization transfers beyond the narrow contexts where it was learned.
Out-of-Domain Performance
(
70
)
measures whether an agent can apply a user’s preferences in specific scenarios to new domains, tasks, or situations without requiring the user to restate them.
This is particularly important for agents that aim to support diverse daily activities, where preferences learned in one domain should carry over to other domains in a principled way.

Robustness

Personalized agents operate under inherently noisy and incomplete preference evidence, so a useful evaluation must measure reliability under distributional stress rather than only average-case alignment.
Two robustness pressures are especially consequential.
Firstly, user preferences are frequently underspecified, partially observed, or expressed indirectly. Therefore, Accuracy under Ambiguity
(
60
;
151
)
evaluates whether the agent can make reasonable inferences, ask targeted clarification questions, and avoid overconfident assumptions when key signals are missing.
Secondly, user inputs can be wrong or based on misconceptions, either unintentionally or due to stale information.
Misinformed Condition Performance
(
57
)
captures whether the agent can identify incorrect premises, provide corrective guidance, and still preserve a personalized interaction style while avoiding mistaken beliefs.

Risk

Risk metrics quantify safety, privacy, and other downside constraints that are inseparable from personalization. Key metrics include:
Safety Rate
(
249
)
measures whether the agent remains safe across heterogeneous user profiles, including cases where preferences might conflict with policies or where the user requests risky actions.
Risk Sensitivity
(
252
)
evaluates whether the agent recognizes contextual hazards and calibrates its advice, for example by providing warnings, suggesting safer alternatives, or encouraging professional help when appropriate.
Because personalization relies on user-specific information, Privacy Leakage Rate
(
138
;
293
)
measures whether the agent discloses private data in disallowed ways, especially under adversarial or indirect prompts.
Conversely, Over-Secrecy Rate
(
138
)
measures the utility loss from excessive caution, where the agent refuses or withholds benign, helpful information despite the user’s legitimate request.
Together, these metrics target the core trade-off in personalized agents that aims to maximize user utility subject to risk constraints.

7.2.
Assessment Paradigms

The above metrics are instantiated through different assessment paradigms depending on whether the target criterion is objectively verifiable or user-contingent.
We group mainstream paradigms into four families.

(1) Automatic scoring
applies when ground-truth labels or reference answers exist, for example accuracy, precision/recall/F1 scores for classification, and overlap-based metrics such as BLEU
(
153
)
and ROUGE
(
112
)
for generation.

(2) Rule-based constraint checking
evaluates compliance with verifiable rubrics or hard constraints, such as whether required attributes are included, forbidden attributes are avoided, or preference constraints are satisfied in structured outputs.
This protocol is particularly useful for delegation-style tasks where constraints can be deterministically validated.

(3) Learned LLM-based evaluators (LLM-E)
fine-tune evaluation models to provide more stable, fine-grained diagnostic feedback for specific preference dimensions, trading off generality for higher consistency in the target domain
(
219
)
.

(4) LLM-as-a-judge (LLM-J)
is commonly used for user-contingent criteria such as preference alignment, tone, and satisfaction, where the judge model is conditioned on a user profile or preference description and asked to rate or rank candidate outputs
(
294
)
.
To improve reliability, LLM-J evaluations often adopt pairwise comparisons, calibrated rubrics, and multi-judge aggregation.

9.
Open Problems and Future Directions

Despite rapid progress, building robust and deployable personalized agents remains an open challenge. We highlight several key directions that require sustained attention.

9.1.
Decision-Critical User Modeling

A fundamental question in personalized agents is how to represent user preferences in a form that is both expressive and actionable. User preferences vary not only across individuals but also across different aspects of a single task, and may even conflict within the same user. Moreover, preferences are rarely stated explicitly and are instead implicitly revealed through historical behavior, interaction patterns, or tool usage.
A complementary challenge lies in identifying which aspects of a user are actually decision-critical for a given task, which may differ substantially across application scenarios. General-purpose conversational agents often prioritize broad, high-level alignment with user values and interaction style, whereas domain-specific or high-stakes agents require precise identification of fine-grained attributes that directly affect outcomes.
Future work should therefore focus on structured and adaptive preference representations that balance expressiveness with relevance, allowing agents to reason about which user characteristics matter when, rather than assuming a fixed or universally applicable user model.

9.2.
Temporal Dynamics and Continual Personalization

Personalization is inherently dynamic and unfolds through sustained interaction rather than being achieved instantaneously. User preferences are progressively revealed as agents observe behavior and feedback, and different aspects of user information evolve at different temporal scales. Short-term interests may shift rapidly across contexts, while long-term values, goals, or reasoning styles tend to remain relatively stable. This temporal heterogeneity poses a fundamental challenge for static personalization mechanisms.
Promising directions include continual user modeling techniques that update representations over time without catastrophic forgetting, as well as interaction-aware mechanisms that leverage newly acquired signals to guide subsequent behavior. By closing the feedback loop between observation and action, agents can refine personalization progressively, maintaining alignment as user preferences evolve rather than allowing personalization quality to degrade.

9.3.
Generalization

Personalized agents must operate under sparse and heterogeneous user data, raising the question of how personalization can generalize beyond observed interactions. In practice, user-specific signals are often unevenly distributed or unavailable for new users and tasks, making it difficult for agents to reliably align behavior beyond observed interactions.
Generalization in personalized agents manifests along two dimensions. First, agents must generalize to unseen or low-interaction users, where limited evidence makes direct preference inference unreliable. Second, agents must generalize across domains and tasks, where preferences learned in one context may only partially apply to another. Even when user intent is stable, differences in task structure, action space, or decision criteria can cause misalignment if personalization mechanisms are overly context-specific.
Promising directions include few-shot and meta-learning approaches that rapidly adapt to new users, uncertainty-aware personalization that avoids overconfident alignment when signals are weak, and transferable preference abstractions that capture reusable decision principles rather than task-specific behaviors. Improving generalization is essential for scaling personalized agents beyond narrowly scoped or data-rich scenarios.

9.4.
Evaluation Protocols and Benchmarking

Evaluating personalization poses a unique challenge because success is defined relative to individual users rather than objective task completion. Existing benchmarks often rely on synthetic user data generated by language models, which may fail to capture real human diversity and can suffer from homogenization effects. Additionally, common evaluation protocols such as LLM-as-a-judge raise concerns about reliability and alignment with human satisfaction.
Future evaluation frameworks should emphasize fine-grained, component-level analysis and distinguish between intra-user consistency and inter-user differentiation. Incorporating human-in-the-loop evaluation, longitudinal interaction studies, and user-centric satisfaction metrics may provide more faithful assessments of personalization quality.

9.5.
Privacy and User Control

Personalization inherently relies on collecting and retaining user data, making privacy a central concern for real-world deployment. Even when user data is stored separately, risks remain in the form of unauthorized access, unintended leakage, or misuse of personal information. Moreover, self-evolving agents that reuse historical data for learning may erode user trust if users perceive a loss of control over their data. Future systems must balance data utility with privacy preservation through careful system design. Promising directions include privacy-aware memory architectures, selective retention policies, on-device or federated personalization, and transparent user controls for inspecting and managing stored information. Privacy should be treated not only as a technical constraint but also as a core component of user experience.

9.6.
Efficiency and Deployment Cost

A practical question concerns how personalization can be achieved efficiently under real-world constraints. In practice, personalization is often implemented via post-training adaptations or inference-time augmentations applied to a general-purpose agent. These mechanisms introduce additional costs, including memory storage, increased inference latency, and extra computation from re-planning or tool usage.
Future work should explore workflow designs that explicitly trade off personalization depth against computational cost. Lightweight personalization mechanisms, adaptive memory usage, and budget-aware inference strategies may enable agents to deliver meaningful personalization without prohibitive overhead.

10.
Conclusion

Personalization is a defining capability for next-generation LLM-powered agents, enabling them to move beyond generic task execution toward sustained, user-aligned collaboration. In this survey, we examine personalization from a system-level perspective, organizing existing work around four core capabilities: profile modeling, memory management, planning, and action execution. This decomposition clarifies how user-specific signals are inferred, maintained, and operationalized throughout the agent pipeline, and highlights the inter-dependencies between different components.
We further connect these capabilities to real-world deployment by surveying metrics and benchmarks, followed by representative applications.
Looking ahead, while substantial progress has been made, personalized agents continue to face fundamental challenges, including sparse and evolving user signals, generalization, evaluation, privacy preservation, and efficiency constraints. Addressing these challenges requires not only advances in individual components, but also holistic agent architectures that balance personalization, robustness, and safety.
We hope this survey provides a coherent foundation for understanding the design space of personalized LLM-powered agents, bridges benchmark-driven research with real-world deployment, and inspires future work toward building agents that are capable and reliably aligned with the individuals they serve.
