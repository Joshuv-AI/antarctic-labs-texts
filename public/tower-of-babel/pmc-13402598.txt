Correlated singular flat bands on the surface pentagonal lattice of ferromagnetic CoS2
Authors: Yuting Qian, Younsik Kim, Du Hyung Kim, Taekoo Oh, Yoonseok Hwang, Wonshik Kyung, Junseo Yoo, Sehoon Kim, Suyoung Lee, Hyunjee Song, Mi Kyung Kim, Cheng-Maw Cheng, Min-Seok Kim, Se Young Park, Changming Yue, Jungpil Seo, Changyoung Kim, Bohm-Jung Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flat bands have been proposed in various geometrically frustrated lattices, yet their experimental realization has been limited only to the two-dimensional (2D) kagome and three-dimensional (3D) pyrochlore lattices. While synthesizing bulk crystals with desired lattice geometry is generally challenging, the surfaces of 3D bulk materials sometimes naturally host 2D lattice structures hardly achievable in 2D bulk crystals. Thus, the electrons localized on such 2D surfaces can be a new platform for exotic flat bands residing on the unexplored lattice geometry. Here, we report the first experimental observation of singular flat bands with band crossings in the 2D pentagonal lattice on the surface of the 3D ferromagnetic topological semimetal CoS2. We demonstrate that the coupling between localized surface flat bands and extended bulk topological bands gives rise to the surface non-Fermi liquid behavior characterized by the quasiparticle scattering rate with linear temperature dependence and pronounced quasiparticle broadening. This study of CoS2 highlights metallic pyrite materials as an ideal platform for exploring the exotic properties of spin-polarized singular flat bands localized on the surface pentagonal lattice, which provides a fresh perspective for investigating correlated electron phenomena in pentagon-based materials.

Introduction

Flat band materials have recently become a compelling platform for exploring magnetism, superconductivity, and other strong correlation phenomena1,2. Crystalline flat bands have been theoretically predicted in various geometrically frustrated lattices1,3,4, yet their experimental realization was achieved quite recently only in two different types of lattice structures, i.e. the two-dimensional (2D) kagome lattices5–8 and the three-dimensional (3D) pyrochlore lattices9–11. Since crystal synthesis with desired lattice geometries is generally challenging, finding alternative material platforms hosting distinct lattice geometries beyond the kagome or pyrochlore type motif is quintessential to realize the unexplored exotic flat band physics predicted in various geometrically frustrated lattices4.

An intriguing possibility comes from 2D pentagonal lattices, which can also host partially degenerate flat bands protected by non-symmorphic symmetry12–14. However, it is demanding to realize 2D pentagon lattices due to the pentagonal tiling rules12,15–18. Most of the theoretically predicted 2D pentagon materials are found to be metastable and difficult to synthesize experimentally. Only a few pentagonal 2D materials, such as PdSe2, PdPSe/S, NiN2, and metastable monolayer PdTe2 have been synthesized17,19–23. Moreover, the flat band issue has never been experimentally addressed in this group of materials, to our best knowledge.

An alternative route lies in exploiting the surfaces of 3D crystals, which can naturally host 2D pentagonal lattices. Since the symmetry of a 2D surface is often lower than that of a 2D bulk crystal17,18, and the surface lattice usually interacts with the underlying bulk, the surface pentagonal lattice can exhibit even new physical phenomena unachievable in regular 2D pentagonal lattice systems24–27. Considering the abundance of available 3D bulk materials with pentagonal surfaces17,18,28–33, experimental demonstration of surface pentagonal lattices and the corresponding characteristic surface band structure can open a new avenue for studying flat band physics beyond the kagome and pyrochlore structure-based materials.

In this Article, by combining angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) and first-principles calculation results from a spin-orbit coupled ferromagnetic pyrite CoS2 (see Fig. 1a–c), we report the first experimental observation of a singular flat band with band crossings in a pentagonal lattice, formed on the surface of a 3D crystal1,34–38. Remarkably, we find that the surface flat band overlaps with the topological bulk bands (Fig. 1d, f) across a large momentum space (Fig. 1e), which dominates the surface properties among different surface states and induces strong correlation behavior (Fig. 1g)11,39. Our observation of singular flat bands and non-Fermi liquid behavior in the surface pentagonal lattice of CoS2 highlights metallic pyrite as a new platform for exploring flat band physics and strong correlation effects in 2D pentagonal lattice systems. More generally, our mechanism for exploring flat-band phenomena on the surface pentagonal lattice of a 3D bulk crystal can be broadly applicable to a wide class of materials with similar surface geometries. Just as the discovery of flat bands in kagome lattices galvanized widespread interest and sparked a wave of experimental and theoretical advances4,40–43, our demonstration of flat-band physics in a previously unexplored pentagonal lattice geometry opens a new frontier in flat-band research and is poised to inspire future investigations into novel quantum phases emerging from lattice geometry and electron correlations.Fig. 1Bulk and surface properties of ferromagnetic CoS2.a The magnetic primitive cell of CoS2 where the arrows indicate ferromagnetic spin structure. b Temperature dependent magnetization curve of CoS2. c Possible terminations of the surface. Bulk and surface crystal structures

Figure 1a shows the cubic primitive unit cell of CoS2 with the lattice constant a = 5.539 Å determined experimentally. Without magnetic ordering, CoS2 has the space group 205 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Pa\overline{3}$$\end{document}Pa3¯). Below 120 K, CoS2 develops ferromagnetism with tiny spin anisotropy, resulting in a highly soft magnetic nature. Figure 1b shows the temperature dependence of the magnetization curve as a function of the external magnetic field in CoS2. The saturated moment of μs = 0.94 μB/Co at 2 K is consistent with the reported data44. Hereafter, we assume a ferromagnetic order parallel to c axis44–49, then the corresponding magnetic space group is 61.436 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P{b}^{{\prime} }{c}^{{\prime} }a$$\end{document}Pb′c′a)50 (see Supplementary Note 1-2 for more details).

In each primitive cell, each Co atom is surrounded by an octahedron formed by six S atoms, and each S atom is surrounded by a tetrahedron composed of three Co atoms and one S atom as shown in Fig. 1a. An important aspect of the structure is that the characteristics of the surface state (SS) strongly depend on the property of sample cleavage planes. There are three kinds of surface terminations as shown in the left panel of Fig. 1c: 1S-termination, 2S-termination and Co-termination, referring to the types of atoms exposed on the surface (see Supplementary Note 3 in detail). Combined with STM measurement, we confirm that 1S-termination is the only experimentally obtained termination in our sample (detailed in the next section). Specifically, 1S-termination means one layer of sulfur atoms exposed on the surface, where a surface Co atom is surrounded by a square pyramid pentahedron of S, contrary to the bulk Co atom coordinated by S-octahedron. Due to the broken S-octahedron, another surface state emerges on top of the Fermi arc states51. This surface state, referred to as the crystal-field-splitting surface state (CFSS), induced by broken local crystal field forms a nearly flat band closely linked to the unique structure of the 1S-termination. That is, the topmost surface layer of 1S-termination is a pentagonal lattice, where Co atoms are fourfold-coordinated, while S atoms are threefold-coordinated, forming a typical type-2 pentagon as building blocks16, as shown in the right panel of Fig. 1c.

STM observation of 1S-termination

Considering the importance of the surface termination, it is natural to experimentally confirm the dominance of the 1S-termination via STM measurements on CoS2 single crystals cleaved along the (001) direction. Figure 2a and b present the wide-range topography and corresponding height profile, respectively. They show an atomically flat surface with a step of around 5.4 Å, which is close to the lattice constant of CoS2. We identify that the cleaved surface of CoS2 is 1S-termination, as this termination forms a charge neutral plane, as indicated in the inset of Fig. 2a. The 1S-termination is evident in the fine-scale topography shown in Fig. 2c, which exhibits the zigzag chain structure with an anisotropic arrangement along a specific direction as depicted by the orange line in Fig. 2c. Even though the 2S-termination also possesses a similar chain, it is very unlikely that the surface is of the single 2S-termination because Co- and 2S-terminations are cleaving counterparts; when a surface has 2S-termination, the opposing cleaved surface has Co-termination, meaning both terminations should occur with equal probability. Given our observation of a single termination over a wide range (Fig. 2a, b), we can confirm that the 1S-termination is the dominant surface termination. The experimental features from surface bands along the kx direction are qualitatively reproduced in the calculations from CFSSs (Fig. 3d,f) with the surface states renormalized by a sizable factor of 2, while the bulk bands are not renormalized. We note that the observed spectral weight shown near the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{M}$$\end{document}M¯ point at the Fermi level in Fig. 3d,e originates from bulk bands. Further experiments along the ky direction (see \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{\Gamma }-\bar{Y}$$\end{document}Γ¯−Ȳ in Supplementary Fig. 2) show that DFT requires a strong renormalization of approximately 6 to compare with the observed surface band dispersions. We attribute the direction-dependent renormalization to the anisotropic surface relaxation. After accounting for surface relaxation effects, the renormalization factor is expected to become more isotropic, with a value of approximately 3~4, providing strong evidence for significant electron correlations (see ref. 54 and Supplementary Note 3 for detailed discussions). Considering the overall consistency between the ARPES results and calculated DFT band structures, the CFSS can perfectly explain the experimentally observed surface states. We note that some parts of the calculated Fermi surfaces near the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{M}$$\end{document}M¯ point are not well resolved, which can be attributed to the suppressed bandwidth and the limited energy resolution of synchrotron-based measurements.

On the other hand, the upper dispersive band of the CFSSs also exhibits a significantly suppressed bandwidth of around 100 meV, typically classified as a flat band, considering that reported flat bands in kagome and pyrochlore systems extend up to several hundred meV6,7,9. Given the small bandwidth and sizable renormalization factor, significant correlation effects are expected. To investigate these correlation effects, we performed temperature-dependent high-resolution ARPES measurements near the Γ point using a 7 eV laser, as illustrated in Fig. 3g. The ARPES spectrum at 6 K shows a sharp structure near the Fermi level, which becomes rapidly incoherent at higher temperatures. This behavior is well captured in the extracted quasiparticle (QP) line widths shown in Fig. 3h (see Supplementary Note 6 for details of the fittings). The extracted QP line widths, which reflect the QP scattering rate, exhibit a linear-T dependence at low temperatures which is unexpected in Fermi liquids55. In addition, the QP line widths exhibit pronounced quasiparticle broadening, a behavior generally observed in strongly correlated materials55,56. We confirmed that the broadening is not caused by simple surface degradation, as verified by temperature cycling measurements (see Supplementary Note 1). Considering that the bulk states of CoS2 exhibit Fermi liquid behavior in the ferromagnetic phase (see Supplementary Fig. 5), the CFSS of CoS2 is distinct from bulk, characterized by strong correlations, and non-Fermi liquid behavior. Additionally, we note that the small yet sharp feature near kx = 0.5π/a, also visible in Fig. 3g, can be attributed to the chiral SSs (see Fig. 3b, Supplementary Notes 1 and 7).

The ARPES results indicate that the CFSS extends over a wide momentum range, far beyond the limited regions where topological Fermi arcs appear. As a result, the global surface band structure of CoS2 is overwhelmingly governed by the CFSS (see details in Supplementary Note 4). This identification was made possible by systematic measurements along multiple high-symmetry lines, with improved resolution.

CFSS as a singular flat band

Let us describe the characteristics of CFSSs dominating the surface energy spectrum of CoS2. As mentioned above, in the bulk crystal of CoS2, each Co atom is surrounded by an S-octahedron, which splits the Co 3d orbitals into eg and t2g states. In the case of 1S-termination, the S-octahedron of a surface Co atom is broken into a tetragonal form as described in Fig. 1a,c, which induces a large on-site energy change of surface Co atoms and further splits the degenerate eg and t2g orbitals. Specifically, the broken S-octahedron with 5 sulfur atoms has C4v point group in which t2g-orbitals split into dxy singlet and {dxz, dyz} doublet while eg-orbitals split into nondegenerate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{{x}^{2}-{y}^{2}}$$\end{document}dx2−y2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{{z}^{2}}$$\end{document}dz2 orbitals57, as shown in Fig. 4b. The splitting of surface Co d-orbitals is confirmed by calculating average energy from PDOS as shown in Fig. 4a. The broken Co eg orbitals combined with sulfur p-orbitals give rise to energy defect states, similar to those in FeS258. We propose that the defect states are exactly the CFSSs extending across the entire BZ near the Fermi level and E = − 0.9 eV, respectively. Discussion

Together, the theoretical framework and the experimental observations obtained through ARPES and STM offer new insight into flat band physics in the surface pentagonal lattice. The broken S-octahedron crystal field induces surface states confined on the surface pentagonal lattice, which host spin-polarized singular flat bands (CFSSs). As CoS2 is a ferromagnetic topological semimetal with various topological nodal structures, both topological surface states and CFSSs are expected upon cleaving the 1S-terminated surface. Notably, our high-resolution laser-based ARPES measurements evidently resolve the Fermi arc state (see Supplementary Fig. 2), chiral states as well as the CFSS. According to our study, CFSSs dominate the global surface spectrum in large momentum regions, while the role of the Fermi arc merely induces slight modulation of the CFSSs, refining the previous understanding (see ref. 51 and detailed discussion in Supplementary Note 5).

As a surface localized singular flat band, the CFSS, residing on the surface pentagonal lattice and interacting with the bulk band, introduces many intriguing properties on the surface. Due to the reduced symmetry of the surface pentagonal lattice, a significant anisotropic renormalization factor is required to align with DFT and ARPES data. Moreover, the high renormalization factor points to strong correlation effects, consistent with the observed suppression of the coherent energy scale, possibly arising from Kondo-like coupling between the surface flat band and bulk conduction band. Our findings of CoS2 highlight metallic pyrite materials as an ideal platform for studying the interplay of topology, magnetism, and correlation effects through the interactions between localized surface flat bands and extended bulk topological bands.

Beyond the pyrite CoS2, a broad range of pentagon-based bulk materials17,28,29 exists (see details in Supplementary Note 10), including elemental systems, binary compounds, and ternary compounds, with examples such as T12-carbon12, 3D penta-graphene61, FeS229, PdSe219, and PdPS62. These materials have strong potential to host pentagonal surface lattices and encompass a wide variety of crystal structures, from high-symmetry cubic phases to low-symmetry monoclinic systems17,63, demonstrating remarkable structural diversity. Many of these materials exhibit intriguing physical properties, including magnetism, ferroelectricity, and electrocatalytic activity17,18. Despite the diversity of pentagon-based bulk materials, the connection between their physical properties and the underlying pentagonal geometry remains largely unexplored64–68. In particular, whether the geometric frustration inherent in pentagonal networks can manifest in their surface electronic states and influence surface magnetism remains an intriguing open question. Our work thus provides a conceptual framework and experimental route to explore such pentagon-induced surface phenomena in a broad class of three-dimensional materials.

Methods

Ab initio calculations

The first-principles calculations were performed with the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) package69,70 based on the density functional theory (DFT). We used the crystal structure of CoS2 obtained from our experiment without structural relaxation, i.e., a = b = c = 5.539 Å. Crystal structures were plotted by VESTA package71. Since CoS2 is an itinerant ferromagnet31 described by Stoner model, we employed the local spin-density approximation (LSDA) without Hubbard U correction to obtain DFT band structure as in previous works31,50,51. Instead of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) method overestimating the band gap, LSDA can well reproduce the electronic pocket near the R point51. The kinetic energy cutoff was set to 500 eV. An 8 × 8 × 8 k-point mesh for Brillouin zone sampling was adopted in the self-consistent calculation. We construct a Wannier tight-binding model based on Co d-orbitals and S p-orbitals by Wannier90 package72. Then band structures with and without spin-orbit coupling are obtained by the Wannier Hamiltonian after fitting with DFT as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. WannierTools73 was employed to calculate surface states by Green’s function method74. The surface-resolved slab band structures and PDOS in Fig. 4, as well as the orbital-resolved slab band structure in Supplementary Fig. 20, are calculated using a 20-layer slab Hamiltonian with local coordinates centered on cobalt atoms.

Crystal growth

CoS2 single crystals were grown by a chemical vapor transport method with Br gas as a transport agent75. A mixture of cobalt powder (0.452 g, 99.9%), sulfur powder (0.521 g, 99.98%), and CoBr2 beads (0.1 g, 99.99%) was sealed into an evacuated quartz tube and placed in a two-zone furnace. The hot and cold zone of the furnace was maintained at 720 ∘C and 600 ∘C, respectively, for 170 h. The grown single crystals are several millimeters in size.

Synchrotron ARPES

Synchrotron-based ARPES measurements were performed at beamlines (BLs) 4.0.3, 7.0.2 of the Advanced Light Source, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, USA and BL 39A1 of Taiwan Photon Source, National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center, Taiwan. Spectra were acquired using Scienta R8000 (BL 4.0.3), R4000 (BL 7.0.2), and DA30 (BL 39A1) electron analyzers. Samples were cleaved in situ at 20 K (BLs 4.0.3, 7.0.2, 39A1) in an ultra-high vacuum better than 5 × 10−11 Torr (BLs 4.0.3, 7.0.2) and 3 × 10−10 Torr (BL 39A1). The energy resolutions were set to better than 30 meV (BLs 4.0.3, 7.0.2), and 15 meV (BL 39A1). All synchrotron-based ARPES measurements were conducted at 20 K unless otherwise noted.

Laser ARPES

High-resolution, laser-based ARPES measurements were performed using a lab-based system at Seoul National University. A home-built Yb-doped fiber laser with a center wavelength of 1064 nm, a pulse width of 50 ps and a repetition rate of 1.5 MHz was used to generate 7 eV (177 nm) light. Spectra were acquired using a Scienta ARTOF 10 k electron analyzer with p-polarized light. Samples were cleaved in situ along the (001) direction at 6 K in an ultra-high vacuum better than 6 × 10−11 Torr. The energy resolution was set to better than 1.5 meV. The beam spot size of the 7 eV light is approximately 40 μm × 30 μm. A closed-loop sample position autocorrection system is applied to compensate for thermal drift during the temperature-dependent measurements76.

STM

The STM measurements were conducted by a home-built STM working at 4.3 K. A piece of CoS2 sample was glued on the sample holder using a conductive epoxy (EPOTEC, H20E). A cleaving post was then mounted on the CoS2 sample using a non-conductive epoxy (EPOTEC, H74F). These epoxy glues generally provided enough adhesion for the cleavage of the sample. The sample was then transferred into the ultra-high vacuum (UHV) chamber whose pressure was lower than 1 × 10−10 torr. The sample was mechanically cleaved in the UHV chamber to expose a pristine surface. Cleaving the sample at room temperature and cryogenic temperature (20 K) did not make a significant difference in the STM images. The differential conductance (dI/dV) spectra were measured using a standard lock-in technique with a modulation frequency (fmod) of 716.3 Hz.
